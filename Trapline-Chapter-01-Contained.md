# Trapline

## Chapter One: Contained

---

### I. False Chanterelles

*Forks, Washington — Monday, October 2, 2006*

Seth Clearwater had his mother's list in one hand, three cartons of eggs stacked in the crook of his other arm, and a moral problem in aisle six.

The list was written on the back of a power-bill envelope in Sue Clearwater's small, slanted capitals. MILK (2). EGGS (3 DOZ). BUTTER. FLOUR — THE BIG BAG. GROUND BEEF, 10 LB. POTATOES. ONIONS. CHARLIE'S COFFEE, THE RED CAN. PAPER TOWELS. And at the bottom, underlined twice: *DO NOT BUY THE CEREAL WITH THE MARSHMALLOWS.*

She'd handed it to him at the front door that morning, along with her car keys and three twenties folded into a tight square.

"Ten pounds of ground beef," she'd said, "is for the house. It's for meals. It is not for you to eat in the parking lot."

"That was one time."

"It was raw, Seth."

"I'd been running all night. I was basically still a wolf."

She'd shut the door in his face. She'd been smiling when she did it, which was new, and good, and which Seth had decided not to think about too hard, because the smile had a lot to do with Charlie Swan, and thinking about his mom and Chief Swan in any kind of detail made him want to go lie down in the middle of Highway 110.

So: the Thriftway on Forks Avenue, a Monday afternoon in October, rain hanging against the big front windows, more mist than drops. Floor wax, the bakery case, the produce misters hissing on a timer. Somebody's kid crying over by the magazines with real commitment.

A woman in a Forks Spartans sweatshirt looked at Seth's bare arms, then at the window, then back at his arms, the way people in Forks always looked at him now. It was forty-eight degrees outside. He was in a T-shirt, cutoffs, and sneakers with no socks, and still a couple degrees too hot. He gave her a friendly nod. She found something very interesting on the cereal shelf.

The cereal shelf was the problem.

The box was right there at eye level, bright purple, a cartoon leprechaun grinning on the front. Seth looked at it. He looked at the list. *DO NOT.* He thought about the fact that he had turned eighteen in August and was legally an adult who could make his own breakfast decisions. He thought about the time his mother had found his Halloween candy stash under the bed and made him eat all of it in front of her until he was sick, and had said, very calmly, "Now you know."

He left the leprechaun where it was.

He was in a good mood. He'd been in a good mood for about three weeks, which was roughly how long Renesmee Cullen had been alive.

Three weeks ago his whole world had been a couple of hours from a war. Sam's pack had been ready to go through the Cullens' front door to get at the thing growing inside Bella Swan, and Seth and Leah had walked out on Sam to stand with Jacob. Then Bella had died and then she hadn't, and the thing inside her had turned out to be a baby girl with bronze curls and Charlie Swan's chin, and Jacob had looked at that baby and imprinted so hard he'd practically fallen over, and that had been the end of the war. Wolves didn't hurt an imprint. It was the oldest law they had.

Now Bella Swan was Bella Cullen, and a vampire, and so far she hadn't eaten anybody. Jacob spent his days lying on the Cullens' living room floor making faces at a baby who could already put her hand on your cheek and show you a picture in your own head, clear as TV. Esme cooked for the wolves now. Emmett had promised to teach Seth to play poker the vampire way, which involved a lot of lying and no blinking. And Edward was his friend. Seth had never had a friend who was a hundred and five years old before. It was cool. Edward thought it was funny that Seth thought it was cool, but Seth could tell he liked it.

So everything was fine. Seth liked it when everything was fine. It didn't happen as often as it should.

He went to find potatoes.

In the produce section, under a hand-lettered card that said LOCAL CHANTERELLES $8.99/LB, a woman was standing at a wooden bin of wild mushrooms.

She was holding one of the chanterelles up to the light, turning it over, frowning at it.

"Uh-uh," she said to it. "Nope. Who did this."

Seth looked around for whoever she was talking to. There wasn't anybody.

She picked up another one, flipped it, and made a small disgusted noise in her throat. Then she started sorting—one into her left palm, the next one back into the bin, the next into her palm. She had the focused, irritated look of somebody fixing a thing that wasn't her job to fix.

He should have walked past. He was on a mission involving onions. But noticing smells was a wolf thing, it didn't turn off, and she smelled like crushed leaves, and wet ground, and woodsmoke, and under all of it something sharp and clean—the smell the air got off First Beach a second before lightning hit the water.

"Are they bad?" Seth said.

"Some of 'em." She didn't look up. Her accent was southern, rounder and softer than the TV kind, a little French around the edges. "Somebody mixed false chanterelles in with the real ones. Look—" She held two up side by side, one in each hand. "This one's got ridges underneath. Blunt, kind of run together, like melted wax. That's what you want. This one's got real gills. Thin. Crowded. Too orange. Looks right from the top. You eat enough of it, you're gonna have a terrible night."

"Like, die?"

"Like wish you had."

Then she looked up.

It was a small thing, the way she did it. Chin first, then her eyes, which were brown with a thin ring of green around the pupil, like the edge of a creek where the moss starts. She looked a little annoyed. She was about to say something else about mushrooms.

And every line that held Seth Clearwater to the ground let go.

He'd heard Jacob try to explain it. Jared had tried, and Quil, and Paul once, badly. Sam never talked about it at all. None of them had gotten close.

Every string that tied him to his life—to his mom's kitchen, to the pack, to the beach at La Push, to the sound of his dad's truck that he still listened for sometimes even though his dad had been dead going on seven months, to his own name—went slack at once, the way a kite string goes slack in the second before the wind turns. For one long, weightless moment there was nothing holding him anywhere.

Then all of them pulled tight again.

Every single one ran to her.

He was still holding the eggs. The top carton was sliding. He felt it go and couldn't make his arm do anything about it, because his arm was busy being part of a person who was looking at her.

Her right hand twitched toward her left sleeve—quick, a flick—and the carton stopped.

In midair. Eight inches above the floor.

It hung there, *stopped*, tilted a little, the lid half-popped, the way a thing in a photograph is stopped. It stayed like that for maybe half a second, long enough for Seth to see that it was staying like that, and then it dropped the rest of the way and landed flat on the linoleum with a soft little pat.

Seth looked at the eggs.

He looked at her.

She had gone gray under the freckles across her nose. Her hand was still in her sleeve.

"Good catch," she said.

Seth hadn't caught anything. He had the list in one hand and the other two cartons in his other arm.

"Did I—"

"You've got real good reflexes," she said firmly. She crouched, picked up the carton, and opened it. "Look at that. Not a single crack. That's a lucky carton." She stood and set it back on top of his stack. Her fingers brushed the inside of his wrist and she pulled them back fast. "Lord. You're burning up. Are you sick?"

"No," Seth said. "I just run hot."

"You run *hot*." She looked at the rain on the windows. "It's forty-eight degrees out there."

"Yeah. I know."

"And you're in shorts."

"I like shorts."

She looked at him for a long second, like he was a mushroom with gills where the ridges should be. Then something in her face gave a little, and she laughed—a short, surprised sound, mostly through her nose.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. You like shorts."

He needed to say something. He needed to say a lot of things, actually, and almost none of them were things you could say to a stranger in the produce section, so his mouth, without asking him, went with: "I'm Seth."

"Layla."

"Clearwater," he said. "Seth Clearwater."

"Broussard." Her eyebrows went up. "Clearwater. Like Creedence?"

"Everybody says that."

"Everybody's right to." She dumped the false chanterelles into a produce bag and knotted it. "I'm gonna tell the man at the register so nobody ends up in the hospital tonight. Then I'm buying the good ones, because I've got a pound of butter at home with nothing to do."

"You're not from here," Seth said, and then wanted to kick himself, because obviously.

"Chalmette. Outside New Orleans."

He knew the name from somewhere. It took a second. "The hurricane."

"Yeah." Something went flat in her voice and stayed there. "My mama's house had eight feet of water in it. She's up in Baton Rouge with my aunt now. I needed—" She lifted a shoulder. "Someplace that wasn't there."

"So you picked Forks."

"I picked the wettest town in the lower forty-eight." The corner of her mouth went up. "I'm aware of the irony."

"What do you do?"

"Survey work. Mosses, lichens, fungi. I count things and write down where they grow."

"For, like, a college?"

There was a flicker—small, fast, but he'd been watching her face like it was the only thing in the building, so he saw it. "For a lab," she said. "A private one."

"There's a lot of moss around here," Seth said.

"There is *so much* moss."

"I could show you some. Moss, I'm useless at. Woods, I know. And the beaches at La Push have tide pools. Sea stars, anemones, those little crabs that—" He made a pinching motion with the hand that had the list in it. "Moss on the rocks, too, probably."

She studied him. He made himself stand still while she did it. It was the hardest thing he'd done in weeks, and three weeks ago he'd been ready to fight his own pack.

"How old are you, Seth Clearwater?"

"Eighteen."

"Uh-huh."

"Eighteen in August. I have a license. It's out in the car."

"I'm twenty-two."

"Okay," Seth said.

"That doesn't bother you?"

He thought about it honestly, because she'd asked him honestly. "Should it?"

She laughed again, and this time she didn't seem to mean to. She dug in a pocket of her canvas coat—a man's coat, waxed and too big for her—and came out with a ballpoint pen. Then she reached over and took his wrist, the one holding the eggs, steadied it, and wrote seven numbers on the back of his hand, with a 360 in front of them.

Her fingers were cold. His skin was so hot the ink went on a little shiny.

"Tide pools," she said. "As friends. I don't know a single person in this whole county except the man at the hardware store, and he only talks about tarps."

"Friends is good," Seth said. "Friends is great."

"Don't wash that off."

"I'm never washing this hand."

"You're gonna wash the hand, Seth."

"Maybe around the numbers."

She shook her head at him, still smiling, picked out a pound of good chanterelles one by one, checking every underside, then gave him a little two-fingered wave and went off toward the registers, a pencil stuck through the knot of dark hair at the back of her head, rubber boots squeaking on the floor.

Seth watched her go until a man in a Thriftway apron came over and asked if he was finding everything okay.

"Yeah," Seth said. "Yeah. I think so."

He made it all the way out to the parking lot before he realized he had the eggs, the milk, the potatoes, and nothing else on the list. He went back in. He went back out. He went back in for the ground beef.

When he was finally sitting in his mother's car with the rain whispering on the roof, he looked at the back of his hand for a long time. Then he thought about phasing tonight—about how the second he went wolf, Leah would be in his head, and Jacob, and they'd get every bit of it, the mushrooms and the boots and the green ring around her eyes, before he'd even found the words for it himself.

Leah was going to be weird about it. Then Leah was going to kill him.

And Edward. Edward would hear it from the bottom of the driveway.

He thought about the eggs. He thought about the carton hanging in the air, tilted, the lid half-open, waiting.

Then he started the car, and he drove home very carefully, the way you drive when you're carrying something that matters.

### II. Pack Mind

*The Cullen side of the line — Monday, October 2, 10:41 p.m.*

Leah Clearwater was running the north loop when her brother phased in, and the first thing he brought with him was a girl's face.

It hit her like a door swinging into her muzzle. Fluorescent light. Orange mushrooms. Brown eyes with green at the center, looking up, annoyed—and then the thing that came after the looking. The lines snapping loose. The lines pulling taut. All of it pouring out of Seth's head into hers, because that was how the pack worked: no door to shut, nothing that was yours if you were wolf when you thought it.

Leah stumbled. Her front paw came down wrong on a root and she skidded in the wet fern and caught herself and kept running, because stopping would mean thinking about it.

She thought about it anyway.

Another face, another night. Sam standing in her mother's kitchen, two years ago, not looking at her. Sam's voice, careful and wrecked, saying her cousin's name like it had been carved into him. Emily. The way Sam had looked at Emily the first time—Leah had been in the room for it, she had *watched it happen*—and how she'd known, the way you know you've stepped off a curb you didn't see, that she was never going to be looked at like that again by anyone.

*Leah—* Seth's voice in her head was horrified. *Leah, I'm sorry, I'll stop, I can stop thinking about her—*

*You can't,* Leah thought. *So don't try. You'll just think about her harder.*

He tried anyway. It was like watching somebody try not to think about a polar bear. The girl's face came back four times in three seconds.

*Seth.*

*Sorry.*

*Seth, I'm not mad.* It was mostly true. She could feel him checking whether it was true—poking at it, the way he poked at everything—and she let him look, because there wasn't any choice, and because he was her little brother and he deserved to know that under the part of her that was bleeding there was another part that was so glad for him it hurt about the same.

Seth went quiet in that particular way that meant he'd seen it and didn't know what to do with it.

Then a third mind landed in the pack, heavy and bright and already laughing.

*WHOA.* Jacob. He'd phased out back of the Cullens' house; Leah could see what he saw—the dark yard, the lit glass wall, Bella's shape moving behind it with the baby on her hip. *Whoa, whoa, whoa. Seth! Dude!*

*Hey, Jake.*

*Don't "hey, Jake" me. Who is she?*

*Her name's Layla,* Seth thought, and the name came with so much attached to it that Leah's eyes watered.

*She's pretty,* Jacob thought. *She's really pretty. She's—how old is she?*

*Twenty-two.*

*Huh.*

*I'm eighteen, Jake.*

*Yeah, I know, I was at your birthday. I'm just saying—huh.* Leah felt him decide not to care, the way Jacob decided things: all at once, with his whole chest. *Okay. That's great, man. Did you ask her out?*

*I asked her to the tide pools.*

*The tide pools,* Leah thought.

*As friends.*

*Oh my God.*

*She said as friends! She doesn't know anybody here. She said friends, so I'm her friend.* Seth's mind did a thing Leah recognized from Sam's, from Jared's, from Quil's—a kind of settling, a bracing, like somebody setting his feet. *That's what she needs right now. So that's what I am.*

And that was the part that killed Leah. That was always the part. The imprint made you whatever the other person needed. A friend. A brother. A protector. Quil was imprinted on a two-year-old, and he was the best babysitter in La Push. The wanting was fine. The trouble was that there was never any question about it. Seth was going to love this girl the way the tide was going to come in.

She shoved that thought down where her brother couldn't see it. It didn't work; nothing worked in here; but she shoved anyway.

*Back up,* she thought instead. *Back up. The eggs.*

*What about the eggs?*

*Show me the eggs again.*

He did. They all watched it together—Seth's memory of it, sharp and bright, because wolf memory was always sharp and bright. The carton sliding. The girl's hand twitching toward her sleeve. The carton stopping eight inches off the floor and hanging there, crooked, perfectly still. Then dropping.

Jacob had stopped laughing.

*Do it again,* Jacob thought.

Seth did it again.

*You were holding the other two cartons, genius,* Leah thought. *And the list. You didn't catch anything.*

*Maybe I kicked it?*

*Your feet are on the floor in the memory, Seth.*

*Eggs don't float,* Jacob thought slowly.

*No kidding.*

*Maybe I'm—I don't know. Maybe I imagined it. I was kind of having a moment.*

*The moment came after. In that part you were still just looking at her.* Leah ran the ridge above the river, her paws throwing up wet needles. She made herself think it plain. *She did that. She did something with her hand and it stopped.*

*Leah—*

*She could be anything. All I'm saying is she did that.*

*So what is she?* Jacob asked. *Like—what? Like Alice?*

*I don't know. She smelled human.* Seth sent the smell. Crushed green things, wet ground, woodsmoke. And the other thing, faint, under all of it. *And like right before lightning.*

*People don't smell like lightning,* Leah thought.

*She does.*

There was a pause while all three of them ran in three different directions in the dark and thought about that.

*Leave it alone,* Jacob said finally. *For now. Seth, you just—be her friend, okay? Find out what she is. Don't push. If she's dangerous—*

*She's not dangerous.*

*If she is,* Jacob thought patiently, *we'll know. You'll know. You're gonna know everything about her pretty soon, man. That's how this works.*

*Fine,* Leah thought. *I'll leave it alone when I've smelled her myself.*

*She smells nice,* Seth thought.

*Everybody smells nice to you. You think Emmett smells nice.*

*Emmett smells kind of like a car air freshener,* Seth thought. *That's nice-adjacent.*

Jacob laughed so hard in his own head that Leah missed a step.

*Also,* Jacob said, *you know Edward's gonna know the second you walk in.*

*Edward already knows,* Seth thought, a little sheepish. *I stopped by the house after the store, and Edward was on the porch, and he just kind of—looked at me.*

*What'd he say?*

*He said congratulations. And then he said, "Eggs?" and kind of frowned.*

*Great,* Leah thought. *So the mind reader's interested. That's always good.*

They ran the rest of the loop without talking much. Seth kept thinking about tide pools. Jacob kept thinking about the baby, who was asleep now, and about the girl's hand twitching toward her sleeve.

Leah thought about her father.

She did that sometimes on the north loop, in the dark. Harry Clearwater's heart had quit the same day Leah phased for the first time, right in front of him. There was a part of her that would always believe she had killed him. She'd never said it out loud. She'd never had to. Everyone in the pack had seen it in her head a hundred times. Every grief got shared eventually, whether you were finished with it or not.

Her dad would have liked this girl, probably. He'd have liked anybody who could tell one mushroom from another. He'd have taken her fishing.

*Leah,* Seth thought, very quietly, from the other side of the river.

*Shut up, Seth.*

*Okay.*

She ran. Her brother ran. Somewhere off to the east, a little girl with bronze curls slept in a cottage in the woods, and her mother sat beside her all night, not sleeping, because she couldn't anymore.

Leah was glad for him. She was. It just turned out that glad, on Leah Clearwater, felt pretty much like getting hit by a truck.

### III. Full Moon

*North Fork of the Calawah River — Sunday, November 5, 2006, 12:52 a.m.*

Thirty-four days.

Seth hadn't meant to count. His brain had just started keeping a tally on its own, like the numbers rolling on a gas pump: thirty-four days since the mushrooms.

He was running the far east loop, up the North Fork of the Calawah, where the river ran fast and loud between banks of moss thick enough to lose a shoe in. Leah had the west side tonight; he could feel her in the back of his head, steady and sour, running the ridge above the Sol Duc. Jacob was at the Cullens', unphased, probably asleep on the floor next to Renesmee's crib.

The rain had quit around eleven. Now the clouds were pulling apart, and every time they did, the moon came through—big and white and full—and the whole forest went silver.

Thirty-four days, and in those days he'd taken her to the tide pools at Second Beach on a Saturday morning minus tide, and she'd knelt on the black rocks for two and a half hours and named everything in every pool—giant green anemone, ochre sea star, the little purple shore crabs that ran sideways from her shadow—while Seth held her bag and her thermos and was completely, stupidly happy. He'd introduced her to his mom. Sue had fed her fry bread and been friendly and polite, and then, after Layla left, had looked at Seth across the kitchen table with her eyebrows all the way up and said, "Twenty-two."

"Mom."

"I'm just saying a number, Seth."

"She's my friend."

Sue had looked at him for a long time. She knew exactly what had happened to her son in the produce section of the Thriftway; Seth had watched her figure it out at the door, the second Layla laughed at something he said. "I know she is, baby," she'd said finally, very gently, and gone back to her crossword.

Layla rented a little one-story place on the north edge of town, where the streets gave up and the trees took over—a woodstove, a sagging porch, moss on the shingles thick as carpet. She'd fallen asleep on his shoulder twice on her ugly plaid couch halfway through *Ghostbusters*, and he hadn't moved for two hours either time even though his arm went dead. He'd never been past the second bedroom. That door was always shut. It smelled the strongest of lightning, and of boiling herbs and burnt sugar and struck matches. Some Saturdays she cancelled and said she had to work nights. She never said at what.

And he still hadn't told her.

Jacob hadn't ordered him not to. Jacob hated giving orders. He'd just asked. *Imprints get told. That's the rule. But you tell her when it's right, man. You'll know.*

Seth had thought every single day was the right day. And every single day he'd also thought about the egg carton hanging in the air, and the shut door, and figured maybe she had something of her own she wasn't ready to tell him either. So he'd waited. He was good at waiting, it turned out. Who knew.

They hadn't kissed. He hadn't tried. She'd said friends.

*You're doing it again,* Leah thought from six miles away.

*Doing what?*

*The counting thing. Thirty-four. I can hear the numbers.*

*Sorry.*

*It's like living inside a calendar.*

He grinned with his wolf mouth, tongue out, and leaped a fallen cedar without breaking stride, and came down on the other side into a pocket of wet air—

—and smelled her.

He stopped so fast his paws plowed furrows in the moss.

Crushed green things. Wet ground. Woodsmoke. Lightning.

Fresh. Minutes old. Here.

*Leah.*

*What?* She felt it hit him, the cold drop in his stomach, and her own pace changed instantly. *Seth, what?*

*Layla's out here.*

*Out where?*

*Here. The North Fork. Like seven miles from her house.* He had his nose down, turning. The trail went upslope into the old timber. *Leah, it's one in the morning.*

*Is there anything else on the trail?*

He made himself do it right, layer by layer, the way Sam had taught them. Elk, a day old. Rain. Rotting cedar. Layla, Layla, Layla. No sickly-sweet burn. No vampire.

*Nothing,* he thought. *Just her.*

*Then go look,* Leah thought. *Quietly. Don't let her see you.*

He went.

The trail climbed through hemlock and old Sitka spruce into a place where a windstorm had laid a dozen big trees down like matchsticks and opened the canopy. The gap was thick with salal and, in the middle, a patch of something low and feathery and gray-green that Seth had never noticed in his life.

She was kneeling in the middle of it.

The clearing was bright enough that Seth could see the dirt on her knees. But there was another light, too—small, steady, white, about the size of a fist, hanging in the air above her left shoulder with nothing holding it up. It moved when she moved, like it was on a string.

She had a little curved silver knife in one hand. She was cutting the feathery plants one stem at a time, low to the ground, and laying each stem in a cloth spread on the moss beside her. And she was singing under her breath, in French, in a voice that went a little flat on the high notes:

*"Jolie blonde, regardez donc quoi t'as fait…"*

Seth crouched in the salal at the edge of the clearing with his heart going like a drum.

*What is that?* Leah's voice in his head was very still. She was seeing it through his eyes. *Seth. What's that light?*

*I don't know.*

*There's nothing holding it up.* A pause. *Like the eggs.*

*I know, Leah.*

Layla cut another stem, sang a line about somebody leaving somebody, laughed at herself softly, and cut another.

And Seth, who weighed a lot more than he looked even as a wolf and had been crouching motionless in wet shrubs for far too long, shifted his back foot about an inch to the left to take the cramp out of it and put it squarely on a dead alder branch.

It broke like a gunshot.

Layla was on her feet and turned around before the sound finished. She didn't scream. The little knife was gone, dropped or pocketed, and in its place, in her right hand, she was holding a stick—a slim, dark, reddish stick about as long as a ruler—and she was pointing it at the salal where Seth was crouching, and the end of it was lit up white-hot like the light above her shoulder.

He should have run. Leah was saying *run*, very loudly.

He stood up instead. His body did it before he'd decided anything, because she was afraid, and he couldn't stand having her be afraid of something and not be able to see what it was.

Layla saw him.

Seth knew what he looked like. He'd seen himself in Leah's head and Jacob's. A sandy-colored wolf the size of a horse, rangy, long in the leg, standing in moonlit brush with his ears up and his eyes catching the light.

Her face went through a lot of things very fast. Then it settled on something Seth didn't expect at all.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, *cher*. Look at the size of you."

She didn't back up. She lowered the stick an inch, slowly, and turned her free hand palm-out toward him.

"Okay," she said, in the low, even voice of somebody talking to a spooked horse. "Okay, big boy. I see you. I don't want to hurt you. You don't want to hurt me. That's all right."

*She's talking to you like a dog,* Leah thought.

*She's being really calm, though.*

*She's holding a—Seth, what is that? Is that a weapon?*

Layla took one careful step to the side, toward her cloth full of cut plants. The light above her shoulder brightened. She murmured something under her breath, two words that sounded like Latin, and the light swelled to three times its size and burned so bright and so white that every shadow in the clearing jumped back into the trees, and Seth flinched and squinted, his eyes stinging.

"Sorry," she said, sounding like she meant it. "Sorry. I just need to see you better, baby. You are *huge*."

*Seth. Get out of there.*

*I'm going to change.*

*Don't you dare—*

*She's scared, Leah. She's acting calm, but I can smell it on her. She's really scared.* He was already backing into the dark of the trees, out of the light, his eyes on her the whole way. *I'm going to change and I'm going to tell her.*

*Jacob said when it's right—*

*It's right,* Seth thought. *She's standing in the woods at one in the morning holding a magic stick and pointing it at me. If it's not right now it's never going to be.*

Leah thought something he didn't catch, very fast, and then she thought, *Fine. Fine. Put your shorts on first, idiot.*

He let go.

The pack went out of his head the way sound goes out when you dunk under water, and he was on his hands and knees in the wet salal, human, steaming, cold air slapping his bare back. He fumbled his cutoffs loose from the cord around his ankle and yanked them on, hopping, almost going over in the brush.

Then he walked out into the clearing with his hands up.

"Layla," he said. "It's me. It's Seth. Please don't point that thing at me. Whatever it is."

She swung the stick toward him so fast the end of it whistled. Then she saw his face, and her arm stopped.

"Seth?" Her voice cracked high. "What are you—it's one in the morning—there was a—did you see a—"

She looked at him. Barefoot. Shirtless. Steam coming off his shoulders in the cold, curling up into the light. She looked past him, at the dark gap in the trees where the wolf had gone.

She looked back at him.

He watched her put it together. It didn't take long. She was smart—that was one of the first things he'd known about her, before the mushrooms were even done. Her mouth opened and stayed open.

"That was you," she said.

"Yeah."

She looked at the stick in her hand like she'd forgotten she was holding it.

"And that," Seth said carefully, "is a wand. Right? That's a magic wand."

The light above her shoulder flickered.

"…Yeah," Layla said.

They stood there in the moonlight and looked at each other.

Then Layla made a noise that started as a gasp and turned into a laugh halfway through, and she put the back of her hand against her mouth and laughed into it, high and helpless, until her shoulders shook. And Seth, who had been so scared his knees were trembling, started laughing too, because she was, and because thirty-four days of keeping his mouth shut had just ended in a clearing in the middle of nowhere with a girl holding a magic wand.

"Oh my *God*," Layla said through her fingers.

"I know."

"You're a *wolf*."

"Kind of. Yeah. Sometimes."

"You're a—" She waved the wand vaguely. The light bobbed. "You're a *shapeshifter*. An Animagus? No. No, you can't be, you were enormous—"

"A what?"

"Never mind." She pressed both hands over her face, wand and all. "Seth Clearwater, I swear to God."

"You're a witch," Seth said. It came out full of wonder. He couldn't help it. "Like an actual witch. The eggs. You did the eggs."

She dropped her hands. "You saw the eggs."

"Of *course* I saw the eggs, they were floating!"

"*Arrested*, technically, it's a—oh, *Lord*." She stared at him. "I'm breaking about nine different laws right now. You know that? Just standing here. There's a whole government whose entire job is to make sure you don't know what I just told you."

"There is?"

"MACUSA. The Magical Congress of the United States of America. They have a building in New York. They have a department for it." She laughed again, wildly. "They used to execute people for less than this, Seth."

"Oh," Seth said. He thought about that. "Okay. Well. I won't tell them."

"*Thank* you."

She sat down right there in the moss, like her legs had decided for her. After a second, Seth sat too, a careful few feet away. The light above her shoulder drifted lower and hovered between them, like it wanted to hear.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. You first."

So he told her.

He told her about the Quileute, and the old stories—the spirit warriors, Taha Aki, the Cold Ones. How the stories turned out to be true. How the Cold Ones came back and the boys of the tribe started phasing, one after another, Sam first, then Jared, then Paul, then Embry, then Jacob, then Quil. How Leah had phased, the first girl anyone had ever heard of, and how their dad's heart had stopped that same day, and how Seth had phased right after. He told her about the pack mind. About patrols. About the treaty. He told her that the Cold Ones were vampires, and that some of them lived right there outside Forks, and that they were his friends.

Layla had been listening with her chin on her knees. At *vampires* she lifted her head.

"Vampires," she said.

"Yeah."

"Here."

"Like ten minutes outside town. The Cullens. Doctor Cullen works at the hospital."

"And you're *friends* with them."

"They eat animals," Seth said. "Deer and elk and stuff. Mountain lions. Emmett likes bears."

She stared at him. "Seth, I've *met* a vampire."

"You have?"

"At my cousin Angelique's wedding. In Lafayette. Friend of the groom's family—real pale, real polite, velvet jacket. He ate half a pan of boudin noir by himself and told my grandmother she had beautiful circulation." She shook her head. "We have vampires, cher. In my world. Some of 'em are perfectly decent and some of 'em you lock your doors against. But they look like people. Pale. A little—" she wiggled her fingers "—ghoulish." She frowned. "What do yours look like?"

Seth thought about how to explain the Cullens to someone who'd never seen them.

"Like statues," he said finally. "Like if a statue of the most beautiful person you ever saw got up and walked around. They're cold. They're hard—like, rock hard. Their eyes are gold if they eat animals and red if they eat people. And in the sun they—" He hesitated. "They kind of sparkle."

Layla's face did something complicated.

"They *what*?"

"Sparkle. Like—diamonds? It's really bright. It's actually kind of pretty."

"Your vampires sparkle."

"Bella hates when people say sparkle."

"Who's Bella?"

"She's a vampire. She used to be Chief Swan's daughter. She's still Chief Swan's daughter, she's just also a vampire now. She just had a baby. Well—like a month ago. The baby's half vampire. Jacob imprinted on the baby."

Layla closed her eyes.

"I'm going to need you," she said, "to say every single part of that again, slower, later, with a diagram."

"Okay."

She opened her eyes. "What's imprinted?"

And there it was.

He'd known it was coming for thirty-four days. He still wasn't ready. He picked at the moss beside his knee, and the light above them dimmed a little, as if it had noticed something.

"It's a wolf thing," he said. "It happens to some of us. You see somebody, and—everything changes. The whole world kind of—reorganizes. Around them. You'd do anything for them. You'd be anything for them. Whatever they need. If they need a friend, you're their friend. If they need a brother, you're that. If they need—more—" He stopped. Swallowed. Kept going, because she'd asked, and because Jacob was right, you told them. "It happened to me. In the Thriftway. At the mushrooms."

Layla didn't say anything.

"With you," Seth said. "Just to be clear."

She was very still. The light above them had gone small and dim and steady, and in the moonlight he couldn't read her face at all.

"That sounds," she said slowly, "an awful lot like a love potion."

"It's—"

"No, listen. Listen to me." She sat up straight. "There's a potion. Amortentia. Most powerful love potion in the world. They teach you about it at school—I went to a school, it's in Massachusetts, it's called Ilvermorny, I'll tell you later—they teach you about it so you know to never, ever touch it. Because what it makes is obsession, Seth. People call it love because it looks like love from the outside. But the person who drinks it doesn't get a say. They don't *choose*." Her voice had gone rough. "That's the worst thing you can do to somebody. Take away the choosing."

Seth sat with that.

He could have argued. He could have told her what Jacob always said, about how it was all about what *she* needed. He could feel the words. They felt like something you'd say to win.

He told her the truth instead.

"I don't know how to make you feel it from the inside," he said. "I really don't. But I can tell you what happened after. After the Thriftway, I had a choice about every single thing. You said friends. So I chose to be your friend. I was really, really happy being your friend. I'd keep choosing that. Every day. If that's what you want, that's what I'll be, for the rest of my life, and I'll still be happy." He made himself look at her. "And if you said tomorrow you never wanted to see me again, I'd stop. It'd hurt like hell. But I would."

The light between them brightened, very slightly.

"And what do you want?" Layla said quietly.

"Layla."

"No. I'm asking. You've told me what you'll *do*. You tell me what you *want*."

He swallowed.

"You know what I want."

"Say it."

"I want to kiss you," Seth said. "I've wanted to since the mushrooms."

She stared at him.

Then she laughed—a soft, cracked, disbelieving sound—and said, "*Since the mushrooms*," and then she leaned across the few feet of moss between them and put her cold hand on his hot face and kissed him.

It was a little clumsy. Her nose bumped his. She tasted like the cold and like some kind of mint. Seth had never kissed anybody before in his life, not really, and he didn't know what to do with his hands, so he just held very still and let her, and then she made a small sound against his mouth and he stopped being still.

The light above them went up like a flare.

They broke apart. It was hanging there, blazing, lighting up the whole clearing like noon. Layla looked up at it and went red to the roots of her hair.

"That's—" she said. "That's not supposed to—I didn't do that on purpose."

"It's okay," Seth said, grinning so hard it hurt.

"It's *embarrassing*."

"It's awesome."

She jabbed the wand at the light. It shrank, sulkily, back down to the size of a fist. Then she put her forehead against his shoulder and breathed, and he put his arm around her, and they sat like that in the cold and the moonlight for a while, and Seth thought that if this was the only thing that ever happened to him for the rest of his life it would be enough.

Then Layla lifted her head.

"Wait," she said.

"What?"

"Your sister. And your pack. You said they hear what you think."

"When we're phased. When we're wolves."

"So when you were a wolf just now—"

"Leah saw everything up until I changed back."

"So she saw the light. And the wand."

"…Yeah."

"And the next time you turn into a wolf—"

"Everybody gets everything," Seth admitted.

"*Everything* everything?"

He thought about the last twenty minutes. He thought about Jacob, and Leah, and the kiss, and the light going off like a flare.

"Yeah," he said. "Pretty much."

Layla made a small, strangled sound.

"And you said one of the vampires," she said slowly, "reads minds."

"Edward."

"*Edward*."

"He's really nice."

Layla stood up and brushed off her knees with fierce little slaps. She folded the cloth of feathery plants into a bundle and stuffed it into her coat. Then she stood there with her wand in her hand and her jaw set, and Seth knew that look. It was the look she'd had at the mushroom bin. The look of somebody about to fix something that wasn't her job to fix.

"Okay," she said. "Then I'm doing this my way."

"What's your way?"

"I'm not letting a pack of wolves and a mind-reading vampire find out about the entire wizarding world secondhand from the inside of your head, Seth Clearwater. No offense."

"None taken."

"I'm going to meet them. All of them. Your sister, your Jacob, your Cullens. Tomorrow. Today. Whatever it is now." She took a breath. "I'm going to tell them myself, the right way, so they get it right. Because if I've got to break the Statute of Secrecy, I am going to break it *properly*."

### IV. The Statute

*The Cullen house — Sunday, November 5, 6:04 p.m.*

The Cullen house always smelled like a candy store that had caught fire.

Leah stood on the back deck with her arms crossed and breathed through her mouth. It didn't help much. Vampire smell got in anyway—sickly-sweet, the kind of sweet that burned the inside of your nose like bleach. Through the big glass wall, the Cullens were arranged around their enormous white living room like a magazine spread. It was full dark and raining again, and Leah was in cutoffs and a tank top and bare feet on the wet boards, and she didn't feel cold at all.

She'd gotten the whole thing at nine that morning, when Seth phased for his run and the entire night poured out of him like a dam breaking: the clearing, the floating light, the girl with the stick, *imprinted*, *Amortentia*, *since the mushrooms*, the kiss—*the kiss*—and the light going up like a flare. Leah had lain down in the ferns and thought *Oh my God* about forty times in a row. Then Jacob had phased in, gotten all of it in one hit, and sat down in the middle of the Cullens' backyard so hard that Emmett came out to check on him.

And Edward had been standing on the porch when Seth walked up the driveway that afternoon, already knowing. His eyes had gone wide and then very thoughtful, and he'd said, "Seth. I think you'd better come inside and tell Carlisle. Slowly."

Now it was six, and the witch was coming.

A green Ford Ranger with a dented tailgate came laboring up the long hidden drive and stopped beside Rosalie's red BMW, which made it look like a dog that had wandered into a car show. Seth came off the front porch so fast he basically flew.

Leah went around the side of the house to watch. She told herself it was to get upwind. It was also to see the girl's face before the girl saw hers.

Layla Broussard got out of the truck slowly. Five-nine, maybe, brown skin, freckles across her nose, dark hair in a knot with a pencil stuck through it, exactly like in every one of the nine thousand memories Seth had of her. She stood with her hand on the truck door for a second, looking up at the three stories of glass and white, and Leah watched her square her shoulders the way you do before you jump into cold water.

Then Seth reached her, and Layla turned, and for a second her whole face changed.

Leah looked away. It was the only private thing she had left. She used it.

When she looked back, they were walking toward the porch together, Layla's hand in Seth's, Seth talking a mile a minute about how Esme had made coffee even though none of them drank it, and how Rosalie was nice too, she just didn't show it—

"Seth," Layla said.

"Yeah?"

"Breathe."

"Okay."

Leah stepped out of the shadows at the corner of the house.

Layla saw her and stopped. Leah let her look: five-ten, all bone and muscle, short black hair she cut herself, a face people usually stepped back from.

"You're Leah," Layla said.

"Yeah."

"I've been told," Layla said carefully, "that you saw me through your brother's eyes last night."

"Some of it."

"Which some?"

"The part with the wand," Leah said. "Then he changed and I got the rest this morning. All of it."

Layla's chin came up. "All of it."

"Every second."

A muscle jumped in the girl's jaw. Leah waited for the flush, the stammer, the embarrassment. What she got instead was a long, level look.

"Well," Layla said. "Then I'm sorry. That's a hell of a thing to have dumped in your head before breakfast."

Leah blinked.

She got closer and breathed in—crushed green things, woodsmoke, and under it, sure enough, that sharp, clean ozone smell. Lightning over water. It smelled like something that could go off.

"You do smell like lightning," Leah said.

"I'll take your word for it."

"What is that?"

"Magic," Layla said. "I think. I've never had anybody sniff me before. Professionally."

Seth made a choking sound.

Leah looked at her a while longer. Then she jerked her chin at the door.

"They're in there," she said. "All of them. The blonde one's going to look at you like you're gum on her shoe. Don't take it personal. She looks at me like that every day."

"Noted," Layla said.

---

They really were all in there.

Carlisle stood near the piano with Esme at his side, his hands folded like a doctor about to deliver good news. Rosalie perched on the arm of the white sofa, her face arranged into polite boredom. Emmett lounged on the sofa itself, huge, grinning. Jasper stood against the far wall where he could see every door and window at once, the way he always stood, like the soldier he'd been. Alice sat cross-legged on top of the piano. On the loveseat by the fire, Bella held Renesmee in her lap, Edward beside her with his hand on the back of her neck, and Jacob sprawled on the rug at their feet, as close to the baby as he could get.

Bella's eyes were still red, going amber slowly at the edges. Leah watched Layla see them and decide not to react. Renesmee looked about a year and a half old, though she'd been alive eight weeks—bronze curls, her mother's old brown eyes, and a little crease between her brows exactly like Charlie's when he was reading a box score.

Layla stopped a few feet inside the door and looked around the room, one face after another, and Leah saw her throat move when she swallowed.

"Lord," Layla said finally. "Y'all are *pretty*."

Emmett laughed—a big, booming laugh that shook the glass. Alice clapped her hands. Even Rosalie's mouth twitched.

"Thank you," Esme said warmly, stepping forward. "Welcome, Layla. I'm Esme. Can I get you some coffee? I made it fresh."

"Seth told me you don't drink it."

"I don't," Esme said. "But you might."

"Then yes, ma'am. Thank you. Black, please."

Edward was watching Layla with his head a little tilted. Leah knew that look. That was the reading look.

"She's terrified," Edward said quietly. "And she's decided to be brave about it anyway."

Layla turned and looked at him.

"Rude," she said.

The room went silent.

Then Edward's mouth curved—a real smile, a small one. "My apologies. I can't turn it off."

"So Seth tells me." Layla folded her arms. "I'd ask you to stay out of my head, but I hear that's not how it works."

"It isn't."

"Then I'll just have to think nice thoughts."

"You're thinking," Edward said, "that I look like a cologne advertisement."

Bella snorted. Emmett laughed so hard he slid off the sofa.

Layla went bright red and stared at the ceiling. "This is going great," she said. "This is going really, really great."

Carlisle stepped forward. "Layla. I'm Carlisle. Thank you for coming. I've lived a very long time and thought I'd seen most of what the world had to offer. I'm finding it was a great deal less than I believed." He spread his hands. "We would very much like to understand. If you're willing to show us."

Layla looked at him for a long moment.

Then she reached inside her coat and drew out her wand.

Leah felt every vampire in the room go still. Jasper's weight was suddenly forward. Edward's hand tightened on Bella's neck.

Layla held the wand up flat on both palms, the way you'd show somebody a knife you weren't going to use.

"Swamp mayhaw," she said. "Rougarou hair. Eleven and a half inches. A lady named Beauvais made my grandmother's wand in New Orleans back in the twenties. The shop that made mine still uses her pattern." She turned it so the firelight ran along the grain. "It's a tool. Like a scalpel, Doctor. It's not going to do anything I don't tell it to."

"May I?" Carlisle said.

"Look all you want. Don't touch it. It doesn't like strangers."

Carlisle leaned close, his face full of plain, open hunger to *know*.

"Show them," Seth whispered. "Show them the cup thing."

Esme had come back with a white ceramic mug of black coffee. Layla took it, thanked her, took one sip, and then set it down on the glass coffee table in front of Emmett.

She tapped it once with the wand and said a word Leah didn't catch.

The mug was gone.

In its place was a little brown sparrow with a streaky breast, cocking its head. It hopped once, looked at Emmett, then flew—a whir of wings around the room over all their heads—and landed on the piano next to Alice's knee. Alice made a sound like a kettle.

Layla held out her hand. The sparrow came back to her palm. She set it on the table and tapped it again, and it was a mug, the coffee still steaming.

Nobody said anything.

Then Emmett said, "Do me. Make me a bird."

"Human Transfiguration's hard. You'd probably come out a bird with a vampire problem." Layla considered him. "I can lift you, though."

She swished and flicked the wand and said, clearly, "*Wingardium Leviosa*."

Emmett rose off the sofa—a foot, then two, then stalled, sitting in the air with his feet dangling and the biggest grin Leah had ever seen on a vampire. Layla's arm trembled. Sweat broke out on her forehead.

"What," she gritted, "are you *made* of?"

"Mostly attitude," Emmett said happily.

She lowered him, not quite gently. The sofa frame creaked. Rosalie looked at him and, very slightly, smiled.

"So there are more of you," Rosalie said. Her voice was cool. "Witches."

"Witches and wizards. A lot more." Layla wiped her forehead with her coat sleeve. "Every country's got us. America's got MACUSA. Britain's got a Ministry of Magic. Schools, newspapers, hospitals. Prisons." She hesitated. "And a law. The International Statute of Wizarding Secrecy. Came into force in 1692. We hide. That's the whole thing. We hide from the No-Majs—from non-magic people—and if we get caught, we fix it. We wipe memories. We make the story go away."

"Wipe memories," Jasper said quietly. It was the first thing he'd said.

"There's a charm for it."

"And you're breaking that law," Carlisle said, "by being here."

"By being here. By saying this. By standing in your living room." Layla's mouth twisted. "Except—here's my argument, if anybody ever asks me, which God forbid. The Statute is about No-Majs. You all—" she gestured around the room "—are vampires. In my world, vampires are classified. There's paperwork. You're—beings. And the wolves are shapeshifters, which is about as magic as anything I've ever seen. So the way I figure it, everybody I'm telling is already magical. I'm just filling in some gaps." She sighed. "That's the argument. I don't know if it'd hold up in front of MACUSA. MACUSA's never been real big on arguments."

Carlisle had gone quiet in a way Leah hadn't seen before.

"1692," he said. "In London?"

"The Statute's international. But the British signed it, sure."

"I was born in London," Carlisle said. "In the 1640s. My father was an Anglican pastor." He paused. "He hunted witches. Witches, werewolves, vampires. He led the hunts himself. He was very zealous about it." Another pause. "He burned a great many people."

The room was so quiet Leah could hear the river.

"Did he catch any?" Layla said quietly. "Real ones?"

"I doubt it. I believe he burned a great many frightened people who had done nothing at all." Carlisle's voice was even. Leah had never heard him sound so old. "It was what I was doing—hunting with him—when I was made."

Layla looked at him a long moment.

"Real witches mostly knew how to stay out of it," she said. "We had the Flame-Freezing Charm. Turned the fire into a tickle. There was a witch called Wendelin the Weird who liked it so much she let herself get caught forty-seven times." A faint, crooked smile. "They tell you that one in History of Magic so you won't feel too bad. But the people who couldn't do that charm—" She shook her head. "No-Maj or not, I'm sorry about them, Doctor. And I'm sorry about your father."

"Thank you," Carlisle said. He sounded like he meant it.

"So what else is real?" Bella asked suddenly. She'd been quiet, holding Renesmee, watching everything with those red eyes. "If witches are real. What else?"

Layla let out a breath. "How long have you got?"

She told them. Dragons. Unicorns. Giants, mostly gone. Goblins who ran the banks. House-elves. "Werewolves—real ones, the moon kind. It's a disease. Lycanthropy. You get bit, you change at the full moon, you don't remember yourself. Nothing like Seth."

"*Children of the Moon*," Edward said softly.

Layla looked at him.

"That's what the Volturi call them," Edward said. "Caius hunted them almost to extinction in Europe. Centuries ago."

"The who?"

There was a pause around the room—a different kind of quiet, heavier.

Carlisle explained. He did it simply: the Volturi, the oldest and most powerful coven in the world. Aro, Caius, Marcus. Volterra, in Italy. How they made themselves the enforcers of the one law vampires had—*keep the secret*—and how they enforced it. How a human who learned the truth and wasn't changed was killed. How the guard was made up of vampires with gifts, collected over centuries. Jane, who could make you feel you were burning alive just by looking at you. Alec, who could take away every sense you had. Demetri, who could find anyone, anywhere. Chelsea, who could bind people's loyalties.

"And Aro reads minds," Edward said. "As I do. Except Aro needs to touch you. And when he does, he gets everything. Every thought you've ever had."

Leah watched the color go out of Layla's face.

"So they're—you're telling me there's a secret vampire government in Italy with a Statute of Secrecy," Layla said. "And it kills people."

"Yes."

"And they don't know about us. Wizards."

"I've never heard Aro think of anything like you," Edward said. "And I was in his head. Briefly. Last spring." His jaw tightened at the memory. Bella reached up and put her hand on his.

"Aro collects gifts," Edward went on, more quietly. "If he knew a person like you existed—someone who could do what you just did—he would want you. Very, very much."

Seth had gone stiff beside Layla on the sofa. Leah saw his hand close around Layla's.

"Then let's make sure he never knows," Carlisle said, firmly enough to break the mood. "Layla, we'll keep your secret. Our existence depends on the same thing yours does. We're very practiced at silence."

"I appreciate that, Doctor."

"I can see you," Alice said abruptly.

Everyone looked up at her. She was still sitting on the piano, her head tilted, her eyes unfocused in that way that made Leah's skin crawl.

"Your future, I mean. When you're alone, it's grainy, like looking through a rain-streaked window. Every time Seth's in it, it goes fuzzy. The dogs do that."

"Hey," said Jacob.

"*Wolves.* Sorry." Alice smiled at Layla. "You, in a little kitchen, making a face at something boiling. Nothing bad. Nothing yet."

"Nothing yet," Layla repeated. "That's comforting."

Renesmee squirmed in Bella's lap.

She'd been staring at Layla the entire time. Now she twisted around and held her arms out, opening and closing her small fists, and made a soft, imperious sound.

"She wants to say hello," Bella said. She looked at Layla. "She—she'll want to touch your face. She shows you things. It doesn't hurt."

"I warned her," Seth said quickly.

Layla nodded. She came over and knelt by the loveseat, slowly, and held still, and Renesmee reached out with one small hand and laid it flat on her cheek.

Layla's eyes went wide.

Leah could guess what she was seeing. It was the picture Renesmee showed everybody that week: Seth, wolf, sandy and huge and goofy, crouched on the Cullens' lawn while the baby rode on his back holding fistfuls of his fur.

"Oh," Layla breathed. "Oh, look at that. Look at him."

Renesmee took her hand away. She looked very pleased with herself.

"That's—" Layla sat back on her heels. "That's Legilimency. Sort of. Backwards. She puts the picture in. Legilimency takes it out."

"Legilimency?" Edward said sharply.

Layla turned. "It's—a branch of magic. Reading minds. Some witches and wizards can do it. It's hard, it's rare, and most of us think it's about the rudest thing you can do to a person." She glanced at him. "No offense."

"None taken." Edward was very focused now. "There are wizards who can do what I do."

"Some. Different from you, from what Seth says. You just hear it. We have to go in after it. You look somebody in the eye and push." Layla shrugged. "And there's a way to block it. Occlumency. Closing your mind. Making it a wall."

"Can you?"

"Me? Lord, no. I can barely close a kitchen cabinet."

Edward sat back. His face was thoughtful and very still.

"It's useful to know it exists," he said quietly.

"Okay, I've got a question," Jacob said from the floor. "If there's a whole government and a whole—magic world. And they don't know about us. And they don't know about vampires like these guys." He looked up at Layla. "Are they gonna find out?"

Layla hesitated.

"I don't know," she said. "I'm not gonna tell 'em. I'm a girl who counts moss. But I'll be honest with you. Our people are good at finding things out. The British especially. They've got a whole department nobody's allowed to talk about."

"Great," Leah said from the doorway.

"Yeah," Layla said. "Great."

---

Later—after Carlisle had asked something like two hundred questions and Emmett had made her lift him three more times—Jacob stood up and stretched and said, "I gotta tell Sam."

Layla winced.

"Sorry. He's the other alpha. And the council—my dad, Sue, Old Quil." He waved a hand at the wand, at all of it. "They kept us a secret for like a thousand years."

"Fine," Layla said. "Fine. Just—keep it small. Please."

"Small as we can," Jacob promised.

Leah walked out onto the front porch to get away from the smell, and found Seth and Layla out there in the dark. The rain had stopped. The truck was ticking as it cooled. Layla was holding something out to Seth on her palm.

It was a stone. A flat, gray-green river stone about the size of a half-dollar, worn smooth, with a hole straight through the middle. A leather cord was threaded through the hole.

"Found it on Second Beach," Layla said. "When you took me. Holed stones are lucky. Hag stones, my grandmother called 'em. You look through the hole and you see what's really there."

"It's cool," Seth said.

"It's better than cool. I worked on it all day." She closed his fingers around it. "If you ever need me—if something comes at you that you can't bite—you bite this. Hard. And think of me. And I'll come."

"Come how?"

"I'll *come*," Layla said. "Wherever you are. Doesn't matter. It'll pull me right to you."

Seth stared at the stone.

"Wait," he said. "What if I'm a wolf?"

"That's why you bite it, baby. Even you can't push a button with a paw."

Leah leaned on the porch post.

"What if he bites it by accident?" she said.

Layla turned around. If she was surprised to find Leah there she didn't show it.

"Then I show up," she said, "and he buys me dinner."

Leah looked at her. She looked at her brother, who was tying the cord around his left wrist—Layla had charmed it to fit him either way, boy or wolf—double-knotting it, then triple-knotting it, with his tongue between his teeth.

She thought about Volterra. About a blond man who read every thought you'd ever had with one touch.

"Make it a nice dinner," Leah said, and went back inside before either of them could see her face.

### V. Pattern of Life

*Bogachiel River, six miles south of Forks — Monday, November 20, 2006, 5:40 a.m.*

Harry Potter woke at twenty to six because he always did.

He lay still for a minute and listened. Rain on canvas. The river below the bank, running high and brown after a week of weather. A varied thrush somewhere up in the hemlocks giving its one long, flat whistle, waiting, giving it again. Nothing else. On the bedside table, the Galleon was cold, and the numerals around its edge read all zeroes.

Twelve days.

He got up.

From the outside, the tent was a faded green canvas ridge tent of the sort Boy Scouts had used in the seventies, pitched under a stand of hemlock on a half-acre riverside lot he rented from a retired logger named Dale Pruett for three hundred dollars a month, cash. Dale had asked two questions—what an Englishman wanted with a wet lot on the Bogachiel in November, and whether he was running from something—and had forgotten both by the time Harry's truck reached the end of his drive.

Inside, the tent was a flat. Hermione had done the same charm on a beaded handbag at seventeen. Harry had spent most of 2002 learning to do it properly and most of 2003 learning to do it to a tent without the floor going soft at the corners. A flagstone kitchen with a proper range. A sitting room with a fireplace. A bedroom, a bathroom with a shower that held its pressure, and a corridor that ran back thirty feet farther than the tent was long, to three doors.

He showered and shaved and fried bacon—American, streaky, wrong; he'd made his peace with it—and two eggs, and made tea from a box of PG Tips he'd brought in his luggage, because there were limits. On the shelf over the range sat a red-and-cream Dansette record player from a car boot sale in Croydon. It had taken him a weekend to work out why electricity and dense enchantment got on like cats and bathwater, and another to make the Dansette play inside a magical structure anyway, loud. It played Led Zeppelin, mostly *Physical Graffiti*, because that was the record that lived on it. He dropped the needle and "Custard Pie" came on, which felt right with a fry-up.

He ate standing, looking at the map on the kitchen wall.

He'd drawn it himself: the Olympic Peninsula from Cape Flattery to Grays Harbor, with the national park in green and the reservations in brown. Across it, in red, ran the lines, like stitching. They followed the ridges, the river corridors, the old elk trails and mountain passes—the routes anything on foot would take, however fast it was. Forty-four anchor stones. Three hundred and some miles of line. On the coast, he'd laid the line across every river mouth from the Hoko to the Quinault and left the sand between them for last. Nothing walked up out of the Pacific.

The design was his. The base of it came from somebody else.

The base was a spell he'd found scrawled in the margin of a secondhand Potions textbook at sixteen, in the cramped hand of a boy who'd called himself the Half-Blood Prince. *Levicorpus (nvbl).* Hoist a person into the air by the ankle, upside down, as though by an invisible hook. He had later learned who wrote it and had spent a long time wanting nothing to do with it. Then, at twenty-four, he'd needed a snare that would hold something too strong to tie and too fast to chase, and he'd gone back to it.

He'd married the hoist to a Full Body-Bind, and the pair of them to a ward line that could lie dormant across forty miles of forest, and he'd spent the better part of two years on the trigger. The trigger was the hard bit. A line that caught everything was useless. This one checked three things, in order, in the instant something crossed it: a pulse; whether the thing was walking; and whether there was human blood in it, less than a month old. No pulse, upright, a gutful of people—the line closed.

The Cullens could walk across his lines a hundred times a week and never feel a thing. Deer blood. Elk. Their visitors were their business, so long as their visitors ate the same. The wolves had heartbeats like kettledrums. Hikers had pulses.

A Cullen who slipped would be a different matter. Harry had thought about that and hadn't lost any sleep over it.

He finished the eggs, rinsed the plate, and went down the corridor.

The first door was the armory.

Most wizards he knew thought guns were a Muggle affectation, like television or dentistry. Harry had stopped arguing about it. A gun did one thing very well and needed no concentration, which left all of his concentration free for everything else.

The racks ran floor to ceiling on three walls. A Remington 870 with the barrel cut down to the legal minimum. An Accuracy International L96, British Army issue, with a scope he'd charmed to see through fog and rain. Two SIG P226s. A Browning Hi-Power that had been the first gun he'd ever owned and that he still carried most days under his left arm. Goblin-made knives in a case on the back wall. Below them, crates of ammunition, and every cartridge in every crate had a ring of runes cut around the brass by hand.

That had been 2004. Nineteen months of work, most of it failure. A spell would sit happily in a wand, or a stone, or a Galleon; put one in a cartridge about to be detonated and it came apart. He'd lost the skin off three fingers and his eyebrows twice. The answer had been in a seventeenth-century goblin text on metallurgy that no wizard had read in three hundred years, because nobody wanted to sit through four hundred pages of goblins being rude about wizards to get to it. Now he could load a Reductor Curse into a 12-gauge slug, fire it from two hundred yards, and have it go off on impact.

He'd tested every load on a two-ton block of Cornish granite, because the Volterra reports all said the same thing: the skin was like stone. The Reductor slugs took fist-sized chunks out of the block. The Severing rounds cut it like cheese. The Incendio rounds had set it on fire, which granite wasn't meant to do.

He checked the 870's tube and the Hi-Power's magazine and went to the third door.

The specimen room was cold. He kept it that way.

It was long and white-tiled and quiet except for the high hum of the stasis charms. Shelves of jars ran down both sides. A basilisk fang, yellowed at the root, from a serpent he'd killed at twelve. A bell jar with a grey, wet human hand in it that had come out of a lake in a cave in 1997 and still, now and then, curled its fingers. A Nundu whisker as long as his arm. A jar of something black and shapeless from a job in Romania that didn't have a name yet and moved when it thought nobody was looking.

At the far end stood a stone basin, wide and shallow, carved around the rim with runes. And past it, behind a wall of glass three inches thick, was the cell.

Steel table, bolted to the floor. Restraints of goblin steel, sized for a man, rated for considerably more than a man. Runes cut into the tiled floor in a circle around it. A tray of instruments under a cloth. A camera on a tripod. A notebook.

All of it had been waiting twelve days.

He stood and looked at the empty table for a while.

He'd gone into the Department of Mysteries in September 1998, four months after the war. Kingsley had offered him the Auror Office and everybody had expected him to take it. He'd said he was tired, which had been true.

The rest of it was that he had lost his godfather through an archway in a room on Level Nine, and nobody in the building could tell him what was on the other side. He had died in a forest and woken somewhere that looked like King's Cross and walked back. He had held, at one time or another, all three of the Deathly Hallows. His mother had died for him and it had done something to his blood that nobody, not even Dumbledore, could fully explain. He'd spent seven years being thrown at magic like a stone at a window. He wanted to know how it worked. All of it.

Eight years so far, and he wasn't finished.

He'd sat his N.E.W.T.s at the Ministry in 1999, after a year of Hermione drilling him at the kitchen table at Grimmauld Place until he did Arithmancy in his sleep. Three years of probation, mostly carrying things. Croaker had taught him Occlumency the way it should have been taught at fifteen: slowly, every day, for two years, with nobody shouting. Wandless magic had taken three years before he could Summon a teacup without his wand and four more before he could do it without thinking. Ward theory. Runes. Goblin metallurgy. Legilimency, deep and shallow. Spell construction, which was mostly arithmetic and stubbornness. He'd built the carrier rounds, and the line, and a way of listening to a mind wide and shallow that the Department now taught its new field officers as the Potter Method, which he hated.

Field officers in the Department carried a standing authorization for all three Unforgivable Curses. The Aurors had had it in Crouch's day and lost it after the first war; the Department had quietly kept it. The form had a box marked *Previous use, if any (specify)*. He'd written: *Imperius, Gringotts, 1 May 1998. Cruciatus, Hogwarts, 2 May 1998.* He'd thought about adding that he'd meant it, the second time, and decided the Department could work that out for itself.

The empty table sat there under the white light.

He went to call his boss.

---

The mirror lived on the kitchen table, face-down, a square of old glass in a plain wooden frame. Sirius had given him one like it at fifteen and he hadn't opened the parcel until it was too late to use it. He'd had this one made himself.

At half six he turned it over and said, "Croaker."

The face that swam up in the glass belonged to an old man with a long, lined face like a closed walnut and very pale, very sharp eyes. It was half two in the afternoon in London. Behind Croaker's head, Harry could see the dark stone wall of the Department's operations room on Level Nine and the edge of a clock with a dozen hands, none of them for telling the time.

"Potter."

"Sir."

"Anything?"

"Rain," Harry said.

Croaker's expression didn't change. It rarely did. "The Minister had me in yesterday. He wants options on MARBLE by Easter."

"He'll have them when something walks into my line."

"Twelve days, Potter."

"Twelve days is nothing. Your people sat on that clock tower for seven months before they'd put a name to anybody."

"My people," Croaker said, "have now counted nine hundred and sixty-three tourists into the Palazzo dei Priori on guided tours since April. Forty-one have come out."

Harry put down his tea.

He'd read the Volterra file twice. A Floo Network Authority clerk on holiday in Tuscany in the spring, in a crowded, red-draped square on some saint's day, had seen a bare-chested boy standing in the shadow of a clock tower with his arms out and his eyes shut, a girl crashing into him at a dead run, and two men in grey cloaks coming out of a side street faster than any Muggle had ever moved. The Sneakoscope in her handbag had gone off like an air-raid siren. She'd taken a photograph. The Ministry had sent it down to Level Nine, because the Beasts Division had nothing in its register that looked like that.

Seven months of watching later, the Department had names. Aro. Caius. Marcus. Jane. Alec. Demetri. Felix. A coven three thousand years old that ran the vampire world from a Tuscan hill town and fed on tourists. They also had the names of the boy, the girl, and the small dark-haired one with them, from the passports used at Florence airport the next morning. The passports led to a town in Washington State. In June, four of the grey cloaks had flown to Seattle, lost the Department's watchers in a forest west of the city, and come home two days later looking satisfied. The rumour had come out of that: a new kind of vampire, in a wet little town at the edge of the world, that didn't eat people.

"Then they'll want the data sooner," Harry said.

"Yes," said Croaker. "They will."

"I'll get it."

"The Cullens."

"Still off the target list. Animal diet, like the file said. Gold eyes, all of them except the Swan girl, and hers are going. The doctor works at the hospital and nobody's died of him." Harry drank some tea. "There's a child."

Croaker's pale eyes sharpened. "A child."

"Little girl. I saw her once, through Omnioculars, from three miles off. The Swan girl was carrying her across the lawn. She looked about a year old. I haven't got close enough to say what she is."

"Find out."

"In my own time. They're on the list as assets. You don't spook assets."

"The mind reader."

"I keep three miles off."

"And if your Occlumency holds against him?"

"Then one day I'll knock on their door and we'll have a nice chat. Not yet."

Croaker studied him through the glass. "The Americans still don't know you're there."

"The Americans don't know about a lot of things, sir."

"Keep it that way."

"Anything else?"

"No."

"The pie here's very good," Harry said.

"Potter."

"Marionberry. It's a sort of blackberry. You'd like it."

The mirror went dark.

---

He drove into Forks at seven in a black 1979 Ford F-150 he'd bought for cash in Port Angeles. Jeans, flannel shirt, waxed jacket; a fly rod in a tube on the bench seat and waders in the back with real river mud on them, because the first rule of a legend was that you did the thing. He'd fished most mornings for twelve days and caught one cutthroat trout and put it back. Fishing, he'd found, was mostly standing in cold water thinking, which suited him.

His name in Forks was Harry Evans—his mother's maiden name, close enough to his own that he'd turn when somebody said it. He was English, he'd come into some family money, and he was taking a season to fish the Olympic rivers for winter steelhead, which didn't really run until December, so he'd come early to learn the water. Every word of it held up.

He parked in the same spot outside the diner on Forks Avenue and took the same booth at the back—back to the wall, sightline to the door, the window, and the kitchen pass. Twelve mornings. By now he was furniture.

"Morning, hon," said Darlene. She was fifty-eight and had worked at the diner for thirty-one years and had bunions that hurt her every single day, which Harry knew because she thought about them every single time she walked past his booth. "Usual?"

"Please, Darlene."

"Eggs over, bacon, hash browns, wheat toast, coffee black." She wrote it anyway. "And?"

"And a slice of the marionberry."

"Pie for breakfast."

"Fruit, pastry, a bit of sugar," Harry said. "In England we call that a balanced meal."

"In England you people eat beans on toast."

"And we're very proud of it."

She smacked his shoulder with her order pad and went off shaking her head.

He drank his coffee and listened.

The Department called it the wide-and-shallow. The deep work was a drill: you looked somebody in the eye and went in after what you wanted. The wide work went the other way. You let your mind go flat and still, like water, and let the surface of every mind nearby touch the edges of it. Fragments. The things people thought while their mouths said something else. No eye contact. No trace. Most people never felt a thing. It was very rude, and Harry had stopped feeling bad about it a long time ago.

Two loggers at the counter: the mill, layoffs in January, the truck payment. A young mother by the window with a toddler: sleep, please, God, sleep. A man in a Mariners cap reading the *Peninsula Daily News*: nothing much, which was restful.

And the two women in the booth behind his, both in their seventies, both in fleece vests, who were talking out loud as well as in their heads.

"—still sick, apparently. That's what Charlie says. Some kind of bug they picked up on that honeymoon. South America. Carlisle's looking after her."

"Well, he's family now."

"She was always a peaky girl, that Bella."

Harry ate his eggs. He already had that one. The town's story was that Chief Swan's daughter had married the doctor's adopted son in August and come back from her honeymoon with something rare and nasty and was being kept quiet at home. It was a decent cover. He'd decided the doctor built it.

"And that Louisiana girl," said the second woman. "The one with the moss. Did you hear? She's going around with Sue Clearwater's boy."

"Seth? He's a baby."

"He's eighteen, Marjorie, and he's the size of a refrigerator."

"She's twenty-two if she's a day."

"Well, Sue doesn't seem to mind. Sue had her over for supper."

"Sue's going around with Charlie Swan, Sue's got her own business to mind."

Harry let that one go by. A botanist from Louisiana; a local boy. In the women's heads, a quick picture of a tall girl with freckles and a pencil in her hair standing in the post office line. Small-town fuel, no use to him. He filed it under the heading he kept for most of what he heard in the wide-and-shallow, *Not Mine*, and went back to his hash browns.

The bell over the door rang, and Charlie Swan came in out of the rain.

Chief of police, mid-forties, moustache, a face used to being patient. He shook the water from his hat and sat at the end of the counter, where he always sat.

Harry let his mind touch the edges of Charlie Swan's.

It was like listening to a radio through a wall. He got the shape of things—a mood, heavy and tired, and under it a stubborn, bewildered happiness, and under *that*, something big and locked that Charlie was carefully not looking at. No words. No pictures.

Most minds were glass. Some were frosted. Charlie Swan's was brick. The only other minds Harry had met that felt like it belonged to trained Occlumenses, and Charlie Swan was a policeman who liked fishing and baseball and, by the look of things, had no idea he was doing it. Harry had written it down and left it there.

Charlie looked over, the way he did most mornings, and this morning, for the first time, got up with his coffee and walked over to the booth.

"You're the English fella," he said. "Out on Dale Pruett's lot."

"Guilty."

"Charlie Swan." He offered a hand. Harry shook it. Charlie's grip was dry and short. "Mind if I sit?"

"Go ahead."

Charlie sat. He looked at Harry's plate. He looked at the pie. "Little early in the year for steelhead."

"Early hatchery fish. And I wanted to learn the water before it gets busy."

"Huh." Charlie drank his coffee. "Catch anything?"

"One cutthroat. I put him back."

"Where?"

"Sol Duc, below the hatchery."

Charlie nodded slowly. "You don't look much like a fisherman."

"What does a fisherman look like?"

"Wetter," Charlie said.

Harry laughed. It was a real laugh; he hadn't been expecting it. Charlie's moustache twitched.

"Look," Charlie said. "You're out there on your own. In a tent, Dale says."

"It's a good tent."

"I'm sure it is. We've had some reports. Hikers, mostly. Big animals in the woods. Real big. Fish and Wildlife says bears." He looked at Harry across the table, and for one second, through the brick wall, Harry felt the mood shift—a hard, careful *something* that Charlie wasn't saying.

"What do you say?" Harry asked.

Charlie looked at him for a long moment.

"I say be careful out there," he said, and got up. "Enjoy your pie."

"Cheers, Chief."

He watched Charlie Swan go back to the counter. Big animals. He'd seen them himself, twice, from a long way off: wolves the size of horses—a sandy one, a grey, a big russet-red one—moving through the forest east of town the way an infantry section moved when it had trained together a long time. They came and went from Quileute land on the coast as if they owned it, which presumably they did. They had heartbeats you could hear from a hillside. He'd read the tribe's old stories at the library in Port Angeles, drawn the obvious conclusion, and filed it under *Not Mine*. Shapeshifters weren't what he'd been sent for.

Well. Technically everything was his department. This month, his job was vampires.

He finished the pie. It was very good.

---

He drove north afterwards and took the long way, the way he always did: up one forest road, down another, a stop for petrol in Sappho that wasn't strictly necessary, watching his mirrors. He'd never had anybody behind him in Forks he couldn't account for. He did it anyway. Habits kept you alive in the places where nothing seemed to be happening.

He parked at a pullout above the Sol Duc and walked up a ridge through second-growth fir to anchor stone thirty-one—a slab of Welsh slate buried under the moss, cut on its underside with eighty-one runes. He crouched in the rain and held his hand over it and felt the line come up to meet his palm like a struck guitar string, taut and humming, running off east and west over the ridge, forty miles of it, waiting.

Intact. He walked back to the truck.

---

By two he was back in the tent. He showered again, because the rain had got into his collar. He dressed.

Afternoons, he wore the suit.

Most things that hunted, hunted at dusk, and whoever he ended up talking to afterward—a coroner, a sheriff, a coven—would believe a suit. He'd learned that from the Americans: a man in a dark suit could walk onto any accident in the United States and have everybody step back, and nobody ever stopped to ask why.

Charcoal wool, single-breasted. White shirt. Narrow black tie. Black Oxfords, which he'd charmed against mud and water. A black overcoat to the knee, charmed against the same. The Hi-Power in its holster under his left arm. His wand—holly, eleven inches, phoenix feather, mended once with a power he had afterwards put back where it came from—in a sheath along his right forearm. In the inside breast pocket, a black leather wallet with a badge and photo identification that read, to anyone who looked at it, as whatever authority they were most inclined to obey. The Department had made it. He'd have done it better.

He checked the mirror on the back of the bedroom door. Six foot two, broad through the shoulders, dark hair untidy on top no matter what he did to it, green eyes. Twenty-six. A man in a dark suit. He'd do.

He made a pot of tea and took it to the kitchen table and sat down with the Galleon in front of him.

It had been Hermione's idea first, at fifteen, in a hidden room on the seventh floor at Hogwarts: fake Galleons under a Protean Charm, so that when she changed the numbers on hers, everybody else's changed to match. This one was tied to all forty-four anchors. When a line closed, the coin would go hot and the numerals around its edge would rearrange into the anchor's number and a grid reference to within ten feet.

He'd moved the Dansette on to *Houses of the Holy*. Robert Plant was singing about the rain. Outside, the actual rain came down on the canvas, steady and soft.

Harry drank his tea and looked at the coin.

He'd waited twelve days. He could wait thirteen.

### VI. Contained

*North of the Sol Duc River — Monday, November 20, 3:51 p.m.*

In late November the light started leaving around three. By four it was mostly gone, a gray that sat between the trees like smoke, and the rain made everything sound closer than it was.

Leah ran the high side of the loop with her ears flat against the wet. Seth ran the river side, half a mile below her, thinking about Thanksgiving.

*—and Mom says Layla can bring a dish, and Layla says she's going to make gumbo, except she says you can't make real gumbo up here because you can't get the right sausage, so she's going to make "Washington gumbo" and apologize for it the whole time—*

*Seth.*

*—and Charlie's coming, and Charlie said he'd bring the pie from the diner, the berry one—*

*Seth. I swear to God.*

*Sorry.*

He wasn't sorry. He hadn't been sorry about anything for sixteen days, and Leah had lived inside his head for every one of them. She knew the inside of that girl's kitchen better than her own now. She knew the thing Layla did with her wand when she was thinking, tapping it against her teeth. She'd told Seth that if he thought one more time about the way Layla's hair looked when she took the pencil out, she'd bite his ear off. He'd thought about it twice as much.

She was used to it now. Mostly. It hurt about as much as a bruise you kept forgetting about and leaning on.

She came over a rise through a stand of young fir, and the wind shifted, and the smell hit her like a slap.

Sweet. Burning. Bleach-in-the-nose sweet, the way they all smelled. But it was wrong. It was nobody she knew. Under the sweetness was blood—human blood, old and new, a lot of it—and under the blood was something cold and salty and rotten-green, like kelp left on the rocks at low tide. Like the bottom of something deep.

She stopped dead.

*Seth.*

He felt it through her and stopped too. *That's not Cullen.*

*No kidding.*

*Who is it?*

*Somebody who eats people.* She had her nose down, working. The trail was fresh. Minutes. It ran north to south across the slope toward the river and the highway and, past the highway, Forks. *Somebody who eats people and smells like the ocean.*

She threw her head back and howled.

It went out long and low through the rain. Three miles east, Jacob was in the Cullens' kitchen eating Esme's lasagna with a two-month-old on his knee. He'd hear it. He always heard it.

Ten seconds later, he was in their heads.

*Where?*

*North of the Sol Duc. Above the hatchery. One of them. Not ours.*

Jacob saw what she saw, smelled what she smelled, and Leah felt his whole body go cold and hard.

*Coming. Don't do anything stupid.*

*Define stupid.*

*Leah.*

She was already running.

---

They found him at the bottom of the slope, a hundred yards above the river.

He was standing in the middle of a game trail with his head tipped back, smelling the air. Thin. Medium height. Dark hair plastered flat. He was wearing a red rain jacket far too big for him, and Leah knew without having to think about it whose jacket it had been and how he'd gotten it. His feet were bare and gray with mud. His face was the kind of beautiful they all had, chalk-white and completely empty.

He heard them. He turned.

His eyes were red. Bright red. Fresh.

For about a quarter of a second he looked at the two of them—a silver-gray wolf and a sandy one, each the size of a small horse, coming down the slope through the ferns—and Leah saw him do the math.

Then he ran.

He was fast. They were always fast. He went downhill toward the river like something fired from a gun, and Leah went after him, and the world narrowed to his red back and the sound of her own breath.

She was the fastest in either pack. She went down the slope in long, flat bounds and felt herself closing. Seth was coming in from the right, cutting off the water.

*He's going for the river,* Seth thought. *They always go for the river—*

*Then don't let him get there.*

Seth put on a burst of speed she didn't know he had and came out of the trees between the vampire and the water, a big sandy wall, jaws open. The vampire swerved hard left, uphill, back toward the ridge, and Leah swerved with him, and now it was just the two of them and the slope and the dark.

He went up the ridge through the second-growth fir. She was three strides behind. Two. She could smell his skin. She could hear the red jacket flapping. Jacob was in her head going *Wait for me, wait for me, Leah*, and she wasn't going to wait for anybody. One stride. She lunged and her teeth snapped shut an inch behind his heel, close enough that she felt the cold come off it.

He went over the top of the ridge.

And stopped—mid-stride, all at once, as if somebody had grabbed him by one ankle with a hand the size of the sky and yanked straight up. His foot jerked into the air. His body whipped around and flipped over. Then he was hanging upside down ten feet off the ground from nothing at all, swinging a little, the red jacket fallen down over his chest. His arms snapped flat to his sides with a sound like two rocks clapping together. His legs slammed together. He went rigid, straight as a plank, from his dangling hair to the bare foot pointing at the sky.

Leah, who had been doing about forty miles an hour uphill, went straight under him.

She skidded. Her back legs went out. She rolled once through the ferns and came up on her feet ten yards down the other side with her hackles standing straight up and her heart going like a hammer, and turned.

Seth came over the ridge and stopped so hard he slid three yards on his haunches.

They stared.

The vampire hung there. Turning, very slowly, on whatever was holding him. Not struggling. He couldn't. Every part of him was locked solid. Except his eyes. His eyes were moving—rolling, wild, red, darting from Leah to Seth to the trees and back again.

*What,* Leah thought, *the hell.*

*Is he—is he dead?*

*They don't die from hanging upside down, Seth.*

*What's holding him?*

*Nothing's holding him. Look. Nothing.*

Through Jacob's eyes she saw ferns going by in a green blur. *What's happening? What am I looking at? Leah, what is that?*

*I don't know.*

She circled. Slowly. Seth circled the other way. The vampire turned on his invisible line, and his red eyes tracked them, and that was all he could do.

Ten feet up. She could reach that. One good jump and she could get her jaws around his shoulder and rip him down off whatever this was and take him apart the way they'd done with the newborns in the clearing last June—

*Leah. Wait. Wait for me. We don't know what that is.*

*I know what he is. He's a leech who eats people.*

*We don't know what's* holding *him. Wait.*

She bunched her back legs under her anyway.

There was a sound like a branch breaking.

Leah spun. Seth spun.

There was a man standing on the ridge.

Twenty feet away, at the top of the slope, where there had been nothing but rain and fir trees a second before. He hadn't walked up. They'd have heard him walk up. They'd have *smelled* him. He was just there.

He was tall, six-two or so, and wide through the shoulders. Dark gray suit, white shirt, narrow black tie, a long black overcoat to the knees. Black shoes, standing in ankle-deep mud that wasn't touching them. Black hair, short at the sides and a mess on top, and dry. Leah stared at that before she understood it: every raindrop that should have hit his head was hitting something an inch above it and running off in a curve, as if he was standing under a sheet of glass nobody could see.

His eyes were green. Very green. Even in the bad light, she noticed.

He looked at the vampire hanging upside down in the air. He looked at the two wolves the size of horses standing in the ferns below him, hackles up, teeth showing.

His expression didn't change much. He looked like a man who'd stepped off a bus and found it was raining.

Then he reached inside his coat.

Leah's whole body tensed. Seth growled.

The man took out a black leather wallet. He flipped it open with a flick of his wrist and held it up at their eye level, the way cops did in movies, so they could see.

"Afternoon," he said.

English. Very English. English like the bad guy in every single action movie Seth had ever made her sit through.

"This situation is contained," he said. "I'm going to have to ask you to vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

Leah looked at the badge.

It was gold, a shield, with an eagle on it, and under it a card with his picture, and in big blue letters across the top it said DEPARTMENT OF HOMELAND SECURITY.

*His says FBI,* Seth thought.

*What?*

*His badge. It says FBI. Federal Bureau of—*

*Mine says Homeland Security.*

*Mine has a picture of him on it. He's not smiling in it.*

*Mine has a picture too, Seth, it's the same—*

The man snapped the wallet shut.

"It says whatever'll get you to leave," he said. "Clearly it isn't working."

Leah went very still.

He put the wallet away. Then he lifted one hand toward the vampire hanging in the air and made a short, loose gesture with two fingers, like somebody flicking a crumb off a table.

The vampire dropped.

The man stepped under it as it came down and caught it one-armed across his body, and in the same motion swung it level and tucked it under his arm—rigid, straight as a board, head forward, bare feet sticking out behind—like a surfer carrying a board down to the water.

Leah knew what they weighed. She'd torn into one in June, in the clearing, and remembered the weight of the arm in her jaws, like a slab of granite countertop.

The man's shoulders dipped about an inch when he took the weight. He shifted his feet once, settling it. Then he stood there with a full-grown vampire under one arm in the pouring rain, like he was waiting for a crossing light.

*Who the hell,* Leah thought, *is this guy?*

"Someone with a badge, Lassie," the man said.

Every hair on Leah's back stood up.

*Did he just—* Seth thought.

"Yes, Balto," the man said, "I did."

Seth's ears went all the way back.

The man's eyes went a little out of focus. Not much—just enough that Leah saw it. He tilted his head, like he was listening to something far away.

"There's three of you in there," he said. "Hang on." A pause. "The big one's running. Hello, mate. You can slow down. It's handled."

In Leah's head, three miles off, Jacob stumbled.

*Is he hearing you? Is he—Leah, is he reading your minds? Like Edward?*

"Bit like Edward," the man agreed, to the empty air. "Different method. Same result."

*Who are you?* Jacob demanded.

"Busy," the man said.

Leah's lips peeled all the way back off her teeth. She thought, very clearly, in detail, with pictures, exactly what she would do to him if he took one more step, and which part she would start with.

The man looked at her. One eyebrow went up.

"Steady on, Lassie," he said. "We've only just met."

*Stop calling me Lassie.*

"Stop thinking so loudly."

*He's not scared of us,* Seth thought. There was something almost admiring in it, which made Leah want to bite him. *Leah, look, he's not even a little bit scared.*

"I've met bigger dogs," the man said.

"Nice bit of driving, by the way," he added. "You put him straight onto my line. Couldn't have done it better if I'd paid you."

*We weren't driving him anywhere,* Leah thought, furious.

"Course you weren't."

He turned to go.

And as he turned, the long sleeve of his overcoat rode up on his right arm, and Leah saw, and Seth saw, the end of something strapped along the inside of his wrist. A slim, dark stick of polished wood, about the length of a ruler, in a leather sheath.

Seth thought it before Leah could stop him. He thought it so loud it rang.

*Leah, look—he's got a wand. In his sleeve. That's a wand. He's a wizard. Like Layla.*

The man stopped walking.

For a moment he stayed like that, his back to them, the vampire under his arm, the rain running off the air over his head. For about three seconds nobody moved. The only sound was the rain.

Then he turned around.

Now he was looking *at* them. At Seth, mostly. The mild, bored look had gone off his face like somebody had wiped it with a cloth, and what was underneath was very calm and very focused, and Leah, who had stood up to Sam Uley's alpha voice and run straight at a newborn vampire army last June, felt the fur along her spine lift on its own.

"Now," he said quietly. "You shouldn't know that."

Seth took a step back.

The man came one step forward.

"Who told you that word, Balto?"

Leah moved without deciding to. She put herself between him and her brother, head low, shoulders up, the growl coming out of her chest so deep it shook the ferns. The man kept his eyes on Seth.

And Leah felt it, then, through the pack. Something pressing at the edge of Seth's mind. Edward only ever listened. This pushed, like a hand on a doorknob. Seth felt it too. She felt him feel it, felt the panic slam up in him—*Layla, don't think about Layla, don't think about her, don't*—and the harder he didn't think about her, the brighter she got. Her face. Her kitchen. Her wand tapping against her teeth.

And the stone.

Seth ducked his head to his left foreleg, where the leather cord was tied tight above his paw, and bit down on the hag stone so hard Leah heard it click against his teeth.

The man watched him do it.

Leah saw that. She'd go back to it later, over and over, in the dark. The man's green eyes went to Seth's mouth a half-second before Seth moved. He saw it coming. He had plenty of time. He just stood there and let it happen, his head a little on one side, like he was curious what it would do.

The air cracked again, sharper this time, like a gunshot.

Layla was there.

Ten feet to the left, in the ferns, in her big canvas coat with flour on the front of it, her hair half out of its knot and the pencil gone. She landed already turning, knees bent, wand up. Her eyes went from Seth to Leah to the man in the suit to the vampire rigid under his arm, and she didn't stop to ask a single question. She fired before her feet had finished landing.

A bolt of red light shot from the end of her wand straight at the man's chest.

He lifted his free hand, the bare left one, and flicked two fingers to the side, the same lazy little flick he'd used on the vampire.

The red bolt bent.

It turned ninety degrees in midair like it had hit a wall at an angle, went off sideways into the trees, and smacked into the trunk of a big hemlock thirty feet away with a bang. Bark sprayed. Sparks hissed out in the rain.

Layla's face went white. She fired again. Faster. Her whole arm in it.

The man raised the same hand, palm out.

The second bolt stopped.

It just stopped. Four feet in front of him, at the height of his chest. A ball of red light about the size of a fist, fizzing and spitting like a sparkler on the Fourth of July, hanging dead still in the rain. The raindrops falling through it hissed into steam.

He leaned forward a little and looked at it.

He studied it. Head tilted. Narrow-eyed. The way Leah's dad used to look at a fish he'd just pulled up, turning it to the light, deciding if it was a keeper.

"Stunner," the man said. "Decent one." He glanced past it at Layla. "You're snapping your wrist at the end. It's putting a hook on it. That's why the first one was already drifting left before I touched it."

He closed his fingers.

The red light went out like a pinched match.

The man straightened. The vampire was still under his other arm; he hadn't put it down once. He looked at Layla across the ten feet of ferns and rain, and his voice, when it came, was almost polite.

"I could have you arrested and your wand snapped for attacking an Unspeakable," he said. "You know that?"

### VII. Unspeakable

*The ridge above the Sol Duc — 4:09 p.m.*

*Unspeakable.*

Two minutes ago Layla Broussard had been at her stove stirring a roux for a practice gumbo when the hag stone went off in her chest like a fishhook yanked through her breastbone. She'd had just enough time to grab her wand before the pull took her. She'd come out of the squeeze into rain and ferns with her heart going like a rabbit's, seen Seth, and Leah, and a man in a suit holding a *vampire*, and done the only thing her body could think of to do.

And now the man in the suit had caught her Stunner out of the air like a baseball and told her she was holding her wrist wrong.

*Unspeakable.*

She'd heard the word once in her life from somebody who wasn't telling a ghost story. Professor Thibodeaux, History of Magic, fifth year at Ilvermorny. They'd been doing the foreign ministries, and Thibodeaux had gotten to the British Department of Mysteries and stopped, and taken off his glasses, and looked out at thirty fifteen-year-olds.

*Nobody knows what they do,* he'd said. *The people who find out don't generally say. If you ever meet an Unspeakable, Miss Broussard*—because she'd been the one in the back row passing notes—*be polite. Be brief. And for the love of God, child, do not be interesting.*

She had just shot at one. Twice.

That was about as interesting as it got.

She kept her wand up. Her arm was shaking. She couldn't make it stop, so she kept it up anyway, because it was the only thing she had.

She had seen somebody stop a spell in the air exactly once before. Her seventh-year dueling instructor, Madam Okonkwo, had done it as a demonstration—with a week of preparation, a wand, a jinx fired at half speed by a student told exactly when to fire, and a lot of visible sweat. The class had applauded for a full minute. Okonkwo had sat down afterward and asked for a glass of water.

This man had done it with his bare hand. On a Stunner she'd fired at full strength with no warning. While holding a vampire under the other arm. And then he'd *looked* at it.

"You're on American soil," Layla heard herself say. Her voice came out higher than she wanted. "You're—this is MACUSA jurisdiction. You can't just—"

"MACUSA," the man said, "would love to hear from me. Shall I ring them?" He shifted the vampire a little higher under his arm. "I've got a number for Magical Law Enforcement in the Woolworth Building. Lovely people. Very keen on paperwork. Very keen on wand permits." His eyes went to the wand in her hand and back to her face. "You've got a permit for that, Miss Broussard?"

She hadn't told him her name.

She felt the cold go down her arms all the way to her fingers.

"Stay out of his head," she said.

"He's thinking about you very loudly," the man said. "It's hard not to hear. It's like trying not to hear a brass band." His gaze moved to her face and stayed there. "You, on the other hand—"

Something touched her.

It was the strangest thing she'd ever felt. Like a thumb pressing gently on the back of her eyes from the inside. Like pages turning in a book she didn't know she was holding. She saw her mama's kitchen in Chalmette before the water, flash, and the Pukwudgie common room with the hanging gourds, flash, and the sign on the door at the Thriftway, flash, and Seth's face in the moonlight, flash—

She shoved. She didn't know how. She'd never learned. She just *shoved*, the way you'd shove somebody who'd walked into your bathroom, with everything she had.

The pressure went away.

The man blinked. He looked, for the first time, very faintly surprised.

"Don't strain anything," he said. "Layla Broussard. Twenty-two. Chalmette, Louisiana. Ilvermorny." A short pause. "Pukwudgie. I'd have guessed that."

"Get out of my head."

"I'm out." He said it plainly. "And you've told a pack of shapeshifters and a coven of vampires all about the International Statute of Secrecy, in what—a month? Two weeks?" One eyebrow went up. "Busy fortnight."

Layla's face went hot.

"They're not No-Majs," she said.

"No," the man agreed. "They're not." He looked at Seth, at Leah, at the trees around them. "And that's the only reason we're having this conversation out here in the rain. The other place for it is a holding cell in New York."

Seth growled. It came up from somewhere deep in his chest and rolled through the ferns, and Layla felt it in the soles of her boots.

"Easy, Balto," the man said, without looking at him. "Nobody's arresting anybody."

"Yet," Layla said.

"I like to keep my options open."

Something big came crashing through the trees behind him.

Layla flinched. The man didn't. A wolf burst out of the firs at the top of the ridge, so huge that for a second Layla's brain refused to fit it into the space—russet-red, black-lipped, shoulders higher than the man's head, his hackles standing up in a ridge all the way down his back. He hit the ground between the man and the others and planted himself there, head low, every tooth showing, a snarl coming out of him so loud and so deep that Layla felt it as pressure in the air.

The man turned his head and looked at him.

"And here's Clifford," he said.

Layla made a sound she didn't mean to make. It might have been a laugh. It was the most horrified laugh she had ever laughed in her life.

The red wolf's snarl went up a full octave.

"Jacob," the man said, looking at him. His eyes had gone a little unfocused again, the way they had before. "Jacob Black. You're the alpha. You've got a—" He paused. "Ah. You've got a little girl to get home to. So let's keep this short."

Jacob lunged half a step forward and stopped himself, his whole body vibrating.

The man raised an eyebrow at him. "You brought her up, mate," he said mildly. "You've been thinking about her since you left the kitchen."

There was a blur at the edge of the trees. Then another. Layla saw them only when they stopped moving: two pale figures standing among the firs fifty feet away, as if they'd been there the whole time. Edward, his hair dark with rain, his eyes black. Carlisle beside him, in a doctor's long coat, his face very calm.

They'd run from the house, Layla realized. They'd heard Jacob—Edward had heard Jacob—the second he phased.

Edward was staring at the man in the suit.

Layla had never seen Edward's face look like this. It had gone completely blank—the blankness of somebody straining to hear a sound and getting silence.

"I can't hear him," Edward said.

"No," said the man. "You can't."

He looked Edward over—a long, careful look, head to foot, the way you'd look at a car you were thinking about buying.

"Edward Cullen," he said. "From the clock tower."

Edward went still in a way Layla hadn't known a person could go still. Even for them.

Carlisle stepped forward. He kept his hands loose and open at his sides where everyone could see them. "I'm Carlisle Cullen," he said. His voice was the one he used at the hospital. "I don't believe we've met."

"We haven't. I know who you are, Doctor." The man nodded to him. It was almost courteous. "You're on a list of people I'm not allowed to hurt. Congratulations. It's a short list."

"I'm—flattered, I suppose."

"Don't be. It's mostly a list of people who are useful alive."

Carlisle let that sit a moment. Then he looked, very deliberately, at the rigid shape under the man's arm. The red eyes rolled toward him, desperate.

"And our—visitor?"

"He's not your visitor." The man shifted the vampire around so that its face hung right in front of his own, upside down, a foot away. He looked into its rolling red eyes.

He held it for maybe two seconds. Nobody said anything. Layla had the sudden, sick feeling she was watching somebody read a book very, very fast.

Then he swung the vampire back under his arm.

"He came across from Vancouver Island on Friday night," the man said. "Walked across the bottom of the Strait. They don't need to breathe. Pulled a fisherman off his boat out of Sekiu on the way over. Came up on the beach at Shi Shi. Killed two campers at Lake Ozette on Saturday—a man and a woman, late twenties. That's their jacket." He looked at Leah. "That's what you smelled, Lassie. The bottom of the sea."

Leah didn't make a sound. Layla saw her ears move.

"I had every river mouth on this coast lined," the man said. He said it without heat, like a man noting he'd left a window open. "He walked up the sand between them. That's a gap I'll be closing tonight."

"Lined," Edward said.

"What," Carlisle said carefully, "do you intend to do with him?"

"Study him."

"Study," Edward said. His voice had gone very quiet.

"Learn how he's put together. What hurts him. What doesn't." The man's tone didn't change. "What he can do. What can be done to him."

"Why?"

"Because my employers would like to know, Doctor."

Carlisle looked at him for a long moment.

"Your employers," he said slowly, "in London."

The man said nothing.

"You know the Volturi," Carlisle said.

"Never been to Volterra myself," the man said. "Colleagues have. Lovely town. Very old. Lots of steps." A pause. "Terrible tourist retention."

Edward made a sound in his throat.

"You know what they do," Edward said. "In the tower. With the tours."

"I know that since April, Heidi's walked nine hundred and sixty-three people into the Palazzo dei Priori on guided tours," the man said, "and forty-one of them walked back out." He let that sit in the rain. "So yes. I'm going to study him."

Carlisle closed his eyes.

Nobody said anything for a while. Rain ran down the bark of the hemlock where Layla's first Stunner had scorched it. Seth had pressed up against her side, enormous and hot, so hot she could feel it through her coat, and she put her hand in the fur of his shoulder without looking.

"Who are you?" she said.

The man looked at her.

"Harry," he said.

Leah flinched.

It went through her whole body—a hard jolt, as if the name had hit her in the ribs. Seth made a high, small sound in his chest, a whine Layla had never heard come out of him, and pressed harder against her. Even Jacob's snarl stuttered.

The man's eyes went to Leah. Then to Seth. He was still a moment.

"Your dad," he said.

Leah's lips came all the way off her teeth.

The man didn't move. He didn't look away from her. Something in his face changed—not much; Layla wouldn't have noticed it two minutes ago—and then he said, quite plainly, "Sorry, Lassie," and left it there.

Leah turned her head away and stared hard into the trees.

The man—Harry—let a breath out through his nose. When he spoke again, it was to all of them.

"Right. Here's how it's going to go." He lifted his chin toward the forest around them. "There are lines across this whole peninsula. Ridges, rivers, passes. Tonight, beaches. They catch anything that walks without a pulse and has had human blood in it in the last month or so. Doctor, your family can walk across them all day long and never know they're there. So can the wolves. So can anybody with a heartbeat." He looked at Edward. "Your visitors are your business. Make sure they've been eating deer."

"And if they haven't?" Edward asked quietly.

"Then I'll have a matched set."

Jacob growled.

"Yes, all right, Clifford," Harry said, without looking around. "It's Forest Service land, this. Your line's six miles west. I've been very careful about that. You can check."

He looked at Carlisle.

"I'll come by the house tomorrow. Noon. We'll have a proper chat. Indoors, ideally. I'll bring pie."

"We don't eat," Carlisle said faintly.

"More for me and Clifford, then."

Then he looked at Layla.

She made herself hold still for it.

"As for you." He considered her the way he'd considered the Stunner. "I haven't made up my mind about you, Miss Broussard. When I have, I'll let you know." A beat. "Keep the wand. For now. You'll probably want it."

Layla's throat was too tight to say anything. She nodded.

He turned to go. Then he stopped and looked back over his shoulder, at Seth. At the leather cord on Seth's left foreleg, and the gray-green stone hanging from it, wet with spit.

"That stone," he said. "You made that?"

"Yes," Layla said.

He looked at it a few seconds longer—really looked, his eyes narrowing, the way he'd looked at the Stunner hanging in the air.

"Protean Charm on a holed stone," he said, slowly, as if he was reading it off a page. "With a homing anchor. Turned inside out, so the stone pulls you to it." He was quiet a moment. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody," Layla said. "I worked it out."

"Huh."

He looked at her again. It was a different look. Shorter.

"That's good work," he said. "Your anchor's loose, though. You'll have landed ten feet off from where you meant. Tighten the third binding." He turned away. "I knew a girl who did something like it with coins, when she was fifteen. She'd have liked you."

He took one step through the ferns, the vampire under his arm, the rain running off the air above his head, and turned on his heel.

There was a crack like a branch breaking, and he was gone.

---

For a long time, nobody moved.

The rain came down. The river ran. Somewhere off in the trees a varied thrush gave one long, flat whistle and stopped.

Layla's arm was still up. She lowered it. Her wand hand was shaking so hard she couldn't get the wand back into her coat on the first try, or the second. On the third it went in.

She *had* landed off, she thought, stupidly. She'd meant to come out right next to Seth and she'd come out ten feet to the left of him. She'd thought it was nerves.

*Tighten the third binding.*

Beside her, Seth shuddered—a long, rolling shudder that went from his ears to his tail—and then he was gone out from under her hand, and there was a boy crouched in the ferns, naked and steaming, scrambling at the cord on his ankle for his shorts. Layla turned around to give him a second. She looked at Edward instead.

Edward was still staring at the place where Harry had been.

"Nothing," he said. "Not one word. Not a whisper. It's like Bella, before. Except Bella never knew she was doing it." He held up one pale hand and let it fall. "He knew. He was *holding* it shut."

"Occlumency," Layla said.

Edward turned his head and looked at her.

"You said it was rare," he said.

"It is."

"How rare?"

Layla thought about Professor Thibodeaux taking his glasses off. She thought about Madam Okonkwo asking for a glass of water. She thought about a red Stunner hanging in the air four feet from a man's chest, fizzing in the rain, and the man leaning forward to look at it.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I've never met anybody who could do it."

Seth came up beside her, hopping as he yanked his cutoffs up the rest of the way. He was soaking and shaking, and his eyes were huge.

"He called me Balto," he said.

"I know, baby."

"He called Leah *Lassie*."

"I know."

Seth was quiet a second. "Balto was a hero, though," he said. "Like, a real dog. He brought medicine to Alaska."

Layla started to laugh. She couldn't stop it. It came out of her in a wet, shaky rush, and she put her face into Seth's bare, burning shoulder and laughed into it until it turned into something else, and Seth put both arms around her and held on.

Over his shoulder, she saw Leah. The silver wolf was standing very still in the ferns, staring at the empty place on the ridge, her ears flat to her skull.

"What's she thinking?" Layla asked Edward, muffled.

Edward was quiet for a moment.

"She'd like it known," he said, "that she hates him."

"Yeah," Layla said into Seth's shoulder. "Get in line."

Carlisle hadn't moved. He was looking at the hemlock where her first spell had hit—at the black scorch mark on the bark, still steaming faintly in the rain.

"Noon," he said quietly, to nobody. "He said noon."

"He said he'd bring pie," said Seth.

### VIII. Specimen

*The tent, Bogachiel River — 4:16 p.m.*

He came out of the Apparition in the specimen room, where the wards would let him and nobody else, and the cold of the place closed around him like a hand.

The stasis charms hummed on the shelves. In its bell jar, the Inferius hand curled its fingers once and was still.

Harry carried the vampire down the length of the room, past the stone basin, through the glass door into the cell, and laid him on the steel table. The restraints knew what to do. They closed over wrists, elbows, ankles, knees, throat, chest—bands of goblin steel two inches wide—and the runes cut into the floor around the table lit up, one after another, a slow, dull red, until the circle was closed.

He took off his overcoat and hung it on the hook by the door. He took off his suit jacket and hung it over the coat. He rolled his sleeves to the elbow. The Hi-Power stayed where it was.

Then he released the Body-Bind from the neck up.

The vampire's jaw came unlocked with a crack. Its lips peeled back. The noise it made came from somewhere far back in its chest, a hissing, grinding snarl, and every muscle in its body heaved against the steel at once. The table bolts groaned in the floor. The restraints held. Harry had built them to hold a great deal more than this, and he had tested them, and they held.

He waited until it stopped.

"Name," he said.

Nothing. Red eyes, wide and fixed on his face. Hate in them, and under the hate, a great deal of fear.

"Fine," said Harry. He drew his wand. "*Imperio*."

It was like pushing his hand into wet cement.

He'd put the Imperius on goblins, on a Death Eater, on a Muggle bank manager in Zurich, and on a hippogriff once for a bet. Human minds took it like water taking a dropped stone. This one was cold and heavy and very slow to give. He leaned on it. He leaned harder. For four long seconds it held him off.

Then it gave, all at once, and the vampire's face went slack and smooth and peaceful as a sleeping child's.

Harry let out a breath.

"Name," he said again.

"Mattias." The voice was soft. Dreamy. A trace of something Central European in the vowels.

"How old are you, Mattias?"

"I was made in the spring of 1871. In Brno."

"Where did you come from this time?"

"Victoria. On the island. It was getting crowded. Too many eyes."

"Why here?"

"Quiet," said Mattias. "Rain. Hikers. Nobody counts the hikers."

"Do you know the Volturi?"

Even under the curse, even with his face gone smooth and empty as an egg, something went through him—a long, rippling flinch from his shoulders to his feet, against the steel.

"Everyone knows," he whispered.

"Have you seen them?"

"Once."

"Where?"

"Prague. 1958. A man fed in the Old Town Square. In the open. In daylight. Children saw." Mattias's eyes were fixed on the ceiling. "They came for him three nights later. Four of them, in grey. And a girl. A small girl. Fair. Like an angel in a church."

"What did she do?"

"She looked at him." Mattias's voice was barely there. "That was all. She looked at him, and he fell down in the square and screamed. He screamed until they had taken him apart and set him on fire. He screamed in the fire."

"What was her name?"

"Jane," said Mattias.

Harry looked at him for a while.

Then he lifted the curse.

It went out of the vampire like air out of a tyre, and his face snapped back into itself—rage, terror, the red eyes jerking wildly from Harry's face to the wand in Harry's hand and back—and he heaved against the steel again, and the bolts groaned again, and held again.

"Right," said Harry. "Let's find out if it's the same."

He'd been seventeen the first time. Amycus Carrow, in the Ravenclaw common room, for spitting in Minerva McGonagall's face. He'd meant it. Bellatrix Lestrange had told him once, in the Atrium of the Ministry, with Sirius not an hour dead, that you had to mean it, and she'd been right about that, as she had been right about very few things. He had found since that you could mean it in more than one way. You could mean it like a scalpel.

"*Crucio*," he said.

Three seconds.

Mattias screamed. It was a terrible sound, high and raw and grinding, like a band saw going through a church bell, and it went on, and Harry held it, counting, watching—the arch of the back against the steel, the jaw locked open, the eyes rolled up to show nothing but red—and on three he lifted the wand.

The scream cut off.

The vampire lay on the table and shook. Fine, fast tremors, all over. Harry didn't know they could shake. He made a note of it in his head.

"Well?" he said. "Same?"

Mattias's eyes found his face. It took a while.

"…Yes," he whispered. "Like her. Like *her*."

Harry nodded slowly.

He put the Body-Bind back on from the neck up, gently, and the shaking stopped, and the red eyes stayed open and fixed on him. Then he went out of the cell, and closed the glass door, and sat down at the bench by the stone basin with the notebook.

He wrote:

*20.xi.06. 16:24. Subject M. Nomad. Red-eyed. Made 1871 (per subject). Captured line 31, Sol Duc.*

*Imperius: takes. High initial resistance (cf. wet concrete), approx. 4 sec. Full compliance after. Recall detailed.*

*Cruciatus: takes. 3 sec. Marked tremor on release, not previously observed in species.*

*Subject reports Cruciatus subjectively identical to effect of J. (Volturi guard; witnessed Prague, 1958).*

*Implication: J.'s gift and the Cruciatus act along the same pathway, or produce effects the subject cannot tell apart.*

*Query: does a Shield Charm tuned against the Cruciatus stop J.? Does Occlumency?*

*If yes —*

He stopped and looked at the two words for a long time.

Then he underlined them.

---

At five he turned the mirror over on the kitchen table. The Galleon was lying beside it where he'd left it, and the numerals around its edge still read 31.

"Croaker."

The old face swam up in the glass. It was one in the morning in London. Croaker didn't look as if he'd been asleep. Harry had never once seen him look as if he'd been asleep.

"Potter."

"Got one."

Something moved in Croaker's face. On anybody else it would have been a smile. "Condition?"

"Intact. Talking. Born 1871, if he's telling the truth, and he is. Imperius takes. Cruciatus takes." Harry paused. "He says the Cruciatus feels like the little blonde one."

The mirror was silent for a moment.

"That's useful," Croaker said.

"Thought you'd like it."

"The Cullens?"

"Met two. The doctor and the mind reader. And three of the wolves."

"Wolves," said Croaker.

"Shapeshifters. Local. Tribe. Big as horses, sharp as tacks. I'll write it up." Harry picked up the Galleon and turned it over in his fingers. "The mind reader couldn't get in."

"You're sure?"

"He said so. He looked like he'd lost a tooth." Harry put the Galleon down. "I'm having lunch with them tomorrow."

"That wasn't the plan."

"Plan's changed. They've got a witch."

There was a pause.

"They've got a *what*?"

"Local girl. American. Ilvermorny. She moved here a month or two back and fell in with the wolves—one of the wolves—and she's told the lot of them about the Statute. Wolves, vampires, all of them. They know about us." Harry drank some cold tea. "Bit of a temper. She put two Stunners at me. Very good with a Protean Charm."

Croaker's face stayed the same. His silence got heavier.

"Is she a problem?" he said.

Harry thought about it. He thought about a girl with flour down the front of her coat landing ten feet off her mark in the rain and firing a second time, wand arm shaking, at a man who'd just knocked her first spell into a tree, because he'd been standing too close to her boy.

"Haven't decided," he said.

"Potter—"

"I'll let you know, sir."

He turned the mirror face-down.

The Dansette had run out a while ago; the needle was ticking in the run-out groove. He lifted it off. Outside, the rain kept on, and it was full dark.

He went down the corridor to the armory and took the cut-down Remington off the rack and checked the tube, and filled his coat pockets with Severing rounds. Then he went back to the specimen room and stood for a moment at the glass.

Mattias lay on the steel table under the white light, bound from his heels to his chin, and his red eyes followed Harry from one side of the glass to the other.

"Get comfortable, mate," Harry said. "You're the only one I've got."

He put out the light.

Then he went to close his beaches.
