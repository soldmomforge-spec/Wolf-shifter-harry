# Bob's Your Uncle

---

Leah had come back into his head forty minutes ago like somebody kicking a door in, and Seth still hadn't gotten over it.

He'd been waiting for it. He'd sat at the edge of the trees for an hour with his tail going numb in the snow, feeling the hole in the pack-mind where she'd been, and he'd heard every word out there on that blanket with his own ears anyway. He'd been ready. He still hadn't been ready. When she phased she'd landed in the pack-mind all in one go, a whole hour of her at once, and it had hit him the way a wave hits a kid on a boogie board.

The couch. The tart. Dad's name. The book with the torn page, and the one word in green ink. *Don't.* Her own hand closing on the back of the guy's jacket, and the horror after. Sam, standing in a field, a big black wolf she could look at like a tree.

Since then the two of them had sat at the edge of the trees and watched the wizards leave, one crack after another, and the bloodsuckers drift off in their little groups, and the English guy stand around in the middle of the empty field talking to his elf. And Seth had said the same thing about forty times.

*Leah,* Seth said again. *You offered him the* couch.

*Shut up.*

*The grape soda couch.*

*SHUT UP, Seth.*

From a long way off, Jacob tuned in. Seth could feel where he was without trying: trotting through the trees behind the Cullens on the way back to the big white house, with Bella a few yards ahead of him carrying Nessie and Nessie looking back at him over her mom's shoulder. Jacob's head had been full of the clearing for the last forty minutes, the ash, the black star, the little gray elf, Nessie, Nessie, Nessie; he'd been too busy to listen to anything else. It all went sideways when he finally caught up with what Leah had done.

*You offered him the couch?*

*Don't you start.*

*Your mom's gonna kill you.*

*My mom,* Leah said, *is going to make him a sandwich. That's worse.*

Seth couldn't help it. He laughed. It came out of him as a sort of wheezing yip, and Leah's mind came round on him like a snapping jaw, and he ducked his head and kept laughing anyway, because it was true, it was so true, Mom was absolutely going to make the guy a sandwich.

Then he heard boots in the snow behind him and stopped laughing.

The English guy had come across the field. He came without hurrying, hands in his jacket pockets, the same way he'd walked everywhere all morning, and he stopped a few yards off, at the very edge of the trees, and looked at the two of them. Seth had never been looked at by anybody quite like that. It was the way his old basketball coach used to look at the team on the first day of practice: friendly enough, and figuring out who was going to be a problem.

Seth sat up straighter. He couldn't help that either.

"Right," the guy said. "Which way's La Push?"

Leah turned round and ran.

She just went. One second she was standing there with her head up, and the next she was a gray streak going away through the firs with snow kicking up behind her, and the pack-mind was full of nothing but the drum of her paws and a single word, over and over, in a voice so tight it squeaked. *Go. Go. Go.*

Seth looked after her. He looked back at the guy. He whined.

The guy watched Leah disappear into the trees. Then he looked down at Seth.

"Go on, then," he said. "I'll keep up."

Seth went.

He went the way he'd go after Leah any day of the week, flat out, ears back, letting the wolf have it, because Leah when she ran like that didn't slow down for anybody and you either kept up or you got left. The trees came at him and went past. The snow was thin under the big firs, crusted, and his paws broke through it and hit the needles underneath, and he went over a deadfall and down into a gully and up the other side with the cold air pouring into his lungs, and he didn't look back. He didn't need to. The guy was a person. A person on two legs. Seth figured he'd hear him crashing around back there for about thirty seconds and then he'd be gone, and they'd have to circle back for him, and Leah would be furious about that too.

He'd gone maybe two hundred yards when he realized he couldn't hear the crashing.

He couldn't hear anything. Just Leah way out ahead, and his own paws, and his own breath, and the wind.

He looked back.

The guy was right there. About ten feet behind Seth's tail and off to the left, between two trees, running.

Seth nearly ran into a cedar.

He swerved at the last second and caught himself, and his heart was going like crazy, and he looked again. The guy was still there. He was running with his hands loose at his sides and his mouth shut, breathing through his nose, and his boots were coming down on the crust and the needles with hardly any sound at all. He came to a fallen hemlock as thick as a car and went up and over it in one long stride, one boot on the top of it, and came down on the far side and kept going without changing pace, and the trunk didn't so much as shed its snow.

He looked over at Seth.

"Eyes front," he said. "You'll hit a tree."

*LEAH,* Seth yelled into the pack-mind. *LEAH. HE'S KEEPING UP.*

*I know.*

*He's running! On his legs! He's not even breathing hard!*

*I know, Seth.*

*How do you know, you haven't even looked back—*

*Because I can feel him,* Leah snapped. *I can feel exactly where he is. He's ten feet behind you and to the left. He's been ten feet behind you and to the left since the clearing.* A pause, and then, very bitter: *He's been exactly as far behind me as I've been ahead of him the whole way. If I speed up, he speeds up.*

Seth thought about that.

*Are you trying to lose him?*

*No.*

*You totally are.*

Another pause, longer.

*I can't anyway,* Leah said.

She didn't say anything after that. Seth felt her put her head down and just run. Up ahead of him she went over the ridge where the old logging road cut through, and down the other side toward the river, and he went after her, and ten feet behind him and to the left the English guy went after both of them.

Seth stopped yelling. He just ran, and watched.

It was a long way. From the clearing out to La Push was the better part of fifteen miles as the crow flew, and the wolves never went as the crow flew; they went the way the land let them, along the ridges and down through the creek beds and around the clear-cuts, and today, with snow on the ground, it was rougher than usual. Seth knew every foot of it. He'd run it a hundred times since he phased. He'd never once run it with somebody on two legs beside him, and he kept waiting for the guy to fall behind, and he didn't. When they went down the steep bank to the Calawah, Leah went down it in three long scrambling bounds the way she always did, and Seth went down it in four, and the guy went down it at a sort of sliding run, his boots skating on the frozen mud, one hand reaching out to touch a sapling on the way past and let it go, and was at the bottom before Seth was. When they crossed the river, on the stones where it ran shallow over the gravel bar, the wolves splashed straight through. The guy went across the tops of the stones. Seth watched him do it. Wet stones, iced at the edges, and he went across them one after another like they were stair treads, and at the far bank he wasn't even damp.

*Leah,* Seth said, very quietly. *Did you see that?*

*I'm not looking.*

*He went across on the rocks.*

*I said I'm not looking.*

She was, though. Seth could feel her looking. He could feel her watching the guy go across those stones with the corner of her eye, and he could feel her hating herself for it, and under the hate something else, something quiet and steady and very interested, that was keeping track of exactly where the guy put every foot.

Seth decided not to say anything about that. He was fifteen. He knew when to keep his mouth shut.

They hit the highway about a mile north of the turnoff to La Push.

You could hear it before you got there, the long hiss of tires on wet asphalt, and Leah pulled up in the trees above it the way they always did, ears forward, waiting for a gap. Seth came up beside her and stood panting. It was the 101; there was always something on it. A pickup went by, and then a minivan with its wipers going, and then, from the south, the deep diesel grind of a logging truck laboring up the grade, the big kind, three trailers' worth of stripped trunks stacked up behind the cab and chained down.

Leah waited. Seth waited.

The guy came up behind them and stopped. He didn't seem to be waiting for anything. He looked at the road, and at the truck coming up the grade, and at the two of them standing there in the trees with their sides heaving.

"Is this a thing?" he said. "Do you just stand here?"

Seth looked at him. Leah didn't.

"Right," the guy said. "Hang on."

Something cold went down Seth's back.

It started at the top of his head, between his ears, and ran down his neck and spine and all the way to the tip of his tail, and it felt exactly like somebody had cracked a raw egg on his skull. He yelped. He couldn't help it. He spun round in a circle trying to see what was on him, and saw his own back end, and his own back end wasn't there.

It was there. He could feel it. He could see it, sort of, if he looked at it hard: a kind of ripple in the air, the shape of a sandy wolf outlined in the snow and the trees behind it, like looking through the heat off a barbecue. But the color had gone out of him. He looked like the forest. He looked like the snow.

Beside him, Leah had gone the same way. She was a wolf-shaped ripple in the air, and the ripple had its ears flat back and was staring at the guy, and Seth didn't need the pack-mind to know what the look on her face would have been if he could see it.

*What did he DO—*

*I don't KNOW—*

"It wears off," the guy said. "Go on. Before the next one."

The logging truck went past at the bottom of the bank. Seth watched the driver go by, a big bearded guy in a red cap with a cigarette in the corner of his mouth, looking straight up the bank at them. He looked right at them. His eyes went across the place where they were standing and kept on going, bored, and the truck ground on up the grade toward Forks.

Leah went down the bank and across the road in two bounds. Seth went after her. The guy came after both of them, and on the other side, in the trees, the cold egg feeling ran backward up Seth's spine and off the top of his head, and he looked round and his tail was sandy again.

*Okay,* Seth said. *Okay. That was the coolest thing that's ever happened to me.*

*It was creepy.*

*It was creepy AND cool.*

*Seth—*

*He did the lightning, and the fire, and the book thing, and the elf, and now he's made us INVISIBLE, Leah, he's made us—*

*He's showing off,* Leah said.

Seth thought about the guy standing there in the trees asking *Is this a thing? Do you just stand here?* in that flat, bored voice, like he'd been standing at a crosswalk waiting for the light to change.

*I don't think he's showing off,* he said. *I think he just didn't want to wait.*

Leah didn't answer that.

From a long way off, Jacob said, *What happened? Something happened. Why did you two just go weird?*

*He made us invisible,* Seth told him, and showed him, and felt Jacob go very quiet and very thoughtful at the other end, and felt him decide not to say anything to Bella about it yet.

Then, after a moment: *Seth. Tell him my dad wants to see him.*

*Now?*

*Tomorrow. Dad's been on the phone with Old Quil. They want to do the council thing at your place. Ten o'clock.* A pause. *Your mom already said yes. She said if the guy's gonna be under her roof anyway, it saves Dad the drive.*

*Leah, did you hear—*

*I heard.*

They crossed onto the rez a little after that. Seth knew the exact spot, even in the snow; the line wasn't marked on anything, it didn't need to be, it was just there, in the ground, in the smell of things, the place where the treaty said the Cullens stopped and La Push began. Every wolf in both packs knew it the way you knew the edge of your own bed in the dark. Seth went over it without slowing down. So did Leah.

Seth looked back to see what the guy would do.

The guy did nothing. He went over the line at the same pace he'd crossed everything else, without looking down, without a pause. If he felt anything there, it stayed off his face. He stepped from one side of a hundred-year-old treaty to the other like it was a crack in a sidewalk.

Seth wasn't sure why that bothered him. It did, a little. Then it didn't, because the trees were thinning out ahead of them and he could smell woodsmoke and salt and wet cedar shingles, and somebody's dryer vent going, and the sea.

They came out of the trees behind the house the way they always did, behind the woodshed, where you couldn't see them from the road.

It was the same house it had always been. Two stories of gray cedar shingle gone silver with salt and weather, green trim around the windows that Dad had painted the summer before he died and nobody had touched since, a back porch with a sagging step, Mom's truck in the gravel drive with the snow already sliding off its hood. Out here near the ocean the snow had mostly gone to slush. The yard was a mess of it, gray and soft and pocked with drips from the eaves. The sky over the water was the color of a dirty sink.

There was a light on in the kitchen.

Leah didn't stop. She went straight round the back of the woodshed, and Seth felt her phase, felt the pack-mind empty out where she'd been, and then heard her bare feet on the slush, and the back door bang, and her voice inside, low and fast. He couldn't make out the words. He didn't need to. He'd heard her use that voice on Mom before.

He looked at the guy.

The guy had stopped at the edge of the trees. He was standing with his hands back in his pockets, looking at the house. He wasn't breathing hard. He hadn't been breathing hard the whole way. He looked at the gray shingles and the green trim and the sagging back step and the light in the kitchen window, and his face didn't do anything at all.

"Go on," he said to Seth. "Get your trousers on. I'll wait."

Seth went behind the woodshed.

Phasing back was always weird in the cold. It was the snow, he thought; one second he was a wolf standing in it and couldn't feel it at all, and the next second he was a skinny guy standing in it barefoot and he still couldn't really feel it, but his brain *knew* he should, and kept trying to send him the message anyway. He got the cord off his ankle and shook out his cut-offs and pulled them on. He didn't have a shirt. He never had a shirt. He came round the side of the woodshed scrubbing a hand through his hair.

The guy was still standing at the edge of the trees, exactly where he'd left him.

"Hey," Seth said.

"Hey."

"I'm Seth."

"I know." The guy looked him up and down. "You're the one whose shoulder she broke."

Seth's hand went to his shoulder, the left one, before he could stop it. It had healed months ago. It had healed in about a day and a half. "Bella," he said. "Yeah. That was—she didn't mean it. Well. She did. But she was right to. Jake called the baby *Nessie*."

"I heard."

"Like the Loch Ness Monster."

"I heard," the guy said again. Something happened at the corner of his mouth. "I'd have broken more than your shoulder."

Seth grinned. He couldn't help it.

"So," he said. "Harry."

The guy's eyes came to his face and stayed there.

"It's okay," Seth said. He said it fast, before he could think about it too much, because if he thought about it he'd stop. "The name, I mean. Leah's gonna be weird about it. She's weird about everything about Dad, she's been weird about it for two years. But Mom's okay. Mom's—she'll be okay. You don't have to go by Potter or whatever. Mom'll like it, I think. That somebody's saying it again." He stopped. "That probably sounds really messed up."

The guy didn't say anything for a while.

"No," he said finally. "It doesn't."

"Okay."

"Okay."

Seth shifted his feet in the slush. "Can you teach me the lightning?"

"No."

"The invisible thing?"

"No."

"The thing on the rocks? In the river?"

The guy considered that. "Maybe," he said. "If you stop asking."

"I'll stop asking," Seth said, and meant it, for at least another minute.

Behind them, the back door opened.

Seth turned round. The guy had heard it coming, Seth thought; he'd probably heard the whole thing, every word Leah had said in there and every word Mom had said back. He turned his head, slowly, and looked.

Mom was standing in the doorway.

She had her old gray cardigan on, the long one with the pockets, wrapped tight round her with her arms folded across it. Her hair was pulled back in a braid that had gone half undone, like she'd been running her hands through it all morning. She looked tired. She looked like she'd been up since four, which Seth was pretty sure she had. She looked at Seth first, quickly, all over, the way she always did when he came home from anything, checking him for blood; and then her eyes went past him, across the slushy yard, to the man standing at the edge of the trees.

She looked at him for a long moment.

Then she said, "You'd better come in. You'll catch your death out there."

---

He came across the yard without any hurry, and Sue watched him come.

She'd been bracing for it for ten minutes. Ever since the back door banged open and Leah came through it barefoot in her cut-offs, with slush on her ankles and her face set like poured concrete, and stood in the middle of the kitchen and said it all in one breath, without stopping, without looking at her. *Mom. He's outside. He's staying on the couch. Till Friday, maybe longer, I don't know. His name's Harry. Don't. Please don't say anything. Please.* And then up the stairs two at a time, and her bedroom door shutting hard enough to rattle the pictures in the hall.

Sue had stood in the kitchen with the dish towel in her hands for a full minute after that. She'd known most of it already. Billy had called at a quarter to eleven, his voice gone strange and high, to say it was over, the Italians were gone, all of them, some Englishman. And then he'd called back at half past, and been quiet on the line for so long she'd thought they'd been cut off, and then he'd said, *Sue. Sit down.*

She'd sat down on the kitchen floor, as it turned out. She hadn't made it to the chair.

She'd had an hour and a half to get used to the rest of it. She'd thought she was used to it. Then her daughter stood in the kitchen and said the name, and it turned out she wasn't.

The man stopped at the bottom of the back step. He was tall, taller than Harry had been, taller than Sam, with a big heavy frame to him under a beat-up leather jacket that had seen better years. Dark hair that needed cutting. A couple of days of stubble. Green eyes. He looked about twenty-five, maybe a bit older around the eyes. He didn't come up the step. He stood at the bottom of it in the slush, with his hands in his pockets, and she felt the heat come off him from where she stood in the doorway, the way it came off Seth when he'd been running, the way it came off both her children every single day now and had since the winter everything went wrong.

She'd never felt it off anyone who wasn't one of the boys. Or Leah.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said.

English. She'd known he would be. It was still strange to hear it in her own back yard.

"Sue," she said.

"Potter."

She looked at him. He looked back. There was nothing much in his face; it was a closed sort of face, she thought, the face of someone who'd learned a long time ago to keep his business behind it. But he'd said *Potter*, and stopped, and waited, and she understood exactly why.

"Leah told you," she said.

"She mentioned."

"What's your first name?"

He was quiet for a second.

"Harry," he said.

She'd been ready for it. She'd been ready for it for an hour and a half. It went into her anyway, right under the ribs, the way it did when she found one of Harry's old flannel shirts at the back of the closet, or a fishing lure in the junk drawer, or his handwriting on the calendar from two years ago that she still hadn't taken down. A small, sharp, familiar hurt. She let it go in. She let it sit there for a moment. Then she let it go out again, because she'd had two years of practice, and because there was a young man standing at the bottom of her back step in the slush waiting for her to decide whether he could come in.

"Come in, Harry," she said.

It came out fine. Mostly fine.

He came up the step. He wiped his boots on the mat, both of them, carefully, like somebody whose mother had once had words with him about it, and stepped past her into the kitchen, and Seth came in behind him barefoot and shirtless and dripping slush, grinning like a fool.

"Mom, he made us invisible," Seth said. "On the highway. There was a logging truck, and he just—"

"Shower," said Sue.

"But Mom—"

"Shower. And a shirt. And dry socks. You smell like a wet dog."

"I *am* a wet dog—"

"*Seth.*"

Seth went, still grinning, taking the stairs three at a time. A moment later she heard him stop outside his sister's door and say something through it, low and hopeful, and heard Leah say something back through the door that made Seth laugh and go on to the bathroom.

Sue shut the back door. She turned round.

He was standing in the middle of her kitchen with his hands in his pockets, looking round, slowly, the way you look round a room you're going to be sleeping in: at the old yellow linoleum and the table with the leaf that didn't sit flat and the four mismatched chairs, at the window over the sink that looked out at the woodshed and the trees, at the fridge with its layers of magnets and school photos and coupons and the tide table from the Forks Forum. At the coffee maker on the counter by the stove, an old white Mr. Coffee with a brown stain up the inside of the carafe that no amount of vinegar would ever shift.

His eyes stayed on the coffee maker a moment longer than on anything else.

"Coffee?" Sue said.

"God, yes."

She almost laughed. She got the can of grounds out of the freezer and a filter out of the drawer by the stove, and he watched her do it, and she found herself saying, without having planned to, "Coffee's in the freezer. Filters are in this drawer. If you're up before me, help yourself. I'm usually up by six." She measured out the grounds. She measured them the way she'd done it for twenty years, a little heaping, a little more than the can said. "Harry—my Harry—he always made it too strong. Like tar. I could never get him to stop." She tipped the grounds in. "I never did get used to it the other way."

"Strong's fine," he said.

"Good."

She filled the carafe at the sink and poured it in and switched it on, and it began to hiss and gurgle the way it always did, like an old man clearing his throat. She got two mugs down. She stood with her back to the counter while it brewed and looked at him across the kitchen.

He looked back.

"Sit down," Sue said. "Please. You're making my kitchen look small."

He sat down. He took the chair at the end of the table, the one nearest the back door, and she noticed that; she noticed that it was the chair that let him see both doors and the window at once, and that he'd chosen it without seeming to think. Harry had always sat there too, for no reason she'd ever been able to work out, except that he liked to see who was coming.

She poured the coffee before it had finished brewing, the way you weren't supposed to, and the hot plate hissed where it dripped. She put a mug in front of him. "Milk? Sugar?"

"Black's fine."

She sat down across from him with her own. Neither of them said anything for a bit. Upstairs the shower came on with a clank of old pipes. Above the kitchen, in the room over their heads, something creaked once, a mattress spring or a floorboard, and then was still.

He glanced up at the ceiling. Just for a second. Then he looked back down at his coffee.

"My kids were in that field," Sue said.

He looked at her.

"Both of them. Since before it was light. I put breakfast in front of them at four o'clock this morning and I watched them eat it, and I thought, that's the last meal I'm ever going to make for my children." She held her mug in both hands. "Billy called me at a quarter to eleven to tell me it was over. I didn't believe him. I made him say it three times."

"They'd have been all right," he said.

"You don't know that."

He thought about it. "No," he said. "I don't."

"Then let me say thank you."

He looked at her across the table for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and looked down into his coffee, and said, "All right."

That was all. He let her say it, and let it sit there. Sue had sat through enough council meetings to notice that, and she filed it away.

"Billy says you're here for the council," she said.

"Supposed to be. Somebody has to explain the Registry to you, and my boss reckons I'm the one who's going to be hanging about anyway." He drank some coffee. He held it in his mouth for a second, and something at the corner of his eyes eased. "That's good coffee."

"It's too strong."

"It's just right." He set the mug down. "The Registry's a list. That's all it is, mostly. Names. What you are. Where you live. How many of you. So the next time somebody like me gets sent to a field, the people sending him know who's who before anybody starts snapping their fingers." He turned the mug a quarter turn on the table. "Your lot have been on it since the thirties. Just on the wrong page. Somebody filed you under the wrong thing, and nobody ever came out to check."

"What wrong thing?"

He looked at her. Then he said, "Doesn't matter. It's wrong. That's what I'm here to fix."

"And if the council says no? To any of it?"

"Then the council says no. I write *declined* on the form, and that's that." He shrugged. "And the next bloke they send out here, in ten years or twenty, doesn't have a page to read before he comes, and has to make it up as he goes along. Like I did this morning."

Sue thought about that. She thought about what *making it up as he goes along* had meant this morning, in that field.

"I see," she said.

"Thought you might."

She drank her coffee. It was too strong. It was exactly right.

"And my daughter," she said.

He didn't pretend not to understand. "Yeah."

"Billy says she imprinted on you."

"That's the word everybody's using."

"What word would you use?"

He was quiet a long time. Long enough that the coffee maker finished, and gave its last gargling sigh, and clicked.

"Haven't got one," he said finally. "I read a book about it this morning. Well. Two books. The first one had five lines about your lot, and two of those said *insufficient data*. The second one had a page on what happens when it lands on somebody like—" He stopped. Started again. "On somebody it isn't supposed to land on. Half of that page was torn out. The other half says it goes both ways now."

"Both ways," Sue said.

"Both ways."

She looked at him. "You feel it too."

"Yeah."

"What does it feel like?"

He thought about it. "Like somebody tied a rope round my breastbone," he said, "and handed the other end to a wolf who can't stand me."

Sue laughed.

She didn't mean to. It came out of her short and surprised, and she put her hand over her mouth, and then took it away again, because she found she didn't want to take it back. He didn't smile. Something happened round his eyes, though.

"I can feel her now," he said. He tipped his head very slightly toward the ceiling. "She's sitting on the end of her bed, with her back against the wall and her knees up, and she's so angry she can't see straight. And she's listening to every word we're saying."

From the room over the kitchen, there was a short, sharp thump. As if somebody had kicked a wall.

Sue looked up at the ceiling. She looked back at him.

"She's been angry for two years," she said quietly.

"Saw some of it. In Bella's head."

"Then you know what it did to her. The imprinting. Sam."

"Some of it."

"They were together for years," Sue said. "Since high school. Everybody thought—*she* thought—they'd be married by now. She had it all planned. She'd show me pictures of dresses. And then Sam went away for two weeks, and came back a wolf, and the first time he looked at Emily it was over. Just like that. He couldn't help it, I know that now, I know he couldn't. But Leah—" She stopped. She turned her mug round on the table. "And then her father died. And then she found out what she was. All in the same few months. She was nineteen."

He didn't say anything. He sat and listened, with his hands round his mug.

"She thought it would never happen to her," Sue said. "That's the thing she held on to. I know she did. She never said, but I know my daughter. She thought she was safe from it, at least. That was the one thing it couldn't do to her."

"Yeah," he said. "I got that."

"And now it has."

"Now it has."

Sue looked at him across the table. She looked at him the way she'd looked at the doctor in Port Angeles who'd told her Harry was gone, and the way she'd looked at Billy Black across the council fire the first night he told her what her children had become. She looked for a long time.

"What are you going to do?" she said.

"Sleep on your couch till Friday," he said. "Meet your council at ten tomorrow. Get your kids moved onto the right page of a very big book. Try not to make anything worse than it already is."

"And after Friday?"

"Dunno."

She waited.

"Dunno," he said again. "That's honest. I've had about three hours to think about it, and I spent most of them killing people and eating pie. Ask me Friday."

Sue found that she believed him. She also found, a little to her own surprise, that it was the answer she'd have wanted.

"Why Friday?" she said.

He looked into his coffee.

"The book," he said. "The second one. It says we're not to be apart at the full moon. On no account. That's the bit before the page gets torn out." He turned the mug. "Full moon's Thursday night. So I'm staying close till it's done."

"What happens at the full moon?"

"Full moon," he said, and drank his coffee.

Sue looked at him. He didn't look back. He sat there at the end of her kitchen table, in the chair nearest the door, drinking coffee that was much too strong, and he didn't say anything else at all.

"My husband used to do that," she said.

He looked up.

"Say one thing," Sue said, "and then drink his coffee, so he wouldn't have to say the next thing." She set her own mug down. "Drove me crazy for twenty-two years."

He held her eyes for a moment. Then he looked down at his mug, and set it down on the table very carefully, and didn't pick it up again.

Something cracked in the middle of her kitchen.

Sue had been married to a fisherman for twenty-two years and had raised two children who turned into horse-sized wolves when they lost their tempers, and she did not consider herself an easily startled woman. She jumped so hard she knocked her mug over. Coffee went across the table in a long brown tide toward the edge.

It stopped before it got there. It just stopped, a quarter of an inch short of the edge, as if it had run into a wall, and sat there trembling. Then it ran backward across the table into the mug, and the mug stood itself up, and there was nothing on the table at all except a faint brown ring.

She looked at the man. He hadn't moved. His hands were still round his own mug.

Then she looked at the thing standing on her linoleum between the table and the stove.

It was small, smaller than her kitchen stool, and gray, and very old; that was the first thing she thought, how very old it looked, older than Old Quil, older than anything she'd ever seen alive, with skin hanging off its face in soft folds and great tufts of white hair growing out of a pair of enormous ears. It had a white tea towel knotted at one shoulder like a toga. It was holding a battered carpetbag in one hand and a pair of heavy black boots in the other, and it had a razor and a shaving brush tucked into the knot of its tea towel, and a bar of soap.

It looked at Sue with enormous, wet, pale eyes.

"Mom," said Seth's voice from the top of the stairs, delighted, "that's Kreacher."

Kreacher set the carpetbag down. He set the boots down beside it, neatly, side by side. And then he bowed to Sue, a long, low, creaking bow, with his nose almost touching the yellow linoleum.

"The lady's mother," he said.

"Kreacher," said the man, at the table.

"Kreacher is greeting the *lady's mother*, Master, as is proper and correct, and Master will not stop him."

The man opened his mouth. He closed it again. He looked at Sue, and there was something in his face that was very nearly an apology.

"House-elf," he said. "He's mine. Sort of. He brought my clothes."

"And the razor," said Kreacher, straightening, with a look at the man's jaw.

"And the razor."

Sue looked at the elf. The elf looked at her. Then his huge eyes left her face and went round her kitchen, slowly, the way the man's had: the linoleum, the table, the fridge with its magnets, the window over the sink, the stove. He looked at the stove for a long time. He looked at the pots hanging on the rack over it, and the old cast-iron skillet on the back burner, black and smooth as glass from thirty years of bacon fat, and something happened to his face that Sue, after a moment, recognized. It was the look her own mother used to get walking into somebody else's kitchen at a potlatch.

"Kreacher will make dinner," Kreacher announced.

"Kreacher," said the man.

"Kreacher will make the steak and kidney pudding, with the—"

"It's my kitchen," Sue said.

The elf turned to her. His ears went down a fraction.

"It is the lady's mother's kitchen," he agreed, with great reluctance. "Kreacher would never *presume*. Kreacher only wishes to observe that the lady is too thin, and the young master is too thin, and the lady's mother is too thin, and Master is—" he glanced at the man "—Master ate *pie*, but Master will eat again by five o'clock, and Kreacher does not see in this kitchen any *pudding*—"

"Salmon," Sue said.

Kreacher stopped.

"There's salmon in the chest freezer in the garage," Sue said. "Silvers, from October. And I was going to make fry bread." She folded her arms. "You can help, if you want. But it's my kitchen, and it's my fry bread, and you do it my way."

Kreacher looked at her for a long moment. His ears came slowly up off the sides of his head.

"Kreacher," he said, "does not know *fry bread*."

"Then I'll show you."

The elf considered this. He looked at the stove. He looked at Sue. Something went across his old, folded face that she'd have called, on anyone else, the beginning of respect.

"The lady's mother will show Kreacher," he said, and bowed again, not quite so low, and went to look at the skillet.

Sue looked at the man at the end of the table.

He was looking at the elf with an expression she couldn't read at all. Then he looked at her, and his eyebrows went up, very slightly.

"He likes you," he said.

"How can you tell?"

"He hasn't called you a blood traitor yet."

Sue decided not to ask.

---

She showed him the couch a little after two, when the coffee was gone and he'd brought his carpetbag in from the kitchen.

It was in the front room, under the window, facing the old television on its stand. It was brown corduroy gone shiny on the arms, and it had a stain on the middle cushion the size and color of a dinner plate of grape jelly, and a spring in the left-hand seat that came up through the stuffing if you sat on it wrong. Harry had bought it secondhand off a guy in Sappho the year Seth was born. She'd been going to replace it for fifteen years.

The man stood and looked at it.

"Leah wasn't exaggerating," Sue said.

"Leah doesn't strike me as the type."

"The spring's on the left. If you lie with your head on that end, it gets you in the—" She hesitated.

"Kidney," he said.

"Kidney."

"Noted." He put his bag down on the floor beside it. He looked round the room: the TV, the bookshelf full of Seth's old comics and Harry's tackle catalogs, the woodstove in the corner with its pipe going up through the ceiling, the window looking out at the slush and the road and, beyond the trees, a gray glimpse of the sea. He looked at the wall over the TV, where the photos were.

He looked at the photos for a while.

There were a lot of them. School pictures of both kids, every year, in a long staggered row, Leah's front teeth coming and going, Seth's ears getting bigger and then his head catching up. Their wedding picture, Sue in her mother's dress, Harry with a carnation in his lapel and his hair slicked down, looking like a kid at his first communion. And in the middle of them all, in a wooden frame Seth had made in shop class, Harry on the dock at the marina in his orange rain slicker, with a king salmon held up in both arms that was nearly as long as Seth, who was standing beside him, maybe eight years old, grinning so hard his eyes had disappeared. And Leah on the other side of him, twelve or thirteen, scowling at the camera with her arms crossed, and leaning, without seeming to know it, against her father's side.

"That's him," Sue said.

The man nodded. He kept looking at the picture.

"Seth's got his grin," he said.

"Seth's got everything of his." Sue looked at it too. She looked at it most days, and most days she could do it now. "Leah got his temper."

The man looked at the scowling girl in the picture, leaning into her father's side in the orange slicker.

"Yeah," he said. "I can see that."

---

Leah stayed in her room until five.

She told herself it was because she was tired. She was tired; she'd been up since four, and in the field since before light, and she'd run fifteen miles through snow with a rope tied through the middle of her chest and a stranger ten feet behind her brother's tail. She lay on her bed with her back against the wall and her knees up and her eyes shut, and she told herself she was going to sleep.

She didn't sleep. She listened.

She couldn't help that. Nobody in this house had ever been able to keep anything from her since she phased; the walls were thin and old and her ears were a wolf's ears, and she heard everything. She heard the shower shut off with its clank and Seth thump down the stairs in socks. She heard her mother's voice in the kitchen, low and steady, showing the elf how to work the dough. *No. Don't knead it. Just bring it together. Like that.* And the elf's voice under it, creaky and deeply suspicious: *The lady's mother does not knead?* *Not for fry bread.* *Kreacher's old Mistress would have had things to say about bread that is not kneaded.* *Your old mistress isn't here.* A long pause. *No,* said the elf, in an odd voice. *No, she is not.*

She heard the scratch of a pen at the kitchen table. She heard it for an hour and a half, on and off, with long stops in between, and once a muttered *bloody hell* and the sound of paper being crumpled and a fresh sheet torn off a pad. She heard Seth ask what he was writing and him say *my statement*, and Seth ask what a statement was and him say *a list of everybody I killed this morning and why*, and Seth go very quiet for almost a whole minute, which was some kind of record.

She heard the owl.

It came about four o'clock, a soft tapping at the kitchen window, and then the window going up and a rush of cold air and Seth yelling, *Oh my God, is that an OWL, Mom, Mom, there's an OWL—* and the man saying, *It's the Americans'. Don't touch it, it bites.* And the scratch of something on paper, and the window going down again, and Seth, still yelling: *Do you guys just HAVE owls? Like, everybody?* And the man: *Pretty much.* *That's so much better than email.* *It really isn't.*

She lay on her bed and stared at the ceiling and did not laugh.

And all the time, under the noise, there was the line.

It sat in the middle of her chest like a stone in a creek, and the whole afternoon ran past it, and it didn't move. She could feel him through it. She couldn't hear words or thoughts; it was nothing like the pack. She could feel *him*, the way you could feel somebody sitting on the other end of a couch, the weight of him, where he was and roughly what shape he was in. She could feel him sitting at the kitchen table, and she could feel him concentrating, a sort of steady, grinding, irritated focus like a man doing his taxes. She could feel when he got up to get more coffee. She could feel when he went out on the back porch for ten minutes, by himself, and stood there doing nothing, and something in him went slow and quiet and far away, and then came back.

She could feel that he knew she was feeling it.

That was the worst bit. He knew. Every so often, in the middle of the scratching, the line would go *tight* for a second, very slightly, like a fishing line when something bumps the hook, and she knew he'd just checked on her. Just reached up the line and touched it, the way you'd put a hand on the top of a kid's head walking past. And then gone back to his statement.

The first time he did it, she sat bolt upright on her bed with her heart going like a drum.

The third time, she threw a pillow at the wall.

*Stop it,* she thought at the ceiling. *Stop doing that.*

He didn't stop. But he didn't do it again for almost an hour, which she supposed was something.

Jacob came at five.

She heard him before anybody else in the house did, his bare feet on the slush in the road and then on the gravel of the drive, and she knew it was him from the walk. He didn't knock. He never knocked. He came round the side of the house to the back door and opened it and stuck his head in and said, "Sue. Hey. Esme sent some stuff," and her mother said, "Jacob, for heaven's sake, put a shirt on," and Jacob said, "I don't have a shirt, I came as the wolf," and came in anyway.

Leah got off the bed and went to the window. She told herself it was to see what Esme had sent.

From her window she could see down onto the back porch. Jacob was standing on it, in his cut-offs and nothing else, with a big bundle in his arms: blankets, a down comforter in a cream-colored cover, a couple of pillows in cases so white they looked fake. Esme. Of course Esme. Esme would send pillows to somebody who'd burned half her species to ash, if she thought he was going to be uncomfortable.

The man came out onto the porch. He took the bundle.

"Cheers," he said.

"Esme says the pillows are goose down," Jacob said. "She wasn't sure if you were allergic. She said to tell you she's sorry if you are."

"I'm not."

"Okay." Jacob shifted his weight. Leah watched him do it. She'd spent four months in his head; she knew exactly what it looked like when Jacob Black was working up to saying something he didn't want to say. His hands, now that they were empty, didn't know what to do with themselves. He put them on his hips. He took them off again.

"Bella says thanks," he said finally. "For Charlie."

"Tell her she's welcome."

"She also says she still hates you."

"Fair."

Jacob looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked out at the yard, at the slush and the woodshed and the trees.

"Thanks," he said. "For the field. For Nessie." He said it fast, to the woodshed. "I was gonna run with her. If it started. That was the plan. I was gonna run with her and leave all of them, Bella and Edward and everybody, and just keep running, and I didn't even know where to. Brazil, maybe." He swallowed. "So. Thanks."

The man didn't say anything for a moment. Leah watched the back of his head.

"Yeah," he said.

That was all. Jacob seemed to think it was enough. His shoulders came down an inch. Then he glanced up, straight up, at Leah's window, and Leah stepped back from it so fast she nearly fell over her own laundry basket.

"And Leah—" Jacob said.

"Don't," said the man.

"I was just gonna say she bites."

"I'd noticed."

Jacob laughed, short and surprised, and went down the porch step and off across the yard toward the trees, and Leah heard him phase at the edge of them, heard the pack-mind open somewhere off in the distance like a door at the end of a long hall, and close again before she could feel anything through it, because she wasn't in it. She was standing in her bedroom on two legs with her heart going and her back against the closet door.

Downstairs, the back door closed. She felt him go back into the kitchen. She felt him put the bundle of blankets down.

She felt the line go *tight*, just for a second, just a touch.

"*Stop it,*" Leah said out loud, to nobody, and slid down the closet door and sat on the floor.

---

Mom called her down at six.

She almost didn't go. She sat on the floor of her bedroom and smelled the fry bread, which she'd been smelling for an hour, and the salmon, which she'd been smelling for half an hour, and something else, something heavy and sweet and steamy that she didn't recognize, and her stomach made a noise like a garbage disposal, and she went.

He was at the table. Of course he was. He was in the chair at the end, the one nearest the back door, Dad's chair. He'd shaved. She noticed that from the bottom of the stairs; the stubble was gone, and his jaw was clean, and he'd changed his shirt for a dark blue one, and he looked about two years younger and no less annoying. Seth was across from him, in a T-shirt, practically bouncing in his seat. Mom was at the stove with the skillet. The elf was standing on a kitchen stool beside her with a spatula in his hand and an expression of fierce concentration, lifting a round of fry bread out of the oil and laying it on a paper towel with as much care as if it were a newborn.

"It is golden," the elf announced.

"It's golden," Mom agreed. "Good. That one's good."

"Kreacher has done the fry bread," said the elf, and Leah had never heard anything sound so much like a man reporting that he had taken a castle.

Leah sat down. She sat in her own chair, which was across the corner of the table from his, which meant she was closer to him than she wanted to be, and she couldn't move it because it was *her* chair and she'd sat in it her whole life. He kept his eyes on his plate when she sat down.

She felt the line settle. Like a dog lying down.

She hated it.

Dinner was fry bread and salmon, and it was good; it was Mom's, and it tasted like every dinner Leah had ever had in this kitchen, and she ate four pieces of fry bread and a whole side of salmon before she looked up. He ate more. She watched him do it from the corner of her eye, the way she'd watched him on the blanket: steady, quiet, not stopping, Mom putting more on his plate every time it emptied and him not saying no. He ate like the pack ate. Like somebody feeding a fire. She thought about that. She thought about the heat coming off him in the field, and the way he'd gone over that fallen hemlock, and across the stones in the river, and she put it away to think about later.

Seth asked him questions the whole way through.

Seth asked whether everybody in England could do magic, and he said *no*. Seth asked whether there were wizards in La Push, and he said *not that I know of*. Seth asked how old the elf was, and the elf said *one hundred and forty-one*, with great dignity, from the stool by the stove. Seth asked whether he'd ever fought a dragon, and the man put down his fork and looked at Seth for a long moment, and said, "Once. I was fourteen. It was a school thing," and picked his fork up again, and Seth's mouth fell open and stayed open.

"A *school* thing?"

"Long story."

"What kind of *school*—"

"Seth," said Mom. "Let him eat."

The salt was at Seth's end of the table. Leah saw the man glance at it. She saw the salt shaker lift itself up off the table, quite slowly, about an inch, and begin to drift toward him through the air.

"No magic at the table," Mom said, without looking round from the stove.

The salt shaker stopped dead in midair.

It hung there for a second. Then it set itself back down where it had been, very gently, without a sound. The man looked at it. Then he looked at Leah's mother's back.

"Sorry," he said.

"Ask your neighbor to pass it like a person."

He looked at Seth. "Pass the salt?"

Seth passed the salt. His face was doing something complicated. So, Leah realized, was hers. She bent her head over her plate before anybody could see it.

The elf brought out the sweet thing at the end. It was a long, fat, steaming roll of something pale and spongy, studded all through with dark spots, sitting in a pool of yellow custard.

"Spotted dick," the elf said, setting it down in the middle of the table with enormous pride.

There was a silence.

"Spotted *what*?" said Seth.

"Don't," said the man.

"Did he say—"

"It's a pudding," the man said. "It's a perfectly normal English pudding. It's got currants in."

"It's called *spotted*—"

"*Seth*," said Mom.

Seth put his hand over his mouth. His shoulders were shaking. Leah looked down at her plate very hard and bit the inside of her cheek, and felt the line go loose and easy in the middle of her chest, and knew without looking that the man at the end of the table had felt exactly what she was trying not to do, and was trying not to do it too.

She ate two helpings of the spotted dick. She didn't say a word the whole time. It was really good.

---

He went to the couch at ten.

She heard him go. She'd gone back upstairs at eight, right after dinner, before anybody could suggest anything like a family evening in front of the TV, and she'd lain on her bed again and listened. She'd heard Mom show him where the towels were and how to work the woodstove, and the man say *I've got it, thanks, Sue*, and Mom say *goodnight, Harry* in a perfectly ordinary voice, and then go into her room and close the door, and Leah had lain there and thought about her mother saying that name in a perfectly ordinary voice, and had to turn over and put her face in the pillow for a while.

She'd heard Seth go to bed, finally, about nine, still talking. She'd heard the TV go on for a bit, quietly, and then off.

She heard the man lie down on the couch.

She heard the springs. She heard every one of them. He was heavy, much heavier than he looked, and the old couch complained about it in a long, groaning chord, and then there was a shifting sound, and a pause, and then very clearly in the dark, in a low voice, in English:

"*Kidney.*"

Then more shifting. The other way round. A long sigh.

Then quiet.

She lay in the dark and listened to him breathe.

She'd never noticed his heart before; there'd been too much else going on. She noticed it now. It was slow, slower than anybody's she'd ever heard, slower than the pack's, slower than a human's. Forty-something a minute. Big and heavy and steady, like somebody walking a long way at a pace they could keep up all day. She lay there and counted it. She didn't mean to. She got to two hundred before she noticed she was doing it, and then she couldn't stop.

Downstairs in the dark, he was counting hers.

She knew he was. She could feel his attention, lying on the couch, resting on her through the line, the way you rest a hand on something on the seat beside you in a car, and leave it there.

She should have hated it. She tried to hate it. She lay there in the dark and tried as hard as she knew how, and found that she was too tired, and that the thing in the middle of her chest was very quiet, and very heavy, and very still. And that under her skin, down where the wolf lived, something had turned round twice and lain down.

She fell asleep sometime after one.

---

She dreamed she was running.

It was night in the dream, and there was no snow; it was summer, or late spring, the forest thick and dark and wet and green, the ground soft under her paws. She was the wolf. She was going flat out, the way she liked best, the way she only ever got to go when nobody else was in her head, belly low, ears back, the trees coming at her out of the dark and flicking past on both sides. She was fast. She'd always been the fastest. She could feel the ground pouring away under her and the air pouring past her and nothing, nothing at all, holding her back.

There was something running beside her.

She didn't see it at first. She felt it. A weight in the dark, on her left, matching her stride for stride, so close she could feel the heat of it against her flank. It made no sound at all. Its feet came down on the forest floor and didn't break a twig. It was big, much bigger than she was, bigger than Sam, bigger than anything; she could feel the size of it the way you feel a mountain in the dark, a sort of pressure on the air. And it was black. Black on black, so that she only saw it in pieces when they ran through a patch of moonlight: the long slope of a shoulder, the ripple of muscle under fur, a dark ear.

She wasn't afraid of it.

In the dream, that was the strangest thing. She wasn't afraid of it at all. She ran, and it ran beside her, and when she cut left round a big spruce it cut left with her, and when she jumped a creek it jumped with her, and they came down on the far bank together at exactly the same moment, and she felt something in her chest go *yes*.

She nipped at it.

It happened without her deciding to: she turned her head mid-stride and snapped at the black shoulder beside her, quick and playful, the way she used to snap at Seth when he was a puppy. Her teeth closed on fur and let go.

The black wolf turned its head and looked at her.

Its eyes were green.

She ran faster. She ran faster than she'd ever run in her life, laughing in the wolf's way, her tongue out, and it came after her, the whole huge black weight of it coming after her through the trees in total silence, gaining, and she wanted it to, she wanted it to catch her, and she threw her head back as she ran and opened her throat and—

She howled.

And somewhere very close, something howled back.

Leah woke up.

She woke all at once, the way she always did now, with no in-between: one second asleep and the next lying bolt upright in the dark with every nerve in her body singing. Her heart was hammering. Her skin was hot. She was half out of the covers with her fists knotted in the sheets. The clock on her nightstand said 3:12 in red numbers.

The house was dead silent.

She sat there in the dark, breathing hard, and listened. The furnace ticking in the basement. The wind in the trees. The sea, a long way off. Seth snoring through the wall. Mom, across the hall, breathing slow.

Downstairs, nothing.

She'd dreamed it. She'd dreamed the howl. She'd been dreaming, and she'd—

Downstairs, the couch springs gave a long, tortured groan.

It went on and on, much longer than a man rolling over, as if something enormous had shifted its whole weight on the cushions all at once, and then there was a crack, a short, sharp, splintering *crack* of old wood, and a heavy *thump* that she felt come up through the floor and into the frame of her bed. Something big had hit the floorboards. Something a lot bigger than a man.

Then a voice.

It was his voice. It was the same accent, the same flat, dry, unhurried way of talking. But it had gone down. Way down. It came up through the floor rough and deep with something running underneath it, a low rumble like a big engine idling, and the words came out of it perfectly clear.

"Oh, you are *joking*."

Silence.

Leah sat frozen in her bed with the sheets in her fists.

The line had changed. She could feel it. She'd been feeling it all day, sitting in the middle of her chest, heavy and quiet; and now it was *heavier*. Thicker. Like a rope that had been a rope a minute ago and was suddenly a cable. And the thing on the other end of it, downstairs, on the floor of the front room, felt *closer*: closer to the surface, as if something that had been lying a long way down at the bottom of something had come all the way up and was standing right there, just under the skin, looking up at her.

And it was pleased.

She could feel that, too. Through the line. A great, slow, deep, rumbling satisfaction, coming up through the floorboards and into her chest like heat off a stove.

Below that, much smaller and much closer to the top, she could feel the man. And the man was absolutely furious.

She heard him move. Something big getting up off the floor, very slowly and very carefully, the floorboards creaking under a weight they had no business carrying. Then a sound she couldn't place at all, a soft dry rasp, like a stiff brush on canvas. Then a long, slow breath, let out through what sounded like a much bigger chest than a man's.

Then nothing.

She waited. She waited for five minutes, ten, sitting bolt upright in the dark with her knees drawn up, her ears straining. Nothing. No footsteps. No more voice. Just that slow heavy heartbeat, downstairs, going on the way it always did, forty-something a minute, steady as a clock.

Slowly, the line settled. It stayed a cable, heavy as it had become, and it settled, the way it had settled in the field, the way it had settled at dinner. Lay down.

Leah told herself she'd dreamed it.

She told herself that very firmly, in so many words, lying back down with the covers pulled up to her chin. She'd had a weird dream, and woken up weird, and the couch was a hundred years old and had finally broken under him, and he'd fallen off it, and sworn. That was all. That was all it was. She'd go down in the morning and he'd be sitting at the kitchen table drinking that tar Mom called coffee, being annoying, and the couch would have a broken leg, and she'd never mention any of it.

She lay there in the dark and told herself that until she believed it.

And down at the bottom of her, under all the telling, her wolf turned round twice and lay down again with its chin on its paws, like a dog that had just gotten something it had wanted for a very long time.

---

The coffee woke her.

It was twenty to seven by the clock on her nightstand and still dark outside, the window a gray square going slowly paler, and the smell of it was coming up the stairs and under her door, thick and black and bitter. Too strong. Tar. The way Dad used to make it, the way he'd make it every morning at five-thirty before he went down to the marina, so that the whole house smelled of it by the time she and Seth got up for school.

She lay there with her eyes open and breathed it in, and for about two seconds she was twelve years old and it was a Saturday and he was downstairs.

Then she remembered.

She remembered all of it, all at once, in order: the field, the fire, the black star, the rope through her chest, the name, the couch. The dream. The howl. The crack of wood at 3:12 in the morning and the voice coming up through the floor, deeper than it should have been. *Oh, you are joking.*

She'd dreamed it. She'd decided that. She'd decided it at three in the morning and gone back to sleep on it.

The line sat in the middle of her chest, thick as a cable, heavy and settled and humming, and the thing at the other end of it was downstairs in the kitchen.

She got up.

She didn't bother with anything but what she'd slept in, her old basketball shorts and a gray T-shirt of Seth's that had shrunk in the wash. She went out into the hall in bare feet. Mom's door was still closed. Seth was still snoring. She went down the stairs the way she'd gone down them her whole life, skipping the fourth one from the bottom because it creaked, and stopped at the bottom.

The front room was dim. The couch was under the window where it had always been, except that it was sitting at a slant now, its left front corner sagging down almost to the floor. The leg on that side lay on the rug beside it, snapped clean through. The cream-colored comforter Esme had sent was thrown back off the cushions in a heap. The cushions themselves were crushed down deep in the middle, much deeper than a man would crush them, as if something very heavy had been lying there a long time.

There was hair on them. Long, coarse, black hair. A lot of it.

Leah looked at it.

She looked at the kitchen doorway. There was a light on in there; it came out in a long yellow wedge across the hall floor. She could hear the coffee maker making its last gargling sounds. She could hear something else, too: a slow, heavy, steady heartbeat, forty-something a minute, exactly the same as it had been all night.

She walked down the hall and stood in the kitchen doorway.

There was a wolf at the counter making coffee.

It was standing on two legs.

That was the first thing her brain caught on, and it held on to it, because it was the only part that made any kind of sense: it was standing up. It was standing at the counter by the stove with its back to her, on two legs, the way a man stands at a counter, and it was big. It was so big. The top of its head was up near the light fixture; its ears, tall and black and pointed, came within a few inches of the glass shade, and one of them was tipped slightly sideways as if it had already bumped it once and learned. Its shoulders were as wide as the window over the sink. It was covered in fur, thick and dark, charcoal-black on the outside and blacker underneath where it parted at the back of the neck, and the fur at its neck and shoulders was heavier, a deep ruff that disappeared into the collar of a T-shirt.

The T-shirt was orange. A faded, ugly, cheerful orange, with a pair of black letters and a cartoon cannonball on the back, and *CHUDLEY CANNONS* across the shoulders in cracked black print. It fit. It stretched tight across the back but it fit, as though somebody had made it in that size on purpose. So did the gray sweatpants. There was a neat gap at the back of them, low down, and out of the gap came a tail.

It was a wolf's tail. Long and thick and black, hanging straight down behind it toward the floor, perfectly still.

Below the hems of the sweatpants were its feet.

She looked at them because she couldn't look at anything else for a second. They were paws. Big black paws, broad as dinner plates, with thick black pads and claws that rested against the yellow linoleum; but it was standing on them the way a person stands. Flat. Heel down. Its whole weight on the length of the foot, like a man in his socks. It shifted its weight while she watched, from one footpaw to the other, and it went heel to toe, and the old floor under it didn't make a sound.

One of its arms came up. At the end of the arm was a paw, black-furred, and it was holding the glass carafe from Mom's coffee maker.

It held it the way a hand would. It had five digits, thick and black, each one ending in a curved black claw, and a broad padded palm, and the digits were wrapped round the handle of the carafe, and the claws were curled carefully back out of the way. It tipped the carafe and poured coffee into a mug on the counter, slowly, precisely, without spilling a drop, and set the carafe back on the hot plate. Then it reached out with one claw and tapped the side of the mug, once, *tink*, as if checking it was really there.

It smelled like him.

Earth. Pine. Wet stone. And under that, the other smell, the deep dark musk she'd caught in the field from the end of the line, only stronger now, much stronger, so strong it filled the kitchen. Her skin crawled up the back of her neck. Her hackles, which she didn't have, tried to stand.

Its ears turned.

They came round toward her first, both of them, swiveling back on its head before it moved anything else, the way a dog's ears do when it hears its name from the next room. They stayed there, pointed at her, for a long moment.

Then it turned round.

It had a wolf's head. A long black muzzle, black lips, a black nose, the fur shorter over the face and thicker at the jaw. And looking out of it, at her, from under a heavy black brow, were his eyes.

Green. Bright, clear green. Exactly the same.

"Morning," it said.

It was his voice. It was the voice she'd heard through the floor at three in the morning: his accent, his flat dry way of putting words down one after another, but deeper, a long way deeper, with a low growl running along underneath it the whole time like a big engine idling. The words came out perfectly clear. The black lips moved round them. She saw the teeth.

Leah didn't say anything. She couldn't. She stood in the kitchen doorway in her basketball shorts and her brother's shrunken T-shirt and looked at him.

He looked back. Then he turned, unhurried, and got a second mug down off the shelf above the stove. He had to duck his head slightly to see into the shelf. He poured coffee into it. He opened the fridge with one claw hooked through the handle, and took out the milk, and put milk in the mug, and put the milk back, and picked up the sugar bowl and put three spoons of sugar in it with Mom's little spoon, the spoon tiny in the black padded paw. He stirred it. He put the spoon in the sink. He held the mug out to her.

"Milk and three sugars," he said. "That's how Kreacher did it."

She didn't take it.

He stood there holding it out. His tail hadn't moved. His ears had gone halfway back, the way the pack's went when they were bracing for something.

"I hope you know," he said, "I'm blaming you for this."

Leah found her voice. It came out about an octave higher than usual. "*What?*"

"It's Wednesday." His left ear flicked sideways, sharp and irritable. "Not the full moon. Full moon's tomorrow night. I'm not supposed to look like this till tomorrow night. And I can't change back." He set the mug down on the table beside her, since she wasn't going to take it, and stood back. "Tried four times since three o'clock. Five, if you count the one in your downstairs loo, which I don't, because I hit my head on the cistern."

"You—" She stopped. She started again. "You *look* like this? On the full moon?"

"Every full moon, like clockwork, whether I want to or not." He said it flatly, like somebody reading out a bus timetable. "Any other day, only if I feel like it. Change, change back, whenever. No bother. Five *years*, it's been like that. Five years without one single slip. Not on one day that wasn't the moon. Not when I've been hexed, not when I've been shot at, not when I've been dropped off a building in Prague by a bloke who should've known better." His muzzle wrinkled. "And I spend one night on your mum's couch."

"*Why?*"

"Why what?"

"Why do you look like this on the full moon? What *are* you?"

"Next question."

"That's not—you can't just say *next question*—"

"Just did."

"You're—you're a—" She couldn't say it. She didn't know what to say. Every word she had was wrong. The pack were wolves, and wolves didn't stand on two legs in their kitchens in sweatpants. Men didn't have tails. She didn't know what he was. "You're standing in my mom's kitchen," she said finally, "making *coffee*."

"Well, I wasn't going to make tea in this house. Your mum hasn't got any. I looked." He folded his arms across the front of the Chudley Cannons T-shirt. It took him a second; the arms were longer than a man's, and the paws ended up tucked under the opposite elbows. "Only thing I can think of," he said, "is your wolf got mine a bit worked up in the night."

Leah went cold. Then hot. Then cold again.

"My *what*?"

"Your wolf. Somebody had a dream, didn't they." His eyes were very steady on her face. "Somebody went for a run. Somebody did a bit of howling, round about three. And mine heard it, and answered, and got up, and came up the line to have a look." His ear flicked again. "And Bob's your uncle."

"Who the hell is *Bob*?"

"It's an expression."

"It's a *stupid* expression."

"Means *and there you go*. *And that's that*. And I woke up on your mum's front room floor with the couch on top of me and my footpaws hanging out the end of it and a spring stuck in my kidney." He paused. "Well. Somewhere near my kidney. Bit hard to tell, now."

The dream came back to her. All of it, at once, in full color: the dark forest, the soft ground, the huge black shape running beside her in total silence, matching her stride for stride. The green eyes. And her own head turning, and her own teeth closing, quick and playful, on a mouthful of black fur.

Her face went so hot she thought it might actually catch fire.

"Were you—" she said. "Were you *in*—did you—"

"Dunno," he said. "I was asleep. I was dreaming too." He looked at her. "Somebody bit me."

Leah put both hands over her face.

She stood there in the kitchen doorway with her hands over her face and her face on fire and the smell of him filling the whole room, earth and pine and stone and that deep dark musk, and she felt the line in the middle of her chest, heavy as a cable, and at the other end of it she felt the thing that had come up from the bottom of him and was standing just under his skin, looking at her. And it was *pleased*. It was so pleased. It was huge and smug and absolutely delighted with itself, the way Seth's puppy-self used to be when he'd stolen a whole sandwich off the counter and got away with it.

And under her own skin, down at the bottom of her, she felt her wolf get up off the floor where it had been lying all night with its chin on its paws, and stretch, long and slow, and look up the line. And start, very slowly, to wag.

"*No,*" Leah said, through her fingers.

"Yeah," he said. "That's about where I'm at as well."

She took her hands away from her face. He was still standing there with his arms folded. He hadn't moved. His ears had come forward again, a little. His tail—

His tail had moved.

It was moving now. Slowly, side to side, about six inches each way, low down behind him, with the tip of it just brushing the linoleum on each pass. *Swish.* *Swish.*

He felt her look at it. She saw him feel it. His head turned, and he looked down over his own shoulder at his own tail, wagging slowly behind him in the gap in his sweatpants, and he looked at it for a long moment with an expression of profound and personal disgust.

The tail stopped.

It stayed stopped for about two seconds. Then, very slightly, it started again.

He closed his eyes.

Leah picked up the mug of coffee from the table. She did it mostly so she'd have something to do with her hands. It was hot, and it was too strong, and it had exactly the right amount of milk in it and three sugars, and it tasted like every morning of her life before she was nineteen, and she drank half of it in one go and burned her tongue and didn't care.

"The couch," she said, when she could talk.

"Leg went." He opened his eyes. "I'll fix it."

"Mom's going to—"

"Your mum," said a voice behind Leah, "is going to want a cup of that."

Leah turned round. Mom was standing at the bottom of the stairs in her old blue bathrobe, with her hair in its night braid and her feet in Dad's old sheepskin slippers, which she'd worn every morning since the funeral. She'd stopped on the bottom step. Her hand was on the banister. Leah couldn't tell how long she'd been standing there. Long enough, she thought. Mom had always been quiet on the stairs; she'd learned it from years of sneaking past sleeping kids.

Mom looked past Leah into the kitchen. She looked at him for a long, long moment: up from the black footpaws on her linoleum, up the gray sweatpants and the orange T-shirt, up the long black muzzle to the green eyes, and up past them to the tall black ears nearly touching her kitchen light.

Her hand tightened on the banister. Leah saw the knuckles go pale. Then they relaxed again, one at a time.

"Is that coffee?" Mom said.

"It's strong," he said.

"Pour me one."

He turned round and got down a third mug. He had to duck his head for the shelf again. He poured it black, the way she took it, and he came across the kitchen with it in two long quiet strides, heel to toe, and held it out to her across the bottom of the banister, the black padded paw curled carefully round the handle, the claws turned away.

Mom took it. Her fingers brushed the pads. Leah saw her feel the heat of them, and saw her not flinch.

"Harry," Mom said.

"Morning, Sue."

"Are you all right?"

He considered that. "Bit tall," he said. "Otherwise fine. Sorry about the couch."

"That couch," Mom said, "has been trying to die for fifteen years." She drank some of her coffee. She looked up at him over the rim of the mug. Her eyes were very steady. "Billy and Old Quil are coming at ten."

His ears went flat back against his skull.

"Course they are," he said.

From upstairs, a door banged open, and Seth's voice came yelling down the stairwell, thick with sleep: "Mom? Mom, did something break last night? I heard a big—" Feet on the stairs, thundering. "—I heard a big crash, like at three in the morning, and I thought it was a dream, but then—"

He came round the bottom of the stairs at a run, in his boxers and a pair of mismatched socks, and saw past his mother into the kitchen, and stopped so fast he skidded on the hall floor and sat down.

He sat there on the floor at the bottom of the stairs with his mouth open.

For a second nobody said anything.

"*Whoa,*" said Seth.

"Morning," said the wolf in the Chudley Cannons T-shirt.

"*WHOA.*" Seth scrambled up off the floor. He came round his mother and past Leah into the kitchen, and stopped about four feet away, and stared up. And up. "Is that—are you—Leah, is that—"

"Yes," said Leah.

"*DUDE.*"

"Don't," said the wolf.

"Dude, you're—you're *huge*, you're like *seven feet*, you're—are you standing *up*? How are you standing up? Your legs go the wrong way—no, they go the *right* way, they go like a person's, that's so weird, that's the weirdest—can you—is that—" His eyes went down. "Is that a *tail*?"

"Leave the tail alone."

"It's *wagging*."

"It is not."

It was. Leah could see it. It had been going the whole time Seth was talking, slow and low, *swish*, *swish*, a little faster now.

The wolf looked down over his shoulder at it again. He looked at it for a long moment.

"Traitor," he told it.

Seth made a noise like a kettle coming to the boil. He put both hands over his mouth. Behind Leah, very quietly, her mother laughed: a short, startled sound, quickly smothered in her coffee mug.

Something cracked in the middle of the kitchen, and Leah jumped a foot in the air.

The elf was standing on the linoleum by the stove. He had a stack of plates in his arms, very large plates, bigger than any plates Mom owned, and a frying pan balanced on top of the stack, and a string of sausages draped over one shoulder like a feather boa. He looked up at the wolf in the Chudley Cannons T-shirt. He looked mildly put out, and that was all.

"Master is early," Kreacher said.

"I'm aware, Kreacher."

"Master is a whole day early."

"I am *aware*, Kreacher."

The elf's eyes went past him to Leah, standing in the doorway with her coffee mug. They stayed on her for a moment. His ears came up, slowly, all the way.

"Ah," said Kreacher.

"Don't," said the wolf.

Kreacher didn't. He set the plates down on the counter with great care, and took the sausages off his shoulder, and turned to Leah's mother, who was still standing at the bottom of the stairs holding her coffee.

"The lady's mother will permit Kreacher the stove," he said. "Master will need the big breakfast. Master always needs the big breakfast, when he is—" He glanced up at the black ears near the ceiling light. "When he is *furry*."

"Kreacher," said the wolf.

"Kreacher has brought the big plates."

Leah's mother looked at the elf. She looked at the sausages. She looked at the plates, which were the size of hubcaps. Then she looked at the wolf in her kitchen, and Leah saw something happen in her face, some decision being made, quietly, all the way down, the way she made all her decisions.

"Seth," she said. "Go put some pants on. Leah, get the eggs out. All of them. Kreacher, you can have the stove, but I'm doing the eggs." She came the rest of the way down off the bottom step and into the kitchen with her mug, and stopped beside the wolf, who was a good two feet taller than she was, and looked up at him. "And you," she said, "sit down. You're making my kitchen look small."

He looked at her for a moment. Then, very carefully, he pulled out the chair at the end of the table, Dad's chair, and sat down in it.

It creaked. It held.

His tail hung down off the back of the seat behind him, through the gap between the slats. Leah watched it. She watched it hang there perfectly still, for about ten seconds, while Seth thundered off up the stairs and Mom went to the stove and the elf began laying sausages in the pan with a hiss.

Then she went to the fridge to get the eggs. To do it she had to walk right past the end of the table, right past him, close enough to feel the heat coming off him in a wall.

The tail started again.

At ten past eight, with three sausages, six eggs, half a loaf of toast and most of a pound of bacon inside him, Harry went out onto the back porch, shut the door behind him, and got out the mirror.

He had to sit down to do it. Standing up, his ears came within a hand's width of the porch light where it hung from the roof, and he'd already knocked it once coming out and set it swinging. He sat on the top step. It sagged under him with a long complaining groan, and he waited for it to go the way of the couch, and it didn't. He let his weight settle. His tail lay along the boards behind him. He told it to stay there.

It stayed there. For now.

The yard was gray and wet and empty. The slush had frozen overnight and was going soft again in the first of the daylight. Out past the woodshed and the trees he could hear the sea, a long slow breathing, and gulls, and a truck on the road a mile off, and in the house behind him, through the door and the wall, every single thing: Sue running water at the sink, Kreacher scraping the frying pan and muttering about the quality of American bacon, Seth upstairs on the phone to somebody called Brady, whispering so loudly he might as well have been shouting. *No, dude, I'm serious. Seven feet. In a Cannons shirt. I don't know what a Cannons is.*

And Leah. Upstairs in her room again, sitting on the end of her bed, with the door shut. Doing absolutely nothing at all except being furious, and listening, and feeling him through the line the way he could feel her.

He took the mirror out of the pocket of the sweatpants. It looked small in his paw. Everything did this morning. He flipped it open with the tip of one claw and looked at his own face in it, the long black muzzle and the green eyes, and his ears went back on their own, and he made them come forward again.

He tapped the glass with the claw. Twice.

"Sabine," he said.

The glass fogged. It cleared.

Geneva was nine hours ahead; it would be after five in the afternoon there, and Sabine was at her desk. He could see the edge of the window behind her, and the gray Swiss sky, and the corner of a filing cabinet with a dying spider plant on top of it, and her face, and a cup of coffee halfway to her mouth.

The cup stopped.

She looked at him out of the mirror for a long time. She didn't say anything. Her face didn't change. Then, very slowly, she put the cup down on the desk, out of sight.

"Potter," she said.

"Morning."

"It is Wednesday."

"I know what day it is."

"You said *Thursday is Thursday*."

"Yeah, well," Harry said. "Turns out Wednesday's Thursday now."

Sabine closed her eyes. She kept them closed for a slow count of three.

"I said," she said, opening them, "that if it itched, I wanted an owl."

"It did a bit more than itch."

"I can see that."

"And this is quicker than an owl. And the Americans have got the only owl round here, and it bites."

"What happened?"

He thought about how to put it.

He thought about the couch, and the spring in his side, and lying there in the dark at ten o'clock last night listening to her heart going upstairs through the ceiling, quick and angry and then slower, and slower, and then the long slow pull of sleep coming over her at about one, and how the line had gone quiet. How he'd gone down after it without meaning to. He thought about the dream. The forest, summer-dark and wet, and running, the way he hadn't let himself run in months, and something small and gray and fast beside him in the dark, going flat out, and how he'd matched her without thinking about it, stride for stride. How she'd turned her head and nipped him on the shoulder. How he'd looked at her.

How she'd thrown her head back and howled, and something at the bottom of him had stood up and howled back.

He'd felt it go. He'd been asleep, and he'd still felt it: the wolf getting up off the stone floor at the bottom of the dark, unhurried, enormous, and walking up the line toward her, and coming up through him on its way, through the stone and the water and the walls, through every wall he'd ever built, the way it never did except one night a month when the moon pulled it up whether he liked it or not. He'd felt it reach the top. He'd woken up on the floor of Sue Clearwater's front room with the couch tipped half over on him and its leg snapped clean off and his footpaws hanging out past the end of it, and he'd lain there in the dark and looked at his own black paws and said *oh, you are joking* out loud.

Then he'd tried to change back.

It was like putting his shoulder to a door with something the weight of a car lying across the other side of it. Something that had lain down there, comfortable, with no plans to move. He'd tried four times. Five, with the loo. The wolf hadn't so much as twitched. It had lain across the door with its chin on its paws and looked at him, smug and patient, and at the other end of the line, upstairs, he'd felt her wolf lying down too.

"Had a dream," Harry said. "She had a dream. Somebody howled. Here I am."

Sabine looked at him for a long moment.

"Can you change back?"

"No."

"You have tried?"

"Five times."

"Is anyone hurt?"

"Couch."

"The *couch*?"

"Lost a leg. I've fixed it. *Reparo*. Good as new. Well. Good as it was."

"And the girl?"

"Furious."

Sabine let out a breath he hadn't realized she'd been holding.

"Good," she said. "Furious is good. Furious is normal." She picked her coffee back up and drank some, and set it down again. She looked at him over the top of the mirror's frame, and something at the corner of her mouth moved. "I looked it up."

"I told you not to."

"I looked it up," said Sabine, "on the computer of my nephew, who is fifteen. I have now seen things, Potter. I have seen things that cannot be unseen. I have used the Scouring Charm on my own eyes. Twice."

"Did it help?"

"No."

Harry's tail thumped once against the porch boards behind him. He didn't look round at it.

"You are meeting the council like this," Sabine said.

"In two hours."

"Be nice."

"I'm always nice."

"Put on a better shirt."

He looked down at the Chudley Cannons T-shirt. "This is my good shirt."

"It is *orange*, Potter."

"Ron gave it me."

"Ron Weasley," Sabine said, "has no taste, and I have said so to his face, in a meeting, in front of the Bulgarian delegation." Then she stopped. Her face changed. It went quieter. She leaned in toward the mirror on her side, so that her face filled it, and he could see the lines at the corners of her eyes and the shadows under them from a day that had started for her at four o'clock yesterday morning and didn't seem to have finished.

"Harry," she said.

She'd never called him that. Not once, in three years. He looked at her.

"If Wednesday is like this," Sabine said, "then Thursday—"

"Yeah."

"You do not know what Thursday will be."

"No."

"Five years," she said. "Five years, and never once."

"I know."

She looked at him for a long moment through the glass. Then she sat back.

"Call me tomorrow night," she said. "When it starts. Whatever happens. I will be at my desk."

"It'll be four in the morning there."

"Then I will be at my desk at four in the morning." She picked up her coffee. "And on Friday I am sending Pellow."

"Don't send Pellow."

"I am sending Pellow, with his tape measure, and you will let him measure whatever he likes." She looked at him once more, over the rim of the cup. "Be nice to the council, Potter."

The glass fogged, and cleared, and showed him his own muzzle.

He shut it. He put it back in the pocket of the sweatpants. He sat on the top step of Sue Clearwater's back porch in the gray January morning with his ears flat and his tail lying along the boards behind him and the line sitting heavy in the middle of his chest, and he looked out at the trees.

Down at the bottom of the dark, the wolf lay across the door with its chin on its paws, and looked up at him, and didn't move.

*Thursday,* Harry told it. *I said Thursday.*

The wolf thumped its tail on the stone. Behind him, on the porch boards, so did his.

At five to ten, he heard the truck.

Billy Black had seen a great many things in his fifty-odd years, and in the last few of them he'd seen more than most men see in ten lifetimes. He'd sat at his kitchen table and watched his only son come through the door with a bare chest and eyes like somebody who'd been to war. He'd watched Sam Uley turn into a horse-sized black wolf in his back yard. He'd shaken hands with a Cullen, which his own father would have taken a shotgun to him for. He'd buried Harry Clearwater. He considered himself, by now, a man who was not easily surprised.

He sat in the passenger seat of Sam's truck in Sue Clearwater's gravel drive and looked out of the windshield, and found that he had been wrong about that.

"Well," Billy said.

There was a wolf on Sue's front porch.

It was standing in the open front door, or rather in front of it, because it had had to duck to get through, and it was now standing on the porch boards on its two hind legs, which went down to the boards the way a man's legs did, heel and all. It was black. It was so big that the porch roof, which Billy had stood under a thousand times and which was a good eight feet off the boards, looked like it had been built for children. It was wearing a pair of gray sweatpants and a bright orange T-shirt with something written across the front, and it was holding a coffee mug in one of its front paws, and it was looking at the truck.

Beside Billy, behind the wheel, Sam had gone very still.

Billy glanced at him. Sam was looking at the porch. His big hands were on the steering wheel at ten and two, and his knuckles had gone pale, and as Billy watched, Sam's eyes dropped. They went down to the dashboard, all on their own, the way a dog's eyes go down when the big dog walks into the yard; and stayed there a second; and then came back up again, slowly, and Sam's jaw set hard, and Billy could see what it had cost him.

In the back seat, Old Quil leaned forward between the two front seats and looked out at the porch for a long, long time. He was ninety-something; nobody was quite sure how much something, least of all Old Quil. His face was as brown and creased as an old boot. His eyes were black and bright and missed nothing.

He said something, very softly, in the old language.

Billy heard it. He turned his head.

"Taha Aki," said Old Quil, in English, to nobody in particular.

"Don't start, Quil," Billy said.

"I'm not starting anything." Old Quil sat back. "I'm an old man. I'm allowed to say a name."

Jacob came round the side of the house at a jog in his cut-offs, barefoot, steaming a little in the cold; he'd run over as the wolf and phased in the trees. He saw the porch, and stopped at the bottom of the steps, and looked up.

Billy watched his son's eyes go down too. Just for a second. Down to the porch boards, and then back up again.

Jacob swallowed. "Morning," he said.

"Morning," said the wolf on the porch.

"Seth called. He said you were—" Jacob looked at him. All the way up. "He said seven feet."

"Six foot seven," said the wolf. "Seth exaggerates. Ears don't count." Its eyes went past Jacob to the truck. To Billy in the passenger seat. They were green, Billy saw. Very green. "Mr. Black."

"Billy," said Billy, and opened his door.

Getting him out of the truck and into the chair was a two-man job on a good day, and Sue's porch had three steps and no ramp, which had never bothered anyone until Billy's legs went and since then had been a running joke between him and Harry Clearwater for years before Harry died. Usually Jake took one side and Sam took the other and they just lifted the whole chair. Billy had made his peace with it.

Sam got the chair out of the truck bed and unfolded it. Jacob helped Billy down into it. They wheeled him across the gravel to the bottom of the porch steps and stopped there, and Sam and Jacob went to take a side each.

"Want a hand?" said the wolf.

"They usually just—" Billy began.

The chair rose.

It went up off the gravel, smoothly, steadily, with Billy in it, about two feet into the air, and drifted forward over the three steps like a leaf on a pond, and came down again on the porch boards with no more than a faint bump, right in front of the wolf. The whole thing took maybe three seconds. Billy's stomach had stayed somewhere back at the bottom of the steps and took a second longer to catch up.

He looked up. A long way up. The wolf looked down at him.

"Warn a man," Billy said.

"Sorry." The ears went sideways, briefly. "Should've asked properly."

"You did ask. I just didn't answer fast enough." Billy took a breath. His heart was going. He made it slow down. He'd been on the council a long time; he'd learned to make his heart do what it was told. "Jake says you saved my son's life yesterday."

"Jake was going to run with the kid. He'd have been fine."

"Jake says you'd say that." Billy looked at him. "Jake also says you set thirty-two vampires on fire with your fingers and called down lightning on a Romanian. Seth says you can make people invisible and walk across rivers. Sue says you drink coffee like my old friend did, and eat like my son, and that your elf can make fry bread now." He paused. "Quil says you're Taha Aki."

"I didn't say that," said Old Quil, from behind him, climbing the porch steps on his own one at a time with his hand on the rail. "I said a *name*."

"What I want to know," Billy said, "is what I'm supposed to call you."

The wolf looked down at him for a moment.

"Harry's fine," he said.

Billy heard Sue, in the hall behind him, go very quiet.

"Harry it is," Billy said. "Come on, then, Harry. You can push."

They did the council in the front room, because the kitchen was too small for him.

Sue had pulled the kitchen chairs in and set them in a rough half-circle facing the couch, and the couch was standing level again on four legs; Billy looked at the front left one and couldn't see a mark on it. Billy had his chair at one end of the half-circle and Old Quil had the good armchair at the other, and Sue sat between them, and Sam and Jacob stood against the wall behind, the way Alphas stood, though Billy noticed neither of them leaned on it. Seth sat on the stairs. Leah stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded.

The wolf sat on the floor.

He did it without being asked. He came in after them, ducking under the doorframe, and looked at the couch and at the chairs, and then simply folded himself down onto the rug in front of the woodstove with his back against the wall and his long legs crossed in front of him, and his tail curled round beside him on the rug, and the coffee mug in his paw. Sitting like that, his head was level with Billy's.

Billy noticed that. He suspected he was meant to.

"So," Billy said. "This Registry."

Harry told them.

He told them the same way Sue said he'd told her, plainly, in a few words, without dressing it up. A list. Names, what you are, where you live, how many. So the next time somebody gets sent out to a field, they know who's who before anybody starts. They'd been on it since the nineteen-thirties, on the wrong page, under the wrong heading, because somebody in an office in Washington, D.C., ninety years ago had read a report about a pack of big wolves on the Olympic Peninsula and drawn the wrong conclusion and nobody had ever come out to check.

"What conclusion?" said Old Quil.

"Wrong one," said Harry.

Old Quil looked at him with his bright black eyes, and Billy saw him decide not to push it, and saw him file it away.

"And if we say no?" said Sam.

"Then you say no," said Harry. "And I write *declined*, and go home Friday, and you're still on the wrong page."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It's what happens." The ears flicked sideways. "I'd rather you were on the right page. Your call, though."

Billy put up his hand before Sam could say anything else.

"Conditions," Billy said.

"Go on."

"Nobody from your Registry sets foot on this side of the treaty line without the council says so. Not to measure anybody, not to take anybody's blood, not to look. They want to talk to our kids, they ask us first, and if we say no, it's no."

"Fine."

"Our kids' names stay with the council."

Harry thought about that. "Names have to go in," he said. "That's the whole point of it. But the page can be sealed. Council holds the seal. Nobody opens it without one of you three standing there. I can do that. I'll do it myself."

Billy looked at Sue. Sue looked at Old Quil. Old Quil nodded, very slightly.

"Fine," said Billy. "And one more." He leaned forward in his chair. "Charlie Swan."

The wolf's ears came forward.

"Charlie's my best friend," Billy said. "Has been for thirty years. He knows enough to get himself in trouble with your people and not enough to do him any good. Jake turned into a wolf in his back yard. He's seen his granddaughter." He held the green eyes. "Nobody touches what's in Charlie's head. Not your Registry. Not those Americans with the hats. Nobody."

"Already done," said Harry.

"Done?"

"Page four," said Harry. "Investigator Calloway wrote *see Potter*. I'm Potter. I've seen."

Billy opened his mouth.

"Don't ask," said Sue, beside him. "I asked. Just take it."

Billy shut his mouth again. He looked at the wolf on the rug for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and sat back.

"Then we've got a deal," he said. "Quil?"

"Mm," said Old Quil. He hadn't taken his eyes off the wolf the whole time. He was leaning forward in the armchair with his gnarled hands folded on the head of his stick. "One question."

"Go on," said Harry.

"What are you?"

The room went very quiet. Seth, on the stairs, stopped breathing. Billy felt Sam and Jacob go still against the wall behind him.

The wolf looked at Old Quil. Old Quil looked back.

"Next question," said Harry.

Old Quil laughed.

It came out of him in a high dry cackle, like a branch cracking, and went on longer than anyone expected, until he had to wipe his eyes with the back of his hand. Seth, on the stairs, stared at him. So did Jacob.

"Good," Old Quil said, when he could. "Good. A man who tells you what he is, first time you ask, is lying, or he doesn't know." He sat back in the armchair, still chuckling. "That's my question. I'm done."

"And Leah," said Sam.

He said it from the wall behind Billy, low and level, and the room went quiet in a different way.

Billy didn't turn round. He didn't need to. He watched the wolf on the rug instead, and saw his ears go back a little, and his eyes go past Billy's shoulder to Sam and stay there.

"She's in Jacob's pack," Sam said. "She answers to Jacob. Not to you. Whatever this is." He paused. "I want that said. Here. In front of the council."

"Never asked her to answer to me," said Harry.

"I want it said."

"I can speak for myself," said Leah, from the kitchen doorway.

Everybody turned and looked at her.

She was standing exactly where she'd been, with her arms folded and her shoulder against the doorframe. Her eyes were on Sam. And Billy, who had known Leah Clearwater since the day she was born, who had watched her grow up and fall in love with Sam Uley and get her heart torn out of her chest by him in front of the whole rez and spend two years walking around with the hole where it had been, saw her look at Sam now, steadily, across her mother's front room. And saw that there was nothing in it at all.

She looked at Sam the way she'd look at a man she'd gone to school with once, a long time ago, and hadn't thought about since.

"I know you can," Sam said, after a moment.

He said it quietly. Billy heard something go out of his voice when he said it.

Sam looked away first.

Billy turned back to the wolf on the rug. The wolf was looking at Leah. He'd been looking at her the whole time, Billy thought. Since she spoke. And behind him on the rug, very slowly, the end of his tail had begun to move.

The wolf felt Billy look at it. His head turned. He looked down at his own tail, and the tail stopped.

Billy decided he was going to like him.

"One more thing," Billy said. "Then you can all go back to whatever you were doing. Tomorrow night."

"Full moon," said Harry.

"Jake says that's what you say to everybody."

"It's true every time."

"I'm not asking what it is." Billy leaned forward again. "I'm the council. I'm asking about my people. Tomorrow night, whatever you are, whatever *this* is—" he lifted a hand at the black fur, the ears, the tail, the whole of it "—is anybody on this side of the line going to get hurt?"

"No," said Harry.

"How do you know?"

The wolf was quiet for a moment. He turned the coffee mug round in his paw. Then he looked up at Billy, level, his head at the same height as Billy's, his green eyes very steady.

"Because whatever I am tomorrow night," he said, "I'll be here. In this house. And she'll be here. And anything that wants to get at anybody on this side of your treaty line goes through me first." His ears came forward. "That's it. That's all I've got. It's what I'll do."

Billy looked at him for a long time.

Then he looked at Old Quil. Old Quil, in the armchair, with his hands on his stick, nodded once.

"All right," Billy said. "Then that's the council."

---

They went at eleven. Sam drove. Old Quil sat in the back with his stick between his knees and looked out of the rear window at the house the whole way down the drive. Jacob stayed on the porch long enough to say something to the wolf that Leah didn't catch, low and short, and to get something short back; then he went off across the yard and into the trees, and she heard him phase, and the pack-mind she wasn't in opened and closed somewhere a long way off.

Mom went into the kitchen with the elf to start lunch. Seth went with them, still talking.

Leah went out onto the back porch.

She told herself it was for the air.

He was sitting on the top step, where she'd known he'd be. She could have found him with her eyes shut. He was sitting with his elbows on his knees and the coffee mug, empty, hanging from one claw by its handle, looking out at the trees, and the step was sagging under him and holding. His tail lay along the boards behind him.

She sat down on the step.

At the far end of it. As far from him as the step would let her, which wasn't far; it was an old porch, and narrow, and he took up most of it. She sat with her knees up and her arms round them, the way she'd sat on the blanket in the field, and looked out at the trees too.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

The heat came off him in a wall. She could feel it down her whole left side. The smell of him, pine and earth and stone and that dark deep musk underneath, was so strong out here she could taste it. Her skin wanted to crawl. It didn't. It lay down. Under it, at the bottom of her, she could feel her wolf lying down too, with its chin on its paws, looking up the line at him; and at the other end of the line she could feel the thing at the bottom of him, lying across whatever door it had lain across at three o'clock this morning, looking back.

Behind them, on the porch boards, his tail thumped once.

He looked at it.

She looked at it.

"Is it always like that?" Leah said.

"Only lately," he said.

She laughed.

She didn't mean to. It came out of her short and sharp and surprised, the first real one in two days, maybe longer, and she put her hand over her mouth, too late. He didn't look round. He kept looking at the trees. But his ears had come forward, both of them, and the line in the middle of her chest had gone loose and easy, and she knew he'd felt it, all of it, the whole laugh, the way she'd felt the tail.

She took her hand away from her mouth.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow," he agreed.

Out past the woodshed and the trees, the sea went on breathing. The sky over it was white and low and flat, and somewhere up behind it, out of sight, the moon was climbing, one night short of full.

Leah sat on the step and didn't move, and neither did he.
