# Chapter One: Jurisdiction

*Forks, Washington — Friday, October 6, 2006*

---

## I. Leah

The Cullen house smelled like somebody had bleached a funeral parlor and then knocked a bottle of grape syrup over on the floor.

Leah Clearwater had been breathing it for forty-one minutes. She knew because the clock on the stove said 6:29 when Esme waved them in out of the rain, and the clock on the cable box under the giant television now said 7:10, and Leah had checked both more times than any sane person would admit to.

She sat on the floor with her back against the glass wall. The glass made up most of the south side of the house, floor to ceiling, and it was cold through her tank top in a way that felt good against skin that ran at a hundred and eight degrees. More importantly, it was the farthest point in the room from anyone without a heartbeat. Beyond the glass the rain came down in long gray ropes. The Calawah ran black and fast at the bottom of the slope, swollen with a week of storms, loud enough that she could pick its noise out from the ticking of the rain and the soft clack of Emmett Cullen losing at chess.

He was losing to Jasper. He'd been losing to Jasper for forty-one minutes. Every time he lost a piece he said "Okay, okay," like he was the one deciding things.

"Pack meeting," Jacob had said on the phone that afternoon. "My place."

"Your place is a dump on the rez with a broken screen door," Leah had said.

"My other place."

Which meant here. Which meant this. Jacob Black, rightful Alpha, great-grandson of Ephraim Black, did not leave this house anymore unless the house was on fire, and Leah was pretty sure if the house caught fire he'd just carry the kid out and then come back in to sit with the ashes.

He was on the big white couch now, bare feet planted on Esme's rug, elbows on his knees, cutoffs and nothing else, staring across the room at the Blonde's lap the way other guys stared at a TV during the fourth quarter.

The Blonde's lap had the kid in it.

Renesmee Carlie Cullen was twenty-five days old. Leah knew that number without wanting to because Seth said it every morning like a weather report. *Twenty-five days today. Can you believe it.* She looked a year old, maybe more. She'd walked last week. She'd said her first full sentence before most babies figured out their own feet. She had a head of bronze curls, a little dress the color of cream that Alice had almost certainly bought in Seattle for some insane amount of money, and her mother's eyes. Bella's old eyes. Brown and human and wide open, set in a face that belonged to nobody human Leah had ever met.

Leah did not look at the kid for long. She'd learned that the first week. If she looked too long, the kid looked back.

"Okay," Emmett said. "Okay."

"Check," said Jasper.

"Yeah, I see it."

Seth sat cross-legged on the floor by the kitchen doorway with his third plate of Esme's lasagna balanced on his knee. Esme fed them when they came over. Esme fed them a lot. Leah had stopped arguing about it after the first week because it was easier to eat than to explain to a two-hundred-year-old housewife—or however old Esme was—why accepting food from vampires made her skin crawl. The lasagna was good. That made it worse.

Bella sat on the piano bench with her back to the keys and Edward beside her. Neither of them moved. That was the thing about newborns that still got under Leah's skin a month into watching Bella be one: the stillness. Human Bella had fidgeted. Human Bella had tripped over flat floors and chewed her lip and fiddled with her sleeves. This Bella sat like a statue somebody had put in a nice sweater, red eyes fixed on her daughter across the room, and if you didn't know she was there you'd walk right past her.

Carlisle was in the armchair by the fireplace in shirtsleeves, a medical journal open on his knee. He'd come home from the hospital an hour ago smelling like antiseptic and other people's blood, and Leah had watched Bella's head turn toward him and then turn very carefully away.

Alice was curled in the other armchair with a sketchbook. She'd been drawing dresses for half an hour, little pencil scratches, a sleeve, a hem.

"The north line's thin between three and five," Jacob said, not taking his eyes off the kid. "Seth's been pulling doubles."

"I'm fine," Seth said, mouth full.

"You fell asleep on the ridge Tuesday," Leah said.

"For like a minute."

"You were snoring in my head, Seth. I heard you snoring in my *head*."

"So we split it," Jacob said. "Leah takes two to four, Seth takes four to six, I cover—"

Alice's pencil stopped.

It was a small thing. Leah only noticed because the scratching stopped and the silence it left was a different shape. Then Edward's head turned. Then Jasper's hand paused above the board with Emmett's black bishop between two fingers.

"Huh," Alice said.

"What," said Rosalie.

Alice stared at the page without seeing it. Her face did the thing it did when she was looking somewhere else—when she was looking *forward*—and then it did something Leah had never seen it do. It went blank. Annoyed, and then blank.

"The next hour just went," Alice said.

"Went where?" Jasper set the bishop down.

"Gone. It's—" She waved the pencil at nothing. "You know when the dogs are around and everything goes fuzzy at the edges? Like that. Except the dogs are already here and it was fine a second ago. Now the whole hour's just fog. Everyone. All of us." She looked at Edward. "I can't see *any* of us."

"Wonderful," Rosalie said.

Leah heard it then. A long way off, down through the trees, carried on the wet air: an engine. Big. A V8 with a rough idle, old, loud in a way new cars weren't allowed to be anymore. It was on the Cullen road. Nobody used the Cullen road who didn't live at the end of it. There wasn't even a sign at the turnoff—just a gap in the ferns off the highway past the Calawah bridge, a dirt track that wound north through miles of forest.

"Somebody's coming up the drive," Seth said, and put his plate down.

Jacob was already on his feet. Leah watched the line of his shoulders go tight and his eyes flick to the kid and back to the door, and she felt it start in her own spine too, the prickle, the heat building under her skin like somebody had turned on a burner. She breathed through it. Phasing in the middle of Esme's living room would shred her clothes and possibly the rug, and she only had the one pair of cutoffs that fit.

"Edward?" Carlisle said, closing the journal.

Edward had gone very still, head tilted, eyes on nothing.

"I can hear the engine," he said.

The engine got louder. Gravel crunched under heavy tires. Headlights flickered through the trunks of the cedars, broke into pieces against the rain, came on again.

"I can't hear the driver," Edward said.

The room changed. Leah had been around these people long enough now to know the different kinds of stillness they had. There was bored-still and pretending-to-be-human-still and Bella's newborn statue-still. This was the other kind. The kind that came right before something died. Rosalie stood up in one motion with the kid against her shoulder. Bella was across the room and taking her before Rosalie had finished standing. Emmett's chair was empty. Jasper had moved without moving—one second at the chessboard, the next a step closer to Alice, his weight shifted onto the balls of his feet. Esme appeared in the kitchen doorway with a dish towel.

"Nothing?" Carlisle said.

"There's someone in that car," Edward said quietly, "and there's nothing. No thoughts at all. Like Bella. Except Bella's silence is soft, and this—" He stopped. "This is a wall."

Leah filed that away: *Edward's scared*. She'd never once seen Edward Cullen scared of anything that didn't involve Bella dying, and she'd had a front-row seat to that for a month.

The headlights swung across the front windows and cut out. The engine died with a cough.

A car door opened. Leah heard the hinge—a long, rusty, groaning complaint, the sound of a door that hadn't been oiled since Nixon—and then it slammed, hard enough to rattle something loose inside it.

Then nothing.

She waited for the footsteps. Gravel. Then the porch steps, four of them, wood. Then the boards of the wraparound porch. Every single person in this house would hear every one of those sounds. A mouse couldn't cross that porch without eight vampires and three wolves counting its toes.

Nothing.

"Is he floating?" Emmett said, from somewhere near the front hall.

"Emmett," said Esme.

"I'm asking."

Somebody knocked on the door.

Three knocks. Unhurried. The kind of knock that knew the door would get opened.

And then a voice through the wood, low, a little rough, with an accent Leah couldn't place—British? Australian? Something with flatter vowels, the corners of the words clipped off. "I can hear you lot breathing for show in there. Doc, open up, would you? It's pissing down."

Nobody moved.

Carlisle looked at Edward. Edward shook his head once: *nothing*. Carlisle looked at Jasper. Jasper's mouth went thin.

"He's calm," Jasper said, low. "Very calm. Little irritated. A bit tired." A pause. "Hungry."

"Hungry how," Rosalie said.

"Not like that." Jasper frowned. "I think he wants a sandwich."

Carlisle crossed the room. He did it at a human pace, which Leah knew was on purpose, and he opened the front door.

The wind came in first. Cold, wet October air rushing over the threshold, carrying rain and the bruised smell of the cedars and the river. And under all of that, riding in on it—

Leah's back came off the glass.

Pine. Wet earth. Cold stone, like the creek bed behind her mom's house in December, when the ice was just starting to form at the edges. And *wolf*. Some kind of wolf she had never smelled in her life. She knew what the pack smelled like; she'd had their thoughts in her head for seven months, she knew Sam's scent and Jared's and Paul's and her own brother's and Jacob's like she knew her own name. This one was older. Wilder. Something that had come out of a forest she'd never been in.

Her wolf sat up inside her like somebody had said its name.

The man in the doorway had to duck to come under the frame.

He did it without thinking about it, the way tall people did, and even so the tips of his ears brushed the top of the jamb, flattened back, and came up again on the other side. He was six-seven, maybe more. Shoulders that filled the frame side to side. He was covered in fur—charcoal-black, going darker at the roots where the rain had soaked through and parted it into spikes along his neck. A wolf's head. Long muzzle, black lips, a black nose that flexed once as he crossed the threshold, sampling the air. His eyes were green. Bottle-green, the color of the beach glass Seth used to collect off First Beach when he was little.

He was wearing clothes. That was somehow the part Leah's brain caught on. A brown leather jacket, old, the collar worn pale and soft, the shoulders dark with rain. A green-and-black flannel open over a black T-shirt. Jeans, faded at the knees. A belt with a dull silver buckle. Behind him, through a slit somebody had cut and hemmed neatly in the back of the jeans, a long black tail hung low and still.

He had no shoes on. His feet—*footpaws*, her brain corrected, staring—were big and black-padded, five toes each, with dark claws that clicked once against the porch boards as he stepped in and then made no more sound at all on the hardwood. He walked heel-first, like a man. Flat-footed, his whole weight coming down on those pads, and somehow none of it made noise.

He had a leather wallet in one forepaw. Five fingers—*digits*—black claws, thick pads on the palm. He flipped the wallet open with one claw and held it up at Carlisle's eye level.

"Evening. Doctor Carlisle Cullen?"

"Yes," Carlisle said.

"Harry Potter. Auror, Magical Congress of the United States of America." He tilted the badge so the light from the hall fixture caught it. There was a seal stamped into the gold, and under it a photograph that—Leah squinted—moved. The little photograph of the wolf's face looked at Carlisle, looked bored, and looked away. "MACUSA, if you're in a rush. I'm here about your family. Can I come in, or are we doing this on the porch like Jehovah's Witnesses?"

There was a clatter by the kitchen door.

Leah didn't have to look. She knew the sound of her brother dropping a fork.

"Harry?" Seth said. It came out small.

Leah's chest did something ugly and tight. Seven months. Seven months since Mom's phone call and the ambulance and the waiting room at Forks Community and the doctor's face. Seven months since Dad. She hadn't heard anybody say his name out loud in this house. She hadn't heard anybody say it out loud anywhere, much, because Mom couldn't and Seth wouldn't and Leah sure as hell wasn't going to.

The wolf's ears turned toward Seth. Both of them, swiveling like radar dishes, and then his head followed.

"Something wrong with Harry?" he said.

"Our dad was Harry," Seth said.

"Seth," Leah said.

The wolf looked at Seth for a second. Then his eyes went to Leah, across the room, and she watched him put it together—her, Seth, the same jaw, the same nose, the same way they were both standing with their weight forward like they were about to go through a wall.

"Sorry for your loss, bud," he said to Seth, plain. "Potter's fine. Most people go with Potter."

And that was it. One line, and he took the name off the table and left it there. The grief counselor at the high school had done a month of sad eyes at Seth. This guy did a dozen words and moved on.

*Smooth*, Leah thought. *Too smooth. Watch that.*

Carlisle hadn't stepped aside. "You'll forgive us," he said, courteous as ever, "if we're somewhat cautious. We don't receive many unannounced visitors."

"You don't receive any," the wolf said. "It's a three-mile driveway with no sign on it, Doc. I've had easier times finding a parking spot in Manhattan." His ears flicked. "And if I'd wanted to come in without asking, I'd be in. I'm asking."

Carlisle studied him for another long second. Then he stepped back and opened the door wider. "Please."

"Ta."

He came in. Rain ran off the leather jacket and dripped from the end of his tail and pooled under his footpaws on the hardwood. Esme, in the kitchen doorway, looked at the puddle. Then at the doormat he'd walked straight past. Then at the puddle again.

The wolf followed her look, glanced down, and stepped back onto the mat. He scraped one footpaw across it, then the other, claws rasping on the coir.

"Sorry, ma'am," he said. "Boots don't come in my size."

"Clearly," Esme said.

Rosalie, behind Bella and the kid, wrinkled her nose. "Oh, *lovely*," she said. "Another one. Does it shed?"

"Only on the good furniture," he said, not looking at her.

Leah stood with her back to the glass and made herself do the job.

He'd come in with his weight balanced. He'd counted the room in the first two seconds—she'd watched his eyes do it, door, stairs, Edward, Bella, the kid, Jasper, Emmett behind him in the hall, the glass wall, the back exit through the kitchen. His tail hadn't moved since he'd crossed the threshold, which on a wolf meant something. Either tense or controlled; she couldn't tell which yet. His nose had flared twice. Once at Bella. Once at the kid. He had a heartbeat, slow and heavy—she could hear it from across the room, like a bass drum somebody was tapping with a soft mallet. He ran hot. Even from here she could feel it coming off him the way it came off Jacob and Seth: the wolf furnace, the fever that never broke.

His eyes went to her last. They stayed on her a second longer than they'd stayed on anybody else.

And her body—her stupid, traitorous, two-years-celibate body—went *oh*.

Leah stood very still and thought, very clearly, *No.*

*No. Absolutely not. You do not get to do this. Not here, not now, not in a house that smells like a candle shop, not about a* fed. *A fed in a leather jacket. With a* tail.

Her wolf, which had never once in its life listened to her, rolled over inside her ribs and stretched.

It was the smell. That was all it was. Forty-one minutes of vampire stink and then somebody walks in smelling like a forest after the first frost, and of course her nose went stupid. Anybody's would. It was chemistry. It was biology. It was—

The wolf shrugged out of the wet jacket, shook it once by the collar, and hung it on the coat hook by the door like he lived here. Under the flannel his shoulders were broad enough to blot out the hall light. The black T-shirt was damp at the collar and clung to the plane of his chest where the fur lay flat.

—*really* not helping.

*Tall, dark, and furry*, Leah thought, sour as lemon juice. *Great. That's what does it for me now. Fantastic. Thanks, universe. Really appreciate it.*

Across the room, Edward Cullen's head turned a quarter-inch in her direction.

Leah looked right back at him and thought, as loudly and clearly as she could manage, *Mind your own business, Cullen.*

His jaw tightened. He looked away.

"I can't hear anything from him," Edward said under his breath to Carlisle. "Nothing at all."

*Good*, Leah thought, and for the first time in forty-one minutes, she almost smiled.

## II. Edward

Every mind in the house had a sound.

Edward had lived inside that noise for eighty-eight years, and he knew the room by it the way a blind man knows a room by its echoes. Carlisle, by the door: measured, attentive, already cataloguing. *Not Quileute. The proportions are wrong—he's bipedal, and he's holding the shape, it isn't transitional. Clothing intact. A badge. "Magical Congress."* And under that, unbidden, old and faded as a pressed flower, a memory of a stone church in London and a man's voice thundering from a pulpit about witches. Esme, in the kitchen doorway, looking at the water on her floor: *He did wipe his feet. Eventually.* Emmett, in the hall behind the visitor, measuring: *Eight feet? No, seven, call it six-eight. Heavy. Real heavy. I could take him. Probably. Probably I could take him.* Rosalie at Bella's shoulder, all ice: *Wet dog. In the house. Again. With the baby right here.* Jasper beside Alice, already working the angles: *Throat. Knees. He's turned his left side away from the door. Right-handed, or he wants me to think so.* And Alice—Alice was furious, a bright, buzzing, wasp-in-a-jar fury: *Nothing nothing nothing I can't see him I can't see ANY of it what is he—*

Jacob, by the couch, was a wall of heat and suspicion: *Doesn't smell like us. Smells like—what is that, pine? Is that a* wolf *wolf? Where'd he come from? He looked at Nessie. He looked right at Nessie.* Seth was quieter, and sadder, and kinder than anyone had any right to be at fifteen: *He said sorry. He meant it, I think. Dad would've laughed at the tail.*

And Leah.

Edward did not, as a rule, listen to Leah Clearwater. He'd learned that early. Leah's mind was a place full of broken glass and she kept the lights off in it on purpose. But Leah was not, at the moment, keeping the lights off. Leah was looking at the visitor's shoulders, at the black T-shirt clinging damp across his chest, at the place where the tail came out of the jeans, and her thoughts had gone from *threat assessment* to something considerably less professional in the time it took him to hang up his jacket.

*—and the* jeans, *come on, who let him wear those, that's a public safety issue—*

Edward moved his attention away from her the way one would move one's eyes from an open bedroom door.

Then there was the visitor.

Edward reached for him. Gently at first, as he would for any stranger—the reflex was as automatic as breathing had once been—and found nothing. He pushed. Nothing. He knew the quiet of an empty room; he'd heard it in sleepers, in the dying. This had edges. This was a wall. Smooth and seamless and very, very deliberate, like the face of a dam. He could feel the shape of the mind behind it the way you can feel the weight of the water on the other side, and he could not find a single crack.

Beside him, Bella's silence was the same silence it had always been, the soft blankness he'd stopped fighting a long time ago and come to cherish. This was something built.

And in Bella's arms, Renesmee.

His daughter's mind was a bright, fast-moving place, all pictures and no words, and right now every picture in it was of the stranger. The black fur. The green eyes. The tail, especially the tail, which she'd fixed on with the absolute focus she normally reserved for Jacob's hair. Images tumbled over each other: her hand in the fur. Her hand on the big black nose. *Soft? Soft. Wet. Big. Doggy. Not Jacob-doggy. Different-doggy. Touch.*

"Easy," Edward murmured, and touched her curls. Renesmee did not take her eyes off the visitor.

The visitor had walked into the living room as though he'd been invited to dinner. He didn't make a sound doing it. Edward listened for it—the creak of a floorboard under several hundred pounds, the brush of denim, the scrape of a claw—and heard only the rain, and the slow, heavy pump of the man's heart, slower than a wolf's and much slower than a human's. He stopped at the edge of the rug, looked around the room once, and nodded at each of them in turn like a man taking roll call.

"Doc," he said to Carlisle, who had followed him. "Mrs. Cullen." To Esme, a dip of the muzzle. His eyes moved to Emmett, who had drifted in from the hall. "McCarty." To Rosalie. "Miss Hale." To Jasper, and there was the faintest emphasis on it, a hitch of interest. "Major Whitlock."

Jasper's mind went cold and flat. *He knows the rank.*

To Alice. "Miss Brandon."

Alice went rigid. Edward heard it in her—a white flare, a door slamming somewhere inside her. *Mary Alice Brandon. Biloxi. The asylum. The records I had to go digging for myself. How does he know that name, nobody knows that name, I only found it because—*

The green eyes came to Edward, and stopped.

"Masen," the visitor said.

It had been a very long time since anyone had used that name. Edward Anthony Masen had died in a hospital ward in Chicago in the autumn of 1918, and his mother had died in the bed beside him, and the only person alive who remembered either of them was standing three yards away in shirtsleeves.

Edward said nothing. He let his face say nothing.

The visitor's eyes moved on. "Mrs. Cullen," he said to Bella, with a small nod. Then he looked at the child in her arms and didn't say anything at all.

"You've done your reading," Carlisle said.

"I've got a file on your family as thick as my arm, Doc." The visitor walked to the empty armchair Alice had abandoned, looked at it, and then looked at Esme. "Mind if I sit? I've been on the road since Spokane and I'm about to fall over."

"The chair is white," Esme said.

"It is," he agreed.

"You're wet."

"I am." He considered. Then, without any gesture Edward could see, the water lifted off him.

All of it. It came up out of his fur in a fine silver mist, off the shoulders of the flannel, out of the denim, out of the tail, and gathered in the air in front of him into a single trembling ball the size of a grapefruit. It hung there. Rain sheeted against the glass wall behind Leah, and inside, above Esme's rug, a ball of rainwater floated a foot in front of a wolf's chest and did not fall.

Emmett said, "Whoa."

The ball drifted sideways, through the kitchen doorway past Esme's shoulder, and Edward heard it land in the sink with a flat slap.

The visitor's fur stood up dry and slightly fluffed, the way a dog looks after a towel. He smoothed down his chest with one broad forepaw, irritated by it, and sat. The chair took his weight with a creak of springs. He settled the tail around the side, propped his elbows on his knees, and looked at Carlisle.

"Right," he said. "Ask your questions. I can see you've got about forty."

"Magical Congress," Carlisle said.

"Of the United States of America. Unicameral. New York since the eighteen-nineties, the Woolworth Building since they finished building it. We handle everything your Congress doesn't know exists." He tilted his head. "Including, on bad weeks, you."

"I've lived a long time," Carlisle said slowly. "I've traveled a great deal. I've never heard of your Congress, or of anyone who belonged to it."

"That's the idea." He scratched under his jaw with one claw. "Three hundred and some years of the International Statute of Secrecy, Doc. If you'd heard of us, somebody'd be getting sacked."

"My father," Carlisle said, and stopped. Edward heard the memory rise in him, whole: the pulpit, the torches, the long nights in the wet London streets with the mob and the dogs. *Witches.* "My father hunted witches."

"Ja, I know. It's in the file." The visitor's ears went back a fraction, a tired angle. "He burned a lot of herbalists and midwives and old women who talked to their cats. Never got one of ours. By your dad's day we had a charm for fire. Witch gets tied to a stake, she'd flick the flames cold and scream a bit to keep the crowd happy." He shrugged. "There was a witch called Wendelin the Weird who got herself burned forty-seven times on purpose because she liked how it tickled. Your old man would've been gutted."

Carlisle was quiet for a moment. Edward heard something old and tender move in him, something like grief and something like relief. *All those years. All those people. And never once the right ones.*

"Yes," Carlisle said finally. "I suppose he would have."

"So you're a witch?" Emmett said. "Warlock? What's the guy version?"

"Wizard."

"And a werewolf."

"Wrong kind of werewolf," the visitor said. "Full moon's got nothing to do with it. I'm a shifter. Different paperwork."

"Huh," Emmett said, and Edward heard the idea arrive in his brother's head, fully formed and grinning. *Wizard. Okay. But how* tough *a wizard? Little shove. Just to see. Friendly. He won't even—*

"Emmett," Edward said, sharp. "Don't—"

Emmett was already moving. Edward knew what it was, and so did everyone else in the family: the same thing Emmett did to Jacob, to the Denalis, to Bella after she'd beaten him at arm-wrestling and he'd refused to let it go. A lunge, fast as a thrown knife, shoulder-first, meant to knock the man and the chair over backward and then laugh about it.

The visitor didn't look up.

Emmett stopped.

He stopped in the air, a foot from the armchair, his shoulder still dropped for impact, his feet six inches off Esme's rug. Nothing held him. There was no light, no sound, no movement from the wolf in the chair, who sat with his elbows on his knees and his eyes on Carlisle as if nothing had happened. Emmett simply hung there, like a fly caught in amber, and his face went through surprise into outrage into something very close to delight.

"Mate," the visitor said mildly. "Don't."

He set Emmett down. Gently. Feet first, on the rug, upright, as though putting a vase back on a shelf.

Emmett stood there for a second. Then he rolled his shoulder. "Okay," he said. "That's cheating."

"You deserved that," Rosalie said.

"I really didn't. That was a *friendly*—"

"You tried to knock over a federal agent in my living room," Esme said. "Sit down, Emmett."

Emmett sat down. His mind was full of happy, gleeful violence. *How'd he DO that? I didn't even feel it grab me. No hands. I didn't feel it till I was stopped. Gotta try that again. Gotta try that again when Rose isn't looking.*

Jasper's mind was considerably less happy. *He didn't move. Didn't tense. Didn't spike. Calm the whole time.* He leaned toward Carlisle a fraction. "He wasn't worried," Jasper said quietly. "Not even a flicker."

"I can hear you," the visitor said. "Ears." He pointed at one, which swiveled helpfully toward Jasper.

Edward stood very still beside Bella and watched him, and for the first time in a long time he felt the full weight of what he had always taken for granted. He didn't know what this person was going to do. He couldn't hear it coming. He'd spent eighty-eight years walking into rooms already knowing every intention in them, and now there was a wolf in Alice's chair, holding his family's whole history in a file somewhere, and Edward was as deaf to him as any human.

Bella's hand found his arm. She didn't need to look at him; she squeezed, once.

"Mrs. Cullen," the visitor said, turning his head toward the kitchen. His nose twitched. "Sorry. I have to ask. Is that lasagna?"

Esme blinked. "Yes."

"I've had one gas-station burrito since Port Angeles and I think it's still arguing with me." The ears drooped, very slightly. "Any chance."

Edward watched his mother's mind go through several stages in rapid succession: *Absolutely not—he walked into my house uninvited—he did knock—he's been rude—he's been fairly polite, really—he's dripping—he isn't dripping anymore—he's hungry.* The last thought won. It always did with Esme.

"There's plenty," she said.

"Any chance there's pie?"

"Dude," Seth said, wounded. "That's *my* pie."

"There's an apple pie," Esme said. "For Seth."

The visitor looked at Seth. Seth looked back at him, lasagna plate abandoned, mouth in a mournful line. *Come on. Come on, man. She made it for me.*

"He can have half," Seth said, grudging.

"Seth, you're a gentleman and a scholar." The visitor turned back to Esme. "Mrs. Cullen, marry me."

"I'm married," Esme said. "And you dripped on my floor." She went into the kitchen.

Carlisle's mouth twitched. Edward heard the thought behind it: *I like her answer better than I'd like any of mine.*

Across the room, Leah had slid back down to the floor against the glass. Her arms were crossed over her knees. Her face was doing a very good impression of boredom. Her mind was not.

*Okay. Okay, so he can float Emmett like a balloon and he's got no shame and he called Esme* ma'am *and then tried to marry her for pie. Fine. Whatever. Doesn't matter. Has nothing to do with anything. Jesus Christ, look at the size of his* hands—

*Paws. Whatever. Look at the size of them.*

*—bet he could pick me up with one. Bet he wouldn't even have to try—*

Edward fixed his eyes on the rain beyond the glass and thought, with a fervor he hadn't felt since he was mortal, about Chopin.

Esme came back with a plate. She'd cut the lasagna into a square that would have fed a human family of four, and next to it, a slice of apple pie so precisely half of the remaining pie that Edward suspected she had used a ruler. The visitor took it with both forepaws, said "Ta, ma'am," with his whole chest, and set it on his knee. He produced a fork from somewhere—Edward did not see where—and ate a third of the lasagna in two bites.

Then he looked up, chewing, and swallowed, and set the fork down on the edge of the plate.

His tail was still. His ears were straight up. When he spoke, the joking was gone out of his voice, and what was left underneath it was flat and level as a tabletop.

"Right," he said. "Let's talk about how monumentally reckless you lot have been."

And from the floor by the glass, loud and clear and full of something that sounded almost like joy, Leah Clearwater's mind said: *Oh, thank* God. *Finally. Somebody.*

## III. Harry

The house smelled like a Bath & Body Works had exploded inside a morgue.

Harry had braced for it on the porch and it still hit the back of his throat when Carlisle opened the door: sweet and cold and chemical, sugared lilies over something that had stopped rotting a long time ago and never got around to finishing. Eight of them. Eight vampires in one room, plus the kid, who smelled like all of that cut with something alive. Milk and copper and a heartbeat going like a hummingbird on espresso.

Under it, in pockets, the wolves. The big one by the couch, Black—cedar smoke and engine grease and wet dog, an honest smell. The kid brother with the pie—puppy-smell, softer, a lot of oregano. And the sister by the window.

He'd clocked her in the first second because she was the only one in the room who'd looked at him like a job. The vampires had looked at him like a threat and the boys had looked at him like a stranger, and she'd stood there with her back to all that glass and her arms folded and done a count on him—footpaws, tail, paws, eyes, exits—and he'd watched her do it and thought, *Ja, okay. That one's working.*

Tall. Cropped black hair, cut short and badly, like she'd done it herself in a hurry with kitchen scissors. Cutoffs, a gray tank top, bare feet, legs for days. She smelled like the others underneath—wolf, the pack smell, rain and cedar—and on top of that like salt water and woodsmoke and something sharp, something that made his nose want to go back for a second pass.

He put that in a box and put the box on a shelf. Later.

He finished his mouthful of lasagna. It was very good lasagna. He wanted to marry Esme Cullen for real, a little, and he could tell she knew it and didn't care.

"Chicago," he said. "Nineteen-eighteen."

Edward's face did nothing. Edward's face had been doing nothing for five minutes, with the kind of concentration that told Harry it was costing him.

"That's where your file starts, Doc. Influenza ward. A doctor who works nights and doesn't age and signs out a seventeen-year-old who's on the dead list the next morning." He pointed his fork at Carlisle, then at Edward. "Somebody in our Chicago office noticed. Wrote it up. Filed it. And then for about eighty years, you lot were the most boring thing in North America."

"Boring," Rosalie said.

"Glorious, beautiful boring. You don't eat people. You move every few years. You pay your taxes, which is more than I can say for half the wizards in Louisiana. Every twenty years somebody in the department opens your folder, reads 'Cullen coven, animal diet, no incidents,' and closes it again." He took a bite of pie. It was also very good. "That's the dream. That's what we want from every coven on this continent. You were the poster children."

"Were," said Carlisle.

"Were." Harry put the fork down. "Then the last two years happened."

He let that sit. Outside, the rain came down on the glass. Inside, eight vampires didn't breathe and three wolves did, and the kid's heart went *tap-tap-tap-tap-tap*.

"January, two thousand and five," he said. "Forks High School parking lot. Ice on the ground. A van loses traction and goes sideways into a row of cars. And a seventeen-year-old kid who was standing on the other side of the lot gets from there to here faster than a bullet and stops the van. With his hand." He looked at Edward. "Forty-odd witnesses."

"No one saw," Edward said. His voice was very even.

"Somebody saw." Harry looked past him at Bella. "Lay odds it was her."

Bella's face moved. Only a little. Newborns were fast and strong and bad at lying with their faces; nobody'd taught her yet how to hold still in the right way. Her eyes went to Edward, and her mouth went tight, and that was the whole answer.

"There it is," Harry said.

"That was—" Edward started.

"Ja, I know. Saving her life. I'd have done the same. Still a dent the shape of your hand in a Ford van in a school car park, boet." He moved on. "March, this year. Volterra. Saint Marcus Day. Town square full of tourists in red cloaks, and a kid tries to walk out into the sun in the middle of the festival with his shirt off." Edward's eyes went flat. Harry watched it happen. "Our Italian friends sent us a very cross memo about it. There were a lot of exclamation marks. The Italians don't usually do exclamation marks."

"How," Edward said. Just that.

"Not sharing." Harry picked the fork back up. "June. Seattle."

Something changed in the room on that word. He felt it more than saw it: Jasper's attention sharpening to a point, Alice going quiet in a different way, the boy—Seth—putting down a glass of milk very carefully.

"Thirty-odd No-Majs dead or missing in a month," Harry said. "Papers screaming about a serial killer. Gangs. Cults. A lot of theories. And then, middle of June, it all just stops. Like somebody turned off a tap." He looked at Carlisle. "I walked out to a clearing north of here this morning, Doc. Big field. Real pretty. There's a patch in the middle of it where nothing's growing, and the soil smells like burnt sugar and lilies and a lot of fire. Somebody had a bonfire. I'm guessing it wasn't hot dogs."

"That wasn't us," Edward said.

"Ja, figured. It still ended in your back garden." Harry spread his forepaws. "I don't know what it was, exactly. I can guess. I've got a lot of guesses and I don't like any of them. What I *know* is my department had Obliviators in Seattle for three weeks cleaning up eyewitness accounts of 'a pale girl who picked up a car,' and whatever it was, it came here to die."

Nobody said anything. Jasper's eyes had gone somewhere far away and unpleasant. Harry noted that and didn't push.

"August," Harry went on. "You get married. Lovely. No problem. Not my business, frankly. Couple goes on honeymoon, also not my business. Couple comes home early, and the bride stops being seen in town, and the hospital starts losing O-negative. Either she was bleeding out or something was drinking for two." He glanced at the baby, then back. "My money's on both." He looked at Carlisle. "Forks Community's blood bank's been doing some interesting maths since August, Doc. Somebody's going to notice. Somebody always notices. Usually it's an accountant."

Carlisle didn't flinch. He was too old to flinch. But he looked at Harry for a long moment, and then he nodded once, the way you'd nod at a doctor who'd shown you an X-ray.

"Yes," he said. "That's fair."

"Then she dies," Harry said, and he said it more quietly, because there was no point being loud about that part. "Near enough. And a couple of days later she wakes up with red eyes, three miles from a town of three thousand people. And her father's the chief of police."

He let the last word land. Then he turned his head, slowly, and looked at Jacob Black.

"And then somebody," Harry said, "turns into a horse in front of the chief of police."

"I'm not a *horse*," said Jacob.

"You're a big lad."

"He was going to lose her." Jacob's arms were folded so tight the muscle stood up in ropes. The heat was rolling off him in waves; Harry could feel it from the chair, and the edges of his shape were shivering, the way they did when one of them was close to going. "They were going to tell him she *died*, man. That she caught something in Atlanta or wherever and died and he'd never see her again. Or they'd all move away and he'd never know. You know what that would've done to him? You know Charlie?"

"Met him today."

That stopped Jacob for half a second.

"I showed him something," Jacob said, quieter. "So he'd stop asking. So he'd know there was stuff he didn't want to know, and he could—take it. And still have her. That's all."

"Ja," Harry said. "And what you showed him is the one thing every government on this planet has spent three hundred years making very bloody sure nobody sees."

"He's *fine*," Bella said.

It was the first thing she'd said since he'd walked in. Her voice had changed—it did, with them; the cords went smooth as glass and everything came out sounding like the low end of a cello—but it was still a nineteen-year-old's voice, and it was angry.

"Charlie's fine," Bella said. "He doesn't want to know. He told us. Need to know. He doesn't ask, he doesn't look, he just—he comes over and he holds her and he goes home. He's *fine*."

"I know," Harry said. "I checked."

Silence.

"Checked," said Bella.

"Diner on the main road. Lunch today. He had the fish." Harry picked up the last of the pie, considered it, ate it. "I was two booths down with a cup of coffee. Didn't eat. I was working." He chewed. "Took a look."

"A look," Bella said. Her voice had gone very quiet and very flat.

Edward had heard it too. His hand had closed on Bella's arm. "What kind of *look*," he said.

"Legilimency." Harry set the empty plate on his knee. "Mind-reading, near enough. You'd know all about that. Thirty seconds. Surface stuff. I didn't go digging."

The sound Bella made was low and wet and came from somewhere at the bottom of her chest.

Harry had heard newborns growl before. Twice, both times in a basement somewhere near Detroit, both times right before he'd had a very bad night. Bella's was a little different. It had a kid's heartbeat tucked into it; the baby was still in her arms, and the growl went around the baby like the kid was the center of a storm. Her lips came off her teeth. Her weight shifted forward on the balls of her feet and every muscle in her body went the wrong kind of still.

Edward was in front of her before Harry saw him move. Rosalie was at her shoulder with her arms out for the baby. Jacob had taken a step forward without seeming to know he'd done it. Jasper's face went smooth and his eyes went half-lidded, and Harry felt the calm come off him and wash over the room like a tide coming in. It reached Harry and slid over him and he let it, because it was easier than shutting it out and because it was, frankly, nice.

He didn't stand up. He didn't put the plate down. He kept his forepaws loose on his knees where she could see them, and he kept his ears forward and his tail still, and he met those red eyes and held them.

"Easy, Red," he said.

"Don't," Bella said, through her teeth, "call me that."

"Your eyes are red."

"I *know* my eyes are red."

"Sit down, Bella," Edward said quietly. "Please. Give her to Rose."

Bella didn't give the baby to Rose. Bella didn't sit down. But the growl dropped, slowly, back down into her chest, and stayed there, rumbling, like a car idling.

"Here's what he was thinking," Harry said, in the same level voice. "He was thinking about whether the coho were in the Sol Duc yet. And whether Sue Clearwater'd want to come over Saturday and watch the game."

From the floor by the window, Leah Clearwater said, "Oh my *God*."

The kid brother, Seth, grinned. It was a quick, startled grin that he tried to hide behind his glass of milk and failed.

Harry's ears tipped toward the window. *Clearwater.* "Huh," he said.

"Don't," said Leah.

"Wasn't going to."

"You were."

He had been. He let it go.

"And under that," Harry said, back to Bella, "a kid in his front yard going big and furry. Wrapped up in about forty layers of *I'm not thinking about that*. Real tight. Man's got better instincts for keeping things locked up than half my department." He shrugged one shoulder. "So he's fine. You're right."

The growl had stopped. Bella was still watching him like she was deciding where to bite.

"Standard procedure," Harry said, "for a No-Maj who's seen a shift—"

"*No-Maj*," Emmett said, delighted. "Is that what you call—"

"Not now, Em," said Rosalie.

"—is Obliviation." Harry kept his eyes on Bella. "Memory charm. Takes out an afternoon, puts something boring in. He goes home thinking he had a long day and fell asleep watching the game."

Bella moved.

She was fast. She was a month old and she was faster than anything Harry had seen in a long time, and Edward had his arms around her and the baby both before she'd gotten a full step, and she still dragged him six inches across the floor. The sound she made this time was worse than the growl. Rosalie made a grab for the baby and missed. Jacob was shouting something. Jasper said "*Bella*," very sharply, and the calm came off him in a wall this time, a tidal wave, and even Harry felt his own shoulders loosen under it.

Harry didn't move from the chair.

He lifted one forepaw an inch off his knee. Palm out. Pads showing.

"I said *standard*," he said. "Let me finish."

Bella stopped pulling.

"Listen to me." He kept it quiet. Low and steady. He'd learned a long time ago that the louder the room got, the quieter you went. "Chief Swan stays exactly how he is. Need to know. That's his rule and I like his rule. I'd like to keep his rule. Nobody tells him one more thing. Nobody *shows* him one more thing." His eyes cut to Jacob. "Nobody goes big and furry in his yard again to make a point. He doesn't see you hunt, he doesn't see the kid do anything a kid shouldn't, he doesn't find a body in the woods. And if he finds out one more thing than he knows tonight—"

"You'll *wipe* him," Bella said.

"I won't have a choice." He held her eyes. "And I won't call first. So don't make me."

She stared at him. Behind her, holding her, Edward stared at him too, with a pure and total hatred that Harry thought was probably fair.

"You went into my father's head," Bella said. Her voice cracked on *father*. It came out like a crack in glass. "Without asking him. Without asking *me*."

"Ja," Harry said. "I did."

He didn't dress it up. There wasn't anything to dress it up with. He'd sat in a diner with a cup of bad coffee and looked into the mind of a tired, decent man who'd buried a lot of things to keep his daughter, and he'd done it without asking, because asking would have meant telling, and telling would have been the one thing Charlie Swan didn't want.

"It's the job," he said. "Better me with a cup of coffee than an Obliviator out of the Seattle office with a quota."

Bella looked at him for another long second. Then she let Edward pull her back to the piano bench, and she sat. She did not look away from Harry. He thought she'd probably remember this for a very long time. Newborns had good memories. All of them did.

He looked away first, which cost him nothing, and found Leah by the window, watching him. Her face was flat. Her eyes had gone very bright and very sharp, and he had the distinct impression she'd enjoyed every second of that.

"Right," Harry said, and settled back in the chair. "Couple more things. Mrs. Cullen—young Mrs. Cullen—stays out of Forks until those eyes go gold. You want to see your dad, he comes here. Doc." Carlisle looked up. "The blood bank. Stop, or fix the books. I don't care which. I'd prefer stop."

"I'll see to it," said Carlisle.

"And the kid." Harry looked at the baby.

Every adult in the room went tense in a new way. Rosalie's hands curled. Jacob's chin came up.

"She's twenty-five days old," Jacob said. "Leave her out of it."

"Can't. Wish I could." He didn't look away from the child. "Your Italian friends are going to hear about her. Eventually. Somebody'll talk. Somebody always talks." He tipped his head. "Whatever your plan is for when that happens, I'd love to read it."

Nobody answered.

"Doc?"

"We're aware of the risk," Carlisle said.

"Aware's lovely. Aware's a feeling. I asked about a plan."

Carlisle opened his mouth, and closed it again.

"Thought so," said Harry.

And then the kid moved.

She'd been watching him the whole time. That was the first thing he'd noticed when he came in, and the thing he'd kept noticing, the way you keep noticing a smoke alarm chirping somewhere in a house. Big brown eyes. Bronze curls. A round little face that belonged on the front of a box of baby formula. She'd turned in her mother's arms when he walked in the door and she'd watched him walk to the chair and watched him sit and watched him eat and watched him talk, and the whole time—he'd been counting, without quite meaning to—she hadn't blinked.

Fourteen minutes, by his count. Not once that he'd seen.

Now she reached up, slowly, and put her palm flat against Bella's cheek.

Bella's eyes went unfocused. Just for a second—like somebody'd switched a channel behind them. Her face softened, all the rage going out of it, and something else coming in. Something fond and helpless.

Then she looked at Harry.

"She wants to know," Bella said, and her voice had gone strange, a little reluctant, "if she can touch you."

Harry looked at the kid.

The kid looked at Harry.

"Touch me how," he said.

"She can show you things," Edward said. He said it stiffly, like it cost him to give anything away. "Her thoughts. Pictures. Through her hand, skin to skin. That's how she talks, mostly. She wants to show you something."

Harry thought about that.

He thought about it the way he'd think about a stranger at a bar offering to buy him a drink he hadn't seen poured. The kid was twenty-five days old. The kid looked like she was one, maybe closer to two. The kid had a heart going faster than anything that size had any business running, and a face like a porcelain doll, and she'd just reached up and put a slideshow directly into her mother's head without asking the mother first. And she still hadn't blinked.

*Glorified Furby*, Harry thought. One of those big-eyed electronic owls that sat on a shelf and stared at you all night and then said something in a robot voice at three in the morning when nobody'd touched it. Cute. Probably. In a box. In a shop window.

"Yeesh," Harry said. "No. Thank you."

There was a short, ringing silence.

"*Excuse* me?" said Rosalie.

"Nothing personal, Blondie. Nobody gets in my head." He looked at the kid, who was looking at him. Still. "Not even the cute ones. Especially not the ones who don't blink."

"She blinks," said Bella.

"Not since I've been in the room, she hasn't."

"Dude," Jacob said, and he sounded genuinely shocked, which was almost funny. "She's a *baby*."

"She's twenty-five days old," Harry said, "and she's looking at me like she's doing my taxes."

The kid tilted her head. Then she said, in a clear, small, perfectly formed voice, a voice that had no business coming out of a face that size: "*Please?*"

Harry's ears went flat to his skull before he could stop them.

He made himself put them back up. It took a second.

"Nope," he said.

Rosalie stood. She did it fast and smooth and furious, and she held out her arms, and after a moment—a long moment—Bella handed her the baby. Rosalie settled the child against her shoulder and turned toward the stairs, and as she turned she looked back at Harry with the kind of contempt that took decades to perfect.

"Come on, sweetheart," Rosalie said to the baby. "It's bedtime. The *dog* is being rude."

"Night, kid," Harry said.

The kid looked at him over Rosalie's shoulder all the way up the stairs. She didn't blink once.

Jacob had already started after them. He'd taken two steps toward the staircase, his whole body bending toward the kid like a plant toward a window.

"Black," Harry said. "Sit. I'm still talking to you."

Jacob turned around. His eyes had gone flat and black and his shoulders were shaking—a fine tremor, all along the arms, like a pot about to boil. Harry'd seen a hundred people look at him like that. Most of them had been about to do something they'd regret.

"You don't," Jacob said, "tell me what to do."

"Jake," Bella said. She hadn't moved from the bench. Her voice had gone tired. "Stay. Rose has her. We need to hear this."

Jacob looked at Bella. Then at the stairs. Then, finally, at Harry. The tremor kept on, slower.

He sat down on the couch.

He sat down hard enough that Esme winced at the springs, and he folded his arms, and he stared at Harry with an expression that promised a very long and painful conversation at some later date. Harry nodded at him like they'd agreed on something.

"Right," Harry said. "Let's talk about wolves."

## IV. Leah

*Yeesh. No. Thank you.*

Leah was going to remember those four words for the rest of her life. She was going to get them tattooed somewhere. She was going to embroider them on a pillow and leave it on Rosalie Hale's bed.

For twenty-five days she'd watched this entire house fall over itself around that kid. Every vampire, every wolf, her own brother. Jacob most of all, Jacob staring at the kid like the sun came out of her, and the kid staring back with those big brown eyes that never seemed to close, and that hand going up to everybody's face to stick pictures in their heads. And not one person in the house had ever said the thing Leah thought every time the kid looked at her, which was: *that is a deeply weird baby.*

And then a seven-foot fed walked in out of the rain, ate half her brother's pie, and said it.

*Glorious*, Leah thought. *Absolutely glorious.*

She didn't let it get to her face. She kept her face bored. She'd been keeping her face bored since Sam, and she was good at it.

But she was also keeping a list, because she wasn't an idiot. He'd gone into Charlie's head without asking. He'd said so, flat, no apology, like he was reporting what he'd had for lunch. Which meant he could go into anybody's head without asking, and he'd tell you about it afterward if he felt like it. Which meant—

She looked at him. He was sitting with the empty plate on his knee, half turned toward Jacob, one ear swiveled toward the stairs where Rosalie had disappeared with the kid.

*If you've been in my head*, Leah thought at him, testing, *I'm going to rip your tail off and beat you with it.*

He didn't react. Not an ear, not a whisker. Either he wasn't listening or he was very, very good at pretending he wasn't.

Across the room, Edward Cullen's mouth twitched.

*Not you*, Leah thought at Edward. *Nobody asked you.*

"Right," said Potter. "Let's talk about wolves."

Jacob folded his arms tighter. "What about us."

"Two packs. Yours and Uley's. I've got that much." Potter set the plate on the side table, carefully, on a coaster, which Leah figured was for Esme's benefit. "Who runs what?"

"Sam runs his," Jacob said. "I run mine."

"How many in yours?"

"Enough."

Potter's ears tipped. "That's a great answer, Black. Very mysterious. Love it. How many?"

"Depends on the week," Leah said.

Potter's head turned. Those green eyes landed on her, and they stayed on her, and Leah felt it in her stomach and in the back of her neck and in some places she'd been pretending for two years she didn't have anymore.

"Clearwater," he said. "You're his second?"

"Beta."

"Who runs the patrol schedule?"

"Me."

"Walk me through it."

She thought about telling him to go to hell. She looked at Jacob. Jacob looked back at her with an expression that said *your call*, which was the closest thing to respect she'd gotten out of him in a week.

"Our pack holds the house and the line north and east," she said. "Out to the ridge and down to the highway. Sam's people hold La Push and the coast and everything west of the river. We overlap at the Calawah. Two on at all times, three when we can manage it. Shifts run four hours. We rotate at two, six, ten."

"Who's on now?"

"Nobody," Leah said, "because some asshole knocked on the door in the middle of a pack meeting."

"That's fair. I'm the asshole." He didn't sound bothered. "Where's your weak spot?"

"Like I'd tell you."

"North ridge," Potter said. "Three to five in the morning." He scratched under his jaw. "I walked it this morning. Stood on the ridgeline for twenty minutes, right on top of your scent markers. Nobody came."

Leah's jaw tightened. Behind her, rain hammered the glass.

"That's the shift I've been putting Seth on," she said.

"I fell asleep *one* time," said Seth.

"Twice," Leah said. "You fell asleep twice, and you snored in my head both times. I'm moving you."

"Rest of it's tight," Potter said. "Better than I expected, honestly. You lot have done this before."

"Wow," Leah said. "Thanks. I'll put it on the fridge."

He grinned at her. It was a wolf grin, all the way back along the muzzle—black lips pulled up, a lot of very white teeth, one ear cocked—and it was cocky and lazy and completely without shame, and it went straight down her spine and lodged somewhere south of her navel and refused to leave.

*Oh, no*, Leah thought. *Oh, come on.*

Potter turned back to Jacob and started asking about Sam—how long he'd been Alpha, how many in his pack, how they'd split, whether there'd been any trouble since the split—and Jacob answered in grunts, and Leah stood with her back against the glass wall and tried to listen.

She really did try.

It was the paws. That was where it started. He talked with them, a little, the way some guys did—not big gestures, just a flick here and there, a point, a turn of the wrist. Broad black pads. Five long digits. Claws on every one, black and curved and, she'd bet anything, sharp enough to shave with. He'd held a fork with those paws and not dropped a crumb. He'd held Emmett Cullen in the air without touching him. She watched one of those paws come up and rub absently at the side of his neck, the claws sinking into the thick fur there and scratching, and her brain, unprompted, served her a picture of exactly what those claws would feel like digging into her hips.

*Hmm*, said her brain, interested.

*No*, said Leah.

He shifted in the armchair. Recrossed his legs at the ankle. The jeans pulled tight across his thighs—big thighs, wolf thighs, the kind of thighs that had pushed a body that size through a forest all its life—and his tail slid around the side of the chair, slow, the tip flicking once against the floor.

And her eyes went, entirely on their own and without any input from her, to his lap.

*Okay*, Leah thought. *Okay. Real question. Scientific question. How much wolf are we talking. Like—proportionally. Is it just the outside, or does the wolf thing go all the way down. Because if the wolf thing goes all the way down—*

She'd seen dogs. Everybody on the rez had seen dogs.

*Oh my God*, she thought. *The knot. Does he have the—*

*Leah. Leah Clearwater. You are standing in a vampire's living room thinking about a stranger's—*

She glanced across the room.

Edward Cullen was sitting on the piano bench beside his wife, very straight, staring at a fixed point in the middle distance with the expression of a man passing a kidney stone.

And Leah thought: *oh.*

*Oh, he can hear all of this.*

And then she thought: *Good.*

Because there was a list, too, of things Leah Clearwater had had to listen to since the day she phased. The pack mind didn't come with a mute button. She'd had Sam's head in hers for months—Sam's head full of Emily, Emily's smile, Emily's scarred cheek, Emily's mouth, every time he thought of her, every single time, until Leah had wanted to claw her own brain out through her ears. She'd had Jacob mooning over Bella for months. She'd had Jacob mooning over the *baby* for twenty-five days. She'd had Paul and Jared and Embry and Quil and every stupid, horny, teenage thought that went through their stupid, horny, teenage heads, and none of them had ever once been asked to stop.

And Edward Cullen had been reading all of them. All of it. All of her, for months, every ugly, private, broken thing she'd thought about Sam and Emily and her dad and her own useless body—he'd heard every word, and he'd never said a thing, and she'd never been able to do a single thing about it.

Now she could.

*Hey, Edward*, Leah thought, clear as a bell. *You listening? Good. Stay with me.*

Edward's eyes closed briefly.

*So here's what I'm thinking*, Leah thought, and let herself look. Really look. She let her eyes go slow and deliberate up the long legs and over the belt buckle and the black T-shirt to the thick ruff at his throat, and his jaw, and his ears, and she let her brain off the leash.

*I'm thinking he's got me on that couch. Esme's nice white couch, the one over there with the throw pillows. Face down. One of those big paws on the back of my neck, holding me there. Holding me* down. *And the other one on my hip with every single one of those claws in.*

Edward made a small sound.

*Not enough to really hurt*, Leah went on, generously. *Just enough to leave marks. I heal fast. He can do it again tomorrow.*

*And he's big, Edward. You've seen him. He's* big. *All over, I'm betting. And he runs hot—you can feel it from here, he runs hot like us, so for once in my life I'm not going to be the hottest thing in the bed, and I'm not going to have to lie there next to some poor normal guy who's sweating through the sheets because I'm a hundred and eight degrees—*

She'd never thought about that before. It went through her like a jolt, so sharp she almost lost the thread. Nobody had touched her in two years, and she'd stopped expecting anybody to, because who would? Who was going to take on the bitter girl-wolf with the bad haircut and the dead father and the ex who'd left her for her own cousin? Who was going to put their hands on a woman whose skin felt like a fever? Who was going to want *that*?

Something that ran hot too. Something with fur. Something that wasn't afraid of the heat, or of her teeth, or of the wolf.

*Tall, dark, and furry*, Leah thought, a little dazed. *Huh. Who knew.*

She shook it off and went back to work.

*—and that tail*, she thought at Edward, *which I am going to grab, by the way, I don't care if it's rude—and his teeth on the back of my neck like a wolf does, you know, holding on, and him growling, that low thing he does with his voice, the one under the words, I want that right in my ear while he's—*

*—and then the knot, Edward. Think about the knot. Think really hard about it. Think about him* tied, *and me stuck there on Esme's couch, and nobody going anywhere for a good long while, and the whole room just has to sit around and wait—*

"*Leah*," Edward said.

It came out of him strangled. Everyone in the room looked at him. Potter stopped mid-sentence to Jacob, one paw still raised.

Edward had both hands flat on his knees. He had not looked at Leah. He was looking at the floor, at a point about two feet in front of the piano bench, with enormous concentration.

"Please," Edward said, very quietly. "*Please.* Stop."

"Stop what?" said Leah innocently.

Bella turned on the bench to look at her husband. "Edward?"

"It's nothing."

"Oh, that's not nothing," Emmett said, with enormous interest, leaning forward in his chair. "Look at his *face*."

Jasper had gone a little gray around the mouth, which for a vampire was impressive. His hand had found the arm of Alice's chair and was holding it like a railing on a boat. "I'd like everyone," Jasper said carefully, "to think about something else. Anyone. Anything else."

"What's happening?" Alice demanded. "I can't *see* anything."

"You don't want to," said Jasper.

Leah smiled. She let it get to her face this time, slow and mean and satisfied, and she held Edward Cullen's averted profile in her eyes like a trophy.

And across the room, Harry Potter's ears came straight up.

## V. Harry

Harry had smelled it a minute and a half ago.

He'd been halfway through asking Black how long Uley had held the Alpha spot, and the scent had come across the room on the draft from the kitchen—Leah Clearwater's scent, the salt and woodsmoke and wolf of it, with something new folded into it. Something sharper and hotter and much more interested. He'd taken one breath of it, and his ears had wanted very badly to turn, and he'd made them stay pointed at Jacob Black, and he'd put the whole thing in the box with the other thing and put the box back on the shelf.

Her business. If she wanted it to be his business, she'd tell him.

Then Edward Cullen had said *please, stop* in the voice of a man being slowly lowered into an ice bath, and the empath by the window had gone gray, and Leah Clearwater was standing against the glass with a smile on her face like she'd just won a bar fight.

And the box fell off the shelf.

"Hang on," Harry said. He turned in the chair. Both ears up. "Anything good?"

He meant it for the room. Edward looked up. It was reflex, nothing else—someone asked a question and Edward Cullen looked at the person who'd asked it—and for one second those gold eyes met Harry's straight on.

Harry went in.

It was easy. It was the easiest door he'd ever walked through, because nobody had ever thought to lock it. Edward Cullen had spent eighty-odd years reading other people and never once had anybody read him back, and his mind stood wide open on every side like a train station with no walls.

And it was *loud*.

Harry had been inside a lot of heads. Most of them sounded like one person talking in a quiet room. Edward's sounded like Grand Central at rush hour, every platform full. Voices on top of voices. Emmett, roaring with laughter—*what'd she think, what'd she THINK, oh man, look at him.* Jasper, strained, holding on: *please, please, everyone, anything else.* Alice, furious and blind. Carlisle, mildly mortified, very politely trying to think about a journal article on hepatic enzymes. Esme, worried about her upholstery for a reason she didn't understand. Jacob Black, pure irritation: *what is Leah DOING, God, she's such a—.* Seth Clearwater, in a frantic, tuneless loop: *la la la la not listening not listening that is my SISTER la la la la—*

And over all of it, loudest of all, carefully built and lobbed into the middle of the station like a brick through a window: Leah.

A picture. Clear as a photograph. Esme's white couch. Leah, face-down on it. Harry—it was very definitely Harry, she'd gotten the ears right—with one forepaw spread across the back of her neck and the other at her hip, all five claws pressed in. A low voice in her ear. A tail she very clearly planned to grab. And under the picture, in Leah's voice, dry as dust and aimed like a gun: *—and the whole room just has to sit around and wait. Enjoy the show, Eddie.*

Harry stepped back out.

It took less than a second. Edward jerked like he'd touched a live wire. His head snapped back, eyes wide, and for the first time since Harry'd walked in, the stillness broke on him.

"*Get out of my head.*"

"I'm out," Harry said.

"You were *in*—"

"Ja. Little bit." He leaned back in the chair. "Fair's fair, boet. You were in hers."

Edward stared at him. Harry had seen a lot of expressions on a lot of faces, and he didn't think he'd ever seen that exact one before: shock and fury and a deep, sickened offense, the look of a man who'd walked into his own house and found a stranger had already been through all his drawers. Harry'd have felt worse about it if Edward hadn't been going through everyone else's drawers for eighty-eight years.

He didn't feel that bad about it.

He turned his head and looked across the room at Leah Clearwater.

She'd stopped smiling. She was looking at him with her chin up and her arms folded and her eyes very dark, and he could hear her heart going—a fraction fast, a fraction hard—and he could smell that she'd figured out exactly what he'd seen. She looked like she was waiting to see what he'd do with it.

Harry grinned.

"I'm down," he said, "if she's down."

The room went off like a firework.

"Oh my *God*," Seth said. He was on his feet. The glass of milk was on the floor; nobody had seen him drop it. "Oh my God. That's my *sister*. That's my *sister*, man. I'm going—I'm gonna go stand in the river." He went. The back door banged. Harry heard him go down the slope at a dead run, bare feet slapping wet earth, and then a distant, splashing yell.

"Are you *kidding* me?" Jacob was on his feet too. "Leah. *Leah.* He's a *fed*."

"So?" said Leah.

"So he just threatened to wipe Charlie's brain!"

"And told Bella to sit down. And told *you* to sit down." Leah shrugged one shoulder. "Honestly, Jake, that's a point in his favor."

Emmett was laughing. He was laughing so hard he'd slid half off his chair, one big hand pounding the armrest, his whole body shaking. "What'd she think," he wheezed. "Edward. Edward, what'd she *think*."

"No," said Edward.

"Was it the couch? You said something about a couch."

"I did *not*—"

"Whatever it is," Esme said from the kitchen doorway, in a voice that could have chilled wine, "the answer is no. Not on my couch."

"I'd like," said Jasper, rising from the arm of Alice's chair with the deliberate care of a man disembarking from a ship in high seas, "to go for a walk. Right now. Alice. Come walk with me."

"I don't want to go for a walk," Alice said. "I want to know what she *thought*."

"No, you don't."

"I do!"

"Darlin'," said Jasper, with feeling, "I promise you, you do not."

He took her by the hand and they went out the back door after Seth, Alice still protesting, and Harry heard the screen door hiss shut behind them.

Bella had turned all the way around on the piano bench to look at her husband. The newborn rage was gone, at least for now; what was on her face instead was something a lot more human, something that looked a lot like the eighteen-year-old she'd been six weeks ago. "What *did* she think?" Bella asked him.

Edward closed his eyes. "You don't want to know."

"I kind of want to know."

"*Bella.*"

Leah hadn't moved from the glass. She was watching Harry. The rain ran down the window behind her, gray and silver, and her face was very straight, and there was something going on at the corners of her mouth that she was obviously working hard at keeping off it.

"Finish your homework first, Potter," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

Carlisle cleared his throat. He did it with the gentle formality of a man who had been a gentleman since before the invention of the steam engine and was going to keep being one regardless of what went on in his living room. "Perhaps," he said, "we could finish."

"Ja. Sorry, Doc." Harry sat up in the chair and put both footpaws flat on the floor. He let the grin go. "Report's simple. Reckless. Contained. For now. You've got the conditions. Chief Swan stays where he is. Bella stays out of town. Blood bank goes quiet. Somebody comes up with an actual plan for when the Italians hear about the kid, and I'd like to read it." He stood. It took a moment; the chair creaked with relief. "I'll be back. I don't call first."

"We'll consider your conditions," Carlisle said.

"Consider fast." Harry looked at Black, who was still standing, arms folded, glaring at the side of Harry's head like he was trying to set it on fire. "Next stop's Uley."

That got Black's attention. "Sam? What do you want with Sam?"

"Same thing I wanted with you lot. Assessment." Harry shrugged. "He gets the speech without pie."

"You can't just—" Jacob took a step forward. "La Push is the reservation. It's *Quileute* land, man. You don't get to just walk in there with your badge and start—"

"Black." Harry held up a forepaw. "La Push is in Clallam County, Washington. Washington's in the United States. Says so on every map I've ever looked at." He pointed a claw at the jacket on the hook, where the badge sat in the inside pocket. "I'm the Magical Congress of the *United States of America*. It's right there in the name, bud. It's on the letterhead."

"We've got a treaty," Jacob said.

"With the Doc. Your great-grandfather's treaty. I've read it." Harry tipped his head toward Carlisle. "Lovely treaty. Very clear about who bites who and where. Not one word in it about going big and furry in front of the police chief."

"Last time," Leah said, from the glass, "a government man came out to the rez with some paperwork and a nice smile, we ended up with one square mile."

The room went quiet.

Harry looked at her. Her face was still and hard and the corners of her mouth weren't twitching anymore.

"Eighteen fifty-five," Leah said. "Ask anybody in my family how that went."

Harry thought about it for a second. Long enough for her to see him do it.

"Ja," he said. "That's fair."

He turned back to Jacob and spread both forepaws, palms out, pads showing—the universal sign for *hey, hey, easy*. "Look. I'll knock. I'll go through your council. I'll take my shoes off at the door."

"You don't wear shoes," said Jacob.

"So that one's easy." Harry kept his paws up. "I'll say please. I'll say thank you. I'll be so polite your Elders'll want to adopt me."

"Billy's going to hate you," Jacob said.

"Everybody's dad hates me."

"My mom's on the council," Leah said.

Harry glanced at her. "Then I'll bring flowers."

Leah made a noise. It was short and rough and caught at the back of her throat, and she looked annoyed at herself for making it, and Harry was fairly sure it was the closest thing to a laugh he'd gotten out of anyone in the house all night.

He'd been watching her while he talked about Uley, out of the corner of his eye. When he'd said *next stop's Uley*, something had happened to Leah Clearwater's face. It had gone sharp and bright and hungry, the face of a woman who'd been waiting a long time to see somebody get what was coming to them and had just been handed a front-row ticket. He didn't know what the story was. He could guess. Two packs, one girl-wolf, an ex-Alpha she wouldn't name—there was a story there, and it had teeth, and he'd find out tomorrow.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Tonight I've got a motel bed with my name on it, and I've been up since Spokane."

"I'll take you to Sam's tomorrow," Leah said.

"Appreciate it."

"And I'll show you to your motel tonight."

Harry raised an eyebrow ridge at her. "It's on the main road, Clearwater. There's one road."

"Then you won't get lost," Leah said.

"*Leah*," Jacob said. "You've got the two o'clock."

"Seth's got the two o'clock."

From somewhere down the slope, faint and furious and very wet, Seth's voice came floating up through the rain: "*I HEARD THAT. FINE. I HATE BOTH OF YOU.*"

Jacob stared at Leah. The anger was going out of him, and what was coming in behind it was something more tired and more complicated. He was sixteen, Harry thought. Sixteen, with a pack and a baby and a war he hadn't asked for, and now his second-in-command was walking out the door with the government.

"Leah," Jacob said, quieter. "You sure about this?"

"Since when do you care?"

"Since some guy from the government showed up and threatened to scramble Charlie's head." Jacob didn't look at Harry. "I don't know him. You don't know him."

Leah looked at Jacob for a second. Then her eyes slid to Harry, cool and assessing, the same look she'd given him when he walked in. Footpaws, tail, paws, eyes.

"Then I'll keep an eye on him," she said.

Harry decided that was about as good as he was going to get, and better than he'd earned.

He walked to the door. He took his jacket off the hook and shrugged into it, and it settled on his shoulders still faintly damp, smelling of rain and leather and the inside of his car. He pulled the badge wallet out of the inside pocket, checked it, put it back. The wand was in the other pocket, where the regs said it had to be. He hadn't touched it in four years.

"Mrs. Cullen," he said. "Thank you for supper. Best lasagna I've had in this country."

"Wipe your feet next time, Mr. Potter," said Esme.

"Yes, ma'am."

Edward was at the door before him. Harry hadn't heard him move. He stood with one hand on the frame, and his face had gone smooth again, but his eyes were still black at the edges with something that hadn't gone away.

"If you go into Charlie Swan's mind again," Edward said, very quietly, "or into mine—"

"Then you'll know," Harry said, "because I'll tell you." He looked down at him. "Which is more than you've ever done for anybody in this house, mate."

Edward said nothing.

After a second, he took his hand off the frame.

Harry ducked under the lintel and went out into the rain, and he heard Leah Clearwater come after him across Esme Cullen's hardwood floor, bare feet slapping, not trying to be quiet about any of it.

The car was where he'd left it, nose-in at the edge of the gravel turnaround, rain beading on the hood and sheeting off the long black roof. A 1967 Chevrolet Impala, four-door hardtop, black paint so deep it swallowed the porch light. Somebody'd put a lot of love into it before Harry had found it rusting in a field outside Lawrence, Kansas, and Harry had put a lot more in since. It sat low and long and mean on its tires, chrome glinting along the trim, and in the rain it looked like something that had crawled out of the river to hunt.

Leah stopped at the bottom of the porch steps. She looked at the car for a long moment.

"You're kidding," she said.

"She's a lady," said Harry. "Be nice."

He went around to the driver's side. Leah went to the passenger door, and he watched her reach for the handle, and watched her look back at him across the roof, her hair already plastered flat to her head in the rain, her shoulders bare and steaming in the cold.

He didn't say anything about the door. She didn't wait for him to open it.

She hauled it open—the hinge groaning like a dying cow, that same long rusty complaint—and got in.

## VI. Leah

The inside of the car smelled like him.

That was the first thing. Leah sat on the long bench seat with the rain drumming on the roof and breathed in, and under the old leather and the gasoline and the ghost of about four hundred cups of gas-station coffee, there it was again: pine and cold stone and wolf, soaked into every inch of the upholstery like the car had been parked in a forest for twenty years.

After an hour in that house, it was like sticking her head out a window.

Potter folded himself in behind the wheel. He did it in sections—one leg, then the tail, which he swept around and laid along the seat between them, then the rest of him, ducking under the roof liner with his ears flattened. The car was big, a long black boat of a thing with a dashboard the size of a coffee table, and he still filled the driver's side like he'd been poured into it. The bench seat dipped under him. Leah slid an inch toward the middle and caught herself on the door handle.

He turned the key.

The engine caught with a roar that she felt in her teeth and in her tailbone and in the soles of her feet, a big, rough, rumbling bark that settled into an uneven idle, the whole car shivering with it. Rain sluiced down the windshield. He flicked the wipers on and they thumped back and forth across the glass in a slow, heavy rhythm.

There was a shoebox on the floor by her feet. It was full of cassette tapes.

"Ground rules," Potter said, backing the car around in the turnaround with one paw on the wheel and his head turned to look out the rear window. "Driver picks the music. Shotgun shuts her cakehole."

Leah turned and looked at him.

"Say that again," she said.

Potter considered her. He put the car in drive.

"Shotgun's entitled to an opinion," he said.

"That's what I thought." She picked up the shoebox and went through it. Zeppelin. AC/DC. Metallica. Lynyrd Skynyrd. Bad Company. Kansas. Something called Blue Öyster Cult with a hand-lettered label. Every tape had been played so many times the plastic around the spools had gone cloudy. "You know they make CDs now, right? It's two thousand and six."

"I've heard rumors."

She found *Led Zeppelin IV* and shoved it into the deck. The deck ate it with a clunk. She hit fast-forward, listened to the tape whir, stopped it, hit play. Drums came out of the speakers—huge, slow, booming drums that sounded like they'd been recorded at the bottom of a well—and then a harmonica wailing over them like a storm coming in.

"*When the Levee Breaks*," Potter said. He sounded approving.

"It's raining," said Leah.

"It is."

The car went down the long dark drive with its headlights cutting tunnels through the ferns, and the cedars closed in on both sides, black and dripping. Robert Plant started singing about crying, and the rain came down harder.

Leah sat with her bare feet up on the dashboard and her arms folded and watched him drive out of the corner of her eye.

He drove with one paw. He had the other arm along the back of the seat, not touching her, the black claws resting on the leather maybe a foot from her shoulder. His ears had come up now that he wasn't ducking, brushing the roof liner, and they turned toward the road and back toward her and back toward the road again. The dashboard lights painted his muzzle green.

"Where's the accent from?" she said.

"South Africa. A while back."

"That's it?"

"That's the short version."

"What's the long version?"

"Longer," said Potter.

She waited. He didn't add anything. She filed that away, next to the thing about Charlie.

"How old are you, Clearwater?" he said, after a minute.

She snorted. "Twenty. Why, you carding me?"

"Habit. Job." The car came out of the trees onto the highway and turned south toward the bridge. "You don't look much older than your brother."

"Seth's fifteen," Leah said. "I've got five years on him, and I earned every one, thanks."

"Noted."

"Bite me."

"Maybe later."

She almost laughed. She made it into a cough. The Calawah bridge went by underneath them, the river roaring white in the dark below the rails, and then they were on the long straight run into Forks with the rain blowing sideways in the headlights.

"You hustled my brother out of half his pie," she said.

"He offered."

"You looked at him with your big sad ears until he offered."

"It's called a hustle, sweetheart."

Leah turned her head and looked at him.

"Call me sweetheart again," she said, "and I'll take an ear off."

"Noted." One ear flicked. "Which one?"

"Keep talking and you'll find out."

"What's the deal with Uley?" Potter said.

"Ex," said Leah.

He waited. She didn't add anything.

After a second, he nodded, like that was a fair trade, and went back to driving.

"You really going to wipe Charlie?" she asked.

"If I have to."

"Would you tell Bella first?"

"No."

He said it the same way he'd said everything else all night—flat, plain, no wind-up. Leah thought about Charlie Swan, who had sat with her mother in the hospital waiting room the night her dad died, who had fixed the gutter on their house in April without being asked and never mentioned it. She thought about him going home some night after a long day and falling asleep in front of the game and waking up missing an afternoon.

"Okay," she said.

The neon came up out of the rain on the right side of the road: a long, low two-story building, white siding, an office at one end with the lights on and a sign over the lot that said PACIFIC INN MOTEL in red letters. Most of the parking spaces were empty. A minivan with Oregon plates sat outside one of the downstairs rooms with a kid's bike strapped to the roof.

Potter swung the Impala into a space at the far end of the lot, nose in, right outside a door with a brass 14 on it. He cut the engine. Zeppelin died mid-wail. The silence afterward was enormous: just the rain on the roof, and the tick of the cooling engine, and her own heartbeat, which was doing something stupid.

"For the record," Leah said, looking straight ahead through the windshield, "I'm calling you Potter."

"Figured."

"Like, the whole time."

"I said figured, Clearwater."

She looked at him. He was looking at her. The green eyes had gone darker in the dim car, the pupils huge, and his ears were up and turned all the way toward her, and his tail had moved on the seat between them—slid, slow, so that the tip of it was lying against the side of her thigh.

She didn't know when that had happened.

She didn't move away from it.

"Okay," she said. "Good."

They got out into the rain.

---

Two doors down, a heavyset guy in a Mariners windbreaker was standing at the ice machine filling a plastic bucket. He looked up as they came across the lot. Leah watched his eyes go over Potter—over seven feet of wet black wolf in a leather jacket, ears and muzzle and tail and bare black footpaws on the wet asphalt—and settle there, mildly, the way you'd look at a tall guy in a parking lot.

The guy nodded.

Potter nodded back.

The guy went back to his ice.

"He can't see you," Leah said under her breath.

"He sees a big bloke in a leather jacket who needs a haircut." Potter dug a key out of his pocket. It was an actual metal key on a green plastic diamond with the number 14 stamped into it. "Saves a lot of conversations."

"Do I see you?"

He stopped with the key in the lock and looked at her.

"You see what's there," he said.

He opened the door.

The room was a motel room. Wood-paneled walls. Two queen beds with brown-and-orange bedspreads. A framed print of a lighthouse over the TV, a heater under the window rattling away to itself, and a smell of carpet cleaner and old cigarettes from before they'd made the place smoke-free. A green duffel bag sat on the far bed, unzipped, a black T-shirt spilling out of it. On the little round table by the window there was a stack of pale purple paper, the edges curled, and a mug with a cartoon moose on it full of pens.

"Two beds," Leah said. "Expecting company?"

"Only room they had."

"Sure."

He shut the door behind them. He turned the lock. He turned around.

She was on him before he'd finished turning.

She didn't think about it. She'd been thinking about it for an hour and a half and she was done thinking. She got two fistfuls of his flannel and *pulled*, and he came down—he had to come a long way down; she was tall for a girl, and he still had most of a foot on her—and his back hit the door with a thump that rattled the chain, and she went up on her toes and found his mouth.

It was weird. For about two seconds it was very weird. His mouth was a muzzle, long and narrow, and there were a lot of teeth in it, and her nose bumped his and her lips found the corner of his jaw instead of anything useful, and she said "*Hang on*," against his fur, annoyed, and he made a sound low in his chest that was very definitely a laugh.

Then he tipped his head—the way a dog tilts its head at a noise, only slower, only more deliberate—and fit himself to her, and his tongue swept along her lower lip, hot and rough and much longer than any tongue she'd ever had in her mouth, and Leah stopped thinking it was weird.

She stopped thinking anything for a while.

His forepaws came up. One went to the back of her head, the broad pads cupping her skull, claws threading into her short hair and closing, careful, against her scalp. The other went to her waist. It covered most of it. His palm was huge and rough and *hot*—hot like her, hot like the inside of an oven, a hundred-and-something degrees of solid wolf—and when it closed on her hip she felt the five points of his claws resting against her through the denim of her cutoffs, and the sound she made into his mouth was not one she'd known she could make.

He lifted her.

He took his paw off her head, put it under her thigh, and picked her up like she weighed about as much as the shoebox full of tapes, and her legs went around his waist on instinct, and her back hit the door this time, and his whole body pressed into hers from her knees to her chin.

And he was *hot*.

That was the thing that got her. That was what undid something in her chest she hadn't known was done up. Every guy she'd ever touched—every guy she'd ever *been* with, which was one, which was Sam, which was a long time before she'd ever phased—had been cooler than her. And since she'd phased, she'd been a hundred and eight degrees, a walking fever, and the one time some tourist on First Beach had tried to put his hand on her arm he'd pulled it back like he'd touched a stove. She'd gotten used to the idea that she was going to burn whoever touched her. She'd gotten used to the idea that nobody was going to.

He didn't flinch. He was as hot as she was. Hotter, maybe. Pressed against him, she couldn't tell where her heat stopped and his started.

"Okay," she said against his throat, a little wildly. "Okay. *Okay.*"

"You sure?" His voice had gone down into the rumble, the low thing under the words. She felt it in his chest before she heard it.

"Potter," Leah said, "if you ask me that again I'm going to leave."

"Fair." His ears flicked. "One thing."

He took his paw off her thigh—she slid an inch down the door before his other arm caught her—and touched two claws, very lightly, just below her navel. Nothing happened that she could see. Something went through her skin anyway, a faint cool prickle, like peppermint, spreading out under his claws and fading.

"What was that?"

"Belt and braces."

She understood a second later. The laugh came up out of her before she could stop it, and it came out ugly, harsh and short.

"Don't bother," she said. "I can't."

He looked at her. He didn't ask *can't what*. He didn't ask *how do you know*. He didn't say *I'm sorry*. She'd been braced for all three since the day she phased.

"Humor me," he said.

She looked back at him for a long second.

"Fine," she said, and pulled his head back down.

---

The flannel went first. She wasn't careful with it. A button came off and bounced off the paneling and rolled under the heater, and he didn't seem to care. The black T-shirt took longer, because he had to lean down and she had to drag it up over his head and past his ears, and one ear got caught and he swore in a language she didn't know and she laughed into his chest.

Under the shirt he was all fur. Thick, dense, softer than she'd expected over the chest and coarser along the ribs, black at the tips and charcoal-dark underneath, and under the fur he was solid as a tree trunk. She sank both hands into it up to the wrists. He smelled like the forest after the first frost. She pressed her face into the ruff at his throat and breathed in until her lungs hurt.

Her tank top went over her head and somewhere behind her. He looked at her. He took his time doing it. His ears went flat back, slow, and his nose flared, and he said something low and rough in that language again that she didn't need translated.

"You keep doing that," she said.

"Afrikaans."

"What'd you say?"

He told her. In English.

Leah felt her face go hot, which was ridiculous, because it was already a hundred and eight degrees.

"Okay," she said. "Bed."

The bed was too short. That was the first thing they found out. He went down on his back across the brown-and-orange bedspread and his footpaws hung off the end from the ankle, and she sat up on top of him and looked down the length of him at those big black pads sticking out into the room, and she laughed so hard she had to put her forehead on his chest.

"Seven feet of federal agent," she said, "and they give you a queen."

"Six-seven." He was grinning; she could feel it, the big muzzle going wide under her. "And the other bed's the same."

"We'll push them together."

"Later."

His claws came to her hips.

She'd thought about it. She'd built the whole thing in her head in the Cullens' living room, just to make Edward suffer, and some stupid, hopeful part of her had been sure it'd turn out to be a letdown, the way things always were, the way imagining stuff always beat getting it.

Then he spread his paws across her hips, and pressed, and every single one of those ten black claws went in.

Shallow. Exactly enough. Ten bright little points of heat, a sharp sweet sting that went through her like a lit fuse, and she arched her back and heard herself say something in a voice she didn't recognize. When he eased off, there'd be marks. She'd heal them before morning. She didn't care. She wanted them anyway.

"*Again*," she said.

He did it again.

The rest of their clothes went somewhere. She didn't keep track. His jeans had a hole for the tail and a lot of buttons, and she swore at the buttons, and he laughed at her and she bit his shoulder for it, hard, through the fur, and the laugh went down into a growl.

She'd been right about the growl. She'd been right to want it. It started in his chest and came up through his throat and she felt it in every place they were touching, a vibration like standing next to the engine of that car, and when it reached his mouth he put the mouth next to her ear and said her name inside it.

And then she found out about the wolf thing.

All the way down. *Proportional.* She'd joked about it to Edward, thinking it would make him squirm. She stopped joking. She stopped doing anything that took words. For a long, long moment, with his paws on her hips and her palms flat on his chest and the rain coming down outside on the roof of the car, Leah Clearwater forgot every word she'd ever known.

*Oh*, she thought, from somewhere very far away. *Oh. Huh.*

*Well. That answers that.*

After that it got loud.

She grabbed the tail. She'd said she would. He made a sound she'd remember for the rest of her life and rolled them, fast, so fast the bed frame hit the paneling hard enough to knock the lighthouse print crooked, and then she was under him, and he was everywhere, and his weight pressed her down into the mattress, and his teeth closed on the back of her shoulder—the way a wolf does, holding on, careful and not careful at all—and she stopped keeping track of anything.

Somebody in the next room banged on the wall. Twice.

Neither of them paid any attention.

---

The knot was real.

She found that out at the end, and she found it out the way you find out the floor's a lot closer than you thought it was, all at once and with her whole body, and she said "*oh*—" and then "oh my *God*—" and then nothing at all for quite a while.

When she could think again, she was lying on her side with him curled around her back like a wall of heat, one heavy arm over her waist, and they were not going anywhere.

"Potter," she said, into the bedspread.

"Mm."

"How long."

"Twenty minutes." He sounded drowsy, and very pleased with himself, and like he was trying not to laugh. "Give or take."

"*Twenty*—" She started laughing. It shook her whole body. He made a pained noise behind her and she laughed harder. "Oh my God. Oh my *God*. I said that. I said that to Edward. I said *nobody's going anywhere for a good long while* and I was *joking*—"

"I know," said Potter. "I was there."

"You were in his *head*."

"Same thing."

She laughed until she had to wipe her eyes. When she stopped, she lay there in the heat of him with her heart slowing down, listening to the rain and the heater and the slow, heavy drum of his pulse against her spine.

It took her a long time to notice.

She noticed the way you notice a sound has stopped—a refrigerator, a car alarm, something that's been going on so long you'd stopped hearing it. She lay there with her face pressed into a motel bedspread that smelled like carpet cleaner and wolf, and she went looking for the thing that should have been there, the thing that was always there. The ache. The picture of Sam's face, and Emily's, and the two of them in the kitchen of the little house on the rez with the flowers on the windowsill. It sat at the back of her head every day of her life like a stone in a shoe. She'd thought about it on patrol, and in the shower, and lying awake at three in the morning, and every time anybody so much as touched her shoulder.

She hadn't thought about it once tonight.

Not in the car. Not against the door. Not in the bed. Not once.

She'd looked at a stranger across a vampire's living room and wanted him, and she'd decided, all on her own, to have him. Her choice. Her call. Sam had gotten a bolt out of the sky with Emily's name on it, a legend reaching down to grab him by the throat and tell him who he belonged to. Leah had gotten to pick.

She lay very still and held onto that.

"You alright?" he said, low, behind her.

"Yeah," said Leah. Her voice came out rough. "Yeah. Shut up."

He shut up. His arm stayed where it was.

And then she had the second thought, and it was so much worse than the first one that she actually sat up—tried to sit up, and couldn't, and fell back against him with a yelp.

"Oh, *no*."

"What?"

"*Seth*." She clapped both hands over her face. "Oh my God. Next time I phase. Next time I phase, it's all *right there*. In my head. And Seth's head. And *Jacob's*." She heard her own voice climbing. "Jacob's going to see *everything*. Seth's going to see *everything*. My *brother*, Potter."

There was a short silence behind her.

"Ah," said Potter. "Pack mind."

"*Pack mind*."

"Seth's going to need a bigger river."

Leah made a noise into her hands that was half scream and half laugh. "I'm never phasing again. I'm going to live on the rez and sell fry bread. I'm going to become a *person*."

"Want me to—" he began.

She went cold all over. She took her hands off her face.

"Don't," Leah said. "Don't you touch my head."

"I was going to say," he said, "*want me to talk to Black.*"

She lay there for a second. Her heart was going hard.

"Have you been in it?" she said. "My head. Tonight. Ever."

"No."

"Would you tell me if you had?"

He didn't answer right away. She felt him think about it—felt the pause go through his chest, the breath he took and held.

"Probably not," he said.

At least he'd said it. She'd give him that. She'd spent seven months in a pack with people who lied about smaller things than that, and she'd spent an hour tonight watching him tell Bella Cullen to her face that he'd wipe her father if he had to.

She knew exactly where she stood with him now. That would have to do.

"Don't," Leah said again.

"Okay," said Potter.

She didn't believe him all the way. She figured he knew that.

---

Something slid under the door.

She heard it before she saw it—a soft dry scrape against the carpet—and then a sheet of pale purple paper came skating across the floor, and lifted off it, and folded itself, crisp and neat, into a little paper bird. It flapped across the room past the lighthouse print and the rattling heater and hovered over the bed, wings beating, a foot from Leah's nose.

"What," said Leah, "the *hell*."

"Boss," said Potter.

"Your boss sends paper *airplanes*?"

"It's a whole thing. Interdepartmental memo." He reached past her with one long arm—she felt the movement all the way through her, which was its own problem—and plucked the bird out of the air. It went limp and unfolded itself in his paw. He read it over her shoulder.

She read it too. It said, in tidy handwriting: *Potter. Status. — T.*

"T?" said Leah.

"Not sharing."

He held the paper flat against the bedspread in front of her. One of the pens floated across the room out of the moose mug, uncapped itself, and settled between two of his claws. Leah watched it with deep suspicion. He wrote, in a scrawl that leaned hard to the right:

*Cullens: reckless. Contained for now. Conditions set. Full report to follow.*
*Wolves: tomorrow. Uley first.*
*Local contact acquired.*

Leah read the last line twice.

"*Local contact*," she said.

"It's a technical term."

She twisted as far as she could, which wasn't very far, and smacked him across the muzzle with the back of her hand. Lightly. Hard enough to count.

He laughed. He laughed with his whole chest, the rumble of it going right through her back, and the pen dropped out of his claws and the paper folded itself up into a bird again and flapped away across the room and squeezed out under the door into the rain.

"You're an asshole, Potter," Leah said.

"Ja," he agreed.

She settled back against him. Twenty minutes, give or take. She'd stopped counting.

Tomorrow, she'd take him down the road to La Push. She'd walk him up to Sam Uley's front door—Sam's door, Emily's door, the door with the flowers on the windowsill—and she'd stand on the porch with her arms crossed and watch a seven-foot fed with a badge and no shoes tell Sam exactly how badly he'd screwed up. She'd watch Sam's face while it happened. She'd been waiting a long time to see somebody do that.

And the whole time, she'd be watching Potter too. Watching where his eyes went. Watching whose head he went into without asking. Keeping the list.

Outside, the rain came down on the long black roof of the Impala. In the next room, somebody had finally stopped banging on the wall.

Leah Clearwater lay in a motel bed in Forks with a stranger's claws resting on her hip, ten small marks already fading under them, and for the first time in two years, she found that she was looking forward to the morning.
