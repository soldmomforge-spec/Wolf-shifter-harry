# North of Everything

## Chapter Two: Den

---

There was a hole in the bathtub.

It was about the size of a softball, low on the side of the tub nearest Harry, punched clean through the white fiberglass with cracks running out from it like a spiderweb. A few chips of it were lying on the bathmat. Harry's tail was still sticking through it. As Leah watched, the tip of it gave one more happy little thump against the inside of the tub, *whump*, and a fresh crack ran up toward the rim.

They both looked at it.

"Huh," said Harry.

"You broke the tub."

"I broke the tub." He pulled his tail back out of the hole, carefully, the way you'd pull your hand out of a jar, and looked at the hole with his ears tipped forward. Then he looked at it a little harder, and the chips on the bathmat rose up and floated back into place and the cracks ran backward and closed up, and the side of the tub was smooth and white again. "There. Never happened."

"It happened. I watched it happen."

"Nobody'll believe you." His tail started up again, *whump*, against the now-solid side of the tub, and he looked down at it with deep suspicion. "Okay. I'm gonna need to get that under control before the baby's born. I'm gonna knock her out of her crib."

"*Her*," Leah said.

He went still.

"You said *her*," Leah said. She sat up on the bathmat, pushing her hair out of her face with the heel of her hand. Her mouth tasted terrible. She didn't care. "Just now. You said 'knock *her* out of her crib.'"

"I—" His ears went sideways. "Did I?"

"Harry."

"It's a figure of speech."

"*Harry.*"

"I don't *know*," he said, and he looked so honestly startled by it that she believed him. "It just came out. I didn't check. You don't check that at seven weeks, babe, you can barely tell there's anything in there at seven weeks, it's the size of a—" He held up one forepaw with two claws pinched together, very close.

"Blueberry," said Leah, who'd been reading about this exact thing in the anatomy textbook three days ago for a completely different reason, and felt suddenly lightheaded.

"Ja. A blueberry." He looked at his own pinched claws for a second. Then he looked at her belly. Then back at her face. "I could check."

"You can check that? Already?"

"There's a charm. Hermione did it when she was carrying Rose. She did it about forty times, actually, in case it changed. It didn't change." His ears were creeping up. "I can do it. If you want. Or we can wait. People wait. It's a whole thing, the waiting, there's parties—"

"Do it," Leah said.

"You sure?"

"Harry, I swear to God, I have puke in my hair and I'm sitting on a bathmat at six in the morning finding out I'm pregnant when I thought I couldn't even get pregnant. I don't want to wait for anything ever again. Do it."

He did it.

She felt it the same way she'd felt the first one, the cool-water feeling, except this time it didn't start at the top of her head and go all the way down. It came straight in from the front, low, just under her belly button, and stayed there. Narrow. Careful. Like somebody shining a pen light into a dark room. The faint silver glow came and went across her stomach, so faint she wasn't sure she'd seen it.

Harry's eyes went unfocused. His ears stood up so straight they trembled.

Leah watched his face. She'd gotten good at his face over nine months. She knew what it did when he was lying to her about how much pie he'd eaten, and what it did when he was thinking about a job, and what it did when he woke up from a bad dream and didn't want to talk about it. She knew what it did right before he laughed. She'd never seen it do what it was doing now.

It went very, very quiet. Everything in it went still. Then something came up into it from underneath, slow, like the sun coming up over the mountains behind the house.

"Girl," Harry said.

The word came out rough. He cleared his throat and tried again.

"It's a girl. Leah. It's a girl."

Leah didn't say anything.

She couldn't. Her throat had closed up. She sat there on the bathmat with her hand pressed flat over her own stomach, where his charm had been, and she thought, very clearly, *a daughter*, and it went through her like a lightning strike, down through the middle of her and out through the soles of her feet into the floor.

A daughter.

She thought about her mom. About Sue Clearwater braiding her hair on the back step every morning before school, when she was little, pulling so tight it made her eyes water, humming. She thought about the Ruby Beach picture on the nightstand in La Push, her own scowling eleven-year-old face in the too-big Seahawks shirt. She thought about her dad, teaching her to gut a salmon on the tailgate of his truck when she was seven, saying *There you go, kiddo, now you'll never starve.* She thought about how she'd stopped thinking about any of that, on purpose, for nine months, and how it had all just come back up at once like the moose stew.

She thought: *I'm going to braid her hair.*

"Leah?" Harry was watching her. His ears had started to droop. "Babe? You okay? Is that—are you—"

"I'm okay." Her voice came out thin and strange. "I'm okay. I'm—" She pressed her hand harder against her stomach. "A girl."

"A girl," Harry said.

"You're sure."

"I'm sure."

"Like, sure sure? Hermione did it forty times."

He did it again. She felt the cool narrow light go in, and come out.

"Girl," he said.

"Do it again."

He did it again. "Still a girl."

She started laughing. She didn't mean to. It just came up out of her, the same way the crying had, and it mixed in with the crying until she couldn't tell which one she was doing. Harry reached over and pulled her into his lap on the bathroom floor, hair and puke and all, and she let him. She pressed her face into the fur on his chest and laughed and cried, and he wrapped both arms around her and held on, and she could feel his tail going against the tub again, *whump, whump, whump*, and this time he didn't even try to stop it.

"I'm gonna be so bad at this," she said into his chest.

"You're gonna be great."

"You don't know that."

"I know you bit a hundred-and-forty-year-old blood mage on the arm while he was cutting you open," Harry said. "I figure a toddler's gonna be a step down."

"You don't know that either. Have you *met* a toddler?"

"I've met Rose." He thought about it. "Okay. Rose bit me once. She drew blood. Fair point."

Leah laughed again, wetly, and wiped her nose on his fur. He didn't complain. She stayed there for a long time, curled up in his lap on the bathroom floor with the sun coming up gold through the window, listening to his heart. It was going fast. Faster than normal. She could hear it under her ear, and she could feel his breath going in and out, a little ragged, and every so often a hitch in it, like he was trying very hard not to do something.

She lifted her head and looked at him.

His eyes were wet again. He was smiling with his whole face, ears up, every tooth showing, and his eyes were wet, and he was looking at her stomach like it was the most important thing he'd ever seen.

"Harry," she said.

"Mm."

"Are you okay?"

"Ja." He blinked hard. "Ja. I'm—" He stopped. His throat worked. "I've been thinking about it since you told me. In November. About not being able to have—" He stopped again. "I meant it. When I said it was okay. I want you to know I meant it. I'd have been fine. You and me. That would've been plenty."

"I know," said Leah. She did know. She'd known it at the time. She'd watched his face when she told him and it hadn't changed.

"But I just—" He laughed, a short helpless sound. "I'm gonna have a *daughter*, Leah."

She put her hand on the side of his muzzle.

"Yeah," she said. "You are."

He leaned into her hand. His tail thumped the tub. Outside the window a raven landed on the porch rail and looked in at them, and croaked once, and flew away.

---

They were still sitting on the bathroom floor twenty minutes later, when Harry suddenly sat up straight so fast she nearly fell off his lap.

"We need a Healer," he said.

"What?"

"A Healer. A proper one. Somebody who does—" he waved a forepaw at her stomach "—this. Babies. Magic babies. Wolf babies. Whatever she is." His ears were straight up, swiveling, like he was listening for something. "I can do a diagnostic charm, I can tell you there's a heartbeat and it's a girl and she's the size of a blueberry, but I don't know what I'm *looking* at, Leah. I don't know if it's normal. I don't know what normal *is*. Is it supposed to be going that fast? Is she supposed to be that big at seven weeks? I don't *know*—"

"Harry."

"—and you threw up, you don't throw up, what if that means something, what if it's her magic doing something to you, what if—"

"*Harry.*"

He stopped. His ears were pinned flat. His tail had stopped dead.

Leah took his face in both her hands and made him look at her.

"Breathe," she said.

He breathed.

"Okay. We need a doctor. A wizard doctor. I agree. Where do we get one of those?"

"London," said Harry immediately. "St Mungo's. Hermione'll know who's best for this. She knows everybody." He was already looking past her, toward the bedroom, toward the dresser where he kept the little square mirror he used to talk to his friends. "I'll call her. Right now. What time is it in London?"

Leah looked at the clock on the wall over the sink. It said ten past six.

"Afternoon," she said. "Two-ish."

"She'll be at work." He was already getting up, setting her carefully on the bathmat like she was made of glass, standing up so fast his ears brushed the light fixture. "That's fine. She'll answer. She always answers."

"Harry, it's six in the morning—"

"It's an emergency," Harry said, from the bedroom.

Leah sat on the bathmat by herself and looked at the toilet. Then she looked at her own stomach, under the hem of his flannel. It looked the same as it had looked yesterday. Flat. Nothing there.

She put her hand on it anyway.

"Hi," she said quietly, feeling stupid. "I'm your mom. That guy's your dad. He's kind of a lot."

From the bedroom came the sound of Harry saying, "Hermione. *Hermione.* No, nobody's dying. Well. Sort of the opposite."

Leah closed her eyes and leaned her head back against the side of the tub, and smiled.


---

Four thousand miles east, on Level Two of the Ministry of Magic, Hermione Granger-Weasley was forty minutes into a meeting about the revised Werewolf Registry and was seriously considering hexing a man from the Department of Magical Transportation.

"—all I'm saying," the man from Magical Transportation was saying, for the third time, "is that if they're going to be permitted to use the Floo Network during the three days surrounding the full moon, there ought to be some sort of *marking*—"

"A marking," Hermione said.

"On the Floo records. For safety."

"You want to flag every registered werewolf in Britain on the Floo Network. Publicly."

"Not *publicly*. Just—"

Her handbag buzzed.

Hermione looked down at it. The man from Magical Transportation kept talking. Her handbag buzzed again, a deep insistent hum that she could feel through the leather against her ankle, and she knew that hum. There was only one thing in her bag that did that.

"Excuse me," she said, and stood up.

"Mrs. Granger-Weasley, we're in the middle of—"

"Yes, I'm aware. Back in a minute. Don't flag anybody while I'm gone." She picked up her bag and walked out of the conference room and down the corridor, past the long row of enchanted windows showing a sunny afternoon that the Department of Magical Maintenance had apparently decided London deserved, and ducked into the first empty room she found. It was a broom cupboard. She shut the door, lit her wand, and fished the little square mirror out of her bag.

Harry's face filled it.

He was in his tall shape, the wolf one, which she'd gotten used to over the last four years, more or less. All she could see was muzzle and teeth and very bright green eyes and a pair of ears pointed straight up, so close to the glass that one of them was cut off at the top. Behind him she could see a slice of a log wall, and a window full of early-morning blue.

"Hermione," he said. "*Hermione.*"

"Harry? What's—it's six in the morning there, isn't it? Is everything all right? Is Leah—"

"Nobody's dying," Harry said. "Well. Sort of the opposite."

Hermione stopped.

She looked at him. At his ears, which were trembling. At the way his whole face was doing something she'd only ever seen it do a handful of times in the sixteen years she'd known him: Christmas morning, first year, when he'd got presents. The day Teddy was born. The day he'd walked out of the Forbidden Forest.

"Harry," she said slowly. "What do you mean, the opposite?"

"Leah's pregnant."

The broom cupboard was very quiet.

"She's—"

"Pregnant. Seven weeks. She threw up this morning. She doesn't throw up, she *can't* throw up, so I did a diagnostic, and there's a heartbeat, Hermione, there's a heartbeat in there going like a hummingbird, and then I did the charm, *your* charm, the one you did forty times with Rose—"

"I didn't do it forty times."

"You did it forty-three times, Ron counted. And it's a girl." His voice cracked right down the middle of the word. "It's a girl, Hermione."

Hermione put her free hand over her mouth.

She didn't mean to cry. She was in a broom cupboard on Level Two in the middle of a working afternoon with a man from Magical Transportation waiting for her down the hall, and she'd been feeling a little queasy and a little weepy all week for reasons of her own that she hadn't told anyone about yet, not even Ron. But she looked at Harry's face in the little square mirror, his ears trembling, his eyes wet and enormous, and she thought about a cupboard under a staircase in Little Whinging that she'd only ever heard about in pieces, years after the fact, and the tears just came.

"Oh, Harry," she said through her fingers. "Oh, Harry, that's—oh, *Harry*."

"I know."

"Congratulations. Congratulations to both of you. Is she all right? How's Leah?"

"She's great. She's—" He glanced sideways, out of the frame, and his ears flicked. "She's sitting on the bathroom floor talking to her stomach. She thinks I can't hear her. She just told it I'm a lot."

"You are a lot."

"I'm *so* much right now, Hermione, you have no idea." His muzzle came closer to the glass. His ears swiveled. "Listen. I need a Healer. A good one. The best one. Someone who knows about—" he made a vague gesture that went partly out of frame "—*this*. Babies with stuff in their blood. Wolf stuff. She's not a werewolf and I'm not a werewolf and I don't know what this kid is and I don't want some idiot at a Muggle clinic in Whitehorse putting her on an ultrasound and finding a *tail*."

Hermione wiped her eyes on her sleeve and switched over. It was easier. She'd always found it easier.

"Margery Pye," she said.

Harry blinked. "Pye?"

"Healer Margery Pye. St Mungo's, first floor. Creature-Induced. She's semi-retired, she only takes a few cases a year now, but she's the best there is for anything with creature blood in it. She delivered Victoire." Hermione hesitated. "And she was Tonks's Healer, Harry. All through. She delivered Teddy."

Harry went quiet.

Teddy Lupin, son of a werewolf and a Metamorphmagus, born with turquoise hair that changed colour when he cried. Born at the worst possible moment of the worst possible year. Harry's godson. Nine years old now and living with his grandmother in a house full of photographs of his parents.

"She delivered Teddy," Harry said.

"She did. She sat with Tonks for the whole last month, at Andromeda's. Lupin was terrified the baby would inherit his condition, and she was the one who told them it wouldn't. She was right." Hermione pushed her hair back. "She's seventy-four and she doesn't suffer fools and she won't be impressed by who you are. You'll like her."

"Her name's *Pye*," Harry said.

"Yes, I thought you'd like that part."

"Hermione, I might marry her."

"I don't think Leah would let you." Hermione was already digging in her bag for a quill. "I'll owl her this afternoon. She'll want to see Leah in person, so you'll have to come to London. Can Leah travel?"

"I'll get an international Portkey. ICW'll sign off on it. I'll tell them it's a security matter."

"Is it a security matter?"

"It's my daughter, Hermione," Harry said simply. "Everything's a security matter."

Hermione looked at him for a moment. His ears were pinned back, a little. His muzzle had gone flat and still.

She'd seen that look before. She'd seen it in a tent in the Forest of Dean, and on a beach outside Shell Cottage, and on the front steps of Hogwarts with the castle still burning behind him. She'd hoped, in a quiet sort of way, that she wouldn't have to see it for a long time.

"I'll tell Healer Pye to be discreet," she said. "She will be. She's never told a soul what Teddy did to his own nose the first time Andromeda tried to change his nappy, and he's begged her to, so he can hold it over his grandmother."

That got a laugh out of him, short and surprised, and his ears came back up.

"Thanks, Hermione."

"Of course." She found her quill. "Does Ron know?"

"You're the first person I've told. Second, if you count the raven on the porch."

"Can I tell him?"

"Tell him. Tell him he's gonna be an uncle again. Tell him if he says one word about knotting I'll turn him into a teapot."

Hermione decided not to ask.

"I'll call you as soon as I hear from Healer Pye," she said. "Go and look after Leah. Get her some water. Get her some toast, actually, plain toast, that helps with the sickness. And Harry—"

"Mm?"

She looked at his face in the mirror. At the bright green eyes she'd first seen through a pair of taped-up glasses on the Hogwarts Express, when he'd had a smudge of dirt on his nose and no idea who he was.

"I'm so happy for you," she said. "Really. I'm so happy."

His tail must have been going. She couldn't see it. But she could hear it, faintly, through the mirror: a steady, heavy *thump-thump-thump* against something wooden, like somebody knocking on a door.

"Me too," he said. "Me too, Hermione."

The mirror went dark.

Hermione stood in the broom cupboard with her wand lit and her quill in one hand and the dead mirror in the other, and laughed out loud by herself in the dark. Then she put her hand flat on her own stomach, just for a second, the way she'd been doing all week when nobody was looking.

Then she went back to the meeting and told the man from Magical Transportation that if he proposed flagging werewolves on the Floo Network one more time, she would personally see to it that his next Floo trip ended in a chimney in Outer Mongolia.

The meeting wrapped up early.


---

The Portkey was an old Tim Hortons coffee cup with the lid still on.

"ICW's got a sense of humor," Harry said, holding it out to her at quarter to two in the morning, Atlin time, in the middle of the dark kitchen. "Every Portkey out of the Yukon office is a coffee cup. Every one out of the Paris office is a baguette. Don't ask about Rome."

"What's Rome?"

"Don't ask about Rome." He held the cup a little lower, where she could reach it. He'd been holding everything a little lower for twelve days, like she might break something reaching up. "Put a finger on it. Hold onto me with the other hand. Don't let go of either."

"Harry, I've been through worse than a coffee cup."

"You've never done a Portkey. It's like being yanked through a keyhole by your belly button. I'd rather you hold onto me." His ears were pinned back. "Humor me."

She'd been humoring him for twelve days. The laundry basket. The heavy pans moved to the bottom cupboard. The time he'd tried to carry her up the stairs and she'd bitten his ear, gently, and he'd put her down.

She put one finger on the coffee cup and wrapped her other hand in the front of his coat.

"Three," Harry said. "Two. One."

The world grabbed her behind the navel and *pulled*.

---

They landed in a garden.

Leah landed on her feet, mostly because Harry had an arm around her waist and set her down on them. It was a little walled square of grass and rosebushes, wet and green, with a black iron gate and a plane tree in the middle and tall brick houses all around it. The sky overhead was low and gray and muggy. It smelled like rain on hot pavement. It smelled like—

Leah's knees buckled.

"Whoa." Harry caught her. "Whoa, whoa. You okay? Is it the sickness? Do you need to—"

"*Smell*," Leah choked.

It hit her like every truck in the world at once. Car exhaust and hot tar and rotting garbage and curry and cigarette smoke and ten thousand pigeons and the big dirty river somewhere to the south, and under all of it, layered over and over itself into a solid wall of noise in her nose, *people*. More people than she'd smelled in her whole life put together. Eight million of them, all at once.

She doubled over and gagged.

Harry's forepaw came down on the back of her neck. She felt something cool slide over her face, like a damp cloth, and the wall of smell dropped. Not all of it—she could still smell the roses, and the wet grass, and Harry—but the roar behind it went quiet, like somebody closing a window on a busy street.

Leah straightened up, panting.

"Sorry," Harry said. "Should've done that before we left. Scent-dampening charm. It'll wear off in a few hours, I'll top it up." His ears were flat. "London's a lot."

"London's *disgusting*," Leah said.

"Ja, pretty much."

She looked at him. He was in his coat, the long black one, buttoned up, with a gray T-shirt under it that said *AC/DC*. He looked the same as he always looked. Six-foot-seven, black fur, green eyes, a long muzzle, ears. Tail out the back of the coat.

"You're doing the glamour thing," she said.

"Ja. Far as anyone in London knows, I'm a big boring bloke with a beard." He ran a forepaw down the front of his own muzzle. "Can you still see through it?"

"I can see you."

"Huh," Harry said, the way he'd said it every time she'd told him so for nine months. "I still need to figure that out."

---

St Mungo's Hospital for Magical Maladies and Injuries was hiding inside a department store that looked like it had gone out of business sometime around the moon landing.

*PURGE AND DOWSE LTD.*, said the sign over the door, and under it, on a piece of cardboard, *CLOSED FOR REFURBISHMENT*. A few mannequins stood in the grimy windows with chipped noses and crooked wigs. The nearest one was a woman in a green nylon pinafore with false eyelashes coming unglued.

Harry stepped up to the glass and leaned toward the ugly mannequin.

"Morning," he said. "We're here to see Healer Pye. Ten o'clock."

The mannequin nodded. It nodded its whole jointed plastic head, slowly, and crooked one finger at them.

Leah took a step back. "Nope."

"It's fine."

"It just *moved*, Harry."

"It's supposed to." He took her hand. "We walk through the glass. It doesn't hurt. You'll feel a little cool. That's it."

"We walk *through the window*."

"We walk through the window," Harry agreed, and walked through the window, and pulled her through with him.

It felt like walking through a sheet of cold water that didn't get her wet. And then they were standing in a crowded waiting room with rows of rickety wooden chairs, under a lot of bright white light, and the street noise had gone away completely.

Leah stared.

There was a man in the front row with a brass teapot stuck over his whole head, steam coming out of the spout every time he breathed. There was a woman next to him with what looked like three extra arms growing out of her back, all of them knitting. There was a kid of about ten with feathers instead of hair, and a very old man in a nightshirt whose feet were on fire, very slightly, little blue flames licking up around his ankles, which he seemed to find boring. Behind a desk at the far end of the room, a blond witch in lime-green robes was shouting, "*One at a time! One at a time, please!*" at a line of people waving their arms at her, one of whom was holding a toad that was screaming in a human voice.

"Huh," Leah said faintly.

"Yeah," said Harry. "First floor's this way."

---

The first floor said *CREATURE-INDUCED INJURIES* on a sign at the top of the stairs. The corridor beyond it had portraits all along the walls of severe-looking Healers in green robes, a couple of whom looked down their noses at Harry as he passed and whispered to each other. Leah wondered what they saw. Probably the big boring bloke with the beard. They passed a pair of double doors with a sign over them that said *DAI LLEWELLYN WARD: SERIOUS BITES*, and Harry's ears flicked as he went by, and he didn't say anything.

At the very end of the corridor there was a small door painted dark blue, with a brass plate on it.

*H. M. PYE*
*Healer — By Appointment*

Harry knocked.

"It's open," called a voice. "It's always open. Come in before you let the draft in."

The room on the other side of the door was the most cluttered room Leah had ever been in. Shelves crammed floor to ceiling with jars, and the jars full of things floating in yellow liquid—a tiny green dragon curled up like a seahorse, something with too many eyes. Books on every flat surface, books holding up a wobbly corner of the desk. The skeleton of something with wings hanging from the ceiling. A kettle on a gas ring in the corner, just starting to whistle. A saggy brown sofa with a knitted orange throw. An exam couch against the far wall, covered in clean white paper, with a pillow at one end embroidered with the words *BREATHE, LOVE*.

In the middle of all of it, at the desk, sat a tiny old woman.

She was maybe five feet tall, and round, and her hair was white and piled up on top of her head in a bun with two knitting needles stuck through it. She wore lime-green Healer's robes with an emblem stitched on the chest, a wand crossed over a bone, and a pair of tortoiseshell glasses on a beaded chain. She was writing something with a quill. She didn't look up.

"Sit down," she said. "Sofa's for whoever's carrying. Chair's for whoever did it. Kettle's on."

Leah sat on the sofa. Harry looked at the chair—a small, spindly wooden thing—and then looked at himself, and stayed standing.

Healer Margery Pye finished writing. She put down her quill. She took off her glasses and let them drop on their chain, and folded her hands on the desk, and looked up.

She looked at Harry first. A long, steady look, starting at the boots and finishing at the hat he wasn't wearing.

"Drop the glamour, Lord Potter," she said. "I can't examine around it, and it's making my eyes water."

Harry dropped it. Leah couldn't see any difference, but Healer Pye's eyebrows went up a quarter-inch. She looked at him again—the fur, the ears, the muzzle, the tail, the full six-foot-seven of him crammed between a bookcase and a jar of something with tentacles.

"Well," she said. "You're a big lad, aren't you."

"Ja, I get that a lot."

"I'm sure you do." Her accent was Welsh, sing-songy, the words going up and down. "Mind the jar behind you with the tail. That's a Niffler embryo and it's older than you are."

Harry's tail, which had been swinging, went very still.

Then Healer Pye turned and looked at Leah, and her face changed. It got softer around the edges.

"And you're Leah," she said.

"Yeah."

"Hermione said you've got a mouth on you."

"Hermione's not wrong."

"Good. I like a mouth." Healer Pye got up from the desk and came around it. She had to step over three stacks of books to do it. "Let's have a look at you, then. Lie back on the couch for me, love. You won't need to take anything off. Shoes off, if you like, the paper's scratchy."

Leah lay back on the exam couch. The paper crackled under her. The pillow said *BREATHE, LOVE* against her cheek.

Healer Pye pulled a wand out of her bun, where it had been hiding behind the knitting needles, and held it over Leah's stomach, and stopped.

"You can see through it, can't you," she said.

Leah looked up at her. "Through what?"

"His glamour. When you came in, you didn't look at the beard once. You looked at his ears." Healer Pye glanced over at Harry, then back down at Leah. "You can see him. What he really is."

"I've always been able to," Leah said. "Since the first night."

"Mm." Healer Pye tapped her wand against her palm, thinking. "Glamours slide off kin-magic. Old magic recognizes old magic. Whatever's in your blood and whatever's in his speak the same language, love. Some very old language." She shrugged, a small round shrug. "Interesting. Not my department. Hold still now."

Leah looked over at Harry. Harry was looking at her, ears up, head tilted. Neither of them said anything.

Healer Pye began to move her wand.

---

Harry's charm was cool water, quick and narrow. Healer Pye's was slower, and it went everywhere—her head, her chest, her arms, her legs, her hands—and it lingered, the way a doctor's hands linger, pressing here and there and moving on. Healer Pye muttered as she went. Leah caught bits of it. *Heart's strong. Lungs, good. Temperature—goodness, girl, you're like a furnace—*

"A hundred and eight," Leah said. "It's normal. For me."

"Is it, now." Healer Pye made a note in the air with her free hand, and a quill on the desk scribbled something down by itself. "And when did you first change?"

"Last year. March. After my dad died."

"How old were you?"

"Nineteen."

"And your courses stopped?"

"My—" Leah figured it out. "Yeah. Right away. Couple months after. I figured I was—" she stopped. She'd never said it out loud to anybody but Harry. "I figured I was a dead end. There were guys in my—there were guys like me. Back home. They were all fine. It was just me. I was the only girl."

Healer Pye didn't say anything for a moment. Her wand had come to rest over Leah's stomach.

"Your body shut itself down," she said. "I've seen it before, in wolf-blooded women and a few others. The wolf's under strain, or it can't find anything it judges worth the trouble, and it closes up shop. Goes into hibernation. Could've stayed that way for years." She glanced at Harry over her glasses. "Then along comes something with enough magic in it to wake it up."

There was a pause.

"So it *is* his freaky magic," Leah said.

"Crudely put," said Healer Pye. "But yes."

"*Oi*," said Harry.

"I *told* you," Leah said to him, from the couch. "I told you it was your fault."

"You said it was my *knot*—"

"Lord Potter," said Healer Pye, without looking up, "I'm seventy-four years old and I've been doing this for fifty-one of them. There is no word you can say in this room I haven't heard. But I'd thank you not to say that one in front of the Niffler."

Harry shut his mouth. His ears went pink on the inside. Leah, lying on the exam couch, laughed so hard the paper tore.

Healer Pye waited for her to finish. Then she lifted her wand and did something with it, a long slow twist, and light came up out of Leah's stomach.

---

It hung in the air about a foot above her. A shape made of light, pale and shimmering, maybe the size of Leah's palm. Most of it was misty and dim, like fog with a lamp behind it. But in the middle there was something curled up small, and in the middle of *that* there was a tiny spark of silver, flickering. Flickering fast.

*Thump-thump-thump-thump-thump.*

Leah could hear it. Faint and fast. A heartbeat.

She stopped breathing.

Behind her she heard Harry make a sound, a very small sound, low in his throat.

The shape turned slowly in the air. Threads of colour ran through it, thin as hair—some of them a deep, dark green, almost black; a lot of them silver-gray, the colour of fog on the lake; and a very few, a very few, a pale soft gold. Healer Pye leaned in close and put her glasses back on and looked at it for a long time, turning her wand very slightly to turn the shape this way and that.

"Hm," she said.

Leah's heart, which had started again, stopped again.

"What," she said. "What's *hm*. Is something wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong, love. She's healthy as a horse. Strong heart. Strong magic—goodness, she's got more magic in her than some fully grown wizards I've treated." Healer Pye tilted her head at the shape. "Well. That's no human baby."

The room went very quiet.

"What does that mean," said Harry, and his voice had dropped, all the way down.

"Exactly what it says." Healer Pye pointed with her wand at the threads. "Gold's the human part. You see how little there is? A whisker. Silver's wolf. That's from your side, love—" a nod at Leah "—and this deep green, that's his, and his is wolf too, only a different kind. Older, if anything. Wilder." She looked at Harry over her glasses. "With you, Lord Potter, there's no line where the man stops and the wolf starts. The wolf's the whole house. You're all one thing. With her—" a nod at Leah "—it's two halves sharing a body, a woman and a wolf, taking turns. And this little one's taken most of her shape from you." She pointed at the curled thing in the middle of the light. "She's a pup."

Leah stared at the light.

"A pup," she said.

"A pup."

"Like a—like a puppy pup? She's gonna come out a *puppy*?"

"She's going to come out a small version of your fella, I'd think." Healer Pye did something else with her wand, and a hand mirror came floating off one of the shelves, an old silver one with a cloudy glass. She caught it and held it above the shape of light, glass down, and the light poured up into it. "Expectant Glass. Old as the hills. It's not exact, mind you. It's a guess. But it's usually a good one."

She turned the mirror over and held it out.

Leah looked.

The glass was cloudy, like breath on a window. But in the middle of the cloud there was a shape. Small. Curled up on its side, the way babies curled up in the books. It had a round head and two folded-down ears and a short blunt muzzle and a tiny tail tucked around its feet. It was covered in fur. The fur was charcoal, dark, nearly black, like Harry's—except at the tips, where it went silver-gray, every hair of it, so the whole little body shimmered when the mirror tilted. And there was a blaze of pale gray running down the middle of its tiny face, from between the ears to the end of the nose.

Leah's own wolf had a face that color. Light gray. Exactly that gray.

"That's—" Leah's voice cracked. "That's my gray. That's *my* gray."

"Your coat, very likely," said Healer Pye. "His build, his shape. Your colouring woven through. Eyes, I couldn't say yet. Size, I couldn't say. She'll walk on two legs like him, probably, once she's walking. I'd expect she'll be able to shift the way he does eventually, too, but not for years, so don't hold your breath. You'll have a furry baby, love. And a furry toddler. You'll want a lot of lint rollers."

Leah couldn't stop looking at the mirror.

*Thump*, went something behind her.

*Thump. Thump. CRASH.*

Healer Pye's wand flicked out sideways without her looking. A jar that had been tumbling off the shelf behind Harry—knocked loose by his tail, which was going so hard now it was a black blur—stopped dead in midair an inch from the floor and floated gently back up onto the shelf.

"Mind the Niffler," said Healer Pye.

"Sorry," said Harry. "Sorry. Sorry. I can't—it's—" He looked over his shoulder at his own tail like it belonged to somebody else. "It does that."

"I can see that."

Harry came around the end of the couch. He knelt down beside it, which put his head about level with Leah's, and looked at the mirror in her hands. He didn't say anything. He just looked. His ears were straight up. His eyes were wet and green and huge. His tail kept going, *thump, thump, thump*, against the side of the bookcase, and a little shower of dust came down off the top shelf onto his head and he didn't even notice.

"That's her," he said finally, very quietly.

"That's her," said Leah.

"She's got your gray."

"She's got your everything else."

"She's so *small*." His voice cracked on it. "Look how small she is."

"She's the size of a blueberry, Lord Potter," said Healer Pye, not unkindly. "That's what she'll look like when she's born, near as I can tell, give or take. She'll be bigger than this by then." She took the mirror back, gently, out of Leah's hands. "I can make you a copy. People like a copy."

"Yes," said Harry and Leah, at the same time.

---

After that there were a lot of questions.

Harry had a list. Leah hadn't known he had a list. He took it out of his coat, a roll of parchment about the size of a paper towel tube, and when he let go of one end it unrolled across the floor all the way to the door.

Healer Pye looked at it. Then she looked at him.

"Ask me the five that keep you up at night," she said. "Buy books for the rest."

He asked her five. Then six. Then nine.

Most pregnancies with wolf-blooded babies ran short, she told them. Shorter than human. She'd guess thirty, thirty-two weeks for this one, so they should be looking at November, not January. Leah would eat more. A lot more. She'd want meat, a lot of meat, and raw was fine for her, Healer Pye said, though she'd draw the line at anything that had been dead longer than a day. The sickness was the pup's magic pushing against Leah's, finding its place; it would settle by twelve weeks, and in the meantime she'd give them a potion, and Leah should eat ginger and dry toast and not go too long without food.

"And the change?" Harry said. "Can she still—phase? Turn?"

"For now," said Healer Pye. "Till about twenty weeks. Then I'd stop. The pup'll be big enough by then that your magic can get tangled up with hers in the middle of it, love, and I'd rather it didn't." She looked at Leah over her glasses. "And no fighting, I'd think. No running about in the dark biting blood mages."

Leah turned her head slowly and looked at Harry.

"Hermione mentioned it," Harry said quickly.

"Hermione mentions a lot of things," said Healer Pye. "Come back in four weeks. Sooner if there's any blood, any pain, or if she stops moving once she's started. You'll feel her moving early, I'd think. Twelve, fourteen weeks. Wolf babies are wrigglers." She went back around her desk and sat down and picked up her quill. Then she paused and looked at Harry again, and her face went serious.

"Lord Potter."

"Mm?"

"You've been looking at that door like someone's about to come through it since you walked in."

Harry's ears went flat.

"Nobody knows about this child but me and Hermione Granger-Weasley," said Healer Pye. "And now you two. I've kept quieter secrets than this one. I kept Remus Lupin's son a secret from Fenrir Greyback for six months, and I'd do it again." She looked at him over her glasses for a long moment. "Nobody's coming through that door. Sit down for once, before you knock over my Niffler."

Harry looked at the door. Then at Leah. Then at the tiny spindly wooden chair.

He sat down on the floor instead, next to the couch, with his back against it, so that his head was right next to Leah's shoulder, and Leah reached over without thinking about it and put her hand in the fur between his ears and left it there.

His tail thumped once against the floorboards. Then again. Then it settled into a slow, steady rhythm, like a heartbeat, and didn't stop.

It didn't stop the whole way home.


---

The moon came up over the mountains at ten to eleven that night, huge and low and the color of a peach.

Leah saw it from the kitchen window. The sky over Atlin Lake hadn't really gotten dark—it didn't, this close to the end of June, it just went a deep glowing blue and stayed that way—and the moon came lifting up out of the jagged black line of the peaks across the water and laid a long orange road down the middle of the lake, right to the end of their dock.

Harry was standing at the end of the dock.

He'd been out there for twenty minutes. He'd gone out after dinner—moose steaks, four of them, two each, and a whole bumbleberry pie that Darlene had sent on Sunday, eaten straight out of the tin—and he hadn't come back. Leah had done the dishes. She'd put the copy of the Expectant Glass picture, which Healer Pye had made for them in a little silver frame no bigger than a playing card, back on the mantel for the eleventh time, because Harry kept picking it up and carrying it around the house with him and putting it down in strange places. She'd found it on top of the toilet tank once and next to the coffee maker twice.

She took a blanket off the back of the couch and went out.

The night was cool and still. The lake was flat as glass. Somewhere off to the north a loon was calling, that long wavering cry that always made the hair on her arms stand up. The boards of the dock were damp under her bare feet. Harry stood at the very end with his back to her, barefoot too, in his sweatpants and nothing else, black fur gone silver at the edges where the moonlight caught it. His tail was going. It hadn't stopped all day. It swung slow and wide behind him, back and forth, back and forth.

She stopped a few feet behind him and waited.

He tipped his head back.

The howl came up out of him from somewhere very deep. It started low, so low she felt it in the boards under her feet before she heard it, and it climbed, and climbed, and kept climbing, long and full and wavering at the top, and it went out across the flat water toward the moon and hit the mountains on the far side and came back. And came back again, fainter. And again. Until it sounded like there were a dozen wolves out there in the dark across the lake, all answering.

Leah stood there with the blanket around her shoulders and goosebumps all the way down her back.

Down the shore, in the village, a dog started barking. Then another one. Then all of them, every dog in Atlin, howling and yipping and baying at once.

Harry lowered his head and listened to them, ears forward. Then he cupped both forepaws around his muzzle and yelled, at the top of his lungs, across the whole lake.

"*WE'RE HAVING A PUP!*"

It echoed off the mountains. *—a pup—a pup—a pup—*

Leah clapped her hand over her mouth.

"*WE'RE HAVING A PUP!*" Harry yelled again. "*WE'RE GONNA HAVE A FAMILY!*" He threw both arms up in the air. "*YOU HEAR THAT, ATLIN? A FAMILY! I'M GONNA BE A DAD!*"

Across the water, every dog in the village went completely nuts.

Leah was laughing so hard she had to sit down on the dock. Harry turned around at the sound of it, and saw her, and his whole face lit up. His ears went straight up. He was grinning so wide she could see every tooth in his head, all the way back.

"We're having a pup," he told her. Normal volume now. Like it was news.

"I heard," said Leah, wiping her eyes. "The whole Yukon heard. They heard you in Whitehorse."

"Good." He came down the dock and dropped onto the boards beside her, all long legs and tail, and pulled her and the blanket into his lap. His fur smelled like pine and cold water and the moose steaks. His heart was going fast. "I want them to hear. I want *everybody* to hear. I want to go back to that Healer's office and yell it in the Niffler's face."

"Leave the Niffler alone."

"I want to hire a skywriter."

"*Harry.*"

"I want to put it in the *Prophet*. Front page. 'Harry Potter to Become Father, Is Extremely Happy About It, Film at Eleven.'"

"You'd hate that. You'd hate everybody knowing."

He stopped. Some of the light went out of his face, and his ears tipped back, very slightly. "Ja," he said, after a moment. "Ja, I would."

Leah watched him look out at the moon. His tail had slowed. It was still moving, but slower now, a low lazy sweep across the wet boards.

"I didn't have a family," he said. "Growing up."

She knew that. She'd known it for a while, in bits and pieces. The cupboard. The aunt and uncle. He didn't talk about it much, and when he did it was always sideways, in jokes, so you had to put it together yourself.

"I had people I lived with," he said. "That's different. And then I got to school and I had Ron and Hermione, and the Weasleys, sort of, and my godfather for about five minutes." He was quiet for a second. "And then they started dying. Everyone I got. My godfather. My—a teacher I had. Friends. People who'd have been family, if they'd stuck around long enough. It was like a rule. Anything I got, somebody took." He looked down at her. At the blanket over her stomach. "And now there's this."

Leah didn't say anything. She put her hand over his forepaw where it lay on top of the blanket, over her stomach, and left it there.

"I'm gonna be so careful," Harry said, very quietly. "With both of you. You know that, right? I'm gonna be so careful."

"I know."

"Like, really careful. Annoyingly careful."

"Harry, you moved all the pans to the bottom cupboard. I know."

He huffed out a laugh. His tail picked up a little.

Leah sat in his lap and looked at the moon for a while. The dogs in the village had mostly given up. The loon called again. The orange road across the water had gone pale, almost white, as the moon climbed.

Then she stood up, and let the blanket fall, and pulled his flannel off over her head and dropped it on the dock.

"What are you—" Harry started.

She didn't answer. She walked to the end of the dock, and let the wolf come up through her, and was gray and huge and four-legged, standing with her claws dug into the wet boards and the cold night air in her fur. She looked back at him over her shoulder. Then she lifted her muzzle to the moon and howled.

It was a higher sound than his. Thinner. Sharper. It went up like a knife and hung there.

Behind her, Harry made a sound she'd never heard him make. Half laugh, half something else. Then he came and stood beside her at the end of the dock, his shoulder against her shoulder, and tipped his head back, and howled with her.

His voice went under hers, low and huge. Hers went over his, high and bright. Together they went out across the flat water and hit the mountains and came back, and came back, and came back, until it sounded like the whole world was full of wolves.

Down the shore, every dog in Atlin started up again.

They stood there and howled at the moon until they ran out of breath. Then they stood there a while longer, side by side, not howling, just breathing, watching the moon go small and white and high over the lake.

*We're having a pup,* Leah thought, alone and quiet inside her own head. *We're gonna have a family.*

Harry's tail was going against her flank, slow and steady.

It didn't stop all night.


---

Leah walked into the kitchen counter at seven the next morning and bounced off it.

She stood there in the doorway in his flannel, blinking, and put her hand out and pressed it against the edge of the counter. The edge gave under her palm like a sofa cushion. She pressed harder. It squished. She let go and it sprang back into shape, hard and square and solid-looking as ever.

She looked around the kitchen.

The corner of the table had a faint shimmer on it. So did the corners of the cupboards, and the edge of the woodstove, and the handle of the fridge, and the knobs on the stove. The knives had disappeared off the magnet strip over the sink. The fridge had a new piece of paper on it, held up by the moose magnet, that said *PROTEIN — RANKED* in Harry's square handwriting with a long list underneath. *1. Moose. 2. Elk. 3. Beef (raw OK per Healer Pye). 4. Venison.* It went down to forty-one. Forty-one was *Tofu (??? ask Hermione)*.

From the den, she could hear Harry on the mirror.

"—no, Gunnar, this is me *telling* you. Leave. Indefinite. As of today." A pause. "I don't care if it's not a thing. Make it a thing. You're the deputy head of Enforcement, you can make things things." Another pause. His voice went flat and dangerous. "Gunnar. I've taken one holiday in six years and I spent it in Peru tracking down that idiot with the cursed llamas. You owe me. Geneva owes me. Indefinite." A shorter pause. "Thank you. Ja. I'll send you a picture."

Leah went back to the doorway of the kitchen, and looked at the cushioned counter, and the protein list, and the empty knife strip, and closed her eyes.

It went on like that.

He followed her into the bathroom. He followed her out of the bathroom. When she went to get a glass of water he got it for her. When she went to get a second one, out of spite, he got that too, with ice and a slice of lemon, and asked if she wanted a straw. When she picked up the cast-iron skillet to make eggs, he took it out of her hand and made the eggs himself, and then a second plate she hadn't asked for, and then stood there with his tail going, watching her eat them, until she put her fork down and stared at him.

"What," she said.

"Nothing."

"You're watching me eat."

"I'm just making sure you're getting enough protein."

"I've eaten nine eggs, Harry."

"Pye said you'd need more."

"She didn't say *eleven*."

His ears went down a little. "Do you want toast? I can make toast."

By the third day, she'd had enough.

She went for a run at six in the morning, while he was in the shower. She stripped on the porch, phased in the trees, and ran up the shore of the lake through the spruce and the willow, and it felt so good she could have cried. Her own legs under her, her own lungs, nobody carrying anything for her or asking if she needed a straw. She ran three miles up the lake to the mouth of a creek and stopped there to drink, panting, with the sun coming up over the mountains.

She looked up from the creek.

About two hundred yards back along the shore, behind a big glacier-dropped boulder, something was trying very hard to be a boulder too.

It wasn't doing a good job. It was too big, for one thing. It was a wolf—a black wolf, a real wolf, four legs and all, the huge one he'd shown her for the first time back in December. Thirteen feet nose to tail. Six feet at the shoulder. It was lying flat behind the boulder with its belly pressed into the moss, which only made it about as tall as the boulder, and its black ears were sticking up over the top of the rock, and its tail was poking out one side, and the tail was wagging.

Leah stared at it.

The ears went flat. The tail went still.

Leah phased right there on the creek bank, and stood up naked on the cold rocks with her fists clenched, and yelled, "*HARRY JAMES POTTER*," so loud that a pair of mergansers took off off the creek in a panic.

The huge black wolf got up from behind the boulder, very slowly, and came padding up the shore toward her with its head low and its ears flat. It didn't make a sound walking. It never made a sound walking. When it got to her it sat down on the rocks in front of her, which put its head about two feet above hers, and looked down at her with big, guilty green eyes.

"You *followed* me," Leah said.

"I was jogging," said the wolf, in Harry's voice, low and clean despite the size of the jaws it was coming out of.

"You were *hiding behind a rock*."

"I was jogging slowly. Behind a rock."

"I'm *pregnant*, Harry! That's all! Women do it every single *day*! I've been a wolf for a year and a half and nothing's happened to me! I can run *three miles*! I can run *thirty* miles! I ran from *Washington to British Columbia*!" She was shaking. It was cold and she was naked and furious and she was shaking. "You moved the *knives*. You cushioned the *counters*. You called Geneva and quit your *job*. You won't let me lift a *frying pan*. You're following me around like I'm—like I'm gonna fall apart if you look away from me for five *seconds*—"

"I know," said Harry.

"—and I can't *breathe*, Harry, you're—"

"I know," Harry said again. And then, quieter: "I'm sorry."

She stopped.

He'd shifted while she was yelling. She hadn't even seen it happen. He was sitting on the rocks in front of her in his tall shape now, the one she liked, in his sweatpants, with his ears flat back against his skull and his tail curled tight around his own footpaws. His eyes were on the creek.

"I'm sorry," he said again. "I know I'm being a lot. I can feel myself doing it. I just can't—stop." He rubbed one forepaw down his muzzle. "I keep thinking about the last time I had something like this. Something this—" he made a vague, helpless gesture "—important. And every time, somebody came and took it."

Leah's anger went out of her all at once, like air out of a tire.

"You can look after yourself," Harry said. "I know that. You bit a blood mage. I know. But I'm—" He stopped. His throat moved. "I'm scared, Leah. That's all it is. I'm so happy and I'm so scared, and I don't know what to do with either one, so I'm moving the knives."

She stood there on the creek bank for a long moment, naked and shaking, looking at him.

Then she walked over and sat down in his lap.

He wrapped both arms around her right away, the way he always did, and pulled her in against his chest, and the heat of him soaked into her cold skin. She put her face in his neck.

"You can't follow me on runs," she said, muffled.

"Okay."

"You can't hide behind *boulders*, Harry."

"Okay."

"You can come *with* me. If you ask."

"Okay," said Harry, and she felt his tail start to move again, slow, against the rocks.

They sat there for a while. The mergansers came back and landed on the creek and paddled around, keeping an eye on them.

"Leah," Harry said eventually.

"Mm."

"Can I ask you something? And you can say no."

"You're gonna ask anyway."

"Ja." He took a breath. "I want to take you to England."

She lifted her head.

"There's a house," he said. "My family's house. The old one. Potter Manor. It's down in Devon, on the edge of Dartmoor. My grandparents lived there. My dad grew up there. It's been shut up since before I was born." His ears were half up, half down. "It's mine. I've never lived in it. Never even slept there. I went once, after the war. Walked around for an hour and left. It was too big and too empty and there was nobody in it but me." He was quiet a moment. "It's got wards on it that are six hundred years old. Older than Canada. It's on the moor—there's miles of open land all around it, heather and rock and nobody for miles, you could run for days. It's one Floo hop from Healer Pye instead of a Portkey at two in the morning. Ron and Hermione could pop round for dinner." He stopped. "And I could make it safe. Properly safe. Safer than anywhere in the world."

Leah looked at him.

"We don't have to," he said quickly. "I love this house. I love this lake. We'd keep it. We'd come back, summers. But I keep lying awake thinking about how there's nothing between this house and the road but a bit of spruce and a couple of wards I put up in an afternoon, and I just—"

"Okay," Leah said.

He stopped. "Okay?"

She'd been thinking about it, actually, without knowing she was, for days. The road outside. The boats on the lake in summer. The people in Atlin who knew her face now, who'd heard a man yell across the whole lake that he was going to be a dad.

She thought about her wolf, which had been doing something strange ever since the bathroom floor. Getting restless at night. Pacing. Checking the doors. Looking at the house, the way it sat open to the lake and the road, and not liking it. Wanting something.

Wanting a den.

"Okay," she said again. "Let's go to England."

Harry's ears shot straight up.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure. But I get to pick the bedroom. And you have to un-cushion the counters."

"Deal," Harry said, so fast it was almost one word, and his tail went *thump-thump-thump-thump* against the rocks, and the mergansers took off again.

---

They left nine days later.

Harry packed the house in an afternoon. Leah watched from the kitchen table, eating raw moose out of a bowl with a fork, because she'd stopped arguing with that particular craving. He went from room to room with his wand out—he didn't need it, but he said it helped him keep track—and things shrank and floated and folded themselves into a big old steamer trunk with *H. J. P.* stenciled on the lid. The records. The quilt off the bed. The framed letter from Geneva. The copy of the Expectant Glass picture, which he wrapped in a sock first and tucked into a corner where nothing could fall on it.

Sirius's motorbike went into the trunk too, shrunk down to the size of a toy. So did the woodstove, which Leah hadn't expected.

"It's a good woodstove," Harry said, when she looked at him.

The green Ford stayed in the garage, under a dust sheet. The house stayed, with a new set of wards on it and the furniture under sheets and the windows locked.

Leah took the moose magnet off the fridge herself, at the very end, and put it in the pocket of his flannel.

The last thing Harry did was disappear for half an hour on the morning they left. He came back with a crack, out of breath, holding a stack of six pie tins wrapped in tea towels and a folded note.

"Darlene," he said. "Had to tell her we were going."

"You told *Darlene*?"

"I told her we were moving overseas." He set the pies down on the empty counter. "She looked at me for about five seconds and said, 'She's expecting, isn't she.' I didn't say anything. She said, 'Thought so. She had the look.' And then she went in the back and came out with these."

He handed Leah the note.

It was written in blue ballpoint on the back of an order slip, in big loopy capitals.

*CONGRATULATIONS HON. YOU EAT FOR TWO NOW, NOT THAT YOU DIDN'T BEFORE. RECIPE IS ON THE BACK SO HE CAN QUIT STEALING MINE. NAME HER SOMETHING WITH A BACKBONE. — D.*

Leah turned it over. On the back, in smaller writing, was the recipe for Darlene's bumbleberry pie. Every step. Even the cardamom.

She sat there at the empty kitchen table and read it twice, and her eyes went hot, and she didn't know why.

"How'd she know it's a girl?" she said.

"No idea," said Harry. "I didn't tell her. She's Darlene."

Leah folded the note very carefully and put it in the pocket of the flannel, next to the moose magnet.

Then she put one finger on the ICW coffee cup, and wrapped her other hand in the front of Harry's coat, and he counted to three.


---

Potter Manor sat at the bottom of a long green fold in the moor, like something that had been dropped there a very long time ago and had decided to stay.

They came down to it on foot. The Portkey had put them out at the top of the combe, under a huge gray sky, on a hilltop of heather and big lichen-crusted rocks piled up like a giant had been playing with blocks. A *tor*, Harry called it. The wind smelled like wet grass and sheep and peat and rain that hadn't fallen yet. Three shaggy brown ponies looked up from the heather and stared at them, chewing. Somewhere very high overhead a bird was singing a long tumbling song that went on and on without stopping for breath.

"Skylark," said Harry.

"It's *loud*."

"It's showing off."

The house was down at the bottom. Leah could see it from the top of the hill: a long, low building made of the same gray stone as the tors, with steep slate roofs going green with moss, and three tall chimneys, and rows of tall narrow windows divided up into little diamond panes. A square stone tower stood at one end of it, not very tall, with a flat top and battlements like a castle. There was a stream running down past it, and a walled garden behind it, gone completely wild, and in the middle of the walled garden an oak tree so huge and so old that its lowest branches rested on the ground.

"Harry," Leah said.

"Mm."

"That's a castle."

"It's a house."

"It has a *tower*."

"It's a small tower." His ears went sideways. "My great-great-something grandfather built it in fifteen-something to keep an eye out for Spaniards. The Spanish never showed up. He was very disappointed, apparently. There's a portrait of him sulking about it."

They went down the hill. There was a track, mostly grown over, and a pair of stone gateposts at the bottom with no gate between them. Each one had a stone animal on top, weathered almost smooth. Leah thought they might once have been griffins. Or lions. Or very fat dogs.

Harry stopped between the gateposts.

"Hold on," he said. "This bit's a little weird."

He held out his forepaw, and she took it, and they walked forward between the gateposts.

The air changed.

She felt it on her skin first, all over, like walking into a room where the heat was on. Then deeper. Something old and heavy and patient came up out of the ground and the stone and the air and *looked* at her. That was the only word for it. It looked at her the way a dog looks at a stranger at the door, sniffing, deciding. She felt it go over her face, her hands, her chest. She felt it stop, low down, over her stomach.

It stayed there for a long moment.

And then it went quiet and settled, like the dog had sniffed her hand, and decided she was all right, and lain back down by the fire.

Leah let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

"It *knows* her," she said.

Harry was looking at her stomach, his ears straight up. "Ja," he said softly. "Ja, it does. She's a Potter. Wards are keyed to the blood." He swallowed. "I wasn't sure if it would. It's never let anyone through it but me. I tried to bring Ron once. It threw him into the stream."

"Harry."

"He was fine. He was wet."

---

The front door was oak, black with age, studded with iron, and taller than Harry. It swung open by itself before they reached it.

The hall inside was long and dim and smelled like dust and old wood smoke and lavender, very faintly, from somewhere. There was a stone floor, and a fireplace big enough to roast a cow in, and a staircase going up at the far end, and portraits. Portraits everywhere. Up the walls, along the landing, crowded frame to frame. Most of them were asleep. A few woke up as Harry came in, and blinked, and sat up straighter in their frames, and started whispering.

"—is that—"

"—it's the boy, it's Fleamont's grandson—"

"—good *heavens*, what's happened to his *face*—"

"—Euphemia, *Euphemia*, wake up, he's finally come home—"

Harry's ears went pink inside. He looked at the floor.

"Kreacher," he said.

There was a crack.

Something very small and very old was standing on the flagstones in front of them. It came up to about Leah's knee. It had skin like a crumpled paper bag and enormous bat-like ears with tufts of white hair growing out of them, and huge watery eyes the color of old dishwater, and a long drooping nose like a snout. It was wearing a tea towel, very clean, folded and tied like a toga, with a crest embroidered on it in black thread. Around its neck, on a chain, hung a heavy gold locket.

It bowed so low its nose touched the floor.

"Master Harry," it croaked. "Master has come to the Potter house at last. Kreacher has been waiting." It straightened up and looked around the hall, sniffing. "Kreacher came the moment Master called. Kreacher has not had time to clean. This house is filthy. Filthy and *neglected*." It said *neglected* in the same tone Leah's mom used to say *rez dogs*. "It will take Kreacher a week."

"Take a day," said Harry. "I'll help."

The elf looked at him with deep, wounded disgust. Then it turned its big watery eyes on Leah.

It looked at her for a long time. It sniffed. Its nose wrinkled.

"Master has brought a wolf," it said.

"Kreacher," said Harry, in a voice that had gone a lot lower.

"A wolf-woman," said Kreacher, sniffing again. "Kreacher smells it. Wolf, and salt, and cedar trees. A wild thing. Not a witch." It looked her up and down, from her muddy boots to his flannel shirt, with its upper lip curled. "Master's mother was a—"

"*Kreacher.*"

"—a *Muggle-born*," Kreacher finished, stiffly, "and Master's godfather married no one at all and was a disgrace to the Noble House, and Kreacher is only saying, Kreacher is only *wondering*—"

"This is Leah," Harry said. His voice had dropped all the way. The portraits had gone quiet. "Leah is carrying my daughter."

Kreacher stopped.

His enormous ears, which had been drooping, came straight up off the sides of his head like two sails catching wind. His watery eyes went wide. He looked at Leah's face. Then, slowly, down at her stomach. Then back up at Harry.

"An *heir*," he whispered.

"Ja."

"Master has an heir coming. An heir to the House of Potter. And to the Most Ancient and Noble House of *Black*." Kreacher's voice cracked on *Black*. His hand had gone to the gold locket at his throat and was clutching it. "Master Regulus's house will go on. Kreacher's *mistress's* house will go on." His enormous eyes had filled up with tears. He turned to Leah and bowed so low that his nose left a mark in the dust on the flagstones. "Kreacher begs the wolf-woman's pardon," he said, into the floor. "Kreacher spoke out of turn. Kreacher will iron his hands."

"Please don't iron your hands," said Leah, horrified.

"Kreacher will make the nursery," Kreacher said, straightening up, and wiping his eyes on the corner of his tea towel. "Kreacher will clean the whole house. Kreacher will clean it *today*. The heir will not come into a house with dust in it." He looked at Leah again, and his nose wrinkled, but differently this time. Considering. "The wolf-woman should sit down. The wolf-woman should eat. Kreacher will bring food. What does the wolf-woman eat?"

"Meat," said Leah. "Mostly. A lot of it. Raw's fine."

Kreacher looked at her with what she slowly realized was approval.

"*Raw*," he said. "Yes. Good. Strong. The old mistresses of the House of Black ate their beef bloody." He snapped his fingers. "Kreacher will bring the wolf-woman beef."

He vanished with a crack.

Leah stood in the dusty hall with her mouth open, looking at the place where he'd been.

"So that's Kreacher," Harry said.

"He was gonna say something about your mom."

"Ja. He's better than he used to be. Honestly." Harry's ears were still back. "He came with my godfather's house. He's about two hundred years old. He spent most of the war trying to sell me out, and then I gave him that locket, and now he'd die for me. It's complicated."

"He called me *the wolf-woman*."

"He'll come round. He's already coming round. He's bringing you raw beef. That's practically a marriage proposal, from Kreacher."

Leah looked at him sideways. His eyes were fixed on the portraits, very hard, and his ears were pink on the inside.

---

The wards took three days.

He'd warned her, but she still wasn't ready for it.

On the first day he walked the edges—a few hundred acres of moor and bog and woodland, out to a line of old boundary stones his ancestors had set up before the Spanish didn't come. Leah watched him from the terrace at the back of the house, with her feet up on a stone wall and a plate of Kreacher's raw beef in her lap. Every so often he'd stop by one of the stones and put his forepaw flat on it and stand there, perfectly still, while the air over the stone shimmered like heat over a road. Then he'd walk on to the next one.

Hermione came on the afternoon of the first day with a briefcase full of parchment covered in tiny numbers, and sat with Leah on the terrace for an hour, checking his work through a pair of strange brass spectacles with about six lenses each. She kept saying things like "Oh, that's clever," and "He's never going to explain how he did that, you know," and once, under her breath, "*Harry.*" When Leah asked her what was wrong, Hermione said nothing was wrong, it was only that he'd just made the ward recognize hostile intent, which was the sort of thing people wrote doctoral theses about, and then failed.

"What happens if someone with hostile intent tries to get in?" Leah asked.

Hermione looked at the parchment. Then at Leah. "As far as I can tell, they end up back wherever they started," she said. "As a ferret."

"A *ferret*?"

"It's a long story," said Hermione. "It's about a teacher we had."

On the second day he did the Fidelius Charm. That one Leah couldn't see at all. She just knew that one minute she was looking at the house from the top of the tor, and the next minute Harry was standing beside her with a little scrap of parchment, and he was saying, "Read this," and she read it, and it said *Potter Manor is in the Combe of Ashford Water, below Hound Tor*, in his handwriting, and the house that had been sitting at the bottom of the combe the whole time was suddenly sitting there again, like it had blinked back into existence. He gave a copy to Hermione. He wrote another for Ron and another for Healer Pye and sealed them with his own wand.

"Who's the Secret Keeper?" Leah asked.

"Me," said Harry.

"Is that allowed?"

"It is if you're me," said Harry.

On the afternoon of the second day, Leah asked for something.

They were sitting on the terrace. She'd been thinking about it all morning. She'd been thinking about it, actually, since the clearing on the ridge above Tamarack Crossing, and before that, since a newborn in a clearing outside Forks with red eyes and a scream like tearing metal.

"Can you do one for vampires?" she said.

Harry turned his head and looked at her. His ears came forward.

"There are vampires," she said. "Where I'm from. Real ones. Cold. Hard. Fast. In the sun they sparkle. It's disgusting. They smell like bleach and dead flowers. That's what we—" She stopped. She'd never said this much about home. She made herself keep going. "That's what we were for. What *I* was for. My people. That's why we turn into wolves. To kill them." She looked down at her stomach. "I want a ward for them. Something that knows what they are. I don't want one of those things anywhere near her. Ever."

Harry looked at her for a long time.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Okay." He got up. "Tell me everything about what they smell like."

She told him. He listened with his eyes closed and his nose working, like he was smelling it with her. Then he went out to the boundary stones again, all of them, one by one, and put his paw on every one. When he came back it was nearly dark.

"Anything cold and dead that smells like bleach and walks across that line," he said, dropping onto the bench beside her, "is going to have a very bad day."

"How bad?"

"Fire," said Harry. "A lot of fire."

Leah leaned her head against his shoulder. "Good."

On the third day he did it all again.

Every layer. Every stone. The hostile-intent ward, the vampire ward, the anti-Apparition ward, the ones against Dementors and Inferi and anything that came through the ground or the air or the water. All of it, built a different way, he said, so that anyone who found a way through the first set would walk straight into a second one that didn't work the same way at all.

Then, that evening, he did it a third time.

Leah watched him come in from the moor at ten o'clock at night with his fur full of heather and his ears drooping with tiredness. Kreacher had a plate of food waiting for him the size of a hubcap. He sat down at the long table in the kitchen and started eating.

"You warded it three times," Leah said.

"Ja," said Harry, with his mouth full.

"Three separate times."

"Ja."

"Why *three*?"

He swallowed. He thought about it.

"Four seemed excessive," he said.

Leah looked at him across the kitchen table for a long time.

At six o'clock the next morning, she woke up alone in the big carved bed in the room she'd picked, the one in the east wing with the bay window looking out over the moor. She got up and went to the window and looked out. The sun was coming up red over the tors, and the mist was lying in the bottom of the combe like milk in a bowl, and there, a long way off on top of Hound Tor, standing very still with one forepaw flat on the rock and his tail going slow and steady behind him, was Harry.

She opened the window.

"*That's FOUR*," she yelled.

His ears went flat. Even from half a mile away, she could see them go flat.

"*It's a SMALL one*," he yelled back.


---

He asked her on his birthday.

She didn't know it was his birthday until Ron told her. Ron and Hermione came through the kitchen fireplace at six o'clock on the last day of July in a burst of green flame, with Rose asleep on Ron's shoulder and a cake tin under Hermione's arm, and Ron said, "Happy birthday, you great hairy git," and Harry said, "Cheers," and Leah stood there by the stove with a raw steak in one hand and said, "*Your birthday?*"

"Didn't come up," said Harry.

"We've been together *ten months*."

"It's not a big deal."

"He's never liked his birthday," Hermione told her quietly, setting down the cake tin. "Let him off. It's a long story."

So Leah let him off, mostly. She hit him with a dish towel. Kreacher made treacle tart, which Ron told her was Harry's favorite, and Harry ate half of it by himself, and then ate half of the cake Hermione had brought, and Leah ate the other half of the cake and most of a side of beef, and Ron watched the two of them eat with his mouth hanging open and said, "Blimey, it's like feeding time at the Ministry menagerie."

Rose woke up halfway through dinner and climbed down off Ron's lap and toddled under the table and grabbed Harry's tail with both fists, and Harry let her hang off it, swinging her gently back and forth while she shrieked with laughter, and didn't miss a bite.

After dinner he took Leah out to the walled garden.

It was dusk. The sky over the moor had gone pink and gold at the edges and deep blue overhead, and the first stars were out. The garden had come a long way in two weeks, because Kreacher had been at it; the paths were clear now, the roses cut back, the long grass under the oak mown short and smelling green. The oak stood in the middle of it all, so wide and old and low-branched that standing under it was like standing inside a green room. Somebody—Kreacher—had hung little lanterns in the lowest branches, and they were glowing.

Harry stopped under the oak and turned around. His ears were back. His tail had gone still.

Leah's stomach dropped.

"What," she said. "What's wrong? Is it the baby? Did Pye send—"

"Nothing's wrong." He took a breath. "Nothing's wrong. Hang on."

He knelt down.

At his height, kneeling put his eyes exactly level with hers. He reached into his coat with one forepaw and brought out a little box, square, black, worn soft at the corners like it had been carried around for a long time.

Leah stopped breathing.

"I bought this in February," Harry said. "In Whitehorse. The day after the Mongolia job. There's a jeweler on Main Street, an old guy, I walked past his window and saw it and went in and bought it before I could think about it. And I've had it in my coat ever since. Five months." He opened the box.

It was a ring. A simple band of white gold, with a stone set into it. The stone was gray. A soft, smoky, pale gray, like fog on the lake—and then he tilted it in the lantern light, and the gray flashed suddenly blue and green and gold all at once, like the northern lights going off inside it.

"Labradorite," Harry said. "The old guy said that. I didn't know what it was. I just saw it and thought, that's her color. That's what she looks like running."

Leah stared at it.

"I kept waiting for the right time," he said. "Then you threw up in June, and I thought—if I ask now she'll think it's because of the pup. So I waited some more." His ears tipped forward. "I'm done waiting. I don't care what you think it's because of. It's because of you. I want to marry you, Leah. I want you to marry me. Will you?"

Leah opened her mouth.

Nothing came out. She closed it.

She thought about a clearing above a black lake, and a wolf in a Led Zeppelin T-shirt asking if she wanted to get a drink. She thought about a bar with a moose head and a basket of wings and *okay*. She thought about a bathroom floor in Atlin. A dock under a peach-colored moon. A man hiding behind a boulder, very badly, with his tail sticking out.

She thought about Sam, once, a long time ago, telling her forever. She'd believed him. She'd been sixteen.

She thought: *I'm choosing this one. Me. Nobody's making me.*

"Yes," she said.

It came out a croak. She cleared her throat.

"Yes. Obviously yes. You idiot. You've had that in your coat for *five months*?"

"Ja."

"I *wash* that coat, Harry."

"I moved it around. Different pockets. It was very stressful."

She laughed. She was crying, and she laughed, and he took the ring out of the box with two careful claws and slid it onto her finger, and it fit exactly, and the stone flashed green and gold in the lantern light. She grabbed two fistfuls of the ruff at his throat and kissed him so hard he rocked back on his heels.

When she let go his ears were straight up and his tail had started again, so hard it was whipping the grass flat.

"Okay," he said, breathless. "Okay. Good. Okay. Can I ask you something else?"

"What?"

"Marry me tonight."

Leah blinked.

"Tonight," Harry said. "Now. Here. Hermione's licensed to witness a bonding—she does it at the Ministry for people in her department. Ron's here. Kreacher's here. The house is right here." He swallowed. "You said you didn't want to wait for anything ever again. On the bathroom floor. You said it."

She had said that.

She looked back at the house. The lights were on in the kitchen windows. She could see Hermione standing at one of them with her hands pressed over her mouth. Ron beside her, holding Rose, grinning. Down at the bottom of the window, just his ears and his enormous eyes showing over the sill, Kreacher.

"They knew," Leah said.

"Hermione guessed. Ron bet her two Galleons you'd hit me."

"I still might."

"That's fair," said Harry.

Leah looked at the ring on her finger, gray and flashing. She looked at him, kneeling on the grass in front of her, with his ears up and his tail going and his whole face wide open.

"Okay," she said. "Tonight."

---

They did it under the oak, by lantern light, with the stars coming out over the moor.

Leah was barefoot on the grass in a green sundress Hermione had transfigured for her out of a tablecloth in about four seconds, with his flannel thrown over it because the night was getting cold. Harry wore his long black coat, buttoned all the way up, and had spent ten minutes in the downstairs bathroom trying to flatten the fur on top of his head with water, and given up. Ron stood off to one side with Rose asleep on his shoulder again, as witness for the groom. Kreacher stood on the other side in a fresh tea towel, as witness for the Most Ancient and Noble House of Black, weeping steadily and silently into a handkerchief.

Hermione stood in front of them with her wand out and her eyes shining.

"It's very simple," she said. "It's an old bonding, older than the Ministry. The magic does most of the work. You just have to mean it."

"I mean it," said Harry, immediately.

"I know you do, Harry. Hold hands."

Leah put her hand in his forepaw. The pads were rough and cool. His claws curled very carefully around her fingers.

Hermione touched her wand to their joined hands. A thread of gold light spun out of the tip of it and wound around their wrists, once, twice, three times, and stayed there, glowing.

"Say whatever you want to say," said Hermione. "Then say you take each other. That's all."

Harry went first. He looked down at their hands for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"I didn't have a family," he said. "For a long time. Then I had friends who were basically family. And then I lost a lot of people." He glanced at Ron. Ron nodded, once, very slightly. "And then I was sitting in a diner in Tamarack Crossing waiting to catch a blood mage, and you walked in and sat in the back booth and ate six eggs with your back to the wall, and I thought, huh." His ears went pink. "I'm gonna keep you safe. Both of you. I'm gonna make you pancakes every Sunday. I'm gonna let you pick the music sometimes. I'm never gonna hide behind a boulder again." A pause. "I take you, Leah. That's it. That's the whole thing. I take you."

The gold thread pulsed.

Leah looked at their hands. Her throat was tight.

"You showed up with pie," she said. "And then you ate the pie. And then you've been feeding me ever since." Ron snorted. Hermione elbowed him. "You moved all the knives. You cushioned the counters. You warded a castle four times—"

"Three and a small one—"

"*Four times*, Harry. You're a lot. You're so much." Her voice cracked. "I'm gonna yell at you constantly. I'm gonna steal all your shirts. I'm gonna make you un-cushion stuff." She took a breath. "I'm staying. I picked you. Nobody made me. I take you, Harry."

The gold thread blazed.

It flared up bright as the sun for one second, so bright Leah had to squint, and she felt it go *into* her—up her arm, into her chest, down into her stomach where the pup was—and something old and huge and patient, the same something that had looked at her between the gateposts, came up out of the ground under the oak and looked at her again, and this time, instead of settling, it *bowed*.

Every window in the manor lit up at once. All of them. Every single diamond pane, from the kitchen to the top of the tower, blazing gold in the dark.

The oak leaves overhead rustled, all together, though there wasn't any wind.

And then it was over, and the gold thread had sunk into their wrists and disappeared, and there was nothing left but a faint shimmer on Leah's skin, like the ghost of a bracelet, and Harry's forepaw shaking very slightly around her hand.

Hermione lowered her wand. Tears were running down her face.

"That's it," she said. "That's done. You're married. Congratulations, Lady Potter-Black."

Leah turned and looked at her.

"Lady *what*?"

"Potter-Black," Harry said. His tail had started up again, so hard it was whipping his coat around his legs. "My godfather left me the Black title. Lord Black, on paper. So you're both. Potter-Black." His ears went sideways. "Sorry. Should've mentioned. It's a lot of name."

*Black.*

Something went cold and quick down the middle of Leah's back.

She saw, for half a second, a little red house on the rez. A wheelchair ramp up to the porch. A name on a mailbox, hand-painted, flaking. *BLACK.* Billy on the porch with a blanket over his knees. Jacob in the garage with his hands in an engine.

She shut it. She shut it the way she'd been shutting it for ten months, like a door.

It was a common name. It was just a name.

"It's fine," she said. "It's a lot of name. I'll live."

"*Mistress*," Kreacher sobbed, and flung himself at her ankles.

"Okay," said Leah. "Okay, Kreacher. Don't call me Mistress."

"*Lady Potter-Black*," Kreacher sobbed, into her shin.

"That's worse."

Ron came over and clapped Harry on the back so hard it would have knocked anyone else down, with Rose still asleep on his other shoulder. "Mate," he said. "Mate. You're *married*." He looked down at Harry's tail. "That's going to fall off, you know. It hasn't stopped since June."

"Don't care," said Harry. He hadn't let go of Leah's hand. He was looking down at her with his ears straight up and his teeth showing, grinning so wide it looked like it hurt. "Let it."

Leah looked up at him. At the faint gold shimmer on her wrist, and the gray stone on her finger, and her husband's tail going like a windshield wiper in the lantern light.

Her wolf rolled over and purred.

"Lady Potter-Black," she said, testing it.

"Lady Potter-Black," said Harry.

"That's so stupid."

"It's very stupid," he agreed, and kissed her, and every window in the house was still blazing gold behind them.


---

The door to the tower opened for her the next morning.

Leah hadn't known it was locked. But Kreacher came and found her at breakfast, where she was eating eggs with Hermione and Ron and Rose in the kitchen while Harry made a second round of pancakes, and tugged at the hem of her flannel.

"The Lady's rooms," he said. "Kreacher felt them open in the night. When the bonding was made. They have been shut since Mistress Euphemia died. They have not opened for anyone." His enormous eyes slid sideways to Harry, at the stove. "Not even for Master."

"Tried 'em once," said Harry, without turning around. "Got a nasty shock off the doorknob."

So after breakfast they all went up the narrow winding tower stairs, Harry right behind Leah with one forepaw hovering an inch from the small of her back on every step. At the top was a round wooden door with an iron ring for a handle, standing open about an inch.

Inside was a round room with windows on every side, looking out over the moor in every direction. It had a window seat with faded green cushions, and a writing desk, and a fireplace, and a big carved wooden cabinet against the wall between two of the windows. It smelled of dust and old lavender. The cabinet had a keyhole, but no key.

"Touch it," said Kreacher. "Lady should touch it."

Leah put her hand flat on the cabinet door.

Something clicked inside it. The doors swung open.

There were shelves inside, and drawers, and little velvet-lined compartments. Most of them were empty. But on the middle shelf, laid out on dark green velvet that hadn't faded at all, there were things.

A ring. Heavy gold, plain and old, with a single square green stone set flat into it, and something engraved on the inside of the band.

A necklace. A long fine silver chain, with a pendant on the end of it shaped like a teardrop, made of some milky pale stone that had light moving around inside it, very faintly, like a candle behind fog.

A shawl, folded small. Gray-green wool, so fine it looked like smoke.

And, on the shelf below, a silver baby's rattle, tarnished black, and a little book with a cracked leather cover.

Leah stood looking at them.

"Those are the Lady's things," said Kreacher, in a hushed voice. "Kreacher's cousin served the Potters when Kreacher was young. Kreacher has heard of them. The Lady's ring. The Watcher. The Weaver's Shawl." He wrung his hands. "They have been waiting."

"Go on," said Harry quietly, behind her.

She picked up the ring first.

It was heavier than it looked. The inside of the band was engraved with tiny letters she couldn't read, old and curly, and a crest—a little shield with something on it that might have been a griffin. When she slid it onto her right hand, onto the finger opposite the labradorite, it was too big. It hung loose around her knuckle.

Then it tightened. She felt it shrink, smooth and quick, until it fit her finger exactly. It went hot, so hot she nearly pulled it off, and then the heat went *into* her, up her arm, and spread out, and settled.

And something in her head went quiet.

It was a strange feeling. Like a window closing in a room she hadn't known was drafty. Like a lock turning. She stood very still with her hand held out in front of her, staring at the ring.

"What did it just do," she said.

"It's the Lady Potter's ring," said Harry. He'd come up beside her. His ears were straight up. "The wards know you now, all of them, every one I put up. Kreacher has to do what you say. And it guards you. Poisons, potions, curses on the mind." He hesitated. "Compulsions. Anything that tries to get into your head and make you do something. Read your thoughts. Anything like that. Nobody gets in while you're wearing it."

Leah turned and looked at him.

"Nobody," she said.

"Nobody."

"Not even—" She stopped. She meant *Sam*. She meant *an Alpha*. She meant a year of other people's voices inside her skull, and a hook in her gut she couldn't say no to, and Kayleigh walking barefoot up a mountain humming a song that wasn't hers.

"Nobody," Harry said again, gently. "Not even me, Leah. Ever."

She looked down at the ring for a long time. She closed her hand around it.

"Okay," she said. Her voice wobbled. "Okay. Good. I'm keeping that."

"It's yours," said Harry. "It's always going to be yours."

She picked up the necklace next. The chain was cool and slippery, and when she lifted it the light inside the teardrop stone stirred, like it had woken up. She fastened it around her neck. The pendant settled against her breastbone.

It pulsed.

Twice. Two soft flickers of light inside the milky stone, one bigger and slower, one small and quick. And then again. Big-slow, small-quick. Big-slow, small-quick.

Leah put her hand over it.

"That's—" she said.

"That's you," said Harry. His voice had gone rough. "And that's her."

Something chimed in his coat. He pulled out a gold pocket watch—an old one, battered, she'd seen him carry it before and never seen him open it—and flipped it open. Inside, where the watch face should have been, there were two tiny points of light, pulsing. Big-slow, small-quick. Exactly in time with the stone around her neck.

Harry stared at it. His tail had stopped.

"I've had this since I was seventeen," he said. "It was my grandfather's. Came out of the family vault on my birthday. I always thought it was broken."

Hermione had come up on Leah's other side and was leaning in close to the necklace, peering at it through a pair of the strange brass spectacles. She made a small fascinated noise.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, it's a smart necklace."

"A what?" said Ron.

"A smart necklace. Like a—oh, never mind, you wouldn't know, it's a Muggle thing. It *monitors* her." Hermione was turning the spectacles' lenses, one after another. "Heartbeats, both of them. Temperature. There's a shield charm built in that triggers on its own, quite a strong one. A poison detector. And there's a Portkey in it, keyed to—" she squinted "—the hearth downstairs, I think. Emergency only. And it reports to the watch. Everything it knows, the watch knows." She lowered the spectacles and looked at Harry. "Your great-great-grandmother, or somebody, made a baby monitor. Six or seven hundred years ago."

"Iolanthe," said Kreacher reverently. "Lady Iolanthe made the Watcher. When she was carrying her first. Kreacher's cousin told him."

"Iolanthe Peverell," said Hermione, very quietly, and gave Harry a look Leah didn't understand.

Leah reached up to the shelf to get the shawl.

The necklace tightened around her neck. A little squeeze, like a hand on her arm.

She froze.

"Did it just—"

"I think it doesn't want you reaching over your head," said Hermione, delighted.

"I didn't enchant it to do that," said Harry quickly. "I swear. I've never touched it."

Leah lowered her arm, slowly, and looked at him. The necklace loosened. Harry reached up and got the shawl down and handed it to her.

"Unbelievable," said Leah. "*Unbelievable*. It's on your side. The jewelry's on your side."

"I think the jewelry's on *her* side," said Harry, and pointed at her stomach, and his tail started going again.

The shawl, when she shook it out and put it over her shoulders, felt like nothing at all. Like air. And the heat that had been sitting under her skin for a month, the extra heat the pup was making, the heat that had her sleeping on top of the covers with every window in the house open—it eased back to where it had been before June. Comfortable. She stood there in the middle of the round room in Harry's flannel and a centuries-old shawl and a ring and a necklace, and let out a long, long breath.

"Oh, that's nice," she said. "Oh, I'm never taking this off."

"Weaver's Shawl," said Kreacher. "Keeps the Lady at the right heat. Lady Potter-Black runs hot, Kreacher has noticed."

"Kreacher," said Leah, "you might be my favorite person in this house."

Kreacher went the color of a beetroot and vanished with a crack.

Harry picked up the little silver rattle off the lower shelf. It was black with tarnish. He turned it over in his forepaw, very gently. When he shook it, it didn't rattle. It made a sound like somebody far away humming a lullaby, a few notes, soft and sweet. He stood there and shook it again, and listened to it, with his ears forward and his eyes gone somewhere far off.

Then he put it down very carefully on the window seat, and picked up the little leather book, and opened it.

"Recipes," he said, after a second, sounding strange. "It's—Euphemia Potter. My grandmother. It's her handwriting." He turned a page. "*Treacle tart for Fleamont, who is impossible.*" He turned another. "*Bramble jam. James likes it on everything.*"

He stopped talking.

Leah took the book out of his paws and put it down on the window seat next to the rattle. Then she put her arms around his middle, as far as they'd go, and held on, and the necklace pulsed against his chest where she was pressed against him, *big-slow, small-quick*. In his coat pocket, the watch pulsed with it.

On the first Sunday in September, Hermione came through the kitchen fireplace at Potter Manor with Ron behind her and Rose on her hip and a canvas bag full of books over her shoulder, and found that she'd been beaten to it.

Harry was in the library. Kreacher told her so, pointing with a long finger, so that she wouldn't have to go looking. She found him on the big sofa in front of the library fire, in his tall shape, in a gray jumper with a hole worn through one elbow, with Leah stretched out along the sofa beside him and her bare feet in his lap. He was rubbing her feet with one forepaw. In the other, held up close to his muzzle, he had a fat paperback with a pink-and-white cover: *What to Expect When You're Expecting*. It bristled with little colored paper tabs. There were notes in the margins in his square handwriting.

On the low table in front of the sofa there were more. *Dr. Spock's Baby and Child Care.* *The Magical Mother's Companion.* *Accidental Magic in the Under-Fives.* *Beast-Blooded Babies: A Practical Guide for Parents*, by a Healer M. Pye. *So Your Child Has a Tail*, which was very thin and looked privately printed. All of them tabbed and annotated.

Hermione looked at the table. Then at the bag on her shoulder. Then at Harry.

"I brought you some books," she said.

"Oh, brilliant," said Harry, without looking up. "Put them on the pile. Did you know a baby can hear sounds from outside the womb by twenty weeks? She can hear us talking. Right now. She knows our voices."

"Yes," said Hermione, who had told him that in June.

"So I've been reading to her." His tail, hanging off the end of the sofa, was going in a slow, steady sweep across the rug. It had been going in a slow, steady sweep across something every single time Hermione had seen him since the end of June. "Leah says it's weird. I say it's bonding."

"It's weird," said Leah, from the other end of the sofa, with her eyes closed.

She looked well. Hermione made a careful survey, the way she always did now when she saw her. Leah was eighteen weeks along by the calendar, though Healer Pye said the pup was further on than that, and it showed—a round, high, unmistakable curve under the old red-and-black flannel she still wore nearly every day. The gray-green shawl was around her shoulders. The pale stone of the necklace lay against her breastbone, pulsing faintly, two lights. Her face had filled out a little. She'd stopped looking quite so much like something that might bite you.

"Who's Spock?" Ron said, picking up the book off the table and frowning at it.

"A doctor," said Hermione.

"With pointy ears?"

"*Not* that one, Ron."

Rose had squirmed down off Hermione's hip. She made a beeline across the library carpet for the sofa, for the long black tail sweeping back and forth across the rug, and grabbed it with both fists and sat down hard, and rode it. Harry didn't even look round. He just slowed the tail down a little, so she could hold on, and kept reading, and Rose screamed with laughter.

Ron dropped into an armchair and put his feet up. "Your tail's going to fall off," he said.

"So you keep saying."

"I mean it this time. It hasn't stopped in two and a half months, mate. It's going to wear out."

"Don't care," said Harry. "Let it."

Hermione sat down in the other armchair with her bag of now-unnecessary books at her feet and watched them.

She watched Harry finish rubbing one of Leah's feet and start on the other without being asked. She watched Leah say "I'm hungry," without opening her eyes, and Harry say "Kreacher," and Kreacher appear with a plate of raw beef cut into neat cubes and a slice of bumbleberry pie, made from the recipe card propped on the kitchen windowsill, and a glass of milk. She watched Leah sit up halfway and eat all three, and Harry tuck a cushion behind her back while she did it, and adjust the shawl, and check the watch in his pocket, where two lights were pulsing. She watched Leah snap at him—"Harry, I'm *fine*, stop *fussing*"—and Harry say "Okay," and stop fussing for about forty seconds, and then start fussing again, and Leah roll her eyes and let him.

Four years ago Hermione had sat beside a hospital bed in Geneva while healers told Harry to put his affairs in order before the full moon. She'd watched him go into a warded cell to die, or worse. She'd watched him come out of it the next morning at six-foot-seven, with fur, and his own green eyes, and make a joke about needing new trousers.

She'd never seen his tail go like this. Not once, in four years.

"Hermione," said Leah. She'd opened her eyes. "Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"What's it like? The actual—" She waved a hand. "When she comes out. Harry's read all the books. He won't tell me. He gets this look."

"I don't get a look," said Harry.

"You get a look."

Hermione thought about it. She thought about Rose, twenty-two hours of it, and Ron fainting twice, and Healer Pye knitting in the corner.

"It's awful," she said honestly. "And then it's the best thing that's ever happened to you. Both. At the same time." She hesitated. Then, before she could think better of it: "I'll be able to tell you more in February."

There was a pause.

Leah's eyes went wide. Harry looked up from the book for the first time since Hermione had come in. His ears shot straight up.

"*February*?" he said.

Across the room, Ron put his face in his hands. "We weren't telling people yet," he said through his fingers. "We *agreed*."

"I know," said Hermione. "Sorry. It slipped out." She was smiling. She couldn't stop. "Seventeen weeks. Healer Pye says it's a boy."

Harry dropped *What to Expect When You're Expecting* on the floor. Leah let out a whoop. Rose, still riding the tail, shrieked along with her for no reason at all.

"Hermione," said Harry. "*Hermione.* You were *pregnant* in June? When I called you? In the *broom cupboard*?"

"Six weeks," said Hermione. "I hadn't told anyone. I hadn't even told Ron."

"You told *me* first?"

"Technically you told *me* first. I just didn't tell you back."

Harry looked at her with his mouth open and his ears straight up. Then his tail started going so fast that Rose went flying off it and landed on the rug, giggling, and Ron got up to retrieve her, and Harry was off the sofa and across the library and had picked Hermione up out of her armchair in a hug that lifted her feet off the floor.

"Put me down," said Hermione, laughing. "Harry. Put me *down*, I'm pregnant—"

"*Everybody's* pregnant," said Harry, and put her down very, very carefully.

And then Leah gasped.

It was a small sharp sound. Everyone turned. She was sitting bolt upright on the sofa with both hands pressed to the top of her stomach, and her eyes had gone huge.

"Leah?" Harry was back across the room in about half a second. "Leah, what's—is it—"

"Shut up," said Leah. "Shut up. Give me your—*give me your paw*."

She grabbed his forepaw and pressed it flat against the curve of her stomach, low and to the left, and held it there.

Harry went completely still.

Hermione watched his face. She watched him wait. Five seconds. Ten. And then she watched something happen under the pads of his paw, a small hard nudge she could almost see from across the room through the flannel, and she watched his ears go straight up and his eyes go wide and wet and his mouth come open, and his tail stop dead.

Hermione had seen that tail stop maybe twice since June. It stopped now.

"Was that—" he whispered.

"That was her," said Leah. Her voice was shaking. "She's been fluttering for weeks. That's the first time she's *kicked*. Like, really kicked. She kicked when you were *yelling*, Harry."

"She heard me."

"She heard you."

"She knows my voice."

"Apparently."

Harry knelt down on the library floor beside the sofa, with his paw still on Leah's stomach, and put his head down against the curve of it, ear first, very gently. He stayed there. Leah put her hand on the back of his head, in the fur between his ears.

The nudge came again. Hermione saw his whole body jump.

"Hi," Harry said, very quietly, against Leah's stomach. "Hi. Hi, pup. It's Dad. I'm right here."

Ron came and stood beside Hermione's chair with Rose on his hip and put his free hand on her shoulder. Neither of them said anything.

Behind Harry, slowly, the tail started up again.

The owl came on a Tuesday in October, while Harry was asleep.

Leah was up in the tower room. It was hers now. She'd moved her anatomy textbooks up there, and the copy of the Expectant Glass picture, and the moose magnet, which she'd stuck to the iron ring on the inside of the door. She couldn't run on the moor anymore; she'd stopped phasing at twenty weeks like Healer Pye said, and she missed it so much some nights it was like an ache in her teeth. But she could sit in the window seat in the shawl and watch the mist burn off the tors, and that was something.

The owl was a big tawny one with an ICW band on its leg. It tapped on the east window with its beak, and when she let it in it dropped an envelope in her lap, took a piece of bacon off her plate without asking, and left.

The envelope said *L. POTTER-BLACK — CONTRACT TRACKER (ON LEAVE)* in green ink.

Everybody on the tracker roster got the circulars, on leave or not. Mostly she skimmed them and dropped them in the basket for Kreacher to light the fires with. Harry didn't read his at all anymore. He said he'd read them again when the pup was old enough to tell him to go back to work.

She opened this one because she was bored.

*ICW ENFORCEMENT — NORTH AMERICAN REGIONAL OFFICE*
*ADVISORY — CLASS C — FOR INFORMATION*
*REGION: OLYMPIC PENINSULA, WASHINGTON STATE, U.S.A.*

Leah stopped breathing.

*Continued elevation in activity across the region noted since late summer. Transient vampire presence assessed as increased; three unexplained Muggle deaths in the Port Angeles area since August, attributed by local authorities to animal attack. Further reports of exceptionally large wolves in the vicinity of the Quileute reservation at La Push; estimated numbers now in the high teens, up significantly from previous assessments. Local shapeshifter population appears to be expanding rapidly. Cause unknown. No ICW action authorized at this time. Personnel with regional knowledge are invited to submit observations to the Seattle office.*

Clipped to the back of it, somebody had attached a cutting from a Muggle newspaper. The *Peninsula Daily News*. A grainy photo of a logging road in the rain, and a headline.

*HIKERS REPORT 'HORSE-SIZED' WOLVES NEAR LAKE CRESCENT. TRIBAL OFFICIALS DECLINE COMMENT.*

Leah read the advisory three times. Then she put it down on the window seat beside her and sat there and looked out at the moor without seeing it.

*High teens.*

When she'd left, there'd been ten of them. Sam, Jared, Paul, Embry, Quil, Jacob, Seth, Collin, Brady. Her. Collin and Brady had been fourteen. If there were eighteen or nineteen now, it meant eight or nine more kids had phased in a year. Kids. Twelve-year-olds, maybe. Thirteen. Some poor kid running a fever of a hundred and eight in his mom's kitchen and then exploding through the back wall.

It meant something had been there. Something cold. A lot of somethings. That was what made them phase. That was the only thing that made them phase.

*Three unexplained deaths in the Port Angeles area.*

Seth would be sixteen now. Mom would be—

She stopped herself. She knew how to stop herself. She'd been doing it for thirteen months. She put the door back where it went, and leaned on it.

It didn't stay shut.

She thought about Mom. Sue Clearwater, asleep on her side of the bed in the dark, curled toward the empty half. Mom didn't know she was married. Mom didn't know she was pregnant. Mom didn't know that in a month or so she was going to have a granddaughter with gray at the tips of her fur and a blaze down her face the exact color of Leah's own wolf.

She thought about Seth, stupid happy Seth, sixteen and gangly and running around with vampires because he thought they were his friends.

The pup kicked. Hard, under her ribs, like she was annoyed about something.

Leah put her hand on the spot. Under her palm, the pup kicked again. Against her breastbone, the necklace pulsed: *big-slow, small-quick*. Somewhere downstairs, in Harry's pocket, the watch would be pulsing too.

She'd told herself she'd open the door when she was ready. Whenever that was.

She looked down at herself. At the ring on her right hand, the one that meant nobody would ever get into her head again unless she let them. At the gray stone on her left hand, flashing green and gold in the morning light. At her own round stomach under her husband's flannel.

She thought about her husband, who had walked across a clearing through the Killing Curse like it was a parking lot. Who'd warded a whole valley four times. Who'd put a ward on every boundary stone of this place that would set any leech that crossed it on fire.

At nineteen she'd run north alone with a dry bag tied to her leg. Now she had a husband, and a house that bowed when she walked into it, and a pup kicking under her ribs.

Leah picked up the advisory and got up out of the window seat, slowly, one hand on the small of her back the way pregnant women did in movies, which she'd sworn she'd never do, and went down the winding tower stairs.

Harry was asleep on the big sofa in the library, in front of the fire, on his back. He had *Beast-Blooded Babies: A Practical Guide for Parents* open face-down on his chest, rising and falling. One ear was flopped over. His tail hung off the edge of the sofa, and even asleep it was going—slow and steady, back and forth across the rug, the way it had been going since June.

Leah stood in the doorway and looked at him for a long time, with the advisory in her hand and the pup kicking under her ribs.

*Maybe*, she thought. *Maybe we should go and take a look.*

Just a look. From a distance. Nobody would even have to know they were there.

She looked down at the paper. *Cause unknown.*

Harry's tail thumped against the rug, once, in his sleep.

Leah went and sat down on the edge of the sofa beside him, and put her hand on his chest, on top of the book, and waited for him to wake up.
