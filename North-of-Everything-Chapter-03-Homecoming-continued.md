# North of Everything

## Chapter Three: Homecoming (continued)

---

The rain came in under the porch roof the second the door swung open, a cold gray sheet of it, and it hit Leah full in the face.

It smelled like everything at once. Cedar and spruce. Wet gravel. Salt and rotting kelp off First Beach, a mile west through the trees. Woodsmoke from the chimney over her head. And wolf. Wolf in every breath, from every direction, layered so thick she could taste it at the back of her throat—musk and wet fur and the hot electric smell of bodies running a hundred and eight, a dozen different versions of it, some she'd have known blind and some she'd never smelled in her life.

Harry went out first.

He didn't put the glamour back on. He'd dropped it in the kitchen for her mom, and he walked out onto the porch without picking it up again: six-foot-seven of black fur in a long black coat, wet already at the shoulders, ears up and turning, tail hanging straight down behind him and not moving at all. The Christmas lights strung along the porch rail put red and green and blue and orange across the side of his muzzle. He stopped at the top of the steps and stayed there.

Leah came out and stood at his left side. The top of her head came about to his shoulder.

Down in the drive, Sam and Jacob had stopped walking.

They'd come up as far as the front bumper of her mom's Subaru, maybe twenty feet from the bottom of the steps, and they'd both stopped dead when the door opened, the way you'd stop on a trail when something big stepped out of the brush in front of you. Rain ran off both of them. Neither of them seemed to notice it. Sam had on a pair of cutoff sweats and nothing else; so did Jacob. The colored lights shone on the water running down their chests.

Behind them, in the dark, there were eyes.

Leah saw them all at once, the way you'd see stars come out when your eyes got used to the night. Pairs of them, low to the ground and high, in the alders along the edge of the yard and back in the spruce behind the woodpile and out along the dirt road beyond the drive, catching the Christmas lights and throwing them back: green, gold, a flat silver shine. Shapes behind the eyes. Big ones. One of them shifted its weight and a branch cracked under it like a pistol shot.

She knew some of them. She didn't have to see them. Jared, brown with that dark mask around his eyes, at the corner of the woodpile; she'd smelled him on the road in. Embry out by the mailbox, gray with the dark spots down his back. Quil beside him, because Quil was always beside Embry. Paul—she'd know Paul's stink anywhere—at the edge of the alders closest to the house, so close she could hear him breathing, a low thick rumble running under every breath like an engine at idle.

The rest were strangers. Kids. They smelled like kids—sweat and cafeteria pizza and drugstore body spray, under the wolf. One of them, way back at the edge of the alders, smelled so strongly of acne cream it made her eyes water.

*Somebody's little brother*, she thought, and her stomach turned over.

"Leah," said Jacob.

He said it like somebody had punched him. He was staring up at her from the bottom of the steps with the rain running into his eyes. He'd cut his hair short since she'd seen him, short as a Marine's, and his face had changed—gotten older, harder in the jaw and thinner under the cheekbones, the face of somebody who'd had a long year and slept badly through most of it. He smelled the same, though. Rain and pine and the inside of Billy's garage. Motor oil.

"Holy crap," Jacob said. "*Leah.*"

"Hey, Jake."

His face started to do something—split open into a grin, maybe, the big stupid grin she remembered from when they were kids on First Beach—and then his eyes went sideways and up, to Harry, and it stopped halfway and stayed stuck there.

Sam hadn't said anything yet.

He was standing a little in front of Jacob, the way he always stood a little in front of everybody. His shoulders were squared and his feet were set wide in the gravel and his hands hung loose at his sides, and he was looking at her.

She'd wondered, sometimes, on the long runs up the Cassiar, what this would feel like. Standing in front of Sam Uley again. She'd imagined it a hundred ways. She'd imagined her knees going. She'd imagined the old hook pulling in her gut, even though she'd torn herself out of his pack that night behind the woodshed and there was no hook left in her to pull. She'd imagined crying. She'd imagined going for his throat.

She looked at him now in the rain, at the face she used to know every inch of—the mouth that went flat when he'd decided something and didn't want to talk about it, the line between his eyebrows that he'd had since he was seventeen—and waited for something to happen.

Not much did. She felt tired, mostly. A dull old anger, like a bruise you'd forgotten about until you leaned on it.

On her right hand, the heavy gold ring sat quiet and cool.

"Leah," said Sam.

"Sam."

His eyes went to Harry. They stayed there a long time. She watched them go from the ears to the muzzle to the teeth, from the forepaws hanging at his sides to the black claws on the ends of them, down the long coat to the footpaws on the wet porch boards and back up. His nostrils flared. Whatever he smelled, his jaw went tight.

"What is that," Sam said.

"Evening," said Harry.

His voice was low and level, and it carried down the steps through the rain without him raising it. One ear turned toward Sam. The other stayed pointed at the trees.

Sam didn't answer him. He looked back at Leah. "What did you bring onto our land?"

"He's got a name."

"I don't care what its name is." Sam took one step forward, onto the edge of the gravel at the foot of the steps. The rain bounced off his shoulders. "It can't be here." He raised his chin at Harry, and his voice changed, went flat and formal, the voice she'd heard him use at council meetings when he stood up in front of the elders to talk about the treaty. "You can't be here. This is Quileute land. Nothing comes over that line that isn't Quileute. Not the Cullens. Not anything. That's been the law since Ephraim Black." He pointed back down the drive, toward the road, toward the highway, toward everywhere else. "So whatever you are, you get back in that car and you drive it off this reservation. Tonight. Or we put you off it."

In the trees, a growl went up. It started with Paul and ran down the line of eyes through the alders and out along the road, one wolf after another picking it up, until the whole dark edge of the yard was rumbling with it, low and deep, like a truck on the highway that never got any farther away.

Harry's tail flicked once. Just the tip.

"I'm visiting my mother-in-law," he said.

The growling stopped.

It stopped all at once, like somebody had pulled a plug. In the quiet Leah could hear the rain on the porch roof and the Rolls ticking as it cooled and, from the kitchen behind her, her mom saying something soft to the baby.

"Your *what*," said Sam.

Jacob made a noise like he'd swallowed his gum. "Your *mother-in-law*?"

"He's my husband," Leah said.

She lifted her left hand. She didn't plan to. It came up the way your hand comes up to block a punch, and the porch lights caught the stone in the ring and it went gray and then blue and then green and gold all at once, the way it always did.

Sam looked at it.

She watched his face go blank. She'd seen it go blank like that once before, on the morning he came back from two weeks missing in the woods and walked into her mom's kitchen with his hair hacked short and his shoulders twice as wide as they'd been, and looked at her like she was somebody he used to know. It went blank now in exactly the same way. Everything in it switched off.

"You got *married*?" Jacob was saying. "*Married*? Seth was thinking *husband* the whole way over here from the Hoh, the whole time, *husband husband husband*, and I figured he'd fallen on his head—"

"We got married in July," Leah said. "Under a tree. In England."

"*England?*"

Sam wasn't listening to Jacob. He'd turned his head, very slowly, toward the house. Toward the kitchen window and the yellow light behind the curtain. His nostrils flared, once, twice.

Leah knew what he was smelling. She'd smelled it herself every morning for almost a month, first thing when she woke up. Milk. New fur. And under it, so faint you'd miss it if you didn't know what you were looking for, the smell that belonged to nobody but her daughter.

"There's a pup in that house," Sam said quietly.

"Her name is Layla," said Leah.

Sam looked back at her. His mouth opened and nothing came out of it. Then he said, "Yours." He didn't say it like a question.

"Ours."

His eyes went from her to Harry and back, and something worked in his jaw. "You couldn't—" he started.

"Yeah," said Leah. "I couldn't. And then I could."

Sam stood there in the rain and said nothing for a while. Jacob had shut up. In the alders, nothing moved.

Then Sam breathed out through his nose, long and slow, and when he spoke again it was in the other voice. The reasonable one. The one he used to use on her when she was sixteen and crying about something and he'd decided it was his job to make her see sense. She'd forgotten that voice. She'd forgotten how much she hated it.

"Leah," he said. "Listen to me. You don't know what you've done."

"Excuse me?"

"You're one of us. It's in your blood. It's in you the same as it's in me, and it can still happen. Any day. It doesn't care if you're married. It doesn't care about anything." He came closer, onto the bottom step. "You could walk into the Thriftway tomorrow and look at some guy buying a gallon of milk, and that's it. That's your whole life. And everything you've got with—" his eyes flicked up to Harry and away again "—with him, it's gone. Just like that. You know what it's like. You *know*." His voice dropped. "So what are you going to do? When you imprint? What happens to him then? What happens to *her*?"

Leah heard it from a long way off.

It took a second. It took a second for the words to go all the way down and hit bottom, and when they hit, everything inside her went white.

"How *dare* you," she said.

She came down a step. She didn't decide to. Her body did it.

"How *dare* you. *You*." Her voice cracked down the middle and she didn't care. "You stand in my mother's yard—in my *dad's* yard, Sam, he hung those lights, he hung them every year—and you ask *me* that? *You?* I know what it's like. You're right. I know exactly what it's like, because I had to feel you do it. Every single day. For a *year*. Inside my own *head*." She was shaking. "I felt you look at her. I felt your heart do that thing it does. I felt you feel *sorry* about me while you did it, and you know what, that was the worst part, that was worse than all the rest of it put together—"

"Leah—"

"You don't get to ask me that. You don't get to ask me *anything*. You don't get to stand there and tell me my husband's gonna end up like *me*—"

"I'm asking," Sam said, "because I know what it—"

"If she imprints," said Harry, "I'll break it."

Everybody stopped.

He hadn't moved from the top step. He hadn't raised his voice. He was standing exactly where he'd been the whole time, with the Christmas lights on his muzzle and the rain running off the hem of his coat and his tail hanging straight down behind him.

Sam looked up at him. "You'll what."

"Break it," Harry said. "Whatever it takes."

One of his ears swiveled toward Leah, then back to Sam.

"She told me what it did to her," he said. "When it happened to you. What it was like, having to feel it from the inside. She'd never choose that. Not for herself. Not for anybody. So if it ever comes for her, I'll take it apart. I don't care what it is or how it's built or how old it is. I'll find where it's got hold of her and I'll cut it loose." The tip of his tail flicked once more. "That's all."

Beside Sam, Jacob had gone very still.

Leah saw it out of the corner of her eye. Jacob's hands had closed into fists, and the fists were trembling, very slightly, the way their hands always trembled right before they phased. He didn't say anything. His eyes were on Harry and his jaw was clenched so tight she could see the muscle jump in it.

She didn't know what that was about. She filed it away.

"You can't break an imprint," Sam said.

"Ja," said Harry. "I've heard that about a lot of things."

"Nobody breaks an imprint." Sam's voice had gone hard. He came up one more step, so that he was standing two from the bottom and his face was level with Leah's. He wasn't looking at her. "Nobody touches one. Ever. It's the most sacred thing my people have. The spirits choose it. It's how the line goes on, how it's always gone on, since Taha Aki. Whatever you are, you don't get to come onto our land and tell me you'll tear that apart." He was breathing hard. "I won't let you. If she imprints—*when* she imprints—I'll make sure nobody lays a finger on it. That includes you."

Harry went still.

Leah felt it before she saw it. She was standing close enough that the hem of his coat brushed her leg, and she felt everything in him stop. The very tip of his tail, which had been twitching against the porch boards the whole time, went dead. His ears came up, both of them, and turned toward Sam together, and locked there. His nostrils stopped working. Under the coat, his chest stopped moving. He wasn't breathing. One second. Two. Three. The rain ran down his muzzle and dripped off the end of his nose and he didn't blink.

Then he spoke, and the accent was gone.

All of it. The flat vowels and the clipped ends, the *ja* and the *boet*, the rough rumbling sound she'd fallen asleep to every night for fifteen months—gone, like somebody had taken a cloth and wiped a window. What was left was English. Soft and precise and educated, every vowel exactly where it was supposed to be, polite as a man pulling out a chair for you at a dinner party. It was a voice for big dark houses with a lot of old books in them. Leah had never heard it in her life.

"Sam Uley," he said.

Sam didn't answer.

"I want to be quite certain I've understood you, Sam. I'd hate for there to be any confusion between us later." He tilted his head a few degrees, the way he did when something interested him. The ears stayed exactly where they were. "You're telling me that if some old magic in your blood ever reaches into my wife, and takes hold of her, and drags her away from me and from our daughter—against every single thing she has ever wanted for herself—you would stand between her and the person trying to set her free."

"I'm telling you that—"

"Let me finish." Very pleasant. Very patient. "It's only polite."

Sam shut his mouth.

Harry came down one step. It was an old porch, the steps old wood gone soft with rain, and the second one had creaked for as long as Leah could remember. It didn't make a sound under him.

"If that day ever comes," Harry said, "and I don't expect it will—and you stand in my way, I'll kill you. I'd like you to understand that I mean that quite literally. Then I'll walk into those trees and kill every wolf who has ever run beside you. Every one. And then I'll go and find everyone you've ever loved. Your Emily. Your mother. Every friend you've ever shared a beer with. Everyone who'd come to your funeral." He paused, as if he were considering it carefully. "I'll be very thorough, Sam. I'm good at thorough. It's what they pay me for. And I won't be angry. I'd like you to remember that part especially. I won't shout. I won't hurry. I'll simply keep going, one after another, until there's nobody left on this earth who remembers your name—and then I'll go home and make my daughter breakfast."

He smiled.

Every one of his teeth was in it, all the way back. It sat there on his muzzle and didn't move, and his eyes above it were green and bright and fixed on Sam Uley's face and didn't move either.

Leah couldn't breathe.

*Your Emily.* It had gone into her like a slap. Emily, who was supposed to be maid of honor in the wedding Leah used to plan in the backs of notebooks. Emily laughing in a kitchen with flour on her scarred cheek. And *every wolf*—Collin and Brady, who'd been fourteen when she left and were probably fifteen now and still stupid. The kid back in the alders who smelled like acne cream.

She'd heard Harry say a lot of things in fifteen months. She'd heard him say he'd burn down the Olympic Peninsula and send the park a card. She'd heard him lie to her about pie.

She knew exactly what he sounded like when he meant something.

Down on the second step, Sam had gone the color of cold ashes under his skin. He was looking up at Harry with his mouth a little open, and Leah could see in his face that he'd heard the same thing she had. That he believed it.

And then Harry's eyes changed.

It was a small thing. She'd only seen it once before, on a ridge above a black lake, when he'd knelt beside Kayleigh in the starlight and looked at her face for ten seconds without moving. The bright green went a little unfocused, like he was looking at something just past the edge of the world.

In the trees, every wolf moved at once.

---

Sam Uley had spent three years with other people's voices in his head.

He'd been the first. Nineteen and alone, sick with a fever nobody could break, lost in the woods behind his mother's house for two weeks without knowing what he was or why, until Old Quil and Harry Clearwater and Billy Black had come and found him and told him the stories were true. For months after that there had been nobody in his head but him. Then Jared. Then Paul. Then the rest of them, one after another, kids he'd known since they were born, crowding in until his own thoughts were one voice in a room full of shouting. He'd learned to live with it. He'd had to. He was the one who held the room together.

But on two legs his head was his. On two legs there was no pack mind. That was the one rule that had never broken. Whatever happened out there in the trees, when he came home to Emily's kitchen and stood at the sink in his own skin, it was quiet in there. Just him.

He was standing on the second step of Sue Clearwater's porch, in his own skin, with the rain running down his back, and the black wolf a few steps above him had just finished telling him, very politely, how it would go about killing Emily, when something walked into his head.

It came in from behind. That was how it felt—a cold nail driven in at the base of his skull, slow and certain, and then a door he had never known was there swinging open, and something standing in the doorway. He couldn't see it. He could feel it look around.

*DON'T.*

His knees went.

He caught himself on the porch rail. The word was in that voice, the soft English one, and it was enormous. It was as big as the Alpha voice, bigger. It set itself down in the middle of his mind like a stone set down on a table, and sat there, and he couldn't pick it up and he couldn't push it away. His legs stayed his own. Nothing pulled on them. It only sat there and let him look at it.

In the alders, a wolf screamed.

It was one of the new ones, Dylan Littlesea, thirteen years old and five days phased, and he went over sideways into the brush like he'd been shot. All down the treeline the eyes jerked. Jared's head snapped down between his shoulders. Out on the road somebody went flat on his belly in the mud. Behind Sam, Jacob made a sound like he'd been kicked in the stomach and staggered half a step and caught himself on the hood of Sue's Subaru, and Sam heard the sheet metal fold under his hands.

*TEST.*

Sam's vision went gray at the edges. He hung onto the rail.

Leah was still standing on the steps above him.

He saw her through the gray. She was the only one. Every wolf in the trees had flinched, every wolf on the road had flinched, Jacob Black was holding himself up on a car, and Leah Clearwater was standing on her mother's porch steps with the rain on her face, looking down at all of them like she didn't understand what she was looking at. Her face hadn't changed. She hadn't heard it.

It had kept her out. Whatever this thing was, it had walked into every wolf on the reservation, and it had stepped around her.

*ME.*

Then it was gone.

The door swung shut. The nail slid back out of the base of his skull. Sam stood on the step with both hands locked on the porch rail and his heart slamming against his ribs, and he could feel exactly where it had been: a cold place behind his eyes, like the dent in a mattress where somebody had been lying.

Three words. It had come in and said three words and left.

It could have stayed. He understood that, standing there with his knees shaking. He'd felt it look around in there. It could have stayed as long as it liked and gone anywhere it wanted. It had said three words and walked back out and left him to work out on his own that the door didn't lock.

At the edge of the alders, Paul broke.

Sam knew him too well to be surprised. Paul's temper had always run about two seconds ahead of the rest of him. He'd phased the first time because a kid in the cafeteria took the last of his Doritos. He'd phased in front of Bella Swan on the Blacks' front lawn because she'd slapped him. Sam saw the dark silver shape come up out of the alders with its hackles standing straight up and its lips peeled back off its teeth, and he knew exactly what was going through Paul's head, because it was the same thing that always went through Paul's head. *Somebody hit me. Hit them back.*

"*Paul!*"

He put the Alpha into it. All of it. He'd learned years ago how to put the thing in his blood behind his voice, so that it went out of him and into every one of his wolves like a hook through the gut, so that they couldn't refuse it any more than they could refuse their own heartbeat.

"*STAND DOWN!*"

He felt it go out. He felt it land.

Paul didn't stop.

Paul was in the air. He'd come out of the alders in two strides and launched from twenty feet out, straight at the porch, straight at the black wolf on the steps, all that weight coming in low and fast with his jaws open for the throat. Harry didn't step back. He didn't lift a forepaw. He turned his head toward Paul, maybe an inch, the way you'd turn your head toward somebody who'd said your name across a room.

Paul's eyes changed in midair.

Sam saw it. He was looking right at them. They rolled back until the whites showed all the way around, wild and white and terrified, and Paul twisted. Twisted his whole body in the air like a cat thrown off a roof, tail whipping around, all that mass turning on nothing, and came down.

On Sam.

Sam had time to see it coming. He didn't have time to do anything about it.

Paul hit him square in the chest and took him off the step, and they went down together into the gravel, and the Subaru's side mirror snapped off with a crack like a rifle shot, and before Sam could get a breath in, Paul's jaws closed on his left shoulder.

He felt the teeth go in. Through the skin, through the muscle, and down, and something broke under them with a wet crunch he heard from inside his own body. His collarbone. White pain went through him from his ear to his hip.

The fire came up his spine.

He didn't choose it. He never got to choose it. The pain lit it and it went up through him like a match dropped in a gas can, and he burst out of his skin into the wolf—the big black wolf, the biggest of all of them, the one he'd been for three years—and the cutoffs went to rags, and Paul was on top of him, and Sam heaved.

He'd always been bigger than Paul. He'd always been stronger. He put every pound he had into it.

Paul didn't move.

Paul's legs were locked. Locked like iron posts driven into the gravel. Paul's jaws let go of his shoulder, and blood poured hot down Sam's foreleg, and before Sam could twist free the jaws moved and closed again—on his throat.

They stopped short of closing all the way. The teeth sat on his windpipe. They pressed. They held there.

And Sam was phased now. He was in the pack mind. It hit him like walking into a burning building.

Paul was screaming.

Inside, where nobody could hear but them, Paul was screaming. *I can't stop—Sam, I can't stop, I can't, something's got my legs, something's in here with me, I'm trying, I swear to God I'm trying, Sam, SAM—* Over and over. And around Paul's screaming, the whole pack, every one of them, Jared and Collin and Brady and the new kids, all of them feeling it with him, feeling Paul's teeth on their Alpha's throat and Paul's terror behind the teeth, and nobody able to do a thing about it.

And underneath all of it, Sam felt the other thing.

He reached for Paul with the Alpha bond the way he'd reached for him a thousand times. Down the line, to the place in Paul where the hook sat. The place where Sam's orders went. The place that was his.

Something else was sitting there.

It was the cold thing from the doorway. It was sitting in Paul's head exactly where Sam's hold should have been, and it had its grip on the same line Sam held, the same hook, and it was holding it harder. Sam pulled. *PAUL. LET GO.* The order went down the line the way it always went, and hit that cold grip, and stopped dead. Like shouting into a wall.

"That's what it feels like, Sam."

The voice came from above him. Out loud this time. The soft English one.

Sam rolled his eyes up. Harry was standing on the porch steps, right where Paul would have hit him. He hadn't come down any farther. His forepaws hung at his sides. Rain ran off his coat. He was looking down at the two of them in the gravel with his head tipped a little to one side.

"Somebody else holding the leash," Harry said. "Your legs going wherever they're told. Screaming inside, and nobody listening." His ears tipped forward. "You've been doing it to them for three years. You did it to her. I thought you ought to see what it looks like from the outside. Just the once."

The grip let go.

Sam felt it happen. The cold thing opened its hold on the line and was gone, and the line went slack, and Paul's jaws came open.

Paul scrambled backward off him so fast his claws threw gravel. He went down flat on his belly in the mud by the woodpile, ears pinned, tail jammed between his legs, and in the pack mind he was making a sound Sam had never heard out of him, a thin high keening that went on and on, and he was looking at his own front feet. Staring at them. Like they belonged to somebody else.

Sam lay on his side in the gravel with the blood running out of his shoulder and the rain coming down on him.

He could feel the bone beginning to knit. He could feel every one of his wolves in the pack mind, shaking. He could feel Paul's shame, thick and hot and sick.

And from somewhere a long way off, from more than a year and a lot of rain away, he remembered a night behind his own woodshed. Remembered putting the Alpha behind his voice and saying *we go tonight*, and feeling it go into every one of them. Feeling it go into her. Feeling her legs bunch up under her without her permission, and her mind, wide awake inside it, screaming *no*.

He'd felt that. He'd felt it, and he'd told himself it was what she needed.

*Oh*, Sam thought.

He didn't get up.

---

Leah hadn't heard a thing.

She'd seen it. She'd seen every wolf in the trees jerk at once like somebody had cracked a whip over their heads. She'd seen the kid in the alders go over sideways into the brush. She'd seen Jacob grab the hood of her mom's car to stay on his feet and Sam's knees buckle on the step, and she'd stood there on the porch steps in the rain with no idea what was happening, and then she'd looked up at Harry's face and understood.

He was talking to them. Inside.

All of them. Every wolf on the reservation. And none of it had come anywhere near her.

She looked down at the ring on her right hand. It sat cool and quiet. She didn't know if it had stopped him, or if he'd stepped around her on purpose. She thought about the round room at the top of the tower, and the ring going hot on her finger and then settling, and his voice. *Not even me, Leah. Ever.*

And then Paul had come out of the alders, and she'd known Paul's eyes when they rolled. She'd known that twist in midair. She'd known exactly what it felt like from the inside, to have your own legs bunch under you because somebody else had decided they should.

She'd felt it behind the woodshed. She'd felt it every time Sam said *go*.

She stood on the porch and watched her husband do it to Paul Lahote while Paul screamed inside, and something in her stomach turned over and kept turning.

And underneath that, in a small, mean, low-down place she wasn't proud of, something else said, very clearly: *Good. Let him watch.*

She didn't like that it was there. She didn't like either of them. She stood there with both of them sitting in her at once, the sick feeling and the mean one, and couldn't make either of them leave.

Paul was flat in the mud by the woodpile, whining. Sam was a black wolf on his side in the gravel with blood all down one foreleg, and he wasn't getting up.

Harry came down the rest of the steps.

He walked past Sam. He didn't look down at him. He walked past Jacob, who had straightened up off the hood of the Subaru and left two handprints pressed into the metal, and Jacob stepped back out of his way without seeming to know he was doing it. He walked past the Rolls, and down to where the drive ran between the two big alders by the road, and the wolves along the road backed up as he came. One step. Two. Three. A couple of the young ones turned and ran.

He didn't hurry. Heel, toe. Heel, toe. His tail hung straight down behind him and the hem of his coat swung around his shins, and his footpaws made no sound at all on the gravel.

At the end of the drive, where the gravel met the dirt of the road, her dad had put in a mailbox the year Leah was born. It was still there. It leaned. *CLEARWATER*, it said on the side, in white paint her dad had touched up every spring. Harry stopped beside it with his back to the house and looked out at the road for a moment, at the dark wet wall of trees on the other side.

Then he held out his right forepaw, palm down, over the gravel.

The rain stopped.

Just there, in front of him. A column of it, three feet across and ten feet high, stopped falling. Leah could see the drops hanging in the air, thousands of them, catching the Christmas lights from the porch sixty feet behind. Then they began to glow. Faint at first, then brighter. White, and then gold. She knew that color. She'd seen it come up out of a granite shelf on a ridge above a black lake and wrap itself around two dead girls like a pair of arms.

The drops pulled together.

They ran into each other, thousands at once, and the light got thicker, and began to have edges. It went dark at the edges, darker, the color of wet iron. She saw a boot take shape out of it first, a big armored boot set flat on the gravel, plate over plate. Then a second one. Then the shins, and the knees, and the thighs, all steel, a dull dark charcoal the same color as the fur at the tips of Harry's ears, and the light was still in there, burning, behind every seam where one plate met the next. It came up through the hips and the belt and the chest. A breastplate, broad and plain, with something worked into the middle of it in silver that she recognized before she'd finished seeing it: a griffin, rearing, wings up. The same one engraved on the inside of the ring on her right hand. Pauldrons. Arms. Gauntlets, every joint articulated, opening and closing once on the empty air as if they were trying themselves out.

Then the helm. Rounded on top, a long narrow slit across the front, and behind the slit the white-gold light, burning steady, with nothing else in there at all.

It was eight feet tall. Harry stood six-foot-seven, and it stood over him by more than a head. It stood there in the rain, and where the rain hit the seams of its armor it hissed and went up in thin threads of steam.

The gauntlets closed.

A sword came out of the light between them, as if it had been there all along and was only now deciding to be seen. A long straight two-handed blade, nearly as long as Leah was tall, dark steel like the rest of it, with the same light running down the middle of it in a thin line from the crossguard to the point.

Nobody on the reservation made a sound. Not the wolves. Not Jacob. Leah could hear the rain, and the hiss of it on the steel, and her own heart.

Harry spoke to it without turning around. He used the English voice, the soft one, and he pitched it to carry, so that every wolf in every tree heard it as clearly as if he'd been standing beside them.

"Anyone not of my blood," he said, "who tries to cross onto this ground—"

He stopped. The rain ran off the end of his muzzle.

"Kill them."

The helm bowed. Once, slow and deep.

Then the knight turned the point of the sword down and set it in the gravel at its feet, right on the line where the drive met the road, and folded both gauntlets over the pommel, one on top of the other. It straightened. The burning slit of its visor turned, slowly, from one end of the treeline to the other. Left to right. Along the road. Across the alders. Into the spruce behind the woodpile.

Wherever it looked, the wolves backed away.

Harry turned around and walked back up the drive.

He came up past the Rolls, and past Jacob, and stopped beside the place where Sam lay in the gravel. He looked down at him for a moment. When he spoke, the accent had come back. Flat vowels. Clipped ends. It came back all at once, like a light switching on, and somehow that was worse than when it had gone.

"Right," Harry said. "You two are already on the inside of him. I'd go now, while that's still true."

Sam lay there a second longer. Then the black fur went, and it was Sam on his hands and knees in the gravel, naked and soaked, his left shoulder a mess of torn skin and blood that was already pulling itself back together. He got up. It took him two tries. He stood there swaying in the rain with his arm clamped against his ribs, and he looked at Leah.

His face wasn't blank anymore. She didn't know what was in it. She didn't want to.

"Leah—" he said.

"Go home, Sam," said Leah. "Go home to Emily."

He stood there. The rain ran down him. For a second she thought he was going to say something else, and she didn't know what she'd do if he did.

He didn't. He turned around and walked down the drive.

He walked naked down her father's gravel drive in the rain, holding his arm, past the Rolls and past the Subaru with its side mirror lying in the mud, and when he came to the end of it, to the mailbox, to the line, he walked right past the knight. The knight didn't move. Only the burning slit of its visor turned, and followed him, all the way across the road and into the trees on the far side. Sam didn't look at it. He didn't speed up. He kept walking until the dark took him.

In the alders, the eyes went with him, one pair at a time. Jared. The kids. Paul last of all, belly to the ground, ears flat, looking at nobody, slinking along the edge of the woodpile and across the road and away.

Then there was only Jacob.

He was still standing by the Subaru with the rain running into his eyes and his two handprints in the hood beside him. Out on the road behind him, his own wolves waited—Embry, Quil, a few others she didn't know—keeping well clear of the mailbox.

"Leah," Jacob said.

"Jake."

He looked at her for a while. He looked different, standing there on his own without Sam in front of him. Younger, maybe. Tired.

"Seth says you've got a kid," he said.

"Her name's Layla."

"Layla." Jacob said it carefully, like he was trying it out. Then the grin came, finally—smaller than the big stupid one from First Beach, more crooked, more honest. "Congratulations, Leah. For real. I mean it."

Her throat closed up. "Thanks, Jake."

Then his eyes went to Harry, and the grin went away.

"You said you'd break an imprint," Jacob said.

"Hers," said Harry. "If it ever happened to her. If she wanted it gone."

"And what about mine?"

So that was it. Leah looked at Jacob's hands. They had closed into fists again.

Harry looked at Jacob for a long moment, head tipped to one side, ears forward.

"I don't know anything about yours," he said. "Yet. Seth told us a bit. I'd like to hear the rest from you." His tail flicked once. "Tomorrow morning. Here."

Jacob looked at him. Then past him, down the drive, at the eight-foot figure standing at the end of it with its gauntlets folded on its sword and the light burning in its visor.

"Is that thing going to let me back in?"

"Come to the end of the drive and shout," said Harry. "Somebody'll walk you in."

Jacob was quiet a second. Then he nodded, once, short.

He turned and jogged down the drive. When he passed the knight he didn't look at it, but Leah saw his shoulders go tight, and saw the visor turn and follow him. Halfway across the road he phased mid-stride without breaking pace, and it was a big russet wolf that went into the trees on the far side, with Embry and Quil and the others falling in behind him, and then they were gone too.

The yard was empty.

The rain came down on the gravel and the cars and the two handprints in the hood of the Subaru. At the end of the drive the knight stood with its visor turned toward the dark, and the rain hissed off its armor.

Harry came back up the steps. He made it as far as the top one.

Leah grabbed the front of his coat in both fists.

He stopped. He didn't pull away. He looked down at her with his ears halfway back and rain dripping off the end of his muzzle onto her knuckles.

"That voice," she said.

He didn't say anything.

"Where did that come from?" Her own voice came out rough. "Harry. Where did that *come* from?"

He was quiet a long time. The rain drummed on the porch roof. Inside, in the kitchen, her mom was saying *shh, shh, baby*, and Layla was making a small fretful sound.

"Somebody I used to have in my head," Harry said finally. "For about seventeen years. He talked like that." His ears went flat. "Some of it stuck."

"Who?"

"The war one," said Harry. "Long story."

She thought about Vulk on the ridge. About how his face had gone the color of paper when Harry told him his name.

"You did to Paul what Sam used to do to me," Leah said.

"Ja."

He didn't argue. He didn't explain.

"Once," he said. "So Sam would see it from the outside."

"Don't ever do it to me."

"I can't. You're wearing the ring."

"I *know* I'm wearing the ring, Harry." Her fists tightened in his coat. "Don't ever *want* to."

His ears went all the way back.

"I won't," he said quietly.

"You didn't put it in my head. Whatever you said to them."

"No."

"Was that the ring? Or was that you?"

"Me," said Harry. "I promised."

She stood there holding the front of his coat in the rain and looked up at him for a long time. At the green eyes and the wet fur and the long black muzzle she'd woken up next to every morning for fifteen months. He'd just told Sam Uley he'd kill Emily. In that voice. And she'd known he meant it.

She didn't know what to do with that. She didn't know where to put it.

Inside, Layla started to cry for real.

Harry's ears snapped toward the sound so fast she heard them move. His whole body leaned toward the door.

Leah let go of his coat.

"Go," she said.

He went.

She stood on the porch by herself for a minute, with the lights her dad had hung making colors in the rain, and looked down the drive at the knight. It didn't move. The light burned in the slit of its visor, steady as a pilot light, and steam came up off its shoulders.

Then she went in and shut the door.

---

Sue Clearwater had watched all of it from the front window, with her granddaughter in one arm and the edge of the curtain in her other hand.

She hadn't heard any of it. The window was shut and the rain was loud on the porch roof, and whatever her daughter's husband had said out there, he hadn't said it loudly. But she'd seen Sam's face. She'd watched him come up the steps, one and then another, with his jaw set the way it had been set two years ago when he stood in this kitchen and told her Leah would be all right, Leah would get over it, it wasn't anybody's fault. And she'd watched the big black wolf on the top step go still. Stiller than she'd known a living thing could go.

Then, behind her in the hall, Seth had made a sound.

She'd turned around. Her son was on his knees on the hall runner. Six-foot-four and sixteen years old, on his knees, with both hands pressed flat to the sides of his head and his face gone the color of paper. "Mom," he'd said. Only that. In a voice she hadn't heard out of him since he was six and running a fever and sure there was something under his bed.

By the time she'd reached him it was over. He'd looked up at her with his eyes huge. "He was in my head," he'd said. "Mom. He was *in my head*."

And through the window behind her, Paul Lahote had come out of the trees.

Now it was forty minutes later, and the thing that had been in her son's head was sitting on the bench under her kitchen window with her granddaughter asleep on his chest.

He'd folded himself down onto the bench the same way as before, knees nearly up at his chin, tail wrapped around the bench leg. He'd taken off the long coat and hung it over the back of the bench, where it steamed gently in the heat off the stove, and under it he had on a plain gray T-shirt worn thin at the collar. The baby lay along his breastbone in her gray blanket with her small silver-tipped ears poking up through the holes in her hat, and one of his forepaws was spread across her back with the black claws drawn all the way in. Every so often one of his ears turned toward the window behind him. Toward the end of the drive.

He'd fixed the screen door when he came in. Sue had seen it happen. He'd walked into the kitchen with Layla already crying in his arms and gone straight to the bench, and the door Seth had torn half off its hinge had lifted itself off the linoleum behind him and swung back into its frame, and the bent hinge pin had straightened with a small metal squeal and slid home. He hadn't looked at it once. He'd been busy licking the baby's ears.

Sue had put more oil on for the fry bread. She didn't know what else to do with her hands.

Leah had come in a few minutes after him, shut the front door, gone down the hall to the bathroom, and come back out with her face washed and her hair wet and her eyes red at the rims. She was sitting at the table now with a mug of coffee wrapped in both hands. She hadn't drunk any of it. She was watching her husband. Every so often she looked away from him, at the window, at the stove, at the salmon magnet on the fridge, and then her eyes went back.

The chili had been on since noon. Sue had made it for Seth, who ate like a logging crew, and there'd been enough in the pot for a week. She looked at the three of them—her daughter and her son and the wolf on the bench—and thought it might last till nine.

"Seth," she said. "Freezer."

Seth went out the back door, carefully this time, and came back with his arms full of the smoked salmon he'd done in his dad's smokehouse in October, the first batch he'd ever done alone. He put it on the counter. Then he stood there by the counter for a second with his hands flat on it and his shoulders up around his ears, and Sue watched him decide something.

"Okay," Seth said. He turned around. "Okay. I'm just gonna say it." He pointed at Harry. "Dude. You were in my *head*."

Harry looked up from the baby. "Ja. Sorry. That one wasn't meant for you."

"It was in *everybody's*. Jake's lot. Sam's lot. Mine." Seth thumped his own chest. "And next time I phase I'm gonna find out exactly what every single guy in both packs thought about it, and I *really* don't want to."

"Seth," said Sue.

"What? It's true. Collin's gonna have thought something dumb. Collin always thinks something dumb."

"What did he say?" said Sue.

They both looked at her. At the table, Leah lifted her head.

"In your head," Sue said to Seth. "What did he say?"

Seth glanced at Harry. Harry's ears tipped sideways, and he didn't say anything, and Sue took that for permission.

"'Don't test me,'" Seth said. "That's all. 'Don't. Test. Me.' Three words." He swallowed. "It was like—you know at the pool in Port Angeles, when you're little, and you go off the high board into the deep end and you open your eyes and look down and you can't see the bottom? Like that. Like there wasn't any bottom."

Sue turned the fry bread. The oil spat.

"And outside?" she said. "Before that. What did he say to Sam?"

Leah told her.

She told it flat, staring into her coffee. Sam and the treaty. Sam and *what are you going to do when you imprint*. What Harry said back. What Sam said after that. And then the rest of it, the part in that voice—Leah didn't try to do the voice; she just said the words, in order, the way you'd read a list off the back of a receipt. *Your Emily. Your mother. Every wolf who's ever run beside you. Everyone who'd come to your funeral.*

When she got to the end, the kitchen was very quiet. The oil hissed. The rain ran down the window behind Harry's head.

Sue lifted the last piece of fry bread out of the pan and laid it on the paper towels and turned off the burner. She wiped her hands on her apron. Then she turned around and looked at the wolf on her kitchen bench.

He looked back at her. His ears were half down. The forepaw on the baby's back didn't move.

"Emily is my family," Sue said.

"I know," said Harry.

"I held her the week she was born. I taught her to make bread. She sat at that table with my daughter every Sunday for years, and when it happened—when Sam—" She stopped. Started again. "She hasn't been inside this house since. She sends a card at Christmas. I keep them."

"I know," Harry said again.

"You said you'd kill her."

"I said what I'd do if Sam made me." He was quiet a moment. "He won't make me."

"And if he does?"

Harry looked down at the baby on his chest. At the small round head with the pale gray stripe down the middle of the face, rising and falling with his breathing.

"Then I would," he said.

At the table, Leah closed her eyes.

Sue stood with her back to the stove and looked at him for a long time.

She had sat through council meetings for twenty-two years. She'd heard Old Quil tell the story of Taha Aki and the third wife a hundred times. She'd heard him call imprinting a gift. A blessing from the spirits. The thing that made sure the line went on strong. She'd heard Billy agree with him. She'd sat there with her hands folded in her lap and her coffee going cold, because her husband was on that council and the stories were the stories and you didn't argue with the stories in front of the elders.

And she had watched it take her daughter apart.

She'd watched Leah stop eating. She'd watched her stop laughing. She'd watched her girl turn into something hard and sharp that bit anybody who came near her, and she'd watched Sam Uley's face every time he came into this kitchen afterward, so full of sorrow and guilt, and still looking past Leah toward the door like there was somewhere else he'd rather be. And she'd never said one word. Not to Old Quil. Not to Billy. Not to Sam.

"Old Quil would call you a monster," Sue said.

"Some days he'd be right," said Harry.

He said it plainly. He didn't sound sorry about it, and he didn't sound proud of it.

Sue looked at him a while longer. Then at her daughter, at the table with her eyes shut and her hands around a mug of coffee she hadn't touched.

"If it ever happens to her," Sue said, "you break it."

Leah's eyes came open.

"Whatever it takes," said Sue. "You hear me? You break it."

"Ja," said Harry.

"Mom," Leah said.

"I sat in that room for twenty years listening to them call it a gift." Sue picked up the plate of fry bread and carried it to the table and set it down harder than she'd meant to. "I'm done listening." She went back for the chili. "And I'm going to pray every night that Sam Uley's gotten smarter since he was nineteen."

"Don't hold your breath," said Leah.

"He kind of has, actually," said Seth.

Everybody looked at him.

"What? He has. A little." Seth shrugged his big shoulders. "He apologized to Jake. After the thing in January. A real one. It was super weird."

"Sit down," said Sue, "and eat."

She made Indian tacos, because that was what you did with fry bread and chili, and because her Harry had liked them better than anything else she cooked and she hadn't made them since March of last year. Fry bread, chili on top, cheese, lettuce, chopped onion. She put the smoked salmon out on the cutting board beside them.

Harry had to give up the baby to eat. Sue watched him work out that he'd have to. He looked at the plate. He looked at Layla. His ears went back. Then he looked at Sue, and she held out her arms, and he leaned across the corner of the table and laid his daughter in them, both forepaws under her until he was sure Sue had her. He kept his eyes on the baby for a good five seconds after he'd let go.

Sue sat down with Layla in the crook of her left arm and ate with her right.

The three of them went through the fry bread like it was paper. Sue counted, because she couldn't help it. Seth ate eleven. Leah ate seven and half the salmon. Harry ate nine, holding each one in one forepaw and tearing it with his teeth from the side so the chili wouldn't go everywhere, which it did anyway. He peeled strips off the salmon with one claw and ate them two at a time. When the fry bread was gone Sue sent Seth back out to the freezer for the rest of the salmon, and that went too.

"This is lekker, Sue," Harry said, about halfway through. "Properly lekker."

"I don't know what that means."

"Good," said Leah, with her mouth full. "It means good. He says it about everything."

"I say it about things that are good."

"You said it about the Portkey."

"The Portkey *was* good. It got us there."

Layla slept through all of it. She lay in the crook of Sue's arm with her small black nose working in her sleep, smelling chili and salmon and woodsmoke, and once her tiny tail lashed under the blanket and then lay still again. Sue kept looking down at her. She couldn't stop. Every time she looked up from the baby she found Harry's eyes on the two of them, and every time he looked away.

Seth wiped his mouth on the back of his hand.

"Okay," he said. "So is that thing gonna kill me?"

"Which thing?" said Harry.

"The—" Seth pointed his fork toward the front of the house. "The eight-foot death robot. At the end of the drive. I'm on patrol at five tomorrow. Am I gonna walk down there and get cut in half?"

"No."

"Because 'anyone not of my blood,' I heard that, Harry, everybody heard that, they heard it in *Neah Bay*—"

"You're her uncle." Harry nodded at the baby in Sue's arm. "Half of her's Clearwater. Her blood's your blood. He reads blood the way old wards do. As far as he's concerned, you're mine."

Seth thought about that. "Huh," he said. "Cool." He reached for more salmon. "Cool cool cool."

"And me," said Sue.

"You're her granny," said Harry. "You're about as much my blood as she is."

Sue looked down at the baby. She didn't say anything.

Then, because there was no point putting it off, she said, "Charlie comes on Sundays."

Leah choked.

She choked on a mouthful of salmon and coughed until her eyes watered, and Harry reached over without looking and thumped her on the back with the flat of his forepaw, and when she could breathe again she stared across the table at her mother with her face red.

"Charlie *Swan*?"

Seth's whole face lit up. "Oh, man," he said. "Nobody told you."

"Told me *what*?"

"Mom and Charlie—"

"We watch the game," said Sue.

"They watch the game," said Seth, "every Sunday. He brings the beer. Last week he brought *flowers*."

"They were for the table."

"They were *roses*, Mom."

Leah stared at her. Sue looked down at the baby and gave her a great deal of attention. Her ears felt hot.

"Mom," Leah said.

"He was alone," said Sue, "and I was alone. And he knows what it's like to have a child you're not allowed to ask about." She looked up. "Eat your chili."

Leah opened her mouth. Shut it again. Then she laughed—a short, startled, wet laugh, the first one Sue had heard out of her since the porch—and put her face in her hands.

"Charlie Swan," Leah said through her fingers. "*Charlie Swan.*"

"He's a good man."

"He's *Bella's dad*."

"Everybody's got something," said Sue.

Harry's ears had gone flat. He was looking toward the front of the house again.

"Sunday," he said.

"Sunday."

"That's the day after tomorrow."

"It is."

"He'll be fine," Harry said. "Muggles can't see the knight. I put a notice-me-not on him before I made him. Charlie'll come up the road and get a very strong feeling he left the stove on."

"And then he'll turn around and go home," said Sue, "and I'll have a cooler full of Rainier and the Seahawks on and nobody to drink it with."

Harry's ears went sideways. He thought about it.

"Meet him at the end of the drive," he said finally. "Take his hand and walk him in. When one of you walks somebody in, that's a guest. He knows the difference." His tail shifted against the bench leg. "Anyone who comes on their own—"

He didn't finish.

"Gets the stove," said Sue.

"Muggles get the stove," said Harry.

He didn't say what everybody else got. Nobody asked him.

Seth had been chewing. He swallowed. "Charlie's kind of not a regular Muggle, though," he said. "Not anymore. He knows about us."

Harry's ears came up. Both of them. "Knows what?"

"About the wolves. Jake showed him."

"Showed him."

"Last year. Like, October? After Bella was—after she came back." Seth glanced at Leah. "Charlie was freaking out. Bella was supposed to be sick, and then she wasn't, and he couldn't see her, and he was gonna go up to the Cullens' with a warrant or a shotgun or something. So Jake went over there and took his shirt off and went wolf right on Charlie's back lawn. So Charlie'd know weird stuff was real, and stop asking."

The kitchen got very quiet.

"And it worked?" said Harry.

"Kind of. Charlie's whole thing now is 'need to know.' He doesn't want to know. He just wants to see Bella and Nessie and not ask." Seth shrugged. "He sees them Saturdays."

"And me Sundays," said Sue.

Harry wasn't looking at either of them. He was looking at the tabletop, at the place where his plate had been, and his ears had gone back, and stayed.

"Seth," he said.

His voice had changed again.

Sue heard it. It was his own voice, the South African one he'd used at her table all evening, and every word in it had been set down level and square, one after another, like a man filling in a form in his head.

"Tell me about the Cullens," Harry said. "All of it. From the night Leah left."

---

It took a long time.

Sue had heard most of it already, in pieces, over fifteen months. From Seth, mostly, when he came home from patrol too wound up to sleep and sat at this table eating cereal out of a mixing bowl at two in the morning. From Billy. From Charlie, a little, on Sundays, in the careful sideways way Charlie said things when he was trying not to know them. She'd never heard it all at once. She'd never heard it with somebody asking questions.

He asked a lot of questions.

He didn't interrupt Seth much. He waited till Seth stopped, and then he asked one, short, and waited again. He never once took his eyes off Seth's face. His tail stayed wrapped around the bench leg and didn't move. His forepaws lay flat on the table in front of him.

"How many of them?"

"Carlisle and Esme, they're like the parents. Edward. Alice and Jasper. Emmett and Rosalie. And Bella now. And Nessie."

"Nine."

"Yeah."

"Where?"

"The big house past town. Off the highway, up this long drive through the trees. The whole back of it's glass. It looks out over the Calawah. And Edward and Bella and Nessie have a cottage in the woods behind it. Esme fixed it up."

"They hunt?"

"Animals. Deer. Elk. Mountain lions. Emmett likes bears." Seth shrugged. "That's why their eyes are gold. The ones that eat people have red."

"And the doctor."

"Carlisle. He works at the hospital in Forks. He's like the best doctor they've got. He put Jake's bones back together that time a newborn crushed him."

Harry's ears went flat and stayed there. "A vampire works in a Muggle hospital."

"He's been doing it for like three hundred years. Bella says blood doesn't even bother him anymore."

"And Bella."

Seth glanced at Leah. Leah had gone very still across the table.

"She—um. Edward turned her," Seth said. "Right after Nessie was born. Nessie broke her—" he waved a hand at his own ribs, his spine "—everything. On the way out. Bella was dying. So Edward bit her."

"What do people in Forks think happened to her?"

"That she got really sick. Some weird disease. That she went away somewhere for treatment and then went to college back east."

"And the child?"

"They tell everybody Nessie's Edward's niece. That his brother died and they adopted her."

"How old is she now?"

"Fifteen months." Seth looked at Leah. "She was born, like, four days after you left."

Leah's coffee mug stopped halfway to her mouth. She set it down again without drinking.

"She looks five," Seth said. "Maybe six now. She talks like a grown-up. Better than me. She's read all of Billy's books, the whole shelf. Carlisle measures her every day. Edward says she'll be all the way grown in about seven years and then she'll just—stop."

"Stop."

"Stop getting older. Forever. Like them."

"What does she eat?"

"Blood, mostly. She'll eat people food if Jake makes her. She hates eggs."

Harry's ear twitched. "And she can do something. You said."

"Yeah. She puts her hand on your face and you see what she's thinking. Pictures. Like a movie. It's freaky the first time."

"Who's seen her? Outside the family. In town."

Seth thought about it. "Charlie. Mom. The council. Billy. Um—they don't really take her to town. She went to Port Angeles with Rosalie in the summer to get shoes. And Mrs. Newton saw her once at the Outfitters and told Charlie she was the prettiest little girl she'd ever seen."

"When was that?"

"July, maybe?"

"And what does she look like now, next to July?"

Seth opened his mouth. Closed it.

"Bigger," he said slowly. "Like—a lot bigger."

Harry didn't say anything to that. He waited.

"Tell me about January," he said.

Seth told him. Sue had heard this part before, too, but she listened anyway, with the baby asleep in her arm.

A vampire from Alaska, one of the Cullens' friends, had seen Nessie in the woods and thought she was something called an immortal child. A vampire baby. "It's like the worst crime there is, for them," Seth said. "Way worse than eating people." She'd gone to Italy and told the ones in charge. "The Volturi. They're like the vampire government. They live in this old city on a hill." And the Volturi had decided to come and kill the baby, and everybody who'd made her.

So the Cullens had called everyone they knew. Vampires had come from all over. Alaska. Ireland. Egypt. Two sisters and a cousin from somewhere in the Amazon. A pair of old Romanian ones who wanted a fight. Nomads from all over, wandering ones, with no homes at all.

"How many?" said Harry.

"Like twenty? Twenty-something. They stayed at the Cullens' for weeks."

"What did they eat?"

Seth hesitated. "The ones with red eyes promised to hunt way outside. Like, Seattle and further."

Harry's muzzle twitched. He didn't say anything.

Then, on a gray morning right after New Year's, the Volturi had come. "In the snow," said Seth. "To the clearing up past the Cullens', where they play baseball. Like thirty-something of them in these long black cloaks, walking out of the trees all together. And a bunch of witnesses they brought with them. And us." He'd gone quiet for a second, remembering. "Both packs. Everybody. We came out of the trees on the Cullens' side. Sam and Jake, side by side, all of us. They didn't know about us. You should've seen Aro's face."

"Aro," said Harry.

"He's the boss one. He can read everything you've ever thought by touching your hand." Seth swallowed. "They talked for, like, hours. In the snow. It was the scariest thing I ever—" He stopped. "Well. Till tonight."

Harry's ears tipped sideways. He let it go.

"Then Alice showed up," Seth said. "She'd been gone for weeks. She came out of the woods with this guy from South America, Nahuel. He's like Nessie. Half and half. He's a hundred and fifty years old, and he's fine, and he's never hurt anybody, and that proved Nessie wasn't dangerous. And Aro said okay and they left." He picked at the edge of the table. "They killed the one who told on them, first. Irina. In front of everybody. Before they went."

"January," said Harry.

He turned his head and looked at Leah.

"Geneva sent a bulletin in January," he said. "By owl. Pacific Northwest, vampire activity, Washington State. Observe only." His ears were flat. "I burned it."

"Because I looked at you," said Leah quietly.

"Ja."

Sue didn't know what they were talking about. She saw her daughter's face, though, and she saw Leah's hand come across the table and rest on the back of his forepaw, and stay there.

"The Italians," Harry said. "Volterra."

Seth stared at him. "You *know* them?"

"We've got a file. They keep to themselves and they keep their own kind quiet, and the Confederation leaves them alone for it. Old arrangement. Older than the Statute." He was quiet a moment. "They came all the way here with thirty of their own, and stood in a field for hours, and left without what they came for."

"Yeah."

"Then they'll be back," said Harry. "One day. People like that don't like leaving without what they came for."

Seth didn't say anything to that.

"And since January?"

"They keep coming." Seth's voice had gone smaller. "The nomads. I guess everybody heard. All the vampires everywhere. They come to look at her. Most of them are polite about it, and the Cullens let them see her, and they go. Some aren't polite."

"August."

"Yeah. Two of them. They killed a couple of guys camping up the Elwha, and a lady walking her dog on the waterfront in Port Angeles. Sam's lot chased them all the way to the Strait and lost them on the water." Seth's jaw went tight. "The paper said cougar."

"And the wolves in the paper. Lake Crescent."

Seth winced. "Brady and Collin. Showing off. There were hikers on the trail and they went right past them. Sam was so mad he made them run the whole reservation boundary for a week."

Harry nodded, very slightly, like he'd expected that.

"And the thing north of the lake," he said. "The trail that stops."

"Yeah."

"It doesn't go up. It Apparates. Disappears on the spot." His voice hadn't changed. "Something that can do what I can do, more or less. And that smell—rot and copper under something sweet—that's what blood magic smells like when somebody's been using it a long time to stay alive past when they should've died."

"Like Vulk," said Leah.

"Like Vulk."

"Who's Vulk?" said Sue.

"Later, Mom," said Leah.

"Something like that collects hearts," said Harry. "Rare ones. That's what it lives on. And this coast has got a half-vampire child nobody in my world has ever heard of, and nineteen born shapeshifters running around in the trees." He stopped.

He didn't say the rest.

He didn't have to. Sue saw Leah's eyes go across the table to the baby asleep in Sue's arm, and stay there. She felt her own arm tighten around the small hot weight of her granddaughter before she'd told it to.

And then he went quiet.

Sue had seen people go quiet before. She'd sat across council tables from Old Quil and Billy Black for twenty-two years and watched them go quiet when they were deciding something, and she'd been married to a man who went quiet before he said something nobody wanted to hear. Her Harry used to take his glasses off and fold them and put them on the table in front of him, and everybody knew to wait.

This one sat on her kitchen bench with his forepaws flat on the table and Leah's hand resting on top of one of them, and he stopped. His tail, which had been wrapped around the bench leg the whole time, unwrapped itself and hung straight down to the floor and didn't move. His ears went back flat against his skull. He was looking at the baby in her arms. He didn't look at anything else.

Seth opened his mouth.

Sue put her free hand on his wrist, and he shut it.

The clock over the stove ticked. The rain ran down the window. Out at the end of the drive, she knew without looking, the knight was standing with its gauntlets folded on its sword and the light burning in its visor, and the rain was going up off its shoulders in steam.

A minute went by. Most of another one.

Leah didn't move her hand.

"I'm on leave," Harry said.

"Harry," said Leah.

"I'm on leave." He said it to the tabletop. "I called Gunnar in June. Indefinite. I've got a daughter who isn't a month old. I bought a *plane* so nobody would cough on her." He rubbed one forepaw slowly down the length of his muzzle, from the eyes to the nose, and let it drop. "And I'm sitting in your mum's kitchen listening to the worst Statute breach I've heard about in five years."

"The what?" said Seth.

Harry looked up at him.

"The International Statute of Secrecy," he said. "Sixteen eighty-nine. Every magical government on earth signed it. It says we stay hidden. Us, and everything like us. Wizards. Vampires. Werewolves. Dragons. Your lot. Anything that isn't supposed to exist stays where Muggles can't see it, and when it doesn't, the Confederation comes and puts it back." His ears flicked. "Your people have been inside it for three hundred years. Nobody ever bothered you because you never needed bothering. You kept to yourselves. You kept it in the family. You told the stories round the fire and the Muggles thought they were stories."

"And now?" said Sue.

Harry held up one forepaw and counted off on his claws. He did it slowly, one claw at a time, and he didn't raise his voice for any of them.

"A Muggle police chief has watched a boy turn into a wolf on his back lawn." One. "A child who looks a year older every month or so is living in a town of three thousand people where everybody knows everybody, and the shop lady's already remarked on her." Two. "Fifty-odd vampires and every wolf on this reservation stood in a field outside town last winter and Geneva knew about it inside a week." Three. "Dead Muggles in Port Angeles. A vampire working in a Muggle hospital. Nineteen horse-sized wolves in the local paper." Four, five, six. He'd run out of claws on that forepaw. He put it down. "And something with a taste for hearts has been hunting your woods since October."

Nobody said anything.

"Any one of those," Harry said, "Geneva sends a letter. A polite one. All of them together—"

"The advisory," said Leah slowly. "The one that came in October."

"Class C," said Harry. "For information. No action authorized." He looked at her. "Every Class C on the books gets looked at again on the first Monday in January. The Statute Review sits in Geneva. Gunnar chairs it. And somebody's going to read this one properly—they will, because the Seattle office has been asking for observations since October and sooner or later some hiker on the Lake Crescent trail with a long lens sends them one—and it goes to Class A."

"And then what?" said Seth.

"A Class A doesn't get a letter."

"What does it get?"

"A team."

The rain ran down the window.

"What kind of team?" said Sue.

"Mine," said Harry. "Hit wizards. Six of them, usually. With orders to remove the problem entirely." His ears didn't move. "And the Americans'll want in, because it's their soil. MACUSA's never once in three hundred years been gentle about secrecy."

"What does 'entirely' mean?" said Sue.

"It means entirely. Every Cullen. The child. Whatever's in the woods. And then they'll look at the Peninsula Daily News, and somebody on that team is going to ask whether nineteen giant wolves on a reservation in Washington are part of the problem too." He paused. "Depending on who's leading it, the answer might be yes."

Seth had gone white again.

"Us?" he said.

"Maybe."

"You can't—they can't just—"

"I've been on that team, Seth." Harry said it quietly. "Twice. Once in Moldova, in oh-five. A coven in the hills that had been feeding off the same three valleys for forty years, and a Muggle journalist got close. We got the file on a Tuesday. The orders said 'entirely.' We were done by the Thursday."

The clock ticked.

"Were there any kids?" said Seth. His voice cracked on it.

"No."

"What if there had been?"

Harry looked at him for a long moment.

"Then they'd have sent somebody who'd have done it anyway," he said.

Seth stared at him. Then he put both elbows on the table and his face in his hands.

"She's a *kid*," he said through his fingers. "She's like five. She reads Billy's books. She hates *eggs*. And Jake—Harry, Jake would—if anything happened to her, Jake would just—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

"I know," said Harry.

"You can't let them."

"No," Harry agreed.

"So what do we *do*?"

Harry looked at the baby in Sue's arms for a while longer. Then at Leah's hand on top of his forepaw. Then up, at Seth.

"They move," he said.

"Who?"

"The Cullens. All nine. The child. Somewhere a long way from anyone. No hikers. No newspapers. No shop lady who saw her in July. No police chief who's known them since high school." His ears tipped back. "And the nomads stop finding her, because nobody knows where to look anymore. And with no leeches coming through, your kids stop phasing in the middle of social studies. And the wolves in the paper turn into a story some hikers told once." His tail shifted, once, against the floor. "And when Gunnar opens that file in January, there's nothing left in it to remove."

"They've got friends in Alaska," Seth said slowly. "The Denali ones. Up past Anchorage somewhere."

"Further than that, if I can manage it."

"And Jake goes with them," said Seth. "He has to. He goes wherever she goes. He can't—the imprint—" He stopped.

"Ja," said Harry. "I figured."

Sue put her hand on her son's arm. He didn't shake it off. He sat there with his face in his hands and his shoulders shaking, very slightly, and she knew exactly what he was seeing. His Alpha. His best friend. Gone north somewhere with no forwarding address.

She thought about Charlie, too, sitting on her couch every Sunday with a can of Rainier in his hand, not asking. Seeing his daughter on Saturdays. Seeing his granddaughter get bigger every week and saying nothing about it, because he'd decided he could live with anything as long as they were close enough to drive to.

She didn't say his name. She didn't have to. When she looked up, Harry was looking at her, and his ears had gone back a notch further, and she understood that he'd already thought of it.

"I know," he said quietly.

"And if they don't want to go?" said Leah.

Harry was quiet for a moment.

"Then I'll make the case," he said. "Nicely. Twice, if I have to." His tail lay still on the floor. "I'd rather it was me knocking on their door than six people from Geneva who've never heard of them and don't care how old she is."

"How long have they got?" said Sue.

He looked down at the tabletop.

"Two weeks," Harry said. "Give or take."

Leah's hand closed on his forepaw.

Nobody said anything for a while. The rain kept coming down. In the crook of Sue's arm, Layla stirred, and made a small sleepy grumbling sound, and her tiny tail lashed once under the blanket and lay still.

Harry's ears turned toward the sound.

"Seth," he said.

Seth lifted his face out of his hands. His eyes were red.

"When you go out at five," Harry said, "find Jacob. Tell him I still want him here in the morning. Nine o'clock. Tell him to come to the end of the drive and shout, and one of you'll walk him in." His ears tipped forward. "And then tell him to get word to the Cullens. A wizard from Geneva would like a word with them. Somewhere that isn't their house and isn't this one. Tomorrow, or the day after. Their choice."

"Edward'll already know," said Seth.

Harry's head tilted. "How?"

"He reads minds. Everybody's. Except Bella's." Seth wiped his nose on his wrist. "If any of us has been within about a mile of that house since we came in tonight, he's heard all of it already. He probably knows what you had for breakfast."

Harry was quiet for a second.

"Does he," he said.

Something moved in his face. Around the muzzle and the eyes. Sue had seen the same thing in it on her porch, through the window, right before he went still, and she didn't like it any better the second time.

"Huh," said Harry. "Well. That'll be interesting."

Leah woke up at two in the morning in her old bedroom and didn't know where she was.

Then she did. The ceiling was the same ceiling she'd stared at through four years of high school, with the water stain over the window shaped like a fish. The poster of the 2005 Seahawks was still on the closet door, curling at one corner. The dresser was the same dresser with the same broken bottom drawer. But the walls were in the wrong place. They'd been ten feet apart her whole life, and now they were further than that—she couldn't tell how much further; every time she tried to look straight at the corner, her eyes slid off it, like they'd slid off the marks on Vulk's stones—and the narrow twin bed she'd slept in since she was nine was a double now, with room in it for a six-foot-seven wolf and her and a baby and a little left over.

He'd done it while she was in the shower. He hadn't said anything about it. Her mom was going to notice in the morning.

The other side of the bed was empty. The sheets still held his heat.

Harry was standing at the window.

He had Layla on his chest. He was in the gray T-shirt and his sweatpants and nothing else, standing with his back to her, and Layla was a small dark curve against the fur over his breastbone with his forepaw spread across her back. He'd cracked the window an inch. The cold air came in and smelled of rain and cedar and the sea.

Through the glass, at the end of the drive, she could see the knight.

It hadn't moved. It stood exactly where he'd left it, at the line where the gravel met the road, with its gauntlets folded on the pommel of its sword. The light in its visor burned steady in the dark, a narrow bar of white-gold. The rain had eased to a drizzle, and it still went up off its shoulders in thin threads of steam.

Against her breastbone, the necklace pulsed. *Big-slow. Small-quick.* Somewhere in the pocket of his coat, hung over the chair by the dresser, the old gold watch would be pulsing with it.

Harry was murmuring something.

She lay still and listened. His voice was low, barely a voice at all, the way he talked to Layla at four in the morning when he thought nobody else was awake. His own voice. Flat vowels, clipped ends.

"—sorry, pup," he was saying. "I know. I know it's Christmas. Dad's got to go back to work for a bit."

Layla made a small sleepy sound against his chest. It might have been the purr.

"Not long," Harry told her. "Two weeks. Give or take. And then we'll go home and Kreacher can make a fuss over you, and you can pull Uncle Ron's ears, and I'll never take another job as long as you live." A pause. "That last bit was a lie. Don't tell your mum."

"Your mum heard," said Leah.

His ears went flat. He didn't turn around.

She got up. The floorboards were cold under her bare feet. She went and stood beside him at the window, close enough that her shoulder pressed against his arm, and looked out at the knight with him.

"You hate this," she said.

"Ja."

She stood there and thought about it. About all of it.

She'd run north to get away from this. From the Cullens and the treaty and the pack mind, from Sam and Emily and Jacob's sad face and Bella Swan's dying, from the whole stinking mess of it. She'd run till she was somewhere nobody could hear her think. And then she'd closed the door behind her on purpose, and leaned on it, and told herself she'd open it when she was ready.

She'd opened it. She'd read the advisory in the window seat in the tower and walked down the stairs with it in her hand and woken him up. *Maybe we should go and take a look.*

Now it was open. And her husband was going to spend Christmas making the Cullens leave Forks, and her little brother was going to lose his Alpha, and Charlie Swan was going to lose his daughter and his granddaughter, and somewhere north of Lake Crescent something that smelled like Vulk was walking around in the trees.

"I'm coming," she said.

He looked down at her.

"Tomorrow. Jake. The Cullens. All of it." She kept her eyes on the knight. "I'm tracking for you. Like Mongolia. Like the goblin in Yellowknife. Whatever's out there north of the lake, I'm finding it."

"Leah—"

"I'm not asking, Harry."

He was quiet for a long time. Out at the end of the drive the knight's visor turned, slowly, along the line of the road and back again, and settled.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"You'd go anyway. I'd rather you were next to me when you did." His ears came halfway up. "And you're better at it than I am. Your nose."

"I know I am," said Leah.

She leaned her head against his arm. He shifted Layla a little higher on his chest, and his other forepaw came around and settled on the small of Leah's back, claws tucked all the way in.

His tail had been hanging still behind him since dinner. She'd noticed. She'd been noticing all night. Now, with her head on his arm and his daughter on his chest, it moved. Just the tip. Once against the back of her leg, and then again. Slow.

Out at the end of the drive, the knight stood in the rain with its sword in the gravel and the light burning in its visor.

It didn't move all night.
