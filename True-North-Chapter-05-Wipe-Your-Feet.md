# True North

## Chapter Five: Wipe Your Feet

---

### I.

The humming lasted to the first creek.

Leah let it go for about two hundred yards. It had a stomping, idiot beat, and he was flat on every third note, and the rain was coming down through the hemlocks in that fine gray way that soaked you without ever seeming to land. Behind them the Cullens' lights had gone to a glow and then to nothing. Ahead the timber closed in black, and the ground ran down toward the river through sword fern and salal and old fallen trunks gone soft as bread under the moss.

"You know what that song's called," she said.

"Back in Black." He stepped over a nurse log without breaking stride. "Truck stop in Whitehorse. Nine days it's been in there. I can't get it out."

"You walked past Jacob *Black*," Leah said, "humming *Back in Black*."

The humming stopped.

His ears went sideways, then flat, then sideways again. He walked three more steps.

"Eish," he said.

"Yeah."

"Did he notice?"

"He rebuilt the whole engine in his Rabbit to that song. He noticed."

Harry looked back over his shoulder toward the house they couldn't see anymore. Water was running off the end of his muzzle in a thin string. The fern was still stuck in the burnt fur between his ears, bobbing when he moved his head.

"Ja," he said. "Well. That'll be on my form as well."

She couldn't tell whether he was lying. His muzzle hadn't twitched. Hers would have. She filed it.

---

They came down to the river where it ran wide and shallow over cobbles, loud with the rain. Leah stopped on the gravel bar and looked west.

Seventeen miles to La Push, give or take. Wide around the edge of Forks, across the La Push road, then through the timber along the Quillayute to the sea. On two feet, even her two feet, an hour and a half. She'd done it once last spring with a cracked rib and Paul yelling in her head the whole way.

She could phase. The bracelet sat on her left wrist, black and gray and green, and she could feel it there like a hand that wanted to close. Ten seconds and she'd be on four legs, and home in twenty minutes.

And Jacob would be in her head before she got her paws under her. And Seth, who had told her an hour ago with both hands clamped over his ears that he never wanted to know, would know.

"I'm not phasing," she said.

"Didn't ask you to." Harry had stopped beside her, holding his tail up off the wet stones. "How far?"

"Seventeen miles."

"Right." He looked at her. At the river. Back at her. His ears went halfway down, which she was starting to learn meant he was about to say something she wouldn't like. "I could take you."

"Take me."

"Four legs. You'd ride." The ears went a little lower. "Teddy did. Rode me round Ron's garden for an hour screaming his head off. Hair went orange. I'm told that means happy."

"I'm not eight."

"No. You'd scream less." He waited. "Or we walk. I walk next to you, and we get to your mum's at one in the morning instead of half twelve, and she's sat up waiting either way."

Leah thought about her mother at the kitchen table. She thought about the pad by the phone and the note under the salmon magnet. Eleven days. She hadn't been home in eleven days.

She thought about sitting on him.

Her wolf thought about it too. Her wolf had opinions.

"If you tell anybody," she said.

"Who'd I tell?"

"Hennessey."

"Hennessey'd make me fill in a form." His tail flicked once. "Passenger Conveyance, Unlicensed, Lupine. Three copies."

"Is that real?"

"Probably. Somewhere in Geneva there's a drawer for it."

He dropped forward.

She watched, because she couldn't not. There was no sound at all. The upright shape of him folded down onto its forepaws and then poured outward, longer and lower and heavier, the long gray coat and the brown one over his arm and her paper bag under it all folding into him like water closing over a stone. Three seconds. Then a wolf stood on the gravel bar, taller at the shoulder than the top of her head, so black in the rain and the dark that her eyes kept trying to slide off him into the trees behind.

The fern was still stuck on top of his head.

He folded his front legs and lay down on the cobbles. Even lying down, his back came up to her chest.

"Grab the ruff," he said. The big head turned until one green eye was a foot from her face. "Behind the ears. You won't hurt me. Foot on my leg."

She put her boot on his foreleg just above the paw, which was the size of a dinner plate and had stones pressed into the pads. She took two fistfuls of the thick fur behind his ears. It was coarse on top and dense underneath and so hot it steamed in the rain. She pulled herself up and swung her leg over.

It was like sitting on the back of a couch that breathed. His back was so wide her knees spread farther than was comfortable. Through her jeans she could feel muscle shifting under her, big slabs of it, and his ribs going in and out, and somewhere down under all of it his heart, slow and heavy as a drum in another room.

"All right?" he said. The words came up through his back into her legs more than through her ears.

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Say anything."

"Wasn't going to." A pause. "Lean forward. Hold on."

He stood up. The ground fell away. Her stomach went with it.

Then he ran.

The first thing was the quiet. She'd run these woods a thousand times on her own four feet and she'd always been loud about it: branches snapping, ferns slapping her face, her paws tearing up the moss. He made nothing. The ferns parted in front of his chest and closed behind them with a hiss of water, and that was the only sound anywhere. His paws came down and the moss barely took the print. She could feel every stride through her whole body, a heavy surge and then a long, weightless reach, and she kept bracing for the jolt at the bottom of it, and it never came.

Trunks went past, black on black, close enough to touch. She flattened herself along his neck with her face in his ruff and the rain needling the back of her head.

He jumped the river at a narrow place without slowing down. For one long second there was nothing under them but black water going white over rocks. She grabbed fur so hard her knuckles cracked. Then they were over and running again, and somewhere in the middle of it a sound came out of her that she would have denied to anybody who asked.

"Did you just laugh?" he said.

"No."

"Ja, you did."

"Watch where you're going."

"I can see in the dark, Leah."

"Then watch it *harder*."

They went wide around Forks. She knew it by smell even with her face buried: the sawmill and its wet sawdust, the gravel pit, somebody's woodstove, the high school's dumpsters behind the gym, a dog in a backyard who caught their scent and went from barking to silence in the middle of a bark. They crossed the La Push road in one bound between a pair of headlights coming around the curve. She saw the truck's high beams flare across the trunks behind them, and then they were back in the trees, and the truck went by with its radio playing country, and nobody had seen a thing.

Two miles past the road he stopped so suddenly she slid forward onto his neck.

"What?"

He didn't answer. His head was down and his nose was working, deep and slow, the way she'd seen Jacob work a trail that was three days cold. The ruff had come up under her hands.

She smelled it a second after he did, because she'd been smelling it for two years and had stopped noticing it was there. Behind them, faint and old and everywhere through the timber, bleach and sugar. In front of them, none. It stopped at a line as straight as a fence. And on the line itself, on the trunks and the stumps and the big moss-covered boulder ten feet ahead, the pack. Sam. Paul. Jared. Embry. Jacob. Herself, faint, from last spring. Rain and cedar and fur, laid down over and over until it had soaked into the bark.

"There's a line," Harry said.

"Treaty line. Seventy years old. The Cullens don't cross it. They cross it, it's war."

"Your lot marked it."

"Every week."

He stood with his front paws a foot short of it. He didn't move.

"Leah," he said. "Can I?"

She looked down at the back of his head. At the burnt tuft. At the fern.

"You're not a Cullen."

"Didn't ask what I was." His ears turned back toward her. "Asked if I can."

Nobody had ever asked her that about this line. Sam had drawn it in his head and they'd all run it. Jacob had inherited it. The elders talked about it at council fires the way they talked about the tide. In two years nobody had once asked Leah Clearwater who got to cross.

"Yeah," she said. It came out rougher than she wanted it to. "Go."

He stepped over.

---

A mile on, something came crashing through the salal behind them.

Leah twisted around. A wolf came tearing out of the dark on their left, sandy and long-legged and gangly, ears flopping, tongue out, close enough that she could see the white rim of his eye roll up to look at her sitting on Harry's back. He yipped. Twice. Then he put his head down and tore off ahead toward La Push, and she could hear him for a full minute after he'd gone: every branch, every fern, every log he jumped and clipped with a hind foot.

"Who was that?" Harry said.

"Seth."

"He's loud."

"He's fifteen."

"Where's he off to in such a hurry?"

"Home. To tell Mom."

"Tell her what?"

"That I'm riding through the woods on you like a pony."

Harry ran in silence for a few strides.

"I'm going to need a better story," he said.

"There isn't one."

---

They came out above First Beach a little after midnight.

She told him to stop, and he stopped at the edge of the trees where the spruce gave out into dune grass and drift logs. She slid down off his shoulder and nearly went over, because her legs had forgotten what they were for.

The beach was black. The surf came in white out of the dark in long lines and broke and ran up the gravel hissing and slid back. Past the break, James Island stood up off the water like the hull of an overturned ship, and the sea stacks beyond it like broken teeth. The rain was blowing in sideways off the Pacific, and it smelled of salt and kelp and cold, cold water.

Fifty degrees, her dad used to say, hauling his old wetsuit up over his shoulders on the gravel right about there. Fifty on a good day. Paddle, Leah. Paddle.

She walked down to the drift logs and stood there with the rain in her face.

Harry didn't follow. When she looked back, he'd lain down at the edge of the grass with his chin on his paws, a black lump at the tree line like a boulder nobody had noticed before, and he was looking at the water.

She stayed until she was cold. For her, that took a while.

Then she walked back up the beach. He got up when she reached him and went upright again the same way he'd gone down, without a sound, and the gray coat and the brown coat and her paper bag were all exactly where they'd been. The bag was dry. She didn't know how. She didn't ask.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay."

They walked into the village.

---

La Push at midnight was porch lights and pickups and rain. A trampoline in somebody's yard with a puddle in the middle. Smoke from a chimney. A dog came out from under a porch to bark at them, got one good look at Harry, and backed under again without finishing.

Leah walked faster.

She had one thing left in a box, and she'd been sitting on the lid since Wednesday.

He'd said it in the clearing. *Harry. Harry Potter.* She'd been kneeling on the grass with her arm open to the bone and his coat around her, and the name had gone into her like a splinter, and she'd done what she did with splinters. She'd left it in. She'd walked up the hill and into the bar and beaten him at pool and never once said it out loud. When she phased at one in the morning, there had been no boxes in the pack mind, and Seth had seen it. Of course he had.

Seth hadn't said one word about it. Not that night. Not once since.

In two years, it was the only tactful thing Seth had ever done.

She should tell Harry. Here, in the street, before the door.

She stopped walking and turned around and opened her mouth.

"Hold still," she said instead, and reached up and pulled the fern out of his head.

He held still. He had to bend for her to reach. She picked the last broken bits of it out of the burnt tuft between his ears, and the fur there was coarse and crisp at the tips and smelled of smoke. His ears went flat under her hands, and when she was done they came back up very slowly.

"Was that there the whole time?"

"Since the bog."

"At the Cullens'?"

"The whole time."

"Nobody said anything."

"Emmett waved at you."

He shut his eyes for a second. "Ja. That tracks."

Her house was the fourth one down, small and gray, with a rusted-out boat trailer in the side yard and the porch light already on. Of course it was on.

"Want me to do the bloke?" he said quietly. "Glamour. Flannel. Your mum could look at a man for a bit before she has to look at the rest."

Leah thought about it.

She thought about Sue Clearwater, who had sat at the council fire for a year and a half listening to Billy and Old Quil talk about cold ones and treaty lines and which boys were running hot, and had kept every bit of it behind her face without once telling Charlie Swan. Who had seen Seth come out of the trees on four legs, grinning with his tongue out. Who had knelt on the kitchen floor and held her husband's head in her lap for forty minutes while the ambulance came from Forks.

"No," she said. "She should see."

"She'll see the teeth."

"She's seen teeth."

The front door opened before they reached the steps.

---

### II.

Sue Clearwater had been making chili since twenty minutes to midnight.

Seth had called from the Cullens' telephone and said, in one breath, *Mom Leah's back she's okay she's coming home and she's bringing the wolf guy and he's staying with us and he called Nessie a Furby I have to go bye*, and hung up before she could get out a single word.

So she'd made chili.

Twenty-two years married to a man who fished, and she'd learned that when a day went sideways you went to the kitchen, because a kitchen did what you told it. Two pounds of hamburger. Then the other two, because she knew her son. Onions. Three cans of kidney beans, two of tomatoes, chili powder out of the big jar from the Thriftway in Forks. Then a second pot, because the first one was too small for what a houseful of wolves could do to it. Then the fry bread dough, because her hands wanted something to push against, and she stood at the counter working it with the heels of her palms while the clock over the stove went from ten to twelve to midnight.

The note was still on the fridge.

She'd read it so many times the paper had gone soft at the creases. *Mom. I'm okay. I need to go away for a while.* It had been under the salmon magnet for eleven days. Charlie had seen it on Tuesday when he came by with a casserole somebody from the station's wife had made, and he'd asked, very carefully, if she wanted him to take it down for her. She'd said no. He hadn't asked again. That was the thing about Charlie. He never asked twice.

Wednesday night Seth had come through the back door at a dead run, crying, naked, shouting something about a man with a stick, about Leah tied up, Leah bleeding, Leah screaming, and Sue had stood at this counter holding the edge of it so hard she'd found bruises across her palms in the morning. Then the phone. Collect. *Tell them it's Leah.* Then, after one in the morning, Seth out in the wet yard rolling around in the grass laughing so hard he couldn't breathe, gasping out something about knock-offs, and when she went out on the step in her robe and asked him what on earth, he'd looked up at her with grass in his hair and said, "Nothing, Mom," and grinned for two days straight.

He'd called the man the wolf guy all week. The wolf guy did this. The wolf guy's got a coat. The wolf guy's on a *frog*, Mom, like a card, Jake saw it. Never a name. Seth, who could not keep a single thing to himself, who'd told her about his first kiss before he'd finished having it.

She hadn't thought anything of it until about ten minutes ago, standing at the counter with her hands in the dough. Now she was thinking about it.

At six minutes past midnight the back door banged open and Seth came in pulling his cut-offs up with one hand, dripping and steaming and grinning so wide she could see his back teeth.

"She's *riding* him," he said.

Sue put down the rolling pin. "Riding him."

"Like a *horse*. Mom. He's *huge*. He's bigger than Jake. He's bigger than *Sam*. He's like—" Seth held one hand way up over his own head, then gave up and held the other one up too. "And he doesn't make any noise. None. I could hear *me* the whole way and I couldn't hear him *at all*. It's so creepy. It's so cool."

"Put a shirt on."

"Is that chili?"

"Shirt, Seth."

He went down the hall. He came back without a shirt and with a piece of fry bread already in his mouth, which she hadn't seen him take.

"They stopped at the beach," he said around it. "I could smell them. Leah went down to the water." He swallowed. His face changed a little. "By where Dad used to go in."

Sue turned the heat down under the pot.

"Okay," she said.

She dried her hands on the towel over her shoulder and went to the front of the house. She wasn't going to have her daughter knock on her own front door like a stranger. Through the little window in the door she could see the street, the rain slanting through the light at the end of the block, the Ateara boys' trampoline. And her daughter coming up the street in the rain, in clothes Sue had never seen, beside something that walked upright and was taller than the Atearas' fence.

Sue watched them stop in the street. Watched Leah turn and reach up and pull something out of its head, standing very close. Watched it bend down so she could reach.

She opened the door.

---

The porch light was a bare bulb under a glass dome screwed into the wall above the door, at a height that had made sense for every person who'd ever stood on that porch until now. When he came up onto the bottom step, the light caught him from the chest down. His head stayed dark. Then he came up one more step and it caught two tall black ears, and two eyes that were very green.

Sue had known what her children were for a year and a half. She'd seen Seth come out of the trees on four legs, all sandy fur and big feet and pleased with himself. She'd seen Leah, once, at the edge of the yard on a night in March, gray and silent in the rain, staring at the house like she didn't know whose it was. She'd sat at the council fire and listened to Billy Black and Old Quil Ateara talk about the cold ones the way old men talked about the price of gas.

She'd never had a wolf stand up on two legs on her porch in a long gray coat with a silver clasp at the throat, holding a paper bag and a second coat over one arm, like a man come to pick up a girl for a dance.

It looked at her. There was a burnt patch of fur on top of its head, between the ears, and the whiskers on one side of its face had shriveled into little black curls. Rain beaded on the coat and didn't soak in. It smelled, as far as Sue could smell anything, of wet wool and pine.

"Mom," Leah said.

Sue looked at her daughter.

Leah's hair was wet and chopped and dripping into her collar. She had on a dark green top Sue had never seen, wrapped and tied at the side, and jeans, and a pair of brown pull-on boots a size too big. Her face was different. Sue couldn't have said how, exactly. She'd been looking at that face for nineteen years. For the last two it had looked like a fist. It didn't look like a fist.

And low on the left side of her neck, just where the green top crossed her collarbone, there was a mark.

A crescent. Two curved rows of small dents, deep and even, healed over with skin that shone silver under the porch light like the inside of a shell.

Sue looked at it for a long time.

She did not say anything about it. She put it away on a high shelf for later.

"You're home," she said.

"Yeah."

"You're wet."

"It's raining."

"I can see it's raining." Sue reached out and put the back of her hand against Leah's cheek, then her forehead, the way she'd done every time one of her kids came in from outside since they were small enough to carry. Leah had run at a hundred and eight degrees for a year and a half; there was no fever left to check for. Sue did it anyway. Leah let her. She stood on the step with her eyes half shut and let her mother's hand rest on her forehead for two whole seconds, which she had not done since she was fourteen.

Then Sue took her hand back and looked up. A long way up.

"Mrs. Clearwater," the wolf said. The voice was low and rough, with something growling along underneath the words, and the accent was nothing Sue had ever heard: English in some places, something else in others, the vowels landing in strange spots. "Evening. Sorry about the hour."

"Mom," Leah said. "This is Harry."

Sue's hand stopped on the doorframe.

*Harry.*

Twenty-two years of saying it. *Harry, boots off my floor. Harry, the bilge pump's going again. Harry, Leah's crying, it's your turn.* She'd said it to the 911 operator, kneeling on the kitchen linoleum with his head in her lap and Leah gone out the back door on four feet. *Harry. Harry. Stay with me, Harry.* She hadn't said it out loud since the funeral. Billy said it. Charlie said it, carefully, the way you'd pick up something that might be cracked. Sue didn't.

Behind her in the hall, Seth had stopped chewing.

The wolf's ears had been straight up. They came down halfway. His eyes went from Sue to Leah to the hall behind her, where Seth was standing with half a fry bread in his hand, and back to Sue. She watched him read every one of their faces, the way a man reads a map in a hurry.

"Something wrong with it?" he said quietly.

Nobody answered him.

Leah looked down at the porch boards.

"My dad's name was Harry," she said.

The wolf's ears went flat to his skull.

"Ah," he said.

The rain came down on the porch roof. Down the street a car door slammed.

"I can go by Potter, ma'am," he said. "If that's easier. Plenty of people do. Mostly the ones who don't like me."

Sue looked at him. At the singed whiskers and the flattened ears and the rain beading on the gray wool. At her daughter, standing beside him on the step with her chin down, not looking at either of them.

"Did your mother name you Harry?" Sue said.

The ears came up an inch. "Ja. Her and my dad."

"Then that's your name." Sue stepped back from the door. "Wipe your feet."

He looked down at what he had for feet: long and black and padded and clawed, and as far as Sue could tell, cleaner than her own. He looked back up at her. Then he wiped them on the mat anyway, one and then the other, carefully, scraping each one twice, like somebody had told him to once and meant it.

He ducked to come through the door, and the tips of his ears brushed the top of the frame anyway. His tail came in behind him and swept the umbrella stand by the door, and it went over in a clatter of her husband's big green golf umbrella and three of Seth's broken ones.

"Tail," Sue said.

He pulled it in tight against the backs of his legs. "Sorry."

Seth set the umbrellas back up. He did it without taking his eyes off the wolf once.

"Hi again," Seth said.

"Howzit, Seth."

"*Howzit*," Seth repeated under his breath, delighted.

"Coat," said Sue.

He took off the gray coat. Under it he wore a dark shirt with no sleeves and dark trousers that stopped above his ankles, and a leather belt with pouches on it, and his arms were covered in black fur, and they were very long. He looked around the little hall for somewhere to put the coat. There was a row of hooks by the hall closet. Two of them had Seth's sweatshirts on them. One had Sue's rain jacket.

He hung the gray coat on the fourth hook and the brown coat over it. He put Leah's paper bag on the floor underneath. He did not touch the closet door.

His nose was working. She watched it. His head turned, slowly, toward the closet, and stayed turned for about a second, and then turned away again.

Sue didn't know what he smelled in there. She had a fair idea.

"Kitchen," she said. "Before it burns."

---

The kitchen light hung over the table on a chain, a round stained-glass shade in green and amber that her husband had bought at a yard sale in Port Angeles because, he said, it looked like a jellyfish. The wolf's ears hit it on his way through. It swung, throwing green and amber light around the room. He put up a forepaw and stopped it, gently, with the pads, and ducked under it.

Leah came in behind him.

Sue saw her daughter's eyes go to the fridge. To the salmon magnet, and the note under it. Leah stood looking at it for a second with her face doing nothing at all.

Then she crossed the kitchen to the counter, and on the way she stepped around the square of linoleum in front of the stove. She didn't look down. She didn't slow down. She just went around it, the way she had every single time she crossed this kitchen for a year and a half.

Sue saw the wolf see it. His ears turned toward Leah and stayed there. He didn't say anything.

"Sit," Sue said.

He looked at the table. Four chairs. Three of them were the spindly pine ones with rush seats that had come with the house. The fourth, at the head, was a big oak captain's chair with arms, dark with forty years of hands. It had been her husband's father's, and then her husband's.

Leah went still at the counter. Seth went still in the doorway.

The wolf looked at the big chair. Then at Leah. Then at Seth. Then he pulled out one of the pine chairs and began to lower himself onto it.

It creaked like a tree going over in a storm.

"Not that one," Sue said. "You'll break it."

He stopped halfway down.

She tipped her head at the head of the table. "That one."

"Mom—" Leah said.

"It's a chair, Leah. It's the only one in the house that'll hold him." Sue went to the stove and took the lid off the big pot. "Sit down before you fall down. All of you."

She'd sat in it herself, a few times, at night, when the house was quiet. It was a chair.

The wolf looked at her back for a long moment. Then he sat down in the oak chair very carefully, like it might be made of sugar, and it took his weight without a sound. His tail went down through the gap between the back and the seat and lay along the floor. His knees came up nearly to the edge of the table.

Seth sat. Leah sat, after a second, in her old place on the left.

Sue put the big blue mixing bowl down in front of the wolf.

He looked at it. It held about as much chili as most people ate in a week.

"You get the big bowl," Sue said.

"Ta," he said. Then, after a second, "Thank you."

She gave him a spoon. In his forepaw it looked like a spoon from a child's tea set.

---

### III.

He ate the first bowl in about four minutes.

Sue watched him do it from the stove, where she was dropping rounds of dough into the hot oil in the cast-iron pan. He held the mixing bowl against his chest with one forepaw and used the tiny spoon with the other, and he didn't talk, and he didn't look up. He ate the way Seth had eaten the first month after he started running hot, the way her husband used to eat after twelve hours on the boat: like a man putting fuel in a tank before the tank ran dry.

The fry bread came out of the oil puffed and golden, and she stacked it on a plate lined with paper towels and put the plate in the middle of the table with the honey bear next to it.

Potter looked at the plate. His nose went. His ears went straight up.

"What's this?"

"Fry bread," said Seth, who already had two.

Potter took one in his claws and tore it in half. Steam came out. He squeezed honey onto it in a careless spiral, most of which went on his paw, and ate it in two bites, and sat there chewing with his eyes half closed.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said when he'd swallowed, "that is lekker."

"What?"

"Good. Very good." He held up the empty mixing bowl. "Could I—"

"Pot's on the stove."

He got up, ducked under the jellyfish lamp, and went to the stove. Sue stepped aside. He didn't take the whole pot. He took a lot of it. She watched exactly how much, and did the math, and added another trip to the Thriftway on Monday to the two she'd already planned.

He came back and sat down. There was honey in the fur on his chin and on the singed whiskers.

"What happened to your face?" Sue said.

He paused with the spoon halfway up.

"I set him on fire," Leah said.

Sue looked at her daughter. Leah was eating chili, neatly, not looking up.

"Good," said Sue.

Potter's ears went sideways. Seth made a noise into his fry bread.

"It was a bar," Leah said. "They have a beer. You drink it and you burp fire. Second pint, you get a flame."

Sue considered that for a while.

"And you burped it on him."

"He told me if I burped it on him it was his fault."

"I did say that," said Potter.

"Uh-huh," said Sue, and turned the next round of fry bread over in the oil.

---

"He called Nessie a Furby," Seth said, ten minutes later.

Sue had sat down at last, at the foot of the table, with a small bowl she wasn't eating. Seth was on his third bowl. Leah was on her second. Potter was on his third mixing bowl, which was the equivalent of about nine of Seth's, and was showing no sign of slowing down.

"Seth," Leah said.

"He did! In front of everybody! In front of *Rosalie*. She hissed at him, Mom. Like a cat. Like an actual cat."

"I said she was *like* one," said Potter.

"You said she was a *glorified* one."

"That's still *like*. It's a simile." He pointed his spoon at Leah. "She knows words. Ask her."

Sue looked at Potter.

"You called Bella's baby a Furby," she said. "In the Cullens' living room. To their faces."

"Ja."

"Why?"

He set the spoon down in the bowl. His ears went halfway back.

"She didn't blink, ma'am. Six minutes. Then she grabbed my tail and tried to put a picture in my head without asking." He picked up a fry bread and looked at it instead of at her. "I didn't care for it."

Sue had been hearing about the baby for three weeks. From Seth, with the same face he used to have about puppies. From Billy, on the phone, grave and slow. From Jacob not at all, because Jacob didn't come by anymore, because Jacob was at that house every hour of every day, because he had looked at a newborn and gone stupid in exactly the way Sam Uley had gone stupid over Emily. Sue hadn't seen the child. She'd been asked to keep Charlie from seeing the child, which was a different thing.

She kept her face still.

"Rosalie hissed at you," she said.

"Ja."

"Huh," said Sue, and that was all she said about it.

---

"How old are you?" she said, around the fourth mixing bowl.

Potter looked up. "Twenty-six."

Sue looked at Leah.

"She's nineteen," Sue said.

"Ja."

"That's seven years."

"Ja. I can count, ma'am."

"Mom," Leah said.

"I'm just saying the number out loud." Sue turned her spoon over in her bowl. "What do you do? For work."

"I find people who've run. Bring them back."

"And if they won't come?"

He took his time with that one.

"Mostly they come," he said.

"He killed a guy," Seth said, "with, like, a laser—"

"Seth."

"—a *purple* one, it went right *through* him—"

"*Seth*," said Sue and Leah together.

Seth subsided into his chili.

Sue looked at Potter across the length of the table. He looked back at her. He didn't fidget. He didn't look away and he didn't try to smile at her, which she noted, because men who'd done something they shouldn't usually tried to smile at her.

"Seth says that man hurt her," Sue said.

"He did."

"Seth says you stopped him."

"She found him first." Potter's voice didn't change. "Found him by smell from a kilometre down the road and went straight through his wards after a girl he'd taken, knowing he was waiting for her. I turned up after."

Sue looked at Leah. Leah was looking at her chili. Her ears had gone red.

"That sounds like her," Sue said.

Leah didn't look up.

"And now you're here to look at us," said Sue.

Potter put his spoon down.

"At the Cullens," he said. "And at your lot. Ja."

"I sit on the tribal council."

His ears came straight up. "Do you."

"Since my husband died. It was his seat."

"Then you're who I ought to be talking to."

"You're talking to me." Sue folded her hands on the table. "What are you going to write?"

He looked at her for a second. Then, to her surprise, he answered.

"Right now? Wolves, large, several, seen by the public on more than one occasion. Photographed off a highway." He picked up a fry bread. "Bloke called Paul."

Sue closed her eyes.

"The 101," Leah said.

"I've told that boy," said Sue, with her eyes still closed. "I have *told* that boy." She opened them. "What happens to them? If you write it like that?"

"Somebody else comes. Somebody who's never met a wolf that talks. They bring forms, and a lot of people, and they register every one of your kids. Numbers. Classifications." His ears went flat. "There's a box on mine for my teeth."

"My children," Sue said, "are not getting numbers."

"No, ma'am. Not if I can help it."

"Can you?"

"Depends on Paul."

Sue looked at him across the table for a long moment.

"I'll talk to Paul," she said.

---

The mirror went off at ten to one.

It was a high, angry buzzing, like a hornet in a jar, and it came from one of the pouches on Potter's belt. He ignored it. It got louder. It started to rattle.

"Your pocket's talking," Sue said.

"It does that."

"*Potter*." A man's voice, small and tinny and very annoyed, came out through the leather. "Potter, I know you're there. The Director has seen the *Sentinel*. The Director would like to know why the front page of the *Sentinel* has you being *set on fire*—"

Potter sighed through his nose, unbuttoned the pouch, and took out a small bronze mirror about the size of a compact. Sue leaned over to look. There was a face in it. A narrow, tired face with thinning hair, a man in a dressing gown who looked like he'd been awake for days.

"I'm eating, Hennessey."

"It's *morning* here, Potter, it's nearly ten o'clock on a *Sunday* and I have been on the Floo with Geneva since *six*—" The face's eyes went sideways and found Sue. They widened. "Who's that? Is that *another* one? Potter, how many civilians have you—"

Potter looked around the kitchen. His eyes landed on the bread box on the counter, the old enamel one with the roll-top lid. He reached over without getting up, rolled the lid back, put the mirror inside face up, and rolled the lid shut.

The voice went on inside it, muffled and outraged.

Sue looked at the bread box. She looked at him.

"You put your boss in my bread box."

"He likes it in there. It's dark. It's quiet."

"Mrs. Pillay put the Furby in the freezer," said Seth.

"Who," said Sue, "is Mrs. Pillay?"

"My neighbour," said Potter. "Downstairs. In Cape Town."

"Does she put things in the freezer a lot?"

"Things that talk to her in the dark."

Sue looked at the bread box, which was still muttering, and decided she didn't need to know anything else about Mrs. Pillay.

"Leave him in there," she said. "He's fine."

---

The phone on the wall rang at four minutes past one.

Sue knew who it was before she got up. Nobody else called this house after midnight. She took the receiver off the hook and stretched the cord out into the hall, around the corner, which had never once given her any privacy in twenty-two years and didn't now.

"Hi, Charlie."

"Sue. Hey. Sorry. I know it's late." Charlie Swan sounded the way he'd sounded for weeks, like a man talking from the bottom of a well he'd been trying to climb out of for a while and had stopped expecting to. "Couldn't sleep. Was just sitting here. Carlisle called earlier. Says she's turning a corner. Says maybe I can see her soon." A pause. "He keeps saying soon."

"That's good, Charlie."

"Yeah. Yeah, I guess." Another pause. She could hear the TV going low in his living room. A ballgame, replaying. "You okay? You sound funny."

Sue looked back around the corner into her kitchen.

There was a seven-foot wolf sitting in her dead husband's chair eating chili out of her mixing bowl, with honey on his whiskers. Both his ears were pointed straight at the hall. Seth was pretending not to listen. Leah wasn't pretending.

"Leah's home," Sue said.

"Oh—" Charlie's voice came up out of the well a few feet. "Sue. That's great. That's real good. Is she okay? Is she all right?"

"She's fine."

"Where'd she go?"

"Canada."

"*Canada*." She could hear him shake his head. "Huh. Well. Kids. Tell her—tell her welcome home from me, okay? And hey, you want me to come by tomorrow? I could bring donuts. Those maple ones from the place in Forks she likes."

Sue looked at the wolf.

"Not tomorrow," she said.

"Oh. Okay. Sure. No, that's—you should have her to yourself. That's good." He cleared his throat. "Night, Sue."

"Night, Charlie."

She hung the receiver back on the hook and stood there a moment with her hand on it.

When she came back into the kitchen, Potter was looking at her. He'd heard all of it. She'd known he would. He didn't say anything.

"Don't," Sue said.

"Didn't say anything, ma'am."

"You were going to."

"He was," said Leah.

Potter looked at the phone on the wall. His ears had gone halfway down.

"He sounds knackered," he said.

Sue didn't know the word. She knew what it meant.

"His only daughter got married," Sue said, "and got sick two weeks later, and nobody will let him see her. He sits up every night. He calls the Cullens' house every day. He calls me after."

"And you know why."

"Yes."

"And he doesn't."

"No."

"About any of it?" He tipped his muzzle at Seth, then Leah. "Them? Her?"

"No."

He looked at her for a while.

"That's a lot to carry, ma'am," he said.

Sue sat back down in her chair at the foot of the table and picked up her spoon.

"Eat your chili," she said.

---

When the fourth mixing bowl was empty and the fry bread was down to two, Sue put her spoon down.

"Leah," she said. "Your neck."

Seth inhaled a kidney bean.

He coughed for a while. Leah thumped him on the back without looking at him. When he could breathe again he stood up so fast the pine chair went over backward.

"I'm gonna shower," he said. "I'm gonna shower *right now*. For a long time. Really long. With the water really loud." He picked up the chair, then picked up his bowl, then put the bowl back down. "Goodnight. I love you. Bye."

He was gone down the hall. The bathroom door slammed. A second later the shower came on at full blast, and Seth began to sing over it, loudly and badly, something that was mostly the word *baby*.

Sue looked across the table at her daughter.

Leah looked back. Her chin was up. It had been up like that at four years old when she'd cut her own bangs with the kitchen scissors.

Sue looked at Potter.

"Did you do that?"

"Ja," he said.

"I did one on him too," said Leah.

Potter reached up and hooked one claw into the neck of his dark shirt and pulled it a few inches sideways. Sue leaned forward. On the left side, where his neck ran down into his shoulder, the black fur grew crooked over an old scar, a long uneven line like a part. And around it, cutting across it, there was a ring of short new bristles where the fur was growing back in. She could see the shape of it. She'd seen that shape on a sandwich Leah had bitten into at five years old and left on the counter.

Potter let go of the shirt.

Sue sat back.

"You don't scar," she said to Leah.

"I know."

"You came home in June with your face open from here to here, and it was gone by breakfast."

"I know, Mom."

"So what is it?"

Leah looked at Potter.

"Don't know yet," Potter said.

Sue turned her head slowly and looked at him.

"You don't know."

"No, ma'am."

"You bit my daughter," Sue said, "and you don't know what it does."

"Neither does she." His ears were all the way back, but he kept looking at her. "Nobody's ever done it before. Not her people. Not mine. We're finding out."

"Finding out."

"Ja."

The shower roared down the hall. Seth hit a high note and missed it.

Sue looked at her daughter for a long time. Nineteen. Taller than her by four inches. Faster than anything on the coast. Two years of coming home angry, eating standing up at the counter, going out again without a word. Two years of a face like a fist.

"Did you want it?" Sue said.

"Yes," said Leah.

She said it right away. She didn't look at him first.

Sue nodded once.

Then she stood up and took her bowl to the sink.

"He's on the couch," she said, with her back to them.

"*Mom*."

"My house, Leah."

"I'm nineteen—"

"And it's my house." Sue ran the water. "He's on the couch."

"Couch is fine, ma'am," Potter said.

"It's too short for you."

"Most things are."

---

He stood up to clear the table, and every dish on it stood up with him.

The mixing bowl. The plates. The spoons, Seth's abandoned bowl, the frying pan off the stove, the empty fry bread plate with its greasy paper towels. All of it lifted an inch off the table and hung there, and then began to drift toward the sink in a slow, tidy line, like ducks crossing a road.

Sue turned around from the sink with her hands dripping.

"Put those down," she said.

Everything stopped in midair.

Then it all came down, very gently, exactly where it had been, without a sound. The frying pan settled back onto its burner. A spoon rolled once and stopped.

Potter's ears went sideways.

"In this house," said Sue, "we wash by hand."

He looked at her for a second. Then he picked up the mixing bowl in his forepaws, the ordinary way, and brought it to the sink.

"Leah," Sue said. "Go and get Seth out of that shower before he uses all the hot water, and then take one yourself. You smell like a forest."

Leah looked from her mother to Potter and back.

"Don't," she said to both of them, and went.

Sue ran the sink full and squeezed in the dish soap. She handed him the sponge without a word. He took it. He had to stand bent over to reach the sink, and his elbows stuck out wide on both sides, and his tail swung once behind him and knocked a dish towel off the oven handle. He looked back at it. He held his tail still.

He washed with the pads of his forepaws. The suds got into the fur up to his wrists and stayed there in clumps. He went slowly. He went around the rim of every bowl and under every spoon, and he held each plate up to the light from the jellyfish lamp before he handed it to her. Sue dried.

She took the third bowl he gave her, looked at it, and put it back in the sink.

He watched her do it. His ears went sideways. He didn't say anything. He washed it again.

They did the plates. They did the spoons. They did the big pot, which he scrubbed for a long time, carefully, claws in.

"What do you want with her?" Sue said to the window over the sink.

He didn't answer right away. He scrubbed the pot.

"I'd like her to come back to Cape Town with me some time," he said, "and set fire to the other curtain."

Sue looked at him.

"Long story," he said. "She'll tell you. Or she won't."

"That's not an answer."

"No." He rinsed the pot and held it out, and she took it. "I'd like her to keep deciding things. Whatever she decides. I'd like to be around for it, if she wants me around. That's the lot."

"That's a lot of *if*."

"Ja."

Sue dried the pot. She put it in the cupboard under the counter where it lived.

"Her father," she said, "took two years to ask me to the movies."

His ears came up. "Slow bloke."

Sue gave him a look she had given four hundred teenagers in twenty years of running the summer lunch program at the community center.

"Sorry," said Potter.

"He was careful," Sue said.

"Ja." He pulled the plug. The water went down the drain with a long gurgle. "I'm trying, ma'am."

"Try harder."

"Yes, ma'am."

On the windowsill over the sink there was a photograph in a cheap gold frame, faded at one corner from twenty years of sun. Three men standing on the dock at the marina with a boat behind them and a big king salmon held up between them, all three of them grinning. Potter's eyes had gone to it twice while he washed. Now he looked at it for longer.

"Who's that?"

"My husband," said Sue. She pointed with the dish towel. "Charlie Swan. And Billy Black, before his legs went."

His left ear flicked. Once. Sharp, like a horse twitching off a fly.

He looked at Billy's face in the photo for a long moment. Billy at thirty-something, broad, laughing at something off to one side, his hair in a braid down his back and the salmon's tail in his fist.

"You know Billy?" Sue said.

"No, ma'am." His eyes stayed on the picture. "Met his son tonight."

"Jacob."

"Ja."

"What did you think?"

He looked away from the photo at last. His face didn't give her anything.

"He's got a lot on," he said.

Sue hung the dish towel over the oven handle where it went. She looked at the fur on her kitchen floor. There was a lot of it. There was fur on the oak chair, and fur on the linoleum under it, and fur on the rug by the door in a drift like dryer lint. There were black hairs floating in the last of the dishwater.

"You're shedding on my floor," she said.

He looked down at the floor. His ears went flat. "It's spring. Where I live. Sorry."

"It's September here."

"I know, ma'am. My coat doesn't."

Sue took the last two pieces of fry bread off the plate, put them on a paper towel, and held them out to him.

"Porch," she said.

---

### IV.

The rain had thinned to a drizzle that hung in the porch light like smoke.

Harry sat on the top step with his footpaws on the third one down and the two pieces of fry bread on a paper towel on his knee, and ate the first one slowly, because there were only two.

Behind him, through the screen door and down the hall, the house went on without him. Seth had come out of the bathroom and been sent to bed and was now in his room, telling somebody on the phone, in a whisper he thought was quiet, that the wolf guy had washed the *dishes*, dude, like with his *paws*. Leah was in the shower. Mrs. Clearwater was in the kitchen wrapping the leftover chili in foil and putting it in the fridge, and every so often she stopped moving, and he could hear her standing still.

And in the bread box, Hennessey was still talking.

"—not a *joke*, Potter, the Director has asked me to confirm in writing that the young woman in the photograph is not a *registered* anything, and I said I'd *ask*, and I'd like to be able to *ask*—"

Harry ate his fry bread.

He'd worked out three things in that kitchen, and none of them were any of his business.

The first was the floor. Leah had crossed the kitchen twice, and both times she'd gone around the same square of linoleum in front of the stove. She hadn't looked at it either time. She didn't need to. Her feet knew where it was. Her mother had watched her do it and said nothing, and he'd watched her mother watch, and said nothing, and that was where he'd left it.

The second was the hall closet. He'd smelled it when he hung up the coat: diesel and salt water and engine grease, cheap aftershave, pipe tobacco or something like it, all of it faded down to almost nothing, held in a dozen wool shirts that nobody had worn for a long time. A man who'd fished. A man who'd been gone a while. He'd hung his coat next to them and kept his paws off the door.

The third was the photograph on the windowsill.

Billy Black at thirty-something, holding a salmon by the tail, laughing at somebody out of frame. A wide, square face. A heavy jaw. A set to the eyes that Harry had seen an hour ago on the man's son at the edge of the Cullens' lawn, glaring up at him in the rain.

And something else. Something about the line of the brow, the shape of the face under the laugh. He'd looked at it, and something had gone *click* very quietly in the back of his head, the way a key goes into a lock it fits.

He'd taken the click and put it in a room and shut the door on it. Ten years of practice. Occlumency was mostly that: knowing which door to shut, and shutting it before you'd finished reading what was on the other side.

It wasn't his. Leah had a box of her own with that name in it. He'd watched her sit on the lid of it all night, and it was hers to open or not.

He finished the first fry bread and looked at the second.

The shower shut off.

---

She came out ten minutes later in gray sweatpants and an old navy hoodie with LA PUSH across the front in letters that had mostly peeled off, barefoot, with her wet hair pushed back off her face. She smelled of somebody else's shampoo, coconut and something chemical, and under it, still, faintly, of him.

She sat down on the top step next to him. Close. Her shoulder against his arm.

He held out the second fry bread.

She looked at it. She looked at him.

"That's your last one."

"Ja."

She took it and ate half of it in one bite and gave him back the other half. He ate it. Neither of them said anything about it.

The rain ticked on the porch roof. Down the street a TV was going in somebody's front room, blue light flickering on the curtains. Out past the houses, past the trees, he could hear the surf coming in on the beach, a long low roar every few seconds like something very big breathing in its sleep.

"You stepped around it," he said.

She went still against his arm.

"You noticed."

"Ja."

She was quiet for a long time. He didn't push. He looked at the street.

"He was standing at the stove," she said finally. "I'd been to the gas station. Sam and Emily were there in his truck, at the pump, and she was laughing at something he said, and he had his hand on the back of her neck. And I came home and I was yelling. At Dad. About Sam. About nothing. I'd been hot for days. I thought I had the flu." She turned the rest of the paper towel over in her fingers. "And then I wasn't standing on two feet anymore. I was standing on four. In the kitchen. And Dad was looking at me. And his hand went here."

She put her fist flat against her own chest, over her heart.

"The ambulance takes forty minutes from Forks," she said. "Mom held him the whole time. I was out back in the trees. I couldn't change back. I couldn't figure out how. I could hear her through the wall saying his name."

Harry didn't say anything.

He leaned a little, so that more of his side was against hers, and kept it there.

"I don't step on it," Leah said. "That's all. I just don't."

"Ja."

"Don't tell Mom."

"She knows, Leah."

She let her breath out slowly.

"Yeah," she said. "She does."

The surf came in. Somewhere off toward the beach an owl called, two notes, and didn't call again.

"Did you know?" she said. "About the name?"

"No."

"I knew Wednesday. In the clearing. When you said it." She pulled her knees up inside the hoodie. "I didn't tell you. I thought about telling you about ten times."

"Your brother called me *the wolf guy* all night," Harry said. "In front of your mum. I thought it was a nickname."

"He's been doing it since Wednesday. On purpose."

Harry thought about Seth on the lawn at the Cullens', fifteen and grinning with his arm still not quite right from a newborn's grip, saying *are you, like, with her?*

"Good kid," he said.

"Don't you dare tell him that. He'll be unbearable."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

Behind them, through the screen door, Mrs. Clearwater had stopped moving in the kitchen again. Harry could hear her standing at the counter with her hands still, close enough to the door to hear.

He let her.

---

The wind came round at twenty past one.

It had been blowing in off the ocean all night, salt and kelp and rain. Then it turned, the way it does on a coast after midnight, and came down out of the trees behind the village instead. Spruce. Wet earth. Woodsmoke from the chimney down the road.

And wolves.

Three of them. Rain and cedar and fur, like Leah, from the same place and the same blood, and each of them different. One was young and nervous and smelled of the cheap body spray teenage boys drown themselves in. One was angry, hot and sour, like a pan left on a burner. And one was heavier than the other two, and older, and walking out in front, and on top of the wolf smell there was woodsmoke and coffee and a woman's lotion, something with cucumber in it, laid on like a hand on a shoulder.

A fourth stayed back in the trees. He could hear it breathing. Four legs. It stopped moving at the edge of the timber, two hundred yards out, and stayed there.

Leah went rigid against his arm.

"Company," Harry said.

"I know." Her voice had gone flat and quiet. "I can smell them."

Three men came out of the trees at the bottom of the street, on two legs, in cut-off jeans and nothing else, steaming in the rain.

Harry looked at them. The one in front was big. Six-five, maybe six-six. Broad through the chest and shoulders in the way of a man who'd carried heavy things up hills since he was a boy. Short black hair. A wide, grave face that looked like it had been carved out of something hard and then left out in the weather for a long time. He walked up the middle of the street like the street was his. The two behind him were younger and leaner and flanked him a pace back, one on each side, the way soldiers walk with an officer.

"Sam," Leah said.

"Which one?"

"In front."

Harry looked at him for a while longer.

"Want me to go inside?" he said.

"No."

"Want me to do anything?"

"Don't do *anything*."

"I'm eating."

"There's no food left."

Harry looked down at the paper towel on his knee. There were crumbs on it. He picked one up on the end of a claw and ate it.

"I'm eating," he said again.

Leah made a sound that was halfway to a laugh and halfway to something else, and didn't move away from his arm.

The three men turned in at the gate.

---

### V.

Jared had caught it at two minutes to midnight, running the inside of the line west of Forks.

Sam had been asleep. Emily had been asleep against him with her hair across his chest and her bad side turned into the pillow, the way she always slept, and he had come out of a dream about nothing into Jared's head all at once, because Jared had phased so hard and so loud it was like somebody kicking open a door.

*Sam. Sam, something just crossed the line.*

Something big. Through Jared's nose it had come off the timber like heat off a stove: wolf, enormous, nothing Sam knew and nothing from here, a smell like pine pitch and wet granite. It had come over the line at a run from the Cullen side and kept going west toward the village without slowing down. It had left prints in the soft ground at the creek crossing that Jared could have lain down in.

And riding on top of it, soaked into it, all through it: Leah.

Sam hadn't smelled Leah in weeks. Not since the night she followed Jacob out of the pack and out of his head, and it had been like somebody switching off a radio in the next room after a year of it playing too loud. He'd been ashamed of how glad he was. He was still ashamed. He'd never told anybody. There'd been nobody to tell; there was nobody in his head now who hadn't been glad too.

He'd slid out from under Emily's arm without waking her. He'd phased in the yard. Paul and Embry had come in on the way, and the three of them had run the trail, and the trail had gone down onto First Beach and stopped by the drift logs for a long while, and then gone straight up through the village to Sue Clearwater's front door.

Sam had made them stop at the tree line. They'd stood there in the dripping spruce above the village for most of an hour, downwind of the house, while Sam decided whether to go down at all. Then he'd made Paul and Embry phase back with him. He'd left Jared in the trees on four legs, watching.

You didn't go to an elder's house at one in the morning on four legs. Not even for this.

He came in at Sue's gate in his cut-offs with the rain running down his back and steam coming off his shoulders, and he stopped.

There was a porch light on. There was Leah, on the top step, in a hoodie Sam had last seen on Harry Clearwater at a basketball game, with her wet hair pushed back and her knees up. She was sitting close against something.

It was eating crumbs off a paper towel.

It was sitting on the top step of Sue Clearwater's porch the way a man sits on his own porch, with its back to the screen door and its feet on the third step down, long black padded things with claws on the ends. Its knees were up near its chest, and it was all black fur, with long arms resting across the knees and a long muzzle and two tall ears that turned toward Sam as he came through the gate, and stayed there. A tail lay along the step beside it, black, the end of it hanging over the edge.

It looked at him with green eyes and went on eating crumbs.

Up close the smell was worse than through Jared's nose. Wolf, but nothing like the pack. Pine and stone and cold water. And underneath that, so faint he almost missed it, something else: salt and cedar and woodsmoke, like the inside of Billy Black's kitchen on a winter morning. It made no sense. Sam put it down and didn't pick it up again.

Behind him, Paul and Embry came up to the gate and stopped on either side of it.

"Leah," Sam said.

"Sam," said Leah.

Her voice was flat and even. She didn't look away from him. She didn't look at him the old way either, the way she had for a year after, every time he walked into a room: like a dog that had been kicked and was waiting to see if the foot was coming again. She just looked.

Sam made himself look at the other one.

"You're on Quileute land," he said.

"Ja." The voice was low and rough and had a growl in it that made the hair on Sam's arms lift, and an accent from nowhere he'd ever been. "Evening. You'll be Sam."

"What are you?"

"Tired, mostly." It ate another crumb. "Harry Potter. I work for a lot of people in Geneva who'd very much like me to be in bed. Leah asked me over."

"Leah doesn't get to—"

Sam stopped. It was Sue's house.

"Her mum did too, technically," the thing said. "Told me to wipe my feet. I wiped them."

Its eyes went past Sam's shoulder to the gate, and stayed on Embry.

"And you'll be Embry," it said.

Embry jumped. "Uh. Yeah?"

The thing looked at Embry for a long moment. Its nose worked, slowly. It looked him up and down, from his bare feet to his wet hair and back.

"Hm," it said.

"What?" said Embry.

"Nothing." It went back to its crumbs. "Disappointed."

Embry stared at it. Then at Leah. Then back. "What'd I *do*?"

"Nothing, boet," said the thing. "That's the problem."

Leah made a sound into her knees.

"Who the hell *is* this guy?" Paul said.

The green eyes moved over to him. "And you're Paul."

"How do you know my—"

"Stay off the 101."

Paul's mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

"There's a photo," the thing said. "Hiking forum. American. Big gray wolf in a clearing off the highway, size of a Land Rover, broad daylight. Four hundred comments." Its ears flicked sideways. "Half of them reckon you're Bigfoot's dog."

Embry made a strangled noise.

Paul's face went dark red. Sam saw the tremor start in his hands, then his forearms, the skin rippling like something under water was trying to get out. Heat poured off him. Paul had been the worst of them at holding it since the day he first phased, and he'd taken a swing at Sam over a joke about Rachel Black's cooking not three weeks ago.

"*Paul*," Sam said.

He put the weight behind it. The voice. It went out of his chest and down into Paul like a hand pressing on the back of his neck, and Paul stopped. The tremor stopped. Paul stood at the gate breathing hard through his nose, glaring.

On the porch, the thing hadn't moved. It hadn't even put the paper towel down. Its ears had come up, very slightly, when Sam used the voice, and turned toward him, the way a dog's ears go up at a whistle. Then they relaxed again.

"Lekker trick, that," it said. "Saves on shouting."

Sam ignored it.

"Does Jacob know he's here?"

"Jake met him," said Leah.

"Jacob let something like that onto our land—"

"Jake didn't let anybody do anything. I did." Leah's chin came up. "And Jake's not your problem anymore, Sam. Neither am I."

"You're on *my* side of the line. This is *my*—"

"It's my porch, Sam."

The screen door had opened behind them. Sue Clearwater stood in it, in her slippers and an old cardigan with a dish towel over her shoulder, looking down at Sam the way she'd looked at him when he was nine and had put a baseball through her kitchen window.

"Sue," said Sam. "I'm sorry. It's late. Jared caught—"

"I know what Jared caught. He's sitting on my steps." Sue looked past him at the gate. "Paul. The 101."

Paul's mouth opened. "What? How does *everybody*—"

"*Paul*."

Paul shut his mouth.

"I will be speaking to you," Sue said, "about the 101."

She stood there a moment longer. Then she went back inside. The screen door eased shut on its spring. Sam could see her through it, not far, a shape moving in the kitchen at the end of the hall. She had left the inside door open.

Sam turned back to the porch.

"What are you here for?" he said.

"Work." The thing finally put the paper towel down on the step beside it. "The Cullens. And you lot. There's people a long way off who'd like to know what you are and whether anybody else has noticed." It tipped its muzzle at Paul. "Somebody has."

"So you're here to spy on us."

"I'm here to write something sensible before somebody else comes and writes something stupid."

"And Leah's helping you."

"Leah's keeping me honest."

"Leah," Sam said, "has known you for *four days*."

"And you knew Emily," said Leah, "for one look."

It went into him like a nail.

Sam stood very still on Sue Clearwater's front walk with the rain on his shoulders and felt it go in. She'd said it quietly, the way you'd tell somebody the time.

He looked at her.

And that was when he saw her neck.

Low on the left side, where the hoodie had slipped. A crescent. Two curved rows of small deep marks, healed silver, shining a little in the porch light.

He knew what that was. He'd grown up with a dog under every porch on the rez. He'd been a wolf for two years. He knew what it meant when one animal put its teeth there on another and the other one let it.

And Leah didn't scar. Sam knew that better than anybody living. He'd been inside her head the night in June when a newborn opened her face from her temple to her jaw, and he'd felt the pain of it like it was his own, and he'd felt it close by morning.

Something went through him that he didn't have a name for, and didn't want one.

"He bit you," Sam said.

"Yeah," said Leah.

Sam looked up at the thing on the step.

"You *marked* her," he said. His voice had gone low. "Like an animal."

"She marked me back."

It said it easily, the way it had said everything else. It reached up with one long black forepaw and hooked a claw in the collar of its shirt and pulled it sideways.

On the left side of its neck, where its neck ran down into its shoulder, the black fur grew crooked over some old scar. And over the scar, around it, cutting across it, there was a ring of short new fur growing back in a shape that Sam knew.

He'd run beside Leah for a year. He'd seen her jaws close on a newborn's arm through her own eyes, from two feet away. He knew the spacing of her teeth the way he knew the road to his own house.

The thing let go of its collar.

Sam stood there.

"Leah," he said, and it came out wrong, rough and too quiet. "You don't know what he is. You don't know what that *does*. You don't scar. Nothing scars you. You don't know what he's done to—"

"And that's your business how?"

"Because I—"

He stopped.

Because he what?

He heard it hanging there. They all heard it. Embry shifted his weight at the gate. Paul had gone very quiet.

"Because you're one of us," Sam said.

The thing on the step licked a smear of honey off the side of one claw. It took its time about it.

"Boet," it said.

Sam looked at it.

"Don't be cross with her," it said, "for finally getting laid."

The street went silent.

"*Harry*," said Leah.

"What? He's thinking it. Look at his face." It didn't look away from Sam. Its ears were up, both of them, easy and forward, and its tail had started moving along the step, slow, side to side, like a man tapping his foot to something on the radio. "You had her, boet. Before the fur, before any of it. Fastest wolf this coast has ever had, the only one of her in God knows how long, sharp as a tack and twice as mean, and she was going to *marry* you. If you'd been a proper, functioning wolf, you'd have known what you had." It shrugged, a lot of shoulder moving under black fur. "You didn't. So."

"You don't know anything about—"

"I know you looked at somebody else and walked off. Rest's not my business." Its tail swept the step. "And you'll get no complaints from me, mind. None whatsoever. Your loss, my gain. If anything I owe you a fruit basket."

Then its ears tipped forward a little further. It leaned in, forearms on its knees, the way a man leans in across a table to tell you something just between the two of you, and it dropped its voice.

It dropped it into a whisper. The kind of whisper Sam's mother used in church during the sermon to tell his aunt something about somebody two pews up. The kind built to carry to the back wall.

"Only I do wonder sometimes," it whispered, "whether a regular old human was really worth the trade. Because the sex—" it paused, "—is *fucking great*. Especially the rough shit."

It sat back.

It closed its eyes halfway, and it hummed. Deep in its chest. Long and low and satisfied, like a man tasting something very good off the end of a spoon.

"Mm," it said. "Mhm. Mhm-mhm-mhm*mmm*."

From the far end of the hall, through the open door and the screen, came Sue Clearwater's voice, perfectly level.

"I can *hear* you, Harry."

Embry made a sound like a balloon with the air coming out of it. He doubled over at the gate with both hands on his knees.

Paul barked. One short, involuntary bark of laughter, loud as a gunshot in the wet street. Then he looked at Sam, and swallowed the rest of it so hard Sam heard his throat click.

Leah had both hands over her face. Her shoulders were shaking. Sam couldn't tell, for a second, which way.

Then she lowered her hands, and her face was red from her collar to her hairline, and she was laughing. Silently, helplessly, with her eyes wet. And she wasn't looking at Sam. She was looking at the thing next to her, the way she'd looked at the ocean when she was sixteen.

Sam had last seen her laugh like that the summer before he phased. On the dock at First Beach, with Harry Clearwater soaking wet beside her and Seth screaming, after Harry'd gone off the end of it trying to show Seth how to cast.

He'd been there. He'd been the one standing next to her.

The heat came up through him like a grass fire.

It hadn't done that in a year. He'd built a wall around it after Emily, after that night, after the three long ragged lines across her face that he would see behind his eyes on his deathbed. He held it now. He held everything, all the time. And it came up through the wall anyway, into his chest and down his arms and into his hands, and he felt his spine begin to lengthen.

"*Don't*," he said, and his voice had gone to gravel, "you *talk about Emily*."

The thing stood up.

It didn't do it fast. It unfolded off the step, slowly, one piece at a time, footpaws and then knees and then the long back, until it stood on the porch at its full height. Sam had been the tallest man in La Push from fifteen until Jacob Black shot past him last year. He had to tip his head back. Not much. A couple of inches, and then the ears on top of that.

It didn't growl. It didn't bare its teeth. Its ears had stayed up. Its tail had stopped moving, and hung straight down behind it, still as a plumb line.

It was waiting to see what he'd do.

Sam's hands were shaking. He could feel fur under his skin. He could feel the shape of his jaw starting to change.

"Sam Uley," said Sue Clearwater, behind the screen. "If you phase in my front yard, I will call your mother."

The heat stopped.

It just stopped, the way a pot stops boiling when you take it off the stove. Sam stood on Sue's front walk with his hands open at his sides, breathing.

"Sorry, Sue," he said.

"Uh-huh."

Sam made himself look at the porch.

"The council," he said, "is going to want to see him."

"I'm on the council, Sam," said Sue.

"Billy. And Old Quil." He kept his voice level. It cost him. "Noon. At Billy's. He comes. Or he goes back where he came from tonight."

There was a silence from behind the screen.

"I'll be there," Sue said. "So will he."

"I'll bring a pie," said the thing on the porch.

Sam didn't answer it. He looked at Leah.

She'd stopped laughing. She was sitting on the top step with her arms around her knees, looking at him, and her face was still red, and her eyes were still wet, and there was something in them he'd never seen there before. Not once. Not in a year of hearing every thought she had.

It took him a second to understand what it was.

She didn't hate him.

"Go home, Sam," Leah said. She said it quietly. She said it like she meant it kindly. "Emily's waiting up."

Sam stood there.

Then he turned around and walked out through Sue Clearwater's gate, between Paul and Embry, and up the middle of the wet street toward the trees, and the two of them fell in behind him, one on each side, a pace back.

He didn't look back.

He knew, the way you know a thing in your body before your head catches up to it, that if he had looked back she wouldn't have been watching him go. And that was the first time in his life that had been true.

Behind him, faint and getting fainter, Embry said, "What does he mean, *disappointed*?"

"Shut up, Embry," said Paul.

At the edge of the trees, Jared was waiting on four legs. Sam didn't phase. He walked past Jared into the dark, on two feet, and kept walking, and he didn't let himself think about anything at all until he could smell the cucumber lotion on Emily's pillow through his own bedroom window.

---

### VI.

Leah watched the three of them walk up the street into the dark until the trees took them.

Sam didn't look back. She'd known he wouldn't. She found she didn't need him to.

She waited for the thing that always came after Sam. The stone under her ribs. The one she'd carried around for two years like something swallowed wrong, that turned over every time he walked into a room or crossed her mind or ran past her in the pack with Emily all through his head. She'd felt it turn over just now, when he came through the gate. She'd felt it when he said *one of us*.

She reached for it.

It was there. She could find the shape of it. It had stopped hurting to touch. It felt like the mark on her neck: a thing that had happened to her, healed over, shiny, and hers.

She turned on the step and punched Harry in the thigh as hard as she could.

"Ow."

"*Emily*?"

His ears went flat. "Ja."

"You stood on my mother's porch and said that about *Emily*."

"Ja. I did."

"She's got three scars on her face from him." Leah's voice was shaking and she hated that and kept going. "Right across. From her hair to her mouth. He did it in her own kitchen because she was standing too close when he lost it. She didn't trade anything, Harry. She didn't *get* anything. She got looked at, same as me, and nobody asked her either. She didn't get a vote any more than I did."

He was quiet for a second. His tail had gone completely still on the step.

"That one was about him," he said. "And I hit her with it."

"Yeah. You did."

"Sorry."

"Don't tell *me*. I don't even like her half the time." Leah looked back over her shoulder at the screen door. "Tell my mom. Emily's her niece."

"Yes," said Sue Clearwater, from the end of the hall. "Tell me."

She came down the hall and stood behind the screen with her arms folded and the dish towel still over her shoulder.

Harry turned around on the step to face her. He didn't stand up. He sat on the top step looking up at her through the screen, with his ears flat.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said. "That was out of line, about Emily. I don't know her. I've never met her, and I used her to take a swing at him. Sorry."

Sue looked at him through the mesh for a long time.

"And the rest of it?" she said.

His ears went sideways.

Leah waited. She could see him thinking about it. She could see him decide.

"The rest of it was true, ma'am," he said.

Sue closed her eyes.

She stood there with her eyes closed for about five seconds. Leah watched her mother's mouth move, very slightly, and thought she might be counting.

Then Sue opened them.

"Couch," she said. "Now. Both of you inside, and him on the couch, and you in your own bed, Leah Clearwater, and I don't want to hear one more word from anybody in this house until it's light out."

She turned around and went down the hall. Her bedroom door shut. Not a slam. Sue didn't slam doors. She closed them very precisely, so you could hear exactly how precisely.

The porch was quiet.

"She heard all of it," Leah said.

"Ja."

"The *rough shit*, Harry."

"I stand by it."

She punched him again, same spot. He let her.

"She's on the council," Leah said. "She's going to sit in a room with Billy Black and Old Quil Ateara tomorrow and decide what to do about you, and the last thing she heard you say about me was—"

"Better she heard it from me than worked it out on her own." His ears came up a little. "She'd already seen your neck."

Leah opened her mouth to argue with that and found she couldn't.

She sat back on the step. The drizzle had stopped. The clouds were moving fast overhead, and through a gap in them she could see three or four stars, the right ones, in the right places. Somewhere up there past the clouds the Dipper was hanging over the Olympics where it always hung. She didn't need to see it. She knew where it was.

"He wanted to say something," she said. "Sam. When he said *because I*. He stopped."

"Ja. I heard."

"What do you think he was going to say?"

Harry was quiet for a while.

"Doesn't matter," he said. "He didn't say it."

Leah thought about that.

She thought about Atlin. About a stoep in Africa with the sun coming up over a valley, and him sitting at the far end of the wall with a mug of coffee saying *I'll take that imprint apart, string by string*. Saying he'd never met anything tied onto a soul that he couldn't get his claws into eventually.

She thought: *He could do it to Sam's.*

She sat with it. She made herself look at it all the way through, the way you'd make yourself look at a wound to see how deep it went. Harry, with his claws in whatever it was that tied Sam to Emily, pulling it out a thread at a time. Sam waking up some morning looking at Emily and feeling nothing much. Sam remembering what he'd had. Sam coming back up Sue Clearwater's front walk in the rain.

She looked for the part of her that wanted that.

She looked for a long time.

It wasn't there. She couldn't find it anywhere.

A laugh came out of her. Short and surprised, like a cough.

Harry's ears turned toward her. "What?"

"Nothing."

"That was a something laugh."

"It was nothing." She stood up and brushed off the seat of her sweatpants. "You called Embry *disappointing*."

"He is disappointing."

"He doesn't even know what he did."

"That's the most disappointing part." He stood up too, slowly, and she had to tip her head back again. "Bloke had a year next to you. In the rain. Every night."

"He's scared of me."

"Ja," said Harry. "Everybody with any sense is scared of you. Doesn't stop you asking."

Leah looked at him in the porch light. At the singed whiskers, which had a quarter-inch of new black coming in at the roots. At the burnt tuft. At the collar of his shirt, still pulled a little crooked where he'd hooked it aside to show Sam the marks her teeth had left.

She reached up and straightened it. She didn't know why. Her hands did it.

"Noon tomorrow," she said. "At Billy's."

"Ja."

"That's Jacob's dad's house."

"Ja."

They looked at each other.

Neither of them said anything else about it. She could feel it sitting there between them anyway, like a third person on the step. Her box. His door. The woodstove in Billy Black's kitchen, with the crack in its door, that smelled of cedar and salt water. The photograph on the windowsill over the sink.

"You're bringing a pie," she said.

"I said I would."

"Where are you going to get a pie at eleven in the morning on a Sunday?"

His ears went sideways.

"Esme," he said.

Leah stared at him. "You're going to go back to the Cullens' and ask Esme Cullen to bake you a *pie* so you can take it to the *Quileute tribal council*."

"She made a cherry one. It was cooling."

"You can't take a *vampire's pie* to—"

"Why not? It's a good pie."

"Because—" Leah stopped. She tried to think of a reason that wasn't *because it would be the funniest thing that had ever happened in La Push*, and couldn't, and gave up.

"Inside," she said. "Before Mom comes back out."

---

The couch was a brown plaid thing her dad had bought off a guy in Sappho in 1994, six feet long on a good day, with a sag in the middle cushion where her dad used to sit and watch the Mariners lose.

Harry looked at it the way he'd looked at the pool cue.

He sat down on it. It complained. He lay back. His head went on one arm and his knees went up in the air, and his footpaws went over the other arm and hung there, and his tail came out from underneath him and lay along the floor like a rope somebody had dropped. His ears were mashed flat against the armrest.

"It's lovely," he said.

"You're lying."

"I'm lying."

Leah went to the hall closet. She stood in front of it for a second with her hand on the knob.

Then she opened it.

The smell came out. Diesel and salt and engine grease and the drugstore aftershave he'd bought in a two-pack every Christmas, faded almost to nothing. A dozen flannel shirts on wire hangers, red and green and blue, all of them worn soft at the elbows. His big rubber boots on the floor. On the shelf, the blanket. The wool one from the boat, gray with a red stripe, that he used to bring into the house on cold nights and throw over whichever kid had fallen asleep on the couch.

She hadn't opened this closet in a year and a half.

She took the blanket down. She shut the door.

She went back into the living room and threw it over him without saying anything. It came to his knees. His footpaws stuck out the bottom.

He looked down at the blanket. His nose worked. His ears came up off the armrest, slowly, and then went soft, tipped a little forward, the way they had on Marg's drive when she'd told him to keep the coat.

He didn't ask whose it was.

"Night, Clearwater," he said.

"Night, Potter."

She went down the hall to her room. It was exactly how she'd left it eleven days ago: the bed unmade, a pair of sneakers kicked under the desk, the poster of the Seahawks her dad had put up when she was twelve that she'd never bothered to take down. She got into bed in the sweatpants and the hoodie.

She lay on her back and looked at the ceiling.

Through the wall she could hear the house. Seth snoring in the next room, a soft whistling snore he'd had since he was a baby. The fridge humming in the kitchen. Her mother not sleeping, in the room at the end of the hall. The bread box, very faintly, still muttering.

And the couch, creaking, as something seven feet long tried to find a way to fit on six feet of plaid, and gave up, and went still.

She lay there with her hand over the mark on her neck, feeling it pulse under her palm, slow and steady, like something keeping time.

Her wolf was curled up under her ribs. It had quit the purring and the preening and the rolling over and all the other stupid things it had been doing all week. It lay there with its chin on its paws and its eyes open, listening to the couch.

She knew exactly how it felt.

---

### VII.

Sue gave up on sleep at half past three.

She'd lain on her back in the dark with her eyes open for an hour and a half, listening to her house, and her house had too many things in it tonight to listen to. So she got up and put on her cardigan and went out to check the locks, which she had done every night for a year and a half and which had never once needed doing.

The back door was locked. The kitchen was clean. The pot was in the fridge under foil. There was still fur on the linoleum under the oak chair, and she stood and looked at it for a while, and decided it could wait until morning.

The bread box was talking.

"—Potter. Potter, I can hear you *snoring*. I'm in a *bread box* and I can hear you. I have a two o'clock with the Director, and I would very much like to know what I'm going to *tell* him—"

Sue rolled back the lid.

The little bronze mirror lay face up on the crumbs at the bottom. The narrow, tired face in it looked up at her and stopped mid-word. It had shaved since she'd last seen it, and put on a tie.

"He's asleep," Sue said. "Call back at a decent hour."

"Madam, I am a senior officer of the International Confederation of—"

"It's half past three in the morning in my kitchen."

The face opened its mouth. Then it closed it. Then, to her surprise, it sighed, a long, tired, put-upon sigh, the sigh of a man who had been handling something difficult for a long time.

"Is he behaving himself?" it said.

Sue thought about it.

"No," she said, and rolled the lid shut.

---

She went down the hall to the living room.

He was on the couch. He didn't fit. Nobody would have fit; he'd given up trying sometime after midnight, and now he lay on his back with his head jammed against one armrest at an angle that looked painful, and his knees bent up in the air, and both long black footpaws hanging off the far end. His tail had slid off the cushions and lay across the rug and halfway to the TV. One ear was mashed flat. The other stood up off the armrest, and twitched every time he breathed out, and he was snoring like a diesel engine idling at the dock.

There was a blanket over him.

Gray wool, with a red stripe along one edge. It came to his knees.

Sue stood in the doorway and looked at it.

She knew where that blanket lived. She knew whose closet it was in, and she knew the last time anybody had opened that closet door, because it had been her, the morning of the funeral, to get out a shirt she'd ended up not using. It had been on the shelf. It had smelled like the boat. Her husband used to bring it in off the boat on cold nights in October and throw it over whichever of the kids had fallen asleep in front of the TV, and they'd wake up in the morning smelling like diesel and complain about it, and do it again the next week.

She hadn't taken it down. Seth hadn't. Seth wouldn't open that door if the house was on fire.

That left one person.

Sue stood there for a long time with her hand on the doorframe.

Then she looked at the coat on the hook in the hall, the long gray one with the silver clasp, hanging next to her husband's closet door, with the shabby brown one over it. She looked at the photograph on the wall by the front door: Leah at six on her father's shoulders on First Beach, both of them soaked, both of them yelling. She looked back at the couch.

"Harry," she said.

She said it very quietly. Not to him. Not to anybody. She said it to the dark hall, to see what it would sound like in her own mouth in her own house after a year and a half.

It sounded like a name.

That was all. A name. Somebody's mother had picked it out and somebody's father had agreed, and now it was lying on her couch with its feet hanging off the end, snoring.

Down the hall, a door opened. Very softly. A bare foot on the floorboards. A floorboard Sue had known about for twenty-two years, the one outside Leah's room, creaking.

"Leah Clearwater," Sue said, without turning around. "Bed."

The foot stopped.

A long pause.

The door shut again.

On the couch, the snoring had stopped. Neither of his eyes had opened. One ear had come up off the armrest and turned toward the doorway where she stood.

"Night, Mrs. Clearwater," he said, to the ceiling.

"Go to sleep, Potter."

"Ja."

The ear went back down. A few seconds later the snoring started up again, slow and even, and the tail on the rug gave one small twitch and went still.

Sue went to the front door and turned off the porch light.

In eight hours she was going to drive to Billy Black's little red house with a wolf in her passenger seat and a vampire's pie on his knees, if Leah was to be believed, and sit at Billy's kitchen table across from Billy and Old Quil and Sam Uley, and say something about him. She didn't know yet what it was going to be. She knew he'd washed her dishes, badly, and that she'd rewashed one, and that he'd watched her do it and kept his mouth shut. She knew he'd put Hennessey in her bread box. She knew he'd stood up when Sam started to come apart in her yard and then done nothing at all with it, which was harder than doing something. She knew what he'd said about Emily, and that he'd said sorry, and that he'd meant the rest of it. She knew her daughter had opened that closet.

She didn't know if any of that added up to anything. It was a long way from adding up.

She went back down the hall to bed. On the way past Leah's door she stopped, and listened, and heard her daughter breathing on the other side of it, slow and deep, already asleep.

Sue Clearwater stood in her hallway in the dark between the two of them, and the house was full, and for the first time in a year and a half she lay down and slept straight through until morning.
