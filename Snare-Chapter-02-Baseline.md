# Snare

## Chapter Two — Baseline

---

**The Sol Duc foothills — the same night**

Leah looked down at her feet.

They were black to the ankle. Four miles of forest road and game trail in the dark, through two creek crossings and a slope of wet clay and one patch of devil's club she'd walked straight through because the line in her chest went that way and she hadn't felt like arguing with it. Under the mud they were fine. Her feet were always fine now. In July she'd walked across a broken beer bottle at a bonfire on First Beach and hadn't noticed till Quil's cousin started screaming.

There was a mat on the top step. An actual doormat, the brown bristly kind, worn bald in the middle.

She looked at him. He looked back at her with his eyebrows up and the rain running off his hair, holding the tent flap open like a hotel doorman.

She wiped her feet.

She did it slowly and hard, left and then right, glaring at him over her shoulder the whole time, and most of the mud stayed exactly where it was. Then he moved two fingers down by his side, a small flick, and her feet were clean. All at once. Clean and dry, the soles and the toes and the backs of her heels, as if she'd just stepped out of a bath.

Leah jerked so hard she nearly went off the step.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Do that. Magic at me. Without asking."

He thought about it. She watched him think about it, which she hadn't expected. "Fair," he said.

She went down the stairs.

There were nine of them, plain scrubbed wood, and at the bottom the tent stopped being a tent. It was a kitchen. A big one, bigger than her mom's, with a stone-flagged floor and a ceiling higher than the Cullens' and a long table down the middle of it, old wood, scarred and scrubbed pale. A black iron stove the size of a car took up most of one wall, ticking and breathing heat, and copper pans hung over it on hooks. There was a dresser with mismatched plates on it. There was a sink. Over the sink, where there should have been a canvas wall and then a hillside full of mud, there was a window, and in the window was the ocean.

A little cove. Gray cliffs. Gray water, coming in slow and black and folding white on a strip of sand. The sky over it was just starting to go pale at the bottom edge. A gull went across it.

Leah stood at the bottom of the stairs and stared at it.

"Where is that?"

"Cornwall." He'd come down behind her. He went past her, not close, and around the end of the table to the stove. "England. Bottom left corner."

"It's morning there."

"Coming up on five. I never fixed the time zone."

"Why not?"

He picked up the kettle, which was already steaming, and looked at it like he was trying to remember. "Couldn't be bothered," he said finally. "Liked knowing what time it was somewhere else."

She didn't know what to do with that, so she looked at the rest of the room instead.

It smelled like him. That was the first thing and the biggest. It smelled like him the way her mom's kitchen smelled like her mom, soaked into the wood and the stone and the air — pine and cold creek water and that big slow wolf underneath — and over it, everything else. Coffee. Bacon fat. Black tea. Copper. Woodsmoke from the stove. Gun oil, faint, from somewhere below. Old paper.

And from somewhere further down, under all of it, so faint she'd have missed it if she hadn't been hunting the smell for a year: sweet. Rotten-sweet. Bleach and burnt sugar and dead flowers.

The leech.

Her lip went up off her teeth on its own. She made it go back down.

"Coffee or tea," he said.

"You have coffee?"

"Course I've got coffee."

"Coffee. Black."

He got down two mugs from the dresser. They didn't match. One was plain white. The other was blue and had a cartoon donkey on it and the words *I'VE BEEN TO CLOVELLY*, and it was chipped on the rim. He poured coffee from a pot on the back of the stove into the white one and tea from a brown teapot into the donkey, and he put milk and two sugars in the tea without measuring, and carried both mugs to the table and set the white one down at the far end. Then he went back and leaned against the counter by the sink with the donkey mug, as far from her as the room let him get.

She noticed that. She noticed he'd done it on purpose.

Leah went down the length of the table and sat on the bench at the far end, with her back to the wall and her eyes on him and the stairs both, the way she'd sat in every room since she'd phased. She wrapped both hands around the white mug. The coffee was good. It was better than good. She hated that.

The line in her chest lay quiet between them. Twenty-odd feet of kitchen. She could feel it there, steady and humming faintly, like a power line on a dry day.

He drank his tea and looked at her over the rim of the mug. In the light of the kitchen, with his overcoat off and the black jacket gone and his shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow and no tie, he looked younger than he had in the woods. Late twenties. Black hair sticking up in back. A face that was mostly angles and a nose that looked like it had been broken once and set straight. Forearms that had done a lot of something. The green eyes.

The shoulder holster was still on. The ivory handle of that old gun stuck out under his left arm.

"How long have you been watching us," Leah said.

"Thirteen days."

He said it right away. No pause. She'd expected a pause.

"All of us?"

"Mostly the Cullens. You lot when you were near them. Which is often."

"From where?"

"Across the river from the big house, mostly. There's a bank of salal on the far side, about forty feet up. I lay in it with a pair of Omnioculars for three nights the first week." He drank his tea. "Got very wet. Saw a lot of baseball."

"Omni what?"

"Binoculars. Fancy ones. They record."

"You recorded us."

"Yes."

She stared at him. He looked back at her and didn't look sorry and didn't look anything else, either.

"Are you going to hurt my family?" Leah said.

"No."

"My brother."

"No."

"Jake."

"No."

"The Cullens."

"No." He stopped. He frowned slightly, at nothing, as if he'd just heard himself. "Not unless one of them came at me, and they won't. They're more use to—"

His mouth shut.

It shut hard. She heard his teeth click. She watched his jaw go rigid, the muscle in the side of it standing out like a knot of rope, and his throat work, and nothing come out. His hand tightened on the donkey mug until she thought it was going to break. For three seconds he stood perfectly still by the sink with his mouth clamped shut and his eyes gone flat and far away, and then he breathed out through his nose, slowly, and set the mug down on the counter.

"Well," he said. His voice was rough. "That's that, then."

"What was that?"

"That," he said, "was an oath."

"A what?"

"An oath. A promise. There are some things I'm not able to say." He touched his own lower lip with his thumb, an odd small gesture, like he was checking it was still there. "Physically able. I swore, a long time ago, and the swearing went into the words. When I go to say one of those things, my tongue stops working. That's all. It's like having your mouth sewn shut. Doesn't hurt much."

Leah thought about that.

"So who you work for," she said slowly, watching his face. "That's one of those things."

He opened his mouth. She watched it happen again: the jaw locking, the throat working, nothing at all coming out. He shut his mouth and gave her a look.

"Sorry," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it, a little. "I had to see."

"Yeah," he said. "I'd have done the same."

He picked his tea back up. He didn't drink it. He was looking at her with a different expression now, narrow and careful, and it took her a second to place it. It was the same way Layla had looked at the man in the suit in the clearing, hunting for seams. He was looking at her like she was a thing he couldn't read.

"Ask me something else," he said.

"What?"

"Anything. Something I'd never tell you."

"Why?"

"Because I've answered the last six things you asked me without thinking about it," he said, "and I've been doing this for eight years, and I don't answer anything without thinking about it. Ask me something."

Leah looked at him across the long table. She thought about it. She thought about the badge that had said two different things to two different people, and *says what it needs to*, and *Agent Page*, and *Never heard of him*.

"Is Page your name?" she said.

"No."

It came out of him flat and fast, like a cough. He stood there after it. She watched it land on him. Something went across his face, too quick to catch, and then his eyes came back into focus on her, very sharp.

"Right," he said quietly. "I'm going to try something. Bear with me."

"Okay."

"I'm with the United States Fish and Wildlife Service," he said, "and I'm here investigating the— the—"

He stopped.

He tried again. She watched him try. His mouth was moving, and the words were there, she could see them, she could practically see their shape — but something was happening to them on the way out. It looked nothing like what had happened before. There was no lock, no jaw clamping. It was more like watching someone try to walk up a down escalator. He'd get a word out and the next one would come out wrong.

"I'm with the— I'm *not*." He put his mug down again. "I'm not with Fish and Wildlife. I'm not American. I've never been to a Fish and Wildlife office in my life. I made it up on day four because the woman at the petrol station asked and it was the first thing in my head." He stared at the wall above her head. "Huh."

"Did you just try to lie to me?"

"Yes."

"On *purpose*?"

"As a test."

"And?"

"Can't," he said.

The stove ticked. In the window over the sink, a wave folded white on the little Cornish beach and drew back.

"I lie for a living," he said. He sounded mildly interested, the way you'd sound noticing it had started raining. "I've lied to a Romanian vampire hunter, a Bulgarian minister, two of my closest friends and a woman I was sleeping with for eleven months, in four languages, on the same day, once. I'm very good at it. It's one of the things I'm best at." He looked at her. "And I can't tell you I'm with Fish and Wildlife."

Leah's hands had gone tight on the coffee mug.

She thought about what she'd come here to say. She'd worked it out on the drive, all forty minutes of it, in Jacob's car with the busted muffler roaring and the heater stuck on high. She'd worked it out on the four-mile walk in the dark. *I came here to tell you to get off the peninsula. I came here to tell you I don't care what this is and I don't care what you are, you've got until Saturday to pack up your tent and your leech and your fake badge and get off our coast.*

She opened her mouth.

"I came here," she said, "because I couldn't stand not to."

She shut her mouth so fast she bit her tongue.

The man by the sink went perfectly still.

Leah slapped her hand over her own mouth. Her face went hot, hotter than it already was, so hot she could feel it in her ears. That wasn't what she'd said. That wasn't what she'd *meant* to say. She'd had it ready, she'd had the whole thing ready, she'd—

It was true, though. That was the problem. She'd sat on the Cullens' deck for twenty minutes with the line running out of her chest into the dark, and she'd told herself she was going to stay put, and she'd lasted twenty minutes. And then she'd stolen Jake's car.

She took her hand away from her mouth. Very carefully, deliberately, testing it, the way you'd test a sprained ankle, she said: "I don't care what you do with the leech."

"I care what you do with the leech," her mouth said instead.

"*Oh my God.*"

"Huh," he said again.

"Stop saying *huh*."

"Sorry."

"This isn't—" She stood up off the bench. She didn't know why. She didn't have anywhere to go. She stood there at the end of the table with her fists clenched, and the line in her chest hummed between them. "This doesn't *happen*. I've been inside Sam's head since the day I phased. I've been in Jake's. Jared, Quil, Paul — I know what imprinting does. I know what it feels like, I know everything about it, I've had my nose rubbed in it every day of my life since I phased. It makes you *want*. It makes you need to be near them. It makes you into a — a big dumb dog on a chain." She was breathing hard. "It doesn't do *this*. Sam can lie to Emily. Sam lies to Emily all the time. He tells her he's fine when he's not, he tells her patrol was quiet when it wasn't. Jake lies to Bella about once an hour."

"Does it go both ways?" he asked. "For them?"

"What?"

"For Sam and his — Emily. When he looked at her. Did she feel it, too? The rope?"

Leah opened her mouth to say *of course*, and stopped.

She thought about it. She made herself think about it, really think about it, which she never did, because thinking about Sam and Emily for longer than four seconds made her want to put her fist through a wall. She thought about Emily's face that day in the yard. Emily, who'd been Leah's cousin and her best friend and who'd looked at Sam the first time like he was a stranger who wouldn't stop staring at her. Emily, who'd said *no*. Who'd said no for weeks. Who'd gotten three scars down the side of her face for her trouble, the day Sam lost his temper standing too close to her, and said yes anyway, afterward, because the wanting from his side was so big that it had swallowed her up like a tide.

Emily had come around. Emily had fallen in love with him. But she'd never felt the gravity go. Leah had been in Sam's head when Emily told him so, once, gently, in bed, years ago. *I don't know what it's like for you, baby. I just know I love you.* And Sam had felt sorry for her. That she didn't get to feel it.

Kim didn't feel it with Jared. Claire was barely three. Renesmee was ten weeks.

"No," Leah said slowly. "It doesn't go both ways. Not for any of them. It's — the wolf's side. Just the wolf's side. The other person just — gets picked."

He was looking at her.

"It went both ways," he said.

"I know it went both ways."

"I felt it go in." He touched his chest. "And I felt it come back. Round mine, and round yours, and back again. It's a loop. It tied off."

"I *know*, I was there."

"So if the one-way kind makes you want to be near somebody," he said, slowly, working it out as he went, the way she'd seen Carlisle work out things out loud when he was really interested, "maybe the two-way kind—"

"Does more," Leah said.

"Does more."

"Great," Leah said. "That's great. That's just great."

She sat back down. Her legs did it more than she did. She put both hands flat on the scrubbed wood of the table and looked at them. They were shaking a little. Not much. She made them stop.

"I hate this," she said.

It came out easy. It came out true. It came out of her with no trouble at all, and she felt a vicious kind of relief at that, because at least the thing in her chest hadn't taken *that* away from her.

So she tried the next one.

"I hate—" she said, and looked up at him, at the man by the sink with his rolled sleeves and his donkey mug and his gun. "I hate *you*."

It didn't come out.

That was the thing. *I hate* came out fine. Then her mouth just kept going, without her, and what it said was: "I hate — *this*. I hate this. I hate this."

Her voice cracked on the last one.

She put her elbows on the table and her face in her hands and sat there.

It was quiet in the kitchen for a long time. The stove ticked. The waves in the window came in and went out without making any sound. She heard him move, finally — heard his boots on the stone floor, coming down the length of the table — and then stop, still a good ten feet away. Then the scrape of the bench on the other side, as he sat down halfway along it, leaving room.

"Noted," he said quietly.

She laughed into her hands. It wasn't much of a laugh.

"Can I tell you something," he said.

"Can you *not*, apparently?"

"Apparently not." She heard him turn the donkey mug around on the table. "I don't mind it."

Leah looked up.

He was sitting sideways on the bench with one arm along the table, looking at the mug, not at her. His face was doing something she couldn't read again. She was starting to understand that his face did that a lot, that it had a lot of practice not giving things away, and that this — whatever this was — was it not giving something away and failing.

"The not lying," he said. "To you. I've been lying to everybody I've met for eight years. Every day. All day. You get so you don't notice you're doing it. You build the lie before the other person's finished asking the question. You have it ready." He turned the mug again. "And I just tried, and it wouldn't come, and I stood there over by the sink waiting to mind. And I don't. I don't mind at all. I feel like I've been carrying something for a very long time and somebody just took it off me." He was quiet a second. "That's mad. That's completely mad. I should mind."

"But you don't."

"No," he said. "I like it."

Leah looked at him across the table.

She felt it, then. She hadn't noticed it before, not under her own noise, but now that she was looking for it, it was right there: something coming down the line from his end. Faint. Like a radio in another room, playing a song she couldn't quite hear the words to. She couldn't hear his thoughts. What came down the line was the shape of them. And the shape of what came down the line just then was a kind of loosening. Like a fist opening up.

He'd meant it. All of it. She didn't need the imprint to tell her that, though it was telling her anyway.

And here was the thing that made her want to scream: she liked it, too.

She'd spent a year and a half with no privacy. None. Every thought she'd ever had since the first time she phased had been dragged out into the open and spread on the table for Sam and Jared and Paul and the rest of them to pick over, every ugly jealous bitter little thing about Sam and Emily, every bad night, every time she'd cried on patrol. They'd *taken* it. They'd never asked. They'd never been able to help it, and somehow that made it worse.

This was nothing like that. She'd said it, *I came here because I couldn't stand not to*, and it had come out of her and gone to him and stopped. It hadn't gone anywhere else. He hadn't taken it. She'd given it. And sitting there at the end of his long scrubbed table with her face hot and her hands shaking, Leah Clearwater realized that she'd liked giving it to him, and that she'd do it again, and that she couldn't lie to herself about that any more than she could lie to him.

"Okay," she said. Her voice came out very quiet. "Okay. Me too."

"You too what?"

"I like it, too," she said. "Asshole."

The corner of his mouth went up. The crooked, private thing she'd seen in the clearing, when Seth and she had been arguing about the badge.

"That one came out easy," he said.

"That one's true."

"Fair."

He drank his tea. She drank her coffee. They sat there.

---

It was maybe ten minutes later that she noticed his hand.

He'd gotten up to put more water in the kettle, and he'd come back and sat down on the bench again, not quite as far down as before. He had his right hand flat on the table near his mug, and he was pressing down on it. Hard. The fingers were spread and the knuckles had gone pale. The muscles in his forearm stood out under the skin.

She looked at it. Then she looked at his face.

He was breathing slowly. Very slowly. In through the nose and out through the mouth, the way Sam used to make the new guys breathe in the first week when they were having trouble keeping from phasing every time somebody said something they didn't like.

And she could smell it. The wolf in him. She'd been smelling it since the clearing, faint under everything, and now it wasn't faint at all. It had come right up to the surface. It filled the kitchen like smoke. Pine and cold stone and creek water and something huge and wild and alive, and her own wolf, down in the middle of her chest, lifted its head and *whined*.

She heard herself make the sound. Out loud. A thin, high little noise in the back of her throat, the noise a dog makes at a door.

Leah clapped her hand over her mouth for the second time that night.

He'd heard it. Of course he'd heard it. He'd gone still, all of him, and he was looking at her, and his eyes had changed. She couldn't have said exactly how. The green was the same green. But the pupils had gone wide, very wide, so wide they ate most of the color, and the way he was looking at her had gone very focused and very simple, the way a wolf looks at you.

"Sorry," he said. His voice was lower than it had been. It had a rasp underneath it, a kind of rumble. "Sorry. Give me a second."

"What's happening?"

"He wants to see you."

"Who?"

"Mine." He pressed his hand down harder on the table. "Him. The wolf. He wants to see yours. He wants—" He shook his head once, sharp, like a dog shaking off water. "He's been at me since the clearing. All afternoon. Through everything. And it's worse with you in the room. A lot worse."

"You're holding him down."

"Trying."

"Can't you just—" She didn't know what she was asking. "Let him out?"

He laughed, short and rough. "Not in here. He'd take the kitchen apart. He's big." He breathed out. "And I don't — I don't let him out. That's sort of the point. I've kept that side of me under wraps for a long while. Years. Put him down and kept him down. Didn't need him. Didn't want him. Had a lot of other things to be getting on with." He looked down at his own hand on the table, pressed flat and white. "And I'm pretty sure that's permanently done now."

"Permanently," Leah said.

"For now."

"*Permanently for now?*"

"Best I can do," he said, and the corner of his mouth went up again, even with his jaw clenched and his hand pinned to the table, and Leah laughed. She didn't mean to. It came out of her short and surprised, nothing like the ugly sound she'd made in the clearing, and it was the first time she'd laughed at anything in about a month.

His wolf liked that. She could tell. She could *feel* it, coming down the line, a kind of lurch of interest, like a dog's ears going up.

"He likes that you laughed," he said, a little helplessly.

"I know. I can feel him."

"Can you."

"Yeah."

They looked at each other down the table.

"Mine wants to see yours, too," Leah said. It came out before she could stop it, and she didn't try very hard to stop it, because she knew by now it wouldn't work. "She's been — she's been going crazy. Since the clearing. She keeps wanting to — I keep almost phasing. In the car. On the walk. I almost phased in the Cullens' living room, which would've gone over *great*."

"So phase."

"I can't."

"Why not?"

"Because the second I phase, my whole pack's in my head. Jake, Seth. If they're phased, they're in. And Seth's phased about eighteen hours a day. And then they'd see this." She waved her hand at the kitchen, the stove, the window full of the ocean, him. "All of it. Where you are. What you've got down there. Everything."

He was quiet for a second. Then he said, "They can't hear you in here."

"What?"

"The tent's warded. Every way I know how to ward a thing, and I know a lot of ways. Half of it's to stop anything getting in your head from the outside. Mind magic. Scrying. Anything that reaches." He tipped his head back toward the stairs and the canvas and the rain. "Anything that's trying to get into a mind in here from out there stops at the canvas. Including, I'd bet, your pack line."

Leah stared at him.

"You'd bet."

"I'm fairly sure."

"*Fairly sure?*"

"Never had a wolf in here before," he said. "Theory's sound."

She didn't say anything. She couldn't. She sat there at the end of his table with her hands around a cold mug of coffee and thought about what it would mean. Phasing. Being the wolf. Running, maybe, if there was anywhere to run. And her head being *quiet*. Nobody in it. No Seth thinking about Layla in the shower, no Jake thinking about Nessie, no Sam, ever, at all.

She hadn't been alone in her own head as a wolf since the first time. Not once. Not for one second.

She didn't notice she'd put her hand on her chest until he looked at it.

"Later," he said.

"What?"

"You can try it later. If you want. There's a room downstairs that's big enough." He took his hand off the table, slowly, testing, the way you'd take your hand off a dog's collar. It stayed off. He flexed the fingers. "I've got to go back down, first."

"Down where?"

He looked at the floor. At the stone flags under their feet. And she smelled it again, coming up faint from somewhere under all of it, rotten-sweet.

"The leech," she said.

"I wrote *pain response, tomorrow* in my notebook about three hours ago." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I'm not going to sleep tonight. I'm fairly sure of that, too. So I might as well get on with it."

"Get on with what?"

He looked at her. She watched him decide something. It took about a second, and it went across his face, and then she felt it come down the line — a kind of shrug. A *sod it.*

"There are six rooms down those stairs," he said. "I stood outside an hour ago and told myself you were never going to see any of them. Not one. Not ever." He shook his head. "Lasted about forty seconds once you got here. There are things I can't tell you. You've seen what happens when I try. But there's nothing in the oath about what you *see*."

Leah sat very still.

"I'm going to go down and finish what I started," he said. "You can stay up here. Have more coffee. There's bacon in the cold box. Or you can come down and watch." He got up off the bench. "Your choice."

"You'd let me watch."

"I don't think I can stop you seeing anything at this point," he said. "Might as well show you."

Leah got up.

---

The second set of stairs was stone. They went down along the curve of a wall, and the air got cooler and drier as they went, and the smell of the kitchen thinned out behind them and the smell of paper came up to meet them. Old paper. A lot of it. Leah had been inside the Forks library exactly twice in her life and it hadn't smelled like this. This smelled like a hundred years.

The room at the bottom was round. Three-quarters of the wall was books, floor to ceiling, so high there was a ladder on a brass rail to reach the top. The last quarter was one long shelf at chest height, and on it, in a row, were notebooks. Cheap ones. Black covers, card bindings, the kind you could get at the Thriftway in a three-pack. Somebody had painted numbers on the spines in white paint, by hand. Leah's eye ran down the row without her meaning it to. One, two, three. Twenty. Forty. Sixty-one, sixty-two, sixty-three.

The first few were soft and bent, the spines cracked white from being opened and shut a thousand times. The last few were still stiff.

He'd stopped at the bottom of the stairs and was waiting for her to look. He didn't say anything. She got the feeling he'd stopped there on purpose.

"What are these?"

"Notes."

"On what?"

"Everything." He looked at the shelf, not at her. "I wanted to understand all of it. Every kind of magic there is. How it works, and why it works, and what it costs you, and what you can do with it that nobody's done before. That's the first one, on the end. I was eighteen."

"Eighteen." She looked at the row again. "That's — sixty-three of them."

"Sixty-four. Sixty-four's downstairs. I started it tonight."

"How long?"

"Eight years, near enough."

"Why?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought it might be one of the oath things. He was just deciding how to say it.

"There was a war," he said. "Where I come from. I was in it. I was very young and I didn't know very much, and a lot of people died who might not have if I'd known more." He said it flat, like reading off a list. "Afterwards I decided I was never going to be that ignorant about anything again. And then I just — didn't stop."

Leah looked at the notebooks. Then she looked at him. He was still looking at the shelf.

She thought about the clearing. About Layla's face when she'd been sitting in the ferns with blood on her lip, and the way she'd said *three people in two hundred years*, and how her voice had shaken. Sixty-four notebooks. Eight years. Every single day, it looked like, from how the spines were worn.

"Okay," Leah said.

He looked at her, then. Something about the way she'd said it seemed to surprise him.

"Just okay?"

"What do you want, a cookie?"

The crooked thing happened at the corner of his mouth, and he went on through the round room to a door on the far side, and she followed.

The next room was a workshop. Long benches, tools on pegboards, a vise, coils of wire, a lathe. Half a clock lay in pieces on one bench with its insides spread out on a white cloth in neat rows, and on another bench sat a flat gray river stone the size of her palm with lines cut into one face of it. Lines that hurt to look at. They went in directions that her eyes didn't like. She looked away and her eyes watered.

"Ward anchor," he said, without turning around. "Don't look at it too long."

"Now you tell me."

"Sorry."

The room after the workshop was narrow and long, and lit by a row of plain glass globes hanging from the ceiling, and it was full of guns.

Leah stopped in the doorway.

There was a rack on one wall. A shotgun with the stock cut down short and black marks burned into the wood. A rifle with a brass body that gleamed under the globes. Something big in a case. On the other wall, knives. Swords. A short sword that looked very old, in a scabbard of worn leather. A long dagger with a silver blade that seemed to give off its own faint light. A felt-lined shelf at shoulder height with an empty, gun-shaped dent in it.

He'd reached up without looking and touched the ivory grip of the pistol under his arm, the way you'd touch your pocket to make sure your keys were still there.

"You have a lot of guns," Leah said.

"I have the right number of guns."

She narrowed her eyes at him. "Is that true?"

"It's true to me."

She thought about whether that counted. She decided she'd let it go. She looked at the shotgun with the burned stock. "What's in that one?"

"Rock salt."

"Salt."

"Salt's good for a lot of things."

"Layla buys salt," Leah said. "Six things of it. The day Seth met her. He said she lied about it. She said she was brining a ham."

"Layla knows what she's doing," he said, and went on to the last door at the end of the armory, and stopped in front of it, and turned around.

He didn't say anything. He just stood there with his hand on the handle and looked at her, and she understood that this was the last place to change her mind. She could feel him waiting, down the line. He wasn't pushing. He wasn't pulling, either.

The sweet smell was coming under the door. Thick. Rotten. Bleach and burnt sugar and dead flowers and blood. Her stomach turned over and her wolf came up off the floor of her chest and stood there with its lips back.

"Open it," Leah said.

He opened it.

---

The cold came out first. It was the dry, hard cold of a meat locker, and it hit her bare arms and legs and she felt it the way she hadn't felt cold in a year, a sharp clean sting on the skin. She stepped through it. The door swung shut behind them with a soft, wet sound, like a jar lid sealing.

The voice started up in the middle of a word.

"—*arek*. Marek. My name is Marek. I was born in Łódź in eighteen ninety-one. My mother's name was Hanna. My father's name was—"

It was a man's voice. Light, accented, with a crack in it. It came from the middle of the room, from inside a box made of glass.

Leah looked at the room first, because it was what she'd been trained to do. Shelves on three walls, steel and glass, full of jars and boxes and bottles with labels. Something in a jar that looked like a huge black spider's leg, as long as her arm. A box with a tooth in it, a tooth as long as her forearm, curved and yellow-white with a black tip. A bell jar with silver hair in it that moved on its own, slowly, like weed in water. A big glass jar of some cloudy liquid with a paper label on it in thick black capitals: *DO NOT SHAKE*. Underlined three times.

Then she looked at the box.

It was big. Eight feet on a side, maybe ten feet tall, sitting in the middle of a circle cut into the stone floor. The circle was full of lines of silver hammered flat into the rock, lines that did the same thing to her eyes as the stone in the workshop. The walls of the box were glass, thick glass, three inches at least, and so clear that it was hard to tell where they were except by the faint shimmer at the edges where the light caught. Inside it, under a hard white light, there was a steel table bolted to the floor. And on the table there was a vampire.

It was on its back. Chains of some dull white metal ran across it, at its chest and its hips and its thighs and its ankles and its wrists, and each chain was pinned to the table by a big bolt with marks cut into its head. It was still wearing the red jacket. Its bare feet were black with dirt. Its brown ponytail hung off the end of the table. Its face was turned up toward the ceiling, and its mouth was moving, and its red eyes were wide and fixed and blinking at the light.

"—my father's name was Tadeusz. I was born in Łódź—"

Then its nostrils flared.

Its head turned. Just the head, and only a little, as far as it could go, and the red eyes came round and found her through the glass, and stopped.

"*Pies*," it said.

Leah blinked.

"It's Polish," said the man beside her. "For dog."

"Pies," the vampire said again, staring at her. Its voice had changed. It had gone lower. "Pies. Dog. Girl-dog. I *smell* you."

"Yeah," said Leah. "I smell you, too."

There was one stool outside the glass, a plain wooden one, with a black notebook sitting on top of it. He picked up the notebook. Then he looked at the stool, and at her, and turned his hand over in the air like he was turning a doorknob, and there were two stools. The second one was just there. Same wood, same height, same scuff marks on the legs. She hadn't seen it arrive. There'd been floor, and then there'd been a stool, and there had been nothing in between.

She stared at it.

"Sit if you want," he said. He sat on the first one, and opened the notebook on his knee, and took a pencil out of his shirt pocket.

Leah didn't sit. She came and stood behind him, a little to one side, where she could see the vampire and the notebook both. He didn't seem to mind. He tilted the notebook a little so she could read it, and she read it.

His handwriting was blocky and fast. *Subject: male. Nomad. Self-identifies as MAREK. Born Łódź 1891, turned approx. 1914.* A list of numbers she didn't understand, with units after them. *Surface temp. Density.* Then: *Legilimency — viable. Confirmed.* Underlined. *Subject's mind approx. ten times human speed. Like trying to read a newspaper off the side of a passing train. All of it's there. Recall total. Subject forgets nothing. Every face, every meal.*

And under that, smaller: *D. Okafor, 28 Oct. 41 others since 1994 that I counted. Probably more. Stopped counting.*

Leah read it twice.

"Forty-one," she said.

"That I counted. Since ninety-four." He didn't look up. "Before that it gets hard to keep track. They blur, for him, a bit. Older ones. Not the faces. The order."

"You looked. In its head."

"Yes."

"At all of them."

"Yes."

She looked at the vampire on the table. It looked back at her. Its lips had drawn back very slightly off its teeth.

"The hiker," she said. "Daniel. Did he say anything?"

The pencil stopped.

She felt it, down the line. Not much. A small hitch, like a car going over a bump. She hadn't asked it to hurt him. She'd asked because she wanted to know. And he could have said *I don't know*, if he'd been anyone else, or *nothing*, or he could have just not answered. He'd decided, she could feel him decide, upstairs in the dark, not to write it down. She could feel that, too, faintly. A door he'd shut.

He opened it.

"He said *please*," the man said. "He said *please, man*. He had his hands up. He said he had a daughter."

Leah didn't say anything.

"I didn't write that bit down," he said. "Didn't seem like the Department's business." He stopped, and his jaw went tight for a second, and she realized that *the Department* had come out of him before the oath had caught up with it. Then he breathed out. "Seems that one's allowed. Generic enough."

"The Department," Leah said.

"Don't push it. It hurts when it catches."

"Okay."

On the table, the vampire had gone quiet. It was listening to them. Its red eyes went from her face to his and back.

He closed the notebook on the pencil to hold his place and stood up and set it on the stool.

"Right," he said. "Couple of things to do before the main event. Stay this side of the glass."

He walked up to the box and put his hand flat on the glass, and the glass gave. It went soft under his palm, like the skin on a pudding, and his hand went into it, and then his arm, and then he just stepped through. It closed up behind him without a ripple. He was inside the box with the vampire, and there was nothing between them at all.

Leah's whole body went tight. Her wolf hit the inside of her ribs so hard it hurt.

"Hey," she said. "*Hey.*"

"It's fine." He'd gone to a little steel tray on a stand beside the table. There were tools on it. "He can't move anything below the jaw."

"His *jaw* is the part with the teeth!"

"I'd noticed." He picked up something from the tray. A long glass tube with a rubber bulb on one end, like an eyedropper, but a foot long. And a pair of silver tongs. "Marek. Open."

The vampire clamped its mouth shut.

The man looked at it for a second. Then the vampire's mouth came open, as if something had taken hold of its jaw from both sides and pulled. It stayed that way, wide open, the lips peeled back off the teeth. Its eyes rolled and bulged. A sound came out of its throat like a car alarm a long way off.

The man leaned over it and slid the glass tube into the side of its mouth, down along the gum, and squeezed the bulb, and let go. Clear liquid came up into the tube. A lot of it. It was thin and clear and it caught the light like oil, and Leah smelled it from the other side of three inches of glass and her stomach heaved. It smelled like the vampire, but more. Like the vampire boiled down into a cup.

"Venom," he said. He held the tube up to the light and looked at it. "He's got a lot of it. Gland's in the gum, up the back. Fills up when he's angry or scared. He's both."

"That's what turns people."

"That's what turns people. And burns, if you get it in a cut. And seals a wound, if he licks it, which I'd quite like to know more about." He squeezed the venom out of the tube into a little glass vial and stoppered it and held it up to his eye. Then he took a grease pencil off the tray and wrote on the vial. "*V-one. Twenty-two forty-seven.*" He set it down in a rack. "Thank you, Marek. Close."

The vampire's mouth snapped shut so hard its teeth clacked.

He stepped back out through the glass the same way he'd gone in. Leah let out a breath she hadn't known she'd been holding, and he heard it, and gave her a look over his shoulder.

"It was fine," he said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were loud."

"Get out of my—" She stopped. He wasn't in her head. She knew he wasn't. She could feel the difference now. "Shut up," she said instead.

"Fair," he said, and sat back down on his stool, and opened the notebook, and turned to face the box.

"Marek," he said. "Let's talk about Florence."

---

It took about twenty minutes. Leah stood behind him the whole time and didn't sit down.

She'd seen Edward do his thing a few times. Edward would get very still and stare at nothing and say something quietly, and then you'd find out he'd heard your entire life story while you were deciding what to eat for lunch. With Edward, the person never knew anything was happening.

The vampire knew.

The man asked it a question. Then he looked at it. That was all. He sat on the stool with his elbows on his knees and the pencil in his hand and looked through the glass at the vampire's face, and the vampire's face went through things. First it tried to look somewhere else, and found it couldn't — its eyes would go off to the side and slide back to his like they were on rubber bands. Then it shut them. Then its eyelids opened, slowly, on their own, while it whimpered. And then it started to *remember*. Leah could see it. You could see it on its face, like someone was flipping through a photo album that it didn't want to look at, page after page after page, too fast to stop.

Every so often he'd write something. Every so often he'd say something out loud. Mostly to her. Like he was narrating.

"Nineteen fifty-three. Florence. He'd got sloppy. Left a body in the Arno where the tourists could see it. Three bodies, actually, over a week." Pencil. "Two of them came for him. A big one — very big — shaved head. And another one. Tall, dark, quiet. A tracker. He tried to run. He got as far as Bologna."

"Felix," the vampire said, through its teeth. "And Demetri. Felix and Demetri."

"Felix and Demetri," the man agreed, and wrote it down. "They took him to Volterra."

Leah's skin went cold.

"Volterra," she said. "Italy. That's — the Volturi. That's them. That's where they live."

"Yes."

"The Cullens talk about them like they're the mob."

"They are the mob." He kept his eyes on the vampire. "Older than the mob. Bigger. Better organized. Down under the clock tower. There's a hall. Round, stone, no windows. Three chairs at one end." He was quiet a moment, reading. "Three of them in the chairs. A dark one in the middle. Long hair. Skin like — like old paper. Like an onion skin. Cloudy eyes. Smiling."

"Aro," the vampire whispered.

"Aro." He wrote. "One on the left. White hair. Doesn't smile. Looks like he wants to eat the room. And one on the right who looks so bored he might be dead. More dead."

"Caius. Marcus."

"Thanks, Marek." Pencil. Then he went still. Leah watched his back. She couldn't see his face from where she was standing, but she could see the vampire's, and it had gone from scared to something else. Something way past scared.

"He touched you," said the man, quietly. "Aro. He came down off the chair and took your hand."

The vampire made a sound Leah had never heard anything make. A little dry whimper, like a dog in a dream.

"What did it feel like, Marek?"

"Like being *opened*." The words came out of it in a rush, high and cracked. "Like being opened like a letter. Everything. Everything I ever was. Every hunt, every face, my mother, the war, the first one I ever — all of it. In one second. And he *laughed*. He read it all and he laughed and he said, *What a messy little life.*"

"Did anything stop him? Anything you tried to keep back?"

"*Nothing* stops him."

The man wrote for a long time. Leah read it over his shoulder. *A. — total read via skin contact. Instantaneous from subject's POV. Subject attempted to withhold — failed. Unknown whether trained mind would fare better. Untested.* He underlined *Untested* twice.

"The girl," he said. "There was a girl. Small. Blond. Standing by the wall."

Leah watched the vampire's whole body try to move.

It couldn't. The chains held, and whatever else was holding it held, too. But she saw it try. Every muscle in it tried to curl up on the table like a kid in a thunderstorm, and couldn't, and its mouth opened and shut and opened, and nothing came out but air.

"Jane," the man said, softly. "She's called Jane. She looked at you."

"She *looked* at me."

"And?"

"And I *burned*." Its voice cracked down the middle. "I burned like the change. Like the three days. All of it, all at once, everywhere, every part of me. She just looked at me. She didn't even blink. She *smiled*."

"How long did she do it for?"

"Forever."

"Eleven seconds," said the man, and wrote it down.

Leah stood behind him and looked at the back of his head. At the black hair sticking up. At the pencil moving.

"You're going after them," she said.

His hand stopped writing.

She watched it happen. She was watching for it. His shoulders went tight under the white shirt and she saw his jaw clamp, the muscle jumping in the side of it, and she heard his breath stop. He sat there for three, four, five seconds, with the pencil not moving. Then he breathed out, slowly, through his nose, and kept writing.

He hadn't said anything at all.

"Your silence," Leah said, "is pretty loud."

"Isn't it," he said, without turning around.

The vampire on the table was looking at her again. Its red eyes had gone narrow.

"Him," it said. "He's going to Volterra?" And it laughed. It was a high, shaky, ugly little laugh. "He is going to *Volterra*? Oh, little man. Little hot-blooded man with your pencil. They'll take you apart. They'll take your head off and Aro will hold it in his lap and *read* it. They'll—"

"Marek," said the man. "Hush."

The vampire's mouth shut. It didn't look like the vampire had decided to shut it.

The man finished writing. He closed the notebook on the pencil and set it on his knee and sat there for a minute, looking through the glass. Then he turned his head and looked up at her over his shoulder.

"Here's the next bit," he said.

---

He told her what it was before he did it.

She'd remember that afterward. That he could have just done it. That nothing in the world had made him stop and turn around on that stool and tell her, except that she was standing there.

"There's a curse," he said. "Where I come from there are three of them that'll get you sent to prison for the rest of your life for using. Just for using them once, on anyone, for anything. This is one of them. It does one thing. It hurts. That's all it does. It makes a body hurt as much as a body can hurt, everywhere at once, for as long as you hold it."

"And you can use it," Leah said. "You're allowed."

The jaw. The clamp. The breath stopping. Then letting go.

"Right," Leah said. "Loud."

"I'm going to put it on him for three seconds," he said. "Then I'm going to stop and write down what happened. Then five seconds. Then I'm done for tonight." He looked at her. "You can go upstairs. Nobody'd blame you. I'd not blame you."

"Why do you need to know?"

"Because one day," he said, "somebody's going to need to know whether you can put one of these on the floor without fire. And how long it stays down. And whether it gets back up angry or gets back up scared."

That was all. He said it the way he'd said *Rock salt*, with nothing added.

Leah looked at the vampire on the table. It was looking back at her. Straight at her. Like it knew who in the room might still have something in them that would be on its side.

*Please, man. I've got a daughter.*

"I'll stay," Leah said.

He nodded. He turned back to the box.

He didn't stand up. He didn't do anything with his hands except lift the right one off his knee and point two fingers at the glass, loosely, the way you'd point at something across a street.

The vampire screamed.

Leah hadn't known anything could make that sound. It was a scream like metal tearing, like a car being ripped in half on the highway, high and raw and enormous, and it filled the cold stone room and bounced off every wall and came back louder. The vampire's body couldn't move — whatever had it from the jaw down held — but it *tried*. Everything in it tried at once. She saw the chains across its chest go tight as wires. She heard them groan. She heard the steel table shriek where its legs went into the floor, and she saw one of the bolts with the marks cut into its head lift, a quarter of an inch, out of the stone, and a crack go across the stone around it like a spiderweb. The glass of the box hummed, a deep, low note she felt in her teeth.

"One," the man said, calmly, under the scream. "Two. Three."

He lowered his hand.

The scream cut off as if a switch had been thrown. The vampire lay on the table with its mouth open, making a sound like dry sobbing, its chest heaving up and down under the chains. Vampires didn't need to breathe. It was breathing anyway, fast and ragged, in and out, in and out, like something running for its life.

The bolt that had lifted settled back into the stone with a small click. The man glanced at it and made a note.

Leah realized she'd stopped breathing. She made herself start again.

She'd thought she'd like it. That was the thing. She'd thought about it on the way down the stairs, and she'd thought: *good*. She'd been killing these things for a year, and she'd hated every one of them with her whole body, every time. She'd thought she'd stand here and watch it scream and feel the way she felt when her teeth went into one of them and it came apart like rock.

She didn't. She didn't like it. She didn't feel sorry for it, either. She looked inside herself for sorry, honestly, and it wasn't there. She thought about forty-two people and a guy on his knees on the Hoh River Trail with his hands up, and she didn't feel sorry for it at all.

What she felt was strange. Like standing at the edge of something very high.

She looked at the back of his head. He was writing.

And she could feel him, down the line. She'd been feeling him the whole time, she realized, the whole three seconds, without paying attention. She paid attention now. The shape of him came down the line, faint and steady, and she held still and tried to read it, the way she'd read a scent.

There was nothing in it that liked what he'd just done. There was nothing in it that hated it, either. It was very, very calm and very, very *closed*, like a door that had been shut and locked and had a chair put under the handle. Behind the door there was something, but she couldn't tell what. In front of the door, there was just counting. *One. Two. Three.*

"Do you like it?" Leah said.

The pencil stopped.

"No," he said.

"Do you hate it?"

He was quiet. She felt him look, down the line. Felt him go and check, honestly, behind the door, the way she'd just checked herself for sorry.

"No," he said.

"So what do you feel?"

"Mostly I'm counting." He turned a page. "Afterwards, sometimes, I'm tired. Sometimes I'm hungry. Sometimes I can't sleep. Sometimes I sleep fine." He was quiet a second. "That's it. That's all there is. I looked."

She believed him. She didn't have any choice about believing him, but she'd have believed him anyway.

"I don't feel bad for it," Leah said. It came out low. "The leech. I looked for it, and it's not there. I don't feel bad for it at all." She swallowed. "I feel bad that I don't."

He turned around on the stool and looked at her.

He looked at her for a long time, with his green eyes and his flat calm face, and then something in the face moved, just a little. She felt something come down the line with it. It felt like somebody putting a hand on her shoulder.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "That's about right. That's about where it lives."

He turned back around.

"Marek," he said. "Who were you meeting?"

The vampire's sobbing breath stopped. Its eyes went wide.

Leah didn't understand, for a second. Then she did. He'd asked the question *now*. He'd asked it right before. So that when the next five seconds came, the answer would be right there, at the front of the vampire's head, where it couldn't—

He lifted two fingers.

The scream came back. Louder. The chains groaned. The table shrieked. The glass sang in its deep low voice. Leah stood with her fists clenched and her nails in her palms and made herself watch.

"One," said the man. "Two. Three." He'd gone very still on the stool. His head had tipped a little to one side, the same way it had tipped in the clearing when Seth started thinking about Layla. "Four. There it is." His voice didn't change. "Five."

He lowered his hand.

The scream stopped. The vampire lay on the table and heaved.

He sat there for a moment without writing anything. Then he opened the notebook and wrote fast, filling half a page, and Leah watched the words come out of the pencil.

*Two. A pair. Female — ILSE. Old. Turned Hanover, approx. 1720s. Served in the V. guard approx. 1790–1810, left with A.'s leave — unclear why. KNOWS THE HALLS. Male — CORWIN — her mate. Big. Turned 1950s, Montana. Travelling north from Portland. Rendezvous w/ subject at Lake Quinault, night of the new moon.*

He stopped. He looked at the ceiling of the specimen room for a second, as if there were a calendar on it.

"Five days," he said.

Then he wrote one more line, and Leah read it, and her whole body went cold for the second time that night.

*Purpose of visit: Ilse has heard there are dogs on this coast who kill their kind. She wants one.*

"She wants one," Leah said.

"For a pet," the man said. His voice was flat. "That's how he's got it in his head. That's the word she used to him. A pet." He was quiet a moment. "She's collected things before. He's seen some of them."

"Things."

"You don't want to know."

"Tell me anyway."

He turned and looked at her. Then he told her. It took about four sentences. When he was done, Leah went and sat down on the second stool, the one that had come out of nowhere, because she'd decided her legs had been holding her up for long enough.

They sat side by side in front of the glass.

"I want her dead," Leah said.

"I want her alive."

They looked at each other.

It had come out of both of them the same way, flat and true and with no room in it for anything else. She felt his come down the line. She felt hers go up it. Neither one of them budged.

"Well," he said, after a moment. "That'll be fun."

"Why?" said Leah. "Why alive? She's a — she's a *collector*, she — why would anybody want her alive?"

He opened his mouth. The jaw locked.

She watched it. She was starting to be able to see it coming. And this time, just before the lock, she'd felt something come down the line, a sharp, fast flare of it: *Knows the halls.*

He breathed out. Let it go.

"Volterra," Leah said slowly. "She was a guard there. She knows the inside. She knows where everything is and where everybody sleeps and where the doors are." She stared at him. "You want to *ask her*."

He didn't say anything.

"Loud," Leah said, very quietly.

On the table, the vampire had turned its head again. As far as it could. Its red eyes had found her through the glass, and they were wet-looking and wide, and its mouth was shaking.

"Girl," it said. "Pies. Girl. Please." Its voice was a whisper. "Make him stop. You can make him stop. You've got him, I can see it, I can *smell* it on him, he'll do what you say. Tell him to stop. I'll tell him anything. I'll tell him everything. *Please.*"

Leah looked at it.

"You ate a guy who had a daughter," she said.

The vampire stared at her. And then its face did something terrible. It *smiled*. A small, tired, crooked smile, with the red eyes still wet over it.

"Oh, girl-dog," it said. "They *all* have daughters."

Leah was on her feet.

She didn't remember standing. She was just standing, right up against the glass, so close her breath fogged it, with her fists clenched and her whole body shaking and the wolf in her chest *roaring*. She could feel the heat coming up her spine. She could feel it in her fingertips. In about two seconds she was going to phase right here in this cold stone room, in her cutoffs and her tank top, and come apart all over the floor of it. And she didn't care. She wanted to go *through* the glass.

"Do it again," she heard herself say.

Behind her, the man didn't move.

"Do it *again*."

"No," he said.

She whirled on him. "*Why not?*"

He was sitting on the stool with the notebook closed on his knee, looking up at her. He looked very calm. He looked the way he'd looked in the clearing with Layla throwing fire at him.

"Because I've got what I need for tonight," he said. "And you don't want that."

"Don't tell me what I—"

"You want him dead," he said. "That's a different thing. It's fair. I'd want it, too. I'd want it, in your place, more than I've wanted most things. But it's a different thing, and I don't do the other one." He didn't look away from her. "I don't do it because I'm angry. I don't do it because I want to. I only do it because I need to. Otherwise you end up somewhere I've been, and you don't come back from there the same."

Leah stood there, breathing hard, with the heat crawling up her spine.

She wanted to hit him. She wanted to scream at him. She wanted to tell him he didn't know anything about her, about what she wanted, about anything.

She couldn't. Because it was true. All of it. She'd felt it come down the line while he said it, and it was true, and the worst part was that she'd known it was true about herself before he'd even opened his mouth.

The heat went back down her spine. Slowly. She let it.

She turned around and looked at the vampire on the table. It had stopped smiling.

"When do you kill it?" she said, without turning back round.

"When it stops being useful."

"And then how? Fire?"

"Not fire," he said. "There's another curse. The third of the three. It kills. Anything living, any living thing, one flash and it's done, and there's nothing anyone's ever found that stops it." He paused. "I don't know if he counts as living. Nobody does. That's the last thing on the list."

"You'll find out."

"I'll find out."

Leah looked at Marek, who was looking at the ceiling again, and whose lips had started moving.

"—*arek*. My name is Marek. I was born in Łódź—"

"When you do it," Leah said. "Can I be here?"

There was a pause behind her. She felt it on the line. It felt like someone writing something down, very carefully, somewhere it wouldn't get lost.

"Yes," he said.

He stood up. She heard the stool scrape. He picked up the notebook and the vial rack, and went to the door, and opened it, and stood there holding it for her. She looked at the vampire one more time. Then she went out through the door ahead of him into the dry air of the armory, and the door swung shut behind them with that soft jar-lid sound.

The voice stopped in the middle of the word *Łódź*, like it had been cut with a pair of scissors.

---

Harry put the vial rack in the cold box under the workshop bench and shut the lid and stood there with both hands on it.

Behind him, Leah had stopped in the armory doorway. He didn't have to turn round to know where. The line told him. It told him, roughly, everything now: that she was leaning on the doorframe with her arms folded, that her heart was still running fast from the specimen room, that the heat had gone back down her spine but hadn't gone far. He could feel the wolf in her pacing. Up and back, up and back, behind her ribs, the way his was.

He'd stood in this exact spot three hours ago, give or take, and told himself that she would never see any of it.

He'd been wrong about a few things in his life. He tried to think of one he'd been wrong about faster.

"You said there was a room," Leah said. "Big enough."

He turned round.

She hadn't moved from the doorway. She was standing in it with the light of the armory globes behind her, barefoot on the stone, in her cutoffs and her black tank top with her short hair still damp from the rain, and her face was set hard. Her eyes weren't. Her eyes kept going past him, to the floor, to the walls, to nothing, and he knew what she was looking for. She was looking for the quiet. Trying to imagine it.

"There's a room," he said.

The door to the long room was in the back of the workshop, behind a tall cabinet of drawers he kept full of things he didn't need. He moved the cabinet with a look. He hadn't opened the door since Bucharest. It stuck a little on the frame and then gave.

The long room was sixty yards from end to end, give or take, and he'd never bothered to measure the ceiling. It went up into the dark. The floor was bare stone, flat and pale and scuffed, and it was marked all over: chalk circles, old and new, layered over each other; long black scorch marks like skid marks; a painted strip down the middle, faded nearly white, for dueling; the pale ghost of a crater near the far wall where he'd once got something badly wrong. A rack of practice dummies stood against the left-hand wall with holes burnt through most of them. The air smelled of stone dust and old smoke and him.

Most of the things in notebooks nine through fifty-two had been learned in this room. Some of them more than once.

Leah walked past him into it and stopped in the middle and turned in a slow circle, looking up into the dark.

"You built this," she said.

"Yeah."

"In a tent."

"Bit of a tent."

She looked at the scorch marks on the floor and didn't ask, and he was grateful for that, and he felt her feel him be grateful and decide not to say anything about that, either. It was going to take some getting used to.

"Turn around," she said.

He turned around.

He stood facing the open door, with the workshop beyond it and the lathe and the half-clock spread out on its white cloth. Behind him he heard her pull the tank top over her head. Heard the cutoffs. Heard her roll them, quick and practised, and the creak of the leather cord round her ankle as she tied them to it. Then nothing for a moment except her breathing.

He felt it on the line before he felt it in the air. A kind of gathering. A breath held.

Then the heat came off her like the door of a furnace swinging open.

It hit him between the shoulder blades. He heard the sound of it, a deep shudder, bone and muscle and fur all moving at once, with nothing on her to tear — and the smell of her came out into the long room and filled it, all of it, right up to the dark ceiling. Rain and cedar and mud and the sharp clean musk of a wolf, a female wolf, young and lean and furious.

His own wolf hit the inside of his ribs like a battering ram.

Harry put one hand on the doorframe and held on.

He stood there and breathed. In and out. The way he'd taught himself, years ago, in a cold room in London, when he'd first put the thing down and had to learn to keep it there. In through the nose. Out through the mouth. He felt the wolf throw itself at the walls of him, once, twice, a third time. He felt his skin prickle all over and his gums ache and his vision go sharp and strange at the edges. He didn't let it out. He'd been told to stay in this skin, and he'd stay in it. But he couldn't stop it looking.

And behind him, on the line, Leah had gone completely, utterly still.

He felt it. It was the biggest thing he'd felt come down the line yet. It came down to him like a wave that's too big to be a wave, the kind that empties a whole beach before it arrives. He wasn't in her head. What came was the shape of something, an enormous astonished silence, so big and so sudden that for a second it felt like the whole line had gone dead.

Then a sound. Very small. A high, thin whine, there and gone.

Then nothing at all.

Harry stood with his hand on the doorframe and didn't turn round. He waited. He'd been told to turn round, and he hadn't been told to turn back.

He heard her claws on the stone. Slow. Coming toward him. Click. Click. Click. They stopped right behind him.

Something cold and wet pressed into the back of his hand, the one hanging at his side.

He turned round.

She was smaller than he'd remembered, up close. Smaller than the boy by a long way, and lean, every rib showing under the gray coat, and her coat was darker along her spine and pale at the throat and still damp from the rain, so that it stood up in points along her back. Her head came up to about his chest. Her ears were flat. Her eyes were on his face. Brown eyes. Human eyes. Wet.

She was shaking.

"Leah," he said.

She shoved her nose into his hand again, hard.

He went down on his knees.

He didn't decide to. His wolf did it, mostly, and he let it. He went down on the stone in the dust so that his face was lower than hers, because that was how it was done, and some part of him knew that the way he knew how to breathe. And the wolf in him came all the way up to the front, up behind his eyes, and *looked* at her.

He'd thought, upstairs, that he'd known what it wanted. He'd known about one-tenth of it.

The room got very sharp. Every sound in it came up loud and clear, the tick of the stone cooling somewhere and the hiss of the globes in the armory and the rain, faint, drumming on the canvas two floors up. Her heartbeat. Big and fast. And her smell, which had been a smell before, a good one, came apart into a hundred smells all at once, and he knew every one of them. Rain. Cedar. The mud of four miles of game trail. Coffee, still on her breath. The sweet stink of the specimen room, caught in her fur. The fear, and the anger, and under them, rising, something else, something that smelled the way a field smells after a storm.

The sound that came out of his chest was low and long and rough, like a big engine turning over in the cold, and nothing human had made it. He felt it in his own breastbone.

Her ears came forward.

She answered him. The same sound, higher and smaller, from the bottom of her chest.

Then she came in. She put her nose to his face, his cheek, his jaw, and breathed in deep. She went down along his neck, under his ear, into his hair. She pushed her whole head into the side of his throat and *breathed*, and he held still and let her, and the wolf in him stood behind his eyes with every part of it alight and held still, too. He could feel it quivering. He could feel it straining at the skin of him like a dog pressed against a glass door. It wanted out. It wanted to be in its own body, standing nose to nose with hers, as big as it was. It had never wanted anything this much since he'd known it was there.

He kept it in. It was the hardest thing he'd done all day, and he'd done a few.

"Hello," he said, into her fur. His voice came out in the same low rasp. He didn't know which of them he was talking to.

She pulled back and looked at him. Her eyes were very wet.

"Is it quiet?" he asked.

She stared at him. Then she pushed her nose into his hand again, his open palm, and held it there. Pressed it there. Not letting go.

He looked at her.

"Do you want me to hear you?"

She pressed harder.

He went in.

He went in slower than he'd ever gone into anyone. He didn't push. He'd been pushing doors open for eight years, every kind of door, the cheap ones and the good ones and the ones with traps on the hinges. He didn't push this one. He put a hand on it, and waited, and felt it open from the other side.

*It's quiet*, said Leah.

Her voice in his head was the same as her voice out loud. Rough. Low. Thick with something.

*It's quiet. It's so quiet. There's nobody in here. There's nobody, there's no Seth, there's no Jake, there's — there's nobody. There's never been nobody. Not once. Not since the first time. A year and a half. There's always somebody. And it's just me in here.*

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

*It's just me. And you. Just you, now. Because I let you.* She was shaking all over. *Oh my God. Oh my God, it's so quiet.*

"I know," Harry said.

She stood there in front of him on her four feet with her nose in his palm and shook. He didn't move his hand. He knelt on the stone in the dust and let her shake, and down the line he felt it go through her, wave after wave of it, a year and a half of it, all the noise she'd carried. Every patrol. Every thought somebody else had taken off her without asking.

After a long time it slowed down. After a longer time it stopped.

*Your wolf's huge*, she said.

He laughed. He didn't mean to. It came out of him rough and short.

*He's looking at me. Out of your eyes. I can see him in there.* She tilted her head. *He's an idiot. He's wagging. I can feel him wagging.*

"He's been asleep a long time," Harry said. "Give him a minute."

*He's still wagging.*

"Yeah, well."

She pulled her nose out of his hand and looked at him. Her ears were all the way forward now. She looked, for the first time since he'd seen her, like something other than a loaded gun.

*Can I run?*

He looked down the long room. Sixty yards of bare stone, give or take, and the dark overhead, and the black scorch marks and the white chalk circles.

"It's not much," he said.

*It's quiet*, said Leah, and turned, and went.

She went from standing to flat out in about two strides. He'd seen her run in the clearing, through the eyes of the boy and then through his own, but that had been a chase. This was her running for the sake of it. She went down the long room like something fired from a gun, low and flat and silent, her claws barely ticking the stone, and hit the far wall and turned on it and came back. She went past him close enough that the wind of her moved his hair. She went round the room. She went round it again, wide, along the walls, past the burnt dummies and the old crater, and her ears were flat back and her tail was straight out behind her and she was making a sound, a sound with no words in it at all, that he felt down the line like a kid shrieking on a swing.

Harry knelt in the dust in the middle of the room and watched her, and his wolf watched her, and every part of it *ached* to go after her.

He stayed on his knees. He'd been told to stay in this skin. He'd stay in it.

But he could watch. And he found he didn't mind that at all.

She ran for a long time. When she finally slowed down she came back to him in a long easy lope and stopped right in front of him, sides heaving, tongue out. She pushed her head into his chest. Right where the line went in. She just leaned there, her whole weight on him, and he put his hand on the back of her neck in the wet gray fur and they stayed like that.

*I like it*, she said.

He waited.

*That you can't lie to me.* He felt it come down the line with the words, the truth of it, plain as a stone. *I like that you can't. I've had people lie to me my whole life. Sam told me he still — even after. Even when I was in his* head *and I could see it wasn't true. He kept saying it. I like that you can't. I like that you don't* want *to.*

"Same," said Harry.

*Don't let it go to your head.*

"Bit late for that."

She snorted into his shirt. Then she pulled back and looked at him.

*Turn around*, she said. *I'm changing back.*

---

They sat on the floor of the long room afterward, with their backs against the wall by the door, a foot of stone between them. She had her cutoffs and her tank top back on. Her hair was wild. She'd pulled her knees up to her chest and wrapped her arms around them, and she was looking out at the long room, at the scuffed floor where she'd run, and she looked tired, and she looked about ten years younger than she had when she'd walked into the clearing that afternoon.

He'd got his tie out of his pocket at some point and was turning it over in his fingers. He didn't know why.

"What's your name," Leah said.

She didn't look at him when she said it. She said it to the room.

He knew she wasn't asking for *Page*. He knew she knew he could just not answer. And he knew, sitting there on the floor with his back against cold stone and the line lying easy between them for the first time since the clearing, that he wasn't going to not answer.

"Harry," he said.

She went still.

He felt it on the line. It wasn't anything he could have guessed. It hit her like a slap. It went through her and he felt it go — a jolt, and then something folding up, the way a person folds up around a punch to the stomach. She'd turned her head and was staring at him. Her face had gone a strange gray color under the brown.

"Say that again," she said.

"Harry."

"Harry."

"Yeah."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she put her forehead down on her knees.

"My dad's name was Harry," she said, into her knees.

He didn't say anything. He waited.

"Harry Clearwater." Her voice was muffled. "He was on the tribal council. He fished. He fixed Mom's car every time it broke, which was always. He used to sing Creedence in the kitchen and he couldn't sing. He couldn't sing *at all*." She lifted her head. Her eyes were dry. They were dry the way a riverbed's dry. "He died a year and a half ago. Heart attack."

"I'm sorry."

"I phased," Leah said.

He looked at her.

"In the kitchen," she said. "The first time. I didn't know what was happening. Nobody'd told me. Nobody told girls, there'd never been a girl, nobody thought there would be. I was standing in the kitchen and I was so angry about — everything — about Sam, and I got hot, and I got hotter, and then I just — came apart. In front of him. In the kitchen. And he looked at me, and he grabbed his chest, and he went down." She was looking at the long room again. "And I couldn't do anything. I had *paws*. Seth called 911. Seth was sixteen. By the time I figured out how to change back, the ambulance was already—" She stopped. "Mom says it wasn't me. Mom says it was his heart, his heart was bad, his dad's heart was bad. Seth says it wasn't me."

"What do you say?"

"I say it was me," said Leah. "I've never said that out loud. Not to anybody. Not even to me. Not even in my *head*, because somebody was always in there to hear it." She laughed, a small, broken sound. "And the first time I say it, it's to some English spook in a hole in the ground, and he's got my dad's name."

The long room was very quiet.

Harry turned the tie over in his fingers.

He could have told her it wasn't her fault. Anybody else would have. He could feel the words sitting there, ready, the way the lies always sat ready. *It wasn't your fault. You couldn't have known. You were a kid.* He went to say them.

They wouldn't come.

He didn't know if it was her fault. That was the trouble. He didn't know anything about her dad's heart. And the thing in his chest that wouldn't let him lie to her wouldn't let him tell her something he didn't know, either, just because it would be kind.

"I don't know if it was you," he said. "I wasn't there. I don't know anything about his heart."

She was looking at him.

"But I know you've been carrying it like it was," he said. "For a year and a half. In a head where you couldn't even put it down for a minute, because somebody'd see you do it." He looked at the tie in his hands. "That's a long time to carry something heavy with nowhere to set it down."

Leah didn't say anything. He felt the line go tight between them and then go slack, slowly, the way a rope does when somebody on the far end finally lets their arm drop.

"Your turn," she said, after a while.

"My turn what?"

"Something you've never said." She wiped her nose on the back of her wrist. "Fair's fair."

He thought about it. He thought about a lot of things. There was a lot to pick from, and most of it was locked up behind the oath, and most of the rest was locked up behind something else, older, that he'd never needed an oath for.

"My mum and dad died when I was one," he said. "Somebody killed them. In our house. At night." He turned the tie over. "I don't remember them. I've seen pictures. My aunt and uncle took me in, and they hated me. Hated the magic. Hated anything that wasn't normal. They'd have hated you." He paused. "I slept in a cupboard under their stairs till I was eleven."

Leah stared at him.

"A *cupboard*."

"There were spiders," said Harry. "I named them."

"That's not funny."

"It's a bit funny."

"It's not funny at *all*." She was still staring at him. "A cupboard. Under the *stairs*. Till you were eleven."

"Yeah."

"Have you ever told anybody that?"

He thought about it. "Two people," he said. "And they found out on their own, mostly. I've never told anybody. Not like that." He looked at her. "Not on purpose."

She was quiet. Down the line, he felt something come up out of her and go toward him — a hot, fierce, protective lurch, like a dog going for somebody's throat on his behalf. Somebody who wasn't there. Two somebodies, in a semi-detached house in Surrey, who'd been dead to him for nearly ten years.

It was the strangest feeling he'd ever had. Somebody being angry for him.

"What's the rest of it?" Leah said.

"The rest of what?"

"Your name. Harry what?"

He'd known it was coming. He sat with it a second anyway.

"I'm not going to tell you that one yet," he said.

"Can't or won't?"

"Won't. It's not the oath." He was careful. He didn't have to be careful, because he couldn't say anything that wasn't true, but he was careful anyway, because the true thing had a lot of edges. "There are people out there who know my surname. A lot of people. Some of them would come here if they found out I was here, and some of the ones who'd come aren't nice. And if I tell you, Seth's girlfriend works out who I am in about four seconds flat the next time she hears you say it. She's clever. She's been trying to work me out since the clearing." He looked at the long room. "And the next time you phase, your brother's going to be in your head. And whatever's in your head goes to him. And whatever goes to him goes to her."

Leah was quiet.

"So it's not that I don't want to tell you," Harry said. "It's that I don't want to tell *Seth*."

She looked at him. Then she looked down at her own bare ankle, at the leather cord tied twice around it.

"Then fix it," she said.

"What?"

"Fix it." She lifted her chin. "You fixed this room. You put a whole room in a tent where nobody can hear me. You can make a stone that pulls a witch through the air from her kitchen. Layla did that. You're better than Layla. She said so." She jabbed her chin at the long room, the quiet in it. "So fix it. Make it so they can't hear me unless I let them. Make it so I get a door."

Harry looked at her.

He opened his mouth to say *that's not how it works*. He didn't, because he didn't know if that was true. He didn't know how the pack line worked. He'd had it in his own head for half an hour this afternoon, through the boy, and it had felt like a party line, like a big old copper telephone wire with everyone on it at once. And he'd built wards against every kind of mind magic he knew. And a ward was only a wall with a door in it, really, if you were good at it.

He thought about it. He actually thought about it, sitting on the floor, for about a minute, and she sat beside him and let him, and he felt her watching him think and not interrupting.

"Yeah," he said slowly. "Yeah, I think I can fix that."

---

He did it at the workbench in the next room, with her sitting on the edge of it beside him, swinging her bare heels.

There was a wooden dish at the back of the bench with eleven river stones in it, flat and gray, the leftovers from the forty-one anchors he'd cut out of the Sol Duc in his first week. He picked one out and turned it over in his fingers. It was smooth and cold and about the size of a quarter, and when he held it up to his eye he could see the faint grain of the rock running through it in thin pale lines.

"It remembers being part of the river," he said. "They all do. Layla knew that. That's why she used two halves of one stone for your brother's." He set it flat on the bench. "This one's going to remember being a wall."

"A wall."

"A wall with a door in it. The pack can knock. You decide whether it opens. You decide what they see, if it does." He put the tip of his index finger on the stone. "You'll have to practice. Might take a few goes before it's smooth."

"How do you know it'll work?"

"I don't," he said. "First draft. Might need adjusting."

He felt her take that in, down the line. It went in easy. He realized she liked that he'd said it. *I don't.* She'd been around a lot of people who were sure about things, he thought, and been told a lot of things by them.

He drew the rune.

He did it with his fingertip, slowly, and where his finger went the stone smoked. A thin line of white smoke came up out of it, and the smell of lightning, and the rock went dark and glassy along the line his finger traced. It wasn't a big rune. Eleven strokes. He'd never drawn it before. He'd invented it about ninety seconds ago, sitting on the floor, out of three older ones he knew well, and he could feel it settling into the stone as he cut it, the way a key settles into a lock when it's right. He drew the last stroke and lifted his finger.

The stone lay on the bench, smoking a little, with its dark mark on it.

"Don't look at it too long," he said.

"I know." She was looking at the ceiling. "Now you tell me."

He cut a length of thin leather lace from a coil on the bench, and threaded it through a hole he put in the stone with a look, and knotted it. Then he held it out to her on his palm.

She didn't take it right away.

"Why," she said.

He knew what she was asking. He could feel her asking it, down the line, and he could feel what was under it, which was the oldest kind of question, the kind people ask when they've been given things before and found out later what the things cost.

"Two reasons," Harry said. "One, there are things in your head now about me that I don't want going down a pack line to anybody. This place. Him downstairs. My name. That's true. It's selfish and it's true." He looked at her. "Two, you've wanted a door since the day you phased and never had one. And I've got one I can give you." He held the stone out a little further. "Both of those are true. You can feel which one's bigger."

She could. He felt her feel it. He felt her look at both of them, carefully, the way she'd looked at him in the clearing, and he felt her find the bigger one.

She took the stone.

She bent and tied the lace into the cord around her ankle, beside the cutoffs, with fast hard fingers, and pulled the knot tight with her teeth. Then she sat back up on the bench and looked at it, there on her ankle, small and gray with its dark mark.

"Thank you," she said.

It came out of her rough and quiet. He felt how true it was. He felt, too, that she hadn't said it to anyone and meant it in a long time.

"You're welcome," said Harry.

---

She fell asleep at about two in the morning on the sofa in the sitting room off the kitchen. It was a big ugly brown leather Chesterfield he'd bought in a junk shop in Swansea, and she'd sat down on it to finish a second cup of coffee, and she'd put her head back, and she'd gone. Just like that. The mug was still in her hand.

He took the mug out of her fingers. She didn't stir.

He thought about a blanket and decided against it. She ran hotter than he did. She'd kick it off.

He went into the war room and sat down at the long table, in the dark, with the map in front of him. The silver lines lay quiet over the gray wood. The red light at the top of the rise above the Calawah had reset itself hours ago. Nine gold pinpricks glowed steady by the river where the Cullens were. Down past the Quinault, past the southern edge of the national forest, the outer line ran across the neck of the peninsula, from the harbor to the canal, faint and silver and still.

On the stand in the corner, the slate had a new line of chalk on it.

*Good. Pace yourself. — C.*

He looked at it for a while. He didn't pick up the chalk.

He sat in the dark and thought about it. Not the slate. The rest of it. The six rooms he'd sworn she'd never see, and the forty seconds that had lasted. The specimen room. The long room. The cupboard. He'd told her about the cupboard. He'd said his own name out loud to someone for the first time since he'd signed the Department's book in a room with no windows, and he hadn't had to. Nothing had made him. The thing in his chest wouldn't let him lie to her, but it hadn't made him say any of it, either. It had only taken the lies away. Everything he'd told her after that, he'd told her because he'd wanted to.

He'd told her more in four hours than he'd told anyone in eight years.

He thought he ought to mind. He looked for it, the way she'd looked for sorry in the specimen room. Somewhere in him there ought to be the old cold voice that had kept him alive in eleven countries, the voice that said *what does she know now, who will she tell, what will it cost*. He went and looked for it.

It was there. It was talking. He could hear it.

It just wasn't very loud anymore. Mostly what he felt, sitting in the dark, was light. Like a man who'd been carrying a pack so long he'd stopped noticing it, and set it down at last, and stood up, and found out how tall he was.

Through the wall, through the kitchen, down the line, he could feel her sleeping. Slow and even. Deep. The deepest sleep, he'd have bet, she'd had since the day she phased.

The wolf in his chest had stopped pacing a while ago. It had lain down behind his ribs with its head on its paws, facing the wall, facing her. It was nowhere near asleep. It was watching.

Harry sat at the map in the dark until his watch said a quarter to six, and then he got up and put the coffee on.

**The Sol Duc trailhead — morning**

Jacob's Rabbit was parked crooked across two spaces at the Sol Duc trailhead, with the driver's door not quite shut and the seat shoved all the way back.

Jacob stood in the gravel lot in the gray light, barefoot, in a pair of cutoffs, with the rain coming down on his shoulders, and stared at it.

He'd built that car. He'd built it out of three junkers and a summer, in Billy's garage, before vampires, before wolves, before Bella, back when the biggest thing in his life was whether he could get his hands on a master cylinder for a 1986 Volkswagen for under forty bucks. Nobody drove it but him. Nobody had ever driven it but him. Bella had sat in the passenger seat about a hundred times and never once asked to drive, because she knew.

The seat was shoved all the way back. Leah had long legs.

"Jake," said Seth, behind him. "You okay?"

"She *stole my car*."

"You left the keys in it."

"Everybody leaves the keys in it! It's *my car*! Nobody steals my car!"

"Leah did," said Seth, reasonably.

Jacob put both hands on the roof of the Rabbit and leaned on it and breathed.

It had been a long night. It had started at midnight with Billy's phone going off in the kitchen, and Billy wheeling himself into Jacob's room with the cordless and that look on his face, and Sue Clearwater on the other end, very calm in the way that meant she wasn't, asking if Jacob knew where her daughter was. He hadn't. He'd been at the Cullens' till eleven. He'd seen Leah go out onto the back deck and not come in, and he'd figured she'd phased and gone home, and when he'd got back to La Push he'd been so tired he hadn't even noticed his car wasn't at the end of the Cullens' drive.

So he'd phased, and Seth had already been phased, and Seth had already been looking.

No Leah. Anywhere. Not in the pack line. Not on the beach. Not at the Cullens'. Not at Sam's, where she'd never go. Not at the rez, not in Forks, not on any trail between them. That meant she was human, which meant she could be anywhere. Seth had run the whole western half of the peninsula by two in the morning, thinking about it the whole way, and thinking about it the way Seth thought about everything, which was out loud and in full color, so that Jacob had gotten the whole story about six times.

Jacob had found out about the imprint the way he found out about everything in this pack. He'd phased, and gotten hit in the face with it.

He'd had to phase back and sit down on Billy's porch steps in the rain for a while after that.

Then at four in the morning, Alice had called. Alice never called. Alice said she'd tried to look for Leah and couldn't, the way she could never see any of them, and then she'd said: *Edward wants to talk to you*, and Edward had come on, and said very quietly, *Jacob, she's gone to him*. That was all. *She's gone to him.* And then Bella in the background, saying *Edward, give me the phone*, and then Bella's voice, the new one, the bell-voice, saying, *Jake. Leave her alone. I mean it. Leave her alone and let her figure it out.*

And he'd wanted to. He really had. He'd sat on Billy's steps till the sky went gray and argued with himself about it, and the argument had gone like this: Leah was twenty-one years old. She was a grown woman. She didn't need him to come and get her. She'd hate him for coming to get her. She already kind of hated him. He wasn't Sam, he didn't give orders, he didn't run anybody's life, that was the entire *point* of him.

And on the other side: she'd imprinted on a guy who'd held her face-down in the dirt with one hand. A guy who knew Jacob's name and the treaty line and where the Cullens lived. A guy who had a vampire in a hole somewhere and was going to do God knew what to it. A guy Layla Kincaid — who'd made Emmett *float*, who could stop the *rain* — had sat on the Cullens' sofa with an ice pack on her ribs and said would kill Jacob without being upset about it.

Then he'd remembered his car.

That had made it his business.

"Okay," Jacob said, and straightened up off the roof of the Rabbit. "Okay. Which way?"

Seth was already standing at the edge of the gravel with his nose up, the way they got sometimes even in human form, when there was something on the wind they wanted badly enough. He pointed up the trail. Then off it, to the left, into the trees.

"That way," he said. "Bare feet. She walked."

"Of course she walked."

"And Jake." Seth's face did the thing it did. "We should stay like this. Human. When we get there."

"Why?"

"Because last time we showed up as wolves," Seth said, "he put us on the ground."

Jacob looked at him.

"Fine," he said.

---

It was four miles. Leah's trail was easy to follow, even human. She hadn't been trying to hide it. She'd gone straight as a string, up the forest road, and off it onto a game trail along a creek, and through two crossings, and up a clay slope where her bare feet had slid and left long smears in the mud. She'd gone right through a stand of devil's club, the thorny stuff, and Jacob went around it, and Seth went around it, and neither of them said anything.

The man was everywhere up here.

That was the first thing that made the back of Jacob's neck go tight. He'd only ever smelled the guy secondhand, through Seth's memory and Leah's, and that wasn't the same. But he knew it as soon as he caught it. Pine and cold creek stones and wet wool, and coffee, and something else under it. It was on the trail. It was on the trees. It was on rocks by the creek where somebody had stood and looked at the water. It was old and new, layered, like the guy had been walking these woods every single day for two weeks and never once going the same way twice.

And then, about three miles in, faint, coming up through the wet ground — sweet.

Jacob stopped dead.

Seth stopped beside him. They both stood there with their heads up. It was so faint he'd almost have thought he was imagining it. Rotten-sweet, like dead flowers, like bleach. Like a leech. But there was no leech here. No trail of one. The sweet stink wasn't coming *from* anywhere along the ground. It was coming up *out* of it, up ahead, from somewhere under the hill.

"Jake," Seth said, very quietly.

"Yeah. I know."

They kept going.

The trail went over the shoulder of a ridge and down into a hollow, a little bowl in the hillside under some big old Sitka spruces, and Leah's footprints went straight down into it. Jacob started down after them.

And stopped.

He'd left the stove on.

He was sure of it. Completely, totally sure. Back at the house, at Billy's. He'd put a pot of water on at three in the morning to make coffee and he'd gotten distracted on the porch and he'd never turned it off, and Billy was asleep in his chair in the front room, and the pot had boiled dry by now, and the pan was red-hot, and the kitchen was—

Jacob had already turned around. He'd already taken three steps back up the slope.

"Jake," said Seth, from behind him, in a strange voice. "Jake, I think I left — I think I left my—"

They both stopped.

They looked at each other.

"Layla said," Seth said slowly. "Remember? At the Cullens'. There's a spell. People who don't have magic walk up on a place and suddenly they want to leave. They remember something. They have to go."

Jacob stood there on the slope with every nerve in his body screaming at him that his house was on fire. He could see it. He could smell the smoke.

He couldn't smell the smoke. He could smell spruce and rain and Leah and that guy and, faint, a leech.

"Son of a *bitch*," he said, and turned back around, and walked down into the hollow.

It felt like walking into a headwind. Like walking into the ocean at First Beach in January, against the waves, with the cold pushing on every inch of him and his body telling him to go back, go back, *go back*. He set his jaw and put his head down and kept walking. Behind him he heard Seth swear, softly, which Seth never did, and follow.

Then, all at once, the wind stopped.

He was at the bottom of the hollow. There was a fire ring, made of river stones, with a little fire going in it, crackling and smoking in the rain. There was a camp chair beside it, a cheap folding one, with the seat split down the middle. There was a tent behind it under the spruces. A tiny green tent. A two-man tent, faded and saggy, with its flap tied back.

Jacob was sure it hadn't been there a second ago.

There was a man in the camp chair.

He was in shirtsleeves, white shirt with the cuffs rolled to his elbows, black trousers, black boots, and a leather holster across his shoulders with the ivory grip of a gun sticking out under one arm. He had a blue mug in both hands. The mug had a cartoon donkey on it. He was sitting with his elbows on his knees and his long legs stretched out toward the fire, and he looked up at Jacob when Jacob came out of the trees, and he didn't look surprised at all.

Leah was standing beside the chair.

She was wearing a long black wool overcoat. *His* overcoat. Jacob knew it from Seth's memory, the way it had swung around the guy's legs in the clearing. On Leah it came down past her knees. She'd pushed the sleeves up to her elbows. Under it she had on her cutoffs and her black tank top, and her legs were bare and her feet were bare, and she had a white coffee mug in one hand.

She looked at Jacob. She didn't look surprised, either.

"Morning, Jacob," said the man.

Jacob stood there and stared at the two of them.

He didn't decide to think what he thought next. It just came up out of him, the way things did when he got angry, hot and fast and with pictures. *I'm going to rip his arms off, I'm going to rip his arms off and beat him with them, I'm going to—*

"You're not, actually," said the man. "But I appreciate the energy."

Jacob's mouth fell open.

"Stay out of his head," said Leah.

"He's shouting."

"Then plug your ears."

The man looked up at her. Something went across his face that Jacob couldn't read at all. Then he said, "Fair," and that was all, and Jacob felt it go — something he hadn't even known was there. A pressure, like a hand that had been resting on top of his skull so lightly he'd never have noticed it, lifting off.

He'd been in Jacob's head from fifty yards off. Since the trees. Maybe since the slope. Jacob hadn't felt a thing.

"Hi, Leah," Seth said, very carefully, from behind him.

"Hi, Seth."

"Mom called Billy," Jacob said. His voice came out rougher than he wanted. "At midnight. She's freaking out."

Something went across Leah's face at that. "I'll call her."

The man reached into his trouser pocket without looking and came out with a phone, a cheap black flip phone, the kind you bought at the gas station with the minutes already on it, and tossed it up to her underhand. She caught it without looking, either.

"Burner," he said. "Keep it. My number's the only one in it."

"You have a *phone*?" said Jacob.

"I have several phones."

Leah flipped it open and looked at it and closed it again and put it in the pocket of the overcoat. She looked at Jacob.

"You stole my car," Jacob said.

"Borrowed."

"The keys were *in* it!"

"That's kind of your problem, Jake."

Behind him, Seth made a small strangled sound, like somebody trying very hard not to laugh in church. Jacob didn't turn around. He looked at the overcoat. At the way Leah had it wrapped around her, with one hand holding it closed at the front, though she'd never been cold a day since she phased. None of them had.

"Why are you wearing his coat?" he said.

"It's raining," said Leah.

The man in the camp chair turned his head and looked up at her.

It was a quick look. Not even a second. But Leah's eyes snapped down to him, and narrowed, and her jaw went tight, and she gave him a look back that Jacob had seen on her face a hundred times, usually right before she bit somebody. The man looked away, back at the fire. His mouth did a small crooked thing at one corner and then didn't.

Jacob looked from one of them to the other. He had no idea what had just happened. He had the strong feeling it had been about him, and that he'd lost.

"Leah," he said. "Come home."

"I'm going home. Later."

"Now."

And it was right there. He felt it come up in him, the thing that lived in his chest since the day he'd walked away from Sam. The voice. The *alpha* voice. Every wolf in his pack had to do what that voice said; it went into them under the skin and they couldn't fight it any more than they could fight the moon. He felt it come up into the back of his throat, deep and heavy, and his mouth was already opening to put it into the next word—

"Don't you *dare*," said Leah.

Jacob stopped.

She'd gone white around the mouth. She was standing very still in that long black coat, and she was looking at him with a look he'd seen before, from the inside, in her head. A long time ago, back in Sam's pack, the first month after she phased, when Sam had used the voice on her for the first time. *Leah, stop.* Just that. *Leah, stop thinking about it.* And she'd had to. She'd had to stop thinking about Sam, about the two of them, about everything, because Sam told her to, and Jacob had felt what it had done to her from the inside, and it had been like watching somebody get a door slammed on their hand.

He'd sworn, when he left Sam, that he'd never do that to anybody.

He closed his mouth. He swallowed the voice back down where it lived.

"Sorry," he said.

Leah looked at him a long moment. Then she nodded, once, short.

Jacob turned on the man in the chair.

"What did you do to her?" he said.

The man drank his tea and thought about it.

"Nothing she didn't ask for," he said.

The heat came up Jacob's spine so fast he didn't even feel it start. It was just there, all at once, a shudder going through his whole body, his hands curling into something that wasn't quite hands yet. He took a step forward. He could feel his teeth changing in his gums.

"Jake," said Seth, and grabbed his arm. "Jake. *Jake.* You can't."

"Watch me."

"No — Jake, you *can't*." Seth's hand was locked on his arm. Seth was strong. Seth was nearly as strong as Jacob, and he was hanging on with everything he had. "The *law*, Jake. The law."

Jacob stopped.

The heat stood in his spine. He felt it there, trembling, wanting out.

"The law," he said.

"You *said* it." Seth's voice was cracking. "You said it to Sam. In the rain, outside the Cullens', when he was going to come for Nessie. You stood there and you said it, *it's our most absolute law*, you said no wolf can ever hurt another wolf's imprint, you said—"

Jacob stood there in the rain.

He'd said it. He remembered saying it. He remembered how it had felt, standing between Sam's whole pack and the big glass house, with Nessie a few hours old inside it, and saying it — knowing it was the one thing in the world Sam couldn't get around. It was older than either of them. It was older than the treaty, older than Ephraim Black. It was the one thing every wolf knew in his bones before anyone ever taught it to him. *You do not touch another's imprint. Ever. For any reason.* It was the only reason Nessie was alive.

He turned his head very slowly and looked at the man in the camp chair.

"Sorry," said the man. "What law?"

"No wolf can hurt another wolf's imprint," Seth said, in a rush. "Ever. It's like — it's the most absolute law there is. Jake used it to stop Sam from killing the baby. It's — Leah imprinted on you, so you're — nobody in the pack can—"

The man looked up at Leah.

"Is that right?" he said.

"Yes," said Leah.

He looked back at Jacob. He sat there in his split camp chair with his donkey mug, in his shirtsleeves, with the rain coming down on him, and thought about it. Then he said, mildly:

"So I'm a protected species."

"Don't push it," said Leah.

Jacob made a sound.

He didn't mean to. It came up out of him before he could stop it, and it was the heat going back down, and it was something else too, and by the time it got out of his mouth it was a laugh. A big helpless awful laugh, the kind that hurt your ribs. He put his hands on his knees and bent over and laughed in the rain. He couldn't stop. Seth let go of his arm. The heat went out of his spine all at once like air out of a tire.

"Of course," he managed. "Of *course*. Of course it's *that*. The one law. The *one*. I used it. I *used* it, and now—"

"Jake?" said Seth.

"I'm fine." He wasn't. He straightened up, wiping his eyes. "I'm fine. This is fine." He looked at Leah. "You know what this is? This is the universe getting back at me. For Sam. For — you know what, never mind."

Leah was looking at him. She didn't laugh. But something at the corner of her mouth moved, for just a second, and Jacob saw it.

"Yeah," she said. "Think about that, Jake."

---

It was Seth who asked about the other thing. Of course it was Seth.

"Leah," he said. "Last night. I looked for you everywhere. I was phased the whole night, I ran all the way to Neah Bay. You were never in there. Not once." He frowned. "Did you not phase at all?"

"I phased," said Leah. "Twice. Once last night. Once this morning, out here, to try something."

Jacob stared at her. "Then why couldn't we—"

Leah reached down, without a word, and pulled up the leg of the overcoat a few inches, and stuck out her bare foot. There was the leather cord around her ankle, the one they all wore, the one she tied her clothes to. And tied into it now, next to the little roll of cutoffs, there was a flat gray stone the size of a quarter on a thin lace. There was a dark mark cut into one side of it.

Jacob looked at the mark. His eyes watered. He looked away.

"Phase, Jake," said Leah. "Go on. Try."

He looked at her. He looked at the man, who was looking into the fire and drinking his tea and very carefully not saying anything.

Jacob went behind a spruce. He got his cutoffs off and tied them to his ankle. He let go.

It was easy, after this long. Heat up the spine, and the world coming apart and going back together bigger, and four feet in the wet spruce needles and the whole forest pouring in through his nose. And the pack. The pack line. It lit up in him the way it always did, the second he phased, like a switchboard coming on. Seth wasn't phased; Seth's place in it was dark. Leah's place in it—

Leah's place in it was dark, too.

Then he felt her come in. Behind the tent, out of sight. He felt the heat of her phase, the way he always did. He felt her *arrive* in the pack line, the way he always did, like a light switching on in the next room.

And then nothing.

She was *there*. He could feel her there, the way you can feel somebody standing on the other side of a door. But there was nothing coming through. No thoughts. No pictures. No bitter, sarcastic, endless running commentary, which was what being in Leah's head had always been like, every patrol, every night, since the day she'd joined his pack — Leah thinking about Sam, Leah thinking about Emily, Leah thinking about how stupid everybody was and how much she hated all of them and how she wished she was anywhere else. None of that. Just a closed door, and her behind it.

Then, very clearly, one single thought came through. Just one. And then the door shut again.

*Knock first, Jake.*

He phased back so fast he fell over.

He lay on his back in the wet needles behind the spruce, human, naked, staring up at the branches, and breathed. Then he got up and got his cutoffs back on, with his hands not entirely steady, and walked back out to the fire.

Leah came round the side of the tent a minute later, pulling the overcoat closed. The man had been holding it for her. Jacob had seen him do it, out of the corner of his eye — seen him take it from her and turn his back while she went behind the tent, without anybody asking him to, and stand there holding it till she came back.

"What *is* that," Jacob said.

"It's a door," said Leah.

She said it simply. Just like that. *It's a door.* And she stood there in the long black coat with her arms wrapped around herself and her chin up, and her face did something Jacob had never seen it do, in all the time he'd known her, which was his whole life.

It looked *peaceful*.

He'd been in her head. That was the thing. He'd been in her head for months. Since the day she'd come over from Sam's pack to his. He knew what it was like in there. He'd felt the way she'd hated it, every second of every patrol, the way she'd hated that there was nowhere in the whole world to put anything down where somebody wouldn't see it. He remembered a night in September, out on the ridge above the Cullens' place, when she'd cried on patrol. Just once. She'd tried so hard not to, and she couldn't stop, and she'd known that he and Seth could feel every single second of it, and that had made it worse. And the two of them had run the whole rest of the patrol pretending they couldn't feel it. And she'd known they were pretending. And that had been worst of all.

He looked at the stone on her ankle. Then he looked at the man in the camp chair.

The man was looking at the fire. He hadn't said a word.

Jacob stood there for a long moment. A lot of things went through him. Most of them weren't things he wanted to say out loud.

"Okay," he said finally.

The man looked up at him.

"Okay," Jacob said again. He didn't know exactly what he meant by it. He thought maybe the guy did. He thought maybe the guy didn't need to be in his head to know.

The man nodded once. "Okay," he said.

---

"Since you're here," the man said, a minute later.

He'd got up out of the chair and put the donkey mug down on the seat of it. Standing, he was shorter than Jacob, but not by much, and broader than he'd looked sitting down. He stood by the fire with his hands in his trouser pockets.

"The one I took yesterday," he said. "He's not alone. He was meeting two others. A pair. They're coming up from Portland. A woman called Ilse and a man called Corwin. She's old. Three hundred, near enough. He's younger, and bigger, and he does what she says." He paused. "They were meant to meet up at Lake Quinault in five days, on the new moon."

Jacob felt his hands start to curl.

"Three of them," he said. "On the peninsula. On our—"

"Two now," said the man. "I've got one."

"What do they want?"

The man looked at Leah. Leah looked back at him. Something passed between them that Jacob didn't get. Then Leah turned to Jacob.

"She wants a wolf," Leah said. Her voice was flat. "One of us. She heard there were wolves on this coast that kill leeches. She wants one to keep. Like a pet."

Seth made a sound. Jacob didn't make a sound. The heat came up his spine again and he let it come, and stood there in the rain with it running in him, and he didn't phase, because he was getting good at that, now, and because there was nobody here he could put his teeth into.

"Then she dies," he said. "Both of them. The second they cross onto—"

"The man you can have," said the man. "If you want him. I want the woman alive."

Jacob stared at him.

"*Alive*?"

"Alive."

"She wants to put one of us on a *leash*, and you want her *alive*? Why?"

"Classified," said the man.

Leah snorted.

Jacob looked at her. She was looking at the fire. She'd put her hand over her mouth.

"Leah," he said.

"He needs to ask her some things," Leah said, through her hand. She took it away. Her face was serious again. "She knows things. About — other leeches. Bad ones. Trust me, Jake. You want him to ask."

"Trust *you*? You've known him for like eighteen hours."

"Yeah," said Leah.

That was all she said. *Yeah.* And she said it in a voice Jacob had never heard her use, steady and simple and sure, without one single sharp edge on it anywhere. She said it like she was telling him what color the sky was.

Jacob didn't have anything to say to that. He looked at the man.

"Lake Quinault," he said slowly. "You know that's not ours, right? Half that lake's on the Quinault reservation. It's their land. Not ours. Not the Cullens'. Not the government's. Theirs."

The man paused.

"Is it?" he said.

"You going to go flash your badge at the Quinault Nation, too?"

"I'll take it under advisement," said the man.

"I have to tell Carlisle," said Jacob. "And Sam. Sam's going to have to know. If there's two of them coming, and one of them wants—"

"Tell whoever you like," the man said. "You've got five days."

He stopped.

He'd been about to say something else. Jacob saw him open his mouth for it. And then he just stopped, mid-breath, and looked down at his own left hand.

There was a ring on it. A plain silver band on the third finger, nothing on it. Jacob hadn't noticed it before. The man was looking at it, and as Jacob watched, a thin curl of steam came up off the man's finger where the ring touched the skin, in the cold rain.

Beside him, Leah's hand had gone flat to her own chest. Right over her heart. She didn't seem to know she'd done it.

"What," said Jacob. "What is that—"

"Wait here," said the man.

He turned around and ducked into the tent.

The tent was a two-man tent. It was maybe four feet high and six feet long, and Jacob could see the far end of it and the back wall and the sag in the canvas where the rain was pooling. The man ducked in through the open flap, and went in, and kept going, and didn't come out the other side. He didn't hit the back wall. He went in and *down*, like he'd gone down a flight of stairs, and was gone.

Jacob stared at the tent. Seth stared at the tent. Leah didn't stare at the tent. Leah was staring at nothing, with her hand pressed to her chest, and her face had gone very still.

"Leah," said Seth. "What's going on?"

"I don't know," said Leah. "He's — something's wrong. Something's happening. I can feel him — he's—"

The man came back up out of the tent.

He'd been gone maybe thirty seconds. He came up and out of it with his suit jacket on now, the black one, buttoned, and the narrow black tie back around his neck, knotted, the sunglasses folded in his breast pocket. He looked exactly like he had in Seth's memory of the clearing. He came out still straightening the tie with one hand.

"Two," he said.

"Two what?" said Jacob.

"Two leeches, Jacob." The man finished the tie. "Hanging upside down from a tree on my outer line, down by the Wynoochee River, about fifty miles south and east of here as the crow flies. One male, one female. Same anchor stone. Same minute. Thirteen minutes ago."

Nobody said anything.

"Five days," said Leah, finally.

"He thought five days," said the man. "He believed it. I was in his head, he believed every word. She didn't tell him she was coming early." He looked south, through the spruces, as if he could see fifty miles of forest from there. "Because she's old, and she doesn't trust anybody, and she wanted to look at the ground before he got here. See who was waiting for her. That's what I'd have done."

"And now she's hanging from a tree," said Seth.

"For now." The man's face was flat. "The snare's never held anything older than about a hundred years. She's nearly three hundred. I don't know how long it'll hold her. Nobody does." He paused. "I'd quite like to be there when she finds out."

He looked at Leah.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to, Jacob realized. He just looked at her, and held out his hand, palm up, the way you'd hold it out to someone to help them over a log.

Leah looked at the hand.

Then she looked at Jacob.

"Leah," Jacob said. He didn't know what came after it.

"Go tell Carlisle," Leah said. "Tell Sam, if you want. Tell Mom I'm fine." She took her hand off her chest. "I *am* fine, Jake."

And Jacob looked at her face, in the rain, in that long black coat, and the awful thing was that he believed her. He'd known Leah Clearwater his entire life. He'd never once seen her fine. And she was standing in front of him, about to go off and do something completely insane with a guy who'd pinned her to the dirt twenty hours ago, and she was fine.

She put her hand in the man's.

"Wynoochee Lake," said the man, to Jacob, over her head. "North end. West side of the river, up the drainage. You'll smell it." He paused. "Fifty miles. Bring your teeth."

"You *want* us there?"

"If the snare holds," the man said, "I want the man dead and the woman in a box. If it doesn't hold, I want teeth. Lots of them. As fast as you can get them to me."

"I'm going to throw up on you," Leah told him.

"That's fair," said the man.

He turned on the spot.

The crack was flat and sharp, like a branch going in a hard frost. The air folded in where they'd been standing and folded back out again. Rain fell through the place where they'd been. The fire crackled. The donkey mug sat on the seat of the split camp chair with steam coming off it.

And the tent was gone.

Jacob knew it was still there. He was looking right at the place where it was. But he couldn't see it anymore, he couldn't see it at all, and his eyes kept sliding off the spot, and all of a sudden he was absolutely certain, completely, one hundred percent, that he'd left the stove on at Billy's.

"Jake?" said Seth.

Jacob stood there in the hollow with the stove burning in his head and the rain on his shoulders and the sweet leech-stink still coming up faint out of the ground under his feet. He looked south. Fifty miles. Through the national forest and out the other side, over the Quinault and down the long ridges to the Wynoochee, with two leeches hanging in a tree at the end of it. And Leah. And him.

"Are we going?" said Seth.

Jacob untied the cord on his ankle and stepped out of his cutoffs and tied them back on.

"Run," he said.

---

*End of Chapter Two*
