# Chapter One — Break Glass

---

The last thing Cecil Stedman saw on that street was Nolan Grayson's knuckles.

They filled the world. Pale skin over bone, a smear of somebody else's blood drying in the creases, the white cuff of the suit sliding back along a forearm that had never once in thirty years been made to work hard. Behind the fist was the face. The mustache. The patient, pleasant, PTA-meeting face, and the eyes in it gone flat as nickels.

The air in front of Cecil's nose compressed. He felt it on his lips, a pressure like the instant before a car door slams.

Then the street folded up and threw him away.

He hit the teleport pad on his hands and knees. The steel grating bit into his palms. His stomach, which had been somewhere behind his sternum for the last four minutes, decided it had had enough of the whole arrangement and climbed his throat. He got his head turned in time to put it on the grating instead of his own shirt.

"Director—"

"Give me a second." He spat. Wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. Spat again. "Give me one goddamn second."

Nobody gave him anything. The ops floor of the Global Defense Agency did not have seconds to give. It hummed around him at the pitch it only hit on the bad days, forty voices talking low and fast into headsets, keyboards rattling, someone on the far side of the room saying *repeat, repeat, I've lost the drone* in a voice that was trying hard to stay flat and not quite managing. The big wall of screens at the front of the room threw blue and white light across the faces bent toward them.

Cecil got one foot under himself, then the other. The pad tech, a kid named Marsh with a buzz cut and a coffee stain on his lanyard, had a hand hovering near his elbow and didn't seem sure whether he was allowed to touch the Director.

"How close," Cecil said.

Marsh looked at the readout on his console. Looked at it again. "Sir, the lock-and-pull cycle completed at—"

"Close, son. I asked how close."

Marsh swallowed. "Telemetry from your collar puts the impact vector inside eight inches when we pulled you. The time delta was—" He stopped. "It was under a nanosecond, sir. It rounds to zero."

Cecil nodded slowly. He looked down at his hands. They were shaking, a fine high tremor that ran up into the wrists, and he watched them do it with something like professional interest. Two wars and a long list of things that didn't have names. He'd been shot at by men, by machines, by things nobody had ever bothered to classify, and his hands had held still through all of it.

He made fists until they stopped.

"Donald," he said.

Marsh's face did something complicated. "The house went up, sir. The safehouse. Recovery's spinning up a team, but the structure is—there's no—"

"I know what the structure is." Cecil had been standing across the street from it when it went. He'd felt the heat on the side of his face. Donald had done exactly what Donald was supposed to do, one hand on the trigger and the other on Nolan's cape, and the blast had bought them roughly two minutes of Nolan Grayson shaking his head like a dog with water in its ears. Two minutes. That was the going rate for Donald Ferguson these days.

*They'll put him back together.* The thought came cold and practical, filed in the drawer where Cecil kept the things he was never going to say out loud. *They always do. And he won't remember this one either.*

He put it away. He'd feel bad about it later, on a schedule, the way he felt bad about everything.

"Get recovery on Donald's remains before anybody local gets a camera in there. Every piece." He started walking, and the floor came with him, analysts falling into step, tablets held up for him to look at. "Debbie Grayson."

"Extraction confirmed, sir." That was Agent Pruitt, falling in on his left with a tablet, a lean, gray-haired woman who'd come over from the Bureau and never once raised her voice in Cecil's hearing. "Mrs. Grayson's in Secure Suite B. She's—" Pruitt's mouth tightened. "She's asking for her son."

"Course she is. Where's Nolan?"

"Airborne." Someone threw it up on the main wall: a satellite overlay of a suburb Cecil had spent six years watching through a telescope, the street grid soft green, a single red pip moving above it. "He went straight up after you vanished. Hung there for about twenty seconds. Now he's moving. Low and slow. He's quartering, sir. Back and forth."

"He's looking for Mark." Cecil stopped in front of the wall and looked at the little red dot drawing its patient lines over the rooftops. A hunting pattern. A man walking a field looking for a dog that had slipped its collar. "Where's the boy?"

"Phone puts him across town. Stationary. Hasn't moved in twenty minutes."

Cecil rubbed his jaw. "Keep eyes on him. Nobody calls him. Nobody tells him a damn thing. Not yet."

Pruitt's tablet dipped an inch. "Sir. If Omni-Man reaches him before—"

"If we tell that kid what his daddy is over a cell phone while his daddy is in the air looking for him, that kid flies straight to his father and asks him if it's true." Cecil kept his eyes on the red dot. "And then I don't know what happens. Neither do you. I want Nolan busy. I want him so busy he forgets he's looking for anybody." He turned his head. "Sinclair's boys."

"Prepped. Twelve units in the drop bay."

"Drop them. All twelve. Put them between Nolan and the girlfriend's house and tell Sinclair I want them to be annoying."

"Sir."

"The tank in Sublevel Six."

That got a beat of silence from three separate people. Pruitt looked up from her tablet. "Sir, that specimen hasn't been cleared for—"

"I know what it hasn't been cleared for. I signed the paper that didn't clear it. Wake it up, tranq protocols off, put it on a transport, and drop it on Nolan Grayson's head." He pointed at a woman in a headset near the front. "Orbital."

"Platform Two is in position in nine minutes, sir. Platform Four in fourteen."

"Get me a firing solution on Two. I want it hot the second it's overhead."

"Collateral estimate on a residential—"

"Then don't shoot him over a residential. Shoot him where he isn't standing on top of a school." He looked back at the wall. "And find Eve Wilkins. If she's in the air, I want her on a channel. If she's not, keep it that way."

The room broke and moved. Cecil stood in the middle of it for three more seconds, watching the red dot. Then he turned and walked to his office.

It was a glass box at the back of the ops floor, raised four steps so he could see the whole room through it. He went up the steps and shut the door behind him, and the noise of the floor dropped to a murmur through the glass. He did not sit down. If he sat down, his knees would remember what had just happened to them, and he didn't have time for his knees.

He pulled open the bottom drawer of the desk.

There was a phone in it. Cheap, black, the kind you could buy at any gas station in America for thirty bucks, sitting alone on top of a manila folder with a coffee ring on the cover and no label on the tab. The phone had one number in it. Cecil had put that number in three years and two months ago, in a motel room in Mojave, California, with a six-foot-seven wolf sitting on the bed across from him eating a microwave burrito.

He took the phone out and set it on the desk.

He looked at it.

Through the glass, the main wall changed. The drop bay feed came up, a dozen pale shapes in harnesses swinging under the belly of a transport. Sinclair's Reanimen. Corpses with hardware for bones and a twenty-year-old's ideas about what made a good weapon. They'd been trouble once, and Cecil had made them his trouble, the same way he'd made a lot of things his trouble.

Nolan was going to go through them like a lawnmower through wet grass.

That was fine. All they had to do was take time.

Cecil picked up the phone and turned it over in his hand. He'd told Potter, the night in the motel, that he'd only ever call from this number for one reason. Potter had looked at him over the burrito with those flat green eyes and said, *Ja, I know what kind of reason, boet. I've been that kind of reason before.*

And then the son of a bitch had asked if the vending machine took twenties.

Cecil set the phone down again. On the wall, the transport banked, and the bay doors opened on a slab of bright blue suburban sky.

*Wait till they're in the air,* he told himself. *See what he does with them first. Then call.*

He had never, in a long life of making bad calls for good reasons, wanted so badly to be wrong about what he was about to do.

The Reanimen dropped.

---

Far to the west, and seven thousand feet up, Wren Stedman pulled the slicker brush down the length of her boyfriend's back and watched another handful of undercoat come away in a single soft black sheet.

"That's obscene," she said.

"Don't look at it, then."

"I can't *not* look at it. It's the size of a cat." She peeled the mat off the brush pins and added it to the pile on the grass beside her knee. The pile had started, an hour ago, about the size of a softball. It was now roughly the volume of a carry-on bag and stirred every time the breeze came down off the ridge. "I could stuff a pillow."

"You are not stuffing a pillow with me."

"A small one. A throw pillow."

"That's worse." Harry didn't lift his head. He lay on his stomach on the old red-and-black wool blanket with his chin on his crossed forearms, muzzle pointed down the slope toward the cabin, and his voice came up out of the blanket a little muffled. "Then I'd have to sit on the couch and look at it. Every night. A little square of me. Watching."

"You'd get used to it."

"I'd never get used to it, Rocket. I'd wake up screaming."

She snorted and went back in with the brush.

The meadow sat on a shelf of land above the cabin, an acre of bunchgrass and early lupine with a fringe of ponderosa around three sides and the fourth side open to the east, where the foothills fell away in long brown folds toward the plains. At this hour the sun had dropped behind the ridge at their backs, and the light came slanting through the pines in long gold bars, picking out every drifting hair in the air around them. Down below, the tin roof of the cabin threw a flat glare. Her truck was parked beside it, and beside her truck sat the black '69 Chevelle he'd rebuilt in the shed over two winters, which he talked to when he thought she couldn't hear.

A cooler sat at the corner of the blanket. On top of the cooler was a foil pie tin from the diner on Main Street, half a cherry pie gone in it, a fork stuck upright in the remainder like a flag on a summit. His phone lay face-down beside it, a little speaker turned up just loud enough to make out Zeppelin.

He'd taken his shirt off when they got up here, and he lay in nothing but an old pair of Levi's she'd altered for him the first winter they lived together, a hole cut just below the waistband in back and hemmed with blue bias tape on her mother's sewing machine. His tail came out through it and lay along the blanket. Every few minutes it lifted and dropped, once, with a soft thump.

She'd learned to read that thump. It meant *keep going*.

The aviators were pushed up on top of his head between his ears, where they absolutely should not have stayed, and stayed anyway.

She drew the brush down from the base of his skull to the middle of his back, over the long muscle along his spine, and felt it give and settle under the pins. His fur up close had layers, like strata in a road cut. The guard hairs were coarse and charcoal-black, gone almost blue where the light hit them, and under them the undercoat was softer and darker still, a true black, so dense she couldn't see skin when she parted it with her fingers. This time of year the undercoat let go. All of it. Whether anyone liked it or not.

She'd bought the brush at the farm-and-ranch supply in Longmont, along with an undercoat rake with a rubber grip, and had made the mistake of bringing it home in the bag. The cardboard insert had been on top. It said, in red block capitals, PROFESSIONAL DESHEDDING SYSTEM — FOR LARGE BREEDS.

He'd stood in the kitchen and read it twice and looked at her across the counter with his ears all the way back.

"Large breeds," he said now, as if he'd been following her thoughts. He did that sometimes. She'd stopped asking whether it was magic. "I still haven't forgiven you for that, you know."

"It's accurate."

"It's slander. It's a hate crime. I'm a person. I pay taxes."

"You pay taxes because Uncle Cecil made you up a Social Security number."

"And I pay them. Every April. On time. Like a mug." He shifted his weight on his forearms and the long line of his back flexed under her palm. "Two hundred and forty-one years old and I've never once been audited. You know how rare that is? The IRS respects me."

"The IRS is afraid of you."

"Same thing, from where I'm lying."

She laughed and kept brushing.

Two hundred and forty-one. He said it the same way he said the price of gas, flat and offhand, and three years ago she'd thought he was lying, and two years ago she'd thought he was crazy, and somewhere in the last year she'd stopped thinking anything about it at all. It was a number. He was two hundred and forty-one years old, and he liked his pie heated with a scoop of vanilla, and he sang along to Bob Seger in the shower in a South African accent that went all the way out on the long vowels. It was data. She was an engineer. She worked with the data she had.

She worked down toward his left shoulder blade, and her brush caught.

It was the whorl. A palm-sized patch of fur at the top of his left shoulder that grew in a spiral instead of lying flat, the guard hairs turning around a center point like water going down a drain. She'd brushed over it a hundred times. Every time, she slowed.

Under it, she knew, the skin was unmarked. She'd checked. The first month, she'd parted the fur there with her fingers when he was asleep and looked. No scar. No discoloration. Nothing at all to show where three thousand degrees of methane-oxygen exhaust had hit him square in the back.

The fur remembered, though. The fur had grown back wrong.

"You've stopped," he said.

"Sorry."

"You're doing the shoulder thing."

"I'm not doing a thing."

"You're doing a thing. I can feel you doing it. You go all quiet and start breathing like you're counting." He turned his head on his forearms. One emerald eye found her over the curve of his shoulder, the aviators sliding an inch down his skull. "T-plus three," he said.

"T-plus three point four," she said automatically, and then shut her mouth.

He grinned at her. The black lip pulled back off a row of white teeth and the tip of his tongue showed.

"Point four," he said. "You'd know."

She'd know.

She'd been the test conductor that morning in Mojave. Twenty-eight years old, eighteen months on the job, a headset on and a clipboard she didn't need on her knee, sitting in the bunker a quarter mile from Test Stand B with six other engineers and a bank of monitors. It had been the third full-duration hot fire on their forty-thousand-pound methalox engine, and her first as conductor, and she'd been so nervous she'd checked her own countdown checklist eleven times. She still remembered the smell of the bunker. Burnt coffee and dry-erase markers and the antistatic stuff they sprayed on the carpet.

Ignition was clean. Chamber pressure came up on the curve, right where it was supposed to. She'd had about two and a half seconds to feel good about that.

At T-plus three point four seconds, the high-speed camera on the flame trench showed a figure.

Nobody had seen it come. Nothing on the approach road, nothing on the fence line, nothing on the perimeter sensors. One frame the flame trench was empty concrete and a column of blue-white exhaust going into the deflector at Mach three. The next frame there was a shape standing at the bottom of the trench as if it had stepped through a door that wasn't there. Tall. Upright. Black.

The plume hit it in the back.

She'd hit the abort before she knew she'd done it. Even so, a cutoff took time. Valves had to close. Propellant in the lines had to go somewhere. For one point six seconds after she slapped the red switch, that engine kept doing exactly what it had been built to do, and the thing in the trench took all of it.

Then the plume was gone, and there was steam and smoke and the deflector ticking as it cooled, and on the thermal camera a shape lying face-down on the concrete, burning.

It lay there nine seconds. She knew because the camera had a timestamp and she'd watched that footage, frame by frame, maybe two hundred times since. Nine seconds of a body on fire, absolutely still. Every human-factors table she'd ever read said there was nothing left to be still.

At second ten, it put its paws under itself and pushed up.

It stood. It slapped out the flames on its jeans, which were the only thing it had been wearing. Its fur was mostly gone, black skin showing through in patches all over its back and shoulders and arms, and as she watched on the monitor the patches began to fill in again, fur coming up out of the skin like time-lapse grass. It looked around the flame trench. It looked up at the engine on the stand above it, still dripping. It looked straight into the lens of the high-speed camera, as if it knew exactly where that camera was, and its mouth moved.

The camera had no audio. She'd read it off the frame later. So had everyone else in the bunker. It was the first thing her boyfriend had ever said in this world.

*Well, that's rude.*

Then it had climbed out of the trench, walked across two hundred yards of scrub to the bunker, knocked on the blast door with three courteous raps, and when Dave Ochoa from fluids opened it with a fire extinguisher raised over his head like a club, the wolf had looked down at him with its fur still smoking and said, *Howzit. Sorry about your engine. Where am I?*

Wren realized she'd been holding the brush still on the whorl for some time.

"You were dead," she said. "On the telemetry. For nine seconds."

"I've told you, it doesn't count."

"It counts on the telemetry."

"Your telemetry's got opinions." He rolled his shoulder under her hand, a slow lazy shrug. "I was a bit dead. Then I wasn't. It doesn't take, Rocket. Never has. I've been dead more times than I've been to the dentist."

"You've never been to the dentist."

"Exactly my point. Look at these." He bared his teeth at the meadow. "Not one filling. Two hundred and forty-one."

She put the brush down on the blanket and laid her right hand flat on the whorl instead. The fur was hot from the sun, and under it his body ran hotter. Ten degrees above hers, give or take. In January she slept with her feet pressed against the backs of his calves and called him the space heater, and he grumbled and let her.

Her ring clicked against the brush handle where it lay. A plain band of stainless steel on the little finger of her working hand. She'd taken the obligation at graduation with two hundred other engineers in a gym in Golden and slid it on, and it had never come off since. Its edge had worn smooth on the steel of a hundred test stands. She watched it move as she smoothed her palm across the spiral of fur.

"Do you remember it?" she said. "Going through?"

His ears shifted. Both of them came around a few degrees toward her, then held.

"The arch?"

"Yeah."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might not answer. Down the slope, a magpie landed on the Chevelle's hood, considered its reflection, and left. The Zeppelin song ended and something by The Who started up, tinny through the little speaker.

"Cold," he said finally. "That's the first bit. You step through and there's this curtain, this old gray rag hanging in the arch, been hanging there a thousand years, and it moves when there's no wind. People hear voices behind it. I used to hear them. When I was fifteen I heard my godfather in there." He paused. "So you push through the curtain and it's cold, like the inside of a freezer cold, and then very loud, and then you lot were setting me on fire."

"We weren't trying to set you on fire."

"For people who weren't trying you were bloody good at it."

She smiled, a little. She didn't stop the hand.

"Why that day?" she said. "You never told me why that day."

His tail lay still on the blanket.

He took his time again. When he spoke it came out level and even, in the same voice he used to tell her the oil needed changing.

"Teddy died on a Tuesday," he said. "My godson. A hundred and nineteen. He was the last one. Ron went at a hundred and six, Hermione at a hundred and twelve, she'd have been livid about Ron beating her to something. My kids before that." He breathed out through his nose. "You're not meant to outlive your kids, Rocket. There's no word for it. There's a word for when your parents go and a word for when your wife goes, and there's no word for that, because nobody made one. Nobody thought you'd need it."

She kept her hand where it was.

"After Teddy, I sat in my kitchen for about a month. Fed the cat. Didn't do much else. And then one day I put my shoes on and walked into the Ministry, through the front door, down to the ninth floor. Nobody stopped me. I was a bit famous. I think they thought I'd come to visit." One ear flicked sideways. "I'd buried everybody I had. Some of them twice, if you count the portraits, and you should, because the portraits talk back. So I went and stood in front of the arch, and I thought, *right, let's see who's behind the curtain, then.*"

"And it was us."

"It was you. Brilliant engineers. Set me on fire."

She leaned forward and put her forehead down on the top of his shoulder, right against the whorl, and breathed in. He smelled like sun-hot fur and pine pitch and cool dust, the smell of a cellar door opened on a summer afternoon. She stayed there a while.

His tail thumped once on the blanket.

"The Hallows," she said into his fur. "Tell me that part again."

"You've heard that part."

"I like that part."

He made a low sound in his chest, half a growl and half a laugh. "Once upon a time," he said, in the voice he used to read the instruction manuals on her new appliances out loud, "there were three brothers, and Death was a right git to all of them. One got a wand that couldn't lose. One got a stone that brought back the dead, sort of. One got a cloak that made you invisible, even to Death. And about a hundred years later, a very skinny, very stupid seventeen-year-old ended up holding all three in the same afternoon. Didn't mean to. Didn't want them. I dropped the stone in a forest, I put the wand back in a dead man's tomb, and I kept the cloak because it was my dad's."

"And that was enough."

"Apparently." He sounded aggrieved about it. "Turns out Death keeps receipts. You hold all three at once, even for an afternoon, even if you give two of them back, and somewhere a box gets ticked. Master of Death. Sounds very grand. You get a title and no manual. I didn't find out for decades, Rocket, I just kept noticing I wasn't getting older the way everyone else was, and things that should've killed me were, you know." He waggled a paw over the edge of his forearm. "Not."

"And since the arch?"

"Since the arch, louder." He lifted his head and looked down the meadow at nothing. "Back home, the magic was always there, but you had to reach for it. Here it reaches back. Like somebody took the governor off the engine. First week in Mojave I tried to light a candle and took the eyebrows off a motel clerk."

"You never told me that."

"He didn't press charges. Your uncle gave him a very nice fruit basket."

She sat back up and picked up the brush again. Her eyes stung. She ignored that, and went down the left shoulder toward the elbow, where the undercoat was coming out in long tufts.

"He missed Sunday again," she said after a while.

"Cue Ball?"

"Don't call him that."

"I call him that to his face."

"I know, and every time you do it he gets this little vein in his temple and I have to watch it." She pulled a tuft free and added it to the pile. "That's three in a row. He's never missed three in a row. He didn't miss the week the Flaxans came through."

"He's working something."

"He's always working something."

"Ja, but there's working something and there's *working something*." Harry put his chin back down on his forearms. His ears had gone a little flatter. "Since the Guardians, he's been up nights. Your mum said he's been sleeping at the office."

"He doesn't have an office. He has a classified hole in the ground."

"He's been sleeping in the classified hole in the ground." He blew out a breath that ruffled the grass in front of his muzzle. "Seven of the toughest people on the planet die in one room in one afternoon, and the only one left standing is the bloke in the cape. If I was running the GDA, I'd be sleeping in my hole too."

She stopped brushing. "You think something's wrong with that?"

"I think I've got no idea." He said it plainly. "I've never met the man. I've seen him on the news, same as you. I just know that when a lot of people die in one room, there's always a story about it, and the first story's usually wrong."

She thought about Omni-Man on the news. Big, square-jawed, the mustache, the white suit torn and bloodied in every photo the networks ran that week. The whole country had cried about it. She'd cried about it, in the break room at work, eating a yogurt.

"Uncle Cecil would tell you," she said. "If it was something."

His tail flicked. Once. Amused.

"Rocket," he said, "your uncle wouldn't tell me what he had for breakfast if I was holding a gun to his head and he'd had toast."

She laughed despite herself, and he turned over.

He did it all at once, the way he did everything with his body, rolling onto his back on the blanket with a heave of shoulders and a whip of tail, his forearms flopping out to either side. The aviators finally gave up and slid off his head into the grass. He lay there with his ears flopped sideways and his chest bare to the sky, a broad black ruff of fur from collarbone to belt, and looked up at her upside-down.

"Front," he said.

"You are not a golden retriever."

"Agree to disagree."

"You're two hundred and forty-one years old. You have a library card. You've read *Middlemarch*."

"Twice. Do the front."

She sat back on her heels and looked at him. He looked back, upside-down, green eyes half-lidded, mouth open a little in what she'd decided long ago was the lupine equivalent of a smirk. The tip of his tail swept slowly back and forth on the wool.

"You're shameless," she said.

"I'm efficient. You've got the brush out. The front's right here. It's sitting idle." He lifted one forepaw from the blanket and waved it at his own chest, five black claws, pads gray-black and thick as a work glove. "Look at all this inventory."

She leaned over and kissed him on the end of the muzzle, which was cool and slightly damp and smelled like cherry filling.

"You have pie on your face," she said.

"Saving it for later."

She sat up and started in on his chest with the brush, long slow strokes from the collarbone down toward his ribs, and his eyes slid all the way shut. His back leg twitched. She saw it. She did not say anything about it, because she was a kind woman, and because she was saving it for Sunday dinner, if Uncle Cecil ever came to another one.

The meadow ticked and rustled around them. The wind came down off the ridge and stirred the pile of undercoat and lifted a few strands of it out over the grass, where they drifted east toward the plains in the gold light. The Who gave way to something with a slide guitar. Down the hill, the magpie came back to the Chevelle and tried again with its reflection.

She was halfway down his sternum, working a mat loose with the edge of the rake, when the music cut out.

The phone on the cooler buzzed once against the pie tin.

Then it rang. An old-fashioned telephone bell, a sound she'd heard come out of that phone exactly once before, three years ago, on a test call in a motel parking lot in Mojave. He'd set it as the only special ringtone on his phone. Every other number got a buzz.

Harry's eyes opened.

He didn't move otherwise. He lay on his back in the grass, and she watched his ears come up off the blanket, both of them, upright and pointed straight forward. Then, slowly, they went flat against his skull.

His tail went still.

The phone rang again.

He reached out without looking and the phone lifted off the cooler on its own and floated across the eighteen inches of air into his forepaw. He turned it over. She leaned to see the screen.

CUE BALL — BAD DAY.

"Harry," she said.

He sat up. The fur on his chest, half-brushed, stood out in two directions. He swung the phone up to the side of his head, held it against one ear, and when he spoke his voice was the same as it had been a minute ago, dry and a little lazy, all the way down to the accent.

"Uncle Cec," he said. "You only ring from this one when something's on fire."

The first Reanimen hit Nolan Grayson at a closing speed of four hundred miles an hour, and for one long, satisfying second Cecil watched him tumble.

It was a good hit. Sinclair had built them dense, packed their chests with hydraulics and their skulls with ceramic plate, and the one in front had gone in shoulder-first like a linebacker and caught Nolan square between the shoulder blades. The drone feed showed it in clean high-definition: a white-and-red shape knocked sideways across the sky, cape snapping, three more pale bodies piling in behind the first before he could stop himself. They took him down out of the air together in a knot of limbs and went through the roof of a two-story colonial in a burst of shingles and pink insulation.

Somebody on the ops floor said, "Yes," under their breath.

Cecil didn't. He had the black phone in his hand, and his thumb was already on the only number in it.

The roof of the colonial came up in a single piece.

Nolan rose out of the hole with a Reanimen in each hand. He held them by the throat, one in each fist, the way a man holds two bottles of beer by the neck, and he hung there over the wreck with insulation drifting down around him and looked at them. One of them was still swinging at him. Its fist bounced off his cheek. He didn't blink.

He brought his hands together.

The two heads met with a sound the drone's microphone picked up from two hundred feet away. It sounded like somebody dropping a box of dishes. What came apart came apart in a spray of hydraulic fluid and old gray meat and shards of white ceramic, and Nolan opened his hands and let the bodies drop back into the house and turned to meet the next one.

Cecil pressed the button.

It rang. On the wall, Nolan caught a Reanimen by its outstretched arm and tore the arm off at the shoulder and used it to beat the next one out of the sky. It rang again. A third came in low from the side, and he put his hand through its chest. Straight through, sternum to spine. His forearm came out the back of the thing between the shoulder blades with a fistful of wiring, and the Reanimen hung there on his arm like a coat on a hook, its legs still kicking.

The line clicked.

"Uncle Cec." Potter's voice, dry, lazy, that South African lilt sliding along the vowels. Birds behind him. Wind in grass. "You only ring from this one when something's on fire."

Cecil closed his eyes for a second. Opened them.

"Is Wren with you?"

A pause. "She's right here. She's brushing me."

Cecil looked at the wall, where Nolan Grayson was shaking a Reanimen's corpse off his arm the way a man shakes off a wet glove.

Then Mark was beside him, and the two of them went at the specimen together, one from each side.

"No," Debbie said. "No, no, Mark, honey, no—" She turned on Cecil. "He doesn't know. He doesn't *know*, he thinks you're the—tell him. Get him on the radio and *tell him.*"

"Mrs. Grayson. If I tell him now, with his father three feet from him—"

"Then he'll get away from him!"

"Or he'll stop," Cecil said, "and turn around, and ask his father if it's true. Right there. And neither one of us knows what Nolan does then."

Debbie stared at him. Her face had gone white to the lips.

Then she turned and looked up at Potter again. Straight up. She had to tilt her head all the way back.

"Are you here to kill my husband?"

The room had gone quiet around them. Cecil saw two analysts at the nearest console look up, and look down again fast.

"I'm here to stop him, ma'am. I'd like to do it with him still breathing at the end."

"And if you can't?"

"Then I'll make sure your boy gets home."

Debbie Grayson looked at him a while longer. "That doesn't answer me."

"No, ma'am." His ears had come back up. "It's the only one I've got that's true, though."

On the wall, Mark Grayson put his fist through the middle of the specimen from Sublevel Six, and the thing came apart around him in a wet gray spray across all four lanes of the road. It slumped. "Platform Two is overhead, sir," the woman at the orbital console called. "Firing solution is green."

"Hold fire."

"Sir?"

"The boy's right next to him. Hold fire." Cecil watched Nolan and Mark hanging there in the air above the wreck, a father and a son, side by side, both breathing hard. "Keep the solution live. Nobody pushes that button unless I say the words."

"Contact," someone else said, sharp. "High, fast, coming in from the west—"

A third shape came down out of the sky.

Big. Broad through the shoulders. Dark hair streaming. It hit the road in front of Nolan Grayson hard enough to crater the asphalt and stood up out of the crater with its fists clenched, and Cecil heard Debbie suck in a breath.

"That's—" Wren said.

"The Immortal," Cecil said.

Potter's head turned. "Thought all the Guardians were dead."

"They were. He's the Immortal. Mauler Twins put his head back on his neck, and he woke up remembering exactly who took it off." Cecil's jaw worked. "He was out the door before anybody could brief him."

"He's never waited for a plan in his life."

"Huh." Potter's eyes stayed on the wall. "Relatable."

The drone mic caught the Immortal's voice. It was ragged, raw, hoarse from a throat that had spent a long time separated from the rest of him.

"*You killed us!*" He pointed at Nolan. His whole arm shook. "*You killed all of us! Red Rush. Darkwing. Green Ghost. War Woman. Martian Man. Aquarus. You killed them with your bare hands, Nolan, I was there, I SAW you—*"

"Stay with her," he said, and tipped his muzzle at Debbie.

On the wall, Nolan Grayson took one step toward the Immortal.

That was all it took. One step and a reach. The Immortal came in half at the waist. Cecil watched it happen in high definition. Pad three flared white.

On the wall, Nolan rose into the sky. He reached out and took his son by the shoulder, and Mark let him, and the two of them went up together, fast, straight up out of the drone's frame.

Eve Wilkins was still hovering three blocks back when the light flashed in the road.

She'd been hovering there for what felt like an hour and was probably ninety seconds. Long enough to watch the Immortal come apart in the middle of the street like a wishbone. Long enough to hear Omni-Man say *We need to talk* in the same calm, fatherly voice he used on every newscast she'd ever seen him on. Long enough to watch Mark go up into the sky beside him without a word, without looking back, without looking at her.

She'd told herself to move about twenty times. Her body hadn't listened. Cecil had told her to hold back, and she'd held back, and now there was nobody left on the street to hold back from.

Her hands were shaking inside their glow. She made them stop.

The flash came at the edge of the wreck, maybe forty yards from the Immortal's body. A white sheet of light, there and gone, the way Cecil's people always came and went. She braced for agents. Tactical, body armor, black SUVs.

What stepped out of the light was a wolf.

Eve stared.

A wolf on two legs. Standing upright, tall, taller than any man she'd ever stood next to. Black fur. A wolf's head, long-muzzled, ears up and pointed. It had on a green flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbows and a pair of faded jeans, and it was barefoot on the broken asphalt, and its feet—paws, they were paws, long and flat and black, the whole sole planted on the ground like a person's—were standing in a puddle of the specimen's gray blood. A tail hung behind it, very still.

It looked at the specimen's carcass for about a second. Then it turned and looked at the two halves of the Immortal, and walked toward them.

It made no noise. That was the thing Eve noticed afterward, when she tried to tell it to herself in order. The street was full of broken glass and chunks of concrete and loose rebar, and it walked straight through the middle of all of it, heel-to-toe, upright, and nothing crunched.

She dropped out of the sky and landed between it and the body, twenty feet off, with both hands up and glowing.

"Don't move."

It stopped. It looked at her. Its eyes were bright green, very bright against the black fur, and they went from her face to her hands and back again without any hurry at all.

"Wasn't planning on it," it said.

The voice was low and rough and had an accent in it she couldn't place. Something from far away. The words came out of that long muzzle perfectly clean.

"You're the pink one," it said.

"Atom Eve."

"Harry." It dipped its head. "I work for Cue Ball."

She blinked. "For—Cecil?"

"You know another one?" One of its ears flicked out sideways. Then the ear came back around, and the green eyes went past her shoulder to the upper half of the Immortal lying in the road behind her, and stayed there.

Eve didn't lower her hands. She didn't stop him, either, when he walked around her in a wide slow circle, giving her plenty of room, and crouched down beside the body.

The Immortal's eyes were open. His arms had stopped moving. His face was turned up toward the sky, and his mouth was still a little open, as if he were about to shout one more name.

The wolf—Harry—looked at him for a while. Then he reached out with one forepaw, broad and black with thick gray pads on the palm, and closed the Immortal's eyes with the side of one pad, very carefully, one and then the other.

"Somebody bag him up," he said. "Both bits. Get them to Cecil."

"He's dead," Eve said. Her voice came out thin. "He's—he was *dead*, he came back and he's dead again, he's—"

"He's the Immortal." Harry stood. "Says so on the tin. He'll want his bits back."

She opened her mouth and couldn't think of a single thing to say to that.

He tipped his head back and looked at the sky.

It was a hard blue afternoon sky, washed out at the edges by the smoke from the parking garage, and there was nothing in it at all. Mark and Omni-Man were gone. Eve had watched them shrink to a dot and then to nothing, north and up, faster than she could have followed even if Cecil had let her try.

Harry's nostrils flared. Once, wide. Then again, short and quick, three small sniffs, his head turning a few degrees to the left. His ears swiveled forward together and locked.

"Aftershave," he said, mostly to himself. "Something old-fashioned. Blood under it. And whatever that is that comes off them when they fly. Like a lightning strike."

"They went north," Eve said. "Straight up and north. I couldn't—Cecil told me not to—"

"Good. He was right." Harry didn't look down from the sky. "You'd have caught up with them and then you'd have had to decide what to do about it. Nobody should have to decide that today. Not at your age."

"You don't know how old I am."

"You're younger than my car." His eyes had gone slightly unfocused, the green of them fixed on a point somewhere high above the city. "There," he said softly. "There you are."

"There *what* are?"

"Big things leave a hole in the air when they go through it," he said. "For a while. That's a very big thing."

Eve looked up where he was looking. She saw blue. Smoke. A news helicopter circling at a cautious distance, its camera turned toward the two of them.

She looked back at him. He'd rolled his shoulders under the flannel, a long slow loosening that went from his neck all the way down to the tip of his tail, and his tail had begun, very faintly, to move. A small flick at the end. Once. Again.

"You saw what he did," she said. "You saw what he did to the Immortal. To all of them. You saw it."

"I saw it."

"He'll kill you."

"He can have a go." The muzzle curled at one corner. "People have."

"I'm *serious*."

"So am I, Pinky." He looked down at her then, finally, and the curl went out of his muzzle. "Listen to me. You stay here. You help Cecil's people with the big lad, when they get here. And if that kid comes back without his dad—" He paused. "He's going to need a friend who isn't a government. That's you."

Eve swallowed. Her throat hurt.

"And if he comes back *with* his dad?"

The green eyes held hers.

"Then I've had a very bad afternoon," Harry said, "and so has your boss."

He rose.

Eve had flown for years. She knew what lift looked like. She knew the little crouch that came before it, the bracing of the legs, the push. She knew the way air moved off a person when they went up, the dust, the grit, the slap of it against your face.

He came up off the asphalt from a dead standstill, the way smoke comes off a candle, straight up and silent, his footpaws hanging loose. No dust moved. The puddle of gray blood he'd been standing beside didn't so much as ripple. Ten feet up he was still rising slowly. Twenty. At forty feet he turned his head north, and his ears went flat back against his skull, and his tail swept out straight behind him like a rudder.

Then he was gone.

One second there was a black shape over the street with a green flannel shirt snapping around it, and the next there was a long straight line of disturbed smoke running up and north across the sky, and nothing at the end of it.

Two seconds later the sound caught up with her. A single flat crack from very high up, like a door slamming somewhere on the other side of the sky. Car alarms went off all down the street.

Eve stood in the road between a dead monster and a dead immortal with her hands still glowing, staring north.

She touched her earpiece.

"Cecil."

"Go ahead, Eve." His voice was very calm. It was the calmest she'd ever heard it.

"Who the hell did you just send after Omni-Man?"

The channel stayed quiet for a moment. Somewhere in the background behind him she could hear a room full of people working, and under it, faint, a woman crying.

"Somebody I've been saving," Cecil Stedman said.

Then the line went dead, and Eve was alone on the street with the sirens coming, and the smoke, and a long gray scar across the sky that pointed the way they'd gone.
