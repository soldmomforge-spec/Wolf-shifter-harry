# True North

## Chapter Four: Wolf Mojo

---

### I.

The third pint went the way Nomsa had said it would. Leah would learn that about Nomsa. She was right about everything, and she kept count.

Technically it was Harry's second pint, and technically it was half of one, and Leah had drunk it staring him dead in the eye. For about a minute and a half, nothing happened. Harry sat well back in his chair, out of range this time, with the whiskers on the left side of his muzzle shriveled into little black curls and the burnt tuft standing up between his ears, and watched her the way you'd watch a firework that hadn't gone off yet.

"See?" Leah said. A thread of smoke came out with it. "Nothing. Your bar's all talk."

"Give it a minute."

The heat started low, like somebody had dropped a coal down the waistband of her jeans. Then it climbed. It was bigger than the last one, a lot bigger, a balloon filling up in her chest with something that wanted out, and she had exactly one clear thought.

*Not at him. Not twice.*

She turned her head.

The fire came out of her sideways with a sound like a gas burner catching, *WHUMPH*, blue at the root and gold at the tip and a good three feet long. It rolled across the empty table next to theirs and hit the curtains on the window by the hearth.

The new curtains. Heavy dark red velvet with gold tassels, and they went up like they'd been waiting their whole lives for the chance.

The girl by the fire screamed with joy. Grimbald made a sound Leah had never heard come out of anything alive. And Nomsa came down the room with the brass dragon in both arms, planted her feet, and squeezed. A jet of white foam roared out of its jaws, smelling hard of peppermint, and four seconds later the window was a dripping white mess with a black rag hanging in the middle of it, smoking gently, one gold tassel still swinging.

The whole bar burst into applause.

Grimbald stood on his box with his long hands spread flat on the bar, looking at what was left of his curtains. Then he turned his head and looked at Harry.

"Two curtains, Potter."

"She did that one."

"She is with you. *Two curtains.*"

Harry let out a long breath through his nose and dug a fistful of gold out of the pouch on his belt. He counted it onto the bar one coin at a time with the tip of a claw, and kept going after Leah thought he'd stop. When he finally quit, Grimbald made a small, dissatisfied noise, and Harry put down two more.

"Velvet," Grimbald said, sweeping the coins into his apron. "From Lyon."

"Course it was."

"Sorry," Leah told the goblin. Smoke came out with that too. "About the curtains."

Grimbald looked at her with his small black eyes. Then he looked up at the ceiling, at the arrow running from *LEAH* to *sorry about the curtain*, and the two words under it.

"Hm," he said, and climbed down off his box.

Nomsa thumped the dragon down on their table. "Out. Both of you. Before you burn down the rest of my house." On the way past, though, she put a hand on Leah's shoulder, heavy and quick. "Sisi. You come back. Bring him if you have to."

They went out under the ceiling. In the corner by the hearth, Pieter lifted his glass an inch as Leah passed, and Lerato looked her in the eye and nodded once, slow. Leah nodded back. She didn't know what else to do.

"*Bye, Mr. Potter!*" yelled the girl by the fire.

Harry lifted a paw without turning around, and the black door shut on the noise like a lid.

---

The alley was cold and smelled like cats. Behind them the bricks shuffled themselves back into a wall with a soft clicking, like somebody squaring a deck of cards.

Two pitchers of Kokanee on Wednesday had slid off her like rain. Grimbald's stout had stuck. She was hot all the way through, and her feet felt a long way off, and everything was slightly funny. The dumpster was hilarious.

"You okay?" Harry said.

"I set a curtain on fire."

"You did."

"From Lyon. Where's Lyon?"

"France."

"I set a French curtain on fire," Leah said, "in Africa," and had to put a hand on the dumpster.

Harry waited with his paws in his coat pockets. In the light from the street, the burnt tuft between his ears stuck straight up like a small black flame. He looked ridiculous. She looked at him too long, and made herself quit.

"It's half nine," he said. "Want to go home? Condition one. You hated the tin, so I carry you home. My way."

Leah looked down the alley at Long Street, the music and the honking taxis. She thought about cabin four, and lying in that bed by herself with the lake outside and the silence in her head.

"Not yet," she said. "Where do *you* go? When you're done chasing some guy around the planet?"

"Flat in Muizenberg. Over a surf shop. Smells like wetsuits, and the bloke who owns the shop plays reggae at seven in the morning. Biltong lives there. Mrs. Pillay's downstairs."

"And?"

"And what?"

"Your ear did a thing. There's an *and*."

His ears went sideways. "And there's a place up north. In the mountains outside Joburg. Hennessey doesn't know about it. Hermione does, but Hermione knows about everything."

"Show me."

"It's thirteen hundred k's. You'll hate it more than the tin."

"Nothing's worse than the tin."

"This is."

Leah walked up to him. She was five-ten, she'd been the tall girl her whole life, and she wasn't used to tipping her head back for anybody. She tipped it back. Then she held up both arms, the way Seth used to when he was four.

"Carry me," she said.

He looked at her arms. Then he bent and got one arm under her knees and the other behind her back and lifted her like she weighed nothing, which she knew for a fact she didn't. The heat of him came straight through his shirt, higher even than hers. Pine and wet stone and creek water, and singed fur.

"Hold on," he said, close to her ear. "Tight."

She put her arms around his neck and her face in the fur under his jaw.

"Three. Two—"

He didn't say one.

The world squeezed. It squeezed her from every side at once, like the alley had turned into a rubber tube a size too small and somebody was shoving her through it. Her ribs folded in. Her eyeballs pressed back into her skull. The only real things anywhere were the fur under her face and the arms around her, which didn't give an inch.

Then it let go, and there was air, cold and thin and dry, and she was gasping into his neck.

"Put me down," she said.

He put her down. Her legs didn't work. She sat on a rock and put her head between her knees.

"That," she told her knees, "was worse."

"Told you."

"You're going on the list."

"What list?"

"Things you told me that I'm going to hold against you."

"Fair," said Harry.

---

### II.

When she could lift her head, she forgot all about her stomach.

They were up high, on a long hill of grass gone silver in the dark, and the hill fell away below her into a wide black valley with a few scattered lights and, miles off, a long pale sheen of water. Behind her, cliffs, ragged and black against the sky.

And the stars.

She'd thought she knew what stars looked like. She'd grown up on a coast where the clouds parted maybe six nights a year. This was something else. The Milky Way went across the whole sky like a spill of salt, bright enough to throw a gray light on the grass, so thick that the dark lanes through the middle of it looked like smoke.

None of them were right. She looked for the Big Dipper and it wasn't there. She looked for the North Star her dad had shown her off the end of the dock at First Beach, and it wasn't there either.

"Where's the Dipper?" she said.

"Other side of the planet." Harry was looking up too. "You're upside down. We get different stars to make up for it." He pointed with a claw. "Southern Cross. Four in a kite, and the little one off the side. That's the paper. *Southern Cross Sentinel*. The bloke with the camera."

"Great," Leah said. "So I'm going to be on the front page of a constellation."

Low along the southeast horizon, the sky was a long smear of dirty orange, the color of streetlights reflected off a lot of smoke.

"Joburg," he said. "Pretoria, further round. Close enough to get to. Far enough that I don't have to smell it."

She breathed in. Dry grass, dusty, like a barn in August. The cold char of an old grass fire. Rock. Something sweet and thick as honey from somewhere downhill. No fir, no cedar, no salt, no rain. Not one thing she'd ever smelled before.

A bird was calling down the hill, a quick liquid run of notes that tumbled and started over. *Good-lord-deliver-us. Good-lord-deliver-us.*

"Nightjar," Harry said. "Bloke I bought the place off said it was praying."

"Same," said Leah.

Up on the cliffs something barked, hoarse and furious, *wa-HOO*, and something else answered.

"Baboons," Harry said. "They don't like me. I'm a big thing that smells like a wolf and walks past their cliff at night." His tail flicked. "They've got opinions."

He held out a paw, and she let him pull her up. He didn't let go right away, and she didn't make him.

A couple of hundred yards down the track there was an old farm gate between two stone posts, with a rusted tin sign wired to it. *WOLWEKRANS.*

"Wolf Cliff, more or less," Harry said. The first *w* came out of him halfway to a *v*. "Old Boer name. The farmers called hyenas wolves."

"So you bought a house called Wolf Cliff."

"Estate agent thought it was funny."

"Was it?"

"I bought it," Harry said, and opened the gate.

She felt the ward the second she stepped through. Vadas's had been static and cobweb and a chord that buzzed in her teeth. This one closed over her like bathwater. The hair on her arms lay flat.

"It knows you're with me," he said. "Anybody else walks through, they get a strong feeling they left the stove on, and go back to their car. Works on the baboons. Mostly."

The house sat in a hollow under the cliffs, with an enormous old fig growing out of the rocks behind it, its roots poured down the stone like wax. Long and low, rough whitewashed stone, a corrugated iron roof faded to the color of dried blood. A wide stoep ran along the front. Off to one side, a windmill creaked around on its skinny tower, pumping something somewhere with a hollow *clank*. No lights. No neighbors. Nothing but the hill and the stars.

"Mind the third step," Harry said. "It's loose."

He ducked late going through the front door, and she heard the top of his head scrape the lintel. When she followed, she looked up. There was a fuzzy gray smudge on the whitewash, right at the height of his ears.

"Fur," she said.

"Shut up."

The lamps lit themselves as he walked through, one after another. One big room: a kitchen at one end, a stone fireplace at the other big enough to roast a goat in, a long squashy green sofa built for somebody seven feet tall. Every inch of the sofa was covered in black fur. So was the rug. So was the armchair. There was a drift of it in the corner by the door like snow.

"Wow," said Leah.

"It's spring. I shed." His ears went sideways. "Hermione bought me a brush. I've used it twice."

Books everywhere, in stacks on the floor and face-down on the table beside a coffee mug with something growing in it. A sink full of dishes.

"You're a slob," Leah said.

"I've been in Canada nine days."

"Those dishes are older than nine days."

He looked at the dishes. "Maybe."

The photographs by the fireplace were moving. Every one of them. A lanky redhead and a woman with a wild mass of brown hair at their wedding, waving. "Ron and Hermione," Harry said. "Best mates. Since I was eleven." A young couple dancing, a woman with dark red hair and a man with messy black hair and glasses, turning in a slow circle and starting over. Leah saw his eyes in the woman's face and his hair on the man, and didn't ask.

Two young men on a huge motorbike, laughing. One of them handsome enough to be rude about it. The other thin and tired, with a scar across his cheek, laughing hardest.

"Sirius," Harry said. "And Remus. Remus taught me, third year. He was a werewolf. Moon and all. He was ashamed of it his whole life. Used to apologize for it to people who'd never met him."

Leah thought about a gaunt man with three scars down his cheek, and a woman with both hands wrapped tight around her drink.

"That's why you pay for the kettle," she said.

"That's why I pay for the kettle."

The last picture was a kid of about eight, soaked, on a beach, riding the shoulders of a big man in a red flannel shirt. The kid's hair was turquoise; as she watched, it flashed pink and back, and the kid shrieked and grabbed the man's ears. The man was Harry. The human one, six-two and square-jawed, black hair sticking up at the back, grinning.

"Teddy," Harry said. "My godson. Remus's boy. Does that with his hair when he's happy."

"He looks like a highlighter."

"I'll tell him. He'll be thrilled."

---

Her stomach made a noise like a garbage disposal starting up.

"You ate two hours ago," he said.

"I've been on fire since."

The fridge held two coils of sausage in butcher's paper, half a block of cheese with a bite out of it, a jar of chutney, and a carton of milk she could smell from across the room.

"Boerewors," Harry said, taking out the sausage and shutting the door on the milk. "And there's rusks. That's dinner."

"That's a snack."

"Then it's a big snack."

He cooked it on the stoep in a half-barrel on iron legs, over chunks of some hard dark wood he lit by looking at it. He was very serious about it. He stood over the coals with his ears forward and his nose going, turning the coil with a pair of tongs, and when she opened her mouth he held up a paw without looking around.

"Hang on. This is the important bit."

"It's a sausage."

"It's a *braai*," Harry said, like that settled it.

Fat dripped on the coals and flared. The smell came up off the grill, meat and coriander and black pepper and something she couldn't name, and her stomach made the garbage-disposal noise again, so loud that a baboon up on the cliff barked back.

She ate the first coil standing at the stoep wall with her fingers, fat running down her wrist. It was the best thing she'd eaten since the bunny chow, which had been the best since the Lumberjacks, which had been the best since La Push. She was starting to notice a pattern. He ate the second coil, and they split the cheese and dipped hard, dry rusks into the chutney, which he said was a crime against rusks, and did anyway.

Then they sat on the wall and looked at the wrong stars, and the nightjar prayed, and the windmill creaked.

"Can I see it?" she said. "The other one. At the inn you said you could be a big wolf on four legs."

He was quiet a moment. "Ja. Okay."

He walked out onto the flat grass in front of the house and dropped forward.

Her phase was an explosion, heat and tearing and four feet hitting the ground at once. His was slow and silent. He went down on his forepaws and the shape of him poured outward, longer and bigger and lower, shoulders rolling forward and thickening, muzzle stretching, the coat and the clothes folding away into him like they'd never been there. Three seconds. No sound.

Then there was a wolf standing on the grass.

She'd known it would be big. She took a step back anyway.

His shoulder was higher than the top of her head. He was as long as a small car, with a heavy plumed tail sweeping out behind him, and so black in the starlight he was barely there, a hole cut out of the night in the shape of a wolf. His eyes were the same green. He had to weigh well over a ton. The grass under his paws barely bent.

Her wolf came up out of her so fast she grabbed the post behind her.

It wanted *out*. It wanted to drop to four legs and walk around him and find out exactly how much bigger he was, and then run, up into the cliffs, with him behind her. Her spine ached to stretch. She held on to the post and breathed through it.

"Leah?" The voice came out of the big black head exactly the same, low and rough, every consonant clean. "You all right?"

"I can't," she said through her teeth. "If I change, they're there. Seth. Jake. Whoever's phased. It's lunchtime at home; somebody'll be running patrol. The second I go wolf, they see everything. Tonight. All of it."

"Ah," said the wolf. "Including the bit with the curtains."

"*Especially* the bit with the curtains."

He folded down onto the grass, front legs and then back, until he was lying with his chin on his paws like a dog in front of a fire. A black hill in the starlight.

She let go of the post and went to him. Up close he smelled like himself, only so much more of it that it filled her whole head. She put her hand flat on the side of his neck. Coarse on top, soft underneath, and the heat of him came up through it like a stove. He let her. She walked all the way around him, slow, one hand trailing in his fur, the way she'd have circled a new wolf in the pack in her other skin. Then she sat down with her back against his shoulder. It was like leaning on a hill that breathed.

"How many people have seen this?" she said.

"The healers. Hermione. Ron. Teddy, once; he rode me round the garden screaming till his hair went orange. A smuggler in Marseille." A pause. "He's in a cell in Geneva now. I'm told he still has nightmares."

"The whole pack's seen me," Leah said. "Every inch, every time I change. Through their eyes and mine at once. I've never once had a say about who looks."

The huge head came around until one green eye was two feet from her face.

"You've got a say now," he said.

She didn't say anything for a while.

"Change back," she said.

The hill behind her shrank, and she fell backward onto his chest. He was on his back in the grass in his coat and shirt and trousers, all of it exactly as it had been, and she was sprawled across him with her face six inches from the underside of his jaw.

"That keeps happening," Harry told the sky.

"You keep doing it."

"Not on purpose."

"Liar. Your ear twitched."

He laughed, a rumble she felt through her palm. Then he stopped, and she felt his whole chest go still.

"Leah," he said. "Can I kiss you?"

He was lying very still, ears tipped toward her, eyes on her face, waiting to hear what she'd say.

"You're asking," she said.

"I'll always ask."

Something happened to her throat. She grabbed two fistfuls of the fur on his chest and pulled herself up and kissed him.

---

Kissing a wolf, it turned out, was mostly geometry.

She hit the side of his muzzle first, aiming for where a mouth would be on a person. She felt him laugh through his nose. She moved; he tipped his head; there were teeth, a lot of them, long and smooth and very close, and he went still as stone holding them away from her, and she could feel how hard he was concentrating.

She bit his lip.

The sound he made went all the way down to her feet.

After that the geometry sorted itself out. His tongue was long, a fact she filed away for later. One of his paws cupped the back of her head, pads rough against her scalp, claws curled up and away from her skin, holding her like she might break. She kissed him harder to make the point, and his tail thumped the ground beside them like a drum.

When she lifted her head there was fur in her mouth. She spat it out to the side.

"Sorry," Harry said.

"Shut up." He looked dazed, ears all the way back, like somebody had hit him with a shovel. "How long since you did that?"

His ears went flat. "Three years. Bit more."

"Since the bite. Nobody?"

"Who'd have me?" He said it plainly, to the sky. "There's a box on a form for my teeth, Leah. One witch asked Hennessey if I'd had my shots."

Leah thought about a big wolf leaning on a bar in Atlin saying *fake wolves. Knock-offs. Bootlegs.*

"Fake witches," she said.

He went still. Then he laughed so hard she bounced on his chest.

"Knock-offs," Leah said over the top of him. "Off-brand. Made in a garage—"

"*Stop*—"

She kissed him again to shut him up. It worked.

When she pulled back, she got up and stood in the grass, and her legs were shaking, and the Apparition had been a long time ago.

"Inside," she said.

His ears came up. "Leah—"

"Inside, Potter."

---

### III.

The bed took up most of the back bedroom, under the slope of the iron roof.

"That's a bed for a horse," Leah said.

"Three doubles. Permanent Sticking Charm." Harry stood in the doorway with his ears brushing the frame. "I kept falling off the end of the normal ones."

Quilts in six patterns that didn't match. Pillows everywhere. A long dent down the middle in the exact shape of him. Fur. The window was open, and the dry grass smell came in, and the nightjar.

Leah sat on the edge and started pulling off Marg's daughter's boots.

"Before," Harry said.

She looked up. His ears had gone halfway back.

"There's always a before with you."

"There's a thing. A big thing." His ears went flatter. "That's not what I—" He shut his eyes. "Christ."

Leah dropped the second boot and waited.

"The healers at St Mungo's had me for six months after Romania," he told a spot on the wall over her head. "They wrote down every bit of me that came out different. Two volumes. Some of the different bits are under the trousers." He rubbed the back of his neck with a paw. "Chapter nine. There are diagrams. Hermione read it. She hasn't looked me in the eye at Christmas since."

"*Diagrams*."

"She annotated them."

Leah looked at him for a long moment.

"Is this about the knot?"

He made a noise like a man who'd swallowed a bee.

"I grew up on a rez with forty dogs, Potter." Leah peeled off the outer pair of socks. "I've spent a year and a half inside nine boys' heads. I know how it works."

"I'm not a dog."

"Then stop acting like I'm going to break."

His ears came up slowly. "You might. Break."

"I broke my jaw on a wall on Wednesday. Took thirty seconds." She threw the second pair of socks at him. They bounced off his chest. "Come here."

He came.

---

He fumbled the tie on Josie's top with his claws, twice, and on the third try she pushed his paws away and did it herself. It went over the back of the chair, dark green in the starlight.

"Josie lent me that," she said.

"I'll tip Josie."

"Don't you dare."

She had her hands in the fur of his chest, finding out that it went all the way down, thick and dense and softer underneath, and that he was hotter than her, hot as a sauna. When she dragged her nails through the fur over his ribs he made a sound she wanted to hear again. She sneezed.

"Sorry," he said.

"Say sorry about the fur one more time and I'm leaving."

He was careful with everything else, claws in, pads rough on her skin. She wasn't careful at all. She'd been careful her whole life, with Sam and her mom and the whole pack in her head, every thought, every day. She was done.

The quilts ended up on the floor. The two of them put out so much heat that the air in the little room shimmered, and the cold through the window hit her bare back and felt like nothing.

The bracelet stayed on her wrist, black and gray and green. At one point he caught her wrist in his paw and looked at it.

"You kept it on."

"It's ugly."

She'd been the tall girl since fifteen, too tall to be cute, too strong to be pretty the way boys liked, and after the wolf came she'd quit caring. Next to him she was small. He was too much of everything, too tall, too heavy, too hot, too many teeth, and the bulk of him blotted out the window and the whole wrong sky. She found out she liked being small. She was furious about it. She'd deal with that later.

Then there was the thing he'd tried to warn her about in the doorway, and it turned out he hadn't exaggerated.

It didn't work the first time. Or the second. There were moments when it was simply too much, when her body, which could heal a broken jaw in half a minute, didn't know what to do with him. Every time, he stopped. Before she asked. Before she'd worked out she was going to ask, he was holding still over her with his ears back and his breath going hard through his nose, waiting.

"We can stop," he said, the third time. His voice was wrecked. "Leah. It's fine. We can—"

"Don't you dare."

"You're shaking."

"I'm *hot*. Shut up." She grabbed both his ears, which made him yelp. "We're not stopping."

"We've got all night," he said. "That's all. Slowly. We've got all night."

It became the most annoying sentence in the English language. He said it four more times. She told him she'd bite him if he said it again, and he laughed into her hair and said it again, and she bit his arm, and he said it again. He took his time. He took so much time she started swearing at him, and he took that too, patient as a rock in a river, until she quit swearing.

Somewhere in all that patience, it stopped being too much.

She didn't notice it happen. It was too much, and then it was almost too much, and then it was exactly enough, and a sound came out of her that she'd never made in her life, and after that she quit thinking in words.

---

At some point she caught both his paws and put them on her hips. He'd been holding her so carefully. Pads only. Claws pulled so far back into the fur she couldn't feel even the tips.

"I heal," she said into his throat. "So use them."

His paws tightened. She felt the claws twitch under the fur, and stay where they were.

"Not tonight," he said.

"Coward."

"Ja," he said, and kissed her so she'd quit talking about it.

She didn't quit thinking about it.

---

It came up the way the phase came up, from the place under her breastbone where the wolf slept, rising like heat out of the ground. It left her skin alone. It took everything else.

Her sight went sharp, the edges of everything lined in faint gold. A growl came up out of her chest that she hadn't put there, a sound her human throat had no business making, and she felt him go still over her and his ears go straight up.

"Leah?"

Her mouth had found his left shoulder, where the fur grew crooked over an old scar. Where somebody else's teeth had been once, somebody who'd come out of the dark in Romania and marked him without asking. She could smell it there, faint and old under the pine: the sick, sour smell of the corner table at Die Ou Draak.

Her wolf didn't like that smell on him. Her wolf wanted it *gone*.

She bit down.

Fur, then the give of him, then hot copper. He jerked and locked rigid, and a sound came out of him she'd never heard any living thing make, a gasp that turned into a groan and ended somewhere a lot lower.

Then he moved. Fast, faster than in the clearing, and she was on her back and his mouth was at the place where her neck curved into her shoulder, and his teeth were *there*, long and white against her skin.

His teeth rested there. They were shaking.

All of him was shaking, every muscle locked and straining like a dog on a chain, and there was a sound in his chest with no words in it at all. He was waiting. Even now, he was waiting for her.

Her wolf rolled her head back on the pillow and bared her throat.

"Do it," she said, in a low, rough voice that wasn't all hers.

"Leah—" A growl with her name in it.

"*Do it.*"

Something older than either of them answered.

The bite was a bright hot shock, and then something she didn't have a name for, going down her spine and into her bones and out to the ends of her fingers, like the first time she ever phased, with no pain in it anywhere. Inside her, all the way up to the wrong stars, her wolf threw its head back and howled, and Leah howled with it, and if the baboons had opinions about that, they kept them to themselves.

---

A long time afterward she was lying on her side with her back against his chest, his arm over her waist, his tail draped over her legs like a blanket, his breath slow in her hair. She was boneless and soaked and covered in fur, and her neck throbbed, and she hummed all over like a struck bell.

She tried to roll over.

She couldn't.

"Harry. Why can't I move?"

A long silence behind her.

"Ah," Harry said. "Ja. So. That's the bit. The bit in chapter nine."

Leah lay very still. "How long?"

"Healers timed it."

"They *timed* it?"

"Twenty minutes. Give or take."

"*Twenty*—"

"You said," Harry said into her hair, very carefully, "you knew how dogs worked."

She opened her mouth to tell him what she thought of that, and what came out was a laugh, helpless, from the bottom of her chest, the way it had at the pool table. Once it started it wouldn't stop. Because of the bit in chapter nine, he was laughing too, whether he liked it or not, and every time one of them tried to quit, the other set them off again.

When it finally wound down, the nightjar was still going outside. *Good Lord, deliver us.*

"Tell it to shut up," Leah said.

"It's praying for us."

"Too late." She shifted, as much as she could, which wasn't much. "So what do we do for twenty minutes?"

"Talk, I suppose."

"About what?"

"Anything." His breath moved her hair. "Tell me something nobody knows."

She thought about it. "My dad taught me to surf. First Beach. The water's about fifty degrees, and he'd stand out there in a wetsuit older than me yelling *paddle, Leah, paddle*." She stopped. "I haven't been in since he died."

He was quiet for a moment. "I surf."

"I know. Third place. Novice division."

His whole body went still behind her. "You read the signature."

"I read the signature four times, *Sir* Harry."

He groaned into the back of her neck, and she started laughing all over again.

His arm tightened around her. Then, quieter: "I bit you. I never decided to. I didn't think about it at all."

"I know."

"Did it hurt?"

"Yes." She thought about it. "Good."

She reached back over her shoulder, blind, and found his shoulder. The crooked line of the old scar under her fingers, and over it, around it, a ring of fresh marks that were hers. She smelled her fingers to be sure. Pine and wet stone and creek water, and copper, and her. The sick smell was gone.

*Mine*, said something under her ribs, very quietly, and very sure.

She didn't say it out loud. She left her hand where it was.

Behind her, his tail moved over her legs in a slow, steady sweep, the way you'd stroke a cat.

She was asleep before the twenty minutes were up.

---

### IV.

She woke in the dark, wide awake, all at once.

Her body thought it was evening. Back in Atlin it was suppertime, and her body was hungry and wanted to know why it was lying down.

Harry had rolled over in the night and was flat on his back diagonally across all three beds, one arm flung over his head, one footpaw hanging off the far edge, snoring like a diesel truck idling in a parking lot. His left ear twitched every time he breathed out.

His tail was across her face. She moved it. It flopped back. She pinned it with her elbow.

Outside, the sky was going gray at the bottom. A baboon barked up on the cliff, then a whole chorus of them, hoarse and furious, like old men arguing over a parking space.

She slid out from under the arm and the tail and went to find the bathroom.

A tin bathtub on lion's feet. An old, spotted mirror. A wooden hairbrush on the basin with a label still tied to the handle: *For your FUR, Harry. USE IT. Love, H.*

Leah splashed cold water on her face and looked up into the mirror, and stopped.

On the left side of her neck, low down where it curved into her shoulder, there was a mark. A crescent like the edge of a moon: a row of small, deep, even dents as long as her thumb, and a shorter row facing it. Tooth marks. They'd healed. Everything on her healed; the cut from Vadas's wire had been gone by Thursday morning.

These had healed silver. Each dent had closed over with smooth, shiny skin a shade paler than the rest, gleaming in the gray light.

A scar.

She touched it. It didn't hurt. When she pressed on it, a small pulse went down her spine like a plucked string, and she snatched her hand away.

She hadn't had a scar since the day she phased. Cracked ribs, her arm laid open, her face raked by a newborn in June: every bit of it had healed back to nothing.

She thought about Emily.

Her cousin in the doorway of the little house Sam had built her, with her long hair pulled over one side of her face the way she always wore it now. Under the hair, three ragged scars from her hairline across her right eye, dragging down the corner of her mouth. Sam had done that by accident. He'd have cut off his own hand before he hurt Emily on purpose. He'd lost his temper while she was standing too close, and the wolf came out of him, and in the half-second before he got it back, the wolf did what wolves did.

Leah had been in Sam's head when he thought about that night, over and over. The wolf rising. Sam a passenger behind it.

Last night her wolf had come up and taken her mouth and bitten a man. She'd let it. God, she'd wanted it. The deciding, though, the wolf had done that, and she'd come along behind it the way Sam came along behind his.

What else would it decide?

Imprinting was the wolf. Everybody knew that. With Emily, Sam's wolf had looked, and decided for him, and Sam came along behind it and left Leah standing in the rain. Jared. Paul. Quil. Jacob, three weeks ago, his face going slack and stupid over a baby.

Her wolf had just shown her it could take the wheel whenever it liked. It could look at some stranger at a gas station in Port Angeles and decide, and drag her along behind it. And then she'd be Sam, and he'd be her, standing in the rain looking in through the glass.

Or the other way around. Some new wolf could look at *her*. There'd never been a girl; nobody on earth knew what happened when one of them looked at her. Would she wake up one morning and look at Harry and feel nothing? She'd been in Sam's head the morning after he imprinted. When he looked at Leah, it had been like looking at a photo of somebody he used to know.

She gripped the sink so hard the old porcelain creaked.

She'd known him four days. She'd rather cut her own heart out than do that to him.

---

He hadn't moved. The gray light was on him now: the singed whiskers with a quarter-inch of new black at the roots, the burnt tuft, his left shoulder. The old crooked scar, and cutting across it in a rough ring, her bite, the fur already closing over it in short dark bristles so the shape of her teeth stood out on him like a brand.

*Mine*, said something under her ribs again, as calm and sure as before.

She went cold all over.

A red flannel shirt hung over the chair under Josie's top, soft and worn thin at the elbows, the big guy off the fishing boat's. She put it on. It came halfway down her thighs, and she rolled the sleeves four times, and it smelled like him. She went out to the stoep.

The sun wasn't up, but the sky was, going from gray to a thin clean yellow along the eastern edge of the world. The hill fell away in long folds of gold and silver grass, black in places from old burns. The valley ran off flat and brown for miles, and the water she'd seen last night lay in a bent arm between the hills, silver as a dropped knife. Behind her the cliffs were catching the light, red as the inside of a peach and then gold, and big brown birds were dropping off the ledges and tilting out over the valley on wings as wide as a car was long, without flapping once, riding up on nothing. Downhill, a small leafless tree stood alone in the grass, covered in white blossom like it had been snowed on. The honey smell.

It was the most beautiful place she'd ever seen.

She sat on the stoep wall with her knees pulled up inside his shirt, and looked at it, and made up her mind.

---

### V.

He came out ten minutes later with two mugs.

The boards creaked under him, which they hadn't done once last night. She heard him coming because he let her. Loose dark trousers, nothing else, the black fur on his chest rumpled from sleep, two enamel mugs of coffee, ears pricked toward her.

Then he got close enough to smell her, and the ears went back.

He stopped ten feet off, the way he had in the clearing with Josie in his arms. He held out a mug without coming closer, and it crossed the air on its own and stopped in front of her, steaming. Black, strong, a faint taste of smoke.

"You smell scared," he said, sitting on the far end of the wall.

"Rule four."

"Rule four was about your smell. This is about you." His ears stayed back. "What's wrong?"

She'd had every word lined up, the way you'd line up a shot on a pool table. Now he was sitting there with his rumpled fur and singed whiskers, looking at the valley, and every one of them was gone.

"This was a mistake," she said.

His ears went a little flatter. That was all.

"Okay," he said.

"I mean it."

"I can hear you mean it."

"It's my wolf," she said. "Last night. My wolf decided to bite you. It came up and took over and I went along. I *wanted* to. It still did the deciding." She pulled the collar of his shirt aside. "And this. I don't scar. This healed *silver*."

His eyes went to the mark and stayed there. His nostrils flared.

"Imprinting's the wolf," she said. "That's the whole thing. The guy just comes along behind. My wolf just showed me it can take over whenever it wants. It can look at some guy at a gas station next month and decide, and I come along, and you're the one standing in the rain." Her voice cracked. She kept going. "Or some new wolf looks at *me*, and nobody knows what happens then, because there's never been a girl. Maybe one morning I look at you and feel nothing. I was in Sam's head the morning after, Harry. When he looked at me, it was like looking at a picture of somebody he used to know."

She put the mug down so she wouldn't drop it.

"I'm not doing that to you. I'd rather cut my heart out with a spoon. So we stop. Now, while it's four days. You take me back to Atlin, and I go home, and it's done. Before it can be done to us."

She ran out of words.

Harry looked at the valley for a long time.

"Okay," he said.

She'd braced for an argument. He said *okay*, quietly, like she'd asked him to shut a window, and it went through her chest like a blade.

"I'll take you," he said. "Today, if you want. Tin's on the table." He turned his mug in his paws. "Can I tell you something first? Then I'll get it."

She nodded.

---

"You know about my parents," he said. "Bloke came to the house on Halloween when I was one. Killed my dad in the hall and my mum in front of my cot. Then he pointed his wand at me, and it bounced. My mum stood in front of me, and it did something old nobody'd thought to try." He drank. "The curse left something behind."

Leah waited. He didn't look at her while he talked. He looked at the valley, and the vultures, and his coffee.

"He'd spent years making himself hard to kill. The way you do that, you split your own soul. Tear it into pieces, stuff the pieces into things, hide the things. Every time you do it, what's left of you gets thinner and stranger. When the curse bounced and hit him, a bit of him tore loose like a scrap of wet paper and went looking for somewhere to live." He touched the middle of his forehead with the back of a claw. "There was one living thing left in the room."

"You."

"Me. One year old. I carried a piece of somebody else's soul around for seventeen years and never knew. Nobody did. Well. One man did, and kept it to himself, and that's another long story." The baboons were still going on the cliff. One of them screamed. "I could talk to snakes. I'd get his moods. I'd be fine, and then furious out of nowhere, hot and sick with it, and none of it mine. Some nights I dreamed through his eyes. Once I watched a snake bite my best mate's dad from inside the snake. I could taste him." His ears pinned. "When I was fifteen, he worked out the door went both ways. He put a picture in my head of my godfather being tortured. It was fake. I swallowed it whole and went after him, and Sirius came after *me*, and Sirius died."

He set the mug down between his footpaws.

"I was meant to be learning to keep him out. Occlumency. Practise every night. I didn't. I was fifteen and angry and thought I knew better than everybody. Best man I ever knew went through a veil in a stone room because I couldn't keep a door shut." He looked at his own forepaw, turned it over, pads up. "Same night, the bloke got into me properly. Wore me like a coat for about ten seconds. Worst ten seconds of my life, and I've been bitten by a werewolf. So I know what it's like to have something in you that isn't you, Leah. Pushing. Pulling. Deciding. I lived with it seventeen years."

The sun came up over the hills and rolled across the valley and hit the stoep, and the fur on his arms went red-gold at the tips.

"When I was seventeen I found out there was one way to get it out. He had to kill me." He said it like he was telling her he'd walked down to the shops. "So I walked into a forest and let him. Put my wand away and stood there. It killed the bit of him in me, and I came back. Don't ask me how; I've got theories, and they're all rubbish. I came back, and his bit stayed dead."

"You *died*."

"For a bit. Hermione says it counts. Ron says I cheated."

She couldn't think of a single thing to say.

"After the war I couldn't leave it alone. I needed to know where I stopped and it started. Hermione found me the old books, the locked-up ones. When I joined the Confederation they let me into the archive in Geneva, because half the people I chase have done something to a soul. Vows. Blood-pacts. Bindings. A bloke in Lagos bound his own daughter to his debts before she could walk; every time she tried to leave the house, she collapsed." He picked up the mug and looked into it. "It's all threads. Everything that ties onto a soul. Since the forest I can see them, if I look hard enough. Hermione thinks it's because I've been dead. And anything tied can be untied, if you're patient, and if you can stand what it costs."

"What does it cost?"

"It's the closest I ever get to being him. You put your claws right into somebody and pull. You go further into a person than anybody should, and the whole time you can feel how easy it would be to tear." He put the mug down. "I hate it. I've done it three times in four years. The girl in Lagos was one. She's twelve now. Goes to school."

He turned on the wall and looked at her for the first time.

"I've never seen an imprint. I don't know what it's made of or how it works. I've never met anything tied onto a soul that I couldn't get my claws into eventually."

Leah stared at him.

"I like you," Harry said. "A stupid amount. For four days. And if you ever look at some bloke and the world tips over and you don't want it, or some wolf with more destiny than sense looks at you, and anybody thinks that means they get to take you, I'll take that imprint apart. String by string. Till there's nothing left of it but lint."

"Harry—"

"I'll be damned if some wolf mojo messes up what we've got." His ears were all the way forward. His voice hadn't gone up at all. "A week. A year. Ten. I'll sit there with you and do it, however long it takes. And when the magic's out, whatever's left is yours, and you decide. That's all I'm after. You getting to decide."

The windmill creaked. The white tree stood in the sun.

"Wolf mojo," Leah said. Her voice came out strange.

"Technical term."

"You can't promise that. You don't even know if you *can*."

"No," Harry said. "I'll find out."

She looked at him. The singed whiskers and the burnt tuft and the rumpled fur. The ring of her teeth on his shoulder. The steady green eyes.

"And that," he said, tipping his muzzle at the silver mark on her neck. "I haven't looked inside you. I won't unless you ask. My end, I can tell you about. Last night nothing reached into me from outside; I'd know that feeling in my sleep. Whatever made me bite you was mine. Old, and mine." His ears tipped forward. "Maybe your wolf's got old bits too. They're still yours."

Leah's eyes went hot.

She'd quit crying in front of people the year her dad died, and she wasn't going to start on a stoep in Africa in a borrowed shirt. She let her eyes do whatever they were doing, and looked at him through it.

"You're insane," she said. "You've known me *four days*."

"Ja."

"That's—" She didn't have a word for what that was.

She put the mug down. She crossed the ten feet of stoep barefoot, took two fistfuls of the fur on his chest, pulled his head down, and kissed him so hard their teeth clicked.

He made a surprised noise into her mouth. Then his paws settled on her back, pads only, careful, and his tail started going against the stoep wall, *thump, thump, thump*, like a fist on a door.

---

### VI.

From inside the house, something on the kitchen table started buzzing like a wasp in a jar.

Harry ignored it. Leah ignored it for about ten seconds. Then it got louder and started rattling against the wood, and a thin, furious voice came out through the open door.

"*Potter. I can see the ceiling of your kitchen, which means you've left this face-up on a table, which means you're there. Pick it up. POTTER.*"

"I'm going to throw him in the dam," Harry said into her hair.

"Go get it before I do."

He went. She followed him in with her coffee. In the bronze mirror was the same narrow, tired face she'd seen on Wednesday night, in a dressing gown, unshaven, holding up a newspaper.

"Have you *seen* this? *Southern Cross Sentinel*. Sunday edition. Front page."

Leah leaned over Harry's arm to look.

*WOLF WHO LIVED TORCHED IN CAPE TOWN!* Under that, smaller: *Mystery Beauty Sets Potter Alight at Notorious Long Street Bar.*

The photo moved. A girl in a dark green top breathed a long jet of fire across a table at a seven-foot black wolf, who jerked back too late with his eyes going wide. Then it went back to the start and did it again. Every time it looped, the girl behind the flame looked a little more pleased with herself.

"My hair looks good," Leah said.

Hennessey's eyes slid sideways in the glass. They found the red flannel shirt and her bare legs and the mark on her neck, and went very wide.

"Potter. Is that the *hiker*?"

"Morning," said Leah.

"She's wearing your shirt."

"It's a good shirt," said Harry.

Hennessey shut his eyes and breathed in slowly through his nose, like a man counting. When he opened them, the newspaper was gone, and he had the look of a man who'd been up all night for a reason.

"The Director's office has already asked me who she is. I told them I don't know, which is true, and I'd like it to stay true for at least a week." He picked up a folder. "New job. Washington State. The Olympic Peninsula. A little town called Forks."

Leah's mug stopped halfway to her mouth. Harry's ears swung toward her.

"There's a coven. Seven vampires. Animal drinkers, supposedly. Living in the town openly for two years: a doctor at the hospital, five teenagers at the high school until June. The Americans have known about them for decades and left them alone, because they behave."

"So what changed?"

"Seattle, in the spring. Thirty-odd dead or missing. The Muggle papers called it the murder capital of America. MACUSA logged it as vampire activity in April and did nothing, because MACUSA is frightened of its own shadow. In June it stopped overnight, and the same week our people at Sea-Tac logged four visitors from Volterra coming in and leaving three days later."

Harry's tail went still. "The Italians came to tidy up."

"Within ten miles of that coven's house. And the coven is still there." A page turned. "In August one of them married a local Muggle girl. The police chief's daughter. Two weeks later she came down with something so serious that nobody's been allowed to see her since, including her father." Another page: a grainy printout. "And hiking forums. A cluster of sightings on the Peninsula since last year. Wolves. Horse-sized. Gray ones, black ones, a reddish-brown one. MACUSA says hoax. Half the Muggles think it's Bigfoot's dog."

Leah put her mug on the table very carefully.

"And then there's your friend from Ottawa."

"She's not my friend."

"Senior Auror Thibodeaux sent me a memo at four o'clock this morning. She spent her Saturday on American hiking forums." Hennessey held up a sheet. In the middle of it, one word was circled three times in red ink. "She attached your statement from Thursday. She circled the word *dog*."

Leah stopped breathing.

"Geneva wants it assessed before Volterra decides to assess it for us," Hennessey said. "Find out whether that coven's a breach of the Statute, and what those wolves are. Quietly. You're the nearest senior hit wizard to that side of the planet, and you've got a nose. The Director wants a preliminary by Wednesday."

Harry was quiet for a long moment. "Portkey?"

"Being made now. Where do you want it?"

"Atlin."

"Why on *earth* Atlin?"

"Promised somebody."

"Promised *who*? Your coat's on you, I've seen the—" Hennessey stopped. His eyes slid to Leah again. To the shirt. To the mark.

"Rated for two," Harry said.

The silence from the mirror went on a while.

"Twenty-three hundred, Pacific time," Hennessey said finally, like a man stepping off a ledge. "Lakeshore, by that ridiculous boat. It'll be a hubcap."

"Course it will."

"And Potter? Put on a shirt."

The mirror went dark.

---

### VII.

Harry laid the mirror face-down and kept his paw on it a moment, like he was holding it shut. Then he looked at her.

"You know them," he said.

"Forks is fifteen minutes from my house." Her voice was going flat and fast. "The coven's the Cullens. The doctor's Carlisle. The girl who got married is Bella Swan, and she's a vampire now, three weeks in. Her dad's Charlie Swan, the police chief, and he's been dating my mother since the spring."

His ears went sideways.

"And the wolves are us. The gray one's me. The reddish-brown one is Jacob. My Alpha. The one with the baby."

"The baby," Harry said slowly.

"Bella's. She got pregnant on her honeymoon, by a vampire. It broke her bones from the inside, and then it chewed its way out, and Edward had to bite her to keep her alive. She's three weeks old and looks about eight months. And Jake looked at her the day she was born and imprinted."

Harry stared at her. "Your Alpha imprinted on a *vampire's baby*."

"Half. She's got a heartbeat."

He put a paw over his eyes. "Son of a bitch."

"That's my home. That's what I ran two thousand kilometers from. And your boss wants you to go *assess* it." She swallowed. "What if you don't?"

"Then he sends somebody else." He lowered the paw. "Somebody who's never met a wolf that talks, who reads Thibodeaux's memo and sees a word circled three times and an Unforgivable on Canadian soil, and thinks: unregistered creatures, large, dangerous. They'll put your brother on a form, Leah. All of you. Box for your teeth."

She thought about Seth, fifteen and grinning in his sling, wanting a vampire's enchiladas.

"And the Cullens?"

"If it's a breach and they can't fix it, I write *breach*. Geneva calls Volterra. And Volterra comes."

Everybody in the pack knew about Volterra. It had come through Jacob's head out of Edward's: the cloaks, the old papery faces, the little blond girl who could make you burn alive by looking at you. Four of them had come through the woods in June after the clearing. Leah had never seen them. The memory had sat in the pack's heads for weeks like something rotting at the back of a fridge.

"So you have to go," she said.

"I have to go." He came around the table and stopped a few feet off. "You can stay here. I mean it. As long as you like. Nobody can find this place. Biltong'll come up and eat everything in the fridge. You could go out on that hill and change, and nobody would see you but the baboons." His ears went soft. "Nobody'd need a thing from you up here. That's what you wanted when you ran."

She stood in his shirt and looked at him.

"I'm not going to ask you to stay, though," he said. "I'd never. You'd have my tail for it."

Something that had been clenched in her chest since the bathroom mirror let go all at once.

"I'd have your tail," she said. "And your ears. And your good coat."

His ears went straight up. "So you're coming."

"Somebody's got to watch you talk to Edward Cullen."

"What's he like?"

"Like if a sad poem got bitten."

"Lekker," said Harry.

Then it hit her properly.

She was going to walk back into that glass house. Into that bleach-and-candy living room with its white couch and its miracle baby, where everybody had spent three weeks agreeing that everything was fine. Where she'd stood outside in the dark for two years saying it, *this is a disaster, somebody's going to notice*, in her head, to a pack who heard every word and rolled their eyes.

And this time somebody was going to walk in with her and say it out loud. To their faces. In their own living room.

She started to grin.

"That's a very scary face," Harry said.

"I'm going to enjoy this so much," said Leah.

He went and put on a shirt. She was sorry about that.

---

They ate rusks dunked in coffee at the kitchen counter, out of a second tin with an old woman in a bonnet on the lid, and she briefed him with her mouth full.

"Edward reads minds. Everybody's, for a couple of miles. Alice sees the future when people make up their minds, but she can't see wolves. Jasper messes with your feelings. And the baby puts pictures in your head when she touches you. Like a movie. She grabbed my arm the day before I left and showed me my own face."

A ripple went down him from his ears to his tail, like a dog shaking off water.

"In your *head*," he said. "Without asking."

"She's three weeks old."

"Somebody should've asked," said Harry.

"What's MACUSA?" Leah said through a rusk.

"Magical Congress of the United States of America. The American Ministry." His ears went sideways. "Nervous lot. They banned wizards from marrying Muggles for a hundred and seventy-odd years."

"Seriously?"

"Hermione wrote an essay on it. Forty pages. I've read four."

She put her own clothes back on: jeans, two pairs of socks, Marg's daughter's boots, Josie's top. She folded the red flannel shirt and left it on the bed, and then stood there, and picked it up again.

He was waiting in the front room in his dark shirt and the long gray coat with the silver clasp done up, belt and pouches on, a dented red-and-yellow tin under his arm.

"Mrs. Pillay's," Leah said.

"Licensed for a return. Dent it again and it's coming out of my wages." He checked the brass disc. "It's half ten at night in Atlin." He pulled the glamour up, and the big guy off the fishing boat settled over him like a picture printed on glass. She could see straight through it. "Keep the shirt."

She looked at him.

"Collateral," he said.

She knelt on the concrete floor in the fur, across the tin from him, with his shirt under her arm, and put her hand on the lid and took his paw.

"If I hate it, you carry me."

"I'll carry you."

The world grabbed her behind the navel and yanked.

### VIII.

Marg Brewster had been looking at a coat for thirteen hours.

Long and brown and shabby at the cuffs, the size you had to order special. It had been lying folded on the bottom step of cabin four since nine that morning, when the man the size of a garden shed took it off on her drive and put on a gray one. Sometime in the ten minutes Marg was inside rinsing her mug, he and the Clearwater girl had gone. Nobody had driven out. Marg's gravel was loud. She'd have heard.

At noon it had looked like rain, so she'd brought the coat in. At six it cleared, so she put it back out exactly where it had been, because she'd decided that whatever was going on was none of her business, and she wanted the man to see she'd decided it.

Now it was half past ten at night and frost was coming. She was on her porch in her parka and her blanket with her cigarette and her tea with a slug of rye in it, thinking she was sixty-four and too old for this.

There was a crack like a branch snapping in a hard frost, and then there were two people on her gravel where nobody had been a second before.

Marg didn't drop her mug. She'd be proud of that later.

The man was flat on his back with his arms flung out like he'd been dropped from a height. The girl was sprawled across his chest. She rolled off onto the gravel, one hand still on a red-and-yellow cookie tin, swearing steadily and inventively. The man said "Ow" to the sky, the way a man says it when he's said it plenty of times before.

"Leah," Marg said.

The girl's head came up. "Hi, Marg."

"Uh-huh."

Marg went down her steps with the blanket around her shoulders. The man kept getting up for a while. He looked at her, polite, from a foot and a half higher up, and then at the coat on the step.

"You left a coat on my step," Marg said. "Again."

"Ja. Sorry."

"You two leave coats everywhere."

"We're working on it."

Marg looked at Leah. The girl looked like somebody who'd slept, for one thing. The bruised, hunted look she'd carried around all week was gone. She had a red flannel shirt folded under her arm, the same red flannel the man had on.

And low on the left side of her neck, there was a mark. A crescent. Silver in the porch light. The kind of mark that got made with teeth.

It hadn't been there at nine o'clock that morning.

Marg looked at it for a long time. Then she looked up at the man.

The man looked at the sky.

"Uh-huh," Marg said.

"One piece," the man told the sky. "Ma'am."

"Don't ma'am me. Looks like somebody took a piece out of her."

"I took one out of him too," Leah said.

The girl looked straight back at her, chin up, the way she had the night she came up the drive barefoot with no car and no explanation.

"Huh," said Marg.

She thought about the .30-06 in the hall closet. Then she thought about a girl who'd split a whole cord of seasoned spruce in forty minutes and stacked it and come back to ask if there was anything else. That girl could look after herself.

"You leaving?"

"Going home for a while."

"You coming back?"

Leah looked at the man, and back at Marg. "Maybe."

"Uh-huh. You owe me for last night."

"I wasn't here last night."

"Your stuff was."

The girl dug a small, sad fold of bills out of her jeans and counted. Thirty-four dollars. Marg watched her reach the end and go dark in the face and start over, like the number might change. The man's hand moved toward his back pocket.

"Don't," Leah said without looking at him, and the hand stopped.

Marg noted that.

"You split my woodpile," she said. "That's worth a night. Go get your stuff."

That left Marg alone on her drive with the man. He stood there with his hands in his pockets and let her look at him, like a fence post. Marg had known plenty of men who couldn't stand under a look from her for ten seconds.

"You get a car yet?"

"No."

"You walk here?"

"Sort of."

"Uh-huh." She took a drag. "Where you taking her?"

"Washington State. Her home." Low voice, the vowels landing in strange places. "Work. Mine. She's coming to keep me honest."

"Somebody'd better."

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"Everybody's right."

Leah came out with a paper bag, her cut-offs and T-shirt folded on top, the flannel shirt on top of those. Marg picked the brown coat up off the step and handed it to the man.

"Ta," he said.

"Don't *ta* me." She looked at Leah. "Cabin four'll be here. Woodpile'll want splitting again by November."

The girl's face did something, and for a second Marg thought she might come over, and braced. Marg was not a hugger, and neither, she'd have bet, was this girl.

The girl nodded once, hard. "November."

"Uh-huh."

They went off down the drive toward the lake in the dark. Marg watched the man shorten his stride to match the girl's without seeming to think about it, and she'd been married three times and watched men do that for the first month, and she reserved judgment. Halfway down, the girl said something, and the man laughed like a truck engine turning over, and she bumped him with her shoulder hard enough to put a normal man in the ditch. He stayed on the road.

Marg went back up her steps. Her tea had gone cold.

"Huh," she said to nobody, and went inside, and locked the door.

### IX.

The hubcap lay on the gravel at the foot of the *Tarahne*'s cradle, exactly where Hennessey had said: a big dented chrome one off some old American car, with a brass tag wired through a hole, catching the starlight like somebody had flung it off a truck.

Above it the old steamer stood white and enormous, looking out at a lake it was never going back into. Leah had sat on this gravel the morning she met Vadas and told herself none of it was her business.

Harry had dropped the glamour at the shore. The brown coat was over one arm and Leah's paper bag was under the other, because she'd made him carry it.

"Bye," Leah told the boat quietly, and knelt by the hubcap. She put her hand on the rim and took his paw.

"Land on me," he said.

"I always do."

The hook caught her behind the navel, and Atlin went out like a light.

---

He twisted under her the way he had in Cape Town, and they hit together, him first, flat on his back, her face in his collar. The ground gave under them with a deep, wet *squelch*, and cold water came up around her knees.

Rain fell on her face, the fine endless kind that hung in the air more than it fell. Above her, black against a sky barely less black: hemlock and Sitka spruce and red cedar, trunks wrapped in moss, ferns waist-high and dripping.

The smell hit her like a wave. Cedar. Fir. Wet earth and rotting wood and skunk cabbage, salt from the ocean twelve miles west, the river, rain, rain, rain.

Home.

Somewhere west of here, past the trees and the treaty line, her mom was asleep in the little house with the salmon magnet on the fridge.

She'd had a whole day of honey trees and dry grass and the wrong stars, and she'd thought she was ready, and her throat closed up so fast she couldn't breathe.

"Leah?" Harry said from underneath her.

"Give me a minute."

"Take as long as you like." A pause. "We're lying in a bog."

"I *know*."

She rolled off into the ferns and sat up, soaked to the hips. She knew exactly where they were: low ground along the river, a quarter mile east of the Cullens' house. Somebody in Geneva had a sense of humor or no sense of smell.

It was eleven o'clock on Saturday night. She'd left Atlin at nine that morning, gone to Africa, set a curtain on fire, and slept with a wolf, and it was still Saturday.

Harry sat up beside her, bog water streaming off his coat, a fern stuck in the burnt tuft between his ears. His muzzle wrinkled.

"Christ," he said. "Smells like somebody poured bleach over a sweet shop."

Leah stared at him.

"What?" he said.

"Candy store. I've been saying it for two years. Bleach over a candy store." Her voice came out strange. "That's what *I* say."

His ears flicked sideways. "Sweet shop. Candy store. Same thing."

"It's the *same thing*," Leah said, and to her horror her eyes went hot for the second time that day, sitting in a bog over a description of a smell, and she was so angry about it she punched him in the arm.

"Ow. What was that for?"

"Nothing. Get up."

---

Somewhere on the way up through the trees he dried them both. She didn't see him do it. One second his coat was streaming, and then it was dry, and then he glanced at her and her jeans and boots and Josie's top were dry too, like she'd stepped out of a dryer. She'd have thanked him if she wasn't still mad about the candy store.

She walked half a step behind him on purpose, so she could look.

It was stupid. She'd been in his bed eight hours ago. She looked anyway. The long gray coat swinging around his legs as he walked heel to toe, footpaws coming down in the moss and leaving prints and no sound. The width of his shoulders under the wool. The tail swinging out of the split at the back. The tall ears turning, one forward, one back. The high collar, and above it the back of his neck, where she knew there was a ring of marks under the fur that were hers.

Her mom had a shelf of paperbacks in the hall at home, the kind with a woman in a ripped dress swooning against a shirtless guy on a horse. Every one of them had the same three words on the back. *Tall, dark, and handsome.* Leah had always thought it was the stupidest phrase in the English language.

She still did. It was also, apparently, her entire problem now. Seven feet of it, counting ears, walking through the rain in front of her with a fern stuck in its head.

And she couldn't stop thinking about last night.

She tried. She thought about Volterra and Thibodeaux's red circle and her mom. Her brain went right back every time, like a tongue to a sore tooth. The weight of him. The heat. The sound he'd made when she bit his lip. His back moving under her hands under all that fur. His tongue, which she'd filed away on that hill under *later*, and later had turned out to be about one in the morning, and it had more than earned the file. *We've got all night.* The twenty minutes, and how she hadn't wanted to go anywhere anyway. The way he'd held her hips, so carefully, claws pulled all the way in.

She thought about that one longer than she meant to.

Ahead, through the trunks, there was light now. A lot of it, white and electric.

Then she remembered who lived in the house with all the lights. He'd have heard her the second the hubcap dropped them in the skunk cabbage. The whole walk up. The paperbacks. The fur. The *bed*.

Leah thought about that for a second.

Then she thought, very clearly, in her loudest inside voice: *Good.*

"You're doing the scary face again," Harry said without turning around. Both ears had swung back toward her.

"Just thinking."

"Which one's the mind reader again?"

"Bronze hair. Pretty. Looks like he's about to throw up."

"They'll all look like that in about a minute," Harry said, and walked out of the trees.

---

### X.

Alice lost the next hour at one minute to eleven.

Edward heard it go before she said a word. He was standing behind the white couch with one hand on its back, listening to Bella not think, that restful silence he couldn't imagine living without anymore, and to Jacob think about nothing much, the way he did when Renesmee was asleep on his chest: *heavy little thing*, *safe*, *check the Rabbit's brakes tomorrow*.

Then the future in Alice's head went dark.

When the wolves came near, her visions blurred, holes in the picture with the edges still showing. This was a door shutting. The whole next hour, every branch of it, gone at once.

"Edward," Alice said, hands going to her temples. "I can't see the next hour. Something's coming here. I can't see *us*."

Jasper was on his feet beside her. Rosalie rose from the piano bench and was beside Jacob in one movement, between the couch and the window.

And a quarter mile east, down in the wet ground by the river, there was a mind.

He knew it before he caught a single word. He'd lived with it at the edge of his hearing for most of a year: sharp and bitter and loud as a fork dragged across a plate, always at the tree line, always angry.

Leah Clearwater was back.

And she was thinking about—

Edward's hand closed on the couch hard enough that the frame creaked.

He couldn't turn it off. Her thoughts came up through the trees like a car radio played too loud with the windows down, and in ninety years he had never wanted so badly to be deaf. There was a great deal of fur in it. A bed the size of a small room. Heat, and weight, and a voice saying *we've got all night* in an accent he couldn't place, and a pair of very large paws on a pair of hips—

He made himself listen for the other one instead.

Because there was another one, moving beside her through the trees at her pace. A mind. Dense and close and very much alive, like walking past a lit house at night and knowing for certain people were inside. Solid. Right there.

And shut.

He pushed at it. He'd never had to push at anyone except Bella, and Bella was a smooth blank wall. This had a shape. It was like running his hands along a building in the dark, feeling for a door, a window, a crack. There were no cracks. It was real, it was thinking, he could feel it thinking, and he couldn't hear a word.

Two minds in two years that he couldn't open. One of them he'd married. The other was walking toward his house in the dark.

"Jasper," he said.

"I feel it." Jasper had gone very still. "Two. Leah's—" His face did something complicated. "Leah's very *pleased with herself*. The other one's calm. Like standing in a river beside a boulder. I can feel the water going around it. It knows we're here, and it isn't afraid."

On the couch, Jacob's mind lurched awake: *Leah? LEAH'S back?* Then, louder: *who's with her?* From the armchair, Carlisle's, steady as ever: *Everyone stay calm.* From the kitchen, Esme's: *Is that Leah? Oh, thank goodness, her poor mother—* and then, as she caught the second scent, nothing at all for a full second.

Seth came in from the kitchen with half an enchilada in his hand, stopped, flared his nostrils, and dropped the enchilada on the floor. "*LEAH!*"

He was out the back door before anyone could stop him.

Jacob handed Renesmee to Rosalie, who held her close against her shoulder. The baby looked at the window.

Out at the edge of the lawn, on the strip of wet grass where Leah Clearwater had stood every night for a year with her arms crossed, two figures stepped out of the trees.

Edward saw the second one from inside every head in the room at once: the same picture over and over, with a different flavor of alarm in every one.

Taller than Jacob by the height of its ears. Upright, walking heel to toe like a man, and a wolf: black fur, long muzzle, tall pointed ears, a long black tail swinging under the hem of a gray coat, a second coat over one arm and a grocery bag under the other. Edward could hear the rain on the grass and Seth's sneakers squelching across it and Leah's boots sinking into it. From the thing beside her, nothing.

There was a fern stuck in the fur between its ears.

It stopped at the edge of the light and looked in through the glass, one face at a time. When its eyes reached Edward, they stopped. It tipped its head, one ear up and one down, like a dog that's heard its name.

Then it lifted a black forepaw and waved at him.

On the bottom stair, Emmett waved back. "*Emmett*," Rosalie said, without turning her head.

---

Seth hit Leah like a tackle, and her mind went from the bed to *oof* to *SETH, your COLLARBONE* to something underneath that was mostly relief. Then he turned and looked up at the wolf, and his mind went off like a firework, *it's HIM, he's bigger than Jake, is that THE COAT*, and under the fireworks, sudden and fifteen: *wait. Is he WITH her? Mom's going to—*

"Hi," Seth said. "I'm Seth. Her brother."

"Harry," said the wolf. The voice was low and rough, a growl running under it like a bass line, every consonant clean.

"Is that the coat?"

"Ja."

"*Ja*," Seth repeated, delighted. Then his face went serious. "Are you, like. With her?"

The wolf's ears went sideways. It looked at Leah. "Ask her."

Jacob was crossing the lawn the way he'd walked into the clearing in June: slow, loose, the Alpha coming to see what had crossed his line. *She smells like him. All over.* Then the wind brought the wolf's scent across the grass, and Jacob's mind did something Edward didn't expect.

It stopped.

*Cedar. Salt water. Woodstove.* And a picture, very clear: a small kitchen, a battered table, a black iron stove with a crack in its door, Billy Black in his wheelchair with a mug of coffee on a winter morning. *That's Dad's kitchen. Why does he smell like Dad's kitchen?*

Jacob stopped six feet off. For the first time since he'd phased, Edward saw, Jacob had to tip his chin up to look someone in the eye. A couple of inches. All of it ears.

The wolf's nostrils flared, long and slow, the way Jacob's did reading a trail. Its ears locked. Its tail stopped dead. Edward watched the face do it, and the mind behind the face stayed shut.

"Huh," the wolf said quietly.

"What."

"Nothing." The tail started moving again. "Harry Potter. You'll be Jacob."

"Jacob Black."

The wolf's left ear flicked once, sharp, at the name. Edward heard Leah see it: *there it is*. Then, fast, she shoved something into a box in the back of her mind and sat on the lid.

"You called me a knock-off," Jacob said.

"Ja. I did." The wolf looked him over, bare feet to hacked-off hair. "In fairness, I hadn't met you yet."

"And now?"

"Jury's out."

Carlisle was already on the deck. Edward went with him. When something you couldn't read was standing on your lawn, you put yourself between it and your family and made sure your face showed nothing.

The wolf looked up at them. Its eyes went to Carlisle briefly, then came back to Edward and stayed.

"Evening," it said. "You'll be the one who reads minds."

### XI.

"Edward Cullen," Leah said. "Edward, Harry. He's a cop."

"Sort of a cop," said Harry.

"He's here about you."

Leah had been in Jacob's head enough to know every one of Edward's faces: the brooding, the tortured, the I've-been-seventeen-for-a-century. She'd never seen this one. This one looked like a man who'd stepped barefoot on a Lego and was trying to be polite about it.

"Welcome back, Leah," he said through his teeth.

"Thanks," Leah said sweetly, and thought about the twenty minutes.

Edward's jaw went tight.

Carlisle came down the steps into the rain and held out his hand. "Mr. Potter. Carlisle Cullen."

Harry looked at the hand a second, then took it in his forepaw. Carlisle's eyes went down to the black pads and curved claws in his grip and came back up, and his face didn't change at all. Pretty good, Leah thought, for a doctor.

"Doc," said Harry.

"You know who I am."

"There's a file. You're in it. Nice photo. Same haircut since Eisenhower."

Carlisle's mouth went thin. "Come in out of the rain."

---

The smell inside hit Harry like a slap. His ears went flat and his muzzle wrinkled and his tail tucked an inch tighter against his legs. Then his ears came back up.

"Is that *pie*?"

Esme was in the kitchen doorway in an apron, holding oven mitts. She took in the fern, the bog water drying on his footpaws, the two coats.

"Apple," she said. "There's a cherry cooling."

"Mrs. Cullen, I'd like to formally withdraw everything rude I was going to say about this house."

Esme's eyebrows went up. "You were planning to say rude things about my house?"

"Only about the smell, ma'am. You can't help it."

"How kind," said Esme, in a voice like a closing door, and went to cut the pie anyway.

She brought two plates. In a year of guarding this house, Leah had never once eaten anything out of that kitchen, on principle. Esme held the second plate out without a word. Leah looked at it. She looked at Harry, already perched on the arm of a chair with his plate on his knee, fork going, tail sweeping the rug.

She took the plate. It was good. She hated how good it was. When she looked down a minute later there was a second slice on it, and she hadn't seen Esme move. She ate that too.

Harry looked at it from across the room. "You going to finish that?"

"Touch it and you lose the paw."

"Wonderful," said Rosalie from the window, with Renesmee on her hip. "The stray came home and brought a bigger one."

"Missed you too, Blondie," said Leah.

"Goldilocks," Harry said with his mouth full.

Leah considered it. "Goldilocks is better."

Rosalie's lip came off her teeth. On the bottom stair, Emmett snorted, then leaned forward, looking Harry over the way he looked at a grizzly before he went for it. "Arm wrestle. You and me."

"After pie, big man."

Edward hadn't sat down. He stood behind the couch with Bella, staring at Harry, his forehead pinched like somebody straining to hear a radio station through static.

"Edward?" Bella said. "What's wrong?"

"I can't hear him."

Her red eyes went wide. "I'm not shielding him."

"I know you're not."

"Knocking, is he?" Harry swallowed. "Feels like somebody running a fingernail along a window. Occlumency, boet. Took me ten years. Knock all you like. Nobody's coming to the door."

"Bella is the only person I've never been able to—"

"Must make a nice change," Harry said. "A room you can't walk into without being asked."

Leah put a forkful of pie in her mouth to keep from grinning.

Something soft settled over her shoulders like a blanket. Her back loosened. She was halfway to *huh, this is nice* when she realized what it was, and in the same second Harry's ears pinned flat.

"Oi. Whoever's turning the dial on my head. Knock it off."

Jasper, by the stairs, went very still. "You can feel that?"

The drawl was pure Texas. Harry's ears flicked sideways.

"I can feel somebody fiddling with my insides, cowboy. Put it back how you found it."

The blanket lifted off Leah's shoulders. Jasper looked at Harry a long moment with his scarred face blank, and then at Alice.

"I still can't see you," Alice said from the edge of the coffee table, fingertips at her temples. "There's a hole in tonight the shape of you."

"Get that a lot," said Harry.

"I don't like it."

"Nobody does."

---

Renesmee hadn't stopped looking at him.

Leah noticed it the way you notice a dripping tap. Bronze curls, round rosy face, a baby off the front of a baby-food jar. She looked about eight months old; a week and a half ago she'd looked four months. Her huge brown eyes were fixed on Harry.

She was not blinking.

Harry had noticed. His ears had gone halfway back and stayed there, and his tail had quit sweeping and curled tight around his footpaws.

"Is she meant to stare like that?" he said to Leah, very low.

"Yep."

Every vampire in the room heard it. Rosalie's arm tightened around the baby.

Harry finished his pie and got up to take his plate to the kitchen, because apparently somebody had raised him right about that one thing. The shortest way was past the window. He gave Rosalie a wide berth. It wasn't wide enough.

Renesmee lunged half out of Rosalie's arms and grabbed a fistful of his tail.

Harry yelped and went a foot straight up. The plate stayed where he'd dropped it, hanging three feet off the floor, turning gently.

Leah knew the look on the baby's face. Tight with concentration, the way it went when she put her pictures into you. Leah had felt it on her own arm.

Nothing happened.

Renesmee's forehead creased. For the first time since Leah had known her, the baby looked surprised.

"She can't—" Edward said sharply. "Nothing's going through."

Bella was across the room before Leah saw her move. "What do you mean?"

"She showed him something, and it didn't go in. She can feel him there. Big. Solid. She says he's like a tree." Edward was staring at his daughter. "The picture just slid off."

"That's attached, kid," Harry said. He freed his tail one claw at a time, very gently, and curled it around his legs. Then he plucked the plate out of the air and put it on the counter.

Renesmee reached for her mother. Bella took her, and the baby laid her small hand flat on Bella's cheek. Bella's eyes went far away for a second. When they came back, they went to Harry.

"She wants to try again. Properly," Bella said, in the careful, tight voice of a newborn who'd been told a hundred times to keep it down. "That's how she talks. She shows you. She'd like to touch your face. Would that be okay?"

Harry looked at the baby.

The baby looked at Harry. She still hadn't blinked.

"Yeesh," Harry said. "No. No, thank you."

The living room went silent.

"*Excuse* me?" said Rosalie.

"Nothing personal." Harry held up both forepaws. "She's lovely. Lovely curls. It's just, she hasn't blinked." He looked at Leah. "Has she blinked since we came in?"

Leah thought about it. "No."

"Six minutes," Harry said. "My neighbour's granddaughter had a toy like that. Big eyes, never blinked, talked to you in the dark when you thought it was switched off." His ears went flat. "Furby. Mrs. Pillay put it in the freezer."

On the bottom stair, Emmett made a noise like a tire blowing out.

"Did you just call her a *Furby*?" Jacob said.

"I said she was *like* one. A glorified one." Harry's ears flicked sideways. "It's a simile. Ask Leah. She knows words."

Leah had her face in her hands. Her shoulders were shaking.

Rosalie hissed, an actual hiss, like a cat's, and it went through the room like a knife. Bella's eyes went a shade darker. Edward's hand closed on her arm.

"She's a *baby*," Bella said.

"Ja. I've got nothing against babies. I've got a godson, Teddy. Eight. Turns his hair blue when he's happy and talks my ear off, and I'd walk into a fire for him." Harry's ears stayed flat. "He blinks, though. Regular. Like a person." His voice went quieter. "She wants to tell me something, she can tell me. Out loud, when she's got the words. I'll wait. Nobody puts pictures in my head. I had a bloke living in there for seventeen years, and I'm done renting out the room."

Edward's head turned. He looked at Harry with something new in his face.

"She's not a toy," Jacob said roughly.

"Creepy's the word I used."

"She's—" Jacob's voice cracked down the middle. "She's *everything*."

Harry looked at him for a long time, ears flat to his skull.

"Ja," he said quietly. "That's what worries me."

Leah stopped laughing.

Jacob's face went through four things at once, and none of them were good. His hands had made fists, and Leah could smell the heat coming off him, the way it did right before a phase.

"That's *enough*." Rosalie took Renesmee out of Bella's arms in one smooth movement and swept toward the stairs, then stopped at the bottom to give Harry a look that would have stripped paint. "Come on, sweetheart. Let's go somewhere that smells better."

Renesmee looked at Harry over Rosalie's shoulder all the way up the stairs. She didn't blink once.

"Creepy," Harry said under his breath.

---

"Mr. Potter." Carlisle had sat down in the armchair and folded his hands. "Perhaps you'd tell us why you're here."

"International Confederation of Wizards." Harry stayed standing, his back to the wall by the kitchen, where he could see every door. "Think UN for my lot, with fewer sandwiches. There's a law, the Statute of Secrecy. Keeps magic hidden from everybody who isn't magic. Far as Geneva's concerned, it covers you. And you lot have been lit up like a Christmas tree since March."

"We've been extremely careful," Edward said.

"Boet, you've been careful the way a bull's careful in a china shop. Lovely manners. Still a bull." Harry held up one claw. "Seattle. Thirty-odd dead in the spring. Murder capital of America, the papers said."

"That was a vampire named Victoria," Carlisle said. "She raised an army of newborns to—"

"I know. And it got tidied up by four Italians who flew into Sea-Tac in June and flew out three days later. Our people logged them." He watched the room go still. "Ja. Within ten miles of this house."

Nobody said anything.

Second claw. "A wedding in August. Local girl, the police chief's daughter. Two weeks later she's so sick nobody can see her, her dad included." He glanced at Bella. "No offense."

"My dad thinks I'm in a clinic," Bella said stiffly.

"Her dad," Leah said, "is dating my mom."

Harry's ears swiveled. "Is he."

"Since spring."

"Right. So the police chief whose daughter's a vampire is dating the mum of a wolf." He looked around the room. "Who's he got left? Ghost down at the station?"

"That isn't funny," Bella said.

"Bit funny."

Emmett laughed, short, and then looked at Rosalie's empty spot by the window and stopped.

"The pack handles the sightings," Jacob said through his teeth.

"There's a photo on an American hiking forum," Harry said, "of a gray wolf the size of a Land Rover in a clearing off Highway 101. Four hundred comments. Half of them reckon it's Bigfoot's dog."

"That was Paul," said Seth.

"That was Paul," said Leah, at the same time.

Harry looked at her. "Who's Paul?"

"Pain in my ass. Sam's pack."

"Right." He looked back at Jacob. "Tell Paul to stay off the 101."

Third claw. "And there's a kid upstairs who's three weeks old and looks eight months. In half a year she'll look five. There's one primary school in Forks." He looked at Carlisle. "She got a pediatrician, Doc? Other than you?"

Carlisle didn't answer.

"Ja," said Harry. "That's what I thought."

Leah sat on the arm of the couch with an empty pie plate on her knee, having the best night of her life, and last night had been pretty good.

Two years. Two years she'd stood out on that lawn saying this. *Somebody's going to notice. Somebody's going to get killed. This is a disaster with a nice couch.* She'd said it in her head every day to a pack who heard every word and told her to quit being so bitter. Now a seven-foot wolf with a burnt tuft and pie crumbs on his chin was standing in the Cullens' own living room saying it out loud, counting it off on his claws, and every one of them was sitting there taking it.

She looked at Jacob. The muscle in his jaw was jumping.

She felt *great*.

"Here's the problem," Harry said. "I go home and write *breach*, Geneva calls Volterra. You know who answers the phone in Volterra."

The room went very quiet.

"Aro," said Edward.

"Ja."

"If Aro comes, he'll want to touch you," Edward said slowly. "He doesn't need to knock. One touch, and he has every thought you've ever had."

"Let him try."

"You don't underst—"

"Boet, you can't read me from six feet. I'll take my chances with a bloke who has to hold my paw first." Harry pushed off the wall. "I'm the bloke they send so the next one doesn't have to come. And the next one won't knock. The next one brings a team and a stack of forms and puts every wolf on this coast in a box with a number on it." He looked at Jacob as he said it, and then at Carlisle. "So. I stay a few days. I look at everything. I write something sensible. And for it to be true, you lot are going to *get* sensible. No more wolves where hikers can see them. The kid doesn't leave this house till there's a story that holds. Somebody sorts out the police chief before he sorts himself out. That's for a start."

"We'd planned to leave Forks," Carlisle said. "Eventually."

"Good," said Harry. "Plan faster."

"You don't get to walk in here and tell us how to run things." Jacob's voice had gone low. "You've been here twenty minutes."

"Somebody's got to tell you, boet." Harry's ears didn't move. "Your fastest wolf ran two thousand k's to get away from this house. You ever ask her why?"

Jacob opened his mouth. Nothing came out of it.

Leah looked down at her pie plate. Nobody had ever said that out loud. Not in this house. Not on the rez. Not anywhere.

### XII.

"Mr. Potter," Carlisle said, "I'd like you to understand something about this family before you write anything down."

He had a nice voice for a long speech, and he settled into one.

It was about care. Seventy years of care, and the treaty, and the lengths the family had gone to, and what they owed the Quileute people, and Leah caught maybe one word in five, because she was looking at Harry's paws.

He'd leaned back against the wall with his arms folded, one paw resting on his forearm, claws in all the way, the way they'd been all night. She could see the dark tips just showing in the fur.

*Not tonight.*

*Coward.*

She wanted to know what would happen if he wasn't careful.

It arrived whole, the way the good ideas did, and the bad ones too. That ridiculous bed, with the wrong stars going round outside the window. Him behind her. His weight over her back, heavy as a hill. His breath hot on the back of her neck, right on the mark he'd left there, and that low sound starting in his chest that she'd felt all the way through her. His paws on her hips, and this time the claws coming out. Just the tips. Just enough to sting. Just enough to hold her exactly where he wanted her and not let her go anywhere at all. Ten little points of fire. She'd heal by breakfast. She'd want to look at them in the mirror first. His tail curling around her thigh to keep her where she was. That wrecked voice in her ear saying *slowly*, and not meaning a word of it this time. She wanted—

Across the room, Jasper stood up and walked out the back door into the rain without a word.

"Where's Jazz going?" said Emmett.

Nobody answered him. Alice looked at the back door, and then at Leah, and then got up and went after him.

Leah noticed, distantly. She didn't stop. She was thinking about his teeth on the mark, the way the bite had gone through her like a plucked string, and what it would feel like if he did that again *while*—

"Leah," Edward said.

His voice was very quiet and very strained. Carlisle stopped mid-sentence. Everybody turned.

Edward was standing behind the couch with both hands flat on its back, and his face had gone a color Leah wouldn't have believed a vampire's face could go, a bloodless gray-white like old candle wax.

"Please," he said. "*Stop.*"

Leah looked at him. "Stop what?"

"You know *exactly* what."

"I'm just sitting here."

"*Leah.*"

Harry's ears came straight up.

Both of them, all the way, like somebody had yanked them on strings. His head turned from Edward to Leah and back, and his tail lifted a few inches off the rug.

"Stop what?" he said. "Is it anything good?"

"It is *absolutely*—" Edward began.

Harry looked into his eyes.

Straight in. The green went very steady and very still, and for about two seconds nothing happened.

Then Edward's head jerked back like somebody had flicked him between the eyes. His hands came off the couch. "What—what are you—*get out of*—"

Harry's ears went up, and then up some more, which Leah wouldn't have thought possible. His tail started to move. Slowly. Side to side across the rug.

He turned his head and looked at Leah, and grinned. All of it. Every long white tooth in his head.

"Huh," he said.

He'd *seen* it. Whatever Edward had been holding, he'd just helped himself to it. The bed. The claws. The—

Leah's face went hot. Twice in one week. She was going to kill him.

"Ja," Harry said. "I'm down to try that later. If you want."

Silence.

Then Emmett fell off the bottom stair. He just tipped over sideways and lay on the floor with both arms around his stomach, making a noise like an air compressor.

"You," Edward said, staring at Harry. "You were in my *head*."

"Mm." Harry picked a crumb of crust off his chin. "Felt a bit rude, didn't it?"

"You can't just—"

"Funny. That's what I was going to say about you. Every head in this room, every day, and nobody ever gets asked." His ears tipped forward. "How's it feel from the other side, boet?"

Edward opened his mouth. He shut it.

"Mr. Potter," Carlisle said evenly. "In this house, we don't—"

"In this house he does it all day, Doc. I did it once, for two seconds. And he had it sitting right on top, where anybody could've tripped over it."

"I don't want to know," Seth said from the corner, where he'd retreated with both hands clamped over his ears. "I don't want to know. I never want to know. La la la."

"What?" Jacob looked from Leah to Harry to Edward. "What did she *think*?"

Edward looked at him with the face of a man who'd seen something no one should ever have to see.

"I will take it to my grave," he said.

"Bit late for that, boet," said Harry.

On the floor, Emmett howled.

Leah sat on the arm of the couch with her face on fire and her heart going like she'd run from Atlin, and she couldn't look at Harry and couldn't look anywhere else, and under her ribs her wolf had rolled onto its back with all four legs in the air and its tongue hanging out.

*Later*, it said.

*Shut up*, Leah told it, and didn't mean it at all.

---

Carlisle waited for Emmett to get back on the stair with the patience of somebody who'd had three hundred years of practice.

"Perhaps," he said, "we'll continue in the morning."

"Ja. Fine." Harry picked the brown coat up off the chair. "I'll want her growth charts. Don't make that face, Doc; I know you've got charts. I'll want the treaty lines, and I'll want whoever's been running where the hikers go."

"You're welcome to our spare room," Esme said from the kitchen doorway, in the voice she'd have used on anybody standing in her living room near midnight.

"Ta, ma'am." His nose wrinkled. "I'd never sleep. It's the smell. No offense."

"Some taken," said Esme.

"Hey," Emmett said from the stair. "Arm wrestle. You said after pie."

"Tomorrow, big man."

"You're scared."

"Terrified," said Harry, and put the coat over his arm.

"He's staying with me," Leah said.

Every head turned.

"At my mom's," said Leah.

Seth's hands came off his ears. "At *MOM'S*?"

"Mom's dating a guy whose daughter is a vampire," Leah said. "She'll cope."

### XIII.

Jacob went out after them.

He told himself it was to make sure they went west along the river toward the treaty line, and not across Cullen land where Rosalie would make a thing of it. He told himself a lot of things. He caught up with them at the edge of the trees.

"Leah."

She turned. The wolf walked three more steps and stopped with his back to them, looking into the trees with a second coat over his arm, like a guy waiting outside a store. His ears were swiveled straight back. He wasn't fooling anybody.

Up close she smelled like him, all of her, pine and wet stone and creek water soaked in so deep her own rain-on-cedar came through underneath like something heard through a wall. And low on the left side of her neck there was a mark.

Jacob had been in Leah's head every time she'd been hurt. Ribs, arms, the newborn in June that nearly took her face off. Every one healed back to nothing. Leah didn't scar. She'd been smug about it.

This one was silver, and it gleamed in the light from the house.

"What's that?"

"None of your business."

"You don't scar, Leah."

"I know."

He'd been angry for four days. Since Wednesday, since he'd stood in his own head listening to her scream while something he couldn't see tore at her two thousand kilometers away, and held on through it because there was nothing else he could do. Angry again later that night, when she phased and brought all of it with her: beer, pool, a black tail sweeping across her face, and her laughing. He hadn't heard Leah laugh like that in two years.

He'd never asked her a single thing in those two years, either. The wolf had been right about that. Jacob hated that the wolf had been right about that.

"Are you happy?" he said.

Leah looked at him like he'd grown a second head. Then something in her face changed, slowly. She looked down at the wet grass, and back up.

"Yeah," she said. "I think so. Yeah."

Jacob nodded. "Okay."

That was all he had. She looked at him a second longer and nodded back, and turned, and went to the wolf.

The wolf turned around and looked at Jacob over her head.

The collar of the gray coat had come open an inch. On the left side, where his neck ran down into his shoulder, the black fur was broken by a ring of short new bristles in a shape Jacob knew. He'd watched Leah's jaws close on a newborn's arm in June from two feet away, from inside her own head. He knew the spacing of her teeth.

"If you hurt her," Jacob said.

"She'll sort me out herself, boet. You'd be second in line." Harry's tail flicked once. "But ja. Noted."

"Where are you going?"

"My mom's," said Leah.

"Her mum's," said Harry.

"Sue's going to kill you."

"Get in line," Harry said, and the two of them walked into the trees.

Jacob stood at the edge of the lawn with the rain running down his face and the lit glass wall at his back, and breathed in.

Under the bleach and the candy and the wet cedar, the wind was still carrying it, faint and going. Salt water. Woodsmoke. The black iron stove in his dad's kitchen on a winter morning.

On a stranger from England by way of Africa.

He didn't like it. He didn't trust it. He didn't trust *him*, coat or no coat, pie or no pie, and he'd had a damn good reason to come out here and say so, and he'd asked her if she was happy instead.

Tomorrow he was going to sit his dad down at that kitchen table and ask about the white guy. The trader from way back, whose first name nobody remembered.

From the dark under the trees, faint and getting fainter, somebody started humming. Badly. Something with a heavy, stomping beat.

Jacob knew that song. He'd rebuilt the Rabbit's whole engine to it the summer he was fifteen.

AC/DC. *Back in Black.*

He stood in the rain for a long time after that.
