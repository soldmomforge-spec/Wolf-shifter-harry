# True North

## Chapter Four: String by String

---

### I.

The third one was small.

It came up out of her about five minutes after the last of Harry's stout went down, a short, hot, round thing like a hiccup, and what came out with it was a neat little ball of orange flame the size of a fist. It rolled up off her lips and floated toward the ceiling, slow and lazy, like a soap bubble, and burst against the soot about six inches from her own name, and left a fresh black scorch mark beside the *L*.

The bar cheered. Somebody at the next table banged a tankard on the wood.

Across the room, Nomsa looked at the scorch mark. She looked at Leah. She looked at the brass dragon fire extinguisher on the bar beside her, and did not pick it up.

"Hm," she said, loud enough to carry, and went back into the kitchen.

"That," Harry said, "is the nicest thing she's ever said to anybody."

Leah wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist. Her eyes were watering. She could feel the beer moving through her, and under it, already, her own body burning the alcohol off like it burned off everything. She'd never stayed drunk longer than twenty minutes in her life.

"I'm good," she said.

"You're smoking."

"I'm *fine*."

"You're literally smoking, Leah. Out of your ears." He reached across the table and fanned the air beside her head with one big paw. "Wisps. Little wisps."

She swatted his paw away. He let her.

---

They left a little before ten.

Grimbald climbed down off his box to see them to the door, which Harry said, in a low voice on the way across the room, he had never once done for anybody in the five years Harry had been coming there. At the door the goblin looked up at Leah with his small black eyes for a long moment.

"Come back," he said.

Then he looked at Harry.

"Bring money," he said, and shut the door in their faces.

Then the bricks folded shut behind them, and they were standing in an alley that smelled of cat, with the noise of Long Street coming down the far end of it.

Harry pulled the glamour up. The flannel guy shimmered into place over him, ears and all, and he stuck his paws in his coat pockets and looked down at her.

"So," he said.

"So."

"I said I'd take you home. Whenever you wanted." His ears flicked, under the shimmer. "Your call. I can have you back in Atlin before Marg's finished her breakfast coffee."

Leah looked at him. At the green eyes in the dark. At the burnt tuft of fur between his ears, which she could see through the glamour, still sticking up in a ragged little crest where her second pint had caught him.

She'd made up her mind somewhere around the bunny chow. Maybe before. Maybe on the steps of cabin four this morning, with the coat in her hands. She'd been arguing with herself about it all evening, the way she argued with herself about everything, and her wolf had sat under her ribs the whole time with its chin on its paws, completely uninterested in the argument, waiting for her to get to the end of it.

"Where do you live?" she said.

His ears went straight up.

"Above a surf shop," he said. "In Muizenberg. Forty minutes down the coast." He paused. "Mrs. Pillay lives underneath. The floors are very thin and she's very nosy and she's eighty-one and she's never once in six years gone to bed before two."

"Oh."

"There's another place."

"What other place?"

"In the mountains. Outside Joburg." He shrugged, a lot of shoulder under the flannel. "It's where I go when I want to run. Nobody for miles. Baboons, mostly."

"How far?"

"Bit over a thousand kilometres."

"*Harry.*"

"It's a quick trip," he said. "If you hold on."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Is it worse than the tin?" she said.

He thought about it, honestly, which she appreciated.

"Different," he said. "Shorter. Tighter. Most people say it's like being pushed through a garden hose."

"Great."

"You can still say Atlin."

"Shut up," Leah said, and took his arm.

---

It was like being pushed through a garden hose.

It was like being pushed through a garden hose by somebody very strong who was in a hurry. Everything squeezed, all at once, from every direction, her ribs and her skull and her eyeballs, and there was no air and no light and no up or down, only pressure, and his arm under her hand like an iron bar, and then—

Cold air.

Stars.

She was standing on stone, and her knees buckled, and his arm caught her around the waist before she went down, and held her up, and she hung there against his side gasping like a landed fish.

"Breathe," he said. "Just breathe. It goes off in a minute."

"I hate," Leah said, "every single way you people travel."

"Ja. Everyone does."

"How do you *live* like this?"

"Brooms," Harry said. "Brooms are nice. I'll show you brooms."

She straightened up slowly, and her stomach slowly climbed back down from wherever it had gone, and she looked around.

They were standing on a wide stone porch—a stoep, he called it, later—running the whole length of a low, long house with thick stone walls and a deep thatched roof that came down over the eaves like a hat pulled low. In front of the stoep the ground fell away into darkness, steep, wooded, and somewhere far down there in the dark she could hear water running over rocks. Beyond that, on the other side of a deep valley, there was a mountain. A long dark wall of a mountain, ragged along the top, black against the stars.

And the stars.

She'd grown up on the coast, where the clouds came in off the Pacific nine nights out of ten. Here there was no town for miles, the air was dry and thin and high, and the sky was so full it looked like a road: a great pale river of light running from one horizon to the other, grainy and glowing, with dark clouds of dust lying in it like islands, and on either side so many stars that the dark between them seemed to be the exception.

"That's the Milky Way," Harry said, beside her. "The middle of it. You don't get it like this up north. We're looking straight down the galaxy."

Leah didn't say anything. She didn't have anything to say. She stood on the stoep with her head tipped back and her mouth a little open and looked at it.

Somewhere down in the valley, something screamed. A high, sharp, falling yelp, eerie and lonely, like a dog crying.

Her head snapped around.

"Jackal," Harry said. "Black-backed. There's a pair down by the river. They're all right. They mostly yell about each other."

The jackal called again. Another answered it, further off.

The air smelled of dust and dry grass and something sharp and herby she didn't know, and sun-baked rock giving its heat back slowly, and baboon, faintly, from up the mountain.

And Harry. Under all of it, close, pine and stone and creek-water.

She realized she was still holding his arm. She didn't let go.

---

The house was bigger inside than she'd expected, and messier.

One long main room with a stone floor, a huge fireplace at one end and a kitchen at the other, and bookshelves everywhere in between, crammed two deep, with more books piled on the sofa arms and the kitchen counter. A real broom leaned in a corner, long and polished, and she decided not to ask yet. The rug in front of the fireplace had a lot of black fur on it. A mug on the mantelpiece had something growing in it.

"I don't get up here much," Harry said, looking at the mug. "Lately."

And there were photographs.

They were on the mantel and the windowsills and the bookshelves, in frames of every size, and Leah went to look at them, and the first one she picked up *moved*.

She nearly dropped it.

It was a photograph of a man and a woman, standing in a garden, under a tree. The man had messy black hair and round glasses and a grin like he'd just gotten away with something. The woman had long dark red hair and green eyes. Very green eyes. She was holding a baby, and she was laughing, and as Leah watched, the man leaned over and kissed the woman on the cheek, and the baby in her arms reached up and grabbed a fistful of his hair, and he made a face, and they both laughed, and then it started again from the beginning.

"Wizard photos," Harry said, quietly, from behind her. "They move."

"Is this—"

"My mum and dad. And me." He came and stood beside her. "Few months before."

She looked at the woman's eyes. Then at his.

She put the photograph back very carefully, exactly where it had been.

The next one was three teenagers in striped scarves, red and gold, on a snowy hillside. A tall skinny redhead with freckles and a long nose, pulling a face. A girl with an enormous amount of bushy brown hair, rolling her eyes at him. And between them, a skinny boy with messy black hair and round glasses and a thin scar on his forehead, laughing so hard he was bent over.

"That's you," Leah said.

"Ja."

"You're tiny."

"I was fourteen."

"You have *glasses*."

"Had." He tapped the side of his muzzle, under one eye. "Didn't need them after the bite. One of the perks."

She looked at the skinny boy in the photo, and then up at him, seven feet of wolf with his ears brushing the low beams of the ceiling, and back again.

"What do you look like now?" she said. "The other way. When you're a guy."

He was quiet for a second.

"Bit like him," he said, nodding at the photograph. "Taller. Wider. Six-two. No glasses." His ears went sideways. "I can show you. If you want."

Leah turned around and looked at him.

"Is that what you'd rather?" he said. His voice had gone carefully neutral. "I know this is—" He gestured at himself, vaguely, with one big paw: the fur, the muzzle, the tail. "It's a lot. Most people would find it easier with the bloke. You wouldn't be the first to—"

"Don't," Leah said.

He stopped.

"Don't change," she said.

His ears went very still.

She didn't know how to say the rest of it, and she wasn't drunk anymore, so she didn't have that to blame either. She just stood there in his messy living room, with his parents laughing on the mantel behind her, and looked at him.

At the long black muzzle and the green eyes. At the tall ears brushing the beams. At the shoulders, and the burnt tuft between his ears, and the tail, which had stopped moving entirely.

"I like this," she said finally. Her voice came out rough and low and not very steady. "I like *you*. Like this. You don't have to make it easier. I don't want it easier."

He looked at her for a long time.

Then he crossed the room in two strides, silent on the stone floor, and stopped in front of her, close enough that she had to tip her head back to keep looking at him.

"Leah," he said.

"What."

"I'm going to try something," Harry said, "and I'd like you to tell me if it's a bad idea."

"Okay."

He bent his head down. A long way down.

It was awkward. That was the first thing. She had absolutely no idea what to do with a muzzle. Neither, it turned out, did he; he'd clearly thought about it, and clearly not gotten very far, because he stopped about two inches from her face and hesitated, and his ears went sideways, and he said, very quietly, "Ja, I've actually got no idea how this works."

Leah started laughing.

She couldn't help it. She stood there with her face two inches from a wolf's and laughed, and he started laughing too, a low rumble she felt more than heard, and his breath was hot on her face and smelled of beer and curry and smoke, and then she reached up with both hands and took hold of him on either side of his long jaw, in the thick fur under his ears, and pulled him down the last two inches and kissed him.

It didn't work very well. His mouth was the wrong shape and too big and there were a lot of teeth. She found the corner of it, where his lips drew back, and kissed that, and he made a sound low in his chest that she'd never heard a person make, and went completely still, and then his forepaws came up very slowly, very carefully, and settled on her waist.

She felt the claws. Just the tips. Then she felt him draw them in, all ten of them, sheathed, so that all that touched her was the rough hot pads of his palms.

She pulled back an inch.

"Bad idea?" he said. His voice had gone ragged.

"Terrible," Leah said. "Do it again."

---

### II.

There was a lot of him.

That was the thing she kept running into, in every sense, all night. There was a lot of him in every direction, and almost none of it was built the way she was used to things being built.

There was the height, to start with. He had to fold himself practically in half to get anywhere near her, and when he gave up on that and just picked her up, one arm under her and one around her back, like she weighed nothing, she said "Hey!" and then didn't say anything else for a while. There was the heat of him, which was more than hers, which she'd never felt from anybody; she'd spent two years being the hottest thing in every room and now she was pressed against something that ran hotter, and the shock of it went all the way through her. There was the fur, everywhere, thick and soft on top and denser underneath, and the smell of him in it, pine and stone, until she couldn't smell anything else at all.

There was the bedroom, which had a bed in it that was clearly custom-built, very long and very wide and very low, with a frame made of heavy dark wood. There was the tail, which knocked a lamp off the bedside table the second they were through the door, and he said "Ag, *no*," and caught the lamp in midair without looking at it, and set it back on the table with a little flick of something that wasn't his paw, and Leah laughed so hard she had to sit down on the end of the bed.

There was the conversation, which happened about then, because he sat down beside her and the bed groaned and he looked at her with his ears very straight and said, "Leah. Before anything. You've had three pints."

"I'm not drunk."

"I'm asking."

"I burned it off on the porch. I always burn it off. I could walk a straight line on a fence rail." She looked at him. "I want this. I'm stone-cold sober and I want this. Okay?"

He looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded once.

"Okay," he said. "And you tell me. Anything. Too much, too fast, anything at all. You say it, I stop."

"I know."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it," Leah said. "That's why I'm here."

And then there was the rest of it.

Some of it she'd expected. Some of it, when she got there, she had not. She said "Oh," and then, after a moment, "*Huh*," and he went very still above her and his ears went flat and he said, "Ja. That's—I should have—that's a wolf thing. I don't—it's not something I can—" and she put her hand flat over his muzzle to shut him up and said, "Harry. Stop talking. We'll figure it out."

They figured it out.

It took a while. It took patience, which she'd never had much of, and trust, which she had even less of, and a lot of him holding very still with his whole body shaking with the effort of it and his breath coming hard through his teeth while she took her time. It took laughing, twice, once when his tail knocked the lamp off again and once for no reason at all. It took her telling him *wait* and him waiting, and her telling him *okay* and him not believing her until she said it again.

And then, somewhere in the middle of it, she stopped thinking.

She'd always thought. Her whole life she'd never once stopped thinking, never once let go of the wheel, even with Sam, back before, even then some part of her had always been standing back watching. But at some point in the dark of that room with the jackals crying down in the valley, the wolf came up out of her. No fire in the bones this time. Something quieter and older, something that had been sitting under her ribs all week with its ears up, watching him, waiting, and now it simply stood up and took over, and she let it.

It knew what it wanted. It didn't have any doubts about it at all.

And something came up out of him, too. She felt it happen. She felt him stop being careful, by degrees, the way the tide comes in. She felt the growl start low in his chest, a sound she felt through her own spine, and she felt his claws slide out, without him meaning them to, at her hips. Just the tips. Just enough to hold. He froze. She felt him freeze, and felt him start to pull them back—

"Don't," she said. Her voice didn't sound like hers. "Leave them."

He left them.

Near the end, without any warning, her wolf turned her head and set her teeth in the thick muscle at the base of his neck, on the right side, away from the crooked part in the fur, and bit down. To hold. To *mark*. It happened the way you'd catch something falling.

And he went absolutely still for one heartbeat, and then made a sound she'd remember for the rest of her life, and bent his head and did the same to her. Teeth at the curve of her shoulder. Very careful. Very gentle. Not breaking anything. Just there, just closing, just holding, while something in her chest that had been clenched like a fist for two years opened up all at once, like a door.

---

Afterward, she lay on her stomach across most of the enormous bed with her face mashed into a pillow that smelled of him, and her whole body humming, and his tail lying across the backs of her legs like a blanket.

He was on his back beside her with one arm flung over his eyes. His chest was going up and down. Every so often a small sound came out of him, not quite a word.

"Well," Leah said, into the pillow.

"Ja," Harry said.

There was a long pause.

"Rule four's suspended," Leah said.

He took the arm off his eyes and turned his head and looked at her. His ears were all over the place. One of them was folded inside out.

"For how long?"

"Tonight."

"Just tonight?"

"Don't push it."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he rolled onto his side, toward her, and put his nose down into the hair at the back of her neck, very slowly, and breathed in. A long breath. All the way in.

"Rain on cedar," he said, very quietly, against her neck. "Salt water. Fur." Another breath. "And me. You smell like me now."

Something happened in her chest.

"That's the dumbest thing anybody's ever said to me," Leah said.

"I'm aware."

"Do it again."

He did it again.

Down in the valley, one of the jackals called, and the other answered it, close, from right beside it, and then they both went quiet.

---

### III.

The birds woke her.

A raucous, honking, outraged scream, *HAA-HAA-HAA-DE-DAH*, so close it seemed to come from inside the room, answered by another, and another, and Leah came off the pillow with her teeth bared before she knew she was awake.

Beside her, Harry didn't even open his eyes.

"Hadedas," he said, into the pillow. "Ibis. Big ugly grey things. They do that every morning at sunrise. There's a family of them lives on the roof. I've tried everything."

*HAA-HAA-HAAAA.*

"Everything?"

"Everything legal." He pulled the pillow over his head. "Go back to sleep."

She didn't go back to sleep.

She lay there in the gray early light with her heart slowly coming down and the hadedas yelling on the thatch over her head, and looked at him.

He was asleep again already, sprawled on his front with one paw hanging off the bed and his tail curled loosely around her ankle. His ears stuck out from under the pillow, one of them twitching every time a hadeda screamed. There was a long crooked part in the fur on his left shoulder, where the werewolf had bitten him. On his right shoulder, at the base of his neck, there was a new mark. Small. A crescent of tooth-shaped dents in the fur.

Hers.

She put her hand up to her own shoulder. There it was. She could feel it, very faint, a tingling in the skin like the ghost of pressure. She healed too fast for bruises.

She lay there with her hand on her shoulder and the hadedas yelling, and the fear came.

It came the way it always came, all at once, cold, starting in her stomach. It had been gone for one whole night, for the first time in two years, and now it was back, and it brought everything with it.

Sam.

Sam's face, the morning after he'd come back. How he'd looked at her like he was trying to remember who she was. How he'd said *I'm sorry, Lee, I'm so sorry*, over and over, and how it hadn't mattered how sorry he was, because the second Emily walked through the door and he looked at her, he'd been gone. *Gone*. The whole shape of him had rearranged itself around somebody else in the time it took to blink, and he couldn't have stopped it if he'd tried, and the worst part, the part that still woke her up at night, was that she'd been inside his head afterward and she knew he hadn't tried. He hadn't wanted to. Because once it happened, there was nothing left in him that *could* want to.

And she was a wolf now. The same kind. The same blood.

It could happen to her.

Nobody knew if it could. She was the first girl. Nobody knew anything about her. But the elders said it ran in the blood, and her blood was the same as Sam's, and what if one day she was standing in a grocery store, or at a gas station, or walking past some guy on the street in Seattle, and she looked up, and—

And Harry would be Leah.

And she would be Sam.

She'd do to him exactly what had been done to her. She'd look at him one morning like she was trying to remember who he was. She'd say *I'm sorry, I'm so sorry*, and she'd mean it, and it wouldn't matter, and she wouldn't be able to stop it, and she wouldn't want to.

Leah sat up in the bed.

Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them. She hadn't felt this cold since before she phased the first time.

She got up very carefully, unwound his tail from her ankle, and dressed: jeans, Josie's green top with a new tear at the shoulder seam. The bracelet was still on her wrist. It wouldn't come off unless she took it off.

She stood at the bedroom door with her hand on the bracelet for a long time.

Then she went out onto the stoep.

---

The valley was full of mist, lying in the bottom like milk in a bowl, and the cliffs on the far side were red and gold in the first sun. A troop of baboons was moving along the ridge above the house, and a huge male sat down on a boulder and looked at her with his chin in his hand like he was waiting for her to explain herself.

She sat down on the edge of the stoep with her feet hanging over the drop and put her face in her hands.

She didn't know how long she sat there. Long enough for the hadedas to give up and fly off down the valley, honking. Long enough for the mist to start thinning.

She heard him get up. She heard the floor creak, and the kitchen tap, and a kettle going on, and him not saying anything for a while. She heard him come out onto the stoep behind her, silent on his footpaws, and stop. She smelled coffee.

He sat down beside her, a couple of feet away, and put a mug of coffee down on the stone next to her hip. He didn't say anything. He just sat there, with his own mug, and looked at the valley.

She didn't pick up the coffee.

"This was a mistake," she said.

Beside her, he went very still.

She kept her face in her hands. She couldn't look at him. If she looked at him she wouldn't be able to say it.

"I can't do this," she said. "I'm sorry. I can't. Last night was—" Her voice cracked. She pushed through it. "Last night was the best night of my life, okay? That's—" She stopped. "That's the problem. That's exactly the problem."

He didn't say anything. He waited.

"I told you about Sam," she said. "About imprinting. How it works. How you look at somebody and that's it, you're gone, it doesn't matter what you had before, it doesn't matter who you loved. And I told you about the four of them who've done it. And the baby."

"Ja."

"I didn't tell you the rest." She took her hands off her face and looked at the valley, at the mist and the red cliffs and the baboon, anywhere except at him. "I'm one of them. Same blood. Same everything. Nobody knows if it can happen to a girl, because there's never been a girl. But nobody knows that it *can't*, either. And if it can—"

"Leah."

"If it can, then one day I'm going to look at some guy," Leah said, and her voice was going now, she couldn't stop it, "at a gas station, or in a store, or anywhere, some guy I've never met, and that's going to be it. And I'll leave. I won't be able to help it. I won't even *want* to help it. I'll leave you exactly the way Sam left me. I'll look at you like I don't know who you are. And I'll *know* what that's like, I'll know *exactly* what it does to a person, because it's the worst thing that's ever happened to me, and I'll do it anyway." She dragged her wrist across her eyes. "I can't. I'm not doing that to you. I'm not doing it to anybody. So I think we should stop. Now. Before it's—" She stopped. "Before."

The baboon on the boulder scratched its armpit.

Harry didn't say anything for a long time.

When she finally looked at him, he wasn't looking at her. He was looking down at the coffee mug in his paws. His ears were back, partway.

"Can I tell you something?" he said.

"What."

"It's a long one. Bit of a long story."

"You've got a lot of those."

"Ja." He turned the mug around in his paws. "This one's the long story under all the other long stories. I don't tell it much." He was quiet for a second. "Will you let me?"

She looked at him. Then she picked up her coffee and held it in both hands, and didn't drink it, and nodded.

He told her.

---

"When I was one," Harry said, "the dark wizard I told you about came to our house. Voldemort. He killed my dad at the door. He killed my mum in my bedroom, in front of my cot, because she wouldn't get out of the way. Then he pointed his wand at me and used the same curse he'd used on them. The one you saw last week. The green one."

Leah's hands tightened on the mug.

"It bounced," Harry said. "My mum dying for me did something. Old magic. Older than wands. It put something in my skin he couldn't touch, and when the curse hit me, it went straight back at him and tore him to bits. Gave me this." He touched his forehead, between the eyes, above the muzzle, where the fur grew in a thin pale line she hadn't noticed before, jagged, like a lightning bolt. "And it did something else, that nobody knew about for sixteen years."

He took a breath.

"He'd been doing a thing. To his own soul. Very dark magic. The darkest there is. You kill somebody, and it tears your soul. And if you know how, you can take the torn bit and put it in something. A cup. A ring. A book. Hide it away. So that if your body dies, you don't. You stay tied to the world by the bits you've hidden." His ears went flat. "He'd done it five times. And the night he came for me, when the curse rebounded and his body was blown apart, his soul was so ripped up already that another piece came off. And it went into the only living thing in the room."

Leah stared at him.

"Me," Harry said.

The valley was very quiet. Even the baboon had stopped scratching.

"I had a piece of the man who murdered my parents living in me," Harry said, "for sixteen years. I didn't know. Nobody knew. I could speak to snakes, because he could. I had his dreams. Saw through his eyes, sometimes. Felt him when he was angry, like a knife in my head. I thought I was going mad. Then, when I was seventeen, I found out what it was. And I found out the only way to kill him was to get rid of every piece. Including the one in me."

"How," Leah said. Her voice came out very small.

"I walked into a forest," Harry said, "and let him kill me."

She couldn't say anything at all.

"He used the curse again. I didn't fight. I died." He said it plainly, the way he'd said everything, like he was reporting the weather. "For a bit. And I went somewhere. I don't know where. It looked like a railway station. Very clean. Very bright. And there was a—thing, on the floor, under a bench. Small. Raw. Crying. That was the piece of him that had been in me. What was left of a soul after you've torn it that many times." His ears stayed flat. "And I had a choice. To go on, or come back. I came back. And the piece of him didn't. It stayed there, under the bench. And I was just me, for the first time since I was one."

"After the war," Harry said, "I had to know. What had been in me. How it got in. How it was tied on. How it came out. I couldn't sleep, not knowing. So I learned. Everything. Every book on souls in every library that would let me in, and a lot of them that wouldn't. Bonds. Ties. Curses that live in the blood. Things that hook one soul to another. Things that eat them. I've seen what a Dementor does to a soul up close. I've seen what a soul looks like when it's been torn and stuck back on wrong." He turned the mug in his paws. "It's the darkest magic there is. I hate it. I hate that I know it. I've never used it on anybody and I'd like to go on not using it till I die."

He looked at her then. For the first time since he'd sat down.

"But I know it," he said. "I know how souls tie on, Leah. I know how a hook goes into one. I know where the strings are."

Leah held very still.

"And I'm telling you," Harry said, very quietly, "I like you. More than I've liked anybody in a very long time. I don't know what this is yet. You don't either. Maybe it goes somewhere and maybe it doesn't, and if it doesn't, if one day you decide you've had enough of me and my tail and my rubbish pool and you want to go, then you go. That's yours. That's your choice, and I'll be sad about it, and I'll carry your bags."

His ears came up. All the way. And his voice changed, went low and flat and hard, with something running underneath it she'd only heard once before, in a clearing in Canada, right before he put a hole through a man's chest.

"But if anyone," he said, "*anything*, thinks it can take you away from me because your wolf looks at some bloke in a petrol station and decides that's that—then I will find that imprint. I will get into it with every bit of that knowledge I hate. And I will take it apart string by string. Every thread. Every knot. Every hook. Until there is nothing left of it at all." His muzzle drew back, just slightly, off his teeth. "And if it takes a week, or a year, or ten years, I'll sit there and do it. Because I'll be damned if some wolf mojo gets to decide what we've got. It doesn't get a vote. You do. You and me. That's it."

The baboon on the boulder got up and wandered off along the ridge.

Leah sat on the edge of the stoep with the coffee going cold in her hands and looked at him. At the green eyes and the long muzzle and the pale jagged line on his forehead, and the crescent of her own teeth in the fur at the base of his neck.

Nobody had ever said anything like that to her.

Nobody had ever said that it wasn't decided. That it could be fought. That it didn't get a vote.

Her whole life, everybody she knew had treated imprinting like weather. Like gravity. Like something you stood in the rain and took. *It's destiny, Leah. It's sacred. It's bigger than all of us.* Sam had said it. Her mom had said it. The elders had said it. Emily had said it, crying, the first time she'd come to apologize. *Nobody's to blame. It's just how it is.*

And this idiot was sitting on his stoep in the sun with a cup of coffee, telling her that if it ever happened to her he'd take it apart with his teeth.

She put the mug down.

"You'd really do that," she said.

"Ja."

"You don't even know if you *can*."

"Nope." His ears flicked. "Never met an imprint. Don't know how they're built. Might be easy. Might take the rest of my life." He shrugged, one shoulder. "I've got a lot of life. Healers reckon a fair bit more than average, with the wolf in it. I'm not doing anything else with it."

Leah looked at him for a long moment.

Then she reached over and grabbed a fistful of the fur at the front of his chest and pulled him down and kissed him, badly, at the corner of the mouth, the way she'd figured out last night, and held on.

"Okay," she said, against his muzzle.

"Okay?"

"Okay. I'm not breaking up with you."

"Were we going out?"

"*Harry.*"

"I'm just asking. For clarity." His ears were all the way up. His tail had started thumping against the stone of the stoep behind him. "For my records."

"Shut up," Leah said, and kissed him again, and he shut up.

---

### IV.

The mirror went off while she was eating her third plate of eggs.

He'd made an enormous breakfast, eggs and thick coils of spicy sausage he called boerewors and fried tomatoes, cooking the way he did everything, too fast and with too much confidence, and about forty percent of it went on the floor. She was on a stool at the counter telling him about the time Seth had tried to cook a Thanksgiving turkey while the whole pack watched through his eyes, when the mirror in his belt pouch buzzed like a hornet.

Harry's ears went flat.

"No," he said to the pouch.

It buzzed again.

"It's Sunday."

It buzzed a third time, longer.

He took it out and flipped it open. Hennessey's narrow, tired face appeared in the glass. He was wearing a dressing gown. He looked like he'd been up since about four.

"Potter."

"It's Sunday, Hennessey."

"I'm aware it's Sunday. I'm in my dressing gown. My wife is looking at me." Hennessey pinched the bridge of his nose. "Are you somewhere you can talk?"

Harry glanced at Leah. She put down her fork.

"Ja," he said. "Go."

"New assignment. Priority. MACUSA referred it to us at midnight their time, and the Director wants you on it by tomorrow."

"I've got leave. I've got a week's leave. You said—"

"I said a lot of things. It's a vampire matter."

Harry's ears came up. "Ours or the other sort?"

"The other sort. The American variety. The ones the Italians look after." Hennessey's voice went flat and careful. "A coven of them. Nine, possibly more. In a small town in the Pacific Northwest. They've been there some years, very quietly, and MACUSA's left them alone because they've been very quiet, and the Italians have an arrangement with us about that sort of thing that nobody wants to test. But in the last eighteen months, they have been very much *not* quiet."

Leah went cold.

She sat on the stool with her fork in her hand and felt the blood go out of her face.

"Go on," Harry said. His face stayed on the mirror. Both his ears had swiveled sideways, toward her.

"Seattle, last spring. Forty-odd Muggles missing or dead over three months. The Muggle papers called it a serial killer. MACUSA's coroners say it was a pack of new vampires, very young, very hungry, somebody's army. Then the Italians came in June and cleaned it up, which they never do on this side of the water, and MACUSA is very upset that nobody told them. Then the police chief's daughter married into the coven in August and came back from her honeymoon dying of something nobody could name. And then, three weeks ago, she stopped being dying, and her father's been told she's in quarantine with a rare disease, and she's been seen by two hikers in the woods with *red eyes*."

Harry didn't say anything.

"And there's a child," Hennessey said. "Nobody quite knows whose. Seen at the house by a MACUSA observer with an Omnioculars and a great deal of patience. Looked like a newborn on the twelfth. Looked like a toddler by the twenty-sixth."

"Christ," Harry said quietly.

"And," Hennessey said, "there's a tribe of shapeshifters on the reservation next door. Very large wolves. One hunter swore on a Bible to the Forest Service that he'd seen a wolf the size of a horse. And three weeks ago, the police chief—the same police chief, mind—apparently watched a teenage boy turn into one of them in his back garden."

Harry's face stayed on the mirror. His ears stayed on Leah.

"MACUSA wants it assessed," Hennessey said. "Contained, if it needs containing. The Statute's in genuine danger. The police chief alone is a disaster. If the Italians hear about that child before we've sorted it, they'll come over in force, and then we'll have a war in a Muggle town in Washington State, and the Statute goes with it. MACUSA can't go in on its own—half of it's on sovereign tribal land, and the tribe isn't party to any wizarding treaty, and the Americans would rather eat their own hats than start that conversation. So it's us. It's you. You've got experience with large dogs."

Harry's ears went sideways.

"What's the town called?" he said, very evenly.

"Forks," said Hennessey. "Forks, Washington. Clallam County. On the Olympic Peninsula." He squinted out of the mirror. "Why are you making that face?"

"I'm not making a face."

"You're making a face, Potter. And your ears are doing something."

"They're ears. They do things." Harry took a breath. "When do you want me there?"

"Tomorrow, your time. I'll have a Portkey to you by tonight." Hennessey paused. "And Potter? The Canadian woman. Thibodeaux. She's been on the floo to my office twice since Friday asking about your hiker with the dog. I've told her the hiker's of no interest to us." He looked at Harry for a long moment. "I'd like that to remain true."

"So would I."

"Good." The mirror went gray.

Harry put it down on the counter, very carefully, like it might go off again.

Then he looked at her.

Leah sat on the stool with her fork in her hand and her eggs going cold, and she couldn't make her face do anything at all.

"Forks," she said.

"Ja."

"That's—" Her voice came out strange. "That's home, Harry. That's my home. That's six miles from my mom's house. That's—the coven is the Cullens. The baby is Jacob's—is the baby. The kid who phased in front of Charlie is *Jacob*. The police chief is my mom's *boyfriend*."

"I worked that out about halfway through," Harry said. "Your face did it for me."

"What are you going to *do*?"

"Don't know yet. Depends on them." His ears were very straight. "I'm going to go there. I'm going to look at it. I'm going to talk to whoever's in charge, and tell them what they've done, and what's coming if they don't sort it out, and then we'll see. Maybe it's a conversation. Maybe it's a lot of conversations." He paused. "Maybe it's not."

"They'll fight you."

"They might."

"Jacob will fight you. He'll hate you. He already hates you. You called him a knock-off."

"Good," Harry said. "Means he's paying attention."

Leah put her fork down. She looked at the counter. At the eggs and the boerewors and the burnt toast.

She thought about the Cullens' lawn eleven days ago: her arms folded so tight her forearms ached, the baby in Jacob's lap, and everybody nodding along like it was destiny. Nobody saying anything, ever, about the forty dead in Seattle, or Charlie, or what would happen when the Italians—the *Volturi*—heard about that kid.

She thought about somebody walking up onto that lawn and saying *right, you lot*.

"Okay," Harry said. "Here's the thing."

She looked up.

"You can stay here," he said. "If you want. Long as you like. House is yours. Nobody'll bother you. There's a phone in the study that works if you shout at it, you can call Marg and tell her I didn't kill you, and the baboons are mostly harmless if you don't leave food out. I'll come back when it's done." His ears flicked. "Or I can take you back to Atlin. Or anywhere else. You name it."

"Or?"

"Or nothing," Harry said. "I'd never ask you to stay. Not knowing you'd have my tail the second I left." His muzzle twitched. "I've met you."

Leah looked at him for a long time.

Then she picked up her fork again, and ate the rest of her eggs, and his, and the last of the boerewors, while he watched her with his ears pointed straight at her and his tail not moving.

When she was done, she put the fork down and wiped her mouth.

"I'm coming," she said.

His tail started moving.

"Obviously I'm coming," Leah said. "You think I'm going to let you walk onto that lawn on your own? You don't even know which one's Edward. You'll get the blonde one and the big one mixed up. You'll shake hands with the wrong one." She got up off the stool. "And I'm not missing it."

"Missing what?"

"Somebody," Leah said, "finally, *finally* telling them."

### V.

The Portkey was a coffee mug that said *WORLD'S OKAYEST WIZARD* on the side, and it put them down at ten in the morning, Pacific time, in a stand of moss-hung bigleaf maples about a mile north of the Cullens' house, in the rain.

Leah knew where she was before she opened her eyes.

She knew it the way you know your own bed in the dark. Wet moss. Wet cedar. Sword fern and salal and the river, high and brown with the fall rains. Salt, faint, from the coast. And under all of it, threaded through the whole forest like a bad smell through a house, sweet and cold and chemical: vampire.

Home.

She stood in the rain with her eyes closed and breathed it in, and felt her whole body go tight, every muscle at once, and the wolf come up under her ribs with its hackles raised.

"Ja," Harry said beside her, quietly. "I'm getting it too."

She opened her eyes. He was standing in the ferns in his long gray coat, the rain beading on his fur, with his nose up and his ears flat and his lip curled back off his teeth, just slightly, in a way she was pretty sure he didn't know he was doing.

"That's them?" he said.

"That's them."

"Bleach," he said. "Bleach and candyfloss."

"That's what I *always* say."

"It's like somebody poured a chemistry set into a sweetshop." He shook his head, hard, like a dog shaking off water, and his ears flapped. "How do you stand it?"

"I don't," Leah said.

He looked at her. Then he held out his paw.

She took it.

They walked the mile together through the wet woods, and she didn't let go, and somewhere around the half-mile mark she realized she'd stopped thinking about the Cullens at all, and was thinking about him instead.

It was hard not to. He was right there. He was walking beside her through the ferns, silent, heel to toe, with the rain darkening his fur and running off the tips of his ears, and his coat swinging, and his tail swaying behind him, and he was so stupidly, ridiculously, *unfairly* good to look at that it made her want to bite something. Seven feet of him. Tall, dark, and handsome like somebody had taken the phrase and built a person out of it and then, for a joke, made him a wolf. Those shoulders. That jaw. Those *hands*—paws, whatever, she didn't care what you called them, she knew exactly what they could do now.

She thought about last night.

She tried not to, because she was about to walk onto a lawn full of vampires and one of them could read minds, and she knew that, she *knew* that, and she thought about it anyway. She thought about the moment somewhere around two in the morning when he'd stopped being careful. The growl that had started in his chest and come through her spine. His claws sliding out at her hips, just the tips, and him freezing, and her saying *leave them*, and him leaving them. The weight of him. The heat. The fur. The sound he'd made when she bit him. The way he'd bent his head and done the same to her, so gently, teeth at her shoulder, and held there, while—

She stumbled on a root.

He caught her by the elbow without looking. "All right?"

"Fine," Leah said. Her face was hot. "Totally fine. Root."

His nose twitched.

"Rule four's back on," she said quickly.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your nose said something."

"My nose," said Harry, "has been extremely well-behaved this morning, under very difficult circumstances," and kept walking, and his tail swished once behind him in a way that she was absolutely sure was smug.

---

The trees thinned. The ground rose. And then there was the river, wide and loud, and on the other side of it, through the last of the cedars, the lawn.

The big white house. The glass wall. Every light on, even in the middle of the morning, because the cloud was so low and dark.

And on the lawn, in the rain, all of them.

They'd been waiting. Edward would have heard her coming from the minute she landed, her thoughts blaring through the woods like a car alarm.

So they were all there. Carlisle in front, blond and calm, with his hands in the pockets of a gray cardigan. Esme beside him. Rosalie and Emmett. Jasper, at the edge, a little apart, in the way he always stood, like he was measuring the distance to everybody. Alice, small and dark, with her head tilted and a strange look on her face, frowning, like somebody trying to read a page that kept going blank. Edward, with his hand on Bella's shoulder.

And Bella, with the baby on her hip.

The baby was bigger. Of course it was. It was the size of a kid a year old now, maybe more, with a full head of bronze curls and fat cheeks and a little sweater with a duck on it. It was sitting on Bella's hip and looking across the lawn at them.

At Harry.

It hadn't blinked.

And on the porch, coming down the steps fast, in cut-offs and nothing else, Seth. And behind him, slower, much slower, Jacob.

Leah let go of Harry's paw.

She went across the river in one leap, and landed on the bank, and Seth hit her about two seconds later like a truck, all arms, lifting her clean off her feet and spinning her around in the rain, yelling something into her hair that she couldn't make out. He smelled like home. He smelled like Mom's laundry soap and wet dog and enchiladas.

"You're okay," he was saying. "You're okay, you're okay, you're *okay*—"

"I'm okay," Leah said. "Seth. Seth, put me down. You're crushing my ribs."

He put her down. He held on to her arms and looked at her face. Then he looked past her.

Harry had crossed the river without a sound and was standing in the ferns at the edge of the lawn, coat dripping, ears up, the way he'd stood at the edge of the clearing in Canada.

Seth's mouth fell open.

"Oh," Seth said. "Oh, *wow*. Okay. He's really big in person."

"Seth."

"Like, *really* big. Is that a *coat*? Can I—are those *claws*? Leah, are those—"

"*Seth.*"

Seth shut his mouth. He didn't step any closer to Harry. He stayed exactly where he was, Leah noticed, between Harry and the house, with his weight on the balls of his feet. Fifteen years old, and he'd put himself in front of everybody without thinking about it. Same as always.

Harry looked at him for a moment. His ears tipped forward.

"You'll be the brother," he said.

"Uh," said Seth. "Yeah?"

"She talks about you." Harry's tail swung once. "Says you're the worst."

Seth looked at Leah. Leah shrugged. Seth's face did something complicated and then broke into a grin so wide it nearly fell off.

"She *loves* me," Seth said.

"Ja," said Harry. "That's what I said."

---

Jacob got to the bottom of the porch steps and stopped there, in jeans and nothing else, with the rain running down his chest, six-foot-seven and furious, looking at Harry. Harry looked back.

Same height. Exactly the same. She'd never noticed how rarely Jacob had to look anybody straight in the eye.

Jacob's nostrils flared.

She saw it happen. She saw him breathe in, and saw something go across his face—confusion, a flicker of it, there and gone—and saw his eyes narrow.

And she saw Harry's nose go up at exactly the same moment, and his ears go straight up, and stay there, and his head tilt, just slightly, the way it had tilted at her in the clearing. Like a dog that had heard a word it almost knew.

For a second nobody on the lawn said anything at all.

Then Carlisle stepped forward.

"Good morning," he said, in his soft, careful voice. "I'm Carlisle Cullen. Welcome to our home. I believe you must be—"

"Harry Potter," said Harry. He didn't take his eyes off Jacob for another second. Then he did, and looked at Carlisle, and his ears came forward. "International Confederation of Wizards, Enforcement Office. Morning. Here's the deal."

Carlisle's eyebrows went up a fraction.

"You lot," Harry said, pleasantly, to the whole lawn, "have been very, very naughty."

---

Leah stood beside Seth in the rain and watched him do it, and it was the best thing that had ever happened to her.

He didn't raise his voice. He didn't take a step closer. He stood at the edge of the lawn with his paws in the pockets of his coat and his ears pointed at Carlisle, and he went through it, one item at a time, like a man reading a grocery list.

Seattle. Forty-one missing. Three months. "Somebody else's army, I'm told. But you knew. You knew for weeks, and you sat on it, and you let the Italians come and clean up, which is the one thing my lot and the American lot have spent three hundred years trying to keep from happening on this side of the water."

The police chief's daughter. "Married in August. Honeymoon. Comes home dying of something no Muggle doctor could name. Then three weeks ago she's miraculously not dying, and she's in 'quarantine,' and hikers are seeing her in the woods with eyes like stop lights." His eyes went to Bella. To her red-gold eyes. Then back to Carlisle. "You're a doctor, I'm told. That the best cover story you had?"

Carlisle opened his mouth.

"The kid," Harry said, and didn't look at it. "We'll get to the kid."

The wolves. Hikers. The horse-sized wolf the hunter swore to on a Bible. "That one's on your neighbours." His ears flicked toward Jacob. "Who, I'm told, then went and turned into a wolf in front of the police chief, in his own garden, three weeks ago, because he thought it'd be a nice way to break the news."

Jacob went rigid.

"I had a *reason*—"

"I'm sure you did, boet. Hold that thought." Harry didn't look at him. "That police chief. Charlie Swan. He's a Muggle. He knows there's something going on in these woods. He knows his daughter's not his daughter anymore. He's watched a kid turn into a wolf. He's not stupid, from what I hear. And every day he comes over here and holds that baby and doesn't ask questions is a day he's choosing not to ask. One day he'll stop choosing. And then he'll ask somebody. And then I'm going to have to come back here and do something to his memory that I'd very much rather not do to a decent man." His voice didn't change at all. "So that's where we are."

There was a long silence on the lawn.

Rosalie broke it.

"And who exactly," she said, in a voice like broken glass, "made *you* the judge of what we do in our own home?"

Harry looked at her.

"About three hundred years of international law," he said. "And a bloke called Hennessey, in his dressing gown, yesterday morning." His ears went sideways. "I'd have rather stayed in bed, if it helps. I was in the middle of eggs."

Emmett laughed. He tried to turn it into a cough. Rosalie turned her head and looked at him, and he stopped.

Leah stood in the rain and didn't laugh, because if she started she wouldn't stop.

Weeks of standing on this lawn with her arms folded while every one of them acted like the only thing in the world that mattered was a baby with a duck on its sweater. Nobody saying *forty people in Seattle*. Nobody saying *Charlie*. Nobody saying *what happens when the Volturi find out*. She'd thought it every single time she phased, and the boys had told her to shut up, she was just being bitter, she was just being Leah.

And now here he was. Seven feet of wolf in a wet coat, standing on the Cullens' lawn with his paws in his pockets, saying every single thing she'd been thinking for weeks, out loud, to their faces, while they stood there and *took* it.

She looked at him. At the rain running off his ears. At the line of his jaw. At the shoulders under the wet wool, and the crescent of her own teeth somewhere under that collar, where nobody else could see it.

Tall, dark, and terrifying, and hers.

She thought about two in the morning again. She couldn't help it. She thought about his claws in her hips, and his teeth at her shoulder, and the weight of him over her, and how the growl had sounded so close to her ear, and how she'd dug her own fingers into the fur of his back and—

Across the lawn, Edward Cullen flinched.

---

### VI.

He had heard her coming from a mile away.

That was nothing unusual. Leah Clearwater's mind had always been loud: a hard, bright, abrasive thing, like a radio left on in an empty room, full of static and sarcasm and pain. Edward had spent weeks hearing it from the edge of the lawn. He'd been almost glad when she left. It had been very quiet without her.

This morning it had come crashing back into range all at once, a mile north, in the trees, and it was so different he hadn't recognized it at first.

It was still loud. It was still Leah. But the static was gone. The pain was—not gone. Quieter. Pushed back. And in its place, filling up nearly every corner of her head, there was *him*.

Edward had stood on the lawn with his hand on Bella's shoulder and listened to Leah Clearwater walk a mile through the woods thinking, with almost no interruption, about a seven-foot wolf.

And not in any way that Edward had ever wanted to hear anybody think about anything.

He'd told the others she was coming. He'd told them she wasn't alone. He'd told them there was something walking beside her that he couldn't hear.

That was the part he couldn't explain. That was the part that had made Jasper go still and Carlisle go thoughtful and Rosalie's lip curl.

Because there was something walking beside her. He could hear its footsteps—barely, almost not at all, the most silent footfall he'd ever heard from anything that wasn't one of his own kind. He could hear its breathing and its heartbeat, slow and heavy and strong, like a drum. He could hear Leah's mind circling it, fixated on it, painting it in such hot, vivid, embarrassing detail that he could have drawn it.

And from the thing itself, there was nothing.

*Nothing.* Bella's mind was frosted glass; this was a wall. Smooth and blank and seamless, without a crack in it anywhere, and behind it—he was certain of this, he couldn't have said how—something very large, and very awake, that knew perfectly well he was looking.

It was the most unnerving thing he'd experienced in a century.

And now it was standing on his lawn, in a coat, telling his father exactly how badly they'd failed, in a calm, flat, oddly accented voice, and there was still nothing. He'd been pushing at the wall the whole time. Carefully, then less carefully. It hadn't so much as flexed.

Beside him, Alice was frowning so hard it was nearly a wince. *I can't see him. I can't see ANY of this. Since she landed it's just—gone. All of it. Edward, everything after this morning is blank.* Jasper was very still at the edge of the group. *Calm. Very calm. Too calm. Like there's a lid on something. I can't get a sense of what's under the lid.* Emmett was thinking, with great enthusiasm, about arm-wrestling it. Rosalie was thinking several things Edward chose not to listen to.

And Leah.

Leah was standing beside her brother in the rain, watching the wolf take apart every decision the family had made in the last eighteen months, and she was thinking—

He tried not to hear it. He'd been trying not to hear it for a mile. He'd gotten very good, over a century, at turning his attention away from the parts of people's minds that he had no business in.

But it was so *loud*.

*—his claws in her hips, just the tips, and the growl so close to her ear she felt it in her teeth, and the weight of him, and the heat, God, the heat, she'd never in her life been the cold one in a room before, and his teeth at her shoulder so careful while she—*

Edward flinched.

He couldn't help it. It went through him like a blow. He'd heard a great many people think a great many things, but not usually in such detail, and not usually with such enthusiasm, and not usually while his father was standing right there.

*—and she'd dug her fingers into the fur of his back and he'd made that sound, that sound, she'd remember that sound until she died, and then he'd—*

"Leah," Edward said. It came out strangled. "*Please.* Stop."

The lawn went quiet.

Everyone turned to look at him. Then, as one, they turned to look at Leah.

Leah Clearwater stood in the rain and looked back at him with absolutely no shame whatsoever. Her face was red. Her chin was up. And in her head, very clearly and very deliberately, she thought: *Make me.*

And the wolf's ears perked.

They came straight up. Both of them. And swiveled. And the long black head turned away from Carlisle and toward Edward, with an expression of polite, intense interest.

"Sorry," said Harry Potter. "Stop what?"

Edward said nothing.

"She thinking something?" The ears were all the way forward. "Anything good?"

"That," Edward said stiffly, "is between her and—"

The green eyes met his.

And something walked into Edward's mind.

In a hundred and five years, with every mind on the planet open to him like a book left lying on a table, he had never once felt anybody else come into his. He hadn't known it could be done.

It felt like a hand. A big, rough, careful hand, opening a door in his head that he hadn't known was there, and reaching in, and very precisely, with no wasted motion at all, taking hold of the last thirty seconds of what he'd heard, and *looking* at it.

Then it was gone.

The whole thing had taken less than a second. The wall was back. Smooth and blank and seamless.

Edward stood on his own lawn with his hand frozen on Bella's shoulder and every muscle in his body locked, and for the first time in a very long time, he was afraid.

The wolf's muzzle drew back. Slowly. Showing a great many very long, very white teeth.

It was, unmistakably, a grin.

He turned his head and looked at Leah across the lawn.

"Huh," Harry said.

Leah's face, if it was possible, went redder.

"Ja," Harry said. "No. That's—ja." His tail swept once, wide, across the wet grass behind him. "I'm down to try that later. If you want."

Leah made a noise that Edward had never heard come out of a human throat, somewhere between a snort and a growl and a laugh, and put her face in her hands.

Seth said, "*Oh my God*," and put his face in *his* hands.

Emmett laughed so hard he had to sit down on the porch steps.

"You," Edward said. His voice came out low and shaking. "You were in my—you were *inside*—"

"Bit," Harry said. He turned back to face him. His ears were flat now. The grin was gone. "Sorry, mate. Didn't knock. Fair's fair, though. You've been in her head since she landed. And you've been leaning on mine for the last ten minutes, very politely, like a bloke trying a door handle." His voice was perfectly even. "Thought you'd want to know what it's like from the other side."

Edward didn't answer.

"You can stop leaning, by the way," Harry said. "There's nothing in there for you. And there won't be."

Edward stopped leaning.

---

Bella had felt him go rigid. She turned and looked up at him, her red-gold eyes wide.

*Edward? What happened? What did he do?*

He didn't answer her. He couldn't. He wasn't sure he had the words.

And on Bella's hip, Renesmee, who hadn't taken her eyes off the wolf since he'd stepped out of the trees, reached up with one small hand and put it flat against her mother's cheek.

Edward saw the picture as it went into Bella's head. He always did. A bright, clear, insistent image: the wolf, from Renesmee's eyes, enormous and dark and fascinating, with his ears up and his coat dripping. Renesmee's own hand reaching out toward the black fur on his paw. And under the picture, the feeling, the question, the way she always asked things: *Please. Can I? Can I show him? I want to show him.*

Bella blinked.

She looked down at her daughter. Then she looked at the wolf. Then, with the air of someone who was very aware of how strange this was going to sound, she took a step forward across the grass.

"Mr.—Potter?" Bella said.

The wolf's ears turned toward her.

"This is my daughter. Renesmee." Bella's voice was careful. "She's—she can't talk yet. Not really. But she communicates by touch. She puts her hand on you, and she shows you things. Pictures. Feelings." She hesitated. "She'd like to show you something. If you'd let her touch you."

Renesmee looked at the wolf. Her big brown eyes were wide and solemn and steady.

She didn't blink.

The wolf looked at Renesmee for a long moment.

Then he took a very small step backward.

"Yeesh," Harry said. "No. Thank you."

Bella's mouth fell open.

"I'm good," Harry said. He'd put both paws up, palms out, the way you'd back away from a strange dog. His ears had gone flat. "Very kind. Very—ja. Lovely offer. I'm going to pass."

"She just wants to—"

"I'm sure she does. I'm sure it's very nice. I've just—" His eyes went to Renesmee and stayed there, and his ears went flatter. "I've had enough things in my head for one lifetime, ma'am. I keep it shut. Nothing personal."

And then, under his breath—so quietly that only a wolf or a vampire would have heard it, which was to say, everyone on the lawn—he turned his head a fraction toward Leah and muttered:

"She hasn't blinked. Leah. She hasn't blinked *once*. Since I got here. It's like a Furby."

Bella went white. Whiter. Rosalie hissed. Esme's hand flew to her mouth.

And Leah Clearwater, standing beside her brother in the rain, made a sound like a kettle boiling over, and doubled over with her hands on her knees, and laughed until she couldn't breathe.

In her head, as loud as anything Edward had ever heard her think, in a voice of pure, unholy, weeks-in-the-making joy:

*I'm going to marry him.*

### VII.

It took a while for everybody to calm down.

Rosalie had to be physically held back by Emmett, who was still laughing, which didn't help. Bella had gone very quiet and very cold and was holding Renesmee very tightly, and looking at Harry the way she'd looked at Jacob the morning she'd found out about *Nessie*. Carlisle had his hands up and was saying "Please—please—everyone—" in his softest voice, which nobody was listening to. Jasper had moved, without seeming to, to a place where he could get between Harry and the house. Alice was still frowning at nothing.

Harry stood through the whole thing at the edge of the lawn in the rain, with his paws back in his coat pockets and his ears sideways, looking about as sorry as a dog who's just eaten a birthday cake.

"Right," he said, when it had mostly died down. "That was rude. I'll own that. Sorry, ma'am." He nodded to Bella. "She's a very—alert—baby."

"*Alert*," Rosalie said.

"Very alert."

"You compared my niece," said Rosalie, "to a *toy*."

"An electronic toy," said Harry. "With a lot of personality. They were very popular." His ears flicked. "In my defence, you lot have got bigger problems this morning than what I think of your niece's blinking. I'll be here a while. You'll have plenty of time to hate me properly. Let's get on with the rest of it."

Rosalie opened her mouth.

"*Rose*," said Carlisle, quietly.

Rosalie closed it.

Leah straightened up, finally, wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist. Seth was staring at her like he'd never seen her before. She supposed he hadn't. She hadn't laughed like that in front of him since their dad died.

"You okay?" Seth said.

"I'm great," said Leah. "I'm *amazing*."

"You're scaring me a little."

"Good."

Across the lawn, Jacob hadn't moved from the bottom of the porch steps. He'd watched the whole thing with his arms folded and his jaw tight. He hadn't laughed. He hadn't said anything. He'd been looking at Harry the entire time.

Now he unfolded his arms and came across the grass.

He stopped about six feet away. Harry turned to face him. Same height, eye to eye, in the rain, and the lawn went quiet again.

"You called us fake wolves," Jacob said.

"Ja," said Harry.

"Knock-offs."

"That too."

"*Off-brand*."

"I stand by off-brand," Harry said.

Jacob's jaw worked.

"You don't know anything about us," he said. "You don't know anything about what we've been through. What she's been through. You walk in here after a week, and you think—"

"I think," said Harry, "that the only woman who's ever been one of you ran two thousand kilometres to get away from the lot of you, and none of you went after her. I think one of you made her live in his head while he loved her cousin. I think the rest of you had her standing out on this lawn in the rain for weeks, and not one of you ever once asked how she was." His ears were very flat. His voice was very level. "That's what I think, boet. Correct me where I'm wrong."

Jacob didn't say anything.

He looked past Harry at Leah. Leah looked back.

She didn't think anything at him. She didn't have to. He wasn't phased, and neither was she, and it had been a long time since they'd had to look at each other without the pack mind to do the talking. She just looked at him, and let him see her face, whatever was on it.

Jacob looked away first.

Then he looked back at Harry. And his nostrils flared again. And that look went across his face, the same one as before, confusion, quick and sharp, there and gone.

"Why," Jacob said slowly, "do you smell like my dad's kitchen?"

The whole lawn went still.

Harry's ears came up.

He didn't answer right away. He looked at Jacob, very hard, for a long moment. His nose was working. Leah could see it: little twitches, quick, pulling the air in, sorting through it. Rain and cedar and salt water and fur. The same things he'd said about her, in the bar in Atlin. *And something I've never smelled before and can't get out of my head.*

Something went across his own face. She couldn't have said what.

"Don't know," Harry said finally. Quietly. "Why do you smell like me?"

Jacob stared at him.

Leah stood beside Seth in the rain and felt every hair on her arms stand up.

*Black*, she thought. *Head of the Noble and Most Ancient House of*—

Across the lawn, she saw Edward Cullen's head snap toward her, and then toward Jacob, and his eyes go wide.

Nobody said anything.

Then the front door of the big white house opened, and Esme came out onto the porch with a dish towel over her shoulder. She looked at all of them: her family, frozen on the lawn in the rain, and the two wolves, and the very big one in the coat, and Leah, with tears still drying on her face from laughing.

"Have you eaten?" Esme said.

Everybody turned and looked at her.

"I've made breakfast," Esme said, to Harry, in her soft, warm voice. "For Jacob and Seth. There's far too much. There always is. You're very welcome." She paused. "Both of you. Leah. Please."

Harry looked at her for a long moment. His ears went straight up. His tail, behind him, gave one single, involuntary swish.

"Ma'am," he said, with deep sincerity, "I could kiss you."

"Please don't," said Leah. "I'll bite you."

"She will," Harry told Esme. "She has." He started across the lawn toward the porch, and stopped beside Leah, and looked down at her, and his muzzle drew back in that grin again, all teeth. "Breakfast. Then the rest of it."

"Then the rest of it," Leah said.

He went up the porch steps, ducking his head under the eaves, and Esme stood aside to let him in, and the Cullens all turned and watched him go into their house, the way you'd watch something very large and not very tame walk into a china shop.

Leah stood on the lawn in the rain a second longer. Jacob was still standing where Harry had left him, staring after him. Edward was looking at Jacob. Seth was looking at her with his mouth open.

"He called Nessie a Furby," Seth said. "To Bella's *face*."

"Yeah."

"Rosalie's going to kill him."

"She can try."

"Jake's going to kill him."

"Jake can try," said Leah, and went up the steps after Harry, and didn't look back.
