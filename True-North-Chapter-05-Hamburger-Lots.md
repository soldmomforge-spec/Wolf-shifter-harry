# True North

## Chapter Five: Hamburger (Lots)

---

### I.

The humming lasted about a hundred meters.

Leah let it go that long. The trees were dark and dripping, she was full of Esme Cullen's apple pie, which she was going to deny under oath, and for a hundred meters it was nice to walk half a step behind somebody and let him be the one making noise. Then the song got to the guitar part, and he started doing the guitar with his mouth.

"Stop that."

"Stop what?"

"The song."

"It's a good song."

"Jake knows that song." She didn't need the pack mind for it. She could hear him back there at the edge of the lawn the same way she could hear the river and the rain: a big body standing still in wet grass, breathing. The breathing had stopped for a second when the humming started. Now it was going again, slower. "He rebuilt the Rabbit's whole engine to that album the summer he was fifteen. I've had it in my head secondhand for a year. I know every word, and I hate every word."

Harry quit humming. Both his ears swung around backward, toward the house.

"He's still stood there."

"I know."

"In the rain."

"He likes the rain. It makes him feel deep."

Harry snorted through his nose, short, and kept walking.

"He asked if you were happy," Harry said.

"You heard that."

"I hear everything, Leah. It's a curse. Bloke three tables over at Die Ou Draak was having it off with his wife's sister, and I know which hotel."

"Don't."

"I'm not."

"You're about to say something about Jake."

"Wasn't going to." His tail swung once. "I was going to say it's past eleven and I don't know where we're going."

She didn't answer right away. Behind them the lights from the glass wall were breaking up between the trunks into pieces, then into flickers, then into nothing, and it was just the dark and the drip and the cold river smell coming up on the left.

"You said *huh*," she said. "On the lawn. When you smelled him."

"I did."

"What was the huh?"

His left ear flicked once, sharp.

"Something I'm not sure about yet. When I'm sure, I'll tell you. Straight."

She thought about arguing. She thought about the box in the back of her own head with the same thing in it, the one she'd been sitting on since a bar in Atlin so long she'd started to think of the lid as furniture.

"Fine," she said.

"Ja?"

"Fine. Walk."

He walked. She followed. And because she was half a step behind him and a little uphill, she could see the top of his head, and the burnt tuft between his ears where she'd set him on fire half a day ago on the other side of the planet, still standing up like a small black flame.

There was a fern in it.

A whole frond, bright green, a good four inches long. It had been there since the bog. It had been there through the whole Cullen living room: Carlisle's speech, Rosalie's hiss, the Furby, Edward going the color of a candle. Every vampire in that house had looked straight at it, and not one of them had said a word.

She opened her mouth to tell him.

She closed it again.

Her mother could find it.

---

They came out on the bank of the river where it ran fast and black between gravel bars, loud with rain off the mountains. Leah stopped and looked west, where the water went, though there was nothing to see that way but more dark.

"La Push is about fifteen miles," she said. "That way. Ocean's at the end of it."

"Apparate?"

"Touch me with that squeezing thing again and I'll bite you."

"You bit me last night."

"Somewhere you won't like."

His ears went all the way flat for a second. Then they came back up.

"Walk, then. Fifteen miles." He looked at the river and the trees and the black wall of the hills. "You and me, in the dark, through that, we'd be there by half two."

"Mom's up."

"How d'you know?"

"Because Seth called her from the Cullens' phone about four seconds after we walked out. Seth can't keep anything in for longer than a sneeze." Leah crossed her arms. "She's been up since eleven. She's cooking. She cooks when she's scared."

"What's she cooking?"

"Whatever's in the freezer."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"I could carry you," he said.

"No."

"Not like that."

He dropped forward.

She'd seen it once, on the hill under the wrong stars, and it still made her take a step back. He went down onto his forepaws and the shape of him poured outward, longer and lower and bigger, shoulders rolling forward and thickening, muzzle stretching, the long gray coat folding away into him like it had never been there. The brown coat over his arm and her paper bag under it folded too. She saw them go small, the size of a deck of cards, and vanish into the black fur, which she was going to have questions about. Three seconds. No sound at all.

Then there was a wolf standing on the gravel.

His shoulder was higher than her head. He was as long as her mom's truck. The gravel didn't crunch under him. It should have crunched under him like he was a truck, and it didn't. In the dark he was mostly a hole where the trees should have been, black against black, and two green eyes a long way up.

Her wolf came up out of her so fast she grabbed the nearest tree.

It wanted *out*. It wanted to drop to four legs and walk around him nose to tail and find out exactly how much bigger he was, and then it wanted to run. Down the river. Right now. With him behind her, or ahead of her, it didn't care which. Her spine ached with it. Her teeth ached with it.

She looked at the bracelet.

She could phase right here on the gravel and come back out of it in Josie's top and Marg's daughter's boots. And the second she phased, if Seth or Jake so much as twitched, they'd be in her head. Jake could phase to run home to his dad's any second and walk straight into last night. The bed the size of a small room. The twenty minutes. Her own voice saying *do it*.

She let go of the tree.

"Can't," she said. "If I change, they're there. It's not worth it."

"Wasn't going to ask." The big black head came down until one green eye was level with hers. "Climb on."

"I'm not riding you like a pony."

"Teddy did."

"Teddy's eight."

"And he had a lovely time. His hair went orange."

"I'm not eight."

"No," he said. "You're slower."

She stared at him.

"Fifteen miles on two legs," Harry said. "In the rain. In boots that are a size too big for you. While your mum stands at a stove frying everything in her freezer and looking at the clock." The eye didn't blink. "Or half an hour."

Leah stood on the gravel and hated him.

Then she said a word her mother would have washed her mouth out for, and walked to his shoulder.

He folded his front legs and went down onto his chest in the gravel, the way a horse lies down, until his back was at the height of her ribs. She put both hands in the fur. Coarse and wet on top, and under it, the deep soft undercoat, dry somehow, and so hot it was like putting her hands into bread dough. She swung a leg over.

Her knees didn't reach around. Not even close. She was sitting on something the width of a dining table.

"Hands in my ruff," he said. "Up by the shoulders. Hang on as hard as you like. You can't hurt me."

"Don't tempt me."

"Ready?"

"No."

He stood up.

The ground went away. It went a long way away. One second she was sitting on a wolf on a gravel bar and the next she was up in the dark with the tops of the salal under her boots like a lawn, and the river very far down, and her stomach still on the gravel where she'd left it.

"Oh my *God*."

"You all right?"

"Just go."

He went.

---

She was the fastest wolf in the history of the tribe, and she'd been smug about it for a year. She had never once been fast while sitting still.

The trees came at her out of the dark and went past on both sides like fence posts from a car window. Wet branches whipped at her shoulders. She put her face down into his ruff to keep from getting slapped and held on, fists full of black fur, thighs clamped as tight as they would go, and the cold rushed past her ears so hard it roared.

And under her, nothing.

That was the part she couldn't get used to. He was moving faster than she'd ever moved, through ferns and deadfall and over creeks, slabs of muscle bunching and stretching under her, his breathing no harder than a man walking to his car. She could hear the ferns hissing along his flanks and the rain and her own heart.

She couldn't hear him land.

She looked down past his shoulder and watched one huge forepaw come down on a mossy nurse log and lift again. The moss didn't tear. The log didn't creak under the better part of a ton and a half of wolf.

"That's creepy," Leah said into his fur.

"What is?"

"Your feet. They don't make any noise."

"Paws."

"*Whatever.* It's creepy. You know that, right?"

"Ja," Harry said. "I get that a lot."

She steered him with her voice. He went over Highway 101 in one jump, both lanes and both ditches, in front of a log truck with its lights on high, and she ducked so flat against his neck she got fur in her mouth. Then down through planted hemlock and across a clearcut where the stumps stood up out of the slash like rotten teeth.

She knew where the treaty line was the way she knew which stair at home creaked. They crossed it a mile past the highway. He didn't feel it. She did. Something in her chest unknotted that she hadn't known was knotted. Quileute land. Hers.

The smell started then. Under the wet fir and the cedar and the rotting slash, faint and getting stronger with every mile: salt. Kelp. The cold flat breath of the Pacific coming up the valley at night.

She put her face down in his ruff and breathed in, and it was all wolf and pine and wet stone and creek water, and she breathed in again, and under it, coming up the valley, was home.

"Leah?"

"Keep going."

They came down to the Quillayute where the rivers met, wide and black and loud, and he ran the gravel bars along the north bank going west with the water. A heron went up off a snag with a croak like a door hinge.

And on a rise above the river, two hundred meters ahead, something was standing at the edge of the trees.

Leah smelled him before she saw him. Hot, sour, a temper you could smell from a quarter mile, and under it the green-apple shampoo he'd been stealing from Rachel Black since he imprinted on her.

Paul.

He was in his wolf. Silver-gray, big, standing stock-still at the top of the rise with one forefoot lifted off the ground, like a dog that had heard a can opener. His head was turned toward them, and his eyes caught what light there was. He was looking at a black wolf the size of a pickup truck coming down the gravel bar without a sound, with Leah Clearwater on its back.

His jaw dropped open. On a wolf, that was a lot of jaw.

Leah took one hand out of Harry's ruff, held it up in the rain, and waggled her fingers at him.

Paul spun around so fast he fell over. He scrambled up, went back into the trees in a spray of salal, and a second later a howl came up out of the dark behind him, long and high and outraged, the kind of howl that meant *SAM SAM SAM SAM*.

"Friend of yours?" Harry said.

"That was Paul."

"The 101 bloke."

"Sam'll know in about four seconds." Her heart was going. Paul's howl went on and on, getting further away. Another answered it from the hills to the south, lower. Jared. "Three. Two."

"Want me to stop?"

She thought about it. She thought about Sam's face, the way it would look when Paul's head hit his. She thought about every single night for two years.

"No," she said. "Go faster."

He went faster.

---

He stopped in the trees behind the house.

She knew it before he did. She'd walked out of these trees two weeks ago naked with three hundred and forty dollars tied to her ankle, and she'd known them her whole life before that: the big leaning spruce with the old tire swing rope still knotted around one branch, gone gray, the gap in the salal where she and Seth had worn a path as kids, the burn barrel. The back of the house, small and white, with its peeling trim. The back porch light on. The kitchen window lit up yellow.

He went down on his chest in the ferns, and she slid off sideways and landed on legs that had stopped being legs somewhere around the clearcut. She grabbed his shoulder to stay up.

"You all right?"

"Fine." She wasn't. She held onto him anyway.

Behind her, the hill of fur shrank. She felt it go out from under her palm, pulling in on itself, and then he was standing beside her on two footpaws in the long gray coat with the silver clasp done up at the throat, exactly as he'd been, tall ears and all. He held out her paper bag. She took it. He put the brown coat back over his arm.

The fern was still in his fur.

She stood at the edge of the trees, the way she'd stood at the edge of the Cullens' lawn every night for a year, and looked at her own back door, and couldn't make her legs go.

The smell came out of the house through the screen. Lavender hand soap. Old coffee. And frying meat, onions, a lot of both, so much of it that the whole yard smelled of grease.

"Your mum's cooking," Harry said quietly.

"Hamburger."

"A lot of it."

*Milk. Eggs (3 doz). Bread (4). Hamburger (lots).* In her mother's slanting handwriting, on the pad by the phone, right under the note Leah had left on the fridge.

Her throat closed up.

He didn't say anything. He stood beside her in the wet ferns with his ears pointed at the house and his tail still, and waited.

Through the kitchen window, a small woman with a gray-streaked braid down her back, standing at the stove with a spatula, turned around.

---

### II.

Sue Clearwater had been looking at the stove clock for fifty-one minutes.

11:59. Four pounds of hamburger in two pans, the big cast-iron and the dented aluminum one with the loose handle, and two more pounds going in the microwave on defrost. A pot of potatoes boiling over every couple of minutes, which she kept lifting the lid on, which wasn't helping. Two onions. The last of the bread from Thursday. A can of corn she'd opened with her hands shaking so hard she'd cut her thumb on the lid.

At six minutes past eleven she'd been on Charlie Swan's couch, watching him sleep in his recliner with a fishing show on low, snoring a soft whistle at the end of every breath that she'd been pretending for months not to find sweet. Her cell phone had gone off in her purse.

*Mom.* Seth, so loud she'd had to hold the phone away from her ear. *Mom, Leah's back, she's here, she's at the Cullens', she's coming home, she's okay, she's really okay, and she's bringing HIM—*

Then a lot of things she still hadn't sorted out. *He's huge, Mom. He's bigger than Jake. He's got a coat, a real coat. He called the baby a Furby. Rosalie hissed at him. He's kind of rude. He's kind of—I don't know, Mom, he's okay, I think he's okay, he got in Edward's head somehow and Edward went all gray, and Emmett fell off the stairs—*

And then, at the end, quieter, in a voice she hadn't heard out of her son since before his father's funeral:

*She laughed, Mom. Wednesday. Jake and me saw it. She laughed so hard she had to hold onto a pool table.*

Charlie had woken up while she was standing in his front hall pulling on her jacket. *Sue? Everything okay?*

*Leah's home.*

He'd sat up so fast the recliner banged. *Hey, that's great. That's great, Sue. You want me to drive you?*

*No.*

He'd gotten his boots anyway, and then put them down again when she looked at him. He'd kissed her cheek at the door. *Tell her I said welcome home.* He hadn't asked where Leah had been for two weeks. He never asked. He hadn't asked why Sue's son was up at the Cullen house every night when his daughter was in a clinic nobody could visit, either. He looked at her sometimes, very carefully, with his policeman's eyes, and then he looked away and asked if she wanted more coffee.

She was going to have to tell him something soon. She didn't know what. She'd been not knowing what for six weeks.

She'd driven home in the truck with the wipers going and the heater broken, and come in the kitchen door, and taken every package of hamburger out of the freezer.

The note was still on the fridge.

She hadn't taken it down. Two weeks. She'd stood here every morning with her coffee and read it, under the salmon-shaped magnet Harry had bought at the Makah Days the summer Leah was six. *Mom. I'm okay. I need to go away for a while. I'm not hurt and I'm not in trouble. Don't let Seth come after me. I'll call when I can. I love you. L.* The last three words in smaller letters, crammed in at the bottom like Leah had almost not written them.

Sue lifted the lid on the potatoes. They boiled over.

She put the lid back.

She turned to the window over the sink to see if it was still raining, though she knew it was still raining, it was the end of September on the coast, and through the glass, in the light from the back porch, at the edge of the trees where the old tire swing rope still hung off the spruce, her daughter was standing.

Sue put the spatula down very carefully on the counter.

She turned off the burner under the cast-iron. She turned off the burner under the aluminum pan. She turned off the potatoes. She did it in that order, without looking, because her hands knew the stove, and because if she let the hamburger burn she was going to feel stupid about it later.

Then she looked at what was standing next to Leah.

She'd seen her son phase, in this yard, the week after the funeral: his shirt coming apart like wet paper and a sand-colored wolf the size of a horse standing where her boy had been, looking at her with his eyes. She'd seen Sam's wolf. She'd seen Jacob's. She'd thought she knew what big was.

This one was standing up.

That was the first thing. It was standing up on two legs like a man, at the tree line beside her daughter, and it was taller than Leah by almost two feet, and more than that if you counted the ears, which went straight up off its head, long and pointed. Black. She could hardly tell where it stopped and the trees started. A long gray coat that came down to its knees. A long black tail hanging down behind it under the hem. Something folded over one arm.

It was looking at the house. At the window. At her.

Sue went to the back door and opened it and stood on the step in the rain with her apron on.

"Leah," she said.

---

Her daughter came across the lawn.

She walked slowly. Sue watched her the whole way. Jeans Sue had never seen, and boots Sue had never seen, scuffed brown leather, too big, and a dark green top with a wrap front, the kind of top Leah had stopped wearing at sixteen and started calling *stupid*. A paper bag under one arm. Her hair was hacked off short and uneven, the way she cut it herself, and it was wet and plastered to her forehead.

Her face was different.

Sue couldn't have said how. It was the same face. Her father's jaw, Sue's own eyes. The same mouth that had been pressed shut for two years like it was holding something in. But there was something gone from around the eyes. Some line between the brows that Sue had stopped seeing because it had been there so long.

Leah stopped at the bottom of the step and looked up at her.

"Hi, Mom."

Sue came down the step and put her arms around her.

Leah didn't hug. She hadn't since she was thirteen. She stood there stiff as a fence post with her arms at her sides and the paper bag crunching between them, and Sue held on anyway, and after a long time one of Leah's arms came up, and then the other, and closed around Sue's back, and held on.

She smelled strange. Rain and fur, the way she always did now. And under it, on her clothes and in her hair, things Sue couldn't name. Woodsmoke, a different kind from theirs. Wool. Something dry and dusty, like hay. And something sharp and green, like pine.

"You got bigger," Sue said into her shoulder.

"I didn't."

"You did. You were always bigger than me. You got bigger."

Leah made a sound in her throat that didn't have a name. She didn't let go.

Over her daughter's shoulder, at the edge of the trees, the thing in the coat hadn't moved.

It was standing exactly where it had been. Its ears were tipped toward the house. Its tail hung still. It stayed there in the rain at the edge of the yard, like a dog that had been told to wait.

Sue thought about Seth at this table with his cereal, telling her about Leah at the Cullens'. *She won't come inside, Mom. Not ever. Esme asks her every night. She just stands out there at the edge of the trees with her arms crossed all night, like she's guarding the place from the outside.*

She let go of Leah and stepped back.

"Is he coming in," she said, "or is he going to stand out there all night like you did?"

Leah's face did something complicated.

"He's waiting to be asked."

Sue looked at the thing at the tree line for a long moment.

"Well," she said. "He can come and be looked at."

---

It crossed the lawn without a sound.

She listened for it. Seth came across the yard like a load of firewood falling off a truck. This thing was bigger than Seth and bigger than Jacob, walking upright, heel to toe, the way a man walks, and she heard the rain on the roof and the potatoes ticking as they cooled, and nothing at all from its feet.

It stopped at the bottom of the step, a few feet off, and looked at her.

Up close it was a wolf's head. There was no getting around that. A long black muzzle and a black nose, wet, the nostrils moving. Very long white teeth that showed a little at the sides even with its mouth closed. Eyes so green they looked fake, like the eyes on a stuffed animal at the fair, except they were moving, and they were looking at her the way Charlie looked at people. The whiskers on the left side of its muzzle had been burned off short and frizzled, and on the top of its head, between the ears, a patch of fur had been singed and stood straight up.

There was a fern stuck in it.

Sue looked at the fern for a while.

"Bend down," she said.

The ears went sideways. Then it bent, a long way, until the top of its head was level with her chin. She reached up and took hold of the fern and pulled it out of the burnt fur. It came free with a few black hairs.

She held it up between them.

"You've had this all night?"

"Since the bog," Leah said behind her.

"Nobody told me," it said.

The voice was low and rough, with a growl running under it, like an engine idling in another room. And an accent. Something she'd heard on the television, on nature programs. The vowels were all in the wrong places.

"Nobody wanted to," said Leah.

The ears went flat.

Sue dropped the fern on the step. "Is that English?"

"South African, ma'am." It straightened up again, slowly, the way a tall man unfolds out of a small car. "Mostly. Grew up in England."

"And you are?"

"Harry, ma'am. Harry Potter."

Sue stood very still on the step.

The rain came down off the edge of the porch roof in a line of drops between them. In the kitchen behind her the potatoes ticked. Somewhere a long way off up the river a wolf was howling, high and furious, and another was answering.

Harry.

Nobody had said that name on this step since the funeral. People said it on the phone, sometimes, carefully. Billy said it, and Old Quil. *Harry would've wanted*, and *Harry always said*. Nobody had stood at the bottom of her back steps in the rain and said it out loud like it was just a name a person could have.

She looked at Leah.

Leah was looking at her. Her jaw was set hard, the way it had been at the funeral. And Sue understood that Leah had known. Leah had known since the first night, wherever that had been, whatever that had been, and had decided not to say anything, and had carried it two thousand kilometers in that box she kept everything in.

"Mr. Potter," Sue said.

That was all she could manage.

The ears went sideways. The green eyes went from her face to Leah's and back. She watched it notice something, and not know what it had noticed, and not ask.

"Ma'am," it said.

"You'll wipe your feet." She looked down. It didn't have feet. It had long black furry things with dark pads on the bottom, like a dog's, only long, like a man's foot, with claws at the ends, and they were caked in mud and moss and something that smelled like a swamp. "All of them."

"He's only got two, Mom," said Leah.

"Then both."

It wiped its feet on the mat. It did it properly, one and then the other, and then the first one again, while she watched. Then it ducked to come through her kitchen door, and didn't duck enough, and the tips of its ears scraped across the top of the frame with a sound like a broom on a ceiling. Its tail came in after it and hit the broom she kept by the door, and the broom fell over with a crack like a gunshot.

"Sorry."

"Mind your tail," said Sue.

"It's got a mind of its own, ma'am."

"Then mind it for it."

She went in after it and shut the door, and stood with her back against it, and looked at her kitchen.

Her kitchen was small. She'd always known that. Harry had built the cabinets himself the year they were married and hung them a little low, because she was five foot three, and the ceiling had always been low, and the fridge had always been too close to the table. The wolf was standing in the middle of it with its ears an inch from the light fixture, and it made her kitchen look like a dollhouse with a dog in it.

It was looking at the stove. At the two pans of hamburger, and the pot of potatoes, and the stack of bread, and the open can of corn. Its nostrils were going.

Then it looked at her.

"Ma'am," it said, "that's a lot of hamburger."

"I've got two wolves," said Sue. "It's not."

---

### III.

The table had four chairs. It had always had four chairs.

Sue's was nearest the stove, so she could get up without anybody having to move. Seth's was across from hers, with the back leg that wobbled because he'd been tipping it on two legs since he was nine. Leah's was by the fridge. The fourth one was at the end, by the window, with its back to the yard. It was the one with arms, the only one with arms, oak, a little darker than the others from thirty years of hands on it.

The wolf put its paw on the back of that one and started to pull it out.

"Not that one," Sue said.

It stopped.

It looked at her. Its ears went up, then sideways. Then it let go of the chair, pushed it back in until it touched the table exactly where it had been, and went around and pulled out Seth's instead.

It didn't ask why.

Sue noticed that. She stood at the stove with her back to them and turned the hamburger back on and noticed it, and didn't know what she thought about it, and turned the potatoes back on too.

Seth's chair creaked when it sat: a long, slow, groaning creak, like a ship in a storm in a movie, and then a small sharp crack from somewhere underneath, and then silence.

"Sorry," it said.

"That's the wobbly one anyway," said Leah.

"It wasn't before," said Sue.

She brought the hamburger to the table in both pans, because there wasn't a platter in the house big enough, and set them down on folded dish towels in the middle. Sixteen patties between them, fried in their own fat, the edges black and crisp. Then the potatoes, mashed in the pot with butter and canned milk, because she'd forgotten to buy milk again, and the corn, and the bread, and a jar of huckleberry jam with a square of masking tape on the lid that said *Huckleberry — Aug* in Emily's round neat letters. Emily had brought it over Thursday when Sue went to tell Sam that Leah was alive. Sue had come home with it in her purse and couldn't remember being handed it.

Plates. Her mother's plates, the white ones with the thin blue rim, because they were the only ones she had enough of. Forks.

She put a plate in front of the wolf. Then she put a fork beside it, and stood there a second, looking at its paw lying on the oilcloth. Long, black, five fingers, or whatever they were, with dark pads on the palm side and long dark curved claws on the ends of them that were pulled most of the way back into the fur. It could have opened a can of corn without a can opener. It could have opened her.

It picked up the fork.

It held it a little wrong, the way Seth had held a fork when he was four, wrapped up in its whole fist. Then it seemed to notice her looking, and shifted its grip. Then it looked up at her.

"Eat," said Sue.

They ate.

---

Sue had fed wolves for two years. She knew what a hungry wolf looked like.

Leah ate like a hungry wolf. Four patties, fast, with her head down, the way she used to eat when she was a little girl and somebody might take it away. Bread. Potatoes. More bread, with Emily's jam, which she spread on thick and ate in three bites, and then she stopped for a second with the knife in her hand, and looked at the masking tape on the lid, and put the knife down, and then picked it up again and spread more.

The wolf ate like something else.

It ate slowly. That was what got her. It ate one patty at a time, with the fork, with its long head bent over her mother's plate, and it chewed every bite, and then it took another one, and another one, steady as a machine at a sawmill. The fork scraped the plate. The claws, when it reached for the bread, clicked on the oilcloth. Its tail had come around the side of Seth's chair and was lying along the floor by her feet, and every now and then the very end of it moved.

Sue sat down with a cup of coffee she didn't want and counted.

Five patties. Six. A pile of potatoes she'd have called enough for a family. Seven. Two slices of bread with jam. Eight.

Somewhere around the sixth, she'd started thinking of it as *he*.

She didn't like that either.

"Seth says you're a policeman," she said.

He finished chewing. He swallowed.

"Sort of, ma'am."

"What sort?"

He looked at Leah.

"She's on the council," Leah said with her mouth full. "She's going to find out anyway. Just tell her."

He put his fork down.

"There's a lot of people in the world who can do things," he said. "Magic, ma'am. Wands. Spells. It runs in families, mostly. There's a lot more of us than you'd think, and we keep to ourselves, and there's laws about that. I work for the lot that goes after the ones who break them."

Sue looked at him across her kitchen table.

"Magic," she said.

"Ja."

"Like Gandalf."

His ears went sideways. "Gandalf's got a better hat."

Leah snorted into her potatoes.

Sue didn't laugh. She'd buried a husband and found out her children could turn into animals the size of horses and spent six weeks lying to a policeman she was falling in love with. She was out of room for laughing at things tonight. She looked at him and waited.

"Wizard," he said. "Ja. Born one. Went to a school for it in Scotland."

"And this?" She tipped her cup at all of him. The ears. The fur. The tail by her feet.

"Got bitten. Three years ago. Romania. Werewolf."

"Werewolf."

"The other kind. The moon kind. Bloke changes once a month whether he likes it or not and doesn't remember a thing after. Most of them would eat you if you stood still long enough." His ears pinned back for a second. "It didn't take on me like it does on them. I stayed me. Nobody knows why."

"There's another kind," said Sue.

"They smell terrible," said Leah.

"They're people," he said, without looking up.

Leah stopped chewing. She looked at him. Something went across her face that Sue couldn't read, and then she looked down at her plate again.

"How old are you?" Sue said.

"Twenty-six."

Sue looked at Leah. Leah looked at her plate.

"Mm," said Sue.

He picked up his fork again, and then put it back down, like he'd thought better of eating while she was looking at him like that.

"And you're here to look at the Cullens," Sue said.

"Ja. And your lot."

"My lot."

"The wolves, ma'am."

"My son."

He went still.

The ears, the nose, the end of that tail, all of it stopped at once.

"Ja," he said. "Your son."

Sue set the coffee cup down. Her hands were steady. She'd made sure of that before she started.

"Seth told me there's somebody in Europe who wants to know about us," she said. "He said Jacob said you'd write it down. Whatever you see. And send it to them."

"There's a lot of somebodies in Europe," he said, "and they've noticed this coast. Seattle in the spring. The wedding. A photo on the internet of a wolf the size of a car, and people arguing about it. A woman in Ottawa who doesn't like me much read the internet and drew a circle round a word." He turned his cup—Seth's cup, the one with the faded Mariners logo—a quarter turn on the oilcloth with one claw. "I go home Wednesday and tell them what's here. If I say it's sorted, they leave it. If I say it's a mess, they send people who'll make it tidy. If I don't go, they send them anyway."

"And these people. What do they do with a boy who turns into a wolf?"

He didn't answer right away. The rain ticked on the window behind the empty chair.

"They write him down," he said. "Name, age, size, what he eats, how fast he runs. They give him a number. There's a form. I've got one. It's got a box on it for my teeth."

Leah's knife stopped halfway to the jam.

"And if they think he's dangerous?" Sue said.

"Then there's more forms."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the true one, ma'am."

Sue looked at him for a long time.

"So you'll write my son down," she said.

"I'll write something. I'm trying to write something that keeps his name off it."

"Trying."

"Ja."

"That's not very much."

"No," he agreed. "It's what I've got."

She hated that. She hated the way he said it, plain, no sugar on it, like a doctor telling you the test came back. She'd sat in a room at the hospital in Port Angeles while a doctor said things to her in that voice about her husband's heart, and she'd hated him too, and he'd been right.

"Then you'll talk to the council," she said, "before you write one word."

"Gladly. Who's the council?"

"Old Quil Ateara. Billy Black. And me."

His left ear flicked.

Sue saw it. It was a small thing. One flick, sharp, like a horse's ear when a fly lands. And at exactly the same moment, under the table, there was a thump, and Seth's chair creaked, and Leah, who hadn't moved at all above the table, was looking at the wall over the stove with a perfectly blank face.

Sue looked from one of them to the other.

Two people at her table had just kicked each other about something she wasn't supposed to see. She didn't know what it was. She'd find out.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Noon. Billy's house. He has the bigger kitchen."

"Noon," he said. "Ta."

"Don't thank me. I'm not doing it for you."

"No, ma'am."

Leah had gone a little pale under her tan. She was still looking at the wall.

---

"There's one more thing," he said, a few minutes later, and Sue knew from the way he said it that she wasn't going to like it. "The police chief."

Sue put her cup down.

"Charlie," she said.

"Ja."

"What about Charlie?"

"His daughter's a vampire, ma'am. Three weeks now. He thinks she's in a clinic somewhere with something nobody can catch, and he hasn't seen her since the wedding, and he's a copper." He said it all in the same flat voice. "Coppers count days. That's the job. Sooner or later he stops waiting for a phone call, and he drives up there with his badge on. Maybe with a warrant. Maybe with a mate from the state police."

"Charlie is mine," said Sue.

"Ja. Your business."

"Yes, he is."

"For now."

Sue sat very still.

"What does that mean," she said. "*For now.*"

"Means if he walks into that house one night and sees something he can't un-see, it stops being your business and starts being mine. Or somebody else's, who's worse at it." His ears had gone back. "It's on my list, ma'am."

She knew. She'd been lying awake knowing for six weeks. She'd heard Charlie on the phone with Carlisle Cullen, the polite tight voice, *any word, doc?* She'd watched him get his jacket off the hook twice, and put it back twice. She didn't need a stranger with fur on his face to sit in her son's chair and set it on the table in front of her like a dead fish.

"I know Charlie Swan," she said. "You don't."

"That's true."

"Then leave Charlie to me."

"For n—"

"*Harry*," said Leah.

It came out of her daughter sharp and fast, the way you'd say a dog's name when it was going for the garbage, and the second it was out, the whole kitchen went quiet.

Leah's face went red. Then white.

Sue sat there with her hands flat on the oilcloth on either side of her coffee cup and didn't move. The name hung in the kitchen. In her kitchen, over the table, by the empty chair with arms, in her daughter's voice, the way Leah used to shout it down the hall when she was small and didn't want to come in for supper. *Harry, she's being a brat. Dad. Daaad.*

The wolf looked at Leah. Then at Sue. Then at the empty chair.

His ears went flat to his skull and stayed there.

"Ja," he said, very quietly. "Okay."

He didn't say *for now* again.

Sue picked up her coffee. It was cold. She drank it anyway.

---

Leah reached across the table for the jam, and the collar of the green top pulled sideways, and her hair moved, and Sue saw the mark.

It was low on the left side of her neck, where the neck went into the shoulder. A curve, like a fingernail moon. A row of small deep dents, even as stitches, and a shorter row facing it, and the skin over every one of them had gone smooth and shiny and silver, a shade paler than the rest of her, like the scar on the back of Sue's own hand from a canning jar that had broken when she was twenty.

Sue had seen her daughter come home with her ribs cracked so badly she couldn't breathe, and sleep, and wake up whole. She'd seen her come in from the fight in June with her face opened up from the ear to the corner of the mouth, blood all down her neck, and seen the same face at breakfast the next morning, clean, not a mark. She'd stood at the bathroom sink at two in the morning and helped her wash dried blood out of her hair, and there'd been nothing under the blood.

Leah didn't scar.

Sue thought of Emily.

She couldn't help it. She thought of her niece in the doorway of the little house Sam had built for her, with her hair combed down over the right side of her face the way she always wore it now, and the three long ragged lines under the hair that went from her forehead down across her eye and pulled down the corner of her mouth. She thought of Sam's face at the hospital, gray as ash. A wolf's claws. A wolf standing too close.

She looked across the table at the wolf.

At his teeth.

"Leah," she said. "What's that on your neck?"

Leah's hand went to it. She didn't cover it. She just touched it, two fingers, and then took them away.

"Nothing."

"It's silver."

"I know what color it is, Mom."

"You don't scar."

"Everybody keeps telling me that."

Sue turned in her chair and looked straight at the wolf.

"Did you do that to my daughter?"

He didn't look away. She'd give him that, later, when she was lying awake going over all of it. He looked her right in the face with those fake green eyes.

"Ja," he said.

"Did you hurt her?"

"Mom," said Leah. "I bit him first."

Sue closed her eyes.

She sat with them closed for a while. The rain on the window. The potatoes, cold in their pot. Somewhere very far off, up the river, the wolves had stopped howling.

"She's got a matching set on me, ma'am," he said carefully. "If it helps."

"It does not help."

"No, ma'am."

She opened her eyes. Leah was looking at her with her chin up, the way she'd looked at the elders the night they sat in Billy's kitchen and talked about her like she was a flood coming. Daring her.

Sue didn't take the dare. There was a worse question under the first one, and she'd been afraid of it since the minute Seth said *bringing him*.

"Is it the other thing," she said. "Did somebody imprint."

"He's not one of us, Mom."

"He's a wolf."

"He's a different kind."

Sue looked at him. "Is she right?"

He was quiet for a second. His tail moved on the floor by her feet.

"I've never imprinted on anybody, ma'am," he said. "Far as I know I can't. Far as I know."

"Far as you know."

"I've known your lot exist since Wednesday. I'm not going to sit here and tell you I'm sure."

Sue didn't say anything. It was the honest answer. She'd have trusted him less if he'd said anything else, and she didn't want to trust him at all.

"And if somebody does," Leah said, in a voice like a rock dropped in a bucket, "imprint on me, or me on some guy at a gas station, and I don't want it—he says he'll take it apart."

The kitchen went very quiet again.

"Take it apart," said Sue.

"He does it. With other stuff. Curses. He says anything that ties onto you can be untied."

Sue looked at the wolf.

"It's sacred," she said. It came out of her before she could think about it. Thirty years of council fires. Old Quil's voice in the smoke. Billy's. Her own husband's, telling the old stories to Seth on the porch in summer. *The greatest gift of the spirit warriors.* "The imprint. It's the most sacred thing we have."

"It's a leash, Mom," said Leah.

Sue opened her mouth.

She'd sat in Billy's kitchen at the first council meeting after the funeral, in Harry's seat, three weeks a widow, while the old men talked about her daughter like a flood coming. And when they'd run out of things to say about Leah, Old Quil had leaned back in his chair and talked for a while about Sam and Emily instead. What a blessing it was. How it showed the spirits still favored the tribe. Sue had nodded. She'd nodded, and all through it she could hear Leah in the bathroom down the hall at home, the night before, crying with the water running so nobody would hear.

She shut her mouth.

"Can you," she said to the wolf.

"Don't know. Never seen one up close." His ears were back. "I'll find out."

"Don't you say that at Billy's table."

"Noted."

"I mean it. Not one word about taking it apart. Old Quil will have a stroke, and Billy will have you off his porch, and they'll both be right."

"Ja, ma'am."

He picked up his fork. He looked at the cast-iron pan. There were two patties left in it.

He looked at Sue.

"Eat it," said Sue. "God's sake."

---

### IV.

Her mother got up to make more coffee, and Leah sat at the table with her face on fire and wanted to crawl under the house.

She'd said it. Out loud. In this kitchen. Two weeks she'd been carrying it around and not saying it, in Atlin and on a hill in Africa and in a bog a quarter mile from the Cullens', and then she'd opened her mouth at her mother's table with Dad's chair sitting right there empty, and it had come out of her like a sneeze.

Across from her, Harry was looking at the chair with arms.

He'd been looking at it since. His ears were still most of the way back. He had a slice of bread in one paw with Emily's jam on it, and he hadn't taken a bite.

At the counter, her mother ran the tap into the coffee pot. The pipes banged under the sink the way they always had. The water was loud.

"That's Dad's chair," Leah said, very low.

"Ja," Harry said, just as low. "Figured."

"His name was Harry."

His ears went flat to his skull.

He didn't say anything for a while. He put the slice of bread down on his plate, carefully, jam side up, and looked at it.

"You might've mentioned," he said. "On the step."

"I didn't want to say it."

"Ja." His tail moved once on the floor. "Fair."

"I didn't want to think about it. So I didn't."

"Since Wednesday?"

"Since you said it in the clearing."

He looked at her. She looked back. Whatever he was going to say, he didn't say it.

Her mother came back with the coffee pot and filled her own cup, and then, after a second, his. She didn't ask if he wanted it. She set the pot down on a folded towel and sat.

"Potter's fine, ma'am," Harry said. "Or *you*. Or *you there*. I've been called worse in nicer kitchens."

Leah kicked him again under the table, not as hard.

Her mother looked at him over her cup.

"I'll call you what I like in my own kitchen," she said.

"Ja, ma'am."

"And this is a nice kitchen."

"It is, ma'am. Didn't mean—"

"I know what you meant."

He shut his mouth and picked up the bread again and ate it. Leah watched her mother watch him do it, and couldn't tell what her mother was thinking, which almost never happened. Sue Clearwater's face was usually a book with very large print. Tonight it was a wall.

Through the kitchen doorway, down the little hall, the closet door was open a crack. The way it always was, because the latch didn't catch. Leah could see the shoulder of a red-and-black buffalo-check flannel on a wire hanger, and behind it the brown one, and the green one with the torn pocket he used to keep his reading glasses in.

She looked away from it, at the fridge. Her note was still on it, under the salmon magnet, curled at the corners like it had been taken down and read a lot of times.

She drank her coffee too fast and burned her tongue.

---

"So," her mother said. "Where were you."

She said it the way she'd said it when Leah was fourteen and came home at midnight from a bonfire on First Beach smelling like Rainier.

"Canada," said Leah.

"Seth said Canada. Canada's big."

"Northern BC. A town called Atlin. On a lake." She turned her cup. "There's a café. And a woman who rents cabins. I stayed in one."

"With what money?"

"The coffee can."

Her mother's mouth went thin. "I noticed."

"I paid you back." Leah looked at her. "Sort of. I got a job."

"A job."

"Washing dishes. At the café. Ray paid me a hundred dollars cash for ten hours." She heard herself say it and heard how it sounded. "And I split the cabin lady's woodpile. Marg. She gave me a night free for it."

"You split a stranger's woodpile."

"She asked."

Her mother put her cup down.

"I asked you to split ours," she said, "for two years."

The kitchen went quiet. Harry had gone very still across the table, the way he went still when he was trying to be furniture.

Leah opened her mouth to say something mean. It was right there. She'd had two years of practice; she could have done it in her sleep. *She asked nicer.* Or, *Seth splits it.* Or, *You never needed me to, you just wanted me to stay home.*

She shut her mouth.

"Yeah," she said instead. "I know."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked down at her coffee.

"Are you going back?" she said. "To your lake."

"Maybe."

"When?"

"Marg says the woodpile needs splitting again by November."

Her mother didn't say anything. She picked up her cup and drank from it, and Leah watched her swallow, and something in her chest twisted hard.

"I'm here now," Leah said.

"I can see that."

"I'm not going anywhere tomorrow."

"Tomorrow you're going to Billy's at noon," her mother said, "with him," and tipped her head at Harry without looking at him, "and I'm going to sit there and watch Old Quil Ateara meet a wizard. So no. You're not."

---

Leah was turning the bracelet on her wrist again. She'd been doing it all through dinner. Her thumb kept finding it, rolling it around and around, black and gray and green.

Her mother's eyes went to it.

"What's that?"

Leah stopped turning it.

"It's—" She didn't know how to say it. "When I phase, my clothes go into it. And when I phase back, they come out again. On me. Like I never took them off."

Her mother looked at the bracelet. Then she looked at Leah. Then she looked across the table at Harry, who was eating the last patty out of the cast-iron pan and trying very hard to look like he wasn't there.

"You made that," her mother said.

He swallowed. "Ja."

"For her."

"Ja."

Her mother looked at the bracelet for a long time.

"Huh," she said.

Then: "I bought eleven pairs of sweatpants last year."

"Mom."

"Eleven, Leah. Seth goes through them like Kleenex." Her mother drank. "Huh," she said again, to the bracelet, and that was all.

Harry was looking at his plate with his ears halfway up. Leah stamped on his footpaw under the table, and the ears went back down.

---

He got up to do the dishes.

Leah saw it coming. He finished his coffee and stood up, and Seth's chair let out a small sigh of relief, and every plate on the table rose an inch into the air at once and started drifting toward the sink in a neat line like ducks.

"Put those down," said her mother.

The plates stopped. They hung in the air over the table. One of them, Leah's, with a smear of jam on it, turned gently, like a leaf.

"Ma'am?"

"Not in my kitchen."

"It's quicker—"

"I don't care what it is. That's my mother's china." Her mother pointed at the plates with a wet dish brush. "You want to do the dishes in my house, you'll do them with your—"

She stopped. She was looking at his paws.

"—with whatever those are," she finished.

"Paws, ma'am."

"Then with your paws."

The plates came down. All of them, slowly, back onto the oilcloth, in the places they'd been. Not a clink. He picked them up himself, two at a time, and carried them to the sink.

The sink was too low for him. Everything in this kitchen was too low for him. Her dad had hung the cabinets for her mom. He stood at the sink with his shoulders hunched and his head bent and his ears an inch from the light fixture over the window, and when he reached for the dish soap, his left ear touched the glass shade and set the whole light swinging. Shadows went back and forth across the kitchen like a boat in a swell.

"Mind the light," said her mother.

"Minding it."

His tail swung out behind him to balance the hunch, and caught the broom by the back door, which had been stood back up, and knocked it over again.

"*Mind*—"

"Sorry."

Leah got up and picked up the dish towel off the stove handle, the one with the faded salmon on it, and went and stood beside him at the counter.

He handed her a plate. She dried it.

It was stupid how much she liked it. The kitchen smelled like hamburger grease and coffee and dish soap and him. The window over the sink was black and full of rain, and in it she could see the two of them reflected, a girl in a green top with a salmon towel and a seven-foot wolf with his sleeves shoved up past the elbow and soapsuds in the fur of both forearms, standing hip to hip at her mother's sink. Behind them, at the table, her mother sat with her cup and watched.

Leah could see her mother's face in the window, too.

She didn't know what was on it. She looked at it for a long time, in the black glass, and couldn't read it, and then her mother saw her looking and looked away at the fridge.

Harry handed her a plate. His claws clicked on the china, very lightly. He was being careful. She could feel how careful he was being, the way she'd felt it last night, holding all that strength in his paws and putting it down on her mother's plates one eggshell at a time.

She dried the plate. He handed her another one.

Halfway through the forks, he stopped.

His paws went still in the water. Both ears swung around toward the front of the house, away from the window, toward the road. They locked there. His nostrils went once, long and slow.

Then he picked up the next fork and kept washing.

"Company," he said.

Leah didn't smell it yet. She stood there with the towel and a wet fork and waited for it, and it came. Faint through the front of the house, through the gap under the front door, on the wet air coming in off the road.

Wet black fur. Cedar sawdust. Flour and cinnamon, from a kitchen a mile away that she'd stood outside of a hundred times without going in.

And behind it, in the trees across the road, two more. Green-apple shampoo, and something like old sneakers.

Her stomach dropped through the floor the way it always did. Two years, and it still always did.

"Sam," she said.

Her mother put her coffee cup down on the oilcloth.

"At one in the morning," said Sue Clearwater.

---

### V.

Sam Uley walked to the Clearwater house on two legs because you didn't go to Sue Clearwater's door at one in the morning on four.

He told himself that was the reason. The other reason was that he'd come out of his own back door shaking so hard he'd nearly phased on the step, and he'd needed the whole mile of wet road in the dark, barefoot, in cut-offs, with the rain running down his back, to get his hands to stop.

They hadn't stopped. They'd slowed. He kept them in fists.

Paul's howl had come at twenty to twelve. Sam had been out of bed and out the back and on four legs in the wet grass before he was fully awake, with Emily still asleep behind him, and Paul's head had hit his like a car through a wall. *SAM. She's back, she's RIDING it, LOOK AT THE SIZE OF IT—*

A black wolf on the gravel bar along the Quillayute. Bigger than Jacob, who'd been the biggest thing Sam had ever seen on four legs until that second. Long as a truck, moving fast, making no sound at all; Paul had been two hundred meters off with the wind coming to him and hadn't heard a stone turn over. And on its back, with her fists in its ruff, Leah, taking one hand out of the fur to wave.

Paul had replayed the wave five times. Sam had told him to stop four of them.

He'd sent Paul and Jared ahead to the trees across the road from the Clearwater house, told them to stay wolf and stay back, and phased out on his own lawn and stood in the rain until his body quit burning. Then he'd pulled on his cut-offs and started walking.

He knew the rest already. Everybody did. Sue had sat at Emily's kitchen table on Thursday afternoon, gray in the face, and told them Leah was alive. Seth had told Embry the rest at lunch, and by Friday the whole reservation had some version of it. A wolf that stood up. A wolf in a coat. A wolf that killed a man with a stick and then asked Leah out over the body.

He didn't hear Leah anymore.

Not since she'd followed Seth and Jacob out of his pack and into Jacob's, and the whole sour, bitter, furious weight of her had gone out of his head all at once like a stone lifted off his chest. He'd been ashamed of how good it felt. He'd been ashamed of how much he missed it, too, some nights, on patrol, in the quiet where she used to be. Both at once. He'd been carrying both ever since and didn't know where to put either one.

He could smell the house now.

He came around the bend in the road where the old Lisle place had burned down, and the wind came at him off the yard, and he stopped walking.

Hamburger. Onions. Coffee. Sue's lavender soap, which Emily had in her bathroom too, because they bought it together in Port Angeles. Leah. Leah all over the yard, fresh, rain-and-cedar, so strong after all these months that it went into him like a fist.

And the other thing.

Pine. Wet stone. Cold water running over granite. Wolf, big, a different wolf from any of them, from any wolf he'd ever smelled. It was everywhere Leah was. It was laid over her trail across the back lawn like a coat thrown over a chair.

And under it, faint, something he couldn't put a name to. Something that made him think of the inside of Billy Black's house on a winter morning, the stove going.

He frowned. He stood in the road and breathed it in again, and it was gone under the pine.

In the trees across the road, two shapes shifted in the dark. Paul's nose. Jared's. Their eyes caught the porch light.

The porch light was on. So was the kitchen light, at the back. He could hear water running in the sink, and dishes. He could hear somebody humming, low, under the water. A man's voice.

Sam went up the steps.

---

Sue opened the door before he knocked.

She stood in the doorway in her apron with a dish towel over one shoulder, a small woman, the top of her head not much higher than his chest, and she looked up at him the way she'd looked at him for two years: like she was deciding all over again, every time, whether she was going to forgive him. He'd never once seen her finish deciding.

"Sam," she said.

"Sue. I need to—is she—"

"It's one in the morning."

"Paul saw—"

"I know what Paul saw. I heard him. The whole river heard him."

She didn't step aside. Past her, down the short hall, through the kitchen doorway, he could see the kitchen. He'd eaten at that table a hundred times. He'd sat in Harry Clearwater's chair once, at Thanksgiving, the year Leah was seventeen, when Harry was in the hospital with his first scare, and Leah had kicked him under the table for it and then held his hand under the table for the rest of the meal.

There was a wolf at Sue's sink.

It was standing up. It had its back half to him and its head bent under the low light over the window, and the tips of its ears were brushing the glass shade so that the light rocked a little, back and forth, and the shadows in the kitchen swung with it. Black. Black everywhere, darker than his own wolf, so dark the kitchen light didn't get into it. Its sleeves were pushed up past the elbows and the fur on both its forearms was wet and full of soapsuds. A long black tail hung down behind it and curled once around its own ankle, if that was an ankle.

It was scrubbing out Sue's potato pot.

Beside it, at the counter, with a dish towel in one hand and a wet plate in the other, was Leah.

She turned her head and looked at him down the hall.

Sam knew every look Leah Clearwater had. He'd spent four years learning them before he phased and a year after it living inside every one of them from the other side. He knew the one she'd had for him since Emily: hard and flat and furious, a door slammed so many times the hinges were coming off.

She gave him a new one.

She looked at him the way you look at the mailman.

Then the wolf turned its head too, and looked at him, and its ears came up and swung forward, both of them, toward the door. Its nostrils moved. Its eyes were green. Its tail uncurled from around its ankle and swung once, slowly, side to side, and went still.

And Sam smelled it.

All of it, all at once, from the doorway. The stranger on Leah, on her clothes and in her hair and her skin, soaked in so deep that her own smell came up through it like something heard through a wall. He'd smelled that on a person exactly once before. On Emily, the morning after the first night. On himself.

And on the left side of Leah's neck, low, where her neck went down into her shoulder, there was a mark.

A curve of small dents in a crescent, healed over. Silver in the kitchen light.

Leah didn't scar. Leah had never in her life scarred. He'd seen her through Paul's eyes in June with her face opened to the bone, and seen her the next morning at the beach with not a line on her.

The shaking came back into his hands so fast he had to put one of them on Sue's doorframe.

He saw Emily's face.

He always saw Emily's face. Every time. He couldn't stop it. Emily turning toward him in her mother's kitchen, laughing, too close, and the heat going up through him like a match dropped in gasoline, and his hand, and then not his hand.

And now this thing. In Sue's kitchen. With Leah standing right beside it. With its teeth.

"Leah," he said. His voice came out wrong. "Are you all right?"

Leah put the plate in the rack.

"I'm drying dishes, Sam."

He looked past her at the wolf.

"You," he said. "Outside."

The wolf rinsed the pot and set it upside down on the drainboard.

"Just finishing the pots, boet."

The voice was low and rough, and there was something under it like an engine in another room, and an accent he didn't know. *Boet.* He didn't know what that meant either. He knew how it was meant.

"If there's going to be shouting," said Sue, "it's going to be on the porch. Both of you. Out of my kitchen."

The wolf looked at Sue. Then it pulled the plug in the sink and dried its paws on the salmon towel Leah handed it, front and back, carefully, like a surgeon, and gave the towel back. Then it reached over to the table and picked up a slice of bread off the plate there. There was jam on the bread, thick and purple. It picked up the jam jar, too, and looked at it, and set it down again, and Sam saw the strip of masking tape on the lid.

*Huckleberry — Aug.* In Emily's handwriting. Round, neat. He'd watched her write it on her kitchen counter in August with her tongue between her teeth.

The wolf came down the hall toward him eating it.

It had to duck to get out of the kitchen. Its ears scraped the top of the doorframe anyway. It came down Sue's narrow hall, filling it wall to wall, and Sam stood in the front door with every hair on his body standing up, and every part of him that was a wolf was telling him to get out of the way of the bigger thing.

He didn't get out of the way.

The wolf stopped a foot from him. It was taller than Jacob. It was taller than Sam by a head, more with the ears. It smelled like pine and stone and cold water and Leah. It looked down at him, chewing.

Then Sue stepped back from the doorway, and the wolf ducked under the frame, turned its shoulders to get past him, and went out onto the porch.

Its tail brushed Sam's leg on the way by. Sam was almost sure that was on purpose.

He turned around.

The wolf had gone to the far end of the porch, where the swing hung on its chains, the swing Harry Clearwater had hung the year Seth was born. It leaned on the rail beside it with its elbows on the wet wood and the bread in one paw, and took another bite, and looked out at the rain on the road and the dark trees across it, where Paul and Jared were.

"Evening, lads," it said, to the trees.

The trees didn't answer.

The wolf's tail swung once, slowly, and went still. It chewed. It looked about as worried as a man waiting for a bus.

---

### VI.

Leah came out onto the porch with the salmon towel still in her hand because she'd forgotten to put it down.

The porch was small. It had always been small. Two steps down to the walk, a rail on three sides, the green paint coming off it in curls. The swing at the far end on its chains, the one her dad had hung the year Seth was born. Rain dripping off the edge of the roof in a line.

Sam was at the top of the steps with his back to the yard. He was bare to the waist and soaked, his short black hair plastered to his skull, his feet bare and black with road mud. His hands were fists. She could see them going, very slightly, the way an engine goes at idle.

Harry was at the other end, by the swing, leaning on the rail on his elbows, eating Emily's jam on white bread.

Behind her, the screen door creaked, and stopped, and didn't close. Her mother was standing just inside it. Leah didn't have to look. She could smell the coffee and the lavender soap.

Across the road, in the trees, Paul and Jared were trying to be quiet, and failing, the way they always failed. She could hear Paul breathing through his mouth.

"You're on Quileute land," Sam said.

"Ja." Harry swallowed. "Lovely, too. Smells like the sea."

"Who said you could be here?"

"I did," said Leah.

"And I did," said her mother, through the screen.

Sam's head turned. "Sue—"

"My porch, Sam."

Sam looked at the screen door for a long second. Then he looked back at Harry. The muscle in his jaw was going.

"Paul saw you," he said. "Crossing the Quillayute. With her on your back."

"She wouldn't let me Apparate."

"I don't know what that means."

"It means he squeezes you through a garden hose," said Leah. "It's disgusting. I'd rather ride."

Sam didn't look at her. He'd been not looking at her since he came out. She could see him doing it, the way she could see a man not looking at a car crash.

"Seth says you're police," Sam said.

"Seth talks a lot."

"Seth told the whole school."

"Ja, I'd guessed." Harry took another bite. "Sort of police. International. There's people a long way from here who've noticed your coast. I'm here to have a look at the Cullens, and at you lot, and tell them what I see."

"You're here to write us down."

"I'm here so the next bloke doesn't have to." He licked a smear of jam off one claw, slowly, very thoroughly, and didn't look at Sam while he did it. "Your mate over there in the trees. The gray one."

In the trees, Paul went dead silent.

"He's on the internet," Harry said. "Photo of him in a clearing off the 101, standing in the sun like he's posing for a calendar. Four hundred comments. Half of them reckon he's Bigfoot's dog." His ears tipped toward the trees. "Stay off the 101, boet."

A low, offended huff came out of the dark.

"And the one on the left can sneeze," Harry added. "He's been holding it since I came out. It's making me nervous."

There was a pause.

Then, from the trees, an enormous sneeze. Something crashed in the brush. Paul made a noise that Leah, who had lived in Paul's head for a year, knew was a laugh.

Sam's fists tightened.

"Leah," he said. "Go inside."

The voice came out of him the way it always had. Deep and flat and heavy, the Alpha voice, the one that went into your bones and found your legs before your brain could get there. The first time he'd used it on her, two weeks after she phased, she'd been standing on First Beach screaming at him, and it had turned her around and walked her up the sand like a puppet with a string through her spine, and she'd cried the whole way, and he'd heard every second of it.

It went over her like wind over a rock.

Nothing pulled. Her legs stayed exactly where she'd put them, and the voice went past her and out across the yard into the rain and vanished.

She watched Sam realize it. She watched it go across his face. He'd forgotten. For one second he'd forgotten she wasn't his anymore, and reached for her the old way, and his hand had closed on nothing.

"You don't get to do that anymore," Leah said.

"I didn't—" He stopped. "Habit."

"Break it."

At the far end of the porch, Harry had stopped chewing. His ears had gone straight up.

"That the voice?" he said. "The Alpha thing?"

"Yeah."

"Huh." He started chewing again. "Bit loud."

---

Sam turned and looked at her.

Really looked, for the first time since he'd come up the steps. She watched his eyes go over her face, and down, to the left side of her neck, and stop there.

She didn't cover it.

"What did he do to you?" Sam said.

"None of your business."

"You don't scar."

"Is everybody in this town going to tell me that?"

"Leah—"

"I know I don't scar, Sam. I was there. It's my neck."

He looked past her at Harry.

"You marked her," he said. His voice had gone very low. "You put your teeth in her. Like an animal."

Harry finished the bread except for the crust. He looked at the crust. Then he put it down on the rail, on the wet green paint, very neatly, like he was saving it for later, and straightened up off his elbows.

"She did me first, boet," he said.

He hooked one claw into the high collar of the gray coat and pulled it open, and then the collar of the black shirt under it, and turned his left shoulder toward the porch light.

The old scar was there, the long crooked part in the fur where the other werewolf had bitten him. And around it, cutting across it, a ring of fresh marks in the black fur, the hair coming back in short dark bristles so the shape stood out on him like a brand. The spacing of her teeth. Top and bottom. She'd done that about ten hours ago, in Africa, on purpose, and she'd liked it.

"Want a closer look?" Harry said. "She's got a lovely bite."

"*Harry*," said Leah.

In the trees, Paul made a noise like a whoopee cushion. Something growled at him, low. Jared.

Sam was staring at the marks.

She watched Sam's face do the thing it did right before it stopped being a face she could talk to: the jaw going tight and then tighter, the vein coming up at his temple, the eyes going flat and dark and a long way back.

"You've known her four days," Sam said.

"Ja."

"Four *days*. And you—"

"Boet." Harry let go of his collar. The coat fell closed. He picked the crust back up off the rail and pointed it at Sam, and leaned on the rail again, one elbow, easy, the tail swinging slow behind him. "Let me see if I've got this right."

Leah thought: *Oh, no.*

"You've walked a mile," Harry said, "barefoot, in the rain, at one in the morning, to stand on her mum's porch and shake at me. Because she's finally getting laid."

Behind the screen door, her mother made a sound like a kettle that had been taken off the stove too late.

"Sorry, ma'am," Harry said, without turning around.

"I—" said Sam.

"You had her," Harry said. "Years, she says. The only woman who's ever been one of you. Fastest of the whole lot. And she was yours, and she wanted to be." He took a bite of the crust. Chewed. "If you'd been a proper wolf—a functioning one, nose switched on, nothing crossed in the wiring—you'd have known what you had. You'd never have looked at anybody else long enough to fall over. But something in you went off like a car alarm, and you followed it off a cliff."

"You don't know anything about it," Sam said. His voice had gone thick.

"I know what she told me. And I know what I smelled from thirty k's off, with a lake in the way." Harry shrugged, a lot of shoulder moving under the gray wool. "And look. You'll get no complaints from me, boet. None. Honest. I'd buy you a drink. You left the best thing on this whole coast lying on the beach and walked off, and I'm the bloke who tripped over it."

He swallowed. His ears tipped forward.

"Only thing I wonder," he said, and he looked off up the road the way Sam had come, into the dark, toward a little house with a new roof that he had never seen and couldn't have pointed to, "is whether a regular old human was really worth the trade."

The porch went silent.

Leah felt it go into her like a hot coal. Something low and nasty and old jumped up under her ribs and bared its teeth and said *yes*. Two years. Two years of it. Two years of Emily in the doorway with her hair combed down over her face, and Sam's hand on the small of Emily's back at every bonfire, and everybody saying *blessing*, and *the spirits*, and *you'll find your own, Leah, be patient*. Two years and not one person had said the thing out loud. The thing she thought every night on patrol, in Sam's head, where he couldn't help hearing it, and hated her for it, and she hated him for hating her.

*Was it worth it?*

Sam had gone rigid from his neck to his heels.

And then Harry leaned forward off the rail.

A little way, toward Sam, the way you'd lean toward a guy at a bar to tell him a secret. He lowered his voice to a whisper. It was the loudest whisper Leah had ever heard in her life. It would have carried across a gym. It carried across the yard. It carried across the road into the trees.

"Because between you and me, boet," he whispered.

A pause.

"The sex is *fucking* great."

Another pause. His ears went all the way up.

"Especially the rough stuff."

And then, from down in his chest, low and slow and going up and down like a man who'd taken a bite of something and wanted everybody in the room to know how good it was:

"*Mm-hm-hm-hmmm.*"

---

Nobody said anything.

In the trees across the road, something fell over. There was a strangled yelp, cut off. It sounded a lot like Jared had sat on Paul's head.

Behind the screen door, very quietly, her mother said: "Mr. Potter."

It was the voice she'd used on Leah exactly once, when Leah was eleven and had told a teacher at the tribal school to go to hell. A voice like a freezer door.

Leah couldn't feel her face.

It had gone so hot it had gone right past hot into nothing. She stood there on her mother's porch with a dish towel in her hand and her whole head roaring and wanted to die. She wanted to die. She wanted to kill him first and then die. She wanted to go down the steps and keep walking into the ocean.

The rough stuff.

*There hadn't been any rough stuff.* There'd been the bite. The rough stuff was *later*. He'd said later. In the Cullens' living room. In front of *Emmett*. It hadn't even *happened* yet and he was telling *Sam Uley* about it on her *mother's porch*—

Under her ribs, her wolf had flopped over onto its back with all four legs in the air and its tongue out the side of its mouth, and it was laughing so hard it couldn't breathe.

And under that, under all of it, in the place where the hot coal had gone in, the nasty thing was still standing there with its teeth bared. And it had stopped saying *yes*.

Because Emily.

Emily, who'd brought the jam. Emily, who'd sat on the edge of the tub with Leah the week she first phased and held a cold washcloth on the back of her neck while Leah threw up, and hadn't said a word, and hadn't left. Emily, who'd been on the other end of Sam's imprint with no more say in it than Leah had, and who woke up every morning and combed her hair down over three long scars that were never going to go away.

*A regular old human.*

Leah looked at Sam.

Sam was shaking.

It had started in his shoulders. A tremor, very fine, coming down his arms from the shoulders to the fists, and the fists starting to blur at the edges, and the heat coming off him in a wave she could feel on her face from two feet away. She could smell it. Wet black fur, coming up out of his skin.

She was two feet away. She'd come down the porch toward the steps without noticing, toward Harry, to shut him up, and now she was standing right at Sam's elbow.

She knew what happened next. Every time Sam thought about it, she'd seen it from the inside: Emily turning toward him in her mother's kitchen, laughing, too close, and the heat going up through him, and his hand, and then not his hand. She'd lived inside that memory a hundred times. She should move. Her body knew how to get out of the way of a phase. Every wolf on the reservation knew. *Step back. Step back now.*

She didn't move. She was looking at the scars on Emily's face, the way they'd looked in her head every time Sam remembered, and her feet had gone to stone.

Then Harry was there.

She didn't see him cross. One second he was at the rail at the far end of the porch with a crust of bread in his paw, and the next he was between her and Sam. Right there, in the eighteen inches between them, his back to her and his chest a hand's width from Sam's shaking fists, so close that if Sam's arms went, they'd go into the gray wool first. The porch boards hadn't creaked. The crust was sitting on the rail where he'd been.

He kept his paws at his sides. He stood there, a wall of gray coat and black fur, with his tail curled tight against the backs of his legs.

"Not with her stood right here, boet," he said.

His voice had gone quiet. All the cheek had gone out of it.

"You want to change, step off the porch first."

Sam's face changed.

Leah saw it over Harry's shoulder, and she knew what Sam was seeing, because she'd seen it too, a hundred times, from inside his head. Emily. Emily's mother's kitchen. *Too close.*

Harry didn't know that. Harry couldn't know that. Nobody had ever told him. He'd just looked at a wolf about to blow two feet from her and moved.

Sam knew. Sam knew exactly.

The shaking went down by inches. Sam's eyes went from Harry to Leah, and stayed on her, and something went across them that she hadn't seen since the night he came back from the woods different and couldn't tell her why. Then he took a step back. Down. Off the top step. Then another, down onto the walk. Then another, onto the wet grass of the yard, in the rain, where he stood with his head down and his fists at his sides and his whole back heaving, breathing like a man who'd come up from the bottom of a lake.

The heat went out of the air.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

---

Harry turned around.

He looked down at her. His ears were halfway back. He opened his mouth.

"Leave Emily out of it," said Leah.

His ears flicked. "Who—"

"The regular old human." It came out of her low and hard. She was shaking now too, a little. "Her name is Emily. She's my cousin. She makes the jam you've been eating all night."

Harry looked over at the rail. At the crust of bread on the wet green paint, with a smear of purple left on it.

His ears went all the way flat.

"Ah," he said.

"Yeah."

He was quiet for a second. Then: "Ja. That was cheap."

He turned his head and looked down the steps at Sam, standing in the grass in the rain.

"Your Emily's not part of this, boet," he said. "That bit was out of line. Sorry." His tail moved once against the back of his legs. "The rest I meant."

Sam lifted his head.

"Don't," he said. His voice was wrecked. "Don't say her name."

"Fair," said Harry.

Sam looked past him. At Leah. He looked at her for a long time, standing in the rain in the porch light with the water running off his chin.

"Is this what you want?" he said.

Leah stood very still.

Two hours ago Jacob had stood at the edge of the Cullens' lawn and asked her if she was happy. Now Sam. Two years nobody had asked her a single thing, and now everybody was asking, one after another, in the rain, like there'd been a meeting about it.

"Yeah," she said. "It is."

Sam's face did something she would think about later, a lot, lying awake. Then it didn't do anything at all.

"Go home, Sam," she said. "Emily's by herself."

The screen door banged open behind her.

Her mother came out onto the porch in her apron with her arms crossed, small and gray-braided and furious, and stood at the top of the steps next to Harry without looking at him once.

"Council's tomorrow," she said to Sam. "Noon. Billy's house. You'll come. You'll say whatever you want to say there, in front of Billy and Old Quil and me, like a grown man, with a shirt on."

"Sue." Sam's voice was rough. "He's—you don't know what he—"

"I know what he is," said her mother. "I fed him."

Sam looked at her. Then he looked at Harry, one more time, a long, flat, black look, and Harry looked back at him with his ears halfway up, and neither of them said anything.

"This isn't done," Sam said.

"Didn't think it was," said Harry.

Sam turned and walked out of the porch light, across the yard, down to the road, and into the dark. He didn't phase. Leah listened to him the whole way, his bare feet on the wet asphalt, until they turned off at the bend by the old Lisle place and were gone.

In the trees across the road, two big shapes rose up and drifted back into the dark after him. One of them stopped at the edge of the trees and looked back. Silver-gray, ears up, eyes catching the porch light.

Harry lifted a paw and waved at it.

Paul huffed, loud and offended, and went.

Harry turned to the rail, picked up the crust of bread with Emily's jam on it, looked at it for a second, and ate it.

"*You*," said her mother. "Inside."

---

### VII.

He came in ducking. Sue stood in her front hall with her arms crossed and watched the tips of his ears scrape her doorframe again, and his tail come in after him and brush the coat hooks so that every jacket on them swung.

Leah came in behind him and shut the door and leaned on it.

"In my house," said Sue. "On my porch. In front of Sam Uley and half his pack. About my daughter."

"Ja," he said.

"Do you have any idea—"

"Mom," said Leah.

"I'm talking to him."

His ears were most of the way back. He stood in her hall and let her look at him and didn't say anything clever, which was the first sensible thing he'd done since he came out of the trees.

"It was rude, ma'am," he said. "The bit about Emily was worse."

"The bit about Emily," said Sue, "is the only part Sam is going to remember. You understand that? He'll lie awake with it. And tomorrow at noon he'll sit at Billy's table across from you with it, and so will I." She looked at him. "And so will Emily, by breakfast. Paul can't keep his mouth shut either."

His ears went flat.

"And you'll never say that word in my house again," she said. "Or on my porch. Or in my yard."

"No, ma'am."

"It's Mrs. Clearwater."

He went still for half a second. Then: "Mrs. Clearwater."

He said it carefully. His accent did something strange to it, flattened it and pulled it long. *Clea-wa-tah.* She'd been Mrs. Clearwater for twenty-three years, and she'd never heard it said like that.

"You're on the couch," she said.

"*Mom*—"

"He's on the couch, Leah. You're in your room. This is my house."

"I'm nineteen."

"And it's my couch." Sue didn't look at her daughter. She was looking at him. "He's twenty-six. He's old enough to sleep on a couch."

"Couch is lekker, Mrs. Clearwater," he said.

"I don't know what that means, and I don't want to."

She went to the hall closet.

The door was open a crack, the way it always was, and she pulled it the rest of the way, and there they were on their wire hangers, the way they'd been for two years. The red-and-black buffalo check. The brown one. The green one with the torn pocket where he'd kept his reading glasses, which she'd never mended, because then it would be mended. She put her hand flat on the red one for a second. She didn't take it down. She reached past it to the shelf and pulled down the big quilt, the star quilt her mother had made, and the spare pillow, and shut the door with her hip.

She put them in his arms without a word.

The couch was six feet long. She'd known that when she said it. He stood there with her mother's quilt in his arms and looked at the couch, and then down at himself, and then at the couch again, and his ears went sideways.

He didn't say anything.

Leah was standing at the bottom of the stairs with her hand on the post. She looked at him. He looked at her. Something went back and forth between them that Sue was not in on and did not want to be in on.

"Night," Leah said.

"Night, Clearwater."

Sue stood in her hall and heard her husband's name come out of that long black muzzle like it belonged to somebody, and her daughter went up the stairs without looking back, and the fourth stair creaked under her the way it had creaked under Leah since she was three.

---

She went into the kitchen and turned off all the lights but the one over the stove.

The cast-iron pan sat in the drying rack, scrubbed. The pots were done. The forks were standing handle-down in the rack, every one of them, in two neat rows. He'd done the forks the way she did the forks. She looked at that for a while.

The note was on the fridge.

She took it down. She'd never once taken it down in two weeks; she'd lifted the magnet and read it and put it back. Now she took it down, and folded it in half, and in half again, and put it in her apron pocket, and put the salmon magnet back on the fridge with nothing under it.

Then she looked at the grocery list.

*Milk. Eggs (3 doz). Bread (4). Hamburger (lots).* And at the bottom, *coffee*, crossed out, because Charlie had brought her two cans last week without being asked.

She picked up the pen off the counter. She drew a line through *Hamburger (lots)*.

Under it she wrote *Hamburger (more)*.

She stood and looked at it.

Then she sat down at the table, in her own chair, by the stove, with her cold coffee, in the light from the one bulb, and looked at the chair with arms at the end of the table, and didn't think about anything at all for a while.

"Mrs. Clearwater."

It came from the living room, low, out of the dark. She'd forgotten how close the couch was. She'd forgotten how small her house was, before tonight.

She didn't answer.

"I'll go," he said. "If you want. There's a motel in Forks. Or a bog."

"Leah would follow you to the bog."

"Ja. Probably."

Sue turned her cup around on the oilcloth.

"I don't like you," she said.

"That's fair."

"You say that a lot."

"It usually is."

She could see him from where she sat, if she leaned. He was on the couch with his back against one arm of it and her mother's star quilt over his knees, and his legs went right off the end of it and over the other arm, so his long black footpaws hung down in the air like a kid's in a car seat. His tail was on the floor. His ears were up, both of them, pointed at the kitchen, at her. His eyes caught the stove light and threw it back green.

"You're rude," Sue said. "You're too old for her. You're here to write my son down on a piece of paper for people I've never met and never will. You put your teeth in my daughter's neck, and you told Sam Uley about it on my porch."

He didn't say anything.

"Seth said she laughed," said Sue. "On Wednesday. At a bar. He said she had to hold onto a pool table."

"She did."

"I haven't heard her laugh in two years. Not once." Sue looked at her coffee. "I tried. Her brother tried. Emily tried, God help her. Jacob, I think. I don't know." She turned the cup. "Four days, you've known her."

He didn't say anything to that either.

"I don't like that it was you," Sue said.

There was a long quiet from the living room.

"No, Mrs. Clearwater," he said. "I'd not like it either."

She drank her cold coffee.

"Noon," she said. "Billy's. You'll wear a shirt with sleeves."

"I've got a shirt on."

"*With sleeves.*"

A pause.

"Ja," he said. "Okay."

She got up and rinsed her cup and put it in the rack with the others, and turned off the light over the stove, and stood a second in the dark kitchen with her hand in her apron pocket on the folded note.

On the way to the stairs she didn't look at the couch. She felt him watch her go anyway, the way you feel a dog watch you from its bed.

At the bottom of the stairs she stopped.

"When Billy says his own name tomorrow," she said, without turning around, "I'll be looking at your ears."

From the dark living room, nothing. Not a word. Not a creak from the couch.

Sue went up.

---

At twenty to three her daughter's bedroom door opened.

Sue was lying on her back in the dark with her eyes open. She'd been lying like that since she came up. She heard Leah's door, very slow, the hinge that needed oil. She heard one bare foot on the hall floor. Then the other.

"Bed, Leah," said Sue.

The feet stopped.

A long silence.

"I was getting water," said her daughter, from the hall.

"There's a bathroom up here."

Another long silence.

Then the door, slower going back than it had been coming out, and the click of the latch.

From downstairs, from the living room, very low, so low that a person who wasn't listening for it would have thought it was the furnace kicking on, came a sound. A rumble. It went on for a while, and stopped, and started up again, and it took Sue a second to understand that it was laughing.

"I can hear you too," said Sue, to the ceiling.

The rumbling stopped.

Far off up the Quillayute, one wolf howled, once, long and low, and nobody answered it. Downstairs, the couch creaked, and creaked again, as something too big for it tried to find a way to lie down.

Tomorrow at noon she'd sit at Billy Black's kitchen table with the old stove going and the coffee on, and Old Quil would come in with his cane, and Sam would come in with his face shut, and she'd watch a wizard meet her council. She'd watch Billy look at him. She'd watch his ears.

She didn't know what she'd see. She didn't like not knowing.

She lay there, and since she wasn't going to sleep, she did the math.

Sixteen patties tonight, and he'd had ten of them. Two loaves of bread. Most of a jar of Emily's jam. Seth ate four pounds a day on a slow day, and Leah ate three, and now there was this, sleeping on her couch with its feet hanging off the end, and no sign of how long it meant to stay.

The Thriftway in Forks opened at seven. Hamburger was two forty-nine a pound if she bought the big pack.

Sue Clearwater lay awake in the dark doing math about hamburger until the window went gray.
