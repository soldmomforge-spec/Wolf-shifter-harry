# Need to Know

## Chapter Two: Liaison

---

## Part One: The Wall

---

### I. Leah

*The North Fork of the Calawah — Monday, November 20th, 6:41 p.m.*

*Ask him yourself* went out of her like a stone dropped off a bridge, and for about three seconds afterward nobody in her head said anything at all.

Then they both did.

*LEAH.*

*Lee, what does that even mean? We can't ask him! We can't even remember where he—*

*What did he put in your head? Leah. Answer me. What is that thing? Is it hurting you? Is he doing something to you right now?*

*No,* Leah thought, *and no, and no,* and ran.

She went down off the bench the way she'd come up, through the big spruces, the ones as wide as houses, with the river shouting in the gorge on her left. The fog was still hanging between the trunks. She felt it as she went into it, the soft heavy pressure of it, *you've left the stove on, you've got somewhere else to be*, and she felt it open for her. It parted like a curtain. It let her out the same way it had let her in, without a word, the way a screen door lets out the dog that lives there, and it closed behind her, and she was out in the ordinary dark with the ordinary rain coming down through the hemlocks and twenty miles of forest between her and home.

She didn't slow down to think about that. She'd think about that later. There was a lot she was going to think about later.

*Leah,* Jacob thought. Quieter now. Careful. The way you'd talk to somebody standing on a ledge. *Just tell me you're okay.*

*I'm okay.*

*Physically okay.*

*Jake, I'm running twenty miles through the woods in the dark in the rain. Does it feel like I'm not physically okay?*

He went quiet. She could feel him checking. They both were, him and Seth, going over her the way you'd go over a car somebody had borrowed and brought back. Her legs. Her lungs. Her split claws from the ridge, which had stopped hurting hours ago. The ache in her shoulders where the weight had pressed her flat on the granite. Everything was where it was supposed to be.

Then they got to the wall again, and stopped.

She felt them stop. It was like being in a house with two people walking through it, opening doors, and hearing them both come to the same door at the same time and try the handle. She knew exactly where it was. She'd been able to feel it since the bench, since the second it closed: a place in the middle of her head that wasn't hers, smooth and grey and cold as a stone pulled out of the bottom of a river. It had a weight. It had a temperature. When she put her attention against it, the way you'd put your palm flat on a window in January, the cold came back through.

She knew what was behind it. That was the strange part. That was the part that had made her think *huh* on the bench and nothing else. Everything he'd told her in the kitchen was still there, and everything she'd watched in the round room. She could go and look at any of it she wanted, privately, by herself: the jars, the red-eyed thing on the floor, the little grey phone, the old man's voice on the line saying *my dear old friend*. The name of the place where he worked. What he'd come here for. It was all right where she'd left it. None of it had gone fuzzy, and none of it hurt.

She just couldn't hand it to anybody. The second she tried, and she'd tried once, right after the bench, before she understood what she was trying, it closed. Gently. Like a hand on the back of her neck. *No.*

*It's like fog,* Seth was thinking. *Lee, it's like a fog, there's a whole part of your afternoon that's just fog. We can see you go into the tent, and then there's a kitchen, and then the room with the jars, and you're sitting on a chair, and then—* She felt him walk up against it and stop. *Grey. And then you're eating eggs.*

*Six eggs,* Jacob thought. *She ate six eggs. He made her eggs.*

*Yeah.*

*In between the jar room and the eggs. What happened?*

*Stuff.*

*Leah.*

*Stuff I can't tell you, Jake. I literally can't. I'm not being a bitch about it for once. I can't.*

*Is it him? Is he stopping you? Is he, like, in your head right now?*

She thought about that honestly, because there wasn't any point doing it any other way in the pack, and because she'd found out about four hours ago that she couldn't do it any other way with *him*, either, and the habit was already starting to stick.

He was in her head. That part was true. He'd been in there since one o'clock on the ridge, and she could feel him right now, a long way behind her, back up on the bench, in the tent. She could feel that he was standing in the hallway by the door. She could feel that he'd just picked something up. She couldn't have told anybody what he was doing, any more than she could have said what Seth was doing with his front paws at the far end of the pack, but she could feel the shape of him doing it, steady, unhurried, the way you can hear somebody in the next room getting ready to go out.

Then, between one of her strides and the next, he wasn't in the tent anymore.

He was somewhere else. South and east. She felt the line swing round inside her chest like a compass needle when you turn the map, and settle, pointing back the way she'd come hours ago: toward the ridge above the Bogachiel. Toward the granite slab and the crater and the ferns where Layla had come down. He'd gone back there. In a second. With that little page-turning sound she couldn't hear from twelve miles off and heard anyway.

*He's not making me do anything,* she thought. *He's on the ridge.*

*He's on the—how do you even know that?*

*I just know.*

*Why's he on the ridge?*

She knew that too. It was in the kitchen, behind the grey, in his voice: *I'm going to go and clean up that ridge tonight, after you've gone.* She felt the rest of the sentence come up against the wall and stop dead.

*Cleaning,* she thought, which was all the hand on the back of her neck would let past.

*Cleaning,* Jacob repeated.

*Yeah.*

*He's cleaning.*

*If you've got a better word, Jake, use it.*

She came down a long slope through sword ferns that slapped wet against her chest and hit a creek at the bottom and went across it in two bounds and up the far bank, and she let the running take her for a while. It was easier than it had been in two years. She noticed that the way you'd notice your bad knee had stopped clicking on the stairs. She noticed it and didn't look at it straight, because she had a feeling that if she looked at it straight she'd have to stop.

*Get to the Cullens',* Jacob thought. *Everybody's there. Layla's there. Carlisle wants to look at you. Edward wants to—* He hesitated. *Edward wants to look at the thing.*

*Okay,* Leah thought.

There was a silence in the pack mind so complete she could hear her own feet.

*Okay?* said Seth.

*Okay. I'll come.*

*You're not going to argue?*

*Do you want me to argue?*

*No! No. I just. You always argue.*

That was true. She thought about it while she ran. *I always argue.* And she waited, the way you wait for a sneeze, for the thing that always came up in her when Jacob told her where to go. The little hot spike of it. *Who made you boss.* It had come up every single time he'd given her an order since she'd followed him out of Sam's pack in September, even the orders she'd wanted, even the ones she'd asked for. It was part of being her.

It didn't come.

She ran another quarter mile waiting for it, and it still didn't come, and she found she didn't miss it.

*Lee,* Seth thought, slowly, from a long way off. *You feel weird.*

*Weird how?*

*Quiet.* He was feeling around in her the way a kid feels around in a coat pocket for a dollar he knows was there. *You feel really quiet. There's no—*

He stopped.

She felt him find the place where it used to be. She felt Jacob find it a second later. And she felt both of them go absolutely still in the dark, two wolves miles apart, one by the Cullens' river and one somewhere south of it, standing with their heads up and their ears forward like they'd heard a gunshot.

For two years there had been a sound in the back of her head. Everybody in the pack had heard it. Sam's pack had heard it, back when she was in Sam's pack, and Jacob's had heard it since. It was a low, grinding, constant hum, like a refrigerator in an empty kitchen at three in the morning, and it was made of Sam. Sam's face. Sam's voice. Sam's hands. Sam on the porch at Makah with Emily, which she'd never seen with her own eyes and had seen ten thousand times through his. Sam's guilt about her, that thick brown sludge of it, and her own hate, which was worse, and under the hate the other thing, the thing that was still there after everything, that she would have cut out of herself with a fish knife if she'd been able to find where it was attached. It had played under every thought she'd had in the pack mind since the night she phased. Jacob called it the Sam song. Not to her face. She'd heard him call it that in his head, the first week.

*It's gone,* Jacob thought.

*Yeah.*

*Leah. It's gone. The whole—*

*Yeah, Jake.*

*Like, gone gone?*

*Like gone gone.*

He didn't say anything else. She could feel him not saying it. What came off him after a second was the last thing she'd have predicted from Jacob Black on a day when he was this angry. Relief. It came out of him slow and heavy and enormous, like a man setting down a sack of cement he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it was on his shoulders, and she realized, running through the dark, that he *had* been carrying it. Every day since she'd joined him. Her misery in the back of his head every time he phased. He'd never once told her to shut it off. He'd never once complained.

She didn't know what to do with that, so she didn't do anything with it.

Seth, on the other hand, was making a noise.

It came through the pack mind as a sort of high, wobbling whine, the sound a puppy makes when you come home, and it took her a second to understand that her brother was standing in the woods behind the Cullens' house crying. Wolves couldn't cry. Seth was managing it anyway.

*Seth.*

*I'm fine.*

*You're crying.*

*I'm not crying. I'm phased. You can't cry phased.*

*Seth, I can hear you crying.*

*It's allergies,* Seth thought, and kept doing it.

She let him. She ran.

---

She was crossing the Calawah below the forks, where it ran wide and shallow over a bar of fist-sized stones and the water came up to her elbows, cold as metal, when the name landed.

She'd been calling him by it in her head for hours. She'd heard him say it in the tent, standing in the rain with his mouth open like it had come out of somebody else, *Harry Potter*, and *bollocks*, and then a lot of other things had happened very fast and she hadn't stopped to look at it. Since then she'd thought it maybe a hundred times. *Harry.* Running. *Harry.* On the cold wooden chair in the round room. *Harry*, when he'd crouched down on his heels at the edge of the circle and gone into the vampire's head like a man diving off a pier.

And now, in the middle of the river, with her legs going numb, it just turned over and showed her the other side.

*Harry.*

Her dad's name.

Harry Clearwater. Who'd fished off the jetty every Saturday morning of her childhood in a green plaid jacket with a thermos of coffee and a bag of powdered doughnuts he wasn't supposed to have, because of his heart. Who'd called her Lee-Lee until she was twelve and asked him to stop, and then stopped, that same day, without arguing. Who'd taught her to gut a salmon and to change a tire and to throw a punch with her thumb on the outside. Who'd been sitting at the kitchen table on the night she phased, in the spring, eating pie, and had looked up when the screaming started in the yard, and gotten up, and gone to the back door, and seen.

Who'd been on the kitchen floor when she came back. When she came back to herself, hours later, naked and filthy in the woods with Seth standing over her, sandy and shaking, and walked home, and the ambulance was already gone.

She stood in the river.

She'd stopped without meaning to. The water went past her legs. She stood in the middle of the Calawah in the dark with the rain coming down and her chest going, and she waited for it to hurt, because it always hurt, and she'd learned to stand still when it came so it didn't knock her over.

It hurt.

It hurt the way it always did, and then, under the hurt, something else came up through it like a fish coming up through dark water, and she recognized it a second after it surfaced.

He'd have laughed.

Her dad would have laughed until he had to sit down. He'd have laughed the big laugh, the one that started in his belly and came up out of him like a truck engine catching, the one that made people at the other end of the marina turn around. *Of all the names, Lee-Lee.* She could hear him. She could actually hear him say it, in the kitchen, with powdered sugar on his chin. *Of all the names in the world. Well. At least he's got a good one.*

The sound that came out of her wolf was a sneeze and a snort together, and it nearly put her face in the river.

*Did you just laugh?* said Jacob.

*She laughed,* said Seth, astonished, through the allergies. *That's twice in like two months.*

*Lee, why are you thinking about Dad?*

She went still again.

Of course he'd seen. They'd both seen. She'd been standing in a river thinking about their father so hard she could smell the powdered sugar, and they'd seen every bit of it: the jetty and the thermos and the green plaid and the kitchen floor. And then, right where the reason was, right where the joke was, the grey. A smooth cold patch, exactly the size and shape of one name.

She felt Seth walk up to it, and stop, and stand there in front of it with his ears down.

*Is it a bad Dad thing?* he asked. Very small.

*No,* Leah thought. She started moving again, up out of the river, shaking the water off her shoulders as she went. *It's a funny Dad thing. I swear. It's the funniest Dad thing that's ever happened. I'll tell you as soon as I can.*

*Promise?*

*Promise, Seth.*

He believed her. She felt him believe her, the way he always had, every single time she'd ever given him a reason not to, and she put her head down and ran the last eight miles to the Cullen place without thinking about anything at all.

---

The house came up out of the trees at two minutes past seven the way it always did, all at once, a long white box of light with the forest crowding up to the back of it, every window lit, the glass wall at the back throwing yellow out across the lawn and into the rain. The stink of it reached her a quarter mile out. Bleach and burnt sugar and flowers. She'd been smelling it every day for three months and she'd never stopped hating it, and tonight, coming up through the cedars at the edge of the lawn, she noticed that it was just a smell. Bad. Sweet. Familiar. Like the fish plant at the marina in August.

Jacob was on the lawn. Russet, enormous, standing in the rain with his head low and his ears forward, watching the tree line for her. Seth was beside him, sandy and smaller and still sniffing. She saw them both see her at the same time.

*I'm changing,* she thought at them. *Don't look.*

*Nobody looks,* Jacob thought. *We never look.*

*Paul looks.*

*Paul's not here.*

She went behind a big cedar at the edge of the lawn and let the wolf go.

It went the way it always did, all at once, from the middle out. A heat and a shove and a sound like canvas tearing, and then she was on her knees in wet cedar needles with the rain on her bare back and her hands in the dirt, and the pack mind dropped away, Jacob and Seth gone out of her head like somebody had shut a door on a loud party. Her ears rang with the quiet. It was always too quiet afterward. Tonight, kneeling there, she found it didn't bother her.

She wasn't alone in it.

He was still there. Of course he was. The line had come through the change with her the way it had come through everything else, and it ran out of her chest and away southeast into the dark toward the ridge, eight miles, ten, and on the other end of it she could feel him, steady, busy, doing something with his hands to a piece of ground. She knelt in the needles and held still and felt him for a second, the way you'd put your hand on a dog asleep beside you in the dark just to know it was there.

She untied the cord from her ankle and pulled the cutoffs and the tank top off it. They were soaked. They'd been soaked since one o'clock. She pulled them on anyway and stood up.

And the wall moved.

She felt it in the middle of her head, a shift, a scrape, like a stone settling in a riverbed when the water changes. She put her hand on the cedar's trunk. Something was coming through from the other side. It came the way a coin comes through a grate, one edge first, catching, then sliding, then dropping clean into her open hand, and it was small and bright and hers, and she knew what it was before it landed.

*Harry Potter.*

His name. It was on her side now. She could feel the difference. She could feel that if she opened her mouth, right now, and said it out loud to the trees, nothing would close on her throat. And she could feel *him*, at the other end of the line, ten miles away on the ridge with his hands in the dirt, looking up from whatever he was doing. Paying attention to her. Deliberately. She'd felt him pay attention like that in the kitchen, across the table, when she'd been talking about Sam and he'd stopped moving to listen.

He'd done it on purpose.

She didn't know how she knew. She didn't know how he'd done it, whether it was a spell, or whether he'd just reached down the line with the same part of himself that had pulled the fog open for her and taken his own name off a hook and passed it over. But she knew he had. She could feel the shape of what he'd been thinking when he did it, the way you could feel the tail end of somebody's sentence when you walked in on it: dry, plain, a little impatient. *That one's mine. They can have that one.*

She stood behind the cedar in her wet shorts with her hand on the bark.

"Okay," Leah said, out loud, to nobody, to him. "Thanks."

She didn't know if he could hear words that way. She had a feeling he'd get the gist.

She walked out across the lawn.

---

Edward opened the front door before she got to the steps.

He was standing in it with the light behind him, a dark shape in a gray sweater, and he was looking at her in a way she'd never seen him look at anybody. Edward Cullen's face didn't do things. In three months of standing in his living room and on his lawn and at the edge of his woods, she'd seen it go cold and colder and on one occasion, the night his daughter was born, completely blank. She'd never seen it surprised.

It was surprised now. It was trying not to be, and it was losing.

"Don't," Leah said.

He didn't. He closed his mouth and stepped back to let her in.

She went past him into the long white room, dripping on the hardwood, and every single head in it turned.

They were all there. Jacob had been right about that. Carlisle by the foot of the stairs with his arms folded, in a blue shirt with the sleeves rolled up like he'd been working. Esme beside him. Rosalie on the stairs, three steps up, the way she always sat, like the floor wasn't good enough. Emmett on the piano bench, backwards, with his elbows on the keys. Alice and Jasper together in the doorway to the kitchen, Alice with her arms wrapped round herself and her face pinched tight, Jasper behind her with one hand on her shoulder. Bella in the big armchair by the window, Charlie's chair, with the kid on her lap.

Bella's eyes weren't brown tonight. No Charlie, no contacts. They were the newborn color, a deep wine-red already going muddy and dark at the edges, toward something more like amber, and they went over Leah once, fast, top to bottom, the way they went over everybody.

The kid was awake. She was sitting on her mother's knees with a big pad of paper and a fistful of crayons, drawing something purple with four legs. She looked up when Leah came in and her whole face lit up, and she held the picture up so Leah could see it. A horse. Probably a horse. Leah gave her a nod. The kid seemed satisfied with that and went back to drawing.

And on the white couch, under a gray blanket, with a hot-water bottle wedged behind her back and a mug in both hands, Layla.

She looked like hell. Her hair was wet, like she'd showered, and she was wearing a sweater that was obviously Esme's, cream-colored and soft and much too nice, and the honey was gone from her arms. Her face was the color of old paper. There was a bruise coming up on her left cheekbone, purple and yellow at the edges, where something had hit her on the way into the log. She was holding the mug like it was the only thing keeping her on the couch.

Seth was on the floor at her feet. Human, in cutoffs, with his back against her shins, exactly where he'd been sitting the night they'd all met her. His eyes were red. He looked up at Leah with his whole heart on his face, the way he always did, like an idiot, like a Labrador, and Leah looked back at him, and felt something in her chest go loose.

"Hey," she said.

"Hey," said Seth.

Jacob came in through the back door from the lawn behind her, in his cutoffs, with the rain running off his shoulders, and stopped just inside it.

Leah walked past all of them into the kitchen.

She could feel every one of them watching her go. She didn't care. She was starving. She'd eaten six eggs and most of a loaf of bread at a quarter to six in a kitchen at the end of a hallway that shouldn't exist, and she'd run twenty miles since, and she'd found over the last three months that the one dependable thing about the Cullen house was that Esme Cullen cooked for the wolves like she was expecting a football team.

There were two lasagnas on the counter. One of them had a corner gone. Jacob, probably, or Seth. She got a plate down off the shelf and a knife out of the block and cut herself a quarter of the whole one that was left, a slab the size of a brick, and slid it onto the plate, and found a fork in the drawer, and stood at the counter and started eating.

It was really good lasagna. Esme did something with the sausage.

After a while she noticed that it had gone very quiet behind her.

She turned around with her mouth full.

They'd followed her. Not all the way. They'd come as far as the kitchen and stopped in a sort of loose crowd at the edge of it, in the doorway and around the big island, like people at a zoo who'd come up to the glass. Esme. Carlisle. Alice and Jasper. Emmett, grinning. Jacob, right in the front of the doorway, with his arms crossed over his chest so hard the muscles stood out.

"What?" said Leah.

Nobody answered.

She chewed. Swallowed. Took another bite.

"She's calm," said Jasper.

He said it quietly. He said it the way you'd read a number off a gauge. Everybody turned to look at him, and he was looking at Leah with an expression she'd never seen on him either, which made two Cullens tonight. Jasper Hale's face usually looked like a man waiting for a fight he'd been waiting for since 1863.

"I've stood in rooms with you for six months," he said to her. "I've never once felt you calm. Not for a second. Not asleep." He tipped his head slightly. "You're calm."

"I'm eating," said Leah.

"Leah," said Jacob.

"What, Jake?"

He uncrossed his arms. He crossed them again. She watched him try to find a way in, and fail, and give up on finding one, and just go.

"What the *hell*," Jacob said. "What the hell, Leah? How could you imprint on *him*?"

---

She put the fork down.

She did it slowly. She put it down on the edge of the plate, very carefully, the way her mom put things down when she was trying not to say something, and she heard herself doing it and knew exactly where she'd got it from, and that didn't stop her either.

"Oh," said Leah. "You want to do this?"

"Leah—"

"You want to do this, Jake? Here?" She picked the fork back up and pointed with it, straight past him, through the kitchen door, across the long white room, to the armchair by the window where a ten-week-old the size of a three-year-old was coloring a purple horse in her vampire mother's lap. "In *this* house? At least mine isn't a literal baby vampire hybrid."

Bella's head came up like a snake's. "*Leah.*"

"Not you, Bella. Not her." She didn't take her eyes off Jacob. "She's great. She's the best baby on the peninsula. I'd take a bullet for her, I'm pretty sure, which is a really weird thing to find out about yourself, by the way, thanks for that. I'm talking about *him*." The fork came back round to Jacob. "You imprinted on a newborn. On a half-vampire newborn that was, what, three minutes old? Covered in blood? Whose mom you'd been in love with for a year and a half? You were standing in that room with your teeth out, Jake, getting ready to kill her. I was *in your head*. I watched you walk in there to do it."

Jacob's face had gone dark red under the brown. "That's not—"

"And then she looked at you and you fell over. And now you sleep on that couch every night like a dog on a porch, and you're going to marry her in like seventeen years, and everybody in this house is just *fine* with it." She took a breath. "So no. You don't get to stand in this kitchen with your arms folded and ask me how I *could*."

"It's different," Jacob said. "That's totally different, and you know it's—"

"You're right," said Leah. "It is different. You want to know how?"

She held up one finger.

"A. He's not a baby."

Two.

"B. He's human. Mostly. Like ninety percent. More than anybody else in this kitchen, anyway."

Three.

"C. He's also a wolf."

She let that one sit. She watched it hit Jacob and keep going, into Emmett, who said "*Huh*" under his breath, and Carlisle, whose eyebrows went up, and Esme, who put her hand over her mouth.

"So take that," said Leah. "If you really think about it, mine's the best one anybody's ever had. Sam got my cousin. Quil got a two-year-old. Jared got Kim, which, fine, Kim's fine. Paul got your sister, and I'm not even going to *start*. And you got"—the fork went back out the door, toward the armchair, toward the purple horse—"a baby who's going to be taller than me by Easter. And I got a grown man who can turn into a wolf, and catch a falling vampire one-handed, and stop a spell with his fingers, and *also* cook." She stabbed the lasagna. "See? Better."

There was a short silence.

"She's got a point," said Emmett.

"*Emmett,*" said Rosalie, from somewhere behind them, on the stairs.

"What? She does. Scored on a curve, the guy's a catch." Emmett held his hands up. "I'm just saying. The cooking thing."

Leah ate a bite of lasagna. Jacob was staring at her. His mouth opened and closed.

"And do you think I *chose* this?" she said. Quieter.

He didn't answer.

"Is that what you think, Jake? Really?" She set the plate down. "You think I was lying there on that rock with my face in the moss, with that guy's hand on my back holding me down like a bug, that I couldn't even *see*, while he threw Seth's girlfriend twenty feet into a log, and I got up off it, and I looked at him, and I went, *yeah. Him. That one. Sign me up*?" She shook her head. "Did you choose her? When she looked at you?"

"No," Jacob said. Hoarse.

"Did Sam choose Emily? Did Quil choose Claire? Did Quil get to *vote*?"

"No."

"Did Seth choose Layla?"

On the couch in the living room, Seth said, "Kind of," and Layla, very tiredly, said, "Seth, not now."

"*Then don't ask me how I could,*" Leah said. "I couldn't. It just did. Same as yours. Same as everybody's." She picked the fork back up. "Mine just did it better."

Jacob stood in the kitchen doorway with his arms at his sides now, and his face doing about four things at once, and Leah ate her lasagna and watched him, and found that she wasn't even angry. That was the strangest thing about it. Two years of her life, she'd have given her right arm to get this exact shot at Jacob Black in front of a room full of people, and she'd have done it angry, and it would have burned all the way down, and she'd have gone out to the woods afterward and run until she couldn't feel her feet. Tonight she'd said every word of it and she just felt fine. Hungry. Tired. A little bit smug.

Something tugged at the hem of her wet tank top.

She looked down. The kid was standing beside her.

She hadn't heard her come. Nobody ever heard her come; she'd been walking for three weeks and she moved like the rest of them, like a draft. She was holding the purple horse in one hand and reaching up with the other, and her face was very serious under the bronze curls, and Leah, who had not touched Renesmee Cullen on purpose in the ten weeks the kid had been alive, looked down at her for a second, and then crouched, and let the small hot hand land on her cheek.

The picture came in clean and bright, the way the kid's pictures always did. Her own face, the kid's, in a mirror. A question mark over it, drawn in purple crayon. And a feeling under the question: worried. Very worried. Like a kid who'd heard grown-ups saying her name in an angry voice and wanted to know if she was in trouble.

"No," Leah said. "No, kid. You're great. You're totally fine. I'm not mad at you." She jerked her chin up toward the door. "It's him I'm yelling at."

The kid looked over her shoulder at Jacob. Considered him. Put her hand back on Leah's cheek, and this time the picture was Jacob's face, from about two feet below, the way she must see it most of the time: sulky, red, bottom lip out, exactly the face he was making right now in the doorway.

Leah snorted.

"Yeah," she said. "That one."

The kid seemed satisfied. She patted Leah's cheek twice, the way you'd pat a dog, and turned around and walked back across the room to her mother with her purple horse, and climbed up into the armchair, and picked up a green crayon.

When Leah stood up, Jacob was looking at her strangely.

"What," she said.

"You let her touch you."

"She asked."

"You've never let her touch you."

"She never asked about something important before." Leah picked her plate up. "Are we done? Because I'd like to eat this sitting down."

"No," Jacob said. "No, we're not done." He came into the kitchen. He stopped on the other side of the island from her and put both hands flat on the counter and leaned on them, and she could see him getting himself back together, the Alpha part of him coming up through the hurt part like it always did, and she had to give him that: he always got there eventually. "C," he said. "What does C mean? *Also a wolf.* What does that even mean, Leah?"

"Means what it means."

"Seth said there was a thing. Down in him. Behind some kind of locks."

"There is."

"So what is it? Is he one of us? Is he Quileute? Is he from some other tribe somewhere? Is he, like, a werewolf, the movie kind, the full moon kind—"

"Jake. I don't know." She sat down on one of the tall stools at the island and pulled the plate toward her. "He doesn't really know either. He said so. He said it's complicated." She took a bite. "All I know is it's big. It's in him. It's got a wolf's—" She tried to find the word and couldn't. "It's a wolf. Like ours. Except bigger." She chewed. "A lot bigger than you."

Jacob's face went through something.

"Bigger than *me*," he said.

"Yep."

"How would you even know that? You said you didn't see it."

"I felt it." She met his eyes across the island. "It looked at me, Jake. Up the line. Through him. I felt it stand up. It's like standing at the bottom of a mountain in the dark."

"The bottom of a mountain," said Emmett happily, to nobody, to the room. "Oh, I want to meet this guy."

"*Emmett,*" said Rosalie again.

Jacob ignored them both. He was still looking at Leah, and his voice came down, and some of the Alpha went out of it, and what was left was just Jacob. Who'd been her brother's best friend since Seth was in diapers. Who'd been nine years old and terrified of her, once.

"He shot Layla, Lee," he said quietly. "With a gun. He put Seth on his face on a rock. He had us both down like we were nothing, like we weighed *nothing*, and he went through Seth's head like a burglar, and he—"

"He shot the *ground*," said Layla.

Everybody turned around.

She was still on the couch. She hadn't moved. She was looking at them over the rim of Esme's mug with the bruise coming up purple on her cheek, and her voice was exhausted and very clear.

"I keep having to say it," Layla said. "He shot the ground. In front of me. If he'd shot *me* I'd be in two pieces in a fern." She drank some of whatever was in the mug. "I'm not defending him. I'm just tired of saying it."

"Thank you," said Leah.

"You're welcome."

Jacob looked at Layla, and at Leah, and threw his hands up.

"You don't even know who he *is*," he said. "Leah. Come on. You don't know anything about him. He walked out of nowhere in a suit and flashed a badge and called you *Lassie*, and four hours later you're—you're eating lasagna about it like it's *nothing*, like you've known the guy your whole life, and you don't even know his *name*—"

"Harry Potter," said Edward.

### II. Edward

*The Cullen house — 7:11 p.m.*

He'd been listening to her since the river.

He'd felt her come into range three miles out, the way he felt everyone, a voice rising up out of the general murmur of the forest like a radio station coming in clear as you drive over a hill, and he'd known her at once, because Leah Clearwater's mind had a sound to it that no other mind on the peninsula had. He'd been hearing it for six months. On the lawn, at the tree line, on the long nights in the spring when she'd stood guard in the woods outside this house hating every one of them so hard he could taste it.

It had a song in it. That was the only way he'd ever found to describe it to himself. A low grinding song, under everything, all the time, built out of one man's name, and she'd never once stopped playing it.

He'd stood at the glass wall at the back of the house tonight, with Alice's worry going round and round in the room behind him and Jacob's anger going off in the yard like a road flare, and he'd listened to Leah Clearwater come across the river, and he'd heard no song at all.

Her mind was quiet, and full. It ran fast and clear and a little bit fierce, the way a creek runs after the snow goes off it in April, full of small bright things: the rain, the smell of the house, *lasagna, please God Esme made lasagna*, her brother, the purple horse. But under it there was nothing grinding. For the first time since he'd known her, there was room in Leah Clearwater's head.

And then there was the other thing.

He'd heard about it already. He'd heard it in Seth's memory at one o'clock, and in Jacob's in the hour since, every time either of them phased back and stood on the lawn thinking about it: *a wall, a fog, a grey place*. He'd thought, listening to them, that they were describing a hole. A missing piece of time. The kind of gap you got in the minds of people who'd been drugged, or struck on the head, or who'd simply decided, very hard, not to remember.

He knew better as soon as she came close enough for him to hear it properly.

It was a *thing*. It had been set down in the middle of her mind the way you'd set a stone down in the middle of a stream, and her thoughts went round it on both sides. It was cold. That was the first quality he could put a word to. A deep, settled, mineral cold, colder than his family's skin and colder than the dead, like the inside of a mountain. And it was old. He'd stood in a stone room in Volterra once with Aro's hand on his and heard three thousand years of a single mind go past him like the sea, and he knew what *old* sounded like. This was a different kind. This had been made. Built, the way a cathedral was built, over centuries, by a great many hands that had each added a little and gone away. And it had been made to keep things in.

He couldn't hear past it. He'd tried, standing at the glass, the way he'd once tried to hear past the silence in Bella's mind, years ago, sitting across from her in a biology classroom wanting to tear the building down. He'd pressed on it. It hadn't so much as noticed him.

But on this side of it, loose, turning over and over in her thoughts like a coin turned in a pocket, there'd been a name. She'd been touching it since the lawn. *Harry. Harry Potter.* Over and over. The way you'd touch a ring you'd only just put on, to make sure it was there.

He hadn't meant to say it. He'd stood in the doorway listening to Jacob shout, and the name had come up in Leah's head bright and plain and a little bit defiant, *I do so know his name, you idiot*, and his mouth had simply supplied it, the way it sometimes did when he'd been listening too closely and forgot which voices were out loud.

He watched it go round the room.

Most of them didn't react much. Jacob blinked. Emmett said, "Potter?" Rosalie, on the stairs, made a small noise of disdain at the general Englishness of it. Carlisle's mind did what Carlisle's mind always did with a new piece of information: turned it over carefully, filed it, began looking for its neighbors.

Layla Quinn dropped her mug.

It went straight out of both her hands at once, as though they'd stopped working. Emmett caught it. He was across the room and off the piano bench and had it in one hand an inch above the rug before anybody human could have seen him move, and not a drop of the tea in it had spilled, and he stood there holding it, beaming, waiting for someone to notice how fast he'd been.

Nobody did. They were all looking at Layla.

"Say that again," she said.

Her voice was very calm. Her mind wasn't. Edward had been listening to it all afternoon, the deep organ-note structure of it under the ordinary words, the hundreds of Latin keys on their hooks, and at the sound of the name every one of them had swung at once. And under them, flooding up through everything like groundwater, came pictures. He had to stop himself flinching from them. A newspaper, the paper gone soft and yellow, with a photograph on the front page that *moved*: a thin boy with untidy black hair and round glasses, looking up out of the picture and then away, over and over, as if somebody off to the side kept calling his name. A mark on his forehead in the shape of a lightning bolt. A headline in heavy black capitals she'd read at thirteen at her parents' kitchen table in Albuquerque. THE BOY WHO LIVED. Another one, years later, bigger. A castle, burning.

"Harry Potter," Edward said. "That's his name."

"No," Layla said.

"Yes."

"No. No. Uh-uh." She laughed. It came out of her cracked down the middle. "*No me jodas.*" She put her hand over her mouth. She took it away. "Are you sure? Are you *sure*? You're not—it couldn't be somebody else, it's a common enough name over there, it's like John Smith, there could be—"

"It's in her head," said Edward, "and she's been certain of it since this afternoon. He said it to her himself. I'm sure."

Layla sat back against the couch cushions very slowly, as if her back had suddenly started hurting again, and looked up at the ceiling.

"*Ay, Dios mío,*" she said to it.

"You know the name," said Carlisle.

She laughed again. It was a bit better this time. A bit less broken.

"Dr. Cullen," she said. "Everybody knows the name. Every witch and wizard alive knows that name. Every kid at Ilvermorny. Every kid at every school on the *planet*." She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "It's like—I don't know what it's like. It's like asking if you know who Neil Armstrong is."

"Who is he?" said Bella.

She'd gone very still in the armchair, with her arm around her daughter. Edward could feel her stillness, the newborn coiled perfection of it. Her mind he couldn't hear and never had. But he knew her face.

Layla took her hands away from her eyes.

She told them.

She didn't tell it well. She told it in pieces, out of order, the way people told things they'd grown up with and never had to explain; she kept starting a sentence and stopping and going back for something she'd forgotten everybody didn't know. Edward watched it come together in her head while she talked. It was more coherent in there than it was coming out, and he found he had to work not to finish her sentences for her.

There'd been a wizard in Britain, she said. In the seventies. A dark one. The worst one anybody had seen in a hundred years. Worse than the one before him, the one in Europe in the war, who'd been bad enough. People in Britain wouldn't say his name. Even now, even after, a lot of them still wouldn't. They called him You-Know-Who. Over here, in America, people said it, because they were Americans and they said whatever they wanted, but they said it quietly, and they didn't say it after dark. "Voldemort," she said, quietly, and Edward heard the little cold flinch it set off in her, and saw Jasper, by the kitchen door, feel it too. "His real name was Tom Riddle. That came out later."

He'd killed a lot of people, she said. Witches and wizards. Muggles—ordinary people, she said, *you*, people without magic. Whole families. He'd had followers. He'd been winning.

And then, on Halloween in 1981, he'd gone to a house in a village in the west of England to kill a baby.

"Nobody knows exactly why," she said. "There's about a million theories. It doesn't matter. He went there. He killed the father, at the door. He killed the mother, in the baby's room. And then he pointed his wand at the baby and used the Killing Curse." She looked around at all of them. "There's a curse that kills you. Just—kills you. No mark, no wound, nothing. There's no counter. There's no shield that stops it. Nobody in history has ever survived it. Ever. Not once. In two thousand years of records, not one single person."

"And the baby did," said Esme softly.

"And the baby did," said Layla. "And the curse bounced. Came back. And Riddle was gone. Not dead—it turned out later he wasn't dead—but gone. For fourteen years. And there was this one-year-old in the wreck of a house in a village in England with a scar on his forehead." She touched her own, above her left eye. "Right here. Like a lightning bolt. And everybody in the world knew his name by the next morning. The Boy Who Lived." She shook her head. "My mom has a commemorative plate."

"A plate," said Rosalie.

"There were a lot of plates."

She went on. Riddle had come back, she said, when the boy was fourteen. There'd been a war. Not a big war, the way Muggles thought about wars, not tanks and planes; a small, dirty, secret war, a few hundred people on each side in a little country, and a lot of them had died. And in May of 1998 it had ended in a single night at the boy's school, a castle in Scotland, with half of the British wizarding world fighting in the corridors.

"And at the end of it," said Layla, "in front of everybody, in the Great Hall, at dawn, Riddle and the kid fought. One on one. And Riddle used the Killing Curse again, and the kid used a Disarming Charm." Her mouth twisted. "And the curse went back again. And that was it. Riddle was dead. For good, this time. The kid was seventeen."

Nobody said anything.

"A Disarming Charm," Layla said. "*Expelliarmus.* It's the first thing they teach you. The very first dueling spell. Every kid in every dueling club in the world learns it in the first week. It knocks the wand out of the other guy's hand. That's all it does." She laughed, short and helpless. "It's the most famous spell anybody's ever cast. People write papers about it. There's a whole school of dueling theory that's basically about *why it worked*. And I—"

She stopped.

Edward heard her get there. He heard her stand back up on the ridge in the rain and look across twenty feet of wet granite at a man in a long black coat, and he heard her watch her own scarlet spell go out of her wand at his right hand, and she put her face in her hands.

"I threw one at him," she said, into them. "On the ridge. I threw a Disarming Charm at *Harry Potter*."

"He caught it," said Seth helpfully.

"I *know* he caught it, Seth, I was *there*."

"Like Luke Skywalker," said Emmett.

Everyone looked at him. He was still holding Layla's mug.

"What? It's Luke Skywalker. Dark lord, kills the parents, the kid's got some kind of destiny thing, the kid grows up and wins with like the dumbest move in the book—"

"Luke Skywalker blew up the Death Star with a torpedo," said Jasper. "That's a reasonable move."

"It was a really little torpedo."

"Sure," said Layla, through her hands. "Yes. He's Luke Skywalker. If Luke Skywalker did the whole thing with a slingshot when he was seventeen, and then walked off into the desert and nobody ever saw him again."

That stopped Emmett. "Nobody saw him?"

"Nobody." She lifted her head. "That's the other half of it. That's the part people really talk about. After the war, there was a year, maybe less, where he was around. In the papers. Then he just—stopped being. Nobody saw him. Nobody heard from him. Nobody knew where he went." She shrugged, and winced at what it did to her back. "There've been about five hundred theories. He's dead. He went mad. He's living on an island in the Hebrides with a bunch of goats. He's in Tibet. He went into the mountains in Africa somewhere and came back a hermit. There were books about it. People made money off it. My roommate in my fourth year had a poster." She looked at Edward. "He'd be twenty-six."

"He looks about that," Edward said. "In Seth's memory."

"He doesn't look anything like the pictures. The pictures were a skinny little kid with glasses. He was about up to *here* on everybody." She held a hand at her own chin. "Black hair everywhere."

"He doesn't have glasses," said Seth.

"I know he doesn't have glasses, Seth."

"Or a scar."

"I *know*."

Edward listened to her mind under the words. It had stopped flooding. It had gone back to the ridge and was walking across it again, carefully, slowly, the way he'd watched her walk across her own memories all afternoon on this couch, the way a person goes back over a crime scene. He watched her watch the Stunner bend at a right angle a foot from the man's chest. Watched her watch her own Disjunction stop on two of his fingers and sit there fizzing while he looked at it, turning his hand to see underneath. *Oh, that's clever. Did you make that?* And under the replay, quietly, a list. Three names, in a cold careful voice that Edward had learned over the afternoon was the voice she used for work. *Dumbledore. Grindelwald. Riddle.* And then, very quiet: *He's the one who closed it. Eight years ago. At seventeen. With the first spell in the book.*

*And then he went away for eight years and did what?*

He heard her get to the end of that thought and stop, and look at it, and decide not to go any further down it.

He found, a little to his own surprise, that he didn't blame her.

"Harry," said Seth.

He said it quietly. He'd turned round on the floor and was looking past all of them, across the room, to the kitchen doorway, where his sister had come to stand with her plate in her hand.

"*Harry,*" Seth said again. "Like Dad."

Leah looked at him. She didn't say anything.

"That's why you were thinking about Dad," Seth said. "On the run. I saw you thinking about Dad, at the river, the jetty and the—and then right at the end of it, it went grey. I thought—" His voice wobbled. "I didn't know what I thought."

"Yeah, Seth," said Leah. "That's why."

"That's the funny Dad thing."

"That's the funny Dad thing."

Edward heard it come up in her mind, behind the words. He heard it come up in Seth's at almost the same instant, so that for a second the two of them were thinking the same picture across the room: a big man on a wooden jetty in a green plaid jacket with powdered sugar in his mustache, throwing his head back, laughing so hard the gulls went up off the pilings. And under it, in both their heads, the same words in the same voice. *Of all the names, Lee-Lee.*

Edward looked at the floor.

Seth laughed. He laughed with his eyes full and his face screwed up, and then Leah did too, just a little, from the doorway, a snort through her nose, and the two of them looked at each other across the long white room and didn't need anybody else for a minute.

Esme had her hand over her mouth again.

---

It went sideways after that. Edward had known it would. He'd lived with these people for a long time.

Emmett wanted to know if Harry Potter signed things. Layla said she had absolutely no idea. Emmett wanted to know if he'd sign a baseball. Jasper said that was the least important question that had been asked in this house in a decade, and Emmett said it was a *very* important question, and Alice said, from inside her own folded arms, that she couldn't see him, that she couldn't see *any* of it, that every time she tried to look at a future with this man in it there was simply nothing there, a white place, like snow blindness. Rosalie said, from the stairs, that she didn't care whether he was Luke Skywalker or the Pope; he'd taken a living creature into a room full of jars and done things to it, and they'd all seen that in Seth's head, and in Jacob's, from Leah. The circle. The shelves. The man crouched on his heels, saying *Casimir. Where's your phone?*

"He's studying it," said Rosalie. "Us. He's taking one of us apart to see how it works."

"It ate a woman yesterday," said Jacob. "It was wearing her coat."

"I'm not crying for it, Jacob. I'm saying what he's *doing*."

"He's interrogating it," Jasper said. "That's different."

"Is it?"

"Usually," said Jasper, in a tone Edward knew well, the one that meant *I have done this*, and Rosalie looked at him and closed her mouth.

And Carlisle wanted to talk to him. Of course Carlisle did. Carlisle had been wanting to talk to everyone he'd ever met for three hundred and sixty years. He said so in his mild, patient way, from the foot of the stairs: that whoever this man was, he'd come onto the peninsula, and he'd caught a creature that would have killed again, and he hadn't harmed Seth, or Leah, or Miss Quinn, though by every account he could have done so with very little trouble. That he'd made Miss Quinn an offer, a hard one, but an offer. That he'd let all of them keep their memories. That those were not the actions of a man who wanted a war, and that someone ought to go and ask him what he did want, politely, before somebody else went and asked him impolitely.

And Jacob said *politely, sure*, and Rosalie said *who does he work for*, and Layla said *I don't know*, and Edward heard that she meant it. She'd gone over it a hundred times since the ridge. A badge with American printing on it that slid when Seth looked at it. A British accent. A Muggle gun. Every seam she found went nowhere, she thought, like a door with the glyphs scratched off, and the name hadn't helped her at all; it had made it worse. She didn't know who he worked for. She didn't know if he worked for anybody. She'd decided, somewhere in the last hour on this couch, with Carlisle's ice pack on her back, that she very much did not want to guess.

And Leah stood in the kitchen doorway with her plate and listened to all of it, and Edward listened to her listen.

He could feel it building. It came up in her slowly, the way weather comes up over the strait, darkening, and he knew the old anger like he knew his own name, and this had a different weight to it. It was harder. It was cleaner. It was the irritation of a person who's just come back from somewhere terrible and found everyone at home arguing about the wallpaper.

*They don't get it,* Leah was thinking. *They don't get it at all. Plates. Baseballs. Luke freaking Skywalker. They think it's a—*

"Are you guys hearing yourselves?" she said.

Everybody stopped.

She put her plate down on the end of the kitchen island and came out into the living room, barefoot, in her soaked shorts and her soaked tank top, and stood in the middle of the floor, and Edward watched every vampire in the room register, without meaning to, the heat coming off her in the air-conditioned dry of the house. She didn't look at any of them in particular. She looked at all of them.

"You're doing it like it's gossip," she said. "Like he's some guy I met at a bar. You want to know if he signs things. You want to know if he's good enough for me. You want to know if he's *Luke Skywalker*." Emmett opened his mouth and she pointed at him without looking. "Don't." He shut it. "Do you get what walked into these woods today? Do any of you actually get it?"

"Leah—" Carlisle said.

"No. Listen to me. I was there. I was there for all of it. Seth was there for half and he was on his face for most of that." She looked at Layla on the couch. "She was there. Ask her. Ask her what it was like. She's the best fighter any of us have ever seen, and he took her apart like a kid's toy without moving his *feet*. He held me and Seth down at the same time, with his back to us, while he did it. He was holding a vampire up in the air behind him the whole time. Like a coffee table. He wasn't even breathing hard." Her voice didn't go up. Edward had expected it to go up. It went flatter instead, and harder, and he found that much more alarming. "And that's just the ridge. That's just the part you guys saw. You don't know what I saw after. You don't know what he's got in that tent. You don't know what he can do. You don't know what he's *here for*. He's here because—"

She stopped.

Edward was listening to her when it happened, and so he saw it from the inside, which he would think about later, many times, and wish he hadn't.

The words had been coming up out of her the way words come, fast and loud and already shaped, and he'd heard the rest of the sentence forming in her head before it got to her mouth. And the cold thing in the middle of her mind moved.

It came forward. It didn't hurry. It slid across the front of her thoughts the way a hand slides across a mouth from behind, smooth and heavy and quite gentle, and it closed over the words, and over her throat with them. He watched her voice stop. He watched her stand in the middle of the living room with her mouth open and nothing coming out of it, and then her hand came up to the base of her neck, and her chest heaved once, and she made a small hard sound, a choked click, like a swimmer who's come up under ice.

Carlisle moved. Jacob moved. Seth came up off the floor so fast the couch went back six inches with Layla on it and Layla gasped at her back.

Leah put her other hand up, flat, palm out, at all of them. *Stop.*

They stopped.

She breathed. Edward heard her do it. She held very still and didn't fight it, and she let the thing in her head finish closing on what it had closed on, and then she felt it ease, and draw back, not all the way, just enough, the way a hand loosens on a collar when the dog stops pulling. Her throat opened. Air went into her.

She stood there breathing.

"Don't," Edward said quietly. "Don't try that again."

"I wasn't going to," said Leah. Her voice was a rasp. She took her hand off her throat. "I already knew. I just forgot for a second." She looked around at them all. At Jacob, standing frozen halfway across the room. At Seth, white. At Carlisle, who'd stopped two feet from her with both hands out. "That," she said. "*That.* That's what I'm talking about."

"What is it?" said Carlisle. Very gently.

"It's his." She swallowed, and winced. "It's something he carries. It's in him, too. It was in him first. I don't know what it's made of. I don't know how it works. All I know is it's older than him and it's colder than any of you and it just reached out of ten miles of forest and put its hand around my throat because I was about to tell you what the guy's job is." She looked at them. "Ten miles, Carlisle. And you guys want to know if he signs *baseballs*."

Nobody said anything.

"What does it feel like?" said Jasper, from the kitchen door.

Edward heard him ask it. He heard the reason, too: Jasper had felt it from across the room, a spike of something in Leah's emotions so sharp and so foreign it had made the hair stand up on the back of his neck. Something that had come from outside her, cold and impersonal, the way a fence feels when you put your hand on it and it's electrified.

Leah looked at him for a second.

"Like a hand," she said. "Like somebody's hand. It doesn't hurt. It just won't let go." She paused. "It thinks I'm him."

Jasper's eyebrows rose a quarter-inch. He didn't ask what that meant. Edward listened to him decide not to, and file it.

"Two thirty-one," said Alice.

She said it from the kitchen doorway, from inside her own arms. Everybody turned.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I know it's—I know now's not good. But that's why I wanted her here. That's why I asked Jacob to get her here." She looked at Leah. Edward heard her mind, the quick bright skittering of it, the way it had been skittering since the middle of the afternoon, round and round one moment in time like a moth round a porch light. "I need to know what happened at two thirty-one this afternoon."

Leah looked at her. "What?"

"Two thirty-one. Our time."

"Why?"

Alice unfolded her arms. She looked at Edward. He nodded.

"Since Italy," Alice said, to Leah, to all of them, "there's been a visit."

She told it plainly. Edward had heard her tell it to Carlisle at three o'clock and again to Jasper at four and again to herself, silently, in her head, about four hundred times since. In the spring, when he'd gone to Volterra to die and Bella had come to stop him and they'd stood together in the stone room with Aro's hand on his, the Volturi had made a promise. Bella would be changed or Bella would be killed. They'd check.

And since then, every single morning, Alice had looked.

"It's always been there," she said. "Every future I look at. A possibility. They haven't decided anything. A shape. Somewhere out ahead of us. Next spring. Next year. The year after. It moves around. Sometimes it's Caius. Sometimes it's Jane. Sometimes it's Aro himself, in a car, in the rain, on the highway coming up from Port Angeles." Her voice was very steady. "I've been watching it since March, and it's never once gone away. That's how they are. They said they'd check. They always check."

"And at two thirty-one," said Leah slowly.

"At two thirty-one," said Alice, "I saw Aro put down a telephone."

The room was very quiet.

"He was in the tower room. At night. It was nearly midnight there. He was on the phone with someone, and he put it down, and he laughed." Alice's mouth was tight. "And Caius was there, and Aro told him Bella had been changed. That her eyes were already going gold. That Carlisle had kept his word, and the family was as dull as it had always been, and there was nothing in Forks worth the trip." She looked at Bella in the armchair, and at the child coloring in Bella's lap, and back. "And Caius was furious. And Aro thought it was funny. And that was all."

"And the visit," said Carlisle.

"Gone." Alice spread her hands. "Just gone. I've looked forty times since. I've looked at next spring and next year and the year after that. It isn't there. They're not coming. They've stopped thinking about us at all." She took a breath that she didn't need. "Somebody called Aro at two thirty-one this afternoon, our time, and told him we were boring. And he believed them."

Bella's arm had tightened around her daughter so slowly that only a vampire would have seen it. The kid looked up at her mother, puzzled, and went back to her green crayon.

"Who?" said Rosalie.

"I don't know," said Alice. "I can't see anything on this end of it. Whoever it was, they were standing right in the middle of the white place." She looked at Leah. "And so were you. At two thirty-one. You were right in the middle of it."

Everyone looked at Leah.

Edward was already listening.

He'd been listening since Alice said *telephone*, and he'd watched it happen, and he'd known before Alice finished speaking. He couldn't see past the wall. Nothing had changed about that. But a wall in a stream doesn't stop the water from piling up against it, and when Alice had said *Aro put down a telephone*, something on the far side of Leah's wall had flared up so hot and so hard that he'd felt it from across the room. The way you feel a stove through a closed oven door. He couldn't see what it was. He could feel what it *felt* like.

It felt like satisfaction. Fierce, clean, enormous satisfaction, with an edge to it like a blade, the feeling of somebody watching a bully walk into a door. And under that, older, deeper, and very plain, a single thought that sat on this side of the wall, out in the open. Hers. She'd come to it herself, on a cold wooden chair, sometime in the afternoon, and it sat out in the open in her mind like a hand of cards laid face up on a table.

*Nobody's coming for the kid.*

"Leah," Edward said. "Do you know what happened at two thirty-one?"

She looked at him. He watched her think about it. He watched her decide not to try to say any of it, because she remembered the hand.

"Yeah," she said.

"Can you tell us?"

"No."

"Leah," said Bella.

It was the voice of a newborn. Very soft. Very level. A voice with a lot held behind it.

Leah turned and looked at her. And at the kid. And Edward heard her pick her words, slowly, one at a time, testing each one before she put her weight on it, the way you test the ice on a pond.

"Nobody's coming for her," Leah said. "Okay? Not this winter. Not in the spring. Nobody's coming." She waited. Nothing closed on her throat. Edward felt her feel it not close. "I can't tell you how I know. I just do. You're going to have to trust me."

Bella looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked at Edward.

"She does," Edward said quietly. "Know. She's completely certain."

"How certain?"

"More certain than I've ever heard her be about anything."

Bella looked back down at her daughter's bronze head. Her arm loosened, a fraction of an inch. She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to.

"The vampire he caught," Carlisle said.

He said it slowly. Edward could hear him putting it together, piece by piece, in that careful, methodical, physician's mind, the way he'd have laid out the results of a set of tests: a red-eyed nomad, alone, coming up out of the Bogachiel straight toward this house. A man who'd been waiting for it. A room full of jars. A circle. *Casimir. Where's your phone?* A telephone in Volterra, two hours later.

"Was it one of theirs?" Carlisle asked. "Leah. The one in the circle. Did he come from Volterra?"

Edward watched her open her mouth. And close it. He saw the hand come up against the inside of her throat and stop there, waiting, patient, the thumb just resting.

"Carlisle," Edward said.

"I know." Carlisle shook his head. He looked at Leah, and his face was very gentle. "I'm sorry. I won't ask you anything that hurts you. None of us will."

"Thanks," said Leah.

"*Edward,*" said Jasper.

He was still in the kitchen doorway, behind Alice. Edward didn't need to hear him to know what he was about to say; he'd been thinking it since *telephone*, very clearly, in the flat precise voice he used for this sort of thing, the voice that had once moved newborn armies across Texas like pieces on a board.

"Somebody made that call," said Jasper. "Somebody who knows exactly what's in this house, because they took it out of Seth's head this afternoon. Somebody who knows about Renesmee. And who has a red-eyed vampire on his floor who's been to Volterra, maybe, and maybe has a phone number in his pocket." He looked at Leah, and at Edward. "And who just told Aro we were boring. Today. Good. Wonderful." His voice didn't change at all. "And tomorrow he can tell Aro something else."

Edward heard the shape of it land in the room. He heard it land in Rosalie, and in Bella, and in Carlisle, who'd known it was coming and closed his eyes for a moment. He heard it land in Leah, too, and from somewhere behind the wall, very loud, came something that was not a thought so much as a snort: *Oh, if you only knew.*

He didn't say that out loud.

---

So they made a plan.

Edward had watched his family make plans before. He'd watched them make plans for newborn armies and for trackers and for Italian ancient lords who wanted his wife's head on a plate. He knew how it went. Carlisle said *talk*. Jasper said *leverage*. Rosalie said *kill*. Emmett said *all of us, now, at once*, and meant it, and was delighted. Alice said she couldn't see how any of it ended. Esme said nothing and made sure there was food.

Tonight was no different, except that Jacob joined in.

He'd been quiet since *telephone*. He'd been standing in the middle of the room with his fists opening and closing at his sides, looking at Leah, and Edward had been listening to him and hadn't liked anything he heard. Jacob had felt the hand close on her throat. Not the way Edward had, from inside; he'd simply seen it, his packmate, his beta, standing in a room choking on nothing. And something in Jacob had gone hot and simple and very, very old.

"We find him," said Jacob.

"Jake—" Leah said.

"No. We find the tent. Layla, you said wards. Concealment. Okay. So we go back to the ridge and we start there and we walk out in every direction until somebody can't remember where they're going, and then we know we're close. We do it in daylight. All of us. The whole pack, both packs, if Sam'll come." His jaw was set. "And when we find him, he takes that thing out of her head."

"He can't," Leah said. "I don't think he can, Jake, it's not—"

"Then he tells us who he is, and who he works for, and what he's doing here, and what's in the circle. And what he did at two thirty-one." Jacob looked at the Cullens. "And if he won't, there's six of you and three of us and nine of Sam's, and I don't care how good he is."

"I'm in," said Emmett at once.

"I'd want to be there," Rosalie said. "If he's made one call to Italy, I want to know he won't make another."

"It'd need to be fast," said Jasper. "And at once. From every side. He held two wolves and a witch at the same time on the ridge. Hold three. Hold six. Hold twelve. At some point there's a number. Everyone has a number."

"Jasper," said Carlisle.

"I'm only saying what's true."

"I could try and find his wards," said Layla. She'd been quiet for a long time, with her eyes closed against the couch cushions. She opened them now. "If you do it. I could try. I break things. That's what I do. I'd probably die," she added, very matter-of-factly. "But I could try."

"No," said Seth. "Absolutely not."

"I'm just *saying*, Seth."

"I can't see any of it," said Alice. "Any of it. If you go, I can't tell you how it ends. I can't tell you anything. Every one of those futures is just white."

"Then we go in blind," said Jacob. "Fine. We've done it before."

Edward was listening to Leah.

She'd sat back down on the arm of the couch beside Layla's feet while they talked, with her elbows on her knees, and she hadn't said a word. She'd just listened. And Edward had heard it come up in her again, the weather over the strait, and this time it had no anger in it at all. It had something that, if he'd heard it in Carlisle, he'd have called pity.

*They really don't know how it works,* she was thinking. *Jake was* there. *Jake was standing right there in September.*

"You guys know how this works, right?" said Leah.

Everybody looked at her.

She didn't get up. She looked across the room at Jacob, and her face was calm, and very tired, and almost kind.

"What was that one law?" she said. "Jake. The one that stopped Sam. In September. When the kid was born, and he was coming up through those trees out there with the whole pack behind him, Paul and Jared and Quil and Embry and the little ones, to kill her." She tipped her head toward the glass wall, toward the black lawn and the blacker woods. "You remember. You were standing on the porch. You'd just come out of this room. What stopped him?"

Edward watched it hit Jacob.

He watched it go through him the way cold water goes through a man who's just jumped off a dock: all at once, everywhere. He watched the color go out of Jacob's face, and the fists open, and stay open.

"Say it," said Leah.

Jacob didn't say anything.

"*Say it, Jake.*"

"No wolf," Jacob said hoarsely, "can harm the object of another wolf's imprint."

"What's the rest?"

"It's absolute."

"*Absolute,*" Leah said. "Not most of the time. Not unless you're really mad. Not unless he did something to my head. Absolute. That's the oldest law we've got. Older than the treaty. Older than the stories, probably." She looked around the room. "That's you, Jake. And Seth. And Sam. And Paul and Jared and Quil and Embry and Collin and Brady. Every wolf on this coast. None of you can lay a finger on him. You couldn't if you tried. You'd get up to him and your body just wouldn't go." She looked at Jacob. "You remember what that felt like. For Sam. You were in his head when it happened."

Jacob remembered. Edward could hear him remember. He could hear Sam Uley, in Jacob's memory, at the edge of these woods in September, the great black wolf at the head of the pack with every muscle bunched to spring, and Jacob in front of him, between him and the house, and the moment Sam had understood. The way his whole body had stopped like a car hitting a wall.

"And you guys." Leah turned to the Cullens. To Carlisle at the foot of the stairs, and Rosalie on them, and Jasper in the doorway, and Emmett with Layla's mug still in his hand. "You've got a treaty with us. Right? You've had it since nineteen-thirty-whatever. And part of that treaty is, you don't hurt us. Any of us. Or anything that's ours." She held Carlisle's eyes. "You go after him, you've hurt a wolf's imprint. That's going after the pack. Jake. What happens to the treaty if one of them hurts somebody's imprint?"

Jacob closed his eyes.

"It's done," he said. "It's over."

"It's over," said Leah. "And then it's all of Sam's pack on this lawn again, except this time they're allowed. And I'm on the wrong side. With *him*."

The room was absolutely silent.

"Leah," said Carlisle, at last, very quietly. "No one in this house would ever harm someone you'd imprinted on. Not knowingly. Not ever."

"Speak for yourself," said Rosalie.

Everybody looked at her.

She looked back at them from the stairs, cool and blond and perfectly unbothered. Then she looked at Leah, for a long moment. Edward heard her think about it. He heard her think about Emmett, the first time she'd seen him, lying in the leaves in the Appalachians with a bear's claws in his chest. What she'd have done to anyone who'd tried to take him off her afterward. How much she'd have cared what kind of man he'd been.

"Fine," said Rosalie, and looked away.

"That means she likes you," said Emmett.

"Emmett, I swear to God—"

"It does! That's twice now. That's two people she likes this month. That's a record."

Leah stood up off the arm of the couch.

"And for the record," she said, to all of them, "if you went after him, it wouldn't matter. He'd take you apart." She said it simply, the way you'd say it was raining. "I watched him take Layla apart without moving his feet. I watched him do other stuff after that, in the tent, that I can't tell you about. He's not going to sit there and let Emmett hug him to death. And I'd have to stand there and watch him do it to you, all of you, and I couldn't stop him." She paused. Edward heard the next part come up from somewhere much deeper than the rest of it, and heard her look at it, and decide to say it anyway. "And I'm not sure I'd want to."

Esme made a small sound.

Leah looked at her. And something in her face changed. Just a little. "Not you," she said. "Okay? Not you. You make the lasagna." Then she looked at the rest of them again, and the face went back. "So don't."

She went into the kitchen. She picked up her plate. She ate the last two bites of lasagna standing at the counter, and rinsed the plate under the tap, and put it in the rack, and turned around.

"I'm going home," she said. "I'm tired."

"Lee—" said Seth.

"I'm *tired*, Seth. I've had a day." She came back out into the living room. "I'm going home and I'm going to take a shower and I'm going to sleep for about a year. You should take Layla home. She looks like she's going to fall over."

"I'm fine," said Layla.

"You look like you got hit by a truck."

"I got hit by a *bullet*," said Layla. "Near a bullet. Next to a—you know what I mean." She closed her eyes again. "I have to make another bead tonight. For Seth. The last one's in pieces."

"You have to sleep," said Seth.

"I'll sleep after."

"It takes all night."

"So I'll sleep tomorrow."

Leah went to the back door. She stopped there, with her hand on the frame, and looked back at all of them.

Edward was listening to her when she did it, and he heard the line.

He'd been hearing it, faintly, for the last hour, without quite knowing what he was hearing. A sort of pull in her mind, a constant faint pressure to one side, like the feeling of standing next to a big magnet with a nail in your pocket. It ran out of the middle of her and away east, through the wall of the house and across miles of dark forest, toward the Bogachiel. Toward the ridge. Toward wherever he was. He'd thought it was a thought. It was simpler than that: a direction, built into her now, like north.

And she was standing at the back door of his house with her hand on the frame, and every inch of her was leaning east.

She went west.

He heard her choose it. He heard her stand there for one second with the pull of it going through her like a current and look at it, and decide, plainly, without any drama at all: *Not tonight. Home.* And she went out across the lawn in the rain toward the tree line, the opposite way from the ridge, and behind the big cedar where she'd changed he heard the tearing sound, and then the wolf was gone into the trees, west, toward the sea and La Push and her mother's house, and the pull in her mind stretched out behind her like a rope paying out off a spool, and didn't break.

---

He went out on the porch afterward.

He stood at the top of the steps in the rain and listened to her go. Behind him, in the house, Seth was helping Layla up off the couch with an arm round her waist, the way you'd help an old woman, and Layla was letting him, which said a great deal about her back. Jacob had gone out the kitchen door and phased and was running the near loop, hard, the way he always did when he was upset and there was nobody left to fight. Alice was at the glass wall again with her eyes half closed. Esme was wrapping the last of the lasagna in foil for Seth to take.

He thought about a stone room in a tower in Italy, and an old man at a telephone, laughing.

He'd been afraid of that tower since March. He'd built his whole life around being afraid of it, lately. Around the knowledge that someday Alice would look up from the kitchen table and say *they've decided*, and everything in this house that he loved would be measured against the patience of three men who'd been patient since before Christ. He'd thought that ignorance was the only thing keeping them safe. Theirs. Aro's. He'd stood on this porch two weeks ago and wondered how long it could possibly last.

And now Aro had stopped looking. Because someone had called him.

And somewhere out there in the dark, on a ridge he couldn't remember the way to, was a man who'd walked into Seth Clearwater's head this afternoon as if he owned it. Edward had seen Seth's memory of it four or five times by now. The cold pressure. The fingers. The calm, unhurried way the man had gone through the boy's thoughts, flipping, flipping, *there, there, there*, and taken what he wanted, and gone on. Edward had heard every mind on this peninsula for most of a century. He'd never once heard anyone else in it.

He'd always thought, in some small private part of himself he didn't show his family, that it would be a relief. Meeting someone else who knew what it was like. Someone who'd understand what he meant when he said it was like a room where everyone was talking at once.

He thought about Seth's memory of the man's face, standing on the granite with his back to them, hearing them, not bothering to turn round.

He'd wanted, all his life, to meet someone else who could do what he did. He was starting to understand, standing on the porch in the rain, that he'd always assumed that person would feel about it the way he did.

He went back inside.

---

### III. Harry

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — 7:04 p.m.*

The crater was still giving off heat when he got there. Six hours of rain and it was still breathing a faint dry heat up out of the black mud at the bottom, which said something about the round he'd built in August that he'd want to write down later.

He stood on the edge of the granite slab with a torch in one hand that he didn't need and a thermos of tea in the other that he did, and looked at the mess.

It was a proper mess. He'd been in a hurry at the time. A six-foot crater in the moss two feet in front of where the witch had been standing, black mud thrown out of it in a ring, the edges sheared clean in a way no Muggle would ever put down to nature. A boulder twenty feet to the left that had come apart in a shower of gravel and fire. A hemlock limb the size of a man's leg lying in the moss where a Stunner had taken it off. Another tree, further back, with a fresh split down one side where her Bludgeoning Hex had gone in low and found something. A furrow twenty feet long through the sword ferns where she'd gone backward off the slab. A patch of moss to the right of where he'd been standing that was still, faintly, not quite moss: she'd transfigured it into roots, and he'd stepped out of them, and nobody had turned them back.

And the snare stone, under the slab, spent. He could feel it from here. A fist-sized river stone he'd carved in September on a picnic table at the Bogachiel campground, sitting in its hole under the granite with nothing left in it.

He put the thermos down on the rock and the torch beside it and took his coat off and hung it on nothing, at shoulder height, where it stayed.

Then he stopped, because the line had moved.

It moved the way it had been moving all evening, a tug in the middle of his chest, a little left of centre, where his hand kept going to. This time it came as a change in what was at the other end, after an hour of the long steady drag of her running. The wolf was going quiet. Something else was coming up in its place, all at once, the way she'd changed behind the spruce at half six, from the middle out, like a match struck in a cupped hand.

She'd stopped running. She was at the edge of the Cullen lawn. He knew it the way you know which room of your own house you're standing in with your eyes shut. She was kneeling in wet cedar needles, human, with the rain on her back and the pack gone out of her head, and she was holding still and feeling for him.

He held still back.

It was a stupid thing to do. He knew it was a stupid thing to do while he was doing it. He was standing on a ridge in the rain in his shirtsleeves with a cleanup to do and a deadline he'd set himself and a vampire in his back room, and he stood there for about four seconds with his eyes shut and let her find him, the way you'd let a dog in the dark find your hand.

Then he opened his eyes and went and looked at the wall.

He'd felt the other two hit it, an hour ago, while he was standing on the bench outside the tent with his hand still up in the air. He'd felt them come pouring into her head and run straight into it and stop. And he'd felt them walk up and down the length of it since, the brother and the big ginger one, like dogs going up and down a fence, sniffing, looking for the gap. He'd felt the brother's worry come up against it again and again, *what did he put in her head*, and he'd stood there and thought, right. That's what it looks like from their side. Some stranger in a suit put his hand in her head and left something in there.

It wasn't going to stay a small problem. Not with that lot. Not with the Cullen boy, who could hear it too.

He'd thought about it on the way out here. He'd thought about it the way he thought about most things, which was by working out what he had and what he could afford to give up.

The oath was the Department's. Everything it was holding in her head was the Department's: the job, the building, the room with the brains, the old man in the tower, the word *strike*. He couldn't touch any of that, and he wouldn't if he could. He'd sworn on a stone older than the Ministry, and he'd meant it, and the fact that the stone had gone and decided that a Quileute girl in cut-off shorts was him didn't change what he'd sworn.

But it had grabbed his name along with all the rest, because his name had been in the same breath. *Harry Evans*, the legend, the passport, the Islington library card: fine. That was the Department's too. That was a working cover and it could stay where it was.

The other name wasn't.

The other name had never been the Department's. It had been his since before he could walk. It had been in every newspaper in Britain since he was fifteen months old. It had been on a Chocolate Frog card since 1999, which Ron had never got over. It was carved on a bench in the grounds at Hogwarts that he'd never once sat on, and on a plaque in a village in the West Country under the name of a man and a woman he didn't remember. Half the witches and wizards in the world knew it. It was about the least secret thing he owned.

So he reached down the line and took it back.

He didn't know how. He'd think about how later. He did it with the same part of himself that had yanked the line on the bench at one o'clock without asking him first, and opened his own wards like a door. He reached down through his own chest and across the ten miles of dark forest between the ridge and the Cullen lawn, and he found the wall in her head, the oath's grey hand, and he found the one small thing it was holding that was his and nobody else's. And he took it off the hook the way you'd take your own coat off a peg in somebody else's hall.

The oath let him. It didn't even stir. It looked at what he was taking and recognised it, and agreed, as far as a three-thousand-year-old binding agreed with anything: *Yours. Not ours. Fine.*

He felt it go over to her side. He felt her feel it land.

And he felt her, kneeling in the needles with her hand on a cedar, go *oh*.

He didn't get words. He didn't need words. He felt her understand what he'd done, and why, all at once, the way she seemed to understand everything about him that came down the line: fast, sideways, without needing it explained. And then something else came up the line at him, quick and a bit grudging and shot through with something that made his chest go tight, and he worked out a second later that it was *thanks*.

"Yeah," Harry said, to the dark, to the crater. "You're welcome."

He picked the thermos up and had some tea, and then he went to work.

---

Hiding was the amateur's mistake. That was the first lesson anybody learned, the first time they were sent to tidy up after something on a hillside in Wales or a beach in Norfolk. You couldn't hide a crater. You couldn't hide a broken tree. People went walking. People had dogs. People were nosy and bored and carried cameras, and whatever you hid, they found, and then they found the fact that it had been hidden, which was worse.

So you told it a different story.

Lightning was the obvious one. It had been a stormy week. It had been a stormy *year*, by the standards of anywhere on earth that wasn't the Olympic Peninsula, and the National Weather Service had issued a thunderstorm advisory for the coast for Sunday night that he'd heard on the Explorer's radio on the way back from the library in Port Angeles. A ridge was the right sort of place. A big flat granite slab with a stand of stunted hemlock round it was the right sort of place.

He walked the ground first. Slowly, all of it, in a widening spiral out from the slab with his hands in his pockets, the way a bloke from the Met had taught him to walk a scene in 2001 for a job in Croydon. He didn't look for what was wrong. He'd done the wrong; he knew where all of it was. He looked for what lightning would need.

It would need something to hit.

There was a hemlock snag fifteen feet beyond the crater. Dead for years, by the look of it: grey, barkless at the top, the tallest thing on that end of the ridge by about ten feet. He looked at it for a while.

Then he split it.

He did it from the top down, in one line, the way the real thing went. He'd seen a tree in the New Forest that had been hit in the summer of 2002 and he'd stood looking at it for a long time, and he built it from that memory now: a single seam of heat, white and narrow, run straight down the heart of the snag from its broken crown to the roots in about a fiftieth of a second. Whatever sap was still in the wood went to steam. The snag came apart down the middle with a sound like a rifle shot that went echoing off down the Bogachiel valley, and both halves stood there smoking in the rain, and a long spiral strip of what little bark it had left peeled off one side like the skin off an apple and landed in the moss.

He looked at it with his head on one side. Then he went and stood under it and ran the heat the rest of the way, down into the ground, and let it spread.

That was the part people got wrong. Lightning didn't stop at the tree. It went into the earth and out across it, looking for the easiest way to go, and it left tracks. Branching scorch-lines in the moss, fanning out from the roots like the veins in a leaf. And underneath, where it went down through sand or gravel, it fused the stuff into glass: crooked, hollow, knobbly tubes like the roots of some mad plant, that geologists dug up and put in museums. Fulgurites. He'd read about them in a book in the Islington library on his first week back from Uganda, because he'd had a lot of time to read, that winter, and nobody to talk to.

He built the scorch-lines out across the moss, crooked and branching, hissing where they went. He built them out toward the crater. Then he went down to the crater and stood on its rim and looked at it, and remade it.

He didn't fill it in. He made it worse. He reshaped the edges, roughed the sheared rim into something torn and lumpy, pulled the thrown mud out further in one direction, toward the snag, and blew out the side of the crater nearest the tree so that it looked like what it now was supposed to be: a pit dug out of the soft ground by a branch of the strike going down and finding water and turning it to steam in a single instant, the way they did. It happened. There was a photograph in a forestry paper he'd read of a crater at the base of a Douglas fir in Oregon you could have parked a car in.

Then he drew silica up out of the gravel at the bottom of the crater, all of it he could find, and made it into a fulgurite. A good one. Two feet long, forked twice, grey-green and glassy and crumbling, running down from the bottom of the pit at a crooked angle back toward the snag. He left the top of it showing.

The boulder he left alone. Lightning broke rocks. It flaked granite off in sheets when the water in the cracks turned to steam, and the boulder had come apart in a way that would pass if you didn't know what a Blasting Curse looked like close up. He ran one more scorch-line out to it from the main strike, just to join the dots. The hemlock limb the Stunner had taken off he left where it was. Storms took branches. Storms took a lot of branches.

The roots he turned back into moss. It took a second; her work was good and it didn't want to let go. He had to take it apart from the inside, the way you'd unpick a knot, and when it came undone the patch went back to moss with a soft sigh, a bit torn up, a bit flattened, exactly the way moss would look if something heavy had been standing on it during a storm.

And the furrow. Twenty feet of crushed sword ferns where she'd gone backward off the slab through the air, ending at the nurse log. He went down the length of it with his hand held out flat at waist height and the ferns came up off the ground behind him as he went, one at a time, each frond lifting and straightening and shaking the water off itself, like a lot of hands going up at the back of a classroom. When he got to the end he turned round and looked back up the line of it.

You'd never know.

He went back to the slab, finally, and knelt on it, and put his palm on the cold wet granite over the place where the snare stone was buried, and drew the stone up out of its hole through six inches of rock. It came up into his hand like a fish surfacing. Spent. Grey. Still holding a little heat, from what it had done at half eleven this morning. He put it in his trouser pocket. Then he laid a burn across the surface of the slab, where it had been: a starburst, a little scorched flower of branching lines a yard across, the kind of thing lightning drew on rock when it jumped from one place to another in a hurry. He'd lay a new snare stone somewhere along this line tomorrow. Somewhere else.

Then he stood up, and drained the residue.

That was the part that mattered. The rest was for Muggles and hikers and park rangers with clipboards. This was for anybody who might come up here with a wand.

Everything that had been done on this ridge with magic had left a stain. You couldn't see it, but it was there, the way the smell of a cigarette is still in a room an hour after it's been put out. Her Stunners. Her ropes. Her Blasting Curse. Her Disjunction, which had left a pale fizzing smear in the air above the slab like a thumbprint on glass. Her Apparition, which had left the worst stain of the lot: a ragged torn place in the air twenty feet to the left of the slab, where she'd come through from fourteen miles away in a panic with the kitchen radio still going behind her, and torn a hole in the world to do it. He'd been able to feel it from Forks. Anybody halfway sensitive would be able to feel it from the road.

His own work had left nothing. His own work never did. He'd built his sinks into everything he did out here, every spell, every step, so that whatever he did was eaten up as he did it, like footprints filling with sand.

He opened them now. All of them. He stood on the slab and let them run.

It came up off the ridge the way frost comes off a field when the sun hits it. Off the granite. Off the moss. Out of the air. Her Stunners came up out of the hemlocks they'd hit like a fog lifting off water. Her Blasting Curse came up out of the gravel. The thumbprint of the Disjunction came off the air above the slab and folded itself up small and went. And the torn place where she'd come through, the worst of it, he left till last, and stood in front of it, and closed it. Carefully. It had ragged edges, like a hole punched through paper. He worked them flat and smoothed them over and sealed them, one at a time, the way you'd darn a sock, and when he'd done it the air on that side of the slab was just air.

He stood there for a while afterward, in the rain, with his shirt soaked through, and looked at the ridge. And then he walked it again from the top, slowly, in a widening spiral, with his hands in his pockets, and looked at it the way a ranger would. And then the way a Muggle geologist would. And then the way an Auror from Seattle with a good nose and a bad attitude would.

A lightning strike. A good one. A ridge in the rain after a storm, a dead tree split top to bottom, a crater in the soft ground at its feet with a glass root at the bottom you could put in a museum, scorch-marks fanning out across the moss, one rock shattered, a branch down. Nothing on it but weather.

He picked up the thermos, and found the tea had gone cold, and drank it anyway.

---

He'd been feeling her the whole time.

He hadn't been able to help that, and after about the first twenty minutes he'd stopped trying. She was ten miles off to the north-west, in the white house, at the end of the long drive off the highway, and she was standing in a kitchen eating something with a lot of cheese in it. He could tell it was a kitchen because he could feel the counter under her hip. He could tell about the cheese because she was enjoying it so much.

And then, while he was splitting the snag, he'd felt her get angry.

He'd stopped with his hand up. He'd thought for a second that something had gone wrong, that the ginger one had gone for her, or the blonde on the stairs he'd seen in the brother's head. And then the shape of it had come down the line clearer, and it turned out to be the kind of angry he'd felt off Hermione a hundred times, in the common room at school, when Ron had said something unbelievably thick in front of witnesses and she'd decided she was going to take the next twenty minutes to explain to him exactly how thick it was.

He didn't get the words. He got bits. He got her pointing something at someone. He got her holding up her fingers one at a time, *one, two, three*, and he got the third finger come up with a hot hard bright little spike of pride on it that came down the line at him like a thrown dart, *also a wolf*, and he stood on the ridge in the rain with his hand still up and laughed out loud. It went off down the valley like the snag had.

And then the last of it, a sort of satisfied full stop, the feeling of somebody setting a plate down on a table: *See? Better.*

He'd stood there grinning like an idiot for about a minute. Then he'd gone back to the snag.

It had been about forty minutes after that, when he was halfway down the fern furrow with his hand held out flat, that he'd felt it close on her throat.

He'd stopped dead.

It had come down the line at him so sharp and so sudden that for a second he'd thought it was his own. He'd felt the cold of it. He'd felt the grip. He knew that grip; he'd carried it under his collarbone for eight years and felt it close on him twice, and he'd have known it anywhere, the way you'd know your own father's hand on your shoulder in the dark. And he'd felt it close on her. On the inside of *her* throat, ten miles away, in a room full of vampires. Gently. Patiently. The way it had closed on his in a pub in Belfast in 2002, when he'd had six pints and started to tell Fennick something he shouldn't.

He'd felt her stop fighting it. He'd felt her breathe. He'd felt it ease, and sit back, and settle, and leave its thumb resting where it was.

He'd stood in the ferns with his hand still out and every one of the fronds he'd lifted standing up straight around his legs, and felt something go through him that he didn't have a good name for. It came up from the bottom. It came up through the three locks under his breastbone like they were wet paper, and for one second he felt the thing down there in the dark get to its feet and put its head down and *snarl*, a sound so low and so long that it rattled his teeth, and he understood that it wanted to find whatever had put a hand on her and take that hand off at the wrist. And that the hand was his.

His. His oath. His Department. His promise on a black stone in a room with one chair. He'd brought that thing to this peninsula in his own chest, and it had gone down the line he hadn't asked for and laid itself across her head like a lid, and now it had its hand round her throat.

There wasn't anything he could do about it. He'd known that standing on the bench at half six with Gary looking at him. He couldn't take it off her. He didn't know how it had got there. He didn't think anybody living did.

He'd put the thing in the cellar back down. It had taken a while.

Then he'd finished the ferns. And somewhere about the bottom of the furrow, with the last frond coming up and shaking itself off, he'd decided what he was going to do about it, which was the only thing he could think of that he *could* do.

He was going to ring Croaker.

---

He found the first of them at ten to nine, because he went looking.

It was the obvious thing to do and he'd have been embarrassed not to. Croaker had said the Americans were making noises. The Americans didn't make noises about things they couldn't hear. So somewhere on this peninsula, the Americans had an ear.

He stood on the slab and cast for it. Wide, and quiet, and slow: a long flat pulse that went out from the ridge in every direction under the rain like a ripple across a pond, looking for anything that was listening. Anything with an enchantment on it that faced outward. Anything that would notice a bloke on a ridge in the dark if he started doing magic.

It found three.

The nearest was about six miles north, up on the shoulder of a hill above the Sol Duc road. He went there.

It was nailed into a Douglas fir at about head height, on the uphill side, where nobody walking the road would ever see it. A rod of brass the length of his forearm, driven into the trunk like a spike, with a little glass dome on the end of it the size of a pocket watch. Inside the dome, a needle, very fine, quivering. Under the needle, a drum, turning so slowly that he had to watch it for a full minute to be sure it was moving at all, with a strip of parchment wound round it, and on the parchment, a line in black ink. He could see the line through the glass. It wobbled up and down a hair's breadth either side of the middle, steady as breathing.

There was a little brass plate screwed to the rod under the dome. He bent and read it by the light of nothing.

*MAGICAL CONGRESS OF THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA. MAJOR INVESTIGATION DEPT. — FIELD REGISTER No. 14 (OLYMPIC). TAMPERING WITH THIS DEVICE IS A FEDERAL MAGICAL OFFENSE.*

"Course it is," said Harry.

He didn't touch it. He walked all the way round the tree first, slowly, looking at the bark, at the ground, at the air. There was a notice-me-not on it, a decent one but nothing he'd lose sleep over. There was something on it that would very much notice if anybody touched the rod or the dome, and tell somebody about it. And there was a fine thread of something going away from it to the south-east, very faint, toward Seattle: a link. Somewhere in a building in Seattle, he'd bet a month's pay, there was another drum with another needle on it, and every time this one moved, that one did too.

So whatever this had heard, Seattle had heard. As it happened.

He stood in front of the tree with his hands in his pockets and read the drum.

He didn't unroll it. He couldn't, without the thing noticing. He read back through it the way you'd read through a closed book with a light held behind the pages, layer by layer, a fortnight at a time, back along the spool. It was a crude bit of kit. It didn't know direction, or distance, or what kind of magic it was hearing, or who; all it knew was *how much*, and *when*. A needle on a drum. A seismograph. The Americans were always very good at building things that worked and very bad at building things that were subtle.

The line at half eleven this morning went off the top of the parchment.

He'd known it would. He looked at it anyway. A spike, straight up, like a cliff, at eleven fifty-two, and then a jagged ragged mess of smaller spikes for about thirty seconds after it, and then the line falling back, wobbling, settling. The witch arriving. The witch throwing everything she had. He could read the Disjunction in it, the long fat bulge in the middle of the mess, because he knew what he was looking for. Anybody in Seattle wouldn't. They'd just see that for half a minute, somewhere within range of Register Fourteen, somebody had let off the magical equivalent of a box of fireworks.

There was nothing of his on it. He went back over it carefully to be sure. Not the snare. Not the Apparition. Not the bullet. The sinks had eaten everything.

He kept going back along the spool.

And there it was. He'd half wondered. There were little bumps going back weeks. Tiny ones, mostly, nearly every day, a little lift and fall in the line at breakfast and again in the evening, the size of a hiccup. Somebody using magic to do the washing-up. Somebody lighting the stove with their wand because they couldn't be bothered with matches. Somebody heating a bath. And a bigger one, early in October, that he'd have bet was a kitchen spell gone wrong. And then the fourth of November, at about a quarter past six in the evening, a big one: a long steady swell, very smooth, held for something like ten minutes and then dropped all at once, like a breath let out. A whole yard full of rain held still. And the next afternoon, at about four, another: a slow rising flood that went on and on, the line climbing and climbing, a wash of soft light filling a long white room up to the ceiling.

He stood in front of the tree in the rain for a while.

"Oh, Layla," he said.

It wasn't her fault. Not really. She'd grown up American, under MACUSA, where a kid who did a spell in front of a Muggle could get her whole family into a cell, and where you learned from the cradle to look over your shoulder. But she'd come up here to her gran's house at the end of the world to rest, and nobody had told her the Americans had stuck an ear in a tree six miles up the road. Why would they? It was a national park. It was the back of beyond. Nobody had done any serious magic on this peninsula for thirty years except her grandmother, whose bees didn't register on anything because they were a different kind of magic entirely, older and slower, and grew rather than cast.

And then the granddaughter had turned up, and done the washing-up by wand every morning for six weeks. And held a whole yard of rain in the air. And lit up a house full of vampires for a toddler.

*Somebody there has noticed unusual magical activity on the Olympic Peninsula over the last two months.*

That was it. That was the noise. He'd assumed, when Croaker said it, that it was him. That somewhere along the line, laying two hundred and eleven snare stones across three thousand square miles of national park, he'd slipped. It had been her. It had been the witch who kept bees on Calawah Way, lighting up a field register like a Christmas tree for two months, and some bored bloke at a desk in Seattle watching the needle go up every morning at seven and thinking *huh*.

And this morning the needle had gone off the top of the drum.

He went and looked at the other two. They were the same. One on a ridge above Lake Ozette, out to the west, near the coast; one up the Hoh, south and east of Forks, in the trees above the visitor centre. Three registers. A triangle round the north end of the peninsula with Forks somewhere near the middle of it. Same brass, same domes, same plates, Fifteen and Sixteen. Same spike at eleven fifty-two going off the top of the parchment. The Ozette one a bit smaller. The Hoh one, nearest, biggest.

Three needles, three distances. Even a crude bit of kit could do sums with three. They'd have a circle by now, he thought, on a map on a wall in Seattle. A rough one, miles across. With Forks and a lot of the Bogachiel valley inside it.

He stood under the third tree with the Hoh going past in the dark below him and thought it through.

He couldn't touch the registers. He couldn't touch the drums. The moment he laid a finger on any of it, Seattle would know somebody had, and an Auror who'd been mildly curious would become extremely interested. He couldn't take back two months of Layla doing the washing-up. Seattle had all that already, on their own drum, in their own building. That part of it was gone.

What he could do was give the loud day an ending.

The trick was a better story. Somebody had had a fight in the woods south of Forks this morning, loud and short, and then what? Then nothing. Then the woods had gone quiet. That was a story with no ending, and stories with no endings were the ones people came to look at.

So he'd give it one.

He went to Lake Crescent first. Twenty-five miles north-east, where the highway ran along the south shore under the mountains and the water was black and still and very deep. He came out on the gravel at the edge of it in the dark at nineteen minutes to ten, at the far end of a picnic area that was closed for the season, and he turned off his sinks, all of them, for the first time since September. It felt like taking his coat off in the cold.

Then he had a fight with nobody.

He did it in about twenty seconds. He threw a handful of things out over the lake, at nothing, at the dark: a Stunner, two hexes, a Shield Charm raised and dropped, a blast of something he let go off over the water with a flat bang that sent a cloud of ducks up off the shallows quacking. He shaped the whole of it the way he'd read her fight in the drum, the spike and then the ragged mess after, only smaller. Further away. Twenty-five miles further north-east from where it had started, and about a tenth the size. Somebody running. Somebody chasing them. Then he Apparated to Port Angeles, to the far end of Ediz Hook, the long thin spit of gravel that curled out into the strait past the paper mill, where the Coast Guard station was dark and the lights of Victoria were a faint smear on the other side of the water, and did it again, smaller still, at two minutes to ten. A couple of hexes into the sea. A last little flurry.

And then he turned his sinks back on, and stood at the end of the spit in the wind off the water, and was nothing.

He'd checked the ferry timetable on his first week in Port Angeles because he checked everything. The *Coho* made its first run to Victoria in the morning. Somebody in Seattle could make what they liked of that. A fight in the woods, and a chase north, and a last scuffle down by the water, and then nothing at all, because whoever it was had gone across the strait into Canada, where they were somebody else's problem. That was an ending. That was a story you could write up and file and forget, with a polite letter to the Canadians.

Would it hold? He didn't know. It would hold for a bit. It would hold long enough for an Auror from Seattle to have to choose which end to start at.

He stood at the end of the Hook for a while with his hands in his pockets.

He'd felt her leave the white house at about twenty past eight. He'd been halfway down the fern furrow, and he'd felt her stand in the back doorway of that house with her hand on the frame for one second, and he'd felt the line go tight as a bowstring, pointing east, straight at him, so hard he'd put a hand out to a tree. He'd felt every bit of her lean toward him. And then he'd felt her turn the other way and go west, toward the sea, toward her mother's house, and the line stretch out behind her like a rope paying off a spool.

She'd chosen home. Plainly. Without any fuss. He'd felt her do it, and he'd stood in the ferns and found that something in him approved of it so much it was almost funny. That was the right call. That was the call he'd have made.

The thing in the cellar hadn't approved at all. It had paced for an hour.

---

### IV. Harry

*The tent — 10:14 p.m.*

The mirror was where he'd left it, on its nail in the hall by the telephone, in its pound-shop frame.

He stood and looked at it for a minute with his hand on the back of his neck.

He'd come in out of the rain at five past ten and checked the circle first, because you checked the asset. Casimir was where he'd left him. On his back on the stone, rigid, statue-still, with his long black hair fanned out under his head and his ruined little finger lying black on the flagstone, in the dead woman's red jacket. His eyes had come round to Harry when the black oak door opened and stayed on him, red and flat and steady, the whole time he walked round the silver checking the rings, and Harry had looked back at them and felt nothing about them at all. He'd closed the door. He'd put the kettle on. He'd put the kettle back off again without making anything.

Then he'd come out here and stood in front of the mirror.

And the thing in the cellar had stood up.

He felt it do it. He felt it get to its feet down there behind the three locks, slowly, and lower its head, and look at him. It didn't growl. That was the bit that bothered him. It had growled at him plenty over the years: when he was hungry, when he was tired, when the moon was doing whatever it did, when somebody had stood too close to him on the Tube. This was quieter. It just stood there in the dark and looked up at him with its ears back, very still, the way a dog looks at you when you've picked up the lead and it knows exactly where you're going and it would rather you didn't.

It knew what the mirror was. It had been in his chest for eight years of reports to Croaker. It knew what the man on the other side of it did, and what the building he did it in was for. And it knew, now, with a certainty that had come up through the floor of him at about one o'clock this afternoon and hadn't gone back down, what the Department would want if it ever found out about her.

*Don't,* was what it was saying. Plain as day. *Don't you dare.*

"I know," Harry said to it. Out loud, in the hall, like an idiot. "I know. Shut up."

It didn't shut up. It didn't make any noise to begin with. It just stood there.

"He's owed," Harry said.

It looked at him.

"I lied to him," Harry said. "I stood here four hours ago and looked him in the face and lied to him. Eight years. Eight years and I've never once lied to him. Not once. Not when I was nineteen and wrong about everything, and not in Minsk, and not after Ginny, when he asked me if I was fit for duty and I wasn't. I told him I wasn't. He sent me home for a month." He stopped. "I'm not starting now. Not for anybody. Not even for her. *Especially* not for her, because if I start lying to him for her, then she's the reason I'm a liar, and I'm not putting that on her. She's got enough."

The thing in the cellar didn't sit down. But its ears came forward, a little.

"And it's got its hand round her throat," Harry said. "His oath. Ours. I can't do anything about that. But I'm not having it sit there on her while I stand here pretending to the man who runs it that nothing's happened."

He took the mirror down off its nail.

"Croaker," he said.

The glass went grey, and then dark, and then it was the same room. The same desk, the same drifts of paper like snow on a hillside, the same single green-shaded lamp. Except the lamp was off now, and the room was full of the thin cold blue light of an early winter morning in London coming in from a window somewhere to the left, and Saul Croaker was standing up with his back to the desk, by that window, with a cup of something in his hand, looking out.

He didn't turn round.

"Twice in one night, Potter," he said.

"Sir."

"You've never rung me twice in a night. You've never rung me twice in a *week*." The old man drank whatever was in the cup. "It's a quarter past six. I'd just got the tea brewed." He still didn't turn round. "Go on, then."

Harry looked at the back of the old man's bald head in the blue light. He'd stood in front of Croaker a lot of times. He'd stood in front of him at nineteen in a borrowed suit for the interview that wasn't an interview, while Croaker sat behind the desk reading a file and didn't look up for eleven minutes. He'd stood in front of him the morning after Minsk with somebody else's blood still under his nails because he hadn't been able to get it out. He'd stood in front of him the morning after Ginny had packed her things and gone.

"I lied to you, sir," Harry said.

The old man turned round.

He didn't hurry about it. He turned round from the window with the cup still in his hand and looked out of the mirror at Harry with those pale tap-water eyes, and his face did exactly what it always did, which was nothing. Then he walked back to the desk, and set the cup down on a drift of paper, and lowered himself into the chair behind it, slowly, the way old men did, and folded his hands on the blotter.

"When?" he said.

"Four hours ago. When you asked me if there was anything else."

"And there was."

"Yes, sir."

"Go on."

Harry told him.

He'd thought, standing in front of the mirror, that it was going to be hard. That he'd have to find a way into it. Some way to put it that wouldn't sound like a man who'd gone soft in the head from three months of rain. He found when he opened his mouth that he didn't need to find anything; he'd been reporting to this man for eight years, and the reporting voice just came, flat and orderly, and did the work for him, the way it did on a job when you had to tell somebody something nobody would want to hear.

He told Croaker about the ridge. The two wolves and the witch. Taking off his glasses to have a proper look at the grey one, because she was the only thing on the ridge he couldn't read. Looking her in the eye.

He told him what had happened then, as best he understood it, which wasn't very well. The cables. The drop. The line, going into her and down to whatever she had under her, and coming back. Tying itself off at both ends. He told him what the Quileute called it. He told him he'd read it in her brother's head that afternoon, in a dozen other people's memories, and that it was meant to go one way, and that this went both.

He told him she'd followed him to the tent. Through his wards. That the wards had opened for her like a door.

He told him that he couldn't lie to her. That he'd tried, standing on the bench in the rain, and his mouth had told her his real name instead. That she couldn't lie to him either.

And then he told him about the kitchen.

He told him all of it. He told him he'd sat across his kitchen table from a twenty-year-old Quileute shapeshifter at a quarter to two in the afternoon and told her what he was. Where he worked. What the Department was for. What he'd come to the peninsula to do, and why, and what London meant to do about it in a year or two in a hill town in Tuscany. He told Croaker he'd put his hand under his own collarbone while he said it and waited for the oath to close, and that it hadn't. That it had sat there and listened to every word and not twitched. That it didn't think she was outside.

And he told him what had happened at half six, when she'd gone home and her pack had come pouring into her head. That the oath had gone down the line after her, twenty miles, and closed over everything she knew that was the Department's, the way it would have closed over his own throat. That tonight, in a house full of vampires, she'd tried to tell somebody something and it had shut her mouth for her.

He didn't tell him about the phone call. Croaker already had the phone call. He'd told him about the phone call four hours ago, in the version where he'd sat down and planned it and done it for the Department, and that version was true too. As far as it went.

He told him about the child. In the end, he did. He told him the thing in the white house wasn't on the file because nobody in London knew it existed, and that he'd been deciding for nine weeks whether to put it in a report, and that he'd decided this afternoon, crouched over a vampire with a phone in his hand. He told him that the old man in the tower didn't know about it, and wasn't going to.

And then he stopped.

The old man sat behind his desk in the thin blue London morning and looked at him out of the mirror and didn't say anything.

"So I'm compromised," Harry said. "Sir. That's the long and short of it. I'm compromised, and you should know it, and you should send somebody else." He made himself keep looking at the old man's face. "Because right now, if it came to a choice between the Department and her, it'd be her. Every single time. I'm not going to pretend to you it wouldn't. I couldn't stand here and tell you I'd pick you lot, and I'm not going to try."

He stopped again.

"That's it," he said. "That's the lot. I'm sorry I lied to you, sir."

Croaker looked at him for a long time.

Long enough that Harry could hear the rain on the canvas, a long way up. Long enough that the thing behind the locks, which had been standing the whole time with its head down and its ears back, waiting, slowly lifted its head.

Then the old man picked up his tea.

"I know," said Saul Croaker.

Harry didn't say anything.

"I knew four hours ago," said Croaker. He drank some tea. "You've reported to me two hundred and nineteen times, Potter. I keep count. In two hundred and eighteen of them you sounded exactly the same. Bored. Smug. A little bit hungry." He set the cup down. "Four hours ago you sounded like a man who'd been hit very hard in the back of the head with a shovel and was trying not to let on. And then you said *it's the rain, sir*, and I thought, well, there it is. He's found something."

"You didn't say anything."

"I wanted to see if you'd ring back." The old man's face didn't change. "I had a small wager on it with myself. I gave you until Friday."

"And?"

"And it's Monday," said Croaker. "Four hours. I'm almost disappointed. I'd have liked to see how long you could hold out." He leaned back in his chair. It creaked. "Sit down, Potter. You look like you're about to fall over. I can see the hall behind you; there's a chair by the telephone."

Harry sat down in the chair by the telephone.

"Now," said Croaker. "Let me tell you something you don't know, since you've been so generous with me." He folded his hands again. "You're not going anywhere."

"Sir—"

"You're exactly where you need to be." The old man said it the way he said everything, dry and flat, like a man reading out a tide table. "Don't interrupt me. I'm old and I've been up since Tuesday. I've run this Department for nineteen years. Do you know why I've kept you for eight of them?"

Harry thought about it.

"The wandless work," he said. "The—"

"No."

"The guns."

"God, no. I can't stand the guns. Nasty noisy things." Croaker picked his tea up again. "I'll tell you why. In your second year, you came back from Lagos with a man's memories in your head that you'd been told to bring back and hadn't been told what was in them, and you came and stood in front of this desk, and you said, *Sir, I don't think we should use these.* And then you told me why. To my face. And you were right." He drank. "And in Minsk you came back and told me exactly what you'd done, in a basement, to whom, in order, without being asked, and then you asked me if I'd like your resignation, and you meant it. And after your young lady left, I asked if you were fit, and you said no, and you weren't."

He set the cup down.

"And tonight," Croaker said, "you've rung me back four hours after lying to me, to tell me that you did, and that there's a girl, and that you'd pick her over me. That you'd pick her over all of this." He made a small gesture that took in the room, the desk, the drifts of paper, the building above and below it, the whole cold grey weight of Level Nine. "You rang me to tell me that."

"Yes, sir."

"Do you know how many people in this building would have rung me back?"

Harry didn't say anything.

"Two," said Croaker. "Including you. The other one's ninety-four and doesn't leave her office." He leaned forward, very slightly, and for a moment something moved in the tap-water eyes that might, in a different man, have been fondness. "That, Potter, is exactly why I will never get rid of you. You could set fire to the Atrium. You could take that girl to Vegas and marry her in a drive-through chapel in front of a man dressed as Elvis. I would still keep you. Because a man who'll pick nothing over this Department will sell it to the first person who offers him something. And a man who looks me in the eye and tells me there's something he'd pick over it, and then tells me what it is—" He sat back. "That's a man I can trust with the building."

The rain drummed on the canvas, a long way up.

Down under Harry's breastbone, behind three locks, the thing in the dark had gone very still. It was looking up at the mirror. It had its head on one side.

"What about the oath," Harry said, after a while.

"What about it?"

"It doesn't think she's outside. It thinks she's *me*."

"Yes. I gathered." Croaker looked faintly, distantly interested, the way he did when somebody brought him something new for a jar. "It's happened before, you know."

"It *has*?"

"Four times that I know of. In the records. Three were twins. Identical. You'd swear one in, and the other one would find he couldn't keep a secret from his brother either, and then the stone would sort of shrug and take the both of them." A dry, faint, ghost of something passed over his face. "The fourth was in 1652. A man called Hetherington. Swore the oath in the spring, met a woman in the summer, and by Michaelmas the stone couldn't tell them apart. It caused a great deal of trouble."

"What happened?"

"They got married," said Croaker. "And then she burned down a fair amount of London."

"That was 1666, sir."

"These things take time," said Croaker. "She was a very patient woman."

Harry looked at him.

The old man looked back with his face doing nothing at all, and Harry had the strong and not entirely comfortable feeling, which he'd had perhaps a dozen times in eight years, that he had no idea whether Saul Croaker was joking.

"I'm not going to ask you what she is," said Croaker. "The girl. I don't want to know. I'll want to know eventually, probably, because I want to know everything eventually; it's a vice. But I can wait." He picked the cup up and found it empty and put it down. "And no, before you ask, I'm not going to have her put in a jar."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were thinking it so loudly I could hear it in London. I'm not going to have her put in a jar." He said it flatly. "I'd very much like to, mind. A bond strong enough to make the stone on Level Nine think two people are one is the most interesting thing anybody's told me since that photograph came in from Volterra. I'd give a good deal to see it under glass." He paused. "But you'd come and get her. And you'd take the building apart to do it. And I need the building."

"Yes, sir," said Harry. "I would."

"I know you would. That's why I said it." The old man reached for a pen. "Now. Let's talk about what we're going to do with you."

"You just said—"

"I said I'm not sending anybody else. I didn't say I'm leaving you where you are as you are." Croaker found a clean sheet of grey paper under a drift of other ones and began to write on it in his small spidery hand. "You've got a field posting with a target and an end date. Specimen acquired. Job done. By the book, you pack up your tent, put your specimen in a crate, and come home." He didn't look up. "You're not going to do that."

"No, sir."

"No. And the Americans are going to start wondering why a freelance photographer from Islington has been taking pictures of moss in their national park for four months. And the people in this building who aren't me are going to start wondering why you haven't come home." The pen scratched. "So you need a reason to be there that I can write down. And it seems to me that from everything you've just told me, that stretch of coast has more going on per square mile than anywhere else I've got a man. A nest of the cold ones that the Department's marked as useful. Two packs of shapeshifters that nobody in London had heard of until nine weeks ago. A witch with a grandmother who grew a ward out of bees. Whatever it is that's living in that white house that you've just told me about and I'm going to pretend you didn't. And a field register belonging to the Americans, I don't doubt, because there always is." He looked up. "Am I wrong?"

"Three registers, sir. MACUSA. Major Investigation. The witch has been lighting them up for two months."

"Ah," said Croaker. "Well. That explains the noises." He wrote something else. "With the volume of things happening on that peninsula, Potter, you'd likely have been staying a good deal longer whether you'd met your girl or not. That's not a posting, that's a residency. The only question was ever what to call it."

"And what are you calling it?"

"Liaison," said Croaker.

Harry looked at him.

"Liaison to the Quileute," said the old man. He wrote it down. Harry watched him do it, upside down, in the mirror. *LIAISON.* "If that's what it takes to keep you there, so be it. There's never been one. There's never been any reason to have one, because until nine weeks ago nobody in this building knew the Quileute had anything a liaison could liaise with. Now we do. I'll write it up this morning. Standing posting, indefinite. Observe, report, maintain relations." He underlined something. "You'll report what you choose to report. I'll read what you send me. If there's something in the margins of it that you haven't written down, I'll know it's there, and I'll leave it alone. That's what a liaison is, Potter. Every liaison this Department has ever had has been compromised. That's the *point* of them. A man with a foot in each camp. The only thing I've ever needed to know about any of them is which foot they'd stand on when it came to it, and that they'd tell me." He put the pen down. "You just told me."

Harry didn't say anything for a while.

"And the Americans?" he said finally.

"Ah. The Americans." Croaker steepled his fingers. "Tribal land is sovereign land. The Quileute are a nation. They signed a treaty with the United States in 1855 and the United States has been trying to wriggle out of it ever since. MACUSA's never quite managed to work out what its jurisdiction is on a reservation; it's a swamp, legally, and nobody in New York has ever had the stomach to wade into it, because the last time anybody tried, it went very badly." He looked at Harry over his fingers. "A British wizard with no papers in an American national park is a felony. A guest of a sovereign tribal nation, on its land, at its invitation, is a diplomatic incident nobody in New York wants to have. Get yourself invited, Potter."

"Invited."

"By whoever they've got. A council. Elders. Somebody with the standing to say *he's ours, he's here because we want him here*. Get it in writing if you can. Get it in front of witnesses if you can't." Croaker's mouth did something that, on another man, might have been the start of a smile. "I gather you have an in."

"Sir."

"You're still not there, of course. As far as the rest of this building's concerned. As far as Kingsley's concerned. As far as anybody but you and me and your girl are concerned. You're a photographer. You're taking pictures of moss." He picked the pen back up. "But if the Americans come knocking on your tent flap, I'd like you to be able to tell them to go and knock on the tribal council's door instead."

"Yes, sir."

"Full report by Friday. The one you'd want a stranger to read." Croaker was writing again. "Leave her out of it. I'll know she's in the margins."

"Yes, sir."

"And Potter."

"Sir?"

The old man didn't look up. The pen scratched across the grey paper in the thin blue morning light.

"Whatever it is you've been keeping in the cellar," said Saul Croaker, "since Minsk. Feed it."

Harry went very still in the chair by the telephone.

He didn't say anything. He didn't think he could have. Under his breastbone, behind three locks he'd checked every morning for sixty-six days and every day for three years before that, the thing in the dark had lifted its head all the way up and was looking at the mirror with its ears straight forward, the way a dog looks at a stranger who's just said its name.

"How long have you known?" Harry said.

"I run the Department of Mysteries, Potter," said Croaker. "You were never anywhere near as quiet as you thought you were." He signed something with a flourish. "It doesn't bother me. It never did. You'd be surprised what's walked these corridors over the years. Some of it's still here." He blotted the page. "Feed it. Let it out now and then. It's been very patient with you, and in my experience the patient ones are the ones you want to be careful of."

He looked up. For the first time in the conversation, he looked as tired as he'd said he was.

"Go to bed," he said. "You look dreadful."

The mirror went dark.

---

Harry sat in the chair by the telephone for a long time with the mirror face-down on his knees.

The rain went on, a long way up. Down the hall, in the kitchen, the kettle he hadn't used ticked as it cooled. And under his breastbone, in the dark, the thing behind the locks stood where it was for a while, looking at the place where the mirror had been.

Then it turned round three times, the way dogs do, and lay down.

It put its chin on its paws. It kept its eyes open. It turned its head, slowly, until it was facing west. Toward the sea. Toward La Push.

"Told you," Harry said.

It didn't answer. He hadn't expected it to. But he felt something come up through the locks at him, faint, grudging, very much like the thing that had come down the line from the Cullen lawn at five past seven.

He hung the mirror back on its nail. He went and checked the circle once more, because you checked the asset. Then he went down the hall to his room, the small one, the one he liked, and took his boots off, and lay down on top of the covers in his jeans with his hands behind his head and the light off, and looked at the ceiling.

It was twenty-five to eleven.

He was asleep in about four minutes, the way he'd trained himself to be, the way he'd been able to drop off on the floor of a cold tent in a forest in Scotland at seventeen with the whole world hunting him.

He was awake again at ten past twelve, staring at the ceiling, and he didn't go back.

## Part Two: Withdrawal

### V. Leah

*La Push — Tuesday, November 21st, 12:40 a.m.*

The note had been on the kitchen table when she got in, under the salt shaker, in her mom's square capital letters on the back of a Thriftway receipt.

*AT BILLY'S. SETH CALLED. STEW ON THE STOVE. WE'LL TALK IN THE MORNING. LOVE, MOM.*

She'd stood in the kitchen in her wet shorts and read it twice. *Seth called.* Of course Seth had called. Seth had probably called from the Cullens' landline the second she'd started running, with Layla's hand on his back, and told their mother everything in about four breathless minutes, and their mother had listened to the whole thing in silence the way she listened to everything, and said *thank you, Seth*, and hung up, and got her coat, and driven to Billy's.

*We'll talk in the morning.*

Leah had eaten the stew standing at the stove, straight out of the pot, with the big wooden spoon. Then she'd showered until the hot water ran out, which in their house took about six minutes, and stood in the bathroom with a towel round her and her hair dripping and looked at herself in the mirror over the sink for a long time.

She looked the same. That was the stupid thing. She'd half thought she'd look different. Like something that big had to leave a mark you could see. She looked exactly like she'd looked that morning at five, brushing her teeth before patrol: tired, brown, sharp in the face, her hair a ragged black mess she'd cut herself in August with the kitchen scissors because she'd been too angry to sit in a chair at the salon in Forks and have somebody touch her head.

She'd looked into her own eyes in the mirror and waited for Sam to come up in them.

She'd done it every night for two years. Not on purpose. It was just what happened when she looked at herself too long, alone: his face came up behind hers like something surfacing in dark water, and the wire in her chest pulled, and she'd look away.

Nothing came up.

She'd stood there a long time, waiting for it. Then she'd gone to bed.

---

She'd heard her mom come in at a quarter past eleven. The car in the gravel, the front door, the keys in the dish. Her mom's feet on the stairs, slow, because her mom's knees were bad when it rained and it was always raining. They'd stopped outside Leah's door.

Leah had lain very still in the dark with her eyes shut and her breathing slow and even, the way she'd done when she was eleven and had snuck back in through the window from the beach.

Her mom had stood outside the door for nearly a minute. Leah could hear her breathing. She could hear her mom's heart, steady and a little bit fast, the way it went when she'd climbed the stairs. She could smell Billy's house on her, woodsmoke and the coffee Billy burned and the dog.

Then the feet had gone on down the hall, and her mom's door had closed, softly, and Leah had opened her eyes and looked at the ceiling and thought: *she knows I'm awake.*

Of course she knew. She was Sue Clearwater. She'd always known.

That had been an hour and a half ago.

---

She couldn't sleep.

She'd thought she would. She'd been up since half four. She'd run what felt like a hundred miles, and chased a vampire, and been pinned flat to a rock, and stood in a tent at the end of a hallway that shouldn't exist, and watched a man do things to a red-eyed thing on a stone floor that she was going to think about for the rest of her life, and fought with Jacob, and eaten a quarter of a lasagna and a pot of stew. She'd thought she'd hit the pillow and be gone.

She lay on her back on top of the covers in an old T-shirt of her dad's that came down to her knees and stared at the ceiling and every inch of her skin was too hot.

She'd always run hot. They all did. A hundred and eight, Carlisle said. She'd gotten used to it. You got used to sleeping with the window open in November and no blanket, and to the sheets being damp in the morning, and to your mom putting a hand on your forehead when you were sick and taking it away fast and saying *Leah, Jesus*. This was different. This was like the inside of her was a few degrees hotter than the outside, and it couldn't get out, and it was looking for a way.

She got up and opened the window wider. Cold air came in. Rain. The smell of the trees at the back of the yard and, under it, from half a mile off, the sea.

It didn't help.

She lay back down. She turned on her side. She turned on her other side. She turned on her stomach with her face in the pillow, which she never did, and lay there with her heart going too fast and her legs restless under the T-shirt like she'd had four cups of coffee, like she needed to run, like she'd just finished running and needed to keep going.

And under all of it, like a toothache, the line.

It ran out of the middle of her chest and away east. Through the wall. Through the yard and the trees and twenty miles of dark forest, over the Quillayute and the Bogachiel and up the Calawah to the north fork, to a bench of ground above a river under the biggest trees she'd ever seen, to a little green tent with a slug on it. She could feel every mile of it. It was pulled tight as a guitar string from her end to his, and it hummed, and the hum went into her teeth.

It *ached*. That was the only word for it. Like a pulled muscle. Like the morning after you'd slept wrong on your arm. A deep dull pulling ache that started in the middle of her chest and went out from there, into her shoulders and her ribs and her jaw, and wouldn't settle no matter how she lay.

She'd lain awake like this for Sam.

Not like this. She thought about it, lying there with her face in the pillow, because she couldn't not. She'd lain awake for Sam for two years. Every night. She'd lain here in this same bed with the wire in her chest pulling and pulling and pulling, out the window and across the reservation to that little house with the flowers, to *her*, and it had been like holding on to a rope with someone on the other end walking away. You held on, and it went out through your hands, burning, and you held on anyway, because if you let go you'd fall, and you couldn't make them stop walking. You couldn't make them turn round.

This rope wasn't going anywhere.

That was the thing. That was what was keeping her up. She could feel the other end of it. She could feel that it was tied, at the far end, round something just as big as what it was tied round in her. She could feel it pulling *back*.

He was awake.

She knew it the way she'd known all evening where he was. She lay on her stomach in the dark and felt for him down the line, and he was there, at the far end, twenty miles off, lying on his back in that narrow bed she'd seen through the door in the hallway, the one that was made tight like a soldier's. He was on top of the covers too. He had his hands behind his head. He was staring at his ceiling the same way she was staring into her pillow, and his heart was going too fast, and his skin was too hot, and down at the bottom of him, behind those three locks, the big dark thing was on its feet.

Pacing.

She could feel it pace. Up and down. Up and down. Every time it turned at the end of a length it stopped and put its nose to the line and *pulled*, and she felt the pull come all the way down twenty miles of forest and into her chest, and her own wolf, curled up under her ribs, lifted its head every time and whined.

She lay there and listened to the rain.

At a quarter to two in the morning, he got up.

### VI. Harry

*The tent — 1:38 a.m.*

He'd tried everything.

He'd done the Occlumency exercises. The full set, the long version, the one that had got him through the summer after Sirius when he'd been fifteen and couldn't close his eyes without seeing the archway. He'd emptied his mind one room at a time and closed the doors behind him and sat in the last empty room in the dark and waited for sleep to come in and find him there.

It hadn't come. What had come in was the line, pulled so tight it sang, running out of the middle of him through the walls of the tent and away west, and on the other end of it, twenty miles off, a girl lying face-down on a bed with her window open and the rain coming in, not sleeping either.

He'd counted backwards from a thousand in Latin. He'd recited the twelve uses of dragon's blood, and then the forty-one properties of moonstone, and then every track on *Houses of the Holy* in order. He'd got up and made a cup of tea and drunk it standing up in the dark kitchen and gone back to bed. He'd lain on his front. He'd lain on his side. He'd lain on the floor, which sometimes worked, and which didn't.

And the whole time, under his breastbone, behind three locks, the thing had paced.

It had started about ten past twelve, when he woke up. Up and down. He could feel it, the slow heavy roll of its shoulders, the length of its stride, the way it turned at each end and stopped and put its nose to the line and pulled. It didn't throw itself at the locks. It had done that on the bench at half six, when she was standing in front of him in the dark, and it had stopped the second he put his hand on her face. It didn't need to now. It knew where she was. It knew she wasn't going anywhere. It just wanted her.

And it wanted out.

It had been in there since September. Before that it had been in there most of August, on and off, because he'd been busy at the office with the Volterra file. Before that, the last time he'd let it out properly had been a night on Dartmoor in late July, on the open moor above Postbridge with no one for twenty miles, and that had been a very long time ago now.

*Feed it,* Croaker had said. *Let it out now and then. It's been very patient with you.*

He lay on his back in the dark at twenty to two in the morning and looked at the ceiling.

"Right," he said.

---

He got up. He put his boots back on, and the flannel shirt over his T-shirt, and he looked round the little room for anything else and couldn't think of anything, and went out.

Down the hall. Past the kitchen, past the library with its banked fire, past the workshop door. He stopped at the black oak door at the end of the workshop and put his palm flat on it for a second and felt the circle on the other side, all four rings, tight as a drum, and the thing lying in the middle of it, and the thing's eyes, open, red, waiting. Then he went back the way he'd come, up the three wooden steps, and out through the flap into the rain.

Gary was on the canvas by the door. He'd moved about four inches since the afternoon.

"Don't," said Harry.

He walked out to the middle of the bench under the spruces. The river was loud in the gorge below. It was very dark. The rain came down through the canopy in fat slow drops, the way it did in old forest, falling a long way from one branch to the next.

He opened the locks.

He did it in order, the way he'd made them, newest first. The third one he'd laid in the second week of September in the back of the Explorer in a car park at Sea-Tac airport, an hour after he landed, braiding it into his Occlumency shields like a fool, the way he'd never recommend to anybody. It came open with a sound he felt in his back teeth. The second was older; he'd made it in 2004, after Croaker had sent him to Prague and he'd had to sit through four days of meetings in a room with a werewolf who'd kept looking at him across the table and sniffing. It came open slower. And the first one he'd made in a hotel room in Minsk at three in the morning in 2003, with somebody else's blood still under his nails and something huge waking up in his chest for the first time, and he'd had no idea what he was doing then, and he'd built it out of nothing but fear and stubbornness and a lot of shouting, and it was the strongest of the three, and the ugliest.

He stood in the rain for a second with his hand on it.

Then he let it go.

---

It came up the way a diver comes up from deep water. Slowly, and then all at once.

There wasn't a sound. That was the first difference, and he'd always noticed it, and he noticed it again now standing in the rain: the Quileute kids went with a noise like a sheet tearing, a heat and a shove and a crack, all at once, from the middle out. He'd heard it in the brother's head and the girl's. His didn't do that. His came up through him like a tide coming in, a long heavy pressure from underneath, like being lifted, and the world tilted, and leaned, and went *down*, a long way down and forward, and he let it.

His hands went into the moss. They weren't hands when they got there.

He felt it go through him in a wave from the front to the back. His spine lengthening, and lengthening, and lengthening. His shoulders rolling forward and dropping and broadening, and broadening, until they were as wide as the bonnet of the Explorer. His skull drawing out ahead of him into the dark, long and heavy and narrow, his jaw going with it. The ground coming up to meet four broad padded paws spread wide in the soaking moss, and a weight settling down into them, a weight he hadn't carried since July, enormous and solid and balanced, like a building settling onto its foundations. Heat everywhere. And the clothes going with him, the way he'd made them go, folding away into the coat with the rest of him, so that there was nothing left of Harry Potter on the bench under the spruces but a big black shape standing in the rain.

Then the world came in.

It came in through his nose first. It always did. He'd forgotten. Every time, he forgot, and every time it came back in like a door being kicked open: the whole forest, all at once. Every spruce on the bench, and how old each one was, and which side the rain was hitting. The river below, and the stones in it, and the salmon that had come up it to spawn two weeks ago and were lying dead and rotting in the shallows a mile downstream, gone sweet and rank. Elk, three days old, on the slope across the gorge. A bobcat, yesterday. The canvas of the tent. Gary. Woodsmoke from the library chimney. And through all of it, under all of it, from the circle at the back of the tent, faint, sick, sweet as a funeral, the cold one.

And his ears. The rain on every single needle of every single tree. The river as a thousand sounds, one for every stone it went over. Something small and scared in the brush twenty yards off, holding perfectly still, its heart going like a sewing machine.

He stood there and let it all come in.

He was big. He'd forgotten that too, or let himself forget it, the way you forget how tall you are until you hit your head on a doorframe in someone else's house. His shoulder was up among the lowest dead branches of the spruces; he could feel them brushing his back. He'd stood next to a draught horse once, at a show in Devon, and looked down at the top of its head. He'd seen the big ginger one through the Omnioculars from a ridge a mile off in October, and in the brother's head that afternoon, and in hers. Clifford, he'd started calling him in his head, because somebody had to. Clifford was enormous. Clifford was the biggest thing on four legs on this coast and knew it and carried himself like it.

He'd have looked down at the top of Clifford's head.

His coat was black. It always had been, every time, the first time in Minsk and every time since: a deep flat midnight black on the outside, rain beading on it and running off, and underneath, close to the skin, a thick charcoal-grey undercoat that kept the cold out like a wall. He could see a little of it if he turned his head and looked back along his own flank. His eyes he couldn't see. They'd be green. They always were.

The thing that had been in the cellar for two months stood in the rain on the bench above the north fork of the Calawah, on its own four paws, with its nose up, and breathed.

And Harry, who was still Harry, who had never once in three years stopped being Harry in this shape and didn't expect to start, stood inside it and felt it be glad.

It was the simplest thing he'd ever felt. It didn't have any words in it. It had the rain, and the forest, and the weight of its own body, and the pull of the line in the middle of its chest, twenty miles long and tight as wire, pointing west.

It didn't wait for him to decide anything. For once, he found he didn't want it to.

They went.

---

He went down off the bench the way she had, through the big spruces, and the wards opened for him the way they opened for him every time, without being asked. He went into the gorge at a long easy lope and across the north fork in one jump, forty feet, and came down on the far bank on all four paws without a sound.

Without a sound. That was the other thing. He'd never understood it. A wolf his size should have shaken the ground. He'd felt the big ginger one run in the brother's memory and it had sounded like a horse at a gallop. When he ran, he made no noise at all. Not on moss, not on gravel, not on dead branches. His paws went down toe-first and his weight poured into them like water into a jug, and the ground took it and didn't complain. He'd asked an old woman in Uganda about it once, a long time ago, before any of this; not this, exactly, but how she moved, how she could come up behind you in the dry grass of the Rwenzori foothills without you hearing her. She'd looked at him for a while and said *you are carrying yourself. Stop carrying yourself and let the ground do it.* He'd thought she was talking rubbish. He'd thought that for about three years.

He went west.

Down the north fork to where it met the main river, and down the Calawah in the dark, along its banks, under the big trees. Under the Highway 101 bridge at the north end of Forks, where a logging truck was going over at two in the morning with its lights on full, and the bridge hummed over his head like a struck bell, and the driver never knew. Out past the edge of town where the Calawah ran into the Bogachiel, broad and brown and loud, and down the Bogachiel through second-growth timber and old clear-cuts and a pasture with three horses in it that lifted their heads all at once as he went past, and stood frozen, and didn't make a sound either.

Twelve miles. Fourteen. He wasn't tired. He'd have been surprised if he had been; he'd never once got tired in this shape, not even the first night in Minsk when he'd run until dawn. The line pulled him and he went where it pulled.

He went on past the place where the Bogachiel met the Sol Duc and the two of them together became the Quillayute, and down the Quillayute through the flats, and a couple of miles above the river mouth, he stopped.

He knew this line. He'd mapped it in September, the first week, from the county records in the Forks library and the Bureau of Indian Affairs boundary survey from 1889 and a walk of his own along most of it in the rain. It wasn't marked. There was no fence. There was a place on the south bank of the river where the trees changed, second-growth to older, and a place where a logging road gave out into nothing, and that was all. But the Quileute knew where it was, and the Cullens knew where it was, and he knew.

The Cullens didn't cross it. They'd signed a piece of paper in the nineteen-thirties with a man called Ephraim Black that said they wouldn't, and they hadn't, as far as he could tell, in seventy years.

The paper said *cold ones*. He'd read Billy Black's copy of it, through Seth Clearwater's eyes, at one o'clock that afternoon, while he was going through the boy's head looking for a witch. It said cold ones and the land of the Quileute and a lot of other things, carefully, in the formal English of a solicitor's office in Port Angeles in 1936. It didn't say anything about him.

He crossed it.

He went down the Quillayute toward the sea with the river getting wider and slower and saltier on his left and the smell of the ocean coming up the valley at him like a wall. Kelp. Salt. Rotting wood. Fish. Diesel from the marina. Woodsmoke from a lot of chimneys. People. He slowed down. He went in under the trees on the edge of the village and came round the back of it, the long way, away from the road, through the trees at the bottom of a lot of back yards. A dog started barking somewhere off to the left, a big one, chained. He turned his head and looked at it through the trees, and it stopped. Another started up, further off, and he didn't look at that one, and it stopped too, on its own, mid-bark, with a little uncertain whine at the end of it, and didn't start again.

He didn't need to look for the house. The line took him straight to it.

It was small. Two storeys, white clapboard gone grey-green with damp at the corners, a sloping roof, a back porch with a broken step. A yard that ran back from the porch to the trees, overgrown at the far end, with a rusted swing set nobody had used in years and a clothesline with nothing on it. The smell of it came across the wet grass at him: coffee, and fry bread, and stew, and the rose soap the mother used, and the brother, and old fishing gear in the shed, and under it, faint, years old, a man. A big man, with a laugh. Pipe tobacco and powdered sugar. Fading.

And her.

Her, everywhere. On the porch. On the broken step. On the grass, where she'd crossed it a thousand times to the trees and back. In the trees, at the edge of the yard, at the foot of a big old cedar, where she changed.

And above him, at the back of the house, upstairs, under the slope of the roof, an open window with the rain going in.

He came out of the trees at the bottom of the yard, past the swing set, onto the grass, without a sound. He stopped at the edge of the light from somebody's porch two houses down. He sat.

He looked up.

### VII. Leah

*The Clearwater house — 1:52 a.m.*

She'd felt him coming the whole way.

She'd felt him get up off the bed. Felt him walk down that long hallway and out through the flap into the rain and stop on the bench under the trees. Felt the locks come off, one, two, three, every one of them going through her own chest like a door slammed in the next house. She'd sat bolt upright in bed in her dad's T-shirt with both hands pressed flat over her breastbone when the third one went, the big one, the ugly one, and felt the thing at the bottom of him come up.

She'd never felt anything like it. She'd felt Sam change, and Jacob, and Seth, a thousand times, from inside; she knew what it felt like. It was a blast, a shove, a crack, the wolf coming out of you all at once like it had been fired. This was a tide. It came up through him slow and heavy and huge and *glad*, so glad, and her own wolf under her ribs had come up onto its feet and stood there trembling, every hair on end, while it happened.

Then he'd run.

She'd felt the line start to shorten. She'd felt the miles come off it one at a time, nineteen, eighteen, fifteen, ten, so fast she'd put her hand out to the headboard to steady herself. She'd felt him come down the Calawah and the Bogachiel and stop at the line, at the boundary, at the place where the paper said the land was theirs, and stand there for a second, thinking about it.

She'd felt him cross.

And then he'd come down the river toward the sea, and slowed, and come round through the trees at the back of the village, and the line had gotten shorter and shorter until it was almost nothing, until it was a hundred yards, fifty, twenty, until it was right *there*, right outside, so close she could have reached out and grabbed it with her hand.

It stopped.

She sat on the edge of the bed in the dark with her feet on the cold floor.

The ache was gone. She noticed it the way you notice when a car alarm two streets over finally stops. It had been there since the Cullen house, since before, since she'd walked out of his tent at half six, a deep dull pull in her chest and her shoulders and her teeth that she'd stopped being able to tell apart from her own heartbeat. And now it was gone. Like somebody had taken a thumb off a bruise.

She got up and went to the window.

The yard was dark. It was always dark; the porch light at the Atearas', two houses down, threw a little yellow out across the bottom of their yard and the neighbor's, and the rest was black wet grass and the shape of the old swing set and the clothesline and the trees. The rain was coming down soft and steady. She put both hands on the sill and leaned out into it.

Two green eyes looked up at her out of the dark at the bottom of the yard.

They were very green. Even from here. Even in the dark, they caught the bit of yellow from the Atearas' porch light and threw it back the color of a bottle held up to the sun, and they were looking right at her, not blinking, steady and patient as a lighthouse. And they were *high*. They were too high. She'd been looking at wolves' eyes in the dark for two years, and she knew where they were supposed to be, and these were up where a man's would be if the man was standing on another man's shoulders.

Then he moved, and the shape came into the edge of the light.

He was sitting. That was the first thing she understood. He was sitting at the bottom of her mom's yard on his haunches, by the swing set, the way a dog sits outside a door, with his front legs straight and his tail curled round his paws and his head tipped back to look up at her window. And he was black. All the way black, the deep flat black of the sky over the ocean on a night with no moon, rain beading on the top of his coat and running off, and where the light caught him she could see that under the black, close in, there was something darker gray, thick, like smoke.

Black.

Sam was black.

She stood at the window and waited for it to hurt.

It didn't. She stood there and looked down at a black wolf at the bottom of her yard, the way she'd looked at a black wolf at the edge of the woods behind this house a thousand times, in the spring, in the summer, when Sam came to make sure she was all right, when she was still in his pack and he was still her Alpha and she still couldn't breathe when she looked at him. And she felt nothing about Sam at all. She was looking at something else. Sam was a black wolf. This was something black that happened to be a wolf.

And he was *huge*.

She tried to measure him. She did it without meaning to, the way you'd do it with any wolf, by the things around him. The swing set. The top bar of the swing set was seven feet; her dad had built it, and he'd told her so, proud, when she was five. He was sitting down and his shoulders were above it. His head was above it by a lot. His head was nearly level with the bottom of her window, and her window was on the second floor.

Jacob was the biggest wolf in a thousand years of stories. Everybody said so. Jacob said so, more than anybody. If Jacob had been standing beside this thing, his ears would have come up about to its jaw.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Oh, Jake's going to* hate *this.*

The black wolf at the bottom of the yard tipped its head on one side.

"Evening, Lassie," it said.

She nearly went out of the window.

She grabbed the frame. Her heart went off like a gun. She hung on to the window frame with both hands and stared down at it, at him, at the black wolf sitting by her dead father's swing set, and its mouth had moved, the long black jaw had opened and closed, and a voice had come out of it. Low. Rough. Very quiet. English. With a growl threaded through the bottom of it like a bass line, and every single word coming out clean round it, perfectly clear, the way somebody talks with a cough drop in their mouth. His voice.

"You can *talk*?" Leah hissed.

"Course I can talk."

"We can't talk!"

"Well." The great black head tilted the other way. "You've got some catching up to do, haven't you."

"How are you—*how*—"

"Magic, mostly. Bit of practice." His ears went forward. "You going to come down, or am I going to sit here till your mum looks out the window?"

Leah stood at the window with the rain coming in on her face, and looked down at him, and he looked back up at her, and the line between them went from nothing to nothing at all, so short it wasn't there, so tight it didn't need to be.

Under her ribs, her wolf was already moving.

---

She didn't stop to think. She thought later that if she'd stopped to think she'd have had to admit to herself what she was doing, and she'd have had to have an opinion about it, and she'd have had to argue with the opinion, and by the time she'd done all that it would have been dawn. So she didn't stop.

She got the cutoffs off the chair and a tank top off the floor and the leather cord off the bedpost and knelt on the floor in the dark and tied them round her ankle, tight, the way she'd done it a thousand times, fast. She went out of her room in her dad's T-shirt and down the hall in her bare feet, and past her mom's door.

The light was off under it. She stopped for half a second anyway.

Her mom's breathing was on the other side of it. Slow, and even, and a little bit too even. The way Leah's had been, three hours ago, lying in the dark with her eyes shut while her mom stood outside her door.

Leah stood in the hallway and listened to her mother pretend to be asleep.

Then she went on down the stairs, quietly, skipping the fourth one from the bottom that creaked, and through the kitchen, and out the back door onto the porch, and she stepped over the broken step the way she always did and went down onto the grass.

It was cold. It was soaking. She went across it barefoot with the rain coming down on her bare arms and through her dad's T-shirt, toward the bottom of the yard, toward the swing set.

He stood up.

He did it slowly. All of him, unfolding up off his haunches in one long movement, up and up, so that she had to tip her head back, and back, and keep tipping it. And he still didn't make a sound. Something that size, getting up off wet grass, and she couldn't hear it. She could hear the rain on every leaf in the yard and the sea half a mile off and her mother's heart through two floors of the house behind her, and she couldn't hear him move at all.

She stopped about ten feet from him.

The smell of him came across to her on the rain, and hit her in the chest, and kept going.

On the ridge, she'd caught it under the coffee and the pine and the pie, so faint she'd thought she'd imagined it, the ghost of a smell, like the place in the woods where a big animal had slept last night and gone. In the tent it had been stronger, but still locked down, still held under, like a smell through a closed door. Now the door was open.

Pine, and turned earth after rain, and cold stone, like the inside of a cave. Those were still there. And through them, over them, under them, all through them: wolf. A wild, big, old smell, nothing like the pack's. The pack smelled like dog and woodsmoke and the reservation, like home. This smelled like the far side of a mountain range. Like snow on rock, and dark, and something running a long way off under the moon. It went straight down her throat into the middle of her and found her wolf, curled up under her ribs, and her wolf stood up all at once like it had been called by name.

"Hi," said Leah.

"Hi," said the black wolf.

Its voice came from about five feet over her head.

"You're really big," she said.

"Yeah. Sorry."

"Jake's going to lose his mind."

"Is he?" She could have sworn it sounded pleased. "Clifford's not used to looking up at people, I take it."

"*Clifford?*"

"Big red dog. Kids' books. Couldn't help it."

She stood in the rain in her dad's T-shirt and looked up at him and laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, and she put her hand over her mouth, and she heard her own laugh bounce off the side of the shed, and she heard him make a sound up there, a low rumble in that enormous chest, and she understood after a second that he was laughing too.

"I have to change," she said.

"I know."

"Turn around."

The great black head looked down at her for a moment. Then it turned, and he turned with it, slowly, all the way round, until he was facing the trees with his back to her and his tail toward her, and his ears went sideways in a way that she was absolutely sure meant *I'm not looking. I'm a gentleman. I'm extremely not looking.*

She pulled her dad's T-shirt off over her head and folded it, and put it on the seat of the swing, where it would get soaked, and let the wolf go.

---

It went the way it always did. The heat and the shove and the crack, from the middle out, and the world going down and forward and her hands going into the grass. And then she was on four feet in the wet with her nose full of him.

Her wolf didn't wait.

It had been waiting all its life. It had been born screaming in this yard in the spring, ten feet from where it was standing now, with her father in the doorway behind it, and it had been alone every single second since. Alone in the pack. Alone in Sam's head. Alone in Jacob's. The first girl in a thousand years of stories, the one nobody had a word for, running on her own in the dark at four in the morning on the rocks above the Sol Duc where nobody could see.

It wasn't alone now. And it wasn't going to pretend it didn't know that, or wasn't sure, or wanted to think about it first. It didn't think about things first. That had always been her job.

It went straight to him.

He'd turned back round the second she changed. He was standing over her, so tall that her ears came up about to his jaw, the way Jacob's would have, and her wolf went right up under his chin with no hesitation at all and put its nose to his throat and breathed him in, so hard and so long that her lungs ached, and then it went up on its hind legs and put its front paws on his chest and bit him.

On the jaw. Hard. Hard enough that she felt her teeth go in through the black coat and the gray under it and close.

He didn't flinch.

He didn't do anything. He stood there and let her. And then, slowly, he lowered his head, all that long heavy black skull coming down out of the rain, and pressed the side of his face into her neck. Into the thick fur on the side of her throat, under her ear. He pushed his muzzle in there and held it, and she felt him breathe her in the way she'd breathed him, all the way down, and felt something go through the whole length of him, nose to tail, like a shiver going through a horse.

Her wolf let go of his jaw and dropped back down on all four feet.

Then it put its shoulder into his chest and shoved.

He let himself be shoved. She felt him do it. He went back one step, deliberately, his great paws shifting in the grass without a sound, like a big man letting a kid push him over in a game. And then he dropped his front end all the way down to the ground, chest flat in the wet grass, back end high in the air, tail going.

A play bow. The oldest thing there was. She'd done it with Seth when they were first changed, both of them scared out of their minds, in these exact woods. She'd never done it since. Not for anybody.

He came up out of it and was *gone*.

He just went. A black streak across the bottom of the yard, past the swing set, through the gap in the trees by the shed, and gone toward the sea, and he made no sound at all, not one, she couldn't even hear his feet hit the ground. She stood there for half a second with her mouth open.

Then she went after him.

---

She caught him on the beach.

He'd gone straight down through the trees and across the road at the bottom of the village and out onto First Beach, and she came out of the trees behind him at the top of the beach where the drift logs were piled like bones, huge ones, whole trees washed up and bleached gray, and went over them in two bounds and down onto the sand.

The tide was out. The beach was enormous at low tide, flat and hard and shining wet, going away north to the river mouth and the dark lump of James Island, and south to the cliffs of Teahwhit Head, and out in front of her the sea, black, with the white lines of the breakers coming in one after another out of the dark. There was no moon. There was just the faint gray of the clouds, and the sound of the surf, and him, a black shape running south along the edge of the water a hundred yards ahead of her.

She went.

She was the fastest. She'd always been the fastest. Faster than Jacob, faster than Sam, faster than Paul by a mile, and she put her ears back and stretched out flat along the wet sand and she went the way she'd gone on the ridge chasing the red-eye, everything she had, the wind screaming past, and she gained on him. She gained on him like he was standing still. Eighty yards. Fifty. Thirty. She could see the rain coming off his back. She could see his tail.

*Christ,* came down the line at her, astonished. And then, out loud, ahead of her, in that low rough voice with the growl under it: "*Christ*, you're quick."

And then he was faster.

Not a lot. Just enough. She felt him do it. She felt something in the air around him change, a tiny shiver, like heat coming off a road, and suddenly his stride was longer than it had any right to be and his paws were barely touching the sand, and the gap between them stopped closing.

*Cheater,* she thought at him, hard.

"*Resourceful,*" he said, over his shoulder.

*You used magic. You're running with magic. That's cheating.*

"I'm running with what I've got."

*Then I'll run with what I've got.*

She went faster. She hadn't known she could. She'd thought she'd been going as fast as she could go since the spring. She found, out there on the hard wet sand in the dark with the sea on her right and him in front of her, that there'd been a whole other gear in her that she'd never touched, that she'd never had a reason to touch, and she found it, and she put her foot down in it.

She caught him at the foot of the cliffs.

She came up beside him at the very end of the beach, where the sand ran out into black rock and tide pools and the headland went up over them, and she put her shoulder into his ribs at full speed, and he went sideways, and she went with him, and the two of them went over together in a tangle of legs and black fur and gray into the shallow water at the edge of a tide pool, in a spray of salt water and sand, and came up snarling.

She was on top. She didn't know how. He was three times her size and she was on top, with her front paws on his chest and her teeth at his throat, and he was lying on his back in eight inches of salt water with his paws in the air and his tail going and his tongue hanging out the side of his mouth.

"All right," he said. "All right. You win."

*You let me.*

"I absolutely did not let you. You came at me like a train. I'm going to have a bruise."

She stood over him with her paws on his chest and her sides heaving and the sea coming in round them, cold, and looked down into his face, and he looked back up at her. The green eyes. Wide open. The rain coming down on them both.

*You've never done this,* she thought. *Have you.*

It wasn't a question, really. She could feel it down the line. All of it, from his end. Three years of it. Minsk and Dartmoor and a moor in Scotland somewhere, and hillsides in the dark, and the forest above Postbridge in July with nobody for twenty miles. Running on his own. Always on his own. Every single time.

"No," he said quietly. "Never with anybody."

*Me neither.*

"I know."

She got off him.

He rolled up out of the water and shook himself, the whole enormous length of him, nose to tail, and sprayed her with about a gallon of salt water, and she didn't care. She shook herself too. They stood there in the tide pool at the foot of Teahwhit Head in the rain with the sea coming in round their legs.

*Come on,* she thought. *I want to show you something.*

---

Her dad had found it when she was ten.

He'd been fishing off the rocks below the headland, the way he did sometimes on Sundays when he was bored of the jetty, and he'd seen it from below. A dark place, high up in the cliff, above the line where the winter storms reached. He'd climbed up to it that afternoon with her on his back, the two of them, going up a ledge in the rock she'd never have found by herself, and they'd sat in the mouth of it all afternoon with the powdered doughnuts and watched the sea. He'd called it the Eye. He'd said there was a story about it, but he couldn't remember what the story was. He'd said that was probably for the best.

She'd never brought anybody there. She'd never been back since he died.

She went up the ledge first. It was easier as a wolf. It was a lot easier as a wolf, and she went up it with the rock wet and black under her paws and the sea booming below, up and round the shoulder of the cliff to where it opened out, and stopped at the mouth of the cave, and turned round.

He came up behind her without a sound. He had to put his head down to fit under the lip of it.

It was dry inside. It went back about thirty feet into the rock, low and round, with a sandy floor that the wind had blown in over a thousand years, and it was out of the rain and the wind both, and it smelled of salt and stone and old seaweed and, very faintly, after ten years, powdered sugar. And from the mouth of it, looking west, you could see everything. The whole of the sea, out to the edge of the dark. The sea stacks off Second Beach, the Quillayute Needles, standing up out of the water like the stumps of some huge old building, with the surf going white round their feet. And way out, a single light on the water, a boat, moving very slowly north.

She stood in the mouth of the cave and looked at it.

The black wolf stood beside her and looked at it too.

*My dad found this,* she thought at him. *When I was ten. He called it the Eye.*

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. She felt it come down the line at her, slow and careful and very plain, the way he'd been plain with her all day, because he couldn't be anything else: he understood what it was that she'd just shown him. And what it meant that she had.

He put his nose against the side of her face. Just for a second.

Then she turned round, and went into the back of the cave, where the sand was dry and deep and soft.

She turned round three times, the way her wolf had always wanted to, and never once had anybody to do it for.

He stood in the mouth of the cave and watched her do it, with the dark sea behind him and the rain coming down past the lip of the rock, and his green eyes on her, and the line between them so tight and so short and so loud it was like standing inside a struck bell.

Her wolf had never in its life asked anybody's permission for anything.

It didn't start now.

He came in out of the rain.

---

Later, a long time later, with the tide all the way out and starting back, she lay with her head across his foreleg and listened to him breathe.

He was curled round her. All of him, the whole black length of him, nose to tail, in a ring round her on the sand, so that she lay in the middle of him like something in a nest, out of the wind, out of the cold, his heat on every side of her. His chin was on her shoulder. His breath went in and out against the fur of her neck, slow, deep, a long way down. His heart was right under her ear, through his ribs, going slow and heavy and steady, that newspaper-reading heart, the one that hadn't so much as flickered with two wolves at its back.

Out past the mouth of the cave, the sea went on. The Needles stood up out of the dark. The single light of the boat had gone on north and out of sight.

The line was quiet.

She lay there and felt it be quiet. For the first time since one o'clock on the ridge, the pulling had stopped, and the humming, and the ache in her teeth. It lay there between them, slack and easy, like a rope coiled on a dock, tied at both ends and not going anywhere at all.

Under her ribs, her wolf was asleep. Really asleep. She'd never felt it sleep before. She'd thought, if she'd thought about it at all, that it didn't. That it was always up, always watching, always pacing in the dark, the way she was.

It was asleep with its nose in its tail. Down at the bottom of him, so was his.

She closed her eyes.

---

### VIII. Leah

*The Eye, Teahwhit Head — 7:12 a.m.*

When she woke up she was a person.

She didn't know when that had happened. Sometime toward morning, she thought, in her sleep, the wolf had just let go and gone back down and left her there, the way it did sometimes when she was really tired, and she hadn't even felt it. She was lying on her side in the sand at the back of the cave with her knees drawn up, naked, with the cord still round her ankle and the cutoffs and the tank top still knotted onto it, soaked and full of sand. And she was warm. All the way down. She hadn't been cold, not once, all night.

The light was gray. Coming in low across the floor of the cave from the mouth, the thin flat gray of a November morning on the coast before the sun was properly up. The rain had stopped. She could hear the sea, close and loud, and gulls.

And she could hear him breathing.

He was behind her. She could feel him there, the heat of him, all down her back, a big solid wall of it, and she could hear his breath going in and out slow and deep, still asleep. Human, now. She could tell by the sound. Somewhere in the night, his had let go too.

She lay there for a minute with her eyes open, looking at the gray light on the sand, and let herself have it.

Then she thought: *oh my God.*

She sat up.

He woke up instantly. She felt him do it, behind her: no in-between, no groggy, just asleep and then awake, the way a soldier wakes. She felt him go still, and then look at her, and she didn't turn round.

"Morning," he said.

His voice was hoarse. It was the human one. No growl under it. She'd have known it anywhere now.

"Morning," said Leah.

She sat on the sand with her back to him and her knees drawn up and her arms round them, and stared at the mouth of the cave. Outside it, the sky over the sea was going from gray to paler gray. The Needles stood up out of the water, black and wet. A cormorant was sitting on the nearest one, holding its wings out to dry.

Behind her, she heard him sit up too.

She waited for the rustle. She'd been in the pack for two years; she knew what came after you changed back somewhere stupid. The rustle, the swearing, somebody looking for the shorts they'd tied to their leg and finding they'd lost them in a creek somewhere, somebody walking home through the woods with leaves in very personal places. She waited for him to start looking for his clothes.

He didn't. There wasn't any rustle.

She turned round.

He was sitting on the sand about four feet behind her, with his back to the cave wall and his elbows on his knees, in exactly what he'd been wearing in the tent last night. Jeans. Boots. A gray T-shirt with a blue-and-green flannel shirt open over it. All of it dry. All of it clean. The flannel didn't even have a crease in it.

She stared at him.

"You've got clothes," she said.

"Yeah."

"You've got *all* your clothes."

"I've got all my clothes." He looked down at himself, then back up at her, and something happened at the corner of his mouth. "They come with me. I sorted it in 2004. It's a charm on the—"

"You don't lose your *clothes*?"

"Never have."

"Not *once*?"

"Not since 2004."

"I hate you," said Leah, with feeling.

"No, you don't," he said.

He said it plain. He wasn't teasing. And he was right, and she knew he was right, and he knew she knew, and the knowing came down the line from his end to hers and back again like a ball going over a net, and she turned round again very fast and started untying the cord from her ankle.

The cutoffs were full of sand. So was the tank top. She shook them out as best she could and pulled them on, sitting down, with her back to him, and he was looking at the sea. She didn't have to look to know that. She could feel him looking at the sea, very hard, the way you'd look at a painting in a museum to show everyone you were extremely interested in art.

She got the tank top on. She turned back round.

They looked at each other.

He looked like he'd slept. That was the first thing. She'd only known him about eighteen hours and she'd already noticed what he looked like when he hadn't, which was most of the time: something tight round the eyes, a sort of permanent squint, like a man who'd been staring into a strong wind for years. It was gone. He looked younger without it. He looked about his age, which she worked out, sitting there, from what Layla had said last night, was twenty-six. He had sand in his hair. He had three days of stubble. He had, on the left side of his jaw, very faintly, a row of small marks, already fading, in the shape of her teeth.

She looked at them.

He touched them, after a second, with his thumb. He didn't seem bothered.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"So," said Leah.

"So," he agreed.

"That happened."

"It did, yeah."

"We should—" She stopped. She didn't know what they should. She looked at the sea. She looked back at him. "We're not going to make a big deal about it," she said. "Right?"

"Right."

"Because it's not a big deal."

"No."

"We've known each other for like eighteen hours."

"Seventeen and a half," he said. "Ish."

"Seventeen and a half hours. And we went—" She waved a hand at the back of the cave. At the sand. At all of it. "All the way. And it's not a big deal."

"Well," he said. He considered it. He scratched the back of his neck. "I mean, it was good sex. So what's the big deal?"

She stared at him.

"Yeah," said Leah. "But as *wolves*."

"You didn't like it?"

She opened her mouth.

She'd meant to say something. Something sharp. Something with an edge on it, to put him back where he belonged, the way she'd been putting people back where they belonged for two years. She'd had it all ready.

What came out, because she couldn't make anything else come out, because she'd found out yesterday afternoon in a tent in the rain that she couldn't lie to this man about anything, was:

"No, it was *fucking great*."

He grinned.

It went all the way across his face. She'd seen him smirk at her on the ridge, and almost-smile at her in the kitchen, and laugh at her in the yard; she hadn't seen this. It took about five years off him all by itself.

"But," Leah said, louder, pointing at him, because she could feel the line going hot and bright and smug from his end, "next time somebody else phases, they are going to get an *eyeful*. You get that, right? You understand how the pack works? The next time I phase with Jake or Seth, all of last night is just going to be *right there*. In their heads. In *Technicolor*. Seth's going to see it. My *brother*. Jacob's going to see it. Jacob's going to see how *big* you—" She stopped. "He's going to see *everything*."

"I've got nothing to hide," said Harry Potter.

She looked at him.

"You," she said, "are literally the most secret person I have ever met. You've got a thing in my head that strangles me if I tell anybody what you do for a living."

"Not from you," he said. "I've got nothing to hide from you."

That came down the line too. Plain. True. The way everything came down the line from him: no wrapping on it, no box, just the thing, handed over.

She didn't have anything to say to that. So she didn't say anything.

She looked at the mouth of the cave instead, and the gray sky, and the cormorant on the Needle with its wings out. After a while she felt him get up, and come over, and sit down on the sand beside her, close, with his shoulder against hers. He was as hot as she was. She'd never sat next to anybody who was as hot as she was, except the pack.

They sat there and looked at the sea.

---

"Do something about the smell," Leah said, eventually.

He looked at her sideways.

"Seth's got a nose," she said. "*I've* got a nose. You've got a nose, I'm guessing, a pretty good one. Jake's got a nose like a bloodhound. My mom's not a wolf but she's my *mom*." She held up her arm and smelled her own wrist, and made a face. "We smell like—"

"Yeah," he said. "I know what we smell like."

"So do something."

"Already on it."

He didn't move. He didn't take out a wand, or say anything, or wave his hand; he just sat there with his shoulder against hers looking at the sea. And something went over her. It went over her skin from her scalp to her feet like a cool breath, like the first wind off the water in the morning when you've slept outside, and it took something with it. She felt it go. She lifted her arm and smelled her wrist again.

Salt. Sand. Rain. Cold stone. The inside of the cave. The sea.

Nothing else.

She smelled her own shoulder, and then, after a second, she leaned over and smelled his. Salt, rain, pine, earth, stone. Him. Just him. The wild thing under it, the big dark wolf smell, still there, faint, the lock off it now and not going back on. And nothing else at all.

"You can just *do* that?"

"I can do most things."

"Do you have a spell for everything?"

"For most things." He shrugged. "Not your head. I can't touch that. That's your pack. That's—" He made a gesture, vague. "That's the oldest magic on this coast, I'd bet. Older than mine. It's not mine to mess with." He looked at her. "So Clifford's still going to get his eyeful."

"*Don't* call him Clifford in front of him."

"I'm absolutely going to call him Clifford in front of him."

She snorted. She couldn't help it.

Then she looked down at herself. At the cutoffs, sandy and damp and fraying at the hems, with a hole in one back pocket. At the tank top, black, gone gray from washing, with a little tear in the side seam where she'd caught it on a fence in August. At the leather cord, lying on the sand, with its knots in it.

"Can you do the clothes thing?" she said. "For me?"

He looked at her for a moment.

"Hold still," he said.

She held still.

This one she felt more. It went into the cutoffs and the tank top like water going into a sponge. She felt it go into the threads, into the denim and the cotton, and settle there. And then it went into *her*, a little way, just the surface, just her skin, and she felt it find the place where the wolf came out, where the heat and the shove started, the exact spot in the middle of her where it all began, and tie something to it. Lightly. Like a ribbon round a wrist.

"There," he said. "Next time you change, they'll go with you. When you change back, they'll come back. Clean, too. Saves on washing."

She looked down at her shorts.

"Seth goes through nine pairs a week," she said slowly.

"I know. I've been in his head."

"Mom buys them in *bulk*. On clearance. At Newton's. She writes it on the back of the electric bill." Her voice was doing something odd. "Do you know how much we've spent on shorts since March?"

"I'll do Seth's."

"You'll do *Seth's*?"

"I'll do everybody's," he said. "Every one of you who wants it. Both packs. It's not hard. Takes about a second each." He looked at her sideways again. "Is that a big deal? Should I not?"

She sat on the sand in the gray light with her hands on her knees, in a pair of shorts that were never going to rip again, and looked at him.

She thought about Seth, in the store, with fifteen pairs of khakis over his arm. She thought about her mom at the kitchen table doing the bills on a Sunday night with a calculator and a frown, and sighing, and saying nothing. She thought about all of them, Paul and Jared and Quil and Embry and the little ones, Collin and Brady, fourteen years old, walking home naked through the woods at four in the morning in the rain because they'd lost their shorts in the Sol Duc.

"No," she said. "It's a big deal. Do it anyway."

---

They went back along the beach.

The tide was coming in. The sun was up somewhere behind the clouds and the hills, not showing itself, the way it never did, but the whole sky had gone a pale even pewter and the sea had gone the same color, and the beach went away north in front of them flat and wet and shining, with the gulls standing on it in rows, all facing into the wind. The drift logs were piled up at the top of it like bones. James Island stood up off the river mouth at the far end, dark and steep, with trees on top bent over sideways.

They walked. They didn't run. She didn't know why. It just seemed like a walking sort of morning.

He walked on the side nearest the water. She noticed that. She noticed he didn't leave footprints, either, on the wet sand, or hardly any; she looked back once and there was a line of hers going back along the beach toward the headland, deep and clear, and beside it, every so often, a faint scuff, like somebody had been walking very carefully beside her on the very edges of their boots.

"How come you don't leave tracks?" she said.

"Habit."

"That's not an answer."

"An old woman in Uganda told me I was carrying myself," he said. "Took me three years to work out what she meant. Now I can't stop."

She thought about that for a while.

"Uganda," she said.

"Long story."

"You've got a lot of those."

"I've got a lot of those," he agreed. "You can have them all. One at a time." He glanced sideways at her. "Not today. Today I want breakfast."

"Mom'll be up."

"I know. She was up at two."

Leah stopped walking.

He stopped too, and looked back at her.

"She heard me go out," said Leah.

"She heard you go out. And she heard you not come back. She's been sitting at your kitchen table since about six with a cup of coffee, waiting." He looked at her, and his face was very plain, and she realized he'd been feeling it down the line for the last ten minutes the same way she had, through her: her mom's house, half a mile off, the kitchen light on, the coffee. "You want me to come in with you? Or you want to go in on your own? I can go. I can be back at the tent in a second. Your call."

She looked at him. A tall, broad, black-haired Englishman in a flannel shirt with sand in his hair and her teeth marks on his jaw, standing on First Beach at half past seven in the morning, who'd been through a dark lord and a war and Uganda and Minsk and whatever else was in all those stories she hadn't heard yet. Asking her if it was okay to meet her mom.

She thought about her mom at the kitchen table. With her coffee. Waiting.

*We'll talk in the morning.*

"Come in," said Leah. "She'll want to see you."

"Will she."

"She'll want to *look* at you," Leah said. "There's a difference."

"Is there," he said, and fell back into step beside her, on the side nearest the sea.

### IX. Sue

*The Clearwater house — 7:41 a.m.*

She'd heard the back door at six minutes to two.

She'd been lying awake. She'd been lying awake since she came in from Billy's at a quarter past eleven and stood outside her daughter's door listening to her daughter pretend to sleep, and she'd gone down the hall and got into bed and lain on her back in the dark with her hands folded on the quilt the way she used to lie when the kids were small and had a fever, and listened to the house.

So she'd heard the bedroom door. And the feet in the hall. And she'd heard them stop outside her own door, for half a second, and go on.

She'd heard the fourth stair not creak, because Leah had been skipping it since she was eleven.

And then the back door, and the broken step, and nothing.

Sue had got up then. She'd put her feet on the cold floor and her robe on over her nightgown and gone down the hall in the dark, not to the stairs, to the little window at the far end, the one over the back of the house that looked down across the yard to the trees. Harry had put that window in himself in 1987, when she was pregnant with Seth, because she'd said the hallway was too dark. He'd put it in crooked. She'd never told him.

She'd stood at the crooked window and looked down.

Her daughter was crossing the yard in the dark, barefoot in the wet grass, in one of her father's old T-shirts. Going down toward the trees. Toward the swing set. And at the bottom of the yard, by the swing set Harry had built when Leah was five, something was getting up off its haunches.

It was black. It was the size of a moving truck. It stood up slowly in the rain, unfolding, up and up, until its head was above the top bar of the swing set by a good three feet, and it looked down at her daughter, and her daughter stopped in front of it, and Sue Clearwater, who had seen her husband's grandfather's stories walk out of the woods behind her house on four legs in the spring, and had seen both her children go into those woods screaming and come back out as something else, and had buried her husband the same week, put her hand flat on the cold glass of the crooked window and didn't breathe.

They'd talked. She couldn't hear what. She'd seen Leah laugh, once, with her hand over her mouth, a thing Sue hadn't seen in so long she'd almost forgotten what it looked like.

And then Leah had said something, and the black thing had turned round.

All the way round. Slowly. It had turned its back on her daughter, and turned its great head toward the trees, and stood there with its ears out sideways, very still, while Leah pulled her father's T-shirt off over her head and folded it and put it on the swing.

Sue had stood at the window and watched it not look.

She'd thought about Sam, then. She hadn't meant to. She'd thought about the night in the spring when Sam had come up out of these same woods in the gray before dawn with her daughter, both of them human, both of them naked, Leah walking ahead with her arms wrapped round herself and her face like a closed door, and Sam behind her, not looking away. Not out of anything ugly. Sam wasn't ugly. Sam had simply been a boy who'd been changing in front of other boys in the woods for two years and had stopped thinking about it. He hadn't thought about it.

The black thing at the bottom of the yard had thought about it.

Then Leah had changed, and the two of them had gone, and Sue had stood at the crooked window for a long time after there was nothing to see.

She'd gone back to bed. She hadn't slept. At six she'd got up and come down and made coffee, and sat at the kitchen table with her hands round the mug, and waited.

---

They came in at nineteen minutes to eight.

She heard them on the beach road first. Leah's feet, on the gravel, and nothing beside them. She heard them come round the side of the house and across the yard. She heard Leah stop at the bottom of the porch, and say something low and sharp, and a man's voice say something back, and then she heard them come up the steps, Leah stepping over the broken one.

The back door opened.

Leah came in first. In her cutoffs and a black tank top, barefoot, with sand on her legs to the knee and her hair a wild salt-stiff mess. She stopped just inside the door.

She looked at her mother. Sue looked at her.

Sue had been looking at her daughter's face for twenty years. She'd looked at it new and red and screaming in the hospital in Forks, and at five, and at twelve, the day Leah had asked her father to stop calling her Lee-Lee and he'd stopped. At sixteen, the morning after a bonfire on First Beach, when Leah had come into this kitchen with a look on her face like she'd swallowed a lit match. And for the last two years she'd been looking at it every day across this table, and it had been a face she didn't know how to talk to. Sharp. Shut. Hard as a fist, all the time, even asleep. She'd stood over her daughter's bed at night in the summer and looked at it on the pillow and it had been clenched, even then.

It wasn't clenched now.

It was tired. It was sandy. It had a defiant set to the jaw that Sue recognized from about age four. But the fist had come undone. And under it, for the first time in two years, Sue could see her daughter.

She put her coffee down on the table, very carefully.

A man came in behind Leah.

He had to turn his shoulders a little to come through the door. The width of him made the doorway a near thing. He was perhaps six foot two, heavy through the chest and shoulders, with black hair cut short and full of sand and three days of dark stubble along a hard jaw. A nose that had been broken once, a long time ago, and set slightly to one side. Jeans. Brown boots, scuffed. A gray T-shirt and an open flannel shirt, blue and green, that was completely dry. Everything on him was dry, Sue saw. Leah's hair was stiff with salt and she had sand to the knees, and he looked like he'd just stepped out of a closet.

He touched his jaw as he came through the door, on the left side, briefly, with his thumb, the way a man checks he's remembered to shave. She saw Leah watch him do it.

Then he stopped just inside the kitchen, beside her daughter, and looked at Sue.

His eyes were green. A very clear, very dark green, the color of a bottle. He looked at her for about a second and in that second she had the strong and specific feeling, which she'd had once before in her life, in an emergency room in Port Angeles with a highway patrolman who'd come to tell her that her brother had gone off the road on 101 in the snow, of being looked at by somebody who'd already worked out exactly what she was going to do, and was waiting politely for her to do it.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said. "Morning."

English. A low, rough, ordinary voice.

"Morning," said Sue.

"Mom," said Leah. "This is—" She stopped. Sue watched her stop. Watched her daughter stand in the kitchen doorway with her chin up, and look at the floor for a second, and then look back up and say it anyway, with her jaw set. "This is Harry."

Sue didn't say anything.

She sat at the kitchen table with her hands flat on either side of her coffee mug and looked at the man in the flannel shirt, and she felt it go through her the way cold water goes through a wool coat. Slowly. All the way.

"Harry," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

She looked at Leah.

"I know, Mom," said Leah.

"Your father—"

"I *know*."

Sue looked back at the man. He was standing very still. He hadn't moved since he came in except to touch his jaw. He was looking at her, and his face was quite plain, and she understood, looking at it, that he knew. That he'd known before he came through the door.

"My husband's name was Harry," Sue said.

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry."

"How do you know?"

He glanced at Leah. Not for permission; she could see that. Just a glance, the way you'd glance at somebody you were standing beside to check they were still there.

"Leah," he said.

"She told you."

"She didn't have to," he said.

Sue looked at him for a long moment. She thought about what Seth had told her on the telephone yesterday afternoon from the Cullens' house, talking too fast, the way he did, half of it not making sense: *it goes both ways, Mom, it's not like ours, it's both ways, Jake says nobody's ever heard of it.* She'd sat at Billy's kitchen table last night for four hours with Billy and Old Quil and a pot of Billy's terrible coffee trying to work out what that could possibly mean.

She looked at the man standing in her kitchen who'd known her dead husband's name before she said it, and had been sorry about it, and she thought she was starting to.

"Well," said Sue. "He'd have laughed himself sick."

"*That's what I said,*" said Leah.

The man's mouth moved at one corner. Something short of a smile. The place a smile would go.

"Sit down," Sue said. "Both of you. You're making my kitchen look small."

---

He went for Harry's chair.

She saw him do it. He didn't think about it, she could tell; he just came across the kitchen and went straight for the chair at the end of the table, the one with its back to the wall, where you could see the back door and the front hall both at once without turning your head. He pulled it out. He'd started to sit down in it when Leah said, sharp, "Not—"

And stopped.

He'd stopped too. Half down. He looked at Leah, and then at the chair, and then at Sue, and she watched him understand. She watched it go across his face in about half a second.

He started to straighten up.

"Leave him," said Sue.

They both looked at her.

"It's a chair," said Sue. "Sit down in it, for God's sake. Nobody's sat in it for two years. It's starting to look like a shrine." She got up. "Eggs?"

He looked at her for a moment more. Then he sat down in her husband's chair, slowly, with his back to the wall and his hands on the table, and she watched him do it and felt something in her chest that she was going to think about later, on her own, out on the back step, where nobody could see.

"Please," he said. "If it's no trouble."

"It's no trouble. How many?"

He hesitated.

"He'll eat six," said Leah, dropping into the chair beside him. "At least. And bacon. And whatever bread you've got. Mom, he ate a whole pound of bacon before eleven yesterday. I saw it."

"How do you know what he ate before eleven yesterday? You met him at one."

Leah opened her mouth, and shut it, and went slightly red under the brown. "I just know," she muttered.

Sue looked at her daughter. And at the man. And back.

She got the eggs out.

---

She cooked. It was what she did. Harry used to say she'd have made a casserole for the Four Horsemen if they'd stopped by, and then asked them to bring the dish back. She made a dozen eggs and the rest of the bacon and the last of yesterday's fry bread heated through in the skillet, and a fresh pot of coffee, and she put it all down on the table in front of the two of them and stood back and watched him eat.

He ate like her son. That was the first thing she noticed. He ate like Seth ate, like Jacob ate, like every one of those boys who'd come through her kitchen since the spring, steadily, without fuss, without stopping, an amount of food that would have put a normal man in the hospital. Six eggs. Then the other six, when Leah pushed the plate across to him without looking. Half a pound of bacon. Eleven pieces of fry bread, with butter. He said *thank you* when she put the plate down and *this is very good* after the fourth piece of fry bread and nothing else, and he didn't look up much, and he didn't need to. Sue had raised two children and fed a husband for twenty years. She knew what it looked like when a person was actually hungry.

The second thing she noticed was that he'd sat down with his back to the wall.

She'd seen men do that. Harry's cousin Carl, who'd come back from Vietnam in 1971 and sat with his back to the wall in every room for the rest of his life. A state trooper she'd gone out with twice before she met Harry. They sat where they could see the doors. It was a habit you got from somewhere and never got rid of. This man had sat down with his back to the wall in a strange kitchen without even looking round first, the way you'd breathe.

The third thing was harder to name.

She stood at the stove with her back half to him and watched him in the window glass over the sink, eating, and tried to put her finger on it. He was eating breakfast. That was all he was doing. He'd said six words. He was sitting in her dead husband's chair in a flannel shirt with butter on his fingers, and her daughter was beside him, eating off the same plate, leaning so close their shoulders touched.

And the kitchen was smaller.

That was the only way she could put it. Size had nothing to do with it; Seth was bigger, and Jacob was bigger, and they both filled this kitchen to the doorframes and it never felt like this. It was as if there was more of him than there was room for. As if he were sitting very still on purpose, the way you'd hold a big dog still on a short leash in a crowded room, and the effort of it was something you could feel across the table, like heat off a stove.

Sue had grown up on the reservation. She'd grown up on Taha Aki and the spirit warriors and the cold ones, and she'd laughed at them, the way you laughed at your grandmother's stories, until the spring, when they'd walked out of the woods behind her house. She'd sat across this table from Sam Uley the morning after Leah changed, and from Jacob, and from her own son, and she knew what it felt like to sit across a table from something that wasn't entirely a person.

This was something else.

She noted it. That was all. The way she'd note a man was left-handed, or a smoker, or had been in the service. She noted that the man eating eggs in her kitchen was the most frightening thing she'd ever sat across a table from, and that he was making an effort not to be, and that it wasn't really working. And that he knew it wasn't. And didn't seem to care.

"Seth says you're a wizard," she said.

He swallowed. "Yes, ma'am."

"And something else."

"And something else."

"I saw it," said Sue. "Last night. At the bottom of my yard."

Leah put her fork down. The man didn't. He looked at Sue across the table with the fork in his hand and a piece of bacon on the end of it, and she watched him decide something.

"I know," he said. "I heard you get up."

"You heard me get up."

"Your floor creaks. The hall. By the little window." He put the bacon in his mouth, and chewed, and swallowed. "And then you didn't move for about ten minutes."

"You knew I was watching."

"Yes."

"And you turned round anyway. When she changed."

He looked at her for a moment. Then he shrugged, a little, one shoulder.

"Seemed polite," he said.

Sue looked at him. She didn't say anything. Beside him, she watched her daughter look down at her plate, and go very still, and go a little bit red again.

"What do you do?" Sue said. "For a living. Seth says you had a badge."

"I can't tell you that, Mrs. Clearwater. I'm sorry."

"Can't, or won't?"

"Can't." He put the fork down. He put both his hands flat on the table on either side of his plate, the way she'd been sitting when they came in, and she noticed it, and wondered if he had. "There's a thing on me. A promise I made. It doesn't let me. I'd tell you if I could." He glanced sideways. "Leah's got it too, now. She tried to tell the doctor's family last night and it shut her up for her. She'll tell you."

Sue looked at Leah.

"It's like a hand," said Leah. "On your throat. It doesn't hurt. It just doesn't let you."

Sue looked at her daughter for a long time.

"Does she know?" she asked him. "What you do?"

"She knows."

"All of it?"

"All of it."

"Is it bad?"

He thought about that. She watched him think about it. He didn't look away from her while he did it, and he didn't look anywhere else, and he didn't take long.

"Some of it," he said.

"Are *you* bad?"

"Mom," said Leah.

"It's a fair question," he said. To Leah. Then to Sue: "Some of me. Yeah." He picked up his coffee. "Not the bit that's going to be anywhere near your daughter."

"How do you know which bit's which?"

"I've had a lot of practice," he said, and drank some coffee.

Sue stood at the stove with the spatula in her hand. Out of the corner of her eye, she could see the list on the fridge door, under a magnet shaped like a salmon. She'd written it on the back of the electric bill in October, for Seth, and never taken it down. *SHORTS (CHEAP) (LOTS). Propane (2). Batteries D. DO NOT buy candy with this, Seth, I mean it.*

"Are you going to take her away?" she said.

It came out quieter than she'd meant.

The man put his cup down.

"No, ma'am," he said.

"You're English."

"I am."

"England's a long way away."

"It is. I'm not going back to it. Not for a while."

"How long's a while?"

He didn't look at Leah. Sue noticed that. He kept his eyes on Sue's face, and he said it to her, plainly, the way you'd tell somebody the time.

"As long as she's here," he said.

Leah had gone absolutely still in her chair.

Sue looked at them both for a long time. Then she turned round and put the spatula down on the stove and stood there with her back to them and her hands flat on the counter, looking out of the window over the sink at the yard. At the wet grass. At the swing set. At the place at the bottom of it, by the trees, where the grass was flattened in a great broad patch, as if something very large had sat down there in the night and not moved for a while.

She stood there until she was sure of her face.

"Well," she said. "I suppose you'd better have more coffee, then."

---

He noticed the list.

She saw him see it, when she brought the coffee pot over. His eyes went past her to the fridge, and stopped, and he read it. She watched him read it. She watched his face do something complicated.

"Seth," he said, "goes through nine pairs a week."

"In a bad week," said Sue. "In a good week, four."

"I can fix that," he said.

She stopped with the pot in her hand.

"I did hers this morning," he said, and nodded at Leah's cutoffs. "Charm on the cloth. When she changes, they'll go with her. When she changes back, they'll come back. Won't tear. Won't get lost. Won't need washing, much." He looked up at Sue. "I can do Seth's. And Jacob's. And anyone else's who wants it. It takes about a second."

Sue stood there with the coffee pot.

She thought about the clearance rack at Newton's. About Mrs. Newton's face at the register, every time. About sitting at this table on a Sunday night with the checkbook and the calculator and the electric bill, and the phone bill, and the propane, and Harry's life insurance, which had been less than either of them had thought, and doing the sums over, and over, and finding forty dollars somewhere for khaki shorts on clearance because her son was going to go out into the woods at four in the morning and come home naked in the rain. About the night in September when Seth had come in through the back door at five in the morning wearing a garbage bag he'd found in the ditch by the highway, with a hole cut out for his head.

"Do you know," said Sue Clearwater, "how much I've spent on shorts since March?"

"*That's what I said,*" said Leah, again.

"Four hundred and twelve dollars," said Sue. "And sixty cents. I keep the receipts. I don't know why I keep the receipts. I think I was going to send them to somebody." She put the pot down on the table. Hard. "Four hundred and twelve dollars."

"I'll do them today," said the man.

"You'll do them *today*."

"Soon as he gets home."

Sue looked at him. Then she sat down, abruptly, in the chair across from him, and put both her hands over her face, and laughed.

She couldn't stop for a while. She laughed until her eyes ran. She laughed until she had to take her hands away and wipe them on the dish towel, and her daughter was staring at her, and the Englishman was sitting very still in Harry's chair with his coffee, watching her the way you'd watch a pot to see whether it was going to boil over, and that made her laugh harder.

"Mom," said Leah. "Mom, are you—"

"I'm *fine*," Sue said. "I'm fine. Four hundred and twelve dollars." She wiped her eyes. "Harry would have *loved* you," she said to the man. "Do you know that? He'd have had you out on the jetty with a thermos by Saturday. He'd have made you clean fish."

"I'm not bad with a fish," he said.

"No," said Sue. "I don't suppose you are."

---

The phone rang at twenty past eight. It was Billy.

She talked to him standing at the counter with the cord wrapped round her hand, the way she always did, and she kept her back half to the table, and she watched them in the window glass while she did it. Leah, finishing the last of the bacon. The man, with his coffee. He'd turned his head slightly when the phone rang, the way a dog turns its head at a sound in another room, and then turned it back. She'd seen that. She'd also seen that he'd turned it a half second *before* the phone rang, and she put that away, and didn't look at it.

"Nine o'clock," she said, when she hung up. "Billy's coming. Old Quil can't, his hip's bad in the wet, but he says do what we think and he'll come by and look at you himself tomorrow." She sat back down. "Billy's bringing Sam."

She watched her daughter's face.

She'd been watching her daughter's face at the sound of that name for two years. She knew exactly what happened to it. She'd seen it happen across this table, and in the hospital in Forks the night Harry died, and at the funeral, and at Billy's at Christmas, and on the beach at the bonfire in July when Sam had walked down the sand from the parking lot with Emily's hand in his. It went like a door slamming. It went white round the mouth, and something went out of the eyes, and the jaw went tight enough to crack a tooth. Every single time.

Leah looked up from her plate.

"Okay," she said.

That was all. She said it the way she'd have said it if Sue had told her Billy was bringing a pie. She shrugged. She picked up the last piece of fry bread, and tore it in half, and gave half to the man without looking at him, and he took it without looking at her, and she ate her half.

Sue got up from the table and went back to the stove, though there wasn't anything on the stove, and stood there with her back to the room.

She was going to need the back step. She was going to need it for about an hour. Tonight, at nine, when everybody had gone home.

### X. Sam

*The Clearwater house — 8:58 a.m.*

He'd smelled it from the road.

He'd been driving with the window down, the way he always did, because the cab of the truck got too hot with him in it even in November, and Billy in the passenger seat with his good jacket on had complained about the cold for the whole three minutes from the Blacks' house to the Clearwaters', and Sam had smelled it as soon as they turned off the main road onto the gravel. He'd taken his foot off the gas without meaning to. The truck had slowed to a crawl.

"What," Billy had said.

Sam hadn't answered. He'd sat with both hands on the wheel and breathed it in.

Wolf.

Not the pack. Not his and not Jacob's. Not anything Quileute, not anything that had ever been Quileute; he knew the smell of his own people the way he knew the smell of his own hands, and this was nothing he'd ever smelled before. Bigger. Heavier. Wilder. Like the far side of a mountain range. Like snow on rock in the dark. It was in the air over the whole street. It was in the trees behind the Clearwater house, and on the gravel, and on the grass of the back yard, and as he pulled the truck in by the shed and killed the engine and sat there, he could see, through the windshield, at the bottom of the yard, by the old swing set Harry Clearwater had built, a broad flattened place in the long wet grass where something had sat down in the night.

He got out of the truck and walked down the yard to look at it. He didn't decide to. Billy said his name, sharp, behind him. He kept walking.

There were prints at the edge of the flattened place. Not many; the grass held them badly, and the rain had been at them. But there was one at the very edge, in a patch of bare mud under the swing set where the kids' feet had worn the grass away twenty years ago and it had never come back. One forepaw. Deep. Clean. Spread wide.

Sam crouched down beside it and put his own hand next to it, flat, fingers spread.

He'd done this before. They all had. In the spring, the first few weeks, when Jared and then Paul had first changed and nobody knew anything, he'd crouched in the mud by the river with each of them and put his hand down next to their tracks to see how big they'd gotten. It was a stupid thing. It was a thing boys did. It had been comforting, a little, at the time.

His hand fit inside the print with room around it.

He stayed there, crouched in the rain, for a while longer than he needed to. His own wolf had come up under his skin without being asked, the way it did when something was wrong. Its hackles were all the way up. Its ears were all the way back. It didn't want to be here. It didn't want to be anywhere near the thing that had made this print. It had been the biggest thing in these woods for three years, until Jacob, and it had never minded Jacob; Jacob was theirs. This wasn't theirs. And it was frightened. Sam crouched there with his hand in the print and felt his own wolf be frightened, for the first time in its life, and didn't know what to do with it.

"Sam," said Billy, from the truck. "Get my chair out of the bed or come and carry me. I'm getting rained on."

Sam got up.

---

He carried Billy's chair up the porch steps, and then Billy, and set him down in it and pushed him through the back door into the kitchen.

He'd been in this kitchen a thousand times. He'd been in it when he was eight and Harry Clearwater had shown him how to tie a fly at this table. He'd been in it at seventeen, at Thanksgiving, with Leah's hand on his knee under the tablecloth. He'd been in it the morning after she changed, sitting across from Sue, trying to explain, with his voice not working. He'd been in it since, a few times, at council business, when he couldn't avoid it, and every time he'd stood in that doorway and felt the guilt come up in him like floodwater in a basement.

He stood in the doorway.

Sue was at the counter. She looked at him, and gave him a nod, the way she'd given him a nod every time for two years: civil, and nothing else. Leah was standing beside her, leaning on the counter with a mug of coffee, barefoot, in cutoffs and a tank top.

And at the end of the table, in Harry Clearwater's chair, with his back to the wall, there was a man.

He was eating fry bread. He had a copy of the *Peninsula Daily News* open on the table beside his plate, folded back to the sports page. He was big through the shoulders, black-haired, unshaven, in a flannel shirt. He looked up when Sam came in.

His eyes were green. They came up off the newspaper and landed on Sam's face and stayed there.

And Sam's wolf went down.

He felt it happen. It happened under his skin, in the middle of his chest, all at once, between one heartbeat and the next. His wolf, his Alpha wolf, the first wolf of his generation, the one the whole pack had come up around like water around a stone, that had stood its ground in a clearing in June with newborn vampires coming out of the trees on every side and never once given a single inch, put its ears flat to its skull and its belly to the ground and its tail between its legs. It didn't decide to. It just went. The way a dog goes down when something walks into a room that it knows, all the way down in its bones, it cannot fight.

The man hadn't done anything. He'd looked up from a newspaper.

Sam stood in the doorway with Billy's wheelchair handles in his fists, and he felt his face go hot, and then cold, and he felt something come up under the cold that was very old and very angry and had nothing to do with his wolf at all.

"You're on our land," Sam said.

"So I keep hearing," said the man.

English. Of course he was English. Billy had said so last night. Jacob had said so on the phone. Sam had been half hoping it wasn't true.

"You crossed the line last night."

"I did."

"The treaty—"

"Says cold ones," said the man. "I read it." He turned a page of the newspaper. "Billy's copy. Nice paper. Bit foxed at the corners."

Billy, in the wheelchair, made a small sound that might have been a cough.

"You read my copy," Billy said.

"Over Seth's shoulder. Yesterday. He'd seen it once, in your front room, in a frame over the telly, and I was having a look round in his head for something else." The man folded the paper and put it down. "Sorry. Bad habit."

"You were in Seth's *head*?" said Sam.

"Yeah."

"You just—went into a kid's head. Like it was nothing."

"He was thinking very loud." The man looked at him. "So are you."

Sam felt it then.

It was so faint he'd have missed it if he hadn't been listening for it. If Jacob hadn't told him on the phone last night, at eleven o'clock, in a strained flat voice, *he gets in your head, Sam, he just walks in*. It was like a draught. Like a door opened somewhere else in the house, and a little cold air coming down the hall. A pressure at the back of his skull, very light, there and gone.

And then the man's face changed.

Sam didn't know what he'd seen. He knew afterward. He worked it out later that day, sitting in his truck at the end of Emily's road with the engine off for an hour, not going in. He knew what he'd been thinking about in that doorway, because he was always thinking about it in that doorway, every time, he couldn't help it. Emily. Emily on the porch at her parents' house in Makah with the sun on her. And Emily three months later, in the kitchen at his place, and him angry about something stupid, something about her cousin, about Leah, and the heat coming up in him too fast, and the wolf coming out of him a foot away from her. And her face afterward. The three long lines down the right side of it, from her eye to her jaw, that she'd have for the rest of her life.

And Leah. Leah under it, always, at the bottom of everything. Leah at sixteen on the dirt road behind this house. Leah at the funeral. Leah screaming in the yard. Leah in his pack for six months, in his head, every day, with her hate burning at the back of his skull like a coal, and him knowing he'd earned every bit of it.

Something went across the man's face, and settled, and stayed.

The room got cold.

Sam felt it go. Everybody did. It went out from the end of the table where the man was sitting like ripples going out on a pond. Billy pulled his good jacket tighter. Sue, at the counter, put her mug down. The coffee in the pot on the hot plate stopped steaming. It just stopped, mid-curl, like somebody had pressed a button. And the window over the sink, the one that looked out on the yard, went white at the corners. Frost. Fine and feathery, coming in from the edges of the glass in delicate branching lines, the way it came in on the truck windshield in January.

Sam's wolf was flat on the floor of his chest with its eyes shut.

The man sat in Harry Clearwater's chair and looked at him, and didn't say anything at all, and the frost crept in across the window.

"*Not in my kitchen*," said Sue Clearwater.

The man blinked.

He looked at her. And something went out of his face, and the room let go of its breath. Sam felt it happen. The frost on the window stopped creeping. Then it went back, all of it, from the middle out to the corners, and was gone, leaving the glass clear and wet. The coffee on the hot plate started to steam again.

"Sorry, Mrs. Clearwater," said the man.

"Don't be sorry. Just don't do it."

"Yes, ma'am."

"I've only just got the heat up in this kitchen," said Sue. "It's November."

"Sorry," he said again. He didn't look sorry. He didn't look anything. He looked at Sam again, with his face gone back to what it had been when Sam came in, plain and a little bored, a man reading the sports page, and he said: "Does that sometimes. Didn't mean to."

"You were in my head," said Sam.

"Yeah."

"You—*saw*."

"Yeah."

Sam stood there. He didn't know what to do. He'd walked in here ready for a fight. He'd driven over ready for one. He'd been ready for one since eleven o'clock last night, sitting up in bed beside Emily with the phone in his hand and Jacob's voice in his ear, not sleeping. And the fight had walked into his head and looked at the worst thing he'd ever done and made the room go cold with it, and then it had apologized to Sue about the kitchen.

"Stay out of it," Sam said. His voice came out rough. "My head. Stay out."

"I'll try," the man said. "You think very loud."

That was it. That was all he said. He didn't promise. He didn't say sorry again. He picked up his coffee and drank some of it and looked at Sam over the rim, and Sam understood, all at once and very clearly, that this man was perfectly well aware that he'd just frightened an Alpha wolf in front of his own elder, in a kitchen, without moving his hands, and that he didn't care about it at all. He hadn't wanted to. He might have stopped if he'd noticed sooner. But now that it was done, it was done, and he wasn't going to make a show of being sorry for it, and he certainly wasn't going to be anything other than what he was to make Sam feel better.

Sam had met a lot of men who wanted to frighten people. He'd been one, for a while, when he was nineteen and first changed and had no idea what to do with it.

He'd never met one who simply didn't mind that he did.

---

"Hey, Sam," said Leah.

He turned.

He'd been bracing for it since the truck. He'd been bracing for it, if he was honest, for two years. He'd stood in this doorway a dozen times and braced for her, the way you brace for a slap you know is coming, because it always came, even when she didn't say anything. Especially when she didn't say anything. The look. Her face going white round the mouth and her eyes going flat and empty and her jaw locking, and the wire between them, the one that had never gone, the one he'd felt in the pack every day she'd been in it, going tight as a garrote.

He turned round to face it.

She was leaning on the counter with her coffee. She was looking at him.

It didn't come.

He stood there and waited for it, and it didn't come. She was looking at him the way you'd look at somebody from down the road you'd known since you were a kid. Somebody you'd see at the store. A neighbor. Her face was tired and sandy and a little bit wry, and her eyes were just her eyes, and her mouth was just her mouth.

"Leah," he said.

"I'm okay," she said. "Like, actually. You can stop doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face you've been doing for two years. Every time you come in a room I'm in." She did an impression of it. It was very good. It was a sort of wince, a sort of guilty hangdog grimace, a face like a man waiting to be hit, and he recognized it, because he'd seen it in the mirror. "That one. You can stop. I'm okay."

Sam stood in the doorway with his hands still on Billy's chair.

He felt it go. He couldn't have said what it was. The guilt was still there; he'd carry the guilt for the rest of his life and he'd always known that and he'd made his peace with it, more or less, in the long nights. What went was the thing the guilt had been tied to. The weight on the other end. He'd been carrying her pain for two years like a man carrying a sack of stones up a hill, and he'd known he deserved it, and he'd never once put it down, because he'd thought that putting it down would be the worst thing he could do to her. That carrying it was the only thing he had left to give her.

She'd set her end of it down. In the night. Somewhere. He could see it on her face. She'd set it down and walked away from it, and he was standing here holding the other end of nothing.

His knees went.

Not all the way. He caught himself on the doorframe. He stood there with one hand on the frame and the other on Billy's chair and his eyes shut, and he heard Billy say "*Sam*," and he heard Sue put her mug down, and he heard the man at the end of the table not say anything at all.

"Sit down, Sam," said Sue. Not unkindly.

He sat down. In the nearest chair. At the other end of the table from the Englishman, with Billy's wheelchair beside him.

He sat there for a while with his hands flat on the table, and nobody said anything, and that was all right.

---

They did the council in the end, because that was what they'd come for.

Billy ran it. Billy always ran things; he'd been running things since before Sam was born, from a wheelchair since Sam was ten, and he ran this one from the side of the kitchen table with his good jacket still on and a mug of Sue's coffee in his hands, and he ran it the way he ran everything, which was by asking questions in a mild voice and waiting, patiently, for people to say more than they'd meant to.

He didn't get much out of the Englishman. Sam watched him try.

"Jacob says your name's Potter," Billy said.

"It is."

"Seth's girl says that's a name people know."

"Some people."

"She says you're famous."

"She's a bit dramatic."

"She says," Billy said mildly, "that you killed the worst man in the world when you were seventeen."

"She's right about that bit," said the Englishman, and buttered a piece of fry bread.

He didn't say anything else about it. Billy waited. He went on buttering.

He wouldn't say what he did. He said so, plainly, the way he'd apparently said it to Sue: there was a thing on him that didn't let him. Leah backed him up. She said it had shut her throat at the Cullens', and Sam looked at her and believed her, and didn't like it. He wouldn't say who he worked for. He wouldn't say why he was on the peninsula, except that it was something to do with the cold ones, the red-eyed ones, the ones that came through from Canada and up from California and ate hikers. He wouldn't say what he was doing with the one he'd caught, except that it wasn't going to be leaving.

"Leah says you've got it in a room," said Billy.

"I do."

"Seth says you're—what did he say? Studying it."

"Something like that."

"Is it suffering?"

The Englishman looked at Billy for a moment. Sam watched him.

"Yes," he said.

Billy didn't say anything.

"It ate forty-one people this year," said the Englishman. "That I counted. One of them on Sunday night up the Hoh. A dental hygienist from Olympia. She'd stopped to take a picture of the river." He put the butter knife down. "It's suffering. I'm not going to sit at Mrs. Clearwater's table and tell you it isn't. And I'm not going to tell you I'm sorry."

Billy looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, once. And Sam, at the far end of the table, found to his own surprise that he'd nodded too.

He'd killed them. All of them had. They'd torn them apart and burned the pieces and stood in the smoke and been glad. He'd never once thought about whether they suffered. He'd never had to. He'd never had one in a room.

"All right," said Billy. "What do you want from us?"

The Englishman told him.

He wanted to stay. That was the first thing. He was going to be on the peninsula a long time, he said. Longer than he'd planned. He wasn't going to be living on the reservation; he had a place. But he'd be crossing the line. He'd be coming here. He'd be here, he said, glancing sideways at Leah, a lot.

He wanted to be asked.

"Asked," said Billy.

"Invited. By you. By the council." The Englishman folded his hands on the table. "There's people in this country, my sort of people, who are going to come looking for me eventually. The American sort. They've got a field office in Seattle, and they've noticed things on this peninsula, and one of them's going to turn up in Forks sooner or later asking questions. And when they do, I'd like to be able to tell them I'm here at the invitation of the Quileute Tribal Council, on Quileute land, as a guest of a sovereign nation. And I'd like them to have to go and knock on your door about it instead of mine."

Billy looked at him. Then he started to laugh.

It was the big laugh, the real one, that started deep down in his chest and came up, and it went on for a while. It turned into a cough. Sue got up and got him a glass of water and thumped him on the back, and he drank the water, and wiped his eyes.

"You want us to be your alibi," Billy said.

"I want you to be my landlord."

"The United States government," said Billy, "has been trying to get out of the Treaty of Olympia since the ink dried in 1856. And you want me to wave it at them."

"I want you to wave it at a different bit of them," said the Englishman. "The bit nobody's ever told you about. The bit that's never once in a hundred and fifty years worked out what it's allowed to do on a reservation, and is very, very nervous about finding out."

Billy laughed again, softer.

"And what do we get?" said Sam.

Everybody looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it. He'd been sitting at the end of the table with his hands flat on it for twenty minutes, not saying anything, feeling the place in his chest where the weight had been. But he was still Alpha. He still had nine wolves who'd want to know.

The Englishman looked at him.

"What do you get," he said. "Right." He put his hand in his shirt pocket and took something out and put it on the table.

It was a stone. A river stone, gray, smooth, about the size of a hen's egg. There was a mark cut into it, very fine, a pattern of lines that hurt a little to look at, like the edge of something seen out of the corner of your eye.

"I've put a net over the peninsula," he said. "Over the whole of it. From the strait to the Quinault. Two hundred-odd stones like this one, buried, all tied together. It took me three weeks. It's what caught the one Leah chased." He nudged the stone across the table toward Billy with one finger. "It knows what the cold ones taste like. And it knows what they've been eating. Your doctor's lot come up gold. The other kind come up red. Every time one of the red ones crosses onto this peninsula from anywhere, I know about it. That's how I knew yesterday. It's how I'll know the next time."

He took another stone out of the pocket and put it down. And another. And another. Four of them, in a little row on Sue Clearwater's kitchen table.

"These are tied to it," he said. "The net. When a red one comes in, they'll heat up. You'll feel it in your pocket. You'll know before your wolves catch the smell. You'll have time." He pushed one to Billy, one to Sue, one down the table to Sam. "There's one for Clifford, too."

"For who?" said Billy.

"Jacob," said Leah. "He means Jacob."

"Why does he mean Jacob?"

"Big red dog," said the Englishman. "Kids' books."

Billy looked at him. Then he looked at Leah. Then he picked up the stone in front of him, and turned it over in his fingers, and looked at the mark on it, and Sam watched his face. Billy Black was sixty-one years old and he'd been the keeper of the stories since his father died, and he'd sat by the fire every year of Sam's life and told them, and Sam had believed every word, even when he was a kid and they were just stories. He watched Billy hold a piece of the Englishman's net in his hand, and he watched Billy's face do something it didn't usually do, which was look uncertain.

"And the cold ones you catch," said Billy. "The red ones. What happens to them?"

"They don't leave," said the Englishman.

"Ever?"

"Ever."

Billy put the stone in the pocket of his good jacket.

Sam looked down at the one in front of him. He didn't pick it up.

"He's dangerous," he said.

He said it to Billy. To Sue. To the table. He didn't look at the Englishman.

"Yes," said the Englishman.

Sam looked at him.

"You don't deny it."

"What would be the point? You felt your wolf go down when I looked at you. I watched it happen." The man shrugged, a little, one shoulder. "I'm dangerous. I'm dangerous to anything that eats people. And to anybody who'd hurt anybody in this kitchen." His eyes went sideways, very briefly, to Leah, and came back. "Anybody else on this peninsula's got nothing to worry about from me. Including you. You've got the law, anyway. Leah tells me."

"The law," said Sam.

"Absolute, she says. None of yours can touch me." He picked up his coffee. "Which works out nicely, because I'd hate to have to explain to Mrs. Clearwater why there's a wolf through her kitchen wall."

"*Not in my kitchen*," said Sue, from the stove, automatically.

"No, ma'am. Wouldn't dream of it."

Sam sat there.

He looked at the stone in front of him. He thought about his pack. About Jared and Paul and Quil and Embry, and the little ones, Collin and Brady, fourteen years old, who'd changed in September and hadn't stopped being scared since. About the night in June in the clearing with the newborns coming out of the trees and the smell of them, and Leah going for one alone, and Jacob's bones breaking. About the hikers. The hikers every summer, and the ones they never found in time.

He thought about the print in the mud by the swing set, with his hand inside it and room all around.

"And the shorts," said Leah.

Sam looked at her.

"He can fix the shorts," said Leah. "Tell him. Tell him about the shorts."

"Oh, for God's sake," said the Englishman.

"Tell him."

"It's a charm. On the cloth. Your lot change, the clothes go with them, they come back when they change back." He sounded faintly embarrassed. "I did hers this morning. I'll do yours. Anyone who wants it. It's not a big—"

"You can fix the *shorts*?" said Sam.

He heard himself say it. He heard his own voice go up at the end like a kid's. He heard Billy, beside him, start to laugh again, and try not to, and fail, and put his face in his hands.

"Four hundred and twelve dollars," said Sue Clearwater, from the stove, very quietly. "Since March."

Sam looked at the Englishman, and the Englishman looked back at him, and something went across his face. Something short of a smile: a sort of grim, wry, tired understanding. The look of a man who'd once had to walk home through a forest in the rain with nothing on, and remembered.

"Yeah," he said. "I can fix the shorts."

Sam picked up the stone.

---

Billy wrote it on council paper.

He had a folder in the bag on the back of his wheelchair; he always had a folder. He wrote it out slowly, longhand, in his careful square capitals, with Sue's ballpoint, at the side of the kitchen table, and read it out as he went.

*The Quileute Tribal Council welcomes the bearer of this letter as a guest of the Quileute Nation, on Quileute land, at our invitation, for as long as he is welcome. He has the protection and hospitality of the tribe.*

"What name do I put?" Billy said.

"Evans," said the Englishman. "H. J. Evans."

Billy looked up. "Jacob says it's Potter."

"It is."

"So which do I write?"

"Evans." The Englishman finished his coffee. "Evans is on my passport. Evans is a photographer from London who takes pictures of moss. Potter's for friends."

"And are we friends?" said Billy mildly.

The Englishman looked at him. And at Sue, at the stove. And at Leah beside her. And last, for a second longer than the others, at Sam.

"Getting there," he said.

Billy wrote *H. J. Evans*. He signed it, and dated it, *November 21st, 2006*, and passed it to Sue, and Sue dried her hands and signed it. Then Billy turned the paper round and pushed it down the table to Sam, with the pen.

"You're not council," Billy said. "But you're Alpha. You can witness."

Sam looked at it for a long time.

Then he signed his name at the bottom, under Sue's.

---

The Englishman walked him out.

Sam hadn't asked him to. Billy was staying, Billy said; Billy wanted another cup of coffee and to see whether Jacob turned up, and Sue would drive him home. So Sam went out the back door alone and down the porch steps, over the broken one, and across the wet yard toward his truck. And halfway there, he realized the Englishman was walking beside him.

He hadn't heard him come out. He hadn't heard the door, or the steps, or his feet on the grass. He was just there, at Sam's shoulder, with his hands in his pockets, walking.

They got to the truck. Sam stopped. He didn't open the door.

The Englishman stopped too. He stood looking down the yard at the swing set, at the flattened place in the grass by the trees, and he didn't say anything for a while.

Then he said, without looking round: "She's not angry anymore."

Sam didn't say anything.

"I don't know what it was like, before," the Englishman said. "I wasn't there. I've seen it, a bit. In her head, in yours. I saw what you saw, in there." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm not going to tell you it was all right. It wasn't. You know that."

"I know," said Sam.

"But she's put it down. Last night, sometime. I felt her do it." The Englishman turned round and looked at him. "So you can put your end down too. That's all. That's what I came out to say. You can put it down."

Sam stood by his truck in the rain.

He'd been yelled at by Leah Clearwater a thousand times. He'd been hated by her in his own head every day for six months and he'd taken it, because he'd deserved it. He'd been cursed out by Paul and argued with by Jacob and stared down by Billy and wept at by Emily's mother on her porch in Makah. He'd been ready, this morning, driving over, to be hit.

He hadn't been ready for this. For a stranger, an Englishman, the most frightening thing he'd ever stood next to, to walk him to his truck without making a sound, and stand in the rain, and tell him plainly that he could stop.

He looked down at his hands. He was still holding the stone.

"Why do you care?" he said. It came out rough.

The Englishman considered that.

"Because she does," he said. "A bit. Under all of it. She'd never say so." He shrugged. "And because you've been carrying it so long you've worn a groove. I can see it from here."

He turned round and walked back across the yard toward the house. Sam watched him go. He watched the long wet grass not move under his boots.

At the foot of the porch steps, the Englishman stopped and looked back.

"Bring your lot round," he said. "One at a time, or all at once, I don't mind. For the shorts. Tell the little ones I won't bite."

"Will you?" said Sam.

"Not the little ones," said the Englishman, and went up the steps, and in.

Sam got in his truck. He put the stone on the dashboard. He sat there for a while with the engine off and the rain on the windshield and his hands on the wheel, and then he drove home, slowly, the long way, round by the marina and up the hill past the school, and parked at the end of Emily's road with the engine off, and sat there for an hour, and didn't go in.

When he did go in, Emily looked up from the stove and saw his face and put the spoon down.

"Sam?" she said. "What happened?"

He came across the kitchen and put his arms round her and his face in her hair and stood there.

"Leah's okay," he said, into it. "Em. Leah's okay."

---

### XI. Jacob

*The Clearwater house — 9:43 a.m.*

He smelled it from a mile out and his hackles went up so hard they hurt.

He'd been running since half past eight. He'd slept two hours on the Cullens' couch with Nessie on his chest and her curls in his face and her small hot hand on his cheek, showing him pictures in her sleep. Purple horses, mostly, and the blue lights from the witch, and once, very clearly, Leah's face, with a question mark over it. And then he'd woken up at twenty-five past eight with his dad's voice on the house phone in Esme's hand saying *council at nine, Sue's, he's there, he came in with Leah this morning*, and he'd gone out the back door and phased on the lawn without even taking his shorts off. Which was the third pair this week.

He'd run the whole way. West, through the trees, the long way round the edge of Forks, across the river and down toward the sea. The pack mind had been empty the whole way. No Seth. No Leah. Just him, and the rain, and the trees going by, and his own head going round and round the same groove it had been going round since seven o'clock last night. *Harry Potter. Harry Potter. Also a wolf. Bigger than you.*

And then, about a mile out from the Clearwaters', where the trees came down to the back of the village, he'd smelled it.

He'd stopped dead in the ferns.

It was everywhere. On the trees. On the ground. In the air over the whole back end of the village like smoke after a fire. Big. Heavy. Wild. Like the far side of a mountain. Like nothing he'd ever smelled. It made every hair on his body stand up from his ears to his tail, and something under his ribs that was older than him and older than his great-grandfather bare its teeth all on its own.

He'd followed it in.

It went down through the trees at the back of the village the long way round, behind the yards, the way you'd go if you didn't want to be seen. It went right to the Clearwaters' back yard. It went to the bottom of it, by the old swing set, to a great big flattened place in the grass.

And there was a print.

One print. In the bare mud under the swing set, where the grass didn't grow. One forepaw. Deep and clean and wide.

Jacob had stood over it for a long time with the rain coming down on his back.

Then he'd put his own forepaw down beside it.

He'd known before he did it. He'd known from the smell. He did it anyway, the way you push on a bruise. He put his paw down in the mud next to it, flat, spread, carefully, so the edges lined up.

His paw went inside it with room around the edge.

He was the biggest wolf in a thousand years of stories. Everybody said so. His dad said so at the bonfire. Old Quil said so. Sam said so, grudgingly, once, the first time they'd stood side by side after Jacob changed, and Jacob had looked down at the top of Sam's head and felt something go through him that he was a little ashamed of now, but not very. He'd been the biggest thing in these woods for a year. He'd *liked* it.

He'd stood there with his paw in a stranger's print, and he'd thought, very clearly, in a voice that sounded a lot like Leah's: *A head. At least.*

---

He phased back behind the shed and came up onto the porch in his cutoffs with his hair dripping.

The kitchen smelled of coffee and bacon and fry bread and Sue's rose soap and his dad, and of *him*, so strong it was like walking into a wall. Jacob stood in the doorway.

His dad was at the side of the table in his wheelchair with his good jacket on and a mug in his hands and something in his face Jacob hadn't seen for a long time. Sue was at the sink. Leah was sitting on the counter beside the coffee maker with her bare feet dangling, eating an apple.

And at the end of the table, in Harry Clearwater's chair, there was a man.

Jacob had seen him already. In Seth's head on the ridge, and in Leah's, and in his own nightmares at about four that morning. He'd thought, from the memories, that he knew what the guy looked like. Tall. Big shoulders. Black hair. Green eyes. A suit.

No suit. A flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled up, and he had a pencil behind one ear and a sheet torn off Sue's notepad in front of him with a list of names on it, and he was sitting with his back to the wall in Harry Clearwater's chair like he'd sat in it every morning for twenty years.

He looked up.

Jacob felt his wolf put its ears back.

It didn't go down. He wouldn't let it. He stood in the doorway and held it up by the scruff with everything he had, and he felt it fight him, and he felt it want to go down. Flat, belly to the floor, the way he'd felt Sam's go down in the clearing in September when Nessie looked at him from her mother's arms and the law came down on all of them like a hand. It *wanted* to. And that made Jacob angrier than anything had made him in a year.

The man looked at him for about a second.

"You must be the big ginger one," he said.

"*Russet*," said Jacob.

"It's ginger," said Leah, from the counter, with her mouth full of apple.

"It's *russet*, Leah."

"Clifford," said the man.

Jacob stared at him.

"*Who* the hell is *Clifford*?"

"Big red dog," said Seth, behind him.

Jacob turned round. Seth was coming up the porch steps with Layla, the two of them wet from the rain, Seth with his arm round Layla's waist, Layla walking stiff and careful like her back still hurt, which it obviously did. She had shadows under her eyes like bruises. The bruise on her cheekbone had gone yellow-green overnight. Seth was grinning.

"Kids' books," said Seth. "*Clifford the Big Red Dog.* We had it at the library. You used to make me read it to you."

"I did *not*—"

"When you were six."

"I hate all of you," said Jacob. "I hate every single person in this kitchen."

"Hey, Clifford," said Layla, very tiredly, and came past him into the kitchen and lowered herself into the chair beside his dad with a sound like an old woman, and put her face in her hands.

There was a new band on Seth's wrist. Jacob saw it when Seth came past him to the table: pale leather braided with dark cord, snug, and on the inside of it, over the pulse, a black bead the size of a hazelnut, glossy as a beetle. She'd made another one. She'd been up all night making it, by the look of her. Seth kept touching it with his other thumb, the way you'd touch a ring.

---

It was weird, after that. It was weird in a way Jacob didn't have a word for and would think about for a long time.

He'd come here to fight. He'd run twenty miles through the rain to fight. And there was nothing to fight. The guy just sat there. Eating. Adding names to his list with the pencil from behind his ear. Jacob read it upside down from the fridge: *Sam. Jared. Paul. Quil. Embry. Collin. Brady.* And at the bottom, underlined twice, *SHORTS*. Talking to Sue about fish.

Jacob stood by the fridge with his arms folded and watched him.

He watched Layla lift her face out of her hands and look at the man down the table. She looked at him for a long time. Jacob could see her doing it, the thing she did, looking at him the way she'd look at a door with something written on it. Jacob had watched her do it to the Cullens. He watched her do it now, and he watched her give up.

"Is it true?" Layla said.

The man looked up from his paper. "Is what true?"

"You know what."

He looked at her for a second. "Yes."

"You're—"

"Yes."

"You're *him*."

"I'm him," said the man. "Morning, Layla. How's the back?"

"Sore," said Layla.

"Told you. Murtlap. Have you got any?"

"I had some. It was at the bottom of a box. It had gone off."

"I'll bring you some." He went back to his paper. "Hot bath tonight. Six aspirin."

Layla looked at him. Jacob watched her open her mouth and shut it again.

"Why'd you give them your name?" she said finally. "Last night. Edward said you gave it to her on purpose. That it was behind the wall and then it wasn't."

"It's mine," said the man.

That was all. He didn't explain. He said it the way you'd say *it's Tuesday*, and wrote something else on his paper.

"You caught my Expelliarmus," said Layla. "On the ridge."

"You threw it very nicely."

"I threw an Expelliarmus," said Layla, slowly, to the table, to the room, to nobody, "at Harry *Potter*."

"People do," said the man. "It's a bit of a tradition, really. Bloke in Bulgaria did it in 2001. In a pub. I let him keep his eyebrows."

Layla put her face back in her hands.

"Calloway's going to die," she said, into them. "Calloway's going to *die*."

"Who's Calloway?"

"My teacher. My dueling teacher. She's sixty-one. She's been telling people for thirty years that nobody alive could—" Layla lifted her head. "I can't tell her."

"No," the man agreed. "You can't."

"I can't tell *anyone*."

"That's the deal."

"I threw an Expelliarmus at Harry Potter," Layla said, "and I can never tell anybody, ever, as long as I live."

"Now you're getting it," said the man, and for the first time since Jacob had walked in, he grinned. It was a wide, crooked, wolfish sort of grin. It changed his whole face, and it didn't make him look one bit less frightening. It made him look like something that had just found out there was another pie in the fridge.

Layla stared at him. Then, very slowly, she started to laugh. She laughed with her face screwed up and her hand pressed to her bruised back, going *ow, ow, ow*, and couldn't stop.

---

He did Seth's shorts at about ten.

Seth stood in the middle of the kitchen in his cutoffs while the man sat in Harry Clearwater's chair and looked at him, just looked, for about a second. Seth said, "Oh! Whoa. That's—oh, that tickles," and then the man said "Done," and went back to his coffee.

Seth looked down at his shorts. He looked at Leah. He looked at his mom.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"They're just—they'll come with me?"

"Every time."

Seth's face did something. Jacob had known Seth Clearwater his whole life. He'd watched that face at Christmas and at birthdays and at the end of the Mariners' season the one year they nearly made it, and he'd never seen it do quite this. Seth looked like somebody had handed him a car.

"Mom," Seth said. "*Mom.*"

"I know, baby," said Sue.

"I'm never going to have to walk home in a garbage bag again."

"I know."

Seth turned round and looked at the man. And then, before anybody could stop him, before Jacob could even get his arms uncrossed, he went across the kitchen and put both his arms round the guy, round his shoulders, chair and all, and hugged him.

The man went absolutely still.

Jacob saw it. Everybody saw it. He went still the way an animal goes still: completely, instantly, every muscle at once. And for half a second the kitchen got that feeling Jacob had walked into when he came in the door, the too-small feeling, the feeling of something very big sitting very still on purpose.

Then he patted Seth on the back. Twice. Awkwardly. Like a man patting a horse that had put its head over a fence at him.

"All right, Balto," he said. "All right. That's enough."

Seth let go. He was beaming.

"I'll do yours, if you want," the man said to Jacob, without looking at him. "Can't say fairer."

Jacob opened his mouth to say *no*.

He thought about the shorts. The three pairs this week. The pair he'd phased out of this morning on the Cullens' lawn, in shreds somewhere in the grass for Esme to find. The walk home from the Hoh in September in the rain at five in the morning. His dad's face every time.

"Fine," said Jacob, through his teeth.

The man looked at him. Just for a second. Something went over Jacob's skin like a cool breath and sank into his cutoffs and into him, a little, the surface, and tied itself there, lightly, and was done.

"There you go, Clifford," said the man.

---

He took the guy outside, in the end. On the back porch. He didn't ask. He just said "I want to talk to you," and went out the back door and stood on the porch with his arms crossed in the rain, and after a second the man got up and came out after him, and nobody in the kitchen tried to stop either of them.

Leah watched them go from the counter. She didn't say anything. She didn't get down. But Jacob could feel her watching them through the kitchen window the whole time, and he was fairly sure the guy could too.

They stood on the porch.

"You know about her," Jacob said.

"Yes."

"Nessie. You know about her. You got it out of Seth's head."

"I knew about her before that," said the man. "I've known about her since September. I've had her on my map since the day I laid my first stone."

Jacob felt the cold go through him.

"You've known for two months."

"Ten weeks."

"And you haven't—"

"Haven't what?"

Jacob didn't know how to finish it. Haven't told anybody. Haven't come for her. Haven't put her in a jar in that room of yours with the circle. He stood on the porch with his fists clenched under his arms and couldn't make any of it come out.

The man looked at him for a long moment.

"I saw her once," he said. "In October. Through a pair of Omnioculars, from a ridge about a mile off. She was riding on your back. Holding on to your fur with both hands." He looked out at the yard. "She was laughing."

Jacob didn't say anything.

"I'd sooner put my hand in a fire," the man said, "than let anybody near her. Anybody. That's all I'm going to say, because that's all I can say. Leah'll tell you the rest when she can, and she can't."

"Why should I believe you?"

The man shrugged. "Don't, then."

"Leah believes you."

"Leah can tell when I'm lying. Turns out I can't do it around her." He glanced at the kitchen window, and back. "So I don't, much."

Jacob stood there.

He thought about Alice, at the glass wall last night, white to the lips. *Somebody called Aro at two thirty-one this afternoon and told him we were boring.* He thought about Leah standing in the middle of the Cullens' living room with her hand on her throat, choking on nothing, and then saying *nobody's coming for her. Not this winter. Not in the spring.* And Edward saying *she's completely certain.*

He looked at the man on the porch, with the pencil still behind his ear.

"Carlisle wants to meet you," Jacob said. It came out stiff. "Dr. Cullen. He said to ask you. He said to say please."

"Did he."

"He said if you'd come to the house he'd be grateful. Him and Esme. Edward wants to—" Jacob stopped. "Edward wants to meet you. He said you'd know why."

The man was quiet for a moment.

"Yeah," he said. "I expect I would."

"So will you?"

"Tomorrow," said the man. "Tell him tomorrow. Afternoon. I've got things to do today." He looked at Jacob sideways. "Tell him I'll bring biscuits."

"Biscuits?"

"Cookies, Clifford."

"Stop *calling* me that."

"No," said the man, pleasantly, and went back inside.

---

He left at half past ten.

He said goodbye to Sue at the stove and called her *ma'am* again and she told him to stop, and he said yes ma'am, and Jacob watched Sue Clearwater press her lips together hard to keep from laughing. He said goodbye to Billy and put the folded sheet of council paper in his shirt pocket. He told Layla to have the bath. He said *see you, Balto*, to Seth, who was still looking at his shorts.

He didn't say goodbye to Leah. He didn't have to.

She got down off the counter and followed him out the back door and across the yard. Jacob watched them through the window. He didn't mean to; it was just that the window was right there. He watched the two of them walk down the wet grass to the bottom of the yard, side by side, not touching, past the swing set, to the edge of the trees. He watched them stop.

They didn't kiss. They didn't do anything. They stood at the edge of the trees in the rain about a foot apart, and the man said something, and Leah said something back, and shrugged one shoulder. And then the man turned round and walked into the trees.

There was a sound, very small, like a page turning. Then nothing.

Leah stood at the edge of the trees for a while after.

When she came back up the yard, Jacob had moved away from the window and was standing by the fridge with his arms crossed again, like he'd been there the whole time. She came in the back door and saw him and gave him a look that said she knew exactly how long he'd been at the window.

"What'd he say?" said Seth.

"He said *tonight?*" said Leah.

"And what'd you say?"

"I said maybe." She took another apple out of the bowl and bit into it. "Where'd he go?" she said, before anyone could ask, and then answered herself, with her mouth full. "Back to the tent. He's got things to do. He's got to lay another stone where the old one was." She chewed. "And he's going to go and do something to the vampire with a tape measure."

"A *tape measure*?"

"That's what it feels like," said Leah, and shrugged.

---

*The Cullen line, east of Forks — 2:14 p.m.*

They phased at quarter past two, for the afternoon loop.

He'd thought about it. He wasn't stupid. He'd stood at the edge of the Cullens' lawn in his new indestructible shorts with Seth and Leah, and he'd looked at Leah, and she'd looked back at him with an expression on her face that he would think about later, a sort of grim resigned amusement, the look of somebody watching a man walk toward a rake lying in the grass. And she'd said, "Are you sure?"

And he'd said, "It's patrol, Leah. We have to do patrol."

And she'd said, "Okay. I'm just saying. You've been warned. Both of you."

And Seth, beside him, had gone a little bit pale and said, "Warned about *what*?"

And Leah had just looked at him.

They phased.

Jacob went first. The heat and the shove and the crack, and the world going down and forward, and the forest coming up into his nose, and the pack mind coming on like floodlights. Him. Then Seth, a second behind, sandy and bright and anxious. And then Leah.

It hit him like a truck.

Nobody's fault. That was the pack. You couldn't hide anything in the pack. Everything you'd done since the last time you'd phased was just *there*, in your head, and everybody else's head, in one big rush, the way water comes out of a bucket you've tipped over. The kitchen at Sue's this morning. The walk along the beach in the gray. Waking up in the cave. Before that—

Before that, the night.

He got the yard. The window. Two green eyes in the dark at the bottom of the yard. A black shape getting up off its haunches by the swing set, up and up and *up*—

He got the beach. The two of them running along the edge of the water in the dark, Leah going faster than Jacob had ever known she could go, *faster than she'd ever gone for him*, and a black streak in front of her that didn't make a sound. The tide pool at the foot of the cliffs. Leah on top. The black wolf on its back in the water with its tongue out.

He got the cliff. The ledge. The cave mouth, and the sea.

And then he got the cave.

He'd say later, when anybody asked, which nobody ever did, that his brain just refused. That there was this enormous black *thing* in Leah's memory, all of a sudden, everywhere, filling up the whole pack mind from edge to edge, and his brain took one look at it and went *nope*, and shut a door. He'd say that he didn't see anything. He'd say it with a straight face for the rest of his life.

It wasn't entirely true.

Seth phased out.

He didn't decide to. Jacob felt him go. One second he was there, sandy and bright and getting about two seconds into the cave, and the next he was just gone, out of the pack mind like a light switching off, with a sound on the way out like a yelp. Jacob heard it with his ears, too, from twenty feet away in the ferns: a high, strangled, human noise. And then, from the ferns, Seth's voice, human, horrified, in his brand new indestructible shorts:

"*I'm going to throw up.*"

Jacob phased out too.

He came out of it on his hands and knees in the wet ferns at the edge of the Cullens' lawn. In his shorts. His shorts had come with him. They were sitting on him clean and dry and perfect, exactly the way they'd been when he phased, and he looked down at them, and that was somehow the worst part. He couldn't even be mad about the shorts.

He knelt in the ferns.

"He's *bigger than me*," said Jacob Black.

Seth was lying on his back about fifteen feet away with his arm over his face.

"*That's* what you got out of that?" he said, from under the arm.

"He's bigger than me, Seth. He's a *head* bigger than me. I'm the biggest wolf in a thousand years of stories. Everybody says so. *Dad* says so."

A huge gray wolf came out of the trees and sat down on the edge of the lawn in front of them, and looked down at them both, and Jacob could see in its face that it was laughing.

He didn't have to be phased to know what it was thinking. He could see it on her. Leah sat there in the rain with her ears up and her tongue out and her eyes bright, very pleased with herself, and Jacob knelt in the ferns and glared up at her, and he could *feel* the shape of it from here, as clear as if she'd said it in his ear.

*A head. At least.*

"I'm the biggest wolf in a thousand years of stories," Jacob said again.

The gray wolf tipped her head on one side.

He didn't need to be phased for that one either.

*Were.*

### XII. Stroud

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — 3:38 p.m.*

The lightning strike was beautiful.

Margaret Stroud stood at the edge of the granite slab with her hands in the pockets of her long gray coat and the rain dripping off the brim of her hat, and looked at it for a long time, and thought that it was one of the most beautiful lightning strikes she'd ever seen in her life.

She'd seen a few. Twenty-six years with the Major Investigation Department, the last eleven of them out of the Seattle field office, covering everything from the Canadian border to the Columbia and from the Cascades to the sea, and you saw a lot of lightning strikes. Every summer some kid from a Wizarding family went up a mountain somewhere with his first wand and tried something he shouldn't, and set off a fire, and the Muggles put it down to lightning, and the Obliviators went up after him and cleaned up, and somebody from MID went up after the Obliviators to make sure they'd done it right. She'd stood on a lot of ridges in a lot of rain looking at a lot of scorched trees and thinking *is that one ours, or God's?*

Usually you could tell. Usually there was something.

She walked round it. Slowly. All of it, in a widening spiral, the way she'd been taught in 1981 by an old Auror from Chicago named Fitzwilliam who'd smoked cigars and had a glass eye and had once told her that the most important thing a field investigator ever learned was to walk a scene like she had all the time in the world, because the scene did. The scene would keep. You were the one in a hurry.

A dead hemlock snag, split top to bottom, both halves still standing, charred along the seam. A spiral of bark peeled off one side and lying in the moss. A crater at the base of the snag, in the soft ground, ragged and lumpy and torn, mud thrown out of it in one direction the way a steam explosion threw it. She crouched at the edge and looked down into it and found the fulgurite at the bottom, two feet of fused grayish glass forking twice, crumbling at the edges, and she sat back on her heels and let out a breath.

That was nice. That was really nice.

Scorch-lines in the moss. Branching out from the base of the snag in all directions like the veins of a leaf, crooked, irregular, some of them twenty feet long. One of them ran out to a boulder off to the left that had come apart in a way granite did when the water in its cracks went to steam all at once. Another ran out to the big flat slab itself, and across it, and on the slab, where it ended, there was a burn. A starburst. A little scorched flower of branching lines a yard across, burnt into the surface of the rock.

A hemlock limb down. Storms took limbs.

She stood up and took her wand out of her sleeve and did the residue check. All of it, the long version, the one Fitzwilliam had taught her, that took nearly four minutes and made her arm ache. The air over the slab. The air over the crater. The snag, the boulder, the moss, the scorch-lines.

Nothing.

She did it again, because she was a careful woman, and because the needle on Field Register Sixteen up the Hoh had gone off the top of the drum at eleven fifty-two yesterday morning, and so had the needles on Fourteen and Fifteen, and the three of them together had drawn a circle on the map on the wall in the Seattle office that this ridge was sitting right in the middle of.

Nothing. Clean as a whistle. Not so much as a smear.

She put her wand away and stood there.

Lightning didn't leave residue. That was the thing. That was the whole point of lightning. If this was God's, it would look exactly like this: a beautiful, textbook, museum-quality lightning strike on a ridge in the rain, with not a trace of magic anywhere on it. And if it was somebody's, somebody had cleaned up after themselves better than anybody she'd ever seen in twenty-six years, so well that they'd scrubbed the ridge cleaner than nature would have left it on its own.

There'd been a thunderstorm advisory for the coast on Sunday night. She'd checked. The National Weather Service had logged eleven strikes on the peninsula between nine and midnight.

But the needles hadn't gone off on Sunday night. They'd gone off on Monday morning, at eight minutes to noon, in broad daylight.

She took out her notebook and wrote that down.

---

Then there were the other two.

She'd had them on the drum in Seattle since last night, and she'd driven out this morning thinking about them. Two smaller spikes on Register Fourteen, the Sol Duc one, at nineteen minutes to ten and two minutes to ten last night. Much smaller. The same shape as the big one, the same ragged sawtooth after the first spike, about a tenth the size, and getting smaller. Moving north and east. She'd sat in her car on the Edmonds–Kingston ferry at seven this morning with the readouts on her knee and a cup of terrible ferry coffee in the other hand, and drawn it on the back of her map with a pencil.

A fight in the woods south of Forks. Then a chase. North-east, toward Lake Crescent. Then Port Angeles. Then nothing.

The *Coho* had made its first run across to Victoria this morning. She'd checked that too.

It was a story. It was a very good story. It had a beginning and a middle and an end, and the end was somewhere in British Columbia, where it was somebody else's paperwork. She could see exactly how it would read in her report. She could see her supervisor in Seattle reading it, and nodding, and writing *refer to Canadian Ministry of Magic, close* across the top, and going to lunch.

She'd been doing this job for twenty-six years. She didn't trust stories with endings that tidy.

She didn't distrust them either. Not yet. Sometimes things were tidy. Sometimes two idiots had a duel in the woods and one of them ran for the border and the other one chased him and they both got on a ferry.

She stood on the slab with her notebook and looked out across the Bogachiel valley in the rain, the long gray-green fold of it going away west toward the sea, all those miles of trees, and thought about the other thing on the drum. The little thing. The thing she'd been looking at for a month.

Tiny bumps. Every morning, round about seven. Every evening, round about six. Somewhere in the north half of the circle, near the edge, near the town. Somebody doing the washing-up with a wand. And then the fourth of November. And the fifth.

Somebody had moved to Forks. Somebody with a wand, who didn't know there was a register six miles up the road.

That wasn't a crime. People moved. It wasn't even unusual; she had a dozen families on the peninsula on her list, mostly old, mostly quiet, and the drum picked them up now and then. She'd been meaning to come out and knock on a few doors for a month and say *hello, MACUSA, welcome to the Olympic Peninsula, just making sure you've registered your wand*, and it had kept slipping down the list.

But somebody who did the washing-up with a wand every morning at seven didn't normally go up a ridge at noon on a Monday and let off something that put three needles off the top of three drums at once.

She put the notebook away.

She was hungry. She'd been up since five. She went and sat down on a big fallen cedar at the edge of the slab, a nurse log, furred all over with moss and little hemlock saplings growing out of it, with a stand of tall sword ferns crowding round it on the downhill side, and took a sandwich out of her coat pocket in wax paper, and unwrapped it, and ate it looking at the lightning strike.

It really was beautiful.

---

She saw it when she bent down to put the wax paper back in her pocket.

She'd have missed it if she'd been standing. She'd have missed it if the light had been coming from any other direction, or if she'd sat on any other part of the log. The ferns on the downhill side of the nurse log were tall, waist-high, the big Pacific sword ferns that grew everywhere on this coast, and one of them, right at her knee, had its frond turned a little sideways, so that she could see the underside.

There was something on it.

A smear. About the size of her thumb. On the pale underside of the frond, near the stem. It caught the gray light differently from the rain around it. It was thicker than rain. It had a shine to it that rain didn't have.

She sat very still for a moment.

Then she took her wand out again, and a little glass evidence vial out of her inside pocket, and she leaned down and looked at the smear from about six inches away, without touching it.

It was gold. Dark gold, nearly amber, gone tacky and dull at the edges in the cold, a bit of grit stuck in it. There was a tiny smear of the same stuff on the next frond along, and the next. She looked down the slope from the log and saw, now that she was looking for it, that the ferns there were standing a little bit too straight. Every one of them. Very neat. Like they'd been combed.

She looked back at the smear on the frond, and sniffed.

It was honey.

She sat on the nurse log in the rain and looked at it for a long time.

Then she cut the frond off at the stem with a little Severing Charm and lowered it into the vial without touching it, and corked it, and sealed the cork, and labeled it in her small careful handwriting: *BOGACHIEL RIDGE, 11/21/06, 3:52 PM, NURSE LOG DOWNSLOPE OF SLAB. ORGANIC — ? HONEY.* And she put it in her pocket, and sat there a while longer.

Honey.

Nobody carried honey to the top of a ridge in November. There was nothing up here. No trail, no campsite, no picnic table. It was a long, steep, broken spine of rock and stunted hemlock a long way from anywhere, and the only way up was through the trees. And nobody went up through the trees in November with a jar of honey in their pocket and spilled it on the ferns.

She thought about it from every side, the way Fitzwilliam had taught her. A hiker. A hiker with a sandwich, like her, a honey sandwich, who'd sat on the same log and dripped. That was the most likely. It was also the least likely, because she'd walked this whole ridge and hadn't found a single footprint, or a single wrapper, or a single bent twig, and the ferns had been combed.

Somebody had combed these ferns. Somebody who'd been very thorough about every piece of magic on this ridge, every smear and spark and stain of it. And had missed a thumbprint of honey on the underside of a fern, because it wasn't magic, and so they hadn't been looking for it.

She'd met a lot of very careful people in twenty-six years. She'd never once met one who was careful about everything.

She sat on the log for a long time, until the light started going.

---

*Forks Diner — 4:41 p.m.*

The diner was on the main drag, a low building with a gravel lot and a pink neon sign in the window that said OPEN in letters that had been fading since about the Reagan administration. She parked round the side. She went in.

It was nearly empty. Late afternoon, a Tuesday, in the rain. A couple of loggers at the counter. A teenage girl in a booth by the window with a milkshake and a chemistry textbook. And behind the counter, a big gray-haired waitress of about sixty with a pot of coffee in one hand and a voice like gravel in a cement mixer, who looked up when the door went and looked her over, all of her, top to bottom, the hat and the long gray coat and the sensible shoes, in about a second and a half.

"Sit anywhere, hon," said the waitress.

Margaret Stroud stood just inside the door with the rain dripping off her hat and looked round the room the way she looked round every room. Exits. Hands. Faces. The kitchen pass. The window onto the lot.

There was a booth at the back, against the back wall. It had a view of the door, and the lot through the window, and the kitchen pass, and the whole length of the counter, and you could sit in it with your back to the wall.

It was the booth she'd have picked. She went to it.

On the way past the register she stopped.

There was a little wooden rack on the end of the counter by the register, the kind you saw in diners in small towns everywhere, with local things on it for the tourists. Postcards of the Hoh. A stack of maps. Huckleberry jam in little jars with gingham tops. And on the bottom shelf, four jars of honey.

Mason jars. Dark gold, nearly amber. Each one with a strip of masking tape across the lid and a handwritten label in green ink, in a small slanted hand that looked old-fashioned, like somebody's grandmother's.

*QUINN — CALAWAH WAY — FIREWEED & WILDFLOWER — $6.*

Margaret looked at it.

"That local?" she said.

"Maeve Quinn's," said the waitress, coming down the counter with the coffee pot. "God rest her. Kept bees out on Calawah Way for forty years. Best honey on the peninsula. Passed in September." She nodded at the jars. "Those are her last. Her granddaughter's took over the hives now. Nice girl. From New Mexico. Comes in sometimes with that big Clearwater boy from La Push." She lifted the pot. "Coffee?"

"Please."

Margaret Stroud stood by the register for a moment longer, looking at the honey. She thought about the vial in her inside pocket, and the frond in it, and the little gold smear on its underside.

Then she went on to the booth at the back, and slid into it, with her back to the wall.

The waitress followed her with the coffee pot and a mug. She put the mug down on the table and started to pour, and stopped, halfway, with the pot in the air, and looked at Margaret sitting there in the back booth with her hat on the seat beside her, and her face did something.

"Oh," said the waitress. "Hon. Sorry. That's English's booth."

Margaret looked up at her.

"Who's English?" she said.

---

*End of Chapter Two*
