# Need to Know

## Chapter Two: Liaison

---

### I. Leah

*South of the Calawah — Monday, November 20th, 6:52 p.m.*

*—ask him myself? Ask him* myself*? Leah, I don't even know where he* is*, none of us can remember where he is, it's like trying to hold on to a fish, every time I think about that tent it just—*

*Yeah. He's good at that.*

*He's good at* that*? That's what you've got? Leah!*

She ran.

It was full dark under the trees and the rain had thinned to a drizzle, a fine cold mist that hung in the air and beaded on her fur and didn't so much fall as wait for her to run into it. The ground was soft and black and quiet under her feet. She was going south, down the long falling country between the north fork and the main river, and she wasn't going fast. For the first time in her life she didn't feel like going fast. She felt like going at exactly the speed she was going, which was a long, loose, easy lope, the kind of pace she could hold till morning, the kind of pace you used when you weren't running away from anything.

Jacob was shouting in her head. He'd been shouting for six miles.

She let him.

*Lee,* Seth said, under Jacob, small and careful. *Are you okay? Like, actually okay?*

*I'm hungry.*

*You're—*

*Hungry, Seth. I'm starving. He made me eggs but that was like an hour ago.*

*He made you* eggs*?*

*Six. And bacon. He makes them in the bacon fat. They're really good.*

There was a short silence in the pack mind. She could feel both of them in it, Seth at the Cullen house with his arm around Layla on the big white couch, Jacob pacing the Cullens' back porch in human form with his phone in his hand like he was going to call somebody, except he'd phased again twenty minutes ago to yell at her and he hadn't phased back. He was out in the yard now, a big russet shape walking up and down the edge of the lawn, wearing a groove in Esme's grass.

She could see through his eyes. The lit glass wall of the house. Layla's dark head on Seth's shoulder. Edward at the piano, not playing, his hands flat on his knees, his face turned toward the window. Toward the trees. Toward her, she thought, though she was still twelve miles off.

*He made you eggs,* Jacob said finally. Flat.

*Yeah.*

*The guy who held me—who held* Seth *face-down on a rock. Who shot Layla. Who carried a vampire around like a—who* walked on air*, Leah—*

*He shot the ground in front of Layla.*

*Is that supposed to make it better?*

*It's supposed to make it accurate.*

She came down out of the high country onto the flats above the river and turned east, toward the highway, toward the long drive and the white house. She could have gone home. She'd meant to go home. But her mom would be at work at the clinic till nine on a Monday, and the fridge at home had half a casserole in it and some yogurt, and Esme Cullen, who had not needed to eat food for ninety years, had taken it on herself since the summer to keep the Cullens' fridge full for the wolves, and on Mondays she made lasagna.

Leah had decided, somewhere around the north fork, that she wasn't going to have a fight on an empty stomach.

*Leah,* Jacob said. *I'm serious. Come to the house. Now.*

*I'm coming.*

*Now, Leah.*

*I'm* coming*, Jake. God. What do you think I'm doing? Look where I am.*

He looked. She felt him look. She felt him see the river and the highway and the dark shape of the ridge that ran along behind the Cullen place, and work out that she was coming straight there, and had been since she'd left the tent.

*Oh,* he said.

*Yeah. Oh.*

*Why didn't you say?*

*You didn't ask. You've been yelling.*

He didn't say anything to that. She felt him stop pacing.

She ran on.

She could feel the wall. She'd been poking at it on and off since the north fork, the way you poke at a loose tooth with your tongue. The grey place. It sat in the middle of her head, smooth and cool and blank, and everything she knew about the man in the tent that the Department of whatever-it-was would have called its own was behind it. She could get at it. That was the weird part. She could think about any of it, his name, the room with the jars, the old man laughing on the phone, and it was all right there, clear as day, as clear as anything she'd ever known. It just wasn't there for anybody else. When Jacob and Seth went looking, they hit stone.

She'd tried thinking his name at them, on purpose, once, just to see. Right at them. As hard as she could.

Nothing had come out. It was like shouting into a pillow.

She didn't mind. That was the other weird part. She'd hated the pack mind for seven months, every single second of it, hated having nothing that was hers, hated Sam in her head and Paul in her head and Jared and Embry and Quil, hated even Jacob and Seth sometimes, hated being a room everybody could walk into. And now there was a little room in the middle of her that nobody could walk into. Nobody but her.

And him. She could feel him at the other end of the rope. Twenty miles off now and getting further, in the tent, in the kitchen. He'd put a record on. She couldn't hear it, exactly, but she could feel him listening to it, the way you could feel somebody tapping their foot on the other side of a couch.

He wasn't in her head right now. She'd felt him leave, back at the river, when she'd told Jacob to ask him himself. He'd gone back into his own body like a man going back into his own house and closing the door. He'd left it on the latch, though. She could feel that too.

*What are you smiling about?* Seth said.

*I'm a wolf, Seth. I can't smile.*

*You're smiling in your* head*. I can feel it. It's freaking me out.*

*Get used to it.*

---

She came up the long drive at a quarter past seven and didn't bother going round to the back.

She came straight up the middle of the lawn in front of the house, with the light from the big glass wall falling out across the wet grass and every single one of them watching her through it, and she went behind the cedar by the porch steps, the big one Esme had put a bench under, and phased. She untied her cutoffs from her ankle and pulled them on, and her tank top over her head, both still damp, and she came out from behind the cedar barefoot and walked up the porch steps, and Jacob was standing at the top of them.

He'd phased back. He was in his cutoffs, nothing else, with his arms folded and the rain on his shoulders and his face like a thunderhead.

"Leah," he said.

"Jake."

She walked past him. She opened the front door, which was never locked, because what would be the point, and went in.

The living room was full. It was always full lately, because there was always something, but tonight it was full the way a room is full at a funeral, everybody standing in a different spot and nobody sitting comfortably. Carlisle by the fireplace, in his shirtsleeves, with his doctor's bag open on the coffee table. Esme beside him. Rosalie on the stairs, three steps up, where she always sat when she wanted to be above everybody. Emmett at the bottom of the stairs. Jasper and Alice by the kitchen door. Bella on the far end of the couch with the kid in her lap, the kid in pajamas with ducks on them, wide awake, her bronze curls sticking up on one side where she'd been lying on them. Edward at the piano.

Seth and Layla on the near end of the couch. Layla was wrapped up in a big gray blanket, holding a mug of something with both hands. Her face was pale under the brown and she had a bruise coming up along her left cheekbone, and her eyes found Leah the second Leah came in the door and didn't let go.

Everybody else's did the same.

Leah looked round at them.

"Hey," she said.

She walked through the middle of the room, between the couch and the coffee table, past Carlisle's bag and Bella's knees and the kid, who reached out a hand as she went by, and Leah brushed her fingers against the kid's palm without stopping, the way she always did, the way she'd started doing about a month ago without ever deciding to. She went through the arch into the kitchen.

The kitchen was white and steel and enormous and nobody ever cooked in it except Esme, for the wolves. Leah opened the big double fridge.

Lasagna. A whole tray. Covered in foil.

She took it out and put it on the counter and peeled back the foil and took a fork out of the drawer and started eating it standing up, out of the corner of the tray, cold.

It was really good. Esme put sausage in it.

Behind her, she heard Jacob come in through the front door, and the door shut, and the whole room hold its breath.

"Leah," Jacob said from the arch.

She ate another forkful.

"*Leah.*"

"What?"

"*What*? Are you—" He came into the kitchen. She could hear him come in, feel him come in, the heat coming off him filling up the room like a furnace door opening. He stopped on the other side of the counter. "Are you serious right now? You're *eating*?"

"I told you I was hungry."

"You imprinted on the gun guy!"

"Yeah."

"And you're eating *lasagna*!"

"It's Monday," Leah said. "Esme makes lasagna on Mondays." She looked past him, through the arch, at Esme by the fireplace. "It's really good, by the way. Is that fennel?"

Esme opened her mouth. She glanced at Carlisle. She closed it again. "Yes," she said, after a moment. "Fennel sausage. Thank you, Leah."

"It's really good."

"*LEAH*," said Jacob.

She put the fork down.

She turned around and looked at him. He was standing on the other side of the big steel island with both hands flat on it and his shoulders up around his ears and his eyes huge, and for a second, just a second, he didn't look like the Alpha of anything. He looked like the skinny fourteen-year-old who'd sat next to her in the back of her mom's car on the way to the movies in Port Angeles, the summer before everything, with popcorn butter on his fingers and his feet on the back of the seat in front of him.

"How could you," Jacob said. Low. Like it hurt him to say. "How could you imprint on *him*?"

And there it was.

She'd been waiting for it for twenty miles. She'd heard it coming in his head, under all the shouting, the whole way down from the north fork, *how could she, how could she, of all the people on the planet, him*, over and over, like a drum. She'd been waiting for him to say it out loud.

She'd thought she'd be angry. She'd thought that the whole way down, and she'd checked, a couple of times, the way you check a pocket for your keys, waiting to find it. The anger. The old anger, the one she'd carried around for two years like a stone, the one that came up her throat every time anybody looked at her with that face, *poor Leah*, *crazy Leah*, *bitter Leah*.

It wasn't there.

She'd looked for it. She'd turned out every pocket. And where it used to be there was something else, something she didn't have a name for yet, something big and calm and solid, like a hand on the back of her neck.

So she just looked at him.

"How could *I*," she said.

"Yeah! *How could you*? Leah, he's—"

"Jake." She folded her arms. "I'm going to say this one time, and then I'm going to finish my lasagna. Okay?"

He shut his mouth.

"At least," said Leah, "mine isn't a literal baby vampire hybrid."

The whole house went silent.

She felt it. She felt it go through the arch into the living room like a cold draft. She heard Rosalie, on the stairs, stop breathing, which was a thing Rosalie did for effect, since she didn't need to. She heard Bella's arms tighten round the kid. She heard Emmett, at the bottom of the stairs, make a sound like a balloon being let go of very slowly.

Jacob stared at her.

"That's—" he said. "That's not—"

"It is, though," said Leah. "It's exactly that. Literally. You imprinted on a baby. Who is half vampire. Who was about four hours old at the time and had just finished chewing her way out of her mom. You were in the room. You'd gone in there to kill it." She shrugged. "I love that kid. Everybody loves that kid. That kid's great. I'm just saying. You don't get to stand in this kitchen with your hands on Esme's countertop and ask *me* how I could."

Jacob's mouth opened and shut.

"And honestly?" said Leah. She picked her fork back up. "If you really think about it? Mine's the best one. Out of all of them. Out of everybody."

"The *best*—"

"Yeah. The best. You want to know why?" She held up the fork, tines up, like a teacher with a pointer, and counted on it. "A. He's not a baby. Big one. Quil's is three. Yours is like ten weeks. I'm not going to be changing anybody's diapers for the next fifteen years, Jake, I'm not going to be standing around waiting for anybody to grow up. He's grown up. He's done. He's like twenty-six."

"Leah—"

"B." She tapped the second tine. "He's human. He's a *person*. He eats food. He sleeps in a bed. He has a heartbeat. His skin is the normal temperature for somebody who's not dead. He's not going to want to bite anybody's neck. He made me *eggs*, Jake. Nobody in this house can make you eggs." She paused. "Except Esme. Sorry, Esme."

"That's quite all right, Leah," Esme said faintly, from the next room.

"And *C*." Leah tapped the last tine. She leaned forward over the island. "He's also a wolf."

Jacob's face did something complicated.

"You saw it," Leah said. "I know you saw it. In my head. Down at the bottom of him, behind the locks. Big as a house. Seth saw it, Seth told you about it before I was even halfway here. He's a wolf, Jake. He's a *wolf*. I imprinted on a wolf. The only she-wolf in a thousand years of stories, and she imprinted on the only other wolf in the world that isn't one of you idiots." She sat back. She ate a forkful of lasagna. She chewed it. She swallowed. "So take that."

There was a long silence.

Out in the living room, somebody started to laugh. It was Emmett. He was trying not to. He had both hands over his mouth and his big shoulders were shaking, and Rosalie hit him on the arm, hard, without looking, and that made it worse.

Jacob didn't laugh.

He stood there with his hands flat on the island, and Leah watched him try to find the next thing to say, and watched him not find it. He was breathing hard. His hands were shaking, a little, the way they did when he was close to phasing, and he made them stop.

"You don't even know him," he said finally. Quieter. "You met him this afternoon. He held Seth down. He held *you* down, Leah, I saw it, I saw your face in the moss. He hurt Layla. And then you look at him once and you're—you're over here eating lasagna like it's nothing. Like he's—" He stopped. "You don't know what he is."

"I know more than you do."

"Because he *told* you? He could've told you anything!"

"He can't lie to me," said Leah.

Jacob stopped.

"He can't," she said. "We checked. He tried to tell me his name was something else and he couldn't get it out. It just stopped in his mouth. And then the real one came out instead, and he said a word I'm not going to say in front of Esme." She looked down at the lasagna. "And I can't lie to him. I tried. I tried to tell him I was fine, and I couldn't. It's the imprint. It's doing something. It goes both ways, Jake, it's not like yours, it's not like anybody's. He can't lie to me and I can't lie to him."

"That's not how imprinting works."

"Yeah, well," said Leah. "Apparently we didn't read the manual."

He looked at her. He looked at her for a long time across the steel island, and she let him. She stood there in her damp cutoffs with a fork in her hand and let him look, because she could feel him trying to work it out, and she could feel, under all the yelling, what he was actually scared of. The gun was the least of it. So were the vampire under the arm, and the crater, and the walking on air. What scared him was the thing he'd seen in her head on the ridge, the moment when the man had looked at her and everything had let go.

Sam's line going slack. Two years of it. Unwinding out of her.

Jacob had felt it go. He'd been in her head when it went. He was the only person besides Seth who'd ever really known how deep it went.

"Do you think I chose this?" Leah said.

He didn't answer.

"Do you?" She put the fork down. "Is that what you think? That I was standing on that rock with my face still dirty from being held down, and I looked at the guy who'd just done it, and I thought *yeah, him, that one, I'll take that one*?" Her voice didn't go up. She was a little surprised by that. "Did you choose? With the kid? When you went in that room with your teeth out? Did Sam choose Emily? Did Seth go into Newton's for shorts and think, *I'm going to pick the girl with the bad jacket*?"

"No," said Jacob.

"No. You didn't. None of you did. And none of you had to stand there afterward and listen to somebody ask you *how could you*." She looked at him. "So don't."

He looked down at his hands.

"I'm sorry," he said, after a long moment. Quietly.

"I know."

"I just—" He rubbed his face. "Leah, I watched you for seven months. In Sam's head and then in mine. Every single day. I watched what it did to you. And then today, I'm running up from the house, and I see it happen, and I think, *oh, God, here we go, now she's going to be stuck on somebody who's going to hurt her worse.* And it's *him*. Of everybody. The scariest thing I've ever seen, and I've seen the Volturi." He dropped his hands. "I'm scared for you. That's all. That's what that was."

"I know," said Leah. "I know it is."

She did know. She'd known for twenty miles. That was the thing about the pack mind. You couldn't hide the bottom of anything.

"He's not going to hurt me," she said.

"You don't know that."

"I do, though." She picked her fork back up. "He can't. Same as you can't hurt the kid. Same as Seth can't hurt Layla. I could feel it, Jake. Through the rope. Even when he was standing there looking at me like I'd hit him with a truck. Hurting me is the one thing in the world he can't do." She ate a bite. "Also, he said if anybody ever tried to take me apart he'd put his fist through the wall. So."

"Take you apart?"

"Long story."

"*What*—"

"Later."

Jacob opened his mouth.

"*Later*, Jake. I'm eating."

He shut his mouth. He stood there. And then, slowly, his shoulders came down from around his ears, and he let out a long breath through his nose, and he looked at the tray of lasagna on the counter between them, and back at her.

"Is there any left?" he said.

She pushed the tray across the island at him.

He got a fork out of the drawer. He started eating out of the other corner.

They stood there on either side of the steel island in the Cullens' white kitchen, eating cold lasagna out of the tray with their fingers half the time, not saying anything, and it was the closest thing to a normal moment the two of them had had since June. Leah felt it. She felt Jacob feel it. She didn't say anything about it, and neither did he.

Then a voice behind her said, very quietly, "Leah."

She looked round.

Edward was standing in the arch between the kitchen and the living room. She hadn't heard him come. She never did. He was standing with one hand on the frame of the arch and his face very still, that marble face, and his eyes were on her, gold and dark at the same time, and narrowed. Concentrating, like a man trying to read small print across a room.

"There's a wall in your head," Edward said.

"Yeah," said Leah. "I know."

"It's—" He stopped. He tipped his head slightly, listening. "I've been trying to get past it since you came up the drive. I can't. I've never—Bella's mind was silence. This is a *wall*. I can feel there's something behind it. A lot of something. I just can't get to any of it." He paused. "It isn't yours, is it."

"No."

"Whose is it?"

Leah put another forkful of lasagna in her mouth, and chewed, and swallowed, and looked Edward Cullen dead in the eye.

"His," she said.

### II. Edward

*The Cullen house — 7:31 p.m.*

*His.*

She said it with her mouth full, and she thought it at the same time, and the thought came to him the way most of Leah Clearwater's thoughts had come to him for the last five months: sharp, flat, and aimed. Leah thought in edges. She had from the first day he'd heard her, out in the trees at the edge of the field in June with the newborns coming, a hard, bright, furious mind like a broken bottle. He'd learned to listen to her the way you'd pick up glass.

It was different tonight. He'd noticed it the moment she came up the drive. The edges were all still there; she was still Leah. But something under them had changed, the way the ground changes after an earthquake. Something that had always been pulling at her from below, a constant, grinding, sideways pull that he'd heard in her for so long he'd stopped noticing it, had stopped. And something else had taken its place. Something still.

And in the middle of all of it, the wall.

He'd never encountered anything like it. He'd been trying to describe it to himself since she'd crossed the lawn, and the closest he could come was this: it was like walking through a house you knew, a house where every door stood open, and coming to one door in the middle of the hall that was shut and locked and made of stone. And you could hear, on the other side of it, that the room beyond was full. Full of things. Voices, faces, rooms, words. He could hear the shape of them the way you hear a crowd through a wall without making out a single word.

He pressed against it again now. Gently. He'd been doing it on and off for ten minutes.

It didn't give. It didn't even notice. It sat in the middle of her mind, cool and old and indifferent, the way a mountain is indifferent to the weather.

"His," Edward repeated. "The man from the ridge."

"Yeah." Leah went on eating.

"He put it there?"

"No. It just—happened. When I phased. When Jake and Seth came in." She shrugged. "It's his secrets. Work stuff. Things he's not allowed to tell anybody. He told me, because he can't not tell me, and then when the pack went looking, they hit that. He says it's an oath. Something he swore to his bosses. It thinks I'm him."

"It thinks you're—"

"Him. It doesn't know the difference." She scraped at the corner of the tray. "Don't ask me. I don't get it either. He was pretty freaked out."

Behind her, at the island, Jacob had gone still with his fork halfway to his mouth. *It thinks she's him,* Jacob was thinking. *What does that even* mean*.* And under it, louder: *She's not even mad. She's not even a little bit mad. She's just standing there.*

Edward looked at the wall a moment longer.

And then he felt something else.

It came from behind the wall. Or beside it. Or under it; he couldn't have said which, afterward. He felt it the way you feel someone come into a room behind you while your back is turned: a change in the air, a small pressure at the edge of things. Something at the very edge of Leah's mind. Something that hadn't been there a second ago.

Someone.

It had none of Leah's edges. It was cool and quiet and very still and it was *paying attention*, and he knew, with a jolt that went all the way down him, that it was paying attention to *him*.

He went absolutely still in the archway.

"Edward?" said Carlisle, from the fireplace.

He didn't answer.

The someone at the edge of Leah's mind looked at him. That was the only word for it. In a hundred and five years Edward had never once been *looked at* from inside somebody else's head. He'd done the looking. He'd sat in the minds of thousands of people, millions of thoughts, and none of them had ever known he was there, except Aro, who read by touch and knew everything, and Bella, who'd never let him in at all.

This one knew. This one had felt him leaning on the wall and come to see who it was.

And then it spoke.

*Evening,* it said.

The voice was a man's. Low, dry, English. Quiet; it didn't need to be loud. It came through Leah's mind into his like a voice coming down a telephone line from a long way off.

*You'll be Edward. The one from the photograph.* A pause. *You can stop pushing on that, mate. It won't give. It's not hers to give and it's not really mine either.*

Edward stood in the arch with one hand on the frame and didn't breathe.

*Who are you?* he thought. He didn't know if it would work. He'd never tried to answer a mind before. He'd never had one to answer.

It worked.

There was a pause. He felt the someone consider him. He felt it weigh something, the way you'd weigh a coin in your hand before you decided whether to spend it.

*Fair question,* it said. *Seeing as I've been through your friend's head and had a good look round your living room. Fine. Have that much. It's mine to give, anyway.*

And the wall opened.

A crack. The width of a door left ajar. And something came through it, one thing, small and clear and bright as a coin pushed under a door, and lay there in the middle of Leah's mind where he could see it.

A name.

*The rest of it you'll get tomorrow,* the voice said. *From me. In person. Tell your dad I'll bring biscuits.*

The crack closed. The wall was a wall again.

And the someone was gone, all at once, like a man stepping back from a window. Edward felt him go. Felt the edge of Leah's mind go empty and ordinary again.

He let out the breath he hadn't been taking.

"Edward," said Carlisle again, sharper.

"He was here," said Edward.

Every head in the house turned. Even Leah's. She'd stopped eating. She was looking at him with a forkful of lasagna halfway to her mouth, and he heard her think, *oh*, and then, *yeah. He does that.* As if it were nothing. As if it were the most ordinary thing in the world.

"Who was here?" said Jasper, from the kitchen door. His mind had gone cold and quick and tactical the moment Edward said it; he was already looking at the windows, the doors, the lines of approach. "Where?"

"In her head." Edward let go of the arch. "The man from the ridge. Just now. He was—I don't know how. At the edge of Leah's mind. Listening. He knew I was there. He *spoke* to me." He heard how it sounded. He said it anyway. "He reads minds. The way I do. Better, maybe. He found me in her head and he spoke to me."

The silence that came after was very long.

*Oh, that's not good,* thought Emmett, and for once he didn't say it out loud.

*He's been in Seth's head. He's seen the house. He's seen her,* thought Rosalie, and her eyes went to Renesmee in Bella's lap, and her hands closed on the banister.

*What did he say?* thought Carlisle. *What did he say?*

"What did he say?" said Carlisle.

"He said he'd come tomorrow," said Edward. "In person. And that he'd bring biscuits." He paused. "And he gave me his name."

"His *name*?" Jacob, at the island. "He just—gave it to you?"

"He opened the wall. A crack. Just for that. Then he closed it again." Edward looked at Leah. "He chose to. I felt him choose."

"What is it?" said Bella. Her voice was very quiet. He couldn't hear her mind; he never could. He could see her face. "Edward. What's his name?"

Edward turned and looked through the arch into the living room. At all of them. At his family, scattered around the long white room in their funeral places. At Seth on the couch. And at the woman beside Seth, wrapped in the gray blanket with the mug in her hands and the bruise coming up on her cheek, whose mind had been going round and round the same few questions for four hours like a bird in a box, *who is he, who IS he, who does he work for, where does he come from, what is he*, and who had come no closer to an answer than when she'd started.

"Harry Potter," Edward said.

---

Layla's mug hit the floor.

It didn't break. Esme's living room had a rug in it that had cost more than most cars, and the mug bounced on it and rolled and sent a long dark arc of tea across the cream wool, and nobody moved to pick it up. Esme didn't even look at it.

Everybody was looking at Layla.

She'd gone white. Edward had seen people go white before; he'd seen it a thousand times, a hundred thousand, every flavor of it. This was the kind where the blood leaves the face all at once like water down a drain. Her lips had gone gray. Her hands were still curved in front of her the way they'd been when they held the mug.

And her mind—

Her mind *exploded*.

There was no other word for it. It went off like a box of fireworks dropped into a fire, every thought at once, all of them colliding. Words he didn't know and words he did. Pictures. A newspaper, moving, the photograph on its front page *moving*, a thin boy with glasses and a lightning scar on his forehead squinting out of it. A little card, gold-edged, with the same boy's face on it, older, unsmiling, and a name across the bottom. A classroom with a blackboard, and a woman at the front of it writing a date in chalk, *1981*, and underlining it twice. And words, over and over, in a dozen different voices, in a dozen different accents, the way you'd hear a story that everybody in the world had told you, all your life, at different times:

*The Boy Who Lived.*

*The boy who lived. The only person ever to survive the Killing Curse. A baby. One year old. The Dark Lord came to his house on Halloween and killed his parents and turned the wand on the baby in the crib and the curse bounced. It bounced. Nobody knows how. It bounced off a baby and it destroyed him, the most feared wizard in a hundred years, it destroyed him and it left a scar like lightning.*

*And then he came back. The Dark Lord. When the boy was fourteen. And there was a war. And at the end of the war, in 1998, at that school in Scotland, in front of everybody, the boy faced him. Seventeen years old. And won.*

*And then he vanished.*

*He vanished. He walked off the front pages and nobody ever saw him again. Some people said he went to the Himalayas. Some people said he was dead. Some people said the Ministry had him locked up. There were stories. There were always stories. There were children's books. There are CHOCOLATE FROG CARDS—*

"No," Layla said out loud.

Her voice cracked in the middle. Seth put his hand on her back.

"No," she said again. "No. No, no, no. That's not—Edward, that's not possible. He's—that's—" She stopped. She put both her hands over her face. "Oh my God."

"Layla?" said Seth. "Lay. What is it? Who is he?"

She took her hands away from her face. She looked at Seth, and then she looked through the arch at Leah, who was standing at the kitchen island with a fork in her hand, and Edward heard her mind do one single, very clear thing amid all the noise.

It went back to the ridge. It went back to the moment, on the rock, with the rain coming down and her wand up and honey drying on her wrists, when a cold voice in the back of her head had said *the list isn't closed*. It went back to three names. And it added a fourth.

*Of course,* Layla thought. *Of course it was him. Who else could it have been?*

And then, a second later, so loud it made Edward wince:

*I threw a Disjunction at Harry Potter.*

"Who is he?" said Seth again.

"He's—" Layla started to laugh. It was a terrible laugh, high and thin and not far from crying. "He's the most famous wizard in the world, Seth. He's the most famous person in my whole world. Ever. In history. Everybody knows him. *Everybody*. Little kids know him. Little kids in New Mexico who've never been out of their village, they know his name. There's a—he's on a *card*. That comes with chocolate. I had his card. When I was fourteen. I had it on my wall."

"You had his card on your wall," Leah said from the kitchen.

"Shut *up*, Leah."

"No, I'm just—"

"There was a man," Layla said, turning back to the room, to all of them, to Carlisle and Esme and Jasper and Rosalie on the stairs, talking fast, like if she stopped talking she'd fall over. "In Britain. In the seventies, the eighties. He called himself Lord Voldemort. That wasn't his real name. His real name was Tom Riddle. He was the worst—he was the worst thing in our world in a hundred years. He killed people. Hundreds. Thousands, maybe, Muggles, people who weren't magic, anybody. He had an army. He had *giants*. People were so scared of him they wouldn't say his name. In Britain they still won't, some of them. They say *You-Know-Who*. And when Harry Potter was a baby, Voldemort came to his house, and killed his parents, and tried to kill him, and the curse bounced off and destroyed Voldemort instead. Nobody knows how. And then when he was seventeen he killed him. For good. In a duel. In front of hundreds of people. At seventeen. And then he just—disappeared." She stopped for breath. "That's who's in your woods. That's who threw me into a log today. That's who Leah—"

She stopped.

She looked at Leah again.

"Oh my God," Layla said. "Leah. You imprinted on *Harry Potter*."

"Yeah," said Leah.

"You imprinted on the *Boy Who Lived*."

"I don't know what that means," said Leah, "but yeah, probably."

---

Edward had been listening to all of them while Layla talked, the way he always listened to everybody at once, and he'd heard it happen. He'd heard the room, slowly, one mind at a time, *not* understand.

They believed her. They just had nothing to put it next to.

*So he's, what, a celebrity?* Emmett was thinking. *Like a wizard rock star?* He was mostly delighted. *A wizard rock star who shoots the ground with a pistol and makes it explode. That's awesome. That is the most awesome thing I have ever heard.*

*Voldemort,* Jasper was thinking. *A warlord. A man with an army. Defeated in single combat by a seventeen-year-old.* He was trying to picture it, and couldn't, quite; it kept turning into a picture of himself at seventeen, in Texas, in a gray uniform. *A child soldier. A very successful child soldier.* And under that, colder: *How do you fight something like that? How did he hold two wolves and a witch and a vampire at once and not move his feet?* He was building a threat assessment. It had a lot of blank spaces in it.

*A famous wizard,* Rosalie was thinking. *Fine. Wonderful. A famous wizard with a gun is in our woods and he's been inside Seth's head, which means he's seen this house, which means he's seen* her.* Her eyes didn't leave Renesmee. *I don't care how famous he is. I care what he wants.*

Carlisle was fascinated, the way Carlisle was always fascinated, and worried in the way that came after; Esme was worried about the rug, a little, and much more about Layla; Alice was thinking, over and over, with a kind of quiet desperation, *I can't see him. I still can't see him. I can't see anything he touches. I can't see tomorrow*. Bella he couldn't hear at all. He never could. He saw her face, and her arms around their daughter, and that was enough.

And Seth was proud. That was the strangest thing in the room. Under the shock, under the worry for Layla, Seth was quietly, ferociously proud of his sister, and he didn't entirely know why.

Layla heard it too: their silence. She looked round the room at their faces and Edward heard her understand that they didn't understand, and heard her try to find a way to make them.

"You don't get it," she said. "Any of you. You don't get it."

"We get it," said Emmett. "He's famous. He's like the wizard Elvis."

"He's not *Elvis*, he's—"

"So he's famous," said Rosalie, from the stairs. Coldly. "We've had famous before. We've had famous *here*. The Volturi stood in a field in Washington in June and looked at us over a dead newborn, and their master is three thousand years old, and people in our world are afraid to say *his* name too." She tipped her chin up. "I don't care what's on his card."

"You should," said Layla.

"Why?"

And Leah, at the kitchen island, put her fork down on the counter with a small sharp click.

Edward heard it come up in her. He'd heard a great deal come up in Leah Clearwater over five months, and it had always been the same thing, anger, a red, grinding, sideways anger with nowhere to go. This was something cooler. Something with a point on it. She came round the end of the island and through the arch, past Edward, barefoot on the white floor, and stood in the middle of the living room in her damp cutoffs with her arms folded, and she looked at Rosalie on the stairs.

"Because you weren't there," Leah said.

Rosalie's eyes narrowed.

"None of you were," said Leah. "Seth was there. I was there. Layla was there. You guys were sitting in here with the kid, watching cartoons." She looked round at all of them. "So let me tell you. Since you don't get it. Since you think he's *Elvis*."

Emmett had the grace to look sheepish.

"There was a vampire," Leah said, "running from me. Flat out. One of the red-eyed ones. Fast. Scared. And it hit a rock on that ridge and a rope came up out of the ground and hung it upside down by one foot fifteen feet in the air, and it couldn't move. Couldn't move its *face*. One rope. And he'd put that rope there *weeks* ago. And there are more of them. All over. He's got the whole peninsula wired, he told me, three thousand square miles, and every time one of you goes for a walk he knows about it because there's a map on his wall with little gold lights on it, and the little gold lights are *you*."

Rosalie's face went very still.

"And then he shows up," said Leah, "out of nowhere, out of the air, in a suit, and he catches that vampire falling out of the sky with one arm. *One arm.* Like it was a two-by-four. And Layla shows up and throws everything she's got at him, and Layla's good, I've seen her, she can stop the rain, and he didn't move his feet. He didn't take his hand out of his pocket. He threw her spells back at her. He held me and Seth flat on the ground without even looking at us, the whole time, and we couldn't move a hair. Then he pulled a gun out with his other hand, and shot the dirt, *the dirt*, and blew a hole in a mountain the size of this coffee table, and threw her twenty feet into a tree. And then he walked over the hole. On the air. And gave her wand back to her."

Nobody said anything.

"And then," Leah said, "I followed him home. And he's got this tent. It's like the size of a sleeping bag on the outside and on the inside it's bigger than this house. It's got a kitchen and a library and a workshop with a flying motorcycle in it and a room full of guns that never run out. And at the back he's got a room. A round room. With jars. Hundreds of jars, all the way up to the ceiling, and things in them. Things that *move*. Hands. A thing like a black blanket that looks at you. A tooth as long as my arm from a snake he killed when he was twelve, that's so poisonous it kills things that can't die. And in the middle of the room there's a circle in the floor. And that's where he put the vampire." She paused. "And then he asked it some questions."

She let that sit.

"I watched," she said. "The whole time. He did stuff to it I don't have words for. He went in its head and took what he wanted out of it like he was going through a drawer. He made it scream without touching it. He made it *do what he said*. He cut it with the tooth, and the bit he cut off crawled across a jar trying to get back to the rest of it, and couldn't, and *died*. He did all of that in about an hour. And he didn't blink." She looked at Rosalie. "So, no. He's not Elvis. And yeah, you should care what's on his card."

The room was very quiet. Even Emmett.

Edward heard all of them hear it. He heard the blank spaces in Jasper's threat assessment start to fill in, one at a time, and heard Jasper not like a single thing that went into them. He heard Carlisle go from fascinated to something more careful. He heard Rosalie's hands tighten on the banister until the wood creaked.

He heard Layla, on the couch, sit very still and think, *she watched. She sat there and watched all of that. And she's not even shaking.*

"And," Leah said.

They all looked at her.

She shrugged. The arms came unfolded. She turned around and walked back through the arch into the kitchen, past Edward, back to the island, and she picked up her fork, and she looked across the steel at Jacob, who was standing there with his mouth open.

"He's mine," Leah said. She stabbed the last corner of the lasagna. "See? Better."

---

It was Jasper who said it first. Edward heard it coming for a full minute before he did; it built in him the way weather builds, cold and organized.

"We need a plan," Jasper said.

"We need a lot more than a plan," said Rosalie.

"We need to know what he wants," said Carlisle. He'd come away from the fireplace. He stood in the middle of the room with his hands loose at his sides, the way he stood in the emergency room when three ambulances came in at once. "Before we do anything. He's here for a reason. He came here with a purpose. He laid those—snares, did you call them, Leah?—weeks ago. He was waiting for one of the nomads, wasn't he? Not for us."

"Yeah," said Leah, from the kitchen.

"Then he's here for them. The red-eyed ones." Carlisle frowned. "Why?"

Leah opened her mouth.

And Edward watched the wall move.

He saw it happen. He'd have missed it if he hadn't been looking straight at it, but he was, he'd hardly looked anywhere else since she came up the drive. Leah opened her mouth to answer Carlisle, and the answer came up in her mind, he could see it come, a bright sharp shape like a word on the tip of her tongue, *because the*—and it reached the wall. And the wall reached back.

Leah's mouth stayed open. Nothing came out of it.

She tried again. He saw her try. He saw her jaw work. He saw her eyes go wide, and then narrow, and then go furious, and she put her hand to her throat.

"Leah?" said Seth, half rising off the couch.

"I'm fine." It came out hoarse, and then clear. She coughed. She took her hand away from her throat and looked at it. "I'm fine. I just—huh."

"What happened?" said Carlisle.

"I can't say it." Leah sounded more curious than anything. "I tried to say why he's here and it just—stopped. Right here." She tapped her throat. "Like a hand. He said it does that to him. If he tries to tell anybody. It does it to me too." She thought about it. "Huh."

*It's in her voice too,* Edward thought. *The wall goes all the way down.*

"Okay," said Jacob slowly. He'd come round the island. He was standing in the arch beside Edward, and his mind was doing what Jacob's mind did when it was frightened, which was look for something to hit. "Okay. So he's here for some reason he can't tell us, and she can't tell us, and he knows everything about us, and he's got a map with all of you on it, and he's been in Seth's head and seen Nessie. Fine." He looked at Carlisle. "Then we find him."

"Jacob—" Carlisle began.

"No, listen. We find him. Seth and me and Sam's pack too, I'll call Sam, I don't care. All of us. We've got noses. He's got a tent. He can't hide a tent from ten wolves forever, I don't care how many charms he's got on it. We find him and we tell him he's got twenty-four hours to get off the peninsula. And if he doesn't—"

"He'll take all ten of you apart," said Layla, from the couch, "before you finish the sentence."

"Then maybe *they* help." Jacob jerked his chin at Jasper, at Emmett, at Rosalie on the stairs. "Ten wolves and seven vampires. Eight. Even he can't—"

"He has a fang from a snake that kills vampires," said Leah. "In a jar. I watched him use it. It made a vampire's finger go black and fall off and it didn't grow back."

Rosalie went very still on the stairs. So did Emmett. So did Jasper.

"*Then we do it fast*," said Jacob. "Before he can get it out. Jasper, you've done this, you know how to hit somebody before they see it coming—"

And Leah laughed.

Edward had heard her usual laugh perhaps three times in five months and it had always had a blade in it. This one was low and short and genuinely amused. It came out of the kitchen and stopped everybody in the room.

"Jake," she said. "Jake. Come on."

"What?"

She came back to the arch. She leaned against the other side of it, opposite Edward, with her arms folded and her bare feet crossed at the ankle, and she looked at Jacob the way you'd look at a puppy that had just tried, with enormous seriousness, to fight its own reflection.

"You know how this works, right?" she said.

"How what works?"

"This." She tapped her chest, over her heart, where Edward knew the line went in, though he couldn't see it. "Us. Wolves. Imprinting." She tilted her head. "Remind me. What was that one law? The one that stopped Sam?"

Jacob opened his mouth.

"You know the one," said Leah. "The one from about ten weeks ago. When Sam had the whole pack lined up in the trees out there," she pointed through the glass wall, at the dark lawn and the forest beyond it, "ready to come through that window and kill the kid. And kill Bella. And kill anybody who got in the way. He'd decided. He'd given the order. He was coming. And then he didn't." She smiled. It was a very small smile, and not a kind one, and not an unkind one either. "Why didn't he, Jake?"

Jacob had gone completely still.

Edward heard it land in him. He heard it go through him like a bell.

"Because I imprinted," Jacob said slowly. "On Nessie."

"Because you imprinted on Nessie," said Leah. "And our oldest law, the one that's older than the treaty, older than Taha Aki, the one Sam couldn't break even with his Alpha voice, even if he'd wanted to, says no wolf can ever, ever harm another wolf's imprint. Ever. For any reason. Nobody voted on it. It's in the wolf. It's *in us*. You physically can't." She spread her hands. "So."

The room was absolutely silent.

"So," said Leah. "You go ahead and call Sam. You get all ten wolves together. You go find his tent. And then you stand outside it, all ten of you, with your teeth out, and you try. You try to take one step at him. One. And you see what happens." She shrugged. "Nothing's going to happen. You're going to stand there. All of you. Like dogs on a leash. Because he's mine." She looked round at the vampires in the room. "And as far as the treaty goes, a wolf's imprint is a wolf's. Same as family. Same as the kid's family is Jacob's. So you touch him, you're touching the pack." She looked at Rosalie. "Not that you could."

"Is that a threat?" said Rosalie, very softly.

"It's a fact," said Leah. "Layla couldn't touch him. Layla's better at fighting than you are, Rosalie, and she couldn't touch him with a ten-foot pole. Ask her."

Rosalie looked at Layla.

Layla, on the couch, white, with Seth's hand on her back, nodded once.

And then, in the silence, Emmett started to laugh again.

He tried to stop. He really did. Edward heard him try. He put both his hands over his face and bent over at the bottom of the stairs and his whole huge body shook, and Rosalie looked at him like she was going to tear his arm off, and he kept laughing anyway, helplessly, the way he'd laughed at very little since the summer.

"Sorry," he wheezed. "Sorry. Rose. Sorry. It's just—" He came up for air. "The *one law*. She's right. She's totally right. The one law. Jacob used it on Sam to save the baby, and now Leah's using it on Jacob to save the—the gun guy—"

"Don't call him the gun guy," said Leah. "He hates that."

That set Emmett off again.

And Jacob—Edward heard it happen—Jacob, standing in the arch with his fists clenched and his whole plan lying in pieces around his feet, felt something give. He'd been holding on to the anger with both hands, the way people hold on to anger when it's the only thing standing between them and fear, and it had just been taken away from him, neatly, by the last person he'd have expected, using the one tool in the world he couldn't argue with. Because he'd used it himself. Because it had saved the only thing that mattered to him.

He laughed. Once. Short and helpless, like something knocked out of him.

"I hate you," he said to Leah.

"I know."

"I really hate you."

"Yeah, Jake. I know."

---

Carlisle made the plan, in the end. It was the plan Edward would have made, and the plan Carlisle always made, which was barely a plan at all. Wait. Listen. Let him come.

"He said he'd come tomorrow," Carlisle said. "In person. He said it to Edward. Let him come. Let's hear what he has to say."

"And if what he has to say is bad?" said Rosalie.

"Then we'll know," said Carlisle. "And we'll decide then. But I don't believe a man who wanted to hurt us would announce himself first. Or offer biscuits."

"Unless that's exactly what he'd do," said Jasper.

"Unless that's exactly what he'd do," Carlisle agreed. He looked at Leah. "Leah. Is he coming?"

"Yeah," said Leah. She was putting the empty lasagna tray in Esme's sink. "He's coming."

"What time?"

"Don't know. He didn't say. I'll ask him."

"You'll *ask* him," said Jacob. "How? You don't even have a phone."

Leah looked at him.

She didn't say anything. She just looked. And then she tapped her chest again, over the place where the line went in, once, with two fingers.

Jacob closed his eyes.

"Right," he said. "Of course. Sure. Why not."

She was gone ten minutes later. She said goodbye to Esme, and thanked her for the lasagna again. She said goodbye to Seth with a shove to the side of his head that nearly knocked him off the couch. She stopped by Layla on the way past and looked down at her for a moment, and Edward heard her think something about the bruise on Layla's cheek, something that was half apology and half *I'd have done the same thing in his place*, and then she said, out loud, "You had his card on your *wall*?" and Layla threw a cushion at her.

She didn't say goodbye to the kid. She brushed her fingers against the kid's palm on the way past, the way she always did.

Then she went out the front door, and down the porch steps, and behind the cedar, and Edward heard her phase, and heard her go, south and west through the trees toward La Push, at a long easy lope.

He stood at the glass wall and watched the dark where she'd gone.

Behind him the room was coming back to life, slowly: Esme on her knees on the rug with a cloth, Carlisle at Layla's side again with his bag, Jacob on the couch with his head in his hands and Seth's arm around his shoulders. Emmett, finally quiet. Rosalie had taken Renesmee from Bella and was holding her on the stairs, rocking her, very slightly, the way she always did when she was frightened.

Bella came and stood beside him at the glass.

He couldn't hear her. He never could. But he knew her face, and he knew the way she held her shoulders, and he knew what she was going to ask before she asked it.

"Is she right?" Bella said. "About the law?"

"Yes."

"So nobody can touch him. Not the wolves."

"No."

"And we can't touch him because we'd be touching the pack."

"Not without breaking the treaty," said Edward. "And Jacob would never let us. And I don't think we could anyway." He watched the trees. "You didn't hear Layla's mind, Bella. When she was talking about him. You didn't hear what she was comparing him to."

"What was she comparing him to?"

He thought about it. About Layla on the couch, and the three names she'd learned when she was twelve, and the fourth name she'd added in this room an hour ago. About a voice at the edge of Leah's mind that had found him in the dark, in a place where no one had ever found him, and said *evening*.

"Aro," he said quietly.

Bella went very still.

"He's coming tomorrow," Edward said. "With biscuits." He turned from the window. "I'd like to know what he wants before he gets here. And I'd like to know how he knew who I was."

*You'll be Edward,* the voice had said. *The one from the photograph.*

He'd been trying not to think about that part.

He didn't know of any photograph.

### III. Harry

*The tent, and elsewhere — 7:34 p.m.*

He'd been standing at the range with his hands on the iron and *Physical Graffiti* on side two when the oath moved.

It was the second time in one day. Eight years without a twitch and then twice in one afternoon, like a dog that's slept on the hearth since you got it and has suddenly started barking at the post. He felt it shift under his collarbone. A cold flex. Bracing, the way your shoulder braces when somebody leans on a door you're holding shut.

Somebody was leaning on the door.

He went down the line without thinking about it. He'd stopped thinking about the line at all, since she'd left; it was simply there now, the way his own arm was there, and going down it was as easy as looking out of a window. Twenty miles of dark forest went by under him in no time at all. And then he was at the edge of her. At the edge of Leah, in a white kitchen that smelled of tomato and fennel and the burnt-sugar-and-bleach stink of the cold ones so thick he could taste it through her nose. She was eating. She was standing at a steel island eating lasagna out of a tray, and across the island the big russet one, Jacob, was standing with a fork, and she was calm. Through and through, all the way down, she was calm. He'd never felt her calm before. He'd only known her eight hours, but even so.

He liked it. He let himself like it for about a second.

Then he turned and looked at the one leaning on the wall.

It was a mind. A cold one's mind, fast and wide and dark the way the Polish one's had been, but quieter. Ordered. Like a library somebody had kept tidy for a hundred years. And it was *pushing*. Gently, persistently, the way you'd push on a locked door to see if the lock was any good. It had been doing it for a while.

He knew whose it was. There was only one of them who could do that.

He'd known about the boy since June. Gianna had known; Aro had talked about him in front of her, after the business in the square, with the voice of a collector who's missed a lot at auction. *Carlisle's boy hears everything. Every thought in every head around him. Like me, but without the touch. Imagine what he could tell me, if he would only stay.* Harry had built half of what he'd done in Forks around that sentence. Two months of keeping his own head as smooth and dull as a pebble whenever he was within a mile of the white house. A decoy layer on his Occlumency that he'd spent August perfecting, so that if the boy ever did brush past him in the Thriftway, he'd hear a photographer from Islington worrying about his light meter and his overdraft and nothing else at all.

And now here was the boy, leaning on the wall in Leah's head. Trying the lock on Harry's secrets from the inside of the one person he'd never thought to guard against.

Harry looked at him for a moment. Then he did something he'd never done in eight years in the Department, and had never imagined he would.

*Evening,* he said.

The other mind went still as a struck deer.

He'd thought it would. He'd met two other Legilimenses in his life who were any good, and one of them had been Snape and the other had been Voldemort, and both of them had been as shocked as anybody the first time something turned round inside a head they were reading and spoke to them. Nobody expected it. It was like reaching into a dark cupboard for a coat and having the coat shake your hand.

He gave the boy a second to take it in. Then he told him to stop pushing.

*Who are you?* said the boy.

It came back clear and quick and careful, in an American voice with something old underneath it, and Harry found he respected that. It took nerve, to answer. Most people's first instinct, the first time something spoke to them from inside somebody else's head, was to run.

He thought about it.

He thought about it properly, for about the length of one bar of "Kashmir," which was going on in his own kitchen twenty miles away. He thought about it the way he'd been trained to think about anything he gave away: what it cost, what it bought.

The name wasn't the Department's. That was the first thing. They'd given him a lot, but they hadn't given him that; he'd had it since the day he was born, and so had the stone on Level Nine, presumably, since it had felt no need to stop him saying it to Leah at teatime. It was his. He could spend it if he liked.

The second thing was that they'd have it by tomorrow regardless. He was going up that drive in the morning. He'd decided that some time between the circle and the Croaker call, without quite noticing he'd decided. And the witch was in that house, and the witch had grown up in his world, and the minute the witch got a proper look at him in good light without a vampire floating behind him and a crater in front of her, she'd know. There were Chocolate Frog cards. He'd seen the bloody things in a shop in Prague.

The third thing was Leah.

He could feel her, at the island, eating her lasagna, perfectly calm. He could feel the wall in the middle of her, and how much of her afternoon was behind it. And he found that he didn't like it. He didn't like that there was a thing in her she couldn't say. He'd carried a thing like that around for eight years and it had cost him nearly everybody he'd ever loved, and she'd been carrying his for about an hour.

He could give her this much back.

*Fair question,* he told the boy. *Seeing as I've been through your friend's head and had a good look round your living room. Fine. Have that much. It's mine to give, anyway.*

And he reached into the wall, into the cold flexing weight of the oath where it sat across the middle of Leah's mind, and he did what Unspeakables did when the Minister for Magic needed briefing and somebody senior had signed the form. He let go. Just of the one thing. He felt the oath feel him let go, and consider it, and agree. He felt it ease, a crack's worth, a door's width.

And he slid his name through.

He watched the boy pick it up.

*The rest of it you'll get tomorrow,* he said. *From me. In person. Tell your dad I'll bring biscuits.*

Then he closed the door, and came back down the line, twenty miles of forest going the other way under him, and was standing in his own kitchen with his hands on the range and Robert Plant going on about the desert.

He stood there for a minute.

"Huh," he said.

The oath had done what he asked. Exactly what he asked, no more and no less. It had let the name go through the wall in Leah's head because he'd let it go, the same as it would have let it go out of his own mouth. Same rules. Same consent. As if her head and his were the same room.

He filed it. He filed it next to all the other things about today he didn't understand, which was turning into a fairly substantial file.

Then he took his hands off the range and went to get his boots, because there was a ridge above the Bogachiel that looked, to anybody with the right kind of eyes, like somebody had fought a small war on it this afternoon. Which somebody had.

---

He Apparated onto the slab at eight minutes to eight, Disillusioned so thoroughly that the drizzle went round him, and stood still for a long time in the dark, just looking.

It was worse than he'd thought.

His own work was fine. His own work was always fine; he'd been cleaning up after himself since he was seventeen and on the run, and he'd been doing it professionally for eight years, and everything he'd cast this afternoon had gone through his sinks before it left his hands. It had left nothing behind on the ridge but a faint grey smudge here and there like a thumbprint on glass, and even that was fading.

The witch's was a different matter.

He could see it from where he stood. To the right kind of eyes the whole ridge glowed with it. The crack of her Apparition on the far side of the slab, a raw bright star-shaped scar in the air where she'd come through from fourteen miles off in a hurry, still ragged at the edges. The Stunner she'd thrown on arrival, lying in a smear across the hemlock branch it had hit. The ropes, two of them, in pieces in the moss, still faintly twitching. The Blasting Curse he'd turned into the boulder; that one was a black starburst on the shattered rock, so loud he could almost hear it. The roots she'd conjured out of the moss, still sticking up out of the ground like the fingers of a buried hand. And the Disjunction.

The Disjunction was everywhere. He'd sent it back down her line and it had done exactly what she'd built it to do, which was take things apart, and it had taken her shield apart, and a shield coming apart at close range scattered like a bag of flour dropped from a height. There was Disjunction dust all over the slab. It lay on the wet granite in a fine pale shimmer like frost.

He'd been right, in the kitchen with Leah. Anybody with a sensor within fifty miles would have lit up like Bonfire Night.

He crouched down and put his palm flat on the slab and went to work.

He started at the edges and worked in. The ropes first, the roots, the Stunner smear. Those were easy; they were small, and they were clean, and they came up off the ridge into his sinks like dust into a vacuum. The Blasting Curse took longer. It had sunk into the boulder, into the cracks of the rock, and he had to coax it out a thread at a time, crouched in the wet in the dark with his eyes shut, while the drizzle ran down the back of his neck.

The Apparition scar he couldn't do anything about. You couldn't. Apparition left a hole in the world and the world closed it in its own time, and the best you could do was make the hole look like something else.

So he made it look like something else.

He stood up when he'd done everything he could. He looked at the scar, and at the crater, and at the blackened trees, and he thought about what you'd see if you were an investigator from Seattle walking up here tomorrow afternoon with a sensor in one hand and a map in the other. A big burst of raw magic, already fading, centred on a granite slab on the highest point of a ridge, in a week of storms, under trees that had already taken one hit.

He looked up at the sky.

It was low and black and full of water. He could feel the charge in it, faint, the way you could always feel it over this coast in November, a long slow hum of weather coming in off the Pacific. There was a storm out there tonight. Not a big one. A bit of rumbling off toward Cape Flattery an hour ago that he'd half heard over the music. It was moving south.

He reached up into it.

It took a while. Weather was the hardest thing there was. People thought it was easy, because you could make it rain in a teacup with a first-year charm. Weather was the whole sky, all at once, all the way up, and the sky didn't care what you wanted. You asked it, very politely, for a long time, and then you waited.

He asked for nearly fifteen minutes, standing on the slab with his face turned up into the rain and his hands open at his sides.

Then the sky answered.

It came down on the slab like the end of the world. He'd stepped back into the trees for it, thirty yards back, and it still threw him against a hemlock and left him blind and deaf for ten seconds. A single white-hot bolt, as thick as his leg, out of a cloud he could have reached up and touched, straight down into the middle of the crater his own round had made that afternoon. The granite split with a crack he felt in his teeth. The hemlock that had already lost a branch to the Stunner went up like a torch for a few seconds before the rain put it out. The smell of ozone was so strong it made his eyes water.

When he could see again he walked back out onto the slab and looked.

The Apparition scar was still there. It would be there for a week. But it was sitting now at the edge of a fresh lightning strike, a big one, still steaming, a blast pattern of scorched rock and fulgurite spidered out across the granite and into the moss, and the raw stink of real lightning lay over everything, every trace of the witch's work that he hadn't been able to get, the last of the Disjunction dust, all of it, like a blanket thrown over a stain on a carpet.

Anybody from Seattle with a sensor who came up here tomorrow would find a lightning strike on a high ridge in a week of storms, and a lot of confused readings around it, and an Apparition mark that was probably somebody from the MACUSA weather office having a look. They'd write it up. They'd go home.

Probably.

He stood there on the hot steaming rock for a while longer, in the drizzle, just to be sure.

Then he went to find the woman.

---

He'd taken it out of the Pole's head along with everything else, at the circle. The last few seconds of her. He hadn't wanted it and he'd taken it anyway, because you took all of it when you went in that fast, and now it was his to carry. The river. The light on the water. The camera.

He Apparated to the Hoh at twenty past nine.

It was four miles up the trail from the ranger station, where the river came round a long bend under the big maples and there was a gravel bar on the inside of it. He knew the spot. He'd been there in October, taking pictures of moss for Harry Evans's book. The big maples were bare now and the moss on them was black in the dark and the river was loud and high and grey. He stood on the gravel bar with his wand lit, for once, because he wanted to see properly, and he looked.

She was in the water at the downstream end of the bar, caught on a snag. The Pole had put her there. He'd thought it would look like she'd fallen. He'd been in a hurry; he'd heard the wolves on the wind.

Harry stood on the gravel and looked at Dana Whitcomb for a long time.

He didn't touch her. There wasn't anything he could do that would help, and anything he did would only leave something for somebody to find. The police would come. The park service would come. Somebody from the county would put it down to a fall and a cougar, because that was what it looked like, and because that was what they'd put down for every one of the others over the years on this coast, and they'd all be wrong, and it wouldn't matter, because she'd still be dead. Her boyfriend would come up from Olympia. Her mother would come from wherever her mother lived. They'd stand on this gravel bar in the daylight and look at this water.

He found her camera. It was on the bank, in the ferns, where it had fallen. A little silver digital one with a strap. He picked it up with a charm, without touching it, and turned it on, and it still worked, and the last picture on it was the river, the bend, the light coming through the maples at six o'clock last night and lying on the water in long gold stripes.

It was a good picture.

He put the camera back where he'd found it, in the ferns, exactly where it had been.

Then he did the one thing he could do, which was make sure she'd be found tomorrow and not in a fortnight. He put a charm on the gravel bar, small and quiet, the kind that made a hiker on the trail above glance down at the river for no reason at all and keep looking. It would fade in two days. By then it wouldn't matter.

He stood there one more minute.

"Sorry, love," he said. "He's in a jar. If it helps."

It didn't. He knew it didn't. He said it anyway.

Then he went back to Forks and stood in the phone box outside the Thriftway, in the orange light, with a quarter in the slot and the collar of Harry Evans's waxed jacket turned up, and rang the ranger station on the Hoh road. It was closed, of course, at a quarter to ten on a Monday in November. He left a message on the machine. He did it in a flat Midwestern voice he'd borrowed off an Auror from Chicago he'd once spent a very long week in a cellar with, and he said he'd seen something in the river from the trail, four miles up, at the big bend past the maples, and he thought somebody ought to look. And he hung up, and wiped the receiver with his sleeve out of a habit older than magic, and walked back round the side of the building, and was gone.

---

He got back to the tent at ten past ten and the thing under his ribs was waiting for him.

It had been patient all evening. It had lain at the bottom of him behind its three locks while he worked on the ridge and stood on the gravel bar, chin on its paws, eyes open, facing southeast toward the coast. It hadn't pushed. It had let him get on with things. He'd been grateful.

He came in through the flap and down the three steps and hung his jacket on the peg, and looked at the mirror on its nail by the telephone.

And the thing under his ribs stood up.

He felt it go. All four feet, all at once. It stood up behind its locks with its hackles rising and its ears flat back and its lips peeling off its teeth, and it looked up through him at the little black mirror on the wall, the one that said *PROPERTY OF H.M. MINISTRY OF MAGIC* on the back and *abandon hope* under that, and it growled.

He felt the growl in his own chest. Low and long. Like an engine idling.

"Yeah," Harry said. "I know."

It went on growling.

He knew what it didn't like. He didn't need to ask. The mirror was London. London was the place he'd come from and the place that could tell him to go back. London had a room on Level Nine with brains in tanks and a stone that put cold things in your throat, and London had sent him here, and London could take him away again with one line in Croaker's spidery writing on a grey sheet of paper. *Return.* And the thing under his ribs had stood on a rock this afternoon and looked up a line into a grey she-wolf's eyes, and found something it had apparently been waiting for its whole life, and it was not, under any circumstances, going back to London.

"I know," he said again. "I know. Sit down."

It didn't sit down.

He went into the kitchen and put the kettle on, and stood there while it boiled, and didn't look at the mirror. He made a cup of tea. He drank it standing up at the range. He thought about Croaker.

He thought about the old man behind the desk in the green lamplight at two in the morning, with his pale tap-water eyes, writing. *You sound odd, Potter.* He thought about the first time he'd ever met him, in September 1998, four months after the war, in that same office, when Harry had walked in with his hands in his pockets and the whole world still taking his picture everywhere he went, and Croaker had looked up from his paper and said, *I don't want the Boy Who Lived. I've no use for him. If he's here, he can go home. I want to know if there's anybody else in there.* And Harry had stood in front of the desk for a while, and then he'd said, *I don't know, sir. That's sort of why I came.* And Croaker had looked at him for a long time and said, *Sit down.*

Eight years. Eight years, and the old man had never once lied to him. Not about the work, not about the risk, not about what the Department was and what it did and what it would cost. He'd told Harry, that first day, that it would cost him everything that wasn't the work, sooner or later, and it had, and he'd been right, and he'd never once pretended otherwise. He'd sat with Harry at four in the morning in that office after Minsk. He'd sent him to Uganda when he needed sending. He'd never asked him for anything Harry hadn't been able to give.

And at twenty to six tonight Harry had looked straight into that mirror and said *no, sir, nothing else*.

He put the cup down.

It had been the right call, operationally. He'd known that when he made it. You didn't hand London a thing like Leah until you knew what it was and what they'd do with it, and he didn't know either of those things, and the only way to be sure they didn't put her in a jar was to make sure they never knew she existed. It had been clean. It had been tidy. Nobody would ever have found out. It was exactly the sort of lie he'd been trained to tell.

It sat in him like a stone in a shoe.

He'd lied to a lot of people. He'd been lying professionally for eight years. He'd lied to Ministers and Muggle prime ministers and goblins and a Bulgarian vampire-hunter with no thumbs. He'd lied to Hermione and Ron for six years about where he went on Tuesdays. He'd lied to Ginny until she stopped asking. He was good at it. He'd never lied to Croaker.

He went back into the hall.

The thing under his ribs saw where he was going, and went from growling to snarling, and threw itself at the first lock.

He stopped with his hand halfway to the mirror. He stood there with his hand in the air and his eyes shut and the lock groaning in his chest, and he leaned on it, and leaned on it, and it held.

"Listen to me," he said. Out loud. To nobody. To it. "Listen. I'm not going anywhere. I'm not. You felt her. You think I'm going anywhere? I'm not going to let anybody take us anywhere." He breathed. "But I'm not doing *that*. Not to him. I'm not lying to the old man. Not like that. Not ever. So you can sit down and shut up and let me do it properly, or you can throw yourself at that lock all night, but I'm doing it either way."

It hit the lock once more.

Then, slowly, sullenly, it backed off. He felt it back off. It didn't lie down. It stood behind its locks with its head low and its teeth still showing, and it watched him, the way a big dog watches a stranger come up the drive with its owner. Waiting to see.

He took the mirror off the nail.

"Croaker," he said.

---

It took longer this time. It was half past six in the morning in London, and the glass stayed grey for nearly a minute, and Harry stood in the hall and waited and wondered whether, for the first time in eight years, the old man was actually asleep.

Then the room came up. The green lamp. The drifts of paper. Croaker, behind the desk, in the same grey robes, with a cup of tea at his elbow and his pen in his hand and a look on his long lined face that might, in somebody else, have been surprise.

"Twice in one night, Potter," he said. "I'm flattered."

"Sir."

"Has the specimen escaped?"

"No, sir."

"Has something eaten you?"

"No, sir."

"Then I assume this is about whatever it was you didn't tell me five hours ago."

Harry stood very still.

Croaker put his pen down. He picked up his tea. He looked up out of the mirror over the rim of the cup, with the pale eyes, patient.

"Go on," he said.

So Harry told him.

He told him in order, because that was the only way he knew how to tell anything. He told him about the witch, Layla Quinn, curse-breaker, Maeve Quinn's granddaughter, who'd broken the Statute in front of half the town for a Quileute boy and a houseful of vampires and a police chief, and about the deal he'd made with her on his own account on the ridge: her silence for his, a favour owed, to be called in later. He told him about the lattice and the eighth gold light at the white house, the small one with the red coal in the middle, and what it was, as far as he could tell, which was a child. Half human and half the other thing. Born in September. Growing fast. The Italians had a law about children, as far as Gianna's memory went; they'd burned whole covens for less. He told him about the heartbeat the Pole had stood in the trees and smelled at four o'clock this morning, and the phone call, the Imperius held on a three-hundred-year-old mind with Aro of Volterra laughing on the other end of the line, *you sound tired, my friend*, *in the spring*, and how close it had come, for one second, to slipping.

And then he told him about Leah.

He didn't dress it up. He couldn't have, he suspected, even with Croaker; the habit of the afternoon was on him. He told him about the grey wolf on the slab, and taking his glasses off to get a proper look, and the line. What the Quileute called it. What it did. Both ends. The thing under his ribs that he'd kept locked down since September, and how it had stood up. How he couldn't lie to her. How she couldn't lie to him.

He didn't tell him about the oath.

He got to the edge of it and stopped, and stood there, and Croaker watched him stop.

"There's something else," Harry said. "To do with her. To do with the bond. I don't understand it yet. I've got a hypothesis, and I don't like it, and I'm not going to tell you what it is."

"Because you're not sure?"

"Because if I'm right, and I told you, the Department would want her," said Harry. "Sir. I'd want her, if she was anybody else's. I'd want her on the table on Level Nine by Friday. And I'm not going to let that happen. So I'm not going to tell you. And I'm telling you I'm not telling you, because I'm not lying to you again." He took a breath. "I lied to you five hours ago. You asked me if there was anything else and I said no. There was. There was all of that. That's why I rang."

Croaker didn't say anything.

"I'm compromised, sir," Harry said. "That's the long and the short of it. I've got a bond with a local asset I can't break and can't explain and can't control, that I didn't ask for and wouldn't give up now if you offered me the Veil to take apart. I'm running a private arrangement with a foreign national. I've made unauthorised contact with the target. I've sat on intelligence about a hybrid child because I didn't want the Department anywhere near her. And if you rang me tomorrow and said, *Potter, it's her or us*—" He stopped. He made himself finish it. "It's her. Every time. Every single time. I'd walk out of the Department and not look back. I wouldn't even have to think about it." He breathed out. "So you'd better send somebody else. Rookwood's old desk is still empty; there's that girl from the Brain Room, Abernathy, she's good, she's very good. She can be here by Wednesday. I'll hand over the specimen and the lattice and the files. I'll brief her properly. And then I'll—" He stopped again. "I don't know what I'll do. Stay, I suppose. But not for you."

He stopped talking.

In the mirror, Croaker set his tea down on the desk. Very carefully, in the ring it had already made on the wood.

He didn't look surprised. He didn't look anything. He looked at Harry for a long time, out of the green lamplight, with his pale eyes and his mouth like a crack in a wall.

"Have you finished?" he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Good. Sit down, Potter."

"I'm in a tent, sir. There's nothing to sit on in the hall."

"Then stand still and listen." Croaker folded his hands on top of the paper in front of him. "How long have you worked for me?"

"Eight years in September."

"And in eight years, how many times have you lied to me?"

Harry thought about it. "Once, sir. Tonight."

"Once." Croaker nodded slowly. "Do you know how many of the people who've sat in that chair in front of my desk over the last fifty years could say the same?"

Harry didn't answer.

"Two," said Croaker. "You, and a woman called Edith Abernathy, who was the grandmother of that girl you're so keen to send in your place, and who died in 1979 in a room I'm not going to name, doing something I'm not going to describe, because she wouldn't lie to me about how dangerous it was." He picked his pen back up and turned it over in his long fingers. "Everybody else lies to me. They lie to me constantly. Small lies, mostly. Kind lies, some of them. Lies to protect me, lies to protect themselves, lies to protect a project they love more than their own sense. I expect it. I factor it in. I've been factoring it in since before your mother was born." He set the pen down. "And you rang me back at half past six in the morning, Potter, to confess to a lie I would never in a thousand years have found out about. A clean one. A good one. A lie I'd have been proud to teach. Because it sat badly with you."

Harry said nothing.

"That," said Croaker, "is why I'll never get rid of you. Do you understand? I can find power. Power's cheap. Wandless magic, the war, the name, walking through a ward in Minsk that took the Russians thirty years to build. I've got a dozen people downstairs who can do things that would make your hair stand on end. What I can't find, what I've never once been able to find in fifty years in this Department, is somebody who can do all of that and still ring me at six in the morning because a lie sits badly with him." He leaned back in his chair. "You're exactly where you need to be, Potter. You just haven't noticed yet."

Harry stood in the hall of the tent with the mirror in his hand.

Under his ribs, the thing behind the locks had gone very quiet. It was still standing. But its ears had come forward, a little.

"Sir," Harry said slowly, "I don't think you heard the bit where I said I'd pick her over the Department."

"I heard it." Croaker sounded faintly bored. "Everybody picks somebody over the Department, eventually. The ones who don't are the ones I worry about. The ones who don't are the ones who end up in the Veil." He picked his tea back up. "Now. Let's be practical. Tell me what you've got on that peninsula."

Harry told him. It didn't take long; he'd already told him most of it.

"A coven of seven," Croaker said, when he'd finished, "of a species we didn't know existed eight months ago, who don't eat people, who've lived among Muggles for decades without being noticed, who have at least three members with gifts that would be worth a fortune to anybody who could understand them, and who are, on your assessment, likely to be on our side when it comes to it."

"Yes, sir."

"A tribe of shapeshifters we've never heard of, who've been fighting the cold ones on that coast for a thousand years, and who've got a better working knowledge of how to kill them than this entire Department."

"Yes, sir."

"A child that's half of each, that the Italians would burn on sight, that's growing at a rate you can't explain."

"Yes, sir."

"A curse-breaker of real ability who owes you a favour."

"Yes."

"A MACUSA field office in Seattle that's started asking questions in the wrong pubs. A dead hiker who'll be found tomorrow. A specimen in a circle. An Italian nomad who's expected in Volterra in the spring. And a she-wolf who's bound to my best field officer by something nobody understands, and who, if I'm reading between your lines correctly, Potter, and I generally am, has just made you the first wizard in recorded history with a legitimate personal claim on a seat at that tribe's table."

Harry hadn't thought about it like that.

He thought about it like that.

"Yes, sir," he said.

"Then you were never coming home," said Croaker. "Were you. Not for a long time. Not with that much going on in one place. I'd have had to leave you there anyway. All this does is give me a reason to put on paper." He pulled a fresh sheet of grey paper toward him across the desk, and dipped his pen, and began to write. "As of seven o'clock this morning, Greenwich Mean Time, you are reassigned. Long-term. You are the Department of Mysteries' liaison to the Quileute Nation and to the cold ones of the Forks coven. Ambassador, if you like the sound of it better; I don't, it's pompous. Your brief is to build and maintain a working relationship with both, on terms you judge acceptable, for the purpose of mutual defence and the exchange of knowledge relevant to the Volterra problem. You will report to me. Only to me. Nothing goes in the general files. Nothing goes to Level Nine. If anybody asks, you're on a long research sabbatical studying rainforest moss."

"I am, technically, sir."

"Then it won't even be a lie." Croaker kept writing. "You'll need the tribe's leave to stay on their land. Get it. I don't care how. Be polite. Bring a gift. Not biscuits."

"Biscuits are already promised, sir. To the coven."

Croaker's pen stopped. He looked up.

"You've promised the cold ones biscuits."

"Tomorrow morning, sir. I'm going up to the house. I said I'd bring some."

Croaker looked at him for a long, long moment.

Then something happened to his face that Harry had seen perhaps three times in eight years. The crack of the mouth moved. Just at one corner. Just a fraction.

"Bring the good ones," Croaker said. "Not those Muggle things in the blue packet."

"Yes, sir."

He finished writing. He signed it, a long spidery scrawl. He blotted it. He folded it once and laid it to one side.

"Potter."

"Sir?"

"The thing you're not telling me. About her. About the bond."

Harry waited.

"Keep not telling me," said Croaker. "I'm too old to start keeping people in jars. I've never liked it. I've done it, when I had to, and I've never liked it, and I'm not going to start with a girl who's the only thing that's ever made you ring me back at six in the morning." He picked up his tea. "I can't speak for whoever sits in this chair after me. So I'd advise you to make very sure you've got this all sorted before I die."

"How long have I got, sir?"

"I've been asked that by four different Ministers," said Croaker. "I've never told any of them either." He drank his tea. "Go to bed, Potter. You look terrible. And tell your wolf to stop growling at my mirror."

The glass went dark.

Harry stood in the hall with it in his hand for a long time.

Then he hung it back on its nail, and stood looking at it, at the plain black frame and his own face in it. He looked tired. He looked, he thought, about forty. There was moss in his hair from the ridge.

Under his ribs, the thing behind the locks let out a long breath, and lay down.

It lay down with its head up, and its eyes open, facing southeast, the way it had been lying since six o'clock. But it lay down. Its hackles went flat. Its ears came forward. He felt it settle into the dark at the bottom of him like a big dog settling onto a hearth, turning round once, twice, and dropping.

"Yeah," Harry said. "Told you."

It didn't say anything. It didn't need to.

It was twenty past eleven. He'd been awake since half five. He'd caught a vampire, fought a witch, been tied at the soul to a stranger, held an Imperius on a three-hundred-year-old mind while the oldest killer on earth laughed down the phone, pulled lightning out of the sky, stood over a dead woman in a river, and confessed to his boss. He'd eaten two fry-ups, a cheeseburger, and most of a slice of blackberry pie.

He went to bed.

### IV. Leah

*The Clearwater house, La Push — Monday night*

She'd felt him the whole time she'd been home. Faintly, the way you could feel somebody in the next room through the wall. At ten to nine, sitting on the back step in her wet cutoffs with a bowl of cereal, she'd felt him somewhere high and wet and cold, and he was *straining*, all of him, the way you'd strain to hold up the corner of something very heavy, and it went on for a long time. And then something came down out of the sky and hit the mountain, and she felt it go through him, and she'd dropped the bowl.

Down the hill, a long way off to the northeast, so faint she thought she'd imagined it, a roll of thunder came across the water.

She'd sat there on the step in the dark with milk on her feet and listened to it fade.

*Lightning,* she'd thought. *He made it look like lightning. He said he would.*

Half an hour later, in the shower, she'd felt him go somewhere else. Somewhere by a river. She'd felt him stand there for a long time, very still, and what came down the rope from him then was something she didn't have a name for. Heavy. Quiet. Like the way she'd felt standing on the field in June over the ashes of the newborns, when the fire was going out and the smoke was going up and she'd thought, *they were people, once. They were somebody's kids.* Something like that, only older. Tireder. Like he'd stood by a lot of rivers.

She'd stood under the water and felt him feel it, and didn't know what to do, and in the end she didn't do anything. She just stood there, with her hand on her chest where the rope went in, and let him know she was there. She didn't know if he could tell. She thought maybe he could. After a while, the heavy thing eased, a little. Just a little.

That was where she was when her mom's car came up the gravel at twenty past nine. Standing in the shower with her hand on her chest. She heard the engine stop, and heard the car door not open.

She stood under the water with her eyes shut and listened to her mother sit in the car in the dark in the driveway for four minutes.

She knew what that meant. She'd done it herself, outside Newton's, outside the clinic, outside Sam's house that one time in March before she'd known what she was. You sat in the car because the car was the last place where nobody needed anything from you yet. You sat there and got your face ready.

Seth had called her. Of course he had. Seth called their mom about everything; he'd called her from the Cullens' bathroom while Leah was still eating, whispering into his phone with the fan on, like that would stop anybody in that house from hearing. *Mom. Mom, don't freak out. Leah's fine. She's totally fine. But something happened.*

The car door opened. Shut. Footsteps on the gravel, on the porch. The front door. Keys in the dish. Her mom's purse on the hook.

Leah turned off the water.

She came down ten minutes later in sweatpants and one of her dad's old flannel shirts, the green one, with her hair wet and sticking up. Her mom was at the kitchen table in her scrubs, the blue ones with the cartoon bandages on them that the kids at the clinic liked, with her reading glasses pushed up on her head and a cup of coffee in front of her that she hadn't touched. She'd put the kettle on too. Leah could hear it starting to tick on the stove.

Sue Clearwater looked at her daughter coming into the kitchen.

She looked for a long time. She looked the way she'd looked at Leah every single day since March, the careful, sideways, worried look, the one that checked Leah's face the way you'd check the sky for weather. Leah had hated that look. She'd hated it more than anything. She'd felt it on the back of her neck at dinner and in the car and through the bathroom door, every day, *how is she, is she okay, is it a bad day*, and every day it had made her want to put her fist through the drywall.

She stood in the kitchen doorway and let her mom look.

And she watched her mom's face change.

It was slow. It started at the eyes. Her mom was looking at her the way she always looked, checking, searching, and Leah could see her find something she wasn't expecting, and stop, and look again. And then something went out of her mom's shoulders. Something that had been holding them up since March. Leah watched it go. She watched her mother's whole body let down about half an inch, all at once, in her chair, like a coat sliding off a hook.

"Oh," Sue said quietly.

"Hi, Mom."

"Oh, honey."

"I'm fine," said Leah.

And it came out. It came out of her mouth easy and plain and it was *true*. She'd said it a thousand times in seven months and it had never once been true, and her mom had always known, and had always said *okay, honey* anyway. It had been true this time. Leah heard it. Her mom heard it.

Sue put her hand over her mouth.

"Mom—"

"I'm okay. I'm okay." Her mother took her hand away. Her eyes were wet. "Come here. Sit down. Sit down, I made you tea."

"I don't want tea."

"I don't care. Sit down."

Leah sat down.

Her mom got up and took the kettle off the stove and made the tea anyway. Chamomile, with honey. Maeve Quinn's honey, the last jar from last Christmas, Leah saw, the one her mom had been saving. She put it in front of Leah on the table and sat back down across from her and wrapped her hands around her own cold coffee and looked at her daughter over it.

"So," said Sue.

"So."

"Seth says you imprinted."

"Yeah."

"On a white man."

"On an English man," said Leah. "Seth says he's white?"

"Seth says he's English and that he's very pale and that he wears a suit and that he shot Layla."

"He shot the ground."

"Seth said you'd say that."

Leah picked up the tea. She didn't want it. She drank some anyway. It tasted like the bee yard on Calawah Way smelled, sweet and dark and green.

"Seth also says," said her mom carefully, "that he's a wizard. Like Layla. And that he's very famous. And that Layla had a picture of him on her wall when she was fourteen." She paused. "And that he holds people down on the ground without touching them, and walks on air, and has a room full of jars."

"That's all true."

"I figured." Sue turned the coffee cup round in her hands. "Is he good to you?"

Leah opened her mouth.

She'd been ready for a lot of questions. She'd had twelve miles of dark forest between the Cullens' and home to get ready for them. *Who is he, where's he from, how old is he, what does he do, is he dangerous, what does he want with you, what are you going to do.* She'd had answers ready for all of them, some of them true. She hadn't been ready for that one.

"I've known him nine hours, Mom."

"I know. Is he good to you?"

Leah thought about it.

She thought about him on the ridge in his dark glasses, looking bored, calling her Lassie. About his face going white, and his hand going flat on his chest, and *not on a bloody Monday*. About him standing in front of the tent in the rain telling Gary the slug not to look at him like that. About him in the kitchen making eggs, with his back to her, and her being able to feel through the rope how much he was concentrating on the eggs, like they were important, like it was important that hers came out right. About *they'd have you in a jar, Leah*, and *I don't want anybody taking you apart*, and his hand flat on her face in the dark and his whole body shaking with the effort of not letting the thing at the bottom of him come out and frighten her.

"He's honest," she said finally. "He can't not be. Not with me."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know. I'm getting there." Leah put the mug down. "He's—Mom, he's scary. He's really, really scary. I watched him do stuff today I don't even have words for. Stuff that would make Dad—" She stopped. "But he cooked me eggs. And he cut the crusts off the toast without asking, because I don't like crusts, and he knew I don't like crusts because he could feel me not liking them from across the room. And he lied to his boss for me. And then I could feel it eating him up. Even after I left. I could feel it the whole way to the Cullens'. Like a stone in his shoe." She looked down at the tea. "Yeah. He's good to me. I think. I think he's going to be."

Her mom didn't say anything for a while.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, down the hill, the ocean was doing what it always did, the long slow breath of it coming up over First Beach and going out again. Leah had heard it every night of her life. She'd gone to sleep to it since she was a baby. She'd lain awake to it every night since March.

"Your father," Sue said at last, "would have *hated* him."

Leah laughed. It came out of her like a bark. She put her hand over her mouth.

"He would," said her mom. She was smiling too. Her eyes were still wet. "Oh, he would. An Englishman. In a *suit*. With a *gun*. Harry would have sat on the porch with his shotgun across his knees every time he came by. For a year."

Leah stopped laughing.

"Mom," she said.

"What?"

"His name's Harry."

Her mother looked at her.

"His name," said Leah, "is Harry."

For a second Sue Clearwater's face didn't do anything at all. Then she put her coffee cup down on the table, very carefully, the way she put things down when she was trying not to drop them, and put both hands over her face, and Leah couldn't tell for a second if she was laughing or crying. It turned out to be both.

"Of course it is," her mom said through her fingers. "Of course it is. Oh, your father would have hated *that* most of all."

"He'd have made him clean fish."

"He'd have made him clean *every* fish."

"He'd have taken him out on the boat," said Leah, "in February, and pretended the engine was broken, and made him row back."

"He did that to Sam," said Sue.

They both stopped.

It was the first time anybody had said Sam's name in that kitchen in front of Leah in—she didn't know. Months. Her mom had stopped saying it around her sometime last spring, the way you stop saying a word around somebody who's allergic to it. And Seth had stopped. And her aunts. And everybody on the whole reservation, slowly, one by one, until Sam Uley's name had become a sort of hole in every conversation that Leah could feel people walk carefully around.

Her mom had said it by accident. Leah watched her realize. Watched her face go stricken.

"Leah. Honey, I didn't—"

"Mom," said Leah. "It's okay."

Her mom looked at her.

"It's okay," Leah said again. And then she said the thing she hadn't said to anybody. Not to Jacob, not to Seth, not to the Cullens. Not even, out loud, to Harry, though he knew; he'd felt it go. "I can't feel him anymore."

Sue went still.

"Sam," said Leah. "I can't feel him. At all. He's just—gone. There's been this—" she put her hand flat on her chest, on the green flannel, over the place where the rope went in "—this thing. Here. Since I was sixteen. Since before. This thing that went from me to him. And it never went away, even after Emily, even after the wolf, even after I was stuck in his head every day hearing him think about her. It never went away. I tried everything. I ran so far, Mom. You don't even know how far I ran." She swallowed. "And today I looked at a guy in a suit on a rock, and it just—let go. It just slid out. Like a splinter. And it didn't even hurt."

Her mother was crying. Quietly, not making any sound, the tears just running down. She didn't wipe them.

"It doesn't hurt anymore," Leah said. "That's all. That's what happened today. I imprinted on a scary English wizard with a room full of jars, and it doesn't hurt anymore."

Sue got up from her chair.

She came round the table, in her scrubs with the cartoon bandages, and she put her arms around Leah from behind, around her shoulders, and put her face down in Leah's wet hair, and held on. She'd done it every day when Leah was little. She hadn't done it since March. Leah hadn't let her.

Leah let her.

They stayed like that for a long time. The kettle ticked. The ocean breathed. Her mom's tears were warm on the side of her neck.

"Bring him to dinner," Sue said into her hair, finally. "Sunday."

"Mom."

"Sunday. I want to look at him."

"He'll come," said Leah. "He'll come if I ask." She thought about it. "He'll probably bring biscuits."

"Biscuits?"

"He keeps promising people biscuits. I don't know. It's an English thing."

Her mom laughed into her hair.

---

She went to bed at a quarter past ten and didn't sleep.

She'd thought she would. She was so tired. She'd run forty miles today and fought a wizard and been held down on a rock and imprinted and watched a vampire scream and eaten a whole tray of lasagna, and her body felt like it had been put through a wringer and hung out to dry. She lay down in her bed under the window in the little room at the back of the house with the slanted ceiling, the room she'd slept in since she was born, and she pulled the quilt up, and she closed her eyes, and she waited to fall asleep.

She lay there.

The ocean breathed, down the hill.

At the other end of the rope he was moving around the tent. Kitchen. Hall. Kitchen again. Tea, she thought. He drank a lot of tea.

And then, at half past ten, she felt him go tight as a wire.

She sat up.

It was the thing at the bottom of him. She could feel it. It was on its feet, and it was *angry*, angrier than she'd ever felt it, and it was throwing itself against something, the locks, she thought, it was hitting the locks, and she could feel him holding them, she could feel him holding them shut with everything he had and saying something, saying something out loud, she couldn't hear the words but she could feel the shape of them. *Listen to me.* And then he wasn't holding them anymore, he was doing something else, something that took all of his attention, and the thing at the bottom of him stood there behind the locks with its teeth bared and *watched*.

She sat in her bed with her heart pounding and her hand flat on her chest and felt him talk to somebody for nearly an hour.

She couldn't hear any of it. She could feel him, the shape of him, the way he stood. At the beginning he was braced, like a man in a wind. Like a man walking into the principal's office. Then, somewhere in the middle, something changed. Something happened that he hadn't expected. She felt it go through him, a sort of jolt, like missing a step on a staircase in the dark. And after that, slowly, he started to stand differently.

And the thing at the bottom of him, the angry thing behind the locks, put its ears forward.

At twenty past eleven she felt it end. She felt him stand there for a long time afterward, very still, and then she felt the thing at the bottom of him let out a long breath and lie down. It kept its head up. But it lay down.

And she felt him think one word, so clearly that she heard it, actually heard it, in his voice, dry and English and very tired, at the other end of the rope, in the dark.

*Liaison.*

She didn't know what it meant. She knew what the word meant. She didn't know what it meant *now*, for him, for her. She lay back down in her bed and looked at the slanted ceiling and turned it over in her head for a long time.

Then she felt him go to bed.

She felt him lie down, twenty miles away, in the narrow room with the soldier's bed, made tight. She felt him lie there. She felt him close his eyes.

She felt him not sleep.

She knew, because she wasn't sleeping either. She lay in her bed with the ocean breathing down the hill and the rain ticking on the window over her head and the rope running out of her chest into the dark, northeast, up the coast and over the river and up into the big old trees and into a tent the size of a sleeping bag, into a man lying on his back in a narrow bed with his eyes open, staring at the ceiling, and the two of them lay there, twenty miles apart, in the dark, not sleeping.

At eleven-thirty she turned over onto her side.

At midnight she turned onto her back again.

At twelve-forty she got up and went to the bathroom and drank a glass of water standing at the sink in the dark and looked at herself in the mirror, at her own face, with her hair standing up and the dark under her eyes, and she thought, *this is stupid. You've known him nine hours. You've been away from him six. This is so stupid.*

The rope pulled.

Gently. Just a pull, a little one, the way you'd tug on someone's sleeve. Like he'd turned over in his bed twenty miles away and reached out without thinking and found it there and closed his hand on it.

She went back to bed.

At one-fifteen she was lying on her back with her hands on her stomach staring at the ceiling, and she felt him get up.

She felt him sit on the edge of his bed. She felt him sit there for a long time with his elbows on his knees. And she felt the thing at the bottom of him lift its head, and stand, slowly, and come to the bottom of the locks and stand there, and wait, and this time, for the first time all day, it was just standing there. Asking.

And she felt him think about it.

And she felt him decide.

She sat up in bed.

She sat there in the dark in her dad's green flannel shirt with the quilt round her waist and every hair on her arms standing up, and at the other end of the rope, twenty miles away, she felt Harry Potter stand up off his bed, and walk down a long slate hallway, and push open a canvas flap, and step out into the rain.

And she felt him reach down inside himself, to the bottom, to the three locks he'd built in September and checked every morning since, and put his hand on the first one.

And open it.

---

### V. Harry

*The tent — Tuesday, November 21st, 1:15 a.m.*

The ceiling of his room was canvas, the same faded army green as the outside of the tent, stretched taut over the frame and lit very faintly by the glow of the banked fire coming under the door from the library. He'd lain under it most nights for sixty-six days. He knew every seam. There was a water stain shaped like Norway in the corner over the wardrobe, from a leak in October that he'd fixed and not bothered to clean, and a little scorch mark near the middle where he'd tested a Cushioning Charm in bed one night at three in the morning, half asleep, and got the incantation wrong.

He'd been looking at Norway for nearly two hours.

He'd gone to bed at half eleven with every intention of sleeping. He was good at sleeping. He'd learned it on the run in '97, in a different tent, in a different forest, with Ron snoring and Hermione's torch going under her blanket and the whole of Britain hunting them: you lay down, you shut your eyes, you put everything you'd been carrying that day on a shelf in your head, and you went. Whatever happened, whatever you'd done or seen, you went. You'd need it in the morning.

He'd put things on the shelf. The ridge. The circle. The phone call. The river, and the little silver camera in the ferns. Croaker's face in the green lamplight, and the crack of the mouth moving at one corner. *Bring the good ones.* All of it, one thing after another, up on the shelf in a row.

He couldn't get her on the shelf.

That was the trouble. She was at the other end of the line, twenty miles south-west, in a little house above the sea, in a narrow bed under a slanted ceiling. He could feel the slant of it. He could feel the quilt over her. He could feel her lying on her back with her hands on her stomach, staring up at the dark exactly the way he was staring up at Norway, wide awake.

He'd known for an hour she wasn't sleeping either. It was the first thing he'd noticed when he lay down, before he'd even shut his eyes. And every time he'd tried to put it on the shelf, it had climbed down again.

At twenty to one he'd caught himself doing something.

He'd rolled onto his side, toward the wall, toward the south-west, and his hand had come up off the blanket and closed on nothing, in the air in front of his chest. On the line. He hadn't decided to. He'd been half asleep, nearly there, and his hand had gone looking for something in the dark the way your hand goes looking for the other side of the bed, and found the line, and closed on it, and pulled.

He'd felt her feel it. Twenty miles off, standing at a bathroom sink with a glass of water, he'd felt her go still. Then he'd felt her go back to bed.

He'd let go fast. He'd lain there with his face hot, like a boy caught passing notes.

Under his ribs, the thing behind the locks had not lain down since.

It had been lying down when he came to bed. Croaker had done that. It had settled after the mirror went dark, the way a big dog settles when the stranger at the door turns out to be a friend, and it had stayed settled while he brushed his teeth and while he lay down and for a good while after. But it had felt her not sleeping the same as he had, and when his hand closed on the line in the dark, it had lifted its head.

And then it had stood up.

He kept waiting for it to fight. It had fought him all afternoon, on and off, and again at the mirror, hitting the locks so hard he'd felt them groan. Now it stood at the bottom of him behind all three of them with its head up and its ears forward, and it just stood there and looked up at him.

Asking.

He'd never felt it ask before. In all the years since it first turned up, he'd never once felt it ask for anything. It was always him who decided, him who opened the door and him who shut it, and it went where he went and came back when he called and lay down when he told it, and he'd never once wondered whether it minded.

It minded now. It wanted something, very badly, and it was standing at the bottom of him in the dark, and asking.

At a quarter past one he sat up on the edge of the bed.

He sat there with his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging between them and looked at the floor. The slate was cold under his bare feet. He could hear the river below the bench, loud with rain. He could hear Gary, which was a stupid thing to be able to hear, on the canvas by the front flap, moving very slowly in the wet.

"You know what she said," he told it. Quietly. Out loud. "On the bench. *Not today.* I said not today."

It didn't move.

"It *is* today, technically," he said. "It's gone midnight. I'll give you that."

Nothing.

He'd last let it out in August. The second week, a fortnight before he flew out. He'd gone up to the Cairngorms on his own, the way he did every couple of months, to a corrie above Loch Avon where you could go three days without seeing a soul, and he'd opened the locks and let it out and run until the light went. Then he'd put it back. He'd come home and packed and flown out of Heathrow and built the locks in a hotel room near Sea-Tac with the curtains shut, and he'd checked them every morning since, and he hadn't opened them once. Not for a minute. Not when he was alone in the deep woods up the Bogachiel laying stones, with no one for thirty miles. Not even in the tent.

Because there were wolves on this coast. And noses. And he was on a job.

He thought about the noses. He thought about Sam Uley's pack, eight or nine of them, running their lines round the reservation every night; he'd seen their rust dots on the lattice most nights since September, going round and round La Push like the hands of a clock. He thought about what a wolf like that would make of the smell of something like *him*, coming down the river in the middle of the night, onto their land, toward the house of one of their own.

He thought about the treaty. He'd read it, or what Seth's head had of it. *The Cold Ones will not cross onto Quileute land.* It said nothing at all about a wizard. It said nothing at all about whatever he was.

He thought about Croaker. *Get the tribe's leave to stay on their land. Get it. I don't care how. Be polite.* He was fairly sure that turning up as a wolf at one in the morning under a tribal member's bedroom window didn't count as polite.

He thought about all of it, for about a minute, carefully, in order, the way he'd been trained to think about everything.

Then he thought about her lying on her back in the dark, twenty miles away, with her hands on her stomach, not sleeping.

He stood up.

---

He got dressed first. Habit. Jeans, a grey T-shirt, an old black jumper with a hole in the elbow, his boots. Whatever he went in wearing, he came back wearing; that was one of the few things about the whole business he'd ever been able to rely on, and he'd learned the hard way, in a Lapland snowdrift in 2004, to go in wearing something that would keep the cold off when he came back.

He went down the slate hall. Past the library, with the fire banked low in the grate and the one photograph on the mantel, the one of his mum and dad waving at him from a garden in Godric's Hollow, which he didn't look at, because he never did, at night. Past the kitchen. Up the three wooden steps. He put his hand on the canvas flap and pushed it open and stepped out into the rain.

It was black under the spruces. Proper black, the kind you only got in old forest on a moonless night with the cloud right down on the treetops. The rain was coming straight down, steady, soft, a hiss in the needles overhead like a radio between stations. The river roared in the gorge. The air smelled of wet earth and resin and cold stone.

Gary was on the canvas by the flap.

Harry looked down at him.

"Don't," he said.

Gary didn't.

He walked out into the middle of the bench, away from the tent, away from the guy lines, into a clear space between the trunks where the moss was thick and soft and the ground was level. He stood there with the rain coming down on his head and soaking through the jumper. He shut his eyes.

He went down.

Down through himself, the way he'd gone down the line to Leah, except inward. Past the surface, past the Occlumency shields with their decoy layer, the Islington photographer still dutifully worrying about his light meter. Down through the cold stone weight of the oath under his collarbone. Down past the war, which was always there, a long dark room he didn't go into. Down to the bottom.

The three locks were there. Where he'd put them. Big and black and iron-cold, braided into the floor of him like roots into rock, and behind them the thing in the dark was standing, waiting, with its eyes on him.

He put his hand on the first lock.

He felt it look at his hand.

"Right," Harry said. "Just so we're clear. We're going to see her. We're going to sit outside. We're not going in the house, we're not going near her mum, we're not going to start a fight with the neighbours. If anybody else turns up, we're leaving. Understood?"

It looked at him.

"I'll take that as a yes."

He opened the first lock.

It went the way locks go when they've been shut for three months: hard, then all at once. He felt it give. He felt the thing behind it lean forward into the gap, not pushing, just leaning, the way a dog leans into a door it can feel starting to open.

He opened the second.

That one was harder. It had taken more of the strain, all afternoon; it had been hit a lot. It fought him for a second, the iron groaning, and then went.

The thing in the dark was right there now. Right up against the last lock. He could feel the heat of it. He could feel its breath. He could feel it waiting, very still, very patient, with every hair on it standing up and its eyes on him, and he could feel, under the waiting, something he'd never felt from it before in all the years since it had first turned up.

It was happy.

It was so happy it was shaking.

"Yeah," Harry said quietly. "Yeah. All right. I know."

He opened the third lock.

---

The Quileute did it differently.

He'd seen them do it all day, in other people's heads: the boy in the bee yard, coming apart like a sheet torn down the middle; the grey wolf behind the spruce, the canvas-ripping sound. Theirs was violent. Theirs was a bursting.

His had always been a fold.

That was the closest he'd ever got to describing it, in eight years of trying, in a notebook he kept in a locked drawer in the workshop that nobody would ever read. The world folded. He stood in it, and the world folded round him like a page turning, a soft quick doubling-over of everything, and when it opened out again he was standing in the same place. Only he was somewhere else as well. Somewhere much bigger.

The page turned.

It opened out again.

And the forest *came in*.

That was always the first thing. Everything, all at once, the whole of it pouring in through his nose and his ears like water through a broken dam. The smell of the spruces, every single one of them, each one different, the resin and the bark and the needles and the lichen on the north side. The river, not just loud now but *full*, a hundred separate sounds, gravel turning on the bed, a log knocking against a rock forty yards downstream, an eddy sucking at the bank. Elk, a herd of eleven, bedded down a mile to the east, he could tell the bull from the cows by smell. A barred owl on a branch above the tent with a vole in its feet. The cold-stone smell of the gorge. And under all of it, faint, so faint he'd never have caught it on two feet, a thread of something coming up the river valley from the sea on the wind. Salt. Kelp. Woodsmoke from a stove.

And her.

He couldn't have smelled her from here. It was twenty miles. He knew he couldn't. But the line ran out of his chest down that valley toward the sea, and the smell came up it to him anyway, like something carried down a wire. Cedar and rain and woodsmoke and salt and green flannel and chamomile and honey and *her*.

He stood in the clearing among the spruces and breathed in.

He was very tall.

He'd always been tall, like this. Taller than any wolf he'd ever heard of. His head, when he lifted it, was up among the lowest branches of the spruces, and the rain was falling on his back from a lot less far than it had been falling on his head a moment ago. He'd seen the big ginger one through the Omnioculars in October, riding herd round the white house with the child on his back, and he'd thought then, idly, the way you'd think about the height of a building, that he'd look the boy in the eye if they ever stood side by side. He knew now he'd been wrong. He'd be looking down. Not far. A head, about. But down.

He was black. All of him, nose to tail, a black so deep it soaked up what little light there was under the trees and gave nothing back, with a dense charcoal undercoat beneath it that kept the rain off his skin entirely. His clothes were gone; they always went, folded away somewhere in the turn, and they'd be back exactly as he'd left them when he turned again. His paws were the size of dinner plates and sank into the moss without a sound.

And he was himself. That was the other thing about the fold, the thing he'd spent years in the locked notebook trying and failing to describe. He was all still here, all of him, every bit of Harry Potter, the Department and the war and the eggs in the bacon fat and Croaker's pen. He was just also *it*. The two of them in the same skin, side by side, the way two people sit side by side in a car. And they were both looking south-west, down the valley, at the same thing.

He looked down.

Gary was on the canvas by the flap, a long way down now, a small yellow comma in the dark.

He'd have sworn the slug was looking at him.

"Not a word," Harry said.

It came out of him clear and low and English, with a rumble under it that went down into the ground. He'd always been able to talk in this shape; he'd never worked out why, and in the end he'd stopped asking. The words came out the way they always had, only from further up.

Gary said nothing.

Harry turned, and went.

---

He went down the Calawah first, because the line went that way, and because the river would take him most of the way to the sea.

He didn't run, at first. He walked. He'd forgotten what it was like, after three months, and the first mile was all remembering. The weight of himself, how much of it there was, how it moved. The way the ground felt through his pads, every root and stone. The way he could put a paw down on a dead branch and lean his weight onto it so slowly and evenly that it didn't snap. The way the hind paws found the prints of the forepaws in the dark without his looking, so that a tracker coming along behind would think one wolf had walked here, and a small one.

He didn't make a sound. He never had, like this. It was the first thing he'd noticed, years ago, the first time: that something this big could go through a forest and the forest wouldn't know. The owl over the tent hadn't stirred when he passed under it.

At the end of the first mile, where the north fork came down into the main river under the big broken-heart boulder in the middle of the stream, he stopped and stood on the bank and looked down the valley, south-west, toward the sea.

The line pulled.

The thing he shared his skin with leaned into it, and he let it, and they ran.

---

It was the best twenty miles of his life. He'd remember that later, a long time later, when he had a great many other good miles to put next to it, and he'd never once change his mind.

Down the Calawah valley in the dark, along the river, through the old trees and then the second-growth and then the clear-cuts, gone grey and silver in the rain, stumps standing up out of the ground like gravestones. Over the road where it crossed the river at a bridge, in one stride, without touching it, while a logging truck went by underneath with its headlights on and the driver never knew. Down to where the Calawah met the Sol Duc and the Bogachiel, the three rivers coming together into one, and the Quillayute going on alone toward the sea, short and wide and slow. Along the Quillayute's bank, through the alders, with the smell of salt getting stronger and stronger in the wind until it was the only thing.

He ran the way he hadn't run since August. Since before August. Since, maybe, ever. Because always before, on the hill above Loch Avon, on the tundra in Lapland, he'd run because the thing he shared his skin with needed running, the way a dog needs walking. He'd let it out and let it go and followed along behind, and it had run to burn itself out, and come home tired, and he'd put it back.

This time it ran *toward* something. He'd never felt that from it. It ran the whole twenty miles with its nose in the wind and the line taut in its chest, and it didn't tire, and it didn't slow, and it was so happy he could feel it in his own back teeth.

At the edge of the reservation he slowed.

He stopped in the alders above the river, two miles from the sea, and stood very still, and listened.

There were wolves out.

He'd known there would be. He could smell them before he heard them: that hot, wild, cedar-and-earth smell, the same as hers and different, the way two people's soap is the same brand and different. Two of them. Running a line along the eastern edge of the reservation, a mile north of him, moving south. Fast. Bored. He could tell from their gait they were bored. One of them was big and thick and smelled faintly of motor oil and the other was lighter and kept stopping to sniff things.

He didn't know their names. Sam's lot. The other pack.

He went downwind of them. He didn't need to think about how. The wind was coming off the sea, straight up the river, and he moved east along the bank and then north in a long loop through the alders until it was blowing from them to him and not the other way, and then he stood in a stand of young cedars twenty yards from where their line ran, flattened himself to the ground, and let them pass.

They came by at a lope. Two big wolves, a dark grey and a brown, the brown one a fair bit bigger, nose down, ears back against the rain. They went right past him. The dark grey one stopped, twenty yards off, and lifted its head and looked into the cedars where he was lying, for a long second, with its nostrils going.

He didn't breathe.

It didn't see him. He knew it wouldn't. He'd been doing this since he was eleven, one way or another, under one cloak or another; after a while the cloak was optional. What you needed was to be very still and very sure and think about nothing at all. After a moment the grey one put its nose back down and loped on after the brown, and the two of them went on south along their line toward the river and the road, and were gone.

He waited a full minute. Then he got up out of the cedars, and shook the rain off, and went on.

---

The house was at the top of the village, above First Beach, where the road bent round and the houses gave out and the trees started. A small house. Two storeys, sort of: a ground floor and a slanted upper room tucked under the roof, the way they built them here. Grey siding, a porch on the front with a swing on it, a yard round the back that ran up to the edge of the woods. Flowerbeds along the side, dug over and put to bed for the winter. A red Ford in the drive with a dent in the back door. A light on over the porch.

And one window, at the back, under the slant of the roof, with the curtain drawn.

He came up through the trees behind the house and stopped at the very edge of them, where the woods met the yard. Ten yards from the back door. He could hear the house breathing: a refrigerator humming, a clock ticking, a woman asleep in a room at the front of the ground floor, her breathing slow and deep. Sue. He knew her name from the boy's head. And upstairs, at the back, under the slanted roof, in the room behind that window—

Awake. Lying on her back. Hands on her stomach. Exactly the way she'd been an hour ago, two hours ago. Listening to the sea.

The line between them was so short now he could have reached out and touched it.

He sat down.

He sat down at the edge of the trees, in the rain, in the dark, on his haunches, the way a dog sits on a step, with his forepaws together and his tail curled round them and his head up. And he looked up at the window under the slanted roof.

He didn't do anything else. He didn't make a sound. He didn't call down the line, or push, or pull. He'd told it, back at the tent: *we're going to sit outside.* So they sat outside. The rain came down on his head and ran off his black coat and dripped from the end of his nose, and the sea breathed down the hill, and the clock ticked in the dark house, and he sat at the edge of the trees and looked up at her window, and the thing he shared his skin with sat there with him and looked too, and was entirely, completely content.

It was the most patient he'd ever known it.

He'd have sat there till morning. He'd have sat there a week.

He'd been sitting there about four minutes when he felt her feel it.

It went through the line like a plucked string. He felt her go still. Felt her stop breathing, up there, in the dark, under the slanted roof. Felt her lift her head off the pillow. Felt her know, all at once, without any doubt at all, the way you know your own name, exactly where he was. Exactly how far. Exactly which way.

He didn't move.

Up in the window under the roof, behind the glass, the curtain moved.

### VI. Leah

*The Clearwater house — 1:41 a.m.*

She knew before she got to the window.

She'd known since the moment he'd opened the third lock, twenty miles away, in the rain, standing in a clearing among the spruces. She'd felt it go. She'd felt something enormous stand up at the bottom of him and step out into the world, and the world fold round it, and then she'd felt the two of them, him and it, side by side, turn and look south-west down the river valley toward the sea.

Toward her.

And then she'd lain in her bed for twenty-five minutes with her heart going like a drum and felt them come.

She'd felt every mile. The river, the bridge, the alders. The two wolves on the eastern line, Paul and Jared, she knew them by the feel of them through the rope, the way he'd smelled them, motor oil and boredom; and she'd felt him go flat in the cedars and stop breathing while they passed, and she'd stopped breathing too, up here in her bed, as if they could have heard her. She'd felt him come up the hill through the trees behind the house. She'd felt him stop.

She'd felt him sit down.

That was what had done it. Him sitting down. She'd felt him do it, at the edge of the trees, ten yards from the back door, and settle, and look up at her window, and just *stay* there, and she'd felt the thing he shared his skin with stay there with him, and how happy it was to be doing it. How it would have sat there in the rain all night. All week.

She'd lain there for four minutes, feeling that.

Then she'd sat up, and swung her legs out of bed, and gone to the window, and stood with her fingers on the edge of the curtain for one long second, and pulled it back.

The yard was dark. The rain was coming down soft and steady, silver where the porch light caught it. The flowerbeds. The woodshed. The old swing set her dad had put up when Seth was four, rusting, that nobody had the heart to take down. The black line of the trees at the top of the yard.

And in the trees, two green eyes.

They were looking up at her. Green as bottle glass in the dark, catching what little light came off the porch and throwing it back, and they were *high*. That was the first thing her brain did with them, before anything else: it measured. They were too high. They were higher than the top of the woodshed. They were higher than Jacob's eyes would have been, sitting there, and Jacob was the biggest wolf on the coast. She'd sat next to Jacob in the dark plenty of times. She knew where his eyes came to.

These came to about a head above that.

Then the shape came clear round the eyes, the way a shape comes clear when you stop looking for it. Black on black. A wolf, sitting at the edge of the trees like a dog on a step, with its forepaws together and its tail curled round them, so dark it was nothing but a hole in the night with two green lights in it. Huge. Bigger than anything she'd ever seen on four legs. Bigger than Sam, bigger than Jake, bigger than the black bear she and Seth had run off the dump in July. It sat there perfectly still in the rain, and the rain ran off it, and it looked up at her window.

At her.

It didn't move. It didn't do anything at all. It just looked.

She stood at the window in her dad's flannel shirt with her hand on the curtain and looked back.

---

She went down the stairs in the dark without turning on a light. She knew every step. She knew which ones creaked: the third from the top, the one at the turn. She stepped over them. She went past her mom's door, where her mom was asleep, finally, breathing slow and deep, and through the kitchen, and opened the back door as quietly as she'd ever opened anything.

The rain was cold. The porch boards were cold under her bare feet.

She came down the steps and onto the grass and across the yard, past the swing set, past the woodshed. She didn't hurry. She didn't know why she didn't hurry; every single part of her wanted to. But she walked, slowly, the way you'd walk up to a horse, and the rain soaked through the flannel on her shoulders, and the eyes watched her come the whole way.

She stopped about ten feet from the trees.

He was even bigger up close. She had to tip her head back. His chest was level with her face. His head, up there in the dark, was up among the lowest branches of the big cedar behind him, and the rain was dripping off the branches onto his ears. His coat was black, black like the inside of a closed eye, and thick, so thick the rain just sat on it in beads and rolled off. He smelled—

She breathed in, and he filled up her head again, the way he had on the ridge, except there was nothing in the way of it now. No coffee, no cheeseburger, no pie. No suit. Pine and wet earth and cold stone, the inside-of-a-cave smell, and under it, everywhere, all through it, so strong now it made her eyes sting: *wolf*. Old and wild and huge. The smell of the place in the woods where a big animal had slept. The animal was right here.

Her own wolf, inside her, came up onto its feet so fast it made her sway.

The green eyes looked down at her.

"Hello, Lassie," said the wolf.

Leah jumped about a foot.

It came out of his mouth. Out of the wolf's mouth. A man's voice, low and dry and English, with a rumble under it that went down into the ground and came up through the soles of her bare feet. His voice. Exactly his voice. Coming out of a mouth full of teeth the length of her fingers.

"You can *talk*?"

"Always could. Like this." The ears tipped, a little, like a shrug. "Never worked out why. I stopped asking."

"We can't talk."

"I know."

"None of us. Not one. Not in a thousand years. We have to phase back to talk."

"I know," said the wolf. "I'm sorry."

"That's so *unfair*."

"Yeah," he agreed. "It probably is."

She stood there in the rain and stared up at him.

"You said not today," she said. "On the bench. You said not today."

"It's gone midnight," said Harry. "Technically, it's tomorrow."

She laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her short and loud and she slapped her hand over her mouth and looked back at the house, at her mom's dark window, and held her breath. Nothing moved. The house went on breathing. She turned back.

He hadn't moved. He was just looking at her. And through the rope she could feel him, both of him, the man and the wolf side by side in that huge black skin. The man was a little embarrassed. The man was thinking, very clearly, *this was a stupid idea, it's two in the morning, her mum's asleep, what are you doing, Potter.*

The wolf wasn't embarrassed at all.

The wolf was looking at her the way the sun looks at the ground.

"I couldn't sleep," Harry said. Quietly. "Neither could you. I could feel you not sleeping. For two hours. And this one—" his ears went back, a fraction, toward himself, toward the thing he shared his skin with "—this one asked. Nicely. First time it's ever asked me for anything. I thought it'd be rude to say no."

"It *asked*?"

"Stood there at the bottom of me with its ears up and asked." The big head tilted. "Didn't know it could. Learning a lot about it today."

Inside her, her own wolf was pacing. Up and down. Up and down. Pressing against the inside of her ribs like a dog at a gate.

"Mine's going crazy," she said.

"Yeah. I can feel it."

"It wants—"

"I know what it wants," said Harry. "Mine wants the same thing." The green eyes didn't leave her. "It's up to you."

She looked back at the house. At the dark window. At her mom asleep behind it.

She looked up at him.

"Turn around," she said.

He turned his head away. All of it, the whole great black head, swung round and looked off into the trees, and he sat there in the rain with his face to the woods like a dog told to stay.

Leah unbuttoned her dad's flannel shirt.

She took it off and folded it, standing there in the wet grass, carefully, the way her mom folded things. Sleeves in, then halves. She walked back to the porch and put it on the top step under the overhang where the rain wouldn't get it. Then she took off her sweatpants and folded those and put them on top. She stood there on the bottom step for a second in the cold, in the dark, with the rain running down her back.

Then she stepped off the porch, and let go.

---

She'd always hated phasing.

From the first night. She'd hated the way it took her, the way it tore her open, the way it was a thing that *happened* to her, every single time, like being hit by a car from inside. She'd hated the pain of it. She'd hated, most of all, the moment after, when she came down on four feet and fell straight into the pack mind, into that loud crowded room with everybody in it, and couldn't get out.

It didn't hurt this time.

It went through her like it always did, the burst, the tearing, the canvas-ripping sound. But it didn't hurt. Or it did, and she didn't notice, because her wolf was coming up out of her so fast and so glad that there wasn't room in her for anything else, and she came down onto four feet on the wet grass of her mother's yard and the whole night poured in.

And the pack mind was empty.

She'd braced for it. She'd been braced for it every time she phased, for seven months. The room. The crowd. She came down into it and it was dark and quiet and nobody was there. Jacob asleep on the Cullens' couch. Seth asleep in the bee house on Calawah Way with his arm over Layla. Nobody else. Just her, and the dark, and the little grey room in the middle of her with his secrets in it.

And the rope.

The rope was louder like this. Much louder. In her own skin it had been a line, a pull, a hum. In this one it was a *cable*, thick and bright and singing, running out of her chest across ten yards of wet yard into the huge black shape at the edge of the trees.

Who had turned his head back round.

He was looking at her. He'd heard her phase; of course he had. The big black head had come back round from the woods, and the green eyes were on her, and they'd gone wide.

He'd seen her like this once before. In front of the tent, at half past six, in the dark. But he'd been on two feet then, and she'd been bigger than him. Now she was looking up at him again, from a shoulder level with his own, and the wolf in her didn't care, didn't care at all, because the wolf in her had just taken one long breath of him through a nose that was a hundred times better than the one she'd had a second ago, and gone completely to pieces.

She was moving before she'd decided to.

Her wolf crossed the yard at a trot, tail up, ears up, quick and light and bold, like she'd known him all her life, like he was hers and she was going to go and get him. Leah, inside it, had about one second to think *oh God, what are you doing*, and then she was there, in front of him, under him, and she put her nose up and touched it to his.

The world went white.

The cables and the gravity had already happened, on the ridge. This was something else, something that came up through the nose and went straight down to the bottom of her, *him*, the whole of him, the smell and the heat and the size, and she felt the same thing go through him at exactly the same time and come back down the rope at her, so that for a second it was going round and round between them like a bell ringing.

He made a sound.

It came up out of his chest, low, a rumble, so deep she felt it in her own ribs more than heard it. It went on and on. She'd heard a lot of growls in seven months, and this was something else, and she didn't know what. She didn't think he did either; she felt him, the man, inside the wolf, go *what—* and then stop, because he couldn't do anything about it.

And then his wolf bent its head and put its nose to her neck.

She stood absolutely still. She let it. She felt the huge hot breath of him go into her fur, behind her ear, down her throat, along her shoulder. Breathing her. Learning her. Taking its time. She felt his nose push into the ruff of her neck and stay there, and the rumble going on, and the whole enormous black weight of him leaning, very slightly, very carefully, toward her, over her, like a tree leaning over a river.

And her wolf, which had never once in its life put its head down for anybody, put its head down.

She put her head down and turned it and pushed it under his jaw, into the thick black fur of his throat, and rubbed her cheek along it, hard, all the way, from one side of his jaw to the other.

*Oh my God,* Leah thought, from very far away inside it. *Oh my God, what am I* doing.

"I've got no idea," said Harry. His voice had gone rough. "I was going to ask you."

*It's doing it. It's just doing it. I'm not—*

"Mine's not asking me either." The rumble hadn't stopped. "I'm just sort of along for the ride."

*Make it stop.*

"Do you want it to?"

She thought about it. She stood there with her face pushed into the fur of his throat and his nose in the ruff of her neck and the rain coming down on both of them and the rope between them singing like a power line, and she thought about it, honestly, all the way down, because she couldn't think about anything any other way with him.

*No,* she thought.

"No," he agreed. "Me neither."

Her wolf took its face out of his throat, and stepped back, and dropped its front half down onto the wet grass with its back half up in the air and its tail going like a flag.

Leah, inside it, nearly died.

She knew what that was. Every kid on the reservation knew what that was. Every dog on the reservation did it. It was a play bow. Her wolf, her wolf, the gray she-wolf nobody had ever heard of in a thousand years, the one who'd been running alone in the dark since March, who'd fought newborns and torn vampires apart and never once in its life played with anybody, not even Seth, was down on its elbows in the wet grass of her mother's backyard at two in the morning with its butt in the air, wagging, asking a wolf the size of a house to come and play.

*I'm going to kill myself,* Leah thought.

Harry laughed.

It came out of the wolf's chest like a bark, a short huge *huff* that shook the rain off the cedar branches over his head, and the green eyes went bright, and his own tail, she saw it, came off the ground behind him and swept once, twice, through the wet ferns.

And then he dropped down too.

Front end down, back end up, his forelegs out flat on the grass in front of him like two black logs, his huge head down level with hers, tail up and going. A wolf a head taller than Jacob Black, play-bowing at her in the rain at the edge of her mom's yard.

They looked at each other like that for one second, nose to nose, both of them down on their elbows with their tails in the air.

*This is the stupidest thing that's ever happened to me,* Leah thought.

"Same," said Harry.

And her wolf jumped on him.

---

They didn't stay in the yard. They couldn't; they'd have woken the whole village. Her wolf knocked him over about four seconds into it, or he let it, she couldn't tell, and the two of them rolled over once in the wet ferns at the edge of the trees, a tangle of black and grey, and fetched up against the trunk of the big cedar hard enough to bring a shower of water down out of its branches onto both of them. And when they came up out of it, shaking the water off, the porch light was on.

They froze.

Leah looked again. The porch light had been on all along. This was the kitchen light. Behind the window over the sink. Somebody had turned on the kitchen light.

Through the window, she could see her mom.

Sue Clearwater was standing at the sink in her bathrobe, holding a glass of water she'd forgotten to drink, looking out the window into the dark at the top of the yard, toward the trees. Toward the two of them, a grey wolf and a black one, standing very still under the cedar with water dripping off them.

She stood there for a long moment.

Then she put the glass down on the counter. She reached up and pulled the curtain over the window shut. And after a second, the kitchen light went off.

Leah stood under the cedar and stared at the dark window.

"Was that your mum?" said Harry, very quietly.

*Yeah.*

"Did she just—"

*Yeah.*

"Right," said Harry, after a moment. "Right. Okay." He breathed out. "We should probably go somewhere else."

*Yeah,* said Leah. *I know a place.*

---

She took him to the cave.

She'd never taken anybody to the cave. She'd found it in July, alone, at the bottom of the worst week of the summer, when she'd been running every night until she couldn't stand up just to get away from the pack mind, from Sam's head, from everybody. She'd been running the cliffs south of the village, along the top of the headlands, Teahwhit Head, with the sea booming in the dark three hundred feet below, and she'd seen a ledge.

It ran along the face of the cliff, on the seaward side, about two-thirds of the way down. Narrow. Too narrow for a person, almost, and covered in bird droppings, and no way to get onto it from the top except a jump that would have killed anybody who wasn't her. She'd made the jump. The ledge ran along the cliff face for a hundred yards and then turned in, into the rock, and went back. And at the back, out of the wind, out of the rain, out of reach of anything that couldn't fly, there was a cave.

It was dry. That was the miracle of it. Everything on this coast was wet, everything, all the time, and the cave was dry. The floor was sand, fine and pale and blown in from somewhere. The roof was high enough that she could stand in it as a wolf with room over her ears. And the mouth of it looked straight out over the Pacific.

She'd slept there twice in July and four times in August. She'd never told Seth. She'd been very, very careful never to think about it in the pack mind, which was the hardest thing she'd ever done. It was hers. It was the only thing in the world that was only hers.

She led him to it along the top of the headland in the rain, at a run, through the salal and the twisted shore pines, with the sea getting louder and louder below them in the dark. She made the jump without slowing down, the way she always did. And she landed on the ledge and turned round, and her heart was going so hard it hurt, and she thought, *he's too big. He's too big, he won't fit, he can't make that, he'll—*

He came over the edge of the cliff behind her and stepped off.

Into the air, three hundred feet over the sea, and walked down it. The way he'd walked over the crater on the ridge. Like there were stairs there that only he could see. Four paws on nothing, coming down through the rain and the dark and the wind in no hurry at all, and he stepped off the last of the nothing onto the ledge beside her, light as a cat, and looked at her.

*Show-off,* Leah thought.

"Yeah," said Harry. "A bit."

She led him in.

It was just like she remembered. The ledge turning in out of the wind. The rock closing over their heads. The sound of the rain stopping, all at once, and the boom of the surf going hollow and huge in the stone around them. The sand under her pads, dry and soft and cold. She went all the way to the back, where it was darkest and driest, and turned round, and looked at him.

He'd stopped just inside the mouth. He was standing there with his head up, under the high rock roof, and he was looking out.

She came and stood beside him, and looked too.

The sea was right there. Right below them, three hundred feet down, and all the way out to the edge of the world. It was black and grey and moving, a long slow heave of water under the rain, and every few seconds a big swell came in out of the dark and hit the base of the cliff below them and went up white, and the boom of it came up through the rock into their feet. Out in the surf, the sea stacks stood up out of the water like broken teeth, the Quillayute Needles, with trees growing on the tops of them bent sideways by a hundred years of wind. Off to the north, past the point, a single light. James Island. The marina. The village, asleep.

And over it all, the rain, coming in off the sea in long grey curtains, and behind the rain the cloud, low and heavy and endless.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

*It's mine,* Leah thought. *Nobody knows about it. Not even Seth.*

"I know," said Harry. Quietly. "I can feel how much." He turned his great head and looked down at her. "Thank you."

She didn't answer that. She didn't know how.

She went back to the place at the back of the cave where the sand was deepest. She turned round three times, the way her wolf liked to, and lay down, with her nose on her forepaws, facing the sea.

He came after her. Slowly. He stood over her for a second, looking down, and she felt him think about it, the man, carefully, the way he thought about everything. Where to lie. How close. Whether she'd mind.

*Oh my God,* she thought, *just lie down.*

He lay down.

He lay down behind her, between her and the cold back wall of the cave, and curled round her. All of him. His great black body round her back, his chest against her spine, his long legs round her legs, his tail coming round over both of their paws. And his head, last, slowly, came down over her neck and settled there, heavy and hot, his jaw resting across the top of her shoulders, so that she was lying in a ring of black wolf with only her face sticking out, looking at the sea.

The heat of him was unbelievable. She ran at a hundred and eight degrees, all the time, and she'd never once in seven months been near anything that could out-burn her. He was hotter than she was. She lay inside the ring of him and the heat came into her from every side, through her fur, into her bones, and something in her that had been cold and clenched since March, something she'd stopped even noticing, let go.

The rumble started again in his chest. She felt it against her back. Low and slow and steady, like an engine idling.

Her own chest answered it. She didn't tell it to.

They lay like that for a long time, listening to the sea.

*Hey,* she thought, after a while.

"Hm."

*What's a liaison?*

She felt him go still, a little, behind her. Then she felt him smile. She didn't know how she could feel a wolf smile from the inside, but she did.

"You heard that."

*I heard you think it. When you got off the phone.*

"Croaker," said Harry. "My boss. I rang him back. Told him I'd lied to him. Told him everything. Well. Nearly." The rumble went on under the words. "Told him I was compromised. Told him to send somebody else, because if it ever came down to the Department or you, it's you. Every time."

Leah lay very still.

"He wasn't surprised," said Harry. "He was a bit bored, honestly. He said everybody picks somebody over the Department eventually. He said the ones who don't are the ones he worries about." He was quiet for a second. "Then he made me the Department's liaison to your tribe. And to the Cullens. Long-term. He said there's so much going on here I was never coming home anyway." The rumble deepened. "So I'm staying. That's what liaison means. It means I'm staying."

*How long?*

"Long," said Harry. "As long as it takes. Longer."

Behind her, against her legs, she felt his tail move. Once. Twice. A slow heavy thump on the sand.

Her wolf, curled up inside the ring of him, felt it too. Her own tail moved. She couldn't stop it.

*Hey,* she thought again, after a while.

"Hm."

*My dad's name was Harry.*

She felt that go into him. She felt it land, and sit there, and she felt him not say anything for a long time.

"I know," he said finally. Very quietly. "I saw it in your brother's head this afternoon. On the ridge. When I went through it looking for the witch." A pause. "Harry Clearwater. Heart attack. In March. The night you changed." Another pause. "I didn't know what to do with it. I still don't."

*My mom laughed,* Leah thought. *When I told her. She laughed and she cried at the same time. She said he'd have hated that most of all.*

"Yeah," said Harry. "I'd imagine he would." The rumble had gone very soft. "I'd have liked to meet him. I think. Even if he'd have hated me."

*He'd have made you clean every fish.*

"I can clean a fish."

*He'd have made you row back from the river mouth in February.*

"I'd have rowed," said Harry.

She believed him. She lay in the ring of him with the sea booming below and the rain coming in grey off the Pacific and she believed him all the way down, because she couldn't not, and because it was true. He'd have rowed. He'd have rowed all the way back from the river mouth in the February sleet with her dad sitting in the stern grinning at him with his arms folded, and he'd never once have complained.

She closed her eyes.

*Don't go anywhere,* she thought. She didn't mean to. It just came out.

"I'm not," said Harry. "I told you. Liaison."

She felt his jaw settle heavier across her shoulders. She felt the rumble slow. She felt him, the man and the wolf both, at the other end of a rope that was no longer twenty miles long or ten yards long but no length at all, start, very slowly, to let go of the day.

She went first.

She'd remember that, later. That she was the one who fell asleep first. It hadn't happened in seven months. In seven months she'd never once fallen asleep before the sky started going grey, and when she did, she'd dreamed about Sam. Every night. Sam on the porch at Makah with Emily. Sam's back, walking away down the dirt road behind her mom's house.

She fell asleep in a dry cave above the sea at a quarter to three in the morning inside a ring of black wolf, with the rain coming down outside and the surf booming three hundred feet below and the slow heavy rumble going on and on against her back.

She didn't dream about Sam.

She didn't dream about anything at all.

### VII. Seth

*Calawah Way — 6:40 a.m.*

The bees woke him.

They did that now, most mornings. He'd been sleeping at Layla's maybe four nights a week for two weeks, on the big brown couch in the front room the first few nights and then upstairs, and every morning, at about the time the sky started going gray over the trees, the hives along the back fence would start up. A low hum, quiet, coming through the wall, through the floor, through the mattress, the way the ocean came through the walls at home. Layla said they were saying good morning. Layla said her grandmother used to say they were checking the boundary, counting everybody in.

He lay there for a minute with his eyes shut and listened to them count.

Layla was asleep beside him, on her stomach, with her face mashed into the pillow and her hair everywhere and one arm flung across his chest. The bruise on her cheek had gone dark purple in the night. Carlisle had looked at it, and at her back, and said nothing was broken, and she'd be stiff for a week, and given her something for the pain that she'd said was a Muggle drug and she didn't trust it and taken it anyway. She'd fallen asleep at midnight in the middle of a sentence about bone-snakes. She hadn't moved since.

He slid out from under her arm very carefully. She made a noise into the pillow and didn't wake.

He went downstairs in his shorts, through the kitchen, which still smelled of honey from the jars she hadn't finished yesterday, the ones still standing on the counter with their lids off, and out the back door into the yard.

It was barely light. Gray and cold and wet, the mist sitting in the tops of the trees and the grass soaked. The hives hummed. Three or four bees came out of the nearest one as he went past and landed on his shoulder, the way they always did now, and walked around for a second, and went back in. Counting him.

He was on the dawn loop. Jacob had said so last night, before everybody went to bed, sitting on the Cullens' couch with his head in his hands. *Seth, you take dawn. I can't. I'm going to sleep for a hundred years.* Seth didn't mind dawn. Dawn was his favorite. Nobody else was ever phased at dawn and the woods were quiet and you could run and think about whatever you wanted.

He went behind the third hive, where he always went, and untied the spare shorts from his ankle, and tied the ones he was wearing on in their place, and phased.

And fell straight into the sea.

That was what it felt like. He came down onto four feet in the wet grass of Layla's backyard and the pack mind opened up around him, and it was full of ocean. Gray light, gray water, a huge heaving gray sea going all the way out to the edge of the world under a gray sky, and rain coming in off it in long curtains, and the boom of surf so loud and so close he flinched. Sand under his—not his. Somebody's. Sand, dry and cold. And rock overhead.

Leah.

She was phased. She was lying down somewhere, somewhere high, in a cave, looking out at the sea. She'd just woken up. He could feel her waking, slow and heavy and easy, the way you wake on a Saturday with nowhere to be. He'd never felt her wake up like that. He'd felt her wake up every morning for seven months and it was always the same: a jolt, a lurch, like somebody falling off a step, and then *Sam*, right away, first thing, before she'd even opened her eyes.

She didn't think about Sam.

She didn't think about anything. She lay there looking at the sea, heavy and slow and loose, and the first thing in her head, the very first thing, was a sound. A rumble. Low and deep, right up against her back, going on and on like an engine idling.

And then he saw it.

Over her. Across her. On the sand in front of her face, where she was looking, there was a leg. A foreleg. Black. It was lying across in front of her, between her and the mouth of the cave, like a log somebody had put down there, and it was huge. The paw at the end of it was bigger than her whole head. The black fur was so thick and so dark it looked like a hole cut out of the morning. And above her, across the top of her shoulders, heavy, there was a weight. A jaw. Resting there. Somebody's jaw, enormous, lying across the back of his sister's neck like it belonged there.

And the rumble, against her back.

And the smell. *Pine. Wet earth. Cold stone.* And wolf, wolf, *wolf*, so thick and old and wild it filled the whole pack mind up like smoke.

Seth stood in the middle of Layla's backyard with his legs locked and his ears flat and every hair on his body standing straight up.

*LEE?*

He felt her hear him. He felt her go *oh,* the way you go *oh* when you've forgotten to lock the bathroom door.

*Morning, Seth,* she thought.

*LEE. LEE. WHAT. WHERE ARE YOU. WHAT IS THAT. IS THAT*—

*Yeah.*

*IS THAT* HIM*?*

*Yeah.*

*THAT'S HIM? THAT'S—Lee, that's—he's a—you're—are you in a CAVE?*

*Yeah.*

*You have a CAVE?*

*Had,* Leah thought. *Had a cave. It was a secret cave. Now you know about it. Thanks, Seth.*

*How long have you had a cave?*

*Since July.*

*Since JULY? I've been in your head every day since—how did you—*

*Practice.*

In the cave, behind her, the rumble changed. The weight across her shoulders shifted. Seth felt it through her: the huge jaw lifting, a little, off the back of her neck. Felt a breath go out over her ear, hot, slow, that she'd felt every few seconds all night and he'd felt not at all, and it made the hair stand up on his back from fifteen miles away.

And then a voice.

It came through her ears into the pack mind, so he heard it the way she heard it, from about a foot away, low and dry and very English and right next to her head.

"Is that Balto?"

Seth's back legs went out from under him.

He sat down hard in the wet grass of Layla's yard like somebody had kicked his knees, and the bees came out of all six hives at once and hung in the air around him in a cloud, humming.

*It TALKS?*

*Yeah,* said Leah. *He can talk. Like that.* She sounded smug. She sounded so smug. *We can't. He can. It's really unfair. I told him.*

*How is he—how are you hearing—are you—*

"I can hear him through you," said the voice. "Bits. Mostly I can hear *you* hearing him, and you're very loud, Balto, so it's not hard." A pause. "Morning, by the way."

*He can hear me?*

*He can hear me,* Leah thought, *and I can hear you. So, yeah. Basically.*

*Oh my God.*

"Tell him to stop shouting," said the voice. "It's very early."

*He says stop shouting.*

*I HEARD WHAT HE SAID, LEE.*

---

Jacob phased in eleven minutes later.

Seth hadn't meant to wake him. He'd tried not to. He'd sat in Layla's backyard with the bees round him in a cloud and tried, very hard, to think quietly. But he'd never been any good at thinking quietly and he was even worse at it when he was freaking out, and somewhere around the fifth minute he'd lifted his head without meaning to and let out a howl that he'd felt go all the way through himself from his tail to his teeth, and four miles away, on the Cullens' couch, Jacob Black had fallen off it.

He felt Jacob come in. Groggy, furious, scared, all three at once, his head full of the Cullens' living room carpet and Nessie's face peering down at him over the edge of the couch, and then *what, what, what is it, who's hurt*—

And then Jacob saw.

Seth felt him see it. He felt him see the cave, and the sea, and the gray morning, and the black foreleg lying across the sand in front of Leah's face like a fallen tree. And the jaw over her shoulders. And the rumble. And the smell. All of it, in about a second, the way you did.

The pack mind went completely silent.

It stayed silent for a long time.

*Leah,* said Jacob finally. Very calm. The kind of calm that came right before the opposite of calm. *Where are you.*

*In my cave.*

*Your* what*?*

*She has a cave,* Seth said. *Since July. She hid it from us. For four months. I don't even know how.*

*Practice,* said Leah.

*Leah,* said Jacob. *Why is there a wolf in your cave.*

*It's not* a *wolf.*

*Why is* he *in your cave.*

*We couldn't sleep,* Leah thought. *So he came to the house. And then my mom turned the kitchen light on and saw us, so we came here.*

Jacob was silent for a second.

*Your* mom *saw?*

*She closed the curtain.*

*She* closed the curtain*?*

*She closed the curtain and turned the light off and went back to bed,* Leah thought. *She's cool.*

Seth felt Jacob put his head down on his paws, wherever he was. Out on the Cullens' back lawn, by the feel of it, in the wet, with the house behind him and the kid's face pressed to the glass.

*Okay,* Jacob said. *Okay. I need you to do something for me, Leah.*

*What?*

*I need you to stand up.*

Leah didn't say anything for a second. Seth felt her think about it.

Then she stood up.

He felt it through her. Felt her push up out of the sand, out from under the jaw, out of the ring of black, and stand, and shake, the sand flying off her coat. Felt her turn round in the mouth of the cave with the gray sea behind her. And look.

And Seth saw him.

All of him. For the first time. Lying there on the sand at the back of a dry cave in the rock, three hundred feet over the Pacific, with his head up now and his ears forward and his forepaws stretched out in front of him, looking back at Leah. Black. Black from end to end, so black the gray light from the cave mouth just stopped when it touched him. Green eyes. That green, the bottle-glass green from the ridge, from the guy in the suit, the same exact eyes, looking out of a wolf's face.

And so big.

Leah was the same size as Seth. Exactly the same, they'd checked, standing side by side in the yard in the summer while Jacob walked round them going *huh*. She was standing right over him and he was lying down and his head, lying down, was nearly up level with her shoulder.

*Stand up,* Jacob said. Quiet. *Ask him to stand up.*

*Jake—*

*Please.*

Leah didn't say anything. She didn't have to. In the cave, the black wolf looked at her for a second, and Seth felt it, the man inside the wolf, hear what Jacob was asking, through her. Felt him think about it. Felt him decide it was fair.

He stood up.

He did it slowly. He did it the way you'd stand up in a room full of people who were nervous of you, carefully, without any sudden moves. He got his forepaws under him and then his back ones and he stood up in the back of the cave, all of him, and his ears nearly brushed the rock roof, and Leah had to tip her head back to see his face.

Seth looked at him through his sister's eyes.

Jacob looked at him through his sister's eyes.

Seth had stood next to Jacob in the yard in the summer too. Everybody had. Jacob made everybody do it, the week he'd finally gotten bigger than Sam, he'd been so proud of it, he'd made everybody line up. Seth knew exactly where Jacob's head came to, next to Leah's. He knew exactly how much taller Jacob was.

This was taller than that.

This was taller than that by about a head.

The pack mind was so quiet you could have heard a pin drop in it.

*Oh,* said Jacob, very quietly. *Oh, come* on.

"Morning, Jacob," said the black wolf.

It came through Leah's ears, low and dry and polite, and Seth felt it hit Jacob like a snowball in the back of the neck.

*He knows my name.*

*He knows everybody's name,* said Leah. *He went through Seth's head yesterday.*

*Sorry,* said Seth. *Sorry. I'm sorry.*

*He's bigger than me,* Jacob said. He sounded stunned. He sounded like he'd been hit with a shovel. *He's bigger than me, Leah. He's* bigger than me*. By a* head*.*

*About a head,* agreed Leah.

*How is he bigger than me?*

*I don't know, Jake. He's a wizard. Ask him.*

*I'm not going to* ask *him*—

"It's not a competition, mate," said the black wolf, mildly.

*TELL HIM IT'S NOT A COMPETITION,* said Jacob.

*He just said it's not a competition.*

*I KNOW WHAT HE SAID.*

Seth, sitting in the wet grass in Layla's backyard with the bees round him in a cloud and his whole body shaking, started to laugh. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him in great big wheezing barks, wolf laughs, the kind that hurt your ribs, and he put his head down on his forepaws and laughed into the grass, and up in the house, behind him, he heard Layla wake up and come to the bedroom window and push it open and lean out with her hair everywhere and the bruise purple on her face.

"Seth?" she called down. Hoarse. "What's going on? Why are the bees out? Why are you laughing?"

He couldn't tell her. He was a wolf. He couldn't tell her anything.

He just lay there in the grass with the bees all over him and laughed until he couldn't breathe.

In his head, in the cave, three hundred feet above the sea, his sister was standing in front of the biggest wolf any of them had ever seen, in the gray morning, with the rain coming in behind her, and she was happy.

Seth hadn't felt that in her head once in seven months.

He could feel it. It came off her in waves. She was so happy it was coming out of her like heat off a stove, and she didn't even know it, and she didn't care who saw.

And Jacob, out on the Cullens' lawn with his head on his paws and the kid's face at the glass behind him, felt it too. Seth felt him feel it. Felt something in Jacob that had been wound up tight for twenty-four hours, for seven months, for longer, let go.

*Okay,* Jacob said, finally. Quiet. Beaten. *Okay. Fine. He's bigger than me.*

*About a head,* said Leah.

*I hate you,* said Jacob. *I hate you so much.*

*I know, Jake.*

*Tell him the Cullens said ten o'clock. For the biscuits.*

Leah turned round in the mouth of the cave and looked up at the black wolf.

*Jake says the Cullens said ten o'clock,* she thought at him. *For the biscuits.*

The black wolf looked down at her. Seth felt the man inside it smile.

"Ten o'clock," said Harry Potter. "Tell him I'll be there."

### VIII. Harry

*Teahwhit Head — 7:05 a.m.*

He'd slept.

That was the thing he kept coming back to, standing in the mouth of the cave with the grey morning coming up out of the sea and the rain blowing in on his face. He'd slept. Four hours, near enough, without waking once. No dreams that he remembered. He couldn't remember the last time that had happened. Not in the tent, not in his flat in London, not in any of the hotel rooms in any of the cities. Years.

He'd slept four hours on a sand floor in a hole in a cliff with a she-wolf's neck under his jaw, and he'd woken up because her brother had started shouting in her head and the shouting had come down the line into his, and the first thing he'd felt, before he even opened his eyes, was the thing he shared his skin with. Lying there with its chin on her shoulders. Completely, entirely still. More content than he'd ever known it.

He hadn't wanted to move. He'd have lain there till noon.

Then Jacob had asked him to stand up.

He'd done it, because it was a fair thing to ask. He'd stood up in the back of her cave and let the boy look at him through her eyes, and felt the boy see how tall he was, and felt something go out of Jacob Black that Harry suspected the boy had been holding on to for a long time. He hadn't enjoyed it. He'd been on the other end of that look, at sixteen, at seventeen, and he knew what it cost.

*It's not a competition,* he'd said. He'd meant it.

The boy hadn't believed him, and that was fair too.

Now Leah was standing beside him at the mouth of the cave, a grey wolf about her brother's size, her shoulder level with his chest, looking out at the sea, with the pack still chattering faintly in the back of her head. The boy Seth was laughing. He'd been laughing for ten minutes. He'd laughed until the witch came to the window and shouted at him, and then he'd laughed harder. Jacob had gone, phased back, with one last *I hate you* that had come down the line at Harry so clearly through Leah's ears that he'd nearly answered it.

*They're going to be impossible about this,* Leah thought. *For years.*

"Probably," said Harry.

*Seth's going to tell everybody. He's going to tell Embry. He's going to tell Quil. Quil's going to tell Sam's whole pack by lunch.*

"Do you mind?"

She thought about it. He felt her think about it, standing there with the wind in her fur, and he felt her come to the end of it.

*No,* she thought. She sounded surprised.

"Good," said Harry. "Me neither."

She looked out at the sea a moment longer.

*I left my clothes on the porch.*

"I know."

*My mom's going to be up.*

"I know."

*She saw us last night.*

"I know, Leah." He looked down at her. "I was there."

*What are we going to do?*

"Go and say good morning to your mum," said Harry. "I'd imagine."

---

They went back along the top of the headland in the rain.

It was full light now, or what passed for it, a flat white sky lying on the sea, and the shore pines dripping, and the salal soaking. He went first this time. He'd learned the way last night, behind her, in the dark, and he'd learned it the way he learned everything, once. He could feel her behind him in the line and through his ears and his nose and the ground under his paws, and he could feel her watching him go.

*How do you do that?* she thought.

"Do what?"

*Not make any noise. You weigh like a truck. I can't hear you. I'm right behind you and I can't hear you at all.*

"Practice."

*Liar.*

"I can't lie to you, love. You know that." He stepped over a fallen spruce without touching it. "Never could make a noise. Not like this. Even the first time. Don't know why. Same as the talking. I stopped asking."

*You stop asking a lot of things.*

"I ask about everything else," said Harry. "Professionally. Day and night. Eight years. I'm allowed one or two I just leave alone."

She didn't say anything to that. But he felt her think about it all the way along the headland.

At the edge of the village he stopped.

It was quarter past seven on a Tuesday. There were lights on in the houses along the road, and a pickup going down toward the marina with its wipers going, and smoke coming out of chimneys, and a woman in a bathrobe on a porch three houses down with a cigarette and a cup of coffee looking at the rain. People. And two wolves the size of horses, more or less, about to walk through the middle of them.

*Everybody knows,* Leah thought. *Sort of. The old ones know. Everybody else pretends they don't. They see us sometimes. In the trees. They don't say anything.*

"Yeah," said Harry. "Still."

He did the thing he'd been doing since he was eleven, one way or another, under one cloak or another. He thought about nothing, and made sure of it, and let it spread, over himself and over her too. It went out from him and settled over both of them like a sheet of rain.

They walked down the road through the middle of La Push at quarter past seven in the morning, a black wolf and a grey one, past the woman on the porch with her cigarette, past the pickup coming back up from the marina, past a dog on a chain that lifted its head and looked straight at them and whined and put its head back down. The woman with the cigarette looked right at them as they passed. Her eyes went over them and kept going, to the rain, to the road, to the trees. She took a drag on her cigarette.

*That's so creepy,* said Leah.

"Yeah," Harry agreed. "It is a bit."

---

Sue Clearwater was on the back porch.

She was sitting on the top step under the overhang, in the same bathrobe, with a cup of coffee in her hands and another one on the boards beside her, steaming. And on the boards on her other side, folded, a stack of clothes: a pair of grey sweatpants and a green flannel shirt.

She was looking at the trees at the top of the yard.

She'd been looking at them, Harry thought, for some time.

They came out of the trees together and stopped at the edge of the grass, and he let go of the notice-me-not, because there wasn't any point, because this woman had stood at her kitchen window at two in the morning and seen the two of them rolling in her flowerbeds and closed the curtain and gone back to bed. He stood there at the top of her yard in the rain, all of him, in plain view, and looked at her.

She looked back.

She was a small woman. Fifty, maybe. Black hair going grey, cut short. A strong, square, tired face, a face that had buried a husband in March and found out the same week what her children were. She looked at the black wolf at the top of her yard for a long moment, and her face didn't do anything.

He'd been looked at by a lot of people. Most of them had been frightened of him, one way or another, and the ones who weren't had mostly wanted something. He'd learned to read both kinds a long way off.

Sue Clearwater was looking at him the way he'd seen Mrs Weasley look at him once, on a platform at King's Cross when he was eleven, a skinny boy in somebody else's clothes with no idea where he was going. Like she was taking his measure. Like she'd already decided she was going to feed him, and the only question left was how much.

Leah, beside him, made a small sound in her throat.

Sue picked up the folded stack of clothes off the boards. She stood up, and walked down the steps in her slippers and across the wet grass to the woodshed, and put the clothes on the chopping block just inside the door. Then she walked back to the porch, and sat back down on the top step, and picked up her coffee.

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to.

Leah went behind the woodshed.

Harry stood at the top of the yard and looked at Leah's mother, and Leah's mother looked at him, and the rain came down between them.

Behind the woodshed he heard the canvas-ripping sound, quieter than usual. Then some rustling. Then Leah's voice, a human voice, muttering something that made Sue's mouth twitch at one corner.

Then he turned.

He did it where he stood. He thought about it for a second, whether it would be politer to go back into the trees, and decided it wouldn't, because she'd already seen him, and because he'd learned a long time ago that if somebody was going to be frightened of what you were, it was better they were frightened of all of it at once, up front, in daylight, than found it out by halves. So he stood at the top of Sue Clearwater's yard and let the page turn.

The world folded.

It opened out again, and he was standing on two feet in the wet grass, in his jeans and his grey T-shirt and the black jumper with the hole in the elbow and his boots, exactly as he'd stood up off his bed at a quarter past one. Dry. Every stitch of it. The rain started soaking into the jumper's shoulders right away.

Sue's coffee cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Behind the woodshed, Leah came round the corner in the grey sweatpants and her dad's green flannel shirt, barefoot, pulling it straight, and stopped dead.

She looked at him. At the jumper. At the jeans.

"Oh, that is *so* unfair," Leah said.

"What?"

"Your *clothes*." She pointed at him. "Your clothes came back. You just—you're just *wearing them*. They're not even wet. Do you know how many pairs of shorts Seth goes through in a week? Do you have any idea how much money I've spent on cutoffs since March?"

"I'm sorry?"

"You should be."

Up on the porch, Sue Clearwater made a sound into her coffee cup that was very probably a laugh, and put the cup down on the step, and stood up.

"You must be Harry," she said.

He came down the yard toward her. He stopped at the bottom of the porch steps. He put his hands in the pockets of his jeans, and then thought better of it, and took them out again.

"Yes, ma'am," he said. "Harry Potter. Good morning." He hesitated. "I'm sorry about the flowerbeds."

Sue looked down at him from the top step. She looked at him for a long time. At his face, mostly. He let her.

"There's coffee," she said finally, and nodded at the second cup steaming on the boards. "It's probably cold. I'll make more. Come inside. I'm going to make breakfast." She turned to the door, and stopped, and looked back over her shoulder at him. "Leah says you eat a lot."

"I do, yes."

"Seth eats a lot."

"I'd imagine he does."

"Good," said Sue Clearwater. "I like to cook." And she went inside.

---

She made fry bread. A lot of it. She stood at the stove in her bathrobe with a cast-iron pan going and turned out fry bread one after another, golden and puffed and blistered, and stacked it on a plate, and then she fried eggs, a dozen of them, and a whole packet of bacon, and a ham steak, and put all of it on the table in front of him, and sat down across from him with her coffee and watched him eat.

He ate all of it.

He'd been hungry since he woke up. He was always hungry after he turned; it took more out of him than anything, even the big magic, even the lightning. He ate the eggs and the bacon and the ham and nine pieces of fry bread with butter and honey, the honey out of a jar with a handwritten label that said QUINN — 2005 on it in shaky green ink, and Sue watched him do it without saying anything, and when he'd finished the ninth piece she got up and made four more.

Leah sat at the end of the table with her knees up on the chair and her own plate, eating, watching her mother watch him.

He could feel her through the line. She was nervous of this. Of her mother's face. She kept looking from one of them to the other like she was watching a tennis match.

"Thank you," Harry said, when he'd finished the thirteenth piece. "That was—thank you. That's the best breakfast I've had since I got here."

"Better than the diner?"

"The diner doesn't do fry bread."

"No," said Sue. "It doesn't." She looked at his plate. She looked at him. "Leah tells me you can't lie to her."

"No, ma'am."

"And she can't lie to you."

"No."

"So if I ask you something," said Sue, "and she's sitting right there, you'll tell me the truth."

Harry put his fork down.

"I can lie to you, Mrs Clearwater," he said. "If I want to. It's only her I can't." He met her eyes. "I'm choosing not to."

Sue looked at him for a long moment. He felt Leah, at the end of the table, go very still.

"Why?" said Sue.

"Because you made me thirteen pieces of fry bread," said Harry. "And because she loves you. And because you saw us out of the window at two in the morning and closed the curtain." He paused. "And because I've lied to a lot of people. It's my job. I'm good at it. I'd rather not start with you."

Sue didn't say anything.

She picked up her coffee and drank some. She put it down.

"What's your job?" she said.

"I work for the British government. The magical one. There's a Ministry, in London, under the ground. I work in a part of it that—" He stopped. He felt the oath shift under his collarbone. He'd known it would. "I can't tell you what I do, Mrs Clearwater. I'm sorry. There's a—I swore something, a long time ago, and it physically stops me. It's like a hand on my throat. If I tried to tell you, I'd stop talking in the middle of a word."

"Can Leah tell me?"

"No. It's on her too, now. Same hand. We found that out last night."

Sue looked at her daughter. Leah nodded.

"It's true, Mom," Leah said. "I tried to tell the Cullens why he's here. It just stopped. Right here." She touched her throat.

Sue looked at her daughter's throat for a long moment. Then back at him.

"Is it dangerous?" she said. "What you do?"

"Yes."

"For her?"

He thought about it. He thought about it honestly, all the way down, the way he'd been thinking about everything since one o'clock yesterday, because he couldn't think any other way with Leah at the end of the table.

"It might be," he said. "One day. I hope not. I'm going to try very hard to make sure it isn't. But I'm not going to sit at your table and tell you it can't be, because I don't know that, and you'd know I was lying." He paused. "What I can tell you is that there's nobody alive who'd get through me to get to her. Nobody. I've done the arithmetic."

Sue looked at him for a long time.

"Leah told me last night that you're scary," she said.

"I am, yes."

"She said she watched you do things yesterday she didn't have words for."

"She did, yes."

"And she said you cut the crusts off her toast without asking, because she doesn't like crusts."

Harry glanced down the table at Leah. Leah had gone red to the ears and was staring very hard at her plate.

"She doesn't like crusts," Harry said.

"No," said Sue. "She never has. Not since she was three." She picked up her coffee again. "Her father used to cut them off for her. Every morning. Until she was about twelve and told him she was too old for it."

Nobody said anything.

The rain ticked on the kitchen window. Down the hill, the sea breathed.

"Sunday," Sue said. "Dinner. Six o'clock. Seth will be here and so will his girl, and I'm going to make salmon. You'll come."

"I'll come."

"And you'll bring something."

"I'll bring something," said Harry. "I was going to say biscuits. Everyone keeps telling me not to bring biscuits."

"Bring biscuits," said Sue. "I like biscuits."

---

He asked her, after. He'd thought about how to do it all through the thirteen pieces of fry bread, and in the end he did it the way Croaker had told him to, which was politely, and the way he'd been raised, which was in a kitchen, at a table, after he'd eaten, with the dishes in the sink.

He did the dishes first. Sue tried to stop him. He didn't let her. He stood at her sink in his black jumper with the hole in the elbow and washed every plate and every pan and the big cast-iron skillet, by hand, the way he'd washed the Dursleys' every morning for eleven years, and dried them, and asked where they went, and put them there. Leah sat at the table and watched him do it with an expression he couldn't read even through the line, and Sue sat across from her and watched Leah watch him.

Then he dried his hands and came and sat back down.

"Mrs Clearwater," he said. "Leah says you sit on the tribal council."

Sue's eyebrows went up. "Since March. I took Harry's seat."

"Then I need to ask you something," said Harry. "Formally. As much as I can."

She waited.

"My boss," he said. "In London. I told him last night about Leah. About the bond. About what's been going on here. And he's—" He chose the words carefully. "He's asked me to stay. Long-term. He's made me what he calls a liaison. To your people, and to the Cullens. His idea is that there's things going on on this coast that matter a great deal to my people, and that your people and the Cullens know more about some of them than anybody in my world does. And that it'd be better for everyone if we worked together, rather than—" He stopped. "Rather than the alternative."

"What's the alternative?" said Sue.

"The alternative," said Harry, "is that my people find out about yours some other way, one day, without me. And they don't understand what they're looking at. And they do something stupid."

Sue was quiet.

"I can't stay on your land without your leave," Harry said. "I wouldn't. My boss said get it, and he's right, and I'd have asked anyway. I'm on the edge of the national forest now, up the Calawah, and I'll stay there, I don't need to be any closer. But I'll be coming and going. To see her. To work with the wolves, if they'll have me. And I'd rather do that with the council's say-so than without it. So I'm asking. I'm asking you, because you're the one I've met, and because you made me breakfast. And I'll ask anybody else on the council you tell me to ask, however they want me to ask it."

Sue looked at him for a long, long time.

Then she looked at Leah.

Leah looked back at her mother with her chin up and her knees up on the chair and her dad's flannel shirt on, and Harry felt her through the line, holding her breath.

"I'll talk to Billy," Sue said. "And to Old Quil. Quil Ateara. He's the oldest of us. He was a little boy when Ephraim Black made the treaty with the Cullens. He'll want to see you." She paused. "He'll want to see you as the other thing, too. As the wolf. He'll want to see it with his own eyes."

"He can."

"Thursday," said Sue. "At Billy's house. In the evening. Come at seven. Don't bring biscuits to that."

"No biscuits," said Harry. "Understood."

"Bring fish," said Sue. "Old Quil likes smoked salmon. Not the kind from a store."

Harry looked at her.

"I can get fish," he said.

---

Seth and the witch turned up at half past eight.

He heard the truck from a mile off, coming down the road from the highway, an old pickup with a bad muffler and a tail-light that rattled, and he felt Leah hear it too, and felt her go *oh God, here we go*. It pulled into the drive behind Sue's dented Ford and the engine died and the doors opened, and Seth Clearwater came up the porch steps two at a time and through the kitchen door without knocking, and stopped dead in the middle of his mother's kitchen, and stared at him.

Harry, sitting at the table with his fourth cup of coffee, looked back.

"Morning, Balto," he said.

The boy was enormous. He'd known that, of course; he'd seen him from the inside, and in the bee yard in the witch's memory, and on the ridge as a wolf. But he hadn't stood in a room with him on two feet. Six foot four, all legs and shoulders and a face that had never once tried to hide what was on it, standing in his mother's kitchen with his mouth open and his whole head shouting, so loud Harry could hear it without trying at all. *That's him. That's him. That's the wolf. That's the guy. He's in my kitchen. He's eating my mom's fry bread. He's drinking out of my mug.*

Harry looked down at the mug. It said WORLD'S OKAYEST BROTHER on it.

"Sorry," he said, and put it down.

"No," said Seth. "No. It's fine. It's—you can have the mug."

Behind him, the witch came in.

She came in slower. She came in the way she'd come into the Cullens' living room, in the boy's memory, the way you came into a room where you couldn't see the exits; two steps inside the door and then a stop. She was in her green rain jacket with the hood down and her braid over one shoulder and the bruise on her cheek gone purple-black overnight. She looked at Sue. She looked at Leah at the end of the table. And then she looked at him, and her face went very still, and he watched her see him.

Properly. For the first time. In good light, in a bright kitchen, without a vampire or a crater or a gun. Just a bloke in a jumper with a hole in it, at a kitchen table, with a cup of coffee.

He watched her find the face. He watched her go back through a hundred newspapers and a card on a bedroom wall and find it there, younger and thinner and with glasses and a scar, and hold the two side by side, and see that they were the same.

"Hi," said Layla Quinn. Very faint.

"Morning, Quinn," said Harry. "How's the back?"

She stared at him.

"You threw me into a log," she said.

"I did. Yeah. Sorry about that." He held up his hand, palm up, over the table, and thought about a shelf in the workshop nineteen miles away, third from the top, at the left-hand end. Something flew in through the open kitchen window over Sue's sink a second later, fast, and smacked into his palm. A squat glass jar with a cork in it, full of something thick and yellow-green. He held it out to her across the table. "Murtlap essence. Proper stuff. My own. Twice a day on the bruising. It'll be gone by Thursday."

The witch looked at the jar. She looked at the window. She looked back at him.

"You just Summoned that from your tent," she said.

"Yes."

"Your tent is twenty miles away."

"Nineteen."

"Nobody can—" She stopped. She closed her eyes for a second. When she opened them she looked like a woman who'd decided to stop saying *nobody can* around him on the grounds of conserving her energy. "Thank you."

She took the jar.

Seth sat down. He sat down on the chair next to his sister and kept staring. Leah elbowed him without looking. He kept staring.

Layla didn't sit down. She stood just inside the door holding the jar of Murtlap, and looked at him, and he felt the question coming up in her a long way off, the way you can see a wave coming in from a long way out.

"Harry Potter," she said.

"Yeah."

"*The* Harry Potter."

"There might be others. It's not that uncommon a name in Britain."

"Don't," said Layla. "Don't do that. You know what I mean."

"I know what you mean."

"You killed You-Know-Who."

"Voldemort," said Harry. "You can say it. He's dead. It doesn't matter anymore."

She flinched anyway. They always did, the ones raised on it.

"You killed *Voldemort*," she said. "When you were seventeen. In a duel. And then you disappeared. For eight years. And nobody knew where you were. People thought you were *dead*. There were articles. There was a whole—there was a *book*. Some witch in Ohio wrote a book about how you'd gone to live with monks in Bhutan." She stopped for breath. "And you've been, what? Doing what? For *eight years*?"

"Working."

"Working where?"

"Can't tell you."

"For *who*?"

"Can't tell you that either."

"Why not?"

"Because I can't," said Harry. "Literally, Quinn. It's a thing I swore. It stops me." He met her eyes. "Leah can't tell you either. She's got the same thing on her now. Don't ask her. It hurts."

The witch looked at Leah. Leah nodded once.

He watched her think about it. He could feel her going at it the way she'd gone at it on the ridge, the way she went at everything: looking for seams. The English voice. The Muggle gun. The American badge. The way he could walk through a ward. The way he'd known what Murtlap was. All of it lying there in front of her like the pieces of a door with every glyph scratched off.

He watched her come to the end of it, and find nothing, and stop.

"I don't know who you work for," Layla said slowly. "I'm not going to guess. I don't think I could."

"No," Harry agreed. "Probably not."

"Is it bad?"

"Depends who you ask."

"I'm asking you."

"It's necessary," said Harry. "Mostly. Sometimes it's bad as well. I try to keep those two apart. I don't always manage it."

She looked at him for a long moment more.

Then she sat down. She sat down at Sue Clearwater's kitchen table, across from him, with the jar of Murtlap in front of her, and put both her hands flat on the table, the way she'd put them on her grandmother's table, Harry thought, when she'd laid her wand down in front of Seth for the first time.

"Okay," she said. "Two things."

"Go on."

"One. In Forks, you're—"

"Harry Evans," said Harry. "Photographer. North London. Writing a book about moss. I'd like to keep it that way, if you don't mind. That's separate from the favour, by the way. I'm just asking."

"Fine. Harry Evans." She took a breath. "Two."

He waited.

"I threw a Disjunction at you," said Layla. "I threw my *Disjunction* at *Harry Potter*."

"You threw a very good Disjunction at Harry Potter," said Harry. "If it helps. That's the best one I've ever seen. Did you build it yourself?"

She went red. All the way up, to the roots of her hair, round the bruise. Beside her, Seth was looking at her with an expression of enormous, delighted pride.

"Three years," she said. "In Peru."

"Thought so. The way it unravels from the inside, through the weave instead of across it. That's your own work. I've never seen it anywhere else." He picked his coffee back up. "I'd like a copy of the notes, if you've still got them. Separate from the favour. I'd just like to read them."

"She had your card on her wall," said Seth.

The witch turned and looked at Seth with an expression of pure, unadulterated murder.

"Which one?" said Harry.

"What?"

"There were three. Chocolate Frogs. They did three. The first one's me at twelve with my hair sticking up and a face like I've been hit with a shovel. The second one's me at fifteen and somebody's drawn the scar on wrong. The third one—"

"The third one," said Layla, through her teeth, still looking at Seth, "is from '98. After the war. You're standing in front of a castle with your wand out and a cut on your face. You're not smiling."

"Christ," said Harry. "That one. They took that one about four hours after the battle. I'd been awake for three days. I'd just buried—" He stopped. He put the coffee down. "That one's terrible."

"It was the best one," said Layla. "Everybody had that one."

He didn't say anything.

At the end of the table, Leah reached over without looking and put her hand on the table next to his, about an inch away. He felt it through the line, the reach, and he felt what was in it, and it went down through him to the bottom and settled there like a stone into still water.

He looked at the inch of table between their hands.

He didn't close it. Not yet. Not in front of her mother. But he looked at it.

Sue Clearwater, who'd been standing at the stove the whole time with her back to them making more coffee, said, without turning round: "Seth. Get your sister's friend a plate. And you're having some too."

"I ate at Layla's—"

"You had a bowl of cereal. I can smell it. Sit down and eat."

---

At half past nine he went out on the back porch and stood in the rain and looked at the time.

They were going up to the white house at ten. All of them. Leah and Seth and the witch, in the witch's truck, and him in the back of it, because there wasn't room in the cab for three people of Seth's size and one of Layla's, and because he'd said so. He'd said he'd ride in the back. Leah had said she would too. Nobody had argued.

He had biscuits to fetch.

He thought about it for a second. He'd meant to Apparate back to the tent for them; he had a tin in the kitchen cupboard, a proper one, shortbread from a shop on the Royal Mile in Edinburgh that he'd been saving since August for an occasion. Croaker had said bring the good ones. He supposed this was the occasion.

He held his hand out over the porch rail, palm up, and thought about the kitchen cupboard, second shelf, behind the tea.

It came in out of the rain about four seconds later, from the north-east, over the trees: a red tartan tin about the size of a hardback book, travelling low and fast, with the rain bouncing off the lid. It landed in his palm with a smack.

He looked at it. The lid said *FINEST ALL BUTTER SHORTBREAD* and had a picture of a castle on it.

Behind him, in the kitchen doorway, Leah said, "Is that the biscuits?"

"That's the biscuits."

"Those are cookies."

"They're biscuits."

"They're *cookies*, Harry."

"I'm not having this argument with you."

She came out and stood beside him at the porch rail, in her dad's flannel shirt, with her arms folded, and looked at the rain. He could feel her. Calm. All the way down. Calm and a bit nervous and something else under both of them that he didn't have a word for yet, the same something that was under him, the same thing at both ends.

"They're going to be weird," she said. "The Cullens. Rosalie's going to be a bitch. Jasper's going to stand in the corner and look at you like he's working out where to hit you. Emmett's going to ask if you can turn him into a bear."

"Can't, as it happens. Not permanently."

"Edward's going to be in your head the whole time."

"Edward can try."

"Jake's going to be on the porch," said Leah. "Waiting. He's going to stand next to you on purpose to see how much taller you are when you're a person."

"I'm about five inches shorter than him when I'm a person, I'd imagine."

"Yeah," said Leah. "He's going to love that."

He laughed. It came out of him short and surprised and it surprised him; he couldn't remember the last time something had made him laugh like that, without his deciding to. She looked sideways at him, and the corner of her mouth went up.

The rain came down. The sea breathed. In the kitchen behind them Seth was telling his mother, at enormous length and with his mouth full, about bone-snakes.

"Hey," said Leah.

"Hm."

"Thanks. For asking my mom. About the land. You didn't have to do that."

"Yes, I did."

"No, you didn't. You could've just done it. Nobody could've stopped you." She was looking at the trees. "Nobody's ever asked her anything. Not since my dad. Everybody just decides stuff and then tells her."

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. She could feel what he'd have said.

She unfolded her arms. She put her hand on the porch rail, beside his. The one that wasn't holding the shortbread.

An inch away.

This time he closed it.

He put his hand over hers on the wet wood of the rail, and she turned hers over under it, and her fingers went through his, and he felt it go through the line in both directions at once like a current through a wire, and at the bottom of both of them, at the two ends of the rope, two wolves who'd been lying with their heads up since one in the morning put their heads down on their paws, and sighed.

Behind them, in the kitchen, somebody said, "*Oh my God*," very loudly, and it was Seth, and then somebody said, "*Seth*," and it was his mother, and then somebody hit somebody with a dish towel.

Neither of them looked round.

At ten to ten they went down the steps together, Harry with the tartan tin under his arm and Leah barefoot, and climbed over the tailgate into the back of the witch's truck, and sat down side by side on the wet ridged metal with their backs against the cab, in the rain, and the witch started the engine. It coughed twice and caught. Sue stood on the porch in her bathrobe with her coffee and watched them go.

They went up the road through La Push and out past the marina and onto the highway, east, toward Forks, toward the long drive and the white house in the trees.

Toward a family of seven who'd sat up all night waiting for him. Toward a boy at a piano who'd spent a hundred and five years being the only one who could hear, and who'd been told last night, by a voice he couldn't find, that there was a photograph. Toward a small dark girl who'd have spent the whole night trying to see him coming. Toward a child with bronze curls who'd be standing at the glass with her hands flat on it.

Toward Jacob Black, who'd be waiting on the porch.

Harry sat in the back of the truck in the rain with the shortbread on his knees and Leah's hand in his, and watched the trees go by, and felt, for the first time in eight years, that he was exactly where he needed to be.

### IX. Marisol

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — Tuesday, 11:20 a.m.*

The lightning strike was perfect.

That was what bothered her.

Investigator Marisol Tran of the Magical Congress of the United States of America, Department of Magical Law Enforcement, Seattle Field Office, Anomalous Activity desk, stood on the scorched granite slab at the top of the ridge with her sensor in one hand and her wand in the other and the rain dripping off the brim of her hat, and looked at the most perfect lightning strike she'd ever seen.

It had hit the highest point on the ridge, which lightning did. It had hit a slab of granite in a week of storms, which lightning did. It had split the rock in a long jagged crack, and thrown a spray of fused-glass fulgurite out across the stone like frost on a window, and set a hemlock on fire, and the hemlock had burned for a few seconds before the rain put it out, which was exactly what you'd expect from lightning. The ozone was still in the air, faintly, fourteen hours later. Everything was where it should be.

She'd been doing this for eleven years. She'd stood over a lot of lightning strikes. She'd never seen one that was so exactly what a lightning strike was supposed to look like.

She crouched down at the edge of the burn and held the sensor out over it.

The sensor was a brass disc the size of a pocket watch, with a needle under a glass face and a ring of runes round the rim that lit up when there was magic nearby. It was the standard field issue; every investigator on the desk carried one. Yesterday the master sensor in the Seattle office, the big one in the basement that covered the whole of Western Washington, had gone off like a Fourth of July rocket, and the clerks had logged it, and the morning shift had pulled the log, and her supervisor had read it over his coffee and said, *Tran, go and look at that, would you, it's probably weather.*

It had gone off twice. That was the second thing that bothered her. Once at eleven forty-one *yesterday morning*, a huge sustained spike, thirty seconds of it, the kind of reading you got from a full-scale duel between two people who knew what they were doing. And then again, smaller, sharper, at ten to nine last night. When the lightning hit.

Weather didn't do that. Weather didn't fight a duel at lunchtime and come back after dinner to hit the same spot.

The needle on her sensor swung, slowly, over the burn. The runes round the rim lit up in a ragged ring. Lightning, the sensor said. Raw, natural, atmospheric; the signature was unmistakable, a clean white flare. And under it, faint, faint as a watermark, something else.

She held the sensor closer. The needle shivered.

Something else. Lots of something else. Smeared and scattered and fading. Little ghosts of readings that didn't make any sense, all jumbled up together under the lightning like stains under a coat of paint.

She stood up.

She walked round the slab, slowly, in a widening circle, the way they'd taught her at the academy in Albany, with the sensor out in front of her. Most of the ridge read clean. Moss and rock and rain and nothing. Then she got to the far side of the slab and the needle jumped so hard it hit the stop.

She stopped.

There was a scar in the air. She could see it, now she knew where to look: a faint ragged distortion, about head height, like heat shimmer over a road. Star-shaped. Still raw at the edges. An Apparition mark. A bad one, a hasty one, somebody who'd come in from a long way off in a hurry and not cared how they landed.

It was a day old. Maybe a little more.

She'd checked, before she drove out here. That was procedure; you checked whether anybody had a reason to be where you were going. Nobody did. There was no MACUSA activity logged anywhere on the Olympic Peninsula. No weather office survey, no forestry liaison, no Obliviator team, nothing. The nearest registered witch or wizard was a curse-breaker who'd moved into a house in Forks eight weeks ago and filed the change of address on time, like a good citizen. Marisol had read her file in the car. Layla Quinn. Ilvermorny, Thunderbird. Very good. A bad job in Mexico. Retired.

She stood looking at the Apparition scar for a long time.

Then she looked down.

---

The moss was soft and deep on this side of the slab, and the rain had been coming down for fourteen hours, and most of what had been in it was gone. But moss held things. It held them the way a sponge held water. And the ridge had been torn up, at some point in the last day, torn up in a long ragged furrow from the edge of the slab back into a stand of sword ferns, as if something heavy had been dragged through it, or thrown.

She followed the furrow back to a fallen cedar, a big nurse log furred over with saplings. There was a dent in the moss on the side of the log, at about waist height. Shoulder-shaped.

Somebody had been thrown into this log. Hard.

She went back to the slab. She walked the edges of it again, slower this time, crouching, looking at the moss and the ground at the bottom of the rock, and on the north side, where the ground was soft and black under the hemlocks and the rain hadn't quite reached because of the trees, she found the prints.

She stood very still and looked at them.

They were paw prints. Canine, unmistakably; four toes and the pad, the claws showing at the tips. There were a lot of them, overlapping, coming up out of the trees onto the slab in a rush. Two animals. She could tell that much. One set a bit narrower than the other. Both running.

She took a measuring tape out of her coat pocket. It was a Muggle tape, yellow, retractable; she'd bought it at a hardware store in Tacoma six years ago and never found a magical one she liked better. She crouched down over the clearest print, the deepest one, pressed into the soft black earth under the hemlocks, and laid the tape across it.

Nine inches across.

She measured it again.

Nine inches. Nearly ten long, from the heel of the pad to the tip of the longest claw.

A big grey wolf, the biggest you'd ever find in Alaska or the Yukon, had a front paw maybe five inches long. Maybe five and a half, on a monster. She knew that because she'd looked it up once, years ago, on a case in Montana that had turned out to be a werewolf. The werewolf's paws had been about five and a half inches. She'd thought that was big.

This was bigger than that. A lot bigger. And there were two of them.

She stood up slowly, with the tape in her hand, and looked back along the prints, back the way they'd come, into the trees. Into the deep green dark under the hemlocks, going away north and west toward the river, toward the town. Toward the coast.

The rain came down. Somewhere a long way off, a raven called.

She thought about the case file she'd pulled before she left the office this morning, the one on Forks, which every investigator on the Seattle desk had a copy of and nobody ever read, because nothing ever happened in Forks. It was a thin file. Most of it was animal attacks. Hikers, mostly, over the years, decades of them, found in the woods round the peninsula, put down to bears or cougars. A cluster in 1936. Another cluster, smaller, in the fifties. A long quiet stretch. And then, a couple of years ago, they'd started again. And last spring, in Seattle, the string of killings the Muggle papers had called a serial killer, and then a gang, and then nothing, because they'd just stopped.

Nobody on the desk had ever thought much about any of it. Muggle deaths were Muggle business, unless there was magic in them, and there never had been. The sensors never picked anything up.

She looked at the paw print.

Her other radio crackled on her belt, the Muggle police scanner she carried on the peninsula because it was the quickest way to find out what was happening. A woman's voice, flat and bored, from the dispatch in Forks.

"—all units, be advised, Olympic National Park rangers report a recovery on the Hoh River trail, approximately four miles in, at the bend past the maples. Female, thirties. Ranger station received an anonymous tip last night. County ME is en route. Chief Swan, they're asking if Forks can send somebody up to assist."

A man's voice, gruff and tired. "Yeah. Yeah, I'll go up. Tell them I'm forty minutes out."

Marisol stood on the ridge in the rain and listened to the radio go quiet.

Anonymous tip. Last night.

She looked at the lightning strike. At the Apparition scar. At the dent in the side of the nurse log. At the paw prints, nine inches across, coming out of the trees.

She put the tape measure back in her pocket.

She thought about the car. On the way up the Bogachiel road this morning she'd passed a mud-brown Ford Explorer pulled into a logging turnout under the trees, nose out, with rental plates and nobody in it and a dog-eared paperback on the passenger seat. Parked there since yesterday, by the moss on the wipers. She'd written the plate down out of habit. She took her notebook out of her coat and looked at it now.

Washington plates. A rental company at Sea-Tac.

Then she took out her other radio, her MACUSA one, a little silver shell like a compact, and flipped it open, and said into it, "Tran for Henley."

It took a minute. Then her supervisor's voice, with his mouth full. "Henley. What've you got? Weather?"

"No, sir," said Marisol. "I don't think it's weather."

A pause. "What, then?"

She looked down at the print.

"I don't know yet," she said. "I'd like to stay a few days and find out."

---

*End of Chapter Two*
