# Need to Know

## Chapter Two: Liaison

*The Bogachiel River valley and La Push, Washington — November 22–24, 2006*

---

### I. Harry

*Thursday, November 23, 2006 — 12:41 a.m.*

He lay in the dark for an hour and forty minutes, and none of it was sleep.

The rain was the same rain. The river was the same river. The bed was the same bed he'd slept in for nineteen nights, a good wide one with a horsehair mattress and a wool blanket that had come out of the same Norfolk manor as the range, and he lay on his back in it with his hands folded on his chest like a man on a tomb, and stared up at a canvas ceiling he couldn't see.

*Complications?*

*None worth the parchment, sir.*

He kept hearing it. His own voice, easy and flat, the voice he'd used on Aurors and goblins and a Bulgarian deputy minister with a cellar full of Inferi, coming out of his mouth at the one man in the world he'd never used it on before tonight.

*You weren't seen?*

*No, sir.*

*The Cullens remain unaware of you?*

*Yes, sir.*

Three. Forty seconds. He'd counted them afterward, standing at the mirror, the way you'd count the steps back down a staircase you'd just fallen down.

It shouldn't have mattered. He knew that. He'd lied for a living for seven years, and the Department had taught him how, and the man he'd lied to tonight had signed off on most of the teaching. Croaker lied to people. Croaker lied to the Minister about twice a week and to the Wizengamot whenever it sat. Croaker would have lied to God if God had asked him what was behind the door at the end of the ninth-level corridor. It was a department of liars, run by the best liar of them all, and Harry had been one of its better ones for a long time now.

But Croaker had never lied to *him*.

That was the thing he kept coming back to, lying there in the dark. Seven years. Not once. Harry had walked into the Department at nineteen with a list of questions and a voice in his head that hadn't stopped asking them since a stone arch in a room on the ninth level, and the old man had sat him down in an empty office and answered every single one. Even the ones Harry hadn't wanted answered. *No,* Croaker had said, the first day, in that thin dry voice. *It doesn't give anything back. I've looked. I've been looking for forty-one years. I'm sorry.*

He'd been told the truth by very few grown-ups in his life. Dumbledore had told him most of it, in the end, and far too late, and by way of a dead man's memories in a stone basin. Croaker had told him everything up front, the day he walked in, with no basin and no riddles. That was why Harry had stayed. He'd stayed because he'd finally found a man in charge of something who didn't lie to him. The work and the travel and the guns, the long strange years learning magic nobody had taught at school, had all come after that.

And tonight Harry had lied to that man three times in forty seconds, because of a woman he'd known for six hours.

He could still feel her.

She was asleep. Thirteen miles off, northwest, on her mother's couch, and the line between them had gone soft and easy, like a mooring rope with a boat at rest on it. He could feel her breathing, almost. The slow rise and fall of it, coming down the line like a tide.

And the wolf was lying down. For the first time since the clearing. It lay in the dark under his breastbone with its head on its paws, facing the way she was, and for the first time in seventy-one days it was *content*. He could feel that too. A deep, slow, settled kind of content, the way a dog is content in front of a fire.

He thought about the mirror.

The wolf's head came up.

It didn't stand. Not yet. It just lifted its head off its paws and turned it, slowly, away from the northwest and toward him, and looked at him with its ears halfway back. It knew. It always knew, before he did, what he was going to do. It had known in the specimen room what he'd been thinking about the grey one and the word *Obliviate*, and it had come up off the floor of him like a bomb going off.

This time it just looked.

*Don't,* the look said, as plain as words.

"I have to," Harry said to the dark.

The wolf's ears went the rest of the way back.

He swung his legs out of the bed.

---

The wolf stood up.

It stood up all at once, inside him, and planted itself. That was the only word for it. It got its feet under it in the dark and *set* them, the way a big dog sets its feet when you try to drag it somewhere on a lead, and he felt it in his chest like a weight going sideways. He stood in the middle of his bedroom in his shorts and an old grey T-shirt, on the cold-proofed flagstones, with his hand on the doorframe, and felt it lean.

It was afraid, and that was the strange part. In the specimen room it had been rage, white and total, snarling at him over the thought of hurting her. Now it was fear. Plain, animal fear, the kind he hadn't felt off it since the very beginning, and it was all wrapped around one picture, and the picture was the old man's face in the mirror.

The old man had sent them here.

The old man could send them away.

That was as far as the wolf's thinking went, and as far as it needed to go. It didn't understand departments, or memos, or the difference between a lie and the truth. It understood that the long grey face in the glass was the thing that had brought them to this valley and kept them down for seventy-one days and could, if it wanted, pick them up and put them somewhere else. Somewhere far. Somewhere the line would have to stretch across an ocean.

And it had just found her.

"It doesn't work like that," Harry told it. Out loud. His voice was rough. "He's not going to—I'm not *leaving*. I'm not going anywhere. I'm telling him the truth. That's all. That's all it is."

The wolf didn't care. The wolf leaned.

He walked down the corridor anyway. It was like walking into a headwind. Every step, the wolf pushed back against him from the inside, just short of hard enough to stop him (it had never once in all the years really tried to stop him), hard enough that he felt every inch of the corridor go by. Past the bathroom. Past the library door. Into the main room, dark, the turntable silent, the needle parked, the record still on the platter. The mirror on the wall by the door, grey, reflecting nothing but the faint shine of the dead coals in the range.

He stopped in front of it.

The wolf made a sound.

It came out of his own chest. He heard it. A low rough rumble, down under his breastbone, through his ribs, a growl, real and audible, in a man's body, in an empty kitchen at a quarter to one in the morning. He'd never heard it do that. Not in human form. Not once, in all the years.

He put his hand flat on his chest, over the place where the line went in, and stood there with the growl going under his palm like an engine idling.

"I know," he said quietly. "I know, mate. I know. But I owe him this."

The growl didn't stop.

He touched the frame of the mirror and said the old man's name.

---

The glass went cloudy. It took longer than usual. Long enough that Harry started to think the old man had, for once in his life, gone home.

Then the cloud cleared, and the long grey face was there, with the windowless stone wall behind it. Croaker was holding a cup of tea. There was a rack of toast at his elbow, just at the edge of the frame. It was a quarter to nine in the morning in London, and the Head of the Department of Mysteries was having his breakfast at his desk, the way Harry suspected he'd had it every working day since about 1962.

The pale eyes went over Harry once. The T-shirt. The bare feet. The hand on his chest.

The growl, which was still going.

"Was that you?" Croaker said.

"Sort of, sir."

Croaker considered this. He put his cup down on its saucer without a sound.

"Go on, then," he said.

Harry had thought about how to say it, lying in the dark. He'd rehearsed it, the way you'd rehearse a report. Item, item, item. He'd had it laid out in order. It all went out of his head the second he opened his mouth.

"I lied to you, sir," he said.

"Yes," said Croaker.

Harry stopped.

The old man looked back at him through the glass, perfectly calm, his long fingers resting on either side of his saucer. He looked, if anything, like a man who'd been waiting for a bus and seen it come round the corner more or less on time.

"You knew," Harry said.

"I have been reading your face for seven years, Harry. You lied to me three times in a little under a minute. The first was competent. The second was adequate." Croaker picked up a piece of toast and examined it. "The third was dreadful. You have never once in your life said *yes, sir* that quickly. You say *yes, sir* the way other people say *I'll think about it*." He bit the corner off the toast. "I was curious how long it would take you."

"How long it would—"

"To call back." Croaker chewed. Swallowed. "I gave it two hours. You have come in at one hour and forty-one minutes. Slightly ahead of my estimate. I had assumed the other half of you would put up more of a fight." He glanced, very slightly, at Harry's hand on his chest, at the growl going under it. "It appears to be trying."

Harry stood there in his bare feet and couldn't think of one single thing to say.

"Well?" said Croaker. "You've rung me at a quarter to one in the morning, your time, to tell me you lied to me. I already know that. I assume there's more."

---

So Harry told him.

All of it, this time. Standing in front of the mirror with his hand on his chest and the wolf leaning on him from the inside the whole way through, he told the old man everything. The trigger, and the two wolves in the Lariat behind the specimen. The grey one and the sandy one. The badge. The clearing. The youngest wolf's head full of a word he shouldn't have known. A local witch coming out of thin air with her wand up, very good, much better than she had any right to be, and the starlings and the fire and the Colt.

And then the grey one. Coming at him across the moss. Taking off his sunglasses. Looking.

He told it badly. He knew he was telling it badly. He'd given a thousand reports in seven years and he'd never once told one like this, with the words coming out of order and his voice doing things he didn't tell it to do. He told Croaker about the line. About the way it had gone out of her chest and into his, and found the wolf, and come back, and tied off. About not being able to say *no* on the gravel bar. About her not being able to say *I don't care*. About her standing in the specimen room watching him do the Cruciatus and asking him if he liked it.

He told him about the Cullen boy, Edward, standing at the edge of the ward with his face cracked open, and the pack, and the Alpha in a pair of Transfigured sweatpants. About the promise he'd made, through her, about the child. About Thanksgiving.

Croaker listened to all of it without moving.

When Harry ran out, there was a long silence in the mirror. The old man's tea had gone cold. He hadn't touched it again.

"I'm compromised, sir," Harry said. It came out flat and quiet, the way it should have come out the first time. "Properly. You need to send someone else. Pull me out, put somebody else on the Lariat, somebody who can do the job without—" He stopped. Started again. "Without this. You can't have a man out here who's like this. Because if it ever comes to it, sir. If it ever comes down to the Department or her—" He made himself look straight into the glass. "It's her. Every time. Every single time. I'd walk out. I'd walk out of the Department and I'd burn whatever I had to on my way out the door, and I wouldn't lose a minute's sleep over it. You need to know that before you leave me out here."

The wolf, inside him, had gone very still.

It was listening. He could feel it listening. It didn't understand the words, but it understood the shape of them, the way dogs understand the shape of their names in a sentence. It had heard *her*. It had heard *every time*. It stood there in the dark under his ribs with its ears up and its whole body turned toward the mirror, waiting to see what the long grey face would do.

Croaker looked at him for a long, long moment.

"Sit down, Harry," he said.

Harry sat. There was a chair by the long table. He pulled it round so it faced the mirror and sat in it, in his bare feet, with his elbows on his knees.

"When I found you," Croaker said, "you were nineteen years old and sitting on the floor of the Death Chamber in the dark, with your back against the bottom step, talking to that arch. Do you remember?"

"Yes, sir."

"You'd broken in. You'd got past four wards and a Sphinx to do it, and you'd done it at two in the morning, and you were sitting on the floor talking to an arch." Croaker's voice didn't change. "I asked you what you wanted. Do you remember what you said?"

Harry did. He hadn't thought about it in years.

"I said I wanted to know what was on the other side."

"You said," Croaker corrected him, "that you wanted to know what was on the other side so that you could make sure nobody else ever had to find out the way you did. Those were your words. I wrote them down that night. I have them in a file." The pale eyes held his. "You have worked for this Department for seven years, Harry. You have done extraordinary things for it. Things I could not have asked of any other man in that building. And every one of them, every single one, you did for somebody."

"Sir—"

"For people. People you'll never meet, mostly. Some of them you have met. A girl in a village in Albania. Three goblins in a vault in Lyon. That Bulgarian's housekeeper." Croaker lifted one long hand off the desk and let it fall. "I knew that when I hired you. I have counted on it, every day since. I have never once been under the illusion that I came first with you, or that the Department did. A man who would sell the people he loves for the Volterra file would sell me for less. I don't employ those men. I don't trust them, and I've no use for them."

Harry didn't say anything. He couldn't.

"You are exactly where you need to be," said Croaker.

The wolf, in the dark, took a single step forward.

"You rang me," the old man went on, "at a quarter to one in the morning, against what I can only assume is the very loud objection of the other half of you, to tell me a truth that would never have come to light, and that you believed would cost you everything you've just found. That is integrity, Harry. It is the rarest thing in my department. It's the rarest thing in any department. I would not trade it for a hundred men who'd do as they're told." He picked up his cup, looked at the cold tea in it, and put it down again. "It is precisely why I will never be rid of you. You may as well stop asking."

Harry put his face in his hands.

He sat like that for a while, in the dark kitchen, with the rain on the canvas. He felt the wolf, inside him, stand there and look at the mirror. And then, very slowly, he felt it sit. It sat. On its haunches, in the dark, with its head tilted, like a dog that's just been told something it doesn't understand by somebody whose voice it has decided it trusts.

"So," Croaker said. "To business."

Harry lowered his hands.

"The Cullens are no longer category preserve. They are, as of tonight, category cultivate. Carefully. You'll have your word with the doctor when it suits him. You'll be honest with him, within reason. You'll report to me what you can, and you will tell me plainly when there's something you won't." A pause. "I would rather know there's a door I can't open than be told there's no door."

"Yes, sir."

"The protector line." Croaker made a small, precise note on something just below the frame. "If I have to make you the Department's liaison to the La Push protectors in order to keep you on that peninsula, then so be it. I'll write the memorandum myself, this morning. Halloran will have a fit. I shall enjoy that." He looked up. "Though I rather suspect it won't come to an argument. Given the sheer volume of supernatural activity in that one small town—a coven of eight, two packs, a child nobody in this building can classify, a native bond magic that has apparently crossed species and nationality in a single afternoon, a witch who builds wards *you* can't see, and now, God help them, you—I expect you'll be staying a good while longer regardless. It's the most interesting square mile on the planet at present. I'd have to be a fool to pull you out of it."

"And the witch, sir," Harry said. "I made her a promise. Tonight. I said I hadn't seen her. I'm not going to give you her name."

Croaker looked at him.

"Then don't," he said. "Keep it. A man who keeps his word to a stranger who's just tried to kill him will keep it to me." His mouth moved very slightly. "Though if she's as good as you say, I should like to know her name eventually. I'm always short of good people."

"I'll ask her."

"Do." Another note. "And the child."

The wolf's ears came up again. Harry felt it.

"I told the Cullen boy," Harry said, "through her, that nobody touches that kid while I'm here." He held the old man's eyes. "I meant it. I'm not taking it back, sir. Not for Halloran, not for the Board, not for you."

"I wouldn't ask you to," Croaker said. "While you're there, it's the Department's word too. I'll see that Halloran understands that, in words of one syllable, before lunch." He paused. "And when somebody asks, as you said this morning that somebody will, they'll be asking me. And I shall remember very clearly that I did not."

Harry let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

"And the bond itself," Croaker said. "The line. The Department would—"

"No."

Croaker stopped.

"No, sir," Harry said again. Quieter. "Not that. That's hers. That's not going in a file."

They looked at each other through the glass for a long moment. Then the old man nodded, once, slowly, the way he'd nod at a chess move he hadn't seen coming and didn't mind.

"Fair," he said. "Then one question. Not for the file. For me." He put his long hands together on the desk. "Does it hurt you?"

Harry thought about it. About the line in his chest, soft and easy now, with her breathing coming down it like a tide.

"No, sir," he said. "It's—" He didn't have the word. "It's about the furthest thing from that I've ever had."

Croaker looked at him a moment longer. Then something happened to the long grey face that Harry had seen happen exactly twice before, once at a funeral and once in 2002 when a junior researcher had set fire to Halloran's eyebrows. It was very small, and it was gone almost before it arrived.

"Then I'm glad," said Croaker. He picked up his cold tea and drank it anyway. "Go to bed, Harry. You have a dinner to attend."

"How do you—"

"You told me. A minute ago. Thanksgiving, at two o'clock, at her mother's house." The pale eyes glinted. "You said *there'll be pie* in a tone of voice I last heard from you in March of ninety-nine, when the canteen put treacle tart on for the first time since the war."

"That was a very good treacle tart, sir."

"So I gathered. You had four." Croaker reached for the edge of the frame. "And Harry."

"Sir?"

"Don't lie to me again."

"No, sir."

"Unless I ask about the witch."

"No, sir."

"Good night," said Croaker, and the mirror went grey.

---

Harry sat in the chair for a long time afterward.

The kitchen was dark and cold, and the rain came down on the canvas, and he didn't get up. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging loose between them and looked at the grey glass, and he felt the wolf.

It was still sitting. It had stopped growling somewhere in the middle of the old man's speech; he hadn't noticed when. It sat in the dark under his breastbone and looked at the mirror where the old face had been, with its head a little to one side. Like it was trying to work something out. Like it had expected to be hit and had been scratched behind the ear instead and didn't know what to do with that.

"Told you," Harry said to it, softly. "Didn't I tell you?"

The wolf looked at him. Then it looked away, northwest, toward the coast, toward a couch in La Push and a woman asleep on it with her mother's hand in her hair.

It turned round once, the way wolves do. And lay down. With its back very deliberately to the mirror.

Harry laughed. Quietly, alone, in the dark, for the second time that night. Then he got up and put the chair back and went down the corridor to his bedroom and lay down on top of the blanket with his hands on his chest.

This time he slept.

### II. Leah

*Earlier that night — Wednesday, November 22, 7:48 p.m.*

Edward left them at the top of the gravel bar.

He didn't say goodbye. He looked at Leah for a second, standing there barefoot with her hand flat on her chest, and she saw him decide not to say anything about what she was thinking, which for Edward was practically a hug. Then he was gone into the trees, east, toward the big glass house and his father, so fast the ferns didn't move until he'd already passed them.

She stood there a second longer.

*It's pulling,* she'd thought, and she'd known he heard it, and she didn't care.

It was. It went out of her chest behind her, back down the gravel bar to the place where the air had rippled shut, and it was humming, like a power line in the rain. It didn't hurt. That was what she couldn't get over. Every time she'd felt this thing in somebody else's head it had looked like it hurt. Sam staring at Emily across the kitchen like a man staring into a fire. Jared. Quil, with his little Claire. Jacob, ten weeks ago, on that bloody couch. It had always looked like it was tearing something out of them.

This just hummed.

She stripped off the T-shirt and the cutoffs and tied them to her ankle with the cord, and didn't look back at the empty air, and phased.

---

It hit her like walking into a room where everybody's already been talking about you.

They were all there. Jacob, out in front of her, russet and huge, with his ears flat and his whole mind gone tight and careful the way it went when he was trying not to say something. Seth, a little way up the slope with Layla on his back, half of him thinking about Layla and the other half thinking *Leah Leah Leah Leah* so loud it echoed. And out at the very edge, faint, from somewhere over by the Cullens' place, Embry. Embry on patrol. Embry, who'd phased in about ten minutes ago, gotten the whole evening dumped on him at once, and was still sitting down in the middle of a game trail with his mouth open.

*Leah,* Embry thought. *LEAH. You—did you—LEAH IMPRINTED?*

*Shut up, Embry.*

*On a GUY? In a SUIT? Who's—is he a—Leah, he caught FIRE with his HAND—*

*Shut* up*, Embry.*

*I'm just saying, I've been on patrol for nine hours and nothing has EVER happened on patrol and—*

*Embry,* Jacob thought. Just the name. Quiet.

Embry shut up.

And then it all came out of her.

She'd known it would. There wasn't anything you could do about it. She'd been on two legs for an hour and a half, inside that man's tent, inside the ward where nobody could hear her, and now she was back on four and it all went out of her and into them like water out of a bucket kicked over. She felt it go. She felt them get it.

The tent. Bigger on the inside. The kitchen and the records and the long scrubbed table. The white room. The jars. The tooth. The leech in its circle of blue fire, its head coming free, its scream. Harry going into its head, and the blood coming out of his nose. *Crucio*. Three seconds by his watch. The leech shaking. The phone. Demetri's voice, smooth as oil. *Don't make me come looking for you, Marek.* And Harry's face. And the closed door inside him, the room with sheets over the furniture.

*Not while I'm here.*

She felt Jacob take that one. Felt it land in him like a fist on a table. She felt him stop running for half a step.

And then the other stuff. The stuff she'd have given anything to keep.

Sam. The way she'd told it, flat, standing against the white wall. *He looked at her, and he stopped loving me.* And the other thing. The thing she'd never said out loud to anybody, ever, that every one of them had seen in her head a hundred times and never once said anything about. *I think I'm broken.*

And what he'd said back.

She felt them get to it. She braced. She'd been braced for this exact thing every time she phased, since March. For the pity. For the way they'd all go quiet and careful in her head, like people stepping around something on the floor.

Jacob started thinking about a carburetor.

He did it loudly. He did it with his whole mind, the way he used to when they were still in Sam's pack and he wanted to give somebody some privacy: a 1986 Volkswagen Rabbit carburetor, every single part of it, laid out on a workbench in his garage, all the little screws and springs and gaskets, in order, as fast and loud as he could think them. After a second Embry caught on and started thinking about a sandwich. A really big sandwich. Turkey and Swiss, with the good mustard.

And Seth didn't think about anything at all. Seth just went very quiet and warm in the back of her head, like he'd put his arm around her shoulders and wasn't going to say anything about it.

Leah ran.

She ran and let it go by, all of it, the white room and Sam and *broken* and *about the least broken thing I've ever seen*, and nobody looked at it. Not straight on. They let it go past like you'd let somebody go past you in a hallway when they've been crying, and they thought about carburetors and sandwiches until it was gone.

When it was over, she was halfway across the highway, and her eyes were stinging, which was stupid, because wolves didn't cry.

*Thanks,* she thought. To all of them. She didn't say what for.

*For what?* Jacob thought. He was still thinking about the carburetor, a little. *I was just thinking about my car.*

*You don't even have that car anymore. You sold it.*

*I miss it.*

Seth laughed. It went through the pack mind like warm water, and this time Leah let it, and didn't mind liking it.

---

They took Layla home first.

It was the right way to go anyway. The old Hollis place was out on the Calawah road, east of town, and La Push was west, and it wasn't really on the way at all, but nobody suggested anything else. Seth went up the drive at a walk and stopped at the bottom of the porch steps he'd rebuilt, and Layla slid off his back and stood there in her wet socks with her wand in her hand and her sweater missing a sleeve.

The kitchen light was on. The door was standing open. Leah could see through it from the yard, with her wolf eyes, the floor of the kitchen with a coffee mug lying broken on the linoleum and a black-and-white notebook face down next to it in a puddle of cold coffee.

Layla looked at it too.

"I dropped it," she said. "When the knot went. I didn't even—" She stopped. She stood there looking at her own kitchen floor.

Seth pushed his nose into her stomach. Very gently. She put both her arms around his head and held on for a long time.

*She's shaking,* Seth thought. *Leah. She's shaking.*

*I know.*

*She's never been scared of anything. She walked into the Cullens' house. She told Charlie she'd take care of me. She's never—*

*She got blown up today, Seth.* Leah sat down on the wet grass at the edge of the yard. *By a guy who could've done it a lot worse. She knows that. That's what she's scared of.*

Layla let go of Seth's head. She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, the one with no sleeve. Then she looked past him, across the yard, at Leah.

"Leah," she said.

Leah looked back at her.

"I don't know what that was," Layla said. "In the clearing. I don't know what he is. I've never seen anything like him, I've never even *heard* of anything like him, and I've spent my whole life around people who do magic. And he threatened me. And he held Seth down. And then you looked at him and he—" She stopped. "And you went after him. Into the dark. Alone."

Leah didn't move.

"Is it the thing?" Layla said. "The thing Seth has. With me. The imprint."

Leah couldn't answer her. Not with words. Not in this shape. So she did the only thing she could do: she held Layla's eyes, across the yard, and nodded. Once. Slowly. The way a person nods.

Layla stood there with her mouth a little open.

"Oh, Leah," she said softly. "Oh, honey."

Leah knew pity. She'd been swimming in it for two years. This sounded like a woman who'd buried her partner last spring and knew exactly how bad things could get, looking at another woman across a wet yard and saying *I'm sorry this is so hard*.

Leah looked away.

"Go home," Layla said. To Seth. Her voice was steadier. "Go with your sister. I'm fine. I'm going to clean up my kitchen and take a really, really hot bath and drink most of a bottle of wine. Go."

*I can stay,* Seth thought. *Leah, tell her I can stay—*

"Seth." Layla took his big sandy head in both hands again and turned it so he was looking at her. "Go with your sister. She needs you more than I do tonight." She kissed him on the end of the nose. "I'll see you tomorrow. At two. With the coffee." Her mouth went crooked. "Apparently."

She went up the steps and into the kitchen and shut the door.

Seth stood looking at it for a long moment.

Then he turned around and came back across the yard to Leah, and lay down next to her in the wet grass, and put his chin on her back.

*She's mad at him,* he thought. *Harry. She's really mad.*

*She's allowed.*

*Are you mad at him?*

Leah thought about it. She thought about it honestly, because there wasn't any other way to think in the pack mind, and because she found she wanted to know.

*I don't know,* she thought. *I don't think so. I think I'm mad at everything else.*

Seth didn't say anything. He just left his chin on her back.

After a while, Jacob came and lay down on her other side. He didn't say anything either.

They stayed like that for a few minutes, three wolves in the wet grass of a dead woman's yard, while the kitchen light went on upstairs and a bath started running. Then Jacob got up.

*Come on,* he thought. *Your mom's waiting.*

---

It was twelve miles to La Push, and they took it slow.

That was Jacob's doing, too. He set the pace at an easy lope, the pace for a long patrol, and kept it there, and didn't let Seth run ahead. Leah knew what he was doing. He was giving her time. She let him.

The rain had eased off to a drizzle. The clouds were breaking up a little out over the ocean, and somewhere behind them there was a moon, not much of one, a thin new sliver that came and went. They came down out of the hills behind the reservation and the trees opened up and she could smell the sea, salt and kelp and cold rock, and under it the woodsmoke from every chimney on the rez.

*Can I ask you something?* Jacob thought.

*You're going to anyway.*

*Do you want it?*

He didn't say what. He didn't have to.

Leah ran for a while without answering. She could have not answered. In the pack mind you couldn't lie, but you could shut up, and she was good at shutting up. She'd had a lot of practice.

But she thought about the gravel bar. About standing there in her bare feet with her fists clenched, trying to say *I don't care what happens to you*, and her jaw locking up around it like a trap.

*I don't know,* she thought. *I don't know if I want it. I didn't ask for it. Nobody asked me.* She felt the rope hum. *But nobody asked him, either. That's the thing, Jake. That's the thing I can't—nobody asked him. It went both ways. It took him exactly the same as it took me. And he stood there in the rain and he couldn't lie to me and he didn't* want *to, I could feel him not wanting to, and he said—*

*Because I don't want you to leave,* Jacob thought. Quietly. She'd shown them that, too. All of it.

*Yeah.*

*And you left anyway.*

*I left anyway.* She ran. *I wanted to stay. I wanted to stay so bad I could feel it in my teeth. And I left. Because Mom's making pies, and because you guys were standing out there in the rain, and because I'm not—* She didn't know how to finish it. *I'm not Emily. I'm not going to just—fall. I don't know what I'm going to do. But I'm going to be the one who does it.*

Jacob didn't say anything for a while.

*Good,* he thought finally.

That was all. Just *good*. She felt him mean it.

They came up the back way, through the trees behind the Clearwater place, where the yard ran down to the edge of the woods. The porch light was on. The kitchen light was on. There was a smell coming out of the kitchen window, through the screen, that stopped all three of them dead at the tree line like they'd hit a wall.

Butter. Brown sugar. Cinnamon. Pumpkin. Apples. Pastry, a lot of pastry. Something with pecans.

*Oh my God,* Seth thought. *She's doing all of them. She's doing* all *the pies.*

Leah phased.

---

The back porch had a plastic bin on it, the big kind, with a lid. It had been there since the spring. It had been her dad's idea, originally, or that was what Mom said: a bin by the back door with spare clothes in it, for the boys. Sweatpants, shorts, T-shirts, all the biggest sizes from the Goodwill in Port Angeles. Jacob dug through it in the dark and came out with a pair of gray sweats that were about four inches too short for him and put them on without comment. Seth had his cutoffs on his ankle. Leah had hers.

They stood on the porch for a second. All three of them. Looking at the back door.

"You want us to go first?" Jacob said.

"No."

"You want us to wait out here?"

Leah thought about it.

"No," she said. "Come in."

She opened the door.

Her mom was at the counter. She had flour up to both elbows and a rolling pin in one hand and her hair coming down out of its clip, and there were pies everywhere. On the table, on the stovetop, on top of the fridge. Two pumpkin. An apple with a lattice top. A pecan, still bubbling. A fourth crust, rolled out on the counter, waiting for something. The oven was on and the kitchen was hot and the windows were steamed up, and the radio on top of the fridge was playing some old country song very quietly, the way it always did when her mom was cooking and thought nobody was listening.

Sue Clearwater looked up.

She looked at Leah. Just at Leah. Not at Seth, who was behind her, or at Jacob, who was filling up the doorway. Leah watched her mother's face, and watched her see it. Whatever it was. Whatever was on Leah's face right now that Leah couldn't see and couldn't have named.

Her mom put down the rolling pin.

She came around the counter. She didn't wash her hands. She didn't say anything. She just came across the kitchen in three steps and put her arms around her daughter, flour and all, and held on.

Leah stood there in the doorway with her mother's arms around her and her mother's floury hands on her back, and she felt it come up in her like a wave over a sea wall. Everything. The whole day. The clearing and the leech and the rope and the white room and Sam and the word *broken*. She held onto it for one second, two. Then she put her face down into her mother's shoulder and let it come.

She didn't cry. She didn't think she cried. She just stood there and shook, the way Layla had been shaking, while her mom held onto her and didn't let go.

Somewhere thirteen miles east, she felt the rope go tight for a second. Like somebody at the other end had stopped what they were doing and put their hand flat on their chest.

---

Later, they sat at the kitchen table, all four of them, with the pies cooling around them, and Leah told her.

Seth helped. Seth helped too much. Seth kept jumping in with things like *and then he caught the fire, Mom, he CAUGHT it, like a BASEBALL* and *it's bigger on the inside, Mom, it's got a* kitchen *in it*, and Jacob kept kicking him under the table. Jacob told some of it too. The parts Leah didn't want to. The part about the curses. The part about the phone call and Demetri and *from your hand*, and what it meant, and how it meant that a man none of them had known existed this morning had just, very quietly, kept the Volturi from coming to look at Bella.

And at Nessie.

*Not while I'm here.*

Her mom listened to all of it with her hands wrapped around a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking. She didn't interrupt. She didn't ask questions until the end. She was like that. She'd been married to a man on the tribal council for twenty years and she'd learned how to listen to a whole story before she said anything about it.

When it was done, she sat for a long time, looking at the mug.

"It went both ways," she said finally.

"Yeah."

"You're sure."

"I felt it, Sue," Jacob said. "Through her. I was in her head when it happened. It went out of her and into him and it came back. I've never seen anything like it. Nobody has. Not in any of the stories."

Her mom nodded slowly.

"And he can't lie to you."

"No."

"And you can't lie to him."

"No. I tried. On the gravel." Leah looked at her own hands on the table. "I tried to tell him I didn't care what happened to him. It wouldn't come out. My jaw just—locked. Like a dog's."

Her mom's mouth did something. Leah couldn't tell if it was a smile.

"Well," Sue said. "That'll be good for you."

"*Mom*."

"I'm just saying. You've been telling people you don't care about things since you were four years old." She turned her mug around on the table. "What's his name?"

The kitchen went very quiet.

Seth looked at Leah. Jacob looked at the table. The radio on top of the fridge was playing something slow, a man singing about a train.

Leah had known this was coming. She'd known it since the gravel bar. Since the second he said it, standing there in the rain with his hands in his coat pockets, *Harry*, and the name had gone into her like a splinter and she'd shoved it down, hard, all the way down, and not looked at it once since. Not in the white room. Not in the pack mind. She'd run twelve miles without letting herself look at it.

"Harry," she said.

Her mother didn't move.

She sat at the kitchen table with her hands around her mug and looked at her daughter, and didn't move, and didn't say anything, for so long that Leah heard the clock on the wall go round. The pecan pie had stopped bubbling. The man on the radio had gotten on his train.

Then Sue Clearwater put one hand over her mouth.

It was a small movement. Just her hand, coming up, flat, over her lips. Her eyes didn't change. They stayed on Leah, dark and steady, and they went bright and wet and stayed steady anyway.

"Of course it is," her mom said, through her fingers. Very quietly. "Of course it is."

Leah didn't know what to say. She didn't know if she was supposed to say she was sorry. She didn't know if she *was* sorry.

"Mom—"

"No. No, honey. No." Her mother lowered her hand. She laughed. It was a strange, cracked, wet little laugh, and it came out of her like something that had been stuck for a long time. "Your father," she said, "would have thought that was the funniest thing in the world."

Seth made a small sound.

"He would," Sue said. She was still laughing, a little, and wiping her eyes with the side of her floury hand, which only made it worse, now there was flour on her face too. "He would have laughed until he fell off his chair. He'd have said—oh, Lord, he'd have said, *well, at least she won't have to learn a new name.*"

And Leah, who had not laughed since the gravy boat and before that not in a year, put her face down on the kitchen table between her arms and laughed until she couldn't breathe.

---

It was after eleven when Jacob left.

He stood on the back porch in his too-short sweatpants with a whole apple pie in a dish towel under his arm, because Sue had made him take it, *for your dad, Jacob, and you tell him I said it's for him and not for you*, and he looked at Leah through the screen door.

"I'm going to tell Sam," he said.

Leah had known that was coming too.

"Tonight?"

"He needs to know. Tonight. Before Paul gets it out of Embry tomorrow and it goes round the whole rez wrong." Jacob shifted the pie. "The Council's going to want to know, too. Dad, Old Quil. Your mom's already here. They'll want to see him, Leah. A guy like that, on our land, with a wolf in him—they're going to want to look at him."

"I know."

"You okay with that?"

Leah thought about Sam hearing it. Standing in his doorway, with Emily behind him in the kitchen. Hearing *Leah imprinted*. She waited for it to hurt the way it always hurt.

It hurt. A little. Mostly it felt like pressing on an old bruise and finding it had mostly healed while she wasn't looking.

"Tell him," she said. "He should hear it from you."

Jacob looked at her for a second longer. Then he nodded, and went down the steps, and jogged off into the dark with Sue's pie under his arm.

---

She didn't go up to her room. She couldn't face the stairs. She lay down on the couch in the living room under the old Pendleton blanket that had been on the back of it since she was a baby, with the TV off and the lamp off and the smell of pies coming in from the kitchen, and her mom came and sat on the end of the couch by her head.

Seth had gone up. She could hear him snoring through the ceiling already. He'd been asleep about forty seconds after he hit the pillow.

Her mom put her hand in Leah's hair. She didn't say anything. She just stroked it, slow, from the top of her head down to the back of her neck, the way she'd done when Leah was little and had a fever, the way she hadn't done since the night Sam came back from the Makah rez.

"Mom."

"Mm."

"Is it okay?"

Her mom's hand didn't stop.

"I don't know yet, baby," Sue said. "I haven't met him." A pause. "Is he good to you?"

Leah thought about the white room. About the closed door, and the man standing behind it while the leech screamed. About a curse that hurt more than anything in the world, spoken at a normal volume, like *excuse me*.

And about *broken's when something can't do what it's for*. And *I don't want you to leave*. And the sweatpants. And him turning his back on the gravel bar so fast, without thinking, before she'd even finished phasing.

"He does stuff, Mom," Leah said. "Bad stuff. For his job. I watched him do it. He didn't like it, but he did it, and he'll do it again." She stared up at the dark ceiling. "And he's good to me. He's—yeah. He's good to me. He can't be anything else to me. It won't let him."

Her mom's hand kept going. Slow. Top of her head, down to the back of her neck.

"He's coming tomorrow?"

"At two."

"Does he eat?"

Leah thought about the tent. The kitchen. The range. The smell of bacon and something with berries in it.

"I think he eats a lot."

"Good," said Sue. "Then I'll do the stuffing."

Leah closed her eyes.

She didn't mean to fall asleep. She meant to lie there a while and think about it, all of it, and figure out what she was going to do. But the couch was warm and her mom's hand was in her hair and the house smelled like pie, and somewhere out there, thirteen miles east, she could feel the rope.

It had gone soft.

She didn't know why. She didn't know what he was doing out there. She just felt it, all at once, the line between them going easy and slack in a way it hadn't been all night, like he'd stopped pulling on it. Like he'd sat down somewhere quiet, and stopped, and was just listening to something.

Leah lay on her mother's couch and listened to it with him.

And slept.

### III. Sue

*Thursday, November 23, 2006 — Thanksgiving*

The recipe card was in Harry's handwriting.

Sue had it propped against the sugar canister at five in the morning, the way she'd had it propped there every Thanksgiving since 1984. It was an index card, ruled, gone soft and brown at the corners, with a grease spot in the middle shaped like Idaho. *OYSTER DRESSING — HC*, it said at the top, in the big square capitals he'd used for everything, grocery lists and permission slips and the notes he used to leave on the fridge. *2 pints oysters (Neah Bay if you can get em). 1 loaf day-old bread. Celery. Onion. LOTS of butter. Don't be cheap Sue.*

She'd never once been cheap. She'd never once been allowed to forget it, either.

She stood at the counter in the dark with the turkey already in the oven and the coffee going and read the card through twice, though she knew it by heart. Then she got the oysters out of the fridge. She'd driven to Neah Bay for them on Monday, the way she did every year. It was a long drive for two pints of oysters that nobody but Charlie was going to eat.

She'd thought, Monday, driving back along the Strait with the bag on the seat beside her and the rain on the windshield, that maybe this was the year she'd stop. Maybe this was the year she'd do the sausage dressing, the regular kind, the kind the kids actually liked. It had been eight months. Charlie was a good man and he ate the oysters to be kind and she knew it.

She'd come home and put the oysters in the fridge anyway.

Now she stood there at five in the morning and thought about a man she'd never met who had her husband's name and ate a lot, and she got out the big bowl.

---

Leah came into the kitchen at seven.

She'd slept on the couch all night. Sue had sat with her until a little after midnight, with her hand in her daughter's hair, the way she hadn't been allowed to do in two years. Leah had slept like a little girl. Flat on her back, one arm flung up over her head, her mouth open. No twitching. No muttering. None of the hard, fast, angry breathing Sue had heard through the bedroom wall for two years, every night, like her daughter was running from something in her sleep.

She'd just slept.

Now she stood in the kitchen doorway with the Pendleton blanket around her shoulders and her hair flat on one side, and looked at the counter. At the oysters. At the card propped against the sugar.

"You're making it," Leah said.

"I'm making it."

Leah looked at the card for a while. Then she let the blanket drop onto a chair and came over to the counter and picked up the potato peeler out of the drawer.

Sue didn't say anything. She just moved over to make room.

Leah had never once in her life helped with Thanksgiving. Not when she was little, not when she was a teenager, not ever. She'd set the table under protest. She'd carried things out to the car. She'd said cooking was a trap and she wasn't falling into it, and she'd said it so loudly and so often that Harry used to call her *the union rep*. She'd peeled potatoes exactly once, when she was twelve, and taken off the top of her thumb, and declared it a sign.

She stood at the sink now and peeled ten pounds of russets without saying a word, and didn't take off any thumbs.

Sue chopped celery and watched her daughter out of the corner of her eye, and didn't say anything about it, because she'd learned a long time ago that the fastest way to stop Leah doing something was to notice.

At about the seventh potato, Leah stopped peeling.

She stood there with the peeler in one hand and the potato in the other and her head tilted, very slightly, toward the east. Toward the window over the sink, which looked out at the yard and the trees and, beyond them, a long way beyond them, the hills.

"He's up," Leah said.

Sue's knife stopped.

"You can feel that?"

"Yeah." Leah looked down at the potato. Her face did something complicated. "He's—he was asleep. He's awake now. He's—" She stopped. "He's eating something. A lot of something."

Sue went back to the celery.

"Well," she said, after a moment. "That's a good sign."

---

Charlie came at noon, the way he always did, with a six-pack of Rainier and a bag of ice and the Detroit game already on the radio in the cruiser. He came in the back door stamping his feet and saying *something smells good* before he even had his coat off, and kissed her on the cheek, and went to put the beer in the fridge, and stopped.

Leah was setting the table.

She was doing it properly. The good tablecloth. The good plates, the ones with the blue rim that had been Sue's mother's. Cloth napkins, folded. She was going around the table with the silverware in one hand, putting down forks, and she was frowning at each one like it had personally offended her, and straightening it.

Charlie looked at Sue. Sue looked back at him.

"Huh," said Charlie, and put the beer in the fridge, and went and sat down in front of the TV, and didn't ask. He'd gotten very good at not asking. It was one of Sue's favorite things about him.

Billy and Jacob came at one. Jacob was pushing the chair up the ramp Harry had built onto the front porch the year Billy's legs got bad, and he had a pie in a carrier on his lap that he was holding onto like it was the crown jewels.

"Esme," Jacob said, before Sue could ask, and handed it over. "She said to say happy Thanksgiving. And she said she hopes it's okay. She's been practicing. She's never had to make one for somebody who'd actually eat it before." He looked at the pie in Sue's hands. "She made, like, four, to get it right. Nessie ate the first one. Like, with her hands."

It was a cherry pie. The lattice on top was so perfect it looked like it had been done with a ruler. Sue was fairly sure it had.

"You tell her thank you," Sue said. "You tell her it's beautiful."

"She'll cry," Jacob said. "She can't, but she'll do the face."

Billy rolled himself in over the threshold and stopped in the middle of the living room and looked around. At the TV, where the Lions were losing, the way the Lions lost every Thanksgiving. At Charlie, who raised a beer at him. At the table, laid with the good plates. At Leah, standing in the kitchen doorway with her arms crossed, in a dark blue sweater Sue hadn't seen her wear in two years and her hair brushed and pinned up off her neck.

His eyes stayed on Leah a long moment. Billy had known her since she was born. He'd held her at her naming. Sue watched him look at her, at whatever was on her face, and watched his own face go thoughtful and still.

"Leah," he said.

"Billy."

"You look nice."

"Shut up, Billy."

He smiled. It was a slow, deep, private smile, the kind Billy Black hadn't smiled much since Harry's funeral. "Jake told me," he said. "Last night. And then he went and told Sam. And then he came home and ate a whole apple pie standing up in the kitchen and told me again." He looked at Sue. "Old Quil wants to see him."

"I know."

"Tonight, maybe. Or this weekend."

"I know, Billy."

"I'm just saying." Billy turned his chair to face the TV. "If I'm going to have to sit through a meeting about a strange man with a wolf in him, I'd like to have eaten first."

---

Seth and Layla came at half past one.

Layla had a bag of coffee in one hand. It was the expensive kind, with the gold foil and the picture of a mountain on it. She came in the front door with Seth behind her and stopped and held up the bag and looked at Sue with an expression Sue couldn't quite read. Like a woman who'd been told to bring something to a party by somebody she didn't like, and had brought it anyway, and was furious with herself about it.

"I brought the coffee," Layla said.

"Thank you, honey."

"He told me to bring the coffee."

"I heard."

"I'm only bringing it because it's good coffee," Layla said. "And because it's Thanksgiving. And because you'd want good coffee. That's the only reason."

"Of course," said Sue.

Layla had a bruise on her left cheekbone, yellow-green at the edges. She'd tried to cover it with makeup and mostly succeeded. She'd also put on a dress, a dark green one, and boots, and she'd done her hair, and Sue noticed that she kept her right hand near her left forearm the whole time, near the inside of her sleeve, the way you'd keep your hand near a pocket you'd put something valuable in.

Seth kissed his mother. Then he looked around the house. At Charlie and Billy at the TV. At Jacob, already eyeing the pies. At the table. At his sister in the kitchen doorway in her blue sweater with her hair pinned up.

"Whoa," Seth said. "Leah. You look—"

"Say it," Leah said. "Say it and see what happens."

"—nice," Seth finished, and ducked behind Layla.

---

At ten minutes to two, Leah stopped talking in the middle of a sentence.

She'd been arguing with Jacob about something. Sue hadn't been listening. Something about Esme's pie, and whether you were allowed to eat dessert first if the dessert had been made by a vampire. And then Leah just stopped. Her head came round, the way it had at the sink that morning. East. Toward the road.

Everybody in the room noticed. Jacob noticed. Seth, who'd been trying to get a fork into the pecan pie when he thought nobody was looking, noticed and froze with the fork in the air. Billy noticed.

Charlie, at the TV, said, "What?"

Then Sue heard it too.

It was an engine. A motorcycle engine. Coming down the road from the highway. It was a low, deep, old-sounding engine, a thick grumbling rumble that didn't sound anything like the bikes the boys rode around the rez. It got louder. It came round the corner by the Ateara place and down the hill and it slowed, and changed gear, and turned into the gravel at the end of Sue's driveway.

Jacob was at the front window before anybody else could move.

"Oh my *God*," Jacob said.

Sue went to the window.

It was raining again, a fine soft rain, the gray afternoon light coming down through it. And at the end of her driveway, between Charlie's cruiser and Billy's truck, a man was getting off a motorcycle.

The bike was black. It was old, really old, older than anything Sue had seen on a road in years, all chrome and black paint and curves, with a round headlamp and a long low seat and spoked wheels, and it shone like it had been polished that morning. Which, looking at it, it probably had.

And the man was—

Sue had been married to a tribal councilman for twenty years. She'd gone to Olympia with Harry, and to Washington, D.C., twice, for meetings with men from the Department of the Interior and the Bureau of Indian Affairs and once a senator. She'd seen money. She knew what it looked like when it walked into a room. She knew the difference between men who had money and men who wanted you to know they had money, and how the first kind dressed, which was quietly, and how you only noticed afterward that everything they were wearing had been made for them and nobody else.

The man getting off the motorcycle in her driveway was wearing a suit that had been made for him.

It was navy blue. Dark, heavy, soft-looking, the kind of wool that didn't shine. Three pieces, the vest buttoned. A shirt the color of fresh cream, and a tie, dark green, knitted silk, the exact green of something Sue couldn't see from the window yet. Over all of it, a long overcoat the color of a camel, hanging open, falling just past his knees, and when he swung his leg off the bike it moved like it weighed almost nothing. Brown shoes, polished so deep they looked like dark glass. A white handkerchief in the breast pocket, folded in a straight line.

He took a black umbrella out of a leather bag on the back of the bike and opened it with one hand, and stood there in the rain under it for a second, looking at her house.

He was tall. Broad through the shoulders, built heavy, built the way the boys were built. Dark hair, cut short and fresh, like he'd been to a barber that morning. Clean-shaven.

He had a lot of things in his arms.

"What," Charlie said, beside her, "is *that*."

Sue didn't answer. She couldn't take her eyes off him.

He shut the umbrella. He came up the gravel drive with it hooked over one arm and the rest of his arms full, and he came up the porch steps, Harry's porch steps, and stopped at the front door. And didn't knock. Just stood there. Like he was giving the house a second to get ready.

Sue looked over her shoulder.

Leah was standing in the middle of the living room. She hadn't moved since the engine. She was standing with her arms at her sides, in her blue sweater, with her hair up, very still, staring at the front door like she could see through it. Her face was doing the thing it had been doing since last night that Sue couldn't name.

"Leah," Sue said quietly. "Do you want to get it?"

Leah swallowed.

"You get it," she said. "It's your house."

So Sue went and opened the door.

---

He was standing on the welcome mat with his arms full.

An armful of flowers, wrapped in brown paper. White chrysanthemums, big ones, and branches of something silvery-green that smelled like eucalyptus, and in among them some long stems of red winter berries. A wooden box, the slatted kind wine comes in, with French on the side and six bottle-necks showing through the slats. Under that, two pie boxes from the diner in Forks, white cardboard, tied with string. And on top of the pie boxes, balanced, a bag of coffee with gold foil and a picture of a mountain on it.

He looked at her over the flowers.

His eyes were green. That was what the tie was. The tie was the exact color of his eyes, and they were the greenest eyes Sue had ever seen in her life, dark and clear and very steady, and they were looking at her the way you'd look at somebody you'd been told a great deal about and had been nervous to meet.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said.

The voice was English. Leah had said that. Leah had said *he talks like the Queen*, and Seth had done an impression of it at the kitchen table that Sue had made him stop. It was lower than Seth's impression. Rougher at the edges. Polite.

"Sue," she said.

"Sue." He said it carefully. Like he was putting it somewhere he'd remember. "I'm Harry."

She'd known that. She'd known it since last night at the kitchen table. She'd had a whole night and a whole morning to know it.

It still went into her like a hook.

She stood in her own front doorway and looked up at this big strange man in his beautiful suit with his arms full of flowers, and she heard her husband's name come out of his mouth, in that voice, and for a second she couldn't breathe.

He saw it. She watched him see it. Something went across his face, quick and quiet.

"I know," he said. Softly. Just to her. "I'm sorry. Your son thinks very loudly, and he was thinking about his dad a great deal yesterday." A pause. "I'm sorry for your loss."

Sue looked at him.

"He'd have hated your suit," she said.

It came out of her before she could stop it. And the man on her doorstep blinked, once, and then his face broke open into something that she would remember for a long time afterward. It was a grin, sudden and crooked and real, all the way up into those green eyes, like she'd just told him the best joke he'd heard all year.

"Most honest men do," he said. "Can I come in? Only, these are heavy, and I think the coffee's about to go."

Sue stood back and let him in.

---

He stopped two steps inside the door and stood there with his arms full and looked around her living room.

At Charlie, standing by the window with his beer in his hand and his mouth slightly open. At Billy, in his chair by the TV, with his hands folded in his lap and his eyes very sharp. At Jacob, still at the other window, staring past him through the glass at the motorcycle in the driveway like a man looking at a sunrise. At Seth, who was grinning so hard it looked like it hurt. At Layla, beside Seth, with a bag of coffee in her hand that was exactly identical to the bag of coffee balanced on top of his pie boxes.

Layla looked at his coffee. Then at her coffee. Then at him.

"You *told* me to bring it," she said.

"I did," he agreed. "And then I thought, what if she doesn't? What if she's still cross? Can't risk Thanksgiving on that." He nodded at her. "Miss Hollis. You look very well. How's the head?"

"Still attached."

"Glad to hear it."

And then his eyes went past all of them, past Layla and Seth and Billy and Charlie, and found Leah.

She was still standing in the middle of the room. She hadn't moved. She stood there in her blue sweater with her arms at her sides and her hair up off her neck and looked back at him.

Sue had seen a lot of things in her life. She'd seen Sam Uley look at Emily Young across a kitchen and fall into her like a man going off a cliff. She'd seen Jared and Paul and Quil. She'd seen her own son walk in the back door in October with a face like the sun coming up. She'd seen her husband look at her, once, across a high school gym in 1979, and decided on the spot that she was going to marry him, and done it.

She'd never seen anything like this.

They didn't move. Neither of them. They just looked at each other, across her living room, the big man in the doorway with his arms full and her daughter in the middle of the rug. It was both of them, falling toward each other at the same time. Like two people leaning toward each other across a table without knowing they were doing it. Like a rope pulled tight between them that they were both holding.

"Hi," Leah said.

"Hello," said Harry.

"You wore a suit."

"I did."

"It's Thanksgiving. On the rez. Charlie's wearing a Seahawks sweatshirt."

"It's a very nice sweatshirt," said Harry, without looking away from her. "Chief Swan. Good afternoon."

"Evans," said Charlie slowly.

Nobody else said anything.

Then Leah crossed the room. She did it fast, three steps, and stopped right in front of him, close, closer than Sue had seen her stand to anybody in two years. And she reached up, and took hold of his green silk tie, right below the knot, and straightened it.

It hadn't been crooked. Sue could see from the doorway that it hadn't been crooked at all.

Leah held onto it for one second too long. Then she let go and took the flowers out of his arms.

"These are for Mom," she said.

"They are."

"I'll put them in water."

"Thank you."

She turned around and walked into the kitchen with an armful of chrysanthemums and eucalyptus and her back very straight, and didn't look at anybody.

Harry stood there with his wine and his pies and his coffee and watched her go. His face had gone very quiet.

Then he seemed to remember there were other people in the room. He turned and looked at Sue, and at the rest of them, and cleared his throat.

"Right," he said. "Where would you like the wine?"


---

### IV. Charlie

*The same afternoon*

It was the moss guy.

Charlie stood by the window with a Rainier going warm in his hand and watched the moss guy hand Sue a case of wine like he was handing her a sack of potatoes, and tried very hard to make it make sense.

Harry Evans. Photographer. Manchester, England. Corner booth at the diner, back to the wall, two eggs and a lot of bacon at ten, cheeseburger and pie at four. Tipped thirty percent. Said *cheers* to Darlene when she brought the check. Wore a beat-up waxed jacket and a flannel shirt and jeans with paint on them and had a camera bag that looked like it had been dragged behind a truck. Had told Charlie, the second time they'd sat at the counter together, with every sign of meaning it, that he'd come four thousand miles to photograph moss.

The man standing in Sue Clearwater's living room was wearing a suit that cost more than Charlie's cruiser.

Charlie wasn't a clothes guy. He'd worn the same brown sport coat to every wedding and funeral in Clallam County since 1991. But he'd been a cop for twenty-five years, and you didn't do that long without learning what money looked like, and this was the real thing. The quiet kind. The kind that didn't have a logo on it anywhere because it didn't need one. The coat alone. The shoes. The way the jacket sat on his shoulders, like it had been built around him, which it probably had.

And Leah had straightened his tie.

Leah Clearwater. Who Charlie had once watched bite a kid at a birthday party when she was six because he'd touched her cake. Who'd grown up hard and fast and then gone harder, these last couple of years, after the Uley kid and then after Harry, until there were days Charlie couldn't look at her without his chest hurting. Leah had walked across the room in a blue sweater with her hair up and straightened this man's tie like it was the most natural thing in the world, and then she'd taken his flowers and walked off into the kitchen with her ears gone red.

Charlie waited until the man was occupied with Billy, who'd rolled himself over and was looking up at him with his sharpest face, the one he used at council meetings. Then he went into the kitchen.

Sue was at the sink with the chrysanthemums, putting them into the big blue vase. Leah wasn't there. Charlie could hear her somewhere upstairs, walking back and forth over his head, fast.

"Sue," Charlie said quietly.

"Mm-hm."

"That's the moss guy."

"I know, honey."

"The *moss* guy, Sue. From the diner. He told me he was here to take pictures of moss."

"I'm sure he does take pictures of moss."

"He's wearing a suit that costs more than my *car*."

"It's a very nice suit."

Charlie stood there. Sue went on putting flowers in the vase. She did it carefully, one stem at a time, turning each one so the big white heads faced out, and she didn't look at him.

"Is he," Charlie said. He lowered his voice even more. "Is he a need-to-know thing?"

Sue put the last chrysanthemum in the vase. She turned it so it faced out. Then she looked up at him, and her eyes were bright, and she put one floury hand on his arm.

"Oh, honey," she said. "You have *no* idea."

Charlie looked at her for a long moment.

"Figured," he said, and went and got another beer.

---

When he came back through the living room, the moss guy was gone.

Charlie found him through the front window. He was out in the driveway, in the rain, with his long camel-colored coat on and his black umbrella open, standing next to his motorcycle. Jacob Black was crouched down beside it in the gravel in a T-shirt, getting soaked, with his hand flat on the gas tank like it was a horse he was trying to calm down. The moss guy was holding the umbrella over both of them.

The window was cracked an inch at the bottom. Sue always cracked it on Thanksgiving, because of the oven. Charlie didn't mean to listen. He just happened to be standing there.

"—fifty-nine," Jacob was saying. "It's a fifty-nine. Tell me it's a fifty-nine."

"It's a fifty-nine."

"*Bonneville*."

"T120. First year they made them."

"Oh my God." Jacob ran his hand along the tank. Slowly. Like he was afraid it would bite. "It's original. The paint's not original. Somebody's redone the paint. But the engine's—is that the original engine?"

"Most of it. Some of it's been rebuilt. Twice." The moss guy's voice was different out here. Easier. "It belonged to my godfather. It's been in two crashes and a war. And a good long while in a shed in Devon, in about four hundred pieces in cardboard boxes, before I put it back together."

"*You* rebuilt it?"

"Took me a year. Had help. A friend's dad. He knows more about engines than anyone I've ever met, and he's never once got one to do what it's supposed to do."

Jacob laughed. He looked up at the man holding the umbrella over him, and Charlie, through the window, saw his face. He'd known Jacob Black since the kid was in diapers, too. He'd seen him happy and he'd seen him broken and he'd seen him come back from wherever he'd run off to last summer, harder and older. He hadn't seen him look at a grown man like that since Harry Clearwater used to take him and Seth fishing.

"What war?" Jacob said.

The moss guy was quiet for a second.

"Long story," he said. "Come on. You're getting soaked, and Sue'll kill me if you track gravel in."

"Can I ride it? Later? Not now. Later."

"No."

"*Please*—"

"Ask me again after the pie," said the moss guy, "and I'll think about it."

Charlie moved away from the window before either of them turned round.

---

They sat down at three.

Sue's table was a big old oak thing that Harry Clearwater had built himself, the first year they were married, out of a tree that had come down behind the house in a storm. It sat ten if everybody liked each other and eight if they didn't. Today it sat nine, and Charlie sat at the end of it across from Billy, with Sue on his left and the moss guy on his right, and Leah across from the moss guy, between Seth and Jacob.

Charlie hadn't set the seats. Sue had. He was starting to suspect Sue had set a lot of things.

There was the turkey, which Charlie had been allowed to carry to the table but not to carve; Billy carved, from his chair, with an old bone-handled knife that had been his father's. There were the potatoes, a mountain of them, with a lake of butter in the middle. Green beans with the crispy onions. Sweet potatoes with marshmallows on top, because Seth had cried about it once when he was five and Sue had made them every year since. Cranberry sauce from the can, with the ridges, because it wasn't Thanksgiving without the ridges. Rolls.

And at Sue's elbow, in the big brown crock, the dressing.

Charlie knew that crock. He'd eaten out of it every Thanksgiving since he was twenty-three years old. Harry's oyster dressing. It was gray and lumpy and it smelled like low tide and butter, and Harry had loved it more than any food on earth, and so had Charlie, mostly because Harry had. Nobody else at the table had touched it in years. The kids called it *the swamp*. Sue made it anyway. She made it every year, a whole crock, and Charlie ate as much of it as he could hold so the crock would go back to the kitchen looking like somebody had loved it, and the rest of it went in the fridge, and in a week it went in the trash, and nobody ever said anything.

Billy said grace.

He said it the way Billy said most things, quietly, looking at his hands. He thanked the Creator for the food and for the hands that made it. He thanked the sea and the forest. He said a few words for the ones who weren't at the table this year, and he didn't say any names, and he didn't have to. Then he looked up, and his eyes went round the table, and stopped on the moss guy for a moment, and then moved on.

"And for the new faces," Billy said. "Welcome."

"Thank you, Mr. Black," said the moss guy.

"Billy."

"Billy."

Then Sue picked up her glass.

There was wine in it. The moss guy's wine. He'd opened a bottle while Sue was getting the turkey out, using a corkscrew he'd taken from the inside pocket of his very nice jacket, which Charlie had noticed and decided not to think about. It was dark red, almost black in the glass. Everybody at the table had some, even Seth, who was nineteen and therefore, in Charlie's professional opinion, technically not supposed to, and Charlie had decided not to think about that either.

"To Harry," Sue said.

The table went quiet.

She was looking at her glass. Not at anyone. Her voice was steady. "He'd have liked today," she said. "He'd have liked—" She stopped. She looked up, and her eyes went down the table, past Charlie, to the man on his right. The big stranger in the beautiful suit, with the green tie, with her husband's name. She looked at him for a long moment, and the moss guy looked back and didn't look away.

"He'd have liked all of it," Sue said. "To Harry."

"To Harry," said everyone.

Charlie drank. Then he stopped, with the glass still at his mouth, and looked at it.

He wasn't a wine guy. He was a Rainier guy. He'd had wine at weddings and wine at Renée's parents' house back in the day, and it had all tasted more or less like grape juice that had gone wrong. This tasted like—he didn't know what it tasted like. It tasted like it had been in a cellar somewhere since before he was a cop, and had been waiting there, patiently, for this exact afternoon.

At the other end of the table, Layla Hollis had put down her glass and was staring at the bottle in the middle of the table. She reached out and turned it, slowly, so she could read the label.

Her face went very still.

"That's an eighty-two," she said.

"Is it?" said the moss guy.

"That's a nineteen eighty-two Latour." Layla looked up at him. "I did estate work. For three years. We pulled two cases of that out of a dead man's cellar in Taos once and the auction house about wet themselves. That's a—" She looked at the case on the sideboard, with five more bottle-necks sticking out of the slats. "That's a *thousand-dollar bottle of wine*. At least. And you've brought *six*."

The table went very quiet.

Charlie put his glass down. Carefully. With both hands.

"Is it?" said the moss guy again. He looked at the bottle, and then around the table, and Charlie watched him register that everybody was staring at him. "Well. It's very nice. It's been sitting in a cellar for about twenty years doing nothing, and I thought it'd like to get out." He picked up his glass and drank from it. "Seemed a shame to leave it there."

"Where," said Charlie slowly, "does a guy who takes pictures of moss get a case of thousand-dollar wine, Evans?"

The moss guy looked at him.

Across the table, Charlie saw Leah look up from her plate. She was watching the man's face. Watching it the way Charlie watched faces in an interview room, close and steady, waiting for something.

"Family money," the moss guy said. "My parents. And my godfather, after them." He turned his glass by the stem. "They all died young. I was a baby, the first time. Seventeen, the second. They left me rather a lot of it and not much else, and I've never known quite what to do with it, so mostly I don't." He looked up. "Seemed like a good day to do something with it."

Charlie sat there.

He'd heard a lot of people tell him a lot of things across a lot of tables. He knew what it sounded like when somebody was lying to him and what it sounded like when they weren't, and the second kind was a lot rarer than people thought. This was the second kind. He'd have bet his badge on it.

Across the table, Leah looked back down at her plate. She didn't say anything. But her face had gone softer.

"Sorry," Charlie said gruffly. "About your folks."

"Thank you, Chief."

"Charlie."

"Charlie." The moss guy picked up his fork. Then he looked down the table, past Charlie, at the big brown crock at Sue's elbow. "Sue," he said. "What's that?"

---

Later, Charlie would think that was the moment he decided, and that when it came down to it, it came down to the dressing.

Sue looked at the crock. She looked at the man in the suit.

"Oyster dressing," she said. "It was Harry's. My husband's. His recipe." A pause. "Nobody eats it but Charlie."

"I love oysters," the moss guy said.

Sue didn't move.

Then she picked up the big spoon and the crock and passed it down the table, past Charlie, and Charlie took it and passed it to his right, and the moss guy took it in both hands.

He took a real spoonful. A big gray lumpy heap of it, right next to his turkey, and then he took another one on top of that, and passed the crock back. Then he picked up his fork and ate some.

Everybody at the table was watching him. Even Billy. Even Layla. Seth had his fork halfway to his mouth and had frozen there.

The man chewed. He swallowed. He closed his eyes.

"Oh, that's criminal," he said quietly. "That's *bloody* brilliant."

He opened his eyes and looked at Sue down the length of the table. "Is that butter? It's a lot of butter."

"*Lots* of butter," said Sue. Her voice had gone strange. "It says on the card. *Don't be cheap.*"

"He was right." He ate another forkful. And another. He ate the whole first heap and half of the second while everybody else at the table watched him, and then he looked up and saw them all watching, and stopped with the fork in his hand. "What?"

Nobody said anything.

Sue was looking at him. She was smiling. Her eyes were very wet. She didn't wipe them. She just sat there at the end of her husband's table and watched this big strange Englishman eat her husband's dressing like it was the best thing he'd ever put in his mouth, and smiled, and cried a little, and didn't care who saw.

Charlie put his hand over hers on the tablecloth.

"Pass the swamp," Charlie said. "Evans. Pass it back down."

The moss guy passed it back down.

Charlie took a big heap. Then another one, on top of it, the way the moss guy had. And he looked down the table, past the turkey and the potatoes and the sweet potatoes with the marshmallows, at the man in the suit, and the man in the suit looked back at him. For a second, neither of them said anything. Two men, eating oysters, at the end of a table full of people who wouldn't.

"Finally," said Charlie. "Somebody."

The moss guy raised his glass of thousand-dollar wine to him, very slightly, across the table. Charlie raised his own.

Across the table, Leah Clearwater put her fork down and laughed, quietly, into her napkin.

---

He didn't know anything about football.

That came out somewhere around the second helping of everything, when the Dallas game came on in the other room and Jacob and Seth started craning their necks at the TV through the doorway. The moss guy looked over his shoulder at the screen, where a lot of very large men in helmets were standing around in a field, and watched them for about thirty seconds with an expression of deep concern.

"What are they doing?" he said.

"Huddling," said Charlie.

"For how long?"

"Little bit."

"And then what?"

"Then they run into each other."

The moss guy watched the screen. The men finished huddling and ran into each other. Then a whistle blew and they all stopped and stood around again.

"How long does this go on?" he said.

"Game? About three hours."

The moss guy turned around and looked at Charlie with genuine horror. "Three *hours*? Of men standing about in a field?"

"What do you watch, then?" said Charlie. "In England?"

The man thought about it.

"Cricket," he said.

Charlie had heard of cricket. He had a vague idea that it involved men in white sweaters and a flat bat and a lot of nothing happening.

"How long does a cricket game go?"

"Test match?" The moss guy picked up a roll. "Five days."

There was a pause.

"*Five days*," said Charlie.

"With breaks for lunch," said the moss guy, with dignity. "And tea."

Charlie looked at him. Then at the TV. Then back at him. And then he started laughing. He hadn't meant to. It came up out of him from somewhere down around his belt, and he sat there at the end of Harry's table and laughed until his eyes watered, and the moss guy sat there with his roll and his eyebrows up, looking mildly offended, which only made it worse.

"Tea," Charlie managed. "*Tea*."

"It's very civilized," said the moss guy. "You'd like it. You strike me as a man who appreciates a good long sit."

Sue was laughing too, now. So was Billy.

---

It was after the pie that it got strange.

Charlie had been waiting for it, a little. He'd been waiting for it since the man walked in. Not for anything in particular. Just for the moment, the one there always seemed to be lately, when everybody at the table went quiet and looked at each other, and Charlie knew it was time for him to go get some more coffee.

The pie was good. There was a lot of it. Sue's four, and Esme Cullen's cherry, which was the most beautiful pie Charlie had ever seen and which Seth ate a third of before anybody could stop him, and two marionberry pies from the diner in Forks, which the moss guy had brought, and which Charlie recognized because they were Darlene's.

"I know these pies," Charlie said.

"Darlene sends her regards," said the moss guy. "And says to tell you she knows you've been sneaking the Danish on Tuesdays and your cholesterol's going to kill you."

"How does Darlene know about my cholesterol?"

"Darlene knows about everyone's cholesterol. Darlene told me about her gallbladder on my first morning. In detail. Before I'd ordered."

So it was going fine. It was going great, actually. And then Billy, across the table, put his fork down and folded his hands on top of the tablecloth and looked at the moss guy, and said, in a perfectly ordinary voice:

"Jacob says you've got a wolf."

The table went quiet.

Charlie looked at Billy. Then at Sue. Then at the moss guy, who'd gone very still with his coffee cup halfway to his mouth, and whose eyes had gone, just for a second, straight to Charlie.

Charlie knew that look. He'd been getting it a lot lately. It was the look of somebody who'd just realized there was a cop at the table.

Charlie put his napkin down.

"Need to know," he said, to nobody in particular, and stood up. "I'm going to get more pie."

He took his plate into the kitchen.

He didn't mean to listen. He really didn't. He stood at Sue's counter with his back to the living room and cut himself a very slow piece of pumpkin pie, the slowest piece of pie anybody had ever cut, and the kitchen doorway was right there, and the house wasn't very big.

"Yes," the moss guy said, behind him. Quietly.

"Were you born with it?" Billy said.

"No."

There was a pause. Charlie cut his pie.

"I'll tell you a story," Billy said. "It's an old one. Leah and Seth have heard it a hundred times. Jacob's heard it more. You've got time for an old man's story, Harry?"

"I've got time."

So Billy told it.

Charlie had heard bits of it before, over the years. At bonfires, when the kids were little, with Billy and Harry and Old Quil taking turns. He'd never paid much attention. He paid attention now, standing in Sue's kitchen with a piece of pie he wasn't eating.

It was about a chief. A long time ago, Billy said, before the white men came. A chief named Taha Aki, who was a great spirit man, who could leave his body and walk around as a spirit, the way the warriors in those days could. And one day while he was out of his body, a man named Utlapa, who wanted to be chief, stole it. Just climbed into it and walked off with it. And Taha Aki was left a spirit, with no body to go back to, and he watched Utlapa wear his face and do terrible things with it to his people.

"He couldn't go home," Billy said. "His body was taken. He was just a spirit, drifting, with nowhere to be. And he went into the forest, grieving, and he found a wolf."

Charlie had stopped cutting the pie.

"A big wolf," Billy said. "Bigger than any wolf there'd ever been. And Taha Aki asked the wolf if he could share its body. Just for a while. Just long enough to go home and set things right. And the wolf let him in." A pause. "And when Taha Aki came back to his people, he came back as a wolf. And when he got to the village, and saw what had been done, and stood in front of Utlapa wearing his stolen face—he changed. Into a man. A man with his own face. The wolf had given it back to him. Or they'd made it together, the two of them. The stories don't say which."

Nobody said anything in the living room. Charlie could hear the TV, low, some announcer talking about a fumble.

"Taha Aki wasn't born with his wolf," Billy said. "It came to him when he had nothing. When everything had been taken from him and there was nothing left of him but a spirit with nowhere to go. And it chose him. And he chose it back." Another pause. "Our wolves come in the blood now. But the first one didn't. The first one came to a man who'd lost everything, in the dark, and asked him in."

There was a long silence.

"Did yours choose you?" Billy said.

Charlie stood very still in the kitchen.

The moss guy didn't answer for a long time. So long that Charlie thought maybe he wasn't going to. And when he did, his voice was different. It was quieter than Charlie had heard it all day. It was the voice of a man who'd been asked something he didn't get asked very often.

"I'd like to think," the moss guy said, "that we chose each other."

Charlie heard Billy breathe out.

"Then I'll tell the Council," Billy said, "that you're a friend of the tribe. Until you give me a reason to say otherwise."

"I'll take that, Billy. Thank you."

"Old Quil will want to see you. Tomorrow night, maybe. At my house."

"I'll be there."

"Don't wear the suit," said Billy. "Old Quil will think you're from the government."

And the moss guy laughed. A short, surprised, real laugh. And then a funny little pause, like he'd thought of something, and Charlie thought he heard him say, very softly, so softly it was almost under his breath, *Well. He's not wrong.*

Charlie took his pie and went back to the table, and sat down, and ate it, and didn't say anything at all.

---

He caught the man on the porch afterward.

It was dark by then, a little after six. The rain had stopped. Everybody else was inside. Seth and Jacob were fighting over the last of Esme's pie and Sue and Layla were doing something in the kitchen that involved a lot of Tupperware, and Leah was—Charlie didn't know where Leah was. Upstairs again, maybe. She'd gone very quiet after the story.

The moss guy was standing at the porch rail, looking out at the dark yard and the trees, with his hands in his trouser pockets. He'd taken the jacket off. He was in his vest and his shirtsleeves, and he'd rolled the sleeves up, and Charlie noticed he had forearms like a guy who swung an axe for a living. And an old watch. A cheap one, on a leather strap gone soft and dark with wear, the only thing he had on all day that didn't look expensive.

Charlie went and stood next to him at the rail.

They stood there for a while.

"Ran your plate," Charlie said.

"Did you."

"Yesterday. After the diner. Darlene said you ran out the back and left thirty bucks on a twelve-dollar check." Charlie looked out at the yard. "Your bike plate. British plate. I put it through to a guy I know at the consulate in Seattle. Owes me a favor."

"And?"

"Came back registered to a Royal Mail van," said Charlie. "In Leeds."

The moss guy didn't say anything for a second.

Then he started laughing. Very quietly, into the dark, with his head down.

"That's a need-to-know thing, isn't it," Charlie said.

"Very much so, Chief."

"Okay." Charlie nodded. He put his own hands in his pockets. "Then here's what I need to know."

The moss guy turned his head and looked at him. His eyes were very green, even in the porch light.

"Are you going to hurt her?" Charlie said.

"No."

He said it fast. Plain. No pause, no wind-up. Charlie had asked that question of a lot of men over the years, on a lot of porches, and he'd heard a lot of answers, and none of them had ever come out quite like that. Like it wasn't even a question. Like Charlie had asked him whether the sky was up.

"Are you going to bring trouble on this house?"

That one, the man thought about.

He looked out at the dark yard for a long time. Charlie let him.

"Trouble's already here, Chief," he said finally. "You know that better than I do. I think you've known it a lot longer than anyone's given you credit for." He looked at Charlie. "I'm going to do my level best to stand between it and this house. That's what I can promise you."

Charlie looked at him.

"That's a straight answer," he said.

"I'm trying a new thing," said the moss guy.

"How's it going?"

The man considered. "Hard on the jaw," he said.

Charlie didn't know what that meant. He decided he didn't need to.

They stood at the rail a while longer. Somewhere out past the trees, the ocean was going, a long low steady sound, like breathing. Charlie thought about his own house, in town, dark tonight. He thought about the phone call he'd gotten at eleven that morning, before he left. Bella's voice, cool and clear and happy, wishing him a happy Thanksgiving. Saying she was sorry she couldn't come. Saying Nessie had made him a card. Saying she'd see him Saturday.

"You know my daughter?" Charlie said.

He didn't know why he asked it. It just came out.

The moss guy was quiet a moment.

"I know of her," he said.

Charlie waited.

"She's happy, Charlie," the man said. "I've seen her laugh. With her husband, and the little one. Out in the yard, behind the big house, when they didn't know anyone was looking." He paused. "She's happy."

Charlie didn't ask how he'd seen that. He didn't ask from where, or when, or why. He'd stopped asking those kinds of questions a while ago, and he'd found he was mostly better off for it.

He just stood there at the porch rail, in the dark, next to a man in a thousand-dollar vest with a cheap watch on his wrist, and felt something in his chest that had been clenched for about three months let go a little.

"Okay," Charlie said. His voice came out rough. "Okay. Thanks."

"You're welcome."

Charlie cleared his throat. He pushed himself off the rail.

"Pie's going," he said. "If you want any more."

"There's more?"

"Sue makes a lot of pie."

"Charlie," said the moss guy, with deep feeling, turning round to follow him inside, "I think I'm going to like it here."


---

### V. Layla

*The same evening*

He insisted on washing up.

Layla would have bet money against it. She'd have bet a lot of money. She'd spent most of the afternoon watching him from across Sue's living room, waiting for the man from the clearing to show up. The one with the sunglasses and the voice like a closed door, who'd stood on nothing over a six-foot crater and turned her Severing Charm round like it was a paper airplane. She'd been sure he'd show up eventually. Men like that always did. You could dress them up and sit them at a table, but sooner or later they looked at you across the potatoes and you saw it in their eyes.

Instead she'd watched him eat four helpings of oyster dressing and get schooled on football by the Chief of Police and go quiet as a church while Billy Black told an old story. And now here he was at Sue Clearwater's kitchen sink with his shirtsleeves rolled up to his elbows and his expensive vest buttoned and a dish towel over one shoulder, up to his wrists in hot soapy water, washing Sue's mother's good plates one at a time by hand.

"You don't have to do that," Sue had said, when he'd started stacking them.

"Guest washes up," he'd said. "House rules, where I'm from."

"Where are you from?"

"A house with a lot of rules," he'd said, and taken the plates out of her hands, and that had been that.

Sue had looked at Layla. Layla had looked at Sue. And then, before she could work out how it had happened, Layla was standing next to him at the counter with a dish towel in her hand, drying.

She didn't know why she'd done it. Probably because Sue had handed her the towel and then left the kitchen. Sue was very good at leaving kitchens.

They worked for a while without talking. He washed. She dried. He did it properly, she noticed. Hot water. Every plate, front and back, rinse, into the rack. Not fast. Not slow. Like a man who'd been made to wash a lot of dishes at some point in his life, and had made his peace with it.

She noticed a lot of things. That was the problem. That was always the problem, with her. She noticed the cheap old watch on his left wrist, and that he'd taken it off before he put his hands in the water and laid it on the windowsill, face up, very carefully, like it mattered. She noticed that his wand wasn't on his left forearm anymore, where it had been in the clearing. She couldn't see where it was now. She'd been trying to work it out all afternoon. She noticed that every now and then, without seeming to know he was doing it, he'd turn his head a fraction toward the ceiling, toward the room above them, where Leah had gone after the pie and hadn't come down.

And she noticed that she wasn't scared of him. Not right now. Not standing here.

That bothered her more than anything.

"You can ask," he said, without looking up from the sink.

Layla's hand stopped on the plate she was drying.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever it is you've been wanting to ask since half past one. You've been looking at me like I'm a ward you can't find the seam in." He handed her a plate. "Go on. I'll tell you if I can't answer."

She dried the plate. She put it on the stack.

"Where's your wand?" she said.

He laughed. Quietly, down at the sink. "Of course that's the one."

"Where is it?"

"Somewhere I can get it." He rinsed a glass. "Next."

"How did you do the starlings?"

He looked at her sideways.

"The ropes," Layla said. "Incarcerous. You turned them into birds. In flight. No wand. Twelve ropes, twelve—no, more than twelve, there were dozens—dozens of birds. All of them alive. All of them *flying*." She heard her own voice getting faster and couldn't stop it. "You can't Transfigure a conjured object mid-cast. It's a conjured object. It doesn't have a stable form yet. It's still *becoming*. Every textbook I've ever read says you'd get a mess. Half-rope, half-bird, flopping around on the ground. You didn't get a mess. You got starlings. Iridescent starlings. They were *screaming*."

"They were very good starlings," he agreed.

"*How?*"

He took his hands out of the water and dried them on the towel on his shoulder, slowly. He leaned on the edge of the sink.

"You're right," he said. "It doesn't have a stable form yet. That's the whole trick. Everyone thinks that makes it harder. It makes it easier." He looked at her. "A rope that's still becoming a rope hasn't decided it's a rope. You don't have to talk it out of anything. You just have to get there first and give it a better idea."

Layla stood there with the towel in her hands.

"That's," she said, and stopped. "That's insane."

"Bit."

"That would take—the timing on that would be—you'd have to read the spell *as it left my wand*. You'd have to know what it was going to be before it was."

"Yes."

"Nobody can do that."

He didn't say anything. He just went back to the washing up.

Layla dried another plate. Her hands were shaking a little. She made them stop.

"Who taught you that?" she said.

"An old woman in a hut on a mountain in Uganda," he said, "who thought I was the slowest student she'd had in sixty years and told me so every morning." He handed her a glass. "Took me two years. Nearly killed me twice. I'm still not as good at it as she was. She could do it to a spell that hadn't been *cast* yet. She could do it to a spell you were only *thinking* about."

Layla looked at him. At his profile, bent over the sink, the steam coming up around his face.

*Uagadou,* she thought. The African school. The one everybody said did wandless magic better than anyone in the world. The one nobody she knew had ever been to, because they didn't take foreigners, because they didn't need to, because they'd been doing it for a thousand years before anybody else thought of it.

He'd told her that. On purpose. She was sure of it. He'd just handed it to her across the sink like a plate.

And it told her nothing. That was what made her want to scream. She turned it over and over in her head and it told her *nothing*. Uagadou didn't tell her if he was British or Irish or South African or from Mars. It didn't tell her who he worked for, or why he was here, or what he'd done with the vampire. It told her one true thing about one old woman on one mountain, and every door it opened just opened onto another empty room.

"You're very good at that," she said.

"At what?"

"Giving people something true so they'll stop asking for something useful."

He was quiet for a second. Then he smiled, down at the water. It went all the way up into his eyes.

"Clever girl," he said. "Most people don't spot that for weeks."

"Don't call me *girl*."

"Sorry. Clever woman." He rinsed a plate. "Clever, *dangerous* woman, who I'd rather have on my side of a fight than the other side, and who I'd very much like to stop looking at me like she's working out where to hit me with the frying pan."

Layla put the plate down.

"I *am* working out where to hit you with the frying pan," she said.

"I know. I can see you doing it. You keep looking at the back of my head."

"You *threatened* me."

"I did."

"You threatened *Charlie*. You threatened to have the *Obliviators* come for Charlie. And then you walked in here today and ate his favorite food with him and talked about *cricket* and made him *laugh*, and he likes you now, I can tell he likes you, he's practically adopted you, and he has no idea that yesterday you—" She stopped. Her voice had gone up. She brought it back down. "You don't get to do that."

He turned off the tap.

He stood at the sink for a moment with his wet hands resting on the edge of it. Then he turned around and leaned back against the counter and looked at her. Properly. With his whole face.

"I didn't give them your name," he said.

Layla stared at him.

"My boss," he said. "Last night. I told him everything. All of it. I rang him up at a quarter to one in the morning and told him every single thing that happened yesterday, and some things I'd already lied to him about, and he's not a man you lie to twice." He held her eyes. "I told him there's a local witch. I told him there's a Statute breach. Small one, beings and a No-Maj. I told him I'd made her a promise, and I was keeping it, and I wasn't going to give him her name. And he said, *then don't*."

Layla didn't say anything. She couldn't think of anything to say.

"He also said he'd like to know it eventually," he added. "Because he's always short of good people. I said I'd ask. I'm asking. You can say no. Nothing changes if you say no."

"I don't believe you," Layla said.

It came out flat and hard. She meant it. She had to mean it, because if she didn't mean it, she didn't know what she'd do.

He just looked at her. He just looked at her for a long moment with those very green eyes, and then he turned his head, very slightly, toward the kitchen doorway.

Leah was standing in it.

Layla hadn't heard her come down. Nobody ever heard Leah come down. She was standing in the doorway in her blue sweater with her arms crossed and her hair coming down a little out of its pins, and she was looking at Harry. Only at Harry. Like she'd been looking at him all day.

"Ask her," Harry said quietly.

Layla looked at Leah.

Leah looked at Harry.

"Did you give your boss Layla's name?" Leah said.

"No."

Leah held his eyes for a second longer. Then she turned her head and looked at Layla.

"He didn't," Leah said.

"How do you—"

"He can't lie to me." Leah's voice was perfectly flat. "Not a word. He tries, and his jaw locks up. I watched him try. Yesterday, on the gravel." She tilted her head. "And if I wanted to mess with you, I'd tell you he's a lying bastard and you should hit him with the frying pan, just for fun. He isn't. Not to me." A pause. "He didn't give them your name, Layla."

Layla stood there with the dish towel in her hands.

She looked from Leah to Harry and back again. At the two of them, Leah in the doorway and Harry against the counter, not looking at each other now, both of them looking at her. And she felt it. Something in the room, in the air between them, like the hum of a ward. She'd been feeling it all afternoon without knowing what it was, every time they were in the same room. A line. A taut line. Running from the middle of her chest to the middle of his.

She'd broken a lot of curses. She'd felt a lot of magic that took things from people.

This felt like two people holding opposite ends of the same rope, and neither of them letting go.

"Fine," Layla said.

Her voice wobbled. She ignored it.

"Fine," she said again. "I believe you. I believe *her*." She threw the dish towel down on the counter. "Layla Hollis. You can tell him. Tell your boss my name's Layla Hollis, and if he ever wants anything from me he can ask me himself, to my face, and I'll probably tell him to go to hell. And *you*—" She pointed at Harry. "You're going to teach me the starlings."

Harry's eyebrows went up.

"Am I?"

"Yes. You owe me a sleeve."

"I do owe you a sleeve," he agreed.

"And a mug. I dropped my mug. When the knot went. It was my grandmother's."

Something changed in his face. Just for a second.

"Bring it," he said. "The pieces. I'll fix it."

"You can't *Reparo* something that's been broken for a day, the—"

"I can," said Harry.

Layla looked at him.

She believed him. That was the worst part. She stood in Sue Clearwater's kitchen and looked at a man she'd tried to kill yesterday and believed every single word he said, and she didn't even have a rope to blame it on.

"Fine," she said, for the third time. "Fine. Tuesday. My house. Bring your own coffee."

"I'll bring the good stuff."

"You'd *better*."

And then, before she could think about it, before she could stop herself, she laughed. It came out of her short and sharp and surprised, like a cough. She put her hand over her mouth. It didn't help.

Harry was grinning at her.

Leah, in the doorway, rolled her eyes so hard Layla could practically hear it.

"Oh my God," Leah said. "You two are going to be *unbearable*."

---

Layla went back out to the living room after that.

She needed a minute. She sat on the arm of the couch next to Seth, who was asleep sitting up with a plate of pie crumbs balanced on his stomach and his mouth open, and she put her hand in his hair, and she breathed.

From where she was sitting, she could see through the kitchen doorway.

Leah hadn't moved from it. She was still standing there, leaning on the frame, with her arms crossed. And Harry had gone back to the sink. He'd turned the tap on again and was washing the last of the glasses, slowly, and every now and then he'd say something without looking round. Layla couldn't hear what. And Leah would answer, without looking at him either. Short. Two or three words. And once she laughed, a low surprised sound, and Layla saw his shoulders move.

That was all. Just that. Two people in a kitchen, not looking at each other, talking.

Layla had seen a lot of people in love. She'd seen her parents, at the beginning, before it went wrong. She'd seen Danny with his wife, on the one Christmas Layla went down to Albuquerque to have dinner with them, Danny standing behind Rosa at the stove with his chin on top of her head while she stirred the posole. She saw Seth every day, and Seth was about as subtle as a bonfire.

This was quieter than any of those. And it was so much stronger she could feel it from across the room.

Harry finished the last glass. He put it in the rack. He dried his hands, and picked up the old watch off the windowsill and buckled it back on, and turned around.

And Leah, in the doorway, uncrossed her arms.

She didn't do anything else. She just stood there with her arms down at her sides. And Harry stood there with his back against the sink. And they looked at each other. And neither of them said anything at all.

Layla looked away.

It felt like walking in on something. It felt like reading somebody's mail.

Next to her, Seth snored, and the crumbs on his plate jumped.


---

### VI. Harry

*Thursday night, into Friday, November 24*

She walked him out at nine.

Nobody suggested it. Sue was in the kitchen with Layla, putting leftovers into more Tupperware than Harry had known existed in the Western Hemisphere. Charlie had gone home at eight, with a plate of everything wrapped in foil and a slightly dazed expression and a last look at Harry from the door that said *don't make me regret this* about as clearly as words. Billy and Jacob had gone at half past, Jacob pushing the chair down the ramp and looking back over his shoulder at the Bonneville the whole way to the truck. Seth was still asleep on the couch.

Harry got his coat off the hook by the door and said goodnight to Sue, who took his face in both her hands, which nobody had done since Molly Weasley, and looked at him for a long moment, and let go without saying anything. And he turned round, and Leah was standing by the front door with her own jacket on. A beat-up canvas thing, two sizes too big. Her dad's, he thought, without knowing how he knew.

"I'll walk you out," she said.

So she walked him out.

It was a long way down the drive. Forty yards, maybe. It felt longer. It had stopped raining and the sky had cleared off over the ocean in long ragged strips, and there were stars in the gaps, and the thin sliver of new moon going down behind the trees. The gravel crunched under her boots. It didn't crunch under his shoes. He'd been walking on it silently all day without thinking about it, and he noticed her notice, and made it crunch.

She noticed him do that too. He felt her notice it, down the line. Felt something go across it that might have been amusement.

They got to the bike. He put the umbrella back in the saddlebag. He didn't get on.

"Council's tomorrow night," Leah said. "Billy's house. Seven."

"I'll be there."

"Don't wear the suit."

"So I'm told."

She stood there with her hands in the pockets of her father's jacket. She was looking at the bike, not at him.

"The suit's stupid," she said.

Or meant to. What came out was *The suit's*—and then her jaw locked. He watched it happen. Watched her mouth close on the next word and her teeth come together, and the muscle stand out along her jaw, and her eyes go wide. She stood there in the starlight with her mouth shut and her face going slowly, deeply red, fighting it.

He didn't say anything. He didn't move.

Leah unlocked her jaw.

"I like the suit," she said. Through her teeth. Furious.

Harry kept his face perfectly straight. It was one of the hardest things he'd ever done, and he'd once kept his face straight while a goblin bank manager read him a list of his own crimes in Gobbledegook.

"Thank you," he said gravely.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You're *thinking* it. I can *feel* you thinking it."

"I'm thinking it very quietly."

She turned round and glared at him. He looked back at her. And then her mouth twitched, and she clamped down on it, and it twitched again, and she put her hand over it, and he felt the laugh go down the line before he heard it. It hit his chest like a stone hitting a pond.

"I hate this," she said through her fingers. "I *hate* this. I can't even be mean to you."

"You can be mean to me. You just have to mean it."

"*That's the problem.*"

He laughed then. He couldn't help it. And she was laughing too, behind her hand, in her dad's jacket, at the end of her mother's driveway, and he thought he'd never in his life seen anything he wanted to look at more.

She stopped laughing first. She took her hand down.

They stood there.

There was about three feet of gravel between them. It might as well have been the Atlantic. He could feel the line pulling, steady, from the middle of her chest to the middle of his, and he could feel her feel it, and he could feel the wolf. The wolf was on its feet. It had been on its feet all evening, every time she came into a room. It was standing in the dark under his breastbone with its whole body leaning toward her like a plant toward a window, and it didn't understand why he wasn't moving.

He didn't move.

He didn't know what she wanted. That was the thing. He could feel her, but he couldn't read her, not the way he'd read her in the clearing. He wouldn't. He'd stopped himself doing it the second he understood what she was to him, the way you'd stop yourself reading a letter on somebody else's table. And without that, he only knew what the line told him, and the line told him she was standing three feet away in the starlight with her heart going hard, and so was his, and that was all.

Leah reached out.

She took hold of his tie. Again. Right below the knot, the way she had in the living room. She held onto it for a second, looking at it, not at him.

Then she straightened it.

It still hadn't been crooked.

"Go home, Harry," she said quietly.

She let go. She turned around and walked back up the drive, and he stood next to the bike and watched her go, all the way up to the porch, and in through the door. She didn't look back. He felt her not look back, down the whole length of the line, and he felt what it cost her.

The porch light went off.

He got on the bike.

---

He lay in his bed in the tent for four hours, and didn't sleep.

He'd known he wouldn't. He'd known it on the ride home, along the dark wet ribbon of 101 with the Bonneville's headlamp cutting a yellow tunnel through the trees, feeling the line pay out behind him mile by mile. Thirteen miles. It had been thirteen miles last night, too, and he'd slept fine, eventually, after the old man.

Tonight it felt like a thousand.

He kept looking for the hurt and not finding it. What he found was more like a sound. A high, thin, steady sound, like a wire singing in the wind, that he couldn't stop hearing once he'd noticed it. It went out of the middle of his chest and through the canvas wall of the tent and across the river and up the valley and over the highway and through thirteen miles of forest to a house on the coast with its porch light off. And at the other end of it, she was awake.

He could feel that. She was awake. She'd been in bed for hours, he could feel the stillness of her, but it was the stillness of somebody lying very straight with their eyes open in the dark, and every so often it broke. She'd turn over. Turn back. Once, about midnight, he felt her sit up, all at once, and stay sitting up for a long time. Then lie back down.

The wolf was pacing.

It had been pacing since he got home. Back and forth, back and forth, in the dark under his ribs, so steady and so restless he could almost hear its claws on the stone. Every now and then it would stop at one end, the northwest end, and stand there with its nose up, and whine. Not out loud. Not in his throat. But he heard it anyway.

He looked at the ceiling. He thought about the Lariat, and Marek hanging in the cold white room, and the notebook. He thought about Halloran, and Volterra, and the phone in the drawer. He thought about the Council tomorrow night and Old Quil Ateara, who Billy had described, with some relish, as *the meanest man on the Olympic Peninsula, and I say that with love*. He thought about Tuesday, and Layla Hollis's grandmother's mug.

The wire sang.

At ten past one, he heard her get up again. Felt it. Felt her go to her window and stand there.

The wolf stopped pacing.

It stood at the northwest end of him with its nose up and its ears forward and every inch of it pointed the same way, and it didn't whine this time. It just looked at him. Over its shoulder. The same look it had given him the night before, when he'd swung his legs out of bed toward the mirror.

This time it was saying *please*.

Harry lay there and looked back at it for a long moment.

"Seventy-three days," he said to it, out loud, in the dark. "You know that? Seventy-three. I've had you on that lead for seventy-three days."

The wolf didn't move.

"I told her it was done," Harry said. "On the gravel. I told her I was pretty sure it was done for now. Didn't I."

The wolf didn't move.

Harry got out of bed.

---

He didn't go through the tent. He went out through the flap in his bare feet, in the old grey T-shirt and the soft pair of flannel trousers he slept in, onto the gravel bar. The stones were cold and wet. The river was loud, black, going by. The sky had cleared all the way over the valley, and there were more stars than he'd seen in nineteen days, hard and white and close, with the long smear of the Milky Way going down the middle of them like spilled flour.

He walked out to the middle of the bar, inside the ward, where nobody in the world could see him.

And he let go.

It had been seventy-three days. He'd half thought he might have forgotten how. He hadn't. It was like taking his hand off a door he'd been holding shut, and the door swinging open on its own, slowly, and the dark coming in.

The heat came first. It always did. Up from the middle of him, from the place under his breastbone, like opening the door of a forge. It went out through his chest and down his arms and his legs, and his skin went tight, and the world tipped.

He went down. Forward. His hands came down onto the wet stones and they weren't hands when they got there. They were paws, broad and black and heavy, and the stones under them were suddenly very far away and very close at the same time, every edge and grain of them, every smell. He felt his spine stretch out long behind him. Felt his shoulders roll up and forward and go massive. Felt his jaw come out of his face, and his ears go up, and the cold night air go into a nose that could suddenly smell the river and the gravel and the stars, it seemed like, every single thing in the whole valley, all at once, in layers, like reading a book with a thousand pages stacked on top of each other.

And then he was standing on the gravel bar on four paws in the starlight, and the wolf was out.

He stood very still.

He looked down at himself. At his forelegs, black, solid black, the fur thick and dense, with a darker charcoal underneath it where the night air moved it. At his paws, spread wide on the stones. He looked at his shadow on the gravel, long and black under the starlight, and he measured it the way he'd measure anything. Plainly. Against what he knew.

He'd seen the Alpha from the ridge a dozen times. Russet, huge, taller at the shoulder than the black one from the other pack, the one called Sam, by about a head. He'd seen them side by side, once, at the treaty line, in the first week, standing nose to nose in some kind of argument.

He was taller than the russet one by about the same again.

The same head again. A whole head above Jacob Black at the shoulder, the way Jacob stood a whole head above Sam Uley. He'd known that, roughly. He'd known it since the beginning. He'd still never once stood on a gravel bar in Washington State and looked at it with the numbers in his head.

He breathed out. It came out of him in a long cloud of steam.

The wolf didn't care about the numbers. The wolf was out, for the first time in seventy-three days, and it stood on the gravel bar with its head up and its ears forward and every hair on its body standing up with the pure animal joy of it. The cold. The stars. The smell of the river. The ground under its paws. It wanted to run. It wanted to run so badly he could feel it in the long muscles of his back, bunching.

And it wanted to run *northwest*.

The line in his chest had stopped singing and started pulling. Hard. Steady. Like a hand on a lead.

Harry looked at the river. He looked at the dark wall of trees on the other side of it, and the hills, and the long way through them to the coast.

*All right,* he thought, to the wolf. *All right. Go on, then.*

---

He crossed the Bogachiel in two strides.

He went into the black water at the edge of the gravel bar at a lope and the river was up to his chest, cold, so cold, and then it was behind him and he was on the far bank and into the trees, and the water was coming off his coat in a spray, and he was *running*.

He didn't make a sound. He'd learned that a long time ago, in a different forest. Every paw coming down on exactly the right spot, toe first, the weight poured onto it and spread out across the ground like water across a table, so the earth barely knew he was there. Hind paws landing in the prints the forepaws had just left. Not a twig snapping. Not a fern brushing. He went up out of the Bogachiel valley through the old-growth like a shadow going across a wall, and the forest didn't even notice.

It was nothing. Thirteen miles was nothing. He crossed the highway in one long flat leap with no headlights anywhere, the asphalt flashing by under him wet and black. He went through the second-growth on the far side and the clearcuts beyond that, the stumps and the slash and the new little firs, and into the old forest again, where the trees were so big and so close their trunks went up into the dark like the pillars of a cathedral. The ground went down toward the sea. He could smell it. Salt and kelp and cold rock, and woodsmoke, a lot of woodsmoke, from a lot of chimneys.

The reservation.

He slowed.

There were other smells now. Wolves. He'd known there would be. The other pack ran the reservation; he'd watched them do it from the ridge for nineteen days, and he knew their loops the way he knew the rooms of his own tent. He stopped under a big Sitka spruce at the edge of the trees and put his nose up and read the air.

There. Fresh. An hour old, maybe less. A big male, angry-smelling, all sharp edges. That would be the silver-grey one. Paul. He'd run the coastal loop from the river mouth south to the headland and back, and he'd come back through here not long ago.

Harry waited. He listened. He heard the ocean, and the wind in the spruce, and a dog barking somewhere a long way off, a real dog, chained, in somebody's yard. Nothing else.

He went round. Wide. Upwind. Down a gully and along a creek and up again, through a stand of alder, keeping the wind in his face so it carried the grey wolf's scent to him and his own scent away. And he came out of the trees behind the Clearwater house at twenty to two in the morning, without a single wolf on the Olympic Peninsula knowing he'd passed.

The house was dark.

The porch light was off. The kitchen was dark. He could smell the leftovers through the walls. Turkey and pie and oyster dressing. He could smell Seth, asleep on the couch, and Sue, asleep in the back bedroom.

And her.

There was one window lit. Up under the eaves, at the end of the house nearest the trees. A small window, a dormer, with a dim yellow lamp somewhere behind it.

There was a big old cedar in the yard. Harry walked across the wet grass to it, so quietly that the grass didn't even bend, and sat down underneath it.

He sat the way a dog sits. Haunches down, forelegs straight, tail curled round his paws. He tipped his head back and looked up at the lit window under the eaves.

He didn't do anything else.

He didn't call. He didn't make a sound. He didn't reach down the line and pull, though he could feel it pulling on him, steady as a tide. He just sat there in the dark under her mother's cedar tree, with the stars over him and the ocean going somewhere beyond the trees, and looked up at her window.

And waited.


---

### VII. Leah

*Friday, November 24 — 1:40 a.m.*

She'd given up on sleep around midnight.

She'd sat up in bed in the dark and turned the lamp on and sat there with her knees pulled up under the old quilt and her back against the slope of the ceiling, and she'd given up. There wasn't any point. Every time she closed her eyes it was the driveway. Starlight and gravel and his stupid green tie in her fingers. *I like the suit.* Through her teeth. With her whole face on fire.

And the rope.

It hadn't stopped all night. It went out of the middle of her chest, through the wall of her bedroom, through thirteen miles of trees, and it *sang*. That was the only word she had for it. Like a wire in the wind. Like the power lines along the highway on a stormy night, that thin high sound you could hear from inside the car. She lay there and listened to it. She could feel him at the other end of it. Lying still. Not sleeping either.

That was the part that made her want to scream into her pillow. He wasn't sleeping either. She could *feel* him not sleeping. Thirteen miles away, in that tent that was bigger on the inside, lying on his back in the dark the exact same way she was, listening to the exact same sound.

At ten past one, she'd gotten up and gone to the window.

She didn't know why. There was nothing out there. The yard, the cedar, the trees, dark. The sky had cleared. She stood there with her forehead against the cold glass and her breath fogging it and looked east, over the trees, toward where he was. She couldn't see anything. She knew she couldn't. She stood there anyway.

And then the rope changed.

She felt it go. All at once, at the other end. Something *came out*. That was the only way she could think of it. Something huge came up out of the dark at the far end of the line and stood up and shook itself, and the whole rope went wild, humming, jerking, like a fish line when the fish takes it. And she knew what it was. She'd felt it once before, in the clearing, the second the rope hit it.

The wolf.

His wolf. Out. She could feel it. She could feel it standing up out there in the dark, on four feet, with its head up. She could feel *joy* coming off it. Plain, huge, uncomplicated joy, like a dog let off its lead on a beach.

And inside her, without asking, her own wolf stood up.

Leah stood at the window with both hands flat on the glass and felt her wolf stand up under her ribs and turn east and *lean*, and she had to grab the window frame to stay on her feet.

Then the rope started getting shorter.

Fast.

She stood there and felt it happen. Felt the distance going out of it, the way it had gone out of it yesterday in reverse, when he'd vanished from the clearing and the line paid out behind her like a reel. It was coming in now. Steadily. Mile after mile. Across the river, she could feel the cold of the water for a second. Up the valley. Over the highway. Into the trees. Closer.

He was coming here.

She didn't move. She didn't know what she'd do if she moved. She stood at the window with her hands on the glass and her heart going like a drum and felt him come through the forest toward her, faster than anything, faster than Jacob, faster than her, and so quiet she couldn't feel a single twig break. Into the reservation. Round the edge of it, wide, slow, careful, the way you'd go round something you didn't want to wake up.

Then into the trees behind the house.

Then the rope stopped getting shorter.

It went still. It went *taut*. It went from the middle of her chest straight down through the floor of her bedroom and out through the wall and down, down into the yard. Into the dark, under the cedar.

Right there.

Leah looked down.

---

At first she didn't see anything.

The yard was black. The cedar was black, a big dark shape against the darker trees. The grass was silver with wet where the starlight hit it, and black where it didn't. Her lamp was behind her, and the glass had her own reflection in it, faint, a woman in a T-shirt with her hair down and her face too close to the window.

She reached back and switched off the lamp.

And there, under the cedar, low down, were two green eyes.

They were looking up at her.

They caught the starlight the way an animal's eyes catch headlights. Two flat green coins of light, hanging in the dark under the tree, about as high off the ground as a man's shoulders. Steady. Not blinking. Looking straight up at her window.

At her.

She stood very still. Her breath fogged the glass and cleared and fogged again.

Her eyes adjusted. Her wolf eyes, the ones that worked in the dark even when she was on two legs. And the shape came up out of the black around the green eyes, slowly, the way a shape comes up out of dark water.

A wolf.

A black wolf. Sitting under her mother's cedar tree the way a dog sits, haunches down, forelegs straight, with its head tipped back to look up at her window. Black on black on black. She could only see it at all because it was blacker than the dark around it. A shape cut out of the night with scissors.

It was enormous.

She knew wolves. She'd been one for eight months. She'd stood next to Sam's wolf, and next to Jacob's, which was bigger, and she knew exactly how big a wolf could get. She knew it in her bones. And she stood at her window and looked down at this one, sitting quietly under the cedar, and her brain kept trying to make it fit, and it didn't fit. It was too big. Its head, tipped back, was higher than the bottom branches of the tree. If Jacob had been sitting next to it, Jacob's ears would have come about to its jaw.

It didn't move. It didn't make a sound. It just sat there, in the dark, with its green eyes on her window.

Waiting.

Leah's wolf, inside her, made a noise she'd never heard it make. It was lower than a growl, and softer, a sort of rumbling hum, down at the bottom of her chest. She felt it in her ribs. She felt it in her throat.

Down in the yard, the black wolf's ears came forward.

And then, very quietly, so quietly that no human would ever have heard it through the glass, a voice came up out of the dark under the tree. Low. Rough. English.

"Hello, Leah," it said.

Leah fell over.

She didn't mean to. Her knee just went. She sat down hard on the floor under the window with a thump that she was fairly sure woke up half the house, and sat there with her back against the wall and her hand over her mouth.

*It talked.*

Wolves didn't talk. Wolves *couldn't* talk. She'd been a wolf for eight months and she'd tried, in the beginning, when it was new and she was furious and she wanted to scream at Sam with her own voice instead of in his head. You couldn't do it. The mouth was wrong. The tongue was wrong. You could growl and whine and howl and that was it. Everything else had to go through the pack mind.

It had *talked*. It had said her name. In his voice.

She got up on her knees. She put her face back to the glass.

He was still sitting there. His head was a little on one side now. Like a dog that's heard a funny noise.

"You can *talk*?" she whispered. At the glass. She didn't know if he could hear it. She didn't know if she wanted him to.

The black wolf's ears flicked.

"Bit," he said.

And she was gone.

---

She went down the stairs in her bare feet in the dark, three at a time. She knew which ones creaked. She missed all of them. She went through the living room past Seth, asleep on the couch with his arm hanging off and a plate of crumbs on the floor beside him, and he didn't even twitch. Through the kitchen. Out the back door. Across the porch. Down the steps into the wet grass, cold, so cold on her feet she gasped, and round the corner of the house at a dead run.

She stopped at the corner.

He'd stood up.

He was standing under the cedar now, on four legs, turned toward her. And standing up he was even bigger. His back was higher than her head. She was standing there in a T-shirt and her bare feet in her mother's yard, a grown woman, five foot ten, and she had to tip her head *back* to look up at the top of his shoulders. Black fur, thick and dense and wet with dew, going a charcoal gray underneath where the wind moved it. A broad, heavy head. Enormous paws, spread on the grass. And the eyes. Green. Very green. Looking down at her.

He didn't come toward her. He stood exactly where he was.

Leah stood exactly where she was.

The rope between them was so tight she could feel it in her teeth.

Then she pulled her T-shirt off over her head.

She didn't think about it. She'd done it a thousand times. Strip, tie, phase. Her hands knew how. She stepped out of her sleep shorts and rolled them round the shirt and knotted the cord round the bundle and round her ankle in about four seconds, the way she did every night, and she didn't look at him while she did it, and she knew without looking that he'd turned his head away. All the way away. Toward the trees. The same way he'd turned his back on the gravel bar.

Her wolf came up out of her like a wave.

---

The pack mind opened.

There was somebody in it. Of course there was. Somebody was always in it. It was Quil, out on the Cullen side, running the late loop by the river, half asleep, thinking about Claire's crayon drawings, the one with the purple dog. She felt him feel her phase in. Felt him perk up. *Leah? It's two in the morning, what are you doing up, is something—*

He saw through her eyes.

He saw what she was looking at.

*Oh,* Quil thought. *Oh. OH. Oh my God, that is a BIG—Leah, that's—is that HIM? Is that the GUY? He's a WOLF? He's a REAL—that is the BIGGEST—*

*Quil.*

*He's bigger than JAKE. Leah. He's bigger than JAKE. Does Jake know he's bigger than—*

*Quil. Get out.*

There was a pause.

*Getting out,* Quil thought hastily. *Getting out. Totally getting out. I'm gonna phase. I'm gonna run the rest of the loop on two legs. In the cold. In my shorts. That's fine. That's totally fine.* A pause. *Have fun. Don't—um. Okay. Bye.*

He was gone.

And she was alone in her own head for the first time in eight months, standing in her mother's wet yard on four feet, with the black wolf ten feet away under the cedar.

He turned his head back round.

She saw him see her. Her wolf. Gray, silver at the edges, about Seth's size, long-legged and lean. She saw him look at her, all of her, the way he'd looked at her in the clearing yesterday after he took off the sunglasses. Slowly. Like she was something he'd been waiting a long time to see.

And then her wolf walked straight up to him.

Leah didn't tell it to. She would remember that afterward, very clearly, with her face on fire. She did not tell it to. She was standing there in her own head going *okay, okay, let's just, let's think about this, let's just stand here for a minute and*—and her wolf walked across the ten feet of wet grass like it had been waiting its whole life to do it and put its nose against his.

He lowered his head to meet her. He had to lower it a long way.

They breathed.

She breathed him in. All of it, through her wolf's nose, which could smell a thousand times more than her own, and it was so much it made her dizzy. Pine. Earth. Cold stone, like the inside of a cave. The river he'd crossed, still wet in his fur. And under all of it, him. Just him. Something she didn't have a word for in any language she knew, something that went straight down the back of her nose and into the place under her ribs where the rope was tied and *settled* there, like it had always been supposed to be there.

She felt him breathe her in too. Felt his nostrils flare against hers. Felt him go still.

And then her wolf, which apparently had no shame whatsoever, ducked its head and pushed its whole face into the side of his neck.

Into the thick black ruff of fur under his jaw. All the way in, up to her eyes. And *rubbed*.

*What are you doing,* Leah thought at it. *What are you* doing*. Stop it. Stop it right now. We just met him. We met him* yesterday*. You can't just—*

Her wolf didn't stop it.

And neither did his. She felt his head come down over the back of her neck. Felt his chin settle on her shoulders, heavy and hot, the whole weight of that enormous head resting on her like a hand. Felt him lean into her. And she leaned back. She leaned her whole body against his chest, against all that thick black fur, hot as a banked stove, and he was so big and so solid it was like leaning against a hillside, and her tail was going. She could feel it. Her *tail* was *going*, back and forth, back and forth, in the wet grass, like a *dog's*, like a stupid happy *dog's*, and she couldn't make it stop.

She felt him laugh.

Through the rope, without a sound. A big, rumbling, delighted laugh that went down the line from his chest into hers and lit her up from the inside like a match dropped in dry grass. And his wolf turned its head and licked her.

Once. Right up the side of her muzzle, from her jaw to her ear. Like a big dog.

Leah's wolf bit his ear.

A nip, light. A quick little snap of her teeth on the edge of his left ear, the way you'd nip at a littermate.

The black wolf pulled his head back and looked down at her with his green eyes very wide. And then he did something she would not have believed if she hadn't been standing right there in front of it.

He play-bowed.

All the way down. Front legs flat out on the grass, chest down, his enormous back end straight up in the air with his tail going like a flag. A wolf the size of a small truck, crouched in her mother's yard with his butt in the air and his ears up and his eyes dancing, *inviting* her.

Leah's wolf bolted.

---

She ran.

She went round the house and across the road and down the long slope through the trees toward the beach, and she ran flat out, the way she ran when she wanted everything else to go away. And he came after her. She could hear him behind her. No, she couldn't hear him. That was the thing. She couldn't hear him at all. He was right behind her, she could feel him through the rope, she could feel the heat coming off him, and he didn't make a single sound. Not a twig. Not a leaf. It was like being chased by the dark itself.

She burst out of the trees onto First Beach.

The tide was out. The sand was wide and flat and silver under the stars, and the surf was a long white line out at the edge of it, rolling in slow and heavy. James Island stood up black against the sky off to the north, with the trees on top of it like a crown. The air was cold and full of salt and the roar of the waves, and Leah hit the sand at a dead run and went straight down to the waterline and turned south, along the hard wet sand where the waves came up, and *flew*.

She was fast. She was the fastest wolf in either pack. Faster than Jacob. Faster than Sam. She'd been faster than all of them from her very first night, and it was the only thing about being a wolf she'd ever loved, the only thing that had ever been just hers.

He came up beside her.

He didn't pass her. He could have. She felt that he could have, through the rope, felt the huge easy power in him, held back. He came up level with her shoulder, on the ocean side, so close their fur brushed, and he stayed there. Matching her. Stride for stride. A wave came up the sand, white and roaring, and broke over both their legs, and he didn't flinch, and neither did she, and they went through it side by side in a great fan of spray and kept going.

Leah laughed.

She couldn't help it. She was a wolf and she couldn't laugh, not really, but something came up out of her chest anyway, a sort of huff, a bark, and it went down the rope and she felt him catch it and send it back. And she ran faster. And he ran faster. And they went down the whole length of First Beach in the starlight like that, a gray wolf and a black wolf, shoulder to shoulder in the edge of the surf, with the waves breaking white around their legs and the spray going up silver behind them.

---

At the south end of the beach, where the cliffs came down to the sand and the big headland stood out into the sea, she turned inland.

He turned with her. He didn't ask where. She felt him not ask.

There was a trail over the headland. The tourists used it in summer, to get from the road to Second Beach. She didn't use it. She went up the face of the headland through the salal and the scrub, where nobody went, where the ground was steep and broken and full of roots, and he came up behind her without a sound. Over the top. Through the stunted spruce bent sideways by a hundred years of wind. Out onto the far side, where the land dropped away to the sea.

And along the edge. To a place where the rock split.

It was a crack. Just a crack in the top of the cliff, like somebody had taken an axe to the headland and not finished the job. It went down at an angle, steep, a narrow slot between two walls of black rock with ferns growing out of them. You'd walk right past it if you didn't know. Most people would never see it at all.

Her dad had shown it to her when she was eleven.

He'd brought her out here on a Saturday in October, just the two of them, with a thermos of cocoa and a bag of jerky. He'd taken her down the crack, holding her hand on the steep part. And at the bottom, where the slot opened out into the face of the cliff, there was a cave.

She went down the crack now on four feet, carefully, the way she'd gone down it on two at eleven with her hand in her father's. The walls brushed her sides. She heard him behind her, not making a sound, squeezing. It was a tight fit for her. It must have been a very tight fit for him. He came down it anyway.

And at the bottom, it opened out.

The cave was the size of her bedroom, maybe a little bigger. The floor was sand, dry, soft, blown in over the years. The roof was low and dark and smelled of salt. And the whole western side of it was open. Just open. To the sky. To the sea. You stood at the mouth of it, and the cliff dropped away under you, eighty feet straight down to the beach, and in front of you there was nothing at all but the ocean. Going all the way out to the edge of the world. And the sea stacks, standing up out of the water like black teeth, dozens of them, the big ones close in and the little ones farther out, with the white surf going round their feet and the starlight on the water behind them.

There was a ring of old stones near the back, with old black ash in the middle of it. Her dad's fire. The last one he'd ever built here, the last time they'd come. She'd been sixteen. She'd been embarrassed to be out here with her dad. She'd spent the whole night texting Sam.

She'd come here after Sam left.

Alone. Lots of times. That first year, when it was so bad she couldn't breathe in her own house. Before she phased. She'd come down the crack and sit at the mouth of the cave with her knees pulled up and look at the sea stacks and cry, where nobody could hear her. And then she'd phased, and she'd stopped coming, because she couldn't come here without thinking about it, and she couldn't think about anything anymore without the whole pack thinking about it with her. She'd kept it out of her head for eight months. It was the only thing she'd ever managed to keep out of her head. The only place that was still just hers.

She'd never brought anyone here. She'd never brought it into the pack mind. Not once.

She walked to the mouth of the cave and stood there on four feet with the wind coming in off the sea and lifting her fur, and she looked out at the sea stacks, and she felt him come and stand beside her.

He didn't say anything. He looked out at it with her. At the black teeth of the stacks and the white surf and the stars going all the way down to the water.

She felt him understand. Through the rope. She didn't tell him anything. She didn't have to. She felt him feel the ring of old stones behind them, and the cocoa and the jerky and an eleven-year-old's hand, and a sixteen-year-old texting, and a girl crying at the mouth of a cave where nobody could hear. She felt him take it, all of it, and hold it. Carefully. The way you'd hold something somebody handed you that was very old and very easy to break.

"Thank you," he said. Very quietly. In his own voice, out of a wolf's mouth, into the wind.

She didn't know what to do with that. So she did what her wolf wanted.

She turned around three times on the soft dry sand at the back of the cave, the way a wolf does. And lay down.

He stood over her for a moment, looking down. Then he turned around three times too, slowly, carefully, a huge black shape in the dark. And lay down. *Around* her. He curled himself round her body on the sand, his back to the cave wall and his belly to her back, his enormous forelegs stretched out on either side of her, his tail curled over her feet. Like a wall. Like a hill. Like the whole world had turned into black fur and heat and wrapped itself around her.

He ran so hot. Hotter than she did. Hotter than Jacob. She could feel his heart going against her back, slow and heavy, about sixty beats a minute, the heartbeat of a man sitting in a chair reading a newspaper.

She put her head down on his foreleg.

He put his chin down on the top of her head.

And they lay there in her father's cave, the two of them, with the ocean going eighty feet below and the stars going all the way out to the edge of the world, and the rope between them gone so soft and so easy she couldn't feel where it started and where it stopped.

She thought about her dad. About his hand on hers on the steep part of the crack. About the card propped against the sugar this morning, *don't be cheap Sue*. About a big man in a suit at the end of her father's table, eating four helpings of the swamp while her mom cried.

*He'd have liked you,* she thought.

She didn't say it. She couldn't say it. She was a wolf. But she felt it go down the rope anyway, and she felt him catch it, and she felt him go very still against her back.

Then she felt him breathe out. Long and slow. All the way down. Like a man setting down something he'd been carrying a very long way.

She closed her eyes.

The ocean went. The wind went. His heart went, slow and steady against her back, sixty beats a minute.

And Leah Clearwater slept.

### VIII. Sam

*Friday, November 24 — 6:52 a.m.*

Jared found the trail at ten to five.

Sam had been asleep. He'd been asleep for maybe two hours, which was about how long he'd managed to sleep at a stretch since Jacob Black knocked on his door at a quarter past eleven the night before with a whole apple pie under his arm and a look on his face Sam hadn't seen there in a long time. Not since they'd been in the same pack.

*Leah imprinted,* Jacob had said. On Sam's porch. In the dark. Just like that, with no wind-up, the way Jacob said things now.

Sam had stood in the doorway.

*It went both ways,* Jacob had said. *Sam. It went both ways. I felt it. He's—he's not one of us. He's English. He's some kind of wizard, like Seth's girl. He's got a wolf in him. I don't know how. Nobody knows how.* And then, after a long time, when Sam still hadn't said anything: *I thought you should hear it from me.*

Sam had said thank you. He thought he'd said thank you. He remembered Jacob going back down the steps and into the dark with the pie, and he remembered Emily's hand on his back, and then he remembered sitting on the top step of his own porch for a long time, in the cold, with her sitting next to him, not saying anything, holding his hand.

He'd thought he'd feel something specific. He'd been waiting two years to feel it. For the day somebody told him Leah was okay. That she'd found somebody, or something, anything, that made it stop. He'd thought it would feel like a weight coming off.

It had felt like a lot of things. He still didn't know what most of them were.

He'd gone to bed around three. And at ten to five Jared had phased in on the dawn loop and come round the back of the Clearwater place on the way to the river and stopped dead in the yard, and the next second his panic had come down the pack mind like a slammed door and pulled Sam up out of sleep and onto his feet before he was awake.

*Sam. Sam, you need to see this. Sam, there's a—it's in Sue's yard. It's a wolf. It's not ours. It's not Jake's. Sam, it's* huge*.*

---

The tracks were in the wet grass under the old cedar. Sam saw them through Jared's eyes, and then through his own when he got there, standing in the gray before-light with his nose down.

They were the biggest wolf tracks he'd ever seen.

He put his own forepaw down next to one. He'd never had to do that before. He'd been the biggest wolf in the tribe for three years, right up until Jacob outgrew him last summer. He knew what his own paw looked like next to anything. Next to Jacob's, his was smaller by about as much as a man's hand is smaller than a bigger man's. Next to this one, his paw looked like Jacob's would have looked next to his.

He stood there and looked at it for a long time.

The smell was all over the yard. Pine and earth and cold stone, and something under that he didn't have a name for. It smelled like a wolf. It smelled like a pack. It didn't smell like *his* pack, or Jacob's, or any pack that had ever been in his head. And mixed in with it, everywhere, all over the grass, the cedar, the corner of the house, Leah's scent. Fresh. From a few hours ago.

Two sets of tracks went off across the road and down through the trees toward the beach. Side by side.

*Sam,* Paul thought. He'd phased in a minute behind Jared, and he'd been thinking a lot of things very loudly ever since, most of which Sam had been ignoring. *Sam, is that him? Is that the guy Jake was talking about? The wizard? Because that's not a wizard, Sam, that's a* horse*. That's a horse with* teeth*. And he's got Leah—*

*He doesn't have Leah.* Sam put his nose down to the tracks again. To hers, next to his. *Look at them. Look at the stride.*

Jared looked. Paul looked.

Leah's tracks went down the slope toward the beach at a dead run. Long, flat, hard. She'd been flying. And the big tracks were next to them the whole way. Right next to them, with neither one chasing and neither one leading. Stride for stride.

*She was running,* Jared thought slowly. *With him.*

Sam didn't say anything. He went down through the trees after them.

---

The tide had come in and gone out again, and most of the tracks on First Beach were gone. But not all. Up near the top of the beach, at the very south end, where the sand was dry and the waves hadn't reached, Sam found them going up off the beach into the salal, up the face of the headland. Up the face, the steep way, the way nobody went.

He went up it.

It was getting light. The sky over the hills behind him had gone the color of an old bruise, gray and yellow, and the sea in front of him was a long dull sheet of pewter with the sea stacks standing up out of it, black. The wind was cold and steady off the water. He came up over the top of the headland through the bent spruce with Paul and Jared behind him, and followed the scent along the edge of the cliff on the far side, to where it stopped.

At a crack in the rock.

Sam stood at the top of it and looked down.

He'd never seen it before. Three years running the reservation and he'd never once seen it. A slot in the cliff, going down at an angle, narrow, between two walls of black rock hung with ferns. Steep. Tight. He could see the fern fronds broken where something had brushed them going down. Something big. He could see a tuft of black fur caught on a jag of rock about halfway down, black and dense, with charcoal at the roots.

And under the scent of the big wolf, coming up out of the crack on the wind, he smelled Leah.

And something else. Old smoke. Very old, from a long time ago, cold ash and wet stone. And under the smoke—Sam stood very still. Under the smoke, so faint he almost didn't catch it, something that went straight into him like a hook.

Harry Clearwater.

Harry from years ago. Harry now was eight months in the ground. Harry's tobacco, the cheap pipe tobacco he'd smoked out on the porch when Sue wasn't looking. Soaked into the rock down there, into the ash of some old fire, so long ago it was barely a ghost of a smell. But Sam knew it. He'd sat on Harry Clearwater's porch at sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, holding Leah's hand, while Harry smoked that pipe and told him exactly what would happen to him if he ever made her cry.

Sam had made her cry.

*Sam?* Jared thought. *You okay?*

*Stay here,* Sam thought. *Both of you. Stay at the top.*

*Sam, you're not going down there by yourself, that thing is—*

*Stay, Paul.*

He put the Alpha in it. Just a little. Just enough. He felt Paul's legs lock under him at the top of the crack, felt him sit down hard on the rock, felt the furious sputtering in his head that came after. Sam ignored it.

He went down.

---

It was tight. It was very tight. His shoulders brushed both walls the whole way, and twice he had to turn sideways and push. He didn't know how anything bigger than him had gotten down here. He didn't know how anything bigger than *Leah* had. He came round the last bend in the slot with ferns dragging at his fur and the sound of the ocean getting louder, and the walls opened out.

And he stopped.

It was a cave. Not big. Low roof, sand floor. The whole west side of it open to the sea, so the gray dawn light came in flat and cold off the water and filled it up. There was a ring of old stones near the back. Old black ash. That was where the smell was coming from. Harry's fire.

And in the middle of the cave, on the soft dry sand, two wolves were lying curled together, asleep.

One of them was Leah.

He'd have known her anywhere. In any light. In any shape. He'd known her since she was eight years old, and loved her since she was fourteen, and been inside her head for six months while she hated him. He'd know her silver-gray coat and the long lean legs and the way she tucked her nose under her tail when she slept, the same way she'd tucked her face into his neck when they were kids, a hundred years ago, on her dad's porch.

She was asleep. Really asleep. Sam had been in her head every night from March to September, and she had never once slept like this. She slept like a soldier, in pieces, waking at every sound, with her teeth half bared even in her dreams. Every night. He'd felt it. He'd lain in his own bed with Emily asleep beside him and felt Leah lying in hers, three miles away, not sleeping, hating him, hating everything, and there had been nothing he could do about it. Nothing. Not one thing. It had been the worst part of all of it. Worse than the look on Sue's face. Worse than Harry's funeral. Worse than standing at his own wedding with Leah in a bridesmaid's dress in the second row, staring at the floor.

She was asleep now. With her nose tucked under her tail. Breathing slow and deep and even. Her whole body loose.

She was asleep inside the curve of the biggest wolf Sam had ever seen.

It was black. Black as his own coat, blacker, with a charcoal sheen in it where the light from the sea caught the fur. It lay curled around her on the sand like a wall around a garden. Its back was to the cave wall and its belly was to Leah's back and its two huge forelegs were stretched out on either side of her, and its tail was curled over her feet. Its chin was resting on the top of her head. Very lightly. So lightly that Sam, looking at it, understood that the wolf was holding the weight of its own head up off her. Just slightly. Just enough. So she wouldn't feel it.

Its eyes were open.

They were green. And they were looking at him.

---

Sam didn't move.

He'd been the Alpha of this tribe for three years. He'd fought newborns in a clearing and vampires in the woods and a whole hungry army of red-eyed things last June that had come at them out of the trees screaming. He'd killed things. He'd lost people. He'd watched the Volturi walk out of the trees.

He stood at the mouth of that crack and looked at the black wolf, and every hair on his body stood up, and his lips came back off his teeth, and he felt the growl start in his chest, all on its own, the way it would start in front of anything that big, on his land, with one of his—

*Not one of his.*

The growl stopped.

Leah wasn't one of his. Leah hadn't been one of his since September. Leah hadn't been his for a lot longer than that.

The black wolf watched him. It kept its chin on Leah's head. It lay there with its green eyes on him, calm, steady, the way a man might look up from a book at somebody who'd come into the room.

Then it spoke.

"Morning," it said.

It said it very softly. In a man's voice. English, low and rough. Out of a wolf's mouth, in a wolf's cave, at seven in the morning.

"Keep it down, would you? She's sleeping."

Sam's mind went completely blank.

At the top of the crack, Paul's mind did not go blank. Paul's mind went off like a car alarm. *IT TALKS. Sam. SAM. IT TALKS. Did you hear—it TALKED, it said MORNING, it said KEEP IT DOWN, wolves don't TALK, Sam, how does it—*

The black wolf's ears flicked. Its eyes went, very briefly, up. Toward the top of the crack. Toward where Paul was sitting on the rock, out of sight, sixty feet above them.

"It does, Paul," it said, a little louder. Pitched to carry. "It also bites. So I'd unclench."

Paul's mind went blank.

So did Jared's.

So did Sam's, for the second time in about ten seconds.

*He heard me,* Paul thought, very small. *Sam. He heard me think. He knows my* name*.*

"You lot think very loudly," the black wolf said, to nobody in particular. "All of you. It's like living next to a pub." Its green eyes came back to Sam. "You'll be Sam."

Sam looked at it.

*Yes,* he thought. Then, because the thing could apparently hear him anyway and he didn't know what else to do: *Yes. I'm Sam.*

"Harry," said the black wolf.

---

Leah woke up.

Sam saw it happen. He saw her ears move first, and then her eyes open, and then her whole body go from loose to tight in a single instant as she smelled him. She came up off the sand so fast it was like watching a spring let go. She came up out of the curve of the black wolf's body and turned to face the mouth of the crack, and her hackles were up all down her spine, and her lips came back off every tooth she had, and the sound that came out of her was one Sam had heard in his own head a hundred times, and never once aimed at him from outside it.

The black wolf stood up behind her.

Sam had thought he understood how big it was. He hadn't. Lying down, curled on the sand, it had looked enormous. Standing up, it filled the cave. Its back was almost against the low roof. Its head was a full head above where Jacob's would have been, and Jacob's was a full head above his. He had to tip his own head back to see its face. It stood behind Leah with its forelegs on either side of her, not touching her, just there, a black wall at her back.

It didn't growl. It didn't need to.

Sam phased.

He did it right there at the bottom of the crack, without thinking about it. Went from four legs to two in a single lurch and stood there naked on the cold sand with the dawn wind coming in off the sea, and untied his cutoffs from his ankle and pulled them on with his hands shaking, because there were some things you had to say with your own mouth.

He straightened up. He looked at Leah.

She was still snarling. Her eyes were on his face. Brown, the same brown they'd always been. Furious.

"Leah," Sam said.

The snarl got louder.

"I'm not here to—" He stopped. He made himself start again. Slower. "Jared found the trail. In your mom's yard. It's a big trail." He tried to smile. It didn't work. "I just needed to know you were okay. That's all. That's all I came for."

Leah stared at him.

The black wolf, behind her, stared at him too.

"Jacob told me," Sam said. "Last night. He came to the house." He swallowed. "He said it went both ways."

Something went across Leah's face. Her wolf face. He couldn't read it. He'd never been able to read her without the pack mind, not since the beginning, and now he couldn't read her at all.

He looked up at the black wolf.

"You're Harry," he said.

"I am," said the black wolf. "And you're Sam."

"Yes."

The black wolf looked down at him for a long moment, from a full two heads above. Its green eyes were very steady.

"I've heard you're an idiot," it said.

At the top of the crack, Paul came up off the rock with a snarl so loud it echoed down the slot, and Sam heard him start down, heard his claws scrape stone—

"*Paul*." Sam didn't turn round. He put the whole of the Alpha into it this time, all of it, every ounce. "*Stay.*"

The scraping stopped.

Sam stood there on the cold sand in his cutoffs and looked up at the black wolf. At those green eyes. And he thought about Harry Clearwater on his porch, with his pipe. *If you ever make that girl cry, Sam Uley, they'll never find all of you.*

He thought about two weeks gone without a word. About walking back into her life with the truth locked in his throat. About Emily. About the wedding, and Leah in the second row staring at the floor. About six months in his head.

He thought about how many different ways he'd tried, over two years, to tell himself it wasn't his fault. And how every single one of them had been true. And how it hadn't mattered, not once, not even a little bit.

"Yeah," Sam said quietly. "I've heard that too."

The cave was very quiet.

The black wolf looked at him a moment longer. Then something changed in the way it was standing. Sam couldn't have said what. Its ears, maybe. The set of its head. It was a very small thing. But Sam had been a wolf for three years, and he knew what it looked like when another wolf decided you weren't a threat.

Leah had stopped snarling.

She was just looking at him. Her lips had come back down over her teeth. Her hackles were still up, but only a little. She was looking at him with her head a little on one side, the way she used to look at him across her dad's kitchen table when he'd said something stupid and she was deciding whether it was worth yelling about.

"I'm glad, Lee," Sam said.

He hadn't called her that in two years.

"I'm glad," he said again. He didn't know how else to say it. "That's all. That's all I wanted to say. I'm—I'm really glad."

Leah didn't move.

The black wolf turned its head, very slightly, and looked down at her.

"Do you want me to tell him anything, love?" it said quietly. "Or would you rather leave it?"

Leah didn't look round at it. She kept her eyes on Sam. But something went between the two of them anyway, Sam could see it, something passed between the gray wolf and the black one without either of them moving, like a current through a wire. And the black wolf listened to it. Sam watched it listen.

Then it looked back at him.

"She says go home, Sam," it said. "She says Emily will be worried." A pause. "And she says to tell Emily she said happy Thanksgiving."

Sam stood there on the cold sand.

He felt his eyes sting, and he couldn't stop it, and he didn't try. He stood there at the bottom of a crack in a cliff he'd never known was there, in front of a cave Harry Clearwater had built a fire in, with the dawn coming up gray behind him and the sea going in front, and let them sting.

"Okay," he said. His voice cracked. "Okay. I'll tell her."

Leah looked at him a moment longer.

Then she turned around. She turned her back on him. Deliberately. Slowly. And she walked the three steps back across the sand to the black wolf, and leaned her shoulder against his foreleg, and stood there. Facing the sea.

Sam had been on the other end of Leah Clearwater turning her back on him a hundred times. He knew every version of it. He knew the angry one and the hurt one and the one that meant *I'll never forgive you* and the one that meant *I can't look at you right now or I'll break*.

He'd never seen this one.

This one just meant she was done looking at him. And had somewhere better to look.

---

"The Council's at seven," Sam said. To the black wolf. "Tonight. At Billy's. Old Quil wants to see you."

"I'll be there," said the black wolf. "Billy says not to wear the suit."

"Billy's right." Sam hesitated. "Old Quil hates suits. He says they make men look like they're about to take something."

"Sensible man."

"He's going to ask you a lot of questions."

"I'll answer the ones I can."

Sam looked at him. At the green eyes, very calm, over Leah's head. At the size of him. A full head over Jacob. Two over Sam.

"What *are* you?" Sam said. He didn't mean to. It just came out.

The black wolf considered him.

"Busy," it said. "Mostly." And then, after a moment, a little softer: "Hers. Apparently. Same as she's mine. I'll tell Old Quil the rest, if he asks nicely."

Sam stood there for another few seconds. Then he nodded. Just once. And he turned around and went back up the crack.

It was a long climb. The walls scraped his bare shoulders. When he got to the top, Paul and Jared were sitting on the rock, both of them, in their wolf shapes, staring at him.

Paul was still pinned. Sam let him go. He felt the Alpha weight lift off him, and felt Paul's whole body sag with the relief of it, and then felt his mind come back on like a radio somebody had turned up full.

*Sam. SAM. He called you an IDIOT. To your FACE. In front of US. And you just—you just SAID it, you just said "yeah I've heard that too," Sam, what is—*

*Shut up, Paul.*

*—and he's the size of a HOUSE, and he TALKS, and he knew my NAME, and he told me to UNCLENCH, Sam—*

*Shut up, Paul,* Sam thought again. And he was surprised to find, as he thought it, that he was smiling.

He stood at the top of the cliff and looked out at the sea. The sun was coming up behind him. Here it came up over the hills, and it lit the tops of the sea stacks first, one at a time, gold, like somebody going down a row of candles with a match.

He thought about the weight he'd carried for two years. He'd always thought, when it came off, it would come off all at once. Like taking off a pack at the end of a long patrol. It hadn't. It was still there. It would probably always be there, some of it. But it had moved. It sat different. Lighter, somehow, in a way he couldn't explain, like somebody had come along and shifted it to a better spot on his shoulders, where he could carry it.

He thought about a black wolf, a full two heads taller than him, lying on the sand in the dark with its chin resting on Leah Clearwater's head. Holding the weight of its own skull up off her. Just slightly. Just enough. So she wouldn't feel it.

*Come on,* Sam thought to the others. *Let's go home.*

He phased, and they went.

### IX. Leah

*The same morning*

She stood at the mouth of the cave until Sam was gone.

She heard him go up the crack. Heard his shoulders scraping the rock, and his breathing, and then, at the top, Paul's voice coming down the slot, faint, a yelp of pure outrage, cut off. She heard three wolves go away along the top of the headland toward the reservation, toward home.

She didn't watch them go. She stood at the mouth of her father's cave with her shoulder against the black wolf's foreleg, looking at the sea, and the sun came up behind the hills and lit the tops of the sea stacks one at a time, gold.

She waited for it to hurt.

It always hurt, after Sam. Every time. For two years it hadn't mattered what he said or how he said it, *sorry* or *Leah* or *please* or nothing at all. It had hurt. Like pressing on a bruise that never healed, that went down to the bone. She'd gotten so used to it she'd stopped noticing it most days. It was just there. A sore place, under everything, that she walked around without thinking about.

She stood there in the dawn and pressed on it.

It was still there. A little. She thought it might always be there, a little. But it was small now. It was a bruise, the ordinary kind. The kind that turns yellow at the edges and goes away.

*Go home, Sam. Emily will be worried.*

She'd said that. She'd thought it, and he'd said it for her, and Sam had stood there on the sand in his cutoffs with his eyes wet, and she hadn't felt anything break. Not in him. Not in her. It had just been true. She'd thought it and it had been true and that was all.

She felt the black wolf shift his weight against her shoulder.

She looked up at him. He was looking down at her. His green eyes were very quiet.

*Did I do okay?* she thought. She didn't know if he could hear it. She didn't know if she wanted him to.

"You did fine, love," he said quietly.

So he could hear it.

*You called him an idiot.*

"I did."

*To his face.*

"Well," said the black wolf. "He is one."

Leah's wolf made the huffing sound again. The almost-laugh. She couldn't help it.

"He took it well," the black wolf added, after a moment. "I'll give him that. Better than I would've."

*He's not a bad guy,* Leah thought. She was surprised to find she meant it. *He's not. He never was. He just—*

"I know."

*He couldn't help it.*

"I know, Leah." He lowered his head and touched his nose to the top of hers. Very lightly. "Still an idiot."

---

She phased back first.

She went round the curve of the cave wall to where the rock jutted out, where he couldn't see her, though she knew he'd turned his head away anyway. She felt him do it. And she let the wolf go, and stood up on two legs on the cold sand, shivering, with the wind off the sea going straight through her. She untied the bundle from her ankle. Her shorts were damp. Her T-shirt was damp and full of sand and smelled like wet dog, because it was wet dog, technically. She put them on anyway.

When she came back round the rock, he was standing at the mouth of the cave.

On two legs. With his back to her. Looking out at the sea.

He was barefoot. He was wearing a faded gray T-shirt and a pair of soft flannel pants, dark blue, with a pattern on them that she realized after a second was little white sheep. His hair was sticking up on one side, like he'd slept on it. Which he had, she guessed, sort of, about four hours ago, in a bed in a tent thirteen miles away, before he got up and turned into a wolf the size of a truck and ran through the dark to sit under her window.

He was completely dry.

She stood there on the sand and looked at him. At his T-shirt, dry. His flannel pants with the sheep on them, dry. His bare feet on the sand, clean. She'd watched him go through the Bogachiel in her head last night, through the rope. She'd felt the cold of the water. She'd watched him run through a wave on First Beach that broke over both of them up to the shoulder. She'd watched him go down a crack in a cliff hung with wet ferns, squeezing.

There wasn't a speck of sand on him. Not a drop of water. He looked like he'd just stepped out of a closet.

Her own shorts were stuck to her thighs.

"How," Leah said.

He turned round.

"How what?"

"How are you *dry*."

He looked down at himself. At the T-shirt. At the sheep. He seemed to notice the sheep for the first time, and something went across his face that might have been embarrassment.

"Magic," he said.

"That's not fair."

"Bit unfair," he agreed.

"I've got sand in my *shorts*. I've got sand in places I didn't know I *had*. I've been tying my clothes to my leg with a *bootlace* every night for eight months like an *idiot* and you just—you just turn back and you're *dressed*? In *pajamas*? With *sheep* on them?"

"They were a gift," he said, with dignity. "From a friend's mum. Every Christmas, a new pair. She's very consistent."

Leah stared at him.

She opened her mouth to say *I hate you*.

She got as far as *I*.

Her jaw locked.

She stood there on the sand in her damp shorts with her mouth shut and her teeth together and the muscle jumping in her cheek, fighting it. She fought it for a good five seconds. She threw everything she had at it. It didn't move. It was like trying to push a door that had been nailed shut from the other side.

He was watching her. His face was perfectly straight. She could feel, through the rope, exactly how hard he was working to keep it that way.

She unlocked her jaw.

"*Damn* it," Leah said.

And he lost it.

He laughed. He put his head back and laughed out loud at the mouth of her father's cave, with the sun coming up gold on the sea stacks behind him, a big, helpless, delighted laugh that went down the rope and hit her square in the chest. And she stood there with her fists clenched and her face on fire and tried to stay angry, she really did, she tried as hard as she'd tried to say the words.

She couldn't do that either.

---

They sat at the mouth of the cave for a while after that, with their legs hanging over the edge. Eighty feet of air under their feet, and the beach below, and the surf coming in white round the bottoms of the stacks. The sun was all the way up now. It was a clear morning, the first one in a week, cold and bright and blue, the kind you got maybe four times a winter on the coast. The sea was green out near the stacks and blue further out and silver right at the edge where the sun hit it.

He sat with his elbows on his knees. She sat with her knees pulled up. They weren't touching. There was about a foot of rock between them.

The rope didn't care about the foot of rock.

"Your dad built that fire," he said. Not a question.

"Yeah."

"How old were you?"

"First time? Eleven." She looked at the ring of stones behind them. "Last time, sixteen. I spent the whole night texting Sam. Dad sat right there and roasted marshmallows and didn't say anything about it. I was so embarrassed to be out here with him." She looked down at the sea. "He died in March."

"I know."

"His heart. It was the—when Seth and I first phased. It was the same night. Or close. The doctors said he had a bad heart, he'd always had it, it would've happened anyway. Everybody says that." She stopped. "I've never brought anybody here."

"I know," he said again. Quieter.

They sat there.

"Your parents," Leah said. "At dinner. You said they died when you were a baby."

"I was one."

"Both of them?"

"Same night. Same man." He was looking at the sea. "My godfather, after. When I was seventeen. Different man. Same reason, in the end."

Leah looked at him sideways. He didn't look back. His face was very still.

She didn't ask what the reason was. She could feel him not wanting to say it, through the rope. She'd have felt it if he were hiding it. This was more like a door he'd open if she knocked on it. But he'd rather she didn't knock today. Not here. Not in her father's cave, the morning after her father's dressing.

She didn't knock.

"I rang my boss," he said after a while. "Night before last. After you went home. About one in the morning."

"You what?"

"I'd lied to him. Earlier that night. About you. About all of it. Told him there'd been no complications." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I lay in bed for an hour and forty minutes after that and couldn't stand it. So I got up and rang him back and told him I'd lied. And then I told him everything."

Leah stared at him. "*Everything*?"

"Everything that happened. About this—" He touched his chest. "I told him it existed, and that's all. He wanted it for a file. I said no."

"You said *no* to your *boss*."

"I've been saying no to him for seven years. He likes it, I think. He says it's the only reason he keeps me." He looked at the sea. "I told him I was compromised. I told him to send someone else. I told him if it ever came down to the job or you, it'd be you. Every time."

Leah didn't say anything.

She didn't have anything she could say. It had gone straight into her chest, down the rope, the way everything he said went in, true, every word. *Every time.* She sat at the edge of the cave with her knees pulled up and felt it land.

"What did he say?" she said finally. Her voice came out funny.

"He said I was exactly where I needed to be." Something happened at the corner of his mouth. "And then he made me liaison."

"Liaison."

"To the protector line. That's you lot. The packs. The tribe. Officially. On paper." He shrugged. "Apparently I'm the Department's man in La Push now. Billy's going to love that. Old Quil's going to love it even more."

"Liaison," Leah said again. "So you're—what. You're staying?"

"Seems so."

"For how long?"

He turned his head and looked at her for the first time since they'd sat down. His eyes were very green in the morning light. Very clear.

"He said a good while," Harry said. "He said it's the most interesting square mile on the planet, and he'd have to be a fool to pull me out of it." A pause. "I'd have stayed anyway."

She felt that one go in, too.

She looked away from him. Out at the sea stacks, gold at the tops and black at the bottoms, with the white surf going round their feet. Her eyes were stinging. It was the wind. It was cold, and it was coming right off the water into her face, and that was all it was.

"Don't," she said, "say stuff like that."

"Why not?"

"Because I can tell you mean it."

"I do mean it."

"*I know*. That's why."

He didn't say anything else. He just sat there next to her at the edge of her father's cave in his stupid sheep pajamas, with a foot of rock between them, and let it be quiet. And after a while, without looking at him, without deciding to, she put her hand down flat on the rock in the space between them.

After a moment, he put his down next to it.

Their little fingers were touching. That was all. Just the edges of them, on the cold rock, in the sun.

The rope hummed.

---

"I'm hungry," Harry said, eventually.

"You're always hungry."

"I ran thirteen miles in the middle of the night and then lay on a cave floor for five hours with a wolf on my arm. I'm *very* hungry." He looked at her sideways. "Is there pie at your mum's?"

Leah thought about her mother's kitchen. About the counter, and the top of the fridge, and the stovetop, and the inside of the oven where Sue had put the overflow, and the two big Tupperware boxes she'd seen her mom fill with oyster dressing and put on the top shelf of the fridge with *HARRY* written on the lids in black marker. She hadn't known which Harry her mom meant. She didn't think her mom knew either.

"There's always pie," she said.

He stood up. He held his hand down to her.

She looked at it. A big hand, square, with old calluses across the palm and along the inside of the first finger. She thought about not taking it. She thought about standing up on her own, the way she'd stood up on her own for two years.

She took it.

He pulled her up. He didn't let go right away. Neither did she.

"Seth's going to have so many questions," Leah said. "When he wakes up and I'm gone."

"Seth's going to have so many questions regardless."

"Mom's going to look at me."

"Your mum's always going to look at you. That's what mums are for."

"You're going to the Council in *that*?" She looked down at the sheep. "Tonight? In front of Old Quil?"

Harry looked down at himself too.

"Billy said not to wear the suit," he said.

Leah laughed. It came out of her loud and sudden and startled, and it echoed off the back of the cave and out over the edge and down eighty feet to the beach. A gull lifted off one of the sea stacks and wheeled away, crying.

She didn't let go of his hand. She led him back to the crack in the rock, and up it, the steep way, her in front, him behind, the ferns brushing them both. The way her father had led her up it at eleven, with a thermos of cocoa and a bag of jerky, holding on tight on the steep part so she wouldn't fall.

At the top, the wind hit them. Cold and bright and full of salt. The whole coast was laid out in front of them in the sun, the beaches and the headlands and the black teeth of the stacks going all the way north to James Island, and beyond it, smoke going straight up from a hundred chimneys on the reservation, into a blue sky.

Somewhere down there was her mom's house. With the porch light off, and her brother asleep on the couch, and her mother standing at the kitchen window by now with a cup of coffee, looking out at the yard. At the tracks in the wet grass under the cedar. The big ones, and hers. Side by side.

And a fridge full of pie.

"Come on," Leah said. "Before Seth eats it."

She didn't let go of his hand the whole way down.
