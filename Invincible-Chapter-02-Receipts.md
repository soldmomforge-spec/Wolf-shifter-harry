# Chapter Two — Receipts

---

The sirens got there first. Then the SUVs.

Eve stayed where she was, in the middle of the road with her hands still glowing, and watched three black Suburbans come around the corner by the dead pizza sign and stop in a fan across all four lanes. Doors opened. Agents got out in body armor and gloves, moving fast, and two of them pulled a folded black bag out of the back of the lead vehicle and stopped dead when they saw what they were supposed to put in it.

"Both bits," Eve heard herself say.

They looked at her.

"Both halves. Get them both to Cecil." Her voice sounded strange to her, flat, borrowed from somebody else. "He'll want them back."

One of the agents, an older woman with a scar through one eyebrow, looked at her for a long second and then nodded and unfolded a second bag.

Eve let the glow go out of her hands. She looked north.

The scar in the sky was already coming apart. The long gray line of disturbed smoke that had pointed the way they'd gone was fraying at the edges, breaking up into the ordinary haze of a burning parking garage and a city full of car alarms. In another minute there would be nothing left of it at all.

She thought about flying after it.

She thought about Mark's face as he went up. She'd known that face since the first week he'd put on the suit, goofy and nervous and too eager, asking her dumb questions about flight in a voice that cracked. She'd never once seen it look like that. Blank. Emptied out. As if somebody had reached in and switched off whatever was behind it, and he'd gone up into the sky beside his father without a word because there was nothing left in him to say one with.

She could catch them. Probably. She could try.

And then what? Get between Mark and the man who'd just torn the Immortal in half with his bare hands? Pull Mark away by the arm, and watch Omni-Man decide what that was worth?

*If that kid comes back without his dad, he's going to need a friend who isn't a government. That's you.*

She put her feet down flat on the asphalt and stayed.

---

Lake Michigan came up under them gray-green and wrinkled, and Mark watched it come with his eyes stinging from the wind and his father's hand still on his shoulder.

They'd flown for eleven minutes. He'd counted. He'd counted because counting was something to do with his brain other than look at the picture it kept showing him, over and over, of a man coming apart in the middle of a street. The upper half going one way. The lower half going the other. The long red rope between them catching the sun.

His father hadn't said a word the whole way. Mark had tried, twice. *Dad. Dad, what—* and his father had only tightened his hand on Mark's shoulder and said, *Not here,* and kept flying.

So Mark had stopped trying, and started thinking.

There was an explanation. There had to be. His father had sat on the end of his bed every night for a year when Mark was seven and scared of the dark, back before Mark had powers, back when his dad was a guy who wrote travel books and was bad at grilling. His father had taught him to ride a bike in the cul-de-sac. His father had cried at Mark's eighth-grade graduation and pretended he had something in his eye. That man did not rip people in half.

So somebody was making him.

Mind control. It happened. Mark had read the GDA briefing packets Cecil gave him, the thick ones with the coffee rings that he'd mostly skimmed. Psychic villains. Parasites. Alien tech. Something had gotten into his dad, the same something that had gotten into the Guardians' headquarters that night, and it was using him like a puppet. That made sense. That was the only thing that made sense.

They came down out of the sky over the water a mile east of the city.

The breakwater was a long gray strip of concrete laid out on the lake like a dropped ruler, with a squat red-and-white lighthouse at the end of it. Gulls lifted off the concrete as they came in and wheeled away screaming. To the west, across a mile of chop, the whole Chicago skyline stood up out of the shore with the sun going down behind it, every tower turned into a black cutout edged with gold.

His father landed on the concrete near the lighthouse and let go of his shoulder.

Mark landed a few feet away. Waves slapped the breakwater on both sides. Spray came up over the lip in white sheets and fell back. His father stood with his back to the lighthouse and his cape snapping in the wind off the water and the Immortal's blood drying brown down the front of his white suit, and looked at the skyline, as if he'd brought Mark out here for the view.

"Dad," Mark said.

His father didn't turn around.

"Dad. Who's doing this?" Mark's voice came out higher than he wanted. He made it come down. "Who's in your head? Is it—it's somebody, right? Somebody got to you. Somebody got to you that night with the Guardians, and they've been—"

"Mark."

"—making you do stuff, and you can fight it, I know you can fight it, you're the strongest guy on the planet, you just have to—"

"Mark." His father turned around. His face was calm. The mustache, the square jaw, the faint lines at the corners of his eyes. The same face that had been across the breakfast table from Mark every morning of his life. "Nobody is controlling me."

Mark hit him.

His body did it before he'd decided anything, all at once, a straight right with every pound he had behind it and his whole flight speed from a standing start, and his fist landed flush on the side of his father's jaw with a crack that rolled out across the water like a rifle shot.

His father's head turned about two inches.

Mark hit him again. Left. Right. A knee into the stomach. He was screaming something, he didn't know what, the words just tearing out of him, *get out of him, get OUT of him, give him BACK*, and he hit his father in the face over and over, and every time his father's head moved a couple of inches and came back, patient, his hands hanging loose at his sides, his eyes on Mark's face the whole time.

On the sixth punch, his father caught his fist.

He closed his hand around Mark's knuckles almost gently, the way you'd catch a softball somebody lobbed at you, and held it, and Mark pulled and couldn't get it back. It was like having his hand set in concrete.

"Are you done?" his father asked.

Mark was breathing hard. His eyes burned.

"Nobody is in my head, son." His father let go of his fist. "This is me. This is who I am. I've been this the whole time."

"No."

"I'm going to tell you a story now," his father said, "and I need you to listen to all of it. Can you do that?"

Mark didn't say anything. He couldn't. His throat had locked.

His father took that as yes.

He talked for a long time. He talked with the waves coming up over the breakwater and the gulls coming back to land on the concrete one by one, cautious, and the sun dropping lower behind the skyline. He talked in the same voice he'd used to explain long division at the kitchen table. A planet called Viltrum. A people who'd made themselves strong by killing every one of their own who wasn't. Half of them, his father said. They'd culled half their population, deliberately, so that only the strongest would be left to breed. And then they'd gone out into the galaxy, and they'd taken it, world by world. An empire. The greatest one there had ever been.

Earth, his father said, was next. His father had been sent to get it ready. To grow strong here, and earn the trust of the people here, and when the time came, to make sure there was nothing on this planet that could stand in the Empire's way.

"The Guardians," Mark said. His voice came out of him in a croak.

"The Guardians were the strongest defense this world had." His father said it the way you'd say the weather was turning. "They had to go."

"You *killed* them."

"Yes."

"They were your *friends*."

His father looked at him with something like pity.

"They were nothing, Mark. Insects. Clever ones. All of them. They live eighty years if they're lucky, and they spend most of it afraid." He took a step closer. "You're not one of them. You're Viltrumite. You're going to live for a very long time, longer than you can imagine, and you're going to watch everyone you know here get old and die, one after another, like flowers. You'll have nothing at the end of it but us. Your people."

"And Mom?" Mark heard himself ask. "What's Mom?"

For the first time, his father hesitated. It was a short hesitation. A half-second.

"I care for your mother," his father said. "The way you'd care for a pet."

Something in Mark's chest turned over and went cold.

"No," he said.

His father's face didn't change.

"No," Mark said again, louder. His voice cracked down the middle. "No. I'm not doing it. I'm not—whatever this is, I'm not doing it. I'm not going to help you. I'm going to *stop* you."

His father sighed.

It was a small sigh, tired, the sound he made when Mark brought home a C in chemistry. He looked down at the concrete between them for a moment. Then he looked up again, and his eyes had gone flat, the way they'd gone in the street just before the Immortal came apart.

"Then I'm going to have to show you," his father said, "what being weak means."

He drew back his fist.

Mark saw it. He saw all of it, the shoulder rolling back and the weight coming onto the back foot, and he knew how fast that fist was going to come and knew, in a sick white flash, that he couldn't stop it and couldn't get out of the way, and he braced, because it was the only thing left to do.

His father's fist never left his shoulder.

Something hissed across the air between them, a sound like a sheet tearing, and his father's white suit split open across the chest.

A long clean diagonal line, left shoulder to right ribs. The fabric parted along it and fell open, and under it, on his father's chest, a thin pink line appeared on the skin, as if someone had drawn it there with a red pen. One single bead of blood welled up at the end of it and sat there.

His father looked down at it.

Mark looked down at it.

It was the first time in his life he'd ever seen his father cut.

"Evening," said a voice from the empty air ten feet to Mark's left.

His father's head came around. So did Mark's.

There was nothing there. Concrete. Spray. A gull that had landed on the breakwater a few seconds ago, now walking very fast away from that patch of concrete with its wings half-raised.

Then a head appeared in midair.

It was a wolf's head. Long-muzzled, black-furred, with tall pointed ears and a pair of bright green eyes, hanging five and a half feet off the concrete with nothing under it. As Mark stared, a hand—a paw, a big black clawed paw—came out of nowhere beside it and pushed back a fold of something, and the something slid away like water, and the rest of him was there.

He was huge. Taller than Mark's father by half a foot or more, broad through the shoulders, standing upright on two legs with the whole length of his long black footpaws planted flat on the concrete like a man's. He was covered in black fur, and over the fur he was wearing armor. Mark had never seen anything like it. Scales. Overlapping plates of scale, each one the size of a playing card and colored a green so dark it was almost black, shading to a sick bright emerald where the dying sun hit the edges. It covered his chest and his shoulders and ran down his forearms and his shins, and a narrower run of smaller scales wrapped the base of the long black tail that hung behind him. Over all of it, slipping off his shoulders and gathered in one paw, was a cloak, silvery-gray, so thin and fluid that Mark couldn't tell where its edges were.

In his other paw he held a stick.

A wand, Mark realized. It looked like a wand. Fifteen inches of pale gray wood, knobbed along its length like a twig off an old tree.

There was a ring on one of the clawed digits of his paw, a heavy black thing with a dark stone set in it. Around his neck on a fine gold chain hung something that glinted, a little hourglass set inside a pair of spinning rings, and as Mark looked at it he saw the sand inside it was running the wrong way. Up.

"Sorry to interrupt the family meeting," the wolf said. His voice was low and rough, with an accent Mark couldn't place, something that bent the vowels. "Couldn't help overhearing. *Viltrum.*" His muzzle curled. "Sounds like a cough syrup."

Mark's father said nothing. He was looking at the wolf the way Mark had seen him look at a new villain, back when Mark had thought those looks meant his father was worried. Calm. Measuring.

"Who—" Mark started.

"Harry." The green eyes flicked to him. "You're Mark, ja? Your mum's safe. Cecil's people have her. She's at the hole in the ground with my girlfriend, she's not hurt, and she's very, very worried about you." The eyes went back to Mark's father. "And you, boet, I've been told I'm meant to have a word with."

Mark's father tilted his head, very slightly.

"You work for Cecil."

"Everybody works for Cecil eventually," Harry said. "I'm freelance."

Mark's father looked down once more at the thin red line on his chest. He touched it with one finger. He looked at the finger, at the single smear of blood on its tip.

Then he looked back up, and smiled.

Mark had seen that smile before. Half an hour ago, in a street, right before a man came apart in his hands.

"Mark," his father said, without taking his eyes off the wolf. "Stay where you are. This won't take long."

Nolan Grayson had been on this planet for twenty years, and in that time he had learned to listen to it.

He could hear the city a mile west, every working part of it. Engines on Lake Shore Drive. A dog on a balcony in Streeterville. Eight million hearts going in eight million chests, a vast soft drumming like rain on a roof, and under that the wet click of every one of their joints. He could hear his son's heart, eight feet away, going like a bird's. He could hear the gulls' hearts, small and fast, and a fish turning over in the cold water at the foot of the breakwater.

Until it spoke, he had not heard the wolf at all.

That was the thing he turned over, in the half-second he gave himself to think. No heartbeat. No breath. No scent on the wind off the lake. It had stood ten feet from him for some length of time, long enough to hear him speak of Viltrum, and every sense he had, the senses the Empire had spent a thousand generations sharpening, had told him that patch of concrete was empty.

Now he could hear it. Now that the cloak was off its shoulders. Its heart was slow. Fifty beats a minute. Unhurried. The heart of something lying in the sun.

And there was the cut on his chest.

It stung. A thin bright line of sting, like a paper cut, and he could feel the skin along it already knitting, pulling closed. A paper cut. On him. The Guardians had cut him, that night—War Woman's mace, Red Rush's fists—and it had taken all seven of them throwing everything they had. This thing had done it from ten feet away with a stick, without moving.

He filed that. Then he set it aside.

"What are you?" he said.

"Harry." The wolf's ear flicked. "We did this bit."

"That's a name."

"It's the one I've got." It shifted its weight. Its footpaws were bare, long and black and padded, the whole length of them flat on the concrete. Between the plates of that strange green armor, Nolan saw faded denim at the thighs. Blue jeans. Under scale armor. "You want a species, I've got one of those too, but it's a long story and you won't like it."

"You're an animal," Nolan said. "In armor. With a stick."

"Ja, I get that a lot."

Nolan moved.

He went from stillness to full speed in the space between two of the gulls' heartbeats. He had killed things with this movement on more worlds than this one had names for. It was the first thing a Viltrumite child learned and the last thing a Viltrumite soldier stopped practicing: no wind-up, no tell, the whole body arriving at once behind the fist, so fast that the air didn't have time to get out of the way and broke around his knuckles in a cone of white vapor.

His fist went through the place where the wolf's head had been and hit nothing.

The concrete of the breakwater cracked under his forward foot in a star. Spray came up off the lake on both sides in a sheet twenty feet high. And the wolf was standing three feet to his left, exactly where it had been a moment ago, except a single step over.

It had moved before he did.

Nolan felt that land somewhere behind his sternum. He had seen it. The wolf's weight had shifted left a fraction of a second *before* Nolan's own weight had come forward. It had already been going before there was anything to react to.

"Missed," the wolf said.

Nolan turned and hit it again.

Left hook, at the ribs. It wasn't there. Uppercut, from the turn. Not there. He came in low, at its legs, to sweep it off the breakwater into the lake, and it simply wasn't standing where he arrived, and he heard its voice from behind him, unhurried, saying "Nope," and he spun and it was ten feet off on the other side of the lighthouse, and he hadn't heard it move.

No footfall. No displaced air. It was there, and then it was somewhere else, with nothing in between.

He stopped.

He stood on the cracked concrete with the spray falling around him and breathed through his nose, once, slowly, and looked at it. Really looked, the way he'd been taught, past the shape and into the details.

The little hourglass on the chain around its neck. The sand in it was running upward, a thin steady trickle rising from the bottom bulb into the top. As he watched, the trickle sped up, and the two rings around the glass spun faster, and the wolf's green eyes flicked a fraction to the right of Nolan's face, toward the spot where Nolan had been about to move.

Nolan didn't move.

The trickle slowed.

"You're seeing it," Nolan said. "Before I do it."

"Ja," the wolf said. "A bit."

"How far?"

"Far enough." Its muzzle curled at one corner, showing teeth. "You've got very readable shoulders, boet. Anyone ever tell you that?"

Nolan let a breath out through his nose.

Fine. Speed was not going to do it. Speed was the thing the creature had prepared for, and the Empire did not waste force on a thing's strongest point. You found the thing it cared about. You put your hand on that, and you squeezed, and it came to you.

The creature had come here because of Mark.

Nolan turned his head and looked at his son.

Mark was still standing where Nolan had left him, eight feet away by the lighthouse, both fists clenched at his sides, his face white and wet. He met his father's eyes and Nolan watched him understand, a second too late, what that look meant.

Nolan went for him.

It was a short distance. Eight feet. There was no version of eight feet in which anything on this planet could get between him and his son before he arrived.

Halfway there, he hit a wall.

It was transparent. He didn't see it until he was in it. A pane of something, a faint shimmer in the air around Mark like heat over a road, and Nolan's shoulder met it at a speed that would have taken him through a mountain—

—and every ounce of that speed came back.

It *returned* him. The whole of his momentum turned around on the exact line he'd come in on and handed back to him in one piece, and Nolan Grayson left the breakwater going backward at the speed he'd arrived.

The world went gray-green and white. He hit the surface of the lake a hundred yards out and skipped. Hit again. Skipped again, each impact a slap of cold water and a burst of spray. On the fourth one he went under, into a dim churning green, and came to rest in a cloud of silt and bubbles eighteen feet down, a quarter of a mile from the breakwater, with cold water in his nose and the cape wrapped around his head.

He hung there in the green for a moment.

Then he tore the cape off his face and went up.

He came out of the lake in a column of water and climbed into the air and hung there over the chop, dripping, and looked back across the quarter-mile at the breakwater. His son was standing where he'd been, unharmed, with a faint shimmer in the air around him like a soap bubble. The wolf was standing at the end of the concrete with the lighthouse at its back, looking at him.

He could hear its voice from here, though it was barely above conversation.

"That one's called the Bouncer," it said. "I made it up myself. You like it?"

Nolan flew back.

He did it slowly this time. He came in at a walking pace through the air and set down on the far end of the breakwater, forty feet from the wolf, and water ran out of his hair and down his face and dripped off the end of his mustache. The cut on his chest had closed. There was a faint pink line where it had been.

"You're very clever," Nolan said.

"I've had time."

"Cleverness runs out," Nolan said. "Every clever thing I've ever met thought it had more of it than it did. Your tricks have limits. I'll find them. And when I do, you'll be standing in front of me with nowhere to go, and there won't be a stick in the galaxy that helps you."

"Could be." The wolf seemed to consider it. "Could be. Long night ahead, though." It dipped a paw into the gap between two of its armor plates and came out with something small and silver between two claws. A coin. An American quarter. It turned the coin over, once, so the last of the light caught on it. "Bit crowded here, I reckon. Lot of people over there." It tipped its muzzle west, at the city. "Eight million or so. You'd know better than me. You can hear them."

Nolan said nothing.

"Mark," the wolf said, without looking around. "Thornton Quarry. South side of the city, off the interstate. Great big hole in the ground, you can't miss it. If you come, you stay on the rim. That's not optional."

"What—" Mark said.

"Call it, boet," the wolf said to Nolan, and flicked the coin at him.

It came across the forty feet of breakwater spinning end over end, a little silver flicker in the dusk. Nolan watched it come. It was slow. It was absurdly, insultingly slow, a child's toss, and his hand came up and closed around it out of a reflex older than this planet's oldest city, because a Viltrumite caught what was thrown at him. Always. He had been catching things since before he could walk.

The coin was cold in his palm. Much colder than metal had any right to be.

He had time to think, *Oh,* and nothing else.

Something hooked him behind the navel and pulled.

The world wrenched sideways. The breakwater and the lake and the burning skyline went into a single smear of color and sound and *away*, and there was a howling in his ears and a spinning in his skull and no up and no down and no air, and his hand was locked around that coin and he couldn't open it—

—and the last thing he heard, distant, already fading, was the wolf's voice on the wind behind him.

"Heads. You lose."

On the main wall of the GDA ops floor, Nolan Grayson's signal jumped twenty-three miles in under a second.

There was no audio. There'd been no audio for any of it. Platform Two had picked them up over the lake as they came down, and since then Cecil had been watching the breakwater from three hundred miles straight up, a long gray stripe in a sheet of gray-green water, the figures on it reduced by the satellite's best optics to something the size of chess pieces. He'd watched the boy throw punches. He'd watched the boy stop. He'd watched them stand there for a long time, talking about God knew what, while Debbie Grayson stood at his elbow with her fingernails dug into Wren's forearm hard enough to leave marks.

Then a third chess piece had appeared out of nothing beside them, black on the gray concrete, and the room had gone quiet.

They'd watched Nolan swing at it and miss. Swing and miss. Swing and miss. They'd watched him turn on his own son, and Debbie had made a sound in her throat, and then they'd watched Omni-Man go skipping backward across a quarter-mile of Lake Michigan like a flat stone off a pond, four long white splashes laid out in a line on the water, and somebody near the back of the room had said "*Holy shit*," out loud, and nobody had told them off.

And now Nolan's signal was twenty-three miles south, and the black chess piece was gone from the breakwater too, and the boy was alone on it.

"Where is he?" Cecil said.

"Signal's stationary. Coordinates are—" The tech at station four was typing fast. "Thornton Quarry, sir. South Cook County. It's an aggregate quarry, limestone, one of the biggest open pits in the world. It's—sir, it's a mile and a half long and better than four hundred feet deep."

"Who's in it?"

"Shift change was at five. Satellite thermal shows—" More typing. "Nobody. A couple of vehicles parked at the scale house on the north rim. Nobody in the pit."

"He moved him somewhere empty," Wren said quietly.

Cecil looked at her.

She was still standing with Debbie Grayson, still holding her hand, though Debbie's grip had eased a little. Wren's eyes were on the wall. They were red at the edges and very steady.

"That was a portkey," she said. "Same as my ring. He threw something, Uncle Cecil, did you see? Right at the end. He threw something and Nolan caught it."

"How'd he know he'd catch it?"

"Because Harry would have." Wren's mouth twitched. "He says anybody who's fast enough always catches. They can't help it. He says it's the oldest con there is."

"Sir. Second contact at the quarry." The tech pointed. On the wall, the satellite image had swung south, the dark gray scar of the pit appearing out of the green patchwork of the suburbs, a long ragged trench of terraced stone with water lying in the bottom of it in black pools. A second black dot stood on one of the upper terraces. "That's—it's him. The wolf. He just appeared there."

"And the boy?"

"Invincible's airborne, sir. Heading south. Fast."

"Of course he is," Cecil said.

"Drone's on station in thirty seconds. We're getting a lens on them."

The drone feed came up on the left side of the wall.

It was close. It was high-definition. It was the first clear look anyone in that room had gotten at what Potter was wearing, and Cecil heard the reaction ripple through the ops floor in a wave of indrawn breath.

He stood on the haul road at the lip of the pit's second terrace, in the last flat orange light of the sunset, a hundred feet above the place where Nolan Grayson was picking himself up off the stone. Black fur. Bare footpaws planted on the gravel. A long silvery cloak hanging off one shoulder, hood thrown back, so fine the light seemed to go into it and not come out. Under the cloak, armor: overlapping scale plates over his chest and shoulders and down his arms, a green so dark it was nearly black, flashing poison-bright where the sun hit the edges.

"What," Cecil said, "is he wearing."

"Basilisk," Wren said.

"The hell's a basilisk?"

"A snake." She didn't look away from the wall. "A very, very big snake. It lived under his school. It killed people by looking at them. He killed it with a sword when he was twelve." She paused. "He went back for the hide when he was about forty. He says it took him most of a summer to skin and he never got the smell out of his boots."

Cecil stared at her.

"It's spell-proof," she said. "Mostly. And knife-proof. And bullet-proof, he says, but he's never let anyone test it, because he says that's just asking for it."

"The cloak?"

"Makes him invisible."

"I've got a dozen guys in this building can make themselves invisible."

She shook her head. "Nothing can see him under it. Not cameras. Not thermal. Not dogs. He says it was made so Death couldn't find the man wearing it. He's had it since he was eleven. It was his dad's."

On the wall, Potter turned his head and looked straight up into the drone's lens, as if he knew precisely where it was. Then he looked back down into the pit.

"And the stick," Cecil said.

"That's the wand."

"The wand."

"*The* wand. There's a story." She swallowed. "He put it back in a dead man's tomb when he was seventeen. It didn't stay there. Neither did the stone in that ring. He dropped the stone in a forest, and twenty years later it turned up in his coat pocket. They keep coming back to him, all three of them, the wand and the stone and the cloak. He says they followed him through the arch, too. A few weeks after Mojave he woke up in the motel and they were sitting on the nightstand." She shook her head slightly. "And the thing around his neck—the little gold hourglass—that's his. He built it. He calls it the Lookahead. It started out as a Time-Turner, which is—never mind what that is. He took it apart and rebuilt it and now it shows him a few seconds ahead of where he is. Three or four. Five, if he concentrates." She drew a breath. "That's why Nolan couldn't touch him on the breakwater. Harry saw every punch before Nolan threw it."

The ops floor had gone very quiet around them. Cecil became aware that forty people were listening to his niece and none of them were pretending otherwise.

"He's had all this," Cecil said slowly. "The whole time. Three years. Under my nose."

"He keeps it somewhere. I've never figured out where. He just sort of—reaches." She finally looked at him. "You never asked, Uncle Cecil."

"I've got a file on him the size of a phone book."

"And you never *asked*." Her chin came up. "Also, you'd have tried to take it."

He opened his mouth. He shut it again.

She was right. He would have.

"Audio's live from the drone, sir," someone said. "Parabolic. We've got them."

The sound came up through the ops floor speakers: wind across the pit, the high thin whine of the drone's own rotors filtered down to a hum, and under it, distant and clear, a crunch of gravel as Nolan Grayson got to his feet on the terrace below.

Nolan looked up at the wolf. Even at that distance, even on a drone feed, Cecil could read his face. He had watched that face for twenty years. He knew what it looked like when Nolan was angry, and he knew what it looked like when Nolan was pretending to be angry for the cameras. This was a third face, one Cecil had never seen on him. Concentration. The look of a man adding up a column of numbers that would not come out right.

"Teleportation," Nolan said. His voice came through the drone's mic thin and metallic. "Precognition. A force field that returns kinetic energy. Invisibility." He brushed limestone dust off the front of his ruined suit. "Anything else I should know?"

"Loads," said Potter's voice.

Nolan went up at him.

He went up in a spiral, fast, the path curving, changing angle every few yards, the kind of approach you'd use against a gun that tracked. Testing the precognition. Trying to throw so many possible futures at it that it couldn't pick the right one.

Potter's wand moved.

It was the first time Cecil had seen him use it on purpose. A short, sharp diagonal slash, wrist only, the way a man flicks water off his hand. There was no light. There was no sound but a faint thin hiss.

Nolan Grayson came apart.

Not all the way. Three long lines opened across him in midair: one from his right collarbone to his left hip, one down the outside of his left arm from shoulder to elbow, one across the top of his right thigh. They opened as if he'd been unzipped. And out of them came blood, a long red sheet of it that came out of him all at once, the way water comes out over the lip of a dam, and hung in the air behind him as his flight faltered and he came down hard on the terrace forty feet below where he'd been aiming.

He landed on one knee. He put a hand flat against his chest, and blood came out between his fingers.

The worst part was that nothing was damaged.

Nolan lay on his back on the limestone with the sky going purple above him and checked himself, the way he'd been taught to check himself after a fall, joint by joint, from the toes up. Ankles. Knees. Hips. Spine. The crack he'd heard when his back arched had come from the stone under him. Everything was where it belonged. Everything worked.

And for half a second, every nerve he owned had been on fire.

He had been trained in pain. Every Viltrumite was. They began when you were small enough to cry about it, and they kept on until you stopped, and then they kept on a while longer to make sure. He had been burned and frozen and cut. He had been held under the surface of a gas giant's ocean until his lungs collapsed and then been made to swim up. He had learned the oldest lesson of the Empire in his own body: pain was information. It told you where you were hurt and how badly. You listened to it, and then you set it aside.

This pain had told him nothing.

It had no location. No source. No wound underneath it to explain it. It came from everywhere at once and it lied, and his body believed every word of the lie, and for half a second Nolan Grayson had not been a soldier of the Empire or a father or anything else. He had been a thing that screamed.

Then it stopped. And there was no injury to show for it, nothing to heal. Just the memory. And the memory was the worst part, too, because his body remembered and was afraid.

He had not been afraid in his body since he was a child.

He rolled onto his side. The cuts on his chest and arm and thigh pulled and he felt the blood move, warm and slow, out of them and down across his ribs and onto the stone. That was the other thing. They were not closing. He could feel his body trying, the way he could always feel it, a pull at the edges of every wound like a drawstring tightening—and then nothing. As if the thread had been cut. His skin reached for itself across the gap and could not find it.

He was cold. His hands were cold.

"You can stay down, you know."

The wolf's voice came from above him. It never shouted.

"Nobody's going to think less of you, boet. Well." A pause. "I will. But I already do, so it's not really costing you anything."

Nolan got his hands under him.

He didn't rush it. He'd learned that much. Every time he'd rushed it, every time he'd gathered his weight to go, the wolf had said that word, and the world had gone white. So he came up slowly, one knee, one hand on a boulder, keeping his body loose and his weight centered and his mind empty of intention. Nothing to read. Nothing to see coming.

He got to his feet.

The wolf stood above him on the haul road, the cloak hanging off its shoulder, the stick loose in its paw. Behind it the sky over the quarry rim had gone the color of a bruise. Its eyes were on his face. Green. Very steady. They hadn't left his face since he'd landed in this hole.

Nolan rose straight up off the stone.

He did it the way he did everything that mattered: completely, no hesitation, the thought and the act the same thing. *Up.*

And something in his head said *Down*.

It was his own voice.

That was the thing that froze him, for one long, sick instant, three feet off the ground. It was his own voice, in his own skull, in the exact place where his own will lived, and it said *down* with exactly the same weight he'd said *up*, and his body didn't know which one of them to believe. His flight went out like a blown fuse. He dropped. His feet hit the limestone and his knees buckled and he caught himself on one hand.

He knelt there, breathing.

He thought, very deliberately: *Up.*

*Down.*

He thought: *Forward.*

*Stop.*

His legs locked. He'd been about to spring, and they simply locked under him, the order and the counter-order arriving together, two hands on the same wheel turning it opposite ways.

He looked up at the wolf.

The wolf was looking at him. Its eyes had not moved. And Nolan understood, finally, why they had not moved.

"Get out of my head," he said.

"Make me," said the wolf.

Nolan made his mind a wall.

He knew how. The Empire taught that, too: there were species in the galaxy that could reach into a mind, and a Viltrumite whose thoughts could be read was a Viltrumite who could be used, and so you learned to build walls. He'd built them in his youth until they were thick and smooth and seamless, a fortress of stone in the back of his skull, and he'd sat behind them for twenty years on this planet without anyone ever so much as knocking.

He slammed them shut now. He felt them close.

And felt the wolf already inside.

The walls stood. The wolf was simply on the other side of them, with him. Leaning against the inside of the fortress like a man leaning in a doorway.

"Good walls," the wolf said aloud. Its voice was conversational. "Proper job. Somebody taught you right. Your lot build them for you, ja? When you're little?" Its ear flicked sideways. "I've been taking walls apart for two hundred years, boet. Yours are lovely. They're also made of paper."

Nolan surged up off the stone.

This time he skipped the thought entirely and just went, all instinct, the oldest part of him, the part that had been moving before it had words—

"*Crucio*."

White.

Fire. Every nerve. Every inch. His spine bowed, his jaw locked open, and the sound came out of him again, that sound, that thing-that-screams sound, and it went on for an eternity that the part of his mind still counting knew was half a second—

—and stopped.

He was on the ground again. On his face. Limestone grit in his mouth. His fingers dug into the stone and he could feel them trembling and could not make them stop.

"I'll be honest with you," the wolf said from above him. "I could do that all night. I'd rather not. It's a horrible thing and I hate the taste of it. But I can."

Nolan pushed himself up onto his hands.

His arms barely lifted him.

The pain had gone. What replaced it was weight, sudden and enormous, as if every cell of his body had turned to lead in the same instant. His palms sank into the limestone. Into solid stone, an inch, then two, the rock crumbling and compacting under them like wet sand. His knees went into it. The gravel around him popped and ground. He had carried the hull of a starship on his back once, on a world with three times this one's gravity, and it had been easier than lifting his own chest off this floor.

"That one's Deadweight," the wolf said. "Same you. Same size. Just a lot more of you per inch. About twenty times more, at the minute." Its ear flicked. "Takes a bit of getting used to. Don't worry, I'll give it back."

"You," he said. His voice came out rough. "Are an animal."

"Ja."

White.

He lay there, and it passed, and he was counting.

That was the thing. Through all of it, through the pain and the fear and the voice in his skull, some part of Nolan was still a soldier, and the soldier had been counting since the first time the wolf disappeared, and the soldier had noticed something.

It only ever went to certain places.

He lay on the gravel and let his eyes move and kept his head still. He'd seen the wolf vanish nine times since they'd come to this hole. Nine times it had reappeared. The precognition saw where he was going. Fine. But the wolf could only go where it had already decided to be.

"Getting tired?" the wolf asked. It was on the north rim, by the bulldozer. Point one.

Nolan got up.

He got up slowly. He let himself sway. He let his hand go to his chest and come away red, and he looked at it, and let the wolf see him look at it. That part wasn't hard. The blood was still coming. He'd lost more of it in the last five minutes than he'd lost in his entire life before tonight, and his vision had started going soft and gray at the edges, and his hands were so cold now that he could barely feel them.

There was a haul truck parked at the foot of the ramp below him. One of the big ones, yellow, the size of a house, wheels taller than a man. He'd seen it when he landed.

He walked down the ramp to it.

The voice in his head said *stop*. He stopped. He waited. He let the voice see that he was standing still, and that he was tired, and that he was looking at the truck the way a man looks at a thing he's thinking of sitting down against. Then he put one cold hand flat on the steel of the front bumper, and made his mind as empty as he knew how.

The soldier in the back of his skull did one piece of arithmetic. The wolf was at point one, on the north rim. The nearest other point to point one was the foot of the conveyor tower, a hundred and forty yards east.

He threw the truck.

He let his arm do it without thinking, the way it had done ten thousand things without asking his permission, and a hundred tons of steel and rubber left the ground end over end toward the north rim, and in the same instant, with everything he had left, Nolan went east.

Nolan went east, toward the conveyor tower, where the wolf would have to come.

He felt the voice in his skull say *stop* and it caught him by the ankles, but late. He felt the white fire come up in the edges of him like a wave cresting—"*Cru*—"—but late, the wolf's attention split between the truck and him and the voice in Nolan's head. And he was at the foot of the conveyor tower a fraction before the wolf arrived there, and when the wolf came out of nothing at point four Nolan was already reaching—

For the arm. For the wrist. For the clawed black paw with the stick in it.

He saw the hourglass on the wolf's chest spin like a top. He saw the green eyes go wide.

His fingers closed.

On wood.

The wolf had let go of the stick. In the last fraction of a second, seeing it coming, it had opened its paw and let the stick go rather than let him have the paw, and Nolan's fingers closed around fifteen inches of knobbled gray wood while the wolf vanished from in front of him like a light switching off.

Behind him, a hundred tons of haul truck hit the north rim of the quarry with a sound like the end of the world, and crumpled, and slid, and came down the terraces in a long grinding avalanche of steel and limestone.

Nolan stood at the foot of the conveyor tower with the wolf's stick in his hand.

He looked at it. It was very light. It held heat, more heat than his own cold hand. There was nothing special about it that he could see. Wood. Knots. A carved pattern at the base.

"So," he said, to the empty air, "this is what you've been using."

He snapped it in half.

It broke with a dry little crack. Easier than a pencil.

"*Oi!*"

The wolf's voice came from forty yards up the slope, from point five, and for the first time since Nolan had met it, the wolf sounded genuinely outraged.

"What the *f*—what the hell, man? That was a *gift!*"

They were empty.

He had felt the two halves of the stick in his fists a moment ago. He had felt them. Now his fists were closed on nothing. He opened them and looked at his palms, at the blood on them, his own, and there was no stick. No splinters. Nothing.

He looked up.

The wolf stood on the slope above him with the stick in its paw. Whole. Unbroken. Knobbled gray wood, fifteen inches, the carved pattern at the base. Nolan stared at it.

For twenty years on this planet, he had been the strongest thing on it. For longer than that, on more worlds than he could name, he had been one of the strongest things in the galaxy, a member of the strongest people that had ever lived. He knew what strength was. He had been made out of it.

He had broken the thing in its hand, and it had not stayed broken.

"What are you?" he said again. And this time it came out of him differently, and he heard that it did, and so did the wolf.

The wolf looked at him a long moment.

"Told you," it said. "Long story."

Mark heard his father screaming from a mile out.

He'd come south as fast as he'd ever flown, the interstate unspooling underneath him in a long gray ribbon of headlights, and the first scream had reached him somewhere over the Calumet River and nearly knocked him out of the sky. Then it came again.

He'd flown the rest of the way with his teeth clenched so hard his jaw ached.

He came in over the north rim of the quarry as a truck the size of a house came down the terraces.

It was already wrecked when he saw it, folded up on itself, rolling end over end down a slope of broken limestone in a roar of grinding steel and an avalanche of rock, and he pulled up hard and hovered there with the dust coming up around him. Below the dust, in the bottom of a hole so big he couldn't see the far end of it in the dusk, there were two figures.

One of them was his father. Standing at the foot of some kind of tall steel tower. Holding something in his hand.

Mark saw him break it.

Then he heard the wolf yell, outraged, a sound so ordinary and so furious that for one insane second Mark almost laughed—*What the hell, man? That was a GIFT!*—and saw his father look down at his own empty hands. Saw the wolf, forty yards up the slope, turning the stick over in its paw. Whole.

Mark landed on the rim.

His father was covered in blood.

Mark had never seen so much of it. His father's white suit was red from the chest down, soaked through, clinging to him. Long ragged cuts gaped across his chest and down one arm and across one thigh, and they were open, *still open*, and blood was coming out of them in slow steady pulses. The ground under his father's feet turned liquid.

It went gray and thick, like wet concrete when it's first poured. The solid limestone of the quarry floor, which had held up hundred-ton trucks for a hundred years, went soft around his father's boots in a circle ten feet across, and his father sank into it to the ankles, then the shins, then the knees.

He pulled up—and Mark saw it happen, saw his father's body try to rise and then *jerk*, as if a hand had grabbed him by the collar and yanked him back down, and his father's face twisted. He went to the waist.

He threw his arms out flat across the surface, trying to spread his weight, the way you'd do in quicksand, and the stone flowed up over his forearms and took them too.

Mark took one step off the rim and stopped himself with every bit of will he had.

His father went to the chest. He stopped struggling. He held still, buried to the ribs in liquid stone, with his arms out to either side and his head up and his eyes on the wolf, and he was breathing in short hard pulls.

Then something came up out of the stone.

Metal.

It rose out of the gray soup around his father the way a fish comes up out of dark water, slow, gleaming. It came up in bands. Long flat bands, each one as wide as Mark's hand, flowing out of the stone and curling over his father's arms where they lay flat. Wrists. Forearms. Elbows. They closed with a sound like a church bell struck very softly. A broad band came up around his father's chest and settled across it, over the cuts, and locked. Another closed across his shoulders.

The last one came up behind his father's head and curled around his throat.

A collar.

It closed with a click.

The stone went hard.

Mark felt it more than heard it, a deep shiver through the rim under his feet, and the gray soup around his father set solid in an instant and was limestone again, the same pale gray as the rest of the quarry floor. His father was in it to the chest. His arms were in it to the elbows. The dark bands came out of the stone and went over him and back into the stone, and the collar sat at his throat, and his father was not going anywhere.

His father strained.

Mark watched him do it. Watched every muscle in his father's neck and shoulders stand out like cables, watched his face go dark red, watched the veins rise in his temples. Nothing happened.

The metal didn't creak. The stone didn't crack. His father's face went redder and redder and then, all at once, he let go, and slumped against the bands, gasping.

And Mark understood what was wrong with the picture.

His father was *sweating*. His father was gasping like a man who'd just carried a refrigerator up three flights of stairs. His father's strength was the thing Mark had grown up with the way other kids grew up with the weather, a fact of the world, permanent, and it wasn't there anymore. Mark stared at it. His eyes were burning.

The wolf crouched down at the edge of the stone, beside his father.

Not all the way. Mark watched it. The air on the terrace got cold.

Mark felt it on the back of his neck first, a chill like someone had opened a freezer door behind him. His breath smoked. And at the edges of his vision, around the hollow where his father was held, there were people.

He turned his head. They didn't vanish.

They stood in a loose ring on the terrace around the stone, six of them, and they were made of light, or of mist, or of something Mark didn't have a word for. More solid than ghosts. Less solid than people. He could see the quarry wall through them, faintly. He could see their faces perfectly.

Red Rush. Lean, in his red suit, his arms folded.

Darkwing, dark-cowled, motionless.

War Woman, tall, her mace hanging at her side.

Mark knew those faces. He'd had them on his bedroom wall when he was ten, all seven of them on a poster from a comic shop, the Guardians of the Globe standing in front of a flag. He'd watched them on the news a hundred times. He'd watched their funeral on the news.

Red Rush turned his head, after a long time, and looked at Mark. Just looked. Then he nodded, once.

The teleport put Wren down on gravel in the dark with her stomach somewhere around her knees, and the first thing she saw when her eyes cleared was Harry's ears.

They came up. Both of them, at once, straight up, swiveling toward her across forty yards of quarry floor. Then the rest of him turned.

She'd seen him in the armor before. Once. A long time ago, on a winter night, when he'd taken it out of wherever he kept it to oil the scales and she'd sat on the stairs and watched him do it and hadn't said a word. It had looked different in the cabin by the woodstove. Here, under the GDA floodlights that were coming on all along the rim in hard white bars, it looked like exactly what it was: something that had been a monster once, cut up and fitted to the body of a man who'd killed it.

He was gray from the knees down with limestone dust. His fur was spiked and clotted along one forearm with somebody else's blood. The cloak hung off one shoulder, half-invisible in the floodlights, so that pieces of him seemed to be missing. His tail flicked when he saw her. Once.

Debbie Grayson went past her at a run.

She went straight down the slope of loose rock in her flats without seeming to notice it, and the tactical team scrambling down behind her had to hurry to keep up. Mark was standing by the stone. He turned at the sound of her and his face crumpled, all at once, like a little kid's, and then she had him. She had her arms around his neck and his head down on her shoulder, and he was half a head taller than her in a blue-and-yellow suit and he was shaking, and she was saying something into his hair over and over that Wren couldn't hear.

Wren stood on the gravel and let them have it.

Beside her, Uncle Cecil straightened his jacket. He looked down into the quarry for a long moment. Then he started walking.

She went with him.

They came down the terraces together, Cecil picking his way over the rubble with his hands in his pockets, Wren half a step behind, and the closer they came to the bottom the slower he walked. Around them the GDA spread out across the quarry floor: medics with kits, agents with rifles, two techs with some kind of scanner on a tripod who took one look at the dark bands coming out of the stone and stopped dead and started arguing in low voices.

Nolan Grayson was buried to the chest in solid rock.

Wren had seen him on television her whole life. She'd seen him on cereal boxes. She'd stood in a crowd on the Mall in Washington when she was eleven and watched him fly overhead in a parade, a white-and-red streak against the blue, and she'd screamed with everyone else.
