# North of Everything

## Chapter Four: Don't Test Me

---

Sam Uley had smelled it from four miles away.

He'd been in the kitchen with Emily when Seth's thoughts came screaming through the pack link—Jacob's pack, but the two Alphas could feel each other now, like two radios tuned close together, and when one of Jacob's kids lost his mind loud enough, it bled through. *Leah. LEAH. It's Leah, she's back, she's at Mom's, and there's something with her, something big, it smells like—* And then Jacob, sharp and fast, reaching across the gap the way he'd learned to do: *Sam. You need to see this.*

Sam had put down his coffee and walked out the back door into the rain and phased in the yard without even taking his jeans off.

Now he stood at the bottom of Sue Clearwater's porch steps in the rain, barefoot, with what was left of his jeans hanging off him in rags, and looked at the car in the driveway, and then at the front door, and waited.

He could feel his pack in the trees. Seven of them behind the house, in a long loose line through the alders, phased, bellies low, hackles up. Three more coming up from the beach. Jacob's six were out on the road. Their minds were a low hum at the back of his own, all of them pointed at the same thing, the way a compass needle points north. Paul was the loudest, as usual. Paul was always the loudest. *What the hell is that, what the hell is it, Sam, it smells like—*

Sam knew. He didn't know what to call it, but he knew.

It smelled like a wolf. And it didn't.

It smelled like earth, and pine, and cold stone at the bottom of a creek. It smelled like something enormous that had been very carefully put away. And under that, something Sam's wolf recognized before Sam did, something that made the hair stand up along the back of his neck even in human form: a smell like cedar smoke. Like the council fire at the bonfires, when Billy and Old Quil told the old stories. Like something very, very old.

His wolf didn't know whether to growl at it or lie down.

Six feet to his left, Jacob stood in the rain with his arms folded and his jaw set. He hadn't said anything since they'd come up the drive together. He didn't need to. Sam could feel him—not his thoughts, not anymore, but the shape of him across the gap between their packs. Wary. Tense. And under that, something Sam hadn't expected: something that felt almost like relief.

The front door opened.

---

Leah came out first.

Sam's breath went out of him.

He'd thought about this. He'd thought about it more than he'd ever admit to anyone, including Emily. In fifteen months, he'd thought about Leah coming home a hundred different ways. Leah coming back thin and wild and half-starved, the way Jacob had come back from Canada. Leah coming back angry, coming back broken, coming back phased and refusing to change back. Leah not coming back at all. Leah dead in a ditch somewhere in British Columbia and nobody ever knowing.

He hadn't thought about this.

She stood on the porch under the colored Christmas lights in a long dark coat, with her hair long again, down past her shoulders, the way it had been when she was sixteen. Her face was fuller. Softer. There was color in it. She stood straight, with her shoulders back and her chin up, and when she looked down the steps at him there was nothing in her face at all. No anger. No hurt. Nothing he could get hold of.

There was a ring on her left hand. A gray stone, flashing green under the lights.

Then the thing came out of the door behind her, and Sam stopped looking at the ring.

It had to duck to get through the doorway. It straightened up on the porch, and kept straightening, and its ears nearly brushed the porch roof. It stood on two legs like a man, in a long black coat, and it was covered in black fur, and it had the head of a wolf. Long muzzle. Black nose. Tall pointed ears turned toward him. Green eyes. A heavy black tail hanging down behind it, absolutely still.

It came and stood beside Leah, close enough that its arm brushed her shoulder, and looked down the steps at Sam.

In the trees, every wolf in his pack went silent at once.

"Sam," said Leah.

Her voice was flat and level. It was the voice she used to use with substitute teachers.

"Leah," said Sam.

He didn't know what else to say. He'd had a hundred things to say, for fifteen months, and they'd all gone.

"This is Harry," said Leah. "Harry, this is Sam Uley. And that's Jacob Black."

The wolf-thing looked at Sam. Then at Jacob. Its ears flicked, one toward each of them.

"Howzit," it said. Its voice was low and deep and rough around the edges, with an accent Sam had never heard before.

"*Hey*," said Jacob. He sounded a little strangled.

Sam didn't say anything. He looked at the thing called Harry, and Harry looked back at him, and Sam's wolf pressed up against the inside of his ribs and couldn't decide what it wanted.

He made himself speak.

"You can't be here," he said.

Harry's head tipped, very slightly, to one side.

"This is Quileute land," said Sam. "There's a treaty. There've been treaties on this land for seventy years. Nothing comes onto the reservation that isn't one of us. Not leeches. Not—" he stopped, because he didn't know what the end of that sentence was "—whatever you are. I don't know what you are. I don't know where you came from. But you can't be here."

"He's with me," said Leah.

"Leah—"

"He's with me, Sam."

"I can see that. I'm not talking to you. I'm talking to him." Sam looked up at Harry. "You need to leave. Get back in your car and go back to wherever you came from, and take—"

He stopped.

He'd been about to say *take her with you*. He didn't know why. He didn't want that. He'd spent fifteen months wanting the opposite of that.

Leah's face had gone very still.

"He's not going anywhere," she said. "He's my husband."

---

The rain came down. The Christmas lights blinked on the porch rail, red and green and blue and orange.

Sam heard it, in the trees, seven minds and then sixteen and then all of them, the word going down the pack link like a stone dropped down a well. *Husband. Husband. HUSBAND—* Paul's voice the loudest: *Her WHAT? She married THAT? Sam, she—*

"Your husband," Sam said.

"My husband."

"You're married."

"Since July." Leah held up her left hand. The gray stone flashed. "That's what this is, Sam. It's a wedding ring. People get them."

Sam looked at the ring. Then at Harry. Then at her.

Something in his chest had gone tight and strange. He didn't want to look at it. He'd spent fifteen months making himself not look at things.

"And what happens," Sam heard himself say, "when you imprint?"

---

He knew it was the wrong thing the second it came out of his mouth.

He knew it from the way Jacob turned his head and stared at him, appalled. From the way the whole pack link went dead quiet. From the way Leah's face changed. It went white, the way it used to in the first awful months, when he'd say Emily's name in front of her without thinking and watch her flinch. And then it went to stone.

"*How dare you*," Leah said.

"Leah, I'm only—"

"*How dare you.*" She came down one step. Then another. She was shaking. "You. *You*, of all the people on this whole earth. You stand in my mother's yard and you say that word to me. *You.*"

"It's a real question—"

"It was a real question when I was *sixteen*, Sam! It was a real question when you came back after two weeks of being gone and couldn't look at me! It was a real question the day Emily came to stay with my aunt and you walked into the kitchen and *looked at her*!" Her voice cracked right down the middle. "You don't get to ask me that. You don't ever get to ask me that. You don't get to *say that word to me*."

Sam stood in the rain and didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything to say.

Behind Leah, on the porch, Harry had gone very quiet.

Sam saw his head turn. Saw him look down at Leah, at the back of her head, at her shoulders shaking. Saw his ears tip toward her.

"Leah," Harry said. Very low. "That word. Is that the word for what he did? To you and your cousin?"

"Yes," Leah said, without turning around.

Harry was quiet for a moment.

"Right," he said.

He came down the porch steps. He didn't make a sound doing it. Sam had never seen anything that size move without making a sound—not even a leech—but the big black wolf-thing came down three wooden steps that creaked under Sam's own weight every time he came to Sue's for dinner, and they didn't creak. He stopped on the bottom step beside Leah and put one forepaw on her shoulder, very lightly, and looked at Sam.

"I don't know much about it," Harry said. "Imprinting. Leah told me some. Sam looked at her cousin, and something came down on him out of nowhere and decided for him, and he didn't get a choice. And neither did she." His ears were up and forward, pointed at Sam. "That right?"

Sam's jaw tightened. "It's not something you'd understand."

"Try me."

"It's—" Sam stopped. He didn't know how to explain it to something with a muzzle. He didn't know how to explain it to anyone. "It's the most sacred thing we have. It's how the spirits choose. It's—you see her, and she's everything. The whole world. Nothing else matters. You'd be anything for her. It's not a choice because it doesn't need to be. It's *right*. It's the rightest thing that's ever happened to you."

"And if Leah imprints," said Harry, "on somebody who isn't me. Then what?"

Sam looked at Leah. At the back of her neck, at her shoulders under Harry's paw. He made himself say it.

"Then it's right," he said. "Then that's who she's meant for."

"Okay," said Harry. "Then I'll break it."

---

There was a silence.

Sam stared at him.

"You'll *what*?"

"Break it," said Harry. He said it the way you'd say you were going to fix a leaky tap. "If she imprints. On anyone. I'll break it. Whatever it takes."

"You can't break an imprint."

"Ja, I heard you. I'll find a way." Harry's voice hadn't risen. It hadn't changed at all. "Because I know her, Sam. I've known her for fifteen months, and I've lived with her for fourteen, and I've watched her choose me every single day, on purpose, with her eyes open. Nobody made her. And I know for a fact that the one thing in this world she'd hate more than anything is waking up one morning and finding out something reached into her head and decided who she loves for her." He glanced down at Leah. "That's what happened to her once already. Because of you. She's not going to have it done to her twice."

Leah had stopped shaking.

"So if it ever happens," Harry said, "I'll break it. And then she can decide for herself whether she still wants me. That's how it's going to go."

"No," said Sam.

The word came out of him with the Alpha in it. He hadn't meant it to. He felt it go out through the pack link like a shockwave, felt every wolf in the trees flinch and drop lower.

"No," he said again. "You don't get to come onto our land and talk about breaking an imprint like it's a—a *bone* you can snap. You don't know what it is. You don't know what it means to us. It's *sacred*. It's the spirits' choice. I don't care what you are. If Leah imprints, I won't let you break it. I won't let anyone break it. I'll stop you."

Harry went still.

---

It was the strangest thing Sam had ever seen. Leeches went still like marble. Wolves froze before a lunge. Harry stopped. All of him, all at once. His tail stopped. His ears stopped. His chest stopped moving, like he'd stopped breathing. Even the rain seemed to land on him differently, as if it had gone quiet too.

And then he smiled.

It was a small smile, polite, and it went nowhere near his eyes. His eyes had gone very flat and very green and very, very calm.

"Sam," he said.

His voice had changed. It had gone soft. Pleasant, almost. Courteous. The voice of a man asking someone to pass the salt at a dinner party. It was much worse than shouting.

"I want to make sure you understand me," said Harry softly. "So I'm going to say this very slowly, and very clearly, and only once."

Sam's wolf pressed itself flat against the inside of his ribs and didn't move.

"If it ever happens," Harry said, "and I go to break it, and you stand in my way—" He paused, very slightly, as if choosing his words with care. "Then I will kill you. And then I will kill every wolf who has ever run with you. Every single one. I will find the ones in the trees right now, and the ones on the road, and the ones coming up from the beach, and the ones who haven't phased yet and don't even know they're going to. And then, when I've done that, I will find every person you have ever loved, Sam Uley. Every one. And I'll be very thorough about it. I'm always very thorough."

He tilted his head, very slightly, still smiling that small, polite smile.

"I've done it before, you see. To people a great deal more frightening than you."

The rain fell. Nobody moved. In the trees, sixteen wolves had stopped breathing.

And then the voice came.

It came from inside his own skull, behind his eyes, in the place where the pack link lived, the place where only his wolves had ever been. It came in like a blade through the back of his head, cold and huge and perfectly clear, and it was Harry's voice, and it filled everything:

*DON'T.*

*TEST.*

*ME.*

Sam staggered.

He felt it hit the others. All of them. Every single wolf on that property—the seven in the trees, Jacob's six on the road, the three on the beach path, every mind in his pack link and every mind in Jacob's—the voice went into every one of them at once, like the same nail driven into eighteen skulls at the same moment. He felt them all go down. He felt Jared yelp. He felt Collin and Brady drop flat in the mud. He felt Embry, across the gap, in Jacob's pack, stagger into a tree. Beside him, Jacob had grabbed his own head with both hands, his face gone gray.

And it was gone. As fast as it came.

The silence afterward was enormous.

Sam stood at the bottom of Sue Clearwater's porch steps with the rain running down his face and his heart slamming against his ribs, and looked at the thing called Harry, and Harry looked back at him. Calm. Polite. Waiting.

Only Leah hadn't flinched.

Sam saw it. She was standing on the step beside Harry with his paw on her shoulder, looking at Sam with no expression at all, and she hadn't moved. She hadn't heard it. Whatever Harry had done, he'd done it to every wolf on the property except her.

Then, from the trees to Sam's right, Paul broke.

---

Sam felt it happen. He felt it in the pack link a half-second before it happened, the way he always did with Paul—that hot red spike of rage that went up through Paul like a match through gasoline, all thought gone. *That thing—that THING—it threatened Rachel, it threatened RACHEL, it got in my HEAD—*

"*Paul*—" Sam started.

Paul came out of the alders.

He came out like a cannon shot. A huge dark silver-gray wolf, half a ton of muscle and teeth, launching himself out of the trees in one enormous bound with his ears flat and his jaws open, straight at Harry's back. Straight at Harry's unprotected back, with Leah standing right beside him.

Harry didn't turn around.

He didn't look. He didn't move a single muscle. He was still looking at Sam, still smiling that small polite smile.

Paul stopped in midair.

He stopped like he'd hit a wall. All half-ton of him, ten feet off the ground, legs outstretched, jaws open—stopped dead, hung there for one impossible fraction of a second.

And then something slammed him straight down into the earth.

The sound was like a car crash. The ground shook. Mud and gravel exploded up out of Sue's front yard in a great wet spray, and when it came down, Paul was lying in a crater a foot deep, pinned flat on his side, legs splayed, pressed into the mud like a butterfly on a card. He was struggling. Sam could see every muscle in his body straining, could feel his rage and his panic screaming down the pack link. He couldn't move. Not an inch. Something was holding him down that Sam couldn't see.

Harry still hadn't turned around.

He was still looking at Sam. His tail hadn't moved. His ears hadn't moved. His forepaw was still resting lightly on Leah's shoulder.

"Sam," Harry said, in exactly the same soft, pleasant voice.

He tilted his head, very slightly.

"Did I stutter?"

---

Nobody moved.

In the crater, Paul whined. It was a high, thin, frightened sound, a sound Sam had never heard come out of Paul Lahote in his whole life.

Sam made himself breathe. In. Out. His wolf was flat against the bottom of his ribs, belly to the ground, ears pinned, and every instinct he had was screaming at him to do the same thing. To get down. To roll over. To show his throat.

He didn't. He was the Alpha. He didn't get to.

"Paul," he said. His voice came out hoarse. "Stand down."

He put the Alpha into it. All of it. He felt it go out through the pack link, felt Paul feel it.

Paul went limp in the crater.

A second later, whatever was holding him let go. Sam saw it happen—saw Paul's body suddenly lift a half-inch off the mud as the pressure came off, saw him scramble to his feet, shaking, mud dripping off him in sheets, and back away from Harry's back with his tail clamped between his legs and his belly almost to the ground. He backed all the way to the treeline. He didn't take his eyes off Harry once.

Harry still hadn't looked at him.

"Thank you," Harry said to Sam, politely.

And then, all at once, the stillness went out of him. Sam saw it go. His tail moved, once, side to side. His ears came up. His chest rose and fell. His shoulders came down an inch. The flat cold green went out of his eyes like a light being switched off, and they were just eyes again, green and steady and a little bit tired.

He looked, suddenly, like what he'd been when he came out the front door. A very big wolf in a long black coat, standing in the rain with his paw on his wife's shoulder.

Somehow that was worse.

"I came because her mum's here," Harry said, in his normal voice, "and her brother. And because my daughter's got a grandmother in that house who'd never met her until about forty minutes ago." He nodded at the house behind him. "I'll be here a week. Maybe two. I'll be on this land whenever Leah wants to be on this land. I'm not going to hurt anybody who doesn't come at us. I don't care about your treaty, but I'll respect it, because Leah's mum sits on your council and it matters to her." His ears tipped forward. "And I meant every word I said about the other thing. So don't make me."

Sam looked at him for a long time.

Then he looked at Leah.

She came down the last step. She walked across the wet gravel, around the crater in her mother's yard, until she was standing right in front of him. Close enough that he could smell her. She smelled different. Under the cedar and the salt and the rain there was something else on her now, something new—pine and stone and earth, *him*, all through her. And under that, faint and sweet, something like milk.

She looked up at him.

"You don't get to say that word to me," Leah said quietly. "Not ever again. Do you understand?"

Sam opened his mouth.

"Do you *understand*, Sam?"

He thought about a kitchen, a long time ago. Emily at the counter with flour on her hands, turning around. The whole world tilting under his feet. He thought about Leah's face that night, when he'd come to tell her. He'd never forgotten it. He'd never forgotten any of it. He'd carried it around in his chest for four years like a stone, and he'd made her feel it, every day, through the pack link, the whole time she'd been in his head.

"I understand," Sam said.

Leah held his eyes a moment longer. Then she turned her back on him, and walked back across the gravel, and up the steps, and stood beside her husband.

Sam stood there in the rain.

Then he turned around, and walked back down the drive toward the road, and behind him, in the trees, sixteen wolves turned and slipped away into the dark after him without a sound.


---

Jacob didn't leave.

Leah watched him stand there in the rain at the bottom of the steps after Sam had gone, after the last of the wolves had slipped back into the trees, with his arms folded over his bare chest and his hair plastered to his head. He was looking at the crater in her mother's yard. Then at Harry. Then at the crater again.

"So," Jacob said eventually.

"So," said Leah.

"That was—" Jacob stopped. He rubbed the back of his head, where the voice had gone in. "Yeah. That was something."

"Sorry about your head," said Harry.

Jacob looked up at him. Leah watched him do it—watched him tip his head back, and back, the way she had on the ridge above Tamarack Crossing fifteen months ago. Jacob Black was six-foot-seven and used to being the tallest thing in any room. Harry met him eye to eye, and then his ears went up another six inches past that.

"You put that in *everybody's* head," Jacob said.

"Ja."

"At the same time."

"Ja."

"How?"

"Practice," said Harry.

Jacob looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked at Leah. His face did something complicated. She'd known that face since he was in diapers. She'd watched it at a hundred bonfires and a hundred birthday parties, watched it go from round and goofy to long and sharp and older than it should've been. She'd been inside his head for almost a year. She knew what it looked like when he was trying to figure out how to say something and couldn't.

"You look good, Leah," he said finally.

"Thanks, Jake."

"Like, really good. Like—" He waved a hand vaguely at her, at the coat, the hair, all of it. "Happy. You look happy."

"I am happy."

"Yeah." He nodded. "Yeah. I can tell." He looked at the ring on her hand. "Seth was screaming it all the way down the link. Married. A baby. I thought he'd lost it." He glanced at the house, at the front window. "Is she—can I—"

"Later," said Harry, before Leah could answer. Not unkindly. "She's with her grandmother."

Jacob's eyes went to him. Then he nodded again. "Yeah. Sure. Later."

There was an awkward pause. The rain dripped off the porch roof.

"For what it's worth," Jacob said, "I didn't know he was gonna say that. About imprinting. I'd have told him to shut up." His mouth twisted. "Not that he'd have listened."

"He never does," said Leah.

"No. He doesn't." Jacob shifted his weight. "Look. Seth's gonna want you to come out to the Cullens' tonight. Everybody's there most nights. My guys. Bella. The—" he stopped, and something went across his face, quick and soft, there and gone "—the kid. Seth's been talking about you for fifteen months, Leah. He wants everybody to meet you. And you—" he looked up at Harry "—the Cullens should probably know you're here. Before somebody does something stupid."

"Ja," said Harry. "I'd like to meet them. I've got a few things I want to say to them."

Jacob's eyebrows went up. "What kind of things?"

"Grown-up things," said Harry.

Jacob looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the crater again, where Paul had been.

"Okay," he said. "I'll tell them you're coming." He backed up a step. Then another. "Leah. It's—it's really good you're back."

"Visiting," Leah said. "I'm visiting."

"Yeah. I know." He gave her a crooked smile, the old one, the one from before. "Still good."

Then he turned and loped off into the trees, and a second later Leah heard the soft explosion of a phase, and the sound of four huge paws going away fast through the wet undergrowth.

---

They stood on the porch steps in the rain.

Leah didn't say anything for a while. She was looking at the crater. It was about six feet across and a foot deep, a perfect wolf-shaped hole in her mother's front yard, filling slowly with rainwater, with a few silver-gray hairs stuck in the mud around the edges.

"You put him in the ground," she said.

"He jumped at your back," said Harry. "You were standing next to me."

"He jumped at *your* back."

"You were standing next to me," Harry said again, like that settled it.

Leah turned her head and looked at him. He was looking at the trees, where Sam had gone. His ears were still half up. His tail had started moving again, slowly, against the back of his own legs.

"I didn't hear it," she said.

"No."

"I saw them all flinch. Jake grabbed his head. Sam nearly fell over. I didn't hear a thing."

"You weren't meant to." His ears flicked toward her. "I left you out. The ring would've stopped me anyway. But I left you out."

She held up her right hand and looked at it. The heavy old gold, the square green stone. *Nobody gets in your head*, he'd said, in the tower room, in August. *Not even me, Leah. Ever.*

"Would you really?" she said.

Harry didn't pretend not to know what she meant. He was quiet for a moment.

"Sam?" he said. "If he stood between me and you, and you'd been—taken, like that, and I was trying to give you back to yourself, and he was trying to stop me?" He looked down at her. "Ja. I'd go through him. I'd go through every one of them who came at me. I wouldn't feel bad about it."

"And everybody he loves?"

Harry's ears went sideways.

"Emily didn't do anything," he said. "Neither did his mum. Neither did whoever the angry one loves. I don't hurt people who didn't do anything, Leah. I never have. I never will." He looked back at the trees. "Sam doesn't need to know that. Sam needs to go to bed tonight thinking I might. Because then it'll never happen. He'll never stand in the way. And nobody gets hurt."

Leah looked at him for a long time.

"That was scary," she said. "On the steps. The way you talked to him. That voice. That was really scary, Harry."

"I know."

"Where'd you learn to talk like that?"

He was quiet for a moment. "There was a man," he said. "When I was a kid. The one who killed my parents. He talked like that. Quiet. Polite. Like he was asking you to pass the salt. I heard him do it a lot of times, to a lot of people. Some of them were people I cared about." His ears went flat. "Turns out it works. I don't like that it works. But it does."

Leah didn't say anything.

She thought about Sam's face. About the way it had gone gray, at the bottom of the steps, when the voice came. About Paul in the mud, whining like a kicked puppy. About Jacob holding his head.

She thought about Harry standing on her mother's porch steps in the rain, with his paw on her shoulder, telling Sam Uley that if she ever imprinted, he would break it. Whatever it took. Because she'd hate it. Because nobody should get to decide who she loved but her.

Nobody had ever said anything like that to her. Not once. Not her father, not her mother, not anyone on the council, not anyone in the pack. When Sam had imprinted on Emily, everybody had been sorry. Everybody had been so sorry. And everybody had told her, in a hundred different ways, gently and kindly and with tears in their eyes, that it was the spirits' choice, and she'd have to learn to live with it.

Harry had looked at the most sacred thing her people had and said, *Then I'll break it.*

"Harry," she said.

"Mm."

She reached up and grabbed two fistfuls of the ruff at his throat, the way she had on the walkway of Walt's motel in Tamarack Crossing, and pulled his head down, and kissed him, hard, in the rain, on her mother's front steps, in full view of every wolf who might still be watching from the trees.

When she let go, his ears were straight up and his tail had started going again.

"What was that for?" he said.

"Shut up," said Leah. Her eyes were hot. "Just shut up."

"Okay," said Harry.

Behind them, the front door opened. Sue stood in the doorway with Layla in her arms, wrapped in Kreacher's gray blanket, and Seth looming over her shoulder with his eyes like saucers. She looked at the two of them on the steps. Then she looked past them, at the crater in her front yard, slowly filling with rain.

"Is somebody," said Sue, "going to fix my lawn?"

Harry looked at the crater. The mud flowed back into it, smoothly, like water running backward, and the gravel settled, and the grass knitted itself back together over the top, until there was nothing there at all but Sue Clearwater's front yard in the rain, exactly as it had been, with a single silver-gray hair lying on top of it.

"Sorry, Sue," said Harry.

Sue looked at the yard. Then at him.

"Dinner's in an hour," she said. "Wipe your feet."


---

Seth Clearwater had watched the whole thing through the front window with his nose pressed against the glass.

He'd seen Sam come up the drive, and Jake. He'd seen Leah go out on the porch and that giant black wolf-guy go out after her, ducking under the door. He'd seen Sam's mouth move, and Leah's face go white, and then go hard, and her come down the steps at Sam like she was going to bite his face off. He'd seen the wolf-guy—*Harry*, Leah's *husband*, Leah had a *husband*—come down the steps without making the stairs creak, which was impossible, those stairs creaked if you *looked* at them.

He'd seen Sam say something. And Harry go still.

And then Seth had watched every single wolf on the property drop like somebody had cut their strings. Sam had staggered. Jake had grabbed his head. Out in the trees, through the rain, Seth had seen big dark shapes go flat in the undergrowth, all at once, like a wave going through them. And inside his own head—nothing. He'd been phased out, but even so, he'd felt something brush past the edge of him, like a cold wind going by an open door, and keep going. Like it had looked at him and decided to leave him alone.

And then Paul had come out of the trees, and Seth had shouted out loud, and Mom had grabbed his arm—

And Paul had just *stopped*. In the *air*. And then he'd been in the ground.

And Harry hadn't even turned around.

Now Seth sat at his mother's kitchen table with a bowl of elk stew in front of him, and couldn't stop staring.

---

The table had the leaf in it. Mom had pulled it out of the hall closet, the one they only used at Thanksgiving, and the whole kitchen was full of food. Elk stew in the big blue pot. A whole salmon baked on a cedar plank, the way Dad used to do it. Mashed potatoes. Green beans with bacon. Two plates of fry bread, one perfect and golden and one a little bit burnt, because Mom had been looking at Harry instead of the pan. Mom had cooked like she was feeding the whole pack. Seth had seen her do it before, on the bad nights, when she didn't know what else to do with her hands.

Harry was on his fourth bowl of stew.

Seth was used to being the one who ate the most at any table. It was a wolf thing. He'd been out-eating everyone at family dinners since he phased—out-eating Uncle Steve, out-eating his cousins, out-eating Dad's old fishing buddies at the funeral lunch, which Mom said was rude but had let him do anyway. He'd figured he'd out-eat Leah, too, since she was a girl, even though she'd always been able to put away more than she looked like she could.

Leah was on her third bowl. Harry was on his fourth. Seth was on his second, and slowing down, because he kept forgetting to eat.

Harry was eating one-pawed. He had the baby in the other arm. Layla. His *niece*. She was lying along Harry's forearm on her back with her eyes half open and her tiny tail hanging off his elbow, and every few bites Harry would put his spoon down and bend his head and lick the top of her head, between her ears, just once, and then go back to his stew. Like it was nothing. Like it was a normal thing to do at dinner.

Seth couldn't stop watching that either.

"You're staring, Seth," said Mom.

"Sorry." He didn't stop.

Harry glanced up at him over the baby. His ears tipped sideways. "It's fine. Ask."

"Ask what?"

"Whatever you've been wanting to ask since I came down the steps. You've been vibrating for twenty minutes, boet."

Seth looked at Leah. Leah rolled her eyes and waved her spoon at him: *go on*.

"What did you *do* to them?" Seth burst out. "Out there. Everybody just—*dropped*. Sam nearly fell over. Jake grabbed his head. The guys in the trees went down like dominoes, I saw them. And I felt something go *past* me—"

"I had a word with them," said Harry.

"In their *heads*?"

"Ja."

"All of them? At once?"

"Ja."

"How?"

"It's called Legilimency. It's a kind of magic. Getting into people's heads." Harry picked up a piece of fry bread—the burnt kind; he seemed to like the burnt kind—and tore it in half. "Usually you have to look somebody in the eye. But you lot are all connected already, aren't you? That pack thing. You're all in each other's heads anyway. So I just went in through the front door and said it once, loud, and it went everywhere."

Seth stared at him.

"But not me," he said.

"No. Not you."

"Why not?"

Harry chewed his fry bread and swallowed it.

"You're family," he said simply.

Seth opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He closed it. He felt his face go hot, which it never did anymore, because he ran at a hundred and eight degrees and his face was always hot.

"Can you do it to me now?" he said. "Like, just to see what it's like?"

"*No*," said Leah and Mom at the same time.

"I wasn't *asking you*—"

"He's not putting anything in your head, Seth," said Leah. "Eat your stew."

---

It was Mom who brought it up. Seth had known she would. He'd been watching her face all through dinner, and it had that look on it, the council look, the one she got before meetings in the tribal center when she knew Old Quil was going to say something stupid and she was going to have to argue with him.

"Harry," Mom said. She'd put down her fork.

Harry put down his spoon too. His ears came forward.

"You threatened the Alpha of our pack," Mom said. "On reservation land. In front of half the tribe's wolves. On my front porch."

"Ja," said Harry. "I did."

"You understand that Billy Black will hear about it before midnight. And Old Quil. And the rest of the council, by breakfast. You understand that some of them are going to want you off this land, and some of them are going to be very frightened of you, and some of them are going to want to talk about the treaty. A *lot*."

"Ja," said Harry. "I figured."

Mom looked at him for a long moment.

"Are you sorry?" she said.

Harry thought about it. Seth watched him do it. He didn't answer right away, the way grown-ups usually did when Mom asked them something with that look on her face, all quick and nervous. He actually thought about it.

"I'm sorry I did it on your porch," he said finally. "In your yard, at your house, with you inside. That wasn't fair to you. You've had enough happen in that yard." His ears went back a little. "But I'm not sorry I said it. I'd say it again tomorrow. He stood in your yard and asked your daughter what she'd do when the spirits came and took her away from her husband and her baby, like he was asking about the weather. After what he did to her." His voice had gone a little lower. "Somebody needed to tell him no, Sue. Properly. So he'd hear it."

Mom didn't say anything for a while.

Then she picked her fork back up.

"Sam Uley," she said, "has needed somebody to tell him no for four years." She speared a green bean. "I'll talk to Billy."

Leah made a small noise across the table. Seth looked over. She was staring at Mom with her mouth a little bit open.

"What?" said Mom.

"Nothing," said Leah. "Nothing. Pass the salmon."

---

After that it got easier. Seth didn't know exactly when it happened. Somewhere between the second plate of fry bread and Mom getting out the huckleberry pie she'd made for Charlie on Sunday and was going to have to make again now.

"For *Charlie*?" said Leah, with her fork halfway to her mouth.

Mom went a little pink.

"Charlie *Swan*?"

"He comes for dinner," said Mom. "On Sundays. Sometimes Wednesdays."

"Mom's dating Charlie," said Seth helpfully, and got kicked under the table by both of them.

Leah put her fork down. She looked at Mom. Mom looked back at her with her chin up, the same way Leah did it.

"Huh," said Leah finally. And then, after a second, something in her face went soft. "Good. Good, Mom. He's—yeah. Good."

Mom's eyes went shiny for a second. Then she cut the pie.

Seth asked how they met. He'd been dying to ask. He'd been dying to ask everything—where she'd gone, what she'd done, where Harry came from, how a person even *got* a husband who was a seven-foot wolf-wizard with a South African accent—but that was the one that came out first.

Leah told it.

She told it plainly, the way Leah told everything, without dressing it up. She told them about running north, about the silence in her head, about the town on the Cassiar Highway with the diner and the motel and the dog that wouldn't come near her. About the man in the wool coat who smelled like rot under cologne and looked at her through the kitchen window like a cut of meat. About the waitress. About following them up the ridge in the dark, and the stone circle, and the knife, and the chains coming up out of the ground and drinking the heat out of her.

Mom had gone very still. Seth had stopped eating entirely.

"And then," said Leah, "there was this crack. Like a gunshot. And there he was." She jerked her thumb at Harry. "Standing at the edge of the circle in that coat. Holding a plastic pie container from the diner."

"He brought *pie*?" said Seth.

"I ate the pie," said Harry.

"He ate the pie," Leah agreed. "Before he came down. He'd been sitting up on the ridge for an hour waiting for the guy to show up, and he got bored, and he ate it."

"In my defense," said Harry, "it was very good pie."

"And then," said Leah, "the guy threw everything he had at him. Curses. Lightning. Dead people. He threw the—what's it called, Harry, the green one—"

"Killing Curse."

"—the *Killing Curse*, at him, and Harry pulled a rock out of the *ground* to block it. And then he walked across the clearing like he was walking to his truck, and the guy kept throwing stuff, and none of it touched him. Not one thing." Leah picked up her fork again. "And then he killed him. And then he turned around and looked at me, and tilted his head like a dog, and asked me if I wanted to get a drink."

Seth stared at Harry.

"You asked her out," he said. "Right after you killed a guy."

"Timing," said Harry, and licked the top of Layla's head.

Mom made a sound. Seth looked at her. She had her hand over her mouth, and her shoulders were shaking, and for a second he thought she was crying.

She was laughing.

---

It was Harry who changed the subject. He did it so quietly that at first Seth didn't notice.

"Seth," he said. "The thing in the woods. The one that doesn't smell right."

Seth's stomach went cold. Across the table, Leah had stopped eating.

"Tell me about it," said Harry. "Everything you know."

So Seth told him.

It had started in October. Up north of Lake Crescent, out past the Sol Duc road, in the old-growth up there where hardly anybody went in the winter. Quil had smelled it first, on patrol. A trail. Something that wasn't a leech and wasn't a wolf and wasn't a person, either—or maybe it was a person, kind of, but a person who smelled wrong, like burnt wires and rotten meat and copper and something sweet over the top of it all. They'd followed it, Quil and Embry. And it had just stopped. In the middle of the forest. Like whatever had made it had gone straight up into the air.

"It's happened, like, eight times since," Seth said. "Sam's lot have smelled it too. The Cullens have smelled it. Edward says it's the worst thing he's ever smelled, and he's smelled *Paul*. Carlisle doesn't know what it is. Nobody knows what it is. We just keep finding the trail, and then the trail stops."

"Like it went up into the air," said Harry.

"Yeah."

"It did," said Harry. "That's Apparition. Popping from one place to another. That's what I do. It's what the man in Tamarack Crossing tried to do, when I'd got him boxed in." He was quiet for a moment. His ears had gone back, slowly, one notch at a time, the way Seth had watched them go back on the porch. "Somebody up there's a wizard, Seth. And they smell exactly like Vulk did. Rot and copper and burnt air. Blood magic."

Mom had gone pale. "What did he do?" she said. "That man. Vulk. What did he do with the—with the girls?"

Leah answered. Her voice was flat.

"He cut their hearts out, Mom. And used them. To stay young."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"Seth," said Harry. Still quiet. Still calm. But his tail had stopped moving against the bench leg, and Seth noticed it. "The deaths up in Port Angeles. The ones the papers said were animal attacks. Who were they?"

Seth swallowed. "Two hikers in August," he said. "A guy and a girl. They were a couple. That was leeches, nomads—Sam's lot tracked them down, they killed them. And then—" He stopped. He looked at Mom.

"Go on," said Harry.

"There was a girl in November," Seth said. "At the community college in Port Angeles. Peninsula College. She was nineteen. They found her in the woods off the Hurricane Ridge road. The paper said animal attack." He swallowed again. "Sam thought it was leeches too. But there was no trail. None. Not leech, not anything. Just—" He stopped.

"Just that smell," said Harry. "Rot and copper. And then it stopped."

Seth nodded.

Harry looked down at Layla on his arm. She was asleep now, her tiny muzzle pressed into the fur of his elbow, her tail curled around her own small body like a question mark. He looked at her for a long, long time.

"That one wasn't a leech," Harry said.

Nobody said anything.

---

It was Seth who brought up the Cullens, in the end. He didn't mean to start a fight. He just wanted them to come.

"Jake said everybody's out there tonight," he said, while Mom was clearing plates and Leah was elbow-deep in the sink, the way she'd always been, the way she'd been every night of his whole childhood. "At the Cullens'. Embry and Quil and all the new guys. They've been hearing about you for fifteen months, Leah. Every single day. Every time I phased, I was thinking about you, and they all heard it, and now they want to *meet* you. And Harry. And Layla." He looked at Harry. "And Bella will want to meet you, and Carlisle, and Nessie's gonna lose her *mind* when she sees Layla—"

"Ja," said Harry. "I want to meet them. Jake said."

"Really?"

"Really. I've got some things I want to say to them."

Seth grinned. Then he stopped grinning, because Harry's face hadn't changed.

"What kind of things?" he said.

Harry was quiet for a moment. He'd moved Layla up onto his shoulder, and his forepaw was moving slowly up and down her back.

"How long have they been here?" he said. "The Cullens. In Forks."

"This time? Like—four years? Five? Carlisle works at the hospital—"

"And when did the first of you phase? Sam?"

"Like—four years ago. About."

"And now there's nineteen of you," said Harry. "Twenty, with Leah. Nineteen kids on this coast who've turned into wolves since that family moved back to town. Because that's how it works, isn't it? That's what Leah told me. The more leeches there are around, the more of you there are. That's the whole reason you exist." He looked at Seth. "You were fifteen. Collin and Brady were fourteen. The Littlesea kid's thirteen. He went through the wall of a classroom last week."

"The Cullens are *good*," said Seth. His face had gone hot again, but differently this time. "They're *good* people, Harry. They don't hunt people. Carlisle's a *doctor*. They stood with us in January when the Volturi came, they'd have died for us, Bella's my *friend*—"

"I believe you," said Harry. "I do. I'm sure they're lovely. I'm sure Carlisle's a very good doctor." His voice didn't change. "And people keep coming to this coast because they're here. Leeches who aren't lovely. Nomads, coming to look at the half-vampire kid. And they're eating hikers in Port Angeles. And there's horse-sized wolves in the newspaper. And there's a wizard up past Lake Crescent who smells like Vulk, killing girls, in the middle of all of it, where nobody's going to notice one more body." His ears went flat. "And the police chief's daughter is a vampire, living twenty minutes from his house, with a kid who grows a year every three months, and everybody's just—hoping nobody looks too closely."

Seth didn't have anything to say to that. He looked at Mom.

Mom had stopped at the sink with a plate in her hands. She wasn't looking at anybody.

"I've thought that," she said quietly. "I've thought it for a long time. I've never said it out loud."

"*Mom*."

"I've sat on that council for two years watching other people's sons come home with their T-shirts in shreds, Seth. Watching their mothers find out." She put the plate down in the rack, very carefully. "I've thought it."

Leah dried her hands on the dish towel. She turned around and looked at Harry.

"You're gonna tell them that," she said. "To their faces."

"Ja."

"To *Bella's* face."

"Ja. Nicely." He considered. "Mostly nicely."

Leah looked at him a moment longer. Then something went across her face that Seth had never seen there before. Something like a grin, only meaner.

"I'm getting my coat," she said.

---

Mom offered to keep Layla. Of course she did. She held out her arms by the door while Harry was putting his coat on, and said, "Leave her with me. I raised two wolves, Harry. I can manage one little one for a couple of hours."

Harry looked at her. Seth saw his ears go back, just slightly, and his forepaw tighten on the sling where Layla was tucked against his chest.

"I know you can," Harry said. "I know. It's just—"

"She only sleeps on him," said Leah, pulling her boots on. "It's a whole thing. Kreacher built her a nursery. She's been in it for four seconds."

"Who's Kreacher?" said Seth.

"Long story."

Mom looked at Harry, and at the sling, and at the way his whole body had curved around it without him even noticing. Then she lowered her arms. And she smiled—a small, tired, lopsided smile that Seth hadn't seen on her face since before Dad died.

"Harry Clearwater used to do that with Leah," she said. "When she was little. Wouldn't put her down. Carried her around the house in the crook of his arm like a football, all day, every day, for six months. Took her fishing in a backpack." She reached up and touched the top of Layla's small head, very gently, between her folded ears. "Go on, then. Take her. Bring her back."

Harry's ears went pink inside.

"We will," he said.

Seth grabbed his jacket, which he didn't need, and his shoes, which he'd probably destroy, and was out the door before any of them, practically bouncing down the porch steps into the rain toward the giant black car in the driveway.

"Shotgun!" he yelled.

"Back seat," said Leah and Harry at the same time.


---

Bella Cullen had heard the voice second-hand, and it had still made her go cold.

She'd been sitting on the floor of the big white living room with Renesmee, helping her with a thousand-piece jigsaw puzzle of the Sistine Chapel that she was doing upside down to make it harder, when Jacob came in through the back door at a quarter past five. He'd been soaked, and barefoot, and his face had been a strange gray color under the brown. He hadn't said anything. He'd just stood in the doorway, dripping on Esme's floor.

And across the room, Edward had stood up so fast the chair he'd been sitting in skidded back three feet.

"Jacob," Edward had said. "What *is* that?"

Bella hadn't understood. Not until later, when Edward told her—sitting on the stairs, holding her hand, his voice very quiet so Nessie wouldn't hear. He'd seen it in Jacob's head. All of it, replayed, the way Jacob's memories always came to him: bright and jumbled and loud. A huge black wolf in a coat, standing on Sue Clearwater's porch steps. Leah—*Leah*—beside it, with a ring on her finger. Sam saying something about imprinting. The wolf going still. Smiling. Talking in a soft, polite, pleasant voice about killing every single person Sam Uley had ever loved.

And then the other voice, the one that came from inside. Edward had heard that one too, second-hand, an echo of it ringing around in Jacob's skull like a bell that hadn't stopped yet.

*DON'T. TEST. ME.*

"It went into all of them," Edward had said. "Every wolf on the property. At once. Through the pack link." He'd been holding her hand tighter than he needed to. Bella had never known Edward to hold anything tighter than he needed to. "Bella, I've been reading minds for ninety years. I didn't know that was *possible*."

Then he'd shown her the rest of it. Paul flying out of the trees. Paul stopping in midair. Paul in the ground.

"He didn't even turn around," Edward had said.

---

Now it was nearly eight, and the whole house was waiting.

Alice was the worst. She'd been in a temper since noon, pacing the length of the glass wall at the back of the house and glaring out at the dark river like it had personally offended her. "It's a *hole*," she kept saying. "Right in the middle of everything. I had the next week all laid out this morning. Charlie on Sunday, Renesmee's riding lesson on Tuesday, Carlisle's shift on Christmas Eve—and then at noon, *pop*. A hole. Right in the middle of Forks. Everything that goes near it just—disappears. It's like trying to watch television with someone standing in front of the screen."

"Wolves," said Jasper. He was standing very still by the stairs, the way he did when he was trying to taste the mood in a room.

"Wolves I can see *around*," Alice snapped. "Wolves are fuzzy. This is a *hole*. Leah's in it. The baby's in it. And the—*him*. He's a hole with feet."

"So we've got a giant wolf-wizard with a hole for a future coming to dinner," said Rosalie, from the sofa. She was filing her nails, which didn't need filing. "Wonderful. Does he eat people?"

"Jacob says he ate four bowls of elk stew," said Esme, from the kitchen, where she had been cooking for the wolves for two hours, because she always cooked when she was nervous.

"Can I arm-wrestle him?" said Emmett.

"*No*," said everyone.

Carlisle was the only one who seemed calm. He was sitting in the armchair by the fire with a medical journal open on his knee that he hadn't turned a page of in forty minutes. "We'll be polite," he said. "We'll be welcoming. Leah Clearwater is coming home, and she's bringing her husband and her child, and that is a good thing, and we will treat it as one." He looked over at Edward. "Whatever else he is."

The wolves were already there. Some of them. Embry and Quil had come in an hour ago, human, in cut-offs and nothing else, and were sitting on the kitchen counter eating Esme's lasagna straight out of the pan with forks, and four of the new ones were out on the back lawn, phased, lying in the wet grass with their noses on their paws. Bella could hear their hearts. They were all going fast.

Jacob was on the floor beside Renesmee, helping with the upside-down Sistine Chapel. He'd been quiet ever since he came in. Every so often he put his hand on the back of his head.

"Jake," said Renesmee. "You keep touching your head."

"Sorry, Nessie."

"Does it hurt?"

Jacob was quiet for a moment.

"Not anymore," he said.

Then, out in the dark at the far end of the long drive, Bella heard an engine.

---

It was the quietest engine she had ever heard. Even with her ears—and her ears could hear a moth land on a windowsill on the other side of the house—it was barely there. A low, smooth hush, like a long breath let out. Gravel crunching under tires. Headlights swinging through the trees.

Rosalie sat up on the sofa.

The headlights came around the last bend of the drive, and the car came out into the light from the house, and Rosalie stood up so fast the nail file went flying.

"That's a Rolls-Royce," she said.

Everybody looked at her.

"That's a *Rolls-Royce*," Rosalie said again. She'd gone to the glass wall and was standing with both hands pressed flat against it, staring. "That's—no. No. Rolls doesn't *make* that. They've never made anything like that. That's a *truck*. That's a Rolls-Royce *truck*. Where did he *get* that?"

"Rose," said Emmett.

"Shut up, I'm looking."

The car stopped at the edge of the lawn. It was enormous, and black, and so shiny the lights from the house slid across it like water. The back door on the near side opened—backward, hinged at the wrong end—and Seth Clearwater fell out of it, grinning, already shouting something at the wolves on the lawn. The driver's door opened, and Leah got out.

Bella's breath stopped.

She'd known Leah Clearwater a long time. Since they were both little girls, really, in the summers when Charlie used to bring her down to La Push to go fishing with Billy and Harry Clearwater. She'd known her as the scary older girl on the beach, and then as Sam's girlfriend, and then as the angry gray wolf who hated her, who had stood guard outside this very house while Bella was dying in it and thought things about her that Edward refused to repeat.

The woman who got out of that car didn't look angry. She looked like somebody who'd been asleep for a long time and woken up. She stood on the wet gravel in a long dark coat with her long hair blowing in the wind, and looked up at the house, and her face was calm.

And then the far door opened, and the wolf got out.

---

He unfolded out of the back seat of the car like something being unpacked. Up and up. He was in a long black coat that fell to his shins, open over something gray, and he was covered in black fur, and he had a wolf's head. Long muzzle. Tall pointed ears. A tail. He stood beside the car in the rain and looked up at the house, and his ears swiveled, one and then the other, and Bella watched his nostrils flare.

He was holding something against his chest, inside his coat. A small bundle, in a sling.

Bella breathed in.

She couldn't help it. She'd been a vampire for fifteen months, and she'd learned control faster than anybody Carlisle had ever known, and she still breathed in when something new came into range, the way you'd turn your head at a sound. The smells came to her across the wet lawn, through the glass, through the cracks around the door. Rain. Wet gravel. Wet dog, from the wolves on the lawn, the smell she'd learned to tune out. Leah—she knew Leah's smell, cedar and salt and wolf. Seth.

And him.

Earth. Like a garden after rain. Pine. And something cool and clean and mineral, like a stone at the bottom of a creek. Wolf, under it all. A wild kind of wolf. And something else, something she didn't have a word for, something that crackled at the edges like the air before a thunderstorm.

Her throat didn't burn. Not even a flicker. Whatever he was, every instinct she had said: *not food*.

And under his smell, from the bundle against his chest, the smallest smell of all. Milk. Wolf. And that same crackle, faint and sweet.

Beside her, Jasper had gone very still.

"Jasper?" said Alice.

"He's calm," Jasper said slowly. His eyes were fixed on the big black wolf on the lawn. "He's completely calm. He's—happy, almost. Tired. A little bit worried about the baby." He was quiet for a second. "And under that—"

He stopped.

"Under that, what?" said Alice.

Jasper took one step back from the glass. Bella had seen Jasper face down an army of newborns without taking a step back. She'd seen him walk toward the Volturi in the snow.

"Something very large," Jasper said quietly. "And very cold. And very, very still. Like a lake that's frozen all the way to the bottom." He didn't look away from the window. "He's keeping it there on purpose."

---

Carlisle went out to meet them. Of course he did. He went out onto the front porch in his shirtsleeves, into the rain, with his hands open at his sides, and Bella and Edward went with him, and the rest of the family came and stood in the doorway behind.

Seth got there first. He came bounding up the lawn with his face split in half by a grin, and the four wolves who'd been lying in the grass were on their feet now, crowding around him, tails going. Seth was already talking, a hundred miles an hour. "—and she's got a *tail*, Embry, a tiny little *tail*, and her eyes are *green*—"

Embry and Quil had come out of the kitchen behind Bella and gone straight past her down the porch steps. Embry got to Leah first. He didn't say anything. He just grabbed her and hugged her, lifted her right off her feet the way Seth had probably done, and Leah let him, and over his shoulder Bella saw her close her eyes. Then Quil. Then Leah was standing on the lawn with a wolf-kid on either side of her and her hand on Embry's arm, and her face had gone soft and strange.

The big black wolf stood back a little and let them have it. His tail was moving, very slightly. His forepaw was spread over the bundle in the sling.

Then he looked up at the porch. At Carlisle. At Bella. And at Edward.

Bella felt Edward go rigid beside her.

She knew what he was doing. She'd known him long enough. He was listening—reaching, the way he always did with someone new, sliding into their head as easily as walking through an open door. Most people's heads were open doors to Edward. Only hers had ever been shut.

His hand tightened on hers.

"Bella," Edward breathed, so low only she could hear it. "I can't hear him."

She looked at him.

"Nothing," Edward said. "There's nothing there. It's like a—like a wall. Smooth. No cracks." His voice had gone very strange. "It's like yours. But yours is soft. His is—"

On the lawn, the big black wolf tilted his head. His ears swung around and pointed straight at Edward. And then he spoke, quite pleasantly, in a low rough voice with an accent from somewhere a long way south of anywhere Bella had ever been.

"Stay out of my head, boet," he said. "I'll tell you anything you want to know. Just ask."

Edward went absolutely still.

"I wasn't—" he started.

"You were," said the wolf. "It's fine. I'd have done the same, in your shoes." He looked up at the house, at the whole family crowded in the door, and his ears tipped forward. "Hi. I'm Harry. Harry Potter. I'm married to Leah. Sorry about the short notice."

---

Carlisle recovered first. Carlisle always recovered first. He came down the porch steps with his hand out and a smile on his face, the steady doctor's smile that he gave to frightened patients and frightened wolves and frightened newborn vampires alike.

"Carlisle Cullen," he said. "Welcome. Truly. Leah, welcome home." He turned to Harry, still holding out his hand. "And welcome to you, Harry. It's good to—"

He stopped. He'd looked down at what Harry was holding out to shake.

Bella saw it too. A paw, a huge one, black-furred, with dark leathery pads on the palm and five long clawed digits. Harry held it out exactly the way a man would, and waited.

Carlisle looked at it for half a second. Then he took it and shook it firmly, the way he'd have shaken any hand.

"—good to meet you," Carlisle finished.

"Ja, you too," said Harry. "Leah's told me about you. She says you're the only one of the lot of you she ever trusted not to bite anybody."

Carlisle's eyebrows went up. Behind Bella, Emmett choked on a laugh.

"That's very generous of her," said Carlisle.

"I didn't say it was generous," said Leah.

---

Renesmee came out before anyone could stop her.

Bella felt her go. She'd been standing just inside the doorway between Esme and Jacob, and Bella had been watching her the whole time, the way she always watched her, with a part of her mind that never switched off. And then Nessie was gone, slipping out between Jacob's knees and past Rosalie's skirt and down the porch steps into the rain, in her socks, her bronze curls bouncing.

"*Nessie*—" said Edward and Jacob together.

Renesmee didn't stop. She walked straight across the wet lawn, right past the wolves and Seth and Leah, straight up to the big black wolf in the long coat. She stopped about three feet away from him and tipped her head all the way back, and back, until her curls hung down behind her, and looked up at him.

Harry looked down at her. His ears had come straight up.

Bella's whole body had gone tight. She was halfway down the porch steps before she knew she'd moved. Edward was right beside her. Jacob was right behind them both, and Bella could hear his heart going like a hammer.

"Are you a wolf," Renesmee asked, "or a person?"

Harry looked at her for a long moment. Then, very slowly, he knelt down on the wet grass. Even kneeling, he was taller than she was. But not by much.

"Both," he said.

Renesmee considered this.

"I'm both too," she said. "Kind of. Can I show you something?"

Harry's ears tipped sideways. He looked up at Bella, on the steps. A question.

Bella made herself speak. "She shows people things," she said. Her voice came out higher than she meant. "She touches your face, and you see what she's thinking. Pictures. Memories. It doesn't hurt."

Harry looked back at Renesmee.

"Okay," he said. "Show me."

Renesmee reached up with one small pale hand and laid it flat against the side of his muzzle.

Bella watched his eyes go unfocused. Watched them flicker, the way everybody's did when Nessie showed them something. She didn't know what Nessie was showing him. She could guess. The clearing in the snow, probably. The Volturi in their black cloaks. Jacob, huge and russet, standing in front of her. Her family. The house. Herself.

Harry's ears went flat, slowly. Then came back up.

And then something happened that Bella had never seen happen. Not once, in fifteen months, with anyone.

Renesmee's eyes went unfocused too.

Her mouth fell open. She stood there on the wet lawn with her hand on Harry's face and her eyes gone wide and blank and far away, and her face filled up, slowly, with the purest, most total astonishment Bella had ever seen on it.

"*Oh*," Renesmee breathed.

Edward made a sound beside Bella. Jacob took a step forward.

Renesmee took her hand away from Harry's face. She turned around. She looked at Bella with her eyes as big as dinner plates.

"Mama," she said. "Mama, he showed me *back*. He showed me a *castle*. On a lake. With all the windows lit up. And a—Mama, there was a *dragon*. A *real* one. Flying over it. Breathing *fire*." She turned back to Harry. "Was that real?"

"Ja," said Harry. "That one was real."

"Can I see it?"

"One day. If your mum and dad say so."

Renesmee turned back around and looked at Bella again, with her whole face shining, and Bella stood on the steps in the rain and didn't know what to do with any part of herself.

Nobody had ever shown Nessie anything back. Nobody *could*. She'd been reaching into people's heads her whole life—her whole short, strange, too-fast life—and showing them things, and they'd always just *received* it. Like a radio. Like a television screen. And this—this enormous stranger with the muzzle and the accent and the coat had knelt down in the wet grass and let her in, and then reached back and shown her a castle.

Then Renesmee looked at the sling on his chest.

"Is that a *baby*?" she said.

Harry's forepaw had come up, without seeming to think about it, and spread itself over the small bundle. Bella saw it. She knew that gesture. She'd been making it herself, over a different small bundle, fifteen months ago.

Then Harry looked over at Leah. Leah looked back at him and gave a small, short nod.

Harry folded back the top of the sling, very carefully.

A small round furry head came out. Charcoal-black, silver at every tip, with two soft folded ears sticking out through holes in a tiny gray knitted hat, and a pale gray stripe running down the middle of a small blunt muzzle. The baby blinked up at the lights of the house with sleepy green eyes ringed in gold, and yawned, and sneezed.

Renesmee gasped.

"She has *fur*," she said. "She has *ears*. Jake, she has *wolf ears*. Is she a wolf?"

"Both," said Harry. "Like you. Like me. Her name's Layla."

"Does she have a *tail*?"

"Ja. A small one."

Renesmee clasped both her hands under her chin and looked at the baby, and the baby looked at Renesmee, and her small black nose worked, smelling her. Then one tiny paw came out of the sling, five curled digits with five tiny claws, and reached toward Renesmee, and opened and closed on the air.

"Oh," said Renesmee, very softly. "*Oh*."

Behind Bella, on the porch, she heard Rosalie make a sound she'd never heard Rosalie make before. Something like a sob, very quiet, swallowed fast.

Bella looked down at her daughter, standing in her wet socks on the lawn with her hands clasped under her chin, gazing at the tiny furred face in the stranger's sling. Then at the stranger, kneeling there on the grass, with his ears soft and his tail moving slowly behind him through the wet grass, watching the two little girls look at each other.

Then he looked up. Past Renesmee. Past the wolves and Seth and Leah. Up at her, standing on the steps.

And for just a second, Bella saw it. What Jasper had felt. Behind the soft ears and the moving tail, behind the patient green eyes. Something huge and cold and very, very still, looking at her. Weighing her. Taking her measure.

"Bella," Harry said pleasantly. "When you've got a minute, I'd like a word. You and your husband. And your father-in-law." His ears tipped toward the house. "Nothing bad. Grown-up stuff."

Bella's mouth had gone dry. Which was impossible. She didn't have a dry mouth anymore. She didn't have a wet one.

"Okay," she heard herself say.

"Lekker," said Harry, and got to his feet, and Renesmee took his free forepaw without asking, and towed him up the lawn toward the house, already telling him about her puzzle.

---

The pack caught them at the bottom of the porch steps.

Seth had been waiting for this for fifteen months, and it showed. He came up the lawn at a run with the others behind him—Embry and Quil, still human, still in their cut-offs, and four more Bella knew by sight and only half by name, the new ones, the ones who'd phased since spring. They'd shifted back on the lawn and pulled on whatever they'd had tied to their ankles. They were so young. That was what struck Bella every time. Long gangly arms and legs, big feet, shoulders that hadn't caught up with the rest of them, faces that still had a little roundness in them under all that sudden height.

They stopped a careful six feet away from Harry. All of them. In a line, like they'd agreed on it.

"Okay," said Seth, breathless. "Okay. Harry, this is everybody. Everybody, this is Harry. And Layla. This is Embry, and Quil, you know them, you saw them hug Leah. And this is Kyle Penn, and Owen Ward, and Marcus Eastman, and that's Danny Hobucket, he's the youngest, he phased in September."

Danny Hobucket was thirteen. He was standing at the end of the line with his arms wrapped around himself in the rain and his eyes fixed on Harry's ears.

Nobody moved.

Harry looked at them. Bella watched his face. She saw him see it—the way they held themselves, all of them leaning very slightly back, the way Danny kept touching the back of his own head without knowing he was doing it.

Then Harry got down on one knee on the wet grass. Slowly. Renesmee still had hold of one of his forepaws, and she knelt down too, solemnly, in her socks, because he had.

"That wasn't for you lot," Harry said. "Earlier. The thing in your heads. That was for Sam. You got caught in it because you're all wired together, and I didn't have time to be careful." His ears went back a little. "I'm sorry it hit you. It must've hurt."

The kids looked at each other.

"It kind of did," said Owen Ward.

"It was like a brain freeze," said Kyle Penn. "But in your whole skull."

"Ja," said Harry. "I'm sorry. I won't do it to you again. You've got my word."

Danny Hobucket was still staring at his ears. "Are you gonna kill Sam?" he said.

There was a short silence. Behind Bella on the porch, Emmett made a small strangled noise.

"Not today," said Harry.

Danny thought about that. Then, in a smaller voice: "Can you do the thing to Paul again?"

Quil burst out laughing. So did Kyle. Embry put his hand over his face.

"If he jumps at my wife again," said Harry, "ja."

"*Cool*," breathed Danny Hobucket, and the line broke, and suddenly they were all crowding in, all talking at once, all of them wanting to see the baby, *is that her, is she really a wolf, can she phase, does she bite, Seth said she has a tail.* Harry folded back the top of the sling, and they leaned in so close their wet hair dripped on Renesmee, and Layla opened her green eyes and looked up at seven huge damp teenagers and Renesmee Cullen, all staring at her, and sneezed.

Seven teenagers went *awww* at exactly the same time.

Embry had drifted back to Leah. He stood beside her with his hands in the pockets of his cut-offs, watching.

"Leah," he said. "You really married him?"

"Yeah," said Leah.

Embry watched Harry let Danny Hobucket touch one of Layla's tiny folded ears with one very careful finger.

"Cool," said Embry.

Seth looked over his shoulder at the two of them, and Bella saw his face, and she didn't think she'd ever seen anybody look so happy in her life.


---

They did it at the dining table. The long glass one under the chandelier that nobody ever ate at, because nobody in the family ate, and that they used for meetings instead—the family meetings, the war councils, the nights when Alice saw something and everybody sat down and listened. Bella had sat at it a dozen times. She'd never sat at it across from someone who made the chairs look like doll furniture.

Harry didn't sit in a chair. He looked at the chairs, and then at himself, and then sat down on the floor at the end of the table instead, cross-legged, which put his head about level with everyone else's. Layla was asleep in the sling on his chest again. Esme had brought him a plate of lasagna the size of a paving stone, and he'd said, "Thank you, that's lekker," in a voice of such sincere gratitude that Esme had gone and got him a second plate before he'd finished the first.

Leah sat in the chair beside him with her arms folded. Carlisle sat at the head of the table. Bella and Edward sat side by side across from Harry. Jacob had taken the chair next to Bella without being asked, and nobody had told him to move.

The rest of the family was in the living room, thirty feet away, pretending not to listen. Bella could hear them all pretending.

"Right," said Harry, and put down his fork. "I'm going to say some things. Some of them you won't like. I'm going to say them anyway, because somebody should've said them to you a long time ago, and I don't think anybody has." His ears tipped forward. "Is that okay?"

"Please," said Carlisle.

"How long have you been in Forks? This time."

"Nearly five years."

"And in five years," said Harry, "how many kids on that reservation have turned into wolves?"

Carlisle didn't answer. He didn't need to. Bella saw something go across his face, small and quick.

"Nineteen," said Harry. "Twenty, counting Leah. Collin and Brady were fourteen. Seth was fifteen. The Littlesea kid is thirteen. He went through the wall of a classroom last week in the middle of social studies, and his teacher had to go home, and somewhere his mum's trying to work out how to explain to the school why her son's suddenly six foot two and never needs a jacket." His voice didn't rise. It didn't change at all. "That happens because of you. Leah told me how it works. Every one of you that's here makes more of them. You didn't mean it. I know you didn't. It's still true."

Bella opened her mouth.

"I'm not done," said Harry. Not unkindly.

She closed it.

"Then there's the ones who come to look." He picked up his fork again and pointed it, vaguely, at the dark window. "Nomads. Because word got round about January, and about her—" a nod toward the living room, where Renesmee was sitting on Rosalie's lap with her nose pressed against the glass, watching the wolves on the lawn "—and now every leech on the continent with a free weekend wants to come see the half-vampire kid. Some of them are polite about it. Some of them ate two hikers outside Port Angeles in August."

"Sam's pack dealt with those," said Edward. His voice was very tight.

"After," said Harry. "After they'd eaten them." He took a bite of lasagna and chewed it. "And then there's the newspapers. Horse-sized wolves at Lake Crescent. Hikers taking pictures. Tribal officials declining to comment." He reached into his coat with his free forepaw and took out a folded piece of parchment and laid it on the glass table and pushed it across to Carlisle. "That came to Leah by owl in October. Every wizard on the ICW tracker roster got one. That's a few thousand people. One of them's going to read it one day and decide this coast is a problem."

Carlisle unfolded the parchment and read it. His face didn't change, but he read it twice.

"What's the ICW?" said Jacob.

"International Confederation of Wizards," said Harry. "Sort of a United Nations. With wands. I work for them."

"You're a *cop*?"

"I'm on paternity leave," said Harry.

---

Bella found her voice.

"You don't know," she said. "You don't know what it's like. You come in here and you say all this, and you don't know anything about us—"

"Then tell me," said Harry.

"My *dad* is here," Bella said. "Charlie. He's here. He's lived here his whole life. He's the police chief. He's—he's alone, he's been alone for eighteen years, and he's only just started being happy again, with Sue. He *chose* this. He chose to not ask questions so he could keep me. So he could keep *her*." Her voice cracked. "I can't leave him. I can't make him lose me twice."

"Okay," said Harry. "What about the rest of Forks?"

Bella stopped.

"His deputies," said Harry. "The woman at the post office. The kids you went to school with, who'll be home for Christmas next week and see you in the Thriftway looking exactly the same as you did at eighteen. Your daughter's teachers, if she ever has any. The people at the hospital who've been working with Carlisle for five years and have started to wonder why he doesn't get wrinkles." He tilted his head. "Your dad chose not to ask. Good for him. Everybody else in that town didn't get a vote. They just get to live next door to it, and hope nobody notices."

"We were going to move," said Carlisle quietly.

Everybody looked at him.

"In a few years," Carlisle said. "We always do. Every time. We stay as long as we can, and then people start to look at us a certain way, and we go." He put the parchment down on the glass. "We were waiting. For Charlie. For Jacob. For Renesmee to—" He stopped. "For things to settle."

"They're getting worse," said Harry. Every month you stay, there's more of them out on that lawn, and they're getting younger."

"*Jacob* can't leave," said Bella. "You don't understand. He—with Nessie—he *can't*. And he can't leave the tribe either. He's an Alpha. He's—"

"If they go, I go," said Jacob.

Everybody looked at him now.

He was sitting very still beside Bella, with his big hands flat on the glass table. His face had that look on it that Bella had learned to recognize, years ago, before any of this: the look he got when he'd already decided something and was just waiting for everybody else to catch up.

"I've thought about it," Jacob said. "A lot. Since January. I'm not stupid. I see the new kids. I see them come in the first time, all shaking, with their clothes in rags and their moms crying in the parking lot." He looked across the table at Harry. "If they go, I go. Embry and Quil'll go wherever I go, probably. The rest can stay with Sam. Seth—" He glanced at Leah. "Seth's gonna want to come. But that's his call. And his mom's."

Harry looked back at him for a long moment.

"Ja," he said. "I figured you'd say that. That's your business. Yours and hers. And her parents'." His ears tipped forward. "Who goes where is up to you lot, Jake. I'm here to tell them what happens if they don't."

"What's going to happen?" said Edward.

Harry turned his head and looked at him.

"Eventually," he said, "somebody in Geneva reads that piece of paper. Or the next one. Or the one after that. And they decide a coast with twenty shapeshifters and a coven of vampires and a half-human kid and leeches eating hikers and wolves in the newspaper is a breach of secrecy that needs looking at. So they send somebody to look." He picked up his fork again. "And the somebody they send won't be on paternity leave. And won't have a mother-in-law in La Push. And won't care about anybody's treaty."

Nobody said anything.

"Are you threatening us?" said Bella.

Harry looked at her. She made herself hold his eyes. They were very green. Behind them, very far down, she felt it again—that enormous still cold thing, looking out at her.

"No," Harry said. "If I were threatening you, you'd know. Ask Sam." He took a bite of lasagna. "I'm telling you because nobody else has. And because my wife grew up on that reservation, and her brother's one of those kids, and her mum's had to sit through two years of council meetings watching other people's sons come home in shreds." He chewed and swallowed. "Take till summer. Talk to your dad. Talk to Jake. Pick somewhere with a lot of rain and not many people. Alaska's nice. I hear Denali's lovely in June."

---

Leah hadn't said a word the whole time. Bella had been aware of her the whole time anyway—sitting there in the chair beside her husband with her arms folded and her face like a closed door, watching.

Now she unfolded her arms.

"Bella," she said.

Bella looked at her.

"I hated you," Leah said. "For a long time. You know that. I thought things about you, when I was guarding this house, that I'm not proud of. I thought you were a stupid selfish girl who'd made everybody's life worse because you wanted a vampire boyfriend." She said it flatly, without heat. "I don't hate you anymore. I don't think about you enough to hate you. I've got a kid now. I've got a life. You're just somebody I used to know."

Bella didn't say anything. She couldn't think of anything to say.

"But Collin and Brady were fourteen," said Leah. "And Dylan Littlesea is thirteen. And my brother was fifteen. And I just want them to get to be kids. That's all. That's all I want out of this." She looked at Renesmee, through the doorway, on Rosalie's lap with her nose against the glass. Then down at the small sleeping bundle on Harry's chest. "You've got one of your own now. You know what I mean."

Bella looked at her daughter for a long time.

She did know what Leah meant. She'd known for months. She'd lain awake at three in the morning, because she couldn't sleep and never would again, beside Edward in the little stone cottage in the woods, and thought it. *We're the reason.* She'd watched Seth grow six inches in a summer. She'd watched Collin's mother sobbing in the parking lot of the tribal center. She'd thought it, and put it away, because Charlie was here and Jacob was here and Nessie was so happy here.

"Okay," she heard herself say.

Edward turned his head and looked at her.

"Okay," Bella said again. "We'll—talk. We'll talk about it. All of us." She looked at Carlisle. Carlisle nodded, very slowly. "By summer."

"By summer," said Carlisle.

Harry looked at her a moment longer. Then his ears came up, and his tail moved, once, against the floor, and the cold thing behind his eyes went away again, all the way down, wherever he kept it.

"Lekker," he said, and finished the second plate of lasagna.

---

It was Carlisle who brought up the other thing, at the very end, when they were all standing in the front hall and Seth was out on the lawn saying goodbye to the wolves for the third time.

"There's something else in the woods," Carlisle said quietly. "North of the lake. Since October. We've smelled it. We don't know what it is."

"I know," said Harry.

Carlisle's eyebrows went up.

"It's a wizard," Harry said. "A blood mage. Same kind as the one I met Leah over, in Canada. He takes girls. Young women. He cuts their hearts out and uses them to buy himself more time." His ears had gone flat. "The girl they found off the Hurricane Ridge road in November. The one the paper called an animal attack. That was him."

Carlisle had gone very still. Across the hall, Bella heard Rosalie stop breathing.

"I'll find him," said Harry. "He's mine. That's what I do."

"You're on *paternity leave*," said Leah.

"Paternity leave covers it," said Harry.

He turned to go. He had one forepaw on the door handle, and the other spread over Layla in the sling, and Leah was already halfway out onto the porch. Then he stopped. He looked back over his shoulder. Not at Carlisle. At Bella.

"Bella," he said.

She looked at him.

"Keep Renesmee in after dark," said Harry quietly. "Till I've found him. Don't let her out on her own. Not even with the wolves." His green eyes held hers. "He likes girls."

Then he went out into the rain, and Bella stood in the front hall of the big white house and watched him walk down the lawn toward the enormous black car, with his tail perfectly still behind him and his ears turned north, toward the dark woods beyond the lake, and found that for the first time since the snow in January, she was afraid.
