# Snare

## Chapter Two: Rather Clear

---

### I. By Teatime

*The Cullen house — 21 November, 5:10 p.m.*

At ten past five that evening, while the man who called himself Bonham sat in a leather armchair in a tent above the north fork of the Calawah with a shaving mirror face-down on his knee, Edward Cullen heard four minds come up the drive, and every one of them was full of him.

It hit like weather. Edward had been at the piano, not playing, his fingers resting on the keys while Renesmee slept upstairs and Bella read beside her, and he'd caught Jacob's mind at three miles out, and it had been so loud and so tangled that he'd taken his hands off the keyboard and stood up before he knew he'd decided to. Then Leah's, behind it, white-hot and shaking. Then Seth's, which was the worst of the three, because Seth had a way of reliving things that ran them over and over like a film reel caught on a loop. And then the fourth mind, Layla's, which he could hear only as a hum behind a shut door, and the door, tonight, was bowed outward on its hinges as though something on the other side were leaning against it.

He walked to the glass wall and watched the headlights come through the trees.

By the time Seth's truck stopped on the gravel, he had all of it.

He had the leech on the line of light, hanging and turning. He had the crack in the air and the man standing on the moss. The suit. The coat. The shoes with no mud on them. The green eyes. *Afternoon. This situation is contained.* He had the badge, which said FBI to Leah and Homeland Security to Seth. He had the voice, which was English and a little rough, and the words, and the eyebrow that had stopped Leah Clearwater dead in the moss. He had the Stunner turned aside on the back of one gloved finger. He had the two wolves stopping in midair. He had a curse wrapped inside a curse, peeled apart like an orange and handed back. A gun. A sound like a bomb under a parking lot. Layla going backward through the rain.

And he had the last part, which Jacob had been holding at the front of his mind for eleven miles like a man carrying a bowl of water he was afraid to spill.

*Tell them I've no quarrel with them. Tell the doctor I'd like a word. And tell the mind-reader that if he fancies a look round inside my head, he's welcome to try. Once.*

"Edward," said Carlisle quietly, from the foot of the stairs. He'd heard the truck. He'd seen Edward's face.

"Get everyone," Edward said. "All of them. Please."

---

They sat in the great room with the fire lit, because Layla was shivering.

She'd stopped shaking by the time she'd crossed the threshold. Edward had watched her do it, on the porch, in the second before Seth opened the door: one long breath in through the nose and out through the mouth, a soldier's breath, and her shoulders came down and her hands went still. But the cold had got into her anyway, and her jeans were soaked to the thigh and filthy down one side, and there were leaves in her hair. Esme had taken one look at her and gone for blankets without a word.

Seth wouldn't leave her side. He sat on the arm of her chair with one enormous hand on her shoulder as if she might blow away. Leah stood by the hearth with her back to the fire, arms wrapped round herself, still in the cut-offs and black tank top she'd phased into, and wouldn't sit, and wouldn't take a blanket, and wouldn't look at anyone. Jacob sat on the floor with his back against Bella's knees and Renesmee asleep in his arms; he'd gone straight up the stairs when he came in, and come back down with her, and not said a word about why.

Edward stood by the glass. He told it.

He told it plainly, in order, the way the wolves had lived it, and he gave them the man's words exactly as he'd heard them, because he could, because they were there in four heads at once and slightly different in each, and the differences were interesting. In Seth's memory the man's voice had been friendlier. In Leah's it had been colder. In Jacob's it had been tired.

When he'd finished, nobody said anything for a while.

"He picked it up," said Emmett at last. "Like a surfboard."

"Yes."

"One-handed."

"Yes."

"Edward, I weigh what a Buick weighs."

"I know."

Emmett sat back on the sofa. For once in his long life he didn't seem to have anything to add.

"A crater," Rosalie said. She was standing, as she always stood when there were wolves in the room, between the wolves and the stairs, though tonight the wolves had brought the baby down with them and she seemed unsure where to put herself. "With a pistol."

"A forty-five," said Layla. Her voice was rough. "Colt. Old one. My granddaddy had one just like it in his sock drawer." She pulled the blanket tighter. "One round. Into the dirt. Three feet in front of my shield. I had a Shield Charm up that's stopped an Erumpent. It went under it."

"What's an Erumpent?" said Emmett.

"Big," said Layla. "Like a rhino. It explodes."

"*Cool*."

"Emmett," said Esme.

"Sorry."

"What does he want?" said Jasper.

He was standing at the foot of the stairs where he'd stood on the first night Layla came, very still, one hand loose at his side. Edward had been listening to Jasper's mind since the wolves came in, and it was the calmest mind in the house, and the most frightening; it was laying the clearing out like a map on a table, every piece where it had been, measuring distances and angles and times. *He let them see him. He gave them his voice and his face. He didn't care that they had it. A man who doesn't care if you know his face either has nothing to fear from you or doesn't plan to be here long.*

"He said nothing," said Layla. "Today."

"And tomorrow he rings," said Jacob, from the floor. "And we pick up."

"That's leverage," said Jasper. "I'm asking what he *wants*. Why is he here?"

Nobody answered.

"He wanted the vampire," said Carlisle slowly. "That much is plain. He wanted it alive. He's built a trap for it, an enormous one, across the whole of this end of the peninsula, and he built it to let us through. To let the wolves through. To catch only the ones who drink from people." He looked into the fire. "That's a great deal of effort to catch a single nomad. You don't build a net like that for one fish."

"Research," said Leah. It was the first thing she'd said since she came in. She said it to the floor. "That's what he said. *Research*. And I asked him what happens to it and he asked me did I really want to know."

"And you said no," said Edward.

Leah looked up at him with her jaw tight. *Get out of my head, leech.*

"Sorry," said Edward, and meant it.

"He wants to study us," said Rosalie. "That's what you're all dancing round. He wants to take us apart and see how we work. He said we're *no use to him in a jar*. That's a thing you say when you've thought about the jar."

"He said we're not what he's here for," said Carlisle.

"He said a lot of things, Carlisle."

"He did. And he could have taken any of the wolves today, and he didn't. He could have killed Layla, and he didn't." Carlisle turned from the fire. "I'm trying to understand him, Rose. The more I understand him, the better I can keep us all alive."

Rosalie looked at him, and at the child asleep in Jacob's arms, and her mouth went thin.

Up on the stairs, sitting on the third step with her knees drawn up, Alice had said nothing at all. Edward had been half-listening to her the whole time. She'd been doing what she always did when something frightened her: looking. Over and over, down every path she could find. Into tomorrow. Into next week. Into the moment, any moment, when the man in the black coat would come back.

There was nothing there.

"Alice," Edward said.

She looked up.

"I can't see him," she said. Her voice was very small for Alice. "I can't see him at *all*. I've been trying since the wolves came up the drive. Layla's all smeared. The wolves are blank. With him, I look at this afternoon, at the clearing, at where he was, and there's a hole. A hole the shape of a man. Everything around it is fine. Seth, Leah, Layla, the trees, the rain. And in the middle of it, where he's standing, nothing. Like somebody cut him out with scissors."

"Can you see us, after?" said Jasper. "Tomorrow? Next week?"

"Yes. Mostly. Bits." She pressed her fingertips to her temples. "But anything he's in, I can't. Anywhere he's going to be, I can't. If he's standing in this room a week from now, I won't see it until he's already here."

Jasper's mind went very cold and very quiet.

"Then we don't assume we'll have warning," he said.

"No," said Alice. "We don't."

---

"There's one more thing," Edward said.

He'd been holding it back. He looked at Bella. She was sitting on the sofa with her hand in Jacob's hair, very still, and her eyes, still crimson, going slowly toward amber at the rims, came up to his.

"When Jacob arrived in the clearing," he said, "the first thing in his head was Renesmee. He couldn't help it. And the man saw her."

Bella went entirely still. It was a vampire's stillness; nothing human could do it.

"He *saw* her," she said.

"Jacob was thinking about her face. Her hair. How she grows. How she shows you things with her hands. All of it."

Jacob's arms tightened round the sleeping child. "I couldn't stop it, Bells. I tried. I swear I—"

"I know, Jake." Bella didn't take her eyes off Edward. "What did he do?"

"He said she had nothing to fear from him."

"And you believe him?"

Edward thought about it. He'd been thinking about it for twenty minutes, turning Jacob's memory of that moment over and over in his own mind like a stone. It had been the one moment in the whole afternoon when the man's voice had gone flat. *Mate. Calm down. I don't do children. Not ever.* It had come out too fast. There had been something underneath it that Jacob, in the rain, with his whole body screaming at him to kill, had been in no state to hear.

Edward had heard it. Secondhand. Through a wolf's ears. He'd heard a lot of people say a lot of things in a hundred years, and he thought he knew, now, what it was.

"I think he meant it," he said. "I think it's the only thing he said all afternoon that he didn't think about first."

Bella looked at him for a long moment.

"That's not enough," she said quietly.

"No."

"If he comes near her—"

"I know, love."

Bella's hand closed in Jacob's hair. Jacob didn't flinch.

Edward felt it then, the way he'd learned to feel it in the last ten weeks. A sort of tightening in the air of the room, a skin forming over the surface of everything, the way the air goes before a storm. Bella's shield. She'd been learning it. She'd been stretching it, a little further every day, practicing with Edward in the meadow. She didn't know she was doing it now. It came out of her like a held breath and spread across the room until it covered the sofa and Jacob and the child in his arms, and Edward, standing at the window, felt the minds under it go silent. Jacob. Renesmee. Bella herself, as always.

He didn't say anything. He'd tell her later.

"*Bonham*," said Layla abruptly.

They all looked at her. She'd been staring into the fire.

"It's a name," she said. "It's the name he gave. I thought about it the whole drive here. It's a stupid name. It's the kind of name you'd make up on the spot. Like *Smith*." She shook her head. "It means nothing. Like everything else about him. I've been through it and through it. The accent. The badge. The suit. The spells. He never left a single thing behind. Whoever he works for—"

"There's a man," said Bella slowly, "at the diner."

The room turned.

Bella was still looking at Edward. Her voice had changed. Edward heard her think it, half a beat ahead of her words, as she stopped being frightened and started being his wife, the girl who'd worked out what he was with a library book and a car accident.

"Dad called on Sunday," she said. "He always calls Sundays. He said the town's gone crazy for pie this week. He said there's a British guy who comes into the diner every morning. Fly-fishing. For steelhead. He sits in the back booth and eats pie for breakfast and makes Maxine laugh. Dad said he's staying in a cabin out on the Sol Duc." She paused. "He said the guy had brand-new boots."

Layla stared at her.

"*He looked at my boots*," Layla said. "That's what he said. *Nobody tells the Chief. He's already looked at my boots twice.*"

"Dad notices boots," said Bella. "He always has. He says you can tell everything about a man by his shoes."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"He's been sitting in the diner," said Emmett, "eating *pie*. In the middle of town. For two weeks."

"In the back booth," said Bella. "Where he can see the door."

Jasper's mind did something that, in anyone else, Edward would have called admiration. *Of course he did. Of course. Hide in the one place nobody would look for you: the middle of the road with your face out.*

"Should we go and look?" said Rosalie. "Tomorrow morning. One of us. At the diner."

"No," said Carlisle.

"Carlisle—"

"No." He said it gently, and he said it with the voice that, in three hundred and some years, very few of them had ever argued with twice. "He told us he'd come to us. He told us he'd like a word with me. I would prefer to let him. If we go hunting him in the middle of Forks, with Charlie at the counter, someone will get hurt, and I have a very strong feeling that it won't be him."

Rosalie looked at him. Then she turned and looked out the glass wall at the dark, and said nothing.

---

It was late when they left.

Esme had made Layla eat something, a bowl of the soup Esme kept for the wolves, and Layla had healed her own cracked rib at the kitchen table with a tap of her wand and a hiss through her teeth that had made Seth go grey. Leah had finally sat down, on the bottom stair, near Alice, and the two of them had stayed there for an hour without speaking. Edward had been listening to both of them. They'd been thinking about the same thing. A hole in the shape of a man.

At the door, Seth stopped.

"Edward," he said. "Are you gonna?"

"Going to what?"

"Try. His head. He said you could try once."

Edward thought about the clearing. About a Stunner coming off the back of a gloved finger. About a man who'd stood in the moss and listened to two wolves' minds and answered them aloud and hadn't been bothered by it in the slightest, as though hearing what people thought were the most natural thing in the world.

He had never in a hundred years met anyone who could do what he did. He'd never met anyone he couldn't read, except Bella, who was silence. This man would be something else entirely. Edward was sure of that, without knowing how.

*Because he won't want to try twice,* the man had said.

"I don't know," said Edward honestly.

Seth nodded, like that made sense. Then he went out into the dark after Layla, ducking under a lintel he didn't need to duck under, and Edward stood in the doorway and watched the truck's taillights go away down the drive through the trees.

Behind him, the house was very quiet. Alice was still on the stairs. Jasper was standing beside her with his hand on her hair. Up on the second floor, Bella was singing to Renesmee, very low, and Edward could hear in the song that she had no idea she was still holding her shield over the child, and wouldn't put it down all night.

Somewhere out there in the dark, eleven miles away, a man who could take a curse off his finger and hand it back was sitting in a tent with a vampire in a box, and Edward could not hear him at all.

He went back to the piano. He didn't play.

### II. Two Pies

*The tent above the Calawah — 23 November, 9:40 a.m.*

There were two pies on the table, and Harry was trying to decide which one said *I come in peace* to a family of vampires.

The pumpkin was the obvious one. It was Thanksgiving. He'd been told this with great firmness by Maxine at the diner the afternoon before, when he'd come in at closing and asked if she had anything she could sell him whole, and she'd gone into the back and come out with two boxes and said the pumpkin was for *the day* and the marionberry was for *the day after, when you're sick of pumpkin*, and that if he took the pumpkin to somebody's house and they'd already made their own, he'd have to eat a slice of theirs and say it was better, and it wouldn't be.

On the other hand, none of them ate.

He stood with his hands on his hips and looked at the boxes. He was in the suit. He'd thought about that too. The suit said *official*, and he wanted them to know this was official, and he also wanted them to understand that he'd made an effort. He'd shaved. He'd polished the Oxfords, which didn't need it. The Colt was under his arm and the badge was in his pocket and his notebook, the fifty-fourth, was in the other, with a page in it headed CARLISLE CULLEN and twenty-two questions written down underneath.

"Pumpkin," he decided. "Obviously."

The mirror on the shelf by the range went hot.

He felt it from across the room. It was a dull, steady heat coming off the glass, and it had a sound to it, a low hum like a hive, which it had never once made in the four years he'd had it.

He picked it up.

"Potter," said Croaker. The old man's face filled the glass, and behind him was somewhere grey and bare, with a light that buzzed. "Where are you?"

"About to go and have pudding with some vampires, sir."

"Not today."

Harry put the pie box down.

"Kestrel's gone dark," said Croaker.

Harry didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. He'd met Kestrel twice. Once in a safe house in Vienna, once at a funeral in Godric's Hollow that neither of them had admitted to the other they were attending. She had grey eyes and a laugh like a drain unblocking and she'd been in Russia for six years.

"When?"

"Last signal eleven hours ago. Then nothing. Her emergency glass went cold at oh-four-hundred our time."

"Where?"

Croaker told him. It was a long way from Forks. It was a good deal less far from Forks than it was from London.

"You're the nearest man I've got who can do it," said Croaker. "And the only one I've got who can do it alone."

"How long?"

"As long as it takes, Potter."

"The specimen?"

"There's a crate on its way to you. It'll be in your front room in ten minutes. Put it in and seal it and it'll come home. The Thought people have cleared a room." Croaker's pale eyes held his. "And your wires stay up. Leave the lot exactly as it is. Anything walks into them while you're gone, it'll hang until you come back for it."

"Could be a while, if it's what I think it is."

"Then it'll hang a while."

"And the coven? The wolves? I told them I'd—"

"You told them you'd reach them," said Croaker. "You'll reach them when you're back. Ten minutes, Potter."

The glass went dark.

---

The crate came at nine minutes. It arrived the way the Department's crates always arrived, which was all at once and with no fuss, in the middle of the stone floor of the front room where there'd been nothing a moment before: a plain pine box six and a half feet long, stencilled down the side in black letters, AGRICULTURAL SAMPLES — DO NOT OPEN — PROPERTY OF H.M. GOVERNMENT, with a thumb-sized seal of grey wax on the lid. Harry looked at the stencil and thought, not for the first time, that somebody in the Department had a sense of humor and was wasting it.

He went through to the cold room.

Daniel Pruett lay where he'd been left the night before, on his back on the floor of Cell One, rigid, his eyes open. They moved when Harry came in. They'd been moving all night. He didn't sleep. None of them slept.

"Bit of news, Danny," said Harry. "You're going on holiday."

He walked the creature out of the cell on two fingers and through the library and the operations room, and laid it in the crate like a man laying a plank into a skip. Pruett's eyes rolled up at him from inside the box, red, wet, enormous.

"Where—" it said. Its face was free; he'd left it free. "Where are you sending me?"

"Somewhere very quiet," said Harry, "with some people who are going to be terribly pleased to meet you."

He put the lid on. He pressed his thumb into the grey wax. The crate went, the way it had come, all at once.

Then he went round the tent.

He checked the map. A hundred and thirty-nine white lights. The silver hair of the inner ring, unbroken. Nothing red. He checked the armory, and took the knives and two of the tins of Fiendfyre and left the rifle and the shotgun on their racks. He checked the cells. He stood a moment in front of the fourth, where the grey shapeless thing sat in the corner and the lamps dimmed when you looked at it, and said, "Behave," and went out again. He set the wards on the tent itself to their highest setting, the one that would make anyone who came within a hundred yards of the hollow suddenly and urgently remember an appointment in Tacoma.

He went back to the front room and looked at the pies.

He opened the pumpkin. He cut a slice and ate it standing up, quickly, over the box, like a man in a train station. Then he closed the box and put a Stasis Charm on it, and on the marionberry next to it, and left them both side by side in the middle of the scrubbed table, where they'd be exactly as they were whenever he got back.

He thought about the glass house in the woods, and the twenty-two questions in his notebook. He thought about a girl with freckles standing in a thicket of salal with blood on her lip asking *how do I reach you*, and what he'd told her.

He could send something. An owl. A note. *Called away. Back soon.* It would take him four minutes.

He didn't. You didn't. You didn't leave a note on the kitchen table when you went out the back door; you didn't tell people you'd be gone, or for how long, or that you'd ever be back. Every note you left was a thread somebody could pull. He'd told them he'd reach them. He'd reach them.

He took the suit off and put on what he'd need, which was a great deal heavier and a great deal less pleasant to look at. He put the Colt under it. He put the notebook in a drawer.

He turned on the spot in the middle of his own front room, and the tent went quiet behind him, and the two pies sat on the table under their charm, waiting.

### III. Forty-One Runes

*The Sol Duc side — 2 December*

*(Harry Potter was in Russia. Nobody in Forks knew that.)*

The scent was at the base of a western red cedar the width of a pickup truck, and Seth found it on the ninth day.

Layla was forty yards behind him in the salal, picking her way over a nurse-log furred with moss as thick as a mattress, when the sandy wolf stopped dead in front of her and put his nose to the ground and went rigid from ears to tail.

She knew that stillness now. She'd seen it every day for nine days, a dozen times a day, every time he caught a whiff of deer, or elk, or the black bear that was denning somewhere up the slope above the river and that Leah had threatened to evict. It was the pause before he decided. This time he didn't decide. He stayed there, frozen, his whole long body pointing at the roots of the cedar like a compass needle, and then he lifted his head and looked back at her over his shoulder with his enormous dark eyes, and she knew.

"Pine," she said. "Earth. Cold stone."

He dipped his head.

She'd asked him on the second day, sitting on her porch steps with a mug of Folgers going cold in her hands and her rib still aching where she'd healed it: *What did he smell like? Exactly. Tell me exactly.* Seth had screwed up his face and thought about it very hard. *Pine trees. And dirt, like good dirt, like Mom's garden. And cold stone. Like in a cave. And coffee. And pie.* And then, after a long pause, sounding puzzled: *And something kind of like us. A little bit. Underneath.*

She hadn't known what to do with that last part. She'd written it down anyway.

Leah came out of the trees on the far side of the cedar, grey and silent, and stood with her head low, sniffing. Her lip curled. Layla didn't need to be inside the pack mind to read that one.

"Yeah," Layla said. "Me too."

She went down on her knees in the wet moss at the base of the tree and drew her wand out of her hair.

---

It had been eleven days since the clearing, and nine days since Thanksgiving, when he hadn't come.

Everybody had expected him. Nobody had said so, but they had. Alice had sat at the bottom of the Cullens' stairs all day Thursday with her fingers pressed to her temples, staring at nothing, because she couldn't see him coming and she'd decided that meant he was. Jasper had stood at the glass wall from dawn until long after dark. Carlisle had stayed home from the hospital. Esme, being Esme, had laid a place at the dining table nobody ever ate at and then stood looking at it for a while and taken it away again.

Layla had spent Thanksgiving at Sue Clearwater's, with her wand in her hair and her eyes on the door, and Sue's turkey had been very good, and she couldn't remember a single bite of it.

He hadn't come Thursday. He hadn't come Friday. On Saturday, Leah had gone into town in human form in a borrowed jacket and sat in the diner for two hours with a cup of coffee and watched the back booth, which had been empty the whole time. On her way out, Maxine had said to the cook, loud enough to hear: *Wonder what happened to my English boy. Hasn't been in since Tuesday. Paid for two whole pies.*

And then a week of nothing.

Edward said he'd heard no new minds. No one strange. No English voice. Alice said the hole in her visions, the one in the shape of a man, had stopped turning up anywhere at all. Jacob had begun to say, carefully, the way you'd test ice, that maybe the guy had got what he came for. One leech. Research. Done. Gone home, wherever home was.

Layla didn't believe it.

She'd spent six years in places where the thing you couldn't see was the thing that killed you. She'd learned that a trap you couldn't see was still a trap. She'd learned that a man who'd strung a ward-net across a whole end of a peninsula and then walked away from it hadn't walked away from it at all. He'd left it running. He'd left it for later.

And she had sat on her porch the night after the clearing, with the brass bell on the windowsill silent behind her and Seth asleep on the swing with his head in her lap, and thought: *He knows where I live. He knows what I am. He knows what I did. He's got his hand round all of us and his net over Seth's whole land and I don't even know his name.*

*Well,* she'd thought. *I can do something about the net.*

---

The disc came up out of the ground under the cedar with a sucking sound, a hand's depth down, wrapped in roots. She had to cut it loose. She sat back on her heels in the moss with it in both palms, wet and black with earth, and brushed it clean with her sleeve.

Welsh slate. She knew it by the blue in the grey and the fine flat grain. A disc of it the size of a saucer, a little thicker than her thumb, cut smooth on both faces.

Spiralling in from the edge to the center on the upper face, cut into the stone with something finer than any chisel she knew, were runes.

She counted them twice, because she didn't believe it the first time. Forty-one.

"Okay," she said aloud, to nobody. "Okay. Hello."

Seth lay down in the moss next to her with his chin on his paws, watching. Leah stayed on her feet, at the edge of the clearing round the cedar, her ears swinging slowly back and forth, keeping watch on the trees.

Layla held the disc on her flat palm and touched it with the tip of her wand and whispered the gentlest Revealing Charm she had, the one she used on grave-goods so old that a hard look might crack them.

The disc woke.

It was like putting her ear to a beehive. The runes lit, one after another, from the outside of the spiral in, a faint silver light that ran along the cuts like water down a channel, until all forty-one of them were glowing. And with the light came the shape of the thing, the bones of the ward, rising up off the stone the way heat rises off a road, and she looked into it, and her stomach dropped.

She'd broken a lot of wards. She'd been good at it. Teo used to say she was the best he'd ever seen at reading one. *You look at a ward, chica, you see the man who made it.* And she'd always been able to. She could look at a ward and see the hands. A Mayan priest-king's death-hex on a tomb lintel, mean and simple and enormous. A Spanish inquisitor-wizard's gold-curse on a chapel reliquary, fussy, Catholic, self-righteous. Goblin work, which was cold and beautiful and absolutely pitiless. Her grandmother's protection over the house in Port Allen, which had looked, when Layla first learned to see it, like a quilt.

She looked into the ward rising off the slate disc in her palm and she couldn't see the hands at all.

The runes came from a dozen systems at once. That was the first thing. She knew Elder Futhark when she saw it, and there were seven of those; she knew Ogham, and there were five strokes of that, cut along the edge of the spiral like notches on a tally-stick. There were three goblin glyphs, and she'd only ever seen two of them before, on a vault door in Mexico City. There were marks she thought might be Phoenician. There was one she was nearly sure was Akkadian, which she'd only ever read about in a book.

And there were a dozen, twelve or thirteen, that weren't anything. That weren't from any script she'd ever seen in any tomb, any library, any book. Small, clean, angular marks that looked as though they'd been drawn by someone making up a language as he went along, and making it up very well.

And they all talked to each other.

That was the second thing. She sat back on her heels and stared at it. In every ward she'd ever broken, the runes did what runes do; they sat in their places and held their part of the load, the way stones sit in an arch. Take out the keystone and the arch comes down. Find the keystone, and you've found the ward. That was the whole job, mostly. That was curse-breaking.

These runes moved. The stone stayed still; in the ward, in the shape rising off it, she could see the load going round and round the spiral like water in a drain, never stopping anywhere, never resting on any one rune for longer than a heartbeat. There was no keystone. Every rune was the keystone, a fraction of a second at a time, and then it passed the job along to the next.

"Oh, you bastard," Layla said softly.

Seth's ears went up.

"Not you, baby."

She looked further. She made herself. She went down into the ward the way she'd go down into a tomb, slowly, one step at a time, testing every stone.

It was keyed to blood. That she'd expected, after what he'd said in the clearing; she could see the place where three drops of something had soaked into the slate at the very center of the spiral, and she could taste it on the air of the ward, faintly, like copper and lightning. *His*. It was linked to others. There was a thread running out of it, thin as spider silk, off toward the west, and another toward the north, and they ran off through the forest to other discs, and through those to others, and she could feel them out there, faint as stars, in a great broken ring. And down at the bottom of the ward, under everything, folded small, was something she nearly missed.

A tell.

The disc was watching her.

It was a tiny thing. A thread of awareness, no more. If she'd been pushing hard, if she'd been doing anything more than looking, she'd never have seen it at all, and it would have done what it was built to do, which was to tell somebody, somewhere, that someone was at the disc under the cedar on the Sol Duc side.

She went very still. She drew back out of the ward the way she'd come, slowly, step by step, and lifted the tip of her wand from the stone, and let the Revealing Charm go. The runes went dark one by one, from the inside out.

She sat there in the moss with the disc in her palm for a long time.

"Layla?" said Seth. He'd phased. She hadn't heard him go behind the cedar. He came round the trunk in his cut-offs and crouched beside her in the wet, shivering, rain in his hair. "Hey. You okay? You went all white."

"He's watching it," she said.

"What?"

"The ward. There's a— it's like a burglar alarm. If I'd pushed, it would've told him." She turned the disc over. The other face was blank. Perfectly blank, smooth as glass. "I don't know if it'd tell him now, wherever he is. Maybe it'd just write it down. Maybe it'd wait. But it'd know."

"Can you break it?"

She looked at the disc for a long time.

"I don't know," she said. "Seth, I don't— I've never seen anything like this. The runes don't sit still. There's no keystone. Half of them aren't even *real*, they're not from anywhere, he made them up, and they *work*." She heard her own voice go up and made it come down again. "And that's just this one. This is one disc. One. There's a whole ring of them out there. I can feel it."

"How many?"

"I don't know. A hundred. More."

Seth whistled softly.

Leah had come over. She stood looking down at the disc in Layla's hands with her pale wolf eyes, and Layla looked up at her.

"I know," Layla said. "I know what you're thinking."

Leah's head tipped.

"You're thinking, can she or can't she, and if she can't, what was the point of bringing her out here for nine days." Layla closed her fingers round the disc. "I can. I don't know how yet. But I can."

Leah looked at her a moment longer. Then she huffed, once, through her nose, and turned and went back to the edge of the clearing to watch the trees.

"That was a good huff," said Seth. "She likes you."

"She does not."

"She does, she just won't—"

"Seth."

"Okay."

*Calawah Way — 8 December*

It took her six days and most of six nights.

She didn't take the disc home. She'd thought about it, and decided that the tell might not like being moved, and might say so. So she sat under the cedar on the Sol Duc side every day from first light, wrapped in an oilskin coat with a Heating Charm in the lining and the rain hissing off a dome of Impervius over her head, and studied it in place. Seth or Leah or both of them stayed with her, the whole time, in the trees. In the evenings she went home and filled a notebook. By the sixth day she'd filled two.

She found the other anchors by following the threads. Gently. A fingertip of magic along a spider-silk line, never pressing, never pulling. Three more to the north, a mile and a quarter apart, in an arc that bent round toward the Cullens' land and the Sol Duc valley beyond: one under the footing of an old logging bridge, one in a riverbed under two feet of fast cold water, one at the base of a snag that had been dead for a century. Leah found each of them by the smell first. Pine. Earth. Cold stone. She'd started curling her lip every time.

And Layla found the loops.

She hadn't understood, at first, what they were. She'd found the anchors and the threads between the anchors and thought that was the whole of it. Then, on the fourth day, following a thread between the cedar and the bridge, she'd walked into something about four feet off the ground between two hemlocks that she couldn't see at all and felt it go past her like a cobweb across the face.

It was the snare.

It was strung through the forest between the anchors, tree to tree, in a long, low, loose line, like a wire fence somebody had made out of nothing. Every few yards it dropped into a loop, a noose, lying open on the moss or hanging a hand's width above it, waiting. She found one and crouched beside it and looked.

It was the simplest part of the whole thing. It had to be. All it had to do was lie there, and taste whatever walked through it, and close.

It was beautiful.

Even that. Even the dumbest, plainest, most workaday piece of the whole ward, the bit that was just *rope*, as far as its job was concerned: when she looked into it, it was layered like a pastry. It had a sense of taste she couldn't follow past the first few turns. It had a trigger built into it so finely balanced that she could see it shiver when a deer went past two hundred yards off, and settle again, satisfied, *not you*. It had something inside it that she thought must be the paralysis, the thing that had held that vampire stiff as a fence board. And it had its own little tell, its own thread of awareness, running back along the line to the anchors, so that if she cut it, the ward would know where.

She sat back in the moss with her wand in her lap and her heart going.

"I've broken curses six hundred years old," she said. Leah, standing over her, flicked an ear. "I've broken things that killed people who'd been doing this for fifty years. I broke a death-curse on a reliquary in Puebla that the goblins had given up on. I'm *good* at this. And his *rope*—" She looked at the loop lying in the moss. "His rope is better than anything I've ever made."

Leah didn't say anything, being a wolf. But she lay down beside Layla in the moss, which she had never done before, and stayed there.

---

The way in, in the end, was the tells.

She worked it out on the fifth night at her kitchen table at two in the morning, with Seth asleep with his head on his arms across from her and her two notebooks spread out round her and a third one half filled with diagrams. She'd been looking at it wrong. She'd been looking for the keystone, and there was no keystone, so she'd been looking for the place where the load went round the spiral to rest, and there wasn't one. There was no weak point. Every part of the ward held up every other part and passed the weight along. If you took a rune out, the others took up the slack in a heartbeat. If you took an anchor out, the anchors on either side took up its share, the way the people on either side of a man who stumbles in a chain gang take up his weight.

But the tells didn't take up anybody's slack.

Each anchor's tell was only watching itself. It had to be. If the tells had been linked, every anchor would know the moment any other anchor was touched, and the whole ring would light up like a switchboard every time a deer brushed a loop. So each tell watched its own disc, and its own loops, and nothing else.

And each disc, when its neighbor fell, would take the weight. But it would take the weight *because its tell told it to*.

"So you blind them first," Layla said aloud, at two in the morning, to the sleeping Seth. "All of them in a row. You make every one of them see that nothing's wrong. And then you pull them all at once, before any of them can call for help."

Seth snored.

It was a curse-breaker's trick, at bottom. She'd done it in tombs. You fed the tell a picture of itself, a loop, a mirror, so it went on watching and saw only what it expected to see. It was the hardest thing she'd ever learned to do. It had taken her a year with Teo to get it right. And she'd only ever done it on one tell at a time, on a single ward, in a dry tomb, with the light right and no rain and all the time in the world.

On the eighth of December she did it on four, in the dark, in the rain, in a mile and a quarter of forest, before dawn.

---

She remembered it afterward in pieces.

Leah running. That was the first piece. A grey shape in the dark among the trees ahead of her, going from anchor to anchor through the rain like smoke, nose down, and Seth on her other side, and the two of them leading her through the black forest at a pace she couldn't have made alone, with a Disillusionment on herself so the loops wouldn't taste her and a Lumos no brighter than a candle in a cupped hand.

Kneeling at the cedar. Wand on the disc. The mirror-loop coming off the end of her wand like a soap bubble and settling over the tell, and the tell looking at it, and looking at it, and *believing* it. Holding her breath while it did. Moving on.

The bridge footing, where she had to lie on her belly in the mud under the old logging bridge with her arm in the river to the shoulder. The riverbed, where she couldn't reach and Seth went in up to his chest in the black water in his wolf shape and dug the disc out of the gravel with his paws and held it in his mouth, very gently, like a retriever with a bird, while she worked on it with her teeth chattering. The dead snag.

And then the long minute at the snag, when she'd blinded all four and had to take them all at once.

She'd planned it the way she planned everything. She'd thought about it for two days. A ward like that would hold a disc in its place the way muscle holds bone, so she'd go for the blood. You could find the three drops at the heart of the spiral, his three drops, and take them out of the stone, thread by thread, and without them the disc was just a disc. She'd found the place. She'd practiced on the empty air until her hand knew it. She'd laid a thread from her wand to all four discs at once, through the mirror-loops, so that she could reach all of them from the snag. It was the most complicated thing she'd ever tried in her life.

She knelt at the base of the dead snag in the rain with her wand on the fourth disc and the other three hanging off the end of it like fish on a line, and she reached into all four of them at once, and she pulled.

It fought her.

Even blinded. Even with its eyes put out and its tells singing lullabies to themselves, the ward felt her reach into its heart and *knew*, the way a body knows a knife. It held on. It pulled back. She felt it pull back along the threads, toward her, toward her wand, the way he'd pulled back her own curses in the clearing and handed them to her, and for one second she felt it take hold of the spell she was casting and start to turn it round—

She held on.

She held on with everything. She'd never held on to anything like it. She felt her own heart in her teeth, and something hot and wet start out of her nose and run down over her lip, and her hand on the wand went numb to the elbow, and then, all at once, the way a knot comes undone, the blood let go.

All four discs went dark.

And above her, and around her, in the black wet forest, she felt the snare-line go.

She couldn't see it. She'd never been able to see it, not really, only feel it, the faint low hum of it running through the trees like a power line. Now she felt it stop. Along a mile and a quarter of forest, between the cedar and the snag, the hum went out like a light switched off. On either side of the gap, the ward stretched, and strained, and shivered, trying to take up the weight, and couldn't, and settled. Holding. Waiting. With a hole in it two miles wide.

She sat down in the mud at the bottom of the snag.

After a while she realized Seth was in front of her, human, kneeling in the rain, holding her face in his hands. He'd phased and not bothered with the cut-offs. He was saying her name.

"I'm okay," she said. "I'm okay. I'm— oh God, my nose."

"You're bleeding."

"Yeah, it does that." She wiped it on the back of her hand. It came away black in the dark. "It does that when I— when it's big."

"Did it work?"

"It worked."

He let go of her face. He looked round at the dark trees as though he expected to see something.

"I don't feel anything," he said.

"You wouldn't. You never did." She leaned her head back against the dead snag and shut her eyes. The rain came down on her face. "It's gone, Seth. From the cedar to here. There's a hole in his fence."

Leah came out of the dark. Grey and soaked and silent. She stood over the two of them, and lowered her head, and looked at Layla for a long moment with her pale eyes, and then she put her muzzle down, very carefully, and touched it to the top of Layla's head, once.

Then she went off into the dark to keep watch.

"Okay," said Seth, a little dazed. "Okay, that was definitely a like."

---

Layla sat up most of the rest of the night on her porch, wrapped in a blanket, with a mug of tea and a pinch of dittany in a saucer that she kept dabbing at her nose.

She'd done it. She'd beaten one piece of it. Four discs out of however many hundreds, a hole in one arc of the ring. She'd done it with every single thing she'd ever learned and two days of planning and a wolf in a river, and it had very nearly turned her own spell round and handed it back to her.

She thought about the runes that came from nowhere. A whole little alphabet of them, clean and angular and working. She thought about the load that went round the spiral like water and never stopped. She thought about the rope that was better than anything she'd ever made. She thought about the three drops of blood at the heart of each disc that had fought her like something alive.

She'd taken it out of four discs out of a hundred and more. He'd put it into all of them.

She looked down at her hand on the mug. It was shaking. She held it still.

She'd been frightened of him in the clearing. She'd known what he was, the way you know what a storm is when it's on top of you. She hadn't understood, then. She'd thought she understood. She'd thought she'd seen the whole of it, a man who could take a curse off his finger.

Now she'd seen his rope.

She sat on the porch until the sky went grey over the Calawah, and the brass bell in the kitchen window stayed quiet the whole night. Nothing came up the drive. Nothing came out of the trees. Behind her, through the kitchen window, the third notebook lay open on the table, and on the page it was open to she'd copied out, very carefully, the twelve runes that weren't from anywhere, in a column, one under another, with a question mark beside each.

She didn't know, then, that two days later three red-eyed nomads coming down off the Sol Duc in the dark would walk straight through the hole she'd made, and not feel a thing.

### IV. Guests

*The Cullen house — 10 December, 9:52 p.m.*

*(Harry Potter was still in Russia.)*

Alice saw them at a quarter to nine.

She was in the kitchen with Layla, of all people, at the counter, watching the witch make a pot of something with chicory in it that Esme had found at a specialty grocer in Seattle and driven three hours each way to buy, because Esme had heard Layla mention it once. Layla had cried a little when she saw the tin. Then she'd laughed at herself. Then she'd made it, and the whole house smelled of it, burnt and bitter and dark, and Alice had been sitting on the counter swinging her heels and asking Layla whether witches had their own fashion magazines when she stopped talking in the middle of a word.

Layla put down the pot.

They came down the Sol Duc," and then she'd turned and gone to Seth and put her hand on his arm and held on.

Now he listened to the three of them standing in the snow.

The one in the middle was old, older than Carlisle. The mind was Portuguese at the bottom, under four centuries of other languages laid on top of it like coats of varnish, and it was cool and amused and very patient, and it was looking at the house the way a man looks at a ledger. *Vasco*, it thought of itself. *Vasco Teles*. A Lisbon merchant's son, turned in 1611 in a warehouse on the Tagus by something he'd never seen again, and he'd spent the four hundred years since learning that the best thing in the world was to know a thing other people would pay you to keep quiet.

The woman beside him was his. Mireille. French, younger, two hundred years, thin and fair and restless, and her mind was full of a single picture she'd been carrying for four days like a stolen coin: a ridge high in the Olympics, above the snowline, on the sixth, and a long way off down the slope, two figures and a wolf. A dark-haired female with eyes going amber. A great russet wolf. And between them, running, laughing, a small child with bronze curls who could not possibly be running that fast, who could not possibly be leaping like that from rock to rock, who could not possibly—

*Immortal child*, the woman's mind said. *Immortal child. Immortal child.* Over and over, the way a heart beats. Delighted. Terrified.

And the third one, a little behind them, was young. Edward winced at him. Barely a decade. A boy from Portland who'd been turned in a skate park in 1997 and had never really grown up and wasn't going to, now. *Tobias*. He was thirsty. He was so thirsty the whole of his mind was painted red with it, and under the red, he was looking at the glass wall of the house and smelling what was in it. Humans. A human. Something like a human, or more than one, a strange hot salt-and-dog smell he didn't know, and under that, behind it, coming through the walls like music through a floor, something that made the boy's whole mind sit up and *ache*.

Ozone. Lightning. Rain on a hot road.

Edward heard Tobias smell Layla Devereaux, and every muscle in his own body went hard.

"Edward," said Carlisle quietly, beside him.

"They know," said Edward. "About Renesmee. One of them saw her on the sixth, from the ridge above the Hoh. With Bella and Jacob. She thinks Renesmee is an immortal child."

Behind him, on the stairs, he heard Bella's whole body go still.

"And the old one," Edward said, "has thought of a use for it."

Carlisle went out to them. He went alone, though none of them wanted him to, because that was what Carlisle did. Esme stood at the glass with her hand flat on it. Jasper stood at the door. Emmett stood behind Jasper, rolling his shoulders. ---

It went the way Edward had known it would go from the moment he heard the old man's mind come down off the Sol Duc.

She grew, very fast, every day. She learned. She could be taught. She was gentle. She was nothing at all like the old horrors in the old stories. He asked them in, and he asked them to see for themselves, and he asked Rosalie to bring her to the top of the stairs, and Rosalie did, holding her so tight that Edward could hear the child's ribs creak, and Renesmee looked down at the three red-eyed strangers in the doorway with her mother's eyes and put her hand on Rosalie's cheek and asked, silently, *who are they*, and the three of them looked up at her.

Mireille's mind went white with fear and wonder. Tobias's mind barely noticed; it had gone past the child to the kitchen door, and the witch behind it.

Vasco's mind did arithmetic.

*Perhaps,* it thought. *Perhaps she is what he says. Perhaps not. It doesn't matter. It doesn't matter at all. Aro will want to see her. Aro will want to judge her for himself. And when Aro hears there's a child in the woods in Washington with a heartbeat and a seer in the house beside her who sees the future—*

The old man's eyes went past Carlisle's shoulder and found Alice on the stairs, and stayed there. And Edward, listening, heard the shape of what Vasco wanted, under all the rest of it, the way you hear the bass line under a song.

What he wanted, under all of it, was Alice.

With all of them. I understand that you would very much prefer that I didn't. Edward closed his eyes.

Every third night, at midnight, I call him. If I don't call, he goes to the nearest airport, and he flies to Rome, and he takes a car to Volterra, and he tells the brothers everything I've told him. Carlisle didn't look round. He didn't need to. Edward said it under his breath, at a pitch only the vampires could hear: "It's true. All of it. He doesn't know where Lucan is. Lucan moves. He's been told to."

Jasper, at the door, had gone absolutely still.

"And after *for now*?" said Carlisle.

"After now," said Vasco, "we'll talk."

Behind Edward, very softly, Jacob Black said, in a voice that was hardly a voice, "Carlisle, let us."

"No, Jacob."

"We can take them. Right now. All three. Leah's faster than any of them, I've seen her—"

"And Lucan?" said Carlisle, still not turning round. "In three nights, when nobody calls? When he gets on a plane? What happens to Renesmee then, Jacob?"

Jacob didn't answer. Edward heard the answer in his head anyway, and it was a howl.

"No."

Vasco looked at him. Then he smiled, and inclined his head, very slightly, the courtly little bow of a man in a lace collar in a Lisbon counting-house four hundred years ago.

### V. Rather Clear

*The Cullen house — 19 December, 8:30 p.m.*

*(Harry Potter was in Russia until twenty past eight that evening. Then he wasn't.)*

Nine nights later, Leah Clearwater stood with her back to the wall by the kitchen door and watched the old leech walk across the Cullens' great room toward Layla, and thought, very clearly: *this is the one. This is the night it goes.*

She'd been thinking it every night for nine nights. She'd been wrong every time. She'd stood here and watched them come in out of the snow every evening at nine, the old one and his woman and the boy, and sit in Esme's chairs, and talk. The old one talked, mostly. He talked to Carlisle about books, and Italy, and men who'd been dead three hundred years. He talked to Alice. He asked her things. Every night he asked her more things, and she answered less every time, and he smiled every time. The woman watched the stairs. The boy paced.

And every night, the old one asked about the storm.

They'd kept Layla away the first six nights. Seth had begged her to stay home, and she'd said she would, and she hadn't, because on the third night she'd found out from Seth's face what the old one had been asking, and she'd driven out to the Cullen house on the fourth night and walked in the front door with her wand in her hair and said, "My name's Layla. I'm the guest. What do you want?" and the old leech had looked at her for a long time, breathing in, and then said, very softly, "Oh," and nothing else.

He'd known. Leah had watched him know. She didn't know how much. Layla said he couldn't know, not really, not what she was, not what witches were. Layla said he'd only smelled her. But he'd looked at her the way the boy looked at her, and the way Leah had once seen a dog look at a steak on a counter, and every night since then he'd asked a little more.

Tonight, he'd stopped asking.

"I'd like a taste," Vasco said.

He'd got up out of Esme's armchair to say it. He'd stood, and buttoned his long coat, a dark thing, old-fashioned, that he wore like a cloak, and walked across the room, not fast, the way people walk across a room at a party to talk to someone they've been meaning to talk to all night. Toward the kitchen door. Toward Leah, and Seth, and the witch behind them.

"Just a taste," he said. "A single swallow, from the wrist. I'd never hurt you, my dear. I'd never dream of it. I've been sitting in this house nine nights with your scent in my head like a song I can't remember the end of, and I'd simply like to know."

The whole room had gone still. Leah could hear it. You could hear a room go still, when half the people in it didn't need to breathe.

Seth was shaking. It was going up through him, she could see it, she could feel it coming off him like heat off a road, he was right on the edge, inside the house, with Nessie at the top of the stairs in Rosalie's arms and Layla behind him. Jacob had pushed off the wall by the stairs. Emmett had stood up off the sofa. Jasper was already moving, somehow, without seeming to move at all, around the edge of the room.

"Vasco," said Carlisle. His voice was very quiet. "That's enough."

"Is it?" The old leech didn't turn round. "Lucan is expecting my call in three hours, Carlisle. I'm sure he's longing to hear how my evening went."

Behind Leah, Layla had drawn her wand.

Leah heard it come out of her hair. Seth heard it too; she felt him hear it. The old leech heard it as well, and he stopped, eight feet from the kitchen door, and looked past Seth's shoulder at the witch with a little smile.

"There," he said. "That's the stick. Mireille saw you with it in the yard. What does it do, I wonder?"

"Come one step closer," said Layla, "and you'll find out."

Over by the hearth, the boy, Tobias, made a sound.

Leah had been watching him all night. She'd been watching him every night. He was the one who'd go, she'd thought. The old one had control the way a church has walls. The woman was a shadow. But the boy hadn't fed in nine days, because he'd been kept on a leash in a motel in Sequim, and he'd been sitting in this house every night with a witch's blood singing through the walls, and tonight his eyes had gone from red to nearly black and he'd stopped pacing and just stood by the fire, swaying a little, staring at the kitchen door with his lips parted.

He made a sound like a dog makes in its sleep.

"Tobias," said Mireille sharply, from her chair. "*Non*."

The boy's eyes stayed on Layla. His fingers were opening and closing at his sides.

Somewhere in the house, upstairs, Nessie started to cry.

And in the middle of the Cullens' great room, between the old leech and the kitchen door, ten feet from the fire, the air cracked open like a gunshot, and a man was standing on Esme's floor.

---

Leah knew him before she knew she knew him.

She knew him by the smell first. It came off him in a wave, all at once, the way a smell comes into a room when somebody opens the door from outside. Snow, a lot of it, from somewhere far off; this snow was dry and bitter and far colder, and it had coal smoke in it, and diesel, and something burnt, like a car that had been on fire. Cordite. Blood, a little, fresh. And under all of that, through it, the way she'd known it would be: pine. Earth. Cold stone.

He looked different. That was the second thing. No suit. He was wearing a long, heavy, dark grey coat, a working coat, scuffed, with snow on the shoulders that hadn't thawed yet, and a black wool watch cap pulled down to his eyebrows, and heavy boots with snow packed into the treads. There was a cut over his left eye, an inch long, still bleeding a little, a thin dark line running down beside his nose. The knuckles of his right hand were split. He hadn't shaved in a week. He looked tired. He looked like he'd walked a long way.

He looked round the room once. It took about a second. Leah watched the green eyes go over all of it: the Cullens, the wolves, the witch, the child crying at the top of the stairs in Rosalie's arms, the old leech in the long coat, the woman in the chair, the boy by the fire.

"Evening," he said.

The boy by the fire smelled the blood on his face.

It happened faster than Leah could have said it. She saw it, all of it; she was a wolf even in this skin, and her eyes were good. She saw the boy's whole body change, the way a cat's does before it jumps, all at once, every muscle. She saw his face go from a face to something else. She saw him leave the floor.

And she saw the man in the watch cap draw a pistol out of his coat with his right hand, split knuckles and all, and fire it, in one movement, without seeming to aim, without seeming to look, while the boy was still in the air.

There was one sound. A flat, hard *bark*. It filled the great room and slapped back off the glass wall.

The boy was gone.

One instant he was there, in midair, six feet from the man in the watch cap, with his mouth open and his hands out, and the next he was a cloud. A fine, bright, glittering mist, like breath on a cold morning, like the spray off the top of a wave with the sun in it, hanging in the air in the shape a boy had been a moment before.

It hung there for a second.

Then it drifted, very slowly, down, and settled on Esme's pale floor in a thin, shining film, like frost on a window. Like sugar spilled on a counter.

It didn't move.

Leah had seen a lot of leeches killed. She knew what the pieces did. They twitched. They crawled. They rolled toward each other across the ground, inch by inch, trying to find the rest of themselves, and that was why you had to burn them, every piece, fast, before they could. She stood with her back against the kitchen doorframe and stared at the glittering film on the floor and waited for it to move.

It lay there. It didn't twitch. It didn't do anything at all.

The man in the watch cap lowered the pistol. He looked at the shining dust on the floor at his feet for a moment, and then he looked up, round the room, at the Cullens, and at Jacob by the stairs, and at Leah, and at Layla behind her.

"I had been rather clear," he said. "No?"

---

The old leech moved.

He'd been closest. He'd been eight feet from Layla when the man came, and he'd turned at the sound of the crack, and now he went for the kitchen door. For Layla. Leah saw it coming, she saw the long dark coat flare and the hands come up, and she knew what he was going to do; he was going to take the witch, take her by the throat and hold her up between himself and that pistol like a shield. She threw herself sideways to get in front of Seth, to get in front of Layla, she felt the heat come up her spine, she was going to phase right there in Esme's doorway—

The man in the watch cap flicked two fingers.

There was a sound like a pair of marbles dropping onto a wooden floor.

*Click.*

*Click.*

The old leech was gone. The woman, who'd come up out of her chair by the fire, was gone. Where they'd been, two small grey stones lay on Esme's floor, rolling. One rolled to a stop against the toe of Leah's bare foot. The other rolled a long way, in a slow wobbling curve, across the pale boards toward the fire, and stopped against the hearth.

Nobody moved.

The man in the watch cap put the pistol away inside his coat. He walked across the room, and bent, and picked up the pebble by the hearth between his thumb and finger, and looked at it, and put it in his coat pocket. Then he walked over to Leah, and bent again, right in front of her, and picked up the pebble at her feet.

He straightened up. He was close enough that she could see the cut over his eye. It had stopped bleeding. It was closing, very slowly, at the edges, while she watched.

"Lassie," he said. "You look well."

Leah opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

He held up the little grey stone between his thumb and finger in front of her face, as if to show her. It was a river pebble. Smooth. Ordinary. The sort of thing you'd find a million of on First Beach. He turned it over once. Then he put it in his pocket with the other.

"They'll keep," he said.

He turned round to face the room.

"Sorry I'm late," he said. "Traffic."


---

Upstairs, Nessie had stopped crying.

Leah heard her stop. It was very sudden. One second she was wailing, the thin high frightened sound she made when the grown-ups' faces went wrong, and the next she'd gone quiet, and when Leah looked up the stairs the child was twisted round in Rosalie's arms, staring down over the banister at the man in the watch cap with her mouth open and her brown eyes huge.

Nobody else moved.

Jasper was on the man's left, eight feet away. Emmett was on his right. Leah hadn't seen either of them get there. Bella had come down three stairs and stopped, and Leah could feel something coming off her, a sort of pressure in the air, the way the air goes thick in the minute before a thunderstorm. Jacob was halfway across the room. Seth had stopped shaking. He'd stopped doing anything. He was just standing there in front of Layla with his mouth open.

The man in the watch cap looked at Jasper. Then at Emmett.

"Don't," he said.

Jasper didn't move. Emmett, who'd been leaning forward onto the balls of his feet, rocked very slowly back onto his heels.

"Thank you," said the man.

"Where," said Jacob, "the *hell* have you been?"

"Away."

"For a *month*."

"Twenty-six days." The man took off the watch cap. His hair underneath was flattened and damp. He ran a hand through it. "Who's counting."

"We thought you were *gone*."

"I said I'd reach you." He looked at Jacob with mild surprise. "I'm reaching you."

---

Carlisle had gone down on one knee by the glittering film on the floor.

He didn't touch it. He knelt there with his hands on his knee and looked at it the way Leah had seen him look at Nessie's X-rays, very close, very still, his head tipped a little to one side. The light from the fire caught the dust and made it shine. It lay on Esme's pale boards in the shape of a long splash, like water thrown across a floor. It wasn't moving. Leah kept waiting for it to move.

"It isn't trying to come back together," Carlisle said quietly.

"No," said the man. "It won't."

"Ever?"

"Ever."

Carlisle looked up at him. Leah had never seen the doctor look at anyone like that. She didn't have a word for it.

"What did you do?" Carlisle said.

"Something I've been working on." The man put the watch cap in his coat pocket. "Is there a broom? I'd sweep it up, if I were you. I'd put it in a jar. I'd keep the jar somewhere nobody's going to knock it over. That's just me."

"Is he dead?"

"Yes," said the man.

He said it the way you'd tell somebody the time. Carlisle looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked down at the dust again, and something went across his face, and he closed his eyes.

"He was a boy," Carlisle said.

"He was going to open her throat," said the man, and jerked his chin at the kitchen door, at Layla, "in about four more seconds, if I'd been a bit later. Or mine, as it turned out. I'll take mine. I'm used to it." He looked down at Carlisle. "I'm sorry, Doctor. I know you'll have wanted to try talking. I've seen what you're like. I don't talk to things that are in the air coming at me."

Carlisle didn't answer.

---

"Right," said the man, and turned round to face the room. "Somebody want to tell me why there were three red-eyes sitting in your front room drinking nobody's coffee?"

Carlisle told him. He stood up to do it. He told it short and clear, like a report: Mireille on the ridge above the Hoh. The tenth. Vasco. The demands. The nine nights. Alice. The storm. And Lucan, and the phone, and the call every third night at midnight.

The man listened without saying anything. When Carlisle got to Lucan, he looked at his watch.

"Midnight tonight?"

"Yes."

"Three hours." He nodded, as if this were fine. "Plenty."

"You don't know where he is," said Edward.

Edward had been standing by the piano the whole time. He hadn't moved since the crack. Leah had been half-watching him, because Edward was the one who'd know things first, always, and she'd seen his face when the man appeared, and it had been like watching somebody walk into a room and find that the floor wasn't there.

The man turned and looked at him.

"I've got two people in my pocket who've got his number," he said. "That'll do for a start." He tilted his head. "You're the one who listens."

"Yes."

"Go on, then."

"What?"

"You've been wanting to since I came in. You've been wanting to for a month, I'd think. You've got that look." The man spread his hands a little. "I said once. Here's your once."

Edward hesitated.

Leah saw him hesitate, and then she saw him do it. She didn't see anything happen, exactly. Nobody moved. Edward just looked at the man, very hard, his golden eyes narrowing, his whole face going still and focused the way it went sometimes when he was listening to somebody a long way off.

Then his face went white.

Whiter than white. Leah had seen leeches go pale before, when they were angry, when they were hungry, but this was something else, this was like watching the color go out of a photograph. Edward took one step back, and his hand shot out behind him and caught the edge of the piano, and the piano groaned. A long crack went across the black lacquer under his fingers.

"*Edward*—" Bella was off the stairs and beside him before Leah saw her move, with her hands on his face.

Edward was staring at the man.

"There's nothing," he said. His voice had gone strange and thin. "There's— it isn't silence. Not like you, love. It's like— looking down. Like standing at the edge of something and looking down and there's no bottom. It just goes *down*—"

"Told you," said the man. He didn't sound unkind about it. He sounded tired. "Don't do it again, there's a good lad. I'd hate for you to fall in."

Edward didn't answer. Bella held his face between her hands and looked over her shoulder at the man with her red-gold eyes, and Leah felt the pressure in the room go thick, and thicker, like a held breath.

The man looked back at her.

For a second he looked at her the way he'd looked at Seth in the clearing, when Seth had thought the word *wizard*. Very still. Very focused. Leah saw it.

Then his eyebrows went up, just a fraction.

"Huh," he said.

"Stay out of my head," said Bella.

"Can't get in, love." He was looking at her the way Carlisle had been looking at the dust. "That's the interesting bit." He turned his head away, the way you'd stop staring at someone at a bus stop once you'd realized you were doing it. "Later. If you'll let me. Not tonight."

---

He'd turned to the kitchen door.

Layla was still standing behind Seth with her wand in her hand. She'd lowered it at some point. Leah didn't know when. She was standing very straight with her chin up and her face white under the freckles, looking at him, and Leah, who knew a little about the look on somebody's face when they've done something stupid and are waiting for somebody else to say it, recognized it straight away.

"I got back half an hour ago," the man said to her. "I'd a look at my map before I came. Thought I'd see how things had got on while I was out." He paused. "There's a hole in my fence. Two miles wide. On the Sol Duc side."

"Four," said Layla.

"Sorry?"

"Four discs. I took out four." Her voice was steady. Her wand hand wasn't, quite. "In a row. Between the cedar and the dead snag."

The man looked at her for a long time.

"Four," he said.

"Yes."

"In one go."

"Yes."

"Did you blind the tells first?"

Something moved in Layla's face. "Mirror-loops."

"In the rain?"

"In the rain."

"Huh," said the man again. He kept looking at her. It was a different look from the one he'd given Bella. Leah couldn't have said how. "There's nobody I know of who's ever taken one out," he said. "Never mind four in a row. I didn't build them to be taken out." He paused. "I'd like to see your notes, some time."

Layla's chin came up another inch. She didn't say anything.

"And on the tenth," the man said, in exactly the same voice, "three red-eyes came down off the Sol Duc and walked straight through your hole and came here, and sat in this room for nine nights while the old one made plans for the seer, and the boy got thirstier, and tonight the boy was going to open your throat." He let it lie there a second. "Next time you want my fence down, wait till I'm home and ask me. I'll show you the door."

"You told me I can't reach you," Layla said.

"So I did," said the man. He seemed to think about it. "Well. We'll fix that."

---

Esme came across the room with a damp cloth and a small first-aid box from under the kitchen sink, and stopped in front of him and held them out.

He looked at her as if he had no idea what she was doing.

"Your eye," she said gently.

"Oh." He touched the cut. It had closed nearly all the way now; there was only a dark line left, crusted at the edges. "It's all right. Thanks. It does that."

"It's already closed," said Seth, staring.

"It does that," said the man again.

Esme kept holding out the cloth anyway. After a second he took it, and wiped the dried blood off the side of his nose with it, and gave it back to her folded, and said, "Thank you, Mrs. Cullen," and Esme said, "Esme," and he said, "Esme," and Leah had the odd feeling she'd just watched something get decided.

At the top of the stairs, Nessie was still staring at him. She'd got one small hand free of Rosalie's arms and was holding it out, palm forward, over the banister, toward him, fingers spread. The way she did when she wanted to show you something.

Rosalie pulled the hand back in.

The man looked up at the two of them. He looked at the child for about as long as it would take to say her name. Then he looked at Rosalie.

"She's all right," he said. "Nobody's going to hurt her tonight."

"And tomorrow?" said Rosalie.

"Tomorrow I'll be busy," said the man.

---

"What about the other lot?" said Jacob.

Everybody looked at him.

"Sam's pack," Jacob said. He had his arms folded. He was still breathing hard. "Down in La Push. You've been watching us for a month. You were watching us before that. You must've seen them."

"I've seen them."

"Are you going to do to them what you did to us?"

"I didn't do anything to you, Clifford. You came and found me." The man thought about it. "But yes. I'd like to meet them. At some point. Uley, and the rest of them. And your elders, I should think. Your dad."

Jacob went very still.

"Why?"

"Because they're there," said the man, which was not an answer, and everybody in the room knew it. He said it in the same flat, tired, polite voice he'd used to tell Carlisle the boy was dead, and there wasn't anything else in it. Leah listened for something else in it. There wasn't.

"I'll want somebody to take me," he said. "Introduce me. I'll sort it."

Then he turned to Carlisle.

"Doctor," he said. "You'll have a study. They always do. Could I have a word?"

---

### VI. Just Think About It

*Carlisle's study — 9:05 p.m.*

The man stood in front of the painting for a long time before he said anything.

Carlisle had let him go first, through the door at the end of the second-floor hall, into the study, and had followed him, and closed the door behind them. He didn't know why he'd closed it. Every one of them could hear every word through two walls and a floor. Perhaps it was for the man's sake. Perhaps for his own.

The man had walked straight past the desk, and the bookshelves, and the chair, and stopped in front of the big canvas on the wall opposite the window, the one in the heavy gilt frame, and stood looking up at it with his hands in his coat pockets.

It was a Solimena. Carlisle had owned it for nearly three hundred years. It showed a balcony, high up, in a stone palace, with a painted Italian sky behind it going from gold to blue, and on the balcony, in the dark rich robes of a vanished century, four figures looking down at something below. Three of them sat. One stood, a little apart, with fair hair, at the end of the balcony.

"That's you," the man said.

"Yes."

"And them."

"Yes."

The man looked at it for a while longer. At the one in the middle, black-haired, smiling, with a long pale face and fine hands folded in his lap.

"He's the one who touches you," he said.

"Aro," said Carlisle. "Yes."

"And the white-haired one's the angry one. Caius. And the one at the end who looks like he'd like to be dead is Marcus." He tilted his head at the canvas. "Solimena got them very well. Did they sit for it?"

Carlisle looked at him.

"How do you know their names?"

"Picked them up," said the man. "Here and there." He turned round. His face was very tired, and very calm, and told Carlisle nothing at all. "I'd like everything you remember about Volterra, Doctor. Everything you've got."

Carlisle didn't sit. Neither did the man.

"Why?" said Carlisle.

"Can't say."

"Then I can't help you."

"Yes, you can," said the man. "You just don't want to."

He didn't say it unkindly. He said it the way Carlisle might say it to a patient, sitting on the edge of a hospital bed, about a test the patient was frightened of.

"Aro is my friend," said Carlisle.

"Is he."

"He has been for three hundred years. We disagree about a great deal. About nearly everything. But he took me in when I had nowhere, and he let me go when I asked, and he has never once, in all that time, lifted a hand against me or mine." He heard his own voice. It was steady. "You walked into my house tonight and killed a boy in front of my wife, and put two people in your pocket. You've been on this land for six weeks with a weapon I don't understand, catching our kind in a net. You won't tell me your name, or who sent you, or why. And you'd like me to tell you how to walk into my oldest friend's house."

"Yes," said the man.

Carlisle looked at him.

"Four hundred and twelve," the man said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Tourists. Since the spring. The number I've got. Through a door off the Piazza dei Priori, behind a woman with a tour flag and a nice smile. Four hundred and twelve in, and none out. That's the ones we could count. Germans. Japanese. A girls' choir from Lyon." He held Carlisle's eyes. "I'd like that number to stop going up. That's all I'll say about why."

Carlisle closed his eyes.

He knew. Of course he knew. He'd always known. He'd lived there. He'd stood at the back of the great round room in the tower with the drain in the middle of its floor while the doors were opened and the people came in, chattering, cameras round their necks, looking up at the ceiling, and then the doors shut. He'd stood there and not watched, and listened, and left the room afterward and walked out into the night and stood on the walls of Volterra looking at the hills for hours. He'd done that for twenty years, before he'd finally asked to go. He'd never once tried to stop it. He'd told himself it wasn't his to stop.

He'd heard about Heidi. Edward had told him, in the spring, what he'd seen.

"And the child," said the man, more quietly.

Carlisle opened his eyes.

"There's one of them out there right now," the man said, "with a phone in his pocket, and in three hours he's going to wonder why it hasn't rung. And then he's going to get on a plane. And then the old man in the middle of that painting's going to hear there's a little girl in the woods in Washington who grows like a weed and runs like a deer. And they'll come. You know they'll come. You know what they do about the ones they think are immortal children." He paused. "I'll get the one with the phone. I'll get him tonight. But there'll be another one, some day. Somebody'll see her from a ridge. And then they'll come here, and when they do, Doctor, I'd very much like to know the ground."

Carlisle looked at him for a long time.

"You're going to fight them," he said.

The man didn't answer.

"You can't," said Carlisle. "You don't— you haven't seen them. You don't know what Jane can do, what Alec can do. You don't know how many there are. Aro has been collecting gifts for three thousand years. He has a guard of thirty, forty, and every one of them chosen for what they can do to you. You can't possibly—"

"Then tell me," said the man.

Carlisle stopped.

"Tell me what you know," the man said. "That's all I'm asking." He took his hands out of his pockets. "And I don't need you to draw me a map. I don't need you to write it down. I don't need you to say a word, if you'd rather not."

"Then how—"

"Just think about it," the man said.

---


He'd said yes.

He stood in his study with the Solimena behind the man's head and thought about a tour flag, and a girls' choir from Lyon, and Renesmee asleep on Jacob's chest with her fists in his shirt, and he said, very quietly, "All right."

The man nodded. "This'll feel odd," he said. "Don't fight it. It's worse if you fight it."

Then he looked at Carlisle. That was all. He just looked.

It didn't hurt. Carlisle had braced himself for it to hurt, and it didn't. It was like a hand, a very light, very careful hand, coming into a room where he kept things and lifting them off the shelves one at a time and looking at them and setting them back exactly where they'd been. He felt it come in. He felt it go to the far end of the room, to the oldest shelves, the ones he hadn't dusted in two hundred years.

And he felt, with something that was almost relief, the things it reached for rise up on their own to meet it, the way a book falls open where it's been opened most.

He didn't see what the hand saw. Not the way the man did. But he felt the shape of what was being taken, the way you feel the weight of a coat being lifted off your shoulders. A city on a hill. Long evenings. A library with a fire in it, and a voice that loved to argue, and a pale hand turning the pages of a book Carlisle had brought back from Padua. *But you can't mean it, my friend. You can't truly mean to live on the blood of beasts.* Laughter. Real laughter. Twenty years of it.

And the other thing, under it. The thing he'd never told anyone, not Esme, not Edward. How lonely he'd been, among them. How he'd stood at the back of rooms with his hands folded and not watched. How, on the night he'd finally asked to leave, Aro had taken both his hands in his own and held them a long time, reading, and then let go and said, very gently, *Go, then. Find what you're looking for. And come back and tell me about it.*

Carlisle had never gone back.

The hand lingered there a moment. Then it moved on.

He didn't know how long it lasted. It might have been a minute. It might have been ten. When the hand withdrew, it did it the way it had come in, carefully, closing the doors behind it, and Carlisle found that he was sitting in his own desk chair, and didn't remember sitting, and that the man was standing in front of him exactly where he'd been, with his hands back in his coat pockets.

The man looked, if anything, more tired than before.

"Thank you," he said.

Carlisle sat a moment longer, getting his bearings, the way you do after a dream.

"What did you see?" he said.

"Enough."

"All of it?"

"No," said the man. "I left the library. That one's yours." He looked past Carlisle at the painting. "He liked you."

"Yes."

"He'd still like you, I'd think. If he came here." The man was quiet for a moment. "That's not going to help you, if he does. You know that."

"I know."

Carlisle looked up at him. He'd been inside the minds of a great many people, in his own way, over three centuries; a doctor learns to read faces the way other men learn to read print. He'd never in all that time looked at a face and come away with so little.

"What are you?" he said.

The man thought about it. "Tired," he said.

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the one I've got tonight, Doctor." He took a hand out of his pocket and rubbed his eyes with his thumb and forefinger. "I came off a job twenty-two hours ago that I'd rather not think about, and I've got a phone call to beat to Port Angeles, and two of your guests rattling round in my pocket. Ask me again some other time. You might get a better one."

He went to the door. He stopped with his hand on it.

"I won't forget this," he said, without turning round. "What you just did. For what it's worth."

Then he went out.

Carlisle sat at his desk for a long while afterward, alone, looking at the painting.

### VII. Liaison

*The Cullen porch — 9:40 p.m.*

He came out the front door while Leah was standing on the porch in the snow, and stopped beside her, and looked out at the dark.

She'd come out to breathe. That was all. The house was too full. It was full of Esme sweeping glittering dust off her own floor into a mason jar with a dustpan and brush, very carefully, with her lips pressed together, and of Emmett standing over her saying *I can do that, Mom* and Esme saying *I know you can, sweetheart*, and not stopping. It was full of Bella holding Edward's hands, and Edward sitting on the piano bench with his eyes shut, and Jasper and Alice standing very close together by the glass, not talking. It was full of Seth, who had his arms round Layla in the kitchen and wouldn't let go, and Layla, who was letting him. It was full of Nessie upstairs saying *who was that man, who was that man* with her hands on everybody's faces, over and over.

So Leah had come out into the snow in her cut-offs and her tank top, which were all she ever needed, and stood at the top of the porch steps with her arms folded, and breathed the cold air through her nose until the leech-smell went out of it.

The man stood beside her. He'd put the watch cap back on.

"Lassie," he said.

"Don't."

"Fair enough."

They stood there. The snow came down, very lightly, through the light from the glass wall, and lay on the gravel.

"You said you'd want somebody to take you," Leah said. "To Sam's."

"I did."

"You meant me."

"I did," he said again.

She didn't look at him. "Why me? Jake's the Alpha. Seth's—" She stopped. "Seth likes everybody. Seth would introduce you to a bear."

"Seth's girlfriend took four of my wards down," said the man. "I'd like Seth's girlfriend somewhere I can keep an eye on her. And Clifford's got his hands full." He tipped his head toward the glass, toward the stairs inside, and Nessie. "And you're the only one of your lot who can walk into Sam Uley's kitchen and tell him no."

Leah's arms tightened across her chest.

She didn't ask him how he knew that. She knew how he knew. He'd had his hands in all their heads in the clearing, and Sam was in her head the way a splinter's in a thumb, and always had been, and always would be. He knew about Sam and her. He knew about Emily. He'd have seen it all in a second, without even looking, the way you'd see a stain on a white shirt.

She waited for him to say something about it.

He didn't. He looked out at the snow.

"And because you don't like me," he said. "Which means that when you tell him I'm not going to hurt anybody, he'll believe you."

"Are you going to hurt anybody?"

"No."

"Why do you want them?"

"Seven's a better number than three," said the man.

That was all he said. Leah turned and looked at him. She looked at him properly, the way she'd look at a leech's tracks in the mud, trying to read where it was going. She'd been in the clearing. She'd seen his face when Seth thought the word *wizard*, and she'd seen it when Jacob thought about Nessie, and tonight she'd seen it when Bella looked back at him. He did have a face that did things. She knew that now. She'd seen it.

Right now it was doing nothing. He was standing there in the snow in his scuffed coat and his watch cap, looking at the dark, with the face of a man waiting for a bus.

"Seven what?" she said. "Wolves?"

"People who turn into wolves," he said. "Whose dads and granddads did it. Whose great-great-granddads did it. I'd like to talk to them about that. And to the old men who tell the stories. And to Uley, if he'll let me. I'd like to watch somebody phase, if somebody'll let me. I'd like to ask them some questions. When they were born. How old they were the first time. Which side of the family it came down. That sort of thing." He shrugged, very slightly. "Nobody needs to do anything they don't want to."

"And then what?"

"And then I write it down."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

She didn't believe him. She didn't disbelieve him either. She stood in the snow and looked at the side of his face, and she couldn't find anything in it, and that was what bothered her.

"Conditions," she said.

"Go on."

"Nobody gets hurt. Not one of them. Not a scratch."

"Done."

"Emily stays out of it. Kim. Claire. Rachel. The imprints. You don't go near them, you don't ask them anything, you don't—" She stopped. "You stay out of their heads."

He was quiet for a second.

"Done," he said.

"And you stop calling me Lassie."

"No," said the man.

Leah breathed out through her teeth.

"Two out of three," he said. "It's a good deal. I'd take it."

She looked out at the snow. Somewhere down the slope, past the lawn, past the trees, the Sol Duc went by in the dark, and the hole in his fence was out there somewhere, two miles wide, where Layla had knelt in the mud with her nose bleeding and pulled his blood out of the stones.

"When?" she said.

"Not tomorrow. Day after, maybe. Or the one after that." He looked at his watch. "Right now I've got a phone call to beat."

"You don't know where he is."

"No," said the man. "But your little seer might." He turned back toward the door. "Let's go and ask her."

### VIII. Room Nine

*Port Angeles — 20 December, 12:04 a.m.*

Alice had given it to him in the end without being asked twice.

She'd stood by the glass with Jasper's hand on her shoulder and her fingertips pressed to her temples and looked for Lucan, and she hadn't been able to see him, because nobody had ever shown her his face. Then Harry had asked her to look for the call instead. For the phone, at midnight, not ringing. For whatever a man waiting by a phone does when it doesn't ring.

She'd found it in about a minute.

*A motel,* she'd said. *By the water. Port Angeles. There's a sign, blue and white, with a boat on it, and half the letters don't light up. Room nine. Ground floor. He's sitting on the end of the bed with the phone in his hand. Twelve-oh-two, twelve-oh-three. And then he gets up and goes for his car keys.*

So at four minutes past midnight, Harry was standing in the car park of the Harbor View Motor Inn on Marine Drive, in the snow, under a sign with a boat on it that said HAR OR V EW, looking at the door of room nine.

There was a light on behind the curtains. There was a rental car in front of the door, a dark blue Nissan, with snow on the windscreen and none on the bonnet. Somebody had run the engine in the last hour.

The door opened.

A man came out. Young-looking, slim, dark-haired, in a leather jacket, with a phone in one hand and car keys in the other, and red eyes, and he stopped dead in the doorway when he saw Harry standing in the snow ten feet away.

"Evening," said Harry. "Lucan, is it? Vasco sends his regards."

Lucan moved.

He was very fast. Harry had known he would be. He went sideways, for the car, the way a hare goes sideways, and he was halfway to the driver's door before Harry flicked two fingers.

*Click.*

The pebble bounced once on the snowy tarmac and rolled under the Nissan.

Harry sighed. He went and got down on one knee in the slush and reached under the car, and felt about, and found it, and stood up, and brushed the snow off his knee. Then he went and picked up the phone and the keys out of the snow, where they'd fallen, and put them in his pocket with the pebble, and went into room nine and had a look round.

There wasn't much. A holdall with clothes in it that had belonged to several different people. A road atlas. A wallet with three different driving licences in three different names, none of them Lucan. And on the bedside table, next to the motel's own phone, a notepad from the motel with a number written on it in a neat, old-fashioned hand.

It began with 0039. Then 0588.

Harry looked at it for a long while.

Italy. And the dialling code for Volterra.

He tore the sheet off the pad and folded it and put it in his inside pocket. Then he tore off the next sheet too, the blank one underneath, which would have the pressure of the pen on it, and put that in his pocket as well.

Then he took the pebble back out of his pocket, and stood in the middle of room nine with it in his palm, and looked at it, and went in.

---

He'd never read a pebble before. He'd wondered, in the Cullens' great room, whether he'd be able to. It turned out the mind was still there inside it, all of it, packed small and very tight, like a whole house folded up into a suitcase. It was harder to get into. It was a good deal easier to get round once he was in; there was nowhere for it to run.

He didn't go far. He didn't need to.

He found the call on the fourteenth of December. Five days ago. Lucan, on the end of this same bed, at four in the afternoon, after Vasco's call the night before, with the room's phone in his hand and the number in front of him that he'd been carrying round in his head for sixty years, the number every nomad who'd ever wanted something from Volterra had been told at some point, the number that nobody ever called.

A woman's voice had answered. A human voice, soft, Italian, very polite. Lucan had given a name. He'd said he had information. He'd been put on hold. Then a different voice, a man's, light and pleasant and terribly interested, and Lucan had told him.

Lucan had never heard about the witch. Vasco had kept that to himself. Harry went through it twice to be sure, and it wasn't there. Vasco had sat in that house nine nights breathing her in and never told his own partner on the phone. He'd wanted that one for himself.

But the rest of it. A coven on the Olympic Peninsula. The Cullens. Carlisle's lot. A child in the house who grew and ran and leapt like no child should. *An immortal child, master. I'm almost certain of it. I'd hoped I might be of service. I'd hoped there might be a place.*

And the light, pleasant voice at the other end had said, *How very interesting. Thank you, Lucan. We'll be in touch.*

Harry came out of the pebble and stood in the middle of the motel room for a while.

Five days.

He'd been five days too late. He'd been on the far side of the world doing something else that needed doing, and five days ago, while he was doing it, a nomad in a motel room in Port Angeles had picked up a phone and made a call, and the old man in the middle of the Solimena had said *how very interesting*.

"Well," said Harry, to room nine. "Bollocks."

He put the pebble in his pocket with the other two. He turned off the light. He shut the door behind him, and left the keys to the Nissan on the bonnet with the snow coming down on them, and turned on the spot in the car park, and went home.

---

The tent was cold. He'd been gone twenty-six days and he'd only been back for about four hours, and three of those had been somewhere else.

He lit the range. He put the kettle on. He took the Stasis Charm off the pumpkin pie on the table, where it had sat for nearly four weeks exactly as he'd left it, with one slice gone, and he cut himself another and ate it standing up while the kettle boiled. It was still very good pie.

Then he went through into the cold room and put three pebbles into three cells.

He didn't turn them back. Not yet. He'd turn them back when he had the time and the patience and the notebook out and a clear head, and none of those were on offer tonight. He put each one down in the middle of the stone floor of its own cell, small and grey and ordinary, and sealed the glass on each, and wrote three cards and slid them into the three brass frames.

*SPECIMEN 2. Vasco Teles.*
*SPECIMEN 3. Mireille (surname unknown).*
*SPECIMEN 4. "Lucan" (aliases various).*

He stood in front of them a moment.

Down at the end, in the fourth cell, the grey shapeless thing in the corner, the one that had sat there since a mine in the Pennines, moved over a little, as if to make room.

"Don't get ideas," said Harry, and went back out.

---

He made the tea. He sat in the armchair with it. He took down the mirror.

"Croaker."

The old man came up almost at once. He was in his office this time. Harry could see the window behind him, and the grey light of a London morning coming in through it, and a stack of files on the desk that hadn't been there before he went to Russia.

"Potter. You're back."

"I'm back, sir. Got in about four hours ago."

"Kestrel's in St. Mungo's," said Croaker. "Closed ward. She'll keep her hand."

Harry didn't say anything for a moment.

"Good," he said.

"Yes," said Croaker. He looked at Harry out of the glass. "You look terrible."

"Thank you, sir."

"Report."

Harry gave it to him. He gave it plainly and in order. The hole in the fence, and who'd made it, and how. The three who'd walked through it. The one he'd had to put down, and how, and that it had stayed down; Croaker's pale eyes flickered, very slightly, at that. The two in his pocket. The one in Port Angeles.

And then he told him about the call.

Croaker was silent for a long time.

"Volterra knows," he said at last.

"Volterra knows there's a coven here with something in its house the old man would very much like to see. Yes, sir. Five days ago."

"Will they come?"

"I'd think so. The nomads seem to think so. The doctor thinks so." Harry turned the mug in his hands. "Not tomorrow. Not next week. They'll want to look first. They'll want to think about it. But they'll come."

"*Here*," said Croaker slowly. "Not Italy. There."

"Yes, sir."

The old man said nothing for a while. Harry could see him thinking. You could always see Croaker thinking; nothing moved on his face, but the room behind him seemed to go very quiet.

"Then we may not need to go to Italy," Croaker said.

"That had occurred to me, sir."

"What is it they want to see?"

Harry drank some of his tea.

"Something the nomad said they'd done," he said. "Broke one of their laws. Don't know which. The nomad wasn't clear on it. I'll have more when I've had a proper go at the pebbles."

Croaker looked at him out of the glass. It went on for a while.

"Very well," he said.

"There's something else, sir. Something I've got for you." Harry reached into his coat, which was hanging over the arm of the chair, and took out a small glass phial, stoppered, with something silver turning slowly inside it like smoke in a bottle. He held it up to the mirror. "The doctor. Volterra. Everything he had. He was there twenty years, round about seventeen hundred. He let me have it."

"*Let* you."

"Asked him nicely, sir."

Croaker looked at the phial for a long time. Then he looked at Harry.

"Crate it," he said. "Today."

"It'll be with you by lunchtime."

"And the dogs?"

"Wolves, sir. I'm going to see the other pack. Day after tomorrow, most likely. Seven of them. One of the local three's going to take me in."

"Samples?"

"If they're offered."

"Potter."

"If they're offered, sir," said Harry. "That was the deal."

Croaker looked at him a moment longer.

"Get some sleep," he said, and the glass went dark.

Harry put it face-down on his knee.

He sat there for a while. The rain had started up again outside, on top of the snow, and it ticked on the canvas overhead. The range ticked. Through two doorways, down the length of the library, he could see the faint shimmer on the glass of the cells.

He'd lied to Croaker. It was the second time. He'd not much enjoyed either.

He drank his tea, and then he got up and put the phial in a crate, and sealed it with his thumb, and watched it go. Then he lay down on the camp bed in the library with his boots on and slept for nine hours, which was the longest he'd slept in a month.

### IX. Clean Room

*The Cullen house — 21 December, 3:15 p.m.*

*(Harry Potter was eleven miles away in a cold stone room, sitting on a stool in front of three cells, with a notebook on his knee.)*

The lights were Layla's idea, and Esme loved them, and Nessie had lost her mind.

They were ordinary Christmas lights. Three strings of little white bulbs from the hardware store in Forks, on long green wires, that Esme had bought for the tree. Layla had taken them out of their boxes and laid them on the floor of the great room and tapped each string, once, with the tip of her wand, and said the word her grandmother used to say over the porch in Port Allen every December. And the lights had come up off the floor, all three strings, still on their wires, and lifted, and drifted up into the air of the great room like strands of seaweed in a slow current, glowing, and wound themselves round the tree on their own, from the bottom up, round and round, without anybody touching them.

Nessie had stood in the middle of the floor with her head tipped all the way back and her mouth open.

"*Again,*" she'd said. It was one of the words she said out loud now, instead of with her hands. She'd learned it this week.

"Baby, they're already on the tree."

"*Again.*"

So Layla had taken them off the tree and done it again. And again. They were on the sixth time when Edward, who'd been sitting at the piano with the long crack across its lid, playing something soft and slow with his eyes half shut, stopped in the middle of a bar.

Layla looked over. Her wand was still up. The lights were halfway round the tree.

Edward was looking at her.

"There are two people on the drive," he said. "Walking up from the road. They just— arrived. Out of nothing." His eyes held hers. "Their minds are like yours, Layla. They've got the same edge on them. The same brightness."

The lights stopped where they were, in midair, round the tree, and hung there, glowing.

"Two of yours," said Edward quietly.

---

She'd known it would come. She'd been waiting for it since the porch swing, if she was honest. Since the moment she'd pulled her wand out of her hair and lit the lanterns for a boy from La Push and watched his face. Since the night after that, in the armchair by this same fire, when she'd told a room full of vampires that her people would wipe their minds and take her wand. She'd been waiting for it every day. She'd thought it would be the man in the black coat.

She hadn't thought it would be them.

"What are they thinking?" she said. Her voice came out very calm. She'd always had a good body for fear.

Edward listened. His face changed while he did it.

"*Devereaux*," he said. "Your name. Over and over. *Devereaux, L., Ilvermorny, class of 2000.* They have a file. A paper file, in a folder. The woman's carrying it. She's thinking about what's in it." He paused. "*Statute breach. Multiple counts. Disclosure to a shape-shifting indigenous population, La Push, Washington. To an unregistered coven of unknown beings, Forks, Washington. To a No-Maj law enforcement officer, Chief Charles Swan.*" He stopped. "Charlie. They know about Charlie."

Bella, on the stairs, made a sound.

"What else?"

"*Apprehend the subject. Assess. Obliviate where necessary. All parties.*" Edward's voice had gone very flat. "The man's thinking *all parties* means the whole reservation. He's thinking it's going to be a long week. He's thinking about his kid's Christmas pageant."

"Who are they?" said Carlisle.

"MACUSA," said Layla. "My government. Aurors. Police." She lowered her wand. The lights stayed hanging where they were round the tree. "They've come for me."

Seth was across the room before she'd finished the sentence, standing in front of her.

"No," he said.

"Seth."

"*No*. Nobody's taking you anywhere. Nobody's taking anybody's anything. If they come up those steps I'll—"

"You'll what, baby? Bite them?" She put her hand flat on his chest. His heart was going like a drum. "They've got wands. They'll put you on the floor before you're halfway through phasing. And then they'll do your mama, and Jacob's dad, and Charlie, and every one of these people in this house, if they can, and they'll take me home, and you won't remember why you're sad." She heard her own voice crack and made it stop. "I'm going out there."

"Layla—"

"I'm going out there, and I'm going to talk to them, and I'm going to tell them it was me and only me and nobody else needs touching. And maybe they'll listen." She looked past him at Carlisle. "Don't let anybody follow me. Please."

Carlisle looked at her for a long moment.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I don't think I can promise that."

---

She went out onto the porch.

The snow had stuck, a little, overnight; there was an inch of it on the lawn, grey under the low sky, and the gravel drive ran down through it into the trees in two dark ruts where Seth's truck had come up that morning. And coming up the drive, side by side, out of the trees, were two people in long dark coats.

A woman, mid-forties, with a grey streak in her black hair and a folder under her arm. A man, younger, broad, with a shaved head and a scarf. They walked the way Aurors walked, Layla thought, the way she'd seen them walk all her life in New York and New Orleans and once, terribly, at her grandmother's funeral: easy and unhurried and with their wand hands loose at their sides.

She knew the man.

She stood at the top of the porch steps and felt it go through her like ice water. Marcus Tolliver. Pukwudgie. Two years above her at Ilvermorny. He'd been a beater on the house team. He'd asked her to the Midwinter Ball when she was fifteen and she'd said no, and he'd been nice about it. He'd become an Auror. She'd seen the announcement in the *New York Ghost*.

He saw her at the same moment. He stopped walking.

"Layla?" he said.

"Hey, Marcus."

"Jesus, Layla." He looked at the house behind her, at the great glass wall with the lights hanging in the air on the other side of it, and the shapes standing very still behind the glass, and his face went from surprised to sorry to careful in about a second and a half. "What did you *do*?"

"Auror Tolliver," said the woman. She hadn't stopped walking. "Miss Devereaux. My name is Auror Ruth Ashby, Magical Congress of the United States of America, Department of Magical Law Enforcement. We'd like a word with you about some allegations that have been brought to our attention. I'd appreciate it if you'd come down off that porch and keep your hands where we can see them."

Layla came down one step.

"It was me," she said. "Just me. Nobody else in that house did anything. Nobody on the reservation did anything. They just— I told them. That's all. If you need someone, you've got me. You don't need to—"

"We'll decide what we need, Miss Devereaux," said Auror Ashby. She was twenty feet from the bottom of the steps. Fifteen. "Come down, please. All the way."

Layla came down another step.

Behind her, inside the house, she could hear nothing at all, which meant that nine people were standing very still.

Ten feet. Auror Ashby's wand had come up, its tip angled at the snow between them.

"Layla," said Marcus, very quietly. "Please."

And on the bottom step of the porch, between Layla and the two Aurors, with his back to her, the air cracked like a branch, and a man was standing in the snow.

---

He was in the suit again.

That was the first thing she saw, because she was looking at his back: the long black overcoat over the charcoal suit, and the collar up, and the black gloves. The watch cap and the scuffed working coat and the cut over his eye were gone as if they'd never been. He stood on the bottom step with his hands at his sides and his shoes in the snow, and there wasn't any snow on his shoulders.

"Afternoon," he said.

Both Aurors' wands came up and pointed at his chest.

"Who the hell are you?" said Marcus.

The man reached into his inside pocket, slow, the way he had in the clearing, and took out the little black wallet and flipped it open and held it up.

Layla couldn't see what they saw. She was behind him. But she saw their faces.

She saw Auror Ruth Ashby look at the badge and go very still. She saw Marcus Tolliver look at it and blink, and look again, and lower his wand an inch, and then another, as if he'd been told to and didn't remember being told. She saw them both look from the badge to the man's face and back.

"Sir," said Auror Ashby slowly. Her voice had changed. "I— we weren't told there was anyone from—"

"No," the man agreed. "You weren't."

Then he looked at them.

Layla had been watching for it. She'd been watching for it since the clearing. She'd seen him look at Seth like that, and at Bella, and she'd thought she knew what it was. Now she saw it from behind, and there was nothing to see: a man standing quite still in the snow, looking at two people. And the two people stopped. All at once. Both of them. Like someone had put a hand on a clock.

He held it for a second. Two. Three. She understood, watching, that he was reading them. Going through them, the way he'd gone through Seth in the clearing. Fast.

Then he did something else.

She felt it, rather than saw it. She was a curse-breaker; she'd felt a thousand spells go past her and she knew the weight of most of them. She'd seen Obliviators work, in New York, after an incident on the subway when she was nineteen. She knew what a Memory Charm looked like when it went in. It was a blunt thing. A good one took a piece out cleanly, like a coring knife through an apple. A bad one left a hole.

This was something else entirely.

It went into the two Aurors and she felt it *build*. It took the last hour out of them, she could feel that much, the shape of the hole it left. And then, in the hole, faster than she could follow, it put something back. A whole hour. A whole afternoon, complete, made out of nothing, and set into their heads like a pane of glass into a window frame with the putty already dry. She could feel the edges of it. They fit. They fit perfectly. She couldn't find a seam.

She'd never in her life seen anything like it.

The man lowered the badge.

Auror Ashby blinked. She looked at the house. She looked through the man on the step as if he weren't there, and through Layla behind him, and through the glass wall with the lights hanging in the air behind it, and Layla saw on her face exactly what she'd see if she were looking at an empty house at the end of a long gravel drive in the snow with nobody home.

"Well," said Auror Ashby. "That's a bust."

"Told you," said Marcus. He put his wand away. He sounded faintly annoyed, like a man at the end of a long afternoon. "Some No-Maj's idea of a prank. There's nobody here, Ruth. There's been nobody here in months, look at the snow."

"Mm." Ashby looked down at the folder under her arm as if she'd forgotten she had it. "Tip-off said a Devereaux. Ilvermorny. I've got an address in town."

"We went to the address in town. Empty."

"We did, didn't we." She frowned. "Yes. We did." She tucked the folder under her arm. "I'm writing it up as unfounded. Recommend closure."

"Can we go home? My kid's pageant's at six."

"Yes, Marcus, we can go home."

They turned round, both of them, together, and walked back down the drive the way they'd come, side by side, through the snow, talking about the traffic on the way to the Portkey point in Seattle. Neither of them looked back. At the bottom of the drive, where the trees closed in, they stopped, and turned on the spot, one after the other, and were gone. Two cracks, faint and flat in the cold air, like twigs.

Layla sat down on the porch steps.

She didn't decide to. Her legs did it. She sat down in the snow on the steps of the Cullen house with her wand in her lap and looked at the empty drive.

---

The man stood on the bottom step a moment longer. Then he reached into his coat again and took out something else: a small square of glass, a mirror, in a tortoiseshell frame, like an old-fashioned compact. He looked into it.

"It's me," he said.

Layla couldn't see the face in the glass. She could hear a voice, though. Very faint, very old, dry as paper. "Go on."

"Two of theirs from across the water just walked up the doctor's drive with a file on the girl, sir." The man's voice was perfectly ordinary. "Name, year, school. Every count. The pack, the coven, the Chief. Near enough word for word out of my last report."

The voice in the mirror didn't say anything for a while.

"Where are they now?"

"On their way home with a clean room each and nothing to write up. They'll recommend closure. They'll believe it." He paused. "But somebody gave them that file. There's a leak at MACUSA that needs tidying up. And I'd say somebody on our side of the water handed it to them in the first place. I'd start with whoever was at your dinner."

The silence went on a little longer.

"Understood," said the old voice.

"Sir."

He closed the compact and put it back in his pocket.

Behind Layla, the front door had opened. She didn't look round. She could feel them standing there in the doorway, all of them: Seth, and behind Seth, Carlisle and Edward and Jasper and the rest, still and silent. And Edward, she knew, would be listening as hard as he'd ever listened to anything, and hearing nothing. Nothing at all from the man on the step. A hole you could fall into.

The man turned round and looked down at her, sitting in the snow.

"You all right?" he said.

"Who was that?" said Layla.

"My mum," said the man.

She looked at him.

"They had a file," she said. "On me. With everything in it. Everything that happened."

"They did."

"From you." She heard her own voice. "From whoever you— whoever you told."

"From somebody who read something of mine they'd no business reading," he said. "Which is a problem for me, Miss Devereaux, and not for you. Not anymore."

"So your people—"

"Next question."

She stopped. She looked at the drive, at the two sets of footprints going down it in the snow, side by side, to the trees.

"I knew him," she said. "Marcus. We were at school."

"I know. He liked you." The man looked down the drive too. "He'll go home and watch his kid in the pageant and he'll never think about this again. He'll never think about you either, except as a name on a folder for an address that came up empty. That's the kindest thing I could do for him." He paused. "He was going to Obliviate Balto, you know. In about an hour. It was in his head. He didn't want to. He was going to."

Seth, in the doorway, made a sound.

"You could have let them take me," said Layla.

"Could've."

"You said one person knew. In the clearing. You said you'd keep it that way."

"I did."

"Why?"

He thought about it, standing there in the snow in his black coat with his hands in his pockets.

"Because I said I would," he said. "And because if they'd done Balto, he'd have forgotten you. And then who'd catch your coffee?"

She stared at him.

Then, to her own complete astonishment, she laughed. It came out of her all at once, sitting in the snow on the Cullens' steps, a short ragged helpless sound, and it hurt her chest, and her eyes stung, and she put her hand over her mouth.

The man looked faintly pleased with himself.

"Right," he said. He looked past her, at the open door, and the people standing in it. "Lassie's taking me to La Push tomorrow. Somebody tell her nine o'clock." He nodded to Carlisle. "Doctor." And then, to the little girl who'd pushed her way between Rosalie's knees to stare at him round the doorframe, with her bronze curls and her huge brown eyes: "Your lights are stuck, love. Better get the lady to finish them."

And he turned on the spot on the Cullens' bottom step, and was gone.

Inside the house, behind the glass, all three strings of Christmas lights were still hanging in the air round the tree, halfway up, exactly where they'd stopped.

"*Again,*" said Nessie, from the doorway.

### X. Seven

*La Push — 22 December, 9:00 a.m.*

He was waiting at the end of Sam's road when Leah pulled up in her mother's truck, standing in the rain by the mailbox with his hands in his pockets as if he'd been there an hour.

He'd changed again. No suit, no watch cap. He was wearing the clothes she'd heard about from Bella and Charlie, the English-fishing-guy clothes: a waxed jacket gone soft and shiny at the elbows, a flannel shirt, jeans, and a pair of brown leather work boots that had been properly scuffed now, all over, and stained dark at the toes with real mud. He had a notebook in one hand. Black, hardback. He looked like a man waiting to be shown round a farm.

She leaned across and opened the passenger door.

"Morning, Lassie," he said, and got in.

"Your boots are dirty."

"Took them for a walk in the river." He settled the notebook on his knee. "Your Chief's a hard man to fool."

She put the truck in gear and drove down the last two hundred yards of Sam's road without saying anything else.

---

She'd called Sam last night. She'd stood in her mother's kitchen with the phone cord wrapped round her fingers so tight they'd gone white and listened to it ring, and when he picked up and said *Leah?*, in that voice, the one she'd been hearing in her head for years and in the pack mind for most of one, she'd said it all in one breath before she could stop herself. *There's a man. You've heard about him from Jake. He wants to meet the pack. He wants to ask questions. He says nobody gets hurt. I believe him. I don't like him. Nine o'clock tomorrow.*

There had been a long silence on the other end of the line.

*Okay,* Sam had said. *Okay, Leah.*

He hadn't asked her anything else. That was Sam. That was the worst thing about Sam, always. He never asked you the thing you were waiting for him to ask.

So now she pulled up in front of Sam and Emily's little house at the end of the road, with the woods behind it and the sea beyond the woods, and the yard in front of it was full.

They were all there. All seven of them, standing in the wet grass in their cut-offs in the rain, steaming. Sam in the middle, broad and still, with his arms folded. Paul at his right shoulder, already shaking a little, already glaring. Jared, Embry, Quil. The two kids, Collin and Brady, thirteen and fourteen and enormous and trying very hard to look like they weren't scared. And on the porch, under the eave, out of the rain: Billy Black in his wheelchair with a blanket over his knees. Old Quil Ateara beside him, on a kitchen chair, small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, with a cane across his lap. And between them, standing, with her arms folded exactly the way Leah's were folded, her mother.

Sue looked at the man getting out of the passenger side of her truck the way she'd looked at Layla in the kitchen with the frozen fish. Waiting to see which way the wind would go.

Leah got out. She came round the front of the truck and stood beside him in the rain.

Nobody said anything.

The man looked at the seven of them in the yard. He took his time doing it. He looked at each of them in turn, the way Leah had watched Carlisle look at X-rays: one, then the next, then the next. Then he looked up at the porch, at the three old people there, and stopped on Billy, and nodded, once.

"Mr. Black," he said. "Mr. Ateara. Mrs. Clearwater." Then he looked back at the yard, at Sam. "Mr. Uley. Thanks for having me."

Sam didn't unfold his arms.

"Jake says you could've killed his whole pack," he said. "In the clearing. And you didn't."

"That's right."

"Jake says you killed one of them at the Cullens'. Turned it into dust. And two more into rocks."

"That's right."

"Leah says you want to ask us some questions."

"That's right too."

"Why?"

The man looked at him for a moment.

"Because nobody ever has," he said.

---

Paul phased at about twenty past nine.

Leah had known he would. Paul had been shaking since the truck pulled up. Paul had always been the one who went first and thought afterward, and the man had been sitting on the bottom step of Sam's porch for twenty minutes by then with his notebook on his knee, asking Jared quiet questions in that flat English voice—*how old were you, the first time? Was anyone with you? Did it hurt? How long did it take, roughly, from the first time you felt it come up to the moment you were on four legs?*—and writing the answers down in small, cramped handwriting, and not once looking up from the page. And Paul had stood there in the rain listening, getting hotter and hotter, until it came out of him the way it always did.

"You know what?" said Paul. "*Screw* this. You want to see it, English? Here—"

He went. Right there in the middle of Sam's yard, in the rain, in about a second and a half: a sound like canvas ripping, a spray of torn denim, and a huge dark silver-grey wolf standing in the wet grass where Paul had been, with his lips pulled back off his teeth and his head low, six feet from the porch steps, growling.

The two kids jumped. Quil said, "*Paul*—" Sam said, "Paul," in the Alpha voice, and Leah saw it hit Paul like a hand on the back of the neck.

The man on the step hadn't moved.

He'd looked up, when it happened. That was all. He'd looked up from his notebook at the wolf six feet in front of him, and watched it, the way you'd watch a kettle come to the boil.

Then he looked down at his watch.

"Thank you," he said. "That's very helpful." He wrote something. "Would you say that was quicker than usual, or about normal, when you're cross?"

The silver wolf stopped growling.

It stood there in the rain with its lips still pulled back, looking at the man on the step, and Leah watched it not know what to do. You could see it on a wolf's face, if you knew what to look for. Paul had phased to make a point. He'd been ready for the man to flinch, or to put up a hand, or to pin him to the grass like Jacob had told them he'd pinned Leah and Seth. He'd been ready for a fight. What he'd got was a man checking his watch.

"Quicker, I'd guess," said the man, writing. "Bit over a second. Thank you, Cujo."

Embry made a strangled noise and turned it into a cough. Jared put his hand over his face.

Paul, very slowly, turned round, walked to the edge of the yard, and lay down in the wet grass with his back to all of them.

"He'll be fine," said Sam. Something had happened round his mouth. "He does that."

"No harm done," said the man, and turned the page.

---

It went on for most of the morning.

Leah stood by her mother's truck the whole time with her arms folded and watched him do it. She'd been watching him for a month. She'd been pinned to the moss by him in the clearing and she'd seen him turn a leech into glitter in the Cullens' great room. She'd been waiting for him to show something. Anything. The way a leech showed hunger. The way Paul showed temper. The way Edward, when he heard something in someone's head that interested him, went very still and leaned in a quarter of an inch.

He didn't show anything.

He asked questions. One at a time, to each of them, in turn, in the same quiet voice. When were you born. When did you first phase. Who was with you. Was your father one. Your grandfather. Which side of the family. Do you know. Does anyone know. He wrote down the answers. He asked Embry, very carefully, about his father, and Embry's face shut like a door, and the man looked at it for a second and said, "That's all right," and wrote something short and moved on to Quil, and didn't come back to it. He asked the two kids whether they'd been in the same room as a vampire before they first phased, and Collin said *no, sir*, and Brady said *I don't think so*, and the man wrote both down exactly as they'd said them, *I don't think so* and all.

He asked if anybody minded giving him a hair. Just one. From the head, or from the wolf, whichever they liked. He said it the way you'd ask somebody to sign a form.

Sam gave him one. So did Jared, and Embry, and Quil, and after a long look at Sam, the two kids. Paul, from the edge of the yard, didn't move. The man didn't ask him twice. He put each hair into a little paper envelope from his jacket pocket and wrote a name on each one in pencil and put them all away.

He didn't ask for blood. He didn't ask anybody to phase. He didn't ask to look inside anybody's head. Leah watched for it. If he did it, she didn't see it.

Once, at about half past ten, the front door of the house opened, and Emily came out onto the porch.

She had a tray. She always had a tray. Leah felt it hit her low in the stomach the way it always did, the way it had for years now: Emily's face, the three long scars down the right side of it from the brow to the jaw, pulling the corner of her mouth down, and the left side of her face so lovely, and the tray of muffins, still steaming, and the smile.

"I thought," Emily said, "since everybody's out here in the rain—"

The man didn't look up.

He was writing. He kept writing. He kept his eyes on his notebook, on his knee, and he said, without raising his head, "That's very kind. No thank you, ma'am," in exactly the same voice he'd used for everything else.

Leah stared at him.

He'd been eating pie in the diner every morning for two weeks. He'd stood in a clearing in the rain with a leech floating at his hip and talked about pie, of all things, and sounded like he meant it. And he was sitting on Emily's steps in the rain, with Emily standing over him with a tray of blueberry muffins steaming in the cold, and he hadn't looked at her. He hadn't looked at her face. He hadn't once looked at her face since he'd got out of the truck.

*You stay out of their heads,* Leah had said, on the Cullens' porch, in the snow. *Emily. Kim. Claire. Rachel. You don't go near them.*

*Done,* he'd said.

Emily stood there a second longer, puzzled, and then smiled again and went and offered the tray to Sam instead. The man turned another page.

Leah looked away. She looked at the sea, past the trees, grey under the rain. She didn't know what she felt. She'd decided a month ago that she didn't like him, and she'd been very sure about it, and she was still sure. She'd keep being sure. But she'd been watching him all morning and he had not done one single thing she'd asked him not to do.

---

It was Old Quil who told the story, in the end.

The man had asked for it. Politely, at the very end, when he'd been through all seven of them and closed the notebook and opened it again to a fresh page. He'd looked up at the porch and said, *Mr. Ateara, I've been told you're the one who knows the old stories best. I'd be very grateful for one. Any one. The first one, if there is such a thing.*

And Old Quil had looked at him for a long time with his black eyes in his walnut face, and then he'd started talking.

He told it the way Leah had heard it all her life, at bonfires on First Beach, with the sparks going up. Taha Aki. The great chief of the Quileute, long ago, before the Cold Ones came. How the Quileute had been a small people, and a peaceful one, and how they'd had magic in their blood. How Taha Aki could leave his body. How he could send his spirit out of himself and over the land, and see with it, and come back. How all the warriors could, once. The spirit warriors. How a wicked man named Utlapa had stolen Taha Aki's body while his spirit was out of it, and Taha Aki had come back to find someone else wearing him. How he'd wandered the forest as a spirit with nowhere to go, until he found a great wolf, and asked to share it. And the wolf had let him in. And when Taha Aki came back into the village in the wolf's body, and took back what was his, he found he could change. Wolf to man. Man to wolf. And so could his sons.

The man wrote.

He wrote the whole thing down as Old Quil told it, not stopping, his pencil going steadily across the page. His face did nothing. Leah watched it do nothing.

Once, near the middle, when Old Quil got to the part about the spirit leaving the body, the pencil stopped. Just for a second. And then it went on.

Leah was standing close enough to see the page. She saw what he'd written, at the place where the pencil had stopped, in his small cramped hand.

*Spirit.*

He'd underlined it twice.

---

He'd just closed the notebook, and stood up off the step, and was thanking Billy Black with a little nod, when Leah heard the engine.

It was Seth. Coming down Sam's road far too fast in Jacob's Rabbit, bouncing in the potholes, and he slewed it to a stop in the mud at the end of the yard and was out of the driver's door before it had stopped rocking. He was in his cut-offs and nothing else. His face was grey.

"Leah," he said. "Leah, Jake sent me. He's with Nessie, he won't leave her. He said to tell you. Tell everyone."

Every wolf in the yard went still. Paul came up off the grass at the edge of the lawn in one movement and stood there in his wolf shape with his ears forward.

"Tell us what?" said Sam.

"Alice saw them." Seth was looking at Leah. His voice was shaking. "Twenty minutes ago. In the kitchen. She just stopped, in the middle of talking, and she went all— and then she said it. The Volturi. They're coming. All of them. The brothers, and the wives, and the whole guard, Edward says she saw all of them, she saw them all coming across a field toward the house in the snow. For Nessie. They've decided." He swallowed. "She said *when the snow sticks*."

Nobody said anything.

The rain came down on Sam's yard, on the seven of them standing in the wet grass, on the old people on the porch, on Emily in the doorway with her tray, on Seth by the car.

Leah looked at the man.

He was standing at the bottom of Sam's porch steps with the notebook in one hand. He'd stopped where he was when Seth came out of the car, halfway through turning away from Billy, and he hadn't moved since. He was looking at Seth.

Then he tucked the pencil into the spine of the notebook, and closed it, and put it in the inside pocket of his waxed jacket.

"Right," he said. He looked up at the porch. "Mr. Black. When does the snow stick, round here?"

Billy looked at him for a long moment.

"Middle of January," he said. "Usually."

"About a month, then," said the man.

He said it in the same flat, quiet, polite voice he'd used all morning. *How old were you the first time. Thank you, that's very helpful. No thank you, ma'am.* Nothing in it at all.

But Leah had been watching him for a month now. She'd been watching him all morning. And she saw it, just for a second, before it went away: something round his eyes that hadn't been there before, that she had no word for.

He looked like a man who'd been standing at a bus stop in the rain for a very long time, and had just seen his bus come round the corner.

---

*End of Chapter Two.*
