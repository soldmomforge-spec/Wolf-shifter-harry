# Snare

## Chapter One: Words Instead of Teeth

---

### I. Twenty-Two Days

*Forks Thriftway — twenty-two days after Renesmee Cullen was born*

Sue Clearwater's grocery lists had a way of yelling at you.

Seth stood in the baking aisle of the Thriftway with the list held flat against the handle of his cart so the rain dripping off his hair wouldn't smear it. His mom wrote in capitals when she meant it, and she'd meant most of this one.

*EGGS — THREE DOZEN. SETH. THREE.*
*Butter (the real kind)*
*Flour — the GOOD flour, blue bag*
*Lemons x6*
*Potatoes, big bag*
*Dish soap*
*Whatever you want that isn't junk. I MEAN IT.*

Under that, in smaller letters, like she'd added it after a second cup of coffee: *Charlie likes the rye bread. Get the rye bread.*

He'd already put four dozen eggs in the cart, because three dozen was a suggestion and he was eighteen and ate like a wood chipper. The good flour was on the bottom shelf, of course, behind six bags of the bad flour, because the universe had a sense of humor. He crouched and dug for it.

The store smelled like what it was: floor wax, wet cardboard, the bakery case's sugar, old coffee from the machine by the registers, and under it all the cold mineral smell of rain coming in every time the automatic doors sighed open. He'd gotten used to sorting smells without thinking about it. Two aisles over, somebody was buying cat food and had a cat who'd sprayed their jacket. Mrs. Stanley from the post office was by the deli, wearing the same lilac perfume she'd worn since Seth was in kindergarten. A kid near the front had a skinned knee under a fresh Band-Aid, a coppery little spot of blood that Seth registered and dismissed.

Three weeks ago, a smell like that would have made him think of the Cullens' house and Bella on the couch, gray and starving and bruised from the inside out. He'd stood guard on that house for days. He'd been there when the screaming started and when it stopped, and he'd been there when Jacob walked out onto the porch afterward with a look on his face like somebody had picked up the planet and set it down in a different place.

Now Bella was the fastest thing in the woods, her eyes were the red of a stop sign easing toward amber, and there was a baby in that house who already looked like she was a year old and touched your face to show you pictures in your own head.

Weird month.

Seth found the blue bag, hefted it into the cart, and stood up.

The woman at the end of the aisle had nine canisters of Morton salt in her basket.

He noticed the salt first because it was so much salt. The blue cardboard cylinders with the girl and the umbrella, stacked in the basket like ammunition. Then the candles on top of them: a dozen plain white tapers, the cheap kind from the bottom shelf in the household aisle, and two boxes of kitchen matches. Then a jar of cloves. Then, in her other hand, a canister she'd just taken down from the top shelf, which was a stretch for her. She was maybe five-four in her boots.

She was looking at the canister label with the expression of someone checking the expiration date on a parachute.

Rain jacket, dark green, unzipped. A man's flannel shirt under it, blue and black, the sleeves rolled to the elbow. Jeans with the knees worn pale. Dark hair, a lot of it, curling in the damp and wound up on the back of her head and held there with what looked like a chopstick, except the chopstick was darker than any chopstick he'd ever seen and had a slight crook to it, like a twig. Brown skin, a scatter of freckles over the bridge of her nose. Along the inside of her left forearm, from wrist almost to elbow, ran a scar Seth didn't have a word for: thin and white and branching, like somebody had pressed a frost pattern into her skin and it had stayed.

She reached up for a tenth canister.

The basket handle slipped down her arm and the whole thing tipped, and the canister on top went over the edge.

Seth didn't decide to move. He was there, that was all; he was three steps closer than he'd been and his hand was under the canister four inches above the linoleum, and it landed in his palm with a soft cardboard *pap*.

He straightened up with it, a little embarrassed, ready to say something easy and dumb like *whoa, close one*, and she turned her head and looked at him.

Her eyes were dark brown, with a thin ring of something lighter around the pupils, almost gold, almost amber. They narrowed a fraction at how fast he'd moved.

That was the last ordinary thing Seth noticed for a while.

He'd seen this happen. That was the stupid part. He'd *seen* it, from the inside, more times than he'd ever wanted to. That was what the pack was, when you were phased: a room with no walls where everybody's head was open, and when Sam had looked at Emily, when Jared had looked at Kim, when Paul had looked at Rachel, when Quil had looked at a two-year-old girl on a beach and gone soft and stupid and protective all at once, every one of them had carried that moment around afterward like a stone in a pocket, and the rest of the pack had been made to feel the weight of it over and over. Three weeks ago Jacob had looked at a newborn baby across a room and Seth had felt it come down the pack bond like a lightning strike, the whole world going *sideways*, all of Jacob's anger and grief knocked loose in one second and replaced with one fact.

He'd thought he knew what it was.

It turned out that watching somebody else fall down a staircase taught you nothing about falling down one.

The Thriftway stayed exactly where it was. Seth was fairly sure of that, later: the humming freezers, the flickering fluorescent tube over aisle seven, Mrs. Stanley's lilac perfume. Everything else moved. All the things that had been holding him in place, his mom and Leah and the rez and the pack and the Cullens' porch and the beach and the plain stubborn fact of being Seth Clearwater, stayed attached and went quiet, further away, like voices in another room, and in the space they left something new ran straight through the middle of him and tied off on her.

It felt like a tide. A direction. He'd been facing a hundred ways at once his whole life. Now he faced one.

He thought, very clearly: *oh*.

Then, even more clearly: *oh no, Leah's gonna kill me*.

"Uh," the woman said. "Thanks."

Her voice was low and a little hoarse, like she'd had a cold recently or didn't talk much. Flat, American vowels from somewhere a long way from here. Not West Coast.

Seth realized he was still holding the salt.

"Yeah," he said. "Sure. No problem. Hi."

One of her eyebrows went up.

"Hi," she said.

"Sorry. I'm—" He held the salt out. She didn't take it right away; she looked at his hand, and then at his face, and then at his hand again, and he understood suddenly how he must look to a stranger. Six and a half feet of wet Quileute kid in cutoff sweatpants and a T-shirt in October, no jacket, staring at her like she'd just come down from the ceiling on a beam of light.

He made himself look at the salt instead. That helped a little.

"That's a lot of salt," he said.

She took the canister. Her fingers brushed his, and he watched her register it: the heat. He ran at a hundred and eight degrees, give or take, and people noticed if they touched him. Her eyes flicked up to his face again, and a line appeared between her eyebrows.

"You're burning up," she said.

"I run hot."

"That's not a small amount of hot."

"Big family thing," Seth said, which was true. "We're all like this. It's fine. I'm not sick. I promise I'm not, like, contagious."

She studied him a second longer, then let it go. Or let it go the way some people did, which was to put it on a shelf in the back of their head where they could find it later. He knew that look. Charlie Swan had perfected it.

"Slugs," she said.

"What?"

She raised the salt canister an inch. "You said it's a lot of salt. Slugs. I rented a house with a garden, and the garden is apparently a slug resort. They're on the porch. They're on the *door*. One of them was on the inside of my bathroom window this morning, looking at me." She paused. "I'm from Minnesota. We have slugs. We don't have slugs that look like they could take your lunch money."

Seth laughed. It came out louder than he meant it to, and the woman's mouth twitched at one corner like she hadn't expected to be funny and was annoyed that she was.

"Banana slugs," he said. "They're everywhere out here. They're kind of the state animal. Unofficially."

"They're disgusting."

"They're yellow."

"That makes it worse." She settled the canister in the basket with the others. "Okay. Thank you. For the—" She made a little catching gesture. "That was fast."

"Football," Seth said. He had never played football in his life.

"Uh huh."

"I should, um." He pointed vaguely at his cart, which was full of eggs. "My mom sent me. She's making dinner for her boyfriend. Not that you needed to know that. He's the police chief. Also not something you needed to know." He shut his mouth. Opened it again. "I'm Seth."

She looked at him for a long second, like he was a sign in a language she half read.

"Layla," she said.

"Layla," Seth said, and then heard himself saying it, and felt his face go hot in a way that had nothing to do with his body temperature.

"Okay, Seth." She hitched the basket handle back up her arm, and the salt shifted with a sound like gravel. "Thanks again."

She went around the end of the aisle.

He stood there.

He stood there for what was probably only ten seconds but felt longer, listening to her boots squeak away across the linoleum toward the household aisle, then the produce, then the registers. He could pick her steps out of everyone else's now. That was new. He could pick her *smell* out: rain and wool and woodsmoke and something under it like a struck match, like ozone before a lightning storm, a dry electric edge he'd never smelled on a person before. He catalogued that one and didn't know where to file it.

Then he looked down at his hand and realized he was holding a canister of salt.

The tenth one. The one she'd been reaching for when the basket tipped. He'd caught the falling one and handed it back, and at some point during the conversation, without noticing, he'd taken the one off the top shelf too, the way you'd pick up something somebody dropped, and he'd been holding it ever since like an idiot.

"Oh, come on," Seth told it.

He left the cart. He went past the end of the aisle in four long strides and down past the bakery case, past the deli, past Mrs. Stanley, who said, "Seth, honey, slow down, it's wet," and out through the automatic doors into the rain.

She was halfway across the lot, loading paper bags into the passenger seat of an old silver Subaru with Minnesota plates and a dent in the rear quarter panel. The rain had gotten heavier, the way it did here, without any drama, just more of it. It ran in the gutters of the lot and drummed on the roofs of the cars and pocked the puddles.

"Layla!"

She turned. Her hand went, fast and automatic, to the back of her head, to the chopstick in her hair. Then she saw who it was and the hand stopped and came down slowly.

Seth noticed that. He would think about it a lot later.

He jogged up and held out the salt.

"You forgot one," he said. "I mean, I took one. I didn't mean to take it. I just had it."

Layla looked at the canister. Then at him, standing in the rain in a T-shirt in October with water streaming off his hair, holding out a forty-nine-cent canister of salt like it was something important.

She laughed.

The laugh was short and startled, like it had gotten out before she could stop it. But her face changed when she did it, and Seth decided right there in the Thriftway parking lot that he would very much like to see that happen again, as many times as possible, for the rest of his life.

"You're going to be a problem," she said. "Aren't you."

"I'm really not," he said. "I'm like the least problem person you will ever meet. Ask anybody."

She took the salt. Her fingers didn't brush his this time. She was careful about it, he noticed.

"You're going to get pneumonia," she said.

"I don't get sick."

"Everyone gets sick."

"Not me."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she shook her head and got into the Subaru, and he stepped back so she could close the door, and she started it and pulled out of the space and drove off toward the highway, toward the east end of town, toward the bridge over the Calawah.

He stood in the rain and watched the taillights until they turned.

Then he went back inside, because he'd left four dozen eggs in the baking aisle and his mom would actually kill him.

That night he phased at the tree line behind his mom's house a little after ten, to take the late patrol along the treaty line, and the second he came up on four legs he knew he wasn't alone in his head.

*Oh, for God's sake,* Leah said.

She was eight miles north, running the ridge above the Sol Duc, and her voice hit the inside of his skull like a slammed door. He felt her stumble, felt her paws skid in wet needles. Then he felt her see it. All of it. It was right there at the front of his mind, where he couldn't have hidden it if he'd tried: aisle seven, the salt, the dark eyes with the gold ring, the laugh in the parking lot.

*Leah—*

*No. No. Don't you dare.* She came to a dead stop on the ridge. He could feel her breathing, the sharp pump of it, the hot rush of air through her nose. *You went to the store for eggs.*

*I know.*

*You went to the store. For eggs.*

*I got the eggs.*

*Seth.*

There was a third weight, then, warm and amused and very tired, as Jacob phased somewhere in the woods behind the Cullen house. Seth felt him come up on four legs, felt the russet bulk of him shake out his fur, felt him look, the way you'd turn your head toward a noise. Then he felt Jacob see it too.

*Whoa,* Jacob said. *Seth. Dude.*

*I know,* Seth said.

*At the Thriftway?*

*I KNOW.*

Something came down the bond from Jacob that felt like a hand on the back of his neck, a rough brotherly shake. *That's good, man. That's really good. Who is she?*

*Her name's Layla. She just moved here. She's from Minnesota. She's got slugs.*

*She's got what?*

*Slugs. In her garden. It's—it doesn't matter.*

*Did she get a say?* Leah said.

Both of them went quiet.

Leah's thoughts were a closed fist. She'd started running again, hard, flat-out along the ridge with the rain in her face, and the speed of it came through the bond, the burn in her legs, the sting of wet branches. Under it, the way it always was with Leah, the old thing. The thing nobody in either pack ever said out loud anymore. Sam's face, years back, on the day he looked at Emily. Leah's own face in a mirror afterward. Emily's scarred cheek. The wedding invitation Leah had stood at the back of. The way it had felt to be the one somebody's whole life had been pointed at, until it suddenly wasn't, because some ancient wolf instinct had decided for him and nobody had asked her what she thought about it.

*Did she get a say,* Leah said again, quieter. *Did you? Did anybody ever ask any of you if you wanted your brain rewired at the grocery store?*

*Leah,* Jacob said.

*I'm asking a question, Jake.*

Seth stood in the trees behind his mom's house with the rain running off his sandy fur and didn't know what to say. He could feel the thing in his chest, the tide, the new direction, pulling east toward the bridge over the Calawah, toward an old gray house he hadn't even seen yet. It felt like the most obvious thing in the world.

He also knew, with the part of him that had been his sister's little brother for eighteen years, that it wouldn't sound that way to her. Nothing would.

*I don't know,* he said honestly. *I don't know if she gets a say. I just know I'm not gonna do anything she doesn't want. I'll be her friend. I'll be whatever. If she wants me to go away, I'll—* He couldn't finish that one. His whole body balked at the thought. He made himself finish it anyway. *I'll go away.*

*No, you won't,* Leah said. *None of you ever do.*

She cut east and went quiet, the way she could when she really wanted to, locking her thoughts down to the rhythm of running and the smell of the rain.

Jacob sighed through the bond. *Give her time.*

*She's had like five years,* Seth said.

*Yeah.* Jacob's thoughts moved toward the Cullen house, toward a small warm weight asleep upstairs with bronze curls, and softened without him meaning to. *Give her more.*

### II. The Things You Notice

*Forks Diner — twenty-six days after*

The pie had been his mom's idea.

"You don't show up empty-handed," Sue had said, in the kitchen, with her back to him and her shoulders shaking just slightly, which meant she was laughing at him and trying not to. "Not to a woman who just moved to town. Take her a pie. Tell her it's from the Clearwaters. Welcome to the neighborhood. That's all. Do not say anything weird."

"I won't say anything weird."

"You'll say something weird."

He'd said something weird. He'd stood on the porch of the gray house past the Calawah bridge, the one with the crooked mailbox, holding a blackberry pie in a glass dish with tinfoil over the top, and when Layla opened the door he'd said, "My mom made you a pie because of the slugs."

There had been a line of salt across the threshold. He'd noticed it when he looked down to avoid looking at her face too much. It was poured very neatly, a white line maybe a half inch wide, unbroken, running the full width of the doorframe. There was another one on the windowsill to his left.

Layla had looked at the pie, and then at him. "Because of the slugs."

"Like a welcome-to-town pie. And sorry-about-the-slugs. It's a two-in-one."

She'd taken the pie. She hadn't asked him in. But on the fourth day, when he came by to get the dish back, she'd been on the porch in a heavy sweater with a mug in her hands, watching the rain, and she'd said, "Is there anywhere in this town to get coffee that doesn't taste like it was filtered through a sock?"

So now they were in the diner.

The Forks Diner was the Forks Diner. Vinyl booths with duct tape on the tears, a pie case by the register that rotated through the same five pies, a bell over the door, windows fogged at the corners. It smelled like bacon and coffee and the bleach they used on the tables. Half the town ate here at least twice a week. Seth had already said hi to four people on the way to the booth, and two of them had looked at Layla with frank small-town interest that made her shoulders creep up toward her ears.

"Sorry," he said, as they sat down. "Everybody knows everybody."

"I noticed."

"It's like three thousand people. Somebody new is a big deal."

"I noticed that too." She wrapped both hands around the coffee mug the waitress set down and looked at it suspiciously, then sipped it. Her face did something complicated. "Okay. That's not terrible."

"Told you."

"You said it was 'the best coffee on the peninsula.'"

"I've only had coffee on the peninsula."

That got the corner of her mouth to move again. He was keeping a count.

He ordered two bacon cheeseburgers and a basket of fries and a chocolate shake. Layla ordered a grilled cheese and a cup of tomato soup and watched him eat the first burger in about five bites with an expression that was half fascination and half horror.

"Do you have a tapeworm?" she asked.

"Growth spurt."

"You're like seven feet tall."

"Six six," Seth said. "And a half. Still growing, apparently."

"Jesus." She tore a corner off her grilled cheese and dipped it. "Your family all like this? The hot thing and the tall thing and the—" She waved a hand at the burger basket, which was already empty.

"Kind of. My sister's tall. My mom's normal. My dad was—" He stopped.

She looked up.

He hadn't meant to go there. It had been a year and a half and it still caught him sideways sometimes, at weird moments, the way a step in the dark catches you. His dad in his chair. His dad's laugh, which had been big and sudden and too loud for any room he was in. His dad's heart giving out in the kitchen on an ordinary afternoon while Leah stood over him and screamed, and Seth came through the back door and phased for the first time in the yard because he couldn't hold it in.

"He passed," Seth said. "Last year. Heart attack."

"I'm sorry."

"Yeah. Thanks." He took a breath and let it out. "His name was Harry. He was a big guy. He'd have liked you. He liked anybody who'd argue with him."

"I don't argue."

"You argued with me about slugs for like ten minutes."

"That was a discussion."

He laughed, and she looked at him across the table for a second, really looked, without the shelf-in-the-back-of-her-head expression, and something in her face eased a little.

"My partner died," she said. "Back in the spring."

He went still. "Partner like—"

"Work partner." She turned the mug a quarter turn on the table. "I used to work overseas. Salvage, kind of. Old buildings. Old—sites. You go in places nobody's been in a long time and you figure out what's still dangerous and you get it out, or you make it safe. We were in Peru. It went bad." Another quarter turn. "His name was Teo. He was the best I ever worked with. And I was the one who was supposed to see it coming, and I didn't."

Her left hand had come to rest on the table. The white branching scar on her forearm was right there in the light from the window, and Seth looked at it and then looked away, because he figured she'd had enough people look at it.

"So you came here," he said.

"So I came here." She huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "I wanted somewhere nobody would know me. Somewhere quiet. Somewhere nobody was going to come knock on my door and ask me to go back to work. I looked at a map and went, where's the most nowhere place in the country that still has a grocery store?"

"And you found Forks."

"I found Forks."

"Well," Seth said. "We do have a grocery store."

"It's very well stocked with salt."

"Somebody bought most of it."

The corner of her mouth went up and stayed up this time. That was four.

He walked her back to her Subaru afterward. It was raining. It was always raining. He stood a respectful distance away while she unlocked it, and when she turned to say goodbye he said, before he could think better of it, "I know I'm kind of a lot. I just want you to know—like, I'm not trying to be weird. I like hanging out with you. If you just want somebody to show you around, that's great. If you want somebody to get coffee with, that's great. Friends is great. I mean it."

Layla studied him. The rain beaded on the shoulders of her jacket.

"Friends," she said.

"Friends is really great," Seth said.

"You mean that."

"Yeah."

"Huh," Layla said, and got into the car. Then she rolled the window down an inch. "Seth."

"Yeah?"

"Get a jacket. You're making me cold just looking at you."

*Hoh Rain Forest — thirty-four days after*

The Hall of Mosses trail was a little under a mile, a loop that wound through bigleaf maples so thick with moss they looked like they were wearing fur coats. Club moss hung off the branches in long green beards. Ferns grew out of the moss. Smaller trees grew out of the fallen trunks of bigger ones, nurse logs, Seth told her, the old ones going soft and feeding the new ones, rows of hemlocks standing on stilts of roots where the log they'd grown on had rotted away out from under them.

It was the greenest place he'd ever been. He'd been coming here since he was little and he'd stopped seeing it years ago. Watching Layla see it made him see it again.

"It's like being underwater," she said.

They'd left the main loop a while ago. Seth knew a side path down to the river that wasn't on the trail maps, and she'd said yes before he finished asking, which was a good sign. It was a Tuesday afternoon, too late in the season for tourists, and the light came down through the canopy gray-green and dim and soft, and the only sounds were dripping water and the river somewhere ahead.

She'd been quiet for most of the hike. Not uncomfortable quiet. Just quiet, like the place had turned something down in her that was usually up too loud. Once she'd stopped and put her hand flat against the trunk of a Sitka spruce and closed her eyes, just for a second, like she was listening for something through the bark. He'd stood and waited and hadn't asked.

He'd been getting better at not asking. There were a lot of things he wasn't asking about.

Like the way the salt lines on her windowsills were always perfect, even after rain, even though the rain blew sideways under the porch roof. Like the way her house had no TV antenna, no satellite dish, and only one phone, an ancient rotary in the kitchen, and the one time he'd been inside, standing in the front hall while she got her boots, the overhead light had flickered every time she walked under it. Like the way she knew when it was going to rain. She didn't guess. She *knew*, and she was always right, down to the minute. Like the chopstick, which she wore every single day, and which he'd never once seen her take out of her hair.

Like the fact that she'd come out of the rain at the trailhead an hour ago completely dry.

He'd smelled the rain on her jacket when she got out of the car. Then she'd turned her back to him to get her pack out of the trunk, and something had happened. The struck-match smell, sharp and electric for half a second. When she turned around her hair and jacket were dry. Not a drop. He'd stood there with water running down the back of his own neck and watched it start beading on her again, fresh, one drop at a time.

He hadn't said anything. He figured maybe he'd imagined it. He figured, mostly, that he was hardly in a position to start making a list of weird things about other people.

The side path dropped down a bank and came out on a gravel bar by the river. The Hoh ran milky blue-gray here, glacier water down off Mount Olympus, fast and loud and cold enough to stop your heart. A fallen log spanned the side channel to a second gravel bar, maybe fifteen feet across, the bark long gone, the wood slick and pale.

"We can cross on that," Seth said. "There's a cool spot on the other side. There's like a little beach. Sometimes there's elk."

Layla eyed the log. "That's wet."

"Everything's wet."

"That's *extra* wet."

"I'll go first," he said, and went across in five easy steps, arms out for balance only because a person would have put their arms out for balance. He turned around on the far side. "See? Easy."

She gave him a look and stepped up onto the log.

She was good at it. She went carefully and steadily, eyes down, feet flat. She was two thirds of the way across when a chunk of rotten wood under her right boot gave way.

He saw it go. He saw her weight shift wrong, her arms come up, her left foot slip sideways on the slick wood, and he saw the channel underneath, four feet deep and fast and full of rocks, and the next thing he knew he was on the log, he had her by both arms, and they were standing in the middle of it, swaying a little, with a piece of rotten wood bobbing away downstream.

He'd been on the gravel bar. He'd been on the gravel bar, eight or ten feet from the end of the log. And now he was here.

Layla was breathing hard. Her hands had closed in the front of his T-shirt.

She looked at the gravel bar. Then at his feet, planted on the log. Then up at his face.

"Football," Seth said weakly.

She didn't say anything.

She didn't say anything all the way back across the log, or while he walked her up the bank, or for the first ten minutes back up the trail. He could hear her heart. It took a long time to come down.

Then, by a mossy maple near the main loop, she stopped walking.

"Seth."

He stopped too.

"That's twice," she said. "In the store, and now. And there's the temperature thing. And you never wear a coat. And you eat like you're feeding three people." She was looking at him with the line between her eyebrows. "And you knew which part of the log was going to go before I did. You moved before it broke."

"I didn't—"

"You did. I watched you." She tilted her head. "I'm not going to ask."

"Okay," Seth said.

"I'm just letting you know I noticed."

He looked at her, standing there in the green light with her dry hair and her chopstick, and he thought about the struck-match smell at the trailhead, and the salt, and the light that flickered when she walked under it.

"Okay," he said. "I noticed some stuff too."

Something moved in her face, quick and wary, and was gone.

"Okay," she said.

They walked back to the car without talking. Seth figured it was a good silence, as silences went. It was like two people standing on either side of a door, each with a hand on the knob, both deciding not to open it yet.

*Forks Community Hospital — forty-five days after*

It was the box's fault.

The box had come four days ago, by owl, which in the rain on the Olympic Peninsula was its own kind of miracle; the bird had arrived at her kitchen window looking like a drowned feather duster and so offended about it that she'd had to give it half a chicken breast and a warming charm before it would let her near the package. Inside the package, in three layers of dragonhide wrapping and a lead-lined case, was a small wooden box about the size of a hymnal, carved all over with stylized jaguars, and a letter from Gringotts' Lima office.

*Ms. Thorne — We are aware you are on extended leave. We would not ask if we had another option. The client is particular. The fee reflects this. Please advise.*

She'd sat with the letter for two days. Then she'd gotten out her tools.

It was a good box. Old. Pre-Columbian, Chachapoya probably, though somebody had reworked the hinges in the sixteen hundreds. Three nested curses, one of which was a dead-man's lock keyed to blood, one of which was a nasty little rot-hex designed to eat through the hands of anybody who tried to pick the dead-man's lock, and one of which was hiding underneath the other two pretending to be part of the wood grain. The third one was the one that got her. It was very good work.

She'd broken the first two clean. She'd found the third one and been halfway through unraveling it when it bit.

Not bad. Not like Peru. Just a little lash of something sharp and cold across the meat of her left palm, opening it from the base of her thumb to the edge of her hand. She'd sealed the box, put it back in the lead case, and tried every healing charm she knew on her hand, and every one of them slid right off the wound like water off wax. Curse-cuts were like that. The magic that had made them sat in the edges and repelled other magic for a day or two until it faded. If it had been a burn she'd have used dittany. It wasn't a burn, and she was bleeding through her third dish towel, and the nearest wizarding healer she trusted was in Seattle, a four-hour drive and a ferry, and she wasn't going to Apparate with one hand while bleeding, because she'd seen what that looked like.

So she'd driven herself to Forks Community Hospital with her hand wrapped in a dish towel and told the woman at the front desk she'd cut it on a box cutter.

The ER was small. A row of curtained bays, a nurse's station, a vending machine that hummed. An old man in the next bay over was coughing. It smelled of disinfectant and burnt coffee.

The doctor who came through the curtain was the most beautiful man she'd ever seen in her life.

That was the first thing. She noticed it the way you'd notice a car alarm. Blond, maybe thirty, with a face like something off a cathedral, pale as paper. He smiled at her and said, "Ms. Thorne? I'm Dr. Cullen. Let's see what you've done to yourself."

He unwrapped the towel.

She was watching his face when the smell of the blood reached him, because she was always watching people's faces, it was the job, it had kept her alive in tombs where the only warning you got was the change in somebody's expression. And something happened in his face. It was tiny, so tiny she almost missed it. A stillness. For a fraction of a second every muscle in that perfect face simply stopped, the way a deer stops in a field.

Then it was gone, and he was turning her hand gently in his, examining the cut.

His hands were cold.

*Cold*. Cold like river stones, cold like the walls of the tomb in Cusco, and smooth and hard in a way that didn't feel like skin.

Layla kept her face perfectly still. With the hand he wasn't holding, the one in her lap under the edge of the gurney sheet, she curled her fingers in the old shape Teo had taught her, the one you could do without a wand in a pinch if you'd practiced it a thousand times, and she had. It was a curse-breaker's charm. You used it in places where you didn't want to touch anything. It just *read*. It told you if a thing was alive and what kind of alive.

She reached out with it and touched Dr. Cullen very lightly.

The charm came back wrong.

There was something there. A great deal of something, huge and dense and very, very old, a pressure like standing next to a boulder in the dark. And there was no pulse. No heartbeat. No breath moving in his chest except when he spoke. The charm came back to her the way it came back from statues and corpses and the stone guardians of temples, and at the same time it came back *full*, the way it never had from any statue.

"This is a clean cut," Dr. Cullen said. "Very clean. Box cutter, you said?"

"Yes."

"Mm." He looked at the edges of the wound, where a faint gray shine still clung that only someone with the Sight would see, and maybe she imagined it, but he looked at that gray shine a beat longer than he looked at anything else. "It'll need stitches. Eight or nine. I'll numb it first."

"Okay."

His eyes were gold.

That was the second thing. She'd thought they were hazel when he walked in. They weren't. They were gold, butterscotch gold, an impossible color for a human eye.

He worked quickly and well. She'd had stitches put in by mediwitches and by Muggle doctors in four countries and by Teo in a tent with a sewing needle and a bottle of firewhiskey, and Dr. Cullen was the best of any of them. His hands didn't shake. He didn't breathe. She noticed that, too, once she started looking for it: he spoke, and he breathed in to speak, and the rest of the time his chest simply didn't move.

"You're very calm," he said, tying off the sixth stitch. "Most people don't like to watch."

"I've had worse."

His eyes flicked to the white branching scar on her forearm and away. "I can see that."

"Occupational hazard."

"What's your occupation?"

"Salvage," Layla said.

"Ah," Dr. Cullen said, and tied off the seventh stitch, and the eighth. Then he looked up at her, and his face was kind, and his gold eyes were very careful.

"Keep it dry," he said. "Come back in ten days and we'll take them out. And Ms. Thorne?"

"Yes?"

"Be careful with your boxes."

---

*Calawah Way — forty-six days after*

She was waiting on the porch when Seth's truck pulled up.

It wasn't really his truck. It was a rusted-out Ford that belonged to the pack in some loose way, that whoever needed it took, and it coughed when he shut it off. He'd come straight from the Cullens'; she could smell it on him when he came up the steps, though she wouldn't have known what she was smelling a week ago. Something sweet, faintly, like cold flowers. She'd smelled the same thing on Dr. Cullen's hands.

Her own hand was bandaged in white gauze. Seth saw it before he reached the top step, and his face went through something fast and frightening, and he was in front of her before she'd finished standing up.

"What happened? Are you okay? What happened to your hand?"

"I cut it. I'm fine. Sit down."

"Who stitched it?"

"Seth. Sit down."

He sat on the top step. She sat beside him, a foot away. The rain came down off the edge of the porch roof in a ragged gray curtain.

"I went to the ER," she said. "Last night. The doctor who stitched me up was named Cullen."

She watched him go still.

He was bad at stillness. The doctor had gone still like a stone; Seth went still like a kid who'd been caught. His whole body locked up, his shoulders, his jaw, his big hands on his knees.

"Dr. Cullen's great," he said. "He's like the best doctor in the state."

"Seth."

"He is!"

"He doesn't have a pulse."

The rain went on falling.

Seth didn't look at her. He was staring at the yard, at the slug-eaten garden, at the salt line along the bottom of the porch rail that she'd poured fresh that morning.

"His hands are cold," Layla said. "Cold like a rock. He doesn't breathe unless he's talking. He smelled my blood and his whole face stopped, the way something stops when it's hunting. His eyes are gold. And you smell like him right now. You smell like him a lot." She took a breath. "You told me your mom's boyfriend is the police chief. Chief Swan. His daughter married one of the Cullen boys in August. She got sick on her honeymoon. Some rare disease from South America. Nobody's seen her in town since. The whole town talks about it. I've been here a month and a half and I've heard it four times."

Seth made a sound. It wasn't a word.

"I'm not stupid," Layla said. "And I'm not—" She stopped and made herself say it differently. "I've been places. I've seen things that would make most people lose their minds. I'm not going to lose mine. But I need you to tell me what that man is, and I need you to tell me what *you* are, because I think you know, and I think it's the same kind of thing, or the opposite kind of thing, and I think you've been not telling me for three weeks."

He put his face in his hands.

She waited. She was good at waiting. Waiting was most of the job.

When he lifted his head his eyes were wet, and he was laughing a little, helplessly, the way people laugh at funerals.

"I'm supposed to ask first," he said. "I'm supposed to talk to Jake. There's rules."

"Who's Jake?"

"He's—" Seth scrubbed both hands down his face. "Okay. Okay. Okay. Can I show you? It's gonna be easier if I show you. I'm not gonna hurt you. I would never hurt you. I need you to know that, like, more than anything. I would never ever hurt you."

"Show me what?"

"Can we go in the backyard?"

---

The backyard was a quarter acre of soggy grass that ran down to a fence and then to the trees. The firs stood dark and wet against the gray sky. Seth walked her out to the middle of it, then told her to stay put and walked another twenty feet away from her, toward the tree line, and stopped and turned around.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. So. Don't freak out."

"I'm not going to freak out."

"You might."

"I won't."

"I'm gonna have to take my shirt off. It's not a thing. It's just, otherwise it rips."

"Seth—"

He pulled his T-shirt over his head and tossed it on the grass. She had a second to take in the sheer size of him, the shoulders and the arms and the long ropy muscle down his back, like someone had taken a big friendly teenager and kept going. Then he took a breath, and his whole body *shuddered*, a ripple that went from his spine outward. And then there was a sound like canvas tearing, and the cutoff sweatpants were rags on the grass, and there was no Seth.

There was a wolf.

The wolf was the size of a horse. Bigger than a horse. Its shoulder came to the height of her head, and she wasn't standing close. It was sandy, a pale tawny gold like dune grass, with a darker saddle down the spine. Its paws sank into the soggy lawn. Its breath came out of its nose in two great plumes of steam in the cold air. It smelled like rain and dog and pine needles and heat, and under it, faint and electric, there was that thing she'd felt on Seth from the first day in the store: something old. Something that made the hair on her arms stand up. Older than a curse. Something that came up out of the ground here like water.

Its eyes were Seth's eyes. Dark brown and anxious and so, so hopeful.

Layla stood very still.

The wolf whined. It was a very small sound to come out of something that big. It lowered its head, ears back, and took one slow step toward her, and then stopped, like it was waiting for permission.

Her wand was in her hair. She was aware of it the way you're aware of your own heartbeat. Black walnut, thunderbird tail feather, eleven and a quarter inches, made by a Wolfe in Arizona. Thunderbird wands could sense danger. They were known for it. Hers had saved her life twice by going hot against her skull in the second before a trap sprang, so hot it hurt.

It was quiet now. It was humming, very faintly, in a way she'd never felt it hum before. Like a cat's purr. Like recognition.

She walked forward across the wet grass and held out her good hand.

The wolf lowered its huge head all the way down, until its nose was level with her chest, and pushed its muzzle into her palm. The fur was coarse and damp, and its breath was hot as an oven on her wrist, and she could feel the bones of its skull under her fingers, the sheer heavy architecture of it.

"Okay," Layla said. Her voice came out a little shaky. "Okay. Hi."

The wolf made a sound that was very nearly a laugh.

"You're a werewolf."

The wolf snorted and shook its head, a big full-body *no* that almost knocked her over.

"Okay. Okay." She was laughing too now, a little, though nothing was funny. "Shape-shifter, then. Something like that."

A nod. An enormous, enthusiastic nod.

"And the doctor."

The wolf went still. Its ears went back. Then, slowly, very slowly, it nodded again.

"Vampire," Layla said.

The wolf sighed, a long gust of hot air that ruffled her hair, and lay down on the soggy lawn at her feet with its chin on its forepaws, looking up at her with Seth's eyes.

She stood there in the rain in her backyard, a mile from the Forks town limit, with her hand on the head of a wolf the size of a pickup truck, and thought about the jaguar box in its lead case on her kitchen table, and the letter from Lima, and the International Statute of Secrecy, and the wand purring in her hair.

*I picked this town,* she thought, *because there were no registered wizarding households within a hundred and fifty miles.*

"I need a day," she said out loud. "Okay? I'm okay. I need a day."

The wolf whined, very softly. Then it nodded.

She took her hand off its head and walked back up to the house and went inside and shut the door and sat down on the kitchen floor with her back against the cabinets, and put her face on her knees, and did not cry, quite.

On the windowsill over the sink, the salt line lay white and perfect, unbroken.

---

She took more than a day.

She took eleven.

She went nowhere he'd be. She got her stitches out early, at a different clinic in Port Angeles. She unpicked the jaguar box's third curse with her hand still bandaged and sent it back to Lima with a bill. She walked the property line every night and refreshed the salt and the wards under it. She thought about Teo, and about Peru, and about the Statute, and about the long white folders on MACUSA's Department of No-Maj Misinformation shelves that she'd seen exactly once, when she'd interned there the summer she was sixteen. Folders with names on them. Wands snapped. Memories wiped. A whole family in Ohio in 1974, the grandmother a witch, the rest of them No-Maj, who'd known too much; nobody in that family remembered they'd ever had a grandmother.

She thought about the sandy wolf lying in the rain at her feet. She thought about the purr in her wand.

Seth came by twice. Both times he left something on the porch and went away before she could get to the door. The first time it was a thermos of coffee from the diner, still hot. The second time it was a glass jar with a lid that had holes punched in it, and inside the jar was a banana slug the length of her hand, bright yellow, and a note in big, careful handwriting: *THIS ONE WAS ON YOUR STEPS. I RELOCATED HIM. HIS NAME IS GARY. — S.*

She looked at Gary for a long time.

Then she went inside, sat at the kitchen table, and called the number Seth had written on the inside of the pie dish lid a month ago.

---

### III. Thirty-Five Days

*Calawah Way — fifty-seven days after*

He came at dusk. He knocked, which he'd never done before; he'd always just stood on the porch and waited for her to notice him. When she opened the door he stood there in a hooded sweatshirt, which she knew he'd put on for her benefit, and his face was so carefully hopeful it hurt to look at.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi. Come in."

He looked down at the threshold. At the salt line.

"Can I?"

"Yes. Step over it. Don't scuff it."

He stepped over it, very carefully, and stood in her front hall with his hands jammed in the pocket of the sweatshirt. The overhead light flickered as she walked under it toward the kitchen, and she saw him look up at it, and then look at her.

The kitchen was small and warm-colored and old, with yellow linoleum and a wood stove in the corner she'd gotten going an hour ago. The rotary phone on the wall. A table with two chairs. A row of glass jars along the windowsill above the salt line, full of things that weren't spices.

"Sit," she said.

He sat. The chair complained.

She stayed standing. She'd rehearsed this a lot of different ways, over eleven days. None of them had seemed right. Now that he was here, sitting at her table looking up at her like she was about to read him his sentence, all the rehearsals went out of her head at once.

So she just reached up and pulled the chopstick out of her hair.

Her hair came down. It always took a second; there was a lot of it. She shook it back out of her face. Then she held up the chopstick between them, the dark walnut, eleven and a quarter inches, with its little crook two-thirds of the way down and the carved grip worn smooth by eleven years of her hand, and she didn't say anything at all. She just turned toward the stove and flicked it.

The kettle lifted off the burner.

It rose straight up into the air, two feet, three, and turned in a slow circle, steam curling out of its spout. She flicked again and the cupboard over the sink swung open and two mugs floated out and settled side by side on the table in front of Seth. The tea tin opened its own lid. Two bags drifted out and dropped into the mugs. The kettle tipped and poured, and the water went into the mugs in two clean steaming ribbons, and the kettle righted itself and floated back to the stove and set itself down on the burner with a gentle *clink*.

Seth stared at the tea.

Then he stared at her.

"You're a witch," he said.

"I'm a witch."

"Like an actual witch."

"Like an actual witch."

"Like with a *wand*."

"It's a wand." She held it up. "It's been a wand the whole time. You've been calling it a chopstick in your head for a month, haven't you."

His mouth opened. Closed. Opened.

"I knew it," he said. "I knew it, I *knew* it, the salt and the lights and you were dry, Layla, at the Hoh, you were totally dry, I *saw* that—"

"I know you saw it. I knew you saw it."

"I thought I was going crazy!"

"You turn into a horse-sized wolf!"

"That's different!"

"*How?*"

"I don't know!" He was standing up. She hadn't seen him stand. He was laughing, and his eyes were wet again, and he took two steps around the table toward her and stopped himself like he'd hit a wall, his hands half raised. "I don't know. I don't know. Is this—are there more of you? Is it a thing? Is there like a school? Is there a *Hogwarts*?"

Layla stared at him.

"How do you—"

"It's a *book*, Layla! It's a kids' book. Everybody's read it."

"I've—no. I haven't. I don't—" She stopped. Her wand hand was shaking a little. She put the wand down on the table, very carefully, next to the mugs. "There are schools. There are a lot of us. There's a whole—there's a government. There's a law that says I can't tell you any of this. It's a big law. It's international. It's three hundred years old. If they find out I told you, they'll take my wand and snap it in half. They'll wipe your memory. They'll make you forget you ever met me."

That stopped him. All of it, the laughing and the moving, all at once.

"They can do that?"

"They do it all the time."

He looked at her for a long moment. Something moved in his face, slow and serious, and for the first time since she'd met him he didn't look like a kid at all.

"Then why'd you tell me?"

She'd rehearsed an answer to that too. She'd rehearsed a lot of answers. *Because you told me. Because it was fair. Because I'm tired of being alone in a house full of salt at the end of the world.*

What she said was, "Because I kept wanting to."

Seth made a sound, very soft.

"Since the store," Layla said. "Since the parking lot. I kept wanting to tell you things. I haven't wanted to tell anybody anything since Teo, and I kept wanting to tell you everything, and I thought it was just because you were—you. You're easy to talk to. You're the easiest person I've ever talked to. And then you turned into a wolf in my yard and I thought, oh, okay, maybe it's a *thing*. Maybe it's magic. Maybe you did something to me." She swallowed. "Did you?"

He didn't answer right away. That was how she knew he was going to tell her the truth.

"It's called imprinting," he said. "It's a wolf thing. It happens to some of us. You see somebody and it's like—" He spread his hands helplessly. "It's like the whole world turns around and starts pointing at them. It happened to me in the store. When you dropped the salt. I didn't pick it. It just happened." He was looking at the floor. "I think it's just me. On my end. Everybody says it's just the wolf. Sam's wife, Emily, she says she felt like she got a choice. My sister says that's—she says it's not a choice if one person's whole life is bent around the other one. She hates it. She thinks it's the worst thing in the world." He looked up. "I'm not gonna make you do anything. You don't have to—I'll be anything you want. Friends. I told you. Friends is great. It's really great."

Layla walked around the table.

She was a foot shorter than him. She had to tip her head back to look at his face. The heat came off him like a wood stove; she could feel it on her cheeks. She reached up with her good hand and took a handful of the front of his sweatshirt, the way she'd grabbed his T-shirt on the log over the Hoh.

"Friends is terrible," she said. "I'm sick of friends."

And she pulled him down and kissed him.

---

Some time later — not a lot of time, but enough that the tea had gone cold and the wood stove had burned down and nobody had done anything about either — Seth said, into her hair, "So there's something I should probably tell you."

Layla was sitting on the kitchen counter, which put her closer to eye level. She had both arms around his neck. She felt him tense up.

"What?"

"You know how there's rules? About telling people?"

"Yes."

"So. Okay. I'm in a pack. Me and my sister and Jake. Jacob. He's our Alpha. And when we're wolves, we can hear each other. In our heads. Everything. Everything you think."

She leaned back to look at him.

"Everything?"

"Pretty much everything."

"Like—"

"Like if I phase tonight, and Jake's phased, he's going to see all of this. Not on purpose. It's just there. I can't hide it." His ears had gone red. "Leah's going to see it too. And she's going to see the tea and the wand and the floating kettle and she's gonna lose her mind."

"Oh my God."

"And the Cullens—there's—Edward. Bella's husband. He reads minds. Like *reads minds*. Anybody, from a couple miles away. So the second you're anywhere near that house, he's gonna hear everything you're thinking. And I'm over there all the time. I'm friends with them. I hang out with the baby."

"The *baby*?"

"Oh. Yeah. There's a baby. That's—that's a whole other thing."

Layla stared at him.

"So you're saying," she said slowly, "that I just broke the International Statute of Secrecy for one person, and actually I broke it for at least three people and a mind-reading vampire."

"Kind of?"

"And a baby."

"The baby already knows about everything. She's really smart. She shows you stuff. In your head." He winced. "And I think maybe the whole thing is gonna be a lot easier if you just come over and meet everybody. Because otherwise Edward's gonna hear it from me, or from Jake, and then Alice is gonna get weird about it, because she can't see stuff when we're around, and then Rosalie's gonna say something mean, and Leah's gonna say something meaner, and it's gonna be this whole thing."

Layla put her forehead against his chest and laughed until she couldn't breathe.

"Okay," she said finally. "Okay. Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow. I'll meet them." She lifted her head. "Seth. Is the baby a vampire?"

He thought about that one.

"Half," he said.

---

*The Cullen house — fifty-eight days after*

Edward knew before they reached the house.

Layla could tell, because when Seth's truck came up the long drive through the trees and the house came into view — glass, three stories of it, light spilling out into the gray afternoon, a white structure that looked like an architect's argument — there was a man standing on the porch already. Bronze hair. Gold eyes. Pale as the doctor and as perfect, with a face that looked like it had been designed by committee and then refined for a century, and an expression on that face of the purest, most incredulous astonishment she'd ever seen on anybody.

Seth turned off the engine. It coughed.

"That's Edward," he said.

"I figured."

"He's probably been listening since the highway."

Layla took a breath and opened her door.

The man on the porch watched her come up the steps. When she was three steps from the top he said, in a soft voice with an accent from nowhere in particular, "There's a law, isn't there? You've been thinking about it for a mile and a half. The International Statute of Secrecy. Sixteen ninety-two." He tilted his head. "And a school. In Massachusetts. On a mountain."

"Hi," Layla said. "Nice to meet you too."

The corner of his mouth moved. "Edward."

"Layla."

"I know." He glanced at Seth, then back at her, and his gold eyes did something complicated. "I've been hearing about you for a month. His head is not a restful place."

"Hey," Seth said.

"The kettle," Edward said. "That was in his head at the gas station on the corner of Forks Avenue. The *kettle*." He looked like he wanted to laugh and was too unsettled to. "Is it Latin?"

"Is what Latin?"

"The words in your head. Before you do something. There's a word, and then something happens. You're doing it now, without meaning to. You've got about six of them queued up like bullets."

She hadn't realized she was. She made herself unclench her hand.

"Mostly Latin," she said. "Some isn't. Can I come in, or are we doing this on the porch?"

Edward looked at her a moment longer. Then he stepped aside and opened the door.

---

The inside of the house was bright and open and white and full of people who didn't move like people.

There were too many of them, at first. Layla's eyes kept trying to count and losing track, because they shifted and blurred. A small woman with short black hair standing very still at the foot of the stairs; a big dark-haired man on the couch, grinning like it was Christmas; a blonde woman beside him so beautiful it was hard to look straight at her, with her arms folded. A tall man with honey-blond hair and a scattering of scars along his jaw and throat, faint and pale and crescent-shaped, standing near the windows with his eyes on Layla the way a sniper's are on a doorway. A woman with caramel hair and a kind, heart-shaped face. And Dr. Cullen, who came forward first, and smiled, and said, "Ms. Thorne. How's your hand?"

"Healed," Layla said. "Thank you."

"I wondered about that cut," Dr. Cullen said, mildly. "There was something about the edges."

"There was a curse in the edges."

The room went very quiet.

"A curse," Dr. Cullen repeated.

"I break them. For a living. That's the salvage."

The big man on the couch said, "*Sick*," and the blonde woman beside him elbowed him without looking.

On the far side of the room, by the hearth, two more people were standing, and three were not people at all.

The first was a girl about Layla's age. Brown hair, gold-red eyes, the eyes of someone who'd only recently stopped being red; she was holding a child on her hip. The child looked about two. She had a mop of bronze curls and an expression of intent, focused, very adult curiosity, and when Layla looked at her the child looked back.

Next to them, two wolves.

They were in human shape, both of them, but Layla had seen Seth in the backyard and she knew exactly what she was looking at now. A young man, Quileute, enormous, with short black hair and the same heat coming off him that came off Seth, standing so close to the girl and the child that his shoulder nearly touched them. And a young woman a step behind him, tall, taller than Layla by half a foot, with her black hair cropped short at her jaw, wearing cutoffs and a black tank top in November. Her arms were crossed. Her face was beautiful in a hard, uncompromising way, all cheekbone and jaw, and it was arranged into an expression of total contempt.

"That's Jake," Seth said, low. "And that's my sister. Leah."

"Hi," Layla said.

Leah looked her up and down, slowly, from her boots to her hair.

"Seth says you're a witch," she said.

"I am."

"Prove it."

"Leah," Jacob said.

"What? She came all the way up here to tell a house full of bloodsuckers she's a witch. I want to see it." Leah's mouth twisted. "Unless the whole thing's a bit."

The big man on the couch said, "Ooh."

Layla looked at Leah for a moment. She'd met a lot of Leahs, in the field. Usually they were the best people on the team. Usually they were the ones who'd already been burned and had decided not to wait around to be burned again.

She reached up and pulled the wand out of her hair.

This time she made nothing float. She looked around the room, at the white walls and the glass and the books on the shelves, and thought about what the most honest answer would be. Then she pointed the wand at the cold, empty fireplace and said, quietly, "*Incendio*."

Fire bloomed in the hearth. Real fire, high and orange and crackling, out of nothing, on bare stone.

Every vampire in the room moved at once.

They didn't go far. A half step. It was enough. The tall scarred man by the window was suddenly in front of the small dark-haired woman. The blonde woman was on her feet. Dr. Cullen had his hand out, flat, in a soothing gesture, but it had come up fast. And the girl with the child had turned her whole body away from the flames, the child behind her shoulder, before Layla even had time to register that she'd moved.

Layla flicked her wand again and the fire went out. Not even smoke.

"Sorry," she said into the silence. "I should've thought."

"Fire," Edward said, from just behind her, quietly. "It's one of the few things that can kill us."

"I know. I'm sorry. That was dumb." She put her wand away. "I thought it'd be the thing you couldn't explain."

There was a short silence.

Then Leah said, flatly, "Okay. She's a witch."

Something in the room let go.

The big dark-haired man laughed, a huge delighted boom. The small black-haired woman stepped out from behind the scarred man and came straight across the room to Layla, light and quick as a bird, and stopped a foot away and peered up into her face with an expression of deep personal grievance.

"I couldn't see you," she said.

"Sorry?"

"I'm Alice. I see things. Futures. Decisions. And I've been trying to see whatever Seth was so worked up about for a *month*, and you're all *fuzzy*." She waved her hands in front of Layla's face. "Like the dogs. Anything they touch goes blurry. And I thought you were just a regular girl he'd fallen for, and you'd be blurry because he's blurry, and that was annoying but fine, but you're blurry *differently*." She narrowed her eyes. "You're blurry like there's something in the way."

"Maybe there is," Layla said. "We're not supposed to show up in things. Divination, scrying. It's a whole field. People spend careers on it."

"I *hate* it," Alice said, with great feeling.

"Alice," said the kind-faced woman, Esme, "give the poor girl some room."

---

It went on like that for a while. Questions and answers. Layla stood in the middle of the white living room and tried to explain three hundred years of history and law in a way that didn't sound insane, while seven vampires and three shape-shifters and a toddler listened, and some of it sounded insane anyway.

There were witches and wizards all over the world. There were schools. There was a government — several; one in every country, more or less, and in the United States it was called MACUSA, the Magical Congress, and it was in New York. There was a law, the International Statute of Secrecy, that had kept the magical world hidden from the No-Maj world since 1692, and an international body, the Confederation, to enforce it. There were dragons. There were giants, a few. There were vampires.

"Not like you," she said, when the room went quiet. "Ours are different. They have fangs. They burn in sunlight. They're—" She hesitated. "They're mostly considered a nuisance. A dangerous one. There's a registry. Nobody's ever written anything about anything like you. I've read a lot. Nobody's ever written about stone skin, or venom, or eyes like that. You're not in any book."

"Good," said the blonde woman, Rosalie, from the couch.

"We like it that way," Dr. Cullen said, more gently. He'd been quiet for most of it, listening, with his hands folded and his gold eyes moving from face to face. "We have — let's say we have our own government, of a kind. In Italy. And they have a law much like yours. Any human who learns of us must be changed, or killed."

Layla looked at him.

"That's me," she said. "Isn't it? I know about you."

"You're not human," Edward said. He'd been standing at the end of the couch with his arms folded, and he said it slowly, like he was listening for something. "Not entirely. Not the way the law means it." He frowned. "Your thoughts have — a texture. There's something under them. Like a current under water. I've never heard it before."

"Is that a loophole?"

"I've no idea," Edward said. "I don't think it's ever come up."

"The Volturi like loopholes," the scarred man, Jasper, said. He hadn't moved from the window. He had a soft Southern voice, very polite. "When it suits them."

Layla filed the word away. *Volturi.*

"Can I ask something?" Seth said. He'd been standing at her shoulder the whole time, close enough that she could feel the heat of him through her jacket. "Not about the law. About Nessie."

Bella, the girl with the child, turned her head. The child on her hip turned too, all those bronze curls, and looked straight at Layla with great dark eyes. The same color as Bella's must once have been.

"Her name's Renesmee," Bella said.

"Sorry. Renesmee."

"She's growing too fast," Dr. Cullen said quietly. "That's what Seth wants to ask. She was born fifty-eight days ago. She looks two. She speaks — in her way — like a child of five or six. She reads. If she keeps growing at this rate—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The room had gone still in a different way.

Layla looked at the child. The child looked back.

"I'm not a healer," she said. "I'm not a mediwitch. But I know some diagnostics. Old ones. Curse-breakers use them to read things in tombs, to see what something is and how old and whether it's going to try to kill you. I could look. I don't know if I'd see anything. It wouldn't hurt her."

Bella's eyes went to Edward. Edward's eyes went to Layla, and stayed there for a long moment. Listening. Then he gave a short nod.

"She's thinking about a spell called *Specialis Revelio*," he said. "And about a lot of tombs. She means it. It won't hurt."

Layla held out her hands.

The child didn't wait for her mother. She leaned out from Bella's hip, small and very sure, and reached out with one hand and put her palm flat against Layla's cheek.

And Layla was somewhere else.

She was in this room, but from lower down, and looking up at herself. She saw her own face, a stranger's, and the fire that had leaped up in the hearth from nothing, orange and wild. She saw Seth, very tall, his face shining. She saw her own wand, and the feeling of a question mark, a bright curious *what is it, what is it, what is it* — and then, very fast, a bright flicker of other images: a kettle floating, a wolf in a backyard. Things the child couldn't have seen. Things she must have gotten from Seth, or from — from Edward? Edward must have shown them to her, or she'd seen them in someone's face, or—

The hand came away from her cheek. Layla was standing in the Cullen living room again with her heart going hard.

"She shows you," Jacob said. He was grinning a little. "Cool, huh?"

"That's not a spell," Layla said. "That's — that's not anything I've ever seen."

"It's her," Bella said. "It's just her."

Layla drew her wand out of her hair, slowly, and held it up so that everyone could see it, and looked at the child.

"Can I?" she asked her.

The child nodded.

The diagnostic charm was old and fussy and required a great deal of concentration. Layla closed her eyes for it. She traced the old shape in the air, and spoke the old words under her breath, and reached out.

What came back was the strangest thing she'd ever felt. There was a heartbeat, quick as a bird's. There was warm blood. And underneath it, woven all through it, there was the same thing she'd felt in Dr. Cullen in the ER, that great dense ancient stone-cold pressure. And under *that*, faint, a third thing. A thread of something that felt like her. Like the current under the water that Edward had heard in her thoughts.

She opened her eyes.

"She's—" She stopped. "I don't know. There's something in her that's a little like — a little like magic. Like us. A thread of it."

"What does that mean?" Bella said. Her voice was very quiet.

"I don't know," Layla said honestly. "I don't think anyone knows. But I don't think she's dying. I don't feel anything that feels like that. Everything I felt felt like it was *finishing*. Like something heading somewhere. Like it's going to stop." She swallowed. "I'm not a doctor. I could be wrong."

Bella closed her eyes. Edward was beside her. Layla hadn't seen him move.

It was at that exact moment, with Layla's wand in the air and seven vampires gathered around her and the child, that the front door opened and Charlie Swan walked in.

---

He was in uniform. Brown jacket, badge, belt with a gun on it, the works. He had a paper bag from the diner in one hand and was already saying, "Bells, I brought those cinnamon rolls the kid li—"

He stopped.

He looked at the room. At the vampires, frozen in a ring. At the two wolves. At his daughter, with her eyes closed and her husband's arm around her. At his granddaughter — the girl everybody kept telling him was Edward's niece, the girl who had his daughter's eyes. At the strange young woman in the middle of it all, holding up a stick.

Layla, very slowly, lowered the wand.

Behind the hearth, a log that hadn't been there an hour ago gave a small settling *crack*. It was still smoking faintly from her *Incendio*.

"Huh," Charlie said.

Nobody said anything.

Charlie looked at the stick in Layla's hand. Looked at it for a long time.

"Bells," he said. "Is that a wand?"

"Need to know, Dad," Bella said faintly.

"Yeah." Charlie nodded slowly. "Yeah. Okay." He set the paper bag of cinnamon rolls down on the table by the door, very carefully, like it might go off. "Here's the thing. I've been going along with need to know. I've been real good about need to know. I've got a whole drawer up here for need to know." He tapped his temple. "And I'm gonna keep doing it. Because that's the deal. But I've got to tell you, I'm starting to feel like I need to know *a little bit*."

He looked at Layla.

"You're the one who bought all the salt," he said. "At the Thriftway. Mrs. Stanley told everybody."

"Slugs," Layla said weakly.

"Slugs," Charlie agreed. He looked at Seth, who was standing next to her, red to the ears. Then at Leah. Then at Jacob. Then he took a long, deep breath and let it out through his mustache.

"Okay," he said. "Does anybody want a cinnamon roll?"

Nobody moved.

"I'm gonna have a cinnamon roll," Charlie said, and went and sat down at the dining table, and took one out of the bag, and ate it, staring at the far wall with tremendous concentration.

Layla turned to Seth.

"I'm going to prison," she said.

"No you're not," Seth said.

"Seth, that's the *police chief*. He's a No-Maj. He's a No-Maj with a *badge*. Do you have any idea—"

"Hey," Leah said.

They both looked at her. She hadn't moved from beside Jacob. Her arms were still crossed. But there was something different in her face; the contempt had shifted sideways. She was looking at her brother. At the way he was standing, half in front of Layla, all six and a half feet of him angled between her and the room.

"Nobody's going to prison," Leah said. "Okay? Whoever your people are, they'd have to find out first. And nobody in this room's telling." She lifted her chin at the vampires. "Right?"

"Right," Dr. Cullen said.

"Right," said Jacob.

The big dark-haired man, Emmett, said, "Wouldn't dream of it. Can you do the fire thing again?"

"No," said everyone.

Leah held Layla's eyes a second longer.

"You hurt him," she said, "and I'll find out where your school is and eat it."

Layla thought about that.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay," said Leah, and looked away.

---

Two days later, Layla spent an entire afternoon at her kitchen table with a length of braided leather, three strands of her own hair, a single bead of red jasper, a bowl of Seth's blood (a very small bowl; he'd made a big deal of it), and the most complicated piece of spellwork she'd attempted since Peru. It was a tether. A curse-breaker's trick. In the field you gave one to your partner so that if they ever got trapped somewhere you couldn't reach — a collapsing chamber, a sealed vault, a place where the wards wouldn't let you Apparate in — they could break it, and it would pull you straight to them, through almost anything.

She'd made one for Teo. He hadn't had time to use it.

She tied it around Seth's left foreleg, high up, when he was phased, while he lay in her backyard and watched her with his ears pricked and his tail thumping the wet grass.

"It's got some give," she told him, "so it'll stretch when you change. It'll be on your ankle when you're a person. Don't take it off. If you ever need me, you bite the bead. You crack it, and I'll come. Wherever you are."

The wolf licked her face, which she'd been expecting, and then lay with its huge head in her lap for most of an hour while the rain came and went, which she hadn't.
