# Need to Know

## Chapter Two: Off the Lead

---

## Part One: The Wall

---

### I. Leah

*The north fork of the Calawah — Monday, November 20th, 2006, 6:41 p.m.*

She answered the howl because Jacob was not going to stop until she did.

It came up out of her on the run, without her breaking stride, long and high and rising, and it went off up the valley behind her into the dark and the rain. Seth's came in on top of it from somewhere east, ragged at the top the way his always were. Jacob's was still going. For a few seconds the three of them filled the forest from three points on the map. Then they stopped, one after the other, and the river was loud again.

*Cullens,* Jacob thought. *Now.*

*I'm coming.*

*Layla's still here. Carlisle looked at her back. Everybody's here. Everybody's waiting on you.*

*I said I'm coming, Jake.*

She went down off the bench through the big spruces with the rope running out of the middle of her chest and back up the slope behind her. Up into the dark, up past the edge of the fog that had tried to turn her round three hours ago, into a little green tent with moss on its guy lines and a slug on the canvas by the door. She could feel the man at the other end of it. She could feel him the way you can feel which room somebody's in at your mom's house by where the floor creaks. He was standing still. He had both hands flat on something heavy and cold. The stove, she thought. That big black iron stove. There was music going somewhere near him; she couldn't hear it, but she could feel him listening to it, a steady beat he was keeping time to without moving.

And under him, at the bottom, behind the three locks, the big dark thing was lying with its chin on its paws and its nose pointed southeast. After her. Following her down the hill without moving an inch.

Her own wolf knew. It ran the whole way down to the river with one ear turned back.

She hit the north fork at the split boulder and went through it chest-deep, and the cold punched the air out of her, and she came out on the far bank and shook and kept going. South, along the river. Then east, around the top of town, through the cutover land behind the high school where the second-growth fir stood in rows like a crop.

*Lee.* Seth. Careful. Small, for Seth. *Are you okay? Like, actually?*

*I'm fine.*

She felt the both of them stop.

She felt them go looking. That was what you did, in the pack mind, when Leah Clearwater said she was fine. You went looking underneath it for the rest. For *Sam Emily Sam Emily*, the radio left on in the room nobody used. For the grinding. For the stone she'd been carrying in her chest since she was eighteen, that everybody in the pack could feel the edges of every time she phased.

They went looking.

They both found the rope at the same time, and she felt them both flinch from it a little, the way you flinch from a power line humming over a road. It ran out of her and away northwest and it was the loudest thing in her head, and under it, where the stone used to be, there was a lot of room. Clean room. Like a house after somebody's finally hauled away the couch that's been sitting on the porch for two years.

*Huh,* Seth thought, very quietly.

*Yeah.*

*You really are fine.*

*Told you.*

*That's so weird, Lee.*

*Tell me about it.*

Jacob didn't say anything for a while. She could feel him pacing. He was at the edge of the Cullens' lawn, phased, walking up and down the treeline with his hackles up, and every time he got to the end of the lawn he turned round and came back. She could feel him looking at the rope. She could feel him looking at the gray place next to it, the wall, the smooth blank stone in the middle of her afternoon where half of what had happened in that tent had gone and he couldn't follow.

*And there's that,* he thought finally. *That. What is that, Leah?*

*I told you. Ask him.*

*I'm asking you.*

*And I'm telling you to ask him.*

*He put something in your head.*

*He didn't put anything in my head.*

*Then what is it?*

She thought about it. She thought about it honestly, because she couldn't think about anything any other way with the pair of them in there. She went and stood next to the wall in her own mind, the way you'd stand in front of a door in your own house that you'd never noticed before, and she put her nose to it.

It was cold. That was the first thing. Old and cold and patient, like a stone at the bottom of a river. It felt like him. Like the part of him that stood on a rock in a suit and looked bored while two wolves tried to tear his throat out. And on the other side of it, sealed up, she knew exactly what was there. She could have walked through the door any time she wanted. She was on the right side of it. It was the rest of them who weren't.

*It's his,* she thought. *It's his stuff. It's the stuff he's not allowed to tell people. I'm—it thinks I'm him. So I can see it. You can't.*

*That's insane.*

*Yep.*

*Leah.* She felt him stop pacing. She felt him plant all four feet at the end of the lawn and drop his head. *You imprinted on—*

*Don't.*

*—on a guy who—*

*Save it for when you've got a mouth, Jake. I want to see your face when you say it.*

He shut up. She felt him not like it. She felt him decide to let her have that one, and she felt what it cost him, and she ran on toward the house through the black wet trees and let him stew.

---

The Cullen place came up out of the dark the way it always did, all at once, like a ship. Every window lit. The long white walls and the long glass, and inside it, people moving, pale, too fast and then too slow, the way they did when they remembered they were supposed to look human. The stink came off it in a wave a quarter mile out. Bleach and burnt sugar and flowers. She'd stopped gagging on it sometime in September. She still noticed.

She came out of the trees at the north end of the lawn and they were both there.

Jacob was in the middle of the grass, huge and russet, the color of rust on a truck bed in the porch light. He was the biggest wolf anybody on the reservation had ever heard of; there were old men at the bonfire who'd told stories for sixty years and never told one about a wolf that size. Ten feet, nose to tail, and so heavy through the chest and shoulders that he moved like weather. He'd been bigger than Sam since the spring. He'd gotten bigger since the baby.

Seth was off to the side, under the edge of the trees, sandy and long and loose. He'd shot up a foot as a human since Christmas, and his wolf hadn't caught up with him yet; it was all legs and ears still, the way he'd been at fourteen, before the change, when he couldn't walk through a kitchen without hitting something. When she trotted up beside him, she stood a head over him at the shoulder. She always had. He'd catch up eventually. Maybe pass her. Not this year.

His tail did something confused when he saw her. Half a wag. Then it stopped, like it had remembered something, and then it started again.

Jacob came across the lawn at her.

He came stiff-legged, with his head low and his ears forward, and stopped about three feet away and put his nose out, and pulled the air in off her. She stood and let him. She knew what he was getting. Pine. Wet earth. Cold stone, like the inside of a cave. Coffee and bacon fat and gun oil and old books. Beer. And under all of it, faint, the thing none of them had a name for. The ghost-smell. The place in the woods where something big had slept and gone.

Jacob's lip came up off his teeth. Just on one side. It went back down.

*He fed you,* Jacob thought. Flat.

*Six eggs and most of a loaf.*

*He fed you.*

*He's got a really good stove, Jake.*

She went past him, behind the biggest cedar at the edge of the lawn, and phased.

It tore out of her the way it always did, and she was on her knees in the wet needles, human, with her hair in her face, breathing. She pulled the cord off her ankle and unrolled the cutoffs and the black tank top and got them on, wet, and stood up. Her legs were shaking a little. Twenty-some miles, the river twice, the ridge, the run up to the tent, the run down from it. She hadn't eaten since the eggs.

She was starving.

She walked out from behind the cedar and across the lawn, barefoot, through the porch light, past both of them, and up the steps.

The door opened before she got to it. Esme was standing in it, in a cream sweater, with her hands folded in front of her, and her face doing the thing Esme's face did when one of the wolves came in muddy: kind, and worried, and trying very hard not to look at the floor.

"Leah," Esme said. "Are you all right, dear?"

"Is there food?"

Esme blinked. "There's lasagna."

"Great," said Leah, and went in.

---

The whole house was in the front room. All of them. She took it in on the way past without stopping, the way she'd take in a clearing.

Carlisle on his feet by the fireplace with his shirt-sleeves rolled up and a doctor's bag open on the coffee table, and on the table next to the bag a squat brown bottle with no label and a dish of some yellow ointment that smelled like flowers and swamp. Alice and Jasper by the glass wall at the back, close, his hand on her shoulder, both of them watching the door. Rosalie halfway down the stairs, sitting on a step with her arms around her knees. Emmett flat on his back under the piano with his hands laced on his stomach. Edward standing at the end of the piano, absolutely still, his eyes already on her, already listening.

Bella on the big white couch with the kid asleep against her shoulder. Bronze curls all over her mother's sweater, one fist closed on a piece of Bella's hair, mouth open. Bella's eyes came up to Leah's over the top of her daughter's head, gold, without the contacts, and they did not look friendly.

And at the other end of the couch, wrapped up in a gray blanket with her bare feet tucked under her, Layla.

She'd had a shower; her hair was wet and combed straight back. She was wearing a sweater that was not hers, soft and expensive and pale pink and two sizes too small across the shoulders, which meant Alice. There was a bruise on her jaw on the left side, gone yellow-green at the edges already from whatever was in the brown bottle. She was holding a mug of something in both hands. Her wand was lying across her knees.

She looked at Leah. Leah looked at her.

Layla lifted the mug an inch, a sort of toast. Leah nodded at her and kept walking.

She went through into the kitchen, which was huge and white and full of copper pans none of them had ever needed until the wolves started coming over, and opened the oven, and there was a tray of lasagna in it the size of a car door with about a third of it gone. She got a fork out of the drawer. She didn't bother with a plate. She stood at the island and started eating it out of the tray.

Esme came in behind her and, without a word, put a plate down next to the tray, and a glass of milk next to the plate, and went back out.

Leah used the plate. Esme was all right.

Behind her she heard the front door open and close twice. Bare feet on the floor. Seth went straight past the kitchen door without looking in, straight to the couch, and she heard Layla say something low, and Seth say something lower, and then the couch creaking as he sat down on the floor in front of it with his back against Layla's shins.

Jacob came into the kitchen.

He was in his cutoffs and nothing else, the way he always was, the way all of them always were. He stood in the doorway with rain on his shoulders, filling it up, and looked at her eating his lasagna.

"Leah," he said.

"Mm."

"What the hell."

She swallowed. "Which part?"

"Which—" He came into the kitchen. He put both hands flat on the island across from her. Behind him, in the front room, she could feel every one of them listening. They didn't even pretend. Vampires never pretended. "*Him*, Leah. Him. How could you imprint on *him*?"

"Sit down, Jake."

"I don't want to sit down! He shot Layla!"

"He shot the ground," said Layla, from the couch.

"He shot *at* Layla!"

"He shot near me," Layla said. "Precision matters, Jacob."

Jacob closed his eyes for a second. Opened them. "He held you down. You and Seth, both of you, on that rock, like bugs on a board. I saw it in Seth's head. And he went through Seth's head like a—like a guy going through your *underwear drawer*, and he's got a vampire in a jar—"

"Circle," said Leah.

"What?"

"It's a circle. On the floor." She took another bite. "The jar's for the finger."

There was a short silence.

From under the piano, Emmett made a noise. It was the noise of a man trying very hard not to laugh and failing.

"That's—" Jacob pushed both hands back through his hair, hard. "Of everybody. Of everybody in the whole world, Leah. You could've had anybody. Some guy at the gas station. Some guy at the clinic. Some guy in a band. And you go and look at—at a British psycho with a gun, who walks on *air*, who stood in that kitchen and told you he's going to do whatever he has to do to the thing on his floor and you're not going to like it—"

She put her fork down.

She did it carefully, on the edge of the plate. Then she straightened up, and came round the end of the island, and stood right in front of him. He had a foot on her as a human. She'd stopped noticing that around the time she'd first put him on his back in the dirt in June.

"Okay," Leah said. "Let's do this. You want to do this, in front of everybody? Let's do it."

"Lee—" from the couch. Seth.

"No. He started it." She looked up at Jacob. "At least mine's not a literal baby vampire hybrid."

The temperature in the house went down about ten degrees. She felt it on her arms.

On the couch, Bella's head turned. Very slowly. On the stairs, Rosalie unfolded from her knees and stood up.

"That's not—" Jacob's face had gone dark red. "Nessie's not—it's not like that, you know it's not like that—"

"I know it's not like that. Everybody in this house knows it's not like that. You've told us it's not like that about nine hundred times." She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. "She's still ten weeks old, Jacob. You imprinted on a baby. Ten weeks ago. In this house. While she was covered in her mom's blood and you had your teeth out, coming down the stairs to kill her." She held his eyes. "And I didn't say anything. Did I? Not one word. I had the whole pack mind. I could've made your life hell. You know what I thought, the next morning, when I phased and saw it in your head?"

Jacob didn't answer.

"*Well,*" Leah said. "*That's Jacob's life.* That was it. That's all I said. Because it's yours. And this one's mine." She held up one finger. "And if you really think about it, mine's the best one any of us has got. Because he's A, not a baby."

A second finger.

"B. He's a human. Mostly. A grown one. With a job and a car and a stove and a tent that's bigger than this house."

A third.

"And C, he's also a wolf." She let that sit. "Sam got a girl who flinches when he raises his voice. Jared got a girl who thinks he hung the moon. Paul got your sister, who's great, and who thinks he's an idiot, which he is. Quil got a toddler who calls him a pony. You got a baby. Seth got a witch who can stop the rain, okay, fine, that's pretty good."

"Thanks, Lee," said Seth.

"I got the one who held two of us down on a rock without looking at us," said Leah. "So take that."

Nobody said anything.

The rain came down on the long glass wall at the back of the house. On the couch, the kid sighed in her sleep and turned her face into Bella's neck.

"And another thing," Leah said, because she wasn't done, and she could feel that she wasn't done, it was all coming up out of her like it had been sitting in a pot for two years with the lid on. "*How could you.* Like it's a thing I did. Like I went shopping. Do you think I chose this, Jake? Do you actually think I looked at that guy on that rock in his stupid suit with his stupid sunglasses, after he called me Lassie, after he put me on my face in the moss, and thought, *yeah, him, I'll take that one*?"

"I didn't say—"

"You said *how could you*."

"I didn't mean you *picked* him—"

"Did you pick her?"

He stopped.

"Did you pick her," Leah said. "Coming down those stairs. Did you stand there and think about it. Did you weigh it up."

His jaw worked. "No."

"No," she agreed. "So don't you stand in this kitchen and talk to me like I did."

Jacob looked at her for a long time. His hands were still flat on the island; she watched them slowly stop pressing down. His face did several things. He was a lot of things, Jacob, and most of them made her want to bite him, but he had never once in his life been able to stay mad at somebody for being right.

"Okay," he said finally. Low. "Okay. That's—fair."

"I know it's fair."

"I'm still allowed to hate him."

"Get in line," Leah said. "I'm still deciding."

That was true. She heard it come out and knew it was true, the way she'd known in the tent that everything coming out of her was true, and she felt Edward hear it from the front room and go very still at the end of the piano.

"If you call my daughter a hybrid again," said Bella, from the couch, in a voice like a knife being put down very carefully on a counter, "in my house—"

"She is one, Bella." Leah turned round. "You call her one. I've heard you. Carlisle wrote it on a chart."

Everybody looked at Carlisle.

Carlisle, by the fireplace, with the brown bottle in his hand, had the grace to look slightly embarrassed. "I did, actually," he said. "For want of a better term. It's on the growth chart. In the study."

Bella looked at him. Then she looked back at Leah. Something in her face shifted, a fraction, and Leah realized that Bella was, very slightly, very much against her will, trying not to laugh.

"Fine," Bella said. "But you're not allowed to say *literal*."

"Deal."

On the stairs, Rosalie sat back down. Leah saw her mouth. Rosalie Hale was smiling at her. Not much. A little crooked thing at one corner, gone almost before it was there. The kind of smile you'd give somebody who'd just knocked the guy you didn't like on his ass.

Leah went back to the lasagna.

---

She'd got through about half of what was left in the tray when Edward said, from the front room, "Leah."

She didn't turn round. "What."

"There's a part of your mind I can't hear."

She heard the room go quiet again. A different kind of quiet. The rest of them had felt like a family having a fight; this was the other kind, the kind she'd felt on the field in June, when Jasper had stood in front of a bunch of wolves in a clearing and told them how to kill a vampire.

She put the fork down and went back out into the front room with the plate in her hand.

Edward was looking at her. He'd come away from the piano. He was standing in the middle of the room with his head a little on one side, like a man straining to hear something in another room through a wall.

"I could hear it in Seth's head," he said, "and Jacob's, when you were phased. The gray place. I thought it was a wolf thing. Something the pack mind does." He shook his head slowly. "It's in you. Here. In your own head. It's—" He stopped, frowning. "With Bella, there's nothing. Everywhere. Silence. Like a room with no air in it. This is one room in a house where every other door's open, and this one's locked. And it's cold. I've never heard anything cold in a mind before. Minds aren't cold."

"It's his," Leah said.

"What did he put in your head?" said Jacob, from the kitchen doorway.

"Oh my *God*, Jake, he didn't put anything in my head."

"Then what—"

"It came with him. Okay? It came down the rope. It's his stuff. Work stuff." She put the plate down on the coffee table, next to Carlisle's bag. "The stuff he's not allowed to tell anybody."

"Who isn't he allowed to tell?" said Jasper.

It was the first thing Jasper had said. He said it from the back of the room, by the glass, in that low slow voice with the South in the bottom of it, and he was looking at her the way he looked at everything, like he was working out where it would bleed.

"Anybody," said Leah.

"Who says?"

"The people he—"

She stopped.

She hadn't decided to stop. She'd been going to say it. She'd been going to say *the people he works for, he works for a*—and the words had come up her throat in order, easy, like they'd come up his throat in the kitchen with the beer, and they'd gotten about as far as the back of her tongue.

And something closed on her throat.

There was no pain. It was just there, all at once, under her jaw, around the front of her neck, cool and heavy and absolutely certain, like a hand. A big hand, an old one, with its thumb resting very lightly on her windpipe, the way you'd rest your thumb on the trigger of a gun you weren't going to fire unless somebody made you.

She tried to push the next word through it anyway, because she was Leah Clearwater and nobody had ever told her to shut up and gotten away with it.

The thumb pressed.

Just a little. Just enough that the word stopped, and the air stopped behind it, and she stood in the middle of the Cullens' front room with her mouth open and nothing coming out of it. She heard herself make a small sound. She put her hand to her throat. There was nothing there. There was nothing there at all, and she couldn't breathe.

"Leah!" Seth was up off the floor.

"Stop," said Layla.

She'd come up off the couch. Leah didn't see her do it. One second Layla was sitting there wrapped in a blanket and the next second she was on her feet with the blanket on the floor and her wand in her hand, pointing it at Leah's throat, and her face had gone the way it had gone on the ridge. Smooth. Cold. Working.

"Stop talking," Layla said. "Leah. Look at me. Stop trying. Don't push it. Whatever you were about to say, let it go."

Leah let it go.

The thumb lifted. It went away, slowly, the way a hand comes off your shoulder, and the air came back into her all at once and she bent over with her hands on her knees and coughed until her eyes watered.

When she straightened up, Layla had murmured something at the tip of her wand and was looking at Leah's neck through the air in front of it, the way you'd look at something through a magnifying glass. Her lips had gone thin.

"Okay," Layla said quietly. "Okay. Yeah."

"What," said Jacob. He'd come out of the kitchen. He was standing behind Leah with his hands open at his sides like he wanted to hit something and there was nothing to hit. "What is that? What just happened?"

"There's a band on her," Layla said. "Around her throat. Gray. It's old." She lowered the wand a little. "It's a binding. An oath-binding. Somebody swore something, a long time ago, on something old, and it's holding them to it. Holding *him*. And somehow it's sitting on her now too." She looked at Leah. "I've seen them on tombs. In Peru, mostly. On priests. The kind that gets tighter the harder you push. Some of them don't stop."

"Don't stop," said Seth.

"Don't stop tightening." Layla's eyes didn't leave Leah's throat. "So you don't push it. Ever. Okay? Whatever's behind that wall in your head, you leave it there. You don't try to say it. You don't try to write it down. You don't try to get clever and say it sideways. You just don't."

"Who would do that?" said Rosalie. "Who makes a person swear something like that?"

Layla didn't answer her.

Leah looked at her. She'd been in Layla's head, sort of, for two weeks, through Seth; she knew what Layla's face looked like when she was thinking hard about something. It looked like this. And Edward, at the end of the piano, had gone so still he looked like he'd been switched off, and he was looking at Layla too, and a funny expression was coming over his face.

"You're thinking about bees," Edward said.

"I'm thinking about bees," Layla agreed.

"Very hard."

"Very, very hard, Edward. I'm going to be thinking about bees for a long time. You should get used to it." She lowered her wand all the way, and sat back down on the couch, and picked the blanket up off the floor and pulled it round her shoulders again. Her hands were steady. Her face wasn't, quite. "Leave it alone. All of you. Whoever he swore it to, I don't want to know, and I'm not going to guess, and if you're smart you won't either."

Leah stood in the middle of the room with her hand on her throat.

And twenty miles off, at the other end of the rope, she felt him go still.

She hadn't thought about him. She'd been too busy not breathing. But she felt him now, felt him clear as if he'd walked in the door: standing at that black iron stove with his hands flat on it, and all at once not keeping time to anything. He'd felt it. He'd felt the thumb close on her throat. She could feel him feel it, a cold flat shock going through him like he'd put his hand on a fence and found it live, and under the shock something else, something she'd only ever felt in him once before, on the floor of a round room full of jars, when a vampire smiled up at her and said *a heartbeat*.

It was very quiet. And it was very, very angry. And none of it was pointed at her.

Then she felt him decide something.

She felt it the way you'd feel a man ten feet away from you in the dark make a fist and then open it. In the wall in her head, the cold old hand that had been holding everything of his so carefully, so gently, the way he'd have held it himself, moved. One finger lifted. Just one. Like somebody letting go of a coin.

And something dropped through.

It fell out of the wall into the middle of her own head like a stone dropped into a pond. Small. Plain. Something she'd had in her hand since the middle of the afternoon, when he'd stood in the rain by his tent and tried to say one name and his mouth had said another one instead. She'd known it for six hours. She'd just never been allowed to put it anywhere anyone else could see.

She hadn't decided to think it. She didn't have to. It was just there, out in the open, the way your own name is there when somebody calls it across a parking lot.

At the end of the piano, Edward Cullen's eyes went wide.

Leah saw it land. She saw him hear it. She watched it go through him, and she watched him not understand it, and then she watched him look at her, and look at her throat, and work it out. She watched him realize what the man at the end of the rope had just done, from twenty miles away, without asking anybody, because a woman he'd known for six hours couldn't breathe.

"Harry," Edward said slowly. "His name is Harry. Harry Potter."

On the floor by the couch, Seth flinched.

It was a small flinch. Nobody but Leah would have seen it. But she saw it, because she'd done it herself, in the rain, at a quarter to two in the afternoon, standing barefoot by a tent with her mouth open, when a stranger with green eyes had said *Harry* and it had been her dad's name.

She'd put that away. She'd put it away fast and hard, the way you put your hand over a cut before you look at it. She hadn't let herself think about it once, not in the tent, not in the specimen room, not on the run down. And now it was out in the middle of the Cullens' front room, and Seth was looking up at her from the floor with their dad's eyes, and she couldn't look back at him.

She didn't have to. Because on the couch, Layla Quinn had made a sound like she'd been punched in the stomach.

### II. Layla

*The Cullen house — 7:32 p.m.*

The mug went over.

She forgot she was holding it. Her hands went slack and it tipped and the tea went across the gray blanket and into her lap, hot, and she didn't feel it. She was looking at Edward.

"Say that again," Layla said.

Edward looked at her. "Harry Potter," he said. "That's what's in her head. That's his name." He hesitated, which she'd never seen him do. "You know it."

"Do I know it."

She heard herself laugh. It came out high and wrong, the way it had on the ridge, and she put her hand over her mouth to stop it and it came out between her fingers anyway.

Everybody was looking at her. She could feel them looking. Seth on the floor, with his back against her legs, twisting round to look up at her face. Leah in the middle of the room with her hand still at her throat. The doctor by the fireplace with the bottle of Murtlap she'd made Seth drive to Calawah Way to fetch, and that she'd been rubbing into her own ribs an hour ago with her teeth clenched while Esme Cullen held up a towel. The blond woman on the stairs who looked at you like you were a smudge on a window. The scarred one by the glass. Bella, with the baby.

They were looking at her the way you'd look at somebody who'd just had a seizure at a dinner party.

"I threw a Stunner at Harry Potter," Layla said.

Nobody said anything.

"I threw a Disarming Charm at Harry Potter. I tried to wrap Harry Potter's legs up in ropes. I threw a *Disjunction* at Harry Potter." She took her hand off her mouth. "He said it was clever. He said *did you make that.* He said it like he was—like I'd shown him a drawing." She looked down at her lap. The tea had soaked through the blanket and the borrowed pink sweater and the borrowed jeans and was going cold against her thighs. "I'm going to throw up."

"Please don't," said Esme gently. "That sweater's Alice's."

"I'll buy her a new one. I'll buy her ten."

"Who," said Emmett, from under the piano, "is Harry Potter?"

Layla turned her head and looked at him.

He was lying on his back with his hands laced on his chest, looking at her upside down, with an expression of complete, friendly interest. He had no idea. None of them had any idea. She looked round the room and watched it sink in that she was the only person in this house who knew what that name meant, and she had the strangest feeling, like standing at the top of a staircase in the dark and reaching for a step that wasn't there.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Hang on."

She put the mug on the coffee table. She wiped her hands on the wet blanket, which didn't help. She thought about how to say it to people who'd never heard any of it.

"There was a war," she said. "In Britain. In our world. In the seventies, and then again in the nineties. There was a man—a wizard—who wanted to—" she waved her hand, "—run things. Make our people rule yours. Kill anybody who got in the way, Muggleborns, Muggles, anybody. He called himself something else, but his name was Tom Riddle. He was very good. He was one of the three best duelists who's ever lived. There's—everybody who duels knows the three names. I told you on the ridge, Seth. Or I thought it. I don't remember which."

"You thought it," Seth said. "The bead was still going. I felt it. Dumbledore and—"

"Dumbledore, Grindelwald, Riddle." She nodded. "Riddle went to kill a family, in 1981. A couple and their baby. He killed the husband. He killed the wife. Then he pointed his wand at the baby and used the Killing Curse, which nobody had ever survived, ever, in recorded history. And it bounced. It hit the baby and it came back off him and it hit Riddle instead. And the baby lived, with a scar on his forehead like a lightning bolt, and Riddle was gone. For thirteen years."

The rain ran down the long glass.

"He came back," Layla said. "Riddle. In the nineties. And there was another war. And at the end of it, in 1998, at a school in Scotland, in front of about a thousand people, the baby, who was seventeen by then, stood in a hall full of dead people and fought him one on one, and killed him."

Silence.

"That baby," said Emmett slowly. "That was—"

"Harry Potter," Layla said. "The Boy Who Lived. That's what they called him. That's what we all called him. The *New York Ghost* had his picture on the front page for a week. Skinny kid with glasses and a scar. Every kid in America who grew up in my world grew up on that story. I was fourteen. I cut the picture out." She heard herself say it and closed her eyes. "Oh my God. I cut the picture out and stuck it in my trunk at school."

"He doesn't wear glasses," said Seth.

"I noticed."

"And he doesn't have a scar. I was looking right at him. On the rock. When he took the sunglasses off. There's nothing on his forehead."

"I noticed that too, Seth."

"So maybe it's somebody else? Maybe it's, like, a different Harry Potter?"

"It's not a different Harry Potter." She opened her eyes. "I watched a man take my best spell apart with two fingers. It's him."

She'd known it. That was the worst of it. Sitting there in the wet blanket, she found she'd known it, somewhere underneath, since the moment on the ridge when she'd been lying in the ferns with her ears ringing and the cold voice in her head had said *the list isn't closed*. Some part of her had gone straight down the list and gone *who else*, and found one name. One name that had never been on it, because the list was for people who'd fought on that level and Calloway had always said, with her mouth pursed up, that a seventeen-year-old boy who'd got lucky in a hall in Scotland didn't count, nobody knew what had actually happened in that hall, the accounts were a mess, it was probably a fluke, it was probably the Elder Wand, it was probably—

He'd been the one to take the third name off the list. Then he'd walked out of that hall and out of the newspapers, and nobody had seen him since.

"He disappeared," she said aloud. "After the war. Nobody knows where. The *Ghost* used to run a story every six months or so. He's dead. He's in Tibet. He's married. He's not married. He's teaching. He's in hiding. Supposedly he turned down an Order of Merlin, First Class, which nobody does. The British papers said he'd *gone abroad to study*. For eight years. Nobody's had a photograph of him since 1999."

"So what does he do?" said Jacob.

Layla looked at him.

"Nobody knows," she said. "That's the thing. The most famous wizard alive, and in eight years nobody's been able to find out what he does for a living." She picked up the wet mug again just to have something in her hands. "Which tells you something."

"What does it tell you?" said Jasper.

"Nothing I'm going to say out loud."

"Bees," said Edward softly.

"Bees, Edward."

---

She watched them take it in.

She'd seen people take in things that were too big before. She'd sat with a rancher in Socorro once while he took in that the thing in his root cellar that had eaten his dog was older than the state of New Mexico, and she'd watched a lot of people in a lot of places learn that the world had another world folded up inside it like a letter in an envelope. She knew what it looked like when something didn't go in. People's faces got very calm. They got practical. They started talking about what to do, because talking about what to do was easier than looking at the thing.

That was what happened now. She watched it happen, all round the room, like a tide coming in.

"So he's a celebrity," said Emmett, sitting up and very nearly not hitting his head on the piano. "Like a wizard celebrity. That's kind of cool, actually."

"It is not *cool*, Emmett."

"I didn't say it was good. I said it was cool. Different thing."

"I don't care if he's famous," said Jacob. He'd gone back to standing in the kitchen doorway with his arms crossed. "I don't care if he's the Wizard of Oz. He's on our land. He's running around in our woods catching vampires in—in snares, like rabbits, and going in people's heads—"

"He can't be everywhere," said Jasper. He'd come away from the window. He said it the way a man might say *the bridge is out*, a fact to be planned around. "Nobody can. Famous people have faces. People with faces have habits. Habits are predictable." He looked at Edward. "We find out where he eats. Where he sleeps. When."

"I've looked," Edward said. "Where he sleeps is in three heads in this room and I can't see it in any of them. It slides."

"Then we follow him."

"I can't see him," said Alice.

Everybody looked at her. She was standing by the glass with her arms wrapped round herself and her small sharp face pinched.

"I've been trying since one o'clock," she said. "Since Seth came up the drive. I tried to see him coming to this house, I tried to see him leaving the peninsula, I tried to see him eating breakfast. I can't see him decide anything. I can't even find where he *would* be. With the wolves it's like smoke. With him there isn't anything there to see."

"Then we leave," said Rosalie.

She said it from the stairs, flatly, the way Layla's mother used to say *we're going home* at a party where somebody had had too much to drink.

"We've left places before," Rosalie said. "We left here once. We can go to Denali for the winter. Or further. He's here for whatever he's here for, and it isn't us. Let him have the woods."

"We're not running from a man in a tent," said Jacob.

"Nobody asked you, dog."

"I'm asking," said Carlisle gently, before Jacob could answer, "what he wants. If he is what Miss Quinn says he is—and I don't doubt her—then he's a man with a history. A man who's been hurt, and who's hurt others, by necessity. A man who, by her account, was a child at war." He set the brown bottle down on the coffee table. "Men like that can be spoken to. If he's here on some business of his own, and it doesn't concern us, then perhaps the best thing is to approach him openly. Through Leah. Invite him here. Talk."

"*Talk,*" said Jacob.

"It has been known to work, Jacob."

"He's got a *vampire in a circle* on his *floor*—"

"Are you guys hearing yourselves?" said Leah.

They all stopped.

She'd sat down at some point, on the arm of the big chair by the window where Charlie always sat on Sundays, with the plate of lasagna on her knee. She hadn't been eating it. She'd been watching them, the way Layla had been watching them, and her face had the look that Layla was starting to know: the jaguar look, from the day she'd driven past the blue house at forty miles an hour.

"Seriously," Leah said. "Are you listening to yourselves right now?"

She put the plate down on the floor.

"Emmett thinks he's cool. Jake wants to run him off. Jasper wants to watch him sleep. Rosalie wants to go to Alaska, and Carlisle wants to have him over for coffee." She looked round at all of them. "You don't get it. None of you were there. Jake, you weren't there, you got there after. Layla was there. Seth was there. I was there."

"Leah—"

"Layla's the best at this there is. In her world. She told Seth, Seth told me. She was a champion. She's taken apart curses on—on tombs, in Mexico, that killed people, that were older than *you*, Carlisle." She jabbed a finger at Layla on the couch without looking at her. "She threw everything she had at him. Everything. As fast as she could. And he didn't move his feet. He stood there and he took it all apart with his fingers, and he didn't even look at her while he did it, because he was looking at Seth. And while he was doing that, he was holding a vampire up in the air behind him like a coffee table, and he was holding me and Seth flat on a rock. A ton of wolf. Without looking at us. Without moving his hands. I fought him the whole time. I fought him till my claws split." She held up her hand. Layla looked; the nails were short and ragged, and two of them were split down to the quick. "Nothing. I couldn't lift my head."

"We saw," said Jacob quietly.

"You saw it in Seth's head. That's not the same." She stood up off the arm of the chair. "He caught a vampire falling out of the sky with one arm. One. He walked across a hole in the ground on nothing. He's got wards up there in the woods that you can't even remember after you've seen them. They opened for me like a door. You want to follow him, Jasper? You can't find where he lives. You want to run him off, Jake? He'd let you. That's the part you're not getting. He'd let you walk all the way up to that tent with Sam and Paul and the whole pack. He'd let you get right up to the door. And then he'd decide what to do with you, and none of you would ever see it coming, because she can't see him." She pointed at Alice. "And he can't hear him." She pointed at Edward. "And I'd bet you anything he'd be sitting in his kitchen with a beer the whole time, listening to his records."

Nobody said anything.

"I sat in that room," Leah said, quieter. "With the jars. I watched him work. You all saw some of it. You saw maybe half. And I'm telling you, I've seen Jasper fight, and I've seen Sam fight, and I've seen a hundred of those newborn things come screaming out of the trees in June, and I've never been scared of anybody. Never. I'm scared of him." She stopped. "Or I would be."

"Would be," said Carlisle carefully.

"Would be," Leah said. "If he wasn't mine."

She looked at Jacob.

"That man is tied to me," she said. "Both ends. You felt it. You all felt it, in Seth's head, you felt it go down into him and come back. He can't lie to me. He tried. He stood there in the rain and tried to tell me his name was something else and his mouth wouldn't let him. He can't hurt me. He can't hurt anybody I love, not without it going straight back down the rope and into him. He told me things in that kitchen that he's never told anybody in his whole life, things that thing on my throat is there to stop him telling, and it didn't stop him, because it thinks I'm *him*." She spread her hands. "So the scariest thing any of you is ever going to meet in your whole long, boring, dead lives is on our side. Because of me."

She let it sit for a second.

"See?" said Leah Clearwater. "Better."

---

Layla watched the room re-figure itself.

It was like watching a tide go out the way it had come in. All those faces that had gone calm and practical slid, one by one, into something else. Emmett, under the piano, stopped looking like a man watching a movie. Jasper's eyes went to Leah and stayed there and narrowed, the way they did, Layla had noticed, when he was revising a number. Rosalie looked at Leah for a long moment and then looked away out the window. Esme put her hand on Carlisle's arm.

And Jacob, in the kitchen doorway, stood there with his arms crossed and his jaw going, and Layla watched him have the same argument with himself she'd watched him have on the ridge, about the deal. She watched him lose it.

"Even so," said Jasper.

Leah turned to look at him.

"Even so." His voice was even. "I believe you. I believe every word. I'd be a fool not to, after what Seth showed Edward." He came a step closer. "But you said it yourself. He's here for something. He works for somebody, somebody who made him swear an oath strong enough to choke you from twenty miles away. Men like that get orders. Orders change. If whoever it is tells him to come up that drive—"

"He won't."

"If they do. We need to know what we'd do. That's all I'm saying. A plan for if he turns."

"Yeah," said Jacob slowly. "Yeah. Jasper's right. Sam needs to know about this. About him. Both packs. If he so much as looks at the kid, or at Charlie, or—we need to be ready. We need to be able to—"

"Jake," said Leah.

Something in the way she said it made him stop.

"You know how this works, right?" Leah said. "You do know?"

"Know what?"

"What was that one law." She tipped her head. "The one that stopped Sam. In September. When he was coming up this drive with the whole pack behind him to kill the baby and Bella with her. You were standing right there." She pointed at the bottom of the stairs. "What was it that stopped him, Jake? Remind me. What's the law?"

Layla didn't know what she meant. She didn't know the law; Seth had told her a lot, over five weeks, but not that. But she saw Jacob's face, and she saw him know exactly what Leah meant, and she saw it hit him like a bucket of cold water over the head.

He didn't say anything.

"Say it," said Leah.

"No wolf," Jacob said. He said it slowly, like it hurt. "Harms another wolf's imprint. Ever."

"The oldest one we've got," Leah said. "The one nobody's ever broken. Not once. Not in all the stories. You stood right there and hid behind it and it's the only reason she's alive." She pointed at the sleeping child on Bella's shoulder without looking. "And it's the only reason Sam's not still up here with Paul and Jared trying to get through the door."

On the couch, Bella had gone very still.

"So here's your plan," Leah said. "If he turns. Which he won't. Nobody touches him. Not you. Not Sam. Not Paul if he gets mad. Not anybody with a tail. You want to run him off the peninsula, you go through me, and through the same law that's keeping your kid breathing. And you can tell Sam I said so. In those words."

"That law doesn't bind us," said Rosalie, from the stairs, flatly, the way you'd state the weather.

"No," said Leah. "But your treaty does. Your treaty's with Jacob's pack." She turned her head and looked up the stairs at Rosalie, and Layla saw something pass between the two of them that she couldn't name, two women who didn't like each other recognizing the shape of each other's teeth. "And I'm in Jacob's pack."

There was a long silence.

"She's right," said Edward.

Everyone looked at him.

"She's right," he said again. He'd been standing very still all through it, listening. To Leah, Layla supposed; to whatever Leah was thinking, which she'd have bet her grandmother's hives was exactly what Leah was saying. "Any move against him is a move against her. Any move against her breaks the treaty. Any break in the treaty—" He looked at his daughter, asleep on his wife's shoulder. "We all know where that goes."

"Then we wait," Carlisle said. "And we speak to him. When he's willing."

"He'll be willing," said Leah.

"How do you know?"

Leah hesitated. Layla saw her hesitate, and saw her decide something, and say it anyway.

"Because he wants to see the kid."

Bella was on her feet.

Layla didn't see her stand up. Nobody would have. One moment Bella was sitting on the couch, and the next she was standing three feet from Leah with Renesmee clutched to her chest, still asleep, and her gold eyes gone wide and black at the center, and her lips pulled back off her teeth. It was the first time Layla had seen any of them look like what they were. She'd known. She'd known since the gas station. Knowing was different from seeing a girl younger than her stand up off a couch faster than a bullet with murder in her face.

Her wand was in her hand. She didn't remember it getting there. The Thunderbird feather inside it was screaming.

"*What,*" said Bella.

"Bella," said Edward.

"Why does he want to see her? What does he want with her? Has he—Leah, has he *seen* her?"

"Sit down, Bella."

"Has he *seen my daughter*—"

"Yes," said Leah.

She didn't step back. Layla would think about that later. Bella Cullen was standing in front of her three feet away looking like the end of the world, and Leah Clearwater didn't move an inch.

"In October," Leah said. "From a ridge. A long way off. She was on Jake's back, holding on to his fur with both hands. He said she was laughing." Leah's voice had gone strange. Quiet. "That's what he said. That's all he said about her, in his kitchen. That she was laughing."

"He's been *watching*—"

"He's been watching everything, Bella. For two months. That's what he does. And today—" She stopped. Layla saw her throat move. Saw her hand come up halfway to it, and stop, and go back down. Saw her decide not to push. "Today he did something. For her. That I can't tell you about. Because of that." She touched her neck once, lightly. "But I watched him do it. And I'm telling you, he's not going to hurt her. He likes her. He likes her more than he likes any of you, and that's not saying a lot, but it's something." She looked at the sleeping child. "Leave it at that."

Bella stood there. Edward had come up behind her and put his hand on her shoulder, and she was shaking under it, Layla could see, a fine fast tremor all down her body like a struck wire.

Then, slowly, she sat back down.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

---

Leah left at a quarter past eight.

Layla followed her out. She didn't decide to; she was just up off the couch with the wet blanket round her shoulders and out the door onto the porch after her, barefoot, and the cold hit her face and she was glad of it.

Leah was standing at the top of the steps looking out at the dark lawn. She didn't turn round.

"What," she said.

"Nothing."

"You followed me out here for nothing."

"I followed you out here because if I sit in that room another five minutes thinking about bees I'm going to start screaming." Layla came up beside her. The rain had eased off to a drizzle. Out on the lawn, past the porch light, the dark was very dark. "Can I ask you something?"

"Depends."

"Is he okay?"

Leah turned her head and looked at her.

"With you," Layla said. "Is he—you can feel him. Right? Seth says you can feel each other. Is he okay?"

"Why do you care?"

Layla thought about it. She thought about a picture of a skinny kid with glasses, cut out of the *New York Ghost* with a pair of nail scissors by a fourteen-year-old in a dormitory in the Berkshires, and stuck to the inside of a trunk lid with Spellotape, where it had stayed for four years until the tape gave out. She thought about the eight years of nothing after. She thought about a man standing on a ridge in a gray suit, holding her wand in his hand, turning it over, and telling her she was good, and that she'd picked the wrong day.

"Every story I ever read about him," she said, "was about what everybody needed him to do. The whole world. For seventeen years. Kill this, save that, go here, die there." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I never read one about what he got."

Leah was quiet for a moment.

"He got a fry-up and a beer," she said. "And a slug called Gary. And me." She looked back out at the lawn. "He's okay. He's at his stove. He's pissed off. Not at me." A pause. "He's about to go somewhere. I can feel him putting his coat on."

"Where?"

"Didn't say." Leah's mouth went up at one corner. "He's going to clean up your mess on the ridge. He said he would. Make it look like lightning."

"My mess."

"Your mess. He said you were loud."

Layla started to laugh. It hurt her ribs. She did it anyway.

"Tell him," she said, when she could. "Tell him I'll pick up. When he rings. Whenever it is. I'll pick up."

"He knows," said Leah.

She went down the steps and across the lawn into the dark, and behind the big cedar at the edge of the trees Layla heard the canvas sound, the tearing, and then something heavy and fast went away west through the woods toward the coast, and was gone.

She stood on the porch for a while after. The bees would be wanting her. The kitchen floor at home was still covered in broken glass and October honey. She'd left the radio on.

She thought: *Harry Potter has my grandmother's address.*

She thought: *I owe Harry Potter a favor.*

Then she went back inside, because it was cold, and because Seth would be wondering, and because there was nothing at all she could do about either of those things except, possibly, think about bees.

## Part Two: Off the Lead

---

### III. Harry

*The tent — 7:31 p.m.*

He'd laughed out loud at *a literal baby vampire hybrid*.

He'd been standing at the range with side two going, *Kashmir* building up the way it did, and he'd been half in his own kitchen and half twenty miles off in hers, because he couldn't help it and had stopped trying. He couldn't hear what she heard unless he went into her head, and he wasn't going into her head. He could feel her, though. He could feel the shape of her the way you feel the shape of a room in the dark. And when she'd turned round in that kitchen and squared up to the ginger one, he'd felt her plant her feet, and felt her temper come up, clean and hot and enjoying itself, and he'd felt every word of it go out of her like rounds out of a magazine.

He'd got *also a wolf, so take that*: the shape of it, the feeling she'd had saying it, which was about the same feeling he'd had in Belfast in 2002 when he'd put a Ministry of Defence lieutenant colonel on his arse in a car park in front of eleven witnesses.

He'd laughed. Standing alone at his range with his hands on the iron and nobody to hear it but the slug.

Then the oath had closed on her throat, and he'd stopped laughing.

He'd felt it go. Down the line, cold and old and patient, the way it went when it went for him. He'd felt it settle round her neck. He'd felt her not understand what it was. He'd felt her push, because of course she'd pushed, and he'd felt the thumb come down on her windpipe, gently, the way it would have come down on his. And for about two seconds, twenty miles away, at the other end of a rope he'd had for six hours, a woman who'd been running alone in the dark her whole life couldn't breathe, because of something he'd sworn on a rock in a room with no windows when he was eighteen.

He'd stood very still.

Then he'd looked at it. Properly. The way he'd been trained to look at a thing that was doing something he didn't like: from the outside, coldly, all of it at once.

What the oath held, in her head, it held because it was his. Department business. That was what it was built for. Level Nine, Volterra, the circle on the floor, the word *strike*. It was holding those exactly the way it held them in him, and it would go on holding them till one of them died, and there wasn't a thing he could do about any of that and he didn't want to.

But it had his name in there, too.

He'd looked at that for a while. And worked out, standing there with the record going, who'd been holding his name all this time. Him. Eight years he'd been guarding it. Eight years of being Evans and Black and Hollis and Brennan and once, for three weeks in Oslo, a man called Peregrine Thrupp. Eight years of treating the name his mother had given him like a piece of classified hardware, a thing you kept in a locked drawer and took out once a year to look at. He'd been holding it like it was the Department's. So the oath, which didn't know any better, had picked it up along with everything else and held it the same way.

It had been his before the Department ever heard of him. It was the one thing he'd walked in there with.

So he'd let go of it.

That was all. He'd opened his hand, in his head, the way you'd let a coin drop off your palm. And he'd felt it go down the line into her, and he'd felt the oath let it pass, a little puzzled, like a guard at a door watching a man walk out with something he'd come in with.

He'd felt her catch it. He'd felt the room go quiet around her.

He'd felt, a second after that, very faintly, from very far off, somebody on the other side of her make a sound like they'd been kicked.

"Yeah," Harry had said to the range. "That'll be Quinn."

Then he'd stood there a while longer, with *Kashmir* winding down, and thought about what he'd just done.

He'd just given his real name to a coven of vampires that London had in a file, a pack of shapeshifters London didn't know existed, and an American witch who'd been on the books of MACUSA since she was eleven. In wartime that was a hanging offense. In peacetime, in the Department, it was the kind of thing they didn't hang you for because they didn't need to; they just stopped telling you things, one at a time, until one morning you came in and your door didn't open.

He waited to feel something about it. Same as with the specimen.

He felt her breathe. That was what he felt. He felt her, twenty miles away, getting her breath back, coughing, and the thumb lifting off her throat. And something in the bottom of his chest, which had come up onto its feet when the oath went down the line, lay back down.

"Right," Harry said. "Well. That's done."

He took the record off. He put it back in its sleeve.

He went to get his coat.

---

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — 8:06 p.m.*

He came out of the turn onto the granite slab in the dark and the rain, and stood still, and listened.

Nothing. Rain on rock. The river, a long way down. Hemlock dripping. No heartbeat bigger than an owl's anywhere in half a mile, and the owl was up the slope to the north eating something.

He didn't light his wand. He didn't have it out. He'd been seeing in the dark better than most people since round about the time the other thing had started, and it had got better every year, and on a night like this, with the cloud low and no moon, the ridge came up gray on gray around him like an old photograph: the slab, the stunted trees, the black hole of the crater twenty feet off with the rain pooling in the bottom of it.

He went and looked at the crater first.

Six feet across, about three deep. The rock round the lip of it crazed and scorched. Black mud thrown out in a ring, already going soft in the rain. The ferns at the far edge flattened in a long furrow where she'd gone through them, and at the end of the furrow the nurse log, with a dent in its moss the shape of a woman's back.

He'd built that round in August. He'd tested it on Cornish granite. This was the first time he'd fired it at anything with a person standing behind it.

She'd had her shield up. He'd known she had her shield up; he'd read it as it came up, it was a good one, three-braided, and he'd put the round two feet in front of her so the shield would take the full face of it and she wouldn't. He'd known what it would do. He'd calculated it, standing there with her Disjunction still fizzing on his fingertips, in about a quarter of a second.

He looked at the dent in the log.

Then he got to work.

The bead was where he'd seen it go, at the edge of the slab where the boy had been pinned. Two halves of black glass in the moss, split clean down the middle, each one about the size of a fingernail. He crouched and picked them up. They were still faintly hot. Spent magic, the ghost of it, a curl of gold in the dark like the last of a match. He turned them over on his palm. Obsidian. She'd brought the stone from New Mexico; it had been in the boy's head, all of it, her sitting at her grandmother's table till four in the morning with the old words. Nice work. Simple and strong, one shot, built to be bitten. The leather band was there too, in three pieces. He picked those up as well and put the lot in his inside pocket.

Then the crater.

It took him about four minutes. He did it the slow way, which was the quiet way: no big sweeping charm, nothing that would light up on anybody's instruments, just stone and soil persuaded back into the hole a handful at a time, packed down, the scorched rock round the rim flaked off and ground to grit and scattered. He called the moss back over it from the edges, a thread at a time, the way you'd darn a sock. When he was done there was a low hump in the ground that would look, to anybody walking past in daylight, like a place where a root had rotted out.

The boulder she'd blown apart, he couldn't put back. A thing that had been one rock and was now two hundred rocks didn't want to be one rock again, and if you made it, it remembered. So he left the pieces where they were and went to work on the hemlock instead.

There was a big one about thirty yards up the ridge, standing on its own, older than the others, its top already dead and silvered from some old fire. He stood under it and looked up at the clouds.

He didn't do weather much. Weather was big and stupid and it held grudges. But it had been raining on this peninsula for sixty-six days, and the cloud over the ridge was low and heavy and full of the kind of charge that never quite came to anything in a Pacific Northwest November, and it didn't take much. He reached up into it, into the dark over the hemlock, and found the charge, and gathered it, slowly, the way you'd gather a rope hand over hand, until it was sitting in the bottom of the cloud like water in the bottom of a bag.

Then he let it go.

The bolt came down out of the dark and hit the dead top of the hemlock and the whole ridge went white. The sound was enormous. It went off the rock and down the valley and came back up it twice. The top twelve feet of the tree came apart in a spray of splinters and burning bark and the trunk below split down the middle with a noise like a giant tearing a phone book, and the charge ran down it into the ground and out across the rock in a branching scar, glassy, smoking, toward the slab, toward the place where the boulder had been.

He stood in the steaming rain and watched it burn for a bit.

Then he went and walked the scar and dirtied it where it needed dirtying, and sat on the slab, and swept.

That was the real work. The rest was set dressing. On any instrument worth the name, what had happened on this ridge at about ten to twelve that morning had been very loud: a long-range Apparition straight onto the slab from fourteen miles off, a dozen offensive spells in a minute, a Disjunction, which was a thing nobody'd ever measured before, and his own round, which was worse. Most of his own work cleaned up after itself; he'd built it that way. Hers hadn't. Hers was all over the ridge like paint. He sat with his eyes shut and his hands on his knees and went over all of it, the whole half acre, and drew it in. Fold by fold. Stunner, Stunner, Incarcerous, Confringo. The Disjunction, which he took a moment over, because it really was very good, and then folded up with the rest. He fed the lot into the sinks he had running on the peninsula, which ate it, the way they'd been eating everything he'd done for two months.

What was left was the lightning. And under the lightning, because he'd put it there on purpose, a faint, ragged, natural sort of smear, the kind you'd get anywhere a big charge hit a place where the old lines in the ground ran close to the surface. If somebody had been watching this ridge at ten to twelve, they'd have a spike. Now they had a spike with a reason.

He opened his eyes.

Last thing. He went back to the slab and put his hand flat on the granite where the snare had come up, and felt for the stone under it. Still there. Still tied in. He reset it: wound the line back down into the rock, primed it, keyed it, red, for anything that had been feeding on people. It took about a minute. Somewhere far off in the back of his mind, the whole lattice twitched once as the knot on the ridge came back up to tension, and then hummed, and was quiet.

He stood up and looked round the ridge one more time. Rain. Rock. A burning tree. A hump in the moss.

Then he turned on his heel and went to get his car.

---

The Explorer was where he'd left it eight hours ago, in the logging turnout a quarter mile up the Bogachiel road, nose out. There was a ticket under the wiper. He looked at it. It was a Forest Service notice, soaked through, telling him that overnight parking in this turnout without a Northwest Forest Pass was prohibited, and that was all, so he put it in the glovebox with the fishing licence and drove back through Forks at exactly the speed limit, past the diner with its pink sign, past the police station where the lights were on, and out the other side, and up the forest road to the place under the alders where he always parked, about a mile below the bench.

He walked the last mile. The wards let him through without a murmur.

They'd let her through like that. He thought about it, walking up through the dark under the spruces with his hands in his coat pockets. They'd opened for her like they'd been expecting her. He'd built those wards. He'd built them so carefully that the best tracker in Volterra could have walked within a hundred yards of this bench and never known it was here. And this afternoon they'd stood aside for a Quileute girl in cut-offs, because something in him had grabbed the line and pulled before he knew he was doing it.

He hadn't decided to do that. He wanted to know what had.

Gary was on the canvas by the flap. Harry looked at him.

"Don't," he said, and went in.

### IV. Harry

*The tent — 9:52 p.m.*

The mirror was on its nail by the telephone where he'd hung it four hours ago.

He stood in the hall and looked at it for a while.

He'd made a cup of tea. He'd drunk half of it. He'd sat in the library in the big chair with his boots up on the fender and the fire going and a book on Bulgarian blood-wards open on his knee, and he hadn't read a word of it. He'd sat there and listened to her run home. He'd felt her leave the Cullen place and go west through the dark, fast, loose, and come down out of the hills above the river and into the village at the mouth of it, and phase, and go in a back door. He'd felt her stand in a kitchen. He'd felt somebody else in the kitchen with her, a smaller warmth, older, and felt Leah tell her something, and felt the somebody else go very still and then say something back, short, and felt Leah not know what to do with it.

Her mother, he thought. *I imprinted,* Leah would've said, because she wasn't one for working up to things, and her mother would've said something like *Okay.* Just that. And Leah would have stood in the kitchen and wanted her to say more, and been glad she didn't.

Then Leah had gone upstairs. He'd felt her lie down. He'd felt her not sleep.

He'd put the book down.

He looked at the mirror now. His own face looked back at him out of the black frame. Two days of stubble. Eyes that looked like they'd been up since half five, which they had.

He took it down off the nail.

And under his breastbone, the thing behind the locks stood up.

It came up fast. It got up off the floor of him all at once, and stood, and he felt every hair down its back come up, and he felt it look at the mirror through his eyes and *growl*. Low. Long. A sound he felt in his own teeth.

He knew what it was growling at. It had been there at twenty to six, lying behind its locks, listening to him stand in this hall and tell an old man in London *no, sir, nothing else*. It had heard him say *you are not there* and *I'm not there, sir*. It knew what London was. London was the thing that sent him places. London was the thing that could, any morning it liked, send a grey sheet of paper with a single line of spidery handwriting on it, and he'd be on a plane by lunchtime, and twenty miles of forest between him and her would turn into five thousand miles of ocean.

It didn't want him picking up that mirror. It wanted him to hang it back on the nail and go to bed and never pick it up again.

"Lie down," Harry said to it.

It didn't lie down.

"I know," he said. "I know what it is. I know what he is. Lie down."

It put its weight against the first lock, lightly. Letting him know.

He stood in the hall with the mirror in his hand and thought about the old man behind the desk, in the green lamplight, at two in the morning, writing. Saul Croaker had been in the Department since 1955. He'd taken Harry on in the summer of 1998, three months after the war, when Harry was eighteen and couldn't sleep more than two hours at a stretch and had a habit of walking into rooms and checking where all the exits were before he said hello. Croaker had sat him down in the little wood-panelled office and looked at him for about a minute with those tap-water eyes, and then said: *They're all going to want you to be something, Potter. The Aurors. The Prophet. The Ministry. They'll want you to be the boy on the front page. I don't care what you are. I want to know what you can find out.* And he'd handed Harry a file, and the file had been about a door on Level Nine that had been locked since 1743 and nobody had ever got open, and Harry had got it open in five months, and it had been the best five months of his life.

Croaker had never once lied to him. Not in eight years. He'd withheld things, constantly; he withheld things the way other people breathed. But when he told you something, it was so.

And at twenty to six this evening, Harry had stood in this hall and looked him in the eye and said *no, sir, nothing else.*

"I can't leave it," he told the thing in the dark. "I know you want me to. I can't."

He held the mirror up.

"Croaker," he said.

The thing behind the locks bared its teeth, and leaned, and he held it, and the glass went gray and dark and then it was the wood-panelled room again with the green lamp and the drifts of paper. Croaker was in the same chair. He was in the same robes. As far as Harry could tell he hadn't moved since twenty to six, except that the pile of paper on his left was taller and the pile on his right was gone.

He looked up.

"Potter," he said. "Twice in one night. People will talk."

"Sir."

"It's six in the morning."

"You're up, sir."

"Clearly." Croaker put his pen down. He looked at Harry out of the mirror, and whatever he saw made him lay both his hands flat on the desk, one on either side of the paper he'd been writing on. "Well?"

"I lied to you," Harry said.

Croaker didn't say anything.

"Earlier," Harry said. "When you asked if there was anything else. I said no. There was a lot else."

"Go on," said Croaker.

Harry went on.

He did it in order. It was the only way he knew how to do anything. He started with the phone. A cheap pay-as-you-go, one number in it, Italian. The specimen under the Imperius on the floor of the circle, the reins in Harry's hands, and a girl called Gianna on the other end saying *Pronto*, and hold music, and then the old man's voice, delighted, like a child opening a present. He told Croaker what Casimir Wolski had said and what Aro had said back, word for word, because he remembered it word for word. *The girl is changed. Nothing to report. Dogs. I go south. Mexico.* And Aro laughing, and saying Caius would be disappointed, and asking, *Nothing else, my friend? Nothing at all of interest, in all that rain?*

"And the specimen tried to tell him," Harry said. "There, at the end. Came up from under the curse like a fish. I held it."

"Tell him what?"

"That there's a child in the Cullen house."

Croaker was quiet for a moment.

"A child," he said.

"Born round about the tenth of September. The mother's the Swan girl. She was human when she got pregnant. The Cullen boy's the father." Harry heard his own voice, very level. "It's half and half. Heartbeat, blood, venom. It reads on the lattice as both. It's growing fast; it looks about two and a half. It can put pictures in your head with its hand. Or so I'm told. And it's very fond of a large ginger shapeshifter called Jacob, who's bonded to it in a way I don't fully understand yet." He paused. "I've known about it for nine weeks, sir. I didn't put it in a report."

"Why not?"

"Because I didn't know what it was." Harry looked at him. "And I wanted to be the one who found out."

Croaker regarded him for a long moment out of the glass.

"That," he said, "is the most Unspeakable thing you've ever said to me."

"Yes, sir."

"And now?"

"Now I'm telling you. In this mirror. Not in a file." Harry's hand had tightened on the frame. He made it loosen. "If it goes in a file, and the file goes anywhere near anybody who talks to anybody who's ever been to Italy, the child's dead. They'd come for it. Aro'd want it on a shelf. The blond one'd want it burnt. And then they'd burn the house it was in, and everybody who'd known about it, and the town for luck. And if that happened, sir, I'd be very upset."

"Very upset," said Croaker.

"Very."

"I see." Croaker picked his pen up. He didn't write anything. He turned it over in his long fingers. "What else?"

"The witch. Quinn. The one I mentioned in the September file, out on Calawah Way." Harry told him about the bee yard. He told him about the night at the Cullen place, the lights, the police chief in the armchair. He told him about the ridge, and the round two feet in front of her boots, and the arrangement he'd made with her lying in the ferns. "She's broken Clause Seventy-Three about four different ways. MACUSA'd have her under the Woolworth Building for fifteen years. I've told her I won't report it. In return she owes me. Curse-breaking, probably, down the line. She's very good."

"You made a private arrangement with a foreign national who's in breach of her own government's Statute."

"Yes, sir."

"Off the books."

"Yes, sir."

"Anything else?"

Harry took a breath.

"I'm compromised," he said.

Behind the locks, the thing in the dark stopped growling.

It went quiet. It stood very still in the bottom of him with its head up and its ears forward. Listening.

"Compromised," said Croaker.

"Yes, sir."

"How?"

"I'm not going to tell you."

Croaker's face didn't change at all.

"There's someone," Harry said. "Here. Since today. I'm not going to tell you who, or what, or how. I won't lie to you about it, so I'm not going to say anything about it at all." He kept his eyes on the old man's eyes. "But you need to know there's a someone. And if it ever comes to it, sir—if it ever comes down to the Department or her—it's her. Every time. Every single time. I won't pretend different."

The lamp hummed in the little wood-panelled room. Somewhere behind Croaker, out of sight, a clock Harry had never seen ticked.

"So you should pull me," Harry said. "Send somebody else. Hext could do it. She's careful. Or Fawley, if you want someone who doesn't mind the rain. I'll brief whoever you send. Everything I've got, the lattice, the specimen, the lot. I'll hand it over clean." He paused. "I'd just rather not be the one who has to choose. That's all."

Croaker said nothing.

He said nothing for a long time. Long enough that Harry, standing in his own hall in the tent with the mirror in his hand, started to count. Ten. Fifteen. Twenty. The old man sat at his desk in the green light with his pen in his fingers and looked at Harry out of the glass, and his face was as still and patient and colorless as it always was, and Harry couldn't read a single thing in it. He'd never been able to. That was one of the reasons he trusted him.

At thirty-one seconds, Croaker put the pen down. He did not, Harry noticed, look in the least surprised.

"Are you finished?" he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Good." Croaker folded his hands on the desk. "I have been in this Department, Potter, since nineteen fifty-five. I have run Unspeakables in the field for thirty-four of those years. Do you know how many of them have lied to me?"

"No, sir."

"All of them," said Croaker. "Every single one. About something. Their whereabouts. Their expenses. Their contacts. Their health. Their women and their men and their drinking and what they did in a hotel room in Vienna in nineteen seventy-two. I've been lied to in this office more times than I've been told the time. Most of it I caught. Some of it I didn't. Some of it I let go because it suited me." He looked at Harry. "Do you know how many of them have rung me back, four hours later, at six in the morning, to tell me they lied?"

Harry didn't say anything.

"One," said Croaker.

The clock ticked.

"You're exactly where you need to be, Potter," the old man said. "That's my assessment. You're on that peninsula, with that lattice, with that specimen, with a coven we need alive and a pack we didn't know existed and a child that by every text in this building should not be possible. And with *someone*, apparently. Fine." He tapped one long finger on the desk, once. "And you rang me back. That's the other half of my assessment. You rang me back to tell me you'd lied, and to tell me where your loyalty would break, and to offer me your job. That integrity, Potter, is exactly why I will never be rid of you. However much you might occasionally like me to be."

Harry stood very still.

"Hext," said Croaker, with the faintest edge of scorn, "would have told me nothing else, sir, and meant it. And three months from now I'd have found out what that cost me from the Italian papers."

"Sir—"

"I'm not finished." Croaker leaned back. "You are not being pulled. You are being kept. If the only way to keep you on that coast is to make you the Department's liaison to a tribe of hereditary shapeshifters, then as of now, you are the Department's liaison to a tribe of hereditary shapeshifters. I'll have the paper drawn up this morning."

"Sir, the Department doesn't have a liaison to—"

"It does now." Croaker picked up his pen again, which meant the matter was closed. "Think, Potter. You've been there sixty-six days. In sixty-six days you've turned up a new species of vampire with a functioning society and a three-thousand-year history. A pack of shapeshifters with a hereditary trigger and a bond we've never seen in any literature. A child that shouldn't exist. An American witch who invents spells in her spare time. And—" he paused, very slightly, "—whatever else. Something on that peninsula draws things. It's been drawing things for a long time, I suspect. I'd imagine you'd be staying a good while longer whatever you'd said to me."

"Yes, sir," Harry heard himself say.

"The phone call," Croaker said, writing. "You made an unauthorized contact with the primary target, under an Unforgivable, using a captive asset, without consultation, on foreign soil, in the middle of a live operation."

"Yes, sir."

"Did it work?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then it was authorized." The pen scratched. "Write it up. Friday. With the rest. The child goes in a separate file that only I'll see, and I'll keep it in my desk, and if anyone in this building so much as asks me what's in that drawer, I will have their memory of the question removed. Is that sufficient?"

Harry's throat had gone tight. The oath was sitting quiet under his collarbone; this was something further down.

"Yes, sir," he said.

"The witch's arrangement stands. She's yours. I'd like to see that Disjunction one day, if she can be persuaded to show it to someone who won't immediately take it apart."

"I'll ask, sir."

"The Americans I'll deal with. I have a woman in New York who owes me a rather large favor from nineteen eighty-nine. Go and have your look at Seattle anyway. Do it quietly. Do it the way you do things."

"Yes, sir."

"And Potter."

"Sir?"

Croaker didn't look up. He went on writing, in the spidery hand, line after line, and when he spoke it was in exactly the same dry flat voice he used for everything.

"I'm not going to ask about her," he said. "I'd have to know, then. And if I knew, I'd have to do something about it." The pen moved. "The same goes for whatever it is you've been keeping on a lead all these years. I've never asked about that either. I run the Department of Mysteries, Potter. Of course I noticed."

The mirror went dark.

Harry stood in the hall and looked at his own face in the glass.

After a while he hung it back up on its nail.

He stood there a while longer, with his hand flat on the wall beside it, and breathed. In. Out. In the bottom of him, behind the locks, the thing in the dark was standing exactly where it had been standing when the conversation began, with its head up and its ears forward, and he could feel it thinking. As much as it thought. It had heard all of that. *Kept.* *Not being pulled.* *A good while longer.* He could feel it turning those over the way a dog turns over a bone it's been given and isn't sure it's allowed.

"There," Harry said to it. "Happy?"

It looked at him.

Then, slowly, it lay down. It put its chin on its paws. And somewhere very far down, so faint he might have imagined it, he felt its tail thump once against the floor of him.

He went and got his suit.

### V. Agnes

*The Smith Tower, Seattle — 11:14 p.m.*

Agnes Sorrell was on the cuff of the second sleeve when the Seattle drum went quiet.

She was sixty-one years old and she'd been on the night desk of the Detection Section of the Pacific Northwest Field Office of the Magical Congress of the United States of America for twenty-nine of them, because nobody else wanted it, and in twenty-nine years she'd learned to knit without looking at her hands. She was making a sweater for her grandson in Spokane. He was four and he liked dinosaurs. The dinosaur on the front was a stegosaurus, in green, and it had come out looking like a pinecone, and she didn't care.

The office was in the top of the tower. In the pyramid, above the observatory, in the floors the building's elevator operators had never been asked to stop at in ninety-two years of carrying tourists up and down in their brass cages. You got to it, if you were cleared, by going up to the thirty-fifth floor at night when the Chinese Room was closed, and sitting in the carved chair in the corner that the tour guides called the Wishing Chair, and saying the word. MACUSA had picked that chair in 1914. It was somebody's idea of a joke. The legend said any unmarried woman who sat in it would be married within the year. Agnes had sat in it about six thousand times and had been widowed for eleven years and thought, most nights, that whoever'd made the joke ought to be shot.

The Detection floor was one long room under the slope of the pyramid's roof. Brass drums along both walls, forty-one of them, each the size of a beer keg, each turning slowly on its spindle, each with a long needle riding on a roll of paper and an inked nib that drew a line. One drum for each county in Washington and Oregon and Idaho, more or less. The needles drew the background. The hum. Every square mile of ground in the world had a little bit of the old stuff running under it, ley lines, old workings, the leftovers of every witch and wizard who'd ever lived there, and the drums picked it up the way a seismograph picked up the ground breathing. Most nights the lines were long and lazy and gently wobbly, like a child drawing waves.

You learned to read them. Twenty-nine years. Agnes could look down the row of drums at three in the morning and tell you that somebody in Walla Walla had a still going in the barn that wasn't strictly whiskey, and that the old Makah woman out at Neah Bay was doing her winter warding a week early this year, and that something under the ground at Mount St. Helens was having bad dreams again.

The Seattle drum was on the left wall, fourth from the end. King County. It was always the busiest. Seattle had about nine hundred registered witches and wizards and maybe twice that unregistered, and its line was always jagged, always going, like a city street.

It went flat.

She saw it out of the corner of her eye. She kept knitting for a second, because the eye was quicker than the brain and the brain hadn't caught up. Then it did. She put the needles down in her lap.

The line on the King County drum, which had been a busy, jagged, fizzing scrawl for every one of the ten thousand nights she'd sat at this desk, had gone down, in the space of about two inches of paper, to almost nothing. A hair above flat. A long, low, smooth, quiet line, like the line on a hospital monitor when the patient was asleep.

Like the Olympic drum.

She turned her head and looked across the room at the Olympic drum. Clallam and Jefferson counties, the peninsula. Third from the end on the right wall. She'd been looking at it a lot lately. Since the third week of September, its line had been doing exactly that. Lying down. Still breathing, just below where it ought to be. A quiet patch, sitting over a big piece of the peninsula like a hand laid over a mouth. She'd written it up in September. She'd written it up again in October. She'd written it up a third time two weeks ago, in red ink, and underlined *anomalous suppression, artificial, sustained*, and Director Pryce had read it and sighed at her across his desk and said *Agnes, it's a national park, it's the wettest place in the lower forty-eight, things go quiet up there in the winter*, and filed it.

And then at eight minutes to twelve this morning the Olympic drum had gone off the paper.

She'd been at home, asleep. They'd called her in. She'd stood in front of that drum at two in the afternoon in her coat with her hair still in its pins and looked at five spikes and a long ragged smear of noise that went right off the top of the roll, over the spindle, and ink-blotted the brass, and she'd felt something go up the back of her neck that she hadn't felt since she was a girl. She'd written it up for New York. Event 06-1120-A, Olympic Peninsula, Bogachiel drainage, approx. 11:52 a.m. PST, high-amplitude multiple-source discharge, origin unknown, possibly linked to the sustained suppression noted in memos of 9/22, 10/18, 11/6. She'd underlined *possibly linked* twice. Pryce had said he'd send it east in the morning.

Since then the Olympic drum had gone back to sleep.

And now the Seattle drum had gone to sleep too.

Agnes looked at it.

Then she looked down the row, and up the row, and at the drum next to it, Snohomish County, which was busy, and the one on the other side, Pierce, which was busy. Then she looked back at King County, and leaned forward in her chair, and watched the needle.

Now that she was looking, the quiet line had a dip in it. A little trough. And the trough was moving slowly along the paper, a little deeper every turn of the drum, the way the line would dip if you took a sensitive needle and walked something very heavy and very quiet across the floor toward it.

Toward the drum. Toward the tower.

Toward her.

She sat back in her chair and picked her knitting up off her lap and held it in both hands, and looked round the room.

Nobody. Of course nobody. The long room under the slope of the roof, the drums turning, the green-shaded lamps, the rain on the little diamond windows set into the pyramid's sides. The cabinets along the back wall. The registry, in its long oak drawers. The old case files, in the steel ones. Her desk. Her thermos. Her stegosaurus.

The drawer of the registry cabinet marked P–Q was open about half an inch.

She'd closed it. She'd closed it at ten when she'd put away the Pomeroy renewal. She always closed them. She'd been closing them for twenty-nine years.

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked at the old steel case-file cabinet beside it, where the files went to die, and the bottom drawer, the one marked 1930–1939, which nobody had opened since the Truman administration. It was open too. Not by much.

The room was very quiet. Quieter than it should have been. She realized, sitting there, that she couldn't hear the radiator. The radiator under the east windows knocked every ninety seconds like a man trying to get out of a closet, it had done it since 1977, and she couldn't hear it. She couldn't hear the drums turning. She could hear the rain on the windows, and her own heart.

There was a draft on the back of her neck.

Agnes Sorrell did not scream. She'd stopped screaming at things in about 1966. She sat in her chair with her knitting in her hands, and she looked straight ahead at the King County drum, at the little trough in the line that had stopped moving because it had got where it was going, and she said, out loud, in a perfectly level voice:

"I know you're there."

Nothing.

"I know you're there," Agnes said again. "I can see you on my drum. You've brought it with you, haven't you? My quiet patch. The one on the peninsula. You've been sitting in the middle of it since September." She turned her head, slowly, and looked at the empty air beside the registry cabinet. "That's you. That's been you the whole time."

Nothing, for a long moment. The rain.

Then a voice said, from the air beside the registry cabinet, "Yeah. It is."

It was a man's voice. English. Low and a little rough, with gravel in the bottom of it, and not at all bothered.

Agnes's heart was going like a rabbit's. She didn't let it get into her voice. "Are you going to kill me?"

"No."

"Are you going to make me forget?"

A pause.

"Some," said the voice.

She sat with that. She looked down at her knitting. The stegosaurus looked back at her, green, lumpy, ridiculous.

"Do me a favor first," Agnes said.

"What?"

"Tell me I was right."

There was a silence. Longer than the last one. She had the strangest feeling that whoever was standing there in the air by the cabinet, invisible, in her quiet office in the top of the tower, was looking at her properly for the first time. The way you'd look up from a newspaper at a person you'd been talking to without really seeing.

"You were right, Mrs. Sorrell," the voice said. "Sixty-six days. Nobody else in this building noticed. Nobody else in this country noticed, as far as I can tell. You noticed in the first week."

"Nobody else ever does," said Agnes.

"No," said the voice. "They don't, do they."

And then there was a gentle pressure, very gentle, in the middle of her forehead, like a cool hand laid there by somebody checking for fever, and she thought, quite clearly, *oh, he's good*, and then she didn't think anything at all.

---

She woke up at 11:41 with her knitting in her lap.

She was sitting at her desk. The lamp was on. The rain was on the windows. The radiator under the east windows knocked, once, like a man in a closet.

She blinked at the room. She'd dozed off. She did that, sometimes, round about the second sleeve. She was sixty-one.

She looked at the King County drum, which was busy and jagged and fizzing, the way it always was. She looked at the Olympic drum, which was—she frowned. She got up and went over to it and looked closely.

The quiet patch was gone.

The line was back. Long and lazy and wobbly, a child drawing waves, the way the peninsula had looked every winter for twenty-nine years before September. She traced it back along the paper with her finger. It came back up to normal sometime after noon. After the event.

She went back to her desk. Her report was on top of the pile, handwritten, for New York. She read it through.

*Event 06-1120-A, Olympic Peninsula, Bogachiel drainage, approx. 11:52 a.m. PST. High-amplitude discharge consistent with a lightning strike at or near a shallow ley convergence on the ridge above the Bogachiel River. Strike appears to have collapsed the localized dead zone noted in memos of 9/22, 10/18, 11/6, which this analyst now assesses to have been a natural phenomenon, a pressure pocket in the ley associated with unusual seasonal precipitation. Background readings have returned to normal. No further action recommended. A. Sorrell.*

It was her handwriting. Her initials. Her phrasing. *This analyst now assesses.* She always said that. Pryce teased her about it.

She sat and read it twice. It was a good report. It made sense. It was what she'd concluded, she remembered concluding it, standing at the drum at two in the afternoon in her coat. Lightning. A pocket in the ley. Of course. Things went quiet up there in the winter.

She felt very satisfied, and a little tired, and pleased to have it finished.

She picked up her knitting. She got through the rest of the second sleeve and started on the collar. At six, when the day man came on, she put the report in Pryce's tray and put her coat on and went down the Wishing Chair and out through the dark lobby past the brass elevator cages and walked to the bus stop on Third Avenue in the rain.

She was halfway home on the 15 before she realized she was crying a little, very quietly, and couldn't think why. She wiped her face with the end of her scarf and looked out the window at the wet lights going by.

Somebody had said something to her, she thought. Tonight. Something kind.

She couldn't remember who.

### VI. Harry

*The tent — Tuesday, November 21st, 1:12 a.m.*

The registry file on Layla Maeve Quinn had said, in a neat clerk's hand, *Statute compliance: no concerns,* and the date, and Agnes Sorrell's initials. He'd made sure of that. The file from the bottom drawer, 1936, was in his inside pocket, three pages of onionskin and a photograph of a set of very large paw prints in mud beside a dead elk on the Quillayute, with a note in pencil at the bottom: *Investigator recommends no further action. Locals uncooperative. Probably bears.* He'd read it standing in the dark by the steel cabinet with the old woman asleep at her desk six feet away, and then he'd put it in his pocket.

Probably bears. He'd liked that.

He hung the suit up. He hung the coat on its peg. He put the 1936 file on the kitchen table under the salt cellar and the bead halves and the bits of leather beside it, and he stood in the kitchen for a minute in his shirt-sleeves, and then he went and lay down on his bed in his jeans and T-shirt, on top of the blanket, with his boots still on, and looked at the ceiling.

The ceiling of his room was canvas. Army green, the same as the outside. He'd left it that way on purpose when he'd built the room, because he'd liked lying here listening to the rain on it, the way you did in a real tent. He was listening to it now. Steady. Gentle. The drizzle had come back after midnight.

He couldn't sleep.

He'd known he wouldn't. He'd known it standing in the dark in Seattle with his hand on the old woman's forehead, and he'd known it walking back through the wards, and he'd known it the second he lay down. He lay there anyway and gave it a go, because he'd been awake since half five and had fought a curse-breaker and caught a vampire and tortured it and run an Imperius on it down a phone line into Volterra and lied to his boss and then not lied to his boss and broken into a federal facility, and his body was tired. He could feel it. The heavy, sandy, burned-down kind of tired he got after a long job.

His body was tired. The rest of him was pointed southwest like a compass needle.

She was awake.

He could feel it. Twenty miles off, in a small house at the mouth of a river, in a small room at the top of the stairs, lying on her back on a bed that was too short for her, staring at a ceiling. He could feel her not sleeping the way you can feel someone in the bed beside you not sleeping. He could feel her irritation about it. He could feel that some of her irritation was at him.

He could feel, after a while, that she could feel him not sleeping too. And that that was making it worse.

"Brilliant," Harry said to the canvas.

He tried Occlumency. He went through it the way he'd been doing it every night for eight years, the slow sorting, the stacking, emptying the day out of his head a drawer at a time. The ridge. The specimen. The phone. Croaker. The old woman with her stegosaurus. He stacked them and filed them and put them away and closed the drawers, one after another, and lay there in the clean dark empty room of his own mind.

The rope ran right through the middle of it.

He'd never had anything he couldn't put in a drawer before. He lay there and looked at it. It came in through the floor of him, through the clean dark empty room, gold and red and humming, and it went straight out the other side and away southwest across twenty miles of night, and it didn't go anywhere he could put it, because it didn't stop. It went all the way to her. And down, and back.

And at the bottom of him the thing behind the locks was pacing.

It had been lying down since the mirror. Chin on its paws, eyes open, quiet. It had stayed quiet through Seattle, which he'd been grateful for. Since he'd lain down on this bed it had been up. Walking. Up and down the length of the dark under his ribs, up and down, up and down, the way a dog walks the fence at the end of the garden when it can hear something on the other side. Every time it got to the southwest end of the fence, it stopped, and stood, and looked down the line. Then it turned round and walked back.

He lay there and felt it walk.

At 1:58 he said, "Sod it," and got up.

---

He went out through the flap into the dark. The drizzle was soft and fine and cold on his face. The river was loud in the gorge below. The spruces stood round the bench like the pillars of a cathedral with no roof.

Gary was on the canvas by the door. Harry looked at him.

"Not a word," he said.

He walked out into the middle of the bench, onto the moss, away from the tent. He stopped. He stood with his hands loose at his sides and his eyes shut and the rain on his eyelids.

Then he went down to the locks.

He didn't usually go all the way down. He checked them every morning from the top, the way you'd look at the front door from the end of the hall to make sure it was shut. Tonight he went down. Down through the clean dark empty room of his own head and the floor under it, the way the rope went, and down further, to where they were.

Three of them. He'd built them in the second week of September, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the workshop for nine hours with the door locked, and he'd built them the way he built everything, carefully, in layers. The first was Occlumency, a wall, thick and smooth. The second was a binding, braided through the wall and back, old Welsh work he'd adapted. The third was a sort of scent-sink, wound round the other two, that took everything wild and big that came off the thing behind them and folded it down to nothing before it got to his skin. They were good locks. They'd held for sixty-six days and they'd held this afternoon on the bench with her standing ten feet away and the thing behind them throwing itself at them like a ram at a gate.

He stood in front of them in the dark of himself and looked at them. And behind them, the thing that had been pacing stopped pacing, and turned round, and looked at him.

"Go on, then," Harry said.

He undid the first one.

It came apart in his hands the way it was built to, a wall going down a brick at a time. The second, he unpicked. The third, the sink, he just let go of, and it unwound off the other two and was gone. And he felt the thing in the dark come up.

It came up through him the way the tide comes up a beach. All at once and slowly. It came up through the floor of him and through the clean dark empty room and up into his chest and his arms and his legs and the back of his neck and the roots of his teeth. It came up into his skin.

He'd done this a few dozen times, in the years before September. Never got used to it. He'd seen the boy's, in the boy's head: the bang and the bloom and the shredded shorts. His went slower, and it went all the way through. It was like being poured into a bigger glass. His hands went down to the moss, and they weren't hands when they got there. His spine went long. His jaw went long. His shoulders went up and broad and heavy and his chest went deep, and the rain, which had been landing on his face, was suddenly landing on a hide of thick black fur and running off it, and the cold went away, and the world came up round him all at once in a roar.

Smell first. Always smell first. The whole bench, every spruce, every fern, the slug on the canvas, the river, the trout in the river, an elk a mile up the valley lying down in the rain, the smoke from a woodstove in a cabin five miles east, the far faint salt of the sea. Then sound. He could hear the owl again, up the slope, and the mouse it was listening to, and the rain landing on each separate needle of each separate spruce. Then sight. The bench came up out of the dark round him in shades of gray and silver, sharper than daylight, every frond of every fern, every drop.

He stood up.

He stood on four feet on the moss in the rain and shook himself, nose to tail, a long rolling shudder that went down the whole length of him and threw water off in a wheel, and then he stood still and breathed.

He'd never measured himself. He'd never had any reason to. The last time he'd done this was the end of August, on a moor in Northumberland, in the middle of the night, with nothing to measure against but sheep. Now, standing on the bench, he looked across at the tent, and the ridge-pole of it came up to about his elbow. He looked at the nearest spruce, and he could see that if he stood on his hind legs and put his front ones on it, he'd be scratching bark a good fifteen feet off the ground.

He thought about the ginger one. Jacob. He'd seen him in the boy's head, in Leah's, standing in the Cullens' kitchen doorway filling it up, and standing on the lawn as a wolf, which was the shape Harry had seen him in through the Omnioculars from the ridge in October with a laughing child on his back. The biggest thing in their pack. The biggest thing, he'd gathered from the boy's head, in anybody's stories. Harry put that picture next to himself and looked at the two of them, side by side, in his mind's eye.

He had a head on him. A good one.

He looked down at his front feet in the moss. Black. Each one about the size of a dinner plate. The claws were out; he drew them in.

At the edge of the tent flap, a long way down, Gary the slug was very slowly drawing his eyestalks back into his head.

"Yeah," said Harry. "Fair enough."

The words came out of him round the long jaw and the big teeth just as they always had, a bit lower, with a rumble under them like a lorry idling, but clear. He'd found that out the first time, on the moor, by accident, swearing at a sheep. He'd never worked out quite how it worked. He'd stopped trying to. Some of it was magic and some of it was the shape of his mouth and some of it, he suspected, was just that he'd always been a mouthy sod and the thing in the dark had decided early on that it wasn't going to be the one to shut him up.

He stood there in the rain and let himself feel it. The size. The strength. The quiet. The thing he'd been keeping in the cellar for two and a half months, up in every inch of him now, and glad, and not pacing anymore.

It was looking southwest.

So was he.

He went.

He went off the bench and down through the spruces to the river and he made no sound doing it. He never did, in this shape. He didn't know why that was, either. His feet came down where they came down, on moss and stone and root, and the ground took his weight, all of it, and didn't say a word. He crossed the north fork where it ran fast over the stones, and the water went past his legs and he didn't feel the cold, and he came up the other side and went southwest through the old trees, and then through the young ones, and then up and over the long low hills between the Calawah and the Sol Duc, and the forest went past him in the dark, gray and silver, at a speed he'd never once gone in his human shape and never would.

The rope pulled. He let it.

He came down to the Quillayute where it widened toward the sea and crossed it a mile above the village, swimming, with the tide coming in against him, and he came out on the south bank among the driftwood logs and shook himself and went up through the trees behind the houses. Small houses. Wooden. Dark. A dog barked behind one of them and then, catching his scent on the wind, stopped barking very suddenly and went under a porch.

He followed the rope through the back of the village, through the yards and the alders and the blackberry tangles, to a small house with a porch light on over the back step, and a yard full of flower beds gone to stalks for the winter, and an old truck under a tarp beside a woodpile.

He stopped at the edge of the yard, outside the reach of the light.

He sat down on the wet grass.

He looked up at the window at the top of the house.

---

### VII. Leah

*The Clearwater house, La Push — 2:31 a.m.*

She'd told her mom at twenty to ten.

She'd come in the back door barefoot in her wet cutoffs and found Sue on the couch in her bathrobe with the TV on low, watching a cooking show with the sound off, the way she did when she couldn't sleep. Sue had looked up. Sue had looked at her for a while, the way she'd looked at Seth the morning after the store: the coffee look, the one that took everything in and gave nothing back.

"I imprinted," Leah had said.

She'd thought about how to say it all the way home. She'd tried about forty different ways in her head. In the end it had just come out, standing in the middle of the living room with her hair dripping on the carpet.

Sue had looked at her for a long moment.

"Okay," she'd said.

That was it. *Okay.* Then she'd turned back to the TV, where a man with no sound was putting something in an oven, and after a second she'd said, without looking round, "Is he nice?"

Leah had opened her mouth. She'd thought about the ridge, and the crater, and the round room full of jars, and the vampire screaming with its whole face.

"No," she'd said. Because she couldn't say anything else, apparently, now, to anybody. "Not really. Not to most people."

"Is he nice to you?"

"He made me eggs."

Sue had nodded slowly, still watching the silent man and his oven. "Your father made me eggs," she'd said. "The first morning."

And Leah had stood in the middle of the living room with her mouth open, and nothing at all had come out of it, and after a while she'd gone upstairs.

She'd been lying here since.

Her room was the small one at the back, over the kitchen. It had been her room since she was born. The bed was the same bed, a single, pushed into the corner under the slope of the roof, and her feet hung off the end of it now by about four inches, and had since the spring. There were still glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling. Seth had put them up when he was seven and she was ten, standing on her bed in his socks, because she'd been scared of the dark that year and wouldn't tell anybody, and he'd known anyway. She'd never taken them down. They didn't glow anymore. You could just see them, in the light from the porch coming up through the curtain, pale little shapes on the slope of the plaster.

She lay on her back on top of the blanket in a T-shirt and a pair of Seth's old basketball shorts and looked at them and didn't sleep.

The rope wouldn't let her.

It ran out of her chest and up through the ceiling and away northeast, over the house and over the river and over twenty miles of black forest, into a tent. She'd felt him go out of the tent around eight. She'd felt him go somewhere south, east of Forks, and stand on a rock in the rain for a long time, and do something that made the whole sky jump. She'd felt him come back. She'd felt him stand in his hall at ten, and something cold and gray come up out of him toward a mirror, and the thing at the bottom of him get up on its feet and growl, low and long, and she'd felt her own wolf, curled up under her ribs, lift its head and growl back at nothing at all. Then she'd felt him go away again. Far. A long way east, over the water, a city full of lights. She'd felt him stand very still in a high quiet place. And come home.

And lie down.

And not sleep.

She could feel him not sleeping. She'd been feeling it for an hour. Him lying on his back on his bed in his tent twenty miles off, staring up at his ceiling, the same way she was staring at hers, and both of them feeling the other one do it, and both of them getting more annoyed about it by the minute. It was like lying next to somebody who keeps sighing. Except they were twenty miles apart, and he wasn't sighing, and she could feel every single breath.

At five to two, she'd felt him get up.

She'd lain there and felt him go out of the tent. Stand on the moss. Stand there for a long time.

And then she'd felt him go *down*.

Down into himself, the way the rope went. She'd felt it like feeling a man go down a flight of stairs into a basement in a house you're standing outside of. Down to the three locks. She'd felt him stand in front of them. She'd felt the thing behind them stop pacing, and turn round, and look at him.

And she'd felt him open them.

She'd sat up in bed. She hadn't been able to help it. She'd sat bolt upright on top of the blanket with both hands pressed flat on her chest, because something was coming *up the rope*. Coming up out of him and through him and along twenty miles of line and into her and through her and down to the bottom of her, where her own wolf was lying under her ribs. And her own wolf had come up off the floor of her so fast she'd nearly fallen out of bed.

Something enormous had woken up, twenty miles away, and stood up in the rain, and shaken itself.

And her wolf had answered it. Without asking her. It had stood up in the bottom of her and pricked its ears and every hair along its back had gone up, She knew what fight felt like. This was the other thing.

Then he'd started moving. Fast.

She'd lain back down. She'd made herself. She'd lain on her back with her hands flat on the blanket and her heart going and stared at Seth's dead stars, and she'd felt him come. Southwest. Down a river. Over hills. Twenty miles of dark forest going past at a speed she knew, because she was the fastest thing on four legs in the whole of La Push and she knew exactly what it felt like to go that fast, and he was going faster. Across the Quillayute, swimming, the cold of it coming up the rope at her like a breath of winter. Up through the trees behind the village. Through the yards.

And then he'd stopped.

He'd stopped about forty yards away. She could feel exactly where. At the bottom of the yard, past the porch light, between her dad's old truck and the woodpile.

He'd sat down.

He was sitting there now. She'd been lying here for six minutes with him sitting there, and she could feel him looking up at her window. He sat on the wet grass in the dark and looked up, and that was all he did.

She lay there for another minute. Just to make a point. She wasn't sure who to.

Then she got up and went to the window.

She pulled the curtain back.

The yard was dark below her. The porch light made a yellow circle on the grass around the back step, on the flowerbeds her mom had cut down for the winter, on the clothesline with nothing on it. Past the circle, the dark went black. The shape of her dad's truck under its tarp. The woodpile. The edge of the trees.

And out there in the black, about level with the truck, two eyes.

Green.

They caught the porch light and threw it back at her, the way an animal's eyes do at night, the way a deer's do in your headlights. Except a deer's go white, and a dog's go yellow, and these were green. Bright, pale, glass-in-the-sun green. Two of them. Wide apart. Far too wide apart for any animal she knew. And they were up high. Up higher off the ground than Jacob's ever were. Up higher than the cab of the truck.

They were looking straight up at her.

She stood there in Seth's basketball shorts with her hand on the curtain and looked back down at them. She didn't feel scared. She looked for scared, out of habit, and there wasn't any.

*You're kidding me,* she thought, down the rope.

The green eyes blinked. Slowly. Like a cat's.

She let the curtain drop.

---

She went down the stairs barefoot, on the outside edges of the steps where they didn't creak. She'd been doing it since she was fourteen. Past her mom's door, which was shut, with no light under it; Sue had gone to bed at eleven and Leah could hear her breathing, slow and even. Past Seth's door, which was open, with nobody in there; Seth was at Layla's, he was always at Layla's now, his bed hadn't been slept in since the beginning of the month except the night he'd had the flu. Down through the dark kitchen, past the table, past the fridge with Seth's school picture on it from when he was nine and missing his front teeth.

She opened the back door. Very quietly.

She stepped out onto the back step into the porch light, and the cold came up off the concrete through the soles of her feet, and the rain was a fine gray mist in the light, and she stood there and looked out past the edge of it into the dark.

He stood up.

She didn't hear it. That was the first thing. She heard everything; she heard the rain on the tarp over the truck and her mom breathing upstairs through two walls and a mouse in the woodpile. She didn't hear him stand up. One second the green eyes were up at the height of the truck cab and the next they were higher, much higher, and something had moved in the dark, something big, and it hadn't made a sound. Not the grass. Not a twig. Nothing. Like the dark itself had stood up.

He came forward. Into the edge of the light.

She'd seen Jacob come out of the dark a hundred times. She'd seen Sam. She'd seen the size of them, the shock of it, every time, even after a year and a half; there was always that one second where your brain said *that's not right, nothing's that big*.

This was more.

He was black. All black, black like the inside of a cave, black like the sea on a night with no moon, which this was. In the porch light the outer coat had a dull sheen on it, wet, and where the rain had parted it along his shoulders she could see the undercoat, a deep dark charcoal. He was long. Longer than Jacob by a good way. She could see that even with half of him still in the dark. And he was tall. He came forward to the very edge of the light, and stopped, and stood there with his head up, and she had to tip her head back to look at his face, and she was standing on a step.

She knew exactly how tall Jacob was. She'd run beside him every night since September. She'd stood next to him on the Cullens' lawn five hours ago. She put Jacob next to this, in her head, side by side, and Jacob's ears came up to about this one's jaw.

A head. He had a whole head on Jake. And he was built heavier through the chest and the shoulders than Jacob was, and longer in the leg, and he was standing in her mother's backyard on the wet grass next to the cut-down dahlias, and he hadn't made a sound getting there.

The green eyes looked down at her.

"Evening, Lassie," said the wolf.

Leah sat down on the back step.

She didn't mean to. Her legs did it. She sat down hard on the cold concrete with her mouth open and stared up at him.

It had come out of his mouth. Out loud. Out of that long black muzzle, past those teeth, low, with a rumble running underneath it like an engine, but clear as a bell. In an English accent. *His* voice. She'd know it anywhere. She'd been hearing it in her head since one o'clock in the afternoon.

"You can *talk*," she said.

"Always could." The big head tipped, a little, to one side. The ears moved. "Didn't they tell you?"

"Our wolves can't talk."

"Yours can't."

"*Nobody's* can! Jacob can't! Sam can't! Nobody in a thousand years of stories can talk, you have to—you have to *think* at people, it's a whole thing—"

"Well," said the wolf. "I'm a different model."

She stared at him.

He stared back. Calm. Patient. Rain running off his ears. The green eyes caught the porch light and held it.

And down the rope, at the bottom of him, she could feel him laughing.

"Oh, you *ass*," Leah said.

"Language. Your mum's asleep."

"How do you know my mom's asleep?"

"I can hear her." He lowered his head, slowly, until his face was about level with hers, sitting on the step. It was enormous. His nose was about a foot away from hers. She could feel the heat coming off it. She could smell him, really smell him, for the first time with nothing in the way: pine and wet earth and cold stone like the inside of a cave, and under it, filling it, strong now, the thing she'd smelled on the ridge and never been able to find again. Wolf. Big and wild and old. "She's breathing very slow. Through the wall. Upstairs on the left."

"That's creepy."

"Yeah," he agreed. "Bit."

She looked at him. He looked at her.

"Couldn't sleep," he said.

"I know."

"Neither could you."

"I *know*, Harry."

It was the first time she'd said it out loud. His name. She heard it come out of her, and she felt it go down the rope into him, and she felt him feel it, and she felt the thing at the bottom of him, which was not at the bottom of him anymore, which was standing here in her mom's backyard in the rain looking at her, go very still.

Her dad's name. Out loud. In her mom's backyard. To a wolf.

She looked away. She looked at the truck under its tarp.

He didn't say anything. He didn't push. He just stood there with his big head down by hers, breathing, the rain running off him. And after a minute he put his nose very gently against the side of her head, just above her ear, and breathed in. And out. She felt his breath go through her hair, hot.

She closed her eyes.

Under her ribs, her wolf was on its feet and pressed up against the inside of her skin like a dog against a screen door.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Hang on."

She got up. She went past him, down off the step and across the wet grass and round the far side of the woodpile, out of the light, and pulled the T-shirt off over her head and stepped out of Seth's shorts. She didn't bother tying anything to her ankle. She'd deal with it.

She phased.

---

What happened next, she'd think about a lot.

She'd think about it later the way you think about a dream where you did something you'd never do. With your face hot. With a kind of wonder. She was in there; she was in there the whole time, watching through her own eyes. The gray wolf she'd been carrying around under her ribs since the night her father died was the one who walked out from behind that woodpile. And the gray wolf had made up its mind.

It walked straight up to him.

It skipped the circling. It skipped stopping at a distance to size him up, which was what Leah would've done, what she'd done with every single wolf she'd ever met, including her own brother. It walked straight across the wet grass with its head up and its tail up and stopped with its nose about an inch from his chest, and stood there, and breathed him in.

He lowered his head. He put his nose against hers.

And held it there.

She'd seen wolves do that. Real wolves. On TV. She'd seen Jacob and Seth do a version of it, as a joke. She'd never once done it with anybody in her life, and her wolf did it now like it had been waiting to do it since the day it was born, nose to nose, breath to breath, eyes half closed, in the rain at the edge of her mother's porch light at a quarter to three in the morning. His breath was hot. Hers was too. It went back and forth between them.

*What are you doing,* Leah thought at it. Her own wolf. *What are you* doing*? We met him this* afternoon*.*

Her wolf didn't care.

It took its nose away from his and pushed past it. Down along his jaw. Into the side of his neck, into the thick black ruff there, deep, and pulled the smell of him in like air after being underwater. And he did the same. She felt his big head come down along her shoulder and his nose go into the fur behind her ear, and down her neck, and she felt him breathe her in, slow, all of her, and she felt her whole body shiver from her ears to the end of her tail.

*Okay,* she thought. *Okay, this is—*

"Mine's not listening to me either," said Harry. Out loud, very low, right by her ear, with the rumble under it. "If it helps."

*It doesn't help.*

"Didn't think so."

Her wolf pulled back. It looked up at him. It was a head shorter than him at the shoulder, more than a head, and narrower everywhere, built for running where he was built for everything. It looked up at him, and she felt it think something that wasn't in words, that was older than words, something that was just *yes, that one*. And it dropped its front end down onto the wet grass, chest low, rump up, tail going.

A play bow.

Her wolf had just play-bowed to him. In her mom's backyard. Like a puppy.

*Oh my God,* thought Leah. *Oh my GOD.*

His ears went up. All the way up. And through the rope she felt the thing that had been laughing at the bottom of him go absolutely delighted, the way a kid goes delighted at Christmas, and she felt it take over from him for a second the way hers had taken over from her, and she watched him go down on his own front legs on the grass in front of her, enormous, black, his chest nearly on the ground, his tail going back and forth behind him like a flag.

Her wolf nipped his ear.

He huffed. A big gust of breath, like a laugh.

She bolted.

---

They went down through the village. Through the dark yards and the alders and the blackberries, the way he'd come. She led. She was faster. She knew it the second they left the yard, knew it in her legs, and she felt him know it too, and she felt him like it. He was right behind her. She couldn't hear him. She could feel him, a big black shape a stride behind her shoulder in the dark, keeping pace, not trying to pass, and she couldn't hear his feet at all. Hers were loud. Hers hit the ground like anybody's. His didn't make a sound.

Down past the marina, dark, the boats knocking at their moorings. Out across the parking lot by the store, past the Taurus with the dent. Onto First Beach.

The tide was out. The beach went away south from them in a long gray curve, wide and flat and shining, and the surf came in out of the dark in long white lines and broke and ran up the sand and slid back. There was no moon. There were no stars, mostly; the clouds were low. But there was a little light from somewhere, the way there always is at the sea, and the wet sand caught it and held it, and she could see the sea stacks standing out in the surf, black, with their crooked trees on top.

She ran down the beach. Flat out. Everything she had.

He came up beside her.

They ran side by side down First Beach in the dark with the surf on their left. She was faster. He was almost as fast, and he was bigger, and he didn't make a sound, and she was running harder than she'd ever run in her life and she didn't feel tired at all. Her wolf was so happy it felt like it might come apart. She could feel his feeling the same thing. They were so happy, the two of them, that the happiness came up the rope from both ends and met in the middle and she couldn't tell anymore whose was whose.

At the south end of First Beach she went up over the rocks at Quateata without slowing down, and he went with her, and they came down the other side onto Second Beach.

It was empty. It was always empty, this time of year, this time of night. A long beach between two headlands, with the forest coming right down to the sand, and the Needles standing out in the water a half mile off, a whole row of them, black against the dark, like the ruins of something. The surf was loud here. It came in big. It boomed against the rocks at the far end and threw up white.

At the far end was Teahwhit Head.

Nobody went round Teahwhit Head. Not at any tide. It came down into the sea in a wall of black rock and you couldn't get round it and you couldn't get over it, not from the beach; there were signs. She'd been coming to this beach her whole life and she'd never once been past the end of it.

She ran straight at it anyway. Because she was happy, and stupid, and her wolf was running the show.

At the foot of the cliff she stopped, panting, in the surf, with the water swirling round her legs, and looked up.

He stopped beside her. He looked up too.

There was a cave.

She'd never seen it. You couldn't see it from the beach in daylight; you'd have had to be out in a boat. It was about sixty, seventy feet up the face of the headland, a black slot in the black rock, wide as a garage door, with a lip of stone sticking out in front of it like a porch. Sea-carved. Old. Way up above any tide.

He looked at it for a moment. Then he looked at her.

"Hold still," he said.

She didn't hold still. She never held still when anybody told her to. She felt it anyway: something came up under her, all round her, like a hand the size of a truck closing gently around her ribs and her hips and her legs, and she went up.

Off the beach. Out of the surf, water pouring off her. Up past the black rock, up and up the face of the cliff, sixty, seventy feet, with the wind off the sea pulling at her fur and the beach dropping away below her and the surf booming, and her legs going, scrabbling at nothing.

*PUT ME DOWN—*

He put her down. On the stone lip in front of the cave. Gently. All four feet. She stood there shaking with every hair on her body standing straight up and the wind roaring in her ears.

He came up after her. He didn't need a hand. He went up the face of the cliff in about three jumps, the way a cat goes up a fence, rock to rock, black on black, without a sound. He landed on the lip beside her so softly she didn't feel the stone shift.

She turned on him. She bared her teeth. She was going to bite his face off.

He was laughing. She could feel it all the way down the rope. His tongue was hanging out of the side of his mouth.

*You absolute—*

"Sorry," he said. He wasn't. "Couldn't resist."

She bit his shoulder.

Hard enough to mean it. A mouthful of thick black fur and the hide under it, and she held on and shook her head, growling, the way she'd shake a stick. And he just stood there and let her, and through the rope she felt it go into him, and she felt him like it. She felt him like it a lot.

She let go.

She looked at him. He looked at her.

Behind them, the cave went back into the headland, dry, out of the wind. The floor was sand. Fine and pale and soft and cold, blown up here by a thousand years of storms. It smelled of salt and stone and a little of old seal, from a long time ago. In front of them, past the lip, the whole of the Pacific Ocean went away west into the dark, and the Needles stood in it, black, and the surf came in under them, white, booming, and broke against the foot of the cliff seventy feet below and threw spray up at them that didn't quite reach.

Nobody in the world knew where they were.

Her wolf walked past him into the mouth of the cave. It turned round on the sand. It looked back at him.

And the last thing Leah Clearwater thought in words, for a long time after that, was: *Oh. Okay. Apparently we're doing this.*

### VIII. Seth

*OH NO—*

Then she looked south. Off over the dark roofs of Forks, toward the coast, toward La Push, the way the bees had looked the very first night, the way the ward always looked when it was waiting for him. He watched her look. He watched her think. Layla was smart. That was the worst thing about Layla. She was the smartest person he'd ever met and she worked things out about four seconds before you wanted her to.

He watched her work it out.

"*Please.*"

"Oh, *Seth*." Her hand came up over her mouth.

"Don't. Don't, don't—"

"Was it—were they—"

"*LAYLA.*"

She sat down on the wet grass. She couldn't help it, he could see. She sat down hard, holding her ribs, with her other hand over her mouth, and her shoulders started to shake, and she made a sound behind her hand like a balloon letting go, and then she couldn't stop. She laughed so hard she had to lie down on her side in the grass. She laughed until she was crying. She laughed until she was going *ow, ow, ow, my ribs, oh God, ow*, and she still couldn't stop, and the little blue ball of fire bobbed up and down over her head like it was laughing too.

Seth lay in the grass with the bees on him and stared at the sky.

"I'm going to go live in the ocean," he said.

"Okay," Layla wheezed.

"I'm serious."

"I know you are, baby."

"I'm never phasing again. Ever."

"You have to phase, Seth."

"Not for like a *year*."

She laughed harder.

He lay there. The bees walked around on him. After a while some of them started going back to their hives, one at a time, and the ward round the yard went back to dozing, and Layla went from laughing to just sort of hiccuping, and then she lay there on her side in the grass next to him with her hand on his arm, among the bees, breathing carefully through her teeth.

"Jacob," Seth said suddenly.

She looked at him.

"Jacob's on at six. He's going to phase at six. He's going to—" Seth sat up. Bees went everywhere. "Oh my God. He can't phase. He can't. If he phases and they're still—or even if they're not, if she's just *remembering*—"

He went and called him.

Maeve Quinn's phone was a black rotary one on a little table in the front hall with a lace doily under it. He'd never used a rotary phone before Layla's. He put the receiver to his ear.

It didn't ring. Not once. It clicked, and then there was a voice.

"Seth," said Edward Cullen.

Seth closed his eyes.

"I heard," Edward said.

"You—"

"You're three and a half miles from this house, and you're very loud." There was a pause. Edward's voice was very calm, very even, and very carefully nothing at all. "I'll make sure Jacob doesn't phase before morning. I'll tell him you were sick."

"Thank you."

"Of course."

"Edward—"

"Seth." Another pause. "I'm sorry."

The line clicked and went dead.

Seth stood in the hall holding the receiver. Then he put it back in its cradle, very carefully, on the doily. Then he sat down on the floor of Maeve Quinn's front hall with his back against the wall and his knees up and his face in his hands.

From the kitchen, through the open back door, very faintly, he could still hear Layla laughing.

### IX. Leah

*Teahwhit Head — 7:31 a.m.*

She woke up because a sea lion was barking.

It was a long way down. On the rocks at the foot of the Needles, probably, out in the gray. *Ork. Ork. Ork.* Over and over, like somebody who'd lost their keys. She lay there with her eyes shut and listened to it and didn't move.

She was hot. That was the first thing she noticed. Hot all the way through, which she always was, but this was something else, this was the heat of a bed with the blanket pulled up. Her whole back was hot. There was something against it. Something big, and breathing, slow, and her head was lying on something, and the something was furry and very heavy and also breathing.

She opened her eyes.

Gray light. The mouth of the cave, wide, and past it, the morning. The sea. Gray and white and huge, going away to a gray sky. The Needles standing up out of it, black, wet, with gulls going round them. The surf booming at the foot of the cliff. Spray. Wind. It was raining, a little, out there; the rain came in at the mouth of the cave and stopped a few feet inside, on the sand, and left a line.

She was lying on the sand at the back of the cave, in wolf shape. Curled up. And her head was lying on a foreleg that was black, and about as thick as her whole neck, and the rest of him was curled round her back the way you'd curl round a fire. His chin was on top of her shoulder. She could feel him breathing into her fur.

She lay there and remembered.

All of it. In order. It came back up through her like the tide coming up the beach, the yard and the step and *evening, Lassie* and her wolf walking straight up to him across the grass. The run. The cliff. The cave. All of it.

*Oh no,* Leah thought.

She waited for the rest. The rest always came. The cold sick drop in the stomach. The *what did you do*, the *what were you thinking*, the voice that sounded like her and was really about half Sam and half every girl at school who'd ever looked at her sideways. She waited for it to come up and tell her what an idiot she'd been. She lay very still on the sand with her eyes open, looking at the sea, and waited.

It didn't come.

Her wolf was lying there under her ribs with its chin on its paws, and it felt the way a dog feels lying in a patch of sun. Full. Warm. Done. Not interested in anything she had to say about anything.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Okay.*

She looked at the sea for a while.

Behind her, she felt him wake up. She felt it down the rope before she felt it on her back: the slow climb up out of sleep, the way his mind came on, like a house with all the lights coming on one room at a time. She felt him notice where he was. She felt him notice her. She felt him remember.

She felt him not be sorry about any of it. Not even a little.

"Morning," said the wolf behind her. Out loud. Low and rough, with the rumble under it, and sleep in it.

She didn't answer.

He didn't move. He just lay there, curled round her, with his chin on her shoulder, and looked at the sea over the top of her head, the way she was looking at it. A sea lion barked. The gulls went round the Needles.

After a long time she got up.

She walked out from inside the curl of him, across the sand toward the mouth of the cave, and stood there a second with the wind in her face, and phased.

She came out of it on her knees on cold sand, human, naked, with the wind off the Pacific Ocean blowing straight in the mouth of the cave and across every single inch of her skin.

"*Shit,*" said Leah.

She'd known she would be. She hadn't tied anything to her ankle. She looked round anyway, wildly, as though a pair of cutoffs might have walked up here on their own. Nothing. Seth's shorts and her T-shirt were in a heap behind the woodpile in her mom's yard, getting rained on. She'd come up this cliff with nothing at all.

Behind her, there was a sound.

A long low soft sound, like a big breath let out, quieter than any phase she'd ever heard, and she turned her head and looked.

He was standing at the back of the cave. Human. In jeans, and a gray T-shirt, and over the T-shirt a blue-and-black checked flannel shirt, open, and his boots, and all of it bone dry and clean and exactly as it had been, she'd bet anything, when he lay down on his bed in his tent at one in the morning. Not a hair out of place. Not a speck of sand on him. Two days of black stubble. Green eyes.

He looked at her, kneeling naked on the sand in the wind.

She looked at him, fully dressed.

"Oh, you have *got* to be kidding me," she said.

"What?"

"You've got *clothes*?"

He looked down at himself. "Apparently."

"How do you have clothes? Where did they go? Where were they?"

"No idea." He looked back up at her. "Always been like that. Go in wearing something, come out wearing it."

"That's not *fair*."

"Never said it was." He was already shrugging the flannel shirt off. He came across the sand and held it out to her, at arm's length, looking at the wall of the cave over her head with great interest. "Here."

She snatched it out of his hand.

It was dry, and it still held the heat of him, and it smelled of him, pine and stone and coffee, and it came down to about the middle of her thighs. She buttoned it up from the bottom with her hands not quite steady. She'd have to roll the sleeves. They came down past her fingers.

She stood up.

They stood there in the mouth of the cave, a few feet apart. Him in his T-shirt with his hands in the pockets of his jeans. Her in his shirt with her bare legs and her bare feet and her hair, she could tell without looking, full of sand.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"So," Harry said.

"So."

"That happened."

"Yep."

The sea lion barked. *Ork. Ork.*

Leah sat down. On the lip of the cave, at the edge, with her legs hanging over seventy feet of air and the surf coming in below, booming. After a second he came and sat down too, about four feet off. He hung his legs over the edge too, and put his elbows on his knees, and looked at the sea.

"Are we going to be weird about it?" he said.

"I'm not being weird."

"You're sitting four feet away from me."

"*You're* sitting four feet away from *me*."

"I'm sitting four feet away from you because you're sitting four feet away from me. I'm being polite."

"Since when are you polite?"

"I'm very polite. I'm English. It's all we've got." He looked at her sideways. "You're sitting four feet away because I smell like you, aren't you."

She opened her mouth to say no.

"Yes," her mouth said instead.

"Right." He looked back at the sea. "Well, you smell like me. So."

"I'm aware."

"Very strongly."

"I said I'm *aware*, Harry."

They sat there. The gulls went round the Needles. The rain came in at the mouth of the cave, fine and gray, and landed on their knees.

"I mean," Harry said after a while, "it was good sex. So what's the big deal?"

Leah turned her head and stared at him.

"Yeah," she said, "but as *wolves*."

He looked at her. Something moved in his face. It took her a second to work out what it was, because she'd never seen it on him; she'd seen him bored, and amused, and cold, and very angry, and once, on a rock in the rain, completely blindsided. She'd never seen him worried.

"You didn't like it?" he said.

"No—"

His face changed.

"No. God. Hang on." She stopped. She shut her mouth, and opened it again, and just said it. "It was fucking great."

He blinked.

"It was," Leah said. "It was great. It was the best—okay, I'm not finishing that sentence. It was great. Okay?" She pushed her hair out of her face. Sand fell out of it. "But."

"But what?"

"The problem is the pack, Harry." She turned to face him properly. "When I phase. Anybody who's phased sees everything in my head. *Everything.* That's how it works. There's no doors. There's no walls. Well." She touched her throat. "There's one wall. Yours. But that's it. And the next time Jacob phases while I'm phased, or Seth, or—God help me—if I ever have to run with Sam's lot again, they're going to get an eyeful. Of *that*. Of all of that. In high definition. With sound."

He was looking at her very steadily.

"Every single time I phase," Leah said. "For the rest of my *life*."

"Hm," said Harry.

"And it already happened."

His eyebrows went up. "What?"

"Seth." She put her face in her hands. "Seth phased in. Around three. Right in the—right in the middle."

There was a pause.

Then he started laughing.

He tried not to. She watched him try. She watched it come up in him and watched him clamp his jaw shut on it and look out at the sea very hard, and she watched his shoulders start to shake, and then it got out. It came out of him like air out of a tire. He laughed so hard he had to put one hand down on the stone behind him to stop himself going over the edge. He laughed till his eyes watered.

"It's not funny," Leah said through her hands.

"It's a bit funny."

"It's not *funny*, Harry, he's my *brother*, he's eighteen—"

"Oh, poor bloody *Balto*—"

"I told him I was busy!"

"Did you?" He was wiping his eyes. "Did you, love?"

"I said I couldn't. Right now."

"Very polite of you."

"I'm going to push you off this cliff."

"I'd float."

"I know you'd float. I'd still feel better."

"I felt him," Harry said. He'd stopped laughing, mostly. He was grinning instead, a big, wide, completely shameless grin, the first one she'd ever seen on him. "Your brother. Coming in. Like somebody opening a door in a house where you thought you were alone. The—" He tapped his chest. "Mine noticed him. Before I did. Gave him a look."

"I know. I felt it give him a look. *Seth* felt it give him a look. Seth's probably still lying in Layla's yard."

"I'll apologise to him."

"*Don't.*"

"Why not?"

"Because then he'll have to think about it again."

"Fair."

They sat. The sea came in. A long way down, on the rocks under the cliff, something that might have been a seal hauled itself up out of the surf and lay there in the rain.

"Every time I phase," Leah said again. Quieter. "They're going to see it."

"Yeah," said Harry.

"Doesn't that bother you? It's you too. They're going to see you."

He thought about it. She could feel him think about it, down the rope: not much. About as much as he'd thought about the vampire on the floor.

"Leah," he said. "I've got nothing to hide."

And he grinned again, wide, and leaned back on his hands on the cold stone with the rain on his face, and looked at the sea.

She shoved him.

He went over sideways, laughing, and caught himself on his elbow. She didn't push him off the cliff. She thought about it. She sat there with her bare legs hanging over seventy feet of air and her hair full of sand and his shirt buttoned up to her throat, and she felt the corner of her mouth go up, and she didn't stop it.

---

He moved over after that. Or she did. She'd never be sure. At some point the four feet were gone and they were sitting next to each other on the lip of the cave with their shoulders touching, looking out at the Needles, and it was easier.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You just did."

"Can I ask you something else?"

"Depends."

He didn't look at her. He looked at the sea. "Should we be—is there anything we need to be thinking about? After. Is that—" He stopped. She felt him look for the words and not find good ones. "Do your lot—is that a thing that can happen. With you."

She knew what he meant. It took her a second, because nobody had asked her that, ever, in her life. Sam hadn't needed to; they'd been seventeen and careful and then it hadn't mattered anymore. She looked out at the gray water.

"No," she said.

He waited.

"I don't—since I phased. The first time. My body just. Stopped." She said it flat, the way she said everything she didn't want to say. "Nothing. Not once. Not in a year and a half. Carlisle checked. He wanted to run tests. I said no. I don't need tests to know." She shrugged. "Whatever I am, I think I'm the end of it. I think I'm a dead end. The first girl in a thousand years, and it turns out the first girl's just—broken."

He didn't say anything.

He didn't tell her she wasn't. She'd been ready for that; she'd been ready to bite his head off for it. Everybody always told you you weren't. Her mom, the one time. Emily, once, before Leah stopped talking to her. *You don't know that.* He didn't say it. He couldn't have. He didn't know it. And he couldn't lie to her.

"Okay," he said instead.

That was all. *Okay.* The way her mom had said it.

Then, after a while: "Do you want kids?"

"I don't know," Leah said. She heard it come out and knew it was the truest thing she'd said about it in a year and a half. "I don't know. I wanted to get to not know. Like a normal person. I wanted to get to be twenty-five and not sure and have it be my call."

He nodded slowly.

He sat there for a while, looking at the Needles. She could feel him thinking. The cold filing-cabinet thinking she'd felt in the round room was gone. This was slower. Quieter. Something being turned over, carefully, the way you'd turn over a stone you'd found on a beach.

"I've got a library," he said.

She looked at him.

"I'm not promising anything," Harry said. He still wasn't looking at her. "I don't know anything about it. I don't know what you are, any more than you do. I'm telling you I've got a library. And a lab. And a job where nobody asks what I'm reading." He shrugged one shoulder. "That's all I'm telling you."

She looked at him for a long time.

"Don't," she said finally. Not hard. "Okay? Don't—don't make it a project."

"It's not a project."

"You make *everything* a project. You've got a vampire on your floor with forty questions."

"Thirty-one."

"*Harry.*"

"It won't be a project." He looked at her then, finally. Green eyes. Steady. "If it's ever anything, it'll be because you asked. All right?"

She couldn't lie to him. So she didn't say *all right*. She didn't say anything. She leaned her shoulder against his a little harder, and looked at the sea, and that was an answer, and he took it.

---

"You gave them your name," she said, a while later.

"Yeah."

"Last night. At the Cullens'. You did that on purpose."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

He looked at her like she'd asked him why water was wet.

"You couldn't breathe," he said.

She didn't have anything to say to that. She looked away.

"I rang my boss," he said.

That brought her head back round. "What?"

"Last night. About ten." He'd picked up a piece of shell off the stone lip and was turning it over in his fingers. "I'd lied to him earlier. You heard. When you were in the kitchen. *Nothing else, sir.*"

"I heard."

"So I rang him back and told him I'd lied."

She stared at him. "You rang your boss and told him you lied."

"Yeah."

"Why would you *do* that?"

"Because he's never lied to me," said Harry simply. "Not once. Eight years."

She didn't say anything.

"I told him the rest. The phone call. The kid. Layla." He turned the shell over. "And I told him I was compromised. That there was someone. I didn't tell him who, or what you are, or how. I'm not giving him you. Not ever. But I told him there was someone, and that if it ever came to it, him or you, I'd pick you." He shrugged. "Every time. Told him to send someone else."

The sea boomed against the foot of the cliff.

"You told your boss you'd pick me," Leah said slowly. "Over your job. You told him that."

"Yeah."

"After *six hours*."

"Yeah."

"What did he say?"

Harry was quiet for a second. Down the rope she felt something come up in him that she'd felt once before, in the hall, at ten last night, when the mirror had gone dark: something that sat in his chest and made his throat go tight. It had nothing to do with the oath.

"He said I wasn't going anywhere," he said.

He threw the shell off the cliff. They both watched it go, turning over and over, white, all the way down into the surf.

At the bottom of him, behind no locks at all now, the thing in the dark was lying in the sun. Or what it had instead of sun. It had its chin on its paws and its eyes half shut and it was facing her, and it didn't look like it planned to get up for a very long time.

---

They got down off the cliff at a quarter past eight, which was its own argument.

"I'm not doing the floating thing again."

"It's seventy feet."

"I'll climb."

"You'll climb seventy feet of wet rock in bare feet and my shirt."

"I've climbed worse."

"You have not climbed worse. Nobody's climbed worse. There's a *sign*, Leah."

"I'll phase and jump."

"And arrive at your mum's kitchen in the altogether."

She glared at him. He looked back at her, very patient.

In the end he picked her up. Just picked her up, one arm under her knees and one round her back, the way you'd pick up a kid who'd fallen asleep in the car, while she was still in the middle of telling him exactly where he could put his floating thing, and stepped off the edge.

She grabbed his shirt with both fists and shut her eyes and swore at him the whole way down. It took about five seconds. They drifted. The wind pulled at the flannel and her hair. The surf boomed up under them. And then his boots touched the wet sand at the foot of Teahwhit Head without so much as a bump, and he set her down on her feet in the shallows, in the foam, and the water went over her ankles so cold it made her gasp.

"There," he said. "Painless."

She hit him in the arm. Hard. As hard as she could, which was very hard.

He rubbed it. "Fair."

They walked back up Second Beach in the rain. It was a mile, about, and the tide was coming in, and she walked in the wet sand at the edge of the water in his shirt with her bare feet and her bare legs and he walked beside her in his T-shirt in November with his hands in his pockets, steaming a little where the rain hit his shoulders. Neither of them said anything much. They didn't need to. Halfway up the beach his hand came out of his pocket and found hers, and she let it, and they walked the rest of the way like that, up through the trees on the trail at the north end, and over the rocks at Quateata, and down onto First Beach, past the marina, toward the road.

At the edge of the village she stopped.

She sniffed. The wind had come round off the sea and was blowing straight over the both of them toward the houses. She sniffed again. She turned her head and smelled her own shoulder, through the flannel. She smelled her own wrist. She smelled him.

"Oh, no," Leah said.

"What?"

"We smell."

"We smell like the beach."

"We smell like the beach, *and*—" She pressed her hands over her face. "Harry. We smell like *what we did*. Both of us. All over. Anybody with a nose is going to know from a mile away. Seth'll know from the *road*."

He sniffed his own arm, considering.

"Bit," he admitted.

"*Bit?*"

"I could—" He made a vague movement with his hand.

"You could what?"

"Scourgify. Scrub it off. Take about a second."

She looked at him.

She thought about it. She actually thought about it, standing there on the edge of La Push in the rain at eight-thirty in the morning in a man's shirt with sand in her hair. She thought about walking into her mom's kitchen smelling of nothing. Smelling like soap. Like she'd been for a walk.

She thought about Seth, who'd felt the whole thing at three in the morning, sitting at the table, and looking at her, and pretending.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"No." She dropped her hands. "Screw it. They're going to know anyway. They're all going to know the second anybody phases. I'm not going to walk in there smelling like a hotel."

He grinned at her. The wide one again.

"Your call," he said.

"I know it's my call."

She turned and started walking up the road toward her mother's house, and after a second, still grinning, he came after her.

### X. Sue

*The Clearwater house, La Push — 8:41 a.m.*

Seth had come home at twenty to six.

Sue had been up since five, the way she was most mornings now, sitting at the kitchen table with her coffee in the dark before it got light, listening to the house. She'd heard his mother's car before she'd heard him; Seth didn't have a car, Seth had her car most days, the old Taurus with the dent in the back door. She'd heard it come into the drive too fast and stop too hard. She'd heard the door. She'd heard him come up the back step and stand there for a long time with his hand on the knob before he opened it.

He'd come in red. That was the only word for it. Red from the top of his ears to the middle of his chest, like a boy who'd been out in the sun all day without a hat. He'd had a bee in his hair. He'd looked at her sitting at the table, and she'd looked at him, and he'd opened his mouth, and shut it, and sat down across from her and put his forehead down on his folded arms on the table and stayed there.

"Seth," she'd said.

"Please don't," he'd said, into the table.

So she hadn't.

She'd got up and made pancakes. She'd made a lot. She made a lot of everything these days. She'd made them and stacked them on the big plate and put the plate in the oven on low to keep, and put the butter out to soften, and the syrup, and she'd sat back down with her coffee, and Seth had stayed where he was, and every once in a while he'd groaned. Quietly. Into his arms. Like a man in a hospital bed.

She hadn't asked.

She'd raised two children by herself for a year and a half now, and with Harry for eighteen years before that, and if there was one thing she'd learned, it was that the thing they most wanted to tell you was always the thing they'd walk into the sea before saying first.

She'd heard Leah go out at a quarter to three. She'd heard the back door. She'd heard her daughter's bare feet on the step, and then nothing for a while, and then a voice. A man's voice, very low, out in the yard, past the porch light, in an accent she didn't know. And her daughter's voice, sharp, the way Leah's voice went sharp when something had got under her guard. And then the sound she'd been hearing in her own backyard on and off for a year and a half, and never got used to, the tearing. And then nothing.

She'd lain in the dark and listened to nothing for a long time.

Then she'd turned over and gone back to sleep, because she was fifty-one years old and some things were none of her business, and because a long time ago, when she was nineteen and Harry Clearwater was twenty-one, she'd climbed out of her own bedroom window in this same village in the middle of the night to go down to the beach with a boy, and her own mother had lain in the dark and listened to her go and done exactly the same thing.

At twenty to nine, she saw them coming up the road.

She was at the sink, rinsing out the coffee pot, looking out the window over it, the one that looked out on the front yard and the road beyond. She saw her daughter come round the bend by the Atearas' place, barefoot, in the rain, in a man's flannel shirt that came down to the middle of her thighs. Blue and black check. Sleeves rolled up to the elbow. Her hair was a mess. There was sand on her legs to the knee.

There was a man walking beside her.

Sue turned off the tap. She stood there with the coffee pot in her hands and the water running off it into the sink, and looked.

He was tall. Taller than Leah by four or five inches; Leah was five-ten. Broad in the shoulder. Black hair, short, wet. A couple of days of dark stubble. A gray T-shirt, in November, in the rain, with no jacket, and he wasn't cold. She could see from here that he wasn't cold. The rain was hitting his shoulders and coming off as a faint gray haze, the way it came off her son, the way it had come off every one of the La Push boys since the spring.

Her stomach did something.

*Another one,* she thought. Very clearly. *Of course. Of course it's another one.*

They walked very close. Close enough that every few steps their arms touched. Leah was talking, fast, the way she talked when she was angry about something, waving one hand. The man was listening. He had his head bent a little toward her. He said something, short, and Leah stopped talking and hit him in the arm, and he took it, and Sue saw her daughter's face as she did it.

She put the coffee pot down on the drainboard.

She dried her hands on the dish towel. She went and sat back down at the kitchen table across from her son, who still had his forehead on his arms, and picked up her cold coffee, and waited.

---

Seth smelled them before they got to the step.

She watched it happen. His head came up off the table all at once, like a dog's, his nostrils flaring. His face went through about five colors. Then he put his head back down on the table, harder, so his forehead made a little thump on the wood, and said "*No,*" very quietly, to nobody.

The back door opened.

Leah came in first. She stopped two steps inside the door the way she always did, the way her father always had, a habit she didn't know she had: stopping and looking at the room before she walked into it. She looked at her mother at the table. She looked at Seth with his head down. She looked back at her mother.

She had sand in her eyebrows. She had a bruise coming up on the side of her neck, just above the collar of the flannel shirt, and Sue looked at it and looked away.

"Hi, Mom," Leah said.

"Morning."

The man came in behind her. He had to duck a little under the frame; the door had been built in 1958 for people who were five foot nine. He stopped beside Leah, just inside, and stood there.

He had very green eyes. That was the first thing Sue noticed, up close. Green like the bottom of a bottle. The second thing she noticed was that he'd stopped exactly where Leah had stopped, and looked at the room exactly the way Leah had looked at it, the door and the window and the back hall and the people and the distance to each of them, all in about a second. The third thing she noticed was the heat. He was standing six feet from her and she could feel it on her face, like standing near a woodstove.

The fourth thing she noticed was the smell.

She didn't have the nose. None of the women did; none of the men did either, until they phased. But she'd been married eighteen years, and she'd been nineteen once, and her son was sitting across the table from her with his forehead on the wood making a very small, very high sound, like a kettle just before it boils.

She put her coffee down.

"Mrs. Clearwater," the man said.

English. A low voice, a little rough, like he'd been up all night. Which, she thought, he probably had.

"This is Harry," said Leah.

The kitchen went very quiet.

Sue had known. She'd known since about ten o'clock last night, when Leah had stood dripping in the living room and said *I imprinted*, and Sue had sat on the couch watching a man with no sound put something in an oven, and some part of her that was very old and very tired had already known. Leah hadn't said a name. Sue hadn't asked. But she'd lain awake and listened to an English voice in her backyard at three in the morning, and she'd known.

It went through her anyway. Standing in her kitchen. At a quarter to nine on a Tuesday morning in November, in the rain, with the pancakes keeping warm in the oven and the butter softening on the counter.

*Harry.*

She looked at her daughter. Leah was looking at her. Leah's face had the look it had had at nine years old, standing in this kitchen in a wet swimsuit, waiting to find out whether she was in trouble. Under that there was something else, and Sue saw it, and it nearly undid her. Leah was afraid of what her mother's face was going to do when she heard that name.

Across the table, Seth had lifted his head.

The red had gone out of him. He was looking at his mother, and his face had gone very still and very young, and he had his father's eyes.

Sue looked at the man.

He was standing very still in her doorway. He'd seen it. She watched him see it. She watched him look at her face, and at Leah's, and at Seth's, and work something out, and she watched him not understand it, and she watched him decide not to say anything until he did. He didn't fill the silence. He didn't apologize for something he didn't know he'd done. He just stood there in his T-shirt with the rain on his shoulders and waited.

"Harry," Sue said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Of course it is," said Sue Clearwater.

She got up. She went to the stove and got the coffee pot off the drainboard and filled it and put it back on, and stood there with her back to all of them for a minute, looking at the pot, until her eyes stopped stinging.

Then she got another mug down out of the cupboard.

"You drink coffee?" she said, without turning round.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Sit down. You're letting the heat out."

---

He sat down. Leah sat down next to him. Seth sat across from both of them and looked at the table.

Sue poured three coffees and put the pancakes on the table and the butter and the syrup, and the bacon she'd made at seven and kept in the other oven, and the eggs she made now, fast, a dozen of them, in the big pan. She put all of it on the table without saying anything, and sat down at the end with her own mug, and watched him eat.

He ate like her boys.

That was something. She'd been watching young men eat at this table for a year and a half, since the night Seth first phased and came in at dawn and ate everything in the fridge, including half a jar of mayonnaise with a spoon. They ate like they were trying to put out a fire. This one did the same. He was polite about it; he said *thank you* when she passed him the syrup and *these are very good, Mrs. Clearwater* when he'd had about six. But he went through the pancakes like a man shoveling snow. Leah was doing the same thing beside him. They didn't look at each other. They didn't need to; they were passing things without asking. He'd reach for the butter and she'd already be pushing it toward him. She'd want the salt and it'd be in front of her.

Seth wasn't eating.

Seth was sitting across from them with a fork in his hand and a stack of pancakes on his plate that he hadn't touched, looking at a point on the table about six inches in front of his plate. His ears had gone red again.

"Seth," Sue said. "Eat."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry."

"I'm not *hungry*, Mom."

Leah, beside the man, put her fork down. "Seth."

"What."

"Look at me."

"No."

"Seth. Look at me."

He looked up. He looked at his sister for about one second. His face went from red to something closer to purple. He looked back down at the table.

"You smell like—" he started.

"Finish that sentence," said Leah.

Seth opened his mouth. He looked at his mother. He shut his mouth.

"The beach," he said.

"The beach," Leah agreed.

"You smell like the beach."

"We went to the beach."

"Great," said Seth. "Great. That's great. That's—I'm going to go live in the ocean."

"Take a coat," said Leah.

The man laughed. It came out round a mouthful of pancake, and everybody looked at him.

He swallowed. He took a drink of coffee. Then he looked across the table at Seth, very straight, and Sue watched her son go absolutely rigid in his chair.

"Morning, Balto," the man said.

"Please stop calling me that," said Seth.

"No."

"*Please.*"

"No." The man put his mug down. "About last night—"

"Oh my God," said Seth.

"I'm only saying—"

"*Don't.*" That was Leah.

"I was going to apologise."

"*DON'T,*" said Seth and Leah at the same time.

Sue looked from one of them to the other. Her son, purple, looking at the table. Her daughter, glaring at the man, with a corner of her mouth trying to go up and Leah not letting it. The man, looking back and forth between the two of them with an expression of total, mild, innocent confusion that Sue didn't believe for one second.

"What happened last night?" said Sue.

"Nothing," said all three of them.

Sue picked up her coffee.

"Mm," she said.

---

She asked her questions after that. She'd waited long enough.

She asked them the way she'd asked the boys, each of them, one at a time, when they'd started bringing their girls round, back when she still had a husband and a kitchen table and a seat on the council beside him, before the heart attack, before the council had asked her to take his. She asked straight. She didn't dress it up. She was too old and too tired and she'd buried too much to dress anything up anymore.

"Leah says you're a wolf," she said.

The man glanced sideways at Leah. Leah looked back at him, eyebrows up, as if to say *well, you are*.

"Something like," he said.

"Something like."

"I don't know what it is, Mrs. Clearwater," he said. "Honestly. I don't know where it came from or what it is. I know what it does. That's all."

"You're not Quileute."

"No, ma'am."

"You're not from any tribe I know."

"I'm from Surrey," he said. "Little Whinging. It's sort of near London." He paused. "It's very boring. There aren't any tribes."

Leah made a sound into her coffee mug that might have been a laugh.

"Sue," said Sue.

He looked at her.

"Not Mrs. Clearwater," she said. "Sue. You can call me Sue."

He looked at her for a moment longer, very steady, the green eyes, and then nodded once.

"Sue," he said.

"What do you do, Harry?"

There was a pause.

It wasn't long. She'd sat across tables from a lot of men who had something they didn't want to tell her, councilmen and Bureau of Indian Affairs men and the man from the timber company in 1994 who'd wanted the rights to the ridge above the Sol Duc, and she knew what it looked like when somebody went looking for a lie. He didn't go looking for one. He just sat there, and she watched him decide how much to say, and she watched him decide on not very much.

"I find things out," he said. "For people. That's about as far as I can go."

"For who?"

"For people," he said again, very gently. "Who'd be cross if I said."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Yes."

"For you?"

"For other people, mostly."

"Is it going to be dangerous for her?"

He looked at Leah. Leah was looking at the table, very still, with her hands round her mug.

"No," he said. "Not if I can help it. And I can help most things."

"Can you lie to me?" Sue said.

The kitchen went quiet again. Seth looked up. Leah looked up.

"Leah says you can't lie to her," said Sue. "She told me last night. She said it was the first thing she noticed after the—after it happened. Neither of you can." She looked at him. "I want to know if you can lie to me."

"Yes," he said.

"Would you?"

"If it kept her safe," he said. "Or you. Or him." He nodded at Seth without looking. "In a heartbeat."

Sue looked at him for a long time.

"Well," she said finally. "That's honest."

"Mom," said Leah, "you can just ask him things through me. If you want. He can't lie to me. I can't lie to you. Well—I can. But I won't."

"I know you won't," said Sue.

"So you can ask me."

"I know I can." Sue drank her coffee. She looked at the man over the rim of the mug. "I'd rather ask him."

---

The owl came at nine-fifteen.

Sue saw it first. She was sitting at the end of the table facing the window over the sink, and she was about to get up for more coffee, and there was an owl on the sill outside it.

It was huge. She'd lived in La Push her whole life and seen owls in the woods since she was a girl, the little screech owls and the big horned ones that sat on the telephone poles out on the highway at dusk. This was bigger than any of those. Brown and gold, with orange eyes the size of quarters and two tufts on its head like a bad toupee, and it was sitting on her kitchen windowsill in the rain at nine-fifteen in the morning, looking in at her. It had something tied to its leg.

It tapped on the glass with its beak. Twice.

Sue put her mug down.

"There's an owl on my windowsill," she said.

The man turned round in his chair and looked.

"Ah," he said. "That'll be for me."

He got up. He went to the sink and opened the window, and the owl came in through it with the rain, a whole great flap of brown and gold wings that filled up the kitchen and knocked a dish towel off the rack, and landed on the edge of the counter by the toaster and held out its leg at him in a way that, Sue would have sworn, was impatient.

"Hello, Bertram," the man said. "You're a long way from home."

The owl looked at him with enormous scorn. He untied the thing on its leg, which was a roll of gray paper with a wax seal on it, and the owl immediately turned its back on him and began picking at the bacon on the plate by the stove.

"Bertram," said Seth faintly.

"He's from—" The man stopped.

He stopped mid-word. Sue saw it happen. His hand came up toward his throat, about halfway, the way you'd go to touch a sore place, and stopped there. His jaw tightened. He stood very still for a second by the counter with the roll of gray paper in his other hand and his eyes half closed, like a man waiting for a cramp to pass.

Then he breathed out and lowered his hand.

"He's my boss's owl," he said. "That's as far as I can go on that one."

Leah was watching him. Sue saw her daughter's hand go to her own throat, and stop, and go back down.

The man broke the seal. He unrolled the paper and read it, standing by the counter, while the owl ate Sue's bacon.

She watched his face while he read it. She'd thought, when he came in, that he had the kind of face that didn't show much. She'd been right. But she'd been watching faces for fifty-one years, and she watched his now, and she saw something happen round the eyes as he got to the bottom of the page. Something that might have been a laugh. Something that might have been the other thing.

He read it twice. Then he rolled it back up.

"Well," he said. "There you go."

"What is it?" said Leah.

"I've been promoted." He tapped the roll against his palm. "Or exiled. Hard to tell with him."

"To what?"

He looked at Sue.

And Sue, sitting at the end of her own kitchen table, saw him make a decision. She saw him weigh it. She saw him look at her, the woman whose kitchen this was, whose land this was, whose husband's name he had, and decide that this, at least, was hers to know.

"The people I work for," he said carefully, "have decided I'm to stay. Here. For the foreseeable." He paused. She saw his hand twitch toward his throat, and stop. "In an official capacity. As their—" another pause, very slight, "—their liaison. To your people."

"To my people," said Sue.

"Yes, ma'am."

"To the Quileute."

"Yes, ma'am."

Sue put her coffee down.

"Nobody," she said, "asked the Quileute."

The man looked at her. Then, slowly, one corner of his mouth went up.

"No, ma'am," he said. "They never do."

She looked at him for a long time. The owl tore a strip of bacon in half on her counter. Seth had his hands over his mouth. Leah was looking at her mother with an expression Sue hadn't seen on her daughter's face in about two years, and it took Sue a second to work out that it was delight.

"Then you'll come to council," Sue said.

"Ma'am?"

"The tribal council. We meet the first Thursday of the month. At the community center. Billy Black and Old Quil Ateara and me." She picked her mug back up. "If you're going to be *liaison* to the Quileute, then you'll come and sit at the council table and you'll ask the Quileute. Like anybody else. And they'll tell you whether they want a liaison or not." She took a drink. "And if they say no, you'll take that back to whoever it is who sends you birds."

The man stood by her counter with his boss's letter in his hand.

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

"December seventh," said Sue. "Seven o'clock. Wear a shirt."

"Yes, ma'am."

Across the table, Leah put her face down on her arms on the table, exactly where Seth's had been at six that morning, and her shoulders started to shake.

---

Before they went, he gave Seth something.

Sue was at the sink again, washing up, watching them in the window glass. He'd been standing by the back door waiting for Leah, who'd gone upstairs to put on her own clothes and was taking her time about it, and he'd put his hand in the pocket of his jeans and taken something out and held it out across the kitchen to her son.

"Here," he said.

Seth looked at it. Then he looked up, startled.

It was two little halves of black glass. Sue could see them from the sink. Shiny, the size of fingernails. And three pieces of leather.

"That's Layla's," Seth said. "That's the—that's the bead."

"I picked it up," the man said. "Last night. On the ridge. Couldn't leave it lying about. Somebody might find it."

"It's broken."

"It's spent. The stone's fine. Watch."

He held the two halves together on his palm, edge to edge. He didn't say anything. He didn't do anything Sue could see. He just looked at them.

And they were one.

Sue stopped washing up. She stood at the sink with her hands in the hot water and watched it in the window glass. The two halves of black glass went together the way water goes together, without a seam, without a mark, and lay there on his palm whole. The bits of leather, at the same time, slid across his hand toward each other, and knotted, and smoothed, and lay there too, a band.

He held it out to Seth.

"The stone's whole," he said. "The spell's gone. You used it up; that's what it was for. Tell her to fill it again. She'll know how. Took her all night, last time. She'll be faster now she's done it once."

Seth took it. He held it in his big hand and looked at it.

"Thanks," he said.

"You did all right yesterday, Balto," the man said.

Seth looked up.

"On the ridge," the man said. "Calling her. Most people panic and do nothing. Freeze up. Wait for somebody to tell them what to do. You panicked and did something." He shrugged. "Wrong something, as it turned out. But it was something."

"I got her thrown into a log," said Seth.

"You got her *there*," the man said. "What happened after that was me." He looked at Seth for a second longer. "I don't hurt people I don't have to, Seth. I didn't have to hurt her. I made sure of it."

Seth looked at him for a long moment.

Then he closed his fingers over the black bead and put it in the pocket of his shorts.

"Okay," he said.

Leah came down the stairs then. Jeans, a black sweatshirt with the hood up, her own sneakers. She'd brushed the sand out of her hair, mostly. She stood at the bottom of the stairs and looked at the two of them, her brother and the man, standing in the kitchen with something just finished between them, and Sue saw her see it, and saw her decide not to ask.

"Ready?" the man said.

"Where are you going?" said Sue.

"The Cullens'," said Leah.

Sue dried her hands. She turned round and leaned against the sink.

"Why would you take him there?"

"Because they're going to talk about him whether he's there or not," Leah said. "Might as well be there."

"And I want a look at the kid," the man said.

Sue looked at him.

"The little girl," she said. "Bella's girl."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He was quiet a moment. He looked at Leah, and Leah looked back at him, and Sue saw something go between them that she couldn't see, the way she'd seen things go between Jacob and Seth for a year and a half now, at this table, without a word. Then he looked back at Sue.

"Because somebody wanted a look at her on Sunday night," he said. "And he didn't ask nicely."

Sue's hands went still on the dish towel.

"Is she—"

"She's fine. He's dealt with." He said it flat and plain, the way Harry Clearwater used to say *the boat's tied up*. "I want to see her for myself. That's all."

Sue looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded.

"Go on, then," she said.

Leah came across the kitchen and kissed her mother on the cheek, quick, the way she hadn't done in about two years. Then she went and stood by the man at the back door.

"You're not driving?" said Sue.

"His car's in Forks," said Leah. "We're—" She made a face. "He's going to do a thing."

"What kind of thing?"

"You'll see," said Seth, from the table, darkly. "They do that."

The man held out his arm. Leah looked at it like it was a dead fish.

"I'm going to hate this, aren't I," she said.

"Every second," he said.

She took his arm.

There was a small sound, like somebody turning the page of a book, and Sue Clearwater was standing in her own kitchen looking at an empty space by her back door where her daughter had been. A little puff of air came across the room and stirred the dish towel in her hands. The owl, on the counter by the toaster, didn't even look up from the bacon.

Sue looked at the empty space for a while.

Then she looked at her son.

"Yeah," said Seth. "They do that."

Sue went back to the sink. She put her hands in the hot water. She looked out the window at the rain and the road and the bend by the Atearas' place where they'd come walking up together an hour ago, close, not quite holding hands.

"He eats," she said, to nobody. "I'll give him that."

### XI. Edward

*The Cullen house — 9:51 a.m.*

He heard Leah arrive before he heard her arrive.

That was how he'd think of it afterward. Her mind came on all at once, on the front lawn, as if somebody had flipped a switch. Minds came up the drive; he heard them three miles out, getting louder. Hers was simply *there*, a hundred feet from the porch, at full volume, and the first thing in it was:

*NEVER. AGAIN. Never, ever, ever, ever again, oh my God, I'm going to be sick, I'm going to throw up on the Cullens' lawn, never again—*

And beside her, on the lawn, a silence.

Edward was at the piano. He'd been at the piano since six, not playing, sitting on the bench with his hands in his lap, listening to the house. He'd been listening to Jacob sleep on the couch, the way Jacob slept, heavy and sprawling and dreaming of nothing but warmth and the weight on his chest. He'd been listening to Renesmee sleep on Jacob's chest. He'd been listening to Bella, in the kitchen with Esme, not sleeping, because none of them did, going round and round the same small worried track she'd been going round since last night: *he's seen her, he's been watching her, two months, from a ridge, and none of us knew*. He'd been listening, if he was honest, for that. For this. For Leah.

He'd also been not thinking about three o'clock in the morning. He'd been doing that very carefully since three o'clock in the morning.

He got up off the bench.

"They're here," he said.

Every head in the house turned. Jacob was off the couch in one motion with Renesmee still asleep in the crook of his arm. Bella was in the kitchen doorway. Carlisle came down the stairs. Jasper and Alice appeared from the back of the house. Rosalie and Emmett from the garage. Esme behind Bella, drying her hands.

"How?" said Alice. She was looking at the front windows with her face pinched. "I didn't see them come up the drive. I didn't see—"

"They didn't come up the drive," Edward said.

He went to the front door and opened it.

Leah was on the lawn, about a hundred feet out, bent over with her hands on her knees. Jeans, a black hooded sweatshirt, sneakers. She was breathing hard through her mouth and saying something under her breath, steadily, in a low furious monotone, that Edward could hear perfectly well from the porch and that consisted almost entirely of one word.

Beside her stood a man.

Edward looked at him.

Tall. Six-two, perhaps. Broad. Black hair, short. A gray T-shirt, jeans, boots. No coat. Stubble. He was standing with one hand resting lightly on the middle of Leah's back, waiting for her to finish being sick or not be sick, and looking at the house. At the porch. At Edward in the doorway.

And Edward heard nothing.

He'd been ready for that. He'd heard it from Seth's head, and Jacob's, and Leah's: the ridge, the voice in the rain saying *very real, unfortunately for everybody*, the cold sure hand going through Seth's mind like a man through a drawer. He'd known this man was something new. He'd stood in this front room last night and turned the name *Harry Potter* over and over in his own head for six hours, and he'd thought about what a mind like that would sound like from the inside, and he'd thought, privately, with a vanity he'd recognized and not much liked, that he'd be able to hear at least something. Everyone had something. In a hundred and five years he'd met exactly one mind he couldn't hear, and it was his wife's, and it was silent the way snow is silent: everywhere, completely, a soft white nothing that went all the way down.

He stood in the doorway and listened as hard as he'd ever listened to anything, and what he heard was a building.

That was the only way he'd ever be able to describe it. A building. A large one, solid, with a lot of rooms, and he knew it had a lot of rooms because he could *feel* them, the way you can feel the size of a house you're standing outside of at night. There were lights on in it. He could tell that, too, faintly, the way you can tell from the street that somebody's home. But every window was shuttered. Every door was shut. And the walls were so thick and so smooth and so well made that he couldn't hear so much as a footstep through them. There was something in there, plenty, more than there was in most minds. He was simply on the outside of it, and it was built to keep him there.

He stood very still and listened to it, and it did not let him in.

Then one window opened.

He felt it. Like a shutter swinging back on the side of the building, high up. Just one. And through it, quite clearly, in an English voice, dry and unhurried and pitched exactly for him:

*Morning, Edward. Don't.*

And the shutter closed again.

Edward stood in the doorway of his own house.

Out on the lawn, the man looked at him. The green eyes didn't change at all. He took his hand off Leah's back, and Leah straightened up, very pale, and wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist, and glared at the man with an intensity that should have set him on fire.

"Better?" the man said.

"*Never,*" said Leah, "*again.*"

"You said."

"I'm saying it again."

"Noted."

They came up the lawn toward the house. Edward stepped back out of the doorway. He stepped back further than he needed to; he noticed himself do it.

---

They were all in the front room when the man came in.

He stopped two steps inside the door, the way Layla had stopped on her first day, the way Leah always stopped, and looked. Edward watched him look. He couldn't hear it, but he could see it: the eyes going round the room once, unhurried, settling on each of them for perhaps half a second. Jacob by the couch, with the child. Bella in the kitchen doorway. Esme. Carlisle at the foot of the stairs. Rosalie halfway up them. Emmett by the piano. Alice. Jasper.

The eyes stopped on Jasper a fraction longer than the rest. Then went on. Then came, last, to Edward, and stayed.

Nobody said anything.

"Right," said the man. "Morning, all."

"*Ginger,*" said Jacob.

It came out of Jacob like a growl. Edward heard where it came from: Jacob had been lying on that couch since three in the morning with *the big ginger one* going round and round in his head, from Leah, from the memory of the specimen room that the pack had seen, and he had been furious about it for nine hours.

The man looked at him.

"That's you, is it," he said. "The big ginger one."

"I'm not ginger."

"You're a bit ginger."

"I'm *russet*."

"Russet," said the man. "Right. Sorry." He looked at Jacob a moment longer, and then at the child asleep in the crook of Jacob's arm, and something happened in his face that Edward could see and couldn't hear and couldn't name. "Morning, Jacob."

Jacob's jaw worked. He didn't answer.

"Mr. Potter," said Carlisle.

He'd come forward from the stairs, one step. His hands were open at his sides. He had on the face he wore in the emergency room, and Edward could hear him behind it, his mind running clear and steady and kind and very, very careful: *A man with a history. A man who's hurt others. Speak to him the way you'd speak to anyone who's frightened and armed. Except he isn't frightened. Except—*

"Evans," said the man.

Carlisle stopped.

"In public," the man said. "Harry Evans. I'm a nature photographer from Islington, I'm writing a book about rainforests, mostly moss, and I eat at the diner on Forks Avenue every day at eleven. If you need to call me anything outside this house, that's what you call me." He looked at Carlisle. "In here, Harry'll do."

"Harry," Carlisle said. He inclined his head. "Then I'm Carlisle. Welcome to our home."

"Ta."

"Would you like to sit down? Can we get you anything? Esme keeps—"

"I've eaten," the man said. "Leah's mum fed me. Lot of pancakes." He didn't sit down. He stood where he was, two steps inside the door, with his hands in the pockets of his jeans, and Leah stood beside him with her hood up and her arms crossed, and the two of them looked at the room like people waiting to see who'd blink.

"Doc," the man said. "I'm going to tell you some things. Then I'm going to go. It won't take long."

---

He said it the way, Edward imagined, a surgeon would tell you the results of a scan.

"A nomad came through here," he said. "Night before last. Sunday into Monday. Polish, three hundred years old, red eyes, called himself Casimir Wolski. He'd been sent. By your friend in Italy." He paused. "The one with the hands."

The room went cold. Edward felt it. He heard it: every mind in the house going still at once, the way a forest goes quiet when something big walks through it.

*Aro.* Bella. *Oh God. Aro.*

"Aro sent him to find out whether Bella had been changed," the man said. "That's all. Just that. Somebody in Volterra's been nagging him about it, and he thought he'd send someone nobody would notice. Have a look. Report back." He looked at Bella in the kitchen doorway. "He had his look. He reported back. Yesterday afternoon. He told Aro yes, you've been changed, your eyes are going gold, the family's as dull as ever, there's nothing else to report. And then he told Aro he was going to Mexico for the winter."

"And did he?" said Jasper. Very quietly.

"No."

"Where did he go?"

"Nowhere," said the man. "He's not going anywhere."

Jasper looked at him. Edward heard Jasper's mind turn that over, quick and precise, the way it turned everything over, and come down on the only possible reading. He heard Jasper decide not to ask the next question. He heard him file it.

"You said *nothing else*," said Bella.

The man looked at her.

"You said he told Aro there was nothing else." Bella's voice was very steady. Edward could hear what it was costing her. "Nothing else like what?"

The man didn't answer right away.

He looked at Jacob instead. At the couch. At the child asleep in the crook of Jacob's arm, bronze curls all over Jacob's T-shirt, one fist closed on the collar of it, her mouth open. He looked at her for long enough that Edward saw Jacob's arm tighten round her, saw Jacob's lips start to come back off his teeth.

"He smelled her," the man said.

Nobody moved.

"Sunday night. Four in the morning, near enough. He came down off the ridge on the north side and stood in the trees behind this house and he smelled her. A heartbeat in a house where there shouldn't be any. Blood and not-blood." The man's voice didn't change at all. "He stood there for an hour. Breathing her in. Then he went back up the ridge."

Edward felt Bella go, beside him. He felt it before he saw it, before he heard it, a wave of something going through her mind so huge and so white that for a second there was nothing in his own head but the noise of it. Then she was across the room and standing between the man and the couch, between the man and Jacob and her daughter, with her back to them and her teeth bared and her whole body shaking.

The man looked at her. He didn't move. He didn't take his hands out of his pockets.

"It's all right," he said.

"He was *here*—"

"He was. And now he's not, and he's never going to be anywhere ever again. And he didn't tell anybody." The man looked past her, at the child. "He tried to. On the phone. At the end, he tried. He didn't manage it."

"*How do you know?*"

"Because I was holding the phone," the man said.

The room was so quiet Edward could hear the rain on the long glass at the back of the house.

"How," said Jasper, "did a nomad stand in our trees for an hour without any of us hearing him?"

He was looking at Edward.

They were all looking at Edward.

Edward stood in the middle of his own front room, a hundred and five years old, with the most sensitive ears in the house and a gift that let him hear every thought within a few miles, and remembered where he'd been at four o'clock on Monday morning. In the cottage in the trees behind the big house, a quarter mile off. With his wife. With every door in his own mind shut, the way he'd learned to shut them since August, the way he'd never once in a hundred years needed to shut them until he'd had something worth shutting them for.

He'd been busy.

And on the other side of the room, he saw the man look at him, and saw the faintest movement at the corner of the man's mouth, and knew—without hearing a thing—that the man knew exactly where Edward had been and what he'd been doing at four in the morning on Monday, and found it funny.

"He came on the north side," the man said, to Jasper, before Edward could open his mouth. "Downwind. Upslope. Moving very slow. He was good at that; he'd been doing it three hundred years. And she—" a nod at Alice, "—couldn't see him coming, because his future was all wrapped up in the kid's, and she can't see the kid. Can you?"

Alice stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Lucky guess."

"That isn't a guess."

"No," the man agreed. "It isn't."

---

"So," the man said. "Here's how it goes from now on."

He took his hands out of his pockets.

Edward had been waiting for that, without knowing he'd been waiting for it. He'd seen it in Seth's head, in Leah's: the hands coming out of the pockets on the ridge, and the vampire going up in the air, and the wolves going down. He watched the man's hands now. They didn't do anything. They hung loose at his sides. But every person in the room had seen what Edward had seen in Seth's head, and he could hear every one of them watching those hands.

"The kid doesn't leave this house without a wolf," the man said. "Ever. Not to the store. Not to Charlie's. Not for a walk in the garden. Jacob, or Seth, or Leah, or I'm told there's a few more on the reservation who might do at a push. A wolf. Every time."

"She always has Jacob," said Bella. Her voice had gone thin.

"Good. Keep it that way." The man looked at Carlisle. "No visitors. Nobody new sees her. Nobody. Not for a year at least."

"Our cousins," Carlisle said slowly. "In Alaska. The Denali coven. We'd thought, at Christmas—"

"Don't."

"They're family."

"Then they'll understand."

"Harry—"

"Doc." The man looked at him. "Every person who knows she exists is a person who can tell somebody. Every person who's seen her is a person whose hand an old man in Italy can take one day and see her in. That's what he does, isn't it? Takes your hand. Reads the lot." He waited. Edward heard Carlisle think *how does he know that*, and saw the man see Carlisle think it, and didn't hear a thing. "The fewer hands, the better. That's arithmetic."

Carlisle was silent.

"And if we don't take orders from you?" said Jasper.

He said it mildly. He was standing by the glass wall at the back of the room with his arms folded and Alice beside him, and he said it the way a man might ask about the weather. Edward heard what was under it. He heard Jasper's mind laying the room out like a map, the way it always did, every distance, every angle. He heard Jasper decide where he'd go first, if it came to it. He heard him decide.

The man looked at him.

"Then you'll be making your own decisions about her," he said. "And I'll be making mine."

Jasper held his eyes.

"I've done this a long time," Jasper said. "Longer than you've been alive. A good deal longer. I've been in a lot of rooms with a lot of men who thought they were the most dangerous thing in them." He tipped his head, slightly. "I'm trying to work out where you'd start. In this room. If you had to."

"You'd be second," said the man.

He didn't hesitate. He didn't smile. He said it in exactly the tone Edward imagined he'd use to tell you the time.

Jasper's eyes narrowed a fraction. "Who's first?"

The man turned his head and looked at Edward.

That was all. He didn't say anything else. He just looked at him, across the room, steady and green and entirely unbothered. And Edward listened, with everything he had, for something—a flicker, a flash of intent, the faintest echo of a plan—and heard a building with all its shutters closed.

"Because he'd hear the rest of you coming up with a plan," the man said. "Can't have that."

Nobody said anything.

"Who's third?" said Emmett.

Edward turned his head. Emmett was leaning on the piano with his huge arms folded, and his mind was a big bright cheerful blaze of pure, uncomplicated interest.

"You," said the man.

"Why me?"

"Because you'd be having too much fun to stop."

Emmett considered this. "Fair," he said.

"He's not kidding," said Leah. "Just so you know. He's not."

"I know he's not," said Jasper quietly. He was still looking at the man. Edward heard him file it next to Volterra, next to the newborn armies of the South, next to the night in Monterrey in 1863 that Jasper never let himself think about directly. "I can feel him."

The man's eyes went to him.

"I can feel what you feel," Jasper said. "That's mine. Like his." A small nod at Edward. "I've been trying since you came in. You're very calm. Very cold. I can't get much past that. There's a lot you're keeping where I can't reach it." He paused. "There's one thing I can feel all the way through. It's very loud. It's the only thing in you that is."

The man didn't say anything.

Jasper's eyes moved to Leah, standing beside him with her hood up and her arms crossed, and moved back.

"I'd be second," Jasper said. "If it came to it. I believe you. But if anyone in this room ever laid a hand on her, I don't think you'd bother with an order."

"No," said the man. "I wouldn't."

---

"Let's be clear," he said, a little later.

He'd sat down, finally. On the arm of the big chair by the window, where Charlie sat on Sundays. Leah was standing behind him, leaning against the window frame. Jacob had put Renesmee down on the couch, still asleep, under a blanket, and was sitting on the floor in front of it with his back against it and his arms on his knees, like a dog in front of a door. Everybody else had settled, more or less, the way people settle when it becomes clear that nobody is going to start a fight in the next ten seconds.

"I care about her," the man said. He tipped his head back toward Leah without looking. "I care about her brother, and her mum, because she does. I care about Jacob because she'd be annoyed if anything happened to him, and he's her—whatever he is. Alpha. And I'll look after the kid, because she's a kid, and because she's the most interesting thing I've seen in eight years, and because I'm not having that old bastard in Italy put his hand on her. Not ever. Not on my watch." He looked round the room. "The rest of you are a line in a file."

The words dropped into the room like stones into a well.

"The file says you're worth more alive than dead," the man said. "You're useful. You're interesting. You've got a doctor who's spent three hundred years learning how not to eat people and you've got a man who reads minds and a woman who sees the future and a bloke who's fought more wars than I've read about. You're on the right side, more or less. The file says don't touch you." He looked at Carlisle. "I'd like it to go on saying that. Try not to give me a reason to edit it."

Carlisle looked at him for a long moment.

"May I ask," Carlisle said, "who keeps the file?"

"You may," said the man.

He didn't say anything else.

Carlisle waited. The man waited. After about ten seconds, very slowly, Carlisle smiled.

"I see," he said.

"Thought you might."

From the window frame, Leah made a small sound that might have been a snort.

Edward watched her. He could hear her. He could hear all of her, the way he'd always been able to hear her since the spring, except for the one cold locked room. And he listened to her now, watching the man sit on the arm of Charlie's chair and tell a houseful of vampires they were a line in a file.

She was standing against the window frame with her arms crossed and her hood up, and the thing coming off her mind, as clear as a radio, was satisfaction. Sharp and fierce and pleased. *Yeah,* Leah Clearwater was thinking. *Yeah. Tell them.*

And under that, quieter, steadier, something else. Something that sat in the middle of her where the old grinding stone of Sam Uley used to be, and was the size and shape of the man on the chair arm, and that Edward had only ever heard before in one other mind in his life.

His own. About Bella.

He looked away.

---

Emmett lasted about twenty minutes before he couldn't stand it anymore.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. I have to know."

The man looked at him.

"On the ridge," Emmett said. "In Seth's head. You caught him. The nomad. Falling. One arm." He held his own arm out, crooked, demonstrating. "Like a plank. I've been thinking about it all night. I've caught falling stuff. I've caught a *car*. But that's me." He tapped his chest. "You're not me. You're a guy. A guy with a library."

"I'm a guy with a library," the man agreed.

"So how strong are you?"

"Strong enough."

"No, but how strong. Like, numbers."

"Never measured."

"Want to?"

Edward heard it coming about a second before Emmett said it, because Emmett had been thinking about it for nine hours with the single-minded joy of a golden retriever thinking about a tennis ball. He heard Rosalie hear it too, and roll her eyes so hard Edward could hear that as well.

"Arm wrestle," said Emmett. "Me and you. Right now."

"No," said the man.

"Come on."

"I don't do party tricks."

"It's *science*. Carlisle loves science. Carlisle, tell him it's science."

"It is, technically," said Carlisle, who, Edward could hear, very much wanted to know.

"No," the man said again.

Emmett looked crushed. Then he looked sly. "Scared?"

The man looked at him for a long, flat moment.

Behind him, against the window frame, Leah said, "Oh, just do it, Harry. He's not going to shut up."

"He's going to break the table."

"So fix the table."

The man sighed. He got up off the arm of the chair.

"One go," he said.

They used the dining table. Esme's table, the long pale one in the dining room that sat twelve, that she'd found in a barn in Vermont in 1974 and refinished by hand. Esme came and stood in the doorway with her hands clasped in front of her and her mind going *oh, not the table, not the Vermont table, oh, Emmett*. Emmett sat down at one end of it and planted his elbow and held up his hand and grinned like Christmas had come. The man sat down across the corner from him, not quite opposite, and put his elbow on the pale wood, and took Emmett's hand.

Edward watched him do it. He watched the man's hand close round Emmett's, and he watched the man's face, and he saw the faintest flicker go across it at the touch. Cold. Hard. Like taking hold of a statue's hand. The man had touched one of them before—the nomad, under his arm, on the ridge—but Edward imagined he'd been busy, then, and not paying attention to how it felt.

"Jasper," said Emmett. "Count us down."

Jasper, leaning in the other doorway, sighed. "Three. Two. One."

Emmett pushed.

Edward had seen Emmett push before. He'd seen him push over a tree three feet through at the base; he'd seen him push a boulder the size of a van off the top of a cliff for fun; he'd seen him, once, in 1952, push a train. He knew exactly what Emmett pushing looked like, and he watched it now, the whole of Emmett's enormous strength coming down through his shoulder and his arm and into his hand all at once. Emmett's grin went tight. The pale wood under his elbow creaked.

The man's hand didn't move.

It didn't move at all. Edward watched it. It stayed exactly upright, the man's forearm vertical, his hand closed round Emmett's, as if Emmett weren't pushing at all. The man's face didn't change. His breathing didn't change. He sat there at the corner of the table and looked at Emmett with mild interest, the way you'd look at someone across a table who was telling a long story.

Emmett pushed harder.

His teeth came together. His arm swelled. The creak under his elbow became a groan. The whole table shifted an inch on the floor.

The man's hand didn't move.

"Emmett," said the man pleasantly. "You can stop being polite."

Emmett stopped being polite.

Edward saw it. Everything Emmett had. All of it, his whole body behind it, his face a mask of joyful effort, his feet braced on the floor so hard the boards under them cracked, a sound like a gunshot in the quiet room. Rosalie, on the stairs, sat forward.

The man's hand moved.

It moved slowly. It moved the way the hour hand of a clock moves, steadily, without any hurry, and it took Emmett's hand with it. Down. Toward the pale wood. Emmett's arm went with it, shaking, every muscle in it standing out like cable. Halfway. Three-quarters. Emmett's grin had gone to a snarl. The man's expression hadn't changed at all.

And then Emmett's elbow went through the table.

It went with a crack like a lightning strike. The pale Vermont oak split from Emmett's elbow to the far end in one long jagged line, and the whole table folded in the middle like a book closing, and the two halves went down to the floor with a crash that shook the glass in the long windows, and Emmett went down with them. He landed on his back in the wreckage with his hand still locked in the man's hand. The man was still sitting on his chair. He hadn't been pulled off it. He was leaning over a little, his arm extended down, holding Emmett's hand, flat on the floor among the splinters.

There was a long silence.

"Oh," said Esme faintly, from the doorway. "Oh, my table."

Emmett lay on his back in the ruins of the table and stared up at the ceiling.

Then he started to laugh.

It came up out of him like a geyser. He lay there in the splintered oak with the man still holding his hand and laughed so hard the floor shook, a huge delighted roar, a sound Edward had heard maybe a dozen times in seventy years, and every single one of those times it had been at something Rosalie had done.

"*Again,*" Emmett said, when he could speak.

"No," said the man.

"*Again!* Come on! I wasn't ready!"

"You were ready." The man let go of his hand. He stood up. He looked down at the table, split in two on the floor with Emmett lying in the middle of it, and then over at Esme in the doorway, and Edward watched his face change, slightly. The coldness went out of it for a moment and something else came in, something almost sheepish.

"Sorry, Mrs. Cullen," he said.

"It was from Vermont," Esme said. "Nineteen seventy-four."

"Right," said the man. "Hang on."

He didn't move his hands. He didn't say anything. He just looked at it.

And the table came back together.

Edward watched it do it. The two halves rose up off the floor, slowly, Emmett rolling off them with a startled yelp, and turned in the air and met in the middle, and closed. The long jagged crack ran backward along the wood the way a tear runs backward down a cheek in a film played in reverse, and sealed, and was gone. Splinters came up off the floor from all over the room, from under the sideboard and behind the curtains and out of Emmett's hair, hundreds of them, and flew back into it, each one to its own place. The table settled back down on its four legs on the floor exactly where it had been, and was still.

Esme came into the room. Slowly. She came and stood by the table and put her hand on it. She ran her palm along the pale wood, the whole length of it, from one end to the other.

At the far end, she stopped.

"The scratch," she said.

The man looked at her.

"There was a scratch," Esme said. "Here. Emmett did it. In nineteen eighty-one. With a hockey skate. I could never get it out." She looked up at him. "It's gone."

The man shrugged. "Seemed a shame to put it back."

Esme looked at him for a long moment.

"Thank you," she said.

He nodded. He went back into the front room.

Behind him, Emmett sat up in the middle of the floor with his hair full of sawdust that hadn't quite made it back into the table, and looked after him with an expression of pure, uncomplicated love.

"I *like* him," Emmett said.

"Of course you do," said Rosalie.

---

It was Rosalie who spoke to him next. Edward hadn't expected that.

She'd come down the stairs while Esme was standing by the table. She came into the front room and stood at the end of the couch, near Jacob, near the sleeping child, with her arms folded, and she looked at the man where he'd gone back to sitting on the arm of Charlie's chair.

"You have a vampire," she said. "In your tent."

"In a circle."

"Doing what?"

"Lying very still."

"What are you doing to him?"

"Asking him questions."

"How?"

The man looked at her. "Not politely."

Rosalie's mind, which Edward knew better than he wanted to, was very clear and very cold and going a long way back. Back to Rochester. To 1933. To a street in the dark and a man she'd been going to marry and his friends, and what they'd done, and what she'd done to each of them after, one at a time, in the years that followed, in their own houses, in their own beds. She'd been careful. She'd worn a wedding dress to do the last one. Edward had never once in seventy years heard her be sorry.

"And when you're done asking him," Rosalie said.

"I'm burning him," said Leah.

Rosalie's head turned.

Leah was still leaning against the window frame behind the man. She hadn't moved. She was looking at Rosalie, and Rosalie was looking at her, and Edward heard both of them, Leah's sharp and hot and Rosalie's cold and still, and for the first time since the day Edward had brought Bella to this house there was nothing in either of their minds about the other one that wanted to bite.

"He ate a woman," Leah said. "On Sunday. Up the Hoh. Her name was Dana. She was taking a picture of the river. He let her finish taking it." Leah's voice didn't change. "He was wearing her jacket when we caught him. When it's time, I'm burning him."

Rosalie looked at her.

"Good," she said.

That was all. She looked at Leah a moment longer, then at the man, and then she turned and went and sat down on the stairs again, on the bottom step this time, closer.

---

Layla and Seth arrived at a quarter past eleven, in Layla's white pickup with the cracked tail-light, and Edward heard them from the bottom of the drive. Layla's mind came up through the trees like an organ starting, that great deep structure under it humming, and the first thing on top of it, very loud, very deliberate, was:

*Bees. Bees, bees, bees, honey, wax, the queen, Gran's smoker, bees.*

Edward closed his eyes for a moment.

She came in behind Seth, the way she'd come in the first time. Stopped two steps inside the door. Looked round. Her eyes went straight to the man on the arm of the chair and stayed there, and Edward watched her face go through something very complicated very quickly and come out the other side as smooth and still as a pond.

"Mr. Potter," she said.

The man looked at her.

"Don't," he said.

"Sorry. Mr. Evans."

"Harry. In here. If you must."

"Harry." She said it the way you'd say the name of a dog you weren't sure about. Edward heard her think it a second time, *Harry*, privately, with a very small helpless shudder that had in it a skinny boy with glasses on the front page of a newspaper, and a pair of nail scissors, and a trunk lid. He looked away from that, politely. She'd have hated him for hearing it.

"Quinn," the man said. "How's the back?"

"Bruised to buggery," said Layla. "Your words."

"Use the Murtlap?"

"Twice."

"Good." He looked at her a moment longer. "Seth give you your bead?"

She held up her left wrist. The leather band was on it, the black bead lying against the inside of it, over the pulse. "He did. He said you fixed it."

"I fixed the stone. The rest's yours. You'll want to fill it again tonight."

"I know."

"Do it in the kitchen. Same table. Same words. It'll take less time the second go."

She looked at him. "How do you know what table I used?"

"Your boy's very loud," the man said. "We've covered this."

Seth, behind her, went red.

Layla stood there. Edward could hear her think. He could hear her think about a hundred things at once, under the bees, the way that organ-note of a mind did, and he could hear one of them rising to the top and her pushing it back down and it coming up again. *Who are you. Who do you work for. What are you doing here. Who made you swear.* Every one of them pushed down, firmly, one after another, like a woman shutting drawers. *Bees.*

The man was watching her do it.

"You're not going to ask," he said.

"No."

"Why not?"

Layla looked at him.

"Because I like my bees," she said.

There was a pause.

And then the man smiled. It was small. It was brief. It went across his face and was gone, but it was there, and Edward, who couldn't hear a word of what was behind it, saw it, and saw Leah see it, and heard Leah's mind light up like a struck match at the sight.

"Good answer," the man said.

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he said, in a completely different voice, low and quick, the way one craftsman talks to another across a workbench:

"Your Disjunction. The third braid's load-bearing."

Layla's eyes went wide.

"The one that does the unpicking," the man said. "It's the strongest of the three, so it's the one holding the structure up, and it's sitting right on the outside where anybody who reads the spell can see it. That's how I turned it. I didn't have to understand the whole thing. Just that braid. Pull it a quarter-turn and the whole thing points the other way." He held up two fingers, the way he had on the ridge, and turned them slightly. "Put a false braid in front of it. Something that looks load-bearing and isn't. Anybody tries to turn the spell, they'll grab the wrong one, and it'll come apart in their hands, and yours'll go straight through."

Layla stared at him.

Edward heard what was happening in her mind and found he didn't have a word for it. It was a curse-breaker's mind seeing a door she'd spent three years building and believed was locked, and watching a stranger walk up to it and show her where the keyhole was. What came up through her, under the bees and the shock, was something like a pianist hearing someone play her own piece back to her, faster.

"I'd have needed—" she said. "To find that. I'd have needed years."

"You'd have got there," the man said. "Eventually. Somebody'd have turned it on you first, though. Probably on a bad day."

"Like yesterday."

"Like yesterday."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded. Once. Short. And sat down on the end of the couch, near the child, and didn't say anything else, and her mind went quiet under the bees in a way Edward had never heard it go quiet before, the way a workshop goes quiet when somebody's bent over a bench.

---

He did the next thing to Bella.

Edward felt it the way you feel a draft in a room with the windows closed. Bella was standing at the end of the couch, beside Rosalie, near the child, with her arms folded, watching the man. She'd been watching him since he came in. She hadn't stopped. And the man, on the arm of Charlie's chair, turned his head and looked at her, and something went out from him toward her that Edward could not hear, and could not see, and nevertheless felt go past him in the air like a hand.

It hit Bella and stopped.

Bella flinched. Her head came up. Her eyes went wide. "What—"

"Oh," said the man softly. "Oh, that's lovely."

Edward had crossed the room. He didn't remember deciding to. He was standing between the man and his wife, and his lips had come back off his teeth, and a sound was coming out of his chest that he hadn't made since Volterra.

"*Don't,*" he said.

The man looked up at him. Calm. Green. Interested.

"Professional curiosity," the man said.

"You do not *touch*—"

"I didn't touch. I knocked." The man's eyes went past Edward to Bella. "It's a shield. Isn't it. Natural. Round the mind. I've seen one before, in a much smaller way." He tilted his head. "Runs in the family, I'd imagine."

Edward stopped.

He stood there with his teeth bared and his hands curled and that sound still in his chest, and he heard what the man had just said. *Runs in the family.* And he heard Bella hear it, behind him, and go still. And he thought of Charlie Swan, sitting in the big armchair every Sunday with his coffee, Charlie whose mind had always been a blur to Edward, a muffled thing, a radio through a wall. Charlie, who'd been eating his pie at the end of a diner counter every day for nine weeks while a man in a booth by the back wall ate his.

The man had been in Charlie's head. Or tried. Weeks ago.

"Harry," said Leah. Sharp.

The man turned his head and looked at her.

"Sorry," he said.

He said it to Leah, in a different voice, like a man who'd tracked mud across a clean floor. And the draft, the hand, whatever it had been, went away out of the air, and didn't come back.

Edward stood between him and his wife and breathed, which he didn't need to do.

"Sparkles," the man said, mildly, looking up at him, "you can sit down. I'm done."

"What did you call me?"

"Sparkles." The man's eyes didn't move from his. "You stood in a square in Italy once, didn't you. At noon. Under a clock tower. With your shirt off and your arms out." His voice was very quiet. "Don't do that again. It's untidy."

Edward felt it go through him like ice.

He hadn't heard it. He'd heard nothing. But he knew, suddenly, with total certainty, that somewhere in the last ten seconds, while he'd been standing over this man with his teeth bared, the man had walked into his head. Walked in. Into Edward Cullen's mind, which no one in a hundred and five years had ever so much as touched. And gone through it, quick and sure, like a man going through a drawer, and found the worst moment of his life, the clock tower and the noon sun and Bella running across the cobbles, and taken a look at it, and walked out again. And Edward hadn't felt a thing until it was over.

He felt it now. The draft. The faint cool trace of where somebody had been.

The man watched him feel it.

"Turnabout," he said.

---

The child woke up at half past eleven.

Edward heard her come up out of sleep the way she always did, all at once, bright, like someone turning on a lamp. She sat up on the couch under her blanket with her curls flattened on one side and looked round the room. She looked at her father. She looked at her mother. She looked at Jacob, on the floor in front of the couch, and smiled. She looked at Layla, on the end of the couch, and her face lit up, and Edward heard a picture go across the inside of her head of blue lights falling like snow.

Then she looked at the man on the arm of the chair, and stopped.

She looked at him for a long time. Edward listened to her look. His daughter's mind didn't work in words, mostly; it worked in pictures, the way she spoke, bright and fast and certain. He watched the pictures go across it now. The man's face. The green eyes. A sniff, a long one, her small nose working, the way Jacob's did. And a picture, sharp and clear: Jacob, in his wolf shape, enormous and russet. Then Leah, the gray one, from the field in June, from the treeline, from all the times she'd watched the wolves come and go across the lawn. And then—Edward went very still—a third shape. Bigger than the other two. Black. With green eyes. Standing in a dark place.

She'd never seen it. She'd never heard anyone describe it, not in her hearing. She'd smelled it. On him. On Leah. She'd smelled it, and put it together, and drawn it.

She climbed down off the couch.

"Nessie," said Bella.

The child didn't stop. She walked across the carpet in her socks, small and steady, past her mother's legs, past Edward, straight to the man on the arm of Charlie's chair. Every person in the room went still. Jacob came up onto his knees. Edward felt Bella, beside him, gather herself like a spring.

The man didn't move.

Renesmee stopped in front of him. She looked up at him. She was ten weeks old and the size of a child of two and a half, and she stood there in her socks and her nightgown with her curls flat on one side and looked up at a man who'd just told a room full of vampires that they were a line in a file, and she held up her hand.

She held it there, open, asking.

The man looked down at her.

Edward watched his face. He couldn't hear a thing behind it; the building had every shutter shut. But he saw the face. He saw it go very still, and then he saw something happen to it, round the eyes, round the mouth, something that made it look, for a moment, about ten years younger.

The man got down off the arm of the chair.

He went down on one knee on the carpet in front of her, so his face was level with hers. He looked at her hand. Then, slowly, he leaned forward and let her put it on his cheek.

Edward heard the picture go.

He'd felt her do it a thousand times. He'd been on the other end of it every day of her life. He felt her push now, felt the picture go out of her small warm palm and into the man's face, and he waited for it to hit the wall. The building. The shutters. Bella's shield turned her pictures aside sometimes, when Bella wasn't concentrating; he'd thought this would be the same.

It went in.

He felt it go in. He felt the man let it. Somewhere in the building, a door opened, just wide enough for a picture to walk through, and Renesmee's picture walked through it.

He saw it go. The black wolf in the dark place. And the question on it, bright and simple, the way all her questions were: *Is this you?*

The man was quiet a moment. Then he said, out loud, very softly:

"Yeah, sweetheart. Something like that."

Renesmee's face lit up. Another picture, fast: the black wolf, here, on the carpet, in this room, with her sitting on its back the way she sat on Jacob's. Holding on to its fur with both fists.

"Not today," the man said.

Her face fell. Not much. A little. A new picture: the blue lights. Layla's lights, falling like snow from the ceiling. And her own hand reaching up into them, and the ripple of blue going up her arm. And the question: *Can you do that?*

The man looked at her for a long moment.

"Not that," he said. "Something else. Hang on."

He took his face away from her hand, gently. He stayed on one knee. He held his own hand out, palm up, between them, about a foot off the carpet.

Edward saw Layla, at the end of the couch, sit up very straight.

He heard her think it before anything happened. *He's going to—oh, he's not, nobody can do that without—*

A light came out of the man's palm.

Silver. Bright. Colder and stronger than Layla's blue snowfall, and it came up off his hand like smoke off a match and *moved*. It moved like something alive. It poured up out of his palm and onto the carpet between him and the child, and gathered, and took a shape.

Edward heard what the man was expecting it to be, in Layla's head. Layla knew. Layla knew because she'd read every story there was about him since she was eight, and one of the stories, the one everybody told, the one from the war, was about a silver stag driving off a whole flight of Dementors in the dark. She was waiting for the stag. Edward watched her wait for it. Some part of the man, he'd have bet, was waiting for it too.

What came up off the carpet was a wolf.

It stood there, silver, shining, made of light, about the size of a large dog. Lean. Long-legged. Narrow through the chest, built for speed, built the way a greyhound is built, or a cheetah. Its ears were up. Its head was high. It looked round the room with eyes of white light, and then it looked at the child, and dropped its front end down onto the carpet, chest low, rump up, tail going.

A play bow.

Renesmee shrieked with delight. She threw herself at it. It ran. It ran round her, round the man, round the coffee table, in a long bright silver loop through the front room, its feet making no sound on the carpet, and she ran after it laughing, in her socks, with her arms out, and it let her almost catch it and then darted away, and everybody in the room was watching it, and nobody was breathing.

Edward looked at the man.

He was still on one knee on the carpet. He'd turned his head to watch the silver wolf go round. And his face, for the first time since he'd walked in the door, was completely open. No shutters. Nothing held back. Edward didn't need to hear him to read it. He was looking at the silver thing that had come out of his own hand as though it had punched him.

"Huh," the man said, very quietly.

At the window frame, Leah had pushed off the wall.

She stood there, arms uncrossed, hood fallen back, watching the silver wolf run round the room with a child chasing it. Edward heard her see it. He heard her see the long legs and the narrow chest and the ears and the speed and the way it ran, the way it held its head. He heard her know it. He heard her know it the way you'd know your own face in a shop window you'd walked past by accident.

"Is that me?" Leah said.

The man didn't look round. He was still watching it.

"Apparently," he said.

"It's supposed to be a stag," said Layla. Her voice had gone strange. "It's—everyone knows. It was a stag. In the war. Against the Dementors. Everyone knows that story." She was staring at the silver wolf. "They change. Patronuses. Hardly ever. Only when something—" She stopped. Edward heard her stop. He heard the end of the sentence in her head, as clear as print, the thing every witch and wizard who'd ever studied a Patronus knew: they changed for grief, sometimes, and for love, and almost never for anything else—

"I know when they change, Quinn," said the man.

Layla closed her mouth.

The silver wolf came round the coffee table one more time, with the child laughing after it, and then it stopped. It stood in the middle of the carpet and looked across the room at Leah by the window. It looked at her for a long moment with its eyes of white light. Then it trotted across the room to her, silent, shining, and stood in front of her, and put its silver nose up toward her hand.

Leah put her hand out. Her fingers went into the light. Edward heard what she felt: cold, clean, like putting your hand into spring water.

And it was gone. A puff of silver, like breath on a mirror, and nothing.

Renesmee stood in the middle of the carpet with her arms still out, looking at the place where it had been. Then she turned round and looked at the man, and her whole face was one enormous delighted *again*.

"Later," said the man.

He stood up. He didn't look at Leah. Leah didn't look at him. Edward could hear exactly one of them, and what he heard in her was very loud, and very private, and he took himself out of it as fast as he could.

---

They left at noon.

Jacob followed them out onto the porch. Edward heard him go. He'd been on the floor in front of the couch for two hours, watching, with his mind going round and round the same few things like a dog circling to lie down, and when Leah and the man went out the door, Jacob got up and went after them, and Edward went as far as the door and stood just inside it and didn't pretend he wasn't listening. None of them pretended.

The rain had stopped. The lawn was gray and wet under a white sky.

"Hey," said Jacob.

The man stopped at the top of the steps. He turned round.

Jacob came and stood in front of him. They were about of a height, as humans. Jacob was wider. The man was broader through the chest. They stood about three feet apart on the Cullens' porch and looked at each other.

"I'm her Alpha," Jacob said.

"I know."

"Whatever you are. Whatever you do. Whoever you work for. I don't care. I'm her Alpha." Jacob's jaw was tight. "If you hurt her—"

"If I hurt her," said the man, "you won't need to do anything."

Jacob stopped.

"I'll do it for you," the man said. "And I'll do a better job."

Jacob looked at him for a long time. Edward heard him look. He heard Jacob's mind go round, the circling dog, and come down. He heard it settle.

"Okay," Jacob said.

"Okay."

"I still don't like you."

"I'd be worried if you did." The man looked at him a moment longer. "You're good with her. The kid. I've watched you. Two months on my map, a rust-colored dot that never leaves this house. I've seen you sleep on that porch in the rain because she was having a bad night." He tipped his head toward the door. "I've watched you carry her on your back across a ridge like she's made of glass."

Jacob's face did something.

"That's why you get a pass," the man said. "From me. That's the only reason. Don't waste it."

He turned and went down the steps. Leah went with him. She didn't look back at Jacob. Edward heard her mind as she went, and what was in it about Jacob was surprisingly gentle.

At the bottom of the steps, the man stopped again.

He put his hand in his pocket and took out something small and round and copper, and flipped it, and caught it, and looked at it on the back of his hand. He didn't say what it showed.

"Diner," he said to Leah. "Tonight. Six."

"Why?"

"Because the coin said so," he said. "And because I'm owed two slices of pie."

Behind Edward, in the front room, Alice made a small strangled sound. Edward heard why. For half a second, as the coin spun, she'd seen something—a flicker, a diner, a counter, pie, a man in a uniform—and then the coin had landed, and the flicker had gone dark, and she couldn't find it again.

The man held out his arm. Leah looked at it.

"No," she said.

"It's ten miles to the car."

"Then we'll *run* ten miles to the car."

"In your sweatshirt."

"I'll take it off."

He looked at her. She looked at him. Then she pulled the sweatshirt off over her head and threw it at his face, and walked out across the lawn in her bra and jeans to the big cedar at the edge of the trees, and the man stood on the lawn with a black sweatshirt hanging off his face, and Edward heard, very clearly, from inside the building that he couldn't get into, one small sound that might have been a laugh.

Edward heard her behind the cedar, swearing steadily, knotting her jeans round her ankle. Then there was a tearing sound.

A gray wolf came out from behind it. She looked back over her shoulder at the man on the lawn. Then she went into the trees.

The man took the sweatshirt off his face. He folded it over his arm. He looked up at the porch, at the open door, at Edward standing just inside it, and for a moment the green eyes rested on him, unhurried, and Edward felt one shutter on the side of the building swing open.

*She's faster than me,* the man said, inside Edward's head, dry as dust. *Don't tell her I said.*

The shutter closed.

And then the man walked into the trees after her, and there was no sound at all. No tearing. No footsteps. Just the place where he'd been, on the wet grass, and then the place where he wasn't. A moment later, deep in the trees, Edward heard something enormous go away northwest through the forest without breaking a single stick.

He stood in the doorway of his house for a long time after that.

Behind him, Renesmee was standing in the middle of the carpet with her hand pressed to her mother's cheek, showing her, over and over, a silver wolf running in a circle round a coffee table.

### XII. Charlie

*Forks Diner — 6:04 p.m.*

The Explorer was round the side, nose out, where it always was.

Charlie saw it as he pulled into the lot. Mud-brown, 2004, Washington rental plates. He'd run them in October. He'd run them on a slow afternoon because he ran everybody's plates on slow afternoons, and they'd come back to a Hertz franchise at Sea-Tac and a rental agreement in the name of Harry James Evans, British passport, address in London, and a valid credit card that had never once been used. The man paid cash for everything. Charlie had noticed that, too.

He parked the cruiser in his spot by the door. He sat there for a minute with the engine off and the rain on the windshield.

Bella had called him at one o'clock.

She'd called him at the station, which she never did. She'd said, *Dad, I need you to listen to me,* in the voice she'd had since August, the new voice, the one that was Bella's voice and wasn't, the one that sounded like somebody had taken his daughter's voice and polished it. *There's a man. English. Dark hair. Green eyes. If you see him—*

*What,* Charlie had said. *If I see him, what?*

And there'd been a long pause on the line. He'd heard her breathing. Which was something she did now, he'd noticed, mostly when she remembered to.

*He's dangerous, Dad,* she'd said finally. *He's really, really dangerous. But I don't think—I think he's on our side. I think. For now.* Another pause. *He's with Leah Clearwater.*

*With,* Charlie had said.

*With.*

And Charlie had stood at his desk at the station with the phone in his hand and looked out the window at the rain and thought, *Need to know. Need to know, need to know, need to know.*

He got out of the cruiser. He went in.

---

Doreen had his coffee poured before he'd got his hat off. She always did. He went to his stool at the end of the counter by the register and sat on it and put his hat on the counter next to the napkin dispenser, and picked up the coffee, and looked over the rim of it at the booth by the back wall.

The Englishman was sitting where he always sat. Facing the door, with the kitchen pass on his left and the window on his right. Charlie had clocked that booth his first week. You sat there if you wanted to see everyone who came in and everyone who went out and everything that happened in the parking lot, and you wanted your back to a wall. Charlie sat there himself, sometimes, when he wasn't at the counter. He'd sat there a lot in his first year as chief, when he hadn't known anybody yet and didn't know who to watch.

The man had picked it on his first day.

He wasn't alone today. Leah Clearwater was in the booth across from him. Sue's girl. Harry's girl. Charlie had known her since she was born; he'd stood in the hospital in Forks the night she came, at three in the morning, with a box of cigars Harry had made him go buy at the gas station. He'd watched her grow up. He'd watched her go hard over the last two years, after the Uley boy and after Harry, go hard and quiet and mean the way some people do when something's been taken from them and they decide nothing else ever will be. He'd stood next to her at her father's grave. She hadn't cried. Not once, the whole service. He'd thought then that she'd have been better off if she had.

She was eating pie. Blackberry. She had a fork in one hand and her chin propped on the other, and she was saying something to the Englishman with her mouth half full, and the Englishman was listening with his own fork stopped halfway to his mouth.

And she was smiling.

Charlie set his coffee down on the counter.

"Two slices," said Doreen, beside him, wiping the counter that didn't need wiping, watching the same booth. "I saved him two. Told him I would yesterday. He walks in here with the Clearwater girl, sits down, I bring the two slices over, he looks at 'em, and he pushes one across to her without a word. Didn't even ask." She shook her head. "I've been saving that man pie for nine weeks, Charlie. He's never shared it with anybody."

"Huh," said Charlie.

"Sue's girl." Doreen kept wiping. "With English. I'd never have called that one. Never in a million years."

"No," Charlie said.

"Whole town's going to know by morning."

"Whole town knew by lunch, Dor."

"Well," said Doreen, "now they'll know for sure."

She went off down the counter with the coffee pot.

Charlie sat with his coffee and looked at the booth.

He'd been watching that man for nine weeks. He'd have said so to anybody who asked, and nobody had. He'd noticed him the first day in September, the way you noticed anybody new in a town of four thousand, and then he'd noticed him a little more, the way you did when somebody new didn't quite add up. English. Thirty or so. A photographer, the man said to anybody who asked, writing a book about moss. Charlie had seen the camera. It was a real camera, an old Nikon with a long lens, and he'd seen the man use it on the moss on the courthouse lawn. But Charlie had been a cop for twenty-five years, and the way the man walked into a room was the way cops walked into rooms. Or soldiers. Or the two guys from Seattle with the FBI windbreakers who'd come up in '96 about the meth lab on the Sol Duc and sat in that same booth and watched the door.

And the way he ate. Every day at eleven. The same thing. Same booth. Like a man setting a pattern so people would stop seeing him.

Charlie had seen him anyway. He'd thought, a few times, about doing something about it. He'd thought about running the passport, properly, through the federal system, and he'd sat at his desk with the form in front of him, and every single time, he'd found himself thinking about Bella's eyes, and Jacob Black taking his shirt off in Charlie's front yard, and Billy saying *there are things in these woods, Charlie, that have always been in these woods.* And he'd put the form back in the drawer.

Need to know.

Yesterday, though.

Yesterday he'd been sitting right here, on this stool, at about ten to twelve, eating his pie. And the Englishman had been in the booth eating his. And the Englishman had stopped eating, all at once, with his eyes shut, like a man listening to something nobody else could hear. And then he'd opened his eyes and finished his pie. And then he'd got up, and put a twenty on the table, which was too much, and told Doreen he'd *got a call*. Light coming good up the Bogachiel.

Charlie had been sitting twelve feet away.

Nobody's phone had rung.

He'd noticed that. He'd sat there with his fork in his hand and noticed it, and turned it over, and not turned round. And this morning, coming in, he'd heard on the scanner that a Forest Service crew had found a hemlock on the ridge above the Bogachiel split down the middle by lightning. Sometime yesterday. In November. On the peninsula, where Charlie couldn't remember a lightning strike in November in his whole life.

He sat on his stool and drank his coffee and looked at the man in the booth.

The man looked up.

Across the whole length of the diner, through the noise of the kitchen and the radio and the two logging guys arguing about the Seahawks at the table by the window, the man looked up from his pie and met Charlie's eyes. Green. Steady. Not surprised. He'd known Charlie was watching. He'd probably known for nine weeks.

He nodded. Once. Small.

Charlie picked up his hat. He picked up his coffee. He got up off his stool.

He walked down the length of the diner to the back booth and stopped beside it, and stood there, with his hat in one hand and his coffee in the other.

"Chief," said the man.

"Mind if I sit down?"

The man looked at Leah. Leah looked at Charlie. She had a smear of blackberry at the corner of her mouth and her eyes were the same eyes Harry had had, dark and level and taking everything in.

"Hi, Charlie," she said.

"Leah."

She moved over. Charlie sat down beside her, on the bench across from the Englishman, and put his hat on the table and his coffee next to it.

Up close, the man was younger than Charlie had thought. Late twenties, maybe. Older in the eyes. He had two days of stubble and a face that didn't do much, and he was sitting with his back to the wall and his hands on the table on either side of his plate, where Charlie could see them. That was deliberate. Charlie knew it was deliberate, because it was what he'd have done.

"Bella called me," Charlie said.

"Did she," said the man.

"She said you were dangerous."

"I am."

Charlie looked at him.

"She said she thought you were on our side," Charlie said. "For now."

"I am," said the man. "For now. And for later, as it goes."

Charlie took a drink of his coffee. It had gone lukewarm. He drank it anyway.

"Yesterday," he said. "You got up and left. Ten to twelve. Told Doreen you'd got a call."

"I did."

"Nobody's phone rang."

The man looked at him for a moment. Then, very slightly, the corner of his mouth went up.

"Good catch, Chief," he said.

Charlie didn't smile back. He looked at the man across the table, at the green eyes, at the hands lying flat on the formica.

"There's a tree on the Bogachiel," Charlie said. "Hit by lightning. Yesterday. We don't get lightning on this peninsula in November. I've lived here my whole life. I can't remember once."

"Is there not?" said the man. Mildly. "Bad luck, that."

"Yeah," said Charlie. "Bad luck."

They looked at each other.

Leah had stopped eating. She was sitting very still beside Charlie with her fork resting on her plate, and Charlie could feel her watching the two of them, the way you can feel heat off a stove. Her arm was hot where it nearly touched his. Hot like a fever. Like Jacob's. Like all of them.

Charlie thought about asking. He thought about it the way you'd think about stepping off a ledge in the dark. He sat in the booth with his lukewarm coffee and thought about all the questions he could ask, and he could feel every one of them, lined up, waiting. *Who are you. Who do you work for. What happened on that ridge. What happened to my daughter. What is my granddaughter. What is Leah. What are you.* He could feel the edge of all of it under his feet. He'd been feeling it since August.

He asked the only one that mattered.

"Are you here to hurt anybody I care about?" he said.

"No," said the man.

He said it simply. No pause. No wind-up. He looked at Charlie across the table and said *no*, and that was all.

Charlie looked at him for a long time.

He'd been a cop for twenty-five years. He'd sat across tables from a lot of men. He'd learned that you couldn't tell, not really, not from the eyes or the hands or the voice, not the way they said on TV. Good liars were good. The best ones believed themselves. He looked at the man across the table and he didn't know. He honestly didn't know.

"He's telling the truth," said Leah.

Charlie turned his head and looked at her.

She was looking back at him. Steady. With blackberry on her mouth. Harry's eyes.

"How do you know?" said Charlie.

Leah Clearwater looked at him for a moment longer. Then something happened to her face. It took him a second to recognize it, because he hadn't seen it on her in two years, not since before the Uley boy, not since before her dad. It started at one corner of her mouth and went all the way across.

"Need to know," she said.

Charlie stared at her.

Then he started to laugh.

He didn't mean to. It came up out of his chest and out of his mouth before he could do anything about it, a short surprised bark, and then another, and then he was sitting in the back booth of the Forks Diner with his hat on the table, laughing so hard he had to put his coffee down. The two logging guys by the window looked round. Doreen looked round from the register. He didn't care. He sat there and laughed until his eyes watered, and Leah sat beside him grinning, and across the table, the Englishman sat with his back to the wall and his hands flat on the formica and watched him with an expression Charlie couldn't read at all.

"Okay," Charlie said, when he could. He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand. "Okay. Yeah. Fair. Need to know."

"Sorry, Chief," said Leah.

"No, you're not."

"No," she agreed. "I'm not."

Charlie picked up his hat. He turned it round in his hands, the way he did. He looked at it for a while.

"One more," he said.

The man waited.

Charlie didn't look up from the hat. "My family," he said. "Up at the—" He stopped. He didn't know how much the man knew. He didn't know how much he was supposed to say. "My family," he said again. "Are they safe?"

The man was quiet for a moment.

"Safer than they were on Sunday," he said.

Charlie's hands went still on his hat.

Sunday. The man had said Sunday. As though there'd been something on Sunday, something Charlie hadn't known about. Something that had been there, in those woods, near that house, near his daughter and his daughter's daughter, on Sunday night, while Charlie was at home in bed watching the late news. And wasn't there anymore.

He looked up.

The man was looking back at him. And Charlie saw, in the green eyes, for just a second, that the man had chosen those words. Exactly those. That he'd known what Charlie would hear in them and decided Charlie should hear it.

Charlie had sat across tables from a lot of men. He knew what it looked like when one of them picked his words. This one had picked them for Charlie, and handed them over, and let Charlie do the rest.

He stood up. He put his hat on.

"Leah," he said.

"Charlie."

"Tell your mom I said hi."

"I will."

He looked at the man one more time. The man looked back.

"Evans," Charlie said.

"Chief."

"Enjoy your pie."

He went back to his stool at the end of the counter. Doreen had already filled his coffee. He sat down and picked it up and wrapped both hands round it, the way he did, and looked at the coffee machine. He looked at it for a long time.

His pie came. Blackberry. He ate it very slowly.

Behind him, in the back booth, he heard Leah Clearwater say something low, and the Englishman say something lower, and then, for the second time that night and the first time in two years, he heard Harry Clearwater's daughter laugh.

### XIII. Agnes

*The Smith Tower, Seattle — Tuesday, November 21st, 7:12 p.m.*

She'd come in early again.

She told herself it was the sweater. She'd finished the collar on the bus and wanted to block it, and she couldn't block it at home because the cat would sleep on it, and there was the big empty table under the east windows, so here she was, at a quarter past seven, two hours before her shift, with the stegosaurus pinned out damp on a towel on the table and the drums turning along both walls and the radiator knocking like a man in a closet.

She'd stopped believing that around two o'clock that afternoon, when she'd woken up in her bed in Ballard with the light coming gray through the blind and lain there with a feeling she couldn't place. Like a word on the tip of her tongue. Like walking into a room and forgetting what you'd come in for. She'd lain there for an hour trying to find it, and she hadn't found it, and she'd got up and made tea and stood at the kitchen window with the cat winding round her ankles and looked out at the rain and thought, very clearly, for no reason at all: *There's no lightning in November.*

She'd stood there with her tea. At three she'd called Pryce. At seven she'd got her coat.

---

The Olympic drum was turning on its spindle, third from the end on the right wall. She'd gone and looked at it first, before she'd even taken her coat off. Long and lazy and gently wobbling. Waves. A child drawing waves. The peninsula as it had looked every winter for twenty-nine years.

Her report was gone from Pryce's tray. He'd sent it east at noon, he'd said, when she'd called from home at three. *Good work, Agnes. Lightning. I told you, didn't I? It's always something natural up there.* He'd sounded relieved. He'd sounded like a man who'd had a red-ink memo sitting on his conscience for two months and could finally put it down.

She'd said *yes, Hollis*, and hung up.

She sat at her desk now, under the green lamp, with the stegosaurus drying behind her, and she pulled the phone across the desk.

MACUSA didn't hold with Muggle telephones. There was one in the office anyway, an old black rotary one on a little table by the Wishing Chair stair, because in 1962 somebody had pointed out that if the Floo went down in an emergency there'd be no way at all to call for help, and somebody else had grudgingly agreed. Agnes had had it moved to her desk in 1981. Nobody had noticed. Nobody ever noticed anything up here.

She dialed the number from memory. She'd called it four or five times over the years, when the drums did something odd and she wanted to know if the weather had too.

It rang twice.

"National Weather Service, Seattle. This is Gary."

"Good evening, Gary," Agnes said. "My name's Agnes. I'm—" She paused. "I'm a retired schoolteacher. I'm doing a project for my grandson. About lightning. I wonder if you could help me."

"Sure, ma'am." He sounded young and bored and pleased to have someone to talk to. Night shift. She knew the voice. "What do you need?"

"Lightning strikes," she said. "On the Olympic Peninsula. Yesterday. The twentieth. Around noon. Up in the hills above the Bogachiel River, near Forks."

"Okay. Hang on." She heard typing. A long pause. More typing. "Ma'am, I've got the strike data up for the whole state for the twentieth. We get it off the national network. Every cloud-to-ground strike gets logged, time, location, current."

"And?"

"And there's nothing."

Agnes sat very still.

"Nothing on the peninsula all day," Gary said. "Nothing west of the Cascades, actually. Bit of activity way out over the ocean around three a.m., a couple of strikes about two hundred miles offshore, but that's it. Nothing over land. It was a stratiform day, mostly. Rain, low ceiling. No convection." Typing. "Actually, hang on. There's one. Clallam County."

Her heart did something.

"Twentieth of November," Gary said. "Eight-thirteen p.m. Single strike, cloud-to-ground, positive polarity, pretty strong. Ridge above the Bogachiel. That's—huh. That's weird. That's the only one on the whole peninsula for the whole month."

"Eight-thirteen p.m.," said Agnes.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Not noon."

"No, ma'am. Nothing at noon. Nothing all day until eight-thirteen at night. Just the one." A pause. "Is that what you were looking for?"

Agnes looked at the Olympic drum turning on its spindle on the far wall.

"Yes," she said. "Thank you, Gary. That's exactly what I was looking for."

She hung up.

She sat there with her hand on the receiver for a long time.

Then she got up. She went to the long oak cabinets at the back of the room, to the registry, to the drawer marked P–Q. She opened it. She went through it to the Qs. Quackenbush. Quigley. Quill. Quinn, Layla Maeve, b. 1984, Albuquerque NM, Ilvermorny '02, Wand: mesquite, Thunderbird tail feather, 13". Profession: curse-breaker (inactive). Current address: 1140 Calawah Way, Forks WA, Clallam County. Registered 10/2/06.

At the bottom of the card, in blue ink, in a small neat hand that she knew better than she knew her own face, someone had written: *Statute compliance reviewed 11/20/06. No concerns. —A.S.*

She looked at it.

She hadn't reviewed it. She'd never heard of Layla Maeve Quinn. She'd never once in twenty-nine years reviewed a registry card on a night she hadn't logged it, and she hadn't logged it. She checked. She went back to her desk and opened the night book and ran her finger down the twentieth, and there was the Pomeroy renewal at ten o'clock, and there was nothing else.

She went back to the cabinets. The steel ones. The old case files. The bottom drawer, 1930–1939, that nobody had opened since the Truman administration. She opened it.

It was full of yellowed folders in their hanging files, each one labeled in the faded typewriter type of seventy years ago. She went through them. She didn't know what she was looking for. She went through them anyway, one at a time, all the way to the back, her fingers getting gray with dust.

There was a gap.

Between *1936-114, Tacoma, Unlicensed Potion Sales* and *1936-117, Bellingham, Disturbance (Poltergeist?)*. Two hanging files with nothing in them. The tabs were still there. Someone had typed on them in 1936. *1936-115.* And *1936-116.* The second one was empty and always had been; there was dust in the bottom of it, undisturbed. The first one was empty too.

There was no dust in the bottom of the first one.

She knelt on the floor in front of the open drawer with the dust on her fingers and looked at the clean empty file for a long time.

Then she got up, slowly, her knees cracking, and went back to her desk, and sat down.

She didn't remember anything. She tried. She sat there under the green lamp and tried as hard as she'd ever tried to remember anything, and there was nothing there. A night like any other night. The second sleeve. Dozing off. A good report. A satisfied feeling.

And under it, so faint she'd almost have missed it if she hadn't been looking: a voice. A man's voice. Low, with gravel in the bottom of it. Saying something. She couldn't hear what. She couldn't hear the words at all. Just the shape of them, and the feeling she'd had, hearing them. Like being handed something she'd been waiting for her whole life without knowing it.

*You were right.*

She didn't know where that came from. She didn't know who'd said it. She sat there and knew, with total certainty, that someone had.

Agnes Sorrell sat at her desk at the top of the tower in the rain, with the drums turning round her, and the stegosaurus drying on its towel, and the radiator knocking every ninety seconds like a man trying to get out of a closet. And after a long time she reached across her desk and took a yellow sticky note off the pad by the phone, and uncapped her pen, and wrote on it, in her small neat hand.

One word.

She looked at it for a while. Then she peeled it off the pad and stuck it on the inside of her desk drawer, at the back, behind the spare skeins, where nobody else would ever look. Where she'd see it every night when she reached in for her yarn.

She closed the drawer.

The note said: *Who?*
