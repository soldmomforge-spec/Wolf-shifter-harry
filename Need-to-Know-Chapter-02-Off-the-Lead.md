# Need to Know

## Chapter Two: Off the Lead

---

## Part One: The Law

---

### I. Leah

*North Fork of the Calawah — Monday, November 20th, 6:38 p.m.*

*Ask him myself?* Jacob said. *Ask him* myself*? Leah, I don't even know where he* is*.*

*Not my problem.*

*It's literally your problem! It's in your head! There's a—there's a* thing *in your head, this big gray—*

*I know what's in my head, Jake. I'm the one it's in.*

She went down the bank and into the river at the broken-heart boulder without slowing. The water hit her chest like a slap, black and so cold it burned, and the current shoved at her flank, and she leaned into it and went across with her head up and came out the other side streaming. She shook once, hard, from her ears to her tail, and kept going. Southeast. Up the far slope through the dripping hemlocks. The Cullen place was eleven miles that way and she could have run it blind.

Behind her, the rope went back up the hill.

She could feel all of it. It came out of the middle of her chest and ran back over her shoulder, through the trees, across the river, up onto the bench under the big spruces and in through the flap of a little green tent with moss on its lines. And into him. It ran out behind her as she went, easy, getting longer and longer, like line paying out off a reel, and it hummed. At the far end of it he was standing in his kitchen with both hands flat on the iron range. She could feel the iron. Cold under his palms. She could feel the kettle starting to tick as it heated.

And she could hear music.

That was new. It came down the rope faint and tinny, like a radio playing in another room: a big slow thudding drumbeat, and a guitar going round and round on itself, and strings coming in over the top of it, climbing, like a staircase that went up and up and didn't stop. She didn't know the song. Her wolf liked it. Her wolf liked it so much she caught herself running to it, her feet coming down on the beat, and she made herself stop.

*Lee,* Seth said. *Are you listening to* music*?*

*No.*

*You are. I can hear it. It's in the back. Like, dun. Dun. Dun—*

*Seth, I swear to God.*

*What is it?*

*How should I know? It's his.*

That shut them both up for about three strides. She felt them sitting with it, in the pack mind, the way you'd sit with a smell you couldn't place. Jacob was in the trees behind the Cullens' house, where he'd phased the second her head came on. Seth was at the edge of their lawn, under the big cedar by the garage, with his nose pointed at the house, because Layla was in the house and he couldn't make himself be more than about sixty feet from her right now. Leah didn't blame him. She was eleven miles from the other end of her own rope and she could feel every one of them.

*Leah,* Jacob said. He'd gone quiet. That was worse than loud, with Jacob. *What's behind the wall?*

*Stuff.*

*What stuff?*

*His stuff.*

*Did he put it there?*

She thought about that. She went over a deadfall in one long jump and landed in a bed of sword ferns and thought about the thing she'd felt come down the rope from his end, an hour ago, standing in front of the tent with his hand on her face. Cold and heavy and old. Older than the stuff in his jars. It had come down the line into her head without asking anybody, the way a draft comes in under a door, and it had closed over everything he'd told her in the kitchen, like a hand closing over a coin. She'd felt it land. She'd felt it decide. And the part that she kept turning over, running, was that it hadn't felt like *him*. It had felt like something he carried. Something somebody had given him a long time ago, that sat in him the way the bead had sat on Seth's wrist.

*No,* she said. *He didn't put it there. It's his, but he didn't put it there. It went there by itself.*

*That's not better, Leah.*

*Didn't say it was better. You asked.*

*What* is *it?*

She tried to show him. It was the only way, in the pack mind; you didn't explain things, you just let people look. So she let him look at the wall, and she felt him look, and she felt him bounce off it. Gray. Smooth. Like fog standing in the shape of a door. Behind it she knew there were things. She knew them perfectly well. She could have reached out and touched them. She just couldn't hand them to anybody else.

*It's like a lock,* she said. *On somebody else's locker. It's his locker. It's in my hallway. That's all.*

*That's insane.*

*Yeah, well. It's been a weird day.*

*Lee.* That was Seth, small, careful. The careful voice. *Are you okay? Like, actually okay? He didn't—you were in there for hours. In that place. With the jars and that thing on the floor. And you watched him—* Seth didn't finish it. She felt him not want to think about the scream. *Are you okay?*

*I'm fine.*

She felt both of them brace. That was what *I'm fine* usually meant, from her. Everybody in the pack knew the sound of it. *I'm fine* meant there was something going on underneath it like a fire in a coal seam, and you'd better not dig.

And then she felt both of them listen underneath it, the way they always did, the way you couldn't stop yourself doing in the pack mind.

And find nothing.

She ran, and she let them look, and under the *I'm fine* there was only more of it, all the way down. Tired legs. Cold river water drying in her fur. The rope, humming. A song she didn't know the name of, going up and up like stairs. And a feeling she hadn't had in so long she had to stop, for one second, on the lip of a ravine with the rain coming down through the trees, and look at it.

*Huh,* Seth said.

She started running again.

*Lee. Wait. Stop a second.* He sounded strange. *Stop. Listen.*

*I'm listening.*

*No, I mean—listen. To you. Listen to the back.*

She didn't need to. She knew what he meant. She'd known since the ridge.

The back of her head was quiet.

Since the spring there'd been a different song back there. *Sam Emily Sam Emily.* Sam's memory of a girl on a porch in Makah, playing over and over on a loop in Leah's skull like a jukebox somebody had kicked so it wouldn't stop, and under it, Sam's guilt, thick and brown and sticky, and under that, her. Her own voice, going *why not me why not me why not me* so steadily she'd stopped hearing the words, the way you stop hearing a refrigerator. Everybody who'd ever phased with her had heard it. Paul used to snap at her for it. Jared used to pretend he couldn't hear it, which was worse. Jacob had told her once, in June, half asleep on patrol, that it was like running next to a car alarm.

It was gone.

She went looking for it, the way you'd put your tongue in the hole where a tooth was. Nothing. She could think about Sam. She did, on purpose, running down into the ravine and through the creek at the bottom and up the other side. Sam's face. His hands. The end of the dirt road behind her mom's house, the night of the bonfire, when she was sixteen. Emily's porch. Emily's face with the scars down it.

They came up in her head like pictures in somebody else's photo album. She could look at them. She could look at all of them. And they just sat there, being pictures, and didn't do anything to her at all.

*I can't hear Sam,* Seth said. Very quietly. *Lee. I can't hear him anymore.*

*I know.*

*Since—*

*Since the ridge. Yeah.*

Jacob didn't say anything. She felt him not say anything for a long time, sitting in the trees behind the Cullens' house with his chin on his paws, the way he'd gone still in June, in the hospital bed in Billy's front room with half his bones broken, when she'd come in to tell him she was sorry. She'd only ever said sorry to Jacob about three times. She didn't think he'd ever forgotten one of them.

*Huh,* Jacob said, finally.

*Yeah.*

*You're okay.*

*Told you.*

*You never mean it when you say that.*

*Well,* Leah said, *I mean it now.*

She came over the top of the last ridge and there it was below her through the trees, the long white house with every window lit, glass and white walls and that stink coming up off it in waves, flowers and bleach and burnt sugar. She'd smelled it every day for three months. She'd never once stopped noticing it. She noticed it now. It was still disgusting.

It just didn't make her want to bite anything.

She went down through the cedars at the bottom of the slope and stopped behind the big one at the corner of the lawn, the one with the hollow at its root where she kept a spare pair of cutoffs in a plastic bag for emergencies. She didn't need the spare. She'd tied hers to her back leg before she'd phased at the tent. He'd turned his back while she did it, and stood looking at his slug. She'd felt him not look the whole time, through the rope, like a man standing in a doorway with his eyes shut, and she'd felt the bit of him down at the bottom of the rope, the bit behind the locks, look anyway.

She phased.

It came off her like a coat. She was standing barefoot in the wet needles with the rain on her bare back and her breath going white in the dark, and the pack went out of her head like a light switched off, Jacob and Seth gone, the gray and the russet and the sandy, just her. Just her and the rope.

The rope didn't go anywhere. That was the first thing she checked. She stood behind the cedar in the rain with her shorts in one hand, and she checked, and it was still there, running out of her chest and away northwest through eleven miles of black forest, exactly as strong as it had been when she had four feet. Stronger, maybe. Without the pack in her head there was more room for it.

At the other end of it, he'd taken the kettle off. He was standing in the hall of the tent, by the phone, with something in his hand. A small flat thing in a black frame. She could feel the weight of it. She could feel him look at it and not like it.

And down at the bottom of him, behind the locks, the big dark thing had its head up and its lip curled.

*What,* she thought, at the thing in the cellar. She didn't know if it could hear her. *What's wrong with you?*

She didn't get an answer. She got a feeling. A low, flat, sulking grumble, like an old dog that's just watched you pick up your car keys.

Leah pulled her cutoffs on and her tank top over her head and came out from behind the cedar.

---

Seth met her halfway across the lawn.

He was in human shape, in a pair of somebody else's sweatpants, with his hair wet and his feet bare. He didn't say anything. He just came at her across the grass at a dead run and grabbed her in both arms and picked her up clean off the ground, the way he hadn't done since she was nineteen and he was sixteen and he'd first gotten tall enough to try it, and held her there with her feet off the lawn and her face squashed against his shoulder.

"Seth."

"Shut up."

"Seth, put me down."

"Shut up for one second," Seth said into her hair, and held on.

She let him. She stood there off the ground in her brother's arms in the rain, on a vampire's lawn, and let him, and after a while she put her hand on the back of his head the way she used to when he was little and had nightmares about the ocean.

He put her down. He held her at arm's length by both shoulders and looked at her, hard, the way their dad used to look at a fish to see if it was worth keeping.

Then he leaned in and sniffed her.

"Oh, gross," Leah said.

"You smell like him."

"Like a cheeseburger?"

"Like pine trees. And—" He sniffed again. His nose wrinkled. "Gun stuff. Oil. And eggs? Did he make you *eggs*?"

"Six," said Leah. "And bacon. And most of a loaf of bread. He's a good cook."

Seth looked at her with an expression she'd never seen on his face before, like a kid watching a dog walk on its hind legs.

"Lee," he said. "You're smiling."

"I'm not."

She was. She could feel it. She tried to stop and it didn't work, which was getting to be a pattern with her face today.

"Where's Jake?"

"Phasing back. Behind the house." Seth hesitated. "He's kind of—he's really mad. At him, mostly. Kind of at you." He looked over her shoulder at the dark trees, northwest, the way she'd come. "He's been mad since the ridge. He tried to make me take him back out there and find the tent. I couldn't. I told him I couldn't, it slides, and he made me try anyway, like four times. Then Layla told him about the wards and he went and stood in the river for a while."

"In the river?"

"To cool off. It's a Jake thing."

"How's Layla?"

His face changed. "She's okay. Dr. Cullen looked at her. Nothing's broken, he said, it's all bruises. Really bad ones. Her whole back is purple. Like, from here to here." He touched his own shoulder blades, then the small of his back. "Esme ran her a bath and gave her clothes and made her tea and she's been sitting on the couch for five hours not saying anything and thinking really hard. You can see her doing it. It's kind of scary."

"Did she say who he is?"

"She doesn't know. She keeps saying she doesn't know. She keeps saying it like it's the worst thing that's ever happened to her." He paused. "Lee. Do *you* know? Who he is?"

"Yeah."

"Who?"

She opened her mouth to tell him.

And stopped, because she'd already told him. She'd told him an hour ago, without meaning to. It was in her head. It had been in her head since the tent, since he'd stood in the rain with his mouth working and his own name coming out of it like it was being dragged. Seth had seen it. Jacob had seen it. It had gone past them in the pack mind along with the jars and the eggs and the record player, and neither of them had stopped on it, because it was just a name.

"Come on," Leah said. "I'm starving."

### II. Leah

*The Cullen house — 6:57 p.m.*

They were all in the living room. Of course they were. She came in through the glass door from the back lawn with Seth behind her and her bare feet leaving wet prints on Esme's white floor and every head in the room turned at the same time, like a flock of birds on a wire.

Layla was on the long white couch, in a cream-colored sweater that had to be Esme's because it came down past her hands, with her knees pulled up and a mug of something in both fists. Her face was gray under the brown. There was a bruise coming up along her jaw on the left side that Leah hadn't seen on the ridge. She looked at Leah over the mug and didn't say anything, and her eyes went very sharp.

Carlisle was standing by the fireplace. Esme was beside him. The big one, Emmett, was sitting on the arm of a chair with his elbows on his knees, and Rosalie was in the chair, and the little one, Alice, was curled up on the window seat with her arms around her shins and her face pinched up tight. Jasper stood behind her with his hand on her shoulder. Bella was at the bottom of the stairs, alone, very still, and Leah could smell that the kid wasn't with her. Upstairs, then. Put away. Somebody had decided the kid should be put away.

Edward was by the piano.

He was looking at a spot a little to the side of her face, at nothing, the way he always looked at you when he was listening. He'd been listening since she was three miles out, she figured. Since before. Through Jacob and Seth, all afternoon.

His face didn't change. His face never changed. But his eyes went to the middle of her chest, just for a second, where the rope came out, like he could almost see it.

Leah walked past all of them and into the kitchen.

There was a fridge in the Cullens' kitchen that was only for the wolves. Esme had started stocking it in September, after the kid was born and Jacob started living on the couch, and it was the single best thing about this house. Leah opened it. Leftover lasagna. A whole ham. Two gallons of milk. A tray of something with cheese on top that Esme had labeled, in neat handwriting on a strip of masking tape, *JACOB — DO NOT EAT ALL OF THIS*. Leah took the ham out and put it on the counter and found a knife in the block and started cutting.

In the living room behind her, nobody said anything for a very long time.

"Is she—" That was Emmett. Low. Not low enough. "Is she *humming*?"

"Don't look directly at it," said Rosalie.

Leah was humming. She hadn't noticed. It was the song, the one that had come down the rope on the run, the big slow drums and the strings going up. It was still going in the back of her head, faint, like it had gotten stuck there. She stopped. She cut another slice of ham and ate it off the knife.

The back door opened and closed and Jacob came in.

He'd been in the river. Seth hadn't been kidding. He was soaked from the hair down, water running off him, in a pair of cutoffs and nothing else, and he was steaming in Esme's warm kitchen like a pot on a stove. He stopped in the doorway between the kitchen and the living room. He looked at her.

She looked back at him, with the knife in one hand and a slice of ham in the other.

"Hey, Jake."

"Hey," said Jacob.

He didn't move. She watched him not move. She could see him trying to decide what to do with his hands; they kept closing up into fists and opening again. Behind him, through the doorway, she could see the whole living room watching the two of them like a tennis match.

"You okay?" Jacob said.

"Told you I was."

"Yeah. You did." He took a breath. She watched him hold it, and she watched him decide to let it out slow and calm and be the alpha about this, be the reasonable one, the way he'd been practicing since he'd broken off from Sam. She watched him get about halfway there.

Then he lost it.

"Leah," Jacob said, "*how could you imprint on* him?"

It came out of him like a cork. Like he'd been holding it in since one o'clock that afternoon and it had built up pressure in there, in his chest, the whole time he'd stood in the river, and now it was out and he couldn't stop it.

"Him! Of all the—Leah, he *shot* Layla! He pinned you to a rock! He pinned *me*, I mean, he didn't pin me, but he would've! He went through Seth's head like a—like a *burglar*! He's got a *vampire* in a *jar*! He's got a whole *room* of jars! I saw it, I saw it in your head, there's a hand in one of them that *moves*, Leah! There's a, a, a blanket thing that *looks* at you! He tortured that bloodsucker in front of you! On purpose! While you watched!" He was waving his arms now. Water was coming off them. "And you *looked* at him! On the ridge! You looked at the guy in the suit with the gun, who called you *Lassie*, and you just—what, you just went *oh, okay, him*?"

"Jake—" said Seth, from behind her.

"No! No, I want to know! I want to know how! Of everybody on the whole *planet*—"

Leah put the knife down.

She put it down on the counter very carefully, the way her mom put things down, the coffee-cup way. She wiped her fingers on a dish towel. She turned around.

She'd never in her life wanted to bite Jacob Black less. That was the thing. Standing there in Esme's kitchen with ham on her breath, she went looking for it, the way she'd gone looking for the Sam song on the run, and she couldn't find it. She'd wanted to bite Jacob about once a day since the spring. She'd bitten him twice. He'd deserved it both times. It was gone now, the same as the song, and all that was left when she looked at him dripping on the tile was a big, wet, scared, stupid kid who'd been her alpha for three months and was scared out of his mind for her.

It made her want to laugh.

She didn't laugh. She was nice about it. She was nice about it the way the ocean was nice about a sandcastle.

"Okay," Leah said. "Okay, Jake. Let's do it. Let's talk about who imprinted on what."

Jacob's mouth shut.

"At least mine," said Leah, "isn't a *literal baby vampire hybrid*."

The living room went dead silent.

"You imprinted on a *newborn*, Jake. A ten-minute-old half-vampire covered in Bella's blood. You walked into this house to rip its head off, and it looked at you, and that was it. Done. Gravity. And then we all had to *watch* it. In your head. Every single time you phased for three straight weeks. You think I didn't see it? We saw it from every angle. Seth saw it. I saw it. Sam probably saw it from the other side of the reservation, I bet he felt it go off like a firework." She folded her arms. "Did I say anything?"

"You said a lot of things," said Jacob.

"I said *some* things."

"You called her a—"

"I said *some things*, Jake, and then I stopped, because it wasn't your fault, because you didn't pick it. Remember?" She tipped her head. "Or do you think you picked it? Do you think you looked at a baby on the couch with blood all over her and thought, yeah, *that* one, sign me up?"

Jacob didn't answer that.

"Didn't think so," Leah said. "So what do you think I did? You think I *chose* this?" She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. "You think I was lying on my face on that rock with the moss in my mouth, with him holding me down like a bug in a jar, listening to Layla get blown into a log, and I thought to myself, *huh, he's cute, I'll take him*? You were there. You were in my head. You *felt* it. You felt Sam go. You felt everything go. Did that feel like *choosing* to you?"

"No," said Jacob, after a second. It came out of him small.

"No. It felt like falling off a building. Same as yours."

He looked at the floor.

"And you know what?" said Leah. "Now that we're on it. Since you asked. If you really think about it, mine's the best one."

Seth made a noise behind her that might have been a laugh he'd strangled.

"Think about it," Leah said. "Go on. Think. Sam's is my cousin, which, gross, and also, *my cousin*. Jared's is a girl who's been writing his name on her binder since the fifth grade. Paul's is your sister, who is going to kill him one day and I'm going to help. Quil's is *two*. Yours is a baby. A half-dead baby who drinks blood out of a sippy cup, who's going to be a full-grown woman by the time she's seven, and you're going to have to explain that to Charlie someday, and I want to be there for it. And Seth's—" She looked over her shoulder. Seth went pink. "Seth's is actually fine. Seth's is the best of yours. Layla's great."

"Thank you," said Layla, faintly, from the couch.

"You're welcome." Leah turned back to Jacob. "But mine? Mine's better. Mine wins. Because he's, *A*." She held up one finger. "Not a baby. He's a grown man. Like, thirty. He's got a job. He's got a kitchen. He's got a *record player*. *B*." A second finger. "He's a human. Mostly. He eats. He's got a heartbeat. He's not going to sparkle at anybody or drink anybody or grow three inches overnight. And *C*." A third finger. She held them up in front of Jacob's face, three fingers, like a kid counting. "He's *also a wolf*."

"He's *what*?" said Rosalie, from the living room.

"So he gets it," Leah said, ignoring her. "He gets all of it. The phasing and the eating and the running hot and the thing in your chest that wants to go out in the woods at two in the morning and run till it falls down. He's got one. A big one. Bigger than you, I bet." She dropped her hand. "So take that."

Nobody said anything.

The rain drummed on the long glass wall at the back of the room. In the living room, Emmett had both hands over his mouth and his shoulders were shaking. Rosalie was looking at Leah like she'd sprouted a second head. Alice had unfolded on the window seat and was sitting up very straight. And Jacob stood in the doorway between the kitchen and the living room with the river still running off him, staring at her with his mouth slightly open.

"He's a wolf," Jacob said, finally.

"Sort of. He said sort of."

"I saw it. I saw it in your head on the ridge, the thing—the big thing at the bottom of him. I thought it was, I don't know. Some kind of magic thing. Some kind of witch thing." He looked at Layla on the couch, and then back at Leah. "He's an actual *wolf*?"

"He keeps it locked up," Leah said. "Three locks. In the cellar. He said so." She thought about it. "I think the locks are kind of broken now."

"Broken how?"

"I don't know, Jake. I've known him five and a half hours."

She turned back around and picked up the knife and cut another slice of ham.

Behind her, she heard Emmett lose the fight with his hands and laugh, one big helpless honk of a laugh, and then Rosalie hit him, and then he laughed harder.

Way out at the other end of the rope, eleven miles off, the man in the tent was standing in his hallway with the black-framed thing in his hand, and he wasn't laughing. She could feel him not laughing. She could feel him take a breath and hold it, the way you hold it before you jump into cold water.

And the thing at the bottom of him was growling.

It was growling at whatever was in his hand. She could tell that much.

### III. Layla

*The Cullen house — 7:04 p.m.*

The Cruciatus had been in Leah's head.

Layla had been sitting on the Cullens' couch for five hours and twenty minutes, and for about three of those she'd been thinking about that. She hadn't seen it herself; she'd been on the couch with a mug of Esme's tea, feeling her back stiffen up like poured concrete, when Seth and Jacob had come in from the trees at a quarter past six with their faces gone the color of ash. They'd told her. Seth had told her, stammering, the whole of it, everything he'd seen come pouring out of his sister's head into the pack mind when she phased. The tent that was bigger on the inside. The round room full of jars. The vampire on its back inside four rings of silver.

And the man in the suit standing at the edge of the silver, saying one word, quietly, and the vampire screaming.

Seth didn't know the word. He didn't need to. When he described the scream, and the way the frozen body had shuddered against whatever held it, end to end, like a struck bell, Layla had put her mug down on the coffee table so she wouldn't drop it, and gone to the downstairs bathroom, and been sick.

She'd thought about why, afterward, with her forehead on Esme's cool marble sink. She'd seen what was left of the woman from Olympia, in Seth's memory of Leah's memory: a name and an age and a photograph of the light on the river, pulled out of a monster's head and laid on a table. She didn't think she'd have minded, much, if somebody had cut the vampire's head off on the ridge.

What had turned her stomach was how *easily* he'd said it.

She'd heard the Cruciatus cast once. Once in her life, in Belize, in a smuggler's warehouse full of cursed jade, at the end of a job that had gone very wrong; a hired wand had cast it at her crew chief and she'd watched him do it, and she'd watched what it took. The man's whole face had gone ugly with it. You had to mean it, Calloway used to say, in the one lecture a year where she talked about the Unforgivables, standing at the front of the room with her hands behind her back. You had to *want* it. You had to stand there and want another living thing to hurt so badly it would break, and if you didn't want it enough, it didn't work, and if you did, something in you went out and didn't come back.

The man in the suit had said it like someone reading the side of a cereal box. And it had worked.

And then, in Seth's stammering account, he'd said *Casimir. Where's your phone?* and after that there was only the wall.

So she'd come back from the bathroom and sat down, and Esme had brought her fresh tea without a word, and she'd sat with it for an hour, and then another, and she'd gone through everything she knew. Every wizard she'd ever heard of who could do what he could do. Every government. Every service. Every rumor from every bar on the dueling circuit at three in the morning.

She'd come up with nothing. Nothing at all. She'd started to wonder if she'd hit her head on that log harder than Carlisle said.

And then Leah Clearwater walked into the kitchen barefoot and cut a ham and told Jacob Black her imprint was the best one, and held up three fingers, and said he was a wolf.

Layla sat on the couch with her mug and the room tilting very slightly under her and thought: *Of course he is. Of course. Why not.*

---

"Edward."

That was Carlisle. He said it quietly, from the fireplace, while Emmett was still laughing and Rosalie was still hitting him and Jacob was still standing in the kitchen doorway looking like somebody had let the air out of him.

Edward had been standing by the piano since Leah came in. He hadn't moved. He had his head tipped a little to one side, the way Layla had learned over two weeks of Sundays meant he was listening to something none of the rest of them could hear, and his eyes had gone flat and unfocused and very far away.

"Edward," Carlisle said again. "What do you hear?"

Edward came back. Layla watched him do it, a slow blink, his eyes sharpening.

"The same thing I heard through Jacob and Seth," he said. "But closer." He looked at Leah in the kitchen. Leah, eating ham, ignored him with tremendous dignity. "There's something in her head that isn't hers."

"We know," said Jacob. "The wall."

"*Wall* makes it sound crude." Edward frowned. Layla had never seen him look uncertain before; on his face it looked wrong, like a crack in a vase. "Walls are crude. This is—precise. It's been laid over certain things she knows. Very specific things. It's like—" He stopped. He looked at Layla. "You'd know better than I would. You've seen your kind of work. Can I show you?"

"Show me how?"

"Tell you. Exactly. And you tell me what it sounds like."

She put her mug down. "Go."

"It's sealed around everything she learned from him about his work," Edward said. "Who sent him. Where he's from—not the country, the place. The institution. What he does there. Why he's here. There's a phone call in there; I can feel the edge of it. A voice. I can't hear the voice. There's something about Volterra. A great deal about Volterra." His voice went very even on the word. Across the room, Carlisle went still. "And there's something about Renesmee."

Bella moved. She was at the bottom of the stairs one second and in the middle of the room the next, without anything in between, and her eyes, behind the brown contacts she hadn't taken out since Sunday, had gone very wide.

"What about Renesmee?"

"I don't know," said Edward. "I can't see it. I can see that it's there. The seal's thickest there, actually. Thicker than over Volterra. Whatever he knows about our daughter, the thing that sealed it thinks it matters more than almost anything else in her head." He looked at Leah. "Leah. What does he know about Renesmee?"

Leah swallowed her ham. She looked at Edward, and then at Bella, and something in her face changed, just slightly. It went careful.

"Ask him," she said.

"I'm asking you."

"Yeah, and I'm telling you to ask him." She picked up another slice. "He's coming tomorrow."

"He's *what*?" said Jacob.

"Layla," Edward said, without looking away from Leah. "A seal laid over very particular knowledge, by something other than the person who holds it. Something that recognizes what belongs to a certain—place. A certain institution. And seals that, and only that. And leaves everything else."

Layla had been turning it over while he talked, the way she'd turn over a stone from a tomb floor. She knew what it sounded like. She didn't like what it sounded like at all.

"A binding," she said slowly. "An oath-binding. Old. Very old, if it can do that on its own, if it can reach into somebody else's head and decide what to cover without the one who took the oath doing it. Most oath-bindings just stop *you* talking. That's all they do. They sit in your throat." She touched her own throat without meaning to. "There are institutions that use them. Not many. MACUSA doesn't. MACUSA uses ordinary Unbreakable Vows for the really secret stuff, and they hate doing it, because people die." She shook her head. "I don't know one that would do this. That would *follow* the secret. Out of the person and into somebody else."

"Well, this one did," said Edward.

"Then I don't know who he works for. I still don't know. That's—it's the same as before. Everything I find on him goes nowhere."

"Not everything," said Edward.

She looked up at him.

"It's sealed around his *work*," Edward said. "His work only. Whatever the seal is, it doesn't care about him. About who he is when he's not working." He tipped his head toward the kitchen again. "His name's on this side of it."

The room went quiet again.

Layla felt it go quiet. She felt the back of her neck go cold, the curse-breaker's cold, the cenote cold, and she didn't know why, yet. Something in the shape of what Edward had just said. A seal that guarded everything about a man's work, and didn't bother to guard his name.

You didn't guard what everyone already knew.

"What's his name?" she said.

"Harry," said Seth, from by the kitchen door. He said it helpfully. "His name's Harry. Lee thought it about fifty times on the way back."

"I did not."

"You kind of did, Lee."

"Harry what?" Layla said.

Leah, in the kitchen, put down her slice of ham. "Seth, don't—"

"Harry Potter," said Edward.

The mug went out of Layla's hand.

She didn't drop it. Her fingers just opened, all on their own, the way they had in her grandmother's kitchen at noon when the bead cracked. She watched it go. She watched it turn over in the air, slowly, full of tea, on its way to Esme's white rug, and she couldn't have moved to catch it if her life depended on it.

Esme caught it an inch off the floor. Layla didn't see her move. She was by the fireplace and then she was crouched at Layla's knee with the mug in her hand and not a drop spilled, and she looked up into Layla's face with her kind gold eyes and her forehead creased.

"Layla?"

Layla didn't say anything. She couldn't. Her mouth had stopped working.

*Harry Potter.*

It went through her like water through a broken window. She was six years old, at her grandmother's kitchen table, in this house, in the summer, eating toast with honey on it, while her grandmother read out loud from a copy of the *New York Ghost* three weeks old that had come up from Seattle by owl. *They say he's a baby,* Maeve had said, *a baby, Layla, one year old, and the curse just bounced off him like a ball off a wall.* And Layla had said *what curse* and Maeve had said *the worst one there is, mo chroí, the one you never come back from*, and folded up the paper, and gone out to the back step and smoked two cigarettes in a row instead of one, which Layla had never seen her do before or since.

She was thirteen, at Ilvermorny, on the night they'd let the whole school stay up late in the Great Hall, a lot of children who were only half sure what they were celebrating because they'd been babies themselves when it happened, to hear the British headmaster's broadcast on the anniversary. *The Boy Who Lived*. There'd been a card of him in the Chocolate Frogs that year. Everybody wanted it. Eli Mancuso in Thunderbird house had traded his broom for one.

She was eighteen. May of 1998. She was in the North American junior final in Toronto, and she'd won, and she'd come off the platform with her hair full of sweat and her wand arm shaking, and Calloway had been standing at the side of the stage with a copy of the *Ghost* in her hand and a look on her face Layla had never seen there. The headline was so big it took up half the page. *THE DARK LORD IS DEAD.* And under it, a photograph from Britain, black and white and moving, of a hall full of rubble and smoke, and in the middle of it a skinny, filthy, black-haired boy in broken glasses who looked about fifteen, standing very still while people swarmed around him, looking down at something on the floor that the photograph didn't show.

*He was seventeen,* Calloway had said, holding the paper. In a voice Layla had never heard from her either. *Seventeen, Quinn. Riddle. In a straight duel. In front of everybody.*

*How?*

*They're saying luck,* Calloway had said. *That's what they're saying in London. Luck, and some business with a wand.* And she'd folded the paper up and put it under her arm and looked at Layla for a long moment. *I'll tell you something I've learned in forty years of this. Nobody gets that lucky.*

She was twenty, and nineteen, and twenty-one, and he was a ghost story. He'd gone. Everybody knew that. Some time in 1999 he'd walked out of the British Ministry and out of the papers and off the face of the earth, and nobody had seen him since, and every bar on every dueling circuit on three continents had a theory. He'd gone to Africa. He'd gone to Tibet. He'd gone mad from whatever had happened in that hall. He was dead and the British were hushing it up. He'd married a Muggle in Australia and was raising sheep. He'd gone looking for the other two Hallows. Somebody in Rio had sworn up and down that he'd seen him, in 2003, in a market in Lima, buying a goat.

*I'd bet my hives,* her grandmother used to say. *I'd bet my hives that boy's out there somewhere, and I'd bet he's still not done.*

And Layla had stood on a ridge at noon, today, with her wand up, and her hair full of rain, and honey on her wrists, and thrown a Stunner at him.

She'd thrown a Stunner. And a Disarming Charm. And ropes. And a Blasting Curse at his feet, and her own Disjunction, the best thing she'd ever made, and he'd caught it on two fingers and turned it over like a stone and said *oh, that's clever* and handed it back to her.

And then he'd crouched down in the ferns, four feet away, and told her to put Murtlap on her back.

"Layla," said Esme again, gently. "Sweetheart. Breathe."

Layla breathed. It came out of her in a long wheeze, like a punctured tire.

"Say that again," she said. Her voice came out a full octave higher than normal. "Edward. Say it again. His name. Say it again."

"Harry Potter," Edward said. He was watching her very closely. Layla knew he could see all of it; she could feel him seeing it. Her grandmother's kitchen, the *Ghost*, the Chocolate Frog card. Calloway's face. The headline. He'd gone very still, the vampire-still, the stone-still.

"Oh my God," Layla said. "Oh my *God*."

"Who's Harry Potter?" said Jacob.

Layla turned her head and looked at him.

He looked back at her, soaking wet in the kitchen doorway, with a total, honest, open blank on his face. So did Seth. So did Emmett, and Rosalie, and Jasper behind the window seat. Carlisle was frowning, the way he frowned at a word in a foreign language he almost knew. Bella was looking at Edward, and Edward was looking at Layla, and none of them knew. None of them had any idea.

She'd forgotten. For one second she'd forgotten where she was, and who she was sitting with. Of course they didn't know. Nobody outside her world knew. That was the *point*.

"He's—" She stopped. She tried again. "Okay. Okay. Um."

"Like, is he famous?" said Seth. "In your—with the wands and stuff?"

"Is he *famous*," Layla said.

She laughed. It came out wrong, high and cracked, the way it had on the ridge.

"Seth, he's—okay. Okay. In 1981 there was a war. In Britain. In our world, in the wizarding world. A really bad one. There was a man, a wizard, the worst one in a hundred years, maybe more. Tom Riddle. He called himself something else and people were so scared of him they wouldn't say the other name out loud. They still won't. Not in Britain. Not most places." She was talking too fast. She made herself slow down. "He was killing people. Hundreds. Muggles. Witches and wizards. Whole families. He was winning. And on Halloween of 1981 he went to a house in a village in the west of England, and he killed a man and his wife, and then he went upstairs and pointed his wand at their baby, and used the Killing Curse. The one you don't come back from. Nobody comes back from it. Nobody ever had. Ever. In all of history."

The rain beat on the glass.

"It bounced," Layla said. "Off the baby. It bounced, and it hit him instead, and he—went. Disappeared. Not dead, it turned out, but gone. For thirteen years. And the baby was fine. He had a scar on his forehead the shape of a lightning bolt and that was it. The war was just—over. In one night. Because of a one-year-old. They called him the Boy Who Lived. Everybody knows that story. Every witch and wizard alive. In America, too. Our parents told it to us like a bedtime story."

"Was the baby—" Seth started.

"Yes, Seth, the baby was him." Layla pressed both hands flat over her face and spoke through her fingers. "And then Riddle came back. In the nineties. And the war started up again. And in 1998, when he was seventeen, Harry Potter killed him. In a duel. In front of half of magical Britain, at a school in Scotland, after a battle. The darkest wizard of the century, maybe *ever*, and a seventeen-year-old boy beat him. And then a year later he vanished. Walked out of his own life and nobody's seen him since. Eight years. There's a hundred stories about where he went. Nobody knows. *Nobody* knows."

She took her hands down.

"I know," she said. "I know where he went. He went to a ridge above the Bogachiel and put me through a log."

There was a long silence.

"Okay," said Emmett slowly. "So he's like—what. Your guys' Superman?"

"I don't know who that is either, Emmett!"

"He's—you know. Flies. Red cape. Bullets bounce off—"

"*Curses* bounce off him!" Layla said. "That's not a metaphor! That actually happened! That's in the history books! There's a *chapter*!"

"Okay! Okay." Emmett held up his hands. "Jeez."

"Layla." Carlisle had come away from the fireplace. He crouched down beside Esme, by the couch, at Layla's level, and his face was very calm and very serious. "This man. Potter. Would you say he's dangerous?"

She stared at him.

"Carlisle," she said, "you were in the room when Seth told it. He held two wolves on the ground with his mind while he fought me. He caught my curses with his hands. He shot the ground with a pistol and it threw me twenty feet." Her voice wobbled and she made it stop. "There's a list. In dueling. Of the best there ever were, in our world. The ones people still argue about. There were three names on it. Dumbledore. Grindelwald. Riddle. Calloway, my old coach, used to say the list was closed, that nobody alive belonged on it." She swallowed. "Riddle's on it. And a seventeen-year-old killed Riddle. And that seventeen-year-old's been somewhere for eight years getting *better*. And none of those three ever did what he did to me today without a wand. Not one of them. Not in any account I've ever read."

Carlisle didn't say anything.

"So yes," said Layla. "I'd say he's dangerous."

It went round the room. She watched it go. She watched it hit Jasper first. Jasper had been in wars, she'd known that the first day, from his arms and his eyes; he took it the way a soldier takes news of the enemy's numbers. His hand tightened on Alice's shoulder. Rosalie's face went hard. Bella was looking at the stairs, toward the second floor, toward wherever they'd put the baby. Esme's hand had found Carlisle's. Jacob, in the kitchen doorway, had gone gray under the brown, the color Layla figured she'd been all afternoon, and Seth beside him had his mouth open, slowly working it all through. The ridge. The crater. His sister's head.

Edward was looking at Leah.

Leah had finished her ham. She'd put the plate in the sink and run water over it, and she was leaning back against Esme's counter with her arms folded across her tank top and her bare ankles crossed. She'd listened to the whole thing. Layla had watched her listen to it: the curse, the baby, the war, Riddle, the list. She'd watched it all go into Leah Clearwater and come out the other side without leaving a mark.

Now she unfolded her arms. She looked around the room. At every grim, gray, frightened face.

"See?" Leah said. "Better."

### IV. Leah

*The Cullen house — 7:21 p.m.*

They didn't get it.

That was the thing that got her, standing in Esme's kitchen with her arms folded and her plate in the sink. She looked around at all of them, at Layla gone white on the couch, at Jasper's hand tight on Alice's shoulder, at Jacob like a dog somebody had kicked, and she could see them building it in their heads. She could see it going up, brick by brick. *Dangerous.* *The list.* *The worst wizard in a hundred years.* *Seventeen.* They were all standing round in a circle building a monster out of what Layla had said, and getting scareder with every brick, and every brick they laid made her want to laugh harder.

Because she'd been in his kitchen.

She'd stood by his range and watched him open a beer with his thumb. She'd watched him try to tell her his name was Harry Evans and get stuck on it like a car in a ditch, and go *bollocks*, and rub his face with both hands. She'd watched him turn his back on her while she changed and stand there talking to a banana slug. *Don't look at me like that.*

He'd made her eggs. He'd watched her eat them like it was the best thing he'd seen all year. She'd felt him watch.

And yeah, okay, he'd stood at the edge of a silver circle and made a vampire scream until the jars rattled. She'd been there. She'd watched it with her hands gripping the chair. She'd felt the thing he reached for to do it, the cold heavy thing in the locked cabinet, and she'd felt how far back in him it was kept, and how carefully. She knew exactly how dangerous he was. She was the only one in this house who did.

That was why she wasn't scared.

"Lee," said Seth, slowly. "This is—this is kind of a big deal."

"I know it's a big deal."

"No, I mean—he's like, the most famous—"

"I heard her, Seth. I was standing right here."

"And you're *happy*?"

"I'm not unhappy."

"Leah." That was Jacob. He'd found his feet again, some of them. He came into the living room and stood in the middle of the rug, dripping, and looked at all of them in turn, and she watched him decide to be the alpha again, the way he'd tried to in the kitchen. This time he got all the way there. "Leah. He knows about Nessie."

There it was.

"He knows about all of us," Jacob said. "He went through Seth's head and took everything. The house. This room. Her. Charlie. He's got a room full of things in jars that nobody else in the world has, Layla, right? Things nobody's ever seen?"

Layla nodded, slowly.

"And he's got a vampire in there now too. Because he wanted one. Because he set a trap over the whole peninsula, Leah, the whole *peninsula*, I saw it in your head, he showed you, and he sat and waited two months for something to walk into it so he could take it home and cut its fingers off." Jacob's hands were fists again. "And now he knows there's a baby up the hill who's half one thing and half another and there's never been anything like her, not ever, not anywhere. And Edward says the wall in your head is thickest over her." He looked at Edward. "Right?"

"Right," said Edward quietly.

"So what do you think he wants with her?" said Jacob. "You think he wants to *babysit*?"

"He's not going to hurt the kid," Leah said.

"How do you know that?"

She opened her mouth to tell him.

She knew. She knew it the way she knew where her own feet were. She'd been sitting on a cold wooden chair in the round room when he'd crouched at the edge of the circle and made the vampire on the floor tell her about a heartbeat it had smelled in the trees behind this house at four in the morning. She'd watched his face when he heard it. She'd sat there while he held a three-hundred-year-old mind on the end of a rein, sweating, with the old man laughing down the phone from Italy, and she'd felt him put every single thing he had on that rein so the thing on the floor couldn't say the word *heartbeat*. She'd felt the effort of it go through him like a man holding up a truck.

*I'm not having that old bastard put his hand on her. Not on my watch. Not ever.*

"He—" Leah said.

Her throat closed.

It closed like a hand. Gentle. Firm. A cool dry pressure right at the top of her chest, under her collarbone, like a thumb laid across her windpipe, not hurting, just there. Just enough. The words got as far as the back of her tongue and stopped there and couldn't get out, and she stood in the middle of the Cullens' kitchen with her mouth open and nothing coming out of it at all.

She tried again. *He stopped them. He stopped the call. He—*

The thumb pressed. A little harder. Not much. Like somebody saying *no* without raising their voice.

Leah shut her mouth.

She put her hand flat on the top of her chest, where it was, and felt it there. The same cold thing that had come down the rope at half past six. It was sitting under her collarbone now, the way she'd felt it sitting under his in the tent when he'd tapped the place and said *there's an oath*. And it was sitting in her now. Quiet. Patient. Like a guard dog asleep across a door.

"Leah?" said Seth.

She coughed. It hurt a little. She coughed again and it went away, and the cold thing went back to sleep.

"Something just closed," Edward said. He'd taken a step toward her. He was staring at the middle of her chest the way he'd stared when she walked in. "In her head. Just now. When she tried to answer you. It closed over her throat. Like a hand." He looked at Layla. "The binding."

Layla had sat up very straight on the couch. "It's in *her*? Not just over what she knows? It's in her *throat*?"

"It seems to be."

"But that's—she never took it. She didn't take any oath. You can't put one of those in somebody who didn't—" Layla stopped. She looked at Leah. Her eyes had gone very sharp again, the work look, the doorframe look. "Unless it doesn't know she's somebody else."

Leah rubbed her throat.

"Huh," she said. "Okay. That's new."

She thought about the tent. About him standing in the kitchen with his hand on his chest, staring at her. *It doesn't think you're outside. It doesn't think you're anyone else. As far as the oath's concerned, I'm talking to myself.* She hadn't really gotten it then. She'd been too busy freaking out about everything else. She got it now. If the thing in his chest thought she was him, then it thought what was in her head was his. And if it thought what was in her head was his, it would guard it the same way it guarded his. Out of her head. Out of her mouth.

She was a locked box now. Same as him. Somebody had given her a key without asking and then glued her mouth shut around it.

She found she didn't mind that as much as she probably should.

"Okay," Leah said. "So I can't tell you. Fine. That's fine. It doesn't change anything." She looked at Jacob. "He's not going to hurt the kid. I know he's not. You're going to have to take my word for it."

"Your word," said Jacob.

"Yeah, Jake. My word."

"Your word that you got from inside *his* head. With *his* thing in your throat. That he put—"

"He didn't put it there."

"That *got* there, then, because of *him*, and won't let you talk!" Jacob threw his hands up. "Leah, do you hear yourself? You've known him five hours! You said so yourself! And now you can't even tell us what he told you, because he's got some kind of, of *magic hand* sitting on your neck!"

"It's not *his* hand."

"I don't care whose hand it is!"

"Jacob." Carlisle said it quietly, the way he said everything. "Sit down. Please. All of us. Let's think about this."

"I don't want to think about it! I want to do something about it!"

"I know," said Carlisle. "That's why I'd like you to sit down."

Jacob didn't sit down. He stood in the middle of the rug and shook with it, and Leah watched him, and she felt sorry for him, a little, which was new too. She'd spent months wanting to bite Jacob and now she just felt sorry for him. He was so scared. He was so scared for that kid. She'd watched him be scared for the kid every day for ten weeks, and she'd always thought it was kind of pathetic.

She didn't think so now. She thought it was the most normal thing in the world. She could feel the end of her own rope out there, eleven miles off, a little hot knot in the dark, and she thought if anybody in this room had stood up and said *let's go kill him*, she'd have torn their throat out before they got to the door.

Which was, she figured, about to come up.

---

They made a plan. Or they tried to.

It went the way plans always went in this house. Everybody had one and nobody liked anybody else's. Leah leaned against the counter with her arms folded and listened to it go round, and the rain came down on the glass, and out at the other end of the rope the man in the tent was talking to somebody. She could feel his voice in his chest. Low and steady. She could feel the thing at the bottom of him pacing up and down behind its locks, lashing its tail, so unhappy about whatever he was saying that it made her own wolf sit up under her ribs and lay its ears back.

She didn't say anything about that. Nobody had asked.

"We should assume the worst," Jasper said. He'd come out from behind the window seat and was standing by the glass wall with his arms folded, looking out into the dark, the way she figured he must have stood at windows in the war. His voice was flat and Southern and calm. "Let's be honest about what we know. We know he can hold down two wolves without looking at them. We know he can turn back any spell a trained duelist can throw. We know he can carry one of us like a plank of wood. We know he has a weapon that can break stone." He turned his head. "If he decides to come against this house, I don't think we can stop him. I don't think all of us together could stop him. I'd want that said out loud before we decide anything."

Nobody argued with him.

"Then we leave," said Rosalie.

"Rose—"

"No, Esme, I mean it. We leave. Tonight. Denali. Tanya will take us. Or further. Alaska's big." She was sitting forward in her chair with her hands knotted together in her lap and her face like a slammed door. "He knows about Renesmee. He knows what she is. He's got a room full of jars. Do you know what I keep thinking about? I keep thinking about a jar. I keep thinking about a jar with her in it." Her voice cracked. She shut her mouth on it.

"We're not running," Esme said quietly. "We've done that before. It doesn't work."

"It worked in 1936."

"It did not work in 1936 and you know it."

"We don't run," Jacob said. "That's not what we do. We call Sam."

Leah's head came up.

"We call Sam," Jacob said again. He'd started pacing, up and down the rug, leaving wet footprints. "Both packs. All of us. That's—what is it now? Sam's got Jared, Paul, Embry, Quil, Collin, Brady. Plus me and Seth and—" He stopped. He looked at Leah. He didn't finish. "Plus Emmett. Plus Jasper. He held two of us down on the ridge. Fine. Can he hold ten? Ten wolves and two of you, all at once, from every side?" He turned around at the end of the rug and came back. "We don't need to find the tent. He's coming here tomorrow. Leah said so. So we're ready for him. We're in the trees. All of us. We let him walk up the drive and into the house and then we—"

"And then you what?" said Leah.

Jacob stopped.

"Go on," Leah said. "And then you what, Jake?"

"And then we make sure," Jacob said, "that he never gets anywhere near her. Ever."

He meant it. She could see that he meant it. She could see him seeing it in his head: ten wolves coming out of the trees at once, from every side, and the man in the suit in the middle of them on Esme's lawn.

She thought about the ridge. Two wolves pinned flat without a glance. A Blasting Curse stopped six inches off the ground and turned aside like a bug. A witch the best in the Southwest, flying twenty feet backward into a log.

And she thought about what was at the bottom of him. Behind the locks. The thing that was walking up and down down there right now, with its tail lashing, that had stood up at the bottom of the rope on the ridge and made three iron locks groan like a ship in a storm.

She thought if Jacob brought ten wolves out of the trees at that thing, she'd be burying most of them by the end of the week.

And none of that was the point anyway.

"Okay," Leah said. "Jake. Everybody. You guys know how this works, right?"

They looked at her.

"With us," Leah said. "With imprinting. You all know how it works. You were here." She uncrossed her arms. She came away from the counter and walked into the living room, barefoot, onto the white rug, until she was standing in the middle of it with Jacob in front of her. She was a couple of inches shorter than him in human shape. It had never once in her life mattered. "Back in September. When the kid was born. When Sam figured out what she was and decided to come up here with the whole pack and take her apart. Remember?"

Jacob's face changed.

"He was coming," said Leah. "He was right out there." She pointed through the glass, at the black lawn and the trees. "He had everybody. Paul. Jared. All of them. He'd decided. He was going to kill Bella and he was going to kill the baby, and the Cullens were going to fight, and it was going to be a bloodbath, and he'd already made up his mind. And then he didn't. He just stopped. He turned around and he took everybody home." She tipped her head. "Why'd he stop, Jake?"

Jacob didn't say anything.

"Come on. Say it. I know you remember. You're the reason. What was that one law?" She looked at him. "The one that stopped Sam from doing what he was planning?"

The room had gone very quiet.

"Leah," Jacob said.

"Say it."

"That's different."

"*Say it*, Jacob."

Jacob looked at her for a long time. She watched it go through him. She watched him remember standing on this lawn in the rain in September, on four feet, with Nessie ten minutes old in the house behind him and the whole of Sam's pack coming up out of the trees with their hackles up, and she watched him remember how they'd stopped. How every one of them had stopped, at the edge of the grass, all at once, like they'd hit a wall. Because the minute Jacob had looked at the baby and imprinted, the baby had stopped being something they could hunt. It had become something that belonged to one of their own. And there was a law about that. The oldest one. Older than the treaty. Older than the Cold Ones. Older, the old men said at the bonfire, than the tribe's own name.

"No wolf," Jacob said finally, very low, "can harm another wolf's imprint."

"The most absolute law we have," Leah said. "That's what Sam called it. Out there. That night. You heard him. I heard him. *The most absolute law we have.* He couldn't touch her. He couldn't touch her if he wanted to, and he really wanted to. Not him, not Paul, not any of them. Not ever." She let that sit. "So."

"So," said Seth. From the kitchen door. Slowly. "Oh."

"Yeah, Seth. *Oh*."

"Leah—" said Jacob.

"So you can call Sam," Leah said. "Go ahead. Call him. Call everybody. Bring all ten of them up here tomorrow and put them in the trees, and see what happens when they come out at him. See how far they get." She looked around the room, at Jasper by the window, at Emmett on the arm of the chair. "And that goes for the rest of you, too, before anybody gets ideas. He's my imprint. That makes him the pack's. I'm Jacob's second. You put a hand on him, you've put a hand on us, and you know what the treaty says about that. You can ask Jake how he feels about breaking the treaty, if you want. I already know."

Jacob looked like she'd hit him.

"Leah," he said. "Leah, he's got Nessie in his *head*—"

"I know."

"You're telling me to just let him walk in here."

"I'm telling you that you can't stop him walking in here," Leah said. "No wolf can. That's not me. That's the law. That's the same law that's kept your kid alive since September." Her voice went quieter. "And I'm telling you he's not going to hurt her. I can't tell you how I know. You saw. My mouth won't do it. So you're going to have to trust me, Jake. Or you're going to have to figure out how to fight the oldest thing in your own blood. Your pick."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The rain beat on the glass. Upstairs, very faintly, she could hear the kid's heartbeat, fast as a bird's, and a soft voice, Esme's or somebody's, reading to her. Something about a bear.

Out at the end of the rope, eleven miles off, the voice in his chest had stopped. He'd finished talking to whoever it was. And down at the bottom of him, she felt the big dark thing behind the locks stop pacing. It stood still for a second. Then it let out a long breath, and lay down, grumbling, with its chin on its paws. The way her wolf did when it lost an argument.

A second later, the whole rope jumped.

It jumped like a fish on a line. One moment he was eleven miles northwest, in his tent, in the hall, and the next he wasn't; he was *south*, way south, near as far away as she was, out over the Bogachiel, and the line between them had swung through the dark like a compass needle when you turn the map. She felt it swing. She felt him land. Rain. Wet rock. The smell of scorched moss.

Leah turned her head and looked south through the glass wall, at nothing, at black trees.

"What?" said Edward, sharply. He'd seen her do it.

"He just jumped," she said. "Like Layla does. The—Apparition. He's on the ridge."

"How do you *know* that?"

"I know where he is," Leah said. "All the time. Same as Seth knows where Layla is. Same as Jake always knows where the kid is in the house. Don't you guys ever listen?" She stood looking south for another second. "He said he was going to go clean it up. The fight. Layla's stuff. Make it look like lightning, so nobody comes looking. People from Seattle." She glanced at Layla. "Your people."

Layla had gone very still on the couch.

"He's covering for me," she said slowly.

"He's covering for all of us," said Leah. "He said. If anybody from New York ever comes up here looking for you, they find a woman with some bees and a cop with a normal memory and a houseful of boring doctors, and they go home." She shrugged. "That was the deal, right?"

Layla didn't answer. She was staring at the floor.

Leah turned to go.

"Where are you going?" Jacob said.

"Home." She went back into the kitchen and got the ham out of the fridge again, and cut three more slices, and wrapped them in a paper towel, for the run. "Mom's going to want to know. Seth's going to tell her if I don't, and he'll do it wrong. And I'm tired." She looked at Jacob over her shoulder. "He's coming tomorrow, Jake. In the morning. You can ask him whatever you want. He'll probably even tell you some of it. He's pretty bad at keeping his mouth shut, for a spy."

"He's a *spy*?" said Emmett, delighted.

"I didn't say that," said Leah.

"You said *for a spy*!"

"Did I." She took her ham to the back door. She stopped with her hand on the glass. "And Jake? Don't call Sam."

He didn't answer.

"I mean it," she said. "For your sake. If you go to Sam and tell him you need him to help you kill my imprint, he's going to look at you the way you looked at him in September. And you're going to hate it. Trust me." She opened the door. The rain came in, cold, smelling of cedar. "I know what that looks like from the inside."

She went out.

Behind her, nobody said anything. She went across the wet lawn in the dark toward the big cedar at the corner, and she was almost there when she heard the glass door slide open again, and bare feet on the grass, coming fast.

Seth.

He caught up with her at the tree. He didn't say anything for a second. He stood there in somebody else's sweatpants with the rain flattening his hair and looked at her.

"Lee," he said. "Are you happy?"

She thought about it. She actually stood there under the cedar and thought about it, which she wouldn't have done yesterday. Yesterday she'd have said something mean and run.

At the end of the rope, way off south, on a ridge in the rain, the man she'd imprinted on was crouched at the edge of a smoking crater with his hand flat on the rock, doing something to it. She could feel the rock under his palm. Cold. She could feel him concentrating, the clean sharp focus of it, like a man threading a needle.

And she could feel, under the focus, faint, way down, that he was thinking about her too.

"I don't know," Leah said. "Ask me in a week."

Seth looked at her for a long time.

Then he grinned. It went all the way across his face, the big dumb happy one, the one he'd been born with.

"That's a yes," he said. "That's totally a yes."

"Go back inside, Seth."

"That's a *yes*, Lee!"

"I'm going to bite you."

"You'd have to catch me," Seth said, and ran back across the lawn to the house, and to Layla, laughing.

Leah stood under the cedar in the rain. She untied her shorts and her tank top and wrapped the ham in them and tied the whole bundle to her ankle with the cord, the way she'd done a thousand times, and she stood up, and she looked south one more time.

Then she phased, and ran home.

### V. Harry

*The tent — 7:02 p.m.*

The mirror was back on its nail by the telephone. He'd hung it there at ten to six, after Croaker's room went dark, and he hadn't touched it since. Since then he'd cooked a fry-up for a woman he'd known for four hours and watched her eat it. He'd walked her out into the rain and put his hand on the side of her face. He'd stood on the bench in the dark and listened to her run twenty miles down the line, and felt the pack hit the oath in her head like water hitting a dam. He'd come in and put the kettle on and put side two on, loud, and stood at the range.

The whole time, the mirror had hung on its nail in the hall like a bad tooth.

He took it down.

The thing at the bottom of him lifted its lip.

He felt it do it. It had been lying down behind its locks since she left, chin on its paws, facing southeast, the way it had lain for sixty-six days facing nothing at all. When his fingers closed on the black frame it raised its head and looked at the mirror through his eyes, and it didn't like the mirror. It knew what the mirror was. It had been there at ten to six, listening, when Croaker said *you sound odd, Potter*.

It knew what the mirror could do. One word from the man on the other side of it and Harry would be on a plane out of Sea-Tac by Thursday with his tent in his pocket and twenty miles of forest and an ocean and a continent between him and the end of the line. It had worked that out at ten to six. It had been very pleased with him when he'd said *no, sir, nothing else.*

"I know," Harry said to it.

It growled. Low. He felt it in his back teeth.

"I know. Shut up."

He stood in the hall with the mirror in his hand and the record in the kitchen finished, the needle ticking round and round in the run-out groove, and thought about it one more time, carefully, the way he'd think about a ward before he put his hand through it.

He could leave it. That was the plain fact. He'd said *nothing else* and Croaker had said *full report by Friday* and gone back to his writing. If he left it, the lie would sit there on Level Nine in Croaker's head and nobody would ever pick it up and turn it over. He'd write a report on Friday that said *specimen acquired* and *basilisk venom effective* and didn't mention a grey wolf, or a witch, or a phone call, or a child. He was good at reports. He'd written two hundred of them. The DoM had a whole wing of a whole floor full of things it didn't know about because some Unspeakable in the field had decided it was better off not knowing, and nobody upstairs had ever minded, because nobody upstairs ever found out.

He'd stood in Croaker's office in July of 1998, eighteen years old, with his hands in the pockets of a jacket Hermione had bought him, nine weeks after the battle, when there'd been nothing left in him at all. The Ministry had wanted him for an Auror. Everybody had wanted him for an Auror. Kingsley had wanted it so badly he'd come round to Grimmauld Place three times with the forms. And an old bald man in grey robes with a face like a cracked wall had sent a note by owl that said, in its entirety, *If you want to understand what happened to you, come and see me,* and Harry had gone.

Croaker had sat behind his desk in his drifts of paper and looked at Harry with his tap-water eyes for a long time. And then he'd said: *I'm not going to tell you what's in the Department. You'll have to find out. But I'll tell you one thing about me, and you can decide from that. I'll never lie to you. I may not tell you things. I'll tell you I'm not telling you. But I'll never put a false thing in your head and let you walk around with it.*

Harry had spent seven years before that being managed. Kindly, by a man who'd loved him, for his own good, for the greater good, with the truth handed out in pieces and on a schedule that somebody else had written. He knew what it did to you. He'd found out in a forest in Scotland, walking toward a clearing, what it did to you.

*All right,* he'd said to Croaker, at eighteen. *All right. Same goes.*

Eight years. He'd kept it. He'd sat in this hall at ten to six and broken it in about four seconds, for a woman he'd known since one o'clock, and Croaker had looked at him out of the mirror and let him.

The wolf growled again. Harder.

"No," said Harry. "You don't get a vote on this one."

He held up the mirror and said, "Croaker."

---

The glass went grey, and then dark, and then it was the little wood-panelled room with the green-shaded lamp. Croaker hadn't moved. He might have been carved there. He was writing, the same pen, possibly the same line, the drifts of paper exactly as they'd been an hour and a half ago.

It was three in the morning in London. He didn't look up.

"Potter," said Saul Croaker. "Twice in one night. People will talk."

"I lied to you, sir."

The pen stopped.

Croaker looked at the page for a second, at the line he'd been writing. Then he laid the pen down beside it, parallel, the way he always laid it down, and folded his long hands on top of the paper, and looked up out of the mirror.

"Go on," he said.

So Harry told him.

He did it in order. He stood in the hall of the tent with the mirror in one hand and the other hand in his pocket, and gave it to him the way he'd have given a debrief on a job in Lagos or Minsk, flat and complete. The two wolves on the ridge. The witch, Layla Quinn, coming out of an Apparition already firing, and what she'd thrown, and the Disjunction, which was her own work and very good. The deal he'd made with her in the ferns on his own account, which nobody had authorised: his silence on her Statute breach for her services at a time of his choosing. The grey wolf. Her eyes.

He told Croaker what happened when he looked at her. He didn't dress it up. He said *the bond the Quileute call imprinting* and *it went both ways* and *it's tied off at both ends, sir, hers and mine*, and he said *mine* knowing what Croaker would take it to mean, and let him take it.

Then he told him about the oath.

He told him he'd stood in his own kitchen and told a Quileute shapeshifter in wet cut-offs what the Department of Mysteries was and what it did and what it was about to do in Tuscany, in so many words, with Volterra and the word *strike* in it, and the oath had sat under his collarbone and listened to every word and done nothing. He told him why he thought it had done nothing. He told him he'd felt it reach down the line, at half six, into her head, twenty miles off, and close over everything he'd told her, the way it would have closed over his own throat.

He told him about the phone call to Volterra. He told him he'd made it at about four o'clock that afternoon, on nobody's say-so but his own, with the specimen under the Imperius and Aro on the other end of the line, laughing.

And he told him why. He told him about the heartbeat in the trees behind the white house, fast as a bird's, and the child.

Croaker listened to all of it. His face didn't move. Nothing ever moved in it. He sat with his hands folded on the paper and his pale eyes on Harry's, and when Harry got to the child he closed them, the way he had when Harry told him about the tooth. Just for a second. Then he opened them again.

"Is that all of it?" he said.

"Not quite." Harry shifted the mirror to his other hand. "I'm compromised, sir. Properly. I'd pull me, if I were you. Send Fennick, or Maier, or somebody from the Berlin desk. Somebody with a clear head. Somebody who can be trusted to do what the Department needs on this coast."

"And you can't."

"No, sir." He didn't have to think about it. He found it was already there, sitting in him like a stone at the bottom of a river, and had been since one o'clock. "If it's ever the Department or her, it's her. Every single time. I wouldn't even have to decide. That's what you've got out here."

The wolf at the bottom of him had gone completely still.

Harry felt it go still. It had been growling the whole time he talked, a low grinding grumble under everything like an engine left idling, and when he said *pull me* it had stopped. It stood behind its locks with every hair on end, rigid, waiting, the way it had stood on the ridge at the bottom of the line.

If Croaker pulled him, he wasn't going. He knew that. The wolf knew that. They'd both known it since the beginning of the sentence. He'd be out of the Department by the weekend and living in a tent in the woods above La Push with no job and no name and every intelligence service in the magical world wondering what Harry Potter was doing on the Olympic Peninsula, and he'd have to deal with that, and he would.

He just wanted the old man to know he'd been told.

Croaker looked at him for a long time.

"Are you finished?" he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Good." Croaker unfolded his hands. He picked up the pen. He looked at it. He put it down again. "Then I'll tell you what you've got wrong, which is most of it."

Harry waited.

"You've been in my Department for eight years," said Croaker. "I've been in it for fifty-one. I have had Unspeakables lie to me for fifty-one years, Potter. In this office, in that mirror, in reports, in their sleep. I've had them lie to me about money and about women and about what they found behind doors I sent them through, and about what they did there. I expect it. It's in the job. We train it into you, for God's sake. I'm told I'm rather good at catching it." He tipped his head a fraction. "You're the first one who has ever lied to me and then rung me back an hour and a half later at three o'clock in the morning to tell me about it. With details. And an offer to resign."

"I didn't say resign."

"You were about to. I heard it coming." Croaker's mouth, the crack in the wall, moved very slightly at one end. It might have been a smile. Harry had seen it perhaps four times in eight years. "Do you know why I sent you to that peninsula, Potter? Out of everyone on Level Nine?"

"Because I was the best you had for the job."

"Because you're the only one I have who'd tell me the truth when it cost him something." Croaker sat back in his chair. "Anybody can catch a vampire. Fennick could have caught a vampire. I sent you because whatever was waiting on that coast, I wanted to hear about it from somebody who wouldn't decide on my behalf that I was better off not knowing. You've just proven me right. That's exactly why I'll never get rid of you."

Harry didn't say anything.

At the bottom of him, the wolf stood still behind its locks, its ears up.

"You're not compromised, Potter," said Croaker. "Or you are, and I don't care. You've got a woman on that coast you'd choose over the Department. Good. I'd rather know that than not. It means I know where you stand, which is more than I can say for half the people on this floor." He picked up the pen again, finally, and turned it over in his long fingers. "You're right where you need to be."

"Sir—"

"I'm not finished. You'll stay where you are. Your posting is amended as of—" he glanced at something on the desk, "—three-oh-nine this morning, Greenwich. You're the Department's liaison to the Quileute. To La Push, and whatever they call that pack of theirs, and whatever else turns out to live on that coast. If I need to make you a liaison to keep you there, then that's what you are. There. It's done. I'll do the paperwork myself, so nobody above me reads it."

"There's no such post."

"There is now. I've just made it." Croaker made a note on the page in front of him, three words, small and spidery. "And frankly, Potter, given what that peninsula's produced in the last two months, I doubt you'd have been leaving before the spring anyway. Two shapeshifter packs that our files on North America don't mention. A coven of seven that don't eat people and a newborn who's an eighth. A hedge-witch's ward grown out of bees. A child that, by everything this Department has ever known, cannot exist. And one of my own Unspeakables who turns out to be bound to a local at both ends of something we haven't got a word for." He laid the pen down. "I've had less on Level Nine in a decade. You'll be there a good long while yet. Get comfortable."

Harry stood in his hall with the mirror in his hand.

At the bottom of him, the wolf let out a breath.

He felt it go out of it. A long, slow, gusting breath through its nose, like a big dog that's been standing at the door with its leash in its mouth and has just been told *not today*, and has realised that *not today* means *not ever*. It didn't lie down. Not yet. It stood there behind its locks and looked at the mirror with its head on one side.

"The girl," said Croaker.

Harry's hand tightened on the frame.

"She stays out of your report," Croaker said. "All of her. Every word. You'll write the specimen, the tooth, the spells, the field data. You'll write the witch as a Department asset, recruited in the field, at your discretion, which I am now approving retroactively, so your private arrangement isn't private any longer and you can stop looking so pleased with yourself about it. You will not write one word about a grey wolf or what the stone did."

"Sir."

"There are people on this floor who'd want her," Croaker said. "You know who. You know the room. And there are people on the floor above who'd want her more, and who'd have her, if they ever heard that the stone took a Quileute shapeshifter for one of ours. She'd be in a tank by Easter." His voice didn't change. "I am, as it happens, one of the people who decides what goes into tanks. She isn't going into one. The stone doesn't make mistakes, Potter. It's never made one in three thousand years. If it's decided she's one of us, then she's one of us, which means she's cleared, which means she's mine to look after. I look after what's mine."

Harry found he couldn't say anything for a second.

"Thank you, sir," he said.

"Don't thank me. It's policy." Croaker reached across the desk to something Harry couldn't see, at the very edge of the mirror's frame. "Now hold still. This won't be pleasant."

Harry knew what it was before the old man's hand came back into view. He'd seen it once, his first day, in the little room with no windows: a chip of black stone about the size of a walnut, glossy and very cold-looking, broken off the big one in the room downstairs some time before anybody had started writing things down. Croaker held it in his palm and closed his fingers over it.

Under Harry's collarbone, the oath moved.

It felt like a hand that had been resting on a row of piano keys for eight years, very lightly, lifting one finger. Then another. Then a third. Cold went out of three small places in his chest and he felt them go, and felt the space where they'd been, and coughed, and put his free hand flat on his breastbone.

"The nomad," said Croaker. "The telephone call. And the child. You're released on those three, and on nothing else. You may tell the Forks coven what you need to about the specimen and what he smelt in their trees, and about your conversation with Volterra. Not one syllable about this Department. Not Level Nine, not the strike, not me. If you try, the stone will stop you, and it won't be gentle the second time."

"You want me to make contact." Harry was still rubbing his chest. "My orders said—"

"Your orders said observe and do not engage. That was before your witch held a show-and-tell in their sitting room and your shapeshifter walked out of your tent and into their kitchen with my secrets in her head." Croaker set the stone down out of sight. "That coven is compromised to us whether we like it or not. So we'll make it useful. Carlisle Cullen spent the better part of two decades at Aro's right hand in the seventeen hundreds, if your Italian receptionist's memory is to be trusted. I want to know what he knows. The building. The habits. The gifts. What the old one's like when he's bored. Everything." He paused. "You'll get it out of him with words, Potter. Not with your circle. I want that clearly understood. That family stays marked as an asset."

"Understood."

"And the Americans." Croaker had picked up the pen again. He was looking at the page. "Clean your ridge. Tonight. Whatever the Quinn woman did up there, I want it gone before anybody in Seattle reads a needle. I'll ring a woman I know in New York in the morning and make some soothing noises. She owes me for a thing in 1977 she'd rather nobody remembered."

"Yes, sir."

"Full report by Friday. Minus the girl."

"Yes, sir."

Croaker started writing again. The pen scratched. Harry thought the conversation was over, and he was lowering the mirror, when the old man said, without looking up:

"And Potter."

"Sir?"

"That business with the locks. Whatever you've got under your shirt." The pen didn't stop. "Do you imagine I didn't read your medicals in 2003? I signed them."

Harry stood very still in the hall.

"You might stop fighting it so hard," said Croaker, "now that there's something on the other end of it worth the bother. Good night."

The mirror went dark.

---

He stood there for a while.

In the kitchen, the needle was still going round in the run-out groove at the end of side two, *tick, hiss, tick, hiss*, like a clock with a cold. He didn't go and lift it.

At the bottom of him, the wolf lay down.

It did it slowly. It turned round once behind its locks, the way a dog turns round in a basket, and lay down with a great groaning sigh and put its chin on its paws, facing southeast. It wasn't happy about the mirror. It would never be happy about the mirror. But it had listened to the whole thing and it had heard *you'll be there a good long while yet*, and it had decided, grudgingly, with very bad grace, that it could live with that.

"Told you," said Harry.

It flattened its ears at him.

He hung the mirror back on its nail. He went into the kitchen and lifted the needle and put the record back in its sleeve. Then he went to the peg by the door and took down his long coat and put it on, and checked the Colt in its rig under his arm, and stood for a second with his eyes shut, feeling for the far end of the line.

She was in the white house. In the living room, he thought. He could feel a thick rug under her bare feet, and her arms folded, and a lot of voices round her, raised. She was annoyed, and she was enjoying being annoyed, and under the annoyance there was something steady and hard as a keel. She'd just said something. He didn't know what. He could feel the shape of it in her chest, the satisfaction of it, like a door slammed exactly right.

"Go on, Lassie," Harry said, to the empty kitchen, and turned on the spot.

### VI. Harry

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — 7:44 p.m.*

It looked worse in the dark.

He came out of the turn onto the edge of the granite slab where he'd stood that morning, and the rain was coming down soft and steady, and he stood still for a full minute and let his eyes adjust, and looked at what they'd done to the place.

The crater was the first thing. Six feet across and nearly three deep, a black bowl punched into the moss and the thin soil and the bedrock under it, ringed with thrown mud. It had stopped smoking hours ago. Rainwater was pooling in the bottom of it. Twenty feet beyond it was the long furrow where the witch had gone backward through the sword ferns, and the nurse log at the end of the furrow with its moss torn off in a great raw patch where she'd hit it. Off to his left the broken boulder lay in pieces in a fan of gravel and scorched lichen from her Blasting Curse. Behind him in the hemlocks, a branch the size of a man's leg lay in the moss where her first Stunner had sheared it off.

And on the slab itself, a mess of roots.

Hundreds of them, black and wet and thick as his thumb, knotted together over the granite in a mat the size of a double bed. Her Transfiguration. They'd closed on nothing when he stepped out of them and they'd stayed closed, wound round each other in a fist, the way Transfigured things did when the caster left in a hurry and forgot to reverse them. It was good work. They'd still be there in a hundred years if nobody did anything about it.

He crouched down by the mat and put his hand on it.

It took about four seconds. He felt the shape of her spell in it, the same clean, braided way she did everything, and found the end of the thread and pulled, and the roots went soft under his palm and lay down and greened over and were moss again. Wet, ordinary, a bit flattened, as if something heavy had lain on it. He did the same for the branch in the hemlocks; he couldn't put it back on the tree, but he could age the break, so the white of the torn wood went grey and soft and rotten, and it looked like any other deadfall in the forest. He did the boulder. That was harder. He didn't put it back together. He took the burn off the lichen and the fresh edges off the broken faces, and sifted the gravel back into the moss, and when he was done it looked like a rock that had split in a freeze some winter a long time ago.

Then he walked out onto the slab, and went looking for the bead.

He found it about where he'd thought it would be, near the edge where the boy had gone down: two pieces of black glass, each about the size of half a hazelnut, lying in a crack in the granite with the rain beading on them. Obsidian. Cut, not broken; the break ran clean down the middle along a line she'd meant it to, so it would go when bitten. He picked them up and turned them over. There were marks on the inner faces so fine he had to hold them up to his eye in the dark. Old words. Irish, he thought, something from a hedge-book, scratched with a needle by lamplight, over and over and over.

She'd been up all night making it. He could feel that in it. He could feel the hours.

The leather band was a few feet away, in three pieces, where the wolf's teeth had gone through it. He picked those up too. He put the whole lot in the inside pocket of his coat, beside the wallet, and patted it.

Then the crater.

He'd thought about the crater on the way. The rest of it you could hide. The crater you couldn't, not completely, not without filling three cubic yards of hole with conjured rock that would read as conjured to anybody with a decent eye, and that was worse than a hole. So he wasn't going to hide it. He was going to explain it.

There was a dead hemlock at the north end of the ridge, a snag, sixty feet high, bare and grey and leaning, the tallest thing on the spine. He'd seen it that morning. He walked over to it and looked up at it in the rain, and then he looked back at the crater, forty yards off, and did a sum in his head about the way lightning travelled through wet ground, and decided it was close enough.

He put his hand on the snag.

The thing that came down out of the low cloud looked like lightning. It was blue-white and forked and it hit the top of the snag with a crack that he felt in his fillings, and split the tree from crown to roots in a shower of sparks and steam, and ran down it into the ground, and across the wet ridge in a branching shining web, through the moss, along the cracks in the granite, straight into the bottom of the crater and out the other side. For a quarter of a second the whole spine of the ridge was lit up white, every fern and stone and drop of rain. Then it was dark again, and the snag was standing there in two smoking halves, and the air stank of ozone and burnt sap.

He walked back down the ridge and looked at the crater.

The web had gone right through it. The mud in the bottom had fused in places into knots of dull grey glass. There were scorch tracks running out of it across the moss like the spokes of a wheel, fernlike, branching, the burn pattern a strike left on wet ground, which he'd seen once on a golf course in Kent where a Muggle had been killed holding an umbrella. The crater sat in the middle of the pattern like it had always been part of it.

It would do. A ranger who came up here in the spring would see a lightning-split snag and a burn pattern and a blast hole where the current had come out, and go home and tell his mates about it in the pub. A curse-breaker who came up here would see the same, as long as she didn't look too hard, and she wouldn't have a reason to.

Last, the residue.

He walked the whole ridge for twenty minutes in the rain, slowly, with his eyes half shut, the way he walked a scene in Minsk or Belfast. He could feel it everywhere. Her magic. Sharp and gold and frightened, smeared across the slab and the ferns and the air, like a smell. His own, fainter, where he'd caught her spells and turned them. The snare on the slab where the specimen had gone up, a knot in his lattice, still humming. And under all of it the rust-coloured traces of the two wolves, and something else, faint, at the edge of the slab where he'd stood and looked into a pair of dark eyes, that he couldn't name and didn't have a sink for. He left that one. He didn't know what would happen if he tried to clean it up, and he didn't want to find out.

The rest he ate. He let the sinks he carried come up out of the stones in the lattice and drink it, all of it, her gold and his own, until the ridge was quiet. When he was done it was a ridge in the rain with a split tree on it. You could have walked an Auror across it with a Sneakoscope in each hand and got nothing.

He stood on the slab and looked south into the dark, the way the line ran. Twelve miles off, in the white house in the trees, she was standing in the middle of somebody's living room and she'd gone quiet. He felt her feel him. He felt her turn her head and look south through a glass wall at nothing, right at him, as if she could see him standing out here in the rain.

"Evening," Harry said.

She didn't hear him. He hadn't expected her to. He said it anyway.

Then he went to find out what Seattle had been listening with.

---

It took him most of an hour.

He knew roughly what he was looking for. Every Ministry in the world had some way of watching its own back garden, and they mostly worked the same way: something that sat in the ground and felt for magic the way a seismometer felt for earthquakes, and wrote down what it felt. He'd swept the peninsula for them in September, all the obvious places, and found nothing. He'd assumed MACUSA didn't bother out here. Four thousand Muggles and a lot of trees.

He'd been wrong. He knew it the minute he went looking with the ridge as the centre. When you'd just set off a Blasting Curse and a Disjunction and an Apparition fourteen miles long in one spot, the way they felt to the ground came back off anything nearby that was listening, faint, like an echo off a wall.

The echo came back from a knoll four miles west of the ridge, above the Bogachiel road.

There was a Muggle installation on top of it. A fenced square of gravel in a clearing in the trees with a solar panel and a fibreglass box and a little steel hatch set flush into a concrete pad, and a sign on the fence that said PACIFIC NORTHWEST SEISMIC NETWORK — UNIVERSITY OF WASHINGTON — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. A real seismometer. There'd be one in a vault under the hatch, listening for earthquakes, sending its readings down a phone line to Seattle. He'd walked past a dozen of them in September and never looked twice.

There were wards on the fence. American ones, the kind MACUSA used, square and tidy and a bit heavy, like a filing cabinet. They didn't stop him. He went through them the way you go through a bead curtain, and opened the hatch with his hand, and climbed down six rungs of steel ladder into a concrete box the size of a wardrobe.

The Muggle seismometer was in the middle of the floor on its own little pier, a squat cylinder in a foil wrap with a cable coming out of it. And bolted to the wall beside it, where any Muggle technician who came down here would look straight past it, was something else.

He lit his hand and looked at it.

It was brass, mostly. Brass and glass and dark polished wood, the size of a cash register, with a little revolving drum on the front wrapped in waxed paper, and a needle resting on the paper, and a clockwork motor that turned the drum, very slowly, about once a week. The needle was a hair from a Thunderbird's tail, he'd have bet money on it; he could feel it twitch at him the way her wand had twitched in his hand that morning. There was a plate screwed onto the wood below the drum, stamped with a bald eagle with its wings spread, and under the eagle, in small capitals: MAGICAL CONGRESS OF THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA. And under that, smaller still: *SEATTLE FIELD OFFICE. THAUMIC TREMOR GAUGE NO. 14. INSTALLED 4/2004. READ TUESDAYS.*

"Cheeky," Harry said, to the brass box, with some admiration. "Very cheeky."

He crouched in front of it and looked at the drum.

The trace ran across the paper in a fine wobbling line, and you could read it like a calendar. Days of nothing. A little flutter here and there, a week apart, that he guessed was the witch at work in her kitchen; there was even a pencilled note beside one of them in a small neat American hand, *Quinn prop. — hedge-wd. — inherited — NFA*, which he supposed meant *no further action*. Two slightly larger flutters on the fourth and the fifth of November that he knew, from the boy's head, were a yard full of stopped rain and a sitting room full of little blue lights. Nobody had pencilled anything beside those. Somebody in Seattle had read them on a Tuesday and decided the hedge-witch was having a busy week.

Then today.

Today the needle had gone up off the paper like a man who'd sat on a pin. A great jagged spike at eleven forty-seven in the morning, so hard the needle had scored right through the wax into the brass underneath. Then a cluster of smaller spikes on top of it, all bunched together in about forty seconds, sharp as teeth. Then nothing. Then a faint smear at about a quarter to two in the afternoon, which he thought must be him, Apparating back to fetch the Explorer and thinking better of it; he'd been sloppy then, and he knew why.

*READ TUESDAYS.* Tomorrow was Tuesday.

Some poor sod in Seattle was going to unclip this drum tomorrow morning and look at it and fall off his chair.

He thought about it for a minute, crouched in the concrete box with his hand lit and the rain dripping through the hatch.

He could take the drum. That was crude; a missing drum would bring someone running faster than a spike. He could wipe it, run the needle back to before eleven forty-seven and lay down a nice flat line; that was cruder, because a flat line on the one day the whole peninsula had been full of thunder would look exactly like what it was. What he needed was a reason. Something that would make a man in Seattle look at that spike and nod and pencil *NFA* beside it and go and get a coffee.

Thunderbird tail-hair in the needle. A Thunderbird feather in the witch's wand. He'd held that wand for a good thirty seconds this morning in the ferns. He'd felt the feather inside it twitch and spit and try to hex him, and he'd learned it, the way he learned anything he held long enough: the feel of it, the grain, the particular jumping crackle a Thunderbird left in the air.

A Thunderbird's storm, passing over.

They were Arizona birds. Desert birds. Sometimes, not often, a young one got caught in a big front off the Pacific in the autumn and blown north, and spent a few miserable days lost over the wrong mountains, raising storms out of nothing everywhere it flew, until it found its way home or something found it. It had happened in Oregon in 1987. It had happened in Alaska in 1951; there was a Ministry pamphlet about it in the Level Nine library, because the bird had made it all the way to the Aleutians and caused an international incident with the Russians. A storm like that would light up a tremor gauge like Christmas.

He put two fingers on the drum.

It took longer than the ridge. He had to unpick the spike from the wax line by line without leaving a mark, and lay something in under it in the same place that looked like what he remembered of the feather, and then stretch it out, because a storm didn't come and go in forty seconds; it rolled in and rolled out. He gave it a slow approach from the south-east, from about eleven, and a big rolling peak at eleven forty-seven that would explain the scored brass, and a long ragged tail heading north toward the Strait until about one. Then he rubbed out the smear at a quarter to two and gave it back to the tail.

He sat back on his heels and looked at it.

It looked like a bird had come up out of the south over the Bogachiel in the middle of the day, got itself in a temper over the ridge, thrown some lightning at a dead tree, and gone off north to sulk. Which, as of about an hour ago, was more or less what the ridge would say too.

"Somebody in Seattle's going to have a lovely week," Harry said.

He closed the hatch on his way out and went back through the wards and left them exactly as he'd found them, square and tidy and heavy. Then he stood in the gravel clearing in the rain and put his hand in his pocket, and felt the two halves of the obsidian bead, and thought about the woman who'd sat up till four in the morning cutting Irish words into them with a needle. He thought she'd want them back.

Then he went to fetch the car.

---

The Explorer was where he'd left it at ten past twelve that afternoon, in the logging turnout a quarter mile up the Bogachiel road, nose out, with a fine drift of wet hemlock needles across the windscreen and the Chandler still face down on the passenger seat. He came out of the turn beside it and stood for a second in the dark, looking at it, at the ordinary muddy shape of it under the trees.

That morning he'd sat in it with his hands on the wheel and said *gotcha*. Ten hours. He felt like he'd been away a month.

He got in and drove it home the long way, round through Forks, at exactly the speed limit, because that was what Harry Evans did. The town was dark and shut. The diner's OPEN sign was off. He drove past the end of Calawah Way and didn't look down it, though he could see from the road that there were no lights in the blue house with the bees, and that the white pickup with New Mexico plates was gone from the gravel by the side. She'd be up at the white house still, then. With her wolf. He thought about her lying on her back in the ferns asking who the hell he was, and the two halves of black glass in his pocket.

He parked where he always parked, at the end of a dirt forest road a mile below the bench, where the lattice told him nobody had driven since September but him, and walked up through the trees in the rain without a light.

Gary was on the canvas by the flap when he got there. He'd moved about six inches since the afternoon.

"Don't," said Harry, and went in.

It was eleven minutes past eleven. He ate four slices of toast standing up at the range, and drank a pint of water, and took the coat off and hung it up, and went to bed.

### VII. Leah

*La Push — 8:31 p.m.*

Her mom was at the kitchen table when she came in the back door, with a cup of coffee and the phone in front of her on the oilcloth, face down, like a card she wasn't ready to turn over.

Leah stood in the doorway with the rain running off her and the bundle of ham and shorts in her hand. She looked at the phone. She looked at her mother.

"Seth called," she said.

"Seth called," said Sue.

"What'd he say?"

Sue picked up her coffee and looked at it.

"He said," she said, "that you'd imprinted on a man in a suit who'd shot at his girlfriend with a gun. That the man was a wizard, like Layla, but more. That he lives in a tent that's bigger on the inside than the outside and keeps a vampire in it. That he's English. And that he's a wolf. Sort of." She drank. "Seth said *sort of* about four times. I don't think he knew what it meant either."

"Nobody does."

"And that you were happy."

Leah didn't say anything.

"He said that last," Sue said. "Right before he hung up. He said, *Mom, she's happy, I don't know what to do.* And then he started crying." She set the cup down, very gently, on the oilcloth. "Not bad crying. The other kind."

Leah went over to the counter and put the bundle down on it and untied the cord. She put the ham on a plate. She shook out her shorts and her tank top, which were soaked, and hung them over the back of a chair by the stove to dry. She didn't look at her mother while she did any of it, and she felt her mother not look at her, the way they'd been not looking at each other for eight months. Since the night in the yard. Since the ambulance.

"Leah," Sue said.

"What."

"Are you?"

Leah stood at the stove with her back to the table.

She thought about lying. She thought about it on purpose, the way you'd poke a bruise, to see if it was still there. *I'm fine, Mom.* She'd said it to her mother probably four hundred times since her dad died, at this table, at that stove, in that exact voice, and her mother had heard it every time and let it go every time, because what else was she going to do.

She opened her mouth to say it, to see.

It came out fine. It came out easy. *I'm fine.* Nothing stopped it. Nothing closed. It was only with him. She could lie to her mom as much as she wanted.

She found she didn't want to.

"Yeah," Leah said. "Yeah, Mom. I think I am."

She heard her mother breathe out behind her. A long breath, all the way out, slow, like something being set down.

Then Sue said, in a completely ordinary voice, "Is he going to come to dinner?"

"*Mom.*"

"I'm only asking."

"I've known him seven hours."

"Your father asked me to dinner after twenty minutes," said Sue, "and I said yes after ten." She picked her coffee back up. "Is he?"

"I don't know. Probably. He eats a lot."

"Good," said her mother. "I like people who eat."

---

She went to bed at ten. She didn't sleep.

She lay on her back in the dark in her old room under the slope of the roof, with the window cracked an inch to let the heat out because she always ran too hot for the house, and the rain coming down on the shingles a foot above her face, and she didn't sleep at all.

She could feel him.

She'd been able to feel him all day; that was nothing new. But in the white house there'd been voices, and on the run there'd been the run, and at the table there'd been her mom, and there'd always been something else in the way, standing between her and the rope like people in a crowded room. Now there was nothing. Now it was just her, lying still in the dark, and the line running out of the middle of her chest, out through the cracked window and the rain and east, twenty miles, to the end.

He'd gone somewhere at a quarter to eight. South, to the ridge; she'd felt that. Then west, somewhere close to the river road, somewhere small and closed in and made of concrete that smelled of damp and metal, where he'd crouched for a long time doing something very fiddly with his fingers. She'd felt his concentration the whole time, tight and bright and pleased with itself. Then a car. She'd felt the steering wheel under his hands and the slow tick of the indicator as he turned, and she'd thought, lying in the dark, *he drives the speed limit*, and almost laughed.

Then he'd walked uphill through the trees in the rain for a long time.

Then he'd said something to the slug. She was sure of it.

Then toast. Then a coat coming off. Then a bed.

He was lying on his back in it now. She could feel the mattress under him. Narrow and hard, the soldier's bed she'd seen through the open door in the tent's hallway, made up tight. He was lying on top of the blanket with his hands folded on his stomach and his eyes open, looking at the ceiling, and he wasn't sleeping either.

She lay there and felt him not sleep.

Down at the bottom of him, the big thing behind the locks was lying with its chin on its paws. It had been lying that way since about half past seven. It wasn't growling anymore. It had stopped being angry about whatever it had been angry about. It was just lying there, with its eyes open, facing her.

Under her own ribs, her wolf was doing the same thing.

She rolled over onto her side and looked at the wall. She rolled back. She kicked the blanket off, because it was too hot, and then pulled it back up, because it was too cold, which it wasn't, and she knew it wasn't.

*Go to sleep,* she thought at him. *God. Go to sleep. One of us has to.*

He didn't hear her. Or he did and didn't answer. She couldn't tell.

At eleven-thirty she heard her mother come up the stairs and go past her door, slowly, and stop outside it for a second, and go on to her own room. At midnight she heard the clock on the landing. At half past twelve she got up and went to the window and stood there in her T-shirt looking out at the backyard and the trees, at the black wet shapes of them, and the rain coming down through the porch light at the back of the Ateara place two doors down, and she felt the line go out over the trees and away east into the dark, and she put her forehead against the cold glass.

Twenty miles. She'd run twenty miles a hundred times. She'd done it in under twenty minutes once, in June, coming back from the newborn fight with Jacob's blood on her teeth.

She could go. She could just go.

She thought about walking up through the spruces onto his bench, at one in the morning, soaking wet, and pushing through the flap of his tent. She thought about his face. She thought about him sitting up in that narrow bed and looking at her.

She thought she'd die of embarrassment before she got across the river.

She went back to bed.

### VIII. Harry

*The tent — 12:52 a.m.*

He'd tried.

He'd lain on his back on top of the blanket for an hour and forty minutes with his hands on his stomach and his eyes on the ceiling and done every single thing he knew. Occlumency first, the ordinary kind, the old drills: clear the mind, empty it, shut every door, one by one, until there's nothing left but a dark quiet room with no windows. He'd done that every night for eight years, and on nights after Minsk, and nights after Lagos, and the night after the basement in Belgrade that he never thought about, and it had always worked. He'd had a system for it. Shut the doors, one, two, three, and sleep.

There was a door he couldn't shut.

He kept coming round to it. Every time he got the rest of his head dark and quiet and closed, there it was, in the middle of the room, standing open, with the line running out through it and away west into the night. It didn't have a door to close. It just went. It went out of him and through the walls of the tent and away, twenty miles, over the Calawah and the Bogachiel and the highway and the Quillayute, all the way to the edge of the sea, to a house he'd never seen, to a room under a roof. To a bed. He could feel the bed.

She'd been lying on her side. Then her back. Then she'd kicked the blanket off, and pulled it back on. Then she'd got up at half past twelve and stood at a window with her forehead on the glass, and he'd felt the glass, cold on her skin, and he'd felt her think about the river.

Then she'd gone back to bed and she was lying on her back again with her eyes open, the way he was.

He tried the second set of drills. The Department ones. The ones you got when you'd been on Level Nine long enough to need them, the ones that took you down past the dark room into something under it, flat and grey and endless, where you could stay for hours and come back up rested. He'd learned them from Croaker himself in 2001, in that little panelled office, sitting across the desk from the old man in a hard chair with his eyes shut for six weeks.

He got about halfway down and the line pulled.

It didn't pull hard. It didn't have to. It was a small, steady tug, from the far end, like someone turning over in bed and taking half the blanket with them. And he came straight back up out of the grey as if somebody had hooked him under the arms.

He opened his eyes and looked at the ceiling.

"Right," he said. "Fine."

At the bottom of him, the wolf lifted its head.

It had been lying with its chin on its paws since the mirror, facing west, very patient, the way it had lain for two months before that facing nothing at all. It hadn't bothered him. It hadn't pushed at the locks. It had lain there for five hours and forty minutes like an old dog by a fire, with its eyes open, and he'd thought, lying here, that it had more patience than he did, which was saying something.

Now it got up.

It didn't hurry. It stood, all four legs, behind the locks, and stretched, front end down and back end up, long and slow, the way a dog stretches in the morning. It shook itself, ears to tail. Then it walked to the first lock, the one braided into the deepest layer of his Occlumency, the one he'd built first, in a hotel room in Seattle in September with the curtains drawn, and it sat down in front of it and looked up at him.

It didn't throw itself at it. It didn't growl. It just sat there and looked at him. And he knew exactly what it was saying, because it was him saying it, and they both knew that, and there was no point pretending.

*You might stop fighting it so hard,* Croaker had said, *now that there's something on the other end of it worth the bother.*

Harry lay on his back in the narrow bed and thought about the old man saying *I signed them*.

Then he sat up and swung his legs off the bed.

---

He went out barefoot. Boxers and a grey T-shirt, the things he'd lain down in, and the slate cold under his feet in the hall, and the wooden steps, and the flap. The rain was still coming down soft and steady when he came out onto the bench, and the river was loud in the gorge, and the spruces stood round him in the dark like the pillars of a cathedral. Gary was on the canvas by the door, a pale smear in the black.

He walked out into the middle of the bench, where there was room. Twenty feet from the tent. Twenty feet from the nearest trunk.

He stood in the rain and shut his eyes.

The first lock was the hardest, because it was the oldest. He'd built it out of the floor of his own mind. He found the end of the braid and took hold of it and, for the first time in sixty-six days, pulled the other way.

It came undone slowly. He felt every strand of it let go. Something cold went out of the bottom of him like water draining out of a bath, and the wolf on the other side stood up, and he felt it stand, and felt how big it was, how much space it took up down there. He'd forgotten. Two months was long enough to forget.

The second lock was quicker. He'd built it in the tent, in the workshop, the second week of September, out of the same stuff as the sinks on the lattice, so it ate scent. He pulled it apart and felt his own smell come back up out of his skin into the rain all at once: pine and wet earth and cold stone, the way he'd smelled for four years before he came here, the smell the grey wolf had caught on the ridge under the coffee and the pie.

The third lock he didn't pull. He didn't need to. The wolf put its nose against it, from the other side, and *pushed*, very gently, and it opened like a gate that had never been latched.

He went down onto four paws.

It always started in the spine. It always had. A long, deep, rolling pull, like a wave going down the length of him from the base of his skull, and everything in him moved with it, lengthened, thickened, reset. He felt his shoulders go down and forward and broaden. He felt his weight change, spread, settle onto four points in the wet moss, and the moss give under them. He felt his jaw go long. He felt the rain land on him and not reach his skin, landing instead on fur, a great deal of fur, thick and dense and close, and the cold went out of the night like somebody had shut a window.

It didn't hurt. It never had. It felt like stepping through a doorway into a much bigger room he'd forgotten was in his own house.

He opened his eyes.

The bench was brighter. That was always the first thing. He could see every needle on the spruces, every drop of rain coming down through the dark, the white water at the bottom of the gorge a hundred feet below. He could hear the river as twenty separate sounds. He could smell the tent, the canvas and the woodsmoke and the gun oil, and the slug, and an elk that had come down to the water a mile upstream at about nine o'clock, and a hundred other things, all at once, sorted and stacked and labelled the moment they came in.

And he could feel her. She'd been there before, at the end of the line. She was there now with the volume turned all the way up, a hot bright knot twenty miles off in the dark, and the line between them hummed so hard he felt it in his teeth.

He looked down at himself.

Black. He'd always been black. Black right down to the roots, with a dense charcoal undercoat that kept the rain out, and big paws, broad and heavy, the claws drawn up into them out of sight. He lifted his head and looked at the nearest spruce. He knew that tree. He'd walked past it every morning for two months. In human shape the lowest whorl of dead branches came out of its trunk a good foot above his head.

His eyes were level with the second.

He did the sum without meaning to. He always did sums. The big russet one, the boy called him Jacob: he'd watched it through the Omnioculars in October carrying the child on its back across a meadow below the white house, and he'd had a good look at it then, and he'd thought, at the time, *bigger than a horse, and about the size of a Shire stallion at the shoulder*. He'd guessed Jacob at about seven feet, measured to the top of the shoulder. Give or take.

He was, he reckoned, about a head taller than that. And something over twelve feet long, from his nose to where his tail started.

He hadn't been, in 2003. He'd been big then, but not this big. He'd grown. It had kept growing, down in the dark, all this time, on its own.

"Huh," said Harry.

It came out of a wolf's mouth. Low and rough, with a growl running under it like a bass note, but the word was clean. He'd worked on that. It had taken him most of 2004 and a lot of swearing at his own reflection in a mountain lake in Montana, and some help from a charm he'd built himself to shape the air where a wolf's mouth couldn't, and in the end he'd got it so it sounded more or less like him, English vowels and all, which didn't belong in a mouth with teeth like these.

Gary was looking at him. As far as a slug could look.

"Don't," said Harry.

Gary didn't.

Harry turned to the west, where the line ran, and put his head down, and went.

---

He didn't take the trails. There weren't any worth the name, and he didn't want them. He went down off the bench and through the spruces and down the long slope to the Calawah, and he went into the forest the way the forest let him, between the big trunks and over the deadfall and under the hanging moss, and he didn't make a sound.

That was the part he'd liked best, in 2003, when he'd first found it. He'd been very bad at being quiet, as a boy. He'd spent seven years at school getting caught in corridors and tripping on trick steps and dropping things. The wolf didn't drop things. It put each paw down where the last had been, and it rolled its weight on, slow, toe first, the way a cat does, and the moss didn't squelch and the twigs didn't snap and the wet leaves didn't so much as whisper. He'd worked out later that some of it was magic, a little nudge of force under each pad that spread the weight, the way you'd spread your weight on ice, that he did without thinking about it. Most of it was the wolf. It had known how to be quiet long before he'd come along.

He followed the Calawah west through the dark. It was loud with rain, and he ran along the bank of it, flat out, with the trees streaming past on either side and the line in his chest pulling him on like a rope on a sledge. Past the broken-heart boulder. Past the place where the north fork met the south. Out of the big timber and into the second-growth, and the logging roads, and a power line cut, and a gravel pit with a yellow bulldozer parked in it with a tarpaulin over its cab.

At the edge of Forks he crossed the highway. It was one in the morning and nobody was on it but a log truck coming down from Port Angeles with its lights on full, and he waited in the ditch for it to pass, and crossed behind it in two bounds, and was in the trees on the far side before the driver could have looked in his mirror. He went round the town to the south, through the fields at the back of the high school and along the edge of the airfield, and picked the river up again where the Calawah ran into the Bogachiel west of town.

From there it was the Quillayute, all the way to the sea.

He ran it. It was the best run he'd had in four years. Maybe the best ever. The river went wide and flat and brown in the dark, and the land on either side of it went low and boggy, all sedge and alder and black pools, and he went across it like it was a road. He could feel the line getting shorter. Fifteen miles. Ten. He could feel her at the end of it getting bigger, the way a town gets bigger when you drive toward it at night, and she was lying on her back in a bed under a roof with her eyes open, and he didn't think she'd felt him yet. He thought she would soon.

About six miles from the coast he crossed a line.

He knew it the second he crossed it. A smell. A border, marked out on the ground, along a ridge and a creek and back, the way any wolves marked out what was theirs: a dozen of them, at least, he thought, their scent laid down over and over, years of it, rust and musk and something old and wild and salt-edged underneath that he recognised from the ridge that morning. From her. From the boy. The same blood. And he knew where he was. He'd seen the treaty line on the map in his head for two months, a dotted line round the reservation, and here it was under his paws, drawn in wolf.

The other pack. The one she'd run from. The one she'd been part of, before.

He could have sunk his scent. He'd done it for two months. He had the lock for it at the bottom of himself still, unbraided now but still there, and it would have taken him half a second to pick it up and put it back on and go through their country like a ghost, the way he went through everybody's country.

He didn't.

He thought about it, running, for about a quarter of a mile. And then he thought, very plainly, the way the wolf thought things: *I'm not sneaking up on her.*

He'd been hiding on this peninsula for sixty-six days. He'd been hiding for eight years before that, from the papers and the Ministry and everybody who'd ever known his face. He'd built a whole man out of a passport and a library card and a publisher in Bristol, and walked around inside him. He was good at it. It was his job.

He was going to her house. In the middle of the night. He'd go in the front way, in his own skin, smelling like himself, and if somebody from the other pack wanted to come and have a word about it in the morning, they could come and have a word.

He went on toward the sea, and left his scent on everything.

---

The village was dark. Little houses scattered along a couple of roads at the mouth of the river, under the trees, with the sea right there beyond them, loud, the big Pacific swell coming in under the rain and breaking white on the beach in the dark. He came into it from the east through a stand of alders and along the back of a row of yards, and the line in his chest was so short now that it was hardly a line at all. It was a pull, right there. Right ahead. Like walking into the light from a fire.

He stopped at the edge of the trees behind a small two-storey house with a yard and a woodpile and a washing line with nothing on it.

There was a window under the eaves at the back, upstairs. It was open an inch at the bottom. There was no light behind it.

He walked out into the middle of the yard, through the wet grass, without a sound, and sat down.

He sat the way a dog sits, on his haunches, with his front paws together and his tail curled round them. The rain came down on him. It beaded on the black of his coat and ran off. He sat in the dark in the middle of her mother's backyard, a long way from where he was supposed to be, with nothing in his head at all except the window, and looked up at it.

He didn't call her. He didn't need to.

### IX. Leah

*La Push — 1:21 a.m.*

She'd felt him get up.

She'd been lying on her back at ten to one with her eyes open, the way she'd been lying for three hours, and she'd felt him sit up in his narrow bed twenty miles off and swing his legs over the edge of it, and she'd thought *bathroom* and nearly laughed again. Then she'd felt him walk down the hall, and out, and the cold of the slate under his bare feet, and the wet moss, and the rain on his arms, and she'd stopped thinking *bathroom*.

She'd felt him stand still in the rain.

And then she'd felt the locks.

She'd had them in her head since the ridge. She'd felt them there all day at the bottom of the rope: three of them, heavy and cold and iron-tight, wrapped round the big dark thing in his cellar like chains round a crate. She'd felt them strain at the ridge, and groan at the tent. She'd stopped noticing them, mostly, the way you stop noticing a refrigerator.

She felt the first one go.

It went the way a knot goes when somebody patient sits down and picks at it, strand by strand. She lay in bed and felt it come apart at the bottom of him, twenty miles off, and felt something cold go out of him with it, and felt the big dark thing on the other side stand up. All the way up. She felt the size of it. She'd felt it on the ridge, and in the yard by the tent, but it had always been behind something then, iron and cold, like a bear in a cage.

There wasn't anything in front of it now. Nothing but one more lock and then another.

The second went. Faster.

Under her ribs, her wolf came up off the floor so hard she sat up in bed.

She didn't mean to. Her wolf did it. She was sitting bolt upright under the slope of the roof with the blanket in her fists and her heart going like she'd been running, and every hair on her arms was standing on end, and down in the middle of her the gray wolf was on its feet and its ears were up and it was staring east, down the rope, so hard she could feel its eyes.

The third lock didn't go. It opened. She felt the difference. It just swung open, like a gate, from the other side.

And then she felt him change.

She'd felt Seth phase a thousand times. She'd felt Jacob, and Sam, and Paul, and all of them, in the pack mind: the heat, the tearing, the bang of it, like a door blown off its hinges in a gale. This was slow. She felt it go down him like a wave rolling down a beach, from the back of his head to the end of him, and felt everything in him go with it, smooth and long and easy, like a man walking through a door he'd walked through a hundred times. She felt his weight come down onto four feet in the wet moss.

And then, at the end of the rope, there was a wolf.

She sat in her bed and felt it look round. Felt it breathe in. Felt it look at a tree and work something out, carefully, the way he worked things out, and felt it say a word out loud to the slug, and felt the word come out of a mouth that wasn't a human mouth at all.

Then it turned west and came toward her.

She didn't move for the whole of it. She sat in bed with the blanket in her fists and felt him come. She felt him go down the long slope to the river in the dark and run along the bank, and she felt him run the way she ran when she was really running, and then faster than that. She felt the ground go past under him. She felt the trees. She felt a highway and a truck and a ditch. She felt fields, and the river again, wide and flat now, and she knew where he was, she knew it like she knew the inside of her own mouth, the Quillayute, the bottomland, the alders. She felt him cross the treaty line six miles out and stop thinking about it.

She felt the rope get shorter and shorter and shorter.

She felt him come into the village from the east through the Ateara alders, and along the backs of the yards, and stop at the edge of the trees behind her mom's house.

She felt him sit down.

Leah Clearwater got out of bed and went to the window.

---

The yard was dark. The porch light at the back of the Ateara place two doors down was still on, the way it always was, so Mrs. Ateara could see the bins, and it threw a little yellow light along the fence at the side and across a corner of the grass, and the rest was black and wet and silver with rain.

He was sitting in the middle of the yard.

She saw his eyes first.

Two green eyes, up in the dark, catching the light from the Ateara porch. Not low down, where a dog's eyes would be. Up. Way up. Not far below the sill of her window, which was over the kitchen, a story and a half off the ground. Two green eyes in the dark, looking straight up at her. Not blinking.

Then the rest of him came out of the black around them, the way your eyes adjust in a dark room. A shape. A huge shape. Sitting on its haunches in the middle of her mother's lawn with its front paws together and its tail curled round them, like a dog waiting outside a store. The rain coming down on it. Black. Blacker than the night. So black that the only way she could see where it stopped and the yard started was by the rain, where it landed on him and beaded and ran off, a silvery glitter all over the shape of him in the porch light, like frost.

She'd seen Jacob sitting like that. Outside the Cullens' house, under the kid's window, a hundred times. She knew exactly how big Jacob was sitting down.

This was bigger.

He'd been looking at her the whole time. He'd known exactly when she came to the glass. He didn't move. He just sat there in the rain and looked up at her with those green eyes, the bottle-in-the-sun green, the same green that had come up out of a pair of dark glasses on the ridge that morning and knocked the whole world out from under her.

Down in the middle of her, the gray wolf whined.

She'd never heard it make that sound. Not once. She'd heard it snarl and howl and scream and growl and pant, and once, the night her dad died, cry. She'd never heard it whine. It was a small, high, eager sound, like a puppy at a door, and it went right through her, and she put her hand flat on the glass.

Out in the yard, the black wolf's ears went up.

---

She didn't remember the stairs.

She remembered her mother's door at the top of them, and the line of light under it, which was strange, because it was half past one in the morning and her mother had gone to bed two hours ago. She remembered thinking *she's awake* and not stopping. She remembered the kitchen, dark, and her shorts and tank top still damp over the back of the chair by the stove, and grabbing them, and the back door, and the cold.

Then she was on the back step in her T-shirt and her bare feet with the rain coming down on her head, and he was right there.

Twenty feet away. Sitting in the grass. She had to tip her head back to look at his face.

Up close he was something else. Up close she could see how he was built, and he was built differently from them. Jacob and Sam and Paul were big the way bears were big: wide and heavy and muscled, all chest and shoulders. He was longer. Leaner through the middle, and deeper through the chest, with long legs and big heavy paws and a long head, like the wolves in the old pictures at the tribal center, the ones from before anybody had cameras. Black from his nose to his tail. The fur on his ruff was long and thick and heavy with rain. He was breathing slowly. She could see his breath go up in the porch light, white.

He smelled like the ridge. Like the tent, under the eggs and the gun oil. Like pine, and wet turned earth, and the inside of a cave. And like wolf. Strong now, where on the ridge it had been the ghost of a smell, the place in the woods where something big had slept last night and gone. It came off him across the yard as thick as smoke, wild and old and strong, and her wolf breathed it in and shook.

She stood on the step and stared at him.

He looked at her for a long second. Then he tipped his head, very slightly, to one side.

"Couldn't sleep," he said.

Leah nearly fell off the step.

It was his voice. It was absolutely his voice. Low and dry and English, with gravel in it. It came out of the wolf's mouth, out of that long black muzzle full of teeth, rougher than it was in a man's throat, with a growl running underneath the words like an engine idling. But the words were clear. Every one.

"You can *talk*?" she said.

"Can't you?"

"*No!*"

"Huh." The black wolf seemed to think about that. "Learn something new every day."

"How are you—how is that even—"

"Practice. Took about a year. Bit of help." He shifted his weight, and the grass under his front paws didn't make a sound. "Your lot don't?"

"We think at each other. In our heads. When we're phased."

"Well, you'll have to think at me, then," said Harry. "I'll hear you. I've been hearing you all night. You kicked your blanket off at a quarter to eleven."

She went hot to the roots of her hair. "So did you."

"I didn't have a blanket on."

"You know what I mean."

"Yeah," he said. "I do."

He didn't say anything else. He sat there in the rain and looked at her. And the rope between them, twenty feet of it now, nothing at all, hummed like a wire in a high wind.

"What are you doing here?" Leah said.

She knew. She just wanted to hear what he'd say. She wanted to hear him have to say it, when he couldn't lie.

He thought about it. She watched him think about it, the big black head tipping a little the other way.

"Ten to one," he said, "I was lying on my back in bed trying the Department's sleep drills, which have worked for me every night for five years. They didn't work. Every time I got halfway down you turned over and pulled me back up. And the bit of me that was in the cellar—" he lifted his muzzle, slightly, at himself, "—got up and sat down in front of the door and looked at me. And I thought, sod it."

"Sod it," Leah repeated.

"Sod it," Harry agreed. "So I came over. In my pants, as it happens. I'll be in my pants when I change back, so don't be surprised." He looked at her. "I didn't come to make you do anything, Leah. I didn't have a plan. I'd quite like to go for a run, if you'd fancy one. Or I'll sit here till you go back to bed and then I'll go home. Either. I just wanted to be where you were."

Down in her, the gray wolf was pulling at her so hard it hurt.

Leah looked at him. At the green eyes in the dark, and the rain running off his black ruff, and the big front paws in her mother's wet grass. She thought about everything she'd said in the Cullens' kitchen. *He's also a wolf. So he gets it.*

She hadn't known what she was talking about. She got it now.

She put her shorts and her tank top down on the back step. She didn't bother tying them. She'd come back for them.

"Turn around," she said.

He turned his head away. All the way, his whole long muzzle pointing at the woodpile. She could see the line of his throat. His ears stayed turned back toward her, both of them, the way a dog's do when it's pretending not to listen.

"Your *ears*," Leah said.

"I can't do anything about the ears."

She pulled her T-shirt off over her head and dropped it on the step, and came down off it into the wet grass, and let go.

---

Her wolf went straight to him.

She didn't decide it. She'd thought she might. She'd thought, coming down off the step, that she'd phase and stand there for a second and look at him, the way you would, and figure out what to do. Her wolf didn't stand there. It came out of her in the rain with a sound like a sheet tearing and hit the ground running, all four feet, and covered the twenty feet of wet grass in two bounds, and shoved its face into the side of his neck.

She was all the way in it. She was in it the way she'd never been, not once since the spring, no seam, no line between her and the gray thing under her ribs, and she felt the gray thing shove its face into his ruff and breathe in, a great long shuddering breath, pine and earth and stone and wolf, wolf, *wolf*, and she breathed in with it, and she couldn't tell where it stopped and she started, and she didn't care.

He'd turned his head back. He lowered it. He put his long black muzzle down along the side of her face, along her jaw, and pressed, slow and heavy, the way you'd lean on somebody you'd been waiting to lean on for a long time, and she felt him breathe her in too. She felt him do it through the rope. She felt the big dark thing at the bottom of him put its nose to hers, down at the bottom of both of them, where the rope was tied off round her wolf and his, and *know* her.

It made a sound. In his chest, through his ribs, against her face. Low and deep and long. She didn't have a word for it. It went through her bones.

Her wolf nipped his ear.

She didn't know why. It just did it. It took the edge of his big black ear in its front teeth, hard enough to sting, the way she used to nip Seth when he was little and wouldn't stop following her, and let go. He jerked his head back and looked at her. His green eyes had gone wide.

Then he huffed. A short sharp blast of breath out of his nose, right in her face. It was a laugh. She knew it was a laugh. She'd never heard a wolf laugh before and she knew it the second she heard it.

And then he put his head down and shoved her.

Not hard. He just put the flat of his big head against her shoulder and leaned, and she went sideways in the wet grass, and caught herself, and spun round and went for him, and he was already moving. He went down on his front legs with his back end up in the air and his tail going, the stupid play-bow that every dog on the reservation did, every dog she'd ever seen, and he was the size of a pickup truck doing it, and she was so surprised she stopped dead.

He jumped sideways. Silent. All that weight, and he landed in the grass like a cat.

She went for him.

She didn't know how long they did it. A minute. Five. She chased him round her mother's backyard in the rain and he let her, and then he didn't, he turned and went for her and she twisted out from under him and he went past, and she spun and got her teeth in the ruff of his neck and he rolled, the whole black length of him, right over on his back in the wet grass with his big paws up in the air, and she stood over him with her teeth in his fur, growling, and felt him laugh again under her, the whole huge chest of him shaking.

She let go. She stood there over him, panting. He lay on his back in the grass and looked up at her upside down, his tongue hanging out of the side of his mouth, his tail thumping the lawn.

*You're an idiot,* Leah thought at him.

"Yep," said Harry, upside down.

*You're a giant idiot.*

"Fair."

*You're the most famous wizard in the world and you're lying on your back in my mom's yard with your tongue out.*

He went still under her. Just for a second.

"Ah," he said. "Somebody told you."

*Edward. In front of everybody. Layla dropped her tea.*

"I bet she did." He rolled over, slowly, onto his side and then onto his paws, and shook himself, rain going off him in a spray. "And what did you think?"

She thought about it. She stood in the rain with her sides heaving and looked at him, and she let him see. She let him see all of it: the white living room, and Layla gone gray on the couch, *the Boy Who Lived*, *the Killing Curse*, *Riddle*, *the list*. All the faces round the room getting scared, brick by brick. And her, leaning on Esme's counter, finding it didn't do anything to her at all.

*See?* she'd said. *Better.*

He was quiet for a while.

"Right," he said finally, very low. There was a rasp at the edge of his voice that hadn't been there before. "Right. Well."

*Yeah.*

"That's the nicest thing anybody's said about it in about eight years."

*I wasn't being nice.*

"I know," said Harry. "That's why."

Something moved at the edge of her eye. She looked up.

Her mother's window. Upstairs, on the side of the house that looked down over the yard. The curtain had moved. There was a face in the gap, pale in the dark of the room, and a hand at the edge of the curtain, and something in the other hand that might have been a mug of tea.

Leah froze.

Beside her, Harry turned his head and looked up at the window too, and for a second the two of them stood there in the middle of the wet yard, a gray wolf and a black one the size of a truck, looking up at Sue Clearwater.

Sue looked back down at them. For a long, long moment.

Then she let the curtain fall closed.

*Oh my God,* Leah thought.

"Your mum," said Harry.

*Oh my* God*.*

"She didn't look upset."

*You don't know her. That was her coffee face. That was her coffee face but with tea.*

"Is that bad?"

*I don't KNOW, it's never been tea before!*

And that was when the pack mind came on in her head like a stadium.

*—LEE?*

Seth. Way off to the east, phased, on the northern loop behind the Cullen place, where Jacob had put him on a double patrol after the red-eye. He'd been running the boundary in the rain with his head full of Layla and he'd felt her phase, eighteen miles off, and come straight in. And she felt him see. She felt him look out through her eyes in the rain, at her mother's backyard, at the porch light along the fence, at the huge black shape standing beside her in the grass.

*Lee, is that—whoa. Whoa. WHOA. Is that HIM? Is that—oh my God, Lee, he's HUGE.*

*Seth.*

*He's bigger than Jake! Lee! He's bigger than JAKE! Look at him! He's like a whole HEAD bigger than Jake! How is he bigger than Jake, nobody's bigger than Jake, Jake's going to—*

*Seth. Phase out.*

*—freak, he's going to totally freak, he's been so smug since he got bigger than Sam—*

*Seth. Get out of my head. Right now.*

*Oh.* Seth stopped. She felt him look again. Felt him take in where she was, and who she was standing next to, and that it was half past one in the morning, and that her ear still had a little bit of black fur stuck in her teeth. She felt him put it together. *Oh. OH. Oh, God. Okay. Okay! I'm going. I didn't see anything. I'm going to tell Jake I didn't see anything. Have fun! Not too—oh my God, I'm going—*

He was gone.

She stood in the rain with her ears flat back against her skull.

Harry was looking at her. His head was on one side.

"I heard some of that," he said. "Through you. Not all of it. The boy?"

*He's going to tell Jake.*

"He said he wasn't."

*He can't not. It's in his head now. Next time they're both phased, Jake's going to see all of it. You. Me. This.* She looked at him. *The head thing.*

"What head thing?"

*You're a head taller than Jake. Seth says.*

"Oh. Yeah." He looked faintly pleased with himself. "I'd guessed about that."

*Don't look pleased.*

"I'm not."

*You are, I can feel it, you're so pleased right now.*

"Bit," Harry admitted. Then he looked off toward the sea, toward the sound of the surf behind the houses. "Run?"

She looked at him. At her mother's window, dark now behind the curtain. At her shorts and her T-shirt in a wet heap on the back step.

*Yeah,* she thought. *Run.*

---

They went down to the beach.

First Beach, at the mouth of the river, a mile of gray sand and black stones and driftwood logs piled up at the top of it like matchsticks, bleached white, as big around as cars. She'd run it every night since the spring. She'd run it alone. The sea was big tonight, a long heavy swell coming in under the rain out of the dark and standing up white and falling over with a sound like thunder, and the sea stacks stood up out of the water to the south like the ruins of a castle, black against black.

They ran it together.

She went down the sand at the edge of the surf flat out, the way she ran when there was nobody to see, and he came with her. She'd thought he wouldn't be able to keep up. She was the fastest. She'd always been the fastest. He kept up. He ran beside her on the seaward side, so close their shoulders almost touched, black and gray, with the white water coming up the sand around their feet and the spray coming off the top of the waves, and he didn't make a sound. She could hear her own feet on the wet sand, thud, thud, thud, and the sea, and her own breath. She couldn't hear his paws at all. It was like running next to a shadow.

At the south end of the beach they went up over the rocks and onto the headland, through the dripping salal and the wind-bent spruces, along the trail she'd run a hundred times, and down the other side onto Second Beach. Smaller. Wilder. Nobody's houses, nobody's lights. Just the sand and the sea and the stacks standing out in the surf, the big one, the one the old men called the Crying Lady, black and tall with the waves going white round her feet.

And at the south end of Second Beach, a cliff.

She'd never been up there. She'd seen it from the beach every time she ran down this far, a headland of dark rock streaked with green, maybe a hundred feet high, with trees hanging off the top of it at angles and the sea working away at the bottom. There was a dark place in the face of it, about two-thirds of the way up, well above where the tide reached. A cave. She'd always thought it was too high to get to.

Harry stopped at the foot of the cliff and looked up at it.

"That'll do," he said.

*It's a hundred feet.*

"About eighty, I'd say."

*We can't get up there.*

He looked at her. Then he went up the rock.

She didn't know what to call what he did. He went at the cliff from a standing start, and jumped, a long smooth jump with no sound at the end of it, and landed on a ledge twenty feet up as easy as stepping off a curb. Then another, further up and to the side. Then another. Each one landed light as a cat, and she watched his body twist a little in the air each time, his tail swinging, to put his paws exactly where he wanted them. He was standing at the mouth of the cave in about five seconds. He looked down at her.

"Coming?"

*I'm not a cat.*

"I'll catch you."

*You'll what?*

"Go on. Jump at the first ledge. I'll do the rest."

She looked at the first ledge. Twenty feet. Wet rock. She'd jumped twenty feet before, on the ridge, at a vampire.

She jumped.

And halfway up, something took hold of her. Gently. All over at once, like being lifted in a big hand that she couldn't see. The same thing that had pinned her flat to the granite that morning, she realized, the same thing, except now it was holding her up. It carried her the rest of the way, up the face of the cliff, past the ledges, and set her down at the mouth of the cave as softly as you'd set down a sleeping kid, and let go.

She stood there on the rock with all four legs shaking.

*You could've warned me.*

"I did warn you. I said I'd catch you."

*You said* catch*!*

"Well, I caught you."

She looked at him. He was standing at the mouth of the cave with the rain on his back, looking very pleased with himself again.

*You're such a—*

"Yeah," said Harry. "Come and look at this."

---

The cave went back about thirty feet into the rock and was dry. That was the first thing. Dry and sandy-floored, with a low arched roof that came down at the back, and it smelled of salt and stone and old seaweed and, faintly, very long ago, of something that had denned here once and gone. A sea lion, maybe. Something big. The rain came down past the mouth of it in a curtain and didn't come in.

And from the mouth of it you could see everything.

The whole of Second Beach below them, pale in the dark. The sea stacks out in the surf, the Crying Lady and the others, black, with the white water going round and round them. The swell coming in out of the west in long, slow, heavy lines, standing up and falling, standing up and falling. And past all of it, nothing. The sea going out and out into the rain and the dark, all the way to where it stopped being anything you could see, and the sound of it coming up the cliff like breathing.

She stood at the mouth of the cave beside him and looked at it for a long time.

She'd lived next to this sea her whole life. She'd never seen it like this.

"Good, isn't it," said Harry.

*Yeah.*

"I found it in October. I was walking the coast. Laying stones." He was looking out at the water. "I used to come and sit up here sometimes, in the evenings. When I couldn't stand the tent."

*You never came on our side of the line.*

"I did. Twice. You never smelt me." He was quiet for a second. "I had the second lock on then."

She looked at him sideways.

*Is that why you brought me here?*

"I brought you here because I didn't want to go back to the tent and I didn't want to go back to your mum's yard," he said. "And because I thought you'd like it. And because it's the only place I know on this whole coast that nobody else does." He paused. "And because I'm knackered, if I'm honest, and I can't lie to you, so I am. I've been up since half five. I've caught a vampire and duelled a witch and been bound for life to a woman I'd never met and rung my boss twice and faked a Thunderbird. I'd like to lie down."

*You faked a* what*?*

"Tell you tomorrow."

He turned round once in the mouth of the cave, the way a dog turns in a basket, and lay down on the sand, and looked at her.

She didn't think about it. Her wolf didn't let her. She went over and lay down too, against him, along the whole long length of him, her back to his chest, the way she used to lie with Seth when they were very small and the power went out in a storm and the house got cold, and she felt him curl round her. He put his big head down over her neck, heavy. He put his tail across her hind legs. He put one of his front paws over her front paws, the whole huge black weight of it, and left it there.

She'd never been held like this. Not by anybody. Not as a wolf. Not ever. In the pack you didn't touch, much; you were too busy hearing each other. Sam had never held her like this. Sam had held her in a truck, and on a beach, and at the end of a dirt road, and it had been good, but it had been two people holding each other, and this was something else, and she didn't have a word for it either.

Down at the bottom of both of them, at the two ends of the rope, the two wolves lay down together in the dark. She felt them do it. She felt his big black thing curl round her gray one, the same way he was curled round her, nose to tail, and her gray one put its head down on his black one's paws with a long, shaking sigh, like something putting down a weight it had carried so long it had forgotten it had arms.

She hadn't known how tired she was.

She'd been tired for two years, since Sam. Worse since the night in the yard. She'd been running so fast for so long that she'd forgotten what it was like to stop, and she lay on the sand in a cave above the sea with a wolf the size of a truck wrapped round her and the rain coming down outside and the waves coming in, standing up and falling, standing up and falling, and her eyes started to close.

*Harry.*

"Mm."

*What did your boss say?*

He didn't answer right away. She felt him think about it. She felt the shape of it in him, through the rope, and there was something in the shape that she hadn't felt from him before. It was quiet and settled. Like a man who's put his bag down by the door, in a house he's going to live in.

"He said I'm staying," said Harry. "He made me the La Push liaison."

*The what?*

"La Push liaison. The Department's liaison to the Quileute. Official. He made it up on the spot."

*That's not a real job.*

"It is now. He's writing the paperwork himself."

*So you're staying.*

"Looks like it," said Harry. "A good long while, he said."

She didn't say anything. She couldn't think of anything. She lay there with her eyes closed and his paw on hers and listened to the sea.

"Go to sleep, Leah," he said, very low, into the fur at the back of her neck.

*You first,* she thought.

He didn't answer. A minute later she felt his breathing change against her back. Slow and deep and heavy. The newspaper-reading heart, the slow double knock, going slower. She felt him go down. Down past the dark room and the grey place and further, much further, all the way to the bottom, where there wasn't anything at all. Like a stone sinking into deep water.

Nobody pulled him back up this time.

She lay there for a while longer, listening to him sleep. Listening to the sea.

Then she went down after him.

## Part Three: Kashmir

### X. Leah

*The cave above Second Beach — Tuesday, November 21st, 7:14 a.m.*

She woke up because the sea had changed its mind.

That was the first thing she knew. All night the waves had been coming in standing up and falling over, standing up and falling over, heavy and slow, and somewhere toward morning the swell had dropped and the sound had gone softer and longer, a hiss and a drag across the sand instead of a boom. Her ears noticed before the rest of her did. Then her nose. Salt and wet rock and kelp, and gulls, a lot of gulls, and the cold gray smell of fog coming in off the water.

And pine. And earth. And stone. And wolf, all round her, all the way round, so close and so thick it was like waking up inside a coat.

She opened her eyes.

It was light. Gray light, the flat gray of a Washington morning before the sun has made up its mind, coming in through the mouth of the cave. The rain had stopped. The fog had come in instead and was lying on the sea in long white banks, and the stacks stood up out of it with their heads in the clear air and their feet hidden, like old men standing in a bath. The Crying Lady was right there, half a mile out, black and tall, with the gulls going round her.

She was lying exactly where she'd lain down. Her back against his chest. His big black head over her neck. His paw over hers.

She didn't move.

She lay there and did what she always did when she woke up, which was check. She'd done it every morning since the spring. You woke up and before you opened your eyes you went looking for the bad thing, the Sam thing, the car alarm, to see how loud it was going to be today. Some mornings it was quiet and some mornings it was so loud she had to get up and run before she could think.

She went looking for it.

It wasn't there. She knew it wouldn't be. She went looking anyway, all the way to the back, the way you'd check every room in a house after a break-in. Nothing. Just the sea, and the gulls, and the rope, which was so short now that it was hardly a rope at all, running out of the middle of her chest about eighteen inches into the middle of his. Humming.

She'd slept. That was the next thing. She'd slept all night, five and a half hours, maybe six, without moving, without waking up, without dreaming. She hadn't done that since March. She'd forgotten you could.

"Morning," said Harry, above her.

His voice came through his chest into her back. She felt it more than heard it.

*How long have you been awake?*

"About twenty minutes."

*Why didn't you move?*

"You were asleep."

She didn't have anything to say to that. She lay there for another minute and watched the fog. He didn't move either. She could feel him looking at the sea, over her head, and she could feel what he felt looking at it, through the rope, and it was very quiet in there. Quieter than she'd ever felt him. Like a room after everybody's gone home.

*You slept too,* she thought.

"Yeah."

*Like, actually slept.*

"First time in a while." He shifted his head, slightly, so his jaw was resting on the top of hers instead of her neck. "Six hours straight. I don't think I've done six hours straight since about 1997."

*Nineteen ninety-seven?*

"Long story."

*You keep saying that.*

"I've got a lot of long stories, love. You'll get them all. Not before breakfast."

Down at the bottom of them, at the two ends of the rope, the two wolves were still lying curled together in the dark, the big black one wrapped round the gray, nose to tail. Neither of them looked like they had any plans to get up. Hers had its head on his paws. She could feel it, perfectly content, like a cat in a patch of sun.

*Harry.*

"Mm."

*What are you?*

He didn't answer straight away. She felt him not answer. She felt him think about it, really think about it, turning it over the way he turned things over, and she felt the place in him where the answer was. She could almost see it. A door at the back of something, a long way back, shut. Not locked. Just shut.

"I can't lie to you," he said finally. "You know that."

*I know.*

"So I'm not going to. I'm going to tell you *not yet.*" He was quiet for a second. "That's allowed. I worked that out last night, lying in bed. I can't tell you something false. I can still tell you I'm not ready to tell you something true. You can do it to me, too."

*I know. I did it last night. When you asked what Sam was like.*

"Yeah," he said. "I noticed."

She thought about it. She thought about all the things in her own head she hadn't let him near yet. Not hidden, exactly. You couldn't hide anything, not like this. Just not handed over. The night in the yard. The ambulance. Her dad's face. She'd felt him come up to the edge of them last night, once or twice, in the tent, and stop, and go round, like a man walking round a hole in the road.

*Okay,* Leah thought. *Not yet.*

"Thank you."

*But you have to tell me something. One thing.*

He was quiet again.

"It came back with me," he said. "From somewhere I went once. Somewhere you don't come back from, usually. I came back and it came with me. It took a few years to wake up properly." His jaw shifted on the top of her head. "That's what I've got this morning. That's as far as I can go before coffee."

She lay there with that for a while.

*Did it hurt?* she said. *Where you went?*

"No," said Harry. "Not where I went. Getting there hurt."

She didn't ask anything else. She didn't need to. She'd got what she'd asked for, and she could feel, through the rope, what it had cost him to give her even that much. She lay there under his jaw with her wolf curled up in his and watched the fog go out to sea.

Then her stomach growled.

It was a big growl. It went right through both of them. She felt him feel it through his chest against her back, and she felt him start to laugh, and she flattened her ears.

*Don't.*

"I didn't say anything."

*You were going to.*

"I was going to say I'm starving too," said Harry. "I was going to say I could eat a horse. I was going to say, possibly, that I could eat that sea lion that used to live here, if it came back."

*There's a diner in Forks that opens at six.*

"I know. I've been going every day for two months. Doreen's saving me pie." He lifted his head off hers, finally, and the cold came in where it had been. "But I was thinking, actually, your mum's."

Leah went still.

*My mom's.*

"She saw us," said Harry. "Last night. At the window. With her tea." He got up, carefully, one end at a time, the way a big dog gets up off a rug so it doesn't step on anybody. "I'd quite like to knock on the front door before she has to come looking for me."

---

They changed in the mouth of the cave. He went first. He walked to the back where the roof came down, and turned his back to her, and she felt him do it, the long slow roll back the other way, like a wave going out. She didn't watch. She watched the gulls.

"You can look," he said.

She looked.

He was standing at the back of the cave in a pair of navy blue boxer shorts and a gray T-shirt with a hole in the hem.

That was all. Bare feet on the sand. No coat, no suit, no gun, no dark glasses. His hair was flat on one side where he'd been lying on it. The T-shirt and the boxers were bone dry and creased exactly the way they'd have been creased when he got out of bed at ten to one in the morning, as if he'd been wearing them the whole time, which she supposed he had. There was a crease in the T-shirt across his stomach from where he'd lain down on the blanket.

Leah's wolf made a noise she'd never heard it make. It was a kind of snort.

"Go on," said Harry. "Get it out."

She couldn't stop it. It came up out of her chest and out of her nose in a great wheezing honk, and she put her head down between her front paws on the sand and laughed until her sides hurt, a wolf laughing, which she hadn't known wolves could do either, and he stood there in his underwear at the back of the cave with his arms folded and let her.

*You came to my house,* she managed. *At one in the morning. In your* underwear*.*

"I told you I would be."

*You told me* after*.*

"I didn't plan it. I don't plan anything with you, apparently." He looked down at himself. "It comes back the way it went. Whatever I've got on when I change, I get back when I change back. Clothes, glasses, the lot. If I'd had the gun on I'd have the gun." He shrugged. "Took me about a year to sort out. First few times I came back in nothing at all, on a mountain in Montana, in March."

*That's not fair.*

"What isn't?"

*I tie my shorts to my leg. With a cord. Every single time. Seth goes through nine pairs a week. Fifteen in a bad one. He had to buy them by the armload at Newton's, that's how he met Layla. And you just—*

She stopped. He'd got a look on his face. She knew that look. She'd only known him eighteen hours and she already knew that look, because she'd felt it at the other end of the rope in the round room full of jars, and it was the look of a man who has just seen something interesting.

"Huh," Harry said.

*What?*

"That's a solvable problem."

*What is?*

"Yours. The shorts." He was looking at her now the way he'd looked at the Disjunction on his fingers. "Mine's a spell, sort of. A bit I built into the change. Yours isn't a spell, yours is—whatever you are. The old thing. But clothes are clothes. You'd want something on the fabric itself, I'd think, something that knows when you're changing and goes with you and comes back." He was frowning now, a working frown. "The witch did something like it with the bead. On the cord. It stretched when he changed. That's the right road. She just didn't go far enough down it."

*You can do that?*

"Not this morning. Give me a week." He was still frowning. "Maybe two. I'll need a pair of your shorts to muck about with. And your brother's, I suppose, if he's going through nine a week. And probably whatever the big ginger one wears."

*Jake.*

"Him."

Leah looked at him standing at the back of the cave in his underwear, with his arms folded and the working frown on his face, figuring out how to stop her brother losing his shorts.

Something in her chest did something she didn't have a word for.

*Okay,* she thought. *A week.*

"Two, maybe."

*Two.* She stood up. *Turn around.*

He turned round. She felt him turn round, and she felt him not look, the doorway-with-his-eyes-shut feeling, and she felt the big black thing at the bottom of him look anyway, and she let it.

She phased.

The cold hit her bare skin like a bucket of seawater. Fog and wind and the rock under her feet, and she was standing in the mouth of a cave eighty feet up a cliff on Second Beach at a quarter past seven in the morning in November with absolutely nothing on, and her clothes were in a heap on her mother's back step a mile and a half away.

"Oh, *shit*," Leah said.

"Problem?"

"I didn't bring anything. I didn't tie anything. I left it all on the step."

"Ah."

"Don't *ah*. Do something."

"Can I turn round?"

"*No!*"

"Right." She heard him think about it. "What did you leave?"

"Shorts. Tank top. A T-shirt. They're on the step by the back door."

"Hang on," said Harry.

He didn't do anything that she could see. She was standing with her arms wrapped round herself and her back to him, shivering, and she felt him do something, through the rope, a reach, a long one, out of the cave and down the cliff and north up the beach and over the headland, like a man stretching out his arm in the dark for a light switch.

Then something came in through the mouth of the cave out of the fog and hit her in the chest.

She caught it. It was her clothes. All three, the cutoffs and the tank top and the T-shirt, in a neat square bundle. They'd come a mile and a half through the air in about four seconds. They were dry.

They were folded.

Leah stood in the mouth of the cave and looked down at them.

They were folded the way her mother folded things. Exactly the way. The T-shirt in thirds lengthways and then in half, with the sleeves tucked under. The tank top on top of it, the same. The shorts on top of that, flat, with the fly on the inside. And they were warm. Still warm. Warm the way things are warm when they've been sitting on the rail of a stove, and they smelled of the stove, and of her mother's kitchen. Coffee and fry bread and the lemon dish soap.

Her mom had come downstairs. Some time in the night, after the curtain fell, after the two of them had gone off down the beach in the dark. Her mom had come down and opened the back door and picked her daughter's wet clothes up off the step, and taken them inside, and dried them by the stove, and folded them, and put them on the kitchen table.

Waiting.

"Leah?" said Harry.

"Mom folded them," Leah said.

Her voice came out funny. She put the T-shirt on. Then the tank top, which was backward; she didn't notice. Then the shorts. She stood there in the mouth of the cave fully dressed and warm and smelling of her mother's kitchen and found she had to look at the fog for a while.

He didn't say anything. He didn't come over. He stood at the back of the cave with his back to her, still, in his boxers, and he waited, the way he'd waited in the yard by the tent for her to come out from behind the spruce, and she felt him through the rope not saying anything with tremendous care.

"Okay," she said, after a while. "You can turn round."

He turned round.

He looked at her. He looked at her T-shirt.

"Your top's on backwards," he said.

"I know."

"Right."

"Your *pants* are on, Harry."

"Fair point," said Harry, and looked down at himself, and the T-shirt went darker and heavier and rougher and turned into a flannel shirt, red and black check, the sleeves rolled to the elbow. The boxers went down to his ankles and turned into jeans. There were boots on his feet, the same big scuffed brown ones he'd worn on the ridge, and socks under them presumably, and a canvas jacket over the shirt, and none of it had been there a second ago.

He looked like a guy who worked at the lumber yard in Forks.

"That," said Leah, "is so unfair."

"I'll get you your week," said Harry. "Come on. Your mum's waiting."

### XI. Leah

*La Push — 8:02 a.m.*

He knocked on the front door.

She'd thought he was joking. She'd thought, walking up the road from the beach beside him in the fog, past the Atearas' and the Uleys' empty lot and the place where the Black kids used to keep a dead car on blocks, that when they got to her mom's house he'd follow her round the back, the way everybody did. Nobody in La Push used front doors. Front doors were for cops and Jehovah's Witnesses.

He walked up the front path and up the two wooden steps and knocked. Three times. Not loud.

Leah stood on the path behind him with her T-shirt still on backward and stared at the back of his head.

The door opened.

Her mother stood in the doorway in her bathrobe with a cup of coffee in her hand. Coffee, this time. She'd been up for a while; Leah could see that. Her hair was brushed and pinned up, and there was a pan going on the stove in the kitchen behind her, Leah could smell it, bacon, and the radio was on low, the Indian station out of Neah Bay. She looked at Harry. She looked up at Harry, because he had most of a foot on her. She looked at him for a long time. She looked at his flannel shirt and his jeans and his boots and his face.

Then she looked past him at Leah.

"Your top's on backwards," Sue said.

"I know, Mom."

Sue looked back at Harry.

"You were in my yard," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"At one in the morning."

"Yes, ma'am. About half past one. I'm sorry about the hour."

"You were a lot bigger."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Seth says you're a head taller than Jacob Black." Her mother's face didn't change. "He called me at six to say so. He wanted me to know before Jacob found out. He was very excited about it. He said it twice."

"I'd guessed about a head," said Harry. "Nice to have it confirmed."

Sue looked at him over her coffee cup with the coffee face. The full one. Leah had seen grown men from the tribal council fold under that face. She'd seen Billy Black fold under it, once, over a parking dispute at the marina in 2002, and Billy didn't fold for anybody.

Harry stood on the step and let her look at him. He didn't fidget. He didn't smile, or say anything to fill up the quiet. He just stood there in the fog with his hands at his sides and looked back at her, the way you'd stand still and let a doctor look in your ears.

"Are you hungry?" her mother said.

"I'm starving, ma'am."

"Good," said Sue. "Come in. Wipe your feet."

---

He ate eleven eggs.

Leah stopped counting at eleven. Her mother had stopped asking at about seven and just kept putting them on his plate, two at a time, out of the big black pan, with bacon and fry bread and a jar of Maeve Quinn's honey that Layla had given them on the Sunday they'd met at the marina. He ate them at the kitchen table on the chair by the window that had been her dad's chair, with his elbows off the table, saying *thank you* every single time she put more on his plate, and he didn't stop until her mother turned the heat off under the pan and said "That's the last of them."

"Thank you, Mrs. Clearwater."

"Sue."

"Sue," said Harry. "Thank you. That was the best breakfast I've had in about two months."

"You've been eating at the diner."

"Every day."

"Doreen's eggs are rubber."

"Doreen's eggs are rubber," Harry agreed. "Her pie's good, though."

"Her pie's good," Sue allowed.

She sat down across from him with her coffee. Leah was leaning on the counter by the stove with a piece of fry bread, where she'd been the whole time, because she couldn't make herself sit down at the table with the two of them. It felt like watching something on TV. She'd turned her T-shirt round the right way in the bathroom. Nobody had commented.

"So," said her mother. "You're a wizard."

"Yes."

"And a wolf."

"Sort of."

"Seth said *sort of*. Leah says *sort of*. What does it mean?"

"It means I don't know exactly what I am," said Harry. "I know what I can do. I don't know what to call it. I'm still finding out."

"Are you from here? From around here? The peninsula?"

"No. England. Surrey, originally, a little town outside London. Then a lot of other places."

"Your people are in England?"

He was quiet for a second.

"No," he said. "I haven't got people, really. My mum and dad died when I was one. I was raised by my aunt and her husband, and they didn't want me, and I left when I was seventeen and haven't been back. There's a family in England I'm close to. The Weasleys. Big ginger lot. They'd have me for Christmas if I asked." He picked up his coffee. "I mostly don't ask."

Her mother looked at him for a long time.

"How did they die?" she said. "Your mother and father."

"A man killed them. In our house. On Halloween."

Sue didn't say anything.

"It was a long time ago," said Harry. "He's dead now too."

"Did you kill him?"

Leah stopped chewing.

Harry looked across the table at her mother. He didn't look away. He didn't look at Leah, either, though she felt him feel her looking.

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

The radio played something with drums in it. Outside, a car went past slowly on the road, a truck, somebody going down to the marina. Her mother sat with her hands round her coffee cup and looked at the man in her dead husband's chair.

"Good," Sue said.

She drank her coffee.

"Leah tells me," she said, after a while, "that she can't lie to you. And you can't lie to her."

"That's right."

"Is that a wizard thing?"

"I don't think so. I think it's the other thing. The imprint." He turned his cup round on the oilcloth. "I've never heard of it doing that. Neither has she. Neither has Jacob, from what she says."

"It doesn't," said Sue. "I've been married into this family twenty-four years. I've heard every story there is. Billy's heard more. It doesn't do that." She looked at him over the rim of the cup. "So I'll ask you, and she'll know if you're lying, and so will I. Are you going to hurt my daughter?"

Leah put down her fry bread.

She felt it go through him. She felt it go all the way through him and come back, the question, and she felt him look at it from every side, the way he did. She felt him think about the ridge. About London. About a room with no name on a floor called Nine, and people in it who'd want her in a jar. About the old man with tap-water eyes who'd said *she isn't going into one*. About the thing in the cellar, and the locks, and what it could do if it ever really got out, and how little he still knew about that.

She felt him think about all of it. Honestly. The way he had to.

"Not if I can help it," said Harry. "Not ever, if I can help it. And I'd put myself between her and anything that tried."

Sue looked at Leah.

Leah looked back at her mother. She didn't have to say anything. She'd been her mother's daughter for twenty years; her mother had read every lie she'd ever told on her face before she was finished telling it. She just let her mother look.

"It's true, Mom," she said.

"I know it's true," said Sue. "I just wanted to hear him say it."

She got up and took his plate. She took it to the sink and ran water over it, and stood there at the sink with her back to both of them for a moment, looking out of the window over the sink at the backyard. At the grass. At the place in the middle of it where the grass was still flattened in a big round patch, from something very heavy sitting on it in the rain for twenty minutes, and the long scuffed tracks going away from it toward the sea where two wolves had played.

"What's your name?" her mother said, without turning round. "Seth said it on the phone. I'd like to hear you say it."

"Harry," said Harry. "Harry Potter."

Her mother's hands stopped moving in the water.

Leah had known it was coming. She'd known since the tent. She'd known since he'd stood in the rain with his jaw locked and his own name being pulled out of him like a nail, *Harry Potter*, and she'd thought *who's Harry Potter* out loud and then, a second later, something else, under that, something she'd put down very fast and walked away from and not looked at since. She'd walked around it all night. She'd walked around it all the way back to the Cullens, with Jacob and Seth in her head, and she'd felt them both walk around it too. Seth had said *his name's Harry* in the Cullens' living room like it was nothing. Like he was saying it for her, so she wouldn't have to. She'd felt Harry come up to the edge of it last night, in the cave, in her head, and stop, and go round it too, without knowing what it was. Just that it was something she wasn't ready to let him near.

Her mother stood at the sink with her hands in the water.

"Harry," Sue said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"My husband's name was Harry," said Sue Clearwater. "Leah's father. Harry Clearwater. He died in March of this year. In this house. In the front room, while she was in the yard." She turned off the tap. "I don't suppose anybody told you that."

The kitchen went very quiet.

Leah felt it land in him. She felt it go into him like a stone into a pond, and the rings go out. He hadn't known. She could feel that he hadn't. He'd been inside her head for nineteen hours and he hadn't known, because she'd put it behind a door and asked him without words not to open it, and he hadn't.

He looked at her across the kitchen. He looked at her for a long time.

"No," he said quietly. "Nobody told me."

"No," said Sue. "I didn't think so."

She dried her hands on the dish towel. She hung it back on the handle of the oven, very neatly, the way she'd folded Leah's T-shirt in the night. Then she turned round and leaned back against the sink with her arms folded across her bathrobe, and looked at him. And she smiled. It was a small one. Leah hadn't seen it since March.

"He'd have had a lot to say about you," her mother said. "Harry Clearwater. About a white man in a suit with a gun turning up in his yard at one in the morning for his daughter, the size of a truck. He'd have had a *lot* to say."

"I expect he would, ma'am."

"He'd have liked you, though. Eventually." Sue picked her coffee back up off the table. "He liked people who ate."

---

They left at twenty to ten.

Her mother walked them to the door. She stood in it in her bathrobe with her coffee, the way she'd stood when he knocked, and she looked at Harry on the step.

"Sunday," she said. "Dinner. Six o'clock. Billy will be here. He'll want to meet you."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Don't bring the gun."

"No, ma'am."

"And Harry." Sue looked past him at the road, at the fog lying in the trees, toward the village. "Paul Lahote came by the back at half past six this morning. Sam Uley's Paul. He was on patrol last night on our side. He smelled something in my yard. He wanted to know what it was."

Leah went cold. "What'd you tell him?"

"I told him," said her mother, "that I'd seen a bear." She sipped her coffee. "He didn't believe me. He's going to go and tell Sam. And Sam's going to want to know what's been walking around on his land at night, smelling like that, and leaving tracks in my lawn as big as dinner plates." She looked at Harry. "I'd think about that, if I were you."

"I've been thinking about it since I crossed the line, ma'am."

"Good," said Sue. "Then go and see your vampires."

She shut the door.

### XII. Leah

*The bottom of the Cullens' drive — 9:52 a.m.*

Apparition was worse than Layla had said.

Layla had said it was like being shoved through a garden hose. Layla, Leah decided, had been being *nice*. He'd taken her arm at the end of her mom's road, under the trees where nobody could see, and said *hold on, deep breath, don't let go*, and turned on his heel, and the world had folded up around her like a fist and squeezed. Every part of her at once. Her lungs and her ribs and her eyeballs and her teeth. She couldn't breathe. She couldn't see. She couldn't tell up from down or herself from anything else. She'd been squeezed down to the size of a pea and fired out of the barrel of a gun.

Then her feet hit gravel and the world came back all at once, and she staggered, and he caught her elbow.

She bent over with her hands on her knees and breathed.

"You're all right," said Harry. "First time's the worst. Don't be sick if you can help it. Try not to be sick on the boots."

"I'm going to be sick on your boots."

"Fair."

She wasn't sick on his boots. She stood there with her hands on her knees for about thirty seconds until the trees stopped spinning, at the bottom of the Cullens' long drive, at the place where it came off the highway and went up into the forest, and the gravel stopped tipping sideways under her feet, and she straightened up and wiped her mouth.

"Never again," she said.

"That's what everybody says."

"I mean it. I'll run. I'm faster than that anyway."

"You're not," said Harry, "but I admire the attitude."

She looked at him.

He'd changed his clothes again. Some time in the half-second they'd been folded up in a fist together, the lumber-yard flannel and the canvas jacket had gone, and he was standing on the Cullens' gravel in the gray suit. The charcoal one from the ridge, with the white shirt open at the neck and no tie. The long black wool coat over it, down past his knees. The scuffed brown boots, still. And the dark glasses on his face, black, so she couldn't see his eyes.

Under his left arm, under the coat, she could see the shape of the gun.

"Seriously?" Leah said.

"Seriously what?"

"The *suit*?"

"Nobody argues with a suit." He tugged the cuffs of his shirt down out of the coat sleeves, one and then the other. "I've walked into rooms with Aurors and goblins and an Ethiopian river god in this suit. They all did what I said. Your vampires can manage."

"They're not my vampires."

"Your brother's girlfriend's vampires, then."

"You're doing it on purpose. You're going to walk up there looking like that on purpose, to scare them."

He thought about it. She felt him think about it, honestly, because he had to.

"Bit," he admitted. "Mostly it's habit. A bit of it's that I'd like them scared enough to listen and not so scared they do something stupid, and in my experience the suit's about right for that. And some of it—" he looked up the drive, into the trees, toward the white house a mile off that neither of them could see, "—is that they've had a day to sit round and frighten themselves about me. I'd rather they had something real to look at."

She looked at him for a long moment.

Then she held up her hand. Her right hand. Pinky out.

He looked at it.

"What's that?"

"Promise me something."

"What?"

"Promise me," Leah said, "that you're not going to vaporize them."

"I wasn't planning on vaporizing anybody."

"You made a vampire scream till the jars rattled."

"That one had it coming. He ate a dental hygienist."

"You shot Layla into a log."

"I shot the *ground*."

"You held me down on a rock with my face in the moss."

"You were trying to eat me at the time," said Harry. "In fairness."

"Promise me."

He looked at her. Then he looked at her pinky finger, held up between them in the gray air at the bottom of the drive.

"All right," he said. "I promise I won't vaporize the entire coven."

She narrowed her eyes. "The *entire*?"

"I'm leaving myself a bit of room."

"*Harry.*"

"Fine." He sighed. "None of them. I promise I won't vaporize any of them. Not one. Not even the blonde."

"Rosalie."

"Not even Rosalie," said Harry. "Though from what I took out of your brother's head, she'd be first on my list if I had one."

"Pinky promise."

He looked at her finger again. He looked, she thought, a little bit like a man who'd been handed a baby.

"Where I'm from," he said, "there's a version of this where if you break it you drop dead. On the spot. It's called an Unbreakable Vow. You need a witness and a wand and you have to mean it."

"Then do that one."

"No," said Harry.

"Why not?"

"Because I might have to break it," he said. "And then I'd be dead, and you'd be stuck with a rope tied to a corpse, and I don't think either of us fancies that." He held up his own right hand. "So you'll have to make do with this one."

He hooked his little finger through hers.

His hand was hot. Hotter than hers, almost. She'd thought, last night, standing in the yard by the tent with his palm flat on her muzzle, that he ran as hot as they did. He ran hotter. She stood at the bottom of the Cullens' drive with her pinky locked in his, and felt it, and felt the rope between them go tight and bright and hum, and knew he couldn't break this one either. He'd said it to her, and he couldn't say a false thing to her, and he'd meant it all the way down.

"Pinky promise," said Harry. "None of them."

"Okay," said Leah.

She let go.

They walked up the drive.

### XIII. Edward

*The Cullen house — 9:58 a.m.*

He heard her first.

He'd been listening for her since seven. They all had, in their way. Alice was on the window seat with her eyes shut and her face pinched up, trying again and again to look at a morning she couldn't see. Jasper was beside her with his arms folded, listening with his whole body the way soldiers listened, for the sound of a column on a road. Rosalie was upstairs with Renesmee, because she'd said so, flatly, at six o'clock, in a voice that had ended the conversation; *if he wants her he'll have to come through me, and I'd like to see him try it on the stairs*. Emmett was sitting on the bottom step of those stairs, because Rosalie was at the top. Carlisle and Esme were by the fireplace. Bella was beside Edward at the window, standing very close, newborn-still. Seth and Layla were on the couch.

And Jacob was on the porch. He'd been on the porch since six-thirty, when he'd phased for the dawn patrol shift change and Seth had been there in the pack mind, and Jacob had seen. Edward had heard him see. He'd heard it from inside the house, three hundred yards off, through the wall, a single long wordless roar in Jacob's head like a man stubbing his toe on a cinderblock. And then a picture. A gray wolf in a backyard in La Push in the rain, at half past one in the morning, looking up at a black one.

A head taller. Seth had been very clear about it, in the pack mind, and very excited. *Like a whole head, Jake. Look. A whole head.*

Emmett had been unbearable about it since half past six.

So they'd been listening. And at eight minutes to ten Edward heard Leah's mind come into the world at the bottom of the drive, a mile off, out of nothing. No approach. No car, no footsteps on the highway, no wolf in the trees. One instant there was nothing there and the next there was Leah, standing on the gravel, bent over with her hands on her knees and her whole head going *oh God oh God oh God* like a washing machine on spin.

And beside her, nothing.

He'd been ready for nothing. He'd heard nothing all yesterday, through Jacob and Seth: the man on the ridge in the suit was a hole in every memory they had of him. A place in the middle of their thoughts where a mind should have been and wasn't. Edward had lived with Bella's silence for two years now and he'd thought he knew what it was to stand next to someone and hear nothing.

Bella's silence was a closed door. You stood outside it and knew there was a room behind it, warm and lit and full of her, and you just couldn't get in. Charlie's was a radio heard through a wall. Edward stood at the window in his own house and listened to the place beside Leah Clearwater at the bottom of the drive, and found nothing at all. It was like listening for a house and finding the hill bare.

They started up the drive. Edward listened to Leah come. He heard her stop feeling sick. He heard her think something about the suit, about *nobody argues with a suit*, about a promise and a pinky, and a feeling went through her that he didn't have the right to listen to and listened to anyway, because he couldn't not. He heard her think *none of them*.

And beside her, the bare hill.

At the half-mile, it started playing music.

Edward went still.

It came up out of the nothing beside Leah like a radio switched on in an empty room. Loud. Very clear. Drums first, heavy and slow and enormous, a great dragging stamp of a beat like something walking. Then a guitar on top of it, turning round and round on one figure, climbing and falling back, climbing and falling back. Then the strings. He knew it on the second bar. He'd owned the record since the week it came out, in February of 1975, a double album with a cover full of windows. He'd played that side until the grooves went gray.

Kashmir.

It filled the place where a mind should have been. Every corner of it. It was being *played*. On purpose. Bar by bar, note-perfect, at the exact tempo of the record, by someone who knew it so well he could lay it down in his own head like a carpet. And under it, through it, around it, nothing. Not a whisper. Not a word. Not one single thought got out from under it.

"Edward?" said Bella.

"He's playing music," Edward said. "At me."

Emmett turned round on the bottom stair. "He's what?"

"He knows I'm listening. He's been listening for me listening, and he's—" Edward stopped. He listened. The song came up the drive at walking pace, steady, the drums going *stamp, stamp, stamp*. "He's playing Led Zeppelin. Very loudly. In his own head. So that there's nothing else to hear."

There was a pause.

"Which song?" said Emmett.

"Kashmir."

Emmett thought about that.

"That's a *good* song," he said, sounding betrayed.

"It is a good song," Edward agreed. He found he was close to laughing, and that he didn't like it, and that under the laughter something very old and very cold in him had come up onto the balls of its feet. A hundred and five years. He'd never been shut out like this. He'd never once in a hundred and five years walked toward another mind and had it turn round and put a record on.

"It's the same record," said Seth, from the couch. He'd sat up. "Lee was humming it last night. In the kitchen. Remember? With the ham."

Edward remembered.

---

They came up out of the trees at the top of the drive at three minutes past ten, side by side.

Edward watched them come across the lawn through the glass. Leah, in cutoffs and a tank top and a T-shirt over it, her hair flat on one side the way it went when she'd slept on it, barefoot on the cold grass. She was walking the way he'd never seen her walk in three months of watching her, loose, easy, her arms swinging. She was not afraid of anything on this lawn.

Beside her, the man from the ridge.

Edward had seen him a hundred times since yesterday, in other people's heads, from every side. On the ridge in the rain. Walking across the crater on nothing. Crouched in the ferns. At the edge of a silver circle on a stone floor, saying a word. He'd thought he knew what to expect.

He saw a tall man. Six foot two, perhaps, broad in the shoulders, in a charcoal gray suit and a long black coat. Dark glasses. Short black hair, wet from the fog. Scuffed brown boots that didn't go with the rest of it. He walked across the wet lawn without hurrying, with his hands in the pockets of the coat, and he moved like nothing Edward had a name for. His boots didn't make a sound on the grass. He wasn't trying not to make a sound. He just didn't.

He was looking at the house. At the long glass wall. Through it.

Straight at Edward.

"He can see me," Edward said.

"How?" said Bella. "The glass is—"

"I don't know. He can see me." He could feel it. Through the dark glasses, across a hundred yards of lawn, through the morning glare on the glass. He'd been looked at by a great many things. "He knows exactly where each of us is standing."

Then the wind turned.

It came round from the north, off the trees, across the lawn and in through the inch Esme always left open in the window by the kitchen for the wolves, and it brought the man's scent with it.

Edward stopped breathing.

He didn't need to breathe. He did it out of habit, a hundred years of it, so that he wouldn't look strange to humans. He stopped. So did every other person in the room; he heard them stop, all at once, the soft little hitch of seven sets of lungs that didn't need air catching on the same breath.

Pine. Turned earth after rain. Cold stone, like the inside of a cave. Coffee. Bacon, eggs, fry bread, a great deal of all three. Honey. Leah, all over him, gray wolf and salt and rain, the way you smelled of someone after a night lying against them.

And wolf.

His body knew it before his mind caught up. The Quileute smelled, to every one of them, like wet dog left too long in a hot car, rank and sour, a smell that made your lip curl back off your teeth before you'd decided anything. This was older. It was huge and dark and wild and it smelled, if it smelled like anything, like a forest fire a very long way off, at night, when you can't see the flames yet and the wind changes and the smoke comes over the ridge and every animal in the valley lifts its head.

Every instinct in Edward's body, every single one of the instincts that had been sharpened on human blood and the hunting of large animals and a hundred years of being the most dangerous thing in any room, said the same thing at the same time.

*Run.*

He didn't. Nobody did. He heard every one of them not run. He heard Jasper's whole body go tight as wire beside the window seat and stay there. He heard Esme's hand find Carlisle's arm. He heard Emmett, on the bottom stair, think a single long word that Emmett's mother would not have liked, and then, very clearly, *okay, so the ears weren't lying*. He heard Rosalie, upstairs, at the top of the stairs, with Renesmee on her hip, smell it come up the stairwell, and her arms tighten round the child, and her lips come back.

And on the porch, he heard Jacob.

Jacob had smelled it first. He'd smelled it at the top of the drive, before it ever reached the house, and in Jacob's head there wasn't a word for it either; there was just his own wolf, the russet one, coming up out of the floor of him like a dog off a rug, with every hair on end, and in front of it, filling Jacob's whole mind, the black wolf in Seth's memory. A head taller.

Jacob stood at the top of the porch steps with his arms folded so tight across his chest the skin was white over his knuckles.

The man in the suit came across the lawn and stopped at the bottom of the steps.

He looked up at Jacob.

"Morning," he said. English. Dry. Low. "You must be Clifford."

Jacob stared at him.

"My name," Jacob said, between his teeth, "is Jacob."

"I know it is. Your mate there told me." He tipped his head a fraction at the house, at Seth, on the couch, who was staring out through the glass with his mouth open. "Clifford, though."

"Why *Clifford*?"

"The big red dog," said Seth helpfully, through the glass, loud enough to carry. "Jake. Clifford the Big Red Dog. From the books."

"I'm not *red*!"

"You're quite red, mate," said the man in the suit.

"I'm *russet*!"

"Is that what they told you?" The man came up one step. Then another. He did it slowly, and he didn't take his hands out of his pockets, and Jacob didn't move, and then he was standing on the step below Jacob with his face about level with Jacob's chin, looking up at him through the dark glasses.

Edward could hear Jacob's heart going like a hammer.

"I'm told," said the man, "that you're the one who imprinted on the baby."

Jacob's whole body went rigid.

"And I'm told," the man went on, very quietly, "that last night you wanted to call up the other pack, all of them, and stand them in those trees there and wait for me to come up the drive. So you could make sure I never got near her. Ever." He paused. "That's what I'm told."

Jacob looked at Leah.

Leah, on the grass at the bottom of the steps, with her arms folded, looked back at him.

"You told him," Jacob said.

"I told you I can't not," said Leah. "Same as you can't not tell Nessie you love her every time she puts her hand on your face."

"I didn't call them," Jacob said, to the man in the suit. "I didn't call Sam."

"I know," said the man. "I'd have smelt them on the way in." He looked at Jacob a moment longer. "That was the right call, Clifford. It'd have gone badly. Mostly for your lot. A little bit for me." He stepped up past him onto the porch. "You can come in too, if you like. I'll want you for this."

He walked through the front door into the house as if he'd been doing it for years.

Jacob stood on the top step with his mouth open.

Leah went past him up the steps and patted him twice on the arm, like a dog, and followed.

---

He stopped two steps inside the door.

Edward watched him do it. He did it the way Layla had done it, two Sundays ago: stopped, and looked round, and took the room in. Except Layla had done it the way a soldier does it, quick and nervous, counting exits. He did it the way a man does it who's walked into a great many rooms where everybody wants him dead and has stopped finding it interesting. His head didn't move much. The dark glasses went round the room in a slow arc, and Edward could feel them stop on each of them in turn, for exactly as long as it took. Carlisle and Esme by the fireplace. Bella at the window. Alice on the window seat, and Jasper beside her. Emmett on the stairs. The top of the stairs, where Rosalie was standing out of sight with the child, and the glasses stopped there for a fraction longer than anywhere else, and then moved on. Seth on the couch. Layla.

They stopped on Layla.

Layla was sitting at the end of the couch nearest the fire, in her own clothes this morning, jeans and a flannel shirt and her green rain jacket, with a mug of coffee in both hands. Her face was gray. There was a dark bruise along her jaw. Her wand was in her sleeve, held by a leather strap four inches from her palm, and Edward could hear her fingers wanting it so badly they ached. And over all of it, rising like water in a flooded room, a single thought repeating:

*Harry Potter. That's Harry Potter. I threw a Stunner at Harry Potter.*

The man looked at her. He looked at the bruise on her jaw.

"How's the back?" he said.

"Purple," said Layla.

"Murtlap?"

"I didn't have any. Carlisle gave me—something. Arnica. And a lot of ice." She swallowed. "It's fine."

"It's a week of misery, is what it is," he said. "I've got Murtlap. I'll bring some round." He reached into the inside pocket of the long coat. Every vampire in the room tensed. He took out something small and held it out to her across the room on his open palm. "Here. These are yours."

Layla looked at what was in his hand.

It was the bead. Two halves of black glass, the size of half a hazelnut each, and three pieces of a leather cord.

"I found them on the ridge last night," he said. "Thought you'd want them back. There's a lot of hours in those."

Layla didn't move for a second. Then she put her coffee down on the side table and got up, stiffly, because her back hurt, and crossed the room, and stood in front of him, and took the pieces of the bead out of his hand. She looked at them in her palm.

"You can mend it," he said. "If you want. It's cut clean down the middle. You did that well. Bit of glue and the right words and it'll work again. Though you'll want the cord stronger next time. He went through that like it was liquorice."

"Thank you," said Layla. Her voice was very small.

"You're welcome."

She went back to the couch and sat down and held the two halves of the bead in her closed fist, and didn't look at him again, and Edward listened to her mind go round and round the same three words. *Harry Potter. Murtlap. Thank you.*

Then the man in the suit took his left hand out of his coat pocket, and raised one finger, and said, without turning his head:

"Whoever's doing that, stop."

Nobody moved.

Edward knew who it was. He'd heard it start the second the man came through the door. Jasper, by the window seat, with his arms folded and his face blank, reaching. Not much. A soldier's touch, light and careful, the way he'd reach for a frightened horse. He'd laid it over the room like a blanket the moment the scent came in, calm, and slow, and *nothing to worry about here*, the way he'd done for a hundred years whenever a room was about to go wrong. And then he'd reached for the man in the suit, and leaned on him, very gently, to see.

"I said stop," said the man.

Jasper didn't stop. Edward heard him think about it. He heard him decide, very coolly, that he'd rather know what he was dealing with than not, and that if there was going to be trouble it would be better if the man in the suit was a little calmer when it started. And he leaned harder.

The man turned his head and looked at him.

That was all he did. He looked at Jasper across the room through the dark glasses.

And Jasper sat down.

He sat down on the floor. Hard. Right where he was standing, by the window seat, all at once, as if somebody had kicked his legs out from under him, and he sat there on Esme's white rug with his back against the window seat and his hands flat on the floor on either side of him and his mouth open. His face had gone completely blank. His eyes were wide.

"Jasper!" Alice was off the window seat and on her knees beside him. "Jazz, what—"

"Oh," said Jasper.

He was looking past her. At Leah. Leah had come in behind the man in the suit and was standing just inside the door with her arms folded, watching all of this with total calm. Jasper looked at her the way Edward had seen him look at a sunset once, in Texas, in 1951.

"Oh," Jasper said again, very faintly. "Oh, I see."

"See *what*?" said Emmett, from the stairs.

Edward knew. He'd heard it. He'd heard what came back down the line Jasper had reached out along, the instant the man in the suit looked at him. It hadn't been calm. The man had taken Jasper's own calm and put it down somewhere and sent back what was really in him instead, the whole thing, all at once, at full strength, the way you'd turn a hose round on the person holding it.

It had been her. All of it. What he felt about Leah Clearwater. The size of it, and the depth of it, and the shape of the rope that ran from him to her and down into the dark at both ends and tied off there. Jasper had felt the emotions of human beings and vampires for a hundred and fifty years. He'd felt the bloodlust of an army of newborns all at once on a battlefield in Mexico. He'd felt Edward, in 2005, with Bella in his arms in a hospital in Phoenix, and he'd had to leave the room. He'd never felt anything like this.

"Nothing," said Jasper. "Nothing at all, Emmett. It's—private." He shut his mouth. He looked up at the man in the suit from the floor. "I apologize. That was rude of me."

"It was," the man agreed. "Don't do it again. Next time I'll send you what I felt when the specimen screamed, and you won't like that half as much."

Jasper nodded, very slowly.

Leah, by the door, looked from Jasper to the man in the suit with her eyebrows halfway up her forehead.

"What did you just do?" she said.

"Gave him something to feel," said the man.

"Like what?"

"Like you," said the man in the suit, without turning round, and Leah's face went dark red from her collarbone to her hairline, and she shut up.

He turned his head toward the window seat. Toward Alice, still on her knees beside Jasper with her hand on his arm, looking up at the man with her small sharp face gone white.

"You're the one who sees," he said.

Alice swallowed. "Yes."

"How's that going?"

"I can't see you," Alice said. "I've been trying since yesterday. Every single time I look, there's—" She stopped. She frowned, as if she was trying to describe a color. "There's a coin. In the air. Turning over. A copper one. That's all I can see. And it never lands."

The man in the suit put his hand in his trouser pocket and took something out and held it up between his finger and his thumb, so she could see.

A penny. An old English penny, copper, worn smooth, with a crowned portcullis on one side.

"Nineteen seventy-one," he said. "First year of decimal. I've had it since I was eleven." He flicked it. It went up into the air, spinning, flashing in the gray light from the glass wall, and came down into his palm, and he closed his hand on it without looking. "It doesn't land till I want it to."

Alice stared at his closed hand.

"That's not—" She stopped. "That can't work. I see decisions. It doesn't matter how you make them, I still see them."

"You see what people decide," said the man. "I don't decide much. I let the coin do it, and I let it do it at the last second, and I don't look at how it's landed till I've already started moving. Then there's nothing for you to see till it's happened." He put the penny back in his pocket. "It's not perfect. But it's done me all right for two months. You never once saw me, did you? In two months? Not at the diner, not on the ridge above your house at half five in the morning, not in your trees."

Alice didn't answer.

"No," said the man. "I didn't think so."

He turned, finally, to the window. To Edward.

Edward had been waiting for it. He'd been waiting since the bottom of the drive. Since the music came on. He stood at the glass with Bella so close beside him he could feel her stillness against his arm, and looked at the man in the suit, and the man looked back at him through the dark glasses, and in the place where his mind should have been, the drums of Kashmir went *stamp, stamp, stamp*.

"You're the reader," the man said.

"Yes."

"You've been knocking since I was at the bottom of your drive."

"Yes," said Edward. "You've been playing Led Zeppelin since about the half-mile."

"Did you like it?"

"I own it."

"I know you do," said the man. "Original pressing, 1975. Swan Song label. You've played side two till it's gone grey." The corner of his mouth moved. "You've got a lot of Debussy in there too, haven't you. *Clair de Lune*, mostly. On a loop, round the back, when you're worried. And her—" the dark glasses moved, a quarter of an inch, toward Bella beside him, "—in a field. With flowers. In the sun. Every time you close your eyes. And a lot of guilt about it."

The room went silent.

Edward didn't move. He couldn't. He stood at the window in the gray light and felt the floor of himself drop out from under him, very quietly, all at once.

He hadn't felt anything.

That was the thing. That was the only thing. A hundred and five years of hearing every mind that came within two miles of him and he'd never once had to wonder, not for a second, whether anyone could hear his. It was the one door in the world that only opened one way. It had never occurred to him that it could open the other. He hadn't felt a knock. He hadn't felt a draft. He hadn't felt anything at all, and the man had been in, and looked round, and come out again, and was standing on Esme's rug telling him about the meadow.

"When?" Edward said.

"On your steps," said the man. "When I was talking to Clifford. About four seconds." He tipped his head. "You're fast in there. Faster than the one in my circle. Bit like a train. But you weren't watching your own door. You were too busy listening to my music."

"Harry," said Leah, from behind him. Sharply.

"He started it," said the man in the suit, mildly, without looking round.

Bella had gone so still beside him that Edward could feel it, a hard cold stillness, the stillness of a newborn about to do something. He didn't look at her. He put his hand on her arm.

"You're angry," the man in the suit said to Edward. Not unkindly. "I don't blame you. Hundred years of being the only one in the room who could do it, and some English bloke in a suit walks in and does it back. I'd be furious." He took his hands out of his pockets. "For what it's worth, I don't go in uninvited. Not as a rule. Not with people I'm going to have to work with. But you came knocking at mine at half a mile, without so much as a hello, and I thought you'd better know what it felt like." He looked at Edward for a moment longer. "Now you do. We'll call it even. I'll stay out of yours if you stay out of mine. Deal?"

Edward looked at him.

He thought about the meadow. He thought about it on purpose, the field of flowers in the sun with Bella in it, the one thing in his head that he'd have kept from anybody, and he thought about a stranger walking through it in his boots.

He thought about the drums.

"Deal," said Edward.

The music in the empty place where the man's mind was stopped. Just like that. Mid-bar. And there was nothing there again, the bare hill, the house that wasn't.

"Thank you," said the man in the suit.

Then he took off the dark glasses, and folded them, and put them in his inside pocket. And he looked round the room with his eyes.

They were green. Edward had seen them a hundred times since yesterday in other people's memories, and he'd thought they'd been exaggerating, all of them. They hadn't been. They were a green that didn't look like a real color, very clear and very bright and very steady, and they went round the room the way the dark glasses had gone round it, slow, one face at a time, and stopped on Carlisle.

"Dr. Cullen," he said.

"Mr. Potter," said Carlisle.

"Harry'll do."

"Harry, then." Carlisle hadn't moved from the fireplace. His face was calm, the ER face, the car-accident face. Edward could hear his mind under it, and it was not calm at all. It was full of a London street in 1663, and a man with a torch, and a woman screaming in a cellar, and the word *witch*. "You'll forgive us if we're a little uncertain how to begin."

"Begin however you like," said Harry Potter. "That's what I came for."

He sat down in Charlie's armchair by the window, the big one with the coaster on the side table, as if it had been put there for him, and crossed his boots at the ankle, and folded his hands on his stomach.

"Go on," he said. "Ask."

### XIV. Jacob

*The Cullen house — 10:11 a.m.*

He'd thought it would be bigger.

That was the stupid thing Jacob kept thinking, standing in the doorway between the hall and the living room with his arms folded and his whole body shaking with the effort of not phasing. Since one o'clock yesterday he'd seen this guy in other people's heads about a thousand times. On the ridge in the rain, carrying a vampire like a plank. Walking on nothing across a hole in the ground. In a round room full of jars. And at half past six this morning, in Seth's head, in a backyard in La Push, sitting on his haunches in the rain looking up at Leah's window, black as a hole in the night, a whole *head* taller than—

He'd thought it would look bigger. In a human. He'd thought there'd be some kind of sign.

It was just a guy. A tall guy in a gray suit, sitting in Charlie's chair with his boots crossed. He looked like a cop. Like a fed. Like one of those guys in the movies who get out of a black car with an earpiece in. His hair was wet. He needed a shave.

He smelled like the end of the world.

Jacob's wolf had been on its feet since the top of the drive. It was standing up inside him with every hair on end and its lips back and its ears flat, and it wasn't growling. That was the thing that scared Jacob more than anything. His wolf had growled at every vampire it had ever met. It had growled at Sam. It growled at Paul about once a week on principle. It had stood up inside him at the top of the drive and smelled that smell coming across the lawn, pine and stone and fire on the wind, and it had gone dead quiet. The way a dog goes quiet when a bigger dog walks into the yard.

He hated it. He hated it so much he could taste it.

"Who do you work for?" said Rosalie.

She'd come halfway down the stairs. She'd left Nessie at the top, with Emmett standing in front of her like a wall, and come down six steps and stopped, with one hand on the banister, and she was looking at the guy in the chair the way she looked at Jacob, mostly. Like something she'd found on her shoe.

"Pass," said Harry Potter.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting, I'm afraid." He looked up at her. "I'm always difficult. This is something else. Watch."

He sat forward in the chair. He opened his mouth.

"I work for the—"

And stopped.

Jacob saw it happen. Everybody saw it. His throat closed. Right there in the middle of the word, his jaw locked and his throat worked, once, twice, like he'd swallowed something too big, and his face went dark red. He put his hand flat on the top of his chest, under his collarbone. He leaned back in the chair and coughed, hard, three times, and his eyes watered.

"That," he said, a bit hoarse, when he'd got his breath back. "That's why. Nothing personal. There's a thing in me that won't let me say. Edward's had a look at the edge of it in Leah. Ask him."

"It closed on her last night," said Edward quietly, from the window. "The same way. When she tried to tell Jacob something."

"Yeah, I felt it," said Harry. He rubbed his chest. "Sorry about that, Leah. Didn't know it'd do that to you."

"It's fine," said Leah, from by the door.

"It's a bloody liberty, is what it is. It never asked you."

"It's *fine*, Harry."

He looked at her for a second. Then he looked back at Rosalie on the stairs. "So that's who I work for. Somebody. People who'd rather you didn't know who they are. I'm not going to tell you, because I can't, and if I could I wouldn't." He paused. "I'll tell you what I can. Which is more than I could yesterday."

"Why's that?" said Carlisle.

"Because I rang my boss last night and he let me off the lead," said Harry. "On three things. Just three. And they're all about you."

Jacob had heard enough.

He'd been standing in the doorway for five minutes. He'd stood there while this guy walked into the house like he owned it. While he did something to Jasper that put Jasper on the floor. While he flipped a penny at Alice and talked about it like it was a party trick. While he went into Edward's *head*, Edward's, the one guy in the whole world nobody could do that to, and came out again and told him about a *meadow*. And he'd sat down in Charlie's chair. Charlie's. Charlie, who came every Sunday and sat in that exact chair and held Nessie and cried and never asked a single question, because he'd decided he didn't need to know.

And now he was going to tell them about *them*. Like he'd been studying them. Like they were bugs in one of his jars.

And Leah was standing by the door with her arms folded, *letting* him.

"Okay," said Jacob. "No. No, you know what? Enough."

He came off the doorframe and across the rug. He didn't go for the guy in the chair. He knew better than that; he'd been in Leah's head all day yesterday, he'd seen the ridge. He went for Leah. He got her by the arm, above the elbow, the way he'd gotten her by the arm a hundred times on patrol, to pull her back from something stupid, and he pulled.

"Come on," he said. "Leah. Come on. Outside. Now. I want to talk to you without him—"

The room went cold.

That was the only way Jacob could ever describe it, after. He'd try to tell it to Sam, much later, and to his dad, and to Quil one night on the beach with a beer, and he never found a better way. The room went cold. All of it. All at once. Like somebody had opened a door onto January.

The guy in the chair hadn't moved.

He was still sitting back in Charlie's armchair with his boots crossed and his hands folded on his stomach. He hadn't uncrossed his boots. He hadn't unfolded his hands. But his head had come up. And his eyes, the green ones, had gone to Jacob's hand on Leah's arm.

And something had come up behind them.

Jacob saw it. He'd never seen anything like it and he'd never forget it. It was like looking into a window at night, a dark window, and seeing something in the room behind the glass that you hadn't known was there, and it's looking at you. The green of the guy's eyes had gone brighter, or deeper, or both, Jacob couldn't tell, and the pupils had blown wide and black and *round*, and there was nothing human in them at all.

And out of the guy's chest, out of Charlie's armchair, came a sound.

It was low. It was so low Jacob felt it in his feet before he heard it. It came up through the floor and the rug and the soles of his feet and into his shins, a growl so deep it wasn't really a sound, more like a feeling, like the rumble you get through the ground from a train a long way off before you see it. It went on and on. The long glass wall at the back of the room started to hum. Jacob could hear it, a thin high singing in the glass, like a wet finger round the rim of a wine glass. On the side table by the chair, the coaster rattled.

Every vampire in the room went still.

A different stillness from the statue thing they usually did. Jacob saw it go through them like wind through grass. Esme's hand clenched on Carlisle's arm. Alice froze on her knees by Jasper with her eyes huge. Bella was in front of Edward before Jacob saw her move, between Edward and the chair, crouched, her lips back. On the stairs, Rosalie had gone white to the lips and her knuckles were white on the banister. And up at the top of the stairs Emmett, who was the size of a refrigerator and had once tried to wrestle a grizzly on purpose for fun, had taken one step back toward Nessie without seeming to know he'd done it.

They were prey. Jacob saw it. For the first time since he'd met them, every vampire in that room looked like what they were when you put them next to the right thing. Like deer at the edge of a field when the wind changes.

And Jacob's own wolf, inside him, the russet one, the alpha, Taha Aki's heir, the one that had torn newborns apart in June and faced down Sam in September and would have taken on the whole Volturi for Nessie, lay down.

It lay down. Jacob felt it go. It went flat on its belly inside him with its ears pinned to its skull and its tail clamped between its legs, and he felt it, and he couldn't stop it, and he'd never been so humiliated in his life.

"Don't," said the guy in the chair, "grab her."

It was his voice with something else under it. With the growl running through it like a river under ice. The words came out clean and slow and very quiet, and every one of them went into Jacob like a nail.

Jacob let go of Leah's arm.

He didn't decide to. His hand opened.

Leah looked down at her arm. Then she looked at Jacob. Then she looked at the guy in the chair, and her face did something Jacob had never seen it do in his life, which was go soft and exasperated at the same time.

"Harry," she said. "*Harry.* Stop it."

The growl stopped.

It stopped all at once, like a switch, and the glass wall stopped humming, and the coaster stopped rattling on the side table, and the cold went out of the room like a door shutting on January. The guy in the chair blinked. Once. Slowly. And when his eyes opened again they were just green again. Just a guy's eyes. A guy in a suit in an armchair, looking a bit startled.

He unfolded his hands. He rubbed his face with one of them.

"Sorry," he said. His voice was his voice again. It was a little rough. "Sorry. That one wasn't me." He paused. "Well. It was. Different me." He looked at Jacob. "I wasn't going to do anything. I promised her I wouldn't. But the bit of me that's been locked in the cellar for two months doesn't know about promises, and it's not very keen on people grabbing her, and it's had the lid off since about one o'clock this morning." He let his breath out. "I'm still getting the hang of it. Sorry."

Nobody said anything.

Jacob stood there in the middle of the rug with his hand open at his side and his wolf flat on its belly inside him and his face burning.

"I could've stopped it," the guy said. "If I'd been paying attention. I wasn't. I was looking at you. Bad habit." He looked at Leah. "I'm not breaking the pinky promise. Am I? That didn't count?"

"That didn't count," said Leah. "Barely."

"Barely," the guy agreed.

"Jake," said Leah. She hadn't moved from by the door. Her voice was the one she'd used on him on the phone in June when he was in the hospital bed. "He's not going to hurt me. You know he's not. You know he *can't*, same as you couldn't hurt Nessie if you tried. Don't grab me. I'm not going anywhere."

Jacob looked at her. He couldn't say anything. He went back to the doorframe and leaned on it, because his legs had gone strange.

And that was when Nessie came down the stairs.

---

Jacob felt her before he saw her. He always did. It was the thing about the imprint that nobody had ever really explained to him, the thing he'd tried to tell Seth about and failed: you always knew where they were. Every second. In a house, in a forest, in a crowd. He'd known where Nessie was every single second since she was born. He knew she'd been at the top of the stairs behind Emmett, holding on to Emmett's jeans, while the room went cold. He'd felt her heart go fast, like a bird's, when the growl came up through the floor. He'd felt her get frightened.

And then he felt her stop being frightened.

All at once, the way kids did. One second her heart was going like a hummingbird's and the next it had slowed right down, and he felt her let go of Emmett's jeans, and he felt her think, the way she thought, in pictures, without words. A big black dog in a picture book. A bear in a cave. *Jake.*

He felt her decide.

"Nessie—" said Emmett, at the top of the stairs, too late.

She came down the stairs past Rosalie's hand, which grabbed for her and missed, which Rosalie's hands never did, because Nessie was fast, faster than anybody ever expected, and she went down the last six steps in two jumps and hit the rug running, in her little blue dress and her socks, with her bronze curls bouncing, straight across the room toward Charlie's armchair.

"Renesmee!" Bella said.

And Jasper moved.

He'd been on the floor by the window seat with Alice since the guy looked at him. He came up off it like a spring. He'd been a soldier a hundred and fifty years and his whole body did what a soldier's body does when a child runs toward a threat: it went to get between them. He went across the room in a blur, low and fast, toward the chair, toward the man in it, to put himself in front of Nessie before she got there—

And stopped.

He hit the wall.

He didn't hit it with anything. Nothing touched him. One moment Jasper was halfway across the room going faster than Jacob could follow, and the next moment he was flat against the far wall, by the kitchen door, six feet off the ground, with his arms out at his sides and his feet dangling, pinned there like a butterfly in a case. The plaster cracked behind him in a starburst. A picture beside him, a big one, an oil painting of a city on a hill, swung on its wire and hung crooked.

The guy in the chair hadn't looked round.

He hadn't moved at all. He was looking at Nessie, who'd stopped dead in the middle of the rug, three feet from his boots, and was looking up at him with her mouth open.

"Sorry," the guy said, without turning his head. "Reflex. You came at my back very fast." He paused. "You can come down now. Gently. Don't go at her again. She's fine."

Jasper slid down the wall and landed on his feet. He stood there by the kitchen door with plaster dust on his shoulders and his face completely blank.

"Jazz," said Alice faintly.

"I'm fine," said Jasper. "I'm fine."

Nobody else moved.

Nessie stood on the rug in her socks and looked up at the man in the chair.

He looked back down at her.

Jacob had never been so scared in his life. Not in June. Not on the lawn in September with Sam's pack coming out of the trees. He stood in the doorway with his hands in fists and his wolf flat on its belly inside him and every single thing in him screaming *go, go, get her, get her away from him*, and he couldn't move. He'd seen what happened to Jasper. He didn't know what would happen to him. He didn't know what would happen to her, if he went for the chair and the thing in the cellar came up again.

So he stood there. And he watched his whole world stand on the rug three feet from the most dangerous thing he'd ever smelled, and look at it.

She held up her hand.

Not to Jacob. To the man in the chair. Her little hand, palm out, the way she held it up to Charlie on Sundays when she wanted to show him something.

The man looked at the hand.

"Is that how you do it?" he said. Very quietly. His voice had changed again. It had gone low and careful, the voice you'd use to a cat on a high branch. "Your hand? On my face?"

Nessie nodded.

"Right," said the guy. "Hang on."

He didn't move for a second. Jacob saw something go across his face, quick, like a man checking his pockets. Then he leaned forward in Charlie's chair, slowly, his elbows on his knees, until his face was down at her level. He didn't reach for her. He kept his hands where they were, loose, hanging between his knees.

"Go on, then," he said.

Nessie put her hand on his cheek.

Jacob felt it go. He always did; it was the imprint, it was her, he felt every single time she did it to anyone, like a little tug on the line. He felt her push. He felt her push a picture out of her own head and through her palm into somebody else's.

He felt it go in.

And the guy in the chair went completely still.

His eyes went wide. Just for a second. Just a guy's eyes going wide, startled, like somebody who'd walked into a room in the dark and had a light turned on in his face. His mouth opened a little.

"Oh," said Harry Potter. "Oh, you just walked straight in, didn't you."

Nessie smiled at him.

"Straight through," he said. To her. To himself. "Didn't even knock. Straight through every bloody thing I've got. Eight years of—" He stopped. He laughed. It came out of him short and surprised, like he hadn't meant it to. "Well, that's a data point. That's a hell of a data point, kid."

"What's she showing you?" said Bella. Her voice was hard and tight. She'd taken two steps toward them and stopped, because Edward had her arm.

The guy didn't look away from Nessie.

"Clifford," he said. "As a wolf. In a meadow. With her on his back. Lying round the mouth of a cave in a picture book, a bear's cave, keeping the bear safe so it can sleep." He paused. "And a black dog. A big one. In a book, I think. And a question."

"What question?" said Jacob, before he could stop himself.

The guy looked up at him, past Nessie's hand on his face.

"She wants to know if I'm a wolf too," he said. "Like you."

Jacob didn't say anything. He couldn't.

The guy looked back at Nessie.

"Sort of, kid," he said gently. "Sort of. Bigger."

Nessie's eyes went very wide.

"He's not *bigger*," said Jacob.

"He's like a whole head bigger, Jake," said Seth, from the couch. "I told you. I showed you. It's a lot. It's like this much." He held his hands apart in the air, about eighteen inches.

"Shut *up*, Seth."

"I'm just *saying*—"

Nessie was still looking at the man in the chair. Then she pushed again, Jacob felt it, a little tug on the line, and the man's face changed. It went thoughtful.

"She wants to see," he said.

"No," said Bella.

"Not here," said the guy. "I'm not changing in your sitting room, Mrs. Cullen, I'd take out the ceiling." He looked at Nessie. "But I could show you. If you like. The way you show me. I can do it too, a bit. Backwards."

Nessie nodded so hard her curls bounced.

"Edward," Bella said, low.

"It's all right," said Edward. He was staring at the man in the chair. He'd been staring since Nessie put her hand on his face. "Bella. It's all right. I can hear her. I can hear what he's—he's showing her."

The guy put up one hand, slowly, the way you'd move near a nervous bird, and laid two fingers very lightly against the back of Nessie's hand, where it rested on his cheek.

Jacob felt it come back down the line. He'd never felt anything come *back* before. It came into Nessie from outside, through her hand, and he felt it land in her, and he felt her see it, and through her, faint, like a picture at the bottom of a pool, he saw it too.

A beach. In the dark, in the rain, with the surf coming up white. A gray wolf running at the edge of the water, flat out, long-legged, faster than anything, the wind tearing at her ruff. And beside her, so close their shoulders nearly touched, a black one. Huge. Silent. Running on the seaward side with the spray going over them both.

Leah. And him. Last night. First Beach.

Nessie gasped. She took her hand off his face and clapped both her hands together, and she laughed, the laugh Jacob loved more than anything in the world, the high delighted one, and turned round on the rug to Jacob with her whole face lit up and pointed at the man in the chair.

"Big," she said, out loud. "Jake. *Big*."

"Yeah," said Jacob, through his teeth. "Yeah, Ness. I know."

### XV. Edward

*The Cullen house — 10:24 a.m.*

He let Bella take her.

Bella came across the rug and picked Renesmee up and held her, very tight, and Renesmee put her hand on her mother's cheek and showed her the beach, over and over, the gray wolf and the black one running in the dark with the surf going white, until Bella said *yes, sweetheart, yes, I see, I see it*. And she stood there holding her daughter and looking at the man in the chair with an expression that Edward, who could not hear a single thing that went on behind it, could read anyway.

The man in the chair looked back at her.

"You've got something round your head," he said.

Bella's face went blank.

"Round your head," said Harry. "Like a soap bubble. I went to say hello on my way in, same as I did with him." He nodded at Edward. "Same as everybody. And I slid off you like oil off a pan. Couldn't get a grip. Couldn't even find the edge." He tipped his head, looking at her the way, Edward had seen in Leah's memory, he'd looked at a Disjunction on his fingertips. "Your dad's got something like it. I had a go at him in the diner in October. A door with a big bloke sitting against it. But yours isn't a door. Yours is a shield. And I'd bet it's not finished."

"Bella's mind is closed to me," said Edward. "It always has been."

"I'll bet it is."

"And to Aro," said Edward.

He hadn't meant to say it. It came out of him.

The man in the chair turned his head and looked at him.

"In Volterra," Edward said. "Last year. Aro took her hand. He can read every thought a person has ever had with one touch. He took her hand and he got nothing. He was—" Edward stopped. "He was very interested."

"I'll bet he was," said Harry Potter softly. And Edward saw his green eyes go to Bella again and *change*, very slightly, the way they'd changed on Jasper, on Alice, on him. Something behind them took a note.

"She's not a weapon," Edward said.

"Everything's a weapon, mate," said the man. "I just make a list." He looked at Leah by the door. Something passed between them; Edward didn't know what. He didn't need to. He watched the man look at her and watched Leah look back at him and lift one eyebrow by a quarter of an inch, and watched the man in the suit, who had just put Jasper through a wall without looking, sigh. "Noted. She's not a weapon. She's somebody's mum with a very good shield."

"Thank you," said Bella. It came out cold.

"Don't thank me. It was Leah." He sat back in the chair. "Right. Sit down, all of you, would you? Please. You're making me tired, standing round like that. I've got three things to tell you and none of them are short."

Nobody moved.

"Sit," said Leah, from the door. "God. Just sit. He's not going to bite you."

Carlisle sat. He sat on the arm of the sofa by the fireplace, and Esme sat beside him, and after a moment, slowly, the rest of them did too. Alice took Jasper back to the window seat, and he sat in it with the plaster dust still on his shoulders, very straight. Rosalie came down the rest of the stairs and sat on the bottom step where Emmett had been, and Emmett sat behind her, one step up, with his knees on either side of her shoulders. Bella sat on the couch beside Layla with Renesmee in her lap. Edward stayed standing, at the window. He found he couldn't sit.

Jacob stayed in the doorway.

Leah stayed by the door.

"Right," said Harry. "First thing."

He told them about the vampire.

He told it flat. Edward had heard Carlisle tell a family a man was dead in that voice, in the hallway outside an operating room. He told them the vampire's name was Casimir Wolski, and that he'd been born outside Lublin in 1709 and turned in Kraków in 1731, and that he'd fed on humans for two hundred and seventy-five years, and on forty-one of them this year that Harry had counted. That the forty-first had been a dental hygienist from Olympia named Dana Whitcomb, who had stopped by the Hoh River on Sunday evening to photograph the light on the water.

He told them Casimir had come to the peninsula because someone had asked him to.

"Who?" said Carlisle.

"You know who," said Harry.

Carlisle didn't answer that.

"He came to have a look at you," said Harry. "At your family. Quietly. A nomad passing through, nobody'd think twice. He was meant to find out whether your son's wife had been changed yet, and ring Italy and tell them, and then come back to Volterra in the spring so the old man could take his hand and see it all for himself." He paused. "And on Sunday night, about four in the morning, he came down off the ridge on the north side of this house and stood in your trees for an hour. Just there." He lifted one finger off his knee, without looking, and pointed through the glass wall at the forest behind the house. "Behind that big cedar. Breathing."

The room had gone absolutely silent.

"He smelt a heartbeat," said Harry. "In your house. Fast, like a bird's. And a smell he'd never smelt in three hundred years. Blood and not-blood. Warm and cold, both at once." He didn't look at Renesmee in Bella's lap. Edward noticed that he very carefully didn't look at her. "He was going to tell Aro about it. He said so. On my floor. He was looking forward to it."

Bella made a sound. Edward had never heard her make it before. It was very small.

And in the middle of the room, by the door, something happened in Leah Clearwater's head.

Edward felt it. He'd been listening to the wall in her since yesterday afternoon. He'd walked all round it, through Jacob and Seth, and all round it again in person last night in the kitchen; he knew its shape the way he knew the shape of his own piano. Gray and smooth and cold and old. Laid over a dozen things she knew, precisely, like a cloth over a cage.

As Harry spoke, three of them came out from under it.

He watched them come. He watched the cloth lift, a corner of it, and slide back, and there they were in her head, bright and clear as anything else in there. As if they'd always been there. As if nothing had ever covered them at all.

A round room, lined with jars. A cold wooden chair under her. A circle of silver on a stone floor, and a vampire on its back in a woman's red rain jacket, its face moving above a body that couldn't. Its voice, low and lovely and accented. *A heartbeat. In the house of the cold ones. Fast. Like a bird's.*

The man in the suit crouched on his heels at the edge of the silver. A little gray cell phone.

And then a voice.

Edward knew it before it had finished the first word. He'd have known it in his sleep, if he'd slept. He'd have known it at the bottom of the sea. It came out of Leah Clearwater's memory, out of a cheap phone held over a vampire's face on a stone floor twenty miles from here, soft and delighted and light as air.

*Casimir. My dear old friend. How very good to hear your voice. You've been to see our Carlisle?*

Edward sat down.

He didn't mean to. He found he was sitting on the arm of the couch beside Bella, and his hand was on her shoulder, and he couldn't remember crossing the room.

He listened to the whole thing. He couldn't stop. It played in Leah's head the way it had happened, every word, every pause: Casimir's bored, tired voice saying *the girl is changed, she is one of us, nothing to report*. Aro's silvery laugh. *Caius will be so disappointed.* And then the question, and the pause, and the man in the suit crouched on the stone floor putting his whole weight on something Edward couldn't see, holding it down, holding it, while the vampire's own mind came surging up underneath the Imperius, *the heartbeat, tell him about the heartbeat*—

*Dogs,* Casimir said. *The Quileute dogs. Tiresome.*

*Ah, the wolves. Carlisle does keep the oddest company.*

*In the spring.*

*In the spring. Thank you, old friend. You have done me a kindness. I shan't forget it.*

Click.

"What," said Bella. Her voice was barely there. "Edward. What is it. What do you see."

Edward couldn't tell her. He opened his mouth and nothing came out.

"He rang Aro," said Harry Potter, from the chair. "Yesterday afternoon. About four o'clock. From my floor, on his own phone, with me holding his head. And he told him your wife's been changed and her eyes are going gold and there's nothing at all in this house worth a second look." He paused. "And then he told him he was off to Mexico for the sun, and he'd be in Italy in the spring like a good boy."

"He won't be," said Leah, from the door.

"No," said Harry. "He won't."

Carlisle had gone very pale. Paler than a vampire could go, Edward would have said, before today.

"You made him lie," said Carlisle. "To Aro."

"I made him lie to Aro."

"How?"

"Same way I made your boy here sit down." Harry nodded at Jasper on the window seat. "Different spell. Worse one. It doesn't matter how."

"He'll know," said Carlisle. "Aro. He'll know. When Casimir doesn't come in the spring, he'll send Demetri to find him, and Demetri—"

"Demetri can't find him."

Carlisle stopped.

"I asked," said Harry. "Yesterday. I asked Casimir if the tracker could find him where he was. He said no. He said he couldn't feel the world. Said it was like being in a box." He looked at Carlisle. "He's in my box. Demetri can look all he likes."

"And when he looks and finds nothing?"

"Then a nomad didn't come back from his holidays," said Harry. "It happens. Your lot drift. They fall out with each other. They get eaten by bears in Alaska." He shrugged. "And by the spring, Dr. Cullen, it might not matter much what Aro thinks."

Edward heard it. He couldn't have said what he heard; there was nothing in the man's head for him to hear, nothing at all, the bare hill. But Leah, by the door, heard it too. Edward heard her hear it. He heard the wall in her head, the part of it that was still there, the part over *Volterra* and over the word he couldn't see the shape of, go tight. Like a fist closing.

He didn't ask. He found he didn't want to know. Not yet.

"Why?" said Esme.

They all looked at her. She was sitting beside Carlisle with her hand on his knee, and she was looking at Harry in Charlie's chair with her kind gold eyes, and her face was not soft. It was very serious.

"Why did you do it?" Esme said. "You didn't know us. You don't owe us anything. You could have let him say it. You could have sat in your tent and let Aro come here and done what you came to do and gone home." She looked at him. "Why didn't you?"

Harry looked back at her.

"Operational reasons," he said. "Your family's useful to the people I work for. I'd rather you stayed in one piece. If the Italians came over for a look and didn't like what they saw, I'd lose—"

"And for her," said Leah.

Harry stopped.

Edward saw it happen. He saw the man in the chair open his mouth to say the next word, the rest of his sentence, the reasonable sentence about assets and operational reasons, and he saw his jaw stop. Lock. Like it had on *I work for the*. His throat worked. His face went faintly red. He looked across the room at Leah Clearwater, standing by the door with her arms folded and her eyebrows up, and she looked back at him.

"And for her," said Harry Potter, between his teeth. "A bit."

He looked at Renesmee in her mother's lap. It was the first time since he'd told them about the heartbeat in the trees that he'd looked at her at all.

"I saw her through a pair of Omnioculars in October," he said. "From a ridge a mile off. On his back." He jerked his chin at Jacob in the doorway. "Holding on to his fur with both fists. Laughing." His voice had gone flat again. Very flat. "I'm not having that old bastard put his hand on her. That's all. Not on my watch. Not ever."

Nobody said anything.

Renesmee, in Bella's lap, put her hand on her mother's cheek and showed her something. Edward heard it. It was the black wolf on the beach again, running beside the gray one in the dark.

"*Big,*" Renesmee said again, out loud, to her mother, satisfied.

### XVI. Layla

*The Cullen house — 11:40 a.m.*

He spent an hour with Carlisle.

She watched most of it from the couch, holding the two halves of the bead in her fist. They sat at the long dining table in the room off the living room that the Cullens never used for eating, Carlisle at the head and Harry Potter beside him, with his long coat over the back of the chair and his sleeves pushed up, and they talked about Volterra.

She couldn't hear most of it. Carlisle had asked for that, at the start, politely, and the others had drifted out to the kitchen and the porch and upstairs, and Layla had stayed where she was, because nobody had told her to go and because she didn't think her back would let her get up again. She heard pieces. Carlisle's voice, low and careful. Names she didn't know. *Aro. Marcus. Caius. Chelsea. Afton.* Dates. *Seventeen-forty-something.* Once, Carlisle laughed, very softly, at something, and then stopped laughing.

And she heard Harry ask questions.

She'd been interviewed by MACUSA investigators twice in her life, both times after jobs that went wrong. She'd been interviewed by a Brazilian Ministry hit-witch in Manaus with a wand at her throat. She'd never heard questions like his. They came slow and plain and friendly, and they never seemed to go anywhere in particular, and every single one of them was standing in exactly the right place. How did the old one take his tea. Which of the three slept least. Whether the little blond girl was ever allowed out of the building on her own. Where the stairs went in the tower. Who'd built the doors. How many of them had been there in 1750, and how many came and went, and how often the woman called Heidi went fishing, and in what weather.

And Carlisle answered.

Not all of it. She watched him not answer things; she watched him stop, and look at his hands on the table, and say *I'd rather not*. And Harry would say *all right*, and move on, and come back round to it twenty minutes later from a different direction, so gently that Carlisle was halfway through the answer before he'd noticed. Twice Carlisle noticed, and stopped, and looked at him. And Harry looked back and waited, and didn't say anything at all, until Carlisle finished it anyway.

At the end of the hour, Carlisle put his hands flat on the table.

"I lived with them for twenty years," he said. Layla heard that; he'd raised his voice a little. "Aro was my friend. In some ways he still is. He would say so. He'd say it and mean it." He looked at the table. "I know what he is. I've always known. I know what goes on under that building. I left because I couldn't bear it, and I've told myself for two hundred years that leaving was enough." He looked up. "You're asking me for things that will hurt him."

"I'm asking you for things," said Harry. "What I do with them's my business."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," said Harry. "It's the truest one I've got that won't choke me."

Carlisle looked at him for a long time.

"If it came to it," he said, slowly. "If it ever came to a choice. Between Aro and my granddaughter."

"Then you'd choose," said Harry. "Same as Leah would. Same as me. I'm not asking you to choose today, Dr. Cullen. I'm asking you to think about what you'd want to know if he ever came up your drive." He reached into his pocket and took something out and put it on the table between them. Layla couldn't see what it was from the couch. A coin, she thought. Gold. "That'll get warm," he said, "if I want you. Rub it with your thumb if you want me. I'll come."

Carlisle looked at the coin on the table.

"An old friend of mine made the first ones," said Harry. "For a lot of kids who wanted to fight and weren't allowed. She's in charge of a lot of things now."

Then he got up and put on his coat, and Carlisle sat there at the head of the table looking at the coin, and didn't pick it up, and didn't push it away either.

---

He came and stood in front of the couch.

Layla looked up at him. Her whole body went tight. She couldn't help it; it was the ridge, it was the crater, it was a Disjunction coming back down its own line and taking her shield apart like wet paper. Seth, beside her, put his hand on her knee.

"Can I sit?" said Harry.

She nodded.

He sat on the coffee table across from her, with his elbows on his knees, the way he'd crouched in the ferns. Four feet. Out of reach. He'd done that on purpose; she knew he had.

"You're Harry Potter," Layla said.

"Last time I checked."

"I threw a Stunner at Harry Potter."

"You threw eleven things at Harry Potter," he said. "I counted. Twelve if you count the roots, and you should, those were good." He looked at her bruise. "I'd have been a bit gentler if I'd known you were the hedge-witch's granddaughter. I didn't, till after."

"You'd have been *gentler*?"

"Bit."

She laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, and it hurt her back, and she put her hand over her mouth.

"I'm sorry," she said through it. "I'm sorry. It's—you have to understand. My grandmother read about you to me at the kitchen table. In that house. When I was six. There was a card of you in the Chocolate Frogs when I was thirteen and a boy in my house traded his *broom* for it. My dueling coach used to—" She stopped. "And then you just. Went. Everybody's got a story. Somebody in Rio swore he saw you in Lima buying a goat."

"I did buy a goat in Lima," said Harry. "It's a long story. It wasn't for eating."

She stared at him.

"Where did you go?" she said. "In 1999. Everybody wants to know. Where did you go?"

He looked at her for a moment.

"Got a job," said Harry Potter.

That was all. He said it plainly, and he didn't say anything else, and she understood after a second that he'd said everything he was going to say about it, and that it was the truth, and that the truth had a wall behind it a mile high.

"Okay," said Layla.

"I'll tell you something else, though. Since you're on my books now." He glanced round, briefly, at the doorway to the kitchen, where Jacob was leaning with his arms folded, watching them. "Your Seattle field office has had a tremor gauge in a seismometer vault on a hill above the Bogachiel since April 2004."

Layla went cold.

"They read it on Tuesdays," he said. "Today. Somebody'll be unclipping the drum about now, I should think. It'll have your fight on it. Your Blasting Curse, your Disjunction, the Apparition you did from your kitchen. All of it, in one spike, at a quarter to twelve yesterday morning, right over a ridge in a national park."

"Oh, God."

"It won't, though," said Harry. "Because I went and had a look at it last night. And now it's got a Thunderbird on it."

She stared at him.

"A *what*?"

"A young one. Blown north off course in a big front out of the Pacific. Came up over the Bogachiel about eleven yesterday, threw a tantrum over the ridge, split a dead hemlock with lightning, and went off north toward the Strait to sulk." He tipped his head. "There's a split snag on the ridge and a burn pattern and a blast hole to match, if anybody goes up there. Which they will. Their magizoologists'll have a lovely week. They'll be up and down the coast looking for it till Christmas."

"How," said Layla, "did you put a Thunderbird on a tremor gauge?"

"I held your wand," said Harry. "In the ferns. For about thirty seconds. That feather in it's got a lot to say for itself."

Layla looked at him. She looked at her right sleeve, where her wand was strapped. She thought about holding it yesterday, in the ferns, after he'd handed it back, and the feather in it shivering like a struck wire, the way it did when it had been very frightened.

"You learned a Thunderbird," she said, "off my *wand*. In thirty seconds. While you were *talking* to me."

"Twenty-five, probably."

"That's not possible."

"Neither's bending a Stunner," said Harry. "Did it anyway." He stood up off the coffee table. "Your field office has a pencilled note beside your kitchen on the fourth of November, by the way. *Hedge-ward, inherited, no further action.* So that's where you stand with them. No further action. Let's keep it that way, shall we?"

"The deal," said Layla. "The—you said you'd ring. One day. Is this—"

"No," said Harry. "This is me tidying up after a fight I started." He looked down at her. "You'll know when I ring."

He turned to go.

"Harry," said Layla.

He stopped.

She didn't know what she'd been going to say. She sat on the couch with Seth's hand on her knee and the bead in her fist, and she looked up at him, at the man who'd crouched in the ferns four feet away and told her she was very good and had just picked the wrong day, and she thought about Calloway in Toronto in 1998 with the paper under her arm. *Nobody gets that lucky.*

"The Disjunction," she said. "You said it was clever."

"It is clever."

"Would you—" She stopped. Started again. "Would you show me? How you turned it round? What you did to it?"

He looked at her for a moment. Then he smiled. It was a small one, at one corner, there and gone, like Leah's.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, all right. Not today. But yeah."

---

Jacob's phone rang at five to twelve.

It was a cheap one, a flip phone, the kind they all had now because Esme had bought a box of them in September so the wolves could be reached when they weren't phased. It rang in the pocket of his cutoffs in the kitchen doorway and he dug it out and looked at it and his face changed.

"It's my dad," he said.

He went into the kitchen with it. Layla heard him say *hey, Dad*, and then *yeah*, and then nothing for a long time. Then *yeah, I know*. Then *no, he's here. He's right here.* Then a long silence. Then, very quietly, *okay. Okay, Dad. I'll tell him.*

He came back to the kitchen doorway with the phone in his hand.

He didn't look at Leah. He looked at Harry Potter, standing in the middle of the Cullens' living room in his long black coat.

"That was my dad," Jacob said. "Billy Black."

"Tribal elder," said Harry. "Wheelchair. Fishes off the north end of the marina dock on Thursdays when it isn't raining, which is never." He stopped. "Sorry. Habit. Go on."

Jacob stared at him for a second.

"Sam came to see him," he said. "This morning. Sam Uley. Paul smelled something in Sue's yard last night on patrol. And on our side of the line. All the way in from the east, six miles, along the river. And on First Beach. A wolf. Not one of ours. Not one of Sam's. Tracks as big as dinner plates, he said." He looked at Leah now, finally. "Sam wants to know what it was."

"I'll bet he does," said Leah.

"He wants a meeting. Tonight. At the treaty line, at the beach. Both packs." Jacob swallowed. "And he wants *it* to be there. Whatever it was. He said that. *Tell Jacob to bring it.*"

Everybody looked at Harry.

Harry Potter looked at the window for a moment, out at the gray lawn and the dark trees. Then he looked at Leah. And Layla watched something go between them that she didn't need to be able to read minds to read: a question, and an answer, and a corner of Leah Clearwater's mouth going up.

"Tell him I'll come," said Harry.

"Oh," said Leah. She uncrossed her arms. "Oh, this is going to be *good*."

Harry looked at her sideways. "Is it?"

"You have no idea."

"I've some idea. I've been in your head for twenty-three hours." He pulled the dark glasses out of his inside pocket and put them on. "Right. I've got a woman in Forks saving me two slices of pie since yesterday lunchtime, and I don't break promises to Doreen. Are you coming?"

"Are you buying?"

"No," said Harry. "You are. I just paid for this whole family's lives."

Leah looked at him.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm running. I'm not doing the garden hose again."

"Suit yourself. Race you."

"You'll lose."

"Probably," said Harry Potter, and turned on his heel, and was gone with a sound like a page turning.

Leah was out of the door before the sound had finished. Layla heard her hit the porch steps and the lawn, and the soft tearing sound from the corner of the trees, and the long thudding run going away down the drive into the forest, faster than anything had a right to go.

The living room was very quiet.

Seth, beside her, let out a long breath.

"She's happy," he said, to nobody in particular. "That's what that is. That's what she sounds like."

"Yeah," said Jacob, from the kitchen doorway. He was looking at the trees. He didn't sound angry anymore. He sounded tired, and a little lost, and something else under that which Layla thought, if she'd had to name it, might have been relief. "Yeah. I'd forgotten."

Layla opened her fist and looked at the two halves of the bead in her palm. Cut clean down the middle. *You did that well.*

She thought about her grandmother on the back step, at nine o'clock, every night for forty years, smoking her one cigarette, keeping her bees, leaving them all be.

*I'd bet my hives that boy's out there somewhere,* Maeve used to say. *And I'd bet he's still not done.*

"No," Layla said quietly, to the bead. "He's not."

### XVII. Gianna

*Volterra, Tuscany — Tuesday, November 21st, 7:56 p.m.*

The lipstick was called *Rosso Antico*, and she'd been wearing it for four years, since the week she'd started at the desk, and she put a fresh coat on every evening at eight o'clock exactly because the master liked punctuality and because she liked to look her best when they walked past.

Gianna sat behind the polished reception desk in the great cool hall with its flowers and its marble and its tall windows full of the blue Tuscan dusk, and held up her little gold compact, and drew the color on carefully, top lip first. Outside, in the square, a tour group was going home, laughing, their voices coming faintly up through the old stone. Six thousand miles away it would be eleven in the morning, she thought, idly, in America. She didn't know why she thought it. She'd been thinking about America a good deal lately, without knowing why.

The big double doors at the end of the hall opened.

She put the compact away very fast and sat up straight.

It was the master. He came out through the doors with Demetri at his side, the two of them walking slowly, the master's hand resting lightly on Demetri's arm the way it did when he wanted to be close to someone's thoughts. He was smiling. He was almost always smiling. His skin in the lamplight had that soft, papery, milky look, like the inside of a shell, and his red eyes were bright and fond and very, very interested, the way they always were.

Gianna lowered her eyes and kept them on the desk.

"—telephoned last night," the master was saying. "Late. From America. Such a dear old friend. And he told me exactly what I'd hoped to hear about Carlisle's family, and he sounded very tired, and he said he was going south for the winter. To Mexico. For the sun."

"Casimir hates the south," said Demetri.

"He does, doesn't he." The master's voice was light and pleased, the voice of a man who has found a tiny flaw in a jewel and is delighted by it. "And do you know what else he said, my dear? He complained of the rain. *Three months of rain,* he said. *It is in my hair.* Our Casimir." He laughed softly. "Our Casimir, who told me in this very hall in 1888 that the rain in Kraków was the only thing he missed about being alive. Who used to stand out in it in Vienna for whole nights together, in his good coat, with his face turned up to it, like a boy."

Demetri said nothing.

"His voice, I thought," said the master. "But the words had somebody else's shape. I've been turning it over all day." He stopped walking, in the middle of the hall, ten feet from Gianna's desk. "Would you find him for me, Demetri? Just to set my mind at rest. Just to see where our old friend has got to."

Demetri closed his eyes.

Gianna watched him do it from under her lashes. She'd seen him do it before. He stood very still in the middle of the marble floor with his head a little raised, the way a dog lifts its nose to the wind, and his face went blank and far away, and she knew he was out there somewhere, everywhere, feeling across the whole of the turning world for the one particular taste of one particular mind.

It went on for a long time.

Then Demetri opened his eyes. And his face had something on it that Gianna had never once seen there, in four years.

"I can't," Demetri said.

The master's head tilted. "Can't?"

"He isn't anywhere, master." Demetri's voice was low and careful. "He isn't dead. I'd know dead. Dead is a—a silence, a place. I'd feel the place. There's no place. He's just—not. Anywhere. Not in Mexico. Not in America. Not in the world." He frowned. "As if someone has taken him out of it."

The great hall was very quiet.

Then the master laughed.

It was a soft, silvery, delighted laugh, and it went up into the high painted ceiling of the hall and came down again, and Gianna, at her desk, kept her eyes on the polished wood and felt the hair on her arms stand up all the way to her shoulders.

"Then somebody," the master said, "has put our old friend in a *box*."

He turned his head. He looked out through the tall windows into the blue evening, westward, toward the sea, and past the sea, and past everything, with his red eyes very bright.

"How very interesting," said Aro. "Who, I wonder, puts one of *us* in a box?"

Gianna touched up her lipstick, and didn't look up, and waited to be made one of them.
