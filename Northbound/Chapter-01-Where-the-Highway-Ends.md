# Chapter One: Where the Highway Ends

*La Push, Washington, to Port Hardy, British Columbia — Monday, September 18, 2006*

---

## I. Leah

*Seven days*, Seth thought, the second she came over the ridge. *Seven days today. Can you believe it?*

Leah could believe it. She'd been counting too.

She came down off the ridge behind the Cullen place at a lope, with the dawn going gray over the Olympics and the rain finally giving up after a whole night of trying. Her paws were soaked to the skin under the fur. Her ears were full of the river. Her nose was full of the house.

You couldn't get away from the house. Nobody had warned her about that part when she'd crossed the line two and a half weeks ago and followed Jacob Black into whatever this was. Sam's pack only had to smell the Cullens when they ran the treaty line. Leah had to smell them every night from fifty yards out, close enough that the stink got down into her coat and rode home with her and was still in her hair when she woke up. Bleach. Old flowers. And under that a sweetness, thick and cloying, like somebody had spilled a whole bottle of grape cough syrup on the floor of a funeral parlor and left it there to dry.

Eight of them now, counting Bella. Nine, counting the kid.

*She smiled at me yesterday*, Seth thought. He was down by the road, running the last leg of the loop, and his head was full of yellow kitchen light and Esme's hands and a plate of eggs the size of a hubcap. *Like, a real smile. Not gas. Esme says she doesn't get gas anyway, because of the whole—*

*Seth.*

*What?*

*It's six in the morning.*

*So?*

*So shut up.*

He didn't take it personally. He never took it personally, which was the worst part. Seth was fifteen and he'd been a wolf for six months and he still thought the pack mind was the best thing that had ever happened to him, like a sleepover that never ended. He'd heard every ugly thing his big sister had thought for half a year and he still loved her, and Leah could feel him loving her right now, the way you could feel the sun on your back through a jacket.

She hated it. She'd have hated it more if it stopped.

The trees opened up at the edge of the Cullens' lawn. Leah stopped where she always stopped, just inside the shadow of the big cedars, and looked at the house.

Glass and white wood and lights on in every room even though not one person inside it needed to sleep. Through the long south wall she could see the living room, the white couch, the piano. Rosalie Hale on the couch with her shoes off and her legs tucked up, and on Rosalie's chest, the kid. Asleep. A head of bronze curls and one fat little fist curled up against the Blonde's collarbone.

One week old. She looked about four months. By next week she'd look six. Carlisle had a chart.

On the floor next to the couch, with his back against it and his head tipped back on the cushion an inch from that little fist, was Jacob. Human. Shirtless. Out cold with his mouth open.

As Leah watched, the kid stirred. Her hand opened and slid off Rosalie's collarbone and found Jacob's cheek, the way you'd reach for a light switch in the dark, and Jacob's eyes opened. Just like that. Like she'd called his name.

Leah looked away.

*Handoff*, Jacob thought a minute later, and then he was in her head.

He'd phased on the back porch. She heard the door close behind him and the scrape of his claws on Esme's deck boards, and then his mind came open into hers like a door into a room where somebody had the heat cranked all the way up.

It was all the kid.

It was always all the kid. Seven days and Leah still wasn't used to it, and she'd thought nothing could be worse than Sam. Sam's head full of Emily, every single time he phased, Emily's laugh, Emily's mouth, Emily's scarred cheek against his chest, the whole stupid golden weight of it pressing down on Leah's skull until she'd wanted to claw her own brain out through her ears. She'd lived inside that for half a year. She'd thought she'd learned what it was like.

This was different. Jacob's head had a baby in it. A baby's hand on his face, pushing pictures at him—that was the kid's trick, she touched you and you saw what she was thinking, warm and bright and blurry around the edges. Jacob's own face, from below, enormous and grinning. A bottle. Rosalie's hair. Bella's eyes, the new red ones. And every thought Jacob had bent toward that kid the way a compass needle bent north. Every single one. Like the whole world had been unstrung in the space of a breath and restrung around one small warm point in a white house in the woods, and he was so happy about it he couldn't see straight.

Leah stood in the cedars and tried not to have any thoughts at all.

It didn't work. It never worked. She was nineteen years old and she hadn't had a period since the first night she phased, and she'd figured out what that meant all on her own, sitting on the edge of the tub in her mom's bathroom at three in the morning with the door locked. Genetic dead end. That was what she'd called it, out loud, to Jacob, a couple of weeks ago, because she'd had to tell somebody and he'd been the one standing there. Every wolf in two packs was busy finding the one person they were built for and starting families and making plans, and Leah Clearwater was the end of a line. The last page. She'd done the math on it so many times she could do it in her sleep, and in the pack mind, sometimes she did.

Jacob heard it. Of course he did.

The bright warm room in his head went still.

*Leah*, he thought.

*Don't.*

*I wasn't—*

*You were about to. Don't.*

A pause. Down by the road, Seth had gone quiet, too, the way he did when things got bad between the two of them and he didn't know which side to be on.

*Go home*, Jacob thought finally. He sounded tired. *Get some sleep. I've got the line.*

*That an order?*

*It's a suggestion.*

*Great. Suggest it to somebody who cares.*

But she went. She turned around in the cedars and went back up the ridge, and she felt Jacob let her go—felt him not push, not reach after her with the Alpha voice that would have pulled her up short like a choke chain—and she didn't know if that made her grateful or just made her feel like something somebody had decided to leave alone.

---

She ran home the long way. Down through the Calawah drainage and across the 101 in the gap between log trucks, west through the second-growth and the old clearcuts along the Quillayute, with the light coming up behind her and the smell of the Cullens slowly peeling off her in the wet brush. By the time she hit the trees behind her mom's house in La Push the sun was up somewhere behind the clouds and she could smell the ocean again, cold and big and clean.

She phased in the salal behind the shed.

It hit her the way it always did now, a quick hot ripple and then her own two feet in the mud and the September air on bare skin. She stood there a second with her eyes closed and let the silence come down. No Seth. No Jacob. Nobody's thoughts but her own, which was a mixed blessing, considering what her own thoughts usually were.

The leather cord around her left ankle had her clothes knotted into it, the way they all did it now: a pair of cutoffs and a black tank top rolled tight and lashed on with two half-hitches. She untied them and pulled them on damp. Barefoot across the yard. Through the back door into the kitchen.

The house was empty.

She knew it would be before she opened the door; she'd heard no heartbeat inside, smelled no coffee. But she stood in the kitchen doorway anyway and listened to the empty. The fridge humming. Rain dripping off the gutter Charlie Swan had fixed for them back in April. Her mom's car was gone from the gravel out front, and on the fridge door, under the magnet shaped like a salmon, there was a note.

*At Charlie's. Somebody has to make sure that man eats. Lasagna in fridge—350, not 500, I mean it. Love you. —Mom*

Leah read it twice.

Three days ago Jacob had walked into Charlie Swan's backyard and taken his clothes off and phased right in front of him, because Charlie was going to call the cops on the Cullens if somebody didn't give him a reason not to. It had worked. Charlie had seen a seven-foot russet wolf in his yard and had sat down very hard on his own back steps, and then he'd gone over to the big glass house and met a granddaughter who was a week old and looked four months, and a daughter with red eyes, and he'd decided he didn't want to know. *Need to know*, he'd said. That was the deal.

And somebody had to hold his hand through it. Somebody had to drive over to his house every day with a casserole dish and sit with him in front of the game and make sure he ate. Somebody who already knew everything there was to know.

So her mom was over there. Every day now. Leah couldn't even be mad about it. Charlie was a decent man and he'd sat with them all night in the hospital waiting room in Forks back in March, when the doctor came out with that face. Her mom deserved somebody to sit with. Everybody deserved somebody to sit with.

That was the problem. Everybody had somebody to sit with.

She turned around. Her dad's fishing jacket was still on the hook by the back door, where it had been hanging for six months, the green canvas gone stiff in the folds. Nobody had moved it. Nobody was going to move it. She stepped close and put her face in it, the way she did sometimes when the house was empty, and breathed in.

It smelled like the closet.

Last month it had still smelled a little like him. Tobacco he'd quit fifteen years ago and still somehow smelled like, and diesel, and the Old Spice he'd bought in the same blue bottle her whole life. Now it smelled like cedar shelf-paper and dust and the house. Like everything else in it.

She stood there with her face in the canvas for a long time.

Then she went down the hall to her room and opened the top drawer of her dresser.

It was in the back, under the socks, where she'd put it in August. A sandwich bag, the zipper kind. Inside it, her Washington driver's license and two folded twenty-dollar bills and nothing else. She'd made it the week Sam's pack had started talking about Bella's pregnancy like it was a bomb they needed to defuse, the week she'd started lying awake doing math, and she'd never really let herself think about what it was for. Some people kept a bag packed by the door. Leah had a sandwich bag in her sock drawer.

She took it out and looked at it.

What she had was a feeling, the same one she'd had for weeks, like a hand pressing flat on the middle of her chest. *Go*. Go somewhere that didn't smell like bleach and grape syrup. Somewhere nobody had ever heard of Sam Uley or Emily Young or the Cullens or the Quileute tribe. Somewhere nobody knew her name, or her dad's name, or what she'd said in her own head in front of her whole pack at three in the morning.

Get drunk. Get stupid drunk, for once, if she could figure out how. Find somebody who didn't know her from a hole in the ground and do something with him she'd regret, so that for one night the thing she regretted would be something she'd picked.

She folded the sandwich bag in half and pushed it down into the back pocket of her cutoffs, and buttoned the pocket.

In the kitchen she found the pen by the phone and wrote on the bottom of her mom's note, under *Love you*:

*Gone for a run. Don't wait up. —L*

She looked at that for a while, too. Then she added *Love you* and underlined it, because her mom was going to read it a hundred times and she could at least give her that.

She went out the back door. She didn't look at the jacket on the way past.

---

She phased in the trees, and they were both still there.

*Leah?* Seth's head came up fast. He was crossing the road below the Cullens', and he stopped dead on the wet asphalt, ears up. *Where are you going? The line's the other way.*

She didn't answer. She was already moving. North. Up out of the river valley, through the salal and the sword fern and the big dripping hemlocks, with the clothes back on her ankle and the sandwich bag knotted in with them.

*Leah*, said Jacob.

*Don't.*

She felt him take it in—where she was, the direction, the speed she was picking up. She felt him look at the inside of her head the way you'd look at a weather map. She didn't hide it. She couldn't have if she'd tried, but she didn't try. Let him see the kitchen. Let him see the note on the fridge, and the jacket, and the sandwich bag. Let him see the hand pressing on the middle of her chest.

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she hit the top of the first ridge and went over it and started down the other side.

*Okay*, Jacob thought.

That was it. She almost stumbled.

*That's it?*

*You want me to make you stay?*

She thought about it. Honestly thought about it, with both of them listening, because there wasn't any other way to think.

*No.*

*Then go*, Jacob said. *Call your mom when you stop.*

*She's at Charlie's.*

A pause.

*Yeah*, Jacob said. *I know.*

And she felt it from him then, and it was worse than pity. He knew better than to pity her. It was something closer to understanding. He knew about the jacket. He knew about the bathroom. He'd sat in her head for two and a half weeks. He knew exactly why she was going, and he wasn't going to try to fix it, because he couldn't.

She hated him a little for that. She loved him a little for it too. She didn't have time to sort out which.

*Leah, wait*, Seth said. He'd started running. She could feel his paws hitting the road, feel him turn uphill after her, and he was fast, Seth was fast, but she was faster. She'd always been faster. Her and Jacob, and nobody else even close. *Leah, come on. Where are you going?*

*North.*

*North where?*

*Just north.*

And she put her head down and ran.

---

## II. Leah

She went up the Sol Duc and over the top.

There was a way across the mountains from the river valley to the north side of the peninsula that no road had ever bothered with, because it went straight up through a mile of devil's club and windthrow and then straight back down, and Leah took it at a dead run with the sun coming up gray behind her. Hemlock and silver fir. Moss so deep her paws sank to the wrist in it. A creek, and another creek, and a slope of loose shale that went out from under her hind end and dropped her ten feet in a hiss of rock before she caught herself and kept going.

Seth followed her as far as the 101.

She felt him stop at the edge of the road with the morning traffic going by, his chest heaving, and look up at the ridge where she'd disappeared. He could have kept going. She'd have heard him if he had. But Jacob was there, quiet in the back of both their heads, and something passed between the two of them that Leah only half caught, and Seth sat down in the wet ferns by the shoulder and let her go.

*I'll tell Mom*, he thought.

*Tell her I'm fine.*

*Are you?*

She didn't answer that one. She didn't have to. He was in her head.

The north slope was steeper and wetter and full of logging roads, the old ones gone soft and green and the new ones raw red dirt, and she crossed them the way she'd crossed the highway: low and fast, between one breath and the next, so that if anybody was sitting in a pickup at the end of one with a thermos of coffee, all they'd see was something big and gray and gone. By seven-thirty she could smell the strait. By eight she was standing on a gravel beach at the bottom of a bluff west of Pillar Point, with the tide out and the kelp lying in long brown ropes across the stones, looking north at nothing at all.

Fog.

It sat on the water like a lid. Gray and flat and so thick she couldn't see a hundred yards past the surf line. Somewhere out in it, eleven or twelve miles off, was Canada. She knew that the way she knew where north was, in her gut, under the fur. She'd seen it from this beach a hundred times on clear days, a long blue line of mountains across the water. Vancouver Island. Her dad used to point at it from the boat. *Our cousins live over there*, he'd say, meaning the Pacheedaht and the Ditidaht and all the rest of the people up that coast, who'd been paddling back and forth across that water for thousands of years before anybody drew a border down the middle of it.

They'd done it in canoes. She had four legs and a fever that never broke.

*Leah*, said Seth, who was still phased, sitting in the ferns by the road with his ears flat. *Leah. Are you about to go swimming?*

She walked into the water.

*Leah. Leah, that's the ocean. That's, like, the whole ocean. Jake. Jake, she's—*

*I see it*, said Jacob.

*Go to bed, Seth*, Leah thought.

*It's eight in the morning!*

The cold hit her chest and went straight through the fur and she gasped, and then her body did what it always did and swallowed the cold like it was nothing. A hundred and eight degrees. She could have swum in the Arctic. She pushed off the bottom and stretched out and started to paddle, legs going under her in long steady strokes, chin up, the salt stinging her nose and the swells lifting her and setting her down, lifting her and setting her down.

The beach disappeared behind her in about a minute. Then there was nothing but gray. Gray fog above. Gray-green water below, cold and endless and full of sounds: the slap of the chop on her muzzle, the hiss of the surf behind her getting fainter, a sea lion barking somewhere off to the left. Her own breathing. She kept her nose pointed north and her mind on the strokes.

*Jake*, Seth said, small. *Can she do that? Like, actually?*

*She's doing it*, Jacob said.

After a while—she didn't know how long, there wasn't anything to count but strokes, and she lost count—she heard the ship.

She felt it first. A thrum in the water, deep and slow, coming up through her legs and her belly like the bass from somebody's car stereo three blocks away. Then the sound. A big diesel heartbeat, growing. She was in the shipping lane. Of course she was. The whole strait was a highway, and she'd swum straight out into the middle of the road in the fog.

It came out of the gray to her right. A wall. Black steel and rust streaks, rising straight up out of the water higher than the Cullens' house, so close she could see the barnacles at the waterline and the white numbers painted on the bow. Its wake was already rolling toward her, a long smooth hill of water.

Leah swam like hell.

She went left, hard, every leg digging, and the bow went past her close enough that she felt the push of water in front of it like a hand shoving her sideways. Then the wake picked her up and threw her and rolled her under, and for a second she didn't know which way was up—green and black and bubbles and the roar of the screws somewhere below—and then her head broke the surface and she was coughing salt water out of her nose, spinning in the backwash, watching a cargo ship the length of three football fields slide away into the fog without ever having known she was there.

*LEAH*, Seth screamed in her head.

*I'm fine.*

*You almost got hit by a BOAT!*

*It's a ship. Boats are small.*

*THAT'S NOT BETTER!*

Despite everything—the cold and the salt and the ache coming up in her shoulders—she laughed. It came out as a cough and a snort and a mouthful of strait. She shook her head and found north again and kept going.

Jacob didn't say anything. But she felt him relax. She felt him go back to the kid.

---

It took her a little under three hours.

The fog thinned toward the end. The island came up out of it slowly, first as a darkness and then as a shape and then as a shoreline: a steep rocky bluff with second-growth fir clinging to the top and a gravel beach at the bottom covered in drift logs, and a creek mouth, and a smell like home that was also not like home at all. Cedar and salal and kelp and rain, the same as La Push. But the dirt was different. The rock was different. Somebody else's woods.

She dragged herself up onto the stones and stood there with her legs shaking and the water pouring off her in sheets, and shook. Her whole body, nose to tail, like a dog after a bath, salt water flying in every direction. Then she lay down on the warm side of a drift log in a patch of watery sunlight and panted for ten minutes with her eyes shut.

*Did you make it?* Seth said.

*I made it.*

*You're in Canada.*

*Yeah.*

*Do you need, like, a passport? To be a wolf in Canada?*

She opened her eyes and looked at the sky.

*Seth*, she thought. *I love you. Phase out and go to sleep.*

He went quiet. She could feel his surprise. She hadn't said that to him in—she didn't know. She didn't want to know. She felt him turn it over, and feel good about it, and decide not to push his luck.

*Okay*, he said. *Call Mom.*

And he was gone. Just a little click in her head, and then quiet where he'd been.

Jacob stayed a while longer. He didn't say anything. He was out on the back porch at the Cullens', lying with his head on his paws, watching the kitchen window where somebody was moving around with a bottle, and he let her see that, and she let him see the beach and the drift log and the sun. Then he was gone too.

Leah lay on the stones and listened to the silence in her head.

It was huge. She'd forgotten. Six months of somebody else's thoughts coming and going, Sam and Paul and Jared and Embry and Quil and the rest of them, then Jacob and Seth, a radio she couldn't turn off. And now nothing. Just her. Just the waves coming in, and the gulls, and the creek, and her own heartbeat slowing down.

She got up and kept going.

---

The island was long. Longer than she'd figured. She went up the spine of it through the middle of the day, staying high, staying in the trees, through country that had been logged and logged again until the hillsides looked like a patchwork quilt somebody had chewed on. Clearcuts in every color from raw red dirt to knee-high green. Old-growth only in the gullies where the saws couldn't reach. Log trucks on the gravel roads below her, kicking up dust, their engine brakes chattering on the downhills.

Around noon she smelled wolves.

Real ones. She came over a saddle above a long lake and there it was on the wind: musk and old blood and wet fur, a pack of them, close. She stopped. They were on the next ridge over—four of them, gray and brown, lean, standing in a line on a rocky knob in the sun, watching her.

She'd never seen a real wolf before. Not up close. There weren't any left on the peninsula. They'd been shot out before her grandpa was born.

They were small. That was the first thing. The biggest one was maybe a third her size. The second thing was the smell. She'd always figured, without really thinking about it, that real wolves would smell like the pack. One breath set her straight. They smelled like animals—like deer, like elk, like the inside of a den. Her pack smelled like people who happened to have fur. These smelled like they'd never been anything but what they were.

The big gray one lifted its nose and tested the air. Its ears went flat. It made a low sound, almost too low to hear.

Then all four of them turned around and trotted off the back of the knob into the trees, at an unhurried trot, leaving. Getting out of the way of something too big to fight.

Leah watched them go and felt something she didn't have a word for. Then she went on.

---

The land flattened out in the afternoon. The mountains fell back and turned into hills and the hills turned into long, low, soggy country—bog and stunted pine and salal so thick she had to bull through it, and rain, real rain, coming in sideways off the ocean in long cold gusts. She found the highway and shadowed it through the trees. Two lanes. Hardly any traffic. Green signs every few miles, counting down in kilometers to a town she'd never heard of.

PORT HARDY 87.

PORT HARDY 52.

PORT HARDY 18.

The light was going. She'd been on the move for thirteen hours, three of them swimming, and her legs had gone past aching into a kind of humming numbness, and she was so hungry her stomach had stopped complaining and started sulking. She didn't care. She didn't feel anything about anything. She just ran, and the rain ran down her face, and the signs counted down, and somewhere in the last few miles the trees fell away on her right and she could see water again: a long gray inlet, and lights coming on along the far shore, and boats.

The highway bent toward the lights. A big wooden sign stood on the shoulder, carved and painted, with a lamp on it.

She stopped in the trees across from it and read it.

PORT HARDY, it said. And underneath, in smaller letters: *where the highway ends and the adventure begins.*

Leah looked at that for a long time.

Then she went back into the trees to find somewhere to change.

---

## III. Leah

Her clothes were a mess.

She untied them in a stand of shore pine behind a gravel pullout and held them up in the last of the light. The cutoffs had gone stiff as cardboard with dried salt, white lines all down the seams. The tank top had a hole in it she didn't remember, and it smelled like kelp. The sandwich bag had held, though. Her license was dry. The twenties were damp around the edges and a little soft, but they were still twenties.

She pulled everything on. Stood still a minute in the rain and let the heat come up off her skin until the cloth started to steam faintly in the cold air, and the worst of the wet went out of it. Then she ran her hands through her hair, which did nothing, and gave up on her hair.

She walked into Port Hardy barefoot on the shoulder of the highway, at dusk, in the rain.

It wasn't much of a town. She'd figured it wouldn't be. A long main drag that followed the shore of the bay, with the water on one side and the businesses on the other: a hardware store, a marine supply place, a Chinese restaurant with a lit-up sign of a dragon, a bank, a couple of motels, a liquor store with bars on the windows. A harbor full of boats, seiners and trollers and an old wooden packer with its paint coming off, tied up in rows along a dock that stuck out into the gray water. Gulls on every piling. Fish smell and diesel smell and woodsmoke from somebody's chimney. Pickups. A log truck going by empty, its trailer piggybacked up behind the cab, the driver lifting two fingers off the wheel at her the way they did on the reservation.

She lifted two fingers back without thinking about it.

It smelled like rain and salt and people. Ordinary people, a few thousand of them, warm and alive and smelling like coffee and cigarettes and laundry soap and fish. Not a single cold one for three hundred miles. She breathed it in all the way to the bottom of her lungs and held it.

Then she breathed out, and walked past the second motel, and stopped.

It was a two-story place, the Seawind, with a row of doors facing a puddled parking lot and an office at one end with a VACANCY sign in red neon. A dozen cars in the lot. Most of them were pickups or rentals. One of them wasn't.

It was black. Long and low and black, an old Chevy, the kind with the big chrome grille and the four headlights and the back end that went on for about a week. Somebody had taken care of it. The paint was wet and shining under the motel lights, and the chrome was clean, and it was parked nose-out at the far end of the lot like it wanted to be ready to leave in a hurry. Kansas plates.

That was where the smell was coming from.

Leah stood on the sidewalk with the rain running down the back of her neck and every hair on her arms standing straight up.

Wolf.

For one stupid half-second, she thought Jacob had followed her. That was how close it was. Under the rain and the gasoline and the old leather of the car, the same bones as the pack. The same hot, rough, alive smell of people who happened to have fur. Her nose knew it the way it knew her own brother, and her heart jumped, and her whole body turned toward it before her brain could catch up.

Then her brain caught up.

This one was older than anybody in the pack. Wilder. It had pine in it, and wet black earth, and something cold and clean like the stones on the bottom of a creek in December. It had come out of a forest somewhere she'd never been.

And under all of that, running through it like a wire, there was something else.

She knew it. It took her a second to figure out from where. Then she had it: the summer she was twelve, a storm had come in off the ocean in the middle of the night and lightning had hit the big Sitka spruce on the bluff behind First Beach, the one everybody used for a landmark, and split it top to bottom. Her dad had walked her up there the next morning to look at it. The whole bluff had smelled like this. Sharp. Burnt. Something like hot metal and something like rain, like the air itself had been set on fire. *Ozone*, her dad had said, crouching down by the black split trunk with his hand on it. *That's what lightning smells like after.*

Leah looked up at the sky.

Low clouds. Drizzle. No thunder anywhere. No storm for a hundred miles. The air out here smelled like rain and fish and woodsmoke and nothing else.

Except right here, by this car. Where it smelled like a wolf that had been struck by lightning and gotten up and walked away.

Her wolf sat up inside her.

She felt it go, the way you'd feel a dog lift its head off the rug when somebody came up the porch steps. Ears up. Hackles up. Wide awake, for the first time since she'd come out of the strait, and pulling toward that smell like it was on a leash.

*No*, Leah thought.

She said it out loud, under her breath, to the rain. "No."

She was off duty. She was in another country. There was no treaty line here, no Cullens, no Sam, nobody to protect and nobody to report to. Whoever owned that car was somebody else's problem. Maybe he was like her. Maybe he was something else. Maybe Port Hardy, British Columbia had its own wolves and its own legends and its own big dumb Alpha with his own big dumb rules, and if so, he could have the place. She was on vacation. From all of it. From being a wolf, even. Especially from being a wolf.

The trail went out of the lot on the far side of the car and across the road toward the water. Fresh. A couple of hours, maybe less. She could see it in her head the way she could always see a scent when she was paying attention—a bright line laid down across the wet asphalt like somebody had dragged a sparkler.

Leah turned her back on it and kept walking.

---

There was a gas station at the end of the next block, lit up white and flat under its canopy. A sign out front said REGULAR 109.9 in big red numbers, which meant nothing to her until she figured out it was per liter, and then meant less.

The kid behind the counter had a Canucks toque on and a constellation of zits along his jaw, and he looked up from a magazine when the door chimed and kept looking. At her wet hair. At the salt on her shorts. At her bare feet on his floor.

"Hey," he said. "Uh. You okay?"

"Fine."

"You need, like, a phone or something?"

"I need shoes."

He thought about that. Then he pointed at a wire rack by the windshield washer fluid. On it, between a row of ice scrapers and a display of sunglasses nobody in this town had ever needed, hung a dozen pairs of rubber flip-flops in plastic packaging. Pink, green, black. ONE SIZE FITS MOST.

She took the black ones, but they were too small. The pink ones were bigger. She looked at them for a while. Then she took the pink ones.

She also took two hot dogs off the roller grill by the register, the kind that had been rolling since lunch and had gone wrinkled and shiny, and a bag of beef jerky the size of a pillow, and a liter of chocolate milk. She put it all on the counter. The kid rang it up. It came to fourteen dollars and something.

She handed him a twenty. A U.S. one, soft from the swim.

He held it up to the light and looked at it like she'd handed him a foreign stamp. Then he looked at a little card taped to the register and punched some buttons and gave her back eight dollars and change. Canadian. A blue five, a two-tone toonie with a polar bear on it, a gold loonie with a bird on it. Quarters with caribou.

"Exchange rate," he said, and pointed at the card. "We give you a little extra on American."

"Great," said Leah.

She ate the first hot dog standing at the counter in three bites. The second one in two. She tore the plastic off the flip-flops with her teeth and dropped them on the floor and stepped into them, and they were a size too small and the plastic tag was still stuck to the left one with one of those little nylon threads, and she didn't care. She chugged half the chocolate milk.

The kid watched all of this with his mouth slightly open.

"Long day?" he said finally.

"Where's a bar?"

"Like—" He blinked. "Like a bar bar?"

"Like a bar bar."

"Uh. There's the lounge at the hotel up the hill, but that's mostly ferry people. Tourists, and, like, guys from Vancouver in fleece." He pointed out the window, down the main drag toward the harbor. "Or there's the Slack Tide. Down by the government wharf. That's where my dad drinks."

"What's the difference?"

He thought about it seriously.

"The Slack Tide has a pool table," he said. "And my dad."

Leah picked up the jerky and the rest of the chocolate milk.

"Slack Tide," she said.

---

She walked down toward the harbor with the rain in her face and the flip-flops smacking against her heels and the plastic tag on the left one ticking against the sidewalk every other step. The lights were on in the boats now, yellow squares in the wheelhouses. Somewhere out on the water a radio was playing country music.

The Slack Tide was at the bottom of the hill, right on the water, in the ground floor of an old two-story building with cedar shakes on the outside gone black with moss. Neon beer signs in the windows: Kokanee, Molson, a Lucky Lager with the L burned out so it said UCKY. A wooden board over the door with the name burned into it and a carved salmon underneath. A couple of pickups out front, and a guy smoking under the eave with his hood up who gave her flip-flops a long look.

She stopped on the sidewalk across the street and looked at the place.

Then she turned her head, very slowly, and looked down at the road.

The bright line was right there. Crossing the street in front of her. Fresh, sharp, pine and earth and cold stone and that wire of burnt lightning running through it, laid down across the wet pavement as clear as a painted stripe, and going straight in the front door of the Slack Tide.

Leah closed her eyes.

"Of course," she said to nobody. "Of course it does."

She opened them again. She thought about the hotel lounge up the hill. Guys from Vancouver in fleece. She thought about turning around and walking back to the motel strip and finding the cheapest room in town and lying in it with the jerky and the rest of the chocolate milk and staring at the ceiling until she fell asleep.

Her wolf was standing up inside her now. It had quit pulling. It stood there and waited to see what she'd do.

"I'm getting a drink," Leah told it. "That's all. That's the whole plan."

She crossed the street, stepped right over the bright line, and pushed open the door.

## IV. Bev

Monday nights at the Slack Tide, you got the regulars and you got the weather.

Bev Tremblay had owned the place for twenty-two years and tended bar in it for six before that, back when it was still the Harbourview and Ernie Sampson's dad had the license, and in all that time Monday night had never once surprised her. Nine people tonight, counting her. Ernie at the end of the bar with his crossword and his rye and ginger. The two Bjornson boys off the *Ocean Pearl* at the corner table, sunburned and half asleep, splitting a pitcher and an order of wings. Marlene and Don by the window, not talking, the way they hadn't been talking since 1993. TSN on the TV over the bar with the sound off, hockey preseason scores crawling across the bottom. The Weather Network on the other one, a lady in a red blazer pointing at a big green blob coming in off the Pacific.

And at the pool table, Dale Kowalchuk, losing money to the South African.

Third night running.

He'd come in Saturday a little after eight. Tall—six-two, maybe a hair over—and wide through the shoulders the way the fallers got after ten years on a saw, though he wasn't a faller; his hands were wrong for it, too clean. Dark hair that needed cutting and stuck up in the back no matter what he did with it, and she'd watched him do plenty, running his hand through it every time he missed a shot. Green eyes, the color of a beer bottle held up to the light. Three days of stubble. A brown leather jacket that had seen better decades, a green-and-black flannel over a black T-shirt, jeans, work boots. He'd said *Evening* in an accent she'd put down as Australian, and when she'd said so, he'd said *South African* the way some men admitted they were from Saskatchewan.

He'd paid for his first beer in American money. For his second one he'd dug in his jacket and slapped down a coin she'd never seen in her life—big, heavy, gold, stamped with something she couldn't make out—and then looked at it, and said, "Wrong pocket," and swept it back up before she could get a good look. Since then he'd paid in Canadian, and most of the Canadian had come out of Dale Kowalchuk's wallet.

It was a hustle. Bev knew a hustle. Her late husband Gerry had run the exact same one in every beer parlour between here and Nanaimo for fifteen years: lose the first game by a hair, look sheepish, offer double-or-nothing, then get *lucky*. Three nights in a row the South African had lost his first game to Dale by one ball. Three nights in a row he'd gotten lucky after. Saturday Dale had dropped forty dollars. Sunday, sixty.

She let it ride. Dale was a loudmouth who'd been telling everybody in town for a decade that he was the best stick on the North Island, and the humility would do him good. And the South African tipped, and ate like a logging crew—three orders of fish and chips tonight, and he'd asked if the kitchen did pie—and never once raised his voice. But she'd told him Sunday, plain, leaning on the bar: *You start a fight in my place, you're done. I don't care how much you tip.*

He'd grinned at her. A crooked, lazy, I-know-something-you-don't kind of grin that made her want to throw a lime at his head.

"Bev," he'd said, "I'd never."

She'd been tending bar long enough to know what *I'd never* was worth.

There were other things. Little things. She didn't think about them much, because there wasn't anything to think, but they stuck to her the way little things did. The beam by the hallway to the washrooms hung down low, six-foot-six maybe, and at six-two he'd clear it by four inches—but every time he went back there he ducked. Every single time. Like a man who'd banged his head on it once, except she'd been watching since Saturday and he never had. The green Kokanee lamp over the pool table swung when he leaned in under it to shoot, swung like somebody had brushed it, when there was a good foot of air between it and the top of his head. Draft from the door, she figured. The door was always letting in a draft.

And Moose wouldn't come out.

Moose was her dog. Eleven years old, yellow Lab, gone white in the face and fat as a sausage, and he spent every night of his life on the blanket in the back hallway by the kitchen door that Bev pretended the health inspector didn't know about. Moose loved everybody. Moose had loved every drunk, every deckhand, every fisheries officer, every tourist in fleece who'd ever walked in this door. He went around the room every night with his tail going like a windshield wiper, collecting fries.

Since Saturday he hadn't left the hallway. He lay on his blanket with his chin on his paws and watched the South African, all night, without blinking.

*Smart dog*, Bev thought. *Knows a hustler.*

Tonight Dale was down eighty. They were on the last game of the night, and Dale was talking too loud and chalking his cue too much, and the South African was leaning on his stick with his weight on one hip, looking at the table like he was trying to remember where he'd parked.

"Quit stalling, Saffa," Dale said.

"I'm thinking."

"Think faster."

"I'm thinking about the eight off the long rail into the corner," said the South African, "and how much I'm going to enjoy your face when it goes in."

Over at the corner table, one of the Bjornson boys snorted into his beer.

Then the door opened, and the rain came in, and so did the girl.

Bev looked up out of habit, the way she looked up at every door. Then she kept looking.

Tall. Five-ten, five-eleven. Native, maybe from down south, maybe from up the coast; Bev had been in Port Hardy long enough to know better than to guess out loud. Black hair chopped short, ragged, like she'd done it herself with kitchen scissors, and soaking wet. Long arms and long legs and shoulders on her like a swimmer. Twenty-five, Bev would've said, if you'd asked her. Twenty-six.

And dressed for August. That was what stuck. A black tank top with a hole in the side, and a pair of cutoff jean shorts stiff and white along the seams like they'd been dunked in the ocean and dried on a fence. Bare arms in September rain. Bare legs. A pair of pink rubber flip-flops so new the plastic tag was still dangling off one of them by a nylon thread. Under one arm she had a bag of beef jerky the size of a sofa cushion, and in her other hand a liter carton of chocolate milk, half gone.

Runaway, Bev thought. Or trouble. Or both, which was what it usually was.

The girl stood just inside the door with the rain dripping off her and breathed in, slow, through her nose. The way you'd breathe at the top of a mountain. Then her shoulders went stiff, and her jaw went tight, and she walked to the bar and sat down on the stool right in front of Bev and set the jerky and the chocolate milk on the counter.

"Hi," the girl said. "I want a beer."

"I want to see some ID."

The girl gave her a look. It was a good look, flat and dark and tired, a look that had been practicing on people for a while. Then she dug in the back pocket of her shorts and came up with a sandwich bag, of all things, and opened it, and slid a driver's license across the bar.

Washington State. The photo was the girl, a couple of years younger, with longer hair and her mouth set the same way. LEAH CLEARWATER. An address in someplace called La Push. Bev did the math on the birthday.

"Nineteen," Bev said.

"Nineteen's legal here."

"Nineteen's legal here," Bev agreed, and slid the card back. "Long way from home, honey."

"That's the idea." The girl put the license back in the sandwich bag. "Whatever's cheapest and comes in the biggest glass."

Bev pulled her a pint of Lucky. The girl took a twenty out of the sandwich bag, American, soft and a little damp, and put it on the bar.

"American's at par in here," Bev said.

The girl's eyes narrowed. "Kid at the gas station gave me extra."

"Then go drink at the gas station."

The girl looked at her for a long second. Then she pushed the twenty across the wood with one finger, and Bev took it, and made change in Canadian, and didn't feel bad about it at all. Rent was rent.

The girl picked up the pint.

She got it halfway to her mouth. Then her head turned, and Bev watched it happen: watched it turn toward the back of the room, toward the pool table and the green lamp, and stop there. Just stop. Like somebody had grabbed her chin.

Bev followed the look.

The South African was bent over the table under the Kokanee lamp, lining up his eight-ball off the long rail. Cue drawn back. One eye shut. His jacket hiked up in the back, and his flannel, and the lamp swinging a little over him in the draft. Forty dollars on the rail. Dale standing behind him with his arms folded, glaring a hole in the back of his skull.

That was all. A guy shooting pool.

The girl was staring at him like he was a bear that had walked in off the street and ordered a Kokanee.

Bev had seen women look at men across this bar every way there was to look, in twenty-eight years. Hungry and sad and mean and drunk and in love and sizing up a wallet. She'd never seen one look at a man like that. All the color had gone out of the girl's face. Her mouth was open. The beer was frozen halfway to it, tilting, a slow drip of foam running down the outside of the glass and over her knuckles, and she didn't seem to notice.

At the pool table, the South African went still.

Bev saw it. He'd been about to shoot. His shoulders had been loose, the cue sliding back for the stroke. And then it all just stopped, everything at once, the way a deer stopped on the highway when the headlights hit it. He stayed bent over the table. He didn't shoot.

Slowly, he turned his head, and looked over his shoulder, straight down the length of the bar at the girl.

*Oh, hell*, thought Bev Tremblay. *Here we go.*

She reached for a rag.

## V. Leah

There was a wolf at the pool table.

That was the first thing her brain told her, and it told her loudly, and it kept telling her even after she'd blinked twice. There was a wolf at the pool table, under the green Kokanee lamp, holding a cue.

Standing up. On two legs.

He was huge. Six-seven if he was an inch, a head taller than the guy behind him, and the lamp hung so low over the table that the tips of his ears were brushing the bottom of the shade. That was why it was swinging. Charcoal-black fur all over, going nearly pure black at the roots where the rain had gotten into it and spiked it up along the back of his neck. A long wolf's muzzle. Black lips. A black nose. Pointed ears, tall, the left one cocked a little sideways like it had a mind of its own.

And clothes. A brown leather jacket with the collar worn pale, a green-and-black flannel, a black T-shirt, a pair of old jeans faded at the knees. Out the back of the jeans, through a slit somebody had cut and hemmed with real care, hung a tail. Long and black and thick, and so still it might have been painted on.

No shoes. His feet—*paws*, her brain corrected, staring—were bare on the sticky floorboards, five-toed and black-padded, with dark claws. He stood flat on them, heels down, like a man. The forepaws on the cue had five clawed digits each, thick black pads on the palms, wrapped around the wood as easy as fingers.

Nobody was screaming.

That was the second thing. Leah sat on her stool with the beer halfway to her mouth and the foam running down over her knuckles, and she looked around the room, and nobody was screaming. The two sunburned guys at the corner table were drinking their pitcher. The old man at the end of the bar was doing his crossword. The couple by the window were looking at nothing. The big guy in the Stihl hat standing behind the pool table, the one who'd called him *Saffa*, was glaring at the back of the wolf's head the way you'd glare at a guy who'd taken your parking spot.

The bartender was wiping a glass.

*Okay*, Leah thought, very calmly. *So that's it. Six months of voices and you finally cracked. Mom's going to have to drive up here and get you from a Canadian psych ward in a town she'd never heard of until this afternoon.*

Then she breathed in.

And there it was. Pine and black earth and cold creek stone, and the bones of the pack underneath, and that wire of burnt lightning running through all of it—the same smell from the motel lot, the same bright line across the road outside. In here it was everywhere. Soaked into the felt of the pool table and the grip of every cue in the rack and the air around the green lamp, laid down so thick she could have walked through it blindfolded.

So she wasn't crazy. Or she was crazy in a very specific way that smelled exactly like what she was looking at.

At the pool table, the wolf went still.

It was a different kind of still from before. Before, he'd been loose, drawing the cue back, about to shoot. Now every part of him stopped at once. And then the first thing that moved was his ears. Both of them, swiveling around backward on his skull like a pair of satellite dishes, until they were pointed straight at her.

His head came around after them.

He looked at her down the length of the bar. Green eyes. Bright, cold green, like beach glass with the sun behind it, sitting in all that black fur. He looked at her face, and then at the beer in her hand, and then back at her face, and she watched him see that she was looking back. Really looking. At his ears, at his muzzle, at the tail.

The ridge of fur over his left eye went up.

Slowly. One eyebrow, or what passed for one on a wolf, lifting like he'd heard a joke he didn't think was funny yet. And at the same time his nose moved. The black leather at the end of his muzzle flexed, and his nostrils flared wide, once and then again, pulling in air from all the way across the room—past the beer and the fryer grease and the wet coats and the old dog in the back hallway—and sorting through it. Taking her apart. Salt water and cedar and kelp and the strait and the hot dogs from the gas station and, under all that, her.

Leah knew exactly what he was doing. She'd done it to a thousand people.

The jukebox in the corner was playing Bad Company. The bass line thumped in the floor. Under it, so low that the guy in the Stihl hat three feet away didn't so much as twitch, the wolf spoke.

"You smell the way I look, sweetheart."

The voice was rough and low and it came out of that muzzle clear as a radio. An accent she couldn't place—flat vowels, the corners of the words clipped off. British, almost. Australian, almost. Somewhere south of both.

"Reckon that means neither of us is quite what we seem," he said.

Nobody else heard it. Leah watched them not hear it. The old man turned a page. The bartender put down her glass. The guy in the Stihl hat said, at a normal volume, "Saffa. You shooting or what?"

It was a test. He'd said it at a volume nothing human could catch from forty feet away, and he'd waited to see what she'd do with it.

And she'd heard. And her face had told him she'd heard. She'd already failed.

*Fine*, she thought.

She set the pint down on the bar. She kept her voice just as low as his, under the music, and pitched it right at him.

"You look like a Halloween costume somebody really committed to."

His left ear flicked sideways.

Then his lips peeled back from his teeth, and he grinned at her.

It was a lot of teeth. White, and long, and sharp, a whole wolf's mouthful of them, and the canines at the corners were the length of her pinky finger. Black lips curled back off them. A flash of pink tongue. It was the kind of grin that belonged on the front of a motorcycle jacket or the side of a van, the kind of grin that had gotten somebody kicked out of a lot of places.

Something low in Leah's stomach turned over and sat up.

*Oh, no*, she thought. *No. Not the teeth.*

She'd spent half a year in a pack of guys with teeth. But that was inside the wolf; you phased, you got the teeth, you phased back, you got your boring human mouth. Nobody walked around showing them off. Nobody walked into a bar and grinned at you with them like they were a pair of brass knuckles he'd been waiting all night to show somebody.

She resented it. She resented it a lot. Her stomach didn't care.

He turned back to the table. He bent over the cue again, and drew it back, and shot.

He blew it. The cue tip skidded off the side of the white ball with an ugly *click*, and the white went sideways into the long rail and bounced off and rolled, slow and stupid, straight into the side pocket. The eight ball rolled a foot and a half and stopped dead an inch from the corner.

"*Scratch*," said the guy in the Stihl hat, and threw both arms up in the air. "*Scratch!* Ha! Pay up, Saffa!"

The wolf straightened up. He looked at the white ball in the pocket. He looked at the eight, sitting there an inch from the corner. He looked down the bar at Leah, and his ears went back, just a little.

"That," he said, at a normal volume this time, "was on you."

"I didn't do anything," said Leah.

"You know what you did."

The guy in the Stihl hat was already scooping the money off the rail, two twenties, counting them with his tongue between his teeth like a kid with a report card. "Double or nothing," he said. "Come on. One more. Double or nothing."

"Not tonight, Dale."

"Aw, come on—"

"I'm distracted." The wolf set his cue in the rack on the wall. "You'd only take advantage."

He came down the room toward the bar. He had to duck under the Kokanee lamp, and his ear still caught the edge of the shade on the way out and set it swinging, and the bartender glanced up at it and frowned at the door. He walked the length of the floor on those big black-padded footpaws, heel and then toe, and Leah listened for him, because she listened for everything, and the floorboards didn't make a sound. Not one creak. Two hundred-plus pounds of wolf—a lot more, probably—on old fir boards in a hundred-year-old building, and nothing.

As he passed the hallway to the back, an old yellow dog lying on a blanket in the shadows lifted its head off its paws and watched him go by, and then put its head back down very carefully, like it didn't want to draw attention.

He took the stool next to hers. One over, actually—he left the one between them empty, and swung up onto the far one, and it creaked under him like it was thinking about quitting. His tail went over the side of it and hung down. Up close, he was even bigger. She had to look up at him, and Leah Clearwater was five-eleven and didn't look up at a lot of people.

He was hot. She could feel it from two feet away, through the air, the way you felt a woodstove. Hotter than Jacob. Hotter than her.

And she could hear his heart. Slow and heavy and steady, a big muffled thump, like somebody knocking on a door at the far end of a long hall.

"Bev," he said. "Another Kokanee, when you've got a sec. And whatever the lady's having, put it on Dale."

"The hell it is," said Dale, from the pool table.

"On me, then."

The bartender looked at him over the top of her glasses. "With what money?"

"Oi," said the wolf, wounded.

Bev pulled the Kokanee. She set it in front of him, and then she looked at Leah, and then at him, and then at Leah again, and her face did a whole slow complicated thing that ended up somewhere around *I don't get paid enough for this*. She went down the bar to bother the old man with the crossword.

"I can buy my own beer," Leah said.

"At Bev's exchange rate?"

"I heard that," said Bev, from the other end.

"You were meant to," said the wolf.

Leah picked up her Lucky and drank it. All of it, in one long pull, the way she'd been planning to drink it since she'd stood in front of the fridge that morning with her face in her dad's jacket. Cold and thin and a little sour. It went down like water. She put the empty glass back on the bar and waited.

Nothing. Not a flicker. She might as well have had a glass of milk.

He watched her do it. His nose twitched.

"So," Leah said. "What the hell are you?"

"Rude."

"You're a wolf in a leather jacket."

"And you're a wolf in a pair of jean shorts that have been in the ocean." He tipped his muzzle at her in a short, polite almost-sniff. "Salt water. Cedar. Kelp. Big wolf. Female. Young." His ear flicked. "Gas station hot dogs. Two."

"That's creepy."

"You started it."

"*How* did I start it?"

"You walked in here and looked at me." He picked up the Kokanee in one forepaw, the bottle swallowed up in those black pads, the claws clicking against the glass. "Nobody looks at me. That's the whole point."

Leah looked around the room again. At Dale, racking balls by himself and muttering. At the two guys at the corner table, one of whom had looked over at them, at her and then at the wolf, with the face of a guy watching another guy get lucky and not liking it much. At Bev. None of them were looking at the ears. None of them were looking at the tail.

"What do they see?" she said.

"A bloke." He took a drink. "Six-two. Needs a haircut. Bit of a tan. Bad jacket."

"It is a bad jacket."

"It's a *great* jacket. Bev thinks it's a bad jacket. Bev's wrong." He set the bottle down. "Bit of magic. Keeps the locals calm and keeps me from having to answer a lot of questions about my tail in the post office."

"Magic," said Leah.

"Ja."

"Like magic magic."

"Like magic magic."

She waited for him to laugh. He didn't. She thought about it. She thought about the strait, and the ship, and her own paws hitting the beach on the other side. She thought about a family of corpses with gold eyes living in a glass house in the woods, and a baby who'd been born a week ago and could show you pictures by touching your face. In the list of things she'd had to swallow in the last six months, *magic* wasn't even in the top ten.

"So why can I see you?" she said.

"Still working on that." His eyes went over her face, slow, and she let them. "Best guess? Whatever you are, it doesn't take a human's word for things."

He held out a forepaw. Across the empty stool, palm up and then turning sideways, the way you'd offer to shake. Five black claws. Black pads.

"Harry," he said.

Her hand stopped in the air.

It just stopped. Halfway across the empty stool, like it had hit a wall. She heard the name land somewhere in the middle of her chest, right where the pressing hand had been all morning, and she felt her face do something she couldn't stop it from doing.

She hadn't heard anybody say it out loud in a while. Not at home. Her mom couldn't, and Seth wouldn't, and Leah sure as hell wasn't going to. *Harry. Harry Clearwater.* Her dad's name in some stranger's mouth, in a bar at the end of the world, coming out of a wolf.

His ears went up. Then, slowly, they tipped back.

"That a problem?" he said.

"No."

"Your face says it's a problem."

"My face says I need another drink."

He looked at her for a second longer. Then he left the forepaw where it was, out across the stool, and waited.

She shook it.

There was a *crack*. A real one, loud and sharp, like somebody snapping a wet towel. Blue light jumped between his palm pad and her palm—she saw it, a thread of it, bright as a welding arc—and a jolt went up her arm all the way to the shoulder and down into her teeth. She yanked her hand back. He yanked his. The smell hit her a half-second later, sharp and burnt and right up her nose: ozone. Lightning. Like the bluff behind First Beach the morning after the storm.

At the other end of the bar, Bev looked up.

"Static," the wolf said, to her, shaking out his paw. "Sorry. It's the damp."

Bev looked at him for a long, flat moment and went back to her glasses.

Leah held her hand in her lap. Her palm was tingling. Her whole arm was tingling. She opened and closed her fingers.

"Your hand just shocked me," she said.

"Paw."

"*Whatever.*"

"Happens," he said. "Not usually that bad." He flexed his own claws, frowning at them, like they'd done something without asking him. Then he looked up at her. "And you are?"

"Leah."

He waited for the rest of it.

She didn't give it to him.

His ear flicked again. Then he nodded, once, like that was fair, and picked up his beer.

"Well, Leah," he said. "You've just knocked back a pint of Lucky like it was tap water, and you're sitting there stone-cold sober and annoyed about it." He tipped the bottle at her. "Metabolism like a blast furnace?"

She narrowed her eyes at him.

"Thought so," he said. "Join the club, sweetheart. I've had six of these tonight, and I'd pass a breathalyzer." He reached inside his leather jacket. "Lucky for you, I came prepared."

## VI. Harry

The flask was old. Dented on one side where he'd landed on it badly in a ditch outside Lusaka, years back, and there'd been initials on the front once that the inside of his jacket had worn smooth. He set it on the bar between them and left his paw on it.

He knew how it looked. A bloke in a bar at the end of the world, sliding a flask at a girl who'd walked in alone twenty minutes ago. He'd put men through walls for less. So he did it the way you were supposed to.

"Bev," he said. "Two shot glasses, please."

Bev brought them over. She looked at the flask. She looked at him.

"Is that outside liquor in my bar?"

"Medicinal."

"Then it's a five-dollar corkage."

"For a *flask*?"

"For a flask," said Bev, and held out her hand.

He dug through his pockets. Left side of the jacket: receipts, a ferry schedule, half a Mars bar. Right side: a handful of Galleons, which he very much didn't touch, not with Bev standing right there and already suspicious of his money. Jeans: one Canadian twenty, folded small, the last of Dale's. He put it on the bar. Bev made change, and took her five, and went away with the air of a woman who'd won something.

Leah watched all of this with her chin on her fist.

"You're broke," she said.

"I'm between paydays."

"You just lost forty bucks."

"I lost forty bucks because somebody stared at me during my backswing." He uncapped the flask. Poured his own glass first, all the way to the top. The smell came up out of it straight away: smoke and cinnamon and black pepper, and under it the sharp, bright crackle that all the good stuff had, like the air before a storm. He held it up so she could see it, and then he knocked it back.

It went down like a lit match. It always did. A long hot line from the back of his tongue into the middle of his chest, where it opened up and spread out through his ribs and down his arms to the ends of his claws. The first thing in three days that had actually reached him. His ears went loose. He breathed out through his nose, and a thin thread of smoke came out with it—just a wisp, nothing a No-Maj would see in a dim bar unless they were looking, and nobody was.

Leah was looking.

"Did you just—"

"Ja. It does that." He set the glass down, and poured the second one, and pushed it across the empty stool to her. Then he pushed the flask after it, so it sat right in front of her, cap off. "Have a sniff first. Don't take my word for anything."

She gave him a long look. Then she picked up the flask and smelled it, the way he'd smell it—a quick sharp double-sniff, the nostrils flaring—and he watched her sort it. The smoke and the spice and the burn. Nothing else in it, because there wasn't anything else in it. Her eyes came up and found his over the top of the flask.

"It smells like you," she said.

"It's got a kick."

"No. It smells like you. The—" She made a small sharp gesture with her free hand, fingers spreading, like a spark going off. "That."

He didn't have an answer to that one. He took the flask back off her and capped it.

She picked up the glass and drank it.

He watched it hit. Her eyes went wide, then watery, then wide again. She coughed. She put the back of her wrist against her mouth, and her shoulders came up, and he saw the heat roll down through her chest the same way it had through his. And then the color came up into her face—up from her neck, across her cheekbones, into her ears—and she laughed.

Short and startled and out loud. Like it had gotten out before she could stop it.

It changed her whole face. That was the stupid thing. Twenty minutes she'd been sitting there with her mouth in a flat line and her jaw set hard enough to crack walnuts, and then it was gone, and for about two seconds she looked her age.

Harry noticed that, and noticed himself noticing it, and told himself to knock it off.

Behind him, his tail had thumped once against the leg of the stool. He made it stop.

"Oh, my God," Leah said, hoarse. "What *is* that?"

"Imported."

"From where, hell?"

"Scotland. Close enough."

She held out the glass. He looked at it. He looked at her. Then he uncapped the flask and gave her a short one, half what he'd given her before, and she gave him a look like he'd shorted her on purpose, which he had.

He used the time while she drank it to finish taking her apart.

He'd started the second the door opened. Old habit. He'd smelled her before he'd seen her: a big wolf, female, young, so close to his own kind under the salt and the rain that for half a second his whole body had locked up on the backswing. Then the rest of it. Seawater, a lot of it, dried into her clothes and her hair. Cedar. Kelp. Gas-station food. And under all of it, faint, so deep in her hair it must have been there for weeks, something cold and sweet and wrong. Bleach and dead roses and cough syrup.

Vampire. More than one. A lot more than one.

She'd been living in it. That was the thing that had made him look up. Nobody lived that close to vampires and walked into a bar a few weeks later with a heartbeat, unless the vampires were very well-behaved or the person was something they were scared of.

Eleven days past the full moon, too, and she didn't have a werewolf's smell. He knew that smell. Fever and wet dog and wolfsbane and the sour edge of a body that hurt all the time. She smelled healthy. She smelled like a furnace somebody had banked up for the night. Some kind of born shifter, then. The Americas were full of things nobody in the wizarding world had ever bothered to write down, and half the things he'd run into on this continent had looked at him exactly the way she was looking at him now—like he'd shown up uninvited on somebody else's land.

And the name. She'd frozen on *Harry*. Her hand had stopped dead in midair and something had gone across her face that he recognised, because he'd seen it in the mirror for a few years after the war. He didn't ask. He'd been flinched at for his name in a lot of ways in his life. This was a new one. It could keep.

The rest was easy. It was just reading.

"You swam here," he said.

Leah put her glass down. "Excuse me?"

"There's a salt line on your shorts. All the way round, about here." He drew a claw through the air at the level of his own hip. "Dried white. And there's a leather cord on your left ankle, knotted twice, that's been wet and dried out, and it's the kind of knot you tie when you need to carry something and your hands are busy being feet." He nodded down at the floor. "Flip-flops still have the tag on. Your heels have got scrapes on them that are mostly healed already. So you came across the water on four legs, carrying your clothes, and you've been in town less than an hour, and the first thing you did was buy shoes and eat two hot dogs, and the second thing you did was come find a drink."

She stared at him.

"Are you a cop?"

"God, no."

"You talk like a cop."

"I talk like a bloke who hustles pool for a living." He picked up his Kokanee. "Reading people's the job, sweetheart. The pool's just how I get paid for it."

"So you admit it's a hustle."

"I admit nothing. Dale's a grown man."

Which was when Dale arrived.

He'd been working up to it. Harry had heard him over at the pool table, racking up the balls by himself, muttering, sliding the cue back and forth, telling the Bjornson boys what he'd do if he was ten years younger. Now he came down the room in his Stihl hat and his Carhartt vest and leaned on the bar on Leah's other side, close enough that Harry's lip wanted to come up off his teeth. He kept it down.

"Hey," Dale said to Leah. "Hey. Just so you know. This guy? Hustler. Took me for a hundred and eighty bucks since Saturday."

"Hundred and forty," said Harry. "You won forty back."

"Because you *choked*."

"Because I was distracted."

"Because you were making eyes at—" Dale stopped. Looked at Leah. Seemed to realize, a little late, where he was going with that.

Leah looked at Dale for a long moment. Then she looked over her shoulder at the pool table, with the balls racked in their triangle under the green lamp, and something changed in the set of her shoulders.

"You want your money back?" she said.

Dale blinked. "What?"

"Rack 'em. Twenty bucks." She slid off the stool. "Me and you."

"You any good?"

"Guess," said Leah, and walked to the table.

---

She was good.

Harry turned around on his stool with his beer and watched, and it took about four seconds to see it. She played it straight. No sandbagging the first game, no missing on purpose, no letting Dale think he had a chance. She picked a cue off the wall rack without trying any of the others, sighted down it once, chalked it, and broke so hard the crack of it went through the room like a rifle shot. The Bjornson boys both jumped. Two stripes went down.

She ran four more before she missed. Dale got up, and sank two, and blew an easy one in the side pocket, and stood back from the table with his face going red under the hat.

Leah ran the rest. No fuss. No showing off. Every shot straight and hard and mean, like she had somewhere to be after. On the eight, she called the corner pocket and pointed with the end of the cue, and Harry noticed—because he noticed things, it was the job—that before she bent down to shoot, she put her free hand flat on the wooden rail of the table for a second. Just rested it there. The way you'd put your hand on somebody's shoulder.

Then she shot, and the eight went in, and she straightened up and held out her hand to Dale.

Somebody had taught her. Somebody had taught her young, and well, on a table a lot like this one, and had been very proud of her.

He didn't ask about that either.

"You two are working together," Dale said, slapping a twenty into her palm. "This is a setup. You're in on it."

"We met eleven minutes ago," Harry said.

"That's what a setup would say!"

"Dale," said Bev, from behind the bar, not even looking up. "Sit down or go home."

Dale sat down. At the corner table, with the Bjornson boys, where he could glare at both of them over the top of a fresh pint. Moose, in the back hallway, let out a long sigh through his nose and put his chin back on his paws.

Leah came back to the bar. She didn't sit down. She slapped Dale's twenty on the wood right in front of Harry and left her hand on top of it.

"Your next beer," she said, "is on Dale."

He laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, too, a big rough bark of a laugh that made Bev look up and Marlene by the window turn around in her seat, and for a second he forgot to keep the glamour tight on the sound of it, and Bev's eyes narrowed like she'd heard something under the laugh she couldn't put a name to.

He reeled it back in. Bev went back to her glasses.

Leah sat back down. On the stool in between.

---

## VII. Leah

The third shot she made last.

She sat on the middle stool with it between her hands and let it burn. It was still doing the thing. Every sip went down like a road flare and lit up her chest and spread out from there, slow and hot, into her arms and her stomach and the backs of her knees. For the first time in six months there was something in her body that her body couldn't just eat. Her face felt loose. Her shoulders had come down from around her ears. She was warm from the inside, which for somebody who ran at a hundred and eight was saying something.

Drunk was close enough to see from here. She liked the view.

He was on the stool next to her now, with no empty one in between, and the heat came off him like a woodstove with the door open. His knee was six inches from hers. She could hear his heart from here without trying: that slow heavy knock at the end of the long hall. Not one skipped beat. Not one stutter. Not one moment of the dead flat silence she'd spent the last two and a half weeks standing next to.

"So what do you actually do?" she said. "When you're not robbing loggers."

"Freelance."

"Freelance what?"

"Pest control."

She turned her head and looked at him. He was looking at his beer, all innocence, with his ears up.

"What kind of pests?"

"The kind with teeth." He took a drink. "Bit of everything. Ghouls. Things in lakes. Bloke up in Prince Rupert says something's been taking dogs off people's porches, and nobody's seen it, and the RCMP have stopped returning his calls. So I'm going to go up there and have a look."

"Vampires?"

His ears swung toward her. Both of them. He didn't turn his head.

"When they need it," he said.

Leah was quiet a second. Then she said, "So you can smell what's in my hair."

His nose twitched. He'd been waiting for her to bring it up; she could tell. He'd been polite about it all night.

"Bleach," he said. "Dead roses. And something like grape cough syrup that somebody spilled and never cleaned up."

"Yeah."

"Been trying not to mention it."

"It's my whole life right now," Leah said. "Eight of them. Gold eyes, mostly. They eat deer and mountain lions and they've got a treaty with my—with us. They have a house made out of glass in the middle of the woods, and I sit outside it every night and smell them." She turned the shot glass on the bar. "And one of them had a baby last week."

He set his beer down.

"They can't," he said.

"Tell me about it."

"No. I mean they *can't*. That's not—" His ears had gone flat back against his skull. "Their bodies don't change. They don't—that's not a thing that happens."

"It happened. Human mom, vampire dad. She almost died having it. They had to turn her to save her." Leah drank. "Baby's one week old and looks four months. She can show you pictures in your head if she touches your face. And the guy who runs my—the guy I run with—"

She stopped. She didn't know how to say it to somebody who didn't know. Imprinting. Soulmates. The whole world getting unstrung and restrung around a week-old baby, and Jacob so happy about it he couldn't see straight.

"He's in love with it," she said. "With her. Not like *that*. It's a wolf thing. It's a long story."

He looked at her for a long time. His face was hard to read; it was a wolf's face, and she was still learning it. But his ears stayed back, and his nose had wrinkled up a little at the top, and his lips had pulled down at the corners in a way that she figured was the wolf version of what any normal person's face would do.

"I've got till Wednesday," he said finally.

"What's Wednesday?"

"Ferry north. Missed Saturday's by twenty minutes." His ear flicked. "Stood on the dock and watched it pull out. Been stuck here since. Hence Dale."

Leah snorted into her glass. She couldn't help it.

"It's not funny," he said.

"It's a little funny."

"Ja," he admitted. "It's a little funny."

She looked at him. At the stupid leather jacket, and the collar of it turned up on one side and folded down on the other, and the black fur sticking out of the cuffs over his forepaws. At the stubble of shorter fur along his jaw that looked, on a wolf, kind of like stubble. At the way he sat on the stool like he'd been thrown out of better bars than this one and was waiting, without much worry, to get thrown out of this one too. At the place on his forehead, right up under the forelock, where the fur grew in a different direction from everything around it—a little jagged streak, zigzagging down toward his left eye like somebody had drawn it there with a finger.

He caught her looking at it. He reached up and flattened the fur down with one paw, quick and a little embarrassed, like a kid with a cowlick.

It sprang right back up.

"So why *do* you smell like a thunderstorm?" she said.

"Long story."

"You've got till Wednesday."

His lip curled. Not quite a grin. "Somebody tried to kill me when I was a baby. Didn't take. Bit of it stuck around." He shrugged. "That's the short version."

"What's the long version?"

"Much longer."

She waited. He didn't give it to her. She figured that was fair, since she hadn't given him her last name, and she let it go.

"My dad was Harry," she said.

She didn't know she was going to say it until she'd said it. It came out flat and plain and sat there on the bar between them.

He didn't say anything. He turned his head and looked at her and waited.

"He died in March. Heart attack." She turned the glass. "He's the one who taught me pool. On the table at the community center, in La Push. He used to stand behind me and tell me I was holding the cue like it owed me money."

She didn't tell him the rest. That the first time she'd ever phased, it had been in front of him. That the doctors had said heart attack, and that was true, and it was also true that the last thing her dad ever saw clearly was a thing with fur and teeth where his daughter had been standing. That she'd never be able to find out from anybody in the world which one of those had killed him.

Some things you didn't tell a stranger in a bar, even a stranger who'd bought you whisky from Scotland that burned all the way down.

"I'm sorry," he said.

That was all. Two words. He didn't put a hand on her. He didn't tell her it was going to be okay, or that he knew how she felt, or that her dad would've been proud of her for whupping Dale.

She found she was grateful. She drank to it.

"Potter," he said, after a while.

"What?"

"My surname. Potter. Most people go with that. Or *oi, you*." His ear tipped toward her. "You don't have to say the other one."

She tried it. "Potter."

"Ja."

"That's worse."

"It's a terrible name," he agreed. "I've had it my whole life. You get used to it."

She laughed. The second time tonight. It still surprised her, the sound of it.

He grinned at her, and there were the teeth again. Long and white and wicked, the canines curving down over his black bottom lip, a whole mouthful of trouble pointed straight at her. It was a bad-news grin. It was a grin that had probably gotten him punched in a lot of bars and had probably gotten him out of a lot of bars too, depending on who was at them.

"Nice teeth," she said.

"All mine."

"You show them to everybody?"

"Only the ones who can see them." He tipped his head at the room. Dale. The Bjornsons. Bev. "This lot think I've got a nice smile and a bad haircut. You're the first one in a long while to get the full set."

"Lucky me."

"People see a wolf," he said, "and they figure he's going to bite. Doesn't matter what you do. So after a while you quit arguing about it." The grin got wider. "And you just enjoy the teeth."

Leah looked at him.

*They think I'm the bitch*, she thought. *Back home. The bitter one. Poor Leah, crazy Leah, Leah the harpy. Sam's ex. They've all been inside my head and that's what they decided I was, and I quit arguing about it a long time ago.*

She didn't say it. She thought maybe he heard it anyway; his ears had come forward.

"People back home think I'm a bitch," she said instead.

"Are you?"

"Yeah."

"Good," said Potter. "Saves time."

---

She sat there and thought about what she'd come for.

She'd known what she'd come for since she'd stood in her mother's kitchen that morning. She hadn't been able to say it to herself in words until she was in a bar in another country, three drinks deep on something that actually worked, but she'd known. Get drunk. Find somebody who didn't know her name. Do something with him she'd regret, so the regret would at least be her own.

She'd figured on some guy. Some fisherman, some faller, some idiot in fleece up from Vancouver. She'd figured she'd have to be careful with him. Hold back. Watch her hands. She ran at a hundred and eight and she could bend a tire iron, and she'd have to spend the whole night thinking about not breaking him, which was a hell of a way to forget your troubles.

She looked at Potter.

Hot as a stove. A heartbeat she could hear across a room. Pine and black dirt and cold creek stone and lightning, and not one molecule of bleach or dead flowers or grape syrup anywhere on him. Six-foot-seven of fur and claws and teeth, sitting on a stool in a stupid jacket. Nothing cold. Nothing hard as marble. Nothing dead.

You couldn't get further from a leech and still be sitting on a barstool.

He didn't know her last name. He didn't know Sam or Emily or Jacob or the Cullens. He'd never been in her head and never would be. He was getting on a boat Wednesday and going north to look for whatever was eating dogs in Prince Rupert, and she'd never see him again.

And he wouldn't break.

Her heart did something. She felt it go—a hard double-thump, and then faster. She didn't try to stop it. She couldn't have.

Next to her, Potter's nostrils flared.

She watched it happen. The black leather at the end of his muzzle flexed, and his ears came all the way forward, and he went still, the way he'd gone still at the pool table. Then he turned his head and looked at her. Really looked. Those beach-glass eyes, right on her face.

He'd smelled it. Of course he had. She'd have smelled it on him.

She didn't look away.

"Here's what I reckon," he said, low, under the jukebox. Just for her. "You came up here to drink till you forgot your own name. Booze doesn't take, though. Not on you. Not on me." He turned his beer bottle a slow quarter-turn on the bar with one claw. "So that leaves the other thing."

"What other thing?" said Leah.

He leaned in a few inches, enough that the heat of him came over the last of the space between them and she could smell the whisky on him, and the smoke, and the storm.

"If you're anything like me, sweetheart," Potter said, "you're about desperate for a proper tussle in the sheets with somebody who doesn't break if you look at them wrong."

Leah sat very still.

"I don't break," he said.

The jukebox clicked over to something else. Over at the corner table, one of the Bjornson boys said something and Dale laughed, too loud. Bev put a glass up on the rack. Down the hallway, the old yellow dog let out another long sigh.

"That," Leah said, "is the worst line I've ever heard in my life."

"You haven't been to enough bars."

"Does it ever work?"

His ear flicked sideways. "You tell me."

She picked up the shot glass and finished it. All of it, in one go, and let it burn the whole way down, and set the glass upside down on the bar with a click.

"Rules," she said.

"Go on."

"No last names. No phone numbers. You get on your boat Wednesday and you don't look back."

"Done."

"And if you snore, I'm kicking you out of your own bed."

"It's a double," said Potter. "And I'm a big lad. You'll be lucky if you fit."

"I'll fit."

He looked at her for one more second. Then he held out his paw again, across the six inches between them, palm up.

She took it.

The spark jumped again—smaller this time, a quick bright snap she felt all the way up to her elbow, and the smell of ozone curling up between them. She didn't let go. His pads were rough and hot against her palm and his claws rested very lightly across the back of her hand, and she could feel his pulse in them.

"Bev," Potter said, without looking away from her. "We're settling up."

---

Bev came down the bar and took his money, the rest of Dale's twenty and some of Leah's change, and didn't say anything while she rang it in. She just looked at the two of them, back and forth, the same long slow look she'd been giving them all night. Then she leaned across the bar toward Leah and dropped her voice.

"Honey," she said. "You know what you're doing?"

Leah thought about it.

"Nope," she said.

Bev looked at her a second longer. Then she sighed, through her nose, exactly like the dog.

"He paid for three nights at the Seawind up front," Bev said. "In cash. Gloria at the desk told me. If he doesn't pay for the fourth, I'll know who to ask." She straightened up and pointed a finger at Potter. "And you. Don't come back in here tomorrow and try to take any more of Dale's money. He's got a mortgage."

"Wouldn't dream of it, Bev."

"Uh-huh," said Bev.

At the corner table, Dale watched them get up with his mouth hanging open.

"Unbelievable," he said, to nobody. "Un-*believable*."

"Lucky bastard," said one of the Bjornson boys, to his beer.

Leah got her jerky and the rest of her chocolate milk off the bar. Potter held the door. As they went out, the old yellow dog lifted its head off its blanket in the back hallway and watched them go, and didn't put its head back down until the door had swung all the way shut behind them.

---

Outside it was full dark and still raining, the fine cold drizzle that came in off the water and hung in the air under the streetlights like smoke. The harbor was black. The boats creaked at their lines. Somewhere out on the bay, a bell buoy was clanging, slow and lonely.

Across the road, up the hill, the Seawind's red VACANCY sign buzzed against the night. At the far end of the lot, the long black car sat nose-out under the lights, shining wet.

Potter walked beside her up the hill. She listened for him on the wet sidewalk and couldn't hear a thing. Just her own flip-flops, smacking against her heels, and the plastic tag on the left one ticking along beside them every other step.

"Room eleven," he said. "Ground floor. Heater sounds like somebody strangling a goose."

"Great."

"Bed's lumpy."

"You're really selling it."

"Ceiling's low, too. I keep hitting my ears on the light." He glanced down at her. "You'll laugh."

"Probably."

She breathed in. The rain, and the salt, and the woodsmoke, and him. Pine and black earth and cold creek stone and lightning. She breathed it all the way down to the bottom of her lungs and held it there.

No bleach. No dead roses. No grape syrup. Nothing cold. Nothing in it anywhere that made her want to hold her breath.

For the first time in a week, she let it all the way out.

Behind them, down at the bottom of the hill, the highway ran out at the edge of the water. Five hundred kilometers of it, all the way back down the island, and then the strait, and then the mountains, and then a little gray house in La Push with a jacket on a hook by the back door and a note on the fridge. It would all still be there in the morning. She knew that. She'd have to go back to it.

Not tonight.

At the edge of the motel lot, she stopped and looked up at him. He stopped too. The rain had gotten into his fur and spiked it up all over again along his neck, and his ears were turned toward her, and his tail had come up off the backs of his legs and was moving, very slowly, side to side.

He was watching her. Waiting. Like he'd waited with his paw out across the empty stool.

Leah Clearwater had run three hundred miles and swum a strait to get to the end of the road.

She took a step toward room eleven, and held on to his paw, and pulled.

"Come on, Potter," she said. "Show me what doesn't break."
