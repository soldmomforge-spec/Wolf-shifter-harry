# North of Everything

## Chapter One: Tamarack Crossing

---

The night the pack broke, Leah Clearwater was standing in the rain behind the Uleys' woodshed with her ears flat against her skull, listening to nine other minds scream.

It was a lot of screaming. It was always a lot of screaming, but that night it had a shape to it. Sam at the center of it like a fist. Paul's anger, red and stupid. Jared already moving, already planning angles of attack, the way he always did when Sam said *go*. Embry and Quil pulling back from the edges of it, sick. Collin and Brady, fourteen years old and thrilled to their bones, because they were fourteen and nobody had told them yet what it felt like when something you'd bitten stopped moving.

And Jacob. Jacob in the middle of all of it, a hole in the noise.

*The thing she's carrying is a danger to the tribe,* Sam was saying, and it was Sam saying it, but it was also the Alpha saying it, which meant it went into every one of them like a hook through the gut. *We can't let it be born. We go tonight. We kill it, and if the Cullens stand in the way, we kill them too.*

*It,* Leah thought, and then tried not to think anything at all, because the trouble with the pack mind was that every thought you had was everybody's thought.

*It's Bella,* Jacob was saying. *It's Bella in there, Sam.*

*Bella's already gone. You saw her. You told us.*

Leah had seen her too, through Jacob's memory, forty minutes ago. Bella Swan—Bella Cullen, whatever—propped up on a couch in that glass house, ribs like a xylophone, a belly that looked like it was eating her from the inside out. Leah had spent a whole year wishing bad things on Bella Swan. Now one had shown up, and she couldn't stand to look at it.

*We go tonight,* Sam said again, and the hook pulled.

Leah felt it pull. Felt her own legs bunch under her without asking permission. That was the worst part of it, always. She'd taken plenty of orders. She'd grown up the daughter of a council elder; she knew how to swallow things. What she couldn't stand was the way the Alpha's voice went past all the parts of her that got a vote and grabbed the parts that didn't.

And then Jacob said no.

The rest of them talked in the pack mind the way water talked, all leaking and overlapping. Jacob said it like a door slamming. Leah felt it in her teeth. Felt something in the air between them all go tight as wire, and then tighter, and then—

It ripped.

There was no other word for it. One second Jacob was there, a hot furious knot of *no* in the middle of everything, and the next second there was a hole where he'd been, a hole that went down forever, and the whole pack lurched sideways like a truck losing a wheel. Paul howled. Sam staggered—she felt him stagger, felt the shock of it go through him, and under the shock a flash of something she would think about later, much later, when she could stand to: *Ephraim's line. Of course. Of course it would be him.*

Jacob Black had just made himself an Alpha. Right there in the rain. Because Billy's great-grandfather had been chief, and the blood remembered, and when push came to shove the blood had shoved back.

*Jake,* Seth said. Seth, her little brother, fifteen and gangly even as a wolf, standing six feet to her left with his ears straight up. *Jake, wait—*

*Seth,* Leah thought. *Don't.*

He didn't even hear her. Or he heard her and didn't care, which with Seth was the same thing. She felt him reach for that hole Jacob had left, felt him lean into it the way you'd lean into a wind, and then he was gone too. A quiet *click* this time, like someone closing a car door down the street. His voice cut out mid-thought. The space where Seth had been, where Seth had always been since the day she'd phased and found her baby brother already in her head, went dark.

Leah stood very still.

*Leah,* Sam said.

She didn't answer him.

*Leah. I need you with us. I need everybody.*

She could feel the others feeling her. Jared's suspicion. Paul's contempt, which was nothing new. Embry's nervous attention, Quil's. And Sam—Sam underneath all of it, the way he was always underneath all of it, the way he'd been underneath everything since she was sixteen years old and stupid enough to think she knew what forever meant. His guilt was there, like always, a low bad smell. His worry. And behind both, because it was always behind both, the soft gold glow of Emily. Emily's face. Emily's hands. Emily laughing in the kitchen with flour on her scarred cheek and Sam's heart doing that thing it did, that awful helpless thing, that Leah had been forced to feel from the inside every single day for a year.

She could feel the place where Jacob had torn through. She could feel it still, a raw edge, the way you could feel a pulled tooth with your tongue. It was still open. There was a way out.

*Leah,* Sam said, and this time it had the hook in it. *Stay.*

She went through the hole.

For one second, Jacob was there. She landed in his head like falling into cold water—Jacob running flat out toward the Cullens' house, Jacob's shock, *Leah? What the hell are you*—and Seth behind him, Seth's whole mind lighting up like a kid on Christmas morning because his big sister had come after him, *Leah! Leah, you came, you—*

She didn't stop.

She didn't know she could do it until she was already doing it. She just kept going, through Jacob's raw new pack and out the other side, the way you'd run through a house and out the back door without stopping to say hello to anyone. She felt Jacob grab for her with surprise, with something like hurt. He didn't even know how to give an order yet. She felt Seth reach after her with his whole heart.

She pulled. Something in her chest went taut and then went *snap*.

And then there was nothing.

---

Silence.

Leah stood in the rain and listened to it.

The forest was still loud. Rain on the cedar boughs and the ferns and the tin roof of the woodshed. Surf booming down on First Beach, heavy and slow, the way it did in September.

But inside her head there was nobody but her.

She waited for it to come back. For Sam's voice, for Paul's sneer, for Seth's stupid happy chatter. She waited for Emily's face to slide up behind her eyes the way it always did, like a stain coming through fresh paint.

Nothing came.

Leah sat down in the mud. She couldn't help it. Her legs just went. She sat there on her haunches in the rain, a gray wolf the size of a pony, and she shook. She didn't know if she was crying. Wolves didn't really cry. But her eyes were hot and her throat was locked tight, and when she finally made a sound it came out as a thin high whine she'd never heard herself make before.

Quiet. It was so quiet.

She'd forgotten what her own head sounded like when it was empty. She'd forgotten what it was like to have a thought and know it was going to stay hers.

She thought, experimentally: *Sam Uley is a coward who broke my heart and then made me watch him be happy about it.*

Nobody heard.

She thought: *Emily, I loved you. You were my cousin. You were my best friend. I hope you're happy and I hope sometimes it hurts.*

Nobody heard.

She thought: *Dad. I miss you so much I can't breathe.*

And nobody heard that either, and that was the one that did it. That was the one that put her face down on her paws in the mud behind Sam Uley's woodshed and kept it there until the rain had soaked all the way down to her skin.

---

The Cullens' house was where Seth was. Jacob too. If she went and stood on that lawn that reeked like bleach and burnt sugar, Seth would bounce around her like a puppy and she'd get sucked back into a pack mind—a smaller one, still a room full of other people's thoughts. Jacob would think about Bella and Seth would think about how great the Cullens were, and she'd have to *feel* it. The whole sad soap opera.

She'd already done a year of feeling other people's love stories from the inside. She was done.

So she ran home.

The Clearwater house sat at the end of a gravel road behind a stand of alder, a little two-bedroom with blue trim her dad had painted the summer she was twelve. Mom's car was in the drive. Leah phased in the trees out back, stood shivering and naked in the rain while her body remembered how to be a body, and went in through the kitchen door, which nobody on the rez ever locked.

The house smelled like it always smelled. Coffee. The lemon cleaner Mom liked. Fish, faintly, from dinner. Under all of it, so faint now that she wasn't sure if she was really smelling it or just remembering, the cigarettes Dad used to sneak on the back step when he thought nobody knew.

She went down the hall to her room without turning on a light. She didn't need lights anymore. Hadn't for a year.

Jeans. Two pairs of cutoffs. A couple of tank tops, a hoodie, her good flannel, a stack of underwear and socks. She rolled all of it tight and jammed it into the waterproof dry bag Dad had bought her for kayaking the year before he died, the yellow one with the roll-top. She took the coffee can down from the top of her closet and counted what was inside it by feel. Eleven hundred and forty dollars. Two years of shifts at the Thriftway and babysitting the Ateara kids and every birthday card from every aunt. The college fund. Leah had been going to go to UW. Leah had been going to do a lot of things.

She rolled the money into a sandwich bag and the sandwich bag into the dry bag.

Then she went and stood in the doorway of her mother's room.

Sue Clearwater slept on her side, curled toward the half of the bed that had been empty for six months. She looked older asleep. She looked small. There was a framed picture on the nightstand, the one of all four of them at Ruby Beach, Dad grinning with his arm around Mom's shoulders, Seth missing his front teeth, Leah at eleven in a too-big Seahawks shirt scowling at the camera because the wind was blowing her hair in her mouth.

Leah stood there for a long time.

She could wake her up. She could sit on the edge of the bed and tell her. *Jacob broke from Sam. Seth went with him. I went with nobody. I can't do it anymore, Mom. I can't be in that room with all of them. I have to go.* And Mom would sit up and hold her and smell like coffee and lemon cleaner, and say something wise, because Sue Clearwater always said something wise. And Leah would cry, and Mom would make her tea, and in the morning there'd be a council meeting and Old Quil and Billy would be there and everyone would talk about duty and blood and the tribe. And Leah would stay. Because that was what you did.

She went back to the kitchen and wrote a note on the back of a Thriftway receipt.

*Mom—*

*I'm leaving for a while. Jake split off from Sam tonight and Seth went with him. He's fine. He's with the Cullens, which is gross, but he's safe there. I didn't go with either of them. I couldn't.*

*I don't know where I'm going. I just need to be somewhere nobody can hear me think. You didn't do anything wrong.*

*Don't let them send anybody after me. I mean it.*

*I love you. Tell Seth he's an idiot and I love him too.*

*—L.*

She stuck it to the fridge under the magnet shaped like a salmon. Then she went out the back door, tied the dry bag to her ankle with the leather cord she'd been using all summer, walked into the trees until the porch was just a shape in the dark, and let the wolf come up through her like a wave.

---

North.

It was a feeling more than a decision. North was where the trees got thicker and the towns got thinner. North was away from Forks and La Push and the Cullens and the whole stinking mess of it. North was where Jacob had gone last summer, when Bella sent him that wedding invitation and he'd run until he stopped being a person for a while. Leah had hated him for that. She'd hated him for getting to leave, for getting to be sad somewhere private, while the rest of them had to listen to Sam worry about him every night.

She got it now. She got it all the way down.

She couldn't go straight north. The Strait of Juan de Fuca sat on top of the peninsula like a lid, fifteen miles of black cold water, and she wasn't stupid enough to swim it in September with a dry bag tied to her leg. So she went east first, through the logging cuts and the second-growth fir, around the bottom of Hood Canal and the bottom of the Sound. She crossed I-5 at three in the morning at a dead sprint between a semi and a minivan, and somebody honked, and she was gone into the trees before they could have been sure of what they saw.

Then up into the Cascades, where the air thinned and went cold and smelled like rock and snow and nothing else. That was better. She ran ridgelines with the wind coming at her sideways and her fur standing out like a brush. She ran until her pads were raw and kept running until they healed, because they always healed. One of the perks of being a freak.

She crossed into Canada somewhere in the North Cascades on her second night out. No fence, no sign, no border guard. More mountains, more forest, a creek that ran north. She only knew she'd crossed because the next highway she came to had its signs in kilometers.

She stopped on the far side of it and stood there, panting, looking at a green sign that said *HOPE 26*.

Hope. Twenty-six kilometers.

If she'd still been human she would've laughed.

---

She lost track of the days after that.

She ran mostly at night, because the nights were the longest and the emptiest and she liked the stars up here, so many of them it was almost stupid.

She hunted when she got hungry, which was always. Deer, mostly. A mountain goat on a cliff above the Fraser that she nearly fell off a mountain going after, and caught anyway, and ate on a ledge three hundred feet up while an eagle circled overhead screaming at her about it.

She slept wherever she dropped. Once, curled nose to tail in a hollow under the roots of a blown-down spruce with the first snow coming down outside, she slept fourteen hours straight and woke up feeling like somebody had put her back together with all the pieces in the right places.

And the whole time, her head stayed quiet.

That was the part she couldn't get over. She kept waiting for the silence to get lonely, the way everybody always said it would. She kept waiting to miss them. And some nights she did—some nights she'd lie there and think about Seth and her chest would ache, and once she'd woken up from a dream about Dad so real she could smell his flannel and had to get up and run for an hour to make it go away. But the loneliness never got as loud as the voices used to be. Not even close.

She let herself think about Sam. The bonfire the summer she was sixteen, where he'd kissed her the first time. The morning he came back after two weeks missing, all muscle and fever heat and new terrible silences. The day Emily came to visit, and Sam looked at her, and the whole world tilted.

She'd been so angry for so long that she'd forgotten anger was supposed to burn down eventually. Up here, with nobody watching, she let it. She let it burn. She ran it down into the ground under her paws, mile after mile after mile, and somewhere north of Williams Lake she noticed she'd gone a whole day without thinking about Sam at all.

Emily was harder. Emily had been her cousin, her best friend, the maid of honor in the wedding Leah used to plan in the backs of notebooks. Emily had done nothing wrong except get looked at. You couldn't even hate her properly, because imprinting was magic, everybody kept saying so. Destiny. The wolf. Nobody chose it.

Well. Leah had a wolf too. Leah's wolf looked at the world and got mad. That was pretty much its whole personality.

Up here, Leah's wolf seemed to think that was fine.

---

She went north through the Cariboo and the Chilcotin, through country so empty she went three days without crossing a road, then northwest into the Skeena country, where the rivers ran green and loud with the last of the salmon. A grizzly sow fishing a riffle looked up at her once across the water, a long flat considering look, and Leah looked back, and they agreed on something without either of them making a sound, and Leah went the long way around.

She picked up a highway somewhere past the Skeena, a narrow two-lane ribbon running north through the forest with almost no traffic on it. Highway 37, the signs said. The Stewart-Cassiar. She ran beside it in the trees for two days, using it like a compass. Once a camper van came by with Alberta plates, and she smelled the people inside it—sunscreen, cheap coffee, a dog—and her stomach clenched up with something like homesickness. Homesickness for french fries, apparently.

She wanted french fries. She wanted coffee. She wanted a shower and a bed with sheets on it. She wanted to talk to somebody, just once, about nothing.

She was standing on a ridge in the late afternoon, eighteen or twenty days out of La Push—she'd honestly lost count—when the wind shifted and brought her the smell of a fryer.

Leah lifted her head.

Below her the land dropped away into a long valley, a river glinting through it like a dropped knife, and where the highway crossed the river there was a town, if you could call it that. A gas station with two pumps. A motel shaped like an L. A diner with a red roof. A white church. A bar with a lot of pickups outside it. Thirty or forty houses scattered up the hillside, smoke going straight up from their chimneys into the still air.

Leah's stomach made a noise like a garbage truck.

She stood there on the ridge with the wind in her fur and looked down at the little town for a long time. Then she trotted back down into the trees, found a hollow behind a big spruce where nobody would see, and let herself go human for the first time in almost three weeks.

It hurt, the first time after a long stretch, like stretching out a leg you'd been sitting on. She came up on her knees in the spruce needles, naked and gasping, and the cold hit her like a slap. She ran hot—they all did, a hundred and eight resting—so it didn't bother her the way it should've, but it still raised every hair on her body. She untied the dry bag with fingers that had forgotten how to be fingers and got dressed. Jeans. Tank top. Flannel. Hoodie over that, mostly for show. Her hair was full of needles and twigs. She picked out what she could and gave up on the rest.

She stood up, swaying a little on two legs, and looked down at her own hands. They looked strange. Too small. Too pale at the knuckles.

"Okay," she said out loud.

Her voice came out cracked and rusty. She hadn't used it in three weeks. It sounded stupid in the empty forest, and she laughed, and the laugh sounded stupid too.

"Okay," she said again, better this time. "French fries."

She shouldered the dry bag and walked down the hill toward the town.


---

The sign at the edge of town said *TAMARACK CROSSING — UNINCORPORATED — POP. 312*, and somebody had taken a can of spray paint to it at some point and added *+ 1 MOOSE* underneath in drippy orange letters.

Leah stood and looked at it for a minute. Then she snorted, and kept walking.

The diner was called Darlene's. The neon sign on the roof said *EAT* in red letters with the *A* flickering, and under that, in smaller blue ones, *PIE*. When Leah pushed the door open, a bell jangled over her head and the smell hit her like a wall—fryer oil, coffee, onions, bacon, cinnamon, burnt sugar, wet wool—and her knees almost went.

It was a small place. A counter with eight stools, red vinyl patched with duct tape. Six booths along the windows. A pie case by the register with a hand-lettered card propped in front of it that said *BUMBLEBERRY — ASK DARLENE*. A radio on a shelf above the pass-through playing a country station out of Whitehorse, half static. On the wall, a moose head with a Canucks toque on it, and a dozen yellowed photographs of men holding up fish.

Every head in the place turned to look at her. She was used to that. She was six feet tall, she was alone, she'd just walked in off the highway with pine needles in her hair and a yellow kayak bag over her shoulder. She'd have looked at her too.

"Sit anywhere you want, hon," said the woman behind the counter. She was sixty if she was a day, with a gray braid down her back as thick as a ship's rope and forearms like a lumberjack's. Her name tag said *DARLENE*. "Kayleigh'll be right with you."

Leah took the booth in the back corner, the one with her back to the wall and a view of the door. Old habits. She'd only had them for a year and they already felt like old habits.

Kayleigh turned out to be a redhead around Leah's age, maybe a couple years older, with a spray of freckles across her nose and her hair scraped up into a scrunchie and a pen behind each ear. She came over with a coffee pot in one hand and a menu in the other and an expression of open, friendly curiosity that made Leah want to bare her teeth out of reflex.

"Hi! Coffee?"

"God, yes."

Kayleigh poured. "You walk in? I didn't see a car."

"Hitched," Leah said, which was easier than *ran here on four legs from Washington State*.

"Oh, wow. Brave. My mom would kill me." Kayleigh set the menu down. "Special's the meatloaf. Fries are good. Burgers are good. Darlene's pie is, like, legally a controlled substance. You need a minute?"

Leah didn't need a minute. "Can I get two bacon cheeseburgers. Double patty. Large fries. The poutine. A chocolate milkshake. And whatever soup you've got."

Kayleigh's pen paused on her pad. "Is somebody joining you?"

"No."

A beat. Then Kayleigh grinned, a quick, delighted grin that took over her whole face. "Okay. I respect that."

The food came out in two trips. Leah ate all of it. Cheese curds squeaking against her teeth, gravy so salty it made her eyes water, beef barley soup drunk straight out of the bowl. She'd been eating raw deer for three weeks. She'd forgotten salt existed. She'd forgotten *sauce* existed.

When she finally came up for air, Darlene was standing at the end of the booth with her arms folded, looking down at the wreckage with something close to professional admiration.

"Well," Darlene said. "Haven't seen anybody put it away like that since my late husband."

"Sorry," Leah said, wiping her mouth.

"Don't be sorry. Be hungry for pie."

Leah was. Darlene brought her a slice of bumbleberry, hot from the oven, with a scoop of vanilla melting down the side of it, and Leah took one bite and closed her eyes and made a sound she'd be embarrassed about later.

"Yeah," said Darlene, with deep satisfaction. "That's the one."

---

The motel was called the Northern Lights Motor Inn, which was a lot of name for a place with ten doors and a Coke machine. The guy behind the desk was about a hundred years old and wore a Canadiens cap with the logo worn off. His name was Walt, and he took forty-nine dollars cash for one night without asking for ID or even a last name, and handed her a key on a plastic fob with *7* on it.

"Ice machine's broke," Walt said. "Heat's good. Don't feed the dog."

"What dog?"

Walt pointed. Out in the gravel lot, a fat old Lab with a gray muzzle had lumbered to its feet and was staring at Leah with its whole body gone stiff. As she watched, its hackles came up. It whined, low, and backed up three steps, and then turned and slunk away around the corner of the building with its tail clamped between its legs.

Walt watched it go. "Huh," he said. "Normally he's a whore for strangers."

"Dogs don't like me," Leah said.

"Huh," said Walt again, and went back to his newspaper.

Room seven had a double bed with a brown polyester spread and a painting of a moose over it. The carpet was the color of weak tea. The TV got two channels, one of them in French. The shower was a fiberglass stall with a curtain printed with fish.

Leah stood under the hot water until it ran cold, and then under the cold water for ten more minutes because she didn't feel it anyway. She washed her hair three times and watched the water run gray, then brown, then clear.

Then she lay down on the bed, on top of the spread, naked and damp and smelling like cheap coconut, and looked at the ceiling.

The whole town was quiet. Walt's TV through two walls, a hockey game. A truck pulling out of the bar lot across the highway. The river.

Inside her head there was nobody but her.

She fell asleep with the light on and didn't wake up for eleven hours.

---

She paid Walt for another night in the morning, and then, after she'd eaten breakfast at Darlene's—six eggs, hash browns, a stack of pancakes, half a pound of bacon, four cups of coffee—she paid him for a third.

Staying happened the way weather happened. She just kept not leaving.

It was a good town to not leave. Nobody asked her anything. Nobody knew her. In La Push every single person she passed on the street knew her name and her mother's name and her dead father's name, knew she'd been dumped by Sam Uley for her own cousin, knew she'd gone weird and mean afterward, knew she'd started running with Sam's gang of shirtless idiots for reasons nobody would explain. She'd felt their eyes on her every day for a year, pitying or curious or disgusted. Here, people looked at her because she was tall and a stranger, and then they looked away, because it was cold out and they had stuff to do.

On the morning of the second day, while she was working through her second plate of pancakes, Darlene sat down across from her in the booth without asking.

"So," Darlene said. "You running from something?"

Leah's fork stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Don't care what," Darlene said. "Half of everybody up here's running from something. I ran from Edmonton in 1971." She tapped a blunt fingernail on the tabletop. "But you're paying Walt by the night in cash and eating like a lumber camp, and whatever's in that yellow bag won't last forever."

"I'm fine."

"Sure you are. My dishwasher quit Saturday for the tar sands. Twelve bucks an hour, cash, and you eat free. Which, the way you eat, is worth more than the twelve bucks."

Leah looked at her.

"Don't go thinking it's charity," Darlene said. "My hands are killing me. You want it or not?"

Leah thought about the money in the dry bag, eleven hundred and change, minus three nights at Walt's. She thought about how long she wanted to stay gone.

A while. She wanted to stay gone a while.

"Yeah," she said. "Okay."

---

She washed dishes that afternoon. It was hot, wet, stupid work, and she liked it more than she'd liked anything in a long time. The radio over the sink only played classic country, and by four o'clock Leah knew all the words to "Coal Miner's Daughter" and hated herself for it. Darlene ran the grill and swore at it. Kayleigh came in and out through the swinging door every few minutes with bus tubs and stories about the customers.

"Okay, so the guy in booth three," Kayleigh said, on maybe her twelfth trip, dumping a tub on the steel counter beside the sink. "Did you see the guy in booth three?"

Leah hadn't. Leah was elbow-deep in a pot that somebody had burned chili into the bottom of.

"You have to see him. He's so—" Kayleigh made a vague, fluttery gesture with both hands. "He's been in every day this week. He's a geologist for a mining company, looking at jade claims out by Dease Lake. He's from, like, Europe somewhere. And he's so *nice*, and he tips like a crazy person, he left me a fifty yesterday on a twelve-dollar check—"

"He's like forty," Darlene said from the grill without turning around.

"He's *distinguished*," Kayleigh said. "He has gray right here." She touched her own temples. "Like George Clooney."

Leah dried her hands on her jeans and went and looked through the little square window in the swinging door.

Booth three was by the front windows. A man sat in it alone with a cup of black coffee and a newspaper folded in quarters. He was lean and pale and very well put together, in a way that nobody in Tamarack Crossing was put together—a charcoal wool coat that probably cost more than Walt's whole motel, a cream-colored sweater, gray slacks, black leather gloves he hadn't taken off to drink his coffee. His hair was dark and swept back, gray at the temples, just like Kayleigh said. He had a long, narrow, handsome face, with high cheekbones and pale gray eyes and a thin mouth. Silver rings on two fingers of his left hand, over the glove. He looked like he'd walked out of a cologne ad.

He was reading the paper. He didn't look up.

Kayleigh went past the window with a coffee pot, and he lifted his cup without looking, and she filled it, and he said something that made her laugh and touch her hair.

Leah pushed the swinging door open an inch.

The smell hit her low in the gut, like a punch.

Cologne first. A lot of it, something expensive and dark and woody, vetiver and sandalwood and leather. But under the cologne—*right* under it, like it was meant to be covering something—there was something sweet. Sweet the way meat was sweet when you left it in a hot car. And copper, thick and metallic, the way her mouth tasted after a kill. And something else, something sharp and burnt that she didn't have a word for, like a fuse box after a lightning strike, like the air right before a storm.

Leah let the door swing shut. She stood there in the steam with the radio playing Patsy Cline and every hair on her arms standing up.

She knew the leech smell—bleach and honey and cold metal, so strong it made your eyes water. She'd torn apart newborn vampires last June in a clearing outside Forks and smelled it on her own fur for a week afterward. This was something else. Something alive.

Something *rotten*, under the cologne. Alive, and rotten, both at once.

"Leah?" Kayleigh had come back through the door. "You okay? You look like you saw a ghost."

"That guy," Leah said. "What's his name?"

"Mr. Kessler. Anton." Kayleigh said it like it tasted good. "Why?"

Leah looked at her. At the freckles and the scrunchie and the pens behind her ears. At the way she was smiling, a little dreamy, a little slack around the edges.

"No reason," Leah said, and went back to the sink.

---

The next morning he came in at nine, like always, Kayleigh said, and sat in booth three, like always, and ordered black coffee and dry toast, like always.

Leah watched him through the little window in the door every chance she got. He read his paper. He drank his coffee. He talked to Kayleigh every time she came by, and every time she came back into the kitchen her cheeks were a little pinker and her eyes were a little brighter and she was a little bit worse at her job. She brought the trucker at the counter decaf instead of regular. She forgot to put in an order for table two. Twice Leah heard her humming, the same three notes over and over, a weird little minor-key tune that went up and then down and then stopped, like a question nobody answered.

"You okay?" Leah asked her, the third time.

Kayleigh blinked at her. "Hm?"

"You're humming."

"Am I?" Kayleigh laughed. She rubbed absently at the inside of her left wrist, where there was a little red mark like a scratch. "Weird. I don't even know what that is."

The trucker at the counter had been in since eight. He was huge—bigger than Sam, bigger than anybody Leah had ever seen in real life, six-four or six-five, wide as a door, with a thick brown beard and a Maple Leafs cap pulled low and a canvas Carhartt jacket with the sleeves pushed up over forearms like fence posts. He was on his third slice of bumbleberry pie. He had earbuds in, the cord running down into the pocket of his jacket, and every so often Leah could hear the tinny leak of what he was listening to, something old with a lot of guitar. He tapped one boot on the rail of the stool in time with it.

When Kayleigh brought him his decaf he said, "Shame, this is decaf, isn't it?" and when she apologized and went red, he grinned at her and said, "Nah, it's lekker, don't worry. I'll drink it. Builds character."

The accent was weird. Leah couldn't place it. It wasn't Canadian and it wasn't British and it wasn't Australian, though it was sort of in that neighborhood; the vowels were flatter and the consonants were clipped short at the ends. She went back to the dishes. On her next trip to the window she noticed something that made her stop.

He smelled fine.

She'd smelled him when she went out to grab a bus tub from the end of the counter. Coffee, pie, motor oil, and under that—earth. Turned earth, like a garden after rain. Pine. And a cool, mineral smell, like stone at the bottom of a creek. Clean smells. Outside smells. Under those, very faint, something she almost would've called dog, except it was wilder than dog, and it made the hair on the back of her neck prickle.

She stood there holding the bus tub, frowning at the back of his head.

The trucker turned on his stool and looked at her. He had dark eyes—brown, she thought, though the light in Darlene's was bad—and they rested on her for one second, two. Then he nodded at her, friendly, the way you'd nod at anybody, and turned back to his pie.

Leah took the tub back to the kitchen. She didn't like getting caught staring.

At a quarter past ten, Mr. Anton Kessler folded his newspaper, put on his black leather gloves, which he had never taken off, and walked to the register to pay. At the same time, the big trucker got up from the counter with his check in one hand and his phone in the other, scrolling something with his thumb, not looking where he was going. He walked right into Kessler. A light bump, shoulder to shoulder, the way people bump in narrow places.

"Eish, sorry, boet," the trucker said. He put a hand on Kessler's arm to steady him, a quick light touch at the elbow. "My fault. Wasn't looking."

Kessler looked up at him. The pale gray eyes went over the trucker from the boots to the cap, the way you'd look at something stuck to your shoe, and then he smiled a thin, polite smile and said, "Not at all," in an accent that was something eastern European wearing an expensive British suit. He brushed off the sleeve where the trucker had touched it.

The trucker paid for his pie and left. Leah watched him walk out to a dented green Ford F-250 with Yukon plates and drive off north, and she was frowning again. He'd been sitting at Darlene's counter for two hours and she'd never once heard him make a sound when he moved. He'd gotten up off a creaky vinyl stool without the stool creaking.

And then he'd walked right into somebody.

Kessler paid. He left a fifty on the counter for Kayleigh. And on his way out the door he paused, and turned his head, and looked straight through the little square window in the kitchen door, right at Leah.

She didn't look away. She'd never been any good at looking away.

He looked at her for a long moment. His nostrils flared, very slightly, like he was smelling something. Then he smiled. It was a small smile, and pleased, and it went nowhere near his eyes.

He left. The bell jangled behind him.

Leah realized she'd been gripping the edge of the steel counter hard enough to dent it. She let go. There were two shallow finger-shaped grooves pressed into the steel.

"Shit," she said, and covered them with a bus tub.

He came back for lunch, which he'd never done before, Kayleigh said, all glowy about it. He sat in booth three and ordered nothing but coffee. When Leah came out from the kitchen to restock the napkin dispensers, which was the dumbest excuse she'd ever come up with, he was watching her.

He watched her the whole time she worked her way down the counter. When she got to the register, he got up and came to pay, even though his cup was still half full.

Up close, the smell was so strong she had to breathe through her mouth.

"You're new," he said. His voice was smooth and low and very pleasant, the vowels long. "I don't think I've seen you before this week."

"I'm new," Leah agreed.

"You're a long way from home." He said it like it was a statement. Like he knew.

"So are you."

His smile widened a little. He had very white teeth, very even. He took his time counting out bills from a leather wallet, and while he did it he looked at her, really looked, the way she'd seen people look at a horse they were thinking about buying. Kayleigh came up beside her, glowing.

"He asked me to dinner," Kayleigh said.

Leah turned and looked at her.

"Tonight." Kayleigh hugged her order pad to her chest. "At his place. He's renting the old Halvorsen cabin out on the Moose Lake road. He said he'd cook. Who does that? Nobody up here cooks. Kevin Pruitt took me to the Petro-Can for a hot dog for our six-month anniversary." She rubbed at the red mark on the inside of her wrist again and started humming. Three notes up, three down. Stop.

"Kayleigh," Leah said. "Don't go."

Kayleigh blinked. "What?"

*Because he smells like a dead thing wearing cologne. Because he looked at me like I was a cut of meat. Because you've been humming the same three notes all day and you don't even know you're doing it.*

"He's creepy," Leah said, which was the best she could do.

And she went back out to the floor, humming.

Leah thought about calling the RCMP detachment in Dease Lake. *Hi, there's a man in Tamarack Crossing who smells like rotten meat and I think he's hypnotizing a waitress.* She thought about telling Darlene, who thought Anton Kessler was a stuffed shirt who tipped too much, which was a long way from *monster*.

She thought about doing nothing. Kayleigh was a grown woman. Kayleigh wasn't her problem. Leah had run a thousand miles to stop having problems.

At seven, Kayleigh clocked out. She'd put on lip gloss in the bathroom. She waved at Leah on her way out the back door, and her eyes were so glassy now they looked like marbles, and she was humming.

At eight, Leah finished the last of the pans, took her forty-eight dollars in cash from Darlene, ate a meatloaf dinner and two slices of pie standing up at the counter, said goodnight, and walked out into the dark.

She went back to room seven. She took off her clothes and folded them and rolled them into the dry bag and tied the dry bag to her ankle. She opened the back window, the one that looked out over the dark wall of spruce, and climbed out.

The old Lab was lying by the dumpster. He lifted his head and watched her go into the trees, and didn't make a sound.

There was no moon.

Leah noticed it once she was on four legs and moving, the way you'd notice a missing tooth. The sky was clear and black and so full of stars it looked like somebody had spilled sugar across it, and that was all the light there was. Frost cracked under her pads.

She didn't need light. She had her nose.

Kessler's trail was easy. It went out from behind the motel through the trees, old and cold, and then curved west along the river toward the Moose Lake road, and the farther she went the fresher it got. Rot and copper and burnt air. Cologne laid over it like a sheet over a body. She ran it at an easy lope, low and quiet, keeping to the trees, and twenty minutes out of town she came over a rise and saw the cabin.

It sat by itself in a clearing at the end of a rutted gravel track, a squat old log place with a tin roof and a stone chimney. Lights in both front windows, yellow and soft. Smoke from the chimney. Kayleigh's little red Civic parked out front beside a black Range Rover that was so clean it looked fake.

Leah circled downwind and lay down in the brush at the edge of the clearing.

She could hear them. Kessler's voice, low and smooth, saying something she couldn't make out. Kayleigh laughing, high and too long. Dishes. A fork against a plate. Music, something with violins. And under all of it, under Kessler's voice, that three-note tune—up, down, stop—except now it was coming from him. He was singing it, very softly, under everything he said. Over and over.

The hair along Leah's spine stood straight up.

She lay there for an hour. The frost got thicker. An owl hunted along the edge of the clearing and found something and the something screamed. Inside the cabin, Kayleigh stopped laughing. The violins stopped. The fork stopped. There was only the tune, Kessler's voice, low and patient, up and down and stop.

At ten past eleven, the front door opened.

Kessler came out first. He was dressed the same way he'd been dressed at Darlene's—the long coat, the gloves—and he walked down the steps and across the frozen yard toward the trees on the far side of the clearing without looking back.

Kayleigh came out after him.

She was wearing a thin green dress, the kind you'd wear to a wedding in July. No coat. No shoes. Her bare feet were white against the frozen gravel, and her arms were bare, and her breath smoked in the air, and she walked like a sleepwalker, slow and straight, her eyes open and fixed on the middle of Kessler's back. She didn't shiver. She didn't make a sound. Her lips moved, shaping the tune without any voice behind it.

Leah's lips peeled back off her teeth.

They went into the trees on the far side of the clearing. Leah gave them a fifty-yard head start and went after them, belly almost to the ground, every step placed, the wind in her face. The trail climbed up out of the spruce into open rock and wind-twisted pine, toward a ridge above the lake. Kayleigh stumbled once and didn't put her hands out to catch herself. She fell flat on her face. Kessler stopped and looked back, and Kayleigh got up with her nose bleeding and kept walking, and he turned around and kept walking too.

Leah could smell Kayleigh's feet. They were bleeding. Every step left blood on the frozen stone.

*Hang on,* Leah thought. *Hang on, idiot, I'm coming.*

At the top of the ridge, the trees opened up into a clearing.

It was a round, flat place, maybe forty feet across, on a shelf of rock above the black lake. Somebody had set a ring of stones around the edge of it—gray stones, knee-high, a dozen of them, rough and old—and in the middle of the ring there was one big flat stone like a table. The stones had marks cut into them. Leah couldn't see the marks very well in the starlight. She didn't want to. They made her eyes slide off them, the way your eyes slid off something too bright.

The clearing smelled like blood.

Old blood. Soaked into the ground, into the stone, into the roots of the twisted pines around the edges. And under the blood—

Leah stopped dead at the treeline.

There were two long low mounds of earth beside the flat stone. Fresh, or fresh-ish. A month old, maybe. Six weeks. The smell coming off them was the smell of the rot under Kessler's cologne, the real thing, a hundred times stronger, and it was the smell of girls. She could still smell them under the rot. Shampoo. Sunscreen. Bug spray. Somebody's vanilla body lotion.

Kessler walked to the center of the ring. He took Kayleigh by one bare arm and turned her around, gently, like you'd turn a child, and she lay down on the flat stone on her back with her arms at her sides and looked up at the stars.

Then Kessler took off his gloves.

His hands were old.

Leah had been staring at that long, smooth, handsome face for two days, and it was a forty-year-old face, maybe thirty-eight like Kayleigh said, nice skin and gray at the temples. But the hands that came out of those black leather gloves were the hands of somebody who should've been dead for a long, long time. They were gray and spotted and shrunken, the skin hanging loose off the bones like wet paper, the knuckles swollen into knots, the nails yellow and thick and curved. They looked like something that had been dug up.

He flexed them. The joints cracked, loud in the quiet.

From inside his coat, he took out a knife. It had a long thin blade and a handle made of something pale and knobbly that Leah didn't want to look at too closely either.

Leah came out of the trees.

She didn't plan it. She didn't think about it. She saw the knife and she was moving, and then she was flying across the rock with her ears flat and her lips back and everything she had in her legs, and she hit the edge of the stone ring at a dead sprint—

—and hit a wall.

There wasn't anything there. There was nothing in front of her but air, and starlight, and Kessler turning around with his ruined hands and his knife. But she hit it like she'd run full tilt into the side of a truck. Something gave under her, a little, the way a trampoline gives, and then it threw her back. She went tumbling across the rock, ass over ears, and slammed into one of the twisted pines hard enough to crack it.

She lay there for a second with the stars spinning.

"Ah," Kessler said. "There you are."

He came walking across the clearing toward her, and when he got to the ring of stones he stepped right through it like it wasn't there. He stopped about ten feet from her. He looked down at her the way he'd looked at her through the kitchen window at Darlene's, head tilted, pleased.

"I wondered whether you would come," he said. "I hoped you would. I made it a little easy for you, you know. I left you a trail." He smiled. "You are very predictable, your kind. Something about a damsel in distress. You simply cannot resist."

Leah got up. Her shoulder hurt where she'd hit the tree. It was already getting better. She put her head down and growled, low, a sound that came all the way up from the bottom of her chest. It was the growl that had made Paul Lahote back down once, last winter. It was the growl that had made a newborn vampire hesitate, half a second, which was all the time she'd needed to take its head off.

Kessler didn't back down.

"What *are* you?" he said, softly, almost to himself. He was looking her over again. "You are not a werewolf. There is no moon, and no werewolf is that large, and no werewolf smells like—" his nostrils flared "—*that*. Cedar. Salt. Something very old. You are not a skinchanger, either. A skinchanger would smell of the skin she wore." He took a step closer. "I read once, many years ago, of a people on the western coast of this continent. The cold ones' enemies, they called them. Born to it. Wolves in the blood." His pale eyes gleamed. "I thought it was a story."

Leah lunged.

He was faster than she thought. He snapped his old gray hand up without moving his feet, and said something—a word, short and ugly, that made her ears ring—and a line of purple light came off the end of his fingers like a whip and laid her open from shoulder to ribs.

She screamed. It came out as a wolf's scream, high and terrible. She felt the hot pour of blood down her side, felt her leg buckle. She kept coming anyway. Her momentum carried her into him and her jaws closed on his forearm, coat and all, and she bit down as hard as she'd ever bitten anything.

His blood filled her mouth. It was the worst thing she'd ever tasted. It tasted like the smell—like rot and copper and a lightning strike—and it *burned*, burned her tongue, burned the back of her throat, and Kessler was screaming too now, high and furious, and his other hand slapped flat against the side of her head.

The world went white.

When it came back she was twenty feet away, on her side on the rock, and she couldn't move.

She tried. She couldn't. Every muscle in her body had locked up hard, like a cramp, like somebody had poured concrete into her veins and let it set. She could breathe. She could blink. That was all.

Kessler was standing over by the ring of stones, holding his arm. His sleeve was in shreds and the gray arm under it was torn up, the skin hanging in flaps. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he said another one of those ugly words, and the flaps of skin crawled back together like worms and sealed up, leaving the arm gray and whole and spotted.

He breathed hard through his nose. Then he looked at her, and for the first time since she'd laid eyes on him, his face did something real. It twisted up, the handsome mask of it, into something ugly and furious and very, very old.

"*Animal*," he said.

Leah pushed.

She didn't know what she was pushing against. She pushed with everything, the way she'd pushed out of Sam's pack mind and through Jacob's and out the other side. She felt the concrete in her veins crack. Give, a little, in her front legs. She heaved herself up onto them, trembling, blood dripping off her belly onto the frozen rock, and she looked at him and growled.

Kessler's eyebrows went up.

"Oh," he said, in an entirely different voice. Interested. "Oh, *you're* strong."

He raised his hand again.

Chains came out of the ground.

They came up out of the rock under her like snakes, black iron, thick as her leg, covered in more of those marks that made her eyes slide away. They wrapped her legs, her ribs, her throat, her muzzle, and pulled her down flat against the stone, and they were so cold they burned, and where they touched her she could feel them *drinking*. Her strength, her heat, the fire that lived in her chest and made her what she was, draining out into the iron like water down a drain. The more it drained, the heavier the chains got, until she couldn't even lift her head.

Kessler walked over. He knelt down beside her on the rock, careful of his slacks, and reached out one gray, spotted, dead-feeling hand, and stroked her muzzle between the chains.

She tried to bite him. She couldn't open her mouth.

"Shh," Kessler said. "Shh, now. I was going to wait. One heart a night; that is the rule I have always kept. But I think tonight I will be greedy." He stroked her muzzle again. His fingers left a cold, greasy feeling behind, like slug trails. "Do you know what the heart of a creature like you is worth? A born shapeshifter? The girl will give me ten years, perhaps twelve, and a fresh face. Yours—" He sighed, like a man looking at a menu. "A century. Perhaps more."

He stood up. He brushed off the knees of his slacks.

"Wait there," he said, as if she had a choice, and walked back toward the flat stone where Kayleigh lay looking up at the stars, and lifted his knife.

Leah lay in the chains with her blood running out of her and screamed and screamed inside her own head, where for the first time in a year she was completely and totally alone.

Two hundred meters up the ridge, behind a granite outcrop with a clear sight line down onto the clearing, Harry Potter put the last bite of bumbleberry pie in his mouth and chewed.

He'd been there since half ten. He'd watched Vulk come up the slope with the girl walking behind him. He'd watched the shifter follow them, low and quiet and downwind, which was decent fieldcraft for somebody who probably hadn't had any training at all. He'd watched her hit the ward. He'd watched the fight.

She'd bitten him. Good for her.

The pie was very good. Darlene put cardamom in the crust. Harry had asked her about it on Monday, and she'd looked at him like he'd asked about her bra size and then told him anyway.

He closed the plastic clamshell, wiped his muzzle with the back of his forepaw, and went over it one more time.

Casimir Vulk. Born 1863 in Brașov, according to the ICW file. Expelled from Durmstrang in his sixth year for something the school still wouldn't talk about. A hundred and forty-three years old, wearing a face he'd taken off somebody in his late thirties. Thirty-one confirmed victims across four continents; Geneva thought the real number was closer to two hundred. Young women, mostly. He took their hearts and used them to buy more time, more face, more power. Two ICW hit wizards had gone after him in the nineties. One had been buried in a closed casket. They'd never found enough of the other one to bury.

Megan Doyle, twenty-four, from Calgary. Priya Sandhu, twenty-one, from Prince George. Two friends on a hiking trip up the Cassiar in August. Their car had been found at a pullout ten kilometers south of Tamarack Crossing on the twenty-third. The RCMP had searched for two weeks and found nothing. Harry had found them on Tuesday afternoon, in the two mounds beside the altar stone.

He'd needed Vulk to come up here.

That was the part that had taken all week. Vulk had lived as long as he had by keeping a lot of doors open behind him. An emergency portkey—Harry had watched him touch the inside pocket of his coat every time somebody came into Darlene's. A cabin warded like a bank vault. And an anchor: a jar of his own blood mixed with the blood of every girl he'd taken, sealed and spelled and buried somewhere close, so he could heal from damage that would kill anybody else.

Harry had spent Monday finding the anchor. It was under the altar stone. Of course it was.

He'd spent Tuesday afternoon finding the girls, and Tuesday night laying an anti-Apparition jinx over the whole valley, keyed to let exactly one person through.

And this morning he'd sat at Darlene's counter in a glamour, eating pie and waiting for Vulk to pay his bill, so he could walk into him at the register and lift a gold pocket watch out of the inside pocket of his coat and drop an identical gold pocket watch back in its place. The real one, the portkey, was in Harry's coat. The fake one would tell Vulk the time, and not do anything else.

While he'd been at the register, he'd put a shield on the waitress, too. One brush of a forepaw on the counter by her hand while she was making change. Nothing short of a cutting curse at point-blank range would get through it, and the shield would wake him up from a dead sleep if anything tried.

And then he'd waited for the dark of the moon, because blood magic like Vulk's always ran best on a dark moon, and he'd waited for Vulk to bring the waitress up here, because Vulk would be standing on top of his own anchor when he did. And the girls in the ground would be found, and their families would have something to bury.

That had been the plan.

The shifter hadn't been part of the plan.

Harry had smelled her on Monday. She'd come into Darlene's for breakfast while he was sitting at the counter in the glamour, and he'd nearly spilled his coffee. Cedar and salt water and rain, and under that a smell like wolf. Clean wolf. Wild wolf. None of the werewolf stink—no wet dog, no sickness, no curse. He'd sat there with his nose working and come up with nothing he'd ever smelled before.

He'd thought about walking over to her booth. He'd thought about it more than once. She was six feet tall and built like a sprinter and she ate like he did, which was to say like a fire eats a house, and she watched the door the whole time she was in there. She'd had her back to the wall. He'd liked that.

But he'd been working, and you didn't go chatting up the locals when you were three days into setting a trap for somebody like Vulk. So he'd kept his eyes on his pie.

He thought about the healers in Geneva, for no good reason. The ones who'd taken blood off him in 2003, a week after the werewolf in the Carpathians had got his teeth into Harry's shoulder in a burning village. They'd sat him down in a white room and told him to get his affairs in order before the full moon. And then the full moon had come, and he'd gone to sleep in a warded cell at six-two and human and woken up at six-seven with fur, and a tail, and a muzzle full of teeth, and his own mind still sitting right where he'd left it. He'd been able to change back by breakfast. He'd been able to change again by lunch.

The healers had taken a lot more blood after that. Their best guess, after a year of arguing about it, was that there'd been something asleep in his blood for a very long time. Something old, and wolfish, and nothing to do with any curse. It had come in through his father's side, they thought—his grandmother Dorea had been a Black, and the Blacks had married into everything—and it had been lying dormant for generations until a werewolf's curse had come along and woken it up. And then it had done to the curse what Harry did to bacon.

One of the healers had found a note in an old family genealogy. Some Black, a couple of hundred years back, had sailed off to the Americas on a fur company ship and come home years later with more than furs. That was all it said. The healer had thought that was very interesting. Harry had thought it was very interesting too, for about a week, and then he'd had to go to Chechnya.

He'd never really thought about it since. It didn't seem to matter much where it came from. He had what he had.

He hadn't smelled anything like it on anybody else, though. Until Monday.

Down in the clearing, Vulk was kneeling beside the shifter, stroking her muzzle through the chains.

Harry's ears went flat.

He knew those chains. He'd seen them in the file. Vulk had used them on a pair of Nundu cubs in Tanzania in 1971, to keep them alive and weak while he worked out what their hearts were good for. They drained the strength out of anything magical they touched, and the stronger the thing was, the faster they drained it. The shifter was strong. She was bleeding a lot.

She had, at a guess, a couple of minutes.

Vulk got up and went back to the altar stone, toward the waitress, and raised his knife.

Harry stood up. He picked up the pie container, because Darlene would want it back. He checked that his wand was in his right forepaw, which it was, and his coat was buttoned, which it was.

Then he Apparated, and he did it loud.

The crack went off like a rifle shot, flat and huge, and rolled away down the ridge and out across the black lake and came back as an echo.

Leah couldn't turn her head. The chains had her muzzle pinned flat to the rock. But she could roll her eyes, and she rolled them as far as they'd go, and she saw him.

He was standing at the edge of the stone ring, about fifteen feet from her. He hadn't been there and then he was.

He was a wolf. Some of him was. He stood on two legs like a man, and he was tall, taller than anybody she'd ever seen, six-seven at least. He was covered in fur so dark it was nearly black, charcoal at the edges where the starlight caught it. He had a wolf's head—long muzzle, black nose, tall pointed ears standing straight up—and when the starlight hit his eyes they flashed green. His shoulders were broad, and his legs were long and set like a man's legs, heel to toe, in a pair of heavy black boots laced up the shin. A long, thick black tail hung down behind him, nearly to the ground.

He was dressed. That was the part her brain kept snagging on. He had on a long coat in some heavy, dull-black material, cut long and loose like a robe, falling to his shins, with a slit up the back for the tail and a high collar fastened at the throat with a silver clasp. The coat hung open over a faded black T-shirt with a band logo on it, a winged figure, and dark trousers, and a leather belt with a row of little glass vials clipped along it. In his right forepaw—five long clawed digits, dark pads on the palm—he held a thin stick of pale wood, maybe a foot long.

In his left forepaw he held a plastic clamshell pie container from Darlene's.

The wind came across the clearing from him to her, and Leah's nose went: earth. Pine. Cold stone at the bottom of a creek.

*The trucker.*

"Howzit, Casimir," he said.

His voice was low and rough, with a rumble under it, the words coming out clean even through a muzzle full of teeth. And it was the trucker's accent—flat vowels, clipped ends.

Kessler had spun around at the crack. He stood behind the altar stone with his knife up and his ruined gray hands shaking, very slightly, and for one second his face was completely blank.

Then he moved. He didn't say anything. He flung his free hand out and a bolt of something dark purple, almost black, came off his fingers and crossed the clearing faster than Leah could follow.

The wolf-man tipped his head an inch to the left.

The curse went past his ear. It hit one of the twisted pines at the edge of the clearing behind him, and the pine went gray and soft and slumped over sideways like a candle left in a hot car, and the smell of rot rolled out across the rock.

"Ja, no, that's rude," the wolf-man said. He looked over his shoulder at the dead tree, and his ears flicked sideways. "That tree never did anything to you."

"Who are you?" Kessler's voice had gone high.

"International Confederation of Wizards. Office of the Supreme Mugwump, Enforcement Division." The wolf-man set the pie container down on top of one of the standing stones, carefully, like it was something he planned to come back for. "There's a warrant for you, boet. Eleven countries signed it. Twelve, if you count Liechtenstein. Nobody counts Liechtenstein."

"*Hit wizard*," Kessler said, and spat on the rock.

"Mm. That's me."

Kessler's eyes jumped to Kayleigh, lying still on the altar stone. Leah saw him decide. He lunged sideways and grabbed Kayleigh by the hair and hauled her up to sitting, limp as a doll, and put the long thin knife to her throat, and dragged her up against his chest like a shield.

"You will step back," he said, "or I will—"

He stopped.

Kayleigh wasn't there.

His gray hand was closed on nothing. The knife was pressed against nothing. Kayleigh was ten yards away, at the edge of the clearing, lying on a bed of moss that hadn't been there a second ago, under a thick wool blanket that hadn't been there either. Her eyes were closed. She was breathing slow and even, like somebody asleep in her own bed. Over her, faint as a soap bubble, a dome of light shimmered and settled.

The wolf-man hadn't moved. He hadn't lifted the stick. He hadn't said a word.

"Waitress is off the clock," he said. "Next."

Kessler's hand went inside his coat. To a gold watch on a chain. He yanked it out and gripped it hard, his knuckles going white, and his whole body tensed like he was bracing to be jerked away by a hook behind the navel.

Nothing happened.

He looked down at the watch. He shook it.

The wolf-man reached into his coat with his left forepaw and pulled out a gold pocket watch on a chain, identical to the one in Kessler's hand. He dangled it from one claw so it caught the starlight and turned slowly.

"This morning," he said. "Register. You were very rude about my sleeve." His tail flicked once, side to side. "Yours tells the time. It's a nice watch, actually, good Swiss movement. I'd keep it."

Kessler stared at him. Then he twisted on the spot, sharp, the way you'd twist to unscrew yourself from the air—

—and stayed exactly where he was. He staggered, like he'd walked into the edge of a door in the dark.

"Ja, that one either," the wolf-man said. "Whole valley's been jinxed since last night. Nobody pops in or out but me. I would've told you, but you didn't ask."

Leah, lying in the chains with her blood going cold on the rock, felt something rise up in her chest that she didn't recognize at first. It took her a second.

She wanted to laugh.

Kessler's face went through about four different things at once. Then it went still, and cold, and very old, and he dropped the knife on the rock and raised both gray hands and dragged his own yellow thumbnail across his left palm. Black blood welled up. He slapped the bleeding palm flat on the altar stone and said something long and guttural that made the air in the clearing go thick.

The two mounds of earth beside the altar stone moved.

Leah would've pressed herself back against the rock if she could've moved at all. The dirt heaved and split and slid aside, and something came up out of each grave. Two somethings. They pushed themselves up out of the ground on hands that had gone black and swollen and soft, and stood up, swaying, in the rags of hiking clothes. One of them still had a bright orange bandanna knotted around its throat. Their faces—

Leah shut her eyes. She couldn't shut her nose. The smell came over her like a wave.

When she opened her eyes again, the wolf-man's ears had gone flat against his skull. His tail had gone completely still.

"Ah, Casimir," he said, very quietly, and it was a different voice. All the joking was gone out of it. "You stupid, *stupid* bastard."

The two dead things turned toward him. They started forward, shambling, their black hands coming up.

He looked at them. That was all. He didn't raise the stick. He didn't make a sound.

Fire came up out of the rock under their feet.

It was white, and then gold, and it came up in two tall columns that wrapped around the dead girls like a pair of arms. It didn't roar. It didn't spread. The juniper three feet away didn't even smoke. It burned perfectly quiet and perfectly clean, and inside it the two dead girls stopped moving forward and just stood there, and Leah would swear for the rest of her life that they turned their faces up into it like you'd turn your face into the sun. And then the fire sank back down into the rock and went out, and there were two small heaps of pale bone and ash lying neatly on the stone where they'd been standing, and the orange bandanna lying on top of one of them, not even scorched.

"Megan Doyle," the wolf-man said, still in that quiet voice. "Priya Sandhu. They'll go home. I'll make sure."

Kessler threw the Killing Curse.

Leah didn't know that's what it was, then. She'd learn the name of it later. All she knew was that Kessler screamed a word and a bolt of sick bright green light tore out of his hand, and every animal instinct she had lit up at once. *Death.* That light was death. She'd never seen anything so purely and simply made for killing.

A slab of granite tore itself up out of the ridge in front of the wolf-man.

It came up out of the bedrock with a sound like the world cracking open, a slab the size of a car door, and the green light hit it dead center and blew it into gravel. Stone chips sprayed across the clearing and rattled off the standing stones and pattered down on the chains around Leah's ribs. When the dust cleared the wolf-man was standing exactly where he'd been standing. He had a little bit of grit in the fur on top of his head. He shook it out with a flick of his ears, like a dog coming out of a lake.

Then he started walking.

Kessler threw everything he had. Leah watched him do it. Purple light. Red light. A blast of something that turned the air into knives and shredded three pines into toothpicks behind the wolf-man's back. A wave of black smoke that came boiling across the clearing with faces in it, screaming faces. Lightning, actual lightning, cracking down out of the clear sky.

The wolf-man kept walking. Heel, toe. Heel, toe. Unhurried, like somebody walking across a parking lot to his truck. The purple light bent around him like water around a stone. The red light stopped dead in the air a foot from his chest and hung there, fizzing, and then dropped to the rock and went out. The knife-air broke against nothing. The black smoke came at him and parted down the middle like a curtain and went screaming off into the dark on either side. The lightning came down, and he lifted his left forepaw, and the lightning stopped in his palm and crackled there, white and blue, lighting him up from below. He looked at it for a second with his ears pricked forward. Then he closed his paw, and it was gone.

He hadn't even raised the stick. Leah realized that halfway through. The whole time, he'd been holding it down by his side. He was holding it the way you'd hold a pen you'd forgotten you had.

Kessler had backed up against the altar stone. He raised both hands again and something tore loose from the inside of him—Leah could feel it, the air went sick and heavy and wrong—and he flung it at the wolf-man with a scream that cracked his voice in half.

The wolf-man stopped walking. He rocked back half a step, like somebody had shoved him in the chest. He blinked.

Then he pressed one forepaw to his sternum, and burped.

"Blood-boiling curse," he said. "Ja, that doesn't work on me. Some bloke in Prague tried that one. All I got out of it was heartburn." He lowered his paw. "You done?"

Kessler stared at him. His hands were shaking. His whole body was shaking.

"Who *are* you?" he whispered.

The wolf-man tilted his head. Something moved in his face, around the muzzle and the eyes. It was close to a smile. It had a lot of teeth in it.

"Harry," he said. "Harry Potter."

Kessler's face went the color of old paper.

Leah didn't know the name. It didn't mean a thing to her. But she watched it land on Kessler like a hammer, watched his ruined hands drop to his sides and his mouth fall open and every last scrap of fight go out of him all at once, and she thought: *Oh. Okay. So that's a big deal, then.*

"No," Kessler said. "No. You're—they said you were—"

"I get that a lot."

Kessler went for the knife on the rock.

Harry Potter raised the stick, finally. A few inches, just enough to point.

There was no sound. No word. A flash of the same sick bright green light, so quick Leah almost missed it, and Casimir Vulk stopped halfway through bending down for the knife, and folded up on the rock beside his altar stone like a coat dropped off a hook, and didn't move again.

The clearing was silent.

Leah lay in the chains and stared at the body.

She'd seen dead things. She'd made dead things. She'd ripped the head off a newborn vampire in a clearing outside Forks last June and felt nothing but a hot, fierce kind of joy, and slept like a baby afterward. She knew what killing looked like.

This had looked like somebody turning off a light.

Harry Potter stood over the body for a moment. Then the body caught fire—the same white-gold fire, quiet and clean—and burned down to nothing in about three seconds. Not even bones. When it was done there was a scorch mark on the rock, and that was all.

He turned to the altar stone. He looked at it, hard. His nose flared.

The altar stone split down the middle with a crack like a gunshot. In a hollow scraped out of the bedrock underneath sat a big glass jar, sealed with wax and wire, full of something black that sloshed against the glass on its own and made a thin high whine like a mosquito. The jar lifted out of the hollow and hung in the air in front of Harry at eye level, turning slowly.

He looked at it with his lip curled off his teeth.

The jar crumpled like a beer can. The black stuff inside it sprayed out, hissing and screaming, and caught fire before it hit the ground, and burned away to nothing, and the screaming stopped.

The chains around Leah went slack.

She felt it all at once—a rush of heat back into her body, like blood coming back into a limb that had fallen asleep, pins and needles everywhere, so sudden and so strong she gasped. The black iron links around her legs and ribs and throat and muzzle went brittle and red and crumbled into rust, and the rust blew away across the rock in the wind off the lake.

She lurched up onto her feet. Her legs held. Barely. Her side was still bleeding, but slower now. She could feel it knitting, hot and itchy. She stood there swaying with her head low and her hackles up and a growl rolling in her chest, because she didn't know what else to do, and she faced him.

Harry Potter turned around and looked at her.

He tilted his head.

He did it exactly like a dog. Exactly like the old Lab behind Walt's motel did whenever Walt whistled at him, a slow tip of the whole head to one side, ears perked up and pointed straight at her, green eyes bright. He looked at her like that for a long moment, the starlight on his fur, the wind ruffling the thick ruff at his throat.

The stick in his right forepaw was gone.

Leah hadn't seen it go. She'd been looking right at him. He hadn't tucked it in a sleeve or slid it into a pocket or a holster or anything. One second it was there and the next second it wasn't, like it had never been there at all.

"So," Harry Potter said. "Who might you be?"

Leah stared at him.

"And," he said, "this is gonna sound a bit forward, given the—" he gestured vaguely with one forepaw at the scorch mark, the broken altar stone, the shredded trees, the general state of the clearing "—everything. But would you maybe be interested in getting a drink?"

---

Leah stood there on the rock, bleeding, and looked at him.

She couldn't answer him. She was a wolf. She opened her mouth anyway, out of pure reflex, and what came out was a sort of choked huff.

Harry's ears drooped a little. "Ah," he said. "Right. Wolf. Can't talk." He rubbed the back of his neck with one forepaw. "Okay. Um. One bark for yes, two for no? Three for 'I'm calling the cops'? Not that I'd blame you, I just, you know, I'd want to know."

Leah huffed again. Harder.

Then she turned around and limped off behind the biggest of the twisted pines at the edge of the clearing, where he couldn't see her, and let go.

The change hurt more than usual. Everything hurt. She came up on her hands and knees on the frozen rock, naked, shaking, gasping, with a long, ugly, half-healed gash running down her left side from shoulder to hip, and reached for the dry bag on her ankle.

The cord had snapped. The bag was gone. She looked around and spotted it in the starlight thirty feet away, split open where one of the chains had come up through it, her jeans and her flannel and her last clean tank top torn to rags and scattered across the rock.

"Shit," she said, through chattering teeth. Her voice came out a croak. "Shit, shit, *shit*."

"Problem?" Harry called. From the sound of it, he hadn't moved from the middle of the clearing.

"My clothes are wrecked."

"Ah." A pause. "What size are you?"

"*Excuse me?*"

"For the clothes. I can make you some. Don't worry, I'm facing the other way. I'm looking at a very interesting rock."

Leah crouched behind the pine with her arms around her knees, freezing her ass off, and closed her eyes. "I don't know. Large. Tall."

Something landed on the rock beside her with a soft *flump*.

She looked. It was a folded pile of clothes. A flannel shirt, red-and-black buffalo plaid, thick as a blanket. A pair of gray sweatpants. Wool socks. A pair of moccasin slippers lined with sheepskin. They were dry and clean and they hadn't existed a second ago.

She pulled the sweatpants on. They were long enough, which almost never happened. She pulled the flannel over her head without unbuttoning it, and it was huge, it came halfway down her thighs and the sleeves hung past her fingertips, and it smelled—

Earth. Pine. Cool stone at the bottom of a creek.

It smelled like him. Like he'd been wearing it.

Leah stood there behind the pine tree with her nose buried in the collar of a stranger's flannel, breathing in, and the wolf inside her did something it had never done before. It rolled over. It stretched. It made a low, rumbling sound deep in her chest, a sound she'd never made in her life and didn't know she could.

It *purred*.

"Oh, shut up," Leah whispered to it, horrified.

She pulled on the socks and the slippers and walked out from behind the tree.

Harry was standing in the middle of the clearing with his back to her, looking with great concentration at a rock. His tail was hanging still behind him. As she came out he turned around and looked at her.

He looked for a while. He didn't hide that he was doing it. His eyes went from her face to her bare feet and back up again, slowly, and his ears came up, and his tail swung once, side to side.

"Okay," he said. "So you're gorgeous."

Leah's mouth fell open.

"No, sorry, that's—let me start over." He held up both forepaws. "Hi. I'm Harry. I'm a wizard. I hunt evil wizards for a living, and I just killed one. You saw. And you're gorgeous, and you're also, apparently, a wolf. A big gray one. And I've gotta be honest with you, I didn't know that was a box I could tick, but here we are." He considered that. "It's ticked. Box is very ticked."

Leah stood there in his flannel and his sweatpants and stared up at him. Way up. She was six feet tall and she had to tip her head back to look him in the face, and she couldn't remember the last time that had happened. Sam, maybe. Even Sam had only had a couple of inches on her.

In the starlight, this close, she could see him properly. His teeth, when he talked—long and white and sharp, the canines curved like hooks. The width of his shoulders under the coat, wide enough that she wondered how he got through doors. The thick black ruff of fur at his throat. The tail, which had started moving again, slow, a lazy back-and-forth sweep behind his legs.

The wolf inside her looked at the teeth, and at the shoulders, and at the tail, and purred so loud she was afraid he'd hear it.

*Oh no,* Leah thought. *Oh no, he's hot. He's hot as fuck. I am in so much trouble.*

"You just killed a man," she said.

"Ja," Harry agreed.

"In front of me."

"Ja. Sorry about that. I'd have done it somewhere else, but he was about to cut your heart out, so." He shrugged. "Timing."

"And now you're asking me out."

"I am." His ears tipped forward. "I'm told I'm very direct. I've also been told I've got no sense of occasion, but I think that's unfair. I brought pie."

He pointed at the standing stone where he'd set down the plastic container. Leah looked at it. There were a few crumbs of bumbleberry crust in the bottom.

"You ate the pie," she said.

"I ate the pie," he admitted. "In my defense, I've been sitting up on that ridge for an hour."

Leah opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

What came out, after a second, was a laugh. A real one, from the bottom of her stomach, the first one she could remember in—God, months. Since before Dad. It came up out of her and it wouldn't stop. She laughed until her ribs hurt and her half-healed side hurt and her eyes ran, standing in a clearing that smelled like blood and burnt meat and dead magic, wearing a stranger's clothes, in front of a six-foot-seven wolf in a Led Zeppelin T-shirt.

Harry watched her laugh, head tilted again. His tail went faster.

"Is that a yes?" he said.


---

It was a yes. It just took a while to get there, because first there was Kayleigh.

Harry knelt down beside her at the edge of the clearing, and the soap-bubble dome over her flickered and went out. Kayleigh slept on under the wool blanket, her freckled face slack, a smear of dried blood under her nose. Her bare feet stuck out from under the edge of the blanket, white and torn up from the rock.

Harry looked at her feet. Leah watched the cuts close up, one after another, the skin going smooth and pink, and the dried blood under her nose flake away into nothing. He didn't touch her. He didn't move at all.

"I'm going to take the last week out," he said.

Leah's head came up. "Out of her head?"

"Ja."

"You can do that?"

"I can do that."

Leah thought about Sam's voice, the Alpha voice, reaching past every part of her that got a vote. She thought about a year of other people walking around inside her skull. She folded her arms over the giant flannel.

"That's messed up," she said. "Going into somebody's head. Changing stuff."

Harry looked up at her. His ears went back, a little. "Ja. It is." He looked back down at Kayleigh. "He's been in there all week. That song she keeps humming? That's him. That's the hook he put in her. If I leave it, she'll hum it for the rest of her life, and she'll never know why, and every time she does she'll feel like somebody's watching her." He paused. "I'll take Vulk out. Him and the song and the walk up here. She keeps everything else. She'll remember a creepy guest who tipped too much and then left town."

Leah looked at Kayleigh for a long moment. At the lip gloss she'd put on in the diner bathroom.

"Okay," Leah said. "Yeah. Take him out."

Harry nodded. He looked at Kayleigh's face for about ten seconds, very still, his eyes a little unfocused. Then he slid his forearms under her and picked her up, blanket and all, like she weighed nothing.

"Back in a sec," he said, and was gone with a crack.

Leah stood alone on the ridge in the starlight and counted. She got to six.

*Crack.*

"She's in her bed," Harry said, brushing a bit of lint off his coat. "Her mum's snoring down the hall. I put her car back in the drive. She'll wake up with cold feet and a weird feeling she had a bad dream, and that's it."

Then he went and knelt by the two small piles of bone and ash, and for a while he didn't say anything at all. He gathered them carefully into two folded squares of white cloth from somewhere inside his coat, and tied the orange bandanna around one of them.

"RCMP'll get a call tomorrow," he said, without looking up. "Hunter out of Dease Lake with a GPS. They'll find these at the pullout where the girls left their car. They'll do the DNA. Their families will get them back."

"You do this a lot?" Leah asked.

He was quiet a second. "More than I'd like."

He stood up. He picked up the empty pie container off the standing stone and tucked it inside his coat. He looked around the clearing one last time—the scorch mark, the split altar stone, the shredded trees—and the standing stones in the ring, all twelve of them, cracked down the middle at once with a sound like a single gunshot and fell over.

Then he turned to Leah and held out one forepaw, palm up. Dark pads. Long black claws, curved.

"Drink?" he said.

---

The Bent Antler was open till one.

It was a long, low log building across the highway from Walt's motel, with a pool table and a jukebox and a mounted moose head that was bigger and uglier than the one at Darlene's, and neon beer signs in the windows, and about eleven people in it at quarter to midnight on a Wednesday. Every one of them turned to look when the door opened.

They looked at Leah, in a huge flannel and sweatpants and sheepskin slippers, with twigs in her hair. Then they looked at the big guy coming in behind her, and nodded, and went back to their beers.

"Evening, Darryl," said the bartender, a heavyset woman with reading glasses on a chain.

"Evening, Ruth," said Harry.

Leah stopped in the doorway and looked up at him.

He was a wolf. He was a six-foot-seven wolf in a long black coat with a tail sticking out the back of it, and he'd just walked into a bar full of loggers in Northern British Columbia, and nobody had so much as blinked.

"Glamour," Harry said, low, at her shoulder. "Far as they're concerned I'm a big bearded oke called Darryl who drives a green Ford and tips well. You saw him at Darlene's."

"I can see *you*."

His ears went up. "You can?"

"Yeah. You look like a wolf in a bar."

He stood there in the doorway and looked down at her, head tilted, ears pricked forward, for so long that Ruth the bartender said, "You two coming in or what? You're letting the heat out."

"Huh," Harry said. "Huh. That's—okay. That's new." His tail flicked. "Later. I want to think about that later."

They took a booth in the back. Leah sat with her back to the wall. Harry sat across from her and didn't seem to mind having his back to the room, which she noticed, and filed away.

The drinking age in British Columbia was nineteen. Leah was nineteen. She ordered a whiskey, because she'd just watched a man get burned to nothing on a mountain and figured she'd earned it.

Harry ordered a double Jameson, a pitcher of Kokanee, two baskets of wings, a plate of nachos, and two orders of poutine.

"Is somebody joining us?" Ruth asked.

"Nope," said Harry.

Ruth looked at Leah. Leah shrugged.

They ate all of it. Then Harry ordered a burger, looked at Leah, and made it two.

"Magic," he said, with his mouth full. "Burns a lot. Plus the—" he gestured at himself, the fur, the muzzle "—you know. The whole situation. I'm always hungry."

"Same," Leah said.

"I noticed. Saw you put away six eggs at Darlene's on Monday. I was impressed." He drank some of his Jameson. "So. You've got a name?"

"Leah."

"Leah." He said it like he was trying it out. "Lekker. Leah what?"

"Just Leah."

His ears flicked, but he let it go.

"And you're Harry," she said.

"Harry Potter. Pleasure."

She picked at the label on the Kokanee pitcher. "My dad's name was Harry," she said.

She hadn't meant to say it. It just came out. Harry stopped chewing.

"Was," he said.

"He died in March. Heart attack." She didn't look up. "He was fifty-one. He was fishing on the Quillayute with my brother the week before and he was fine. And then he wasn't."

Harry didn't say anything for a while. Everybody always said *I'm sorry* right away, like it was a coin you had to put in a slot. He sat across from her and let it sit there on the table between them. The jukebox was playing something by Tom Petty.

"I lost my folks when I was one," he said finally. "Different thing. I don't remember them. But I used to wonder about it a lot, what it'd be like to have someone and then not." He turned his glass on the table. "I can go by something else, if it's weird. I've got options. 'Darryl's' free."

Leah laughed, surprised into it. "No. Harry's fine. It's just a name."

"It's a good name," he said. "Your dad had taste."

She looked up at him. He was looking at her, steady, green eyes in the dim bar light.

"Can I ask what you are?" he said. "You don't have to tell me. I'm just curious. You're not a werewolf. I know what werewolves smell like. Werewolves smell like wet dog and bad decisions. You smell like—" his nose flared, briefly "—cedar. Salt water. Rain."

"Shape-shifter," Leah said. "It's a family thing. It's in the blood." She drank some whiskey. It burned. "I don't really want to talk about home."

"Okay," Harry said.

That was it. *Okay.* He picked up another wing.

Leah watched him eat it. She waited for the follow-up. The *come on*, the *just tell me where you're from*, the *why not*. Everybody in La Push would've asked. Everybody in La Push already knew. It didn't come. He ate the wing and wiped his muzzle on a napkin and asked her if she'd tried Darlene's meatloaf, and when she said yes, he said it needed more pepper, and they argued about that for ten minutes.

She asked him about the accent. He told her it was South African. "Long story," he said. "Involves a cupboard, a fat uncle in the drill business, and a very bad seven years in Johannesburg." She asked him what a hit wizard was. He told her it was like Interpol, with wands. She asked him where the stick went, the one he'd been holding, and he grinned with all his teeth and said, "What stick?" and that was the only question all night he didn't answer straight.

She asked him why the guy—Kessler, Vulk, whatever—had looked at him like that, when he said his name. Harry's ears went sideways. "I was in a war when I was a kid," he said. "I won it. People who were on the other side tend to remember." He drank. "Vulk was on the other side."

She didn't ask anything else about that. He didn't offer.

Around half past twelve, Ruth called last orders, and Harry put down his glass and folded his forepaws on the table and looked at her.

"Right," he said. "Cards on the table. I've got to stay up here another day, maybe two, finishing up. Paperwork, mostly. Geneva wants a report, the Canadian Ministry wants a report, everybody wants a report." His tail thumped once against the back of the booth. "I'd like to spend that time with you. Breakfast. Lunch. Whatever you want. And after that I'd like to keep seeing you. I live up past Atlin, on the lake. It's about four hundred K from here. For me, that's about a second and a half."

Leah sat back. "You don't even know me."

"Nope," he agreed. "That's the fun part, isn't it?" He shrugged his big shoulders. "I like you. You bit a hundred-and-forty-year-old blood mage on the arm while he was cutting you open. You eat like a lumberjack. You've got a mouth on you. And you laughed at me on a mountain covered in dead people, which I'll be honest, I've been turned down in a lot of ways over the years and nobody's ever done that." He tilted his head. "I'm not going to dance around it. I want to take you out. I'd like to keep doing it. You can say no."

Leah looked at him across the wreckage of the nachos for a long time.

She thought about Sam. About imprinting, and destiny, and a love that hit you like a truck and didn't care what you wanted. She knew what that looked like now. She'd watched it gut her.

She thought about how the wolf inside her had rolled over and purred at the smell of a flannel shirt. How it was purring right now, at the sight of his teeth when he grinned and the line of his shoulders under the black coat and the lazy back-and-forth sweep of his tail against the vinyl booth. She waited to feel the hook. The pull. The something-bigger-than-you, grabbing her by the gut and dragging.

Nothing came. There was her, and him, and a cold basket of wings, and a choice.

It was hers. She could feel it was hers.

"Pick me up at Walt's at seven," Leah said. "You're buying breakfast."

His tail went *thump-thump-thump* against the back of the booth.

---

He walked her across the highway to the motel. The sky had clouded over and it had started to snow, fine dry flakes coming down slow out of the dark. At the corner of the building, the old Lab came lumbering out from beside the dumpster.

Leah braced for the whining. For the slink and the tucked tail.

The Lab took one look at Harry and lost his mind. His whole back end went sideways with wagging. He bounded over and shoved his gray muzzle into Harry's coat, whuffling, and Harry crouched down and scrubbed both forepaws into the scruff of the dog's neck and said, "Hello, hello, who's a good boy, *you're* a good boy," in a high silly voice, while the dog licked his muzzle.

Leah watched this with her arms folded.

"Unbelievable," she said. "He hates me."

"Nah. You smell like a big scary wolf." Harry stood up. "I smell like a big friendly wolf. It's all in the marketing."

At the door of room seven, she stopped and turned around. He stood on the walkway in front of her with snow collecting in the fur between his ears, and he was waiting. He stood there with his tail moving slow behind him and left the distance between them right where it was.

"How does this even work," she said. "With the—" she gestured at his muzzle.

"Carefully," Harry said.

She reached up and grabbed two fistfuls of the thick ruff at his throat and pulled his head down and kissed him.

It was weird. It was very weird, for about two seconds. Then it wasn't weird at all. His muzzle was cool and his breath smelled like whiskey and pine and he made a low surprised rumble in his chest that she felt all the way through her own, and one of his big forepaws came up and settled very carefully on the small of her back, claws tucked, and the wolf inside her purred so loud she was sure the whole motel could hear it.

When she let go of him, his ears were standing straight up and his tail was going like a windshield wiper.

"Seven," she said.

"Seven," he agreed, a little dazed.

She went inside and shut the door and leaned against it. Inside her head, it was quiet. Just her. Just Leah.

She was smiling. She could feel it on her face, stupid and huge.

She didn't stop for a long time.

## Five Months Later

"There's fur in my coffee," Leah said.

"That's protein," said Harry, from the stove.

It was a Tuesday in February, a quarter past eight in the morning, thirty-one below outside, and every window in the kitchen was cracked open two inches. The house was a big old log place on the east shore of Atlin Lake, with a stone chimney and a wraparound porch and a wall of windows facing west over the ice toward the mountains. Harry said it was charmed bigger on the inside. Leah said she didn't care how it worked as long as it kept working.

The windows were open because the two of them, together, in one kitchen, with the woodstove going, turned the place into a sauna. They'd figured that out the first week. They'd fought about it, the second week, because Harry thought the woodstove made the place homey and Leah thought she was going to pass out. They'd settled it the way they settled most things, by yelling for about four minutes and then laughing, and now the stove stayed lit and the windows stayed open and the snow blew in a little on the sills and turned to water there, and every so often a raven landed on the porch rail outside and looked in at them like they were insane.

Leah picked the black hair out of her mug with two fingers and flicked it at him. It missed.

"You're shedding again."

"It's winter. I've got my winter coat in."

"It's on the couch. It's on the bed. It's in the *butter*, Harry."

"I'll get a new butter."

He was standing at the stove in a pair of red-and-green plaid pajama pants with a hole in the seat for his tail that she'd cut herself with the kitchen scissors, and nothing else. His winter coat was in, for sure; the black fur over his shoulders and down his back was thick and plush and charcoal at the tips, and his tail had gone so full and bushy it looked like a feather duster. He had four pans going at once. Pancakes in two of them, bacon in one, eggs in another. A stack of pancakes about a foot high was already sitting on a plate by his elbow, and every so often the spatula lifted up off the counter all by itself and flipped one, and set itself back down. On the record player in the living room, Bob Seger was singing about workin' on mysteries without any clues.

Harry sang along. He was not a good singer. He sang loud, and with feeling, and about a quarter-step flat, and his tail swung in time.

Leah sat at the big pine table with her feet tucked up under her, wearing his flannel—the red-and-black one from the ridge, which she'd never given back—with an anatomy textbook open in front of her and a highlighter in her teeth. Athabasca University, online, Introductory Human Anatomy and Physiology. She was paying for it herself, out of what she made doing contract tracking for the ICW, a job she hadn't known existed in October. In December she'd found a goblin smuggler in a hockey rink in Yellowknife by following the smell of his cigar through six blocks of downtown. Geneva had sent her a check and a very formal letter. Harry had framed the letter and hung it in the hallway. She'd told him that was embarrassing. She hadn't taken it down.

"Hey," she said around the highlighter. "What's the bone in the upper arm."

"Humerus," Harry said, flipping bacon.

"Okay, smartass. What's the thing in your wrist. The little ones. Carpals?"

"I'm a wolf, babe. I've got paws. I don't know what's in your wrist."

"You went to wizard school for seven years."

"And they taught me how to turn a teacup into a rat. Nobody ever turned a rat into a nurse."

She threw the highlighter at him. He caught it without turning around, in his tail, which was a thing she still hadn't gotten used to, and tossed it back over his shoulder onto the table in front of her.

She'd moved in two weeks after Tamarack Crossing.

She'd stayed at Walt's for those two weeks, washing Darlene's dishes and eating Darlene's pie. Harry had done his paperwork and then kept coming back. He'd pick her up at seven every morning in the green Ford and take her to breakfast. He'd take her for drives up the Cassiar. One night he'd taken her up into the sky on a big black motorcycle, an old British thing with a sidecar, that had belonged to his godfather, and had flown her straight up over the valley until Tamarack Crossing was a handful of yellow lights in the dark and the northern lights were snaking green and purple overhead. Somewhere over the river, with her arms wrapped around his middle and her face pressed into the back of his coat, he'd yelled over his shoulder, "Move in with me!" and she'd yelled, "Yes!" before he'd even gotten to the end of the sentence.

She'd shown up on his porch the next day with a duffel bag, a stack of Darlene's Tupperware, and a refrigerator magnet shaped like a moose that Darlene had pressed into her hand on her last shift. The magnet was on the fridge now. Darlene still sent pie, every other Sunday, which Harry fetched. He said it was a two-second trip. Leah suspected he stayed for coffee.

She'd written home once.

That was in November. She'd thought about it for a week and then asked Harry to drive her down to the post office in Whitehorse, so the postmark wouldn't say Atlin, and she'd sat in the truck in the parking lot with a pad of paper on her knee for an hour and written four lines.

*Mom. I'm okay. I'm safe. I'm happy, I think. I'm really happy. Don't look for me. Tell Seth I love him. —L.*

No return address. She'd dropped it in the box and gotten back in the truck and stared straight ahead through the windshield, and Harry had reached over and put one big forepaw on the back of her neck and left it there all the way home, and neither of them had said anything.

Two days after that, he'd asked her, very carefully, if she wanted him to find out how things were at home. "I could know by lunch," he'd said. "Nobody'd ever know I looked."

She'd thought about it. Seth. Mom. Jacob and that whole mess with the Cullens, and Bella, and whatever had been growing inside Bella. Sam and Emily. She'd thought about it for a long time.

"No," she'd said. "I don't want to know. I don't want to know if it's good and I don't want to know if it's bad. Not yet. Maybe not for a long time."

"Okay," Harry had said. And he'd never asked again.

In January a bulletin had come through from Geneva by owl. Leah had seen the corner of it on the kitchen table while he was reading it over his coffee. *PACIFIC NORTHWEST — VAMPIRE ACTIVITY — WASHINGTON STATE — OBSERVE ONLY.* Harry had looked up at her. She'd looked back at him. And he'd folded the bulletin in half without saying a word and set it on fire in the palm of his forepaw and dropped the ashes in the woodstove, and gone back to his eggs.

She'd thought about it later that night. She'd lain awake for a while thinking about it. And then she'd rolled over and pressed her face into the fur on his chest and gone back to sleep, because she'd closed that door on purpose, and she was going to leave it closed until she was ready to open it. Whenever that was.

"Food," Harry announced, and set down the plate of pancakes in front of her, and then the bacon, and then the eggs, and then a second plate of pancakes for himself that was taller than the first one. He sat down across from her and pushed the syrup across the table and looked at her with his ears up.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing." His tail thumped against the leg of the chair. "You've got highlighter on your face."

She wiped her cheek. She got syrup on it instead. He laughed at her, a big barking laugh that rattled the windows, and she kicked him under the table, and he caught her foot between his footpaws and wouldn't give it back.

They ate. They ate all of it. They argued about whether Bob Seger was better than Springsteen (he said Seger; she said he was wrong and also old). He told her about the job he had in Mongolia next week, a smuggling ring moving dragon eggs across the border from China, and asked if she wanted to come and track for him, and she said yes before he finished the question, which was getting to be a habit. Outside, the sun came up over the mountains behind the house and lit up the frozen lake in front of it, a flat white plain miles across, and the light came in through the windows and lay across the table in long gold bars.

Leah sat there with her foot trapped under the table and a mouthful of pancake and looked at it. At the light on the table. At the big black wolf across from her in his stupid Christmas pajama pants, licking syrup off one claw.

She'd thought this was a thing that happened to other people.

She'd thought it was a thing that happened to Emily. She'd thought she'd had her shot at it, at sixteen, and blown it, or had it taken away from her, and that was that. Fate. Destiny. The wolf. She'd thought the rest of her life was going to be running, and being angry, and listening to everybody else be happy inside her own head.

She'd told him, in November. About the not-being-able-to-have-kids thing. She'd made herself do it before things went any further, because she'd done the math, and he was the kind of guy who'd want them. She'd sat on the end of his bed—their bed—with her hands balled up in her lap and told him that after she first phased her body had just stopped. No more periods. Nothing. She'd told him she was pretty sure she was a genetic dead end, and she'd waited for his face to change.

His face hadn't changed. He'd just looked at her for a while with his head tilted.

"Okay," he'd said.

"Okay?" she'd said. "That's it? *Okay?*"

"I grew up in a cupboard, Leah," he'd said. "The people I had fed me sometimes. That was about it. So I went and made a family, out of whoever turned up and stuck around." He'd reached out and taken one of her balled-up fists in his forepaw and opened it up, one finger at a time, very carefully, claws tucked. "So, ja. Okay. Then it's you and me. And whatever strays wander in."

She'd cried. She'd been embarrassed about it for about a week. He'd never once brought it up.

Across the table, Harry finished licking off the claw and caught her looking.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing," said Leah. "You've got syrup on your face."

He didn't. He wiped it anyway. She laughed at him, and he let go of her foot, and she kept it right where it was.

## Nine Months

Leah woke up in June because she was going to throw up.

She knew it the way you know you're falling. One second she was asleep, sprawled across most of the bed with her face in Harry's shoulder and the early sun coming gray through the curtains, and the next second her eyes were open and her mouth was full of spit and her stomach was climbing up her throat like it wanted out.

She made it to the bathroom. Barely. She hit her knees on the tile so hard she cracked it and got the toilet lid up just in time, and then she was heaving, and heaving, and heaving, everything from last night's moose stew coming up in a hot sour rush that burned her nose.

When it finally stopped she knelt there with her forehead on her arm on the cold porcelain, shaking, breathing hard through her mouth.

She didn't get sick.

She hadn't been sick a single day since she first phased. None of them got sick. The fever-heat burned everything out—colds, flu, food poisoning, all of it. Paul had once eaten a gas station burrito that had been sitting under a heat lamp since the Clinton administration on a dare, and nothing had happened to him except he'd been smug about it for a month.

She threw up again.

"Leah?"

Harry was standing in the bathroom doorway. He had to duck to stand in it. He was in the shape she liked best, the tall one, black fur and green eyes, in nothing but the gray sweatpants she'd stolen and then given back. His fur was flattened on one side from the pillow and sticking straight up on the other. His ears were flat against his skull. His tail had gone dead still behind him.

"Babe," he said. "You okay?"

"I'm throwing up," she said into the toilet bowl.

"I can see that." He came in and knelt down on the tile beside her, and one big forepaw settled on her back between her shoulder blades, heavy and cool. His other forepaw gathered her hair up off her neck and held it. "You don't throw up."

"I *know* I don't throw up, Harry."

"Was it the stew? I ate the stew. I'm fine."

"You'd be fine if you ate a tire."

"Fair." His paw moved in a slow circle on her back. "Hold still a sec. Let me look."

She felt him do it. She'd felt him do it before, the couple of times she'd come back from a job with something broken: a feeling like cool water running over her skin, starting at the top of her head and moving down, slow and careful, all the way to her toes. A faint silvery light washed over her, there and gone.

Harry's paw stopped moving on her back.

She waited. He didn't say anything.

She lifted her head off her arm and looked at him.

His ears had shot straight up. All the way up, so stiff they quivered. His eyes had gone very wide and very round, the green almost swallowed by black. His mouth was a little bit open. As she watched, his ears went slowly back down flat again, and then halfway up, and then back down, like they couldn't decide.

"What," Leah said. "What is it. Am I dying?"

"Uhh," said Harry.

"*Harry.*"

"Babe," he said. "Uhm." His tail twitched once, behind him, and went still again. "You're pregnant."

Leah stared at him.

The bathroom was very quiet. Outside a loon was calling somewhere out on the lake. Through the open window she could smell the water, and pine, and the dew burning off the porch.

"I'm what," she said.

"Pregnant. Up the duff. With child. Knocked—" he caught the look on her face and swallowed. "About seven weeks. Give or take."

"That's not possible."

"I'm looking right at it, Leah. It's very possible. It's in there. It's got a heartbeat. It's going like a hummingbird."

"I *can't*. I told you I can't. I haven't had a period since I was *eighteen*, Harry, I'm a genetic dead end, my whole body just *stopped*—"

"Well, it's started again!"

"*How?*"

He opened his mouth. He closed it. His ears went sideways.

Leah pointed one shaking finger at him, still on her knees in front of the toilet with puke on her chin.

"This is you," she said. "This is *your* fault. This is your freaky magic sperm."

"My *what?*"

"Your freaky magic wizard sperm! And your stupid *knot!*"

"Oi!" His ears went flat. "Don't bring the knot into this!"

"I'm *absolutely* bringing the knot into this! The knot is *why* this is happening, Harry!"

"You didn't have to take it!" He threw both forepaws up in the air. "Every time! You could've said no! You said you couldn't have kids! You said, and I quote, 'Don't worry about it, Harry, I'm a dead end, go nuts!' Those were your words! *Go nuts!*"

"*Because I couldn't!*" Leah yelled. "I couldn't have kids, and then you came along with your freaky magic *cum* and your giant stupid wolf *knot*, and you broke my *uterus*—"

"I *fixed* it, if anything—"

"*Nobody asked you to fix it!*"

They glared at each other across the bathroom floor. Harry's ears were pinned. Leah's chin had puke on it. The loon called out on the lake again, long and wavering, and somewhere a raven answered.

Then Harry's tail moved.

Just a little. Just a twitch at the very tip, behind him, against the tile. Then another one. Then a slow, sweeping thump, side to side, *whump*, *whump*, against the side of the tub.

Leah looked at it.

"Don't," she said.

"I'm not doing anything," said Harry.

"Your tail's doing something."

"My tail has a mind of its own, babe, you know this." *Whump. Whump-whump.* His ears were creeping back up. "It's not my fault."

"*Nothing* is ever your fault—"

"We're having a puppy," Harry said.

His voice cracked on it. Right in the middle. And she saw his face—saw the long wolf's face she'd memorized over nine months, every whisker, the place behind his left ear where he liked to be scratched—do something she'd never seen it do before. It went sort of crumpled. His eyes had gone wet and shining. He looked so stunned and so scared and so stupidly, hopelessly happy that it hit her right under the breastbone like a fist.

*I grew up in a cupboard,* he'd told her. *So I went and made a family.*

"Don't call it a puppy," Leah said, and her voice cracked too.

"Cub? Pup? Is pup better?"

"*Harry.*"

"Little furball. Small fry. The bun. Wolf bun—"

She launched herself at him across the bathroom floor. He caught her, of course he caught her, he always caught her, and they went over backward together onto the bathmat in a heap, and she buried her face in the thick fur of his chest and wiped her puke chin on him on purpose, and he laughed, and she laughed, and then she was crying, big ugly gulping sobs she couldn't stop, and he wrapped both arms around her and held on.

"You broke my uterus," she said, muffled, into his fur.

"I know," he said into her hair. "I'm so sorry. I'm the worst."

"You and your stupid knot."

"Me and my stupid knot."

"And your freaky magic cum."

"It's very freaky," he agreed. "I'll have a word with it."

She laughed so hard she got the hiccups. His chest was shaking under her cheek, and his tail was going *whump-whump-whump-whump* against the side of the tub, steady as a heartbeat, and his big forepaw had come down to rest very gently, very carefully, claws tucked all the way in, low across her stomach. Over the place where something the size of a blueberry was going like a hummingbird.

They lay there for a long time on the bathroom floor with the sun coming up.

"Seven weeks," Leah said eventually.

"Give or take."

"Is it—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Is it gonna be a wolf? Like, is it gonna come out with fur? Because I swear to God, Harry, if I have to give birth to a *litter*—"

"One," he said quickly. "Just the one. One heartbeat. I checked twice." A pause. "I don't know about the fur."

"Great."

"Could be fur."

"*Great.*"

"Could be fur *and* magic." He sounded way too happy about it. "Could come out levitating its rattle. Could set the curtains on fire. I set my aunt's hair on fire when I was four. Did I ever tell you that? Accidental magic. It was the best day of my life."

"Oh my God."

"Could have a tail."

Leah lifted her head off his chest and looked at him. His ears were all the way up. His eyes were still wet. He was grinning at her with all of his teeth.

The wolf inside her, the one that had looked at the world for a year and gotten mad, rolled over on its back and purred.

"I'm gonna need so much pie," she said.

Harry's tail went through the side of the tub.
