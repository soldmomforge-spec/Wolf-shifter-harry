# North of Everything

## Chapter Three: Undertow

---

Harry woke up the way he always did, all at once.

One second he was flat on his back on the library sofa with *Beast-Blooded Babies* going up and down on his chest. The next, his eyes were open, his ears were up, and his forepaw had closed over her hand on top of the book. No yawn. No stretch. Leah had asked him about it once, back in Atlin, and he'd said *bad habit from a tent* and gone to make coffee.

"Hey," he said. His voice was thick with sleep. "You okay? She okay?"

"She's fine. She's kicking my liver." Leah held up the paper. "Owl came."

He let go of her hand and took the paper. She watched him read. His eyes went down the page once, fast, the way he read everything, and then back to the top and down again, slower. At *high teens* his ears tipped back. At the clipping stapled to the back—the logging road, the rain, *TRIBAL OFFICIALS DECLINE COMMENT*—they went back further and stayed there.

He laid the advisory face-down on top of the book.

"That's home," he said.

"That's home."

"I burned the one in January." He was looking at the ceiling. "You saw."

"I saw."

"I didn't read it first. Just so you know. I didn't read it and then burn it."

"I know, Harry."

He lay there. The fire popped. Out in the hall, one of the portraits was snoring, a long whistling snore that Kreacher said belonged to a Potter who'd died in 1702 and never stopped being tired.

"There were ten of us when I left," Leah said. "Ten. If there's eighteen now, that's eight kids in a year. You don't get eight kids in a year unless something keeps coming around for them to smell." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the way it used to go in the pack mind when Paul said something stupid. "Collin and Brady were fourteen. The new ones'll be younger. Twelve. Thirteen. Some kid running a hundred and eight in his mom's kitchen and going through the wall."

Harry sat up, carefully, so her hand slid down off his chest into his lap, and the book went with it.

"What do you want to do?" he said.

"I want to look." She'd practiced it on the stairs. It still came out smaller than she meant it to. "Just look. From the trees. I want to smell it. I want to know if Seth's—" She stopped. "I want to see my mom."

He looked at her for a long time. His tail, hanging off the end of the sofa, had slowed down. It hadn't stopped.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. Two conditions."

"Here we go."

"Pye says yes. And we go by Portkey and we come back by Portkey, and I don't go four-legs anywhere near their land." His ears flicked toward the window, toward the moor, toward a coast five thousand miles west. "Eighteen kids who've never seen anything like me come out of the trees at once, somebody gets hurt. Probably not me."

"And if something comes at us?"

"Then I'll stay on two legs and be very rude to it," said Harry.

---

Healer Pye's answer came back that evening by the same tawny owl, which took another piece of bacon on its way out.

*Lord Potter—*

*Portkey, yes. Apparition, no. Changing, no. Fighting, no. Biting, no. Three weeks and not a day over, or I come and fetch her myself, and I will not be pleasant about it. She is to eat every two hours. So are you, by the look of you.*

*—M. Pye*

Leah read it over his shoulder.

"She likes me better," she said.

"Everybody likes you better," said Harry.

---

They left on Friday.

The Portkey was a paper Starbucks cup.

"Seattle office," said Harry, holding it out.

"Of course it is."

"Told you. Every office has a—"

"That's a sponsorship, Harry."

He grinned at her with all his teeth, and counted to three, and the world grabbed her behind the navel.

---

The ICW's Seattle office was over a fish stall in Pike Place Market, up a staircase that only existed if you were holding the right cup. Harry got her down it and out through the market and into a rented black Suburban in about four minutes, with the scent-dampening charm cool over her face and one forepaw on the small of her back the whole way. The kid at the rental counter asked a big boring guy with a beard named Darryl if he wanted the extra insurance. Darryl said yes.

The ferry to Bainbridge. The bridge over Hood Canal. The 101 bending west along the bottom of the Strait, Port Angeles gray and wet, and then the trees closing in and the long black water of Lake Crescent coming up on the left with fog lying on it like steam off a cup. Somewhere along the lake Harry glanced over at her, and she felt the coolness slide off her face, and the peninsula came in through the cracked window all at once.

Wet cedar. Moss. Rotting fir needles and elk and wood smoke and salt, a long way off.

Leah put her hand over her mouth.

Harry drove with one forepaw on the wheel and put the other one on her knee and left it there, and his thumb claw moved back and forth over the denim, slow.

*WELCOME TO FORKS*, said the sign. The Thriftway, where she'd worked two years of Saturdays. The police station, with Charlie Swan's cruiser out front. All of it gray and wet and exactly where she'd left it, which felt wrong. She'd run a thousand miles north and crossed an ocean and gotten married and pregnant, and Forks hadn't even repainted the Thriftway sign.

Then the La Push road, through the trees, and the ocean at the end of it.

---

The house was where she'd left it too.

Gravel road. Stand of alder, gone yellow at the tops. Little two-bedroom with the blue trim Dad had painted the summer she was twelve, faded now, peeling under the eaves. Mom's car in the drive.

Harry pulled in behind it and turned off the engine. The rain ticked on the roof.

"I'll wait here," he said.

"You can come in."

"I'll wait here," he said again. "Go and see your mum."

She looked at him. He had his ears back, a little, and his eyes on the house.

She got out.

She went around the side to the kitchen door, the way she always had, because nobody on the rez used a front door except cops and Jehovah's Witnesses. It wasn't locked. She'd known it wouldn't be. She stood with her hand on the knob and breathed.

Coffee. Lemon cleaner. Fish. The cigarette smell was gone. She'd known it would be gone, and she still went looking for it, and it was still gone. In its place, faint and new, there was something she knew from a hundred backyard barbecues and her father's funeral: Old Spice and gun oil and Rainier beer.

*Charlie Swan.*

Leah closed her eyes. "Oh, Mom," she said under her breath. "Seriously?"

She opened the door.

Her mother was at the sink with her back to the door, washing a pot. She was wearing an old gray sweatshirt that said *QUILEUTE DAYS 1998* and her hair was cut shorter than Leah had ever seen it, just above her shoulders, with more gray in it than there'd been a year ago. The radio was on. Somebody on KONP was reading the tide tables.

On the fridge, under the magnet shaped like a salmon, there was a Thriftway receipt gone soft and yellow at the edges.

Beside it, under a magnet shaped like a crab, there was a sheet of paper torn from a legal pad, folded in thirds and then unfolded again so many times the creases had gone white. Four lines. Leah could read them from the door.

*Mom. I'm okay. I'm safe. I'm happy, I think. I'm really happy.*

Leah's throat shut.

Her mother turned off the water. She stood very still at the sink with her hands in the dishwater, and her head came up, the way Leah's own head came up when the wind changed.

Then she turned around.

Sue Clearwater looked at her daughter standing in the kitchen doorway in a man's flannel and a gray-green shawl, with rain in her hair. Her eyes went to Leah's face. Then down. Leah watched them go down, and stop, and stay there.

The pot slid out of her mother's hands and went back into the sink with a splash.

"Leah," Sue said.

"Hi, Mom."

Her mother crossed the kitchen in three steps with her wet hands held out in front of her and took Leah's face in them, and the hands were cold and smelled like dish soap, and Leah was crying before her mother even said anything else.

"Look at you," said Sue. Her voice cracked. "Look at you. Look at you."

"I know."

"You're—" Her mother's hand went down, slowly, and stopped an inch from the curve under the flannel. She looked up at Leah's face. "Can I?"

Leah nodded. She couldn't talk.

Her mother put her hand flat on the bump. Leah felt the pup shift under it, a slow roll, the way she did when somebody new touched her, like she was turning over to get a better look.

Sue made a sound Leah had never heard her make. It wasn't a word.

"I thought—" her mother said. "Honey. I thought you couldn't. You stopped. I knew you stopped. I buy the groceries, I knew you weren't—"

"I thought I couldn't too."

"Then how—"

"Long story," said Leah, wiping her face on the shawl, which Kreacher would kill her for. "Involves a wizard."

Her mother looked at her.

"A *what*?"

---

Harry was standing beside the Suburban in the rain when they came out onto the back step. He had his coat buttoned to the collar and his forepaws behind his back. His ears were up.

To Leah, he looked like he always looked. Six-foot-seven. Black fur going dark and spiky in the wet. Long muzzle, green eyes, a tail hanging still behind him with the rain running off the end of it.

Her mother looked at him for a while.

"He's very big," Sue said finally.

"Mom. What do you see?"

"A very big man with a beard." Sue squinted. "And a very nice coat."

"Harry," Leah called. "Drop it. She knows about wolves. She birthed two."

Harry looked at her. Then he looked at Sue. He didn't move. Leah didn't see anything change.

Her mother took a step back and grabbed the doorframe.

She stood there with one hand on the doorframe and the other over her mouth and looked at him. At the ears, and the muzzle, and the tail, and the paws behind his back with the claws curled in. The rain came down between them. Down the road, someone's dog started barking and then thought better of it.

Leah had seen her mother hear the news that her husband was dead. She'd seen her mother find out her daughter could turn into a horse-sized wolf, and her son too, inside of a month. Sue Clearwater had been the daughter of a council family and the wife of a council elder. She'd grown up on stories about men who turned into wolves. She'd just never had one standing in her driveway in a long black coat.

"Mrs. Clearwater," said Harry. He didn't come any closer. His voice was low. "I'm sorry to turn up like this. My name's Harry."

Leah saw it hit her.

She saw her mother's face go through something. Saw her look at the wolf in her driveway, who had just said her dead husband's name in a South African accent, and saw her hand on the doorframe go tight.

"Harry," Sue said.

"Ja. I can go by something else. I've told Leah. Darryl's free."

Her mother laughed. It came out of her wet and startled, like Leah's own laugh had come out of her in a bar in Tamarack Crossing a year ago. She put her hand over her eyes.

"No," Sue said. "No. Harry's fine." She took her hand off her eyes and looked at him again, up and up. "You're the father."

"Ja."

"And you married her."

His ears went sideways. "In July. Under a tree. It was a bit sudden."

Sue looked at Leah. Leah held up her left hand, and the gray stone flashed blue and green in the rain light.

Her mother looked at the ring for a long time.

"Well," she said finally. "You'd better come inside before Charlie drives past."

---

Seth came through the trees twenty minutes later.

Leah heard him coming a long way off. A big animal crashing through the alder and the salal at a dead run with no attempt whatsoever to be quiet, which was so exactly Seth that she had to put her coffee down. Then a pause, and a grunt, and a hopping noise that was somebody pulling on cutoffs on one foot. Then the back door banged open so hard it hit the wall.

He'd grown. God, he'd grown. He'd been gangly and fifteen when she left, all knees and elbows, the youngest wolf in the pack and the size of it. Now he filled the doorway. Six-four, maybe more, shoulders like a door, hair cropped short the way they all ended up cropping it. He was barefoot and shirtless and soaked and steaming in the cold. He still had the same face. The same stupid, open, wide-eyed face, like a puppy that had just seen a ball.

"*LEAH*," he yelled.

"Hi, Seth."

"*Leah!* Jake said—Embry smelled you on the road, he said, he said a car came through with you in it, and I said no way, she said don't look for her, and he said dude I *know* what your sister smells like—" He'd crossed the kitchen while he was talking. He picked her up out of the chair. He got about halfway through doing it before he felt the bump against his stomach and froze with her feet six inches off the floor.

He looked down.

He put her back down very, very slowly.

"Uh," said Seth.

"Yeah."

"Leah. Are you—"

"Yeah."

"*How?* You said—in your head, you always thought—"

"Wizard," said their mother, from the stove, without turning around. "Don't ask. I'm still working on it."

Seth's head swung around toward the living room, where Harry was sitting on the floor with his back against the couch, because the couch was too small and the armchair had made a noise when he'd looked at it. He'd been sitting there for twenty minutes holding a mug of coffee in both forepaws, saying *Ja, ma'am* and *No, ma'am* and *It was a blood mage, ma'am*, while Sue asked him questions and Leah watched her mother decide things about him one at a time.

Seth stared at Harry. Harry looked back at Seth.

"Whoa," said Seth.

"Howzit," said Harry.

"*Whoa.* Leah. He's a wolf. He's a wolf on *two legs*. Is he—are you—" Seth took a step toward the living room, nostrils flaring, and stopped. "Why do you smell like us?"

The kitchen went quiet.

Harry's ears came up slowly. "Do I?"

"Kinda. Not like Sam or anybody. Like—" Seth frowned, sniffing. "Like the cliffs. Like how the cliffs smell. You know, Leah. Like when you go off the high one and you come up and the whole back of your throat's salt." He looked at his sister. "Right?"

Leah didn't say anything.

She'd been smelling Harry for a year. Earth. Pine. Cold stone at the bottom of a creek. She'd never once thought *cliffs*. She sat back down in her chair and breathed in, through her nose, the way she used to on patrol, and under the earth and the pine and the wet wool of his coat there was something she'd stopped noticing a year ago because it smelled like the inside of her own head.

Salt. Cedar. Rain.

"Huh," she said quietly.

Harry was looking at her across the kitchen. He didn't say anything either. His tail had come around to lie along the floor by his footpaws. It twitched at the tip, once.

"He can't be glamoured to you," Leah said. "Either. Can he. Seth sees what I see."

"Glamour's off," said Harry. "For your mum."

"Put it back on."

He looked at her. A second passed. "On."

Seth was still looking right at him. "Is something supposed to happen?"

Harry's ears went all the way up. He looked at Seth for a long time with his head tilted, and then he looked at Leah, and then down at the coffee in his paws.

"Huh," Harry said, very quietly.

---

Dinner was salmon and fry bread and a pot of elk stew that Sue had been planning to freeze. They ate all of it. Seth matched them for about half the meal and then sat back and watched Harry go through his fourth bowl with an expression of pure religious awe. Sue stood at the stove and made more fry bread and watched the food vanish and didn't say anything until the end, when she looked at the empty pot and said, "Well, I'll be going to Port Angeles for groceries, then."

Leah asked. She made herself ask.

So they told her, in pieces, over the dishes. Seth told most of it, because Seth told everything. The baby—*Renesmee*, Seth said, *we call her Nessie*—who'd come out of Bella a few days after Leah left and nearly killed her doing it. How Bella had died on a table in the Cullens' house, and come back with red eyes. How Jacob had walked into that room meaning to kill the baby and walked out imprinted on her instead.

Leah put down the plate she was drying.

"On the *baby*," she said.

"It's a big brother thing," said Seth quickly. "For now. It's—"

"Seth. She was a *day old*."

"Minutes," Seth admitted.

Leah looked at her mother. Her mother looked at the dishwater.

Then the winter. Dozens of vampires from all over the world camped at the Cullens', because some vampires in Italy—the Volturi—thought the baby was something she wasn't. A field full of snow in January, wolves and Cullens on one side, Italians in black cloaks on the other. Nobody fought. Seth said the Italians killed the vampire who'd reported the baby, and went home.

"And all those vampires hanging around all winter," Sue said quietly, at the sink, "the boys started going."

"How many?"

"Nine since November." Her mother's hands had stopped moving in the water. "The youngest was twelve. Tommy Lahote, Paul's cousin. He went through his mother's back wall on Christmas Eve."

Leah shut her eyes.

"Sam's got most of them," said Seth. "Jake's got me and Embry and Quil. That's it. It's mostly fine, Leah. The treaty's fine. Nessie's fine." He hesitated. "Something's been killing people up around Port Angeles. Since August. Sam's been chasing it. Nobody can catch it. It doesn't smell like the Cullens' friends, it doesn't smell like the Italians. It's like it's—" He waved his hand. "There and then it's gone."

Leah opened her eyes. Harry was leaning in the kitchen doorway with his arms folded, listening, and his ears had swung toward Seth like two satellite dishes.

"Gone how?" he said.

"Just gone. Trail stops. Like it lifted off."

Harry's ears stayed on him for a second. Then he looked at Leah, and she saw him put it somewhere and close the lid on it.

"Later," he said.

Then her mother turned off the water and dried her hands and sat down across the kitchen table from Leah and took her left hand and turned it over in the light.

"You got married without me," Sue said.

"It was kind of spur of the moment."

"Mm-hm." Sue turned the ring so the stone caught the light over the table. "Your father and I got married at the courthouse in Port Angeles, you know. On a Tuesday. Because I was—" She stopped.

Leah stared at her. "Mom."

"Never mind."

"*Mom.* Was I a—"

"Seventeen days early," said Sue Clearwater, with immense dignity, "and nobody on this reservation can count."

In the doorway, Harry laughed so hard he had to hold onto the frame.

---

She slept in her old bed that night, or tried to.

Her old room was the same. Seahawks pennant. Prom picture with Sam she'd turned face-down on the dresser, still face-down. The closet door open an inch and the coffee can gone from the top shelf. Mom had put clean sheets on the bed, like she'd been doing it every week for a year just in case.

Harry looked at the single bed for a while. Then he looked at it a little harder, and it was a double, and then a queen, and the room didn't seem to have gotten any smaller to fit it.

"Mom's gonna notice," Leah said.

"I'll put it back."

They lay in the dark. Rain on the roof. The surf down on First Beach, heavy and slow. Seth snoring through the wall. The pup pushing against Harry's forepaw where it lay over her stomach, the way she did every night around eleven, like she was checking he was there.

"Leah," he said into her hair.

"Mm."

"Tomorrow. Where do you want to go?"

She'd been thinking about it since the ferry.

"Dad," she said. "I want to go see Dad."


---

The cemetery sat on a hill at the north end of the village, up a muddy track through the spruce, where the ground looked out over the mouth of the Quillayute and the gray hump of James Island and the whole Pacific behind it.

The rain had quit sometime before dawn. It was the kind of morning the coast did in October—everything dripping, fog lying in the tops of the trees, the light coming through it white and flat. Leah's boots sank into the moss between the graves. Harry walked beside her with one forepaw hovering an inch off her back, glamoured to everybody in the world except her and Seth.

The graves up here didn't look like the ones in Forks. People left things. Shells. Toy trucks gone pale in the weather. A carved paddle. A Seahawks cap nailed to a cross. Abalone. Cans of Rainier, unopened, set neatly on the headstones of people who'd liked a beer.

Dad's was near the edge, where the hill dropped toward the river.

Somebody had been up this week. There were fresh yellow chrysanthemums in a mason jar, the Thriftway kind. Tucked into the corner where the granite met the grass was a red-and-white Daredevle spoon. Seth's, Leah thought. Seth had been bringing lures up here since the funeral, a new one every couple of months, because Dad always said a man couldn't own too many lures and then lost one in the first ten minutes.

*HARRY CLEARWATER*, said the stone. *HUSBAND. FATHER. FISHERMAN.*

Leah got down on her knees in the wet. It took a while now. Harry's paw stayed under her elbow the whole way down.

"Hi, Dad," she said.

Her voice came out fine. She'd figured it wouldn't.

She sat back on her heels and didn't know what to say. She'd never been any good at the talking-to-graves thing. Mom did it. Seth did it every week, out loud—told Dad about the Mariners and his grades and which vampire had said what. Leah had come up here twice after the funeral and stood with her hands jammed in her pockets and said nothing, and left.

She reached into the pocket of the flannel and took out the little silver frame.

The cloudy glass. The small shape curled up in the middle of it, folded ears and blunt muzzle and tail tucked around its feet. Charcoal fur, silver-gray at every tip. A pale gray blaze down the middle of its face.

She propped it against the headstone, next to the lure.

"That's her," Leah said. "That's your granddaughter. She's got my gray. I know, Dad. I know what it looks like. She's gonna come out furry. Mom's dealing with it." She wiped her nose on the back of her wrist. "You'd have her gutting fish already. She's not even born and you'd have her gutting fish on the tailgate."

Behind her, Harry had gone very still.

"And that's Harry," she said, without turning around. "I know. About the name. I didn't do it on purpose. He's a wolf, Dad. Sort of. And a wizard. He killed a guy on a mountain and then he asked me out. He makes pancakes. He's gonna be so annoying about her you wouldn't believe it."

She heard Harry let out a long breath through his nose.

He came and knelt on the moss beside her. At his height, kneeling put his head about level with hers. He looked at the stone for a long time with his ears forward.

"Mr. Clearwater," he said finally. He said it to the stone, low and plain. "Sir. I've got her. Both of them." A pause. "That's all."

Leah put her hand in the fur of his forearm and held on.

They stayed like that a while. Fog moved through the spruce. Down at the river mouth, the gulls were fighting over something dead.

Then Harry's ears swung toward the track. A truck door slammed, far down the hill.

"Somebody coming up," he said.

"It's a cemetery, Harry. People come up."

"Ja." He stood. "I'll give you a minute. I'll be at the truck."

"You don't have to—"

"I'll be at the truck," he said, and touched the top of her head with one paw, and went.

She watched him go down between the graves. Forty yards to the Suburban, parked by the gate at the bottom of the track. He leaned on the front bumper with his arms folded and turned his face toward the ocean, very politely, like a man pretending not to listen.

Leah turned back to the stone.

She talked. Out loud, finally. She told Dad about Darlene's pie and Tamarack Crossing and a goblin in a hockey rink in Yellowknife. She told him about the moor, and the skylark that never shut up, and Kreacher. She told him she was taking an anatomy class online and she'd been thinking about nursing, after, when the pup was old enough. She told him she was sorry she'd left Mom. She told him she wasn't sorry she'd left. She said both, and then sat there with her hands in her lap, a little surprised at herself.

Somebody was walking up between the graves behind her.

Rubber boots, squelching. He stopped two rows over. She heard him kneel, and the clink of a glass jar set down on stone, and a long breath out, the kind people let go when they've come to talk to somebody who can't talk back.

She smelled him then. Hand sanitizer. Gas-station coffee. Cedar smoke in his jacket. Under it, skin, clean and ordinary.

She didn't think anything about it.

She picked up the frame and kissed the glass, which was stupid, and put it back in her pocket. She put her hand flat on the top of the stone. "Okay," she said. "I'll come back before we go. Promise. Bye, Dad."

She started to get up. It was harder than getting down. She got one boot under her on the wet moss, and it slid.

"Whoa—" said the man two rows over. She heard him get up fast. "Hey. Careful, it's slick as anything up here. Let me—"

He was coming over. She caught herself with one hand on her father's headstone, got her balance, and looked up to tell him she was fine.

He was maybe twenty-seven. Not tall. Five-ten, maybe. A green rain jacket open over blue hospital scrubs, a lanyard with a clinic badge on it, a Mariners cap with dark hair cut short underneath. A round, plain face with a little scar through one eyebrow, and brown eyes, and a worried crease between them as he held out his hand to help her up.

She looked at him.

Everything let go.

She'd felt it before, from the inside of other people. Sam, at sixteen. Jared. Quil, looking at a two-year-old in Emily's kitchen with his whole heart falling out of him. She'd hated it from the inside of every one of them. She'd never felt it from the inside of herself, and it went through her like a logging road dropping out from under a truck. Every line that ran out of her into the world came loose at once. The one to the gray house on the moor. The one to her mother's kitchen. The one to Seth. The one that ran down her left arm and out through the faint gold shimmer on her wrist and forty yards down the hill to a big black shape leaning on a bumper—she felt that one go slack in her hand, like a rope with nobody on the other end.

And the one that ran down. Into the small curled weight under her ribs.

That one held. She grabbed for it in a panic, and it was there. It was still there. But it had gone quiet, like somebody had reached inside her and turned the volume down on her daughter.

Then something came up out of the ground.

Up through the moss. Up through her father's grave. Old, and cold, and patient, and so big she couldn't see the edges of it. It came up through the soles of her boots into the middle of her, and it took hold of her the way you'd take hold of a compass, and it turned her.

Toward him.

The whole world slid down into a single point, and the point was a man in a green jacket and a stupid Mariners cap with his hand out. The fog could've burned off. The hill could've slid into the river. There was nothing to look at but the crease between his eyebrows and the scar through one of them. He was the only solid thing on a hill full of fog. She'd been falling for a year without knowing it, and he was the ground.

Deep in her chest, her wolf rolled over onto its back.

It stretched, slow and boneless. And it purred.

For him.

*No*, Leah thought.

The man had stopped with his hand halfway out. He'd gone gray under the brown of his face. His other hand was pressed flat to the middle of his chest, the way you'd press on a bruise.

"Sorry," he said. His voice had gone thin. "Sorry. Are you okay? I feel—" He laughed, a small, lost noise. "I'm Isaac. Isaac Wray. I'm at the clinic. You're Sue's girl, right? Leah? I remember you. You pushed me off the dock at Quileute Days when I was nine."

*Isaac*, said everything inside her. *Isaac. Isaac. Isaac.*

*No*, Leah thought. *No, no, no, no, no—*

Against her breastbone, the necklace stuttered. She felt it. *Big-slow, small-quick. Big-slow. Small—* A gap where the little light should have been. One beat too long.

Down the hill, something chimed, high and sharp, like a bell struck once.

Then Harry was there.

She didn't see him come. One second he'd been forty yards away at the truck. The next he was standing between her and Isaac Wray with a forepaw on her arm, and gravel from the track was still pattering down onto the graves behind him where his footpaws had thrown it. The gold watch was open in his other paw. The two lights in it were pulsing again. Big-slow, small-quick. Back in time.

"Leah," he said. "What happened. The watch went—"

She looked up at him.

For one second—she'd count it later, she'd count it a hundred times—she looked up at her husband's face, at the long muzzle and the green eyes and the ears pricked straight up in alarm, and he was somebody she didn't know.

A big strange animal in a long black coat. Standing in her way.

Her wolf growled at him.

It came up out of her before she could stop it, low and ugly, a sound she'd only ever made at things she meant to kill. It came out of a pregnant woman's human throat, and it sounded wrong there.

Harry's ears went flat.

He stayed exactly where he was, with his paw on her arm, and went very still. She watched him look past her at Isaac Wray standing gray-faced on the wet grass with his hand on his chest, and back at her, and put it together. She saw the second he did. His tail had been lashing behind him in alarm.

It stopped.

It stopped dead, and hung there.

"Leah," Harry said, very quietly.

She tore her eyes off Isaac. It was like tearing off a fingernail. She dragged them up to Harry's face and held them there, and her wolf screamed and clawed inside her to look back, *look back, look back, he's right there, he's right there*—

"Get me out of here," she said. "Harry. Get me in the truck. Get me out of here *right now*."

He picked her up without a word. One arm under her knees, one behind her back. He lifted her off the wet ground and turned and walked down the hill between the graves, and she pressed her face into his coat and shut her eyes. She could feel Isaac Wray behind her like a fire at her back. Every step down the hill hurt. It hurt in her chest, in the dead center of her, like something was being pulled out of her on a hook, and with every step it got worse.

"*Wait*," Isaac called behind them. He sounded scared. "Wait—what's—what *is* this—"

Harry kept walking.

He put her in the passenger seat and shut the door and came around the front and got in, and the Suburban went backward down the track so fast the mud came up over the windshield.

Leah sat with both arms wrapped around her stomach and her eyes squeezed shut and rocked.

"I imprinted," she said. "Harry. I *imprinted*."

Under her arms, the pup lay still.

---

It was a mile from the cemetery to the house. Every foot of it hurt.

She told him in the truck, because she couldn't stop talking. In pieces. Out of order. Her voice was going up and down and she couldn't make it stop doing that either.

Imprinting. A wolf sees somebody and that's it. That's the person. Gravity moves. You'd do anything for them. You'd be anything they needed—brother, friend, protector, lover, whatever. You can't stay away. It hurts to stay away. The elders called it a gift. The legends called it the spirit choosing. Sam had imprinted on her cousin. Leah had been with Sam since she was sixteen and he'd seen Emily one afternoon in the Uleys' yard and that was it, he was gone, he was just gone, and she'd had to live inside his head for a *year* and feel him love Emily from the inside—

"I know about Sam," said Harry. His voice was very level. He was driving with both forepaws on the wheel. "You talk in your sleep."

"I *what*?"

"Not much. His name, mostly. Not since about Christmas." He turned into the gravel road. "Keep going."

So she kept going. Jared and Kim. Quil and Claire, who'd been *two*. Paul and Jacob's sister. Jacob and a baby that had been a few minutes old. Nobody chose it. Nobody could say no to it. It didn't care who you were with, or what you wanted, or whose ring you had on.

"And I'm feeling it," she said. "Right now. I can feel exactly where he is. He's still up on the hill. He's sitting down on the grass. He's—" She shut her mouth so hard her teeth clicked. "I can *feel* him, Harry."

Harry pulled up behind Mom's car and turned off the engine and sat there.

He looked at her right hand. At the heavy old gold ring with its square green stone.

"Give me your hand," he said.

She gave it to him. He held it in both paws and looked at the ring for a long time, ears forward. She could feel him doing something, a cool narrow touch like the diagnostic charm, going around the band and into the stone.

"It's quiet," he said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means it never tripped." His voice was still level. It was too level. "Anything that tries to get into your head and make you do something. That's what I told you. Anything that tries to get *in*." He set her hand down gently on her knee.

"Because it came up," Leah said. "Out of the ground. Through Dad."

"Ja."

"It's *mine*. Harry. It's in my blood. It's the wolf. It's the same thing that—" Her voice broke. "The ring thinks it's me."

Harry didn't answer. He reached over and put his forepaw on her stomach, under her crossed arms, and held it there.

"Hold still," he said. "I want to check her."

She held still. She felt the narrow cool light go in and stay, and his ears were straight up, and his eyes had gone a long way off.

"Heartbeat's good," he said after a while. "Strong. She's fine. She's—" He stopped.

"She's what."

"Quiet." He swallowed. "She's just very quiet."

Leah started to cry again.

He leaned across the seat with his paw still on her stomach and put his muzzle down against the curve of it, the way he'd done in the library in September, ear-first.

"Hi, pup," he said, very low. "It's Dad. I'm right here."

They waited.

Nothing. Nothing. Then, small and hard, a nudge against the side of his face.

Harry made a sound like something tearing.

---

Her mother knew the second she saw her face.

Leah watched her know. She watched Sue look at her coming through the kitchen door in Harry's arms with mud on her boots and her face wet, and look at Harry, at his ears flat and his tail hanging dead still behind him. And Leah watched her mother's face go back to a kitchen floor six years ago, where she'd sat holding her sixteen-year-old daughter while she screamed.

"Who," Sue said.

"Isaac Wray."

Her mother closed her eyes.

"Marjorie Wray's grandson," Sue said. "He came home from Tacoma in April. He's a nurse at the clinic. He's—" Her mouth went tight. "He's a nice boy, Leah. He's a very nice boy."

"I don't care if he's nice!" Leah heard her own voice crack off the kitchen walls. "I don't *care*, Mom! I don't want him! I don't want him and I can't stop—I can't—"

She couldn't finish it. Harry put her down on the couch, and Sue sat on one side of her, and Harry crouched down on the floor in front of her. Neither of them said anything. Her mother's arm went around her shoulders. Harry's forepaws came up and covered both her hands where they were twisted together on her knees.

And for a while that was all right. That was almost all right.

"I'm going to get it out," Harry said.

He said it the way he'd said *Harry Potter* to Kessler on the ridge. Plain. Like it was a fact about the weather.

"You can't," said Sue, very quietly. "Harry. Nobody's ever—it's the spirit. It's how it's always been. It's in the stories. Nobody can—"

"I'm going to get it out," said Harry again.

Leah looked down at him. At his ears, flat. At the two forepaws over her hands, the claws curled all the way in, the dark pads cool on her skin.

And she heard herself say, in a voice that came out of her calm and cold and reasonable, "What if I don't want you to?"

The room went silent.

Harry looked up at her.

Leah sat there with her own words hanging in the air in front of her face and felt them like a slap. They'd come out of somewhere underneath her, out of the dark place where her wolf was lying on its back purring at a man on a hill a mile away, and they'd used her mouth to do it.

"That was—" Her voice shook. "Harry. That was *it*. That was the thing. I didn't—I don't—"

"I know," Harry said.

"I *don't*, Harry, I swear to God—"

"I know," he said again. "Leah. I know."

He took his paws off her hands, slowly. He sat back on his heels. His ears were pinned so flat to his skull they'd disappeared into his fur, and his face hadn't moved at all.

"Okay," he said.

It was the same word. The one he always said. *Okay*, in a bar in Tamarack Crossing when she said she didn't want to talk about home. *Okay*, on the end of the bed in Atlin when she said she couldn't have kids. *Okay*, on a creek bank, naked and yelling. She'd heard him say it a hundred times in a year. She'd never once heard it cost him anything.

He got up off the floor and went out the back door and shut it quietly behind him.

---

She didn't sleep. She lay in the bed he'd made big in her old room and stared at the ceiling and felt Isaac Wray.

She knew where he lived. She'd never been there. She didn't know how she knew. A blue single-wide on the river road, past the marina, with a porch light that buzzed. She knew he was awake too. She knew he was sitting at his kitchen table with a cup of tea gone cold, rubbing the middle of his chest. She knew it the way she knew where her own hands were.

The house ticked and settled around her. Seth had gone to the Cullens' for the night. Her mother was asleep, or pretending to be.

Harry hadn't come to bed.

At some point she shut her eyes.

---

She opened them on the gravel road.

She was standing in the middle of it in the dark with her bare feet in a puddle. Rain was coming down, fine and cold. She was wearing his flannel and nothing else, and the shawl had come half off one shoulder, and her hair was stuck to her face. Behind her, a hundred yards back, the porch light of her mother's house glowed yellow through the alders. In front of her, the road went down toward the river.

She was walking. She'd been walking for a while. Her feet hurt.

She stopped.

She looked down at her own bare feet on the wet gravel. White in the dark. A cut on one heel where she'd stepped on something.

She thought: *Kayleigh*.

A thin green dress in the frost. Bare feet on the rock. A girl walking up a mountain in the dark with her eyes fixed on the middle of a man's back, humming a song that wasn't hers.

Leah stood in the rain and shook so hard her teeth knocked together.

"Harry," she said.

"Here."

He was right beside her. Two steps off her left shoulder, on the verge, in the dark. He was soaked through, fur slicked flat to his skull, coat black and dripping. He'd been there the whole time. She knew it as soon as she saw him.

"How long," she said.

"Since the back step." He didn't move. "You came out the kitchen door about twenty minutes ago."

"You've been *following* me."

"Ja."

"You said you wouldn't hide behind boulders anymore."

"Look around," Harry said. "Not a boulder for miles. I'm right here."

She looked at him. He was standing very still in the rain with his arms at his sides and his tail hanging motionless behind him, and his eyes were on her face.

"Why didn't you stop me?"

He was quiet for a long moment.

"I gave you a ring," he said, "that says nobody gets in your head and makes you do things. Not even me. Ever." The rain ran off the end of his muzzle. "You walked out the door. I didn't know if it was you or it. I wasn't going to guess wrong."

Leah's face crumpled.

"It's *it*," she said. "It's it, Harry. Stop me. Please. I'm asking. *Stop me*."

He picked her up.

He did it carefully, the way he did everything with her now, and he turned around with her in his arms and started walking back up the road toward the porch light. She could feel the pull in her chest going the other way, back down toward the river, toward a blue single-wide with a buzzing light, and with every step it pulled harder, a hook through the middle of her. She pressed her face into his wet neck and held on with both fists in his ruff.

"Get it out of me," she said into his fur. "Get it out. Get it *out*."

"I'm going to."

"Promise."

He stopped walking. He stood in the middle of the gravel road in the rain and looked down at her.

"I promise," Harry said.

---

He set her on the bed in her old room. He got a towel from the bathroom and dried her feet and looked at the cut on her heel until it closed. He put the shawl back up over her shoulder.

Then he crouched down beside the bed and said, "Hold still. I want to look at it."

"At what?"

"It."

She held still. He looked at her. Looked hard, the way he'd looked at the altar stone on the ridge before it split down the middle. His eyes went unfocused. His nose flared. His ears turned slowly, one and then the other, like he was listening to something under the floor.

He looked for a long time. Five minutes. Ten. The rain on the roof. Her mother's clock in the hall.

"I can see it," he said finally.

"What does it look like?"

He put one claw, very lightly, on her breastbone, just above the necklace.

"There's a thread," he said. "Comes out of you here. Goes out through that wall—" his ears swung toward the river "—that way. Thin. Sort of red-gold. Very pretty." His voice was flat. "And at your end there's a thing. Where it's tied on. Like a burr. Like something with hooks on it that got caught in your fur." He was quiet a second. "It's sitting on top of your wolf. Hooked in. It's got little roots in it already. Hundreds of them. Fine as hair."

"Is it near her?"

He didn't answer.

"Harry."

"The roots are growing," he said. "They're growing toward her."

Leah stopped breathing.

"Slowly," said Harry. "Very slowly. I've got time." He took his claw off her breastbone. "Not a lot. But some."

"How much?"

"I don't know yet." He got up. "I'm going to find out."

"Where are you going?"

He was already at the door. He stopped there with one paw on the frame and looked back at her, and in the light from the hall his eyes were very green and his face had nothing on it at all.

"I need to see one that's set," he said. "One that's had time to grow in. I need to see what it does when it's finished." His ears flicked toward the window, east, toward Forks, toward a big glass house in the trees she'd only ever seen through Jacob's eyes. "Seth said Jacob's is a year old."

Jacob Black had been awake since a quarter to one in the morning, when Seth phased in the trees behind the Clearwaters' house and his whole head lit up like a road flare.

*Jake. Jake. Jake, Leah imprinted.*

Jacob had been asleep on the Cullens' couch with Nessie's feet in his lap. He'd sat up so fast he nearly put her through the coffee table.

So he knew. He knew all of it, the way you knew things in the pack mind, all at once and from the inside. He knew Leah was back. He knew she was big as a house under some guy's flannel and wearing a ring with a stone in it like the northern lights. He knew the guy was a wolf on two legs who'd eaten four bowls of Sue's elk stew and said *ja* a lot. He knew about the cemetery, because Sue had told Seth and Seth had thought about nothing else since. A nurse from the clinic. Marjorie Wray's grandson. Isaac.

And he knew Leah's face, the way Seth had seen it when he looked in on her at half past twelve: lying on her side in her old room with her eyes open, staring at the wall. Jacob knew what was behind that face. He'd had a year and a half of it inside his own head, every time Sam thought about Emily.

Jacob had sat on the Cullens' couch with his elbows on his knees until the sun came up. Edward had come down at some point and sat on the stairs, and hadn't said anything, which for Edward was a big deal.

At twenty to seven, a black Suburban came up the long drive through the cedars and stopped in front of the house.

Seth was back at the Clearwaters' on the kitchen floor in front of the back door, where the guy had asked him to sit. *If she goes for the door*, the guy had said, *sit in front of it. Don't grab her. Just be in the way.* Seth had phased in the yard for thirty seconds at six to pass that along too.

The driver's door opened. The guy got out.

Jacob stood up off the porch rail.

"Huh," he said.

The guy was a wolf. A big one. He was standing on two legs like a man, in boots, and he was taller than Jacob, which almost never happened anymore. Six-seven, maybe. Black fur going charcoal at the edges. Long muzzle. Ears up. A long coat in some kind of heavy black stuff, open over a gray T-shirt, and a tail out the back of it, hanging straight down and not moving at all.

He shut the truck door. He looked up at the house, at the whole glass wall of it with the sun coming through the trees and hitting it, and at the people standing on the porch.

"What do you see?" Edward said, very quietly, beside him.

"A wolf," said Jacob. "Standing up. In a coat."

"I see a large man with a beard."

"I don't see anything," said Alice from the doorway. She sounded upset about it. "Nothing. He's a hole. Like you are. Like *she* was, the day she left." She pressed her fingertips to her temples. "Everything around him is just—gone."

Carlisle went down the steps. Of course Carlisle went down the steps.

"Good morning," he said. "I'm Carlisle Cullen. Seth told us you might come. You're very welcome here."

The wolf looked at him. His nose flared once.

"Doctor Cullen," he said. His voice was low and rough, and the accent was the weird one Seth had been trying to imitate in his head all night. "Harry Potter." His eyes went past Carlisle, up the steps, and landed on Jacob. "Jacob Black."

"Yeah."

"I need ten minutes with your imprint."

Nobody said anything.

Up on the porch, Rosalie made a sound through her nose. Emmett said, "Dude," under his breath. Behind Jacob, through the open door, he heard Bella stop moving.

"With my *what*?" said Jacob.

"Your imprint." The wolf hadn't moved. "Yours and the girl's. I need to look at it. You're the only one I can get at that's had a year to set." His ears flicked once. "Ten minutes. I won't touch her."

Jasper came out onto the porch behind Alice. Jacob felt the air change. That calm, heavy, blanket-over-the-head feeling Jasper put out when things were about to go sideways, like somebody turning down a thermostat in your chest.

The wolf's head turned. Not fast. He looked straight at Jasper.

"Stop that," he said.

Jasper went still. The calm feeling went away like somebody had flicked a switch.

"Huh," said Jasper. He sounded interested. "You felt it."

"I felt it." The wolf turned back to Jacob. "Ten minutes."

"No," said Bella.

She'd come out of the house. She had Nessie on her hip, which was getting ridiculous, since Nessie looked about five now and was all legs. Nessie had her arms around her mother's neck and her bronze curls in her face and was looking at the wolf on the lawn with enormous brown eyes. Bella's eyes had gone mostly gold since spring. They had a little red left in them. Right now the red was showing.

"No," Bella said again. "I'm sorry about Leah. I am. But you're not doing anything to my daughter."

"I won't touch her."

"My shield's over her," said Bella. "You won't be able to see anything anyway."

"Then drop it."

"No."

The wolf looked at her. He looked at her for long enough that Jacob felt his own shoulders come up. The guy hadn't raised his voice. He hadn't moved. But Jacob had been a wolf for almost two years now, and the part of him that was wolf was standing up very straight and very still and paying attention.

Then the wolf's eyes moved to Edward.

"You're the one who reads minds," he said.

"Not yours," said Edward. "I've been trying since you got out of the car. There's nothing there. It's like trying to read a wall."

"Ja. Now you can."

Edward flinched.

Jacob saw it. Edward Cullen, who'd stood across a snowy field from the Volturi without blinking, flinched like somebody had thrown cold water in his face. His eyes went wide and fixed. His hand came up and closed on the porch rail, and the wood creaked.

He stood there for maybe four seconds. Nobody moved.

"Edward?" Bella said.

Edward didn't answer. He was staring at the wolf on the lawn. Jacob had seen him look like that exactly once, the night Bella died on the table.

"He's seen one," Edward said finally. His voice was strange. "A soul. Outside a body. In a—it's white. Like a train station. There's something under a bench." He swallowed, which vampires didn't need to do. "He carried a piece of one. Somebody else's. For seventeen years. Inside him. He knows what it felt like when it was taken out."

"Edward—"

"He looked at Leah last night. For ten minutes. He's thinking about it right now. There's a thing sitting on her wolf, and it has roots, and the roots are—" He stopped. He looked at Bella. "Love. Let him look."

"Edward, she's our—"

"He's thinking about his daughter," Edward said. "He's thinking about what it'll do to his daughter if he gets this wrong. That's all he's thinking about. That's all he's thought about since yesterday." His hand tightened on the rail. Something cracked. "Bella. Please."

Bella looked at Edward for a long moment. Then down at Nessie.

Nessie was looking at the wolf, with her head tilted on one side. She took one hand off her mother's neck and laid it flat against Bella's cheek. Jacob knew what that meant. She was showing her something.

Bella's face changed.

"Okay," she said quietly. "Okay. Jake?"

Jacob looked down at the wolf on the lawn. He thought about Leah's face in Seth's head. He thought about a year and a half of Sam.

"Yeah," he said. "Okay. Ten minutes."

---

It was the weirdest ten minutes of Jacob's life, and that was saying something.

He sat on the porch steps with Nessie in his lap. The wolf didn't come up the steps. He stood at the bottom of them, a few feet away, and looked.

That was all. He just looked. His eyes went unfocused, like he was looking at something about a foot behind Jacob's head. His ears turned, one and then the other. His nose flared and flared. And he walked around them, slow, on the grass, all the way around the steps, around the side of the porch and back, and the whole time his head was tipped like a dog listening to something under the floor.

The Cullens stood on the porch and watched him and didn't say anything.

"When you saw her," the wolf said, "what went first?"

Jacob blinked. "What?"

"When you imprinted. What let go first? Your pack? Your dad? Your—" his ears flicked at Bella "—her?"

"All of it," said Jacob. "At once." He'd never actually tried to say it out loud. "It was like—everything that was holding me down, holding me to stuff, my dad and the pack and Bella and—all of it just got cut. Like strings. And then there was only her."

"Did it come back? The rest?"

"Yeah. Mostly. Pack came back the next day. Dad—" He stopped. "Took a while."

"How long?"

"Week? Two?"

The wolf's ears went back a fraction. "Leah's has been twenty hours," he said. "Her daughter's still turned down."

Jacob didn't know what to say to that.

The wolf kept walking around them. He stopped right in front of Jacob, a couple feet off, and looked at the middle of Jacob's chest for a long time.

"Ja," he said, very softly. "There you are."

"What?"

"It's on your wolf," the wolf said. "Same as hers. Sitting right on top of it. Except yours has gone all the way through. Like a tree that's grown through a fence." He tilted his head the other way. "And it's tied to her—" a flick of his ears at Nessie "—with a thread. And there's another thread going down."

"Down?"

"Into the ground." The wolf's eyes dropped, slowly, past Jacob's knees, past the steps, down into the dirt. He turned his head, following something nobody else could see, west, toward the coast. "Long way down. Long way west. Toward the water."

Jacob's skin was crawling. "What's that supposed to mean?"

The wolf didn't answer. He'd lifted one forepaw. One claw, the long curved black one on the end of the first digit, reached out, very slowly, toward the middle of Jacob's chest. It stopped about an inch off his shirt.

"This might sting," the wolf said.

He touched something. The claw stopped in the air an inch in front of Jacob's shirt, on something only he could find. And something inside Jacob's chest *clenched*—like a fist closing, like a dog on a chain lunging at the end of it—and the clench went down, down into the middle of him, and he felt it dig in. Felt it grab hold of everything it could reach and *hold on*. He gasped. Nessie went rigid in his lap. Her small hand slapped flat against his cheek and he saw himself through her eyes, gray-faced and gasping, and felt her terror.

The wolf took his claw away.

The fist in Jacob's chest relaxed, slowly. It didn't let go of what it had grabbed.

"*What the hell was that?*" Jacob said.

"That's what it does when something comes at it," said the wolf. He was looking at the place where his claw had been. His voice had gone flat as a board. "It digs in. Grabs everything near it and holds on." He was quiet for a second. "Last night I was going to go in fast. Cut it off her and burn it. I'd worked out how." His ears went flat. "If I'd done that, it'd have grabbed the nearest thing it could hold onto. And the nearest thing is my daughter."

Nobody on the porch said a word.

"So," said the wolf. "That's useful."

He stood there a moment longer. Then Nessie slid off Jacob's lap.

"Ness—" said Bella.

Nessie walked down the steps in her little sneakers and stopped in front of the huge black wolf and looked up at him, way up. The wolf looked down at her. He didn't move.

She reached up and put her hand on his forepaw.

Jacob knew what she was showing him. He could guess. He'd seen it a thousand times—her whole small fierce certainty about him, about *Jake*, the way she saw him, big and safe and *hers*. She'd shown it to Charlie and to the Denalis and to half the Volturi guard. It was the thing she showed people when she wanted them to understand.

The wolf's ears came up, a little. Something moved in his face around the eyes.

"Ja," he said, and his voice had gone different. Low. Careful. "I know, kid. He's yours."

He took his paw back gently. He turned around and walked toward the Suburban.

"Wait," said Jacob. He was on his feet. "Hey. Wait. What is it? You saw it. Tell me what it *is*."

The wolf stopped with one paw on the door handle.

"Something old," he said, without turning around. "Something that came up out of your ground and sat down on your wolf and put down roots."

"It's *mine*," Jacob said. He hadn't meant to yell it. "It's me. It's part of me."

The wolf looked back over his shoulder at him. His tail hung straight and still.

"Then why's it plugged into the ground?" he said.

Jacob opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

"Mr. Potter," said Carlisle. He'd come down off the porch. "If there's anything at all—my library, my notes—I knew Ephraim Black, years ago. I have some of what he told me about the old—"

"It can't help," said the wolf.

He opened the truck door. Then he stopped, and stood there for a second with the door open and his ears back.

"Thanks," he said, to nobody in particular. "For the ten minutes."

The Suburban went down the drive and out of sight through the cedars.

Jacob stood on the bottom step and listened to it go all the way out to the highway. His chest still hurt. It felt bruised, deep in, where the fist had clenched.

"What did you see?" he said. "In his head. When he let you."

Edward was still holding the porch rail. There were finger-shaped dents in it.

"He hasn't slept," Edward said. "He's not going to. Not until it's out of her." He let go of the rail, slowly. "And he's thinking very clearly about what he'll do to anyone who gets in his way. In detail. In order." He looked at Jacob. "Don't get in his way, Jacob."

"Wasn't planning on it."

"No," said Edward. He turned his head west, toward the coast, toward La Push. "But Sam will."

Somebody knocked on Sue Clearwater's front door at five past seven on Monday morning, and she knew who it was before she got to the hall.

Nobody on the rez used the front door.

Harry was in the backyard. He'd been in the backyard since he got back from the Cullens' yesterday morning, twenty-four hours ago, on the patch of wet grass between the woodpile and the alders where her husband used to sneak his cigarettes. He hadn't come inside except to look at Leah. He came in every two hours, on the dot, and stood in the doorway of her room and looked at her for a minute with his eyes gone far away, and went back out.

Seth was asleep sitting up on the kitchen floor with his back against the kitchen door.

Sue opened the front door.

Isaac Wray stood on her porch in his scrubs and his green rain jacket, with his car keys in one hand. He looked like he hadn't slept either. His face was the color of putty.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said. "I'm so sorry. I'm—I was driving to work. I was on the highway. And I turned around." He laughed, the way people laugh in the ER when the doctor tells them something they don't want to hear. "I don't know why I'm here. I don't know why I'm *here*."

Behind Sue, down the hall, a door opened.

She didn't turn around. She heard it. Her daughter's bare feet on the floorboards, slow and even. She heard Seth wake up on the kitchen floor and scramble and come out into the hall, and stop, and stand there. In the way. The way he'd been asked to.

"Leah," Seth said softly. "Hey. Lee. Hey."

The footsteps stopped.

Then Harry was in the front doorway.

Sue didn't see him come through the house. He was just there, between her and the porch, in front of Isaac Wray, wet to the skin, his coat black and dripping on her mat. To Isaac, she supposed, he was a big man with a beard. To her, since Leah had made him drop it, he was what he was. A wolf as tall as her doorframe, with his ears pinned flat and his tail hanging dead still.

Isaac took a step back.

"Stand still," Harry said.

Isaac stood still.

Harry looked at him. Sue had watched him look at Leah like that, every two hours, all night. His eyes went somewhere behind Isaac's head. His nose worked. He looked at the middle of Isaac's chest for a long, long time, and Isaac stood there on the porch with his car keys in his hand and sweat coming out on his forehead in the cold, and didn't move, and didn't know why he wasn't moving.

"Right," said Harry finally. "Go home, Isaac. Or go to work."

"What's happening to me?" Isaac's voice cracked. "I can't stop—I keep—I've known her for *thirty seconds*, I don't even—is she okay? Just tell me if she's okay. I can't stop thinking about whether she's—"

"She's okay," said Harry. "This is going to stop."

"*What* is?"

"Go home, Isaac."

He shut the door.

He shut it so quietly Sue didn't hear the latch catch. He stood there with one forepaw flat against the inside of it. Then he turned around and looked past her down the hall.

Leah was standing outside her bedroom door in his flannel with Seth in front of her. She had both hands pressed over her own mouth. Her eyes were on the front door. They were wet. Her whole body was leaning toward it, like a tree in a wind.

By the afternoon, Sue's backyard didn't look like her backyard anymore.

There were lights hanging in it.

Dozens of them. Threads of light, hanging in the air over the wet grass between the woodpile and the alders, at about the height of her head. Some were red-gold. Some were silver-gray. One was a deep, dark green, almost black, and one was a thin, bright gold, and they hung there turning slowly, tangled up in each other in shapes she didn't understand, like somebody had taken a child's mobile and made it out of fishing line and stars. When the wind came through the alders, they didn't move.

Harry walked between them. Back and forth. He'd been walking between them for hours. Every so often he'd stop and reach up with one claw and touch one, and it would change shape, and he'd stand looking at it with his ears pinned and his tail hanging still. Then he'd walk on.

The card table from the shed stood in the middle of them with a stack of old leather books on it, swollen with damp. One, on top, had no title, and was bound in something Sue didn't like the look of. They'd arrived at noon yesterday with a knee-high creature in a tea towel, who'd appeared on her lawn with a crack like a gunshot, bowed to her, called her *the wolf-woman's mother*, sniffed toward Forks, said "Kreacher smells *dead things*," and vanished.

Propped against a coffee can beside the books was a little square mirror. At three in the morning Sue had heard a woman's voice come out of it, through her bedroom window. *Harry. When did you last sleep?*

*Not now, Hermione.*

There were three plates of food on the woodpile. Sue had made every one of them herself. Fry bread, bacon, eggs, a whole elk steak. They were all cold. A raven had been at the bacon.

That frightened her more than the lights did. She'd watched him eat four bowls of stew.

Leah was sitting on the back step with the shawl around her and her knees drawn up as far as they'd go, which wasn't far. She was watching him. She'd been watching him for most of the day, when she wasn't watching the gap in the alders that pointed toward the river road. Sue could see her hands from the kitchen window. They were knotted together so tight the knuckles were pale.

At two o'clock, Emily came.

Sue saw her come up the gravel road on foot, in a long brown raincoat with the hood up, and stop at the edge of the yard by the alders, and look at the lights in the air. Then at Harry, who'd turned his head to look at her and kept walking. Then at Leah on the step.

Sue put down the dish towel.

Emily came around the side of the house and stood at the bottom of the back step. She pushed her hood back. The scars ran down the right side of her face the way they always had, three long pale lines from the corner of her eye to her jaw, pulling the corner of her mouth down. There was a gold band on her left hand.

Leah looked at her and didn't say anything.

"Hi, Lee," said Emily.

"Emily."

"Can I sit?"

A long pause. Then Leah moved over on the step. Not much.

Emily sat. They sat there side by side, two feet apart, and looked at Harry walking between his lights, and Sue stood at the kitchen window with her hand over her mouth and didn't move, because she'd been waiting six years to see the two of them sit on a step together and she'd never, ever thought it would be like this.

"I was."

Emily flinched.

Emily looked at Harry, out in the yard, walking.

She walked away up the gravel road.

Sue stood at the window and watched her go, and something cold moved in her stomach, because Emily Young was going home to Sam Uley's kitchen with that question in her mouth, and Sue had known Sam since he was in diapers.

I cut the roots off you while it's looking at me, and it *runs*, and I've got the road, babe, I've *already* got the road, it's—" He held up his own left wrist, and the faint gold shimmer around it. Harry looked at her.

"Then I eat it," he said.

Billy Black made a sound that might have been a laugh.

Harry's voice cracked down the middle. "I've got it," he said. "Leah. I've *got* it."

Then his knees went, and he sat down very suddenly in the wet grass, in the middle of all his lights.

Seth Clearwater smelled them coming at a quarter past six on Tuesday night, about four minutes before he heard them, and about five minutes before Jake said it in his head.

*Here they come.*

Seth was in the front yard on four legs, in the rain, in the gap between the alders and the porch. Jake was on his right. Embry and Quil were on his left. They'd been out there since three, when Healer Pye had appeared in the middle of Mom's kitchen with a crack, holding a carpetbag and the dented tartan biscuit tin that had been her Portkey, looked at the huge black shape kneeling on the living room floor, and said, "Lord Potter, you look like something the cat dragged in, and it was a big cat." She'd made him eat. She'd stood over him while he did it. It was the first thing Seth had seen him eat since Saturday.

Harry was inside now, kneeling on the floor by the couch. He'd been there all afternoon, with Leah lying on the couch and Healer Pye in Mom's armchair with her knitting, going over it. What he was going to do. What each of them would do. What could go wrong. Seth had listened through the window. Most of it he hadn't understood.

The part he'd understood was the part where Harry said, "And if my wolf can't eat it, worst case, I'm imprinted on a nurse from the clinic," and Leah said, "That's not funny," and Harry said, "It's a bit funny," and for one second, through the window, Seth had heard his sister laugh.

*How many?* Embry thought.

Seth lifted his nose. Wet fur. Pine pitch. Sam. Jared. Paul, who always smelled faintly like Billy's house now. Collin and Brady. And the new kids, the ones who'd come up all winter, Tommy Lahote and the rest, sweet and sharp and scared.

*Fourteen*, Seth thought. *All of them.*

*Yeah*, thought Jake. *All of them.*

They came out of the trees on the far side of the gravel road in a long gray-and-brown line, and spread out across the yard in the rain. Big. A couple of the new kids were almost as big as Seth. They stood with their heads low and their ears flat, looking at the porch, and at the four wolves standing between them and it.

Sam came out last. The big black one. He stopped in the middle of the line. Seth couldn't hear him anymore—hadn't been able to hear him since the night Jake broke—but he could read him fine. Head up. Ears forward. Steady.

Then Sam walked behind the biggest alder, and came out the other side on two legs, pulling a pair of cutoffs up over his hips, bare-chested in the cold rain.

"Jake," Sam said. "Get your guys out of the way."

Jake didn't move. Seth felt it in his head, Jake's answer, and none of it was polite.

"Jake," said Sam again. "I'm not here to fight you. Move."

The front door opened.

Harry came out onto the porch.

He'd been up since Saturday. Seth could smell it on him. Coffee and no sleep and the sour edge that came off you at the end of a triple patrol. His fur was flat and dull and his coat was buttoned wrong, one button off all the way up. He stopped at the top of the steps and stood there with his forepaws at his sides, and looked down into the yard at thirteen wolves and one man.

His tail hung straight down behind him. It hadn't moved since the cemetery. Seth had been keeping track.

Mom came out behind him and stood on the step with her arms folded.

"Sam," she said.

"Sue." Sam's jaw was tight. "The council hasn't approved this."

"The council's standing on this porch, Sam Uley." Mom's voice could've cut glass. "And I didn't send you."

"Old Quil—"

"Old Quil can come down here and tell me himself."

Sam looked at her a long moment. Then he looked up at Harry.

"You don't know what you're doing," Sam said. He kept his voice level. Seth knew that voice. Sam used it on the new kids, the first time they phased. "It's the spirit. It's older than the tribe. It's how the spirit chooses for us. Nobody's ever cut one. Nobody knows what happens. You could kill her. You could kill the baby." He took a breath. "She's one of ours. The spirit chose for her. You'd take that from her?"

Harry looked down at him for a while.

"It's in my wife," he said.

Sam's mouth went tight.

"Emily came to see Leah yesterday," said Harry. His voice was very quiet, and very rough, and the rain ran off the end of his muzzle. "She sat right there. On that step. And then she went home." His ears had pinned flat. "What did she ask you at breakfast, Sam?"

Sam went still.

All along the line, the wolves shifted on their feet. Seth saw Jared's head swing around toward Sam. Saw Paul's ears go back.

"Don't," Sam said. His voice had dropped. "Don't you talk about her."

"That's why you're here," said Harry. "That's the whole reason. If I can take it out of Leah, Emily's going to ask you to let me take it out of you. And then you'll find out." He tilted his head. "That's what you're scared of. The spirit can look after itself."

Sam's whole body had gone rigid. He was shaking. Seth could see it, the fine fast tremor in his arms and shoulders that came right before you lost it and went wolf.

"I'm not letting you do this," Sam said.

Behind him, the line of wolves came forward a step.

Seth felt his own lips peel back. Felt Jake beside him drop his head and Embry and Quil go low, and the growl came up out of all four of them at once.

Harry came down the steps.

He walked down slow, heel to toe, one step at a time, into the yard, into the rain. He walked right between Seth and Jake. He didn't look at either of them. He stopped about ten feet from Sam, on the wet gravel, and he stood there with thirteen wolves in front of him in the dark, and he looked at Sam Uley.

"Listen to me," Harry said. "All of you. Very carefully."

He said it quietly. Seth heard every word.

"I'm going back inside that house. And I'm going to take that thing out of my wife." He didn't blink. "If one of you puts a paw on that porch—if one of you comes through that door—I will burn everything you have ever loved. Your houses. Your boats. Your council and your stories and this whole coast. I'll burn it down to the rock, and I'll salt the rock, and you'll stand in the ash and try to remember what your names used to be."

The yard was dead silent. Even the surf seemed to have stopped.

"I've been awake for three days," said Harry. "I've got nothing left for being polite." His ears were flat to his skull. "Fucking try me."

The rain stopped.

Seth saw it happen. Every single drop of rain falling over the yard stopped in the air. All of them. Millions of them, hanging there between the alders and the porch and the line of wolves, glittering in the porch light like somebody had thrown a net of glass beads over everything. Seth's own breath steamed out through them. Then, all at once, with a sound like a huge long hiss, every drop turned to steam and was gone. The air in the yard went dry and hot. The gravel steamed. And it smelled—God, it smelled like the cliffs. Salt and cedar and rain on rock, so strong it filled Seth's whole head, so strong it smelled like the inside of his own wolf.

Paul lunged.

Of course it was Paul. The big silver one, out of the line, low and fast, straight at Harry's throat from the side. Seth saw him go and couldn't move fast enough to do anything about it.

Harry didn't turn his head.

Paul stopped in the air. Six feet off the ground, legs out, jaws open, stopped like the rain had stopped. He hung there. He made a high, startled noise. Then he went down—slammed down flat on his back in the gravel at Sam's bare feet so hard the ground jumped, and stayed there, pinned, all four legs in the air, whining.

Harry still hadn't looked at him.

"That one was free," Harry said.

Nobody else moved.

Sam stood there with Paul pinned whining at his feet and the steam rising off the gravel around him, and looked at Harry. Seth watched his face. He watched Sam go through it—every single piece of it, Seth could see it go, the anger and the fear and the thing about Emily—and then he watched Sam's eyes slide past Harry, up to the house.

Leah was at the front window.

She was standing in the window with the curtain held back in one hand and the other hand on her stomach. She was looking out at Sam. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Seth had known his sister his whole life, and he'd lived inside her head for a year, and he'd never seen her look at Sam like that. She looked at him like he was a stranger she was sorry for.

Sam looked back at her for a long time.

Then he turned around and walked into the trees.

Halfway there he phased, and went off into the dark on four legs, and the line broke up behind him and went after him one by one—Jared, and Collin and Brady, and the new kids, low and quick. Paul came loose from the gravel all at once and rolled to his feet and went, tail clamped down, without looking back.

The yard was empty. The gravel was still steaming.

Harry stood there a while longer, looking at the place where Sam had gone.

Then he turned around and walked back toward the porch. On his way past, he stopped beside Seth and put one paw on top of Seth's head, between his ears, and left it there a second.

"Thanks, Seth," he said.

He went up the steps and inside.

*Jake*, Seth thought. *Jake. Would he have—*

Jake didn't answer for a while. He was looking at the open front door.

*Yeah*, Jake thought finally. *Yeah, Seth. He would've.*

Deep green, silver-gray, a whisker of gold.

A thread of red-gold, thin as fishing line, coming up out of her own chest above the necklace and running off through the living room wall toward the river.
