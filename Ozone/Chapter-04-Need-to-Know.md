# Ozone

## Chapter Four — Need to Know

*Jasper, Alberta, to Forks, Washington. January 2007. Forty-eight hours out.*

---

### I.

The hook let go of her somewhere over the Atlantic, or that was how it felt, and the cabin floor came up and hit her in the boots.

Leah stayed upright. Her stomach kept going for another half second without her, and then the world slammed back together all at once: log walls, the cold black woodstove, the moose antler over the door, the orange mug spinning across the kitchen table on its side until it fetched up against the sugar bowl with a *clink*.

Three in the afternoon, Mountain Time. Low gold light coming in sideways off the snow. Twenty-two below on the porch thermometer. Hermione's rain was still running off her chin onto the floorboards, going cold.

Harry hit the floor beside her in a crouch and came up out of it already moving.

He didn't shake the rain off. He crossed the kitchen in three strides, reached into his jacket, and came out with a wooden box the size of a deck of cards pinched between two claws. He set it in the middle of the floor and stepped back. Ozone, sharp and there and gone, and the box grew — in jolts, like an engine catching on a cold morning — until a steamer trunk sat where the rag rug had been. Black leather worn gray at the corners. Brass locks gone green. A set of initials scratched into the leather by the handle and then scratched out again so hard the leather had split.

He flipped the locks with his claw tips, and the trunk unfolded. Trays swung out on brass arms, one under another, over a well that went down farther than the trunk was tall. Racks of corked bottles labeled in his cramped handwriting. Rolled parchment. Copper wire. A sack of coarse gray salt. A leather notebook so fat with loose pages it was held shut with a rubber band. A garment bag folded in thirds.

He went through it fast. Bottles held up to the window, some kept, some put back. The notebook out, pages thumbed. His ears were forward and dead still, his tail stiff behind him, his nostrils flaring every time he uncorked something.

Four months, and she'd never actually watched him work. In Prince George, in October, he'd gone out alone one night and come back at dawn smelling of smoke and somebody else's blood, showered for forty minutes, slept for eleven hours, and never said a word about it. Apparently it looked like this. Quiet and quick and grim, like a chore he'd done a hundred times and hated every single one.

The mark on her collarbone was buzzing like a plucked wire.

She put two fingers on it through the jumper, and this time, because Hermione had told her where to look, she looked. Under her own hum, something was coming back the other way. Thin and cold and pulled tight. She could pick it out now, the way you could pick one voice out of a crowd after it had said your name.

He was afraid. Low and steady, like a pilot light. For her.

"I'm coming with you," she said.

"Ja," said Harry, without looking up. "I know. Boots stay on."

Leah opened her mouth.

She'd had the whole argument loaded. *My brother. My mother. My pack. You don't know the clearing, you don't know which leech is which, and if you leave me here I'll swim the Strait again and beat you there.* She'd been ready for him to put his ears back. She'd been ready to bite him.

"That's it?" she said.

"What did you want?"

"I wanted you to say no so I could tell you where to stick it."

"You'd follow me anyway. Four legs, over the Rockies. You'd likely beat me there." He dog-eared a page. "And I don't know the ground. The town, the pack, the treaty line. You do. Without you I'm walking in blind."

"So I'm useful."

"Very. I'll put it in the paperwork. *Local liaison*."

"Is that a real thing?"

"It will be by midnight."

She stood there with her sweater steaming in the cold air and nowhere to put her argument, and crossed her arms.

"I felt you," she said. "In the garden."

His claw stopped on the page. One second. Then it moved on.

"I know."

"Since when can you feel me?"

"Dawson City."

"Six weeks. You sat on that porch in thirty below and told me it was all on my end. That you were sorry it wasn't fair."

"I said that."

"You lied."

"I put it in a room," Harry said, "and shut the door, and didn't look at the door."

"That's lying, Harry."

"Ja," he said. "It is."

He'd looked up. His ears were turned toward her, and his eyes didn't slide off anywhere. He didn't explain. He let it stand on the floor between them, ugly and true, and she found she didn't have anything to throw at it.

"We're going to fight about this," she said.

"Ja."

"After."

"After."

"Is the door shut now?"

"No."

"Keep it that way."

His ear flicked. "Okay."

He went to the window and stood with his back to her and closed his eyes, and she watched his ears go soft, and felt the edge of whatever he was reaching for through the mark — something good, a long way from this cabin, like holding her palm near a stove.

The stag stepped out of him. Silver-white, antlers nearly to the ceiling beams, bright enough that the snow-glare in the window went dull. It turned its head and looked at her with eyes made of light.

Leah thought *deer*, very loudly, and kept her mouth shut.

"Kingsley," Harry said. Flatter voice. Clipped. A work voice. "Harry. Accepted. Two conditions. First: the Quileute wolves on the Olympic Peninsula are an allied party. They are not targets, and they are not acceptable losses, and they are nobody's problem but mine. Anyone in the Confederation or MACUSA who disagrees can take it up with me in person. Second: the Cullen household and the people standing with them are non-combatants for the duration, provided they keep the Statute. I want both in writing by midnight Pacific, or I stop work and you can find somebody else." A pause. "There isn't anybody else, Kingsley. Harry."

The stag dipped its head and bounded straight through the log wall beside the woodstove, and the cabin went dim and gold again.

*Which threat, Harry? Or everyone standing in that clearing?* She'd screamed it at him in a London garden two minutes ago, on the other side of the world.

"Not acceptable losses," she said.

"That's the condition."

"Will he say yes?"

"He'll grumble. Then he'll say yes." Harry knelt back down by the trunk. "The alternative is finding somebody this side of the Atlantic in forty-eight hours who'll walk into a field of thirty vampires on purpose. There's nobody else that stupid on the books."

"That's a lot of faith in a guy who just told you to go burn people."

"Ja," said Harry. "Hand me the atlas."

---

### II.

It was a Rand McNally road atlas of North America with a spine held together by electrical tape. Harry cleared the table by looking at it — the sugar bowl slid aside, the mug rolled into the corner — and opened it to Washington.

"Show me," he said.

Leah put her finger on the Olympic Peninsula. "Forks." A dot on the 101. "La Push." The end of the 110, on the coast. "Quileute land's this bit. Postage stamp." She traced the treaty line on into the blank green where nothing was printed at all. She'd run it a thousand times in fur; her finger knew the way. "Everything west and south of that is ours. They don't cross. Mostly."

"The Cullens?"

"Here. Ish. Three miles of driveway with no sign on it." She moved her finger. "And the clearing's north of them. Across the river, past where the logging road quits." She stopped. "That's where we fought the newborns. In June."

The memory came up uninvited. A newborn coming out of the trees at her left side, arms like iron bars, the rake of fingers across her shoulder — and Jacob coming in out of nowhere to take it off her, and the crunch of his ribs going. She'd healed in a day. He'd been on his back for three weeks, and she'd never—

"Leah."

"I'm fine."

He took a pencil from the trunk, held it pinched between two claw tips, and drew a big circle around the place her finger had stopped.

"Twelve anchors around the edge. A keystone in the middle." He ticked them in. "Stones. Local ones. A ward sits better on rock that already belongs to the ground. Ron's brother Bill taught me that, in a tent outside Hoedspruit, one summer I wasn't sleeping." He tapped the line. "Five layers. The outside one keeps Muggles off. Hikers, hunters, kids with a six-pack. They get within a quarter mile and remember they left the stove on. Second stops anybody Apparating in or out. Third's the shell. That one's built to hold fire."

"Fire."

"Fiendfyre."

The word sat on the table.

"What's Fiendfyre?"

His ears went back a few degrees. "Cursed fire. It's alive, more or less. Hungry. It makes shapes as it burns — snakes, dragons, things with too many heads. It burns anything. Stone, metal, magic. And it doesn't stop because you'd like it to."

"Will it burn them?"

"I'd bet my truck."

"You bet your truck on everything."

"Never lost it yet."

Leah looked at the circle. Her pack had exactly one way of killing the cold ones: take them apart with your teeth, throw the pieces on a fire, stand in the purple smoke till nothing was left but the smell. It had worked in June. It had also put Jacob in bed for three weeks.

"Have you used it?"

"Three times." He said it like reading off a list. "First time it wasn't mine. A boy at school made it in a room full of a thousand years of junk. It got away from him. It ate the room. It ate him." His tail lay still. "I flew out over it on a broom. I was seventeen. Second time I made it myself, in the bush in Mpumalanga, under a ward I'd spent three days on. It found a weak place on the north side. I lost a hillside before I starved it. Third time, it stayed in the box."

"So one out of two is a coin flip."

"Ja. So this ward's going to be better than the last one." He drew two lines crossing the circle. "Fourth layer, I'll know everything inside the line. Weight on the ground. Heat. Heartbeats, or none. Where everybody is, every second. Fifth layer is walls inside the wall. Bulkheads, like on a ship. Italians at one end of the field, your lot at the other — before I light anything, I drop a wall down the middle. Anybody on the wrong side who isn't who I'm there for, the ward throws out."

"*Throws*."

"Shoves. Hard. They land on their feet." He considered. "Mostly."

"Mostly," Leah said.

"Seth'll hate it."

"Seth'll be alive to hate it."

"How long?"

"Ring tonight. Shell tomorrow. Bulkheads tomorrow night. Done by Saturday morning if nothing goes wrong."

"Something always goes wrong."

"Ja. I know."

She frowned at the map. "They've got leeches coming in from everywhere. Jacob's head was full of them in December. I thought they were building an army."

"Or an audience. If the law's coming for my kid and I can't win the fight, I bring witnesses. People to stand in the field and say *we've seen her, she's fine, go home*." He tipped his head. "That's what I'd do. I might be wrong."

"And if it works? If they talk and go home?"

"Then I fold the ward up and we get burgers. Kingsley said contain. If they get on a plane in Seattle, they're contained." He looked down at the circle a moment. "This time."

She heard how he said it. She filed it with *pack* and *Alpha* and *I don't know where Forks is*.

"How do we get there? You can only pop places you've been."

"And I've never been to Forks."

"There's nothing in Forks."

"Which is why I've never been." He tapped the map. "Portkey. It needs to know where it's going. This map's got Forks the size of a claw tip. I aim at the clearing off this, I'm as likely to land us in a septic tank in Beaver."

"There's a Beaver?"

"It's on the map."

There was. BEAVER, north of Forks, past Lake Pleasant. She'd driven through it a hundred times and never noticed it had a name. She snorted.

"You've been there," Harry said. "The clearing. You know it to the blade of grass."

She stopped laughing.

"You want in my head."

"I want the clearing. Nothing else. You open the door that far, I look, and I leave."

A year and a half of the pack. Every thought she'd ever had hung out like wash on a line for whoever happened to phase, and not one of them had ever asked. Nobody asked. It just happened to you.

He stood across the table with his paws on the back of a chair, waiting.

"Okay," she said.

"Look at me."

It came almost at once — a pressure at the door she'd built in her head on a motel floor in October. Plain and light, like knuckles on wood. She opened it a crack and held the clearing in the crack: June, late in the day, grass to the knee, the long dark wall of trees, resin and warm dirt. She felt him come to the doorframe and stop. He didn't lean. He looked through it the way you'd look through somebody's window at their yard, took the shape of it, and stepped back. The door was hers again.

Three seconds. Four.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"Jacob would've read my whole diary."

"You keep a diary?"

"Shut up, Harry."

He picked the orange mug up out of the corner. *DO NOT DRINK FROM THIS. — H.G.* He turned it once in his paw, and the ozone came up, and a faint blue tint crept into the glaze and stayed.

"Is that legal?"

His ear flicked. "Unregistered international Portkey across a border during a declared Statute emergency." He thought about it. "There's a form."

### III.

"Twenty minutes," Harry said, and unzipped the garment bag.

Leah sat on the end of the bed and watched, because she wasn't going to pretend she wouldn't.

The jacket went on the bedpost. The wet flannel and T-shirt and jeans came off. He closed his eyes, and the ozone flickered, and every wet streak of fur lay down dry and glossy at once.

"You could've done that the whole time," she said, because it had been true in Smithers and it was still true.

"Could've."

Out of the garment bag came a white shirt, pressed so hard the creases looked sharp. Buttoning it was slow going. The buttons were small and his claws were not, and every second one skittered off sideways. His muzzle wrinkled. His tail twitched.

She watched him miss the same button three times.

"Come here."

He came. She did the buttons bottom to top, fast, her knuckles brushing the fur of his chest through the gap, and left the top two open, and did the cuffs. He held his arms out like a kid being zipped into a snowsuit and looked over her head at the wall with enormous dignity.

"You can catch a beer bottle out of the air behind your head," she said.

"Beer bottles don't have buttons."

Charcoal wool trousers, with a hole in the back for the tail hemmed so neatly she'd have had to look twice for the stitches. A charcoal blazer, a shade darker, plain lapels. He shrugged it on and rolled his shoulders to settle it, and the center vent fell open around the base of his tail as if it had been cut for exactly that. No tie. He checked the collar in the spotted mirror and ran a claw under it to lay it flat.

"You own a suit," Leah said.

"Blazer and trousers. A suit matches."

"Why do you even *have* it?"

"Because it makes working with locals easier." He tugged a cuff straight. "Good clothes. Police, councils, landowners, nervous fathers. Same words out of a man in a collar, and they hear them differently. A man in a leather jacket is a drifter who'll be gone by Tuesday. Put the same man in a blazer, and he's a problem that's going to want a signature."

"You're going to see Charlie."

"Who's Charlie?"

"Chief Swan. Bella's dad. Forks police." She decided she was done hesitating about things with him, at least for today. "He's dating my mom. Sort of. Since the funeral. He kept coming around with fish." It came out flat and fast. "He was my dad's best friend. They fished every Saturday for twenty years."

He looked at her in the mirror a moment.

"Then I'll wear the blazer," he said.

"He'll think you're a fed."

"Good."

She looked at him. Charcoal and white, smoke-gray fur at the open collar, the jacket hanging square off his shoulders, the tail swinging slowly out of the vent. Something happened low in her stomach. She kept her face very flat.

His ear flicked.

"I can feel that, you know."

She went hot all over, which nobody would notice.

"Then shut the door."

"Not a chance."

"Then deal with it."

He grinned at the mirror with every tooth he owned.

"Shoes?" she said.

"No."

"You made me wear boots."

"You were visiting a carpet."

"The Cullens have carpets. *White* ones."

"Then the Cullens will have to cope," said Harry, and went out barefoot into twenty-two below.

On the drive, the Chevy sat under a foot of fresh powder. He rested his paw on the fender a moment, the way you'd rest your hand on a horse, and stepped back, and the ozone cracked, and the truck shrank — in jolts, springs squealing, old metal groaning — until a black 1972 Chevrolet C10 the size of a Matchbox car sat on the packed snow, and the foot of powder that had been on its hood slid off sideways in a drift the size of a dinner plate.

He picked it up between two claws, blew the snow off the roof, and tucked it into the inside pocket of his blazer.

"You put your truck in your pocket," Leah said.

"Easier than parking it."

### IV.

The mug went at a quarter past three, Pacific Time, and Leah held on.

She held on through the hook and the roaring dark and the long stretch where she started thinking about septic tanks in Beaver, and then there was grass under her boots and she was standing in the cold, wet, gray air of home.

She knew it before she opened her eyes. Cedar. Rain. Salt coming up the river valleys off the sea. Air so heavy with water you could drink it. She hauled in a breath of it, and then another, and her eyes stung, and she told herself it was the Portkey.

"I hate that," she said.

"You've said."

"I'm going to keep saying it."

The clearing. Exactly the clearing — except the June grass was flattened dead and brown under a crust of old snow in the shadows, and a low lid of cloud sat on the trees, and a thin wet snow was coming down and melting as it landed.

And the smell underneath.

Leeches. A dozen different stinks, two dozen, sweet and cold, crossing and recrossing the field so thick and fresh it reeked like the inside of a florist's cooler. Somebody had been training out here. A lot of somebodies, for days. Under that, older, worn into the edges: Jacob. Seth. Quil and Embry. Running long loops around the outside. Guarding the leeches while they practiced.

Her throat closed up.

Behind her, Harry's nose wrinkled with a long, deliberate pull and a sound like a man stepping in something.

"Florist's," he said.

"Told you."

"I didn't believe you." He turned in a slow circle, ears swiveling. "That's a lot of them."

"Audience."

"Ja."

He walked the field, footpaws leaving no prints in the crust, stopping twice to crouch with his palm pads flat on the ground and his eyes shut. At the north end, a granite boulder the size of a car sat half buried in the dead grass, mossed on one side and crusted with lichen. Leah had jumped it in June with a newborn hanging off her tail.

He put his paw on it. "This one."

"That's a rock."

"Been here since the glaciers. Doesn't want to go anywhere. That's what you want in a keystone."

He handed her the blazer and rolled his shirtsleeves to the elbow. His claws came out with a soft sound like a knife drawn from a leather sleeve. He set one against the granite and pulled, and stone dust curled up like shavings off a plane, a clean pale line a quarter inch deep, the sound setting her teeth on edge.

"What do they say?"

"*Hold*, *hold*, *keep*, *know*," he said, "and a few rude ones of Bill's I've never been able to take out without the whole thing going soft."

It took forty minutes. Leah walked circles with his blazer over her arm, sorting leech trails. Two that smelled of hot sand and spice. Three of peat smoke and sea. Two of crypt dust, side by side everywhere they went. One that smelled like the air right before a lightning strike — too close to Harry for comfort — and she stood on that one a long time with her lip curled.

The light went from gray to blue. Through the mark she could feel him concentrating, like a held note that didn't waver.

When the scraping stopped, the whole face of the boulder was covered in lines and hooks, pale against the wet stone. Harry stood with his paw flat in the middle of them.

"Stand back a bit."

The ozone came up off him the way it had in the Copper Moose when he caught the bottle, only more, so thick she tasted metal. The hair on her arms stood up under the sweater. The runes lit one at a time from under his paw, a cold glacier blue, until the whole rock was glowing.

Then something went *down*.

She felt it in her boots first. A bell struck somewhere deep underground, so low it was mostly in her breastbone, and the wet snow on the grass jumped — every flake lifting an inch in a ring spreading out from the boulder, across the clearing, into the trees, and away.

The glow settled to a faint blue shimmer.

Harry didn't open his eyes. His ears were turning independently, tracking two things at once.

"Deer. Three, in the cedars, forty yards east. One's a yearling. An owl. A vole under the snow by your left boot." His head tilted. "Something hot. Big. Coming in from the southeast, fast, not slowing down."

Leah was already turning. She dropped the blazer on the boulder. The heat came up her spine and her hands started to shake, and she clamped down hard, because if she phased now, whoever that was would be in her head—

A wolf came out of the trees.

Sandy-colored, long-legged, gangly even at the size of a horse, ears too big for his head. He hit the edge of the clearing at a flat gallop, saw her, slammed on the brakes, and went skidding sideways through the snow crust with his legs everywhere, throwing up a wave of slush, and fetched up twenty feet away, panting.

Seth.

Leah's breath went out of her.

He stared at her. His tail started to go. Then he looked past her at Harry, and his hackles rose from neck to tail in one long ripple, and a growl started in his chest.

"Seth," Leah said. "Don't."

The growl stopped. He looked from her to Harry and back, nose working, trying to sort out the two smells tangled together. Then he decided, the way he always did, all at once and with his whole body. He crossed the snow in three bounds, stopped dead, shoved his enormous wet muzzle into her chest, and licked her face from ear to chin with a tongue the size of a bath mat.

"*Gross*. Seth. Get *off*. Go phase. I'm not talking to you like this."

He went bounding into the trees with his tail up like a flag. A minute later he came back out on two legs, barefoot in the snow in a pair of cutoffs that had clearly spent the last hour tied to his ankle. He'd grown again. He was half a head taller than her now, all elbows and ribs, grinning so wide it looked like it hurt.

He didn't slow down. He just ran into her.

She caught him. He was hot the way she was hot, and he didn't flinch, and she didn't flinch, and she wrapped her arms around his skinny ribs and held on so hard he wheezed.

"Leah. You're *here*. You came *back*—"

"I missed you, dummy."

He went completely still. He pulled back and held her at arm's length by the shoulders and stared at her.

"What?" said Leah.

"You *missed* me. You said it out loud. With your *face*. And you hugged back. You never hug back. You do the — the board thing—" He did an impression of a person being hugged against her will. It was very accurate. "What *happened* to you?"

"Nothing happened to me."

"Something happened to you." His eyes went past her shoulder. They went up, and kept going. "Whoa."

Harry stood by the glowing boulder with his sleeves rolled up and his paws in his trouser pockets and his ears politely forward.

"Evening," he said.

"You're a wolf," said Seth. "Standing up. In a *suit*."

"Blazer," said Harry, and nodded at it, folded over the rock. "It's having a rest."

Seth looked at Leah. At the runes. At the snow falling and melting in a perfect ring around the stone. Something in his face went younger and older at once.

"Is this," he said, "the December thing?"

She remembered it from his side: her running mad circles around a cabin in the Yukon, and her little brother in her head going *Leah, what is that, did you just IMPRINT*.

"Yeah," she said. "This is the December thing."

Seth drew himself up. It didn't help much.

"Are you going to hurt her?"

"No," said Harry.

"Because she'd kill you."

"Ja," Harry agreed. "She would."

Seth nodded, like that settled it. "Jake said you promised. He's going to freak. Quil and Embry were phased with me when I smelled you — they saw him. So Jake knows. He's at the house with Nessie." He hesitated. "Charlie's there too. He brings stuff for her. Bella's freaking out, because there's like twenty vampires hiding upstairs and the Romanian ones keep saying stuff about the Italians you can hear through the floor if you're us, and Charlie's just standing on the porch holding a stuffed animal."

"Twenty," said Harry.

"Maybe more. There's a guy in the attic who won't come down."

Harry took the blazer off the rock and put it back on, rolled his sleeves down, and buttoned his cuffs himself on the first try.

Leah narrowed her eyes at him.

"Second try," he said. "You were faster."

"Seth," Leah said. "Run ahead. Tell them we're coming."

"Tell Jake you broke your promise?"

"Tell him—" She took a breath. "Tell him I'm sorry."

Seth's mouth fell open. For a second he looked about nine, the way he'd looked on the beach the summer their dad taught him to cast and he caught his first fish and couldn't believe it was attached to the line.

"*Go*, Seth."

He went, grinning so hard she could hear it, and behind a hemlock there was the deep, detonating sound of a wolf coming into being, and a sandy shape went crashing off southeast with its tail in the air.

"You said sorry," said Harry.

"Shut up."

His tail swung. Once.

At the bottom of the slope, on a logging road with grass up the middle, he set the Matchbox Chevy down between the ruts and stepped back, and it came up in jolts, squealing, until the truck stood full size in the blue dusk, ticking as it settled. The engine caught on the first try. Bob Seger came on halfway through a line.

Leah reached for the eject button. A black paw got there first.

"Driver picks," said Harry.

She put her boots up on the dash, and the old truck rolled down the logging road toward the house by the river where her brother had gone ahead to tell everyone she was sorry.

### V.

Charlie Swan had been standing on the Cullens' front porch for eleven minutes holding a stuffed wolf.

He'd come straight from the station, duty jacket still on, gun still on his hip. The wolf was reddish-brown plush with glass eyes and a stupid red ribbon around its neck that he meant to take off before he handed it over. Twelve ninety-nine at the Thriftway, on the shelf by the lottery tickets. It looked like Jake. He hadn't let himself think too hard about why that mattered.

"It's just a bad night, Dad," Bella said. Again.

She was standing in the doorway with one hand on the frame, exactly where a person would have to go to get past her.

"You said."

"It's Esme's family, from Alaska. And friends of Carlisle's from his residency. From all over — Ireland, Egypt." She said *Egypt* and looked, for a quarter of a second, like she wished she hadn't. "They're all jet-lagged. Everyone's asleep."

It was twenty past five in the afternoon. Charlie had counted nine cars along the drive. Two Tauruses with Hertz stickers. A minivan with Alaska plates. Something low and black and Italian that cost more than his house, parked with its nose in a rhododendron. Every light in the place was on, and there wasn't a sound coming out of it. Twenty years a cop, and he'd never been in a house full of sleeping people that quiet.

Bella blinked at him, hard, twice. She'd been doing that a lot. Her eyes were brown again, the way they'd always been, but a flat sort of brown, muddy at the edges. *Contacts?* he'd asked, back in December. *Yeah, Dad.* He hadn't known she needed glasses.

He'd let it go. He'd been letting a lot go.

Need to know. Jake had stripped down to his skin in Charlie's front yard and turned into a wolf the size of a horse, and Charlie had sat at his kitchen table a long time afterward with a beer he didn't drink, and made himself a deal. He'd take what he was given and not ask for more. He'd keep his daughter. He'd hold his granddaughter — who was Edward's niece, adopted, tragic story, and had Bella's eyes and Bella's frown and grew like a weed — and he wouldn't ask.

Most days he thought it was the best deal on the table.

Seth Clearwater came jogging out of the trees barefoot in a pair of wet cutoffs, in January, steaming faintly like a horse. "Hey, Charlie!" he said, and went around the side of the house at a run.

Bella's face went still. She was listening to something. He knew that look from two years of dinners with Edward at the table — the little tilt of her head, like a radio was playing somewhere only she could hear. Then she turned back to him, and her voice went bright and quick and too high.

"Dad. You really need to go."

"Why? What's coming?"

"Nothing. The roads. They're icing up."

"Roads are fine, Bells. I just drove them."

Headlights swept up the drive.

Charlie read the vehicle before he could read anything else. Round headlights, wide grille, long bed. Black and old and loved — the paint waxed so deep the porch lights went down into it like a pond. A '72 C10, maybe a '71. A dent in the tailgate. British Columbia plates. It pulled in by the garage, and a tape deck cut off mid-riff.

The man who got out of it was six-two, give or take, and broad the way work makes a man broad. Black hair that needed cutting, a jaw that needed a razor. A charcoal blazer over a white shirt open at the collar, dark slacks with a good drape — the kind of outfit Charlie had seen on exactly two FBI men in Clallam County in twenty years.

He was barefoot.

Charlie looked at the man's feet for a while. Wet gravel. Slush. Thirty-four degrees. The man stood on it like it was hotel carpet.

Then the passenger door opened, and Leah Clearwater got out.

Sue's girl. Harry's girl. Gone since September — a note on the back of a phone bill, a dent in the coffee-can money, and Sue at the kitchen table with both hands flat on the wood and a cup of coffee going cold. Charlie had been at a lot of that kitchen table since the funeral. He'd never once pulled out the chair at the end of it. Nobody had needed to tell him.

At the graveside, Leah had stood with her jaw locked and her hands in fists and not cried a drop. Every time since, across that kitchen, she'd looked at Charlie like something she'd scraped off her boot. He didn't blame her. He'd have looked at himself the same way.

She came around the truck in jeans and hiking boots and a big red hand-knit sweater with a gold L on the front, went straight to the man in the blazer, and stood with her shoulder pressed against his arm. Close enough that if Charlie had been her father, he'd have had something to say about it.

She was grinning up at the man at something he'd said. It was so wide and sudden and crooked that for one second, in the porch light with the snow coming down, it was Harry Clearwater's grin, and Charlie had to look at the gutter.

Then she saw him, and the grin went, and something more careful took its place.

"Charlie."

"Leah." He cleared his throat. "Your mom know you're here?"

"Not yet."

"Uh-huh."

The man came up the steps without a sound — not on the gravel, not on the wet wood. Charlie had known two men who walked like that, and both of them had been to a war. He set his weight back on his heels and watched the man's hands.

"Chief Swan." The man held one out.

Charlie shook it. It was hot. Fever-hot, the way Jake's hand was hot, and Seth's. The grip was short and careful. Charlie held on a half second longer than he needed to, the way he'd held a hundred hands through a hundred car windows, and the man let him.

"Harry," the man said.

Charlie let go.

The name went in under his ribs and sat there. *Of course*, he thought. *Of course it is.*

He looked past the man at Leah, at the bottom of the steps with her arms crossed.

"Yeah," Leah said quietly. "I know."

"Dad," said Bella, fast and bright, "this is — Harry's a colleague of Carlisle's. From the hospital in Seattle—"

"Consultant," said the man, at the same moment.

The two words hit the air together. The man's nose wrinkled, slightly, like he'd caught something gone off in the back of the fridge. He didn't look at Bella.

"You a fed?" said Charlie.

"Something like it."

"Which agency?"

"One that doesn't advertise."

"Need to know."

"Ja. Mostly."

"Where's the accent from?"

"South Africa. Mostly."

"You know you're not wearing shoes."

"I'm aware, sir. I run hot."

Charlie looked at Leah, who ran hot, and at the corner Seth had gone around, who ran hot, and decided carefully and on purpose not to think about any of it.

"Dad, it's really late—"

"Chief," said the man, over the top of her, polite and level, "could I borrow two minutes? At your car."

"*Harry*," said Bella.

Charlie went down the steps after him. Bella had said *Harry* like she already knew the name, and nobody had told it to her in his hearing, and that was one more thing on the pile.

The man stopped by the cruiser with his hands in his pockets. Snow settled on his shoulders and melted.

"I need some roads closed," he said. "Logging roads north of the river, anything going up toward the open ground past the end of the Cullens' land. Through Monday morning."

"On what grounds?"

"Bears."

Charlie looked at him a long time.

"That a real bear?"

"It's real trouble. *Bear*'s the word that keeps people home."

"We had wolves the size of horses up in those woods last spring," said Charlie. "Folks still call 'em bears."

"Then folks will believe you."

It wasn't a hard ask. Hunting season was closed. A few guys cut firewood up the Sitkum road on weekends, and kids drank beer at the end of the gravel-pit spur. He could have sawhorses on both by midnight.

"I'm going to run your plates," Charlie said.

"Go ahead."

"They going to come back clean?"

"Ja."

"That's what worries me."

The man's mouth moved. Might have been a smile.

"Is my daughter in trouble?" said Charlie.

He hadn't planned to ask. He'd asked Bella fifteen different ways since September and gotten fifteen versions of *I'm fine, Dad*, and taken every one, because that was the deal.

The man looked at him.

"Yes, sir," he said.

It went all the way down like cold water.

"How bad?"

"Bad enough that I'm here. What she did and didn't do to get there is between you and her. My job's to make sure none of it reaches your town."

"And if it does?"

"Then I've done my job badly."

Charlie didn't like him. He didn't know him well enough to like him, and he had a cop's distrust of any man who showed up in his county knowing more than he did. But it was the first straight answer he'd had in four months, and he wasn't fool enough to throw it back.

"Sitkum road and the gravel-pit spur," he said. "Closed by midnight. Forest Service'll post it in the morning."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me yet." He glanced at the porch. Leah was sitting on the top step with her elbows on her knees, ignoring Bella, who stood behind her talking low and fast. Then Seth stuck his head out the front door and said something, and Leah threw her head back and laughed. Out loud, all the way down.

Charlie hadn't heard her do that in a year and a half. Longer.

"She okay?" he said.

"Ask her."

"You hurt her, I'll know."

"Her mother would kill me first."

"Damn right she would." Charlie opened the cruiser door, tossed the stuffed wolf on the passenger seat — he'd forgotten to hand it over, and he wasn't going back up those steps now — and raised his voice. "Leah."

She looked up.

"Call your mother."

"Tomorrow."

"Tonight, Leah."

She let out a long breath through her nose — the exact sound Harry used to make when Sue told him to take his boots off at the door.

"Tonight," she said.

In the rearview mirror, backing out past the Tauruses and the Italian thing in the rhododendron, Charlie watched the man walk back to the porch, and watched Leah come down to meet him halfway and put her hand flat on the middle of his back under the jacket, like it belonged there, and walk up the steps with him.

Then the trees closed in.

At the bottom of the three-mile drive, waiting to turn onto the highway, he cracked his window an inch to clear the fog off the glass. Under the cold smell of the snow there was something sharper. Metal and electricity, thin and bright at the back of his nose, the way the air went at First Beach on a summer afternoon when the sky turned green over the water. He thought about Harry Clearwater heaving himself up out of that lawn chair. *Okay, kids. Everybody inside. Right now.*

The sky over the highway was low and gray and quiet. Not a flicker in it.

"Huh," said Charlie.

He picked up the radio.

"Dispatch, it's Swan. Get me the after-hours number for the Pacific Ranger District. And see if anybody's got sawhorses at the shop."

### VI.

Bella waited until the cruiser's taillights were gone, her head turning in tiny, smooth increments, like a security camera. Then she turned around.

Her contacts were giving up. The brown had gone thin at the edges, and the red was coming through it like blood through a bandage.

"What did you say to my father?"

Harry stopped two steps down, where he and Bella were nearly eye to eye.

"I asked him to close some roads."

"You had no right. He's not supposed to be *involved*. I have spent four months keeping him out of this—"

"He asked me if you were in trouble," Harry said. "I told him yes."

Bella stared at him.

"He asked a straight question." Harry's ear flicked sideways. "Somebody ought to give him a straight answer."

Edward was at her shoulder. Leah never saw him come; none of them ever did. Bronze hair, gold eyes, a face somebody had carved for a church and got bored of before they'd finished the expression. He was looking at Harry with his head tilted, frowning — the listening look. Leah had been on the wrong end of it for months.

His frown deepened.

"Bella," Edward said quietly. "Inside."

She went, fast, the door rattling in its frame behind her. Edward gave Harry one more long, puzzled look and followed.

"Well," said Leah. "That went great."

"I've got the roads closed."

"You've been here four minutes."

"Six," said Harry.

Somebody filled the doorway. He ducked to get through it — six-foot-seven barefoot, cutoffs and nothing else — and stopped dead, and looked straight across at Harry. Level. Eye to eye.

Leah watched Jacob register it. A flicker of surprise, the look of a guy who hadn't looked straight across at anybody in a year. Then his jaw set and his shoulders came forward an inch, and his eyes went over the blazer and the footpaws and the tail and back up, cataloguing.

Harry looked back with his paws in his pockets and didn't move.

"Jake," said Leah.

Jacob's eyes came to her and stayed there, like he didn't know who she was.

"You promised," he said. "I *asked*, Leah. I didn't order it. I asked."

"I know. I'm sorry."

Jacob shut his mouth.

She came up the steps and stopped in front of him and tipped her head back.

"And thanks," she said. "For June. The newborn that had me. You took it off me, and it broke you up, and I never said anything. Not once." She held his eyes. "So. Thanks. That's all."

He stared at her for a long moment. Then he looked over her head at Harry, very hard, like Harry had done something to her and he was trying to work out what.

"Seth said you'd gone weird," he said slowly.

"I haven't gone weird."

"You just said thank you. To my face. On purpose."

Seth's head popped out behind Jacob's elbow. "She hugged me," he reported. "She said she *missed* me. With her *face*."

"I *did* miss you, you idiot."

"See?" said Seth.

Jacob's nostrils flared, a long deliberate pull, and his lip came a quarter inch off his teeth before he put it back.

"You smell like a thunderstorm," he told Harry. It wasn't a compliment.

"I've been told."

"What *are* you?"

"Off-list," said Leah, and put her hand flat on Harry's back under the blazer, and pushed. "Can we go in? I'm freezing."

"You don't freeze," said Jacob and Seth and Harry, all three at once.

The house hit her like a wall of bleached honey.

She'd forgotten how *much* of it there was in here. And now it was the Cullens plus twenty more, two dozen sweet, cold stinks layered over each other until the air was thick enough to chew. Her eyes watered. Her lip came off her teeth on its own.

She stopped on the mat, bent, and unlaced her boots.

She did it without thinking, the way she had in Hermione's kitchen. She set them side by side on the mat with the toes lined up and straightened, and found the whole room looking at her.

There were a lot of them. On the stairs, along the glass wall, on the white couches and behind them. Clothes from four different decades. Gold eyes, a few. Red, most. That perfect, breathless stillness only the cold ones had.

Leah looked back at them in her socks.

"What," she said.

By the kitchen door, a woman with caramel hair and a heart-shaped face had her hand pressed to her chest and was looking at the boots on the mat.

"Thank you, Leah," said Esme Cullen. "That's very thoughtful."

"Harry's friend has a carpet," said Leah, which made sense to nobody in the room but Harry.

Harry ducked in behind her and stopped on the mat, and twenty-odd heads turned at once. Twenty-odd noses flared. Twenty-odd faces did the small, involuntary thing a face does when somebody opens a carton of turned milk.

On the stairs, Rosalie put the back of her wrist to her nose. "Wonderful. Another one."

"Evening," said Harry.

Nobody answered him.

Whatever Charlie had seen on the porch, the leeches saw the wolf. Every one of them was looking at the ears, the muzzle, the tail swinging out of the back of the blazer, the bare black footpaws on Esme Cullen's white carpet. A couple of the red-eyed ones by the window had shifted their weight back. A dark-haired man with a heavy jaw had moved half a step closer to the woman beside him without seeming to notice.

Harry's nose was wrinkling too. Through the mark, Leah felt him cataloguing — exits, distances, who'd moved when he walked in — cold and quiet and fast.

She stepped in against his side and hooked two fingers through his belt loop.

Her hand just went there. She stood with her hip against his leg in a room full of vampires, and somewhere off to her left, Jacob made a small, strangled noise.

"Have you eaten?" said Esme.

Harry's ears turned toward her. "Ma'am?"

"Either of you. Seth and Jacob are always starving when they come in. There's lasagna. I made three. I never know how many of the boys are coming."

"We had curry," said Leah. "In London. Like six hours ago."

"Then you're hungry," said Esme, who'd been feeding teenage wolves for four months and had learned something.

Harry's tail, which had hung stiff since the door, moved. Once.

"Ja," he said. "Thank you, Mrs. Cullen."

"Esme," she said, and went into the kitchen.

Through the mark came a small, surprised tilt, like stepping onto a stair that wasn't where you'd thought. Leah had felt it from him exactly once before, in Hermione's kitchen, when Rose fell asleep.

"Don't," she muttered.

"Didn't say anything."

The biggest leech in the room had come up off the couch. Emmett. He stopped four feet from Harry and looked him over from ears to footpaws, dimples showing.

"Dude," said Emmett. "Are you a werewolf in a *suit*?"

"Blazer."

"Are you a werewolf in a *blazer*?"

"No."

"You look like a werewolf in a blazer." His eyes went to the footpaws on the carpet. "Esme's going to kill you."

"Esme's making me lasagna."

"Huh. That's worse for you." Emmett stuck out his hand. "Emmett."

Harry shook it. Leah felt him brace through the mark. Emmett squeezed — she heard his knuckles creak — and Harry's paw didn't move, and Emmett's grin went a little fixed and then a lot interested, and they stood there three seconds longer than a handshake needed, neither of them giving an inch.

"Okay," said Emmett, letting go and shaking his hand out. "You arm-wrestle?"

"No."

"Scared?"

"Busy."

"After, then."

"After," said Harry.

"You're gonna lose."

"Ja, probably," said Harry, which made Emmett narrow his eyes in deep suspicion and go back to the couch without taking them off him.

A pale blonde woman stepped up in his place, gold-eyed, smiling sweetly, with a dark-haired man hanging back behind her wearing a grin Leah didn't trust one bit. The woman held out her hand.

"Kate," she said. "Denali."

Leah caught the smell a second too late — the air-before-lightning one from the clearing — and opened her mouth to say *don't*, and Harry took Kate's hand.

*Crack.*

Loud as a cap gun. A blue-white spark jumped between their palms, and every hair on Harry's body stood straight out at once — ears, muzzle, the fur sticking out of his cuffs and collar, all of it, down to the tip of his tail — so that for one second he was twice as wide and looked exactly like he had in the bathroom doorway of room 7 at the Glacier View with a hair dryer in his paw.

Emmett laughed so hard he fell off the couch.

He went over sideways onto the white carpet and lay there pounding it with his fist. Somebody on the stairs snickered. The dark-haired man behind Kate had both hands over his face. Seth, in the kitchen doorway, made a noise like a kettle.

Harry stood there with his fur standing out in every direction, his paw still in Kate's, his face completely blank.

Leah bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted blood.

Harry closed his eyes. The ozone came up, and his fur lay down flat and glossy all at once.

Then a fine halo of Kate's blonde hair lifted off her shoulders and floated around her head, crackling.

"Huh," said Kate. She let go and looked at her palm. "You're grounded."

"I'm a lot of things."

"She did that to me too," said the dark-haired man, still grinning. "Garrett. She does it to everybody she likes."

"She doesn't like me," said Harry.

"No," Kate agreed, smoothing her hair down with both hands and studying him with narrowed eyes. "Not yet."

"*Do it again*," wheezed Emmett, from the floor.

"No," said Harry, and stepped over him, and Leah went with him, her fingers still hooked in his belt loop, because she'd forgotten to let go and didn't feel like starting now.

### VII.

Edward Cullen had heard Leah Clearwater think for a year and a half, and he had never once enjoyed it.

It had been like living beside a radio someone had left on in the next apartment. Whenever she came within a few miles of him, in fur or out of it, there she was: bitter, bright, relentless, sharp as broken glass. Sam. Emily. Her father. The smell of the house. The smell of *him*. Her mind had never been cruel to anyone but herself, but it had never stopped, and he had learned, the way one learns to sleep beside a highway, to let it run.

Now she was sitting on the arm of an armchair in his mother's living room eating lasagna off a plate balanced on her knee, and he could not hear her at all.

There was a door.

That was the only word he had for it. Where the radio had been, there was something shut. He could feel her behind it — a presence, a warmth, the low hum of a mind going about its business — but nothing came through. Not a word. Not a picture. He let his attention brush against it the way he'd let it brush against anyone's, and it slid off.

And the man beside her was worse.

The wolf sat in the armchair with his elbows on his knees and his paws loosely clasped, listening to Carlisle, his ears turned forward. Edward had been listening to him since he got out of the truck. Through Seth's mind first, at a run through the trees — *Leah's HOME and there's a WOLF and he's STANDING UP in a SUIT* — then through Charlie's — a big man in a blazer, barefoot, a fed, and a name that had gone into Charlie like a fist. Through Jacob's, every hair on end. Through twenty-odd other minds as he came in the door.

Never through his own.

Bella was silent to Edward. She had always been. Her silence was a blank place, smooth and featureless, like a wall with no seams. The wolf was a house with every light on and the curtains drawn, and someone very much at home, walking from room to room. He could feel the weight of the man's attention moving through the space — the way it settled on each of them in turn, measured them, moved on. He could not hear one word of what it concluded.

In a hundred years, nobody had been able to do that on purpose.

The rest of the room, he could hear perfectly well.

*—she's HAPPY,* Seth was thinking, from the kitchen doorway, over and over, with a third plate of lasagna. *She's actually happy. Look at her. She's—*

*What did he do to her.* Jacob, on the couch, with Renesmee on his knee and his eyes locked on Leah. *Is it some kind of — no. It's her. She smells like her. She said thank you. She took her BOOTS off—*

*He didn't move.* Emmett, cross-legged on the carpet, flexing his hand. *I squeezed and he didn't move. Ha. His fur. Oh, man. After. After, I'm going to—*

*Two of them now.* Rosalie, on the stairs. *Two wet dogs on Esme's carpet. And he's barefoot.*

*Remarkable,* Carlisle thought, and then, carefully, catching himself: *—or at least unusual. The stone Seth saw in the clearing — a ward, he called it. I'd very much like to examine—no. Later. Be careful.*

From the corner, Eleazar's mind was going around and around one point like a tongue at a sore tooth. *I can't see it. There's something there. Something large. Dense. Like weather. I can't put a name to it.* And from Kate, still smoothing down the last stray wisps of her hair: *He sent it back. He sent it BACK.*

And Leah.

Leah was eating lasagna with her left hand. Her right hand had drifted up, sometime in the last ten minutes, and found its way into the thick fur behind the wolf's ear, and was scratching there. Absently. Slowly. While Carlisle explained, with great care, the theory of witnesses.

Every few strokes, the wolf's left footpaw thumped the floor.

*Thump.* Pause. *Thump-thump.*

He did not appear to notice he was doing it. Neither did she.

Edward heard eleven different minds in the room notice it at once. Jacob's went white with something close to horror. Seth's dissolved. Emmett's had to be physically restrained by Emmett from laughing out loud again.

Edward found himself staring. This was the girl who had once thought, on patrol, in the middle of a downpour, that she would rather chew off her own leg than let anything with a pulse touch her again. He'd heard her think it. He'd heard her mean it.

"So they'll come," the wolf said, when Carlisle finished. His voice was low and clear, with that roll under it. "And you'll show them the kid. And your witnesses stand up and say she's growing, she's not an immortal child, we've seen it. And the Italians go home."

"That's our hope," said Carlisle.

"What can they do? The guard."

They told him. Edward did most of it, because he'd been to Volterra, and he'd been in their heads. Aro, who could read every thought a person had ever had with a touch of his hand. Jane, who could make you believe you were burning alive. Her brother Alec, who could take your senses away one by one until you were nothing but a mind floating in the dark. Demetri, the tracker, who could find anyone, anywhere. Renata, Aro's shield. Marcus, who saw the ties between people. Chelsea, who could loosen those ties, or tighten them.

At *Chelsea*, Leah stopped scratching.

Edward heard nothing from behind her door. He didn't need to. Her lip came off her teeth, and a sound came out of her chest that turned three heads on the stairs.

The wolf put his paw over her knee without looking. The sound stopped.

"Aro doesn't touch me," he said. "Or her. Can he do it through gloves?"

"Through anything," said Edward. "Except Bella."

"Then he doesn't get close enough to try." The ears turned toward Bella. "You're the shield."

"Yes," said Bella. Short.

"How far does it go?"

"Over all of us. If I concentrate."

"Good. Keep it on the twins." His ears came back toward Edward. "The brother. Alec. The one who takes your senses. Does it work on fire?"

Edward opened his mouth. He closed it.

"No," he said.

Somewhere on the far side of the room, the two Romanians — Stefan and Vladimir, who had come halfway around the world out of a pure and patient hatred of the Volturi and had made no secret of it — went utterly still. Edward heard *fire* go through both their minds at once like a match dropped in a dry field.

"What do they eat?" the wolf said.

It was a strange question. It took Carlisle a moment.

"They hunt humans," he said. "As most of our kind do."

"Where?"

Bella answered. Edward felt her do it — felt her decide to, in the set of her jaw, in the way she didn't look at him. "In Volterra. In the tower. One of the guard, Heidi, brings tourists in. Tour groups. She leads them in off the square, and they shut the doors." Her voice was flat. "I heard it. When I was there. A tour group."

The wolf's ears went flat to his skull.

They stayed there. His tail, which had been hanging loose over the side of the armchair, went dead still. And for the first time since he walked through the door, Edward caught something from behind the curtains in that lit house: a shift in the weight of the man's attention. Something settling. Something being written down in ink.

The Romanians smiled. Both of them, together. Edward heard what was behind the smiles and wished he hadn't.

"Here's mine," the wolf said.

He laid it out for them: a ring of stones around the clearing; five layers; a shell built to hold fire; walls he could drop inside the walls. He didn't dress it up. He said *Fiendfyre* and described what it was in about four sentences, and twenty-odd vampires who had lived for centuries in terror of exactly one thing listened to him describe a fire that made its own shapes and didn't stop.

Benjamin, the young Egyptian, had leaned forward. Benjamin could move the elements. Earth, water, wind. Fire. Edward heard his mind light up — *I could shape it, steer it, I've never met a fire I couldn't—*

"Not this one," said the wolf, without looking at him.

Benjamin sat back.

"Burn them," said Stefan, from the corner, in a voice like dry leaves. "All of them. Every one. We will help you."

"No," said the wolf.

Stefan's red eyes narrowed. "No?"

"I'm not here for your grudge. You want to settle something with the Italians, do it on your own time, on some other continent. Inside my ward, you stand where I tell you and you keep your paws in your pockets." His ear flicked. "Hands."

"We came here to witness," said Carlisle quietly. "To stand and be seen. That is all."

"Then witness," the wolf said. "Witness as hard as you can. I'd honestly prefer it. I'm what happens if the witnessing doesn't take."

"You'd burn thirty people alive."

"I'd burn the ones who walk tour groups into a tower and shut the doors," he said, "if they come at you anyway. Ja."

Carlisle held his gaze. Edward heard the doctor's mind working through it — the cost, the principle, the three centuries of trying to be something better than what he was — and arriving nowhere good.

"We would need to discuss that among ourselves," Carlisle said.

"Discuss. I'm building it either way."

That went through the room like a draft under a door.

Edward listened to it go. And under it, the whole time, he'd been listening to the door in Leah Clearwater's head.

He hadn't meant to. That was what he told himself afterward. But the man in the armchair was going to set fire to the field his daughter would be standing in, and Edward could not hear one thing the man thought, and the only mind in the room that knew him was sitting right there on the arm of his chair with her hand in his fur.

He had a family to protect. He had a child.

So he leaned.

It was a thing he rarely did. Most minds were open windows; he didn't have to. But he knew how. He let the rest of the room fall away — Seth's glee, Jacob's dread, the Romanians' long cold patience — and narrowed down to that one closed door, and put his attention against it the way a man puts his ear to a wall.

Through the cracks, things came.

A ceiling. A water stain on it shaped like Florida. A painting of a moose standing in a lake. A porch light, orange, and snow falling through it, and the northern lights going green overhead. Teeth. Long white teeth, closing gently on a collarbone—

Something on the other side of the door *shoved*.

It was hot and hard and furious, and it hit him like a palm to the chest. On the arm of the chair, Leah went rigid. Her hand closed in the fur behind the wolf's ear hard enough to pull.

The wolf turned his head and looked at Edward.

He didn't get up. He didn't move at all, except his head. His ears were up. His eyes were very green.

"Edward," he said.

It was quiet. Pleasant, almost. The whole room went silent.

"If you don't stop trying to get into her head," the wolf said, "I'll go into yours and see what comes loose."

Edward's first thought — he would remember it later, with embarrassment — was *you can't*.

Then the curtains in the lit house opened. Not far. An inch.

Fire.

A vast hall stacked to the ceiling with the junk of a thousand years, towers of it, mountains of it, and fire pouring through it like a flood, alive — serpents of it, rearing; a chimera with three heads; a burning thing with wings and a beak that turned and *looked*. Heat like the inside of a furnace. A smell of scorched dust and molten metal. And a boy's voice, somewhere below, screaming, and then not screaming.

The curtain closed.

Edward realized he'd taken a step backward. He was standing against the glass wall. In a century, nothing had ever been pushed *into* him. He had only ever listened. He had never once been made to hear.

The room had seen him step back. He heard it in twenty-odd minds at once. He heard Emmett think *whoa*, and Rosalie think *what did he just do*, and Jacob think, with a ferocious, ugly satisfaction that Jacob would not have admitted to under torture, *good*.

"Don't you threaten him," Bella said. She was on her feet.

"Tell him to stay out of her head," the wolf said, "and I won't have to."

Edward made himself stand still. He made himself look at Leah. She was looking back at him with her jaw set and her hand still knotted in the fur of the wolf's neck, and behind her door he couldn't hear a thing, and he no longer wanted to try.

"I apologize," he said.

"Don't apologize," said Leah. "Just stop."

He stopped.

### VIII.

It was the kid who broke it.

Leah didn't see her get down. One second Renesmee was on Jacob's knee, the way she'd been since they came in, with her bronze curls and her cream-colored dress and her big brown eyes fixed on Harry like he was the most interesting thing that had ever happened. The next she was standing on the carpet in front of the armchair, looking up.

She looked about two and a half. She was four months old. Leah knew that number without wanting to, because Seth had been counting it out loud every morning since September.

Her heart went like a bird's. Leah could hear it from the arm of the chair — fast and light, a flutter more than a beat. She smelled like blood and sugar and something underneath that was almost human, almost baby, like the top of Rose's head in Hermione's kitchen. Almost.

And the room tilted.

Leah felt it happen. She'd always felt it secondhand before — through Jacob, through the pack, that sick, swooping pull, the whole world leaning toward one small point. She felt it now in the faces around the room. Rosalie, halfway down the stairs. Esme in the kitchen door. Garrett. Kate. Every face in the room turning toward the kid like plants toward a window.

And through the mark, she felt Harry go cold.

It came all at once. The whole of him pulled in and shut, everything that had been easy in him — at the table in Jasper, in the truck, in this chair with her hand in his fur — gone tight and flat and careful. She'd felt fear come off him before, in the garden. This felt like a man who'd stepped out onto a frozen lake and heard it creak.

"You're a wolf," said Renesmee. Her voice was high and clear and much too precise. "Like Jacob."

"A bit," said Harry.

"Can I show you?"

She held up her hand.

"She wants to show you something," Bella said, from the far side of the room. Her voice had changed. Gone soft and proud. "It's how she talks. She puts her hand on you, and you see what she sees."

Harry didn't move.

Through the mark, Leah felt him not want to. Felt it clearly — a flat, hard *no* somewhere under his ribs. And she felt him look at Jacob, and at Rosalie, and at Bella, at all of them watching, and decide it would cost more to refuse.

He held out his paw. Palm up. Pads up.

Renesmee put her small hand on it.

Leah got a splash of it through the bond. Just the edges. That was enough.

Dark. Red and wet and close. The taste of blood, hot and coppery and *good*. And above, a face — a woman's face, gray, sweating, the lips gone blue, the eyes rolling back white in a skull that had gone to bone under the skin. Bella. Human Bella. Dying.

And then something else, right behind it. Something older. Something of his.

Green light. A woman's voice screaming *not Harry, please, not Harry*—

Harry's whole body locked.

Ozone cracked through the room like a gunshot. Every light in the house flickered at once — the lamps, the recessed lights in the ceiling, the glow from the kitchen — and the glass wall behind Edward hummed in its frame, a deep, shivering note. Somebody on the stairs hissed.

Harry pulled his paw back.

He did it slowly. Carefully. Like lifting a paw off a hot stove. His ears were flat to his skull. His eyes had gone very wide and very dark, and his breath came out of him in one long, uneven pull.

Renesmee looked up at him, pleased.

"That's Mama," she said. "When I was born. She was pretty."

"She was dying," said Harry.

The room went still.

"I was *not*—" Bella started.

"You were." Harry didn't look at her. He was still looking down at the kid, and his voice was very quiet. "I've seen dying."

Leah was on her feet.

She didn't remember standing up. She was just suddenly off the arm of the chair and standing in front of it, between Harry and the rest of the room, with her plate of lasagna upside down on the white carpet and her shoulders squared and her hands shaking — the heat coming up her spine, her skin starting to burn. Every face in the room turned to her.

She didn't care. Let them look.

Rosalie came off the stairs in a blur and scooped Renesmee up off the floor and backed away with her, red eyes on Harry, the kid's curls bouncing.

"She's not a monster," Bella said. Her voice was shaking.

"I didn't say she was," said Harry.

"You looked at her like—"

"She got in." He was looking at his own paw, at the pads where the small hand had been. "Past the door. Straight through it. Like it wasn't there."

"Her gift goes through Bella's shield," said Edward, from the glass wall. His voice was careful. "It goes through everything we've found."

"It went through mine." Harry closed his paw. "Only one other person ever did that."

He didn't say who. Leah knew. *A man with no nose who spent the next seventeen years trying to finish the job*. Who'd got into his head at night when he was fifteen and made him see things.

"We don't fully understand the mechanism," said Carlisle, from the hearth, very gently. "It seems likely to be benign. Every indication we have—"

"I'm sure," said Harry.

He sat there for a moment with his eyes closed. Through the mark, Leah felt him doing it — putting it in the room, all of it, the dark and the red and the green light, and shutting the door on it, and leaning his whole weight against the door.

When he opened his eyes, his voice was level again.

"Who's standing in the field on Saturday?" he said.

Jacob answered. He'd stood up when Rosalie took the kid, and he hadn't sat back down. "All of us. Both packs."

"Why?"

Jacob looked at him like he was stupid. "Because of her."

Harry nodded slowly. He looked past Jacob, to the kitchen doorway. Seth was standing there with an empty plate in his hand and his eyes huge.

"And you?" Harry said.

Seth swallowed. "Because Jake is." He glanced at Leah, then back. "And because they're — Edward's my friend. They're my friends. And because if the Volturi win, they don't stop here. Jake said. They'll come for the rest of us after. The packs. All of us."

Harry looked at him for a long moment. Then his eyes went to Bella.

"A field full of kids from the reservation," he said. "Standing in front of thirty of those."

Bella's chin came up.

"Nobody asked them to come," she said.

It landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Leah heard her own growl before she felt it — low and ugly and long, rolling out of her chest. Across the room, Seth's face went blank, the way it went blank when somebody at school said something about their dad. Jacob flinched. Actually flinched, his big shoulders jerking, like somebody had flicked him in the eye.

Edward said, "Bella—"

"That's not what I meant," Bella said quickly. "I meant they chose. It's their—"

"No," said Harry. He said it quietly. "Nobody asked them. That's the bit I don't like."

Bella opened her mouth.

The glass wall lit up silver.

Something came through it from the dark outside — through the glass, through the frame, without a sound — and landed on the white carpet in the middle of the room. A big cat made of light. Tufted ears. Long legs. Short tail. Silver-white, so bright that every vampire in the house hissed and moved at once in a blur, and suddenly Rosalie was in the far corner with Renesmee pressed to her chest and Emmett was in front of her and a chair went over and Garrett had Kate behind him and Stefan and Vladimir were halfway up the stairs, and the only people in the room who hadn't moved were Harry, and Leah, and Esme, who was standing in the kitchen door with her dish towel, looking at the lynx with her head tilted.

The lynx opened its mouth.

"*Harry. Kingsley.*"

That deep, slow, unhurried voice. Leah's knees nearly went.

"*Your conditions are accepted. The Quileute wolves of the Olympic Peninsula are designated an allied party under Confederation protection, effective immediately. The Cullen household and those standing with them are protected non-combatants for the duration, provided they keep the Statute. Anyone who moves against any of them answers to you. Written confirmation will be in Seattle at midnight.*" The lynx's head turned. It seemed, for a moment, to look straight at Leah. "*Try not to set anything on fire before then. — K.*"

It came apart into silver mist and was gone.

Nobody said anything.

Leah stood in front of the armchair with her hands still shaking. *Allied party. Under protection. Effective immediately.* She'd screamed at him in a garden in London five hours ago — *which threat, Harry?* — and he'd stood at a window in a cabin in Jasper ten minutes later and fixed it before she'd finished being scared. He hadn't said a word about it since.

She didn't turn around and look at him. If she did, everybody in this room would see her face.

"What conditions?" said Carlisle, at last.

"That nobody I work for touches your family or her pack," said Harry.

Carlisle was quiet a moment.

"You didn't ask us," he said.

"No."

"And if we'd refused your protection?"

Harry looked at him. "You'd have it anyway."

"Great," said Rosalie, from the corner, over the top of Renesmee's curls. "A babysitter."

Jacob was looking at Harry. Leah knew the look. It was the one Jacob gave a riptide — careful, wary, measuring how far out it went.

"You should've asked Sam," Jacob said. "Before you went and made us your *allied party*. He's going to lose his mind."

"Ja," said Harry. "I'll ask him tomorrow."

"He's going to say no."

"Probably," said Harry. He stood up out of the armchair. He stood taller than everyone in the room but Jacob, and his blazer had a long smear of lasagna down one side where Leah's plate had gone over. He looked down at it. He looked at Esme. "Sorry about the carpet, Mrs. Cullen."

"Esme," said Esme. She was still holding her dish towel. "It's only a carpet."

"It's a white one."

"I have a great many white carpets," said Esme. "Go and build your wall."

### IX.

The rain had turned all the way to snow by the time they came out onto the porch, and it was starting to stick on the railings.

Leah stood at the top of the steps and breathed. Cold air. Wet cedar. No honey, no bleach. She stood there with her boots in her hand and filled her lungs until they ached.

Harry came out behind her and sat down on the top step. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his tail wrapped around his footpaws, and looked out at the dark and the snow and the line of parked cars, and didn't say anything.

She sat down next to him and started lacing her boots.

Through the mark, he felt like a door with somebody leaning on it. Very still. Very tired. She let him be for the whole first boot.

"She got in," he said.

"I know."

"Only one other—"

"I know. You said." She pulled the laces tight. "He's dead."

"I killed him." He said it to the snow. "Nine years ago. And for nine years I've had the door. And a four-month-old walked through it like it was a bead curtain."

"She's a baby."

"I know." His ear flicked. "That's the bit."

She did the second boot. Inside the house, behind the glass, she could hear them — two dozen leeches not breathing, Carlisle's voice low and careful, Bella's not careful at all. Seth's laugh, once, cut off short. Jacob saying something she couldn't make out.

"So," she said. "What do you think of them?"

He was quiet so long she thought he wasn't going to answer.

"Esme," he said, "I'd eat anything she put in front of me."

"You *did* eat anything she put in front of you. Twice."

"It was very good lasagna." His tail moved against the step. "Emmett laughed at my fur. Falling-down laughing. I'd go for a drink with him."

"He's going to make you arm-wrestle him."

"He's going to lose," said Harry. "I'm going to let him think it was close."

"Charlie?"

"Best man I've met this side of the border," said Harry. "He's running my plates as we speak."

"Will they come back clean?"

"Spotless."

"That'll drive him crazy."

"Ja." His muzzle curled, a little. "I hope so."

"Carlisle?"

He thought about it. "I'd let him set my arm. I wouldn't let him decide anything for me."

"He made all of them, you know," said Leah. "Edward, Esme, Emmett, Blondie. Bit every one of them while they were dying. Never asked one of them first."

Harry was quiet a moment. "Ja. He stands like it. Like a man who's always been sure he did right." His ear flicked. "I knew a headmaster like that."

Leah touched the mark on her collarbone through the neck of the sweater, and didn't say anything.

"Edward," Harry said, "took what he wanted out of your head without asking, and then apologized when he got caught. I've met that before, too."

"And?"

He was quiet.

"Say it," Leah said.

"Your Charlie drove out here in his uniform with a stuffed wolf under his arm for that baby," said Harry. "And she lied to him six times in four minutes. I counted." He looked out at the snow. "And then she looked at your brother and said nobody asked him to come."

Leah sat on the step and let that go through her.

A year and a half. A year and a half of *poor Bella*, and *Bella didn't mean it*, and *look at her self-control*. Seth defending her with his collarbone still knitting. Jacob's head full of her for two years and then full of her kid. Her mother at the Swans' table with a casserole. Everybody, every single one of them, looking at Leah like she was the bitter one, the one who couldn't let it go.

"Finally," she said.

"Hm?"

"Nothing." She stood up and stamped her boots into the wet. "Somebody finally said it. That's all."

He looked up at her from the step. His ears were up. Through the mark came something small and dry and pleased.

"Don't let it go to your head," she said.

"Too late."

---

They laid the ring between eight and half past eleven.

Twelve stones, out of the Calawah, where it ran shallow and loud over a gravel bar a mile below the clearing. Leah knew the spot. The round ones, the size of a man's head, gray and black and speckled, worn smooth by ten thousand years of the river. She waded in up to her knees in the black water in her jeans and boots — it was so cold it hurt, even for her — and hauled them out one at a time and set them on the bank, and Harry picked each one up and turned it in his paws and said *ja* or *no* and she threw the *no*s back.

Then they walked the circle.

He'd taken the shape of it from her head, from the map, from the clearing itself — and now he walked it in the dark at a long, silent, loping stride, with the snow coming down through the trees, and Leah walked beside him with a river stone under each arm. Every mile or so he stopped. He picked a spot — once under the roots of a fallen cedar, once in the middle of an old logging cut, once on a rock shelf above a creek — and set a stone down and crouched over it, and carved it with his claw, and put his paw on it, and the ozone came up, and the ground rang under her boots like a bell a long way down.

Twelve times.

She didn't get bored. She'd thought she would. She walked the line in the dark and watched him work, and through the mark she could feel the shape of the thing growing around them — every stone another fixed point, every point pulling on the next, the whole of it drawing tighter and tighter like a net being hauled shut.

At the twelfth stone, on a rise of ground east of the clearing where you could see the faint blue shimmer of the keystone through the trees, Harry crouched for a long time with his paw flat on the rock and his eyes shut.

"Okay," he said.

The ring woke up.

It went around them both, out of the stone under his paw and away in both directions at once, faster than she could turn her head — a long low note that she felt in her back teeth, and every flake of snow in the forest lifting an inch and settling. For a moment she could see it. A faint blue line in the dark, running away through the trees on either side, around and around, closing.

Then it was gone. Invisible. Just the snow and the trees and the dark.

But she could feel it. Through him.

It was like the mark, only huge. Like standing in the middle of a held breath. The whole valley inside the line, laid open — every weight on the ground, every warm thing, every cold one. She couldn't sort it the way he could. It came through the bond as a feeling, vast and quiet and patient, like weather building over the sea.

Harry hadn't opened his eyes.

"Deer," he said. "Forty-one. Elk, a herd of eleven, up the north slope. Two cougars, one of them old." His ears turned. "Two of yours running the line on the east side. Big. Hot. One limps a bit on the left fore."

"Embry," said Leah. "He landed wrong off a log in August. It never set right."

"More of them coming up to the line from the west. In a group. Eight. Nine." His ears went back. "Stopped right at it. They felt it go up."

"Sam's."

"Ja." He breathed out. "Any of the cold ones from that house crosses the line, I'll know it. And the one in the middle of them that's both." He was quiet a moment. "Her heart goes very fast."

Leah didn't answer that.

He opened his eyes and stood up. He was snow-covered to the shoulders, and the blazer was soaked and ruined and still had lasagna on it, and his footpaws were black with mud from the riverbank.

"Seattle," he said. "Midnight. Paperwork."

"I'm coming."

"I know."

"And there's a pay phone at the field office?"

His ear flicked. "On the corner outside. Takes quarters."

"Good," said Leah. "I told Charlie tonight."

She stood beside him on the rise in the dark, in the falling snow, with the whole held breath of the valley humming through the mark on her collarbone, and she thought about her father in his lawn chair on the porch at First Beach. The sky going green over the water. The way he'd lift his chin and breathe in, and his face would change, and he'd heave himself up out of the chair and say it.

Thirty-eight hours, give or take.

The air had changed. She could smell it.

"Okay," Leah said quietly, to the dark, to the ring, to the cold things sitting in the glass house by the river and the hot ones running the line and the ones on a plane somewhere over the curve of the world, coming. "Everybody inside."

Harry put his paw on the back of her neck, hot and heavy, and they went.
