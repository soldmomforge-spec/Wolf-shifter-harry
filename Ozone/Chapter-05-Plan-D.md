# Ozone

## Chapter Five — Plan D

*Forks, Washington, to Lake Crescent. January 2007. The same night.*

---

### I.

Charlie Swan's house sat at the end of a short street on the north side of town, two stories of white siding with a cruiser in the driveway and a porch light somebody had forgotten to turn off. Snow lay over the little front yard smooth and blue, broken only by a shoveled path from the driveway to the steps. There was a Christmas wreath still on the door, a little brown at the edges. Inside, a television was going. A basketball game, the Sonics losing to somebody by the sound of it, a man's voice groaning at the screen.

Leah stood across the street in the dark between two parked trucks and watched the front window.

The curtains weren't drawn all the way. Through the gap she could see the back of a brown couch and the top of Charlie's head, his hair flattened on one side where he'd been leaning on his hand. A bowl of popcorn on the cushion beside him. And on the other side of the bowl, with her feet tucked under her and a mug held in both hands, her mother.

Sue Clearwater had cut her hair. That was the first thing. She'd worn it long and straight her whole life, down past her shoulders, and now it stopped at her jaw, and there was more gray in it than there'd been in September. She was wearing a sweater Leah didn't recognize. She looked thinner. She looked tired.

Then Charlie said something to the television, and her mother laughed.

Leah heard it through the glass and the snow and forty feet of cold air. A real laugh, from the chest, with her head going back. She hadn't heard her mother laugh like that since before the funeral. She went back through all of it, the whole year and a half, and she couldn't find one. Her mother at the kitchen table with her hands flat on the wood. Her mother making coffee and not drinking it. Her mother going over to the Swans' with a casserole and coming home late with pink in her cheeks and not one word to say about it.

Leah had been angry about the casseroles. She'd been angry about a lot of things.

Charlie had his arm along the back of the couch, near her mother, on the cushion behind her shoulder, the way a man rests his arm somewhere he's not sure he's allowed to put it yet. Every so often her mother leaned back against it without seeming to notice. Charlie noticed. Leah could see it from across the street, in the back of his neck, the way it went a little red.

She waited to be angry about that too. She stood in the snow and waited, and what sat down in her chest after a while was quiet, and heavy, and she didn't have a name for it.

Beside her, Harry stood with his paws in the pockets of his good trousers and his ears turned toward the house. He'd put the jacket back on. Snow had collected on his shoulders. He hadn't said a word since they'd cracked into the alley behind the hardware store three blocks away and walked the rest.

"Go in," he said quietly.

"No."

"She's right there."

"I know where she is." Leah didn't take her eyes off the window. "If I go in there, I have to tell her why I'm back. And then she has to sit on that couch for two days knowing what's coming down the highway, and knowing Seth's going to be standing in front of it, and knowing I am too." Her jaw tightened. "She already knows Seth is. Let her have tonight."

Harry was quiet for a moment.

"Whenever you want," he said. "I'll be there or I won't. Your call."

He crossed the street.

He did it without hurrying, footpaws silent in the snow, and walked up Charlie Swan's shoveled path as if he'd been invited, and Leah held her breath the whole way. Nobody looked out. On the television, somebody sank a three. Charlie said, "Oh, come *on*."

At the foot of the porch steps, Harry crouched and laid one paw flat on the bottom step. Then he did the same at each corner of the house. Front left, back left, back right, front right, moving around the outside of it in the dark like a man checking the foundation for cracks. At the last corner he stayed down longer, his ears forward, his eyes shut. The snow around his paw melted in a neat circle down to the dead grass.

Leah felt it through the mark. A small hum this time. Close and tight, like a hand cupped around a match.

He came back across the street and stood beside her again.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it. Anything without a heartbeat comes within fifty yards of that house, I'll know before it reaches the mailbox." His ear flicked. "And it'll have a very bad time trying to get through the door."

"What kind of bad time?"

"The kind I'd rather not describe in front of your mother's house."

Leah looked at the window. Her mother had gotten up. She was standing in the kitchen doorway now with the empty mug, saying something over her shoulder to Charlie, smiling.

"Okay," Leah said. Her voice came out funny. "Okay. Let's go."

---

The clearing was dark and very quiet when they got back.

Harry had cut a second stone on the way, in the woods behind Charlie's street, a granite boulder by a creek, and sent it ahead with a flick, and it was waiting for them now at the south end of the clearing, runes glowing faintly blue in the snow. Two out of twenty-four. Three, counting Charlie's house, which didn't count.

"Ten for the fence," Harry said. "Out in a ring, about two miles from here. I'll cut them. Your pack carries them out."

"If they'll do it."

"If they'll do it."

"Sam won't."

"Then Sam can stand in the clearing on Saturday and find out what happens to people who weren't keyed." He said it mildly. He was already walking to the next boulder, at the edge of the trees on the west side, and brushing the snow off it with one sweep of his arm. "Ask Jacob. Jacob will."

She went behind a spruce and stripped, fast, folding the sweater on top of her boots out of the snow. Harry didn't bother turning around this time. He was cutting stone, the claw going through granite with a sound like a knife through a cabbage, his back to her, and both his ears swiveled toward her anyway.

"Ears," Leah said.

"Working," said Harry.

She let go.

---

It was chaos in there.

She knew it before she was all the way down on four paws. Wolves everywhere in her head, more of them than she'd ever felt at once, every one of them running. Jacob, Seth, Quil, Embry. All of them moving fast through dark trees, their paws hitting snow, their breath tearing. And under the running, like a river under ice, fear.

*LEAH—*

*Leah,* Jacob said, over Seth, *go back.*

*What's happening?*

*Leah, phase out, go back to Canada, I'm serious this time, I'm—*

*Jacob. What's happening?*

He showed her. He didn't mean to. He couldn't help it, any more than she could have, running flat out with his heart going like that.

The Cullens' living room, an hour ago. Carlisle's phone on the glass coffee table, on speaker. Every vampire in the house standing around it. And a high, quick, frightened voice coming out of it from very far away, a voice that sounded like wind chimes in a storm. Alice.

*They've changed it. Carlisle, they've changed everything.* A crackle on the line. *They caught Alistair. Tonight. In the woods north of you. Demetri found him in ten minutes. Aro touched him—* The voice broke. *Aro knows everything Alistair heard in that attic. The wizard. The stone in the clearing. The fire. All of it. And Caius said, why wait for Saturday. And Aro agreed.*

*Where are they, Alice?*

*Coming in from the east. The highway along the lake. They'll stop at the west end, by the water, to make you come to them. Aro won't walk into that clearing now, he knows what's waiting there.* A breath. *An hour. Less. And Carlisle — they're not coming to talk. I can see it. Caius has won. They burned Alistair on the side of the road, and they mean to do the same to everyone standing in front of them, and then the town. The town, Carlisle. Anyone who's seen too much. The chief of police first.*

The memory ended there. Jacob shoved it away, too late.

Leah stood frozen in the snow with her legs shaking under her.

*The chief of police.* Charlie. With his hair flat on one side and a bowl of popcorn on the cushion. And her mother on the other side of the bowl with her feet tucked under her, laughing.

*We're going to the lake,* Jacob said. His voice in her head was flat with effort. *All of us. Both packs. The Cullens. Everybody who'll come. If we meet them at the west end, they don't get to town. That's the plan. That's the whole plan.*

*That's a plan? That's a line of people standing in front of a—*

*It's what we've got.*

*Leah,* Seth said. Small. Running. *Leah, please don't come.*

She could feel him. Fifteen years old and running through the dark toward a lake, toward thirty of the oldest killers in the world, and so scared he could barely keep his paws under him, and running anyway. Toward it. Because Jacob was. Because that was what you did.

*Leah,* Jacob said. *I know you. Don't.*

She let go.

---

She came back to two legs on her hands and knees in the snow, gasping, and was dressed before she'd finished standing up. Jeans. Boots, unlaced. The sweater went on inside out, the gold L against her skin, and she didn't stop to fix it.

Harry had stopped cutting.

He stood by the half-finished boulder with his claw still out, looking at her over his shoulder, ears forward. He'd heard her come back. He'd heard how she came back.

"They're coming now," she said. "Tonight. An hour. Less."

His ears stayed exactly where they were.

"Alice called Carlisle. The Volturi caught Alistair — the one in the attic, the one who ran. Aro read him. He knows all of it. The stones, the clearing, the fire. Everything that guy heard through the floor." The words were coming too fast and she couldn't slow them down. "They burned him on the side of the highway. They're coming in along the lake road from the east, and they're going to stop at the west end of the lake and make everybody come to them. And they're not going to talk. Alice saw it. They're going to kill everyone in front of them and then they're going to go into town, and they're going to start with Charlie, Harry. They're going to start with Charlie Swan, and my mom's sitting on his couch—"

"Leah."

"—and the pack's going. Both packs. And the Cullens. They're all running for the lake right now to stand in front of them so they don't get to town, and Seth's with them, and he's so scared he can't—"

"Leah."

She stopped.

Harry retracted his claw. He looked down at the boulder, at the half-cut row of runes in its face, still faintly glowing. He looked at the other two stones, the one at the north edge and the one at the south, two small blue lights in the dark clearing. Two out of twenty-four. A bowl that would never close around a place nobody was coming to.

His ears went back, slowly, a few degrees. Then forward again.

"Alistair," he said.

"Yeah."

"Ja." He was quiet a second. "That one's on me."

She didn't argue. There wasn't time to argue.

"Which lake?" he said.

"Crescent. Twenty miles east of town. The west end, there's a campground. It's shut for the winter. There's a meadow by the boat launch that goes right down to the water." She swallowed. "My dad used to take me fishing there."

Harry looked at her.

The snow was coming down between them, slow and thin, catching in his ears and on the shoulders of the jacket. His eyes were very green in what little light the runes gave off. And then the corner of his muzzle curled up. Not much. The crooked, sly hitch she'd watched across a pool table in Smithers four months ago, the tip of one fang showing. She'd never seen it look like that. Tired, and grim, and almost fond, like a man who'd just been dealt the worst hand at the table and found it funny.

"Plan D it is," he said.

Leah stared at him. "What happened to A, B, and C?"

"A was they listen." He held up one claw. Then a second. "B was the bowl. C was me in the clearing doing it the hard way, with the fence and the net to help."

"And D?"

"D's the one I didn't want." He shrugged out of the charcoal jacket and folded it, carefully, and laid it on top of the boulder. "Give me two minutes. I need to change again."

"Into *what*?"

He looked at her. The smile went away.

"Something I put in a drawer," Harry said, "a long time ago."

---

### II.

He took a trunk out of his trouser pocket.

It came out the size of a matchbox, the way the truck had gone in, and he set it down on the snow and it grew. It grew fast, with a sound like a deep breath let out, until there was an old wooden school trunk sitting in the clearing, scuffed at every corner, the brass fittings gone green. Somebody had painted letters on the lid a long time ago, in white, and most of the paint had flaked off. Leah could still read them. *H. POTTER.*

He crouched and opened it. Inside were books, and a pile of folded clothes, and a broken telescope, and a lot of nothing in particular. He put his paw flat on the bottom of the trunk and pressed, and the bottom swung away into a second compartment underneath. Then a third under that, and a set of narrow wooden stairs going down into the dark under the third, which was the point at which Leah stopped trying to understand it.

He reached into the second compartment and took out the armor.

It was black. It caught the faint blue light of the runes the way oil catches light on a puddle, a dark rainbow sliding across it, and it was made of scales. Overlapping plates, each one the size of her palm, stitched onto heavy dark leather. A breastplate that would cover him from collarbone to waist. Shoulder pieces. Long guards for his forearms and his shins. It smelled like old smoke and old leather and, very faintly, under that, like something huge and hot and angry that had lived a long time.

"Is that a *dragon*?" Leah said.

"Was." He shook the breastplate out. "Hungarian Horntail. The first one I ever met tried to kill me when I was fourteen. A friend of mine looks after them in Romania. When that one died, of old age, he sent me the hide." His ear flicked. "She'd have hated this."

"The dragon."

"The dragon."

He stripped off the white shirt and dropped it in the trunk. Underneath was only fur, coal-black, smoke at the throat. He pulled a black knitted undershirt on over his head and then the breastplate over that, and it settled onto him as if it had been waiting. It had been cut for him. It had been cut for *this* shape, the long torso, the wide chest, a curve at the back where the tail came out under it. The buckles fastened themselves. He did the forearm guards himself, quick and practiced, pulling the straps tight with his teeth.

"I didn't know you had that," Leah said.

"Nobody does. Kingsley. Ron. Hermione. That's the list."

He reached into the trunk again. He came out with something folded, silvery-gray, so light it seemed to move on its own in the cold air, like a sheet of water that had decided to hold still for a moment. He shook it out. It rippled, and caught the light, and went over his shoulders like a cape.

Where it fell across his left arm, his arm wasn't there.

Leah took a step back without meaning to. The cloak hung from his shoulders down to his knees, and everything it covered was gone. She could see the snow behind him through the place where his arm should have been. His paw stuck out from under the hem, black and solid, holding nothing.

"Cloak," Harry said. "It was my father's. And his father's. A long way back."

"It makes you *invisible*."

"Ja."

"You've had an invisibility cloak this whole time?"

"Since I was eleven."

"And you never—"

"Never needed it." He pushed the cloak back off his arm, and the arm came back. "You can't play pool invisible. They get very cross."

He knelt over the trunk one more time.

This time he went deeper. All the way to the bottom of the third compartment, his whole arm in up to the shoulder, feeling for something. When he brought his paw back out, there were two things in it. A small velvet bag, dark green, with a drawstring. And a long, flat box made of plain wood, the kind a jeweler might use for a necklace.

He opened the bag first, and tipped a ring out onto his pad.

It was ugly. That was what struck her. After the cloak, she'd expected something beautiful. It was a heavy band of dark, dull gold, crudely made, with a black stone set in it. A crack ran straight through the middle of the stone, as if somebody had hit it with a hammer. On the face of the stone, scratched so faintly she had to squint, was a shape: a triangle, with a circle inside it, and a line running down through both.

Harry looked at the ring for a long moment. His ears had gone flat. Then he slid it onto the fourth digit of his left paw, down over the pad and the black fur until it sat snug below the claw.

He opened the box.

The wand inside was long, longer than her forearm, pale gray wood, knobbled along its length like a twig broken off an old tree. It looked like nothing much. It looked like something you'd pick up off the ground in the woods and throw for a dog.

He took it out of the box and closed his paw around it.

And Leah smelled it.

It came up under the ozone. Under the pine and the cold stone and the hot, clean wolf-smell of him that she knew better than her own. It rose out of him like water rising through snow. Cold. Very cold. Turned earth, the deep wet kind from the bottom of a hole. Frost on a headstone in the early morning. The air in a church after every candle has been snuffed out and the smoke has gone. The smell of the bottom of a lake, down where the light stops.

Every hair on her body stood up. Her wolf went flat to the ground inside her, ears pinned, belly in the snow, a whine starting in its throat that she clamped down on before it could get out of her mouth.

She knew the smell of death. She'd been living next to it for a year and a half. The Cullens smelled like sugar poured over a grave, bleach and honey and cold, sweet enough to make you gag. This was what lay underneath the sugar. This was the grave itself.

Harry stood up.

He looked the same. That was the strange part. The same ears, the same green eyes, the same black muzzle with the crease at the corner from years of smirking. The dragonhide on his chest throwing back the blue light. The cloak hanging off his shoulders. The wand held loose in his right paw, pointed at the snow. The ring on his left. The same Harry who'd sewn his own tail hole and thrown up — no, she'd thrown up on *him*. The same Harry.

He smelled like the end of everything.

"I know," he said quietly. He'd seen her face. "It goes when I take them off."

"What *are* they?"

"You remember the story I told you? In the truck, outside Whitehorse. The three brothers, and the bridge."

She remembered. It had been past midnight, and snowing, and she'd had her bare feet up on the dash. Three brothers came to a river too deep to wade, and they made a bridge out of nothing with magic, and halfway across, Death was standing there waiting for them, angry at being cheated. And Death pretended to be pleased, and offered them each a prize. A wand that would win every fight. A stone that would bring back the dead. A cloak that would hide you even from Death himself.

She'd thought it was a bedtime story. She'd fallen asleep before the end.

"These are them," Harry said.

The snow came down. The runes glowed. Leah stood in the clearing and looked at the wand, and the ring, and the silver-gray cloak lying across his armored shoulders, and didn't say anything at all for a while.

"How," she said finally.

"Long story. Most of it's in the war." His ear flicked. "After, I put them away. The stone in a forest. The wand in a dead man's grave. I meant to leave them there. Then a few years ago somebody else went looking for them, and I had to go and get them first." He looked down at the ring. "They've been in a drawer since. I don't take them out."

"Why now?"

"Because there's no bowl." He lifted the wand an inch. "Fiendfyre without a fence around it, next to a town, with your pack standing in front of it. I want every bit of help there is. The wand will hold more than I can hold on my own." His eyes went to the ring. "And the fire's a kind of death. It listens better when I'm wearing all three."

"And the cloak?"

"Jane has to see you to hurt you." He held out one side of the cloak, lifting it like a wing. Under it, the air shimmered and the snow behind it disappeared. "So she won't see us."

Leah looked at the open side of the cloak. At the space under it, where the snow behind simply stopped.

"Us," she said.

"You're coming."

"I'm *coming*?"

"You were always coming. I'd just have spent five minutes losing the argument." His muzzle curled, very slightly. "Under the cloak. Close to me. Don't step out until I say. If something goes wrong, you phase and you run for your brother, and you don't stop to look back."

"And if something goes wrong for *you*?"

"Then you definitely don't stop to look back."

She stepped in close. The smell of him came over her like cold water. Grave dirt and frost and dead candles. Her wolf whined, low, pressed flat against the inside of her ribs.

And then, under it, she found it. Faint as a thread, but there. Ozone. The bitter, electric bite of the air in the last second before lightning hits. The smell from her father's porch. The smell from a bar in Smithers.

Still him.

She put her hand flat on the dragonhide over his chest. It was hot. Hot as he always was. His heart was going under it, slow and heavy, like a drum in another room.

"Okay," she said.

"I need the lake," Harry said. "The meadow. I'll knock."

"Knock, then."

He knocked, and she opened the door, and she gave it to him.

A summer afternoon, a long time ago, when she was ten. The lake so clear you could see down forty feet to the rocks, blue-green and cold enough to make your teeth hurt. Mountains going straight up out of the water on both sides, dark with fir. The gravel at the boat launch hot under her bare feet. The meadow behind it, long grass and wildflowers and a picnic table with initials carved in it. And a rented aluminum boat sitting out on the water, with a big man in it in a fishing hat, waving a trout over his head and laughing with his whole chest, shouting across the water at her: *Leah! Lee-Lee! Get your mother! Look at this one!*

She hadn't meant to give him that part.

Harry stepped back out of her head. Gently. The door clicked shut.

He didn't say anything about it. He stood there for a second with his ears down, and then he reached out with the paw that wore the ring and touched her jaw, once, very lightly, with the back of one claw.

"You've got his chin," he said.

Then he swung the cloak around both of them, and the clearing vanished, and the world squeezed.

### III.

Carlisle Cullen had been dead for three hundred and forty-four years, and he had never once stood on a battlefield.

He'd seen them. In London as a young man, and in the years after, the long wandering decades across Europe, he had walked through the aftermath of a dozen of them with his bag, looking for anyone still breathing who might be saved. He knew what they smelled like the day after. He had never stood on one before the first blow, with the people he loved spread out on either side of him in the snow, waiting.

The meadow at the west end of the lake was white and silent and very cold. Behind them the shut-up campground sat in the dark under the firs, its picnic tables humped with snow, a chain across the entrance road with a sign hanging off it. In front of them the ground ran down in a long gentle slope to a gravel beach and a concrete boat launch, and past that, the lake. It hadn't frozen. It never did. It lay open and black and perfectly still between two walls of mountain, so dark it looked less like water than like a hole cut out of the night, and the snow falling onto it simply vanished.

Esme stood at his right hand. He could feel her there without looking, the way he'd been able to feel her for eighty years. Emmett on his left, loose and ready and humming something under his breath, the way he always did before a fight, because Emmett had decided long ago that the only way to stand in front of death was to be cheerful at it. Rosalie beside Emmett, cold as the lake. Edward and Bella a step behind, Bella's hands clenched at her sides, her shield spread out over all of them like a held breath. Carlisle felt it only faintly, a pressure in the air.

Behind them, the witnesses, the ones who'd stayed. Most of them had. The Irish. The Egyptians. The Denalis, Kate with her hand in Garrett's. The two Romanians, standing apart, their papery faces turned east with an expression Carlisle didn't like to look at.

And in the treeline on both sides, the wolves.

Sixteen of them. He'd counted. Sam's pack on the left, Jacob's on the right, huge shapes in the dark under the firs, steam rising off their backs into the falling snow. Jacob stood at the very front of his line, russet and enormous, with Renesmee on his back. She rode him the way she always rode him, her small hands knotted in his fur, her red coat the only color in the whole white meadow. Carlisle had argued against bringing her. Bella had argued harder for it. Aro needed to see her, Bella said. Aro needed to see her heartbeat with his own eyes, or none of it would matter.

None of it was going to matter. Alice had said so.

He thought of Alice's voice on the phone. *They're not coming to talk.* He thought of Alistair, prickly and suspicious and loyal, who had sat in the attic for a week refusing to come down and then come anyway, because Carlisle had asked. Who had run tonight because he'd heard something through the floorboards that frightened him more than the Volturi did. And who had run straight into them.

*They burned him on the side of the road.*

Carlisle had brought him here.

He'd been thinking about that for an hour. He'd keep thinking about it, he suspected, for a long time.

And he'd been thinking about the wizard. About a wolf in a good jacket standing on Esme's rug telling a room full of the oldest creatures on the continent that they would not feed within a hundred miles of his work. About Edward stumbling back into the piano as if he'd been struck. About Renesmee's hand on that black muzzle, and the look in the wizard's eyes. *Because I don't know what you are yet.* Carlisle had wanted, very badly, to dislike him for that. He'd found it difficult. It was a fair answer. It was the answer Carlisle himself would have given if he'd been honest.

The wizard wasn't here. He'd gone off into the dark with Leah Clearwater three hours ago to cut runes into rocks in a clearing nobody was coming to anymore, and Carlisle didn't know whether anyone had told him.

"They're here," Edward said quietly.

Carlisle looked east.

They came along the shore.

At first there was only a darkness moving through the trees where the highway bent along the water, a shadow in the falling snow. Then it came out onto the open ground of the meadow, and it was them. A long dark column, moving in perfect step, so smoothly they seemed to float. Cloaks. Every one of them in a hooded cloak, gray at the edges of the formation and deepening toward the center, charcoal, then slate, then a gray so dark it was nearly black, and at the very heart of it, three figures in cloaks as black as the lake. They made no sound. The snow didn't crunch under them. They came across the white meadow like a stain spreading through cloth.

Behind them, a crowd. Less orderly. Dozens of vampires in ordinary clothes, nomads and small covens, the witnesses Aro had brought to watch justice done. They hung back at the treeline, shuffling, red eyes bright in the dark.

The column stopped a hundred yards away.

For a long moment nobody moved. The snow fell. Steam rose off the wolves. On Jacob's back, Renesmee went very still.

Then the hoods came down, all at once, in a single rippling motion along the line.

Carlisle knew every face.

Jane, small and pale and lovely, her blonde hair pulled back, standing at the front of the formation with her brother beside her. Alec, dark and quiet, watching with mild interest. Demetri. Felix, enormous, grinning. Renata, close behind the center, one hand resting lightly on the black cloak in front of her. Caius, white-haired and furious. Marcus, gazing at nothing, bored past the point of feeling it.

And Aro.

He stepped forward out of the line, alone, his black cloak trailing in the snow. His skin was thin as onionpaper and as translucent, his eyes a filmed, milky red. His long dark hair was loose. He spread his hands, and smiled, and his voice came across the meadow soft and delighted, exactly as Carlisle remembered it from a thousand evenings in a library in Volterra three hundred years ago.

"Carlisle! My old friend."

Carlisle's chest ached. There was no blood in it to ache with. It ached anyway.

"Aro," he said.

"Look at all of you." Aro's eyes moved over the line, the witnesses, the wolves in the trees, lingering on each with bright interest, as if he were browsing in a shop. "Such a gathering. So many friends." His gaze found Renesmee and stopped there, and something moved behind it. "And so much to discuss."

"We've come to talk," Carlisle said.

"Have you?" Aro tilted his head. "How strange. I was told you'd come to burn us."

Behind Carlisle, the line went still.

"A rock in a clearing," Aro said gently. "A circle. A bowl. A fire that eats whatever it touches. A creature in a wolf's skin who speaks inside people's heads." He smiled. "Poor Alistair heard all of it. I was so sorry to see what it had done to him. He was very frightened at the end."

"You killed him," Carlisle said.

"Caius killed him. I merely listened." Aro spread his hands again. "And then I thought, my dear Carlisle would never lead me into a trap. Not Carlisle. And yet, there it was, a trap, in poor Alistair's memory, waiting for us in the snow. So I chose another place." He gestured at the meadow, the black lake, the mountains, as if offering a gift. "Here. Where there are no rocks."

Carlisle opened his mouth to answer.

Behind him, the air cracked.

It was a small sound. Like a branch snapping in the cold. Every vampire in the meadow turned toward it at once, and so did every wolf, and there was nothing there. Snow, falling onto snow, in the open space between the Cullen line and the witnesses behind it. Nothing at all.

Then the smell reached him.

Carlisle had smelled every kind of death there was. Plague and fever and gunpowder. The battlefields. The hospital wards. His own kind, which was sweet over something rotten, sugar poured on a corpse. He had spent three and a half centuries with death in his nostrils, and he had stopped noticing it the way a miller stops noticing flour.

He had never smelled this.

Cold. Clean. Absolutely final. Turned earth from the bottom of a deep grave. Frost on stone. A church at midnight with every candle out. It came across the snow from the empty place behind him and went into him like a blade, and every instinct he had, three hundred and forty-four years of them, told him to run.

Beside him, Esme made a small sound. Emmett had stopped humming. In the trees, the wolves were flattening, sixteen great shapes going down low into the snow with their ears back. On Jacob's back, Renesmee turned her head and stared at the empty air.

Edward had gone rigid. "It's him," he breathed. "I can't see him. I can't *hear* him. But it's him."

Across the meadow, Aro's smile had gone very still.

"Doctor."

The voice came from the air at Carlisle's left shoulder, close enough to touch. Low, with a rumble under it, and an accent from the other end of the world.

"Step back, please," said Harry Potter. "And take your family with you."

"Mr. Potter." Carlisle didn't turn his head. He kept his eyes on Aro. "We came here to talk to them."

"He's just told you why he came," the empty air said. "Step back."

Across the meadow, Aro had recovered his smile. It sat on his face a little crooked now, like a picture hung in a hurry. "Ah," he said. "The wolf in the walls. Alistair was so very afraid of you." He took a step forward, his head tilted, his milky eyes searching the snow behind Carlisle. "Come out, wizard. Let me look at you. I so love to meet new things."

At the front of the formation, Jane had gone white with fury. She was staring at the place the voice had come from, her small face screwed tight in concentration, and Carlisle knew that look. He'd seen men fall screaming under it in the square in Volterra. He waited for someone behind him to start screaming.

Nobody did. Jane's eyes moved, and moved again, sweeping across the empty air, finding nothing to fix on. Her lip curled back off her teeth.

"I can't see him," she hissed. "Master, I can't *see* him—"

Carlisle stepped back.

He took Esme's arm as he did it, and she came with him. Behind him he heard Edward pulling Bella, and Bella resisting and then giving way. Emmett didn't move at all until Rosalie took him by the collar and hauled, and even then he went backward with his eyes fixed on the empty air, his face caught somewhere between dread and an awful, boyish eagerness.

Ten feet in front of where they'd been standing, a point of light appeared in the air.

It was small. White-hot, the size of a coin, hanging at the height of a tall man's chest. For one second it simply burned there, and the snow beneath it began to hiss.

Then it opened.

Carlisle had heard a foundry door opened once, in Sheffield, in eighteen hundred and something. The roar of it, the wall of heat that came out and slapped you across the face. This was that, a hundred times over. Fire poured out of the empty air in a torrent, and the snow in front of it simply ceased, flashing to steam in a widening wave that rolled across the meadow and boiled off the lake in great white plumes. The heat struck him like a hand. His skin, which hadn't felt real warmth in three centuries, flinched from it.

And the fire had shapes.

He saw them come boiling out of the torrent and take form as they ran. A serpent, thick as a tree trunk and longer than a train, its head the size of a car, uncoiling across the snow with its jaws wide. A dragon, horned and spiked down the length of its tail, beating wings of flame that threw heat across the whole meadow. A flock of great birds made of fire, hooked beaks and burning talons, rising in a screaming spiral. And wolves. A dozen of them, maybe more, long-legged and low, running ahead of the rest across the steaming ground in a burning pack.

The Volturi had a single heartbeat's worth of time to understand what was coming at them.

Felix moved first. He always had. He went forward with a roar, straight at the source of the fire, huge and fast and grinning even then, and the burning wolves met him halfway across the meadow. They went over him like a wave over a rock. For one instant Carlisle could see his shape inside the fire, still running. Then there was a sound like a stone splitting, and a gout of thick purple smoke, and nothing.

Renata threw her hands up. Carlisle felt her gift go out, a great pushing pressure, the same quiet repulsion that had turned aside every attacker who'd come within reach of Aro for a thousand years. It made the mind slide away. It made you forget why you'd come. The serpent came through it as though it wasn't there. The fire had no mind to turn. Renata burned standing, her hand still resting on the black cloak in front of her.

Jane screamed. Her gift lashed out at the fire, at the steam, at the empty air, wild and furious, and found nothing it could touch. One of the burning birds folded its wings and dropped on her out of the sky, and her scream went up into something higher, and then stopped. Alec's mist was crawling out across the snow toward the Cullen line, a dark, slow, numbing fog, and Carlisle felt Bella's shield move. It stretched. He felt it go out past the family, past the witnesses, thin and straining, all the way to the trees where the wolves stood. Whether she'd listened to the wizard on Esme's rug or simply to the sight of Jacob with her daughter on his back, Carlisle would never know. It didn't matter. The fire found the mist before it reached any of them and drank it the way flame drinks spilled oil, a sheet of blue running back along the ground to the boy who'd made it.

On Jacob's back, Renesmee kept her face up. Carlisle saw her in the light of it. She sat very straight with her small hands knotted in the russet fur and watched the fire with Bella's old brown eyes, and Jacob stood at the front of his line with his hackles up and his lips peeled back and his legs braced, and didn't take one step backward.

Demetri ran. Carlisle had known he would. The finest tracker in the world turned and fled east along the shore toward the trees, faster than any living thing could have followed, and the birds came down on him at the treeline in a burning rain.

Caius was shrieking orders. Then he was only shrieking. The dragon took him.

And Marcus stood still.

He had not moved since the fire began. He stood in his black cloak in the middle of the melting snow with the guard burning around him, and Carlisle, watching, saw his face change. The boredom went out of it. The long, endless, grinding weariness that had sat on that face for a thousand years since Didyme died. For just a moment Marcus looked at the fire coming for him, and closed his eyes, and his expression was almost peaceful.

The serpent coiled around him, and he was gone.

Aro turned.

The guard was burning all around him. Renata was a pillar of purple smoke at his back. He turned away from the fire, and looked across the steaming meadow, and found Carlisle's eyes.

He held out one hand.

Carlisle would never know what it meant. An appeal. A reach. A greeting, perhaps, the way he'd held out his hand across a library table on a winter evening three hundred years ago and said, *Tell me everything, my friend, I want to know everything.* Aro held out his hand toward Carlisle across a hundred yards of burning snow, and the dragon came down out of the dark and closed its jaws over him, and that was the end of Aro.

At the treeline, the Volturi's witnesses broke.

They'd stood frozen through all of it. Now they ran, screaming, scattering into the forest in every direction, dozens of them. The fire didn't follow. Carlisle saw it, saw the burning wolves swing wide around the fleeing shapes, saw the birds pass over them without stooping. It was choosing. Something was steering it.

Then there were no cloaks left.

Purple smoke hung over the meadow in a thick, rolling bank, sweet and cloying as church incense. Under it the ground was bare and black and steaming. Thirty-two vampires, three of them among the oldest creatures in the world, had been standing in the snow four minutes ago, and there was nothing where they'd stood but a scattering of small dark things on the scorched gravel that might have been buttons, or pendants, or teeth.

And the fire turned around.

It did it all at once. Every shape in it, the serpent and the dragon and the birds and the wolves, wheeled together like a flock of starlings and came back across the meadow toward the line. Toward the Cullens. Toward the witnesses and the wolves in the trees. The heat came ahead of it in a wall. A wolf in Sam's line yelped. The firs at the edge of the campground caught with a sound like a sheet being torn, and the fire leapt up them, hungry, and the serpent swung its huge burning head toward Jacob and the child on his back.

"*NO.*"

The air ten feet in front of Carlisle tore open, and the wizard was there.

The hood had fallen back. The cloak streamed silver-gray off his shoulders and everything under it was visible now, and Carlisle stared. Black scaled armor over his chest and arms, throwing back the firelight in sliding rainbows of oil. In his right paw, a long pale wand blazing so white Carlisle couldn't look at it directly. On his left, a ring with a black stone, and his left paw was held out flat toward the fire, claws spread, as if he were holding a door shut against a gale. His lips were peeled all the way back off his teeth. The fur along his muzzle was smoking.

Leah Clearwater stood pressed against his back with both hands flat on the armor between his shoulder blades, her face turned into the silver cloak, her eyes squeezed shut.

The fire fought him.

Carlisle had seen men fight horses. He had seen a man fight the sea for his drowning son. This was closer to the second. The dragon reared up, enormous, and turned its burning head and struck at the wizard, and he dragged it back by nothing Carlisle could see. The serpent lashed. The birds came around in a spiral and dove. And the wizard stood in the middle of the steaming meadow with his footpaws braced in the mud and pulled them all back toward him, every one of them, the wand in his paw shaking so hard Carlisle could hear it whine.

He was losing.

Carlisle saw it. The fire swelled. The firs went up in a long line, crackling. The wizard's ears were flat to his skull and his whole body was trembling, and the light from the wand flickered, once, twice.

Beside Carlisle, Emmett started forward. Carlisle caught his arm. "No."

"Carlisle, he's—"

"There's nothing you can do."

And then the wizard took one step forward. Into it.

The fire flinched.

He took another. The burning wolves collapsed into each other. The birds fell out of the sky into the serpent and the serpent folded into the dragon, and the dragon screamed and shrank, and the line of burning firs went out all at once as if someone had pinched every candle in a church. The fire came in toward the wizard from every side, smaller and smaller, hotter and whiter, until it was a single knot of blinding flame the size of a man's head hanging in the air in front of him, roaring and spitting and straining against nothing.

He closed his left paw around it.

And roared.

Carlisle would hear it for the rest of his existence. He'd hear it at the hospital at three in the morning and in the quiet of the house and, he suspected, on the last night he ever had. It was high, and cold, and enormous, a scream of fury and triumph torn up out of something very old and very far down, a sound like a thing that had just put its whole weight on the throat of death and held it there. It went down the length of the black lake and broke against the mountains and came back doubled. In the trees, sixteen wolves went flat to the ground. Every vampire in the meadow took a step back, Carlisle with them. Far off across the water, a whole forest's worth of birds burst up shrieking out of the dark.

Then it stopped.

Silence.

Snow drifting down out of the dark onto bare, black, steaming ground. White steam rising off the lake in long slow curls. The bank of purple smoke rolling out over the water, sweet as incense, thinning.

In the middle of the scorched meadow, the wizard went down on one knee.

Carlisle looked at the place where Aro had been standing. There was a small black scorch on the gravel. Beside it, half sunk in the mud, a pendant on a broken chain. The crest of the Volturi, a shape Carlisle had seen on a thousand cloaks over three hundred years, blackened and still smoking.

Three thousand years, he thought. Four minutes.

The smell of the grave hung over everything.

### IV.

Leah had felt all of it.

Under the cloak, with her face pressed into the cool silver weight of it and her hands flat on the dragonhide, she hadn't seen much. Light through the cloth. Shapes. The heat, coming through everything, so fierce she'd felt her eyebrows go. And the bond, wide open now, with everything coming down it.

The fire had felt like hunger. That was the only word. It had come out of him like something let off a chain, and it had *wanted*, and she'd felt him wanting with it for one long, terrible second, the cold clean joy of it eating through the cloaks. Then she'd felt him take hold. Steer it. Pull it wide around the shapes running for the trees. And when the last of the cloaks was gone, she'd felt the fire turn in his grip like a dog turning on the man holding the leash, and she'd felt him start to lose.

She'd felt *that* most of all. The fire swelling. His grip slipping. The awful, rising understanding coming down the bond that there was a forest behind them, and a town past the forest, and her brother in the trees with his ears flat, and a kid in a red coat on Jacob's back, and his hold was going.

So she'd pushed back.

She didn't know how. She'd never done it before. She'd just leaned into the bond with everything she had, the way she'd leaned against her door when Edward pushed, except the other way. Toward him. She'd shoved every stubborn, furious, stupid, unbreakable thing in her down that line into him and held it there. *You don't get to lose. You don't get to lose this. You promised me burgers in Kamloops, you bastard.*

She didn't know if it helped. She did it anyway.

And then he'd stepped forward into it, and the fire had flinched, and she'd felt him crush it down smaller and smaller into his fist, and the roar had come up out of him.

She'd felt it in her teeth. In her spine. In the mark, which had gone so hot she thought it would burn through the wool. It had sounded like something that had won a war and hated everything it had cost.

Now it was quiet.

She opened her eyes. The cloak had fallen off her shoulders. The meadow was black. Bare, scorched, steaming ground for a hundred yards in every direction, with the snow still drifting down onto it and vanishing in little hisses. Purple smoke hung over the water. The line of firs at the edge of the campground stood scorched and smoking, black to halfway up their trunks.

Harry was on one knee in the mud.

She got down beside him. His head was hanging. The fur along his muzzle was singed to the skin in patches, and it smelled awful, burnt hair and burnt leather. His whole body was shaking, a fine hard tremor running through him like a wire about to snap. The pale wand was still in his right paw, smoking at the tip. The ring was on his left, the black stone in it glowing faintly, sullenly, like a coal.

And the smell. God, the smell. It was thicker now, after. Grave dirt and frost and cold stone and the bottom of the lake. It came off him in waves. Her wolf was pressed flat inside her, whining.

"Take it off," she said.

He didn't answer.

"Harry. Take it *off*."

He lifted his left paw. It took him two tries. He got the ring between his teeth and drew it off over the claw, and spat it into his right paw, and fumbled the green velvet bag out from somewhere under the armor and dropped it in and pulled the drawstring shut.

The smell went.

It drained out of the air around him like water out of a sink. The grave went, and the frost, and the dead candles. Under it, there was the hot clean musk of wolf, and pine, and cold stone, and burnt fur. And ozone. Faint and bitter and electric, the smell from a bar in Smithers, from her father's porch, from the last second before lightning hit the water off First Beach.

Leah breathed it in. She didn't realize how hard she was doing it until her chest hurt.

He laid the wand down across his knee. He unclasped the cloak and pulled it off his shoulders, and folded it, slow and careful, the way he folded everything, and set it in the mud beside him. Then he just stayed there, kneeling, with his paws on his thighs and his head down, breathing.

"Harry."

"That noise," he said. His voice came out shredded, like something dragged over gravel.

"What about it?"

"That was his."

"Whose?"

"Voldemort's." His ears were flat to his skull. "I heard him make it once. In my head, when I was seventeen, the night he found out what we'd been taking from him. I felt him make it." He was quiet a moment. "I sounded like him."

Leah looked at him. At the singed muzzle, and the flat ears, and the paws shaking on his thighs.

She took his face in both her hands. His muzzle was hot, and the burnt fur was rough under her palms, and she made him lift his head and look at her.

"Did he ever put a fire out?" she said.

Harry stared at her.

"Your Voldemort. The man with no nose. Did he ever, once, in his whole life, light something and then put it out before it ate the town?"

Something moved in his eyes.

"No," he said.

"Then shut up."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then his eyes closed, and he leaned forward, and rested his forehead against hers. He was still shaking. She held on.

"I felt you," he said. "At the end. Pushing."

"I didn't know if it'd do anything."

"It did." A breath. "*Burgers in Kamloops*?"

"You promised."

The sound he made was half a laugh and half something else. It hurt to hear.

They stayed like that a while. Snow landed on the back of his neck and melted. The steam rose off the lake in long slow curls, and the purple smoke thinned, and somewhere far off across the water, the birds that had burst out of the trees at the roar were settling again, one by one, back into the dark.

Then he lifted his head, and picked up the wand, and stood. He swayed once. She caught his elbow. He let her.

He held out his free paw, palm up, over the scorched ground.

The stag came out of it. It came slower than it had in the cabin, as if it had come a longer way, but it came bright, silver and enormous on the black earth, its antlers catching the steam and throwing light across it. It looked at him with calm silver eyes.

"Kingsley," Harry said. His voice was still wrecked. He made it steady anyway. "It's taken care of. Volturi leadership and guard destroyed, Lake Crescent, Washington, twenty-three-oh-six local time. Fiendfyre cast without wards. Contained. No human casualties. One local vampire killed by them before I got here, a nomad called Alistair." He paused. His ears went back. "That one's on my sheet. Their witnesses scattered east, unharmed. Statute intact. Send a team out to look at the shoreline and the trees on the south side, and someone with a good Muggle story for the Park Service about a campfire." His ear flicked. "And tell Seattle I won't be in tonight."

The stag dipped its head. It turned, and went bounding away out over the black water, its hooves leaving little silver ripples on the steam, and grew small, and was gone into the dark toward the east.

Leah watched it go.

"Deer," she said quietly.

Harry was silent a moment.

"Stag," he said.

It came out rough, and tired, and his. She let go of a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

Seth got to them first.

He came across the scorched ground at a dead run, human, in his cutoffs, barefoot on the hot black mud, and hit Leah so hard they both nearly went over. He didn't say anything for a second. He just held on, shaking, his face jammed into her shoulder, smelling like smoke and wet fur and fifteen-year-old boy.

"You were *in* it," he said finally, muffled. "Leah, you were in the *fire*—"

"Behind it."

"That's the same thing!"

"It's really not." She pushed him back far enough to look at him. Soot on his face. Snow in his hair. Both arms, both legs, all ten fingers. "You okay?"

"Am *I* okay?" His voice cracked straight up the middle. "You came out of *nowhere*, you came out of the *air*—"

Then he looked past her at Harry, and stopped.

Leah watched it happen on her brother's face. The way he took in the black scales, and the singed muzzle, and the pale wand hanging from one paw, and the folded cloak in the mud. The way his nostrils flared, catching whatever was left of the grave-smell under the smoke. Seth, who'd liked every single person he'd ever met, who'd made friends with the leeches inside a week, took one step back.

Harry saw it. His ears went down a few degrees. He didn't say anything.

"Seth," Leah said.

"Yeah." Seth swallowed. He didn't step forward again. "Yeah. Sorry. I'm — that was—" He stopped. "You smell different."

"He took it off," Leah said. "Most of it."

"Okay," Seth said, in the voice of somebody who did not think it was okay at all.

The others came slower.

Jacob first, still a wolf, huge and russet, walking across the black ground with his head low and Renesmee on his back. He stopped ten yards off. He didn't come closer. The kid sat up very straight with her hands knotted in his fur and stared at Harry with her big brown eyes, and Harry looked back at her, and his ears went flat to his skull and stayed there.

Then Renesmee let go of Jacob's fur with one hand and held it out across the ten yards of black mud. Palm open. Fingers spread. The same way she'd held it up to him on Esme's rug, asking.

Harry looked at the small hand for a long moment. Leah felt it through the bond: the cold drop again, smaller this time, and under it something tired and sad that she didn't have a word for.

He shook his head. Slowly. Once.

Renesmee looked at him a second longer. Then she lowered her hand and put it back in Jacob's fur.

Bella came up beside Jacob and lifted her daughter off his back and held her. She stood there in the snow with Renesmee on her hip, and looked at Harry, and for the first time since Leah had known her, Bella Cullen looked afraid of something that wasn't going to hurt her baby.

"You could have burned her," Bella said.

"I didn't."

"You *could* have. It was right there. It was turning toward her."

"Ja," said Harry.

That was all he said. Bella stared at him a moment longer. Then she turned and walked back across the black ground toward Edward, holding Renesmee so tight the kid squirmed.

Carlisle was standing on the gravel where the Volturi line had been. He'd bent and picked something up out of the mud. A pendant, blackened, on a broken chain. He was turning it over in his fingers.

Harry walked over to him. Leah went with him. She was at his side before she'd thought about it, her shoulder against the dragonhide, the way she'd been on Esme's rug.

"He was my friend," Carlisle said, without looking up. "For twenty years, a very long time ago. He taught me a great deal." He closed his hand around the pendant. "He was also everything Alice said he was. I knew that. I've always known that." He was quiet. "I had hoped."

"I'm sorry," Harry said.

Carlisle looked up at him. "Are you?"

Harry thought about it, the way he had on the porch.

"About that part," he said.

"You didn't give him the chance to speak."

"He spoke, Doctor. He told you he'd killed Alistair, and why he'd come here instead of the clearing." Harry's voice was very tired. "He just didn't think anybody could do anything about it."

Carlisle didn't answer that. He stood with the pendant closed in his fist and looked out at the black water, where the last of the purple smoke was thinning into the steam. Leah couldn't read his face. She'd never been able to read leech faces. She thought, looking at this one, that she might not want to.

Esme came across the gravel to them. She looked at Harry's muzzle, the burned patches where the fur had gone to bare singed skin.

"You're hurt," she said.

"It'll heal by morning."

"Will it?"

"Ja."

Esme looked at him a moment longer, the way Leah's mother looked at Seth when he said he was fine. Then she went to stand beside Carlisle and took his free hand, and the two of them stood together looking at the lake, and didn't say anything else.

Emmett was standing a little way off with his hands jammed in his pockets, staring at the place where the dragon had been. Rosalie stood beside him, white and silent. When Harry passed, Emmett looked up.

"Dude," he said.

Harry's ear flicked.

"*Dude*," Emmett said again, with feeling. He looked at the black ground, and the scorched trees, and back at Harry. "Okay. The rematch is off. I'm not arm-wrestling *that*."

"I'd have let you win."

"No, you wouldn't." Emmett shook his head slowly. "No way you would." He didn't sound like he was joking.

Eleazar had come up quietly from the line of witnesses. He stood a little apart, his calm face very still, studying Harry the way he'd studied him on the porch that afternoon. Leah watched his eyes go over the wand, and the folded cloak, and the green velvet bag that made a small lump under the edge of the armor.

"Well," Eleazar said. "I've seen it used."

"And?"

"I still can't find the edges." He was silent a moment. "I'd like to never see it used again, if I'm honest. I don't think I'll get my wish."

Behind him, the two Romanians were laughing.

It was a horrible sound. Soft and dry and delighted, like paper tearing in a quiet room. They stood together on the black ground where the guard had burned, their red eyes shining, and one of them bent and ran his fingers through the ash and held them up to the light and laughed again.

"Fifteen hundred years," the one with the ash on his fingers said. "Fifteen hundred years we waited, and they burned like kindling. In four minutes. In the snow." He turned his face toward Harry, bright and hungry and grateful, and it was worse than anything else Leah had seen that night. "We owe you, wizard."

"Go home," Harry said. "Hunt a hundred miles from here on the way. I'll know if you don't."

The Romanian's smile didn't change. He bowed, very low, and walked away into the dark with his brother.

"What happens now?" Carlisle said, behind them.

Harry turned.

"There are others in Volterra," Carlisle said. "Not many. But some. The wives. The ones who stayed behind. And the witnesses who ran tonight will tell every vampire on the planet what happened at this lake." He held up the pendant. "Their whole world just changed, Mr. Potter. All of it. Whether they wanted it to or not."

"The Confederation writes to Volterra in the morning," Harry said. "Whoever's left there gets a very polite letter explaining how things are going to work from now on." His ears went back. "And the ones who ran can tell whoever they like. That's rather the point."

"And us?"

"You go home. You hunt deer. You send your daughter to school in seven years with a very good forged birth certificate." He looked at Edward, across the meadow, standing with Bella and the kid. "And your son stays out of people's heads."

Across the black ground, Edward's jaw tightened. He didn't answer. Leah wasn't sure he'd heard. Then he looked straight at Harry, and said, quietly, in a voice meant to carry exactly that far and no farther, "I still can't hear you."

"Good," said Harry.

In the trees on the far side of the meadow, the wolves were going.

Leah watched them. Sam's pack first, a long line of dark shapes slipping back into the firs. At the end of the line, the biggest of them, black as the lake, stopped at the edge of the trees and turned his head.

Sam looked at her across the scorched meadow. A long look.

Leah looked back.

She waited for it. The old drop in her stomach. The ache under her ribs she'd carried around for a year and a half like a stone in her shoe. She stood there in the snow next to a wolf in dragonhide with burnt fur on his face, and waited, and felt it come. Faint. Far off. An old bruise, pressed. Then it went.

Sam turned and went into the trees, and she let him.

Jacob had phased back and was standing by the Cullens with his arms crossed and a pair of cutoffs on, watching her. He jerked his chin at her, once. She jerked hers back. That was all. It was enough.

Seth was still hovering a few yards off, not quite coming closer, not quite leaving.

The snow kept coming down. It was landing on the black ground and staying now, the heat finally gone out of it, a thin white skin creeping back over the scorch.

Harry stood beside her looking out at the lake. The wand was gone somewhere. He'd picked up the cloak and folded it over his arm. The fur on his muzzle was already starting to look less raw, or maybe she just wanted it to. He smelled like smoke and burnt hair and pine and ozone, and nothing else.

"Leah," he said.

"What."

He was quiet a second. His ears came up, slowly, one and then the other.

"Is now the right time to meet your mum?"

Leah stared at him.

Then she laughed. It came out of her cracked and too loud and much too close to something else, and Seth flinched, and across the meadow three different vampires turned their heads.

"It's eleven o'clock at night," she said.

"Ja."

"You're wearing a *dragon*."

"I'll take the dragon off."

"You smell like a barbecue in a graveyard."

"I took the ring off."

"You still smell like the barbecue."

His ear flicked sideways. "I'll have a wash. I'll put the jacket back on. I'll wipe my paws on the mat."

She looked down the slope at the boat launch.

The concrete ramp was scorched black now, and the gravel beside it was black, and the picnic table with the carved initials was gone, if it had ever still been there at all. The water beyond it lay dark and still and steaming. Somewhere out there, forty feet down, were the rocks she'd been able to see on a summer afternoon when she was ten. Somewhere out there, a long time ago, an aluminum boat had sat on the water with a big man in it, waving a trout over his head.

*Leah! Lee-Lee! Get your mother! Look at this one!*

She'd run up the path to get her. She remembered that. She'd run so fast she'd lost a flip-flop.

She looked away from the lake, west, toward the dark line of the trees and the road and the town past them. A little white house at the end of a short street. A porch light somebody had forgotten to turn off. A basketball game on the television, and a bowl of popcorn on the cushion, and her mother with her feet tucked under her and her new short hair, laughing at something Charlie said.

Her mother, who'd sat at the kitchen table with her hands flat on the wood. Who'd made coffee and not drunk it.

Who didn't know yet that the thing coming down the highway had come, and gone, and wasn't ever coming back.

"She's going to hate you," Leah said.

"Probably."

"She's going to look at you the way Dot looked at you."

"I liked Dot."

"She's going to feed you anyway. She feeds everybody. She fed Paul once, and Paul's a *nightmare*."

"Even better," said Harry.

Leah looked at him. At the singed muzzle and the green eyes and the ears, both of them up now, all the way, waiting.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"Yeah." Her voice went funny on it. She let it. "Okay."

She went over to Seth first. He looked at her, and at Harry behind her, and back at her.

"We're going to see Mom," she said.

Seth's mouth fell open. "*Now*?"

"Now."

"With *him*?"

"With him."

Seth looked at Harry for a long moment. At the armor, and the burns, and the cloak over his arm. Harry looked back at him and didn't say anything at all.

"Mom's gonna freak out," Seth said finally.

"Yeah."

"Can I come?"

"You're coming," Leah said. "You're explaining the cutoffs."

She went back to Harry and took his arm. The dragonhide was hot under her hand. His heart was going under it, slow and heavy, like a drum in another room.

"Knock first," she said.

Harry looked down at her.

"I always knock," he said.

---

Three minutes later, Charlie Swan's porch light was still on.

Snow lay smooth over the little front yard. The wreath on the door was still a little brown at the edges. Inside, the basketball game had ended, and somebody had turned the volume down, and two voices were murmuring in the kitchen over the sound of a faucet running.

A very large shape stood on the welcome mat in a white shirt and a charcoal jacket, wiping its footpaws, slow and thorough, one and then the other. A tall young woman stood beside it in a red sweater with the gold L turned on the inside, her hair full of ash, holding its arm. A fifteen-year-old boy in nothing but cutoffs stood on the step below them, barefoot in the snow, hugging himself, though the cold had never bothered him in his life.

Harry lifted one paw and knocked.

Footsteps in the hall. A woman's voice, light, a little puzzled, calling back over her shoulder: "I've got it, Charlie. It's probably Billy."

The lock turned. The door opened.

Sue Clearwater stood in the doorway with a dish towel in her hands and the porch light on her short gray-streaked hair, and looked at her son, and at her daughter, and up, and further up.

The dish towel fell on the floor.

"Hi, Mom," Leah said.
