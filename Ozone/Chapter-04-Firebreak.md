# Ozone

## Chapter Four — Firebreak

*Jasper, Alberta, to Forks, Washington. January 2007. The same afternoon.*

---

### I.

The cabin came up under her feet like a floor in an elevator that had dropped one story too far.

Leah hit the boards in her hiking boots, and her knees went, and she caught herself on the edge of the kitchen table with both hands. The Chudley Cannons mug landed beside her palm with a hard ceramic *clunk*, rolled in a half circle, and stopped against the sugar bowl. Woodsmoke. Cold. The faint, stale smell of their own morning coffee. The clock on the log wall over the stove said 3:01, and the light coming in through the window was the low, gold, sideways light of a mountain afternoon in January, laid across the snow outside in long blue shadows.

Seven thousand kilometers. Thirty seconds ago she'd been standing in a garden in London with rain running down her face.

She was still wet. Water dripped off the hem of Mrs. Weasley's sweater onto the floor. Her hair was plastered flat. Steam was coming off her shoulders into the cold air of the cabin, and her stomach was trying to climb up her throat with half a container of Ron's vindaloo in it, and none of that mattered, because all she could hear was a man's slow voice coming out of a silver cat.

*Upwards of twenty other covens. A large population of giant wolves in the surrounding forest.*

Seth.

"Leah."

The smell of ozone went through the room, quick and sharp, and the wet was gone. Out of her hair, out of the wool, off the floor. Her clothes were dry and warm against her skin, as if they'd just come out of a dryer.

She looked up. Harry was already across the kitchen.

He hadn't stopped moving since they landed. She realized that later. In the moment, she just saw it happening around him: the canvas duffel bag sliding off the chair by the door and unzipping itself in midair. The little red cooler opening and closing. The woodstove's damper swinging shut with a clank. His road atlas lifting off the counter and flapping across the room like a fat, clumsy bird to land open on the table in front of her. He walked through the middle of it with his ears flat and his tail held low and dead still, and he didn't look at any of it. He looked like a man doing arithmetic.

"I'm coming with you," Leah said.

"I know."

"I mean it, Harry. I don't care what that cat said, I don't care what your Kingsley wants, I'm not sitting in a cabin in Alberta while my brother—"

"I know, Leah." He didn't turn around. A pair of black jeans folded themselves in the air beside his shoulder and dropped into the duffel. Her jeans. Then her gray T-shirt. Then the brown hiking boots she'd sworn she'd take off the second she got inside anywhere, which she hadn't, because she was still wearing them. He glanced down at her feet. "Ah. Never mind. You've got those."

She'd been braced for a fight. She'd had the whole thing ready on the Portkey, the seven thousand kilometers of it — *you don't get to leave me behind, you don't get to decide, it's my pack, it's my brother*. It had been sitting in her chest like a loaded gun. Now she stood at the table with her mouth open and nowhere to point it.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's what?"

"You're not going to tell me it's too dangerous?"

"It's very dangerous." A box of matches flew into the duffel. Then a coil of rope. Then, for some reason, a frying pan. "So's leaving you here. You'd follow me. You'd swim the Strait again, and you'd come out of the trees at the worst possible moment with your hackles up, and somebody would mistake you for a Volturi wolf and set you on fire. Probably me." He turned at last. His ears came halfway up. "I'm not going to Seattle first. I'm going straight to Forks. I want to see the ground before an office tells me how to fight on it. I've never been there. You were born there. You know every trail, and where the clearing is, and which of your pack bite. I need a local."

"A local," Leah said.

"You're a local."

"You need me."

"Ja," Harry said. "Don't let it go to your head."

Something in her chest unlocked about half an inch. Not enough. She wiped her palms down her jeans and looked at the atlas spread open on the table — British Columbia, the long blue fjords, the place where Smithers was — and back up at him. "The cat said midnight. A brief in Seattle."

"Seattle has a desk and a kettle and somebody who'll want me to sign things. The brief will keep." He reached past her and turned the pages of the atlas with one claw. Alberta. Idaho. Washington. "The ground won't."

A pair of flip-flops floated in through the bedroom door. Cheap foam soles, drugstore pink, worn thin at the heels. They hovered for a second over the open duffel.

Leah snatched them out of the air.

Harry's ear flicked sideways. "You kept those?"

"Shut up."

"You've had them since Smithers. You've worn them twice. One of them spent a night in a bathroom sink."

"Shut *up*, Harry." She jammed them into the duffel herself, under the jeans, where he couldn't look at them.

He didn't say anything else about it. His tail moved once, a short flick, and went still again.

She couldn't stand still. That was the problem. Her body didn't know where to put itself. She went to the window and looked out at the snow and the black Chevy with frost on its hood, and back to the table, and to the window again. The wolf in her chest was up on its feet, pacing, back and forth, back and forth, the way it had paced the forty minutes he was gone to the IGA. This time it was pacing for a sandy, gangly, fifteen-year-old wolf with a stupid grin who'd thrown himself between Jacob and a newborn and gotten his collarbone snapped for it, and would do it again, and would do it again on Saturday in front of thirty of the oldest leeches in the world.

The heat started climbing her spine. Her hands began to shake.

"Leah."

A paw came down on her shoulder. Heavy and hot, the pads rough through the wool. He didn't squeeze.

"Five minutes," Harry said. "If I turn up with nothing, I'm a man standing in a field. Give me five minutes, and I'll turn up with something."

She shut her eyes. She counted her own breathing, the way he'd taught her on motel floors. In for four. Out for four. The heat in her spine backed down one vertebra at a time.

"Five minutes," she said.

"Five minutes. Sit. Show me where it is."

She sat. He pulled out the other chair, turned it around, and straddled it backward, his arms folded on the backrest, his tail hanging off behind. The atlas lay open between them at Washington State: the whole thing on one page, Seattle a gray smudge on the right, the Olympic Peninsula sticking out on the left like a thumb. Somebody — him — had drawn a little star in ballpoint pen next to Port Angeles, years ago, and written beside it, *good fish & chips*.

Leah put her finger on the coast. "La Push." She dragged it inland a few millimeters. "Forks." Up and east, into the green where the atlas didn't bother marking anything but a forest boundary. "The Cullen house is about here. Off the highway, a few miles out of town, past the river. There's no sign. There's a gap in the ferns, and a dirt road, and then about three miles of driveway."

"Long drive."

"They don't like visitors."

"Nobody who lives at the end of three miles of driveway likes visitors." He took a pencil out of the jar on the windowsill without getting up. The pencil just arrived in his paw. "And the clearing?"

"North of the house. Northeast. Another few miles in." She found a spot that looked like nothing at all, a patch of printed green with a contour line running through it. "There. It's where they play baseball. During thunderstorms, because they hit the ball so hard it sounds like thunder." She heard how that sounded and kept going. "It's where we fought the newborns. In June. It's big. Open. Maybe a quarter mile across, mostly flat, with a rise on the east side. Trees all the way around. Spruce. Hemlock. Some old cedar on the south end. Mountains behind it."

"How far from the nearest house?"

"The Cullens'. Then town. Six or seven miles, maybe."

"Roads?"

"Logging roads. Old ones. There's one comes up from the south and quits about a mile short. Hunters use it in the fall."

"Rivers? Creeks?"

"A creek on the west side. Runs south. It's small."

"Elevation?"

"I don't know, Harry, I don't carry a—" She stopped, because he was looking at her with his ears tipped forward, waiting. "The rise on the east side is maybe fifty feet. Then it goes up into the hills."

He drew a circle on the atlas around the place her finger had been. Then another circle, bigger, around that one. Then a third, bigger still, that swallowed most of the empty green and touched the highway at one edge.

"Three rings," he said.

Leah looked at the circles. "Of what?"

"Wards." He tapped the outside one with the pencil. "This is the fence. Muggle-repelling. Muffling. Anybody without magic or fangs or fur who walks up that logging road suddenly remembers he left the stove on. Hunters. Hikers. Some kid with a camera. Anybody in Forks who hears anything louder than weather on Saturday morning, I've done it wrong." The pencil moved to the middle ring. "This one's the net. It's mine. It's keyed to me. Anything that crosses it, with a heartbeat or without one, I feel it cross, and I know where it is after that. How many. How fast. Which way."

"And the little one?"

"The bowl." He laid the pencil across the inner circle. "Nothing gets out of the bowl unless I open the door."

Leah frowned at it. "Why do you need a bowl?"

Harry was quiet a second. Out in the snow, a gray jay landed on the porch rail, looked in at them, and flew off again.

"Because I might have to use Fiendfyre," he said.

"Which is what?"

"Fire." His ear twitched. "A curse. The darkest kind of fire there is. It comes out of you in the shape of things. Serpents. Dragons. Big birds made of flame. It's hungry, and it grows, and it doesn't much like being told what to do. Water won't put it out. Spells mostly won't put it out. It burns what it touches until there's nothing left to burn." He set the pencil down. "I watched it kill a boy I went to school with. He was seventeen. Somebody taught him how to cast it, and nobody taught him how to stop it."

Leah's mouth had gone dry.

She thought about June. The clearing churned to mud. Newborns coming out of the trees in a wave, red eyes and white teeth, and the crack of them breaking under jaws and paws like ice on a lake. And afterward the fire. You had to take them apart. You had to tear them into pieces and pile the pieces up and burn them, or the pieces crawled back to each other. She remembered a severed hand inching across the grass on its fingertips. She remembered the smoke. Thick and purple and sweet, like somebody burning incense in a church, so thick it stuck in your fur for a week.

"Fire kills them," she said slowly.

"So I've heard."

"You have to rip them apart first."

"Fiendfyre doesn't need the ripping," Harry said.

She looked at him. Then at the three circles on the map. Then back at him.

"And it doesn't care who it eats," she said. "Them. The Cullens. Us. My brother."

"No."

"Harry."

"Which is why the bowl." His voice stayed level. "And why the net. The net will know who's inside, and where they're standing, and which side they came in on. Anybody I've keyed in, I can move. If I have to light it, everybody who's mine comes out of the bowl before the fire goes in. Then I shut the door."

"Everybody who's *yours*."

"Your pack. Your brother. The Cullens, if they'll let me."

"How do you *key* somebody?"

"I touch them." He held up one paw, black pads out, claws curled back. "A handshake does it."

Leah stared at him. "You're going to shake hands with every leech in that house."

"And every wolf."

"Sam's going to bite your arm off."

"He can try."

"Can you control it?" she said. "The fire. Can you actually stop it?"

Harry looked at her for a moment. His ears went back a few degrees.

"Mostly," he said.

It went into her like a splinter. *Mostly.* She'd heard him say it before, in a motel room in Smithers with his back against the door. *I try. Mostly I manage. But mostly isn't always.* She'd laughed about it then. She'd laughed about it for seventy-four minutes.

"The wards are for the mostly," Harry said.

She didn't answer. She pulled the atlas toward her and looked at the three circles until they stopped meaning anything, until they were just pencil on paper over a patch of green where she'd once watched Jacob go down under a newborn with his ribs crushed in on one side like a stepped-on can.

"The mind-reader," Harry said. "Edward. Tell me again. How far?"

"Miles. A few. When we're phased, he hears all of us. When we're not, he hears us anyway, unless we're far enough off." She frowned. "He can't hear Bella. Never could. That's the whole reason he got obsessed with her in the first place."

"And you've been working on your door."

"Every day." Since October. In motel rooms and truck cabs and a cabin outside Dawson City, cross-legged, eyes shut. *Build a room. Put the things you want to keep in it. Shut the door.* She'd gotten good. She'd gotten good enough that when she phased, Seth could feel her mood and nothing else, and complained about it constantly.

"Your door'll hold against listening," Harry said. "Somebody standing outside it, hearing whatever comes under the gap — that, it'll keep out. If somebody leans on it, it won't hold. Not yet. That takes years."

"And if he leans?"

"Then you tell me."

She looked up at him. His face did nothing at all. His tail was still.

"And then what?"

"Then I'll have a word," Harry said.

He got up off the chair. He turned it back around and pushed it in neatly under the table, the way he did with every chair, in every motel, in every diner between here and the Yukon, and the scrape of it on the floorboards was the only sound in the cabin.

Then he lifted one paw, palm out, in the direction of nothing in particular. The air in front of it went bright.

The stag came out of the end of his paw in a burst of silver, enormous in the little kitchen, its antlers almost scraping the log ceiling. It stood there on the floorboards, shining, looking at him with calm silver eyes.

"Kingsley," Harry told it. "Received. On the ground in Forks inside the hour. I'll collect the brief from Seattle when I collect it." His ear flicked. "Tell him I said thanks for the *Priority One*. Tell him I'll be putting in for the overtime."

The stag dipped its head, turned, and bounded straight through the cabin's front wall, and was gone.

Leah watched the place it had been.

"Deer," she said. It came out hoarse.

"Stag."

"It's a deer, Harry."

"It has antlers, Leah." He was already heading for the bedroom, ducking under the doorframe. Halfway through, he stopped, one paw on the frame, ears up. "Five minutes. I need to change."

"Into what?"

"Work clothes," said Harry, and shut the door.

---

### II.

It was six minutes.

Leah knew because she watched the clock over the stove the whole time, standing in the middle of the kitchen with her arms crossed and her boots planted, like somebody waiting for a bus that was late on purpose. She packed the cooler. She unpacked it and packed it again so the biltong was on top. She checked the woodstove, which was out. She checked the window. The Chevy sat in the drive under its skin of frost, and the mountains behind it were going pink at the tips, and somewhere seven hundred kilometers southwest of here her brother was standing in the snow with his ears up, waiting for something that was going to try to kill him.

At 3:09, the bedroom door opened.

"That was six minutes," Leah said.

"I was ironing."

She turned around to tell him what she thought of that, and didn't.

He had to duck under the doorframe to come through. He did it the way he always did it, ears flattening against the wood and popping back up once he was clear, and then he straightened to his full height in the kitchen and stood there, and Leah looked at him.

White shirt. Crisp, the collar open at the throat where the fur went from coal to smoke, no tie. Over that, a jacket she'd never seen before. Charcoal wool, a shade or two lighter than the fur under it, cut wide through the shoulders and narrow at the waist. It fit him like somebody had measured him for it, which meant somebody had, which meant somewhere in the world there was a tailor who'd stood on a stepladder and run a tape measure around Harry's chest and not said one word about it. Dark gray trousers with a crease down the front sharp enough to slice bread. A black belt. At the back, under the hem of the jacket, the tail came out through a slit in the trousers that had been finished off very neatly with a row of tiny dark stitches. He'd brushed the fur on his head back from his brow. It was already trying to stand up again.

Below the knife-crease of the trousers, two broad black footpaws stood flat on the cabin floorboards. Bare.

Leah opened her mouth. Closed it.

"Well?" said Harry.

"You look like a lawyer."

"Thank you."

"That wasn't a compliment."

"I'll take it anyway." He shot one cuff. It was a small, practiced movement, like a man checking a watch he wasn't wearing. "I've been told I look like a funeral director."

"Who died?"

"Nobody yet," Harry said.

It sat between them in the cold kitchen for a second. Leah let it.

"Why?" she said. "The suit."

"It's a jacket."

"*Harry*."

"It makes working with locals easier." A flat leather wallet floated out of the bedroom behind him and over his shoulder, and he caught it without looking and tucked it into the inside pocket of the jacket. "You turn up in flannel and a leather coat, people see a bloke who wandered in off the road. They argue with you. They want to know who you think you are. You turn up in this, they see somebody who's going to write things down. People get careful around somebody who's going to write things down."

"You don't write things down."

"They don't know that." His ear flicked sideways. "And your vampires are old. Some of them are very old. Old people like a jacket."

"What about a tie?"

"A tie says Ministry. Nobody trusts the Ministry."

"And no shoes?"

He looked down at his footpaws, then back up at her, perfectly serious. "There's a limit, Leah."

The laugh came out of her before she could catch it. A short, ugly snort, the first sound like it since a silver cat had come over a garden wall in London. She clapped a hand over her mouth, furious with herself. Her brother was standing in the snow. Her whole pack was standing in the snow.

Harry didn't say anything. His tail flicked once behind him, quick, and went still again.

"Who sews that?" she said through her fingers, nodding at the slit in the back of his trousers.

"I do."

"You sewed your *suit*."

"I sewed a hole in my suit. It's a very good hole." He looked her over: the red sweater with its lumpy gold L, the black jeans, the boots. "You're fine."

"I wasn't asking."

"You were about to."

She had been about to. She shut her mouth on it.

"You're local," Harry said. "Locals get to look like locals. Nobody in that house is going to argue with you about who you think you are. They already know."

The mark under her collarbone was humming. It had been humming since he came out of the bedroom, a low, steady buzz, like a phone left on vibrate in a drawer, and she pressed the heel of her hand against the sweater over it and told it to shut up. It didn't. She'd been looking at him for four months. She'd seen him in flannel and leather and nothing at all, wet and dry and soaped up in a motel shower with his ears pinned to the ceiling. There was no reason a jacket should make any difference whatsoever.

"Truck," she said, louder than she meant to.

"Truck," Harry agreed.

He went out the front door into the snow. Barefoot, in his good trousers, in sixteen below, across the porch and down the steps, leaving a line of broad, deep pawprints behind him. Leah went after him as far as the porch rail and stood there with her breath not fogging.

He laid one paw flat on the hood of the Chevy.

The air cracked with ozone. The old truck folded down on itself in one long, smooth, silent shrinking, like something going away from you very fast while staying exactly where it was, and in two seconds there was a gleaming black toy truck the size of a matchbox sitting in the snow where the C10 had been. Frost on its tiny hood. A dent in its tiny tailgate.

Harry bent, picked it up between two claws, blew the frost off it, and tucked it into the breast pocket of his jacket.

"You're putting your truck in your pocket," Leah said.

"I'm not leaving her in Jasper."

"*Her*?"

"Her." He patted the pocket. "If I lose her, you're helping me look."

"I am not helping you look."

He came back up the steps. The duffel bag was sitting on the kitchen table, zipped; he glanced at it, and it shrank to the size of a deck of cards. So did the cooler. He put one in each side pocket of the jacket. He picked up the Chudley Cannons mug off the table, looked at the message on it — *DO NOT DRINK FROM THIS. — H.G.* — and put that in too, so that the left side of his jacket bulged faintly. "Hermione counts them," he said, when he saw her looking.

He left the cabin key on the table with a note and a fold of cash under the sugar bowl. He looked once around the room — the woodstove, the moose antler over the door, the bed through the bedroom doorway, made with hospital corners — and the front door shut itself behind them, and the lock turned.

Out in the snow, he turned to face her.

"Now," he said. "Apparition."

"Which is?"

"The mug without the mug."

"Worse?"

"Different. Tighter." His ears tipped forward. "And I can't do it to a place I can't picture. I need to be able to see where I'm going, here." He touched one claw to the fur between his eyes. "I've never been to Forks."

"So we drive."

"Fourteen hours. Fifteen, with the border."

"So?"

"Or you show me," Harry said.

Leah went still. "Show you how?"

"Let me look. The clearing. Nothing else."

She knew what he meant. He'd told her about it in the truck, late, back in November, somewhere in the dark between Fort Nelson and Watson Lake: how he could look into a mind if he was close enough and good enough, and he was close enough and good enough. The man with no nose had done it to people. A man who hated him had done it to Harry when he was fifteen, over and over, and Harry had only learned to keep people out later, on his own, after the war. He'd said all of it plainly, with his eyes on the road. He'd never once offered to do it to her.

Everybody had been in her head. That was the thing. Since she was eighteen and came apart on the kitchen floor, every wolf she ran with had been in her head every time she phased, whether she wanted them there or not. Sam had been in her head. Paul had been in her head. Her little brother had been in her head and heard her think about Sam at three in the morning. Not one of them had ever asked.

"Just the clearing," she said.

"Just the clearing."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we drive, and I'll buy you a burger in Kamloops."

She looked at him. At the green eyes, at the jacket, at the snow settling on his ears.

"Fine," she said. "Do it."

He met her eyes and held them. That was all. His paws stayed at his sides, the air stayed clean, and he looked at her, steady, and she felt it.

A knock.

On the door. The one he'd taught her to build, on motel floors, in October. Somebody was on the other side of it, and they had knocked.

Leah's breath went out of her. She'd never felt anything like it. The pack came into your head like water into a basement. This was somebody standing on the step, waiting to be let in.

She opened the door a crack.

He came in like a man coming into a house with his shoes off. She felt him there, careful, taking up as little room as he could, and she gave him the clearing. She thought of it, and it was there. June. The long grass. The rise on the east side, and the trees all the way around, and the sky low and gray. Then the rest of it came, because she couldn't think of that place without the rest of it: newborns coming out of the treeline in a white wave, the crack of them breaking, the mud. Jacob throwing himself in front of her and the newborn's arms closing around his chest. The sound his ribs made. The purple smoke afterward, rolling across the grass like fog.

It took about a second and a half.

Then he stepped back out, and the door shut behind him. Gently. She felt it click.

She stood in the snow with her heart going. She checked. She went through the room behind the door like somebody coming home to check whether a burglar had been in, and everything was where she'd left it. The tailgate. The motel. Dawson City. Her father. All of it, untouched. He'd had the door open, and he'd taken the clearing, and he'd gone nowhere else.

Harry's ears were back. He'd gone a little quiet around the eyes.

"Your Jacob took that for you," he said.

"I know."

"Ja," Harry said. That was all.

He held out his arm, crooked at the elbow, the charcoal wool of the sleeve dusted with snow.

"Hold on," he said. "Both hands. Don't let go."

"Or what? I land in the ocean?"

"Or you land in two places."

"What does that mean?"

"Ron once left a piece of his arm behind on a—"

"*Harry*—"

She grabbed his arm with both hands, and the world turned inside out and squeezed.

It was like being shoved through a garden hose. Her ribs folded in. Her eyeballs pressed back into her skull. Her ears popped, and then popped again, and the air went out of her lungs and didn't come back, and there was nothing but the pressure on every inch of her at once and the wool of his sleeve under her fists—

—and then cold. Wet. Snow under her knees and the smell of spruce and river and *home* slamming into her face like a door, and Leah let go of his arm, folded over, and threw up half a container of Ron's vindaloo into the snow.

Some of it went on Harry.

She stayed bent over with her hands on her knees, breathing. When she could see again, she was looking at a broad black footpaw planted in the snow beside her own boot, and at what was on it.

"Sorry," she croaked.

Harry looked down at his footpaw for a long moment.

"That," he said, "was a very good curry."

"Oh my God."

The mess vanished. Off his paw, out of the snow, gone, with a faint sting of ozone. He didn't comment on it further. He stood next to her with his paws in the pockets of his good trousers and his ears turning slowly in two different directions, listening, while she spat into the snow and wiped her mouth on the back of her wrist.

"Everybody throws up the first time," he said.

"Did you?"

"No. I wanted to." His ear flicked. "Ron did. On his father's shoes."

Leah straightened up, slowly. Her ribs ached. Her ears rang. She turned around.

The clearing lay in front of her under a foot of snow.

### III.

It had stuck.

In all her years on the Olympic Peninsula, Leah could count on one hand the number of times she'd seen snow stick below the mountains. It came, most winters, a few wet flakes that turned to slush on the roads and to mud everywhere else and were gone by noon. This had come down and stayed. A foot of it, smooth and blue-white under a low gray sky, lying across the whole quarter mile of the clearing without a mark on it except the tracks. The spruces around the edge were bowed under it. The rise on the east side was a long white swell like a wave that hadn't broken. Somewhere under all of it was the grass where she'd stood in June with mud to her elbows and a newborn's blood in her teeth.

And it smelled like everybody she'd ever known.

The tracks were wolf tracks, most of them. Big ones, crossing and recrossing the clearing in long patrol lines, days of them layered over each other. She went through the scents without meaning to, the way you'd go through faces in a crowd. Seth, puppy-soft and a little like fry bread. Jacob, cedar smoke and engine grease. Quil. Embry. Sam — her whole body tightened on that one and then let it go — and Paul, and Jared. Collin and Brady. And others. Strangers. Wolves she'd never smelled in her life, young, sharp, overfed and anxious, the way Seth had smelled the first month after he phased.

Under it, everywhere, the leeches. The Cullens' sweet bleach-and-honey, which she knew like her own name. And on top of that, so many others her nose couldn't sort them. Dozens of kinds of sweet, all of them cold. Some of them carried a note under the sugar that turned her stomach: copper, old pennies, the smell of a nosebleed. The ones who didn't eat deer.

And faint, far down at the south end, under the snow, the old purple stink of June. You couldn't burn that out of a place.

Harry walked past her into the clearing.

He went straight out into the middle of it, barefoot in his good trousers, leaving a line of deep pawprints in the clean snow behind him. Snow was falling again, thin and slow, and it caught in the fur of his ears and on the charcoal shoulders of the jacket. Leah followed in his prints because they were there.

He stopped dead center. He turned in a slow circle, ears swiveling, nostrils working. Then he crouched, the crease of his trousers going taut over his knees, and laid both forepaws flat on the snow.

"What are you—"

"Hang on."

The ground hummed.

She felt it through the soles of her boots first, a low vibration, like standing on a bridge with a truck going over. Then in her shins. Then in her chest, under the sweater, where the mark went from a buzz to something close to a song. A clump of snow slid off a spruce branch at the edge of the clearing and fell with a soft *whump*. Every bird in the trees went quiet at once.

Harry's eyes were shut. His ears were up and forward, very still. Snow settled on his eyelashes and he didn't blink it away.

"Twenty-seven," he said.

"Twenty-seven what?"

"Cold ones. In and around the house. Some of them inside, some out in the trees. One in the attic, sitting very still." His muzzle twitched. "One small and hot. Heartbeat like a bird's." A pause. "That'll be the baby."

Leah's skin went tight.

"Sixteen wolves," Harry said.

"Sixteen?" It came out sharper than she meant. "There were ten when I left."

"Some of them are kids. Two of those heartbeats are very young." His ear flicked toward the north. "Your brother's on the ridge on the far side of the river. About four miles. Running."

Then the hum stopped. The birds started again, one at a time. Harry opened his eyes and stood up and brushed the snow off his knees with one paw.

Leah stared at him.

Sixteen. Six new ones, in four months. She knew how it worked. Everybody knew how it worked. The more leeches came, the more boys woke up one day with a fever of a hundred and eight and came apart on their kitchen floors. Twenty-odd strange vampires in a house on the river, and somewhere on the rez there were six mothers who'd found out that the stories were true the hard way, the way her mother had.

Two of the heartbeats were very young.

"Stop doing that," she said.

"Doing what?"

"Knowing where my brother is before I do."

His ears went back a few degrees. "Sorry."

"You're not sorry."

"I'm a bit sorry." He was already walking again, toward the north edge of the clearing, where a granite boulder the size of a pickup sat half buried at the foot of the trees with a cap of snow on it like a hat. He brushed the snow off its face with one sweep of his arm. Then he held up his right paw and let one claw slide out to its full length, black and curved and wicked, and put the point of it to the stone.

The granite peeled away under it like butter under a hot knife.

He cut fast. Leah couldn't follow the shapes. Angles and hooks and long straight strokes, a row of them, then another under it, the stone curling off the claw in pale gray ribbons that dropped smoking into the snow. When he finished the last stroke, the whole row flared blue-white, bright enough that she squinted, and then faded down to nothing more than fresh scratches on an old rock.

"That's it?" Leah said.

"That's one." He retracted the claw and looked at the stone critically, head tilted. "I need twelve like it out on the fence. Seven on the net. Five around the bowl. Twenty-four."

"By Saturday."

"By tomorrow night. I want a day to test it."

"Can I help?"

"You can carry rocks."

"Wow."

"Heavy rocks." His ear flicked sideways. "And if your pack would run the outer stones out to where I need them, I'd save six hours. They cover ground faster than I do in the trees."

"You want my pack to carry your rocks."

"I want your pack to not be on fire on Saturday."

She didn't have anything to say to that. She looked at the runes, and at the snow coming down, and at the trees, where somewhere four miles north her little brother was running a patrol line with his ears up, not knowing she was here.

"I have to tell them," she said. "Before they smell you and lose their minds."

"Ja. I thought you might."

She pulled the sweater off over her head.

The cold hit her bare arms and didn't bother her at all. She folded the sweater, carefully, the gold L on the outside, and held it out to him. He took it and laid it over his forearm like a waiter with a napkin. Then she sat on the boulder and unlaced her boots.

"Turn around," she said.

"I've seen—"

"Turn *around*, Harry."

He turned around. He stood with his back to her and her sweater over his arm and the snow falling on his shoulders, and both his ears swiveled backward toward her anyway, and stayed there.

"Ears," Leah said.

"I can't help the ears."

"You can help the ears."

The ears stayed exactly where they were. She shoved her jeans and boots and the rest under the overhang of the boulder, where the snow wouldn't get them, stood up naked in the falling snow, and let go.

The heat came up her spine like a struck match, and she burst.

*LEAH!*

Seth came in like a bucket of water to the face. Before she'd even finished landing on four paws, before her ears were done unfolding, he was all over the inside of her skull, loud and enormous and so happy it hurt.

*Leah! LEAH! Oh my God, you phased, you never phase in the daytime, where are you, are you — that's the CLEARING, why are you in the clearing, you're HERE, you're actually—*

*Whoa,* said another voice, slower. Quil. *Leah?*

*Is that Leah?* said Embry. *Since when is Leah back?*

And under them all, heavier, quieter, filling the space like a big man stepping into a small room:

*Leah.*

Jacob.

She felt him take in where she was. The clearing. The snow. And she felt all four of them bump into the door at the same time — four minds coming at her the way they always had, like water into a basement — and stop. Slide off. Pool against it and go no further.

*Is that a DOOR?* said Seth.

*What the hell,* said Quil.

*Leah,* said Jacob, very carefully. *How are you doing that?*

*Practice.*

*Since when do you PRACTICE—*

*Seth. Not now.*

They couldn't see behind the door, but they could feel what came out under it. She knew they could. She could feel them feeling it. It had been the same every time she'd phased in since October, and worse since Dawson City: her mood seeping out into the pack mind like heat coming off a stove. They felt it now. She was frightened, and angry, and her stomach was still upside down from the trip, and under all that, steady as a heartbeat, was something else. Something hot and settled and sure that had never once been in her head in the year and a half they'd all been living there.

*You feel like December again,* Seth said, in a much smaller voice.

*Shut up, Seth.*

*You DO, though—*

Then they saw what she was looking at, because she couldn't stop looking at it.

A huge black wolf standing on two legs in the middle of the clearing with his back to her. In a charcoal suit jacket and pressed gray trousers. With her red sweater folded over one arm. Barefoot in the snow.

Nobody said anything for about a second and a half.

*Is that a WEREWOLF?* Seth said. *In a SUIT?*

*Dude,* said Quil. *Is he holding your sweater?*

*Why doesn't he have any shoes on?* said Embry.

*He's with me,* Leah said. *He's here about the Volturi.*

The pack mind went still.

She felt it go through Jacob like a dropped stone through deep water. She felt it hit Seth, and she felt what Seth had been keeping from her every time she'd phased in for four months, because now he couldn't keep it. The fear. Fifteen years old and scared out of his mind, every night, every patrol, lying awake in his room thinking about a line of black cloaks coming out of the trees.

*How do you know about the Volturi?* Jacob said. His voice had gone flat and hard. *Leah. How do you know?*

*The people he works for know. They sent him.* She couldn't explain the rest. The rest was behind the door. *He's here to help. He needs to talk to Carlisle.*

*Leah.* Jacob's weight came down on her. He didn't use the Alpha voice. He didn't have to. *You promised.*

*I know.*

*I asked you. I asked you to stay away.*

*I know you did.*

And then, because he couldn't hide it anymore, it came out of him. All of it. The thing he'd shoved away from her in December so hard she'd felt the shove. Alice, before she left, white-faced in the Cullens' living room: *They're coming. All of them. When the snow sticks to the ground.* The witnesses arriving, coven after coven, red eyes in the windows. The training in the clearing. The kid, Nessie, sitting on Jacob's knee and pressing her hand to his cheek to ask him, in pictures, whether the people in the black cloaks were going to be angry with her. Jacob lying awake on the Cullens' couch at three in the morning. Jacob's whole plan, simple and stupid and absolutely certain: get to the kid first. Get her out. Whatever it costs.

*Look at the ground, Leah,* Jacob said.

She looked at the snow.

*It stuck this morning.*

*Okay,* Leah said, after a while. *Okay.*

*Edward's hearing all this, you know,* Embry said. *Through us.*

*Good. Then he can tell them. Tell the leeches not to do anything stupid.*

*Bring him to the house,* Jacob said. *Walk up the drive. Slowly. I'll tell Sam.*

*Fine.*

*I'm coming to the clearing,* Seth said. *I'm coming right now, I'm four miles out, I can be there in—*

*Seth,* said Jacob. *House.*

*But—*

*House, Seth.*

*FINE.*

Jacob's attention came back to her. Some of the weight went out of it.

*Leah?*

*What.*

*I'm glad you're not dead in a ditch.*

*Shut up, Jacob,* she said, and let go.

She stayed down a second longer than she needed to. Then she got up and dressed, fast, jeans and boots, and held her hand out behind her without looking.

The sweater arrived in it. Harry hadn't turned around.

She pulled it on over her head. The wool was hot from his arm.

He was quiet for a moment. Then he looked down too, at the clean white foot of it lying across the whole clearing, and his ears went flat.

"Then we're on schedule," he said.

He held out his arm again. She looked at it.

"I need the bottom of the drive," he said. "The gap in the ferns. I'll knock."

"I know you'll knock." She took his arm, both hands, and met his eyes. "Knock, then."

He knocked.

### IV.

Seth Clearwater stood barefoot in the snow on the Cullens' front lawn in a pair of cutoffs and nothing else, and tried very hard not to think the words *werewolf in a suit*.

"Seth," said Edward, from the porch. "Please."

"I'm not doing it on purpose!"

"You've done it eleven times."

"Wait," said Emmett, who was sitting on the porch rail with his boots dangling. "Werewolf in a *what*?"

Seth had run the four miles from the ridge in a little under three minutes, phased behind the garage, dragged his cutoffs off his ankle, and come around the side of the house at a jog, and the whole time he'd been thinking it. He couldn't stop. Every time he tried to think about anything else, the picture came back: the clearing through Leah's eyes, the snow, and that huge black shape standing up on its hind legs with Leah's sweater over its arm. Ears like a German shepherd's. A suit jacket. No shoes.

Edward could hear them coming. Seth could hear them too, now, if he held still — two sets of footsteps a long way down the drive, crunching in the snow. One of them was Leah's. He'd know Leah's walk anywhere. The other one he couldn't hear at all, which was creepy, and which he'd decided not to think about.

They were all out on the porch. Pretty much everybody. Carlisle on the top step with his hands in his pockets. Esme in the doorway behind him with a dish towel over her shoulder, like she'd come straight from the kitchen. Rosalie at the far end of the porch with her arms folded. Edward and Bella by the door, Bella standing in front of Edward the way she did now, which still looked weird to Seth, because Bella was a foot shorter than him. And Jacob on the steps, human again, in cutoffs and a T-shirt for the kid's sake, with Nessie on his hip in the little red wool coat Alice had bought her before she left.

Behind them, in every window of the big white house, there were faces. Red eyes. The Irish ones, and the Egyptians, and the two creepy Romanian guys who looked like they'd been carved out of chalk and left in a basement for a thousand years. The big lady from the Amazon. Everybody wanted to see.

Seth wanted to throw up a little. Leah was why.

He'd felt her come into the pack mind an hour ago, and it had been the best thing that happened to him in four months, and also the worst. Because Jacob had sent her away. Jacob had sent her as far away as she would go so she wouldn't be in the clearing on Saturday, and Seth had been glad. He'd been so glad. Every night lying in his room listening to his mom not sleep down the hall, he'd thought: *at least Leah's not here.* Whatever happened to the rest of them, at least she was somewhere in Canada being mad at everybody, and she'd be okay.

Now she was here. Walking up the drive. Slowly, because Jacob told her to.

"She's around the bend," Edward said quietly.

Seth looked.

They came around the last curve of the driveway between the snowed-in cedars, and the first thing he saw was the red sweater. Then the second thing he saw was the guy, and the guy was enormous. Taller than Jacob. Taller than Emmett. Seven feet, maybe, standing up on two legs like a man, black all over, with a long wolf's head and tall ears and a tail. A charcoal jacket over a white shirt, and gray pants with a crease in them, and big black bare paws in the snow.

And the third thing Seth saw, and the one he kept seeing afterward when he tried to explain it to Quil and Embry and couldn't, was that Leah was holding his arm.

Like somebody walking with her boyfriend. Her arm hooked through his, her shoulder right up against him, matching his stride. She was saying something. Seth couldn't hear what. The guy said something back without looking down at her, and his ear flicked sideways, and Leah tipped her head back and laughed.

Seth heard it from a hundred yards away. It went through him like cold water.

He tried to remember the last time he'd heard Leah laugh like that. A real one, loud, from all the way down. He went back through the pack mind, all of it, a year and a half of her, and couldn't find one. He went back further than that. Before she phased. Before Dad. Before Sam came out of the woods after two weeks and wouldn't look at her. Seth had been thirteen. He couldn't find it there either.

"Huh," said Emmett, on the rail. Just that.

Then the wind came down the driveway, and Seth smelled them.

The big guy smelled like the inside of a pine forest after rain and cold stones and something sharp and electric that made the hair on Seth's arms stand up. Like the air before lightning hit the water off First Beach. And Leah smelled like *him*. All over. Like it had soaked into her, like she'd been sleeping in it for months.

Leah saw him then. Her face changed. She let go of the guy's arm and ran.

She hit Seth so hard his feet came off the ground. She'd never been a hugger. Leah's version of a hug, for as long as Seth could remember, was a punch in the shoulder and a *don't be dumb*. Now she had both arms locked around his ribs, and she was holding on like he was the last solid thing on a sinking ship, and his ribs creaked, and he didn't care.

"Hey," he said into her hair. "Hey, Leah."

"Hey, idiot."

She put him down. She grabbed his face with both hands and looked at him, hard, the way Mom looked at him when he came home late. Then she put her hand flat on his bare collarbone and pressed, right where Bella had snapped it.

"Ow," Seth said, automatically.

"Does that hurt?"

"No. I just thought I should say ow."

"*Seth*."

"It's fine! It's totally fine. It's been fine since September, Carlisle said—" He stopped, because Leah's eyes had narrowed, and he remembered what Leah thought about what Carlisle said. "It's fine," he said again.

She looked at him a second longer. Then she was back at the big guy's side. Three steps. Like a rubber band snapping back. She didn't take his arm this time. She stood with her shoulder pressed against his ribs and two of her fingers hooked into the side pocket of his jacket, like it was the most normal thing in the world, like she always stood like that.

The guy looked down at Seth. Up close, his eyes were green. Really green, like a traffic light. He held out a big black paw, leathery pads on the palm, dark claws curled back out of the way.

"Seth," he said. The voice came out of that long muzzle sounding exactly like a guy. An accent Seth didn't know. "Harry."

Seth shook. The paw was hot as a stove and rough as a work glove, and it closed around his hand very carefully.

"Are you why she felt like that?" Seth said. It came out before he could stop it. "In December?"

The guy's ears went back a little. "Probably."

"She ran in circles for three hours. I thought she was having a stroke."

"Seth," said Leah.

"If you hurt her—"

"She'd hurt me first," said Harry.

Seth thought about that. He thought about every single fight he'd ever been in with his sister.

"Yeah," he said. "Okay. That's fair."

Then Harry looked up, past Seth, at the porch. At Jacob. At Nessie on Jacob's hip.

Seth saw it happen. Everybody who saw Nessie for the first time did the same thing — the Irish ones, the Egyptians, the Romanians, even the big scary lady from the Amazon. Their faces went soft. You couldn't help it. She was just like that. Seth had watched it happen to Charlie, who wasn't even supposed to know anything.

Harry's ears went flat back against his skull. His tail, which had been swinging a little behind him the whole walk up, stopped dead. He looked at Nessie like Seth's dad used to look at a downed power line across the road after a storm. Very still. Like the wrong move would light everybody up.

Nessie looked back at him over Jacob's shoulder with her big brown eyes and didn't blink.

Jacob shifted her to his other hip, away from the steps.

"Leah," Jacob said.

"Jacob."

"You promised."

"Yeah. I broke it."

Behind Jacob, Edward had gone very still. "I can't hear him," he said, low, to Carlisle. "At all." Then his eyes moved to Leah, and his forehead creased. "And I can barely hear her."

"Since when can't you hear *Leah*?" Seth said.

Nobody answered him.

Carlisle came down the steps into the snow. He did it slowly, the way he did everything around the wolves, and stopped a polite distance away and held out his hand. "Carlisle Cullen," he said. "Welcome. Though I confess I don't know who I'm welcoming."

"Harry Potter." The big guy shook Carlisle's hand, one quick firm pump. "I'm a contractor with the International Confederation of Wizards. The American office asked for me this morning. I'm here about Saturday."

"*Wizards*," said Emmett from the rail, with enormous satisfaction, as if somebody had finally told him the answer to a riddle. "Like, wands?"

"Sometimes."

"Wonderful," said Rosalie, from the far end of the porch, in the voice she used for the wolves. "Another dog."

"*Wolf*," said Leah.

Seth's mouth fell open.

Leah called herself a dog. She called *all* of them dogs. She'd been calling them dogs since the day she phased, and whenever anybody said *wolf*, she said *same thing*. Seth had heard her say it a thousand times. It was practically her catchphrase.

Rosalie's eyebrows went up. She looked at Leah for a long second. She didn't say anything else.

"Please," Esme said, from the doorway. "Come inside out of the snow. All of you."

Harry went up the steps. At the top, on the mat, he stopped and wiped his paws. He did it for a long time, slow and thorough, one big black paw and then the other, back and forth on the bristles. Esme watched him do it. Seth saw her face do something small.

And Leah sat down on the top step, right there in front of everybody, and unlaced her boots.

She took them off. She lined them up side by side next to the door, neatly, toes to the wall. Then she stood up in her socks and followed Harry inside.

Seth stood on the lawn in the snow and stared at the boots.

Leah had been around this house for weeks back in the fall, when Jacob split off from Sam's pack and the three of them had nowhere else to go. She'd stood by the glass wall in her boots. She'd tracked mud across Esme's floor on purpose and watched Rosalie watch her do it. She'd never once, not one single time, taken her shoes off in here.

Seth thought about the note on the kitchen table back in September. Mom's hands flat on the wood. DON'T SEND SETH, in capitals, underlined.

He thought: *who are you?*

He thought: *please stay like this.*

Then Jacob said "Seth," and he went inside.

### V.

The house smelled like somebody had poured a candy factory into a hospital and left it to sit.

Leah had forgotten. Four months, and her nose had let go of how bad it was, the way you let go of a nightmare by lunch. Bleach and honey and cold, so thick in the big white living room she could taste it at the back of her throat. And under the Cullens' sweetness, two dozen others, every one of them a little different, every one of them dead. The copper under some of them. Old pennies. She breathed through her mouth and it didn't help.

They were everywhere. On the white couches, on the stairs, standing along the glass wall with the snow coming down behind them over the river. A big, broad, dark-haired woman with a girl at her elbow, the girl small and freckled with a cloud of red curls. Two men with skin like crumpled paper. A dark, slender couple by the piano, and a young-looking guy with them who had an easy, open, curious face and red eyes like all the rest. A tall woman with long black braids and a long face, very still, watching Harry the way a heron watches water. A lanky guy with a ponytail leaning on the banister next to a white-blonde woman who looked like she'd been carved for the front of a ship. Red eyes. Red eyes. Gold eyes, a few of them. Mostly red.

Every one of them was looking at Harry. Then at her. Then, every one of them, at the place where her shoulder was pressed against his arm.

She hadn't noticed she was doing it. She noticed now. She stayed where she was.

Harry stopped just inside the room, on the edge of Esme's white rug, and looked around once. She'd watched him do it in bars and diners and gas stations all the way up to the Yukon: door, windows, stairs, exits, faces. It took him about two seconds. His ears turned and settled. His tail hung still.

"Some of you have red eyes," he said.

Nobody moved.

"I know what that means," Harry said. "You're guests in this house, and I'll treat you like guests. While I'm working here, nobody feeds within a hundred miles of it. If somebody does, I'll know about it."

The big dark-haired woman looked at him the way you'd look at a dog that had wandered into a church and started giving the sermon. "And who are you," she said, in an Irish accent you could have cut stone with, "to be setting terms in Carlisle's house?"

"They're reasonable terms, Siobhan," Carlisle said mildly, from behind them.

"I didn't ask if they were reasonable."

The freckled girl at Siobhan's elbow hadn't taken her eyes off Harry. "He means it," she said quietly. "All of it."

Siobhan's mouth thinned. She didn't say anything else. She didn't look happy about not saying it.

Leah felt the change on the far side of the room before she saw it. Edward, by the piano, with Bella in front of him. His eyes had gone unfocused, fixed on Harry, his head tilted a degree to one side. Listening.

Then Edward's face did something complicated.

"Pie," he said.

Harry's ear flicked. "Melktert. Custard tart, cinnamon on top. A woman in Pretoria taught me to make it."

"That's a picture," Edward said slowly. "Somebody painted a picture on a wall."

"It's a very good picture."

On the arm of a couch, Emmett snorted.

Edward didn't laugh. Leah had spent enough of the fall around this house, and a lot more of it in Jacob's head watching Edward through Jacob's eyes, to know his faces. This one was new. "I can feel you," Edward said. "Behind it. All of you. Every inch. You're right there." His voice had gone very quiet. "And I can't get through any of it."

"No," Harry agreed.

Edward's eyes moved off him. And came to her.

She felt it as a draft first. Cold air coming under a door, the way it came under the door of the Glacier View on a frosty night. It slid along the bottom of the door in her head, the one she'd built on motel carpet in October, and found the gap, and pressed at it, and slid off. She held still and kept her face blank and thought about nothing. About the snow on the river. About Esme's rug.

Edward's forehead creased. "She's quiet," he said, to nobody in particular. "Leah's never quiet."

"*Ha*," said Seth, from the kitchen doorway.

Then Edward leaned.

The draft became a shoulder against the door. Cold and steady and enormous, a weight like a car parked against it, and Leah's whole body went rigid. She pushed back. She'd never pushed back against anything in her head before; the pack came in and you let it, there was no pushing. She shoved with everything she had, and the door shuddered, and she felt it give a fraction of an inch at the frame.

Things came out through the gap. She felt them go. She couldn't stop them.

The tailgate of a truck in a frosted gravel lot. A motel bed with its slat gone. A door in Dawson City lying flat in the snow off its hinges, and the northern lights, and teeth closing on her collarbone—

"He *bit* you," Edward said, sharp and appalled.

Something happened.

She felt it through the mark, a jolt like a hand slapped flat on a table. Beside her, Harry stood exactly as he'd been standing, his face blank. Across the room, Edward Cullen jerked as if somebody had shouted directly into his ear, and took a step backward into the piano, and the piano gave a discordant jangle that rang through the whole silent room.

The weight on her door was gone. Leah slammed it shut and leaned on it from the inside, shaking.

"Stop trying to get into her head," Harry said.

He said it at the same volume he'd said *Some of you have red eyes*. Plainly. Like a man reading off a list.

"Do it again, and I'll go into yours," he said, "and we'll see what comes loose."

Nobody in the room breathed. Most of them didn't need to. It was still the loudest silence Leah had ever heard.

Bella moved. One second she was by the piano and the next she was square in front of Edward, a newborn crouch, her lip peeled back off her teeth and a sound coming up out of her chest that made every wolf instinct Leah had stand up on its hind legs. Her eyes were the color of a coal going out.

Harry looked at her.

"Mrs. Cullen," he said. That was all.

"Bella," Edward said, low. He put a hand on her shoulder. "Bella, it's all right. He's — I'm all right." He was looking past her at Harry, and Leah had never in her life seen that look on Edward Cullen's face. As if he'd been walking down a hallway he'd walked down every day for ninety years and somebody had reached out of a wall and grabbed him. "That was you. In my head. You spoke."

"Ja."

"Nobody has ever—"

"I know."

"I needed to know what you are." Edward's voice was strained. "There's a man standing in my family's house I can't hear. My wife. My daughter. You understand I needed to—"

"Then ask me," said Harry. "I'm standing right here."

Edward's jaw worked. He didn't ask.

"Edward," Carlisle said, from behind them. Quiet. Leah had never heard him use that tone on one of his own.

Edward was silent a moment longer. Then he looked at Leah. "I apologize," he said stiffly. "That was inexcusable."

Leah didn't answer. She couldn't, yet. Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and her hands were shaking, and she realized only now that she was pressed against Harry's side from shoulder to hip, all of her, and his arm had come around her back with his paw spread flat between her shoulder blades. Hot. Heavy. Holding still.

She stayed there. In front of all of them. In front of every leech in the room and her Alpha and her little brother. She stayed there and let her breath come back.

"Since when," Seth said, from the doorway, in a voice like he'd just seen a dog walk on its hind legs, "do you let anybody—"

"Shut up, Seth."

Over on the couch arm, Emmett had both eyebrows halfway up his forehead. He looked at Edward, and then at Harry, and then back at Edward, and a slow grin spread over his face.

"Dude," he said to Edward, with deep personal satisfaction. "He's gonna eat you."

"Emmett," said Rosalie.

"What? He is."

Esme came out of the kitchen doorway, past Seth, drying her hands on the dish towel, as if nothing at all had happened in her living room that a clean towel couldn't handle. She looked at Harry, and up, and further up.

"Would anyone like to sit down?" she said. "Mr. Potter, I'm afraid most of my chairs are going to be too small for you."

"I'll stand, ma'am. Thank you."

"Esme."

"Esme," Harry said, and his ears came up. Both of them, all the way, for the first time since the porch.

Leah saw it. She filed it.

Carlisle came around to the front of the room and stood by the cold fireplace with his hands in his pockets. He looked tired, Leah thought. Which was stupid. Leeches didn't get tired. He looked it anyway.

"Mr. Potter," he said. "Please. Tell us why you're here."

Harry told them.

He didn't take long. There was a law, three hundred years old, that kept his kind out of human sight. A lot of governments had signed it. Somewhere in a building in Washington, D.C., the American one had looked at thirty vampires marching across a continent toward a town of three thousand people and asked for help, and the help was him. The Volturi were his target. His orders said *any and all means*.

"My job," he said, "is to make sure that whatever happens on Saturday happens where nobody in Forks sees it, hears it, or dies of it."

"And if the Volturi object to your job?" That was a dark-haired man by the glass wall, with a calm, careful face and gold eyes. Eleazar. Leah remembered him from Jacob's head: the Denali one who used to work for Italy.

"Then they'll have to take it up with me."

Eleazar studied him. He'd been studying him since the porch, Leah realized. Steady, patient, the way a doctor reads an X-ray.

"When someone with a gift walks into a room, I can usually see it," Eleazar said. "Its shape. Roughly how large. Yours I can't find the edges of." He tilted his head. "That might mean it's very large. It might mean something about you interferes with what I do. I wouldn't like to guess. I'd want to watch you use it first."

"Fair," said Harry.

The white-blonde woman on the stairs got up. She came down the last three steps, smiling, and held out her hand. "Kate," she said. "Since we're all being so friendly."

Harry took it.

Leah smelled it before she saw it: a crack of ozone, the sharp electric bite of it, and the fur on the back of Harry's paw stood straight up. The fur along his jaw rose. Kate's smile got wider.

Harry didn't let go.

Kate's smile faltered. She held on another second, two, and Leah watched her push harder — watched it in the set of her shoulders, the way you could see Seth bear down in an arm-wrestle — and then she pulled her hand back and shook it out at the wrist, frowning at it.

"Huh," Kate said.

"Huh," said the lanky guy on the banister, sitting up.

"You're keyed," Harry told her.

"I'm *what*?"

"Can you kill them?"

It came from the two papery men by the window. They'd moved forward without Leah seeing them do it. They stood side by side, both of them staring at Harry with their red eyes gone bright and hungry, and the one who'd spoken leaned forward. "Aro," he said. "Caius. The ones in the black. Can you kill them?"

"Yes," said Harry.

The freckled girl beside Siobhan was very still. "He believes that," she said.

The papery man smiled. It was the worst smile Leah had ever seen on anything.

"We don't want them dead," Carlisle said. "We want them to listen."

"Then we want the same thing," Harry said, "until they stop listening."

Edward spoke from the piano, carefully, the way you'd step onto ice. "You should know what they bring with them. Jane can make you feel as if you're burning alive. With a look."

"Range?"

"Line of sight."

"For how long?"

"As long as she likes."

Harry's ear flicked. "I've had the Cruciatus Curse from Lord Voldemort. In a graveyard. I was fourteen." He said it like somebody mentioning that he'd once had his tonsils out. "I'll manage Jane."

Nobody had anything to say to that. Leah watched them not say it.

"I can stop her," Bella said. It was the first thing she'd said since they walked in. "Jane. Alec. I can shield people from them. I've been practicing."

"Good," said Harry. "Shield the wolves."

"I'm going to shield my family."

"The wolves will be standing in front of your family."

Bella's eyes went hard and flat. Edward's hand tightened on her shoulder.

"And Aro," Edward said, a little too quickly. "Aro only needs to touch you. One touch, and he knows every thought you've ever had."

"Then he'll get pie," said Harry.

Over on the couch, Emmett laughed out loud, and Rosalie hit him in the arm.

Harry laid out the rest in five minutes. Three rings. The fence, and the net, and the bowl. The fire. He used the word Fiendfyre and explained it the way he'd explained it in the cabin, and Leah watched it land on them. Carlisle's face went very still. Esme's hand went to her mouth.

"You want to set fire to a clearing full of us," Rosalie said.

"I want to never have to. If I do, everybody I've keyed comes out of the bowl before the fire goes in." Harry held up a paw. "That's what the handshakes are for."

Emmett's head came up. "That's why you shook Seth's hand? I figured you were just polite."

"I'm also polite."

Emmett looked at him for a long moment. Then he got up off the arm of the couch, came across the rug, and stopped in front of Harry, a big man looking up at a bigger one.

"Okay," Emmett said. "So when it kicks off. Where do you want me?"

Harry's ears came up again.

"Front," he said. "Left of Carlisle. You'll hit things."

"I'm good at hitting things."

"I'd heard."

Emmett stuck his hand out. Harry shook it. Leah watched Emmett squeeze — really squeeze, his forearm cording, the grip that had cracked boulders in half for fun — and watched Harry's paw not move by so much as a hair.

Emmett let go and looked at his own hand. "Huh," he said. "Rematch. After."

"Bring money," said Harry.

He turned then, and looked at Bella across the room, and his ears went back down.

"Where's your father tonight, Mrs. Cullen?"

Bella went still. "At home."

"Alone?"

"With Sue."

Leah's whole body went tight. *Mom.*

"Two humans," Harry said, "fifteen minutes from a battlefield."

"Charlie doesn't know anything. He doesn't want to know. He's safe as long as he doesn't."

"The Volturi won't stop to ask him what he knows before they tidy up."

"I'm not sending my father away."

"My friend Hermione took every memory of herself out of her parents' heads," Harry said, "and put them on a plane to Australia, because a man was coming who'd have used them to get to her. She did it alone. In her kitchen. She was seventeen."

"That's not your business."

"Your father is a human being inside my operational area. He's my business whether you like it or not."

"My mom's with him," Leah said. It came out rough.

Harry didn't look away from Bella. "Then I'll ward the house tonight."

"You'll stay away from my father," Bella said.

The room had gone very quiet again. Carlisle opened his mouth, and shut it.

Harry let it sit. Then he looked past Bella to the couch, where Jacob had sat down with the kid on his knee, both arms around her, watching everything with his jaw set.

"One more," Harry said. "If she's going to be in that clearing on Saturday, the ward has to know her."

"No," said Bella.

"No way," said Jacob, at the same time.

The kid slid off Jacob's knee.

She did it before Jacob could tighten his arms, quick and neat, and landed on Esme's rug in her little red coat. She looked like a small two-year-old, Leah thought, or a big one-year-old. Her walk belonged to somebody much older. She crossed the room to Harry the way a grown woman would cross a room at a party, straight-backed, sure, her bronze curls bouncing, and the whole room watched her do it. Leah could hear her heart. It went like a bird's.

She stopped in front of Harry and held up her hand.

Harry didn't move for a long moment. Then he crouched, the crease of his good trousers pulling tight over his knees, until his face was level with hers.

Renesmee put her palm flat on the side of his muzzle.

Leah felt it through the mark. A drop, cold and sudden, in the bottom of her stomach. His. The first time she'd ever felt anything from him that clearly, a fear so sharp and so specific it took her breath away. Harry's ears went flat. His tail lay dead on the rug. His eyes went somewhere else, wide and green and fixed on nothing, for one second, two, three.

Then he came back. He looked at the kid.

"Because I don't know what you are yet," he said quietly.

Renesmee studied him with Bella's old brown eyes. Then she took her hand back.

"Okay," she said. Her voice was clear and high and perfectly pronounced, like a bell somebody had taught to talk.

Bella crossed the room so fast she blurred, and scooped her daughter up, and held her against her chest, and stood over Harry with her red-gold eyes blazing.

"Get out of my house," Bella said.

"Bella," Esme said softly.

"It isn't—" Carlisle began.

"I'm going." Harry stood up. "I've got a clearing to fence."

Leah was beside him before he'd finished straightening. She didn't remember crossing the space. He went for the door, and she went with him, and the whole room watched them go.

Esme met them in the hall. She had a casserole dish wrapped in foil and a dish towel, and she pushed it into Leah's hands without asking. "Lasagna," she said. "There's more. Come back for supper. Both of you."

"Thank you, Esme," said Harry.

On the porch, Leah sat on the top step and laced up her boots with the dish balanced on her knees. Carlisle had followed them out. He stood at the rail with his hands in his pockets, looking out at the snow on the lawn, at Harry's broad pawprints going down the steps.

"You frightened my family, Mr. Potter," he said.

"Ja. I'm sorry about that."

Carlisle looked at him. "Are you?"

Harry thought about it. "About some of it," he said.

### VI.

By seven o'clock it was full dark in the clearing, and the snow was still coming down.

They sat on the boulder with the runes in it and ate Esme's lasagna out of the dish with two forks Harry produced from somewhere. He ate most of it. Leah let him. She'd lost her appetite somewhere around *get out of my house*, and found it again around the third bite, and lost it again thinking about her mother sitting in Charlie Swan's living room with the TV on, fifteen minutes from here, with no idea her daughter was back.

Harry had taken the jacket off and folded it over a branch, out of the snow. He sat in his white shirt with the sleeves rolled up, and the snow landed on his forearms and melted and ran off the fur.

"What did she show you?" Leah said.

He didn't answer right away. He scraped the last of the cheese off the corner of the dish with his fork.

"Me," he said. "The way she sees me. Big. Dark. Ears flat." He set the fork down. "And a question. *Why are you scared of me?*"

Leah looked at him. "Are you?"

"Ja."

"She's a baby, Harry."

"She's four months old, and she talks like a grown woman, and she can put her hand on your face and push pictures into your head through a wall I've spent ten years building." His ear flicked. "That's part of it."

"What's the rest?"

He was quiet a moment. Out in the dark, snow slid off a branch with a soft sound.

"Everybody in that house loves her," he said. "Your brother. Your Alpha. Two old men from Romania who don't love anything. People flew in from Ireland and Egypt to find out whether she's the thing the Volturi say she is, and took one look at her and decided they'd die for her instead. I watched every face in that room go soft when she walked across the rug. Siobhan's. The Romanians'. Even your brother's, and he sees her every day." He looked down at his paws. "I felt it when we came round the bend in the drive. Like a hand on the back of my neck. Wanting me to love her too."

Leah went cold all over.

"Like an imprint," she said.

"Like something that picks for you."

She thought about Jacob on the couch with both arms around the kid. About Sam, coming out of the woods after two weeks and looking at Emily, and not at her. She'd spent a year and a half hating the thing that did that. Hating it so hard it felt like the only thing holding her together some days.

"I don't think she means to," Harry said. "I don't think she knows she's doing it. Maybe it's how something like her survives." His ears went back. "It's still a hand on the back of my neck."

"So you hate her."

He picked the fork back up, looked at it, and put it down again. "Thirty people are coming a very long way to kill a baby because of what she is." His voice had gone very level. "I know a bit about that."

Leah opened her mouth, and shut it.

A cot. A house in a village somewhere in England. A man with no nose coming up the stairs. He'd told her that part in the truck, in the dark, on a long straight highway outside Fort Nelson, with the tape deck turned down so low she could hear the tires on the road.

"So you're scared of her," she said slowly, "and you're going to keep her alive anyway."

"Ja."

"That's stupid."

"Ja," Harry agreed.

She leaned her shoulder against his. He let her.

"You like Esme," she said, after a while.

"Ja."

"And Carlisle."

"He's been fighting what he is for three hundred years, and he's winning. I knew a man like that, once. Best teacher I ever had."

"And Emmett. God help us all."

"He asked me where to stand." His tail moved, once, in the snow. "Nobody else in that room asked me anything useful."

"You don't like Bella."

"No."

She waited.

"Your brother's collarbone," Harry said. "And her father. She's spent four months working out how her daughter gets out of this alive. I'd bet my truck she hasn't spent an afternoon working out how he does." He looked at her sideways. "And she looked at you in there like you were something on her shoe."

Leah hadn't noticed that. She'd been too busy noticing everything else. She found she didn't care.

"My mom's with him," she said.

"I know. Charlie's house tonight, after the net. Then Seattle at midnight. Then I start on the fence."

"When do you sleep?"

"Sunday."

He got up. He went to the boulder's carved face and laid both paws flat on the runes, the way he'd laid them on the snow that afternoon, and his ears came up and forward and went very still.

The ground hummed.

This time it kept going. It went out from under her boots in a long rolling wave, out across the clearing and into the trees, and she felt it go through her like warm water poured down her back. The snow on the ground lit up. A ring of it, a hundred yards out, glittering faintly blue-white all the way around the clearing in a perfect circle, and then the ring went on out past the trees where she couldn't see it, and the glitter faded. The mark under her collarbone sang so loud she pressed her palm flat against it through the wool.

Every bird in the forest had gone quiet. The snow kept falling.

Harry stood with his paws on the stone and his eyes shut.

"Your brother's on the porch," he said. "Eating more lasagna. Jacob's on the steps beside him. Edward's standing at the glass wall, staring at the trees in this direction." A pause. "Bella's in the cottage. With the baby."

Then his ears swiveled north, hard.

"One of them's leaving," he said.

"Who?"

"The one in the attic. He just went out a window." His muzzle twitched. "Running north. Fast. He's not coming back."

"Good riddance."

"One less witness." Harry's ears went back. "I'd guess that's on me."

Leah didn't argue. She stood by the boulder with the snow landing in her hair and the hum of the net still going through her bones, and she looked at him: his paws on the stone, his eyes shut, his whole body tipped toward the forest, listening to twenty-six cold things and a bird-hearted baby and sixteen wolves and her mother's living room fifteen minutes away.

"Can you feel me?" she said. "Through it?"

His ears came down. He didn't open his eyes.

"Ja," he said.

"Through the ward?"

"Through the bite." A long pause. "Since Dawson City."

She'd known. She'd known since a garden in London, with the rain coming down and Hermione's white face, and she'd been waiting six hours to hear him say it, and it still went into her like a fist.

"You told me it was only me," she said.

"I put it in a room."

"Why?"

"You didn't choose it." He opened his eyes. "I didn't want you carrying me as well."

"That's my call, Harry."

"Ja," he said. "It is."

"Then open the door."

He looked at her for a long moment, across the snow, with the green of his eyes catching what light there was.

"It's open," he said.

And it came. All of it, down the bond and into her chest at once, the way the pack came, except this time it was only him. The weight of Saturday sitting on him like a stone. His fear, for her brother, for her, for a baby he couldn't stand to look at. Tiredness, down deep, older than she'd guessed. And under all of it, steady and hot and absolutely certain, the same thing her wolf had been howling at the stars for three hours outside a cabin in the Yukon.

It matched. Exactly. The way a wolf's hind paw comes down in the print of its fore paw.

Leah stood there in the snow and couldn't say anything for a while.

"Shut up, Harry," she said finally.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were feeling it loudly."

His tail moved in the snow behind him. Slow. Then not so slow.

The air in the clearing had changed. She noticed it now, standing still. It had gone thin and sharp and metallic, the bitter electric bite of it stinging the inside of her nose, like the air off First Beach in the last second before the storm hit. It came off the net. It came off him. It hung over the whole white bowl of the clearing like a held breath.

Her dad would have been up out of his lawn chair by now.

*Okay, kids. Everybody inside. Right now.*

Leah stayed where she was.
