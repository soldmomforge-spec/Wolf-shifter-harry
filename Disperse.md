# Disperse

---

Alec's mist came across the snow low and slow, the way fog lifts off a river before sunrise, except there was no river in the clearing and the sun had been up for hours behind a thin lid of white cloud. It came without wind. It came from the place where Alec stood at the edge of the Volturi's dark wedge, his small hands loose at his sides and his face as smooth and untroubled as a choirboy's, and it rolled forward in a line so straight it looked ruled onto the field. Where it passed, the snow lost its glitter. It went flat and dull under the grey, the way a page dulls when someone breathes on it.

Bella felt it arrive before she saw it touch.

She had the shield out as far as it would go. She had pushed it past the Cullens, past the Denalis and the Irish and the Egyptians, past Zafrina and Senna, past Garrett and Peter and Charlotte and Mary and Randall and the two Romanians standing a little apart with their papery faces turned toward Volterra's black cloaks like flowers toward a sun. She had pushed it over the wolves, every one of them, the whole long uneven line of fur and muscle that ran along the Cullens' flank and bent at the end toward the trees. It sat over all of them like a soap bubble, bowed and thin and stretched to the edge of what she could hold. Holding it felt like holding a rubber band across the width of a room with one finger, knowing that the instant her attention slipped it would snap back into her chest and leave everyone she loved standing in the open.

The mist touched the curve of the shield thirty yards out and stopped.

She felt it as a flat, even pressure, a hand laid against the other side of a window. It spread sideways along the curve, searching for an edge, a seam, a thin place. She didn't give it one. She thought about the shield the way Zafrina had taught her to think about it in the long grey afternoons of practice, as a thing with weight and shape, a thing she could lean on, and she leaned.

The mist pooled against nothing and began to pile up, rising in a slow grey wall that bulged and slid and found nowhere to go.

"Steady," Edward murmured. A human standing beside him would have heard only breath.

"I've got it."

She did. She thought she did. Jane had tried twice already, and both times Bella had felt it as a hot, quick flick against the shield, sharp and small and gone, like a moth hitting a lit bulb. The second time Jane's face had changed. The angelic little mask had cracked straight down the middle and something had snarled out of it, and for a moment Bella had felt a fierce, ugly joy at seeing it. That had been a minute ago. Nothing about this felt joyful now. Now Jane was standing very still beside her brother and staring across the snow at Bella with red eyes that didn't blink, and the mist was piling up against the shield, and somewhere behind the Volturi's front rank Aro, Caius and Marcus had their heads together, the white of Caius's hair bright against the black of their cloaks, deciding whether to kill everyone in the clearing.

Bella didn't look at Renesmee. She didn't need to look. She could hear her.

Renesmee's heart beat high and quick on Jacob's back, a little hummingbird flutter that Bella could have picked out of a stadium. Her small hands were fisted in the russet fur at the base of Jacob's neck. Her knees were tucked in against his shoulders. The backpack hung from her shoulders, heavy for her size, packed by Bella's own hands that morning: the money, the clothes, the passports J. Jenks had made out to Jacob Wolfe and Vanessa Wolfe. If it started, Jacob would run. That was the plan. That was the only plan that mattered. Jacob would run, and Bella would hold the shield over everyone else for as long as she was still in one piece to hold it, and Renesmee would be somewhere far away by the time it ended.

Jacob's flank pressed against Bella's hip, solid and hot through her clothes. A low, constant vibration came through his ribs. He was growling so quietly she could feel it more than hear it.

*Alice,* Bella thought, which she had been thinking all morning. *Alice, where are you.*

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered for weeks.

Across the field, the witnesses the Volturi had brought muttered among themselves in a dozen languages, the sound like wind in dry grass. Some of them had started to look uncomfortable after Irina. The smoke from Irina's pyre had thinned to nothing, but the smell of it lingered on the cold air, heavy and sweet, like incense left burning in a closed room. Bella tried not to breathe it. She tried not to think about Tanya's face when Caius had given the order, or the sound Kate had made, or the way Garrett and Edward had held Kate down in the snow while Zafrina blinded her and Tanya simply stood there and shook.

The mist climbed higher against the shield. It had reached the height of a man and was still rising.

Bella leaned.

The crack came from the middle of the field.

It was a short, flat sound, the snap of a thick branch under ice, and loud enough that Bella flinched. Every head in the clearing turned. On both sides. All at once. The mist stopped moving, hanging in its grey wall like a held breath.

There was a man standing in the snow halfway between the two lines.

Bella had been looking at that patch of ground a quarter of a second before and it had been empty. There were no tracks leading to it. No one had crossed the field. There were just his boots, sunk an inch into the crust, and a ring of loose snow blown outward around them, as if he had been dropped from a height of nothing and the air had moved out of his way.

He had a heartbeat.

She heard it beneath everything else in the clearing, under the snarl of the wolves and the hiss of a hundred vampires drawing breath they didn't need. It was slow and heavy, fifty beats a minute or fewer, the steady thump of a big muscle that was in no hurry at all. Her throat tightened on reflex. Every human heartbeat she had heard since she woke had done that to her, every single one, an instant hot clench of thirst that she had learned to clamp down on and hold.

The smell of him reached her a half-second later on the cold air.

Earth. Turned earth, the kind under leaf litter. Pine resin. Something like river stones in a creek bed, cold and mineral. Under that, a dense animal note that her newborn instincts filed straight into the same drawer as the wolves and then, almost at once, pulled out again and turned over, puzzled. There was blood in it. She could smell the blood, the iron of it, moving under his skin. And her throat, which had burned at the scent of hikers two valleys away, which had burned at Charlie's heartbeat across a living room, didn't burn. It relaxed. It went quiet. Her body looked at this man with a pulse she could count from forty yards away and decided, firmly and without consulting her, that it did not want him.

Heat came off him. In the frozen air, the space above his shoulders wavered faintly, the way the air over a road wavers in August.

He was tall, six foot two at a guess, and broad through the shoulders and chest. He was dressed for a different kind of day. A scuffed brown leather jacket hung open over a grey henley pushed up at the forearms. Dark jeans gone pale at the knees. Heavy boots with the laces wrapped twice around the ankle and tied off at the front. No gloves, no hat, no scarf, in a field where the temperature was well below freezing. His hair was black and looked as though it had been cut by someone in a hurry and not touched since. A couple of days of dark stubble on his jaw. He might have been twenty-five.

He looked left, at the Volturi. He looked right, at the Cullens and their witnesses. He looked down the long line of wolves, and when his head turned Bella saw his eyes properly for the first time. They were green, a clear, bright green. Then he tipped his head back and looked up at the sky, squinting a little, the way Charlie squinted at the clouds before deciding whether it was worth taking the boat out.

"Morning," he said.

English. The accent was English, London or somewhere near it, roughened at the edges.

Edward's hand closed hard around Bella's. She heard him breathe in, and then she heard him say, so low it barely existed, "I can't hear him."

She turned her head a fraction.

"Nothing," Edward said. His voice had gone strange. "There's nothing there. It's like you. Only—" He stopped.

Down the line, one of Sam's wolves had started to growl, a deep, ragged sound, and somebody, Sam probably, cut it off with a sharp huff. The ripple of noise ran along the pack and died. At the far end of Jacob's small group, past Seth's sandy bulk, the slim grey wolf that was Leah had gone utterly still. Her ears were up and forward. Her head was low. She was staring at the man in the middle of the field as though someone had driven a nail through her and fixed her to the ground.

Edward's head turned toward the wolves by the smallest degree. Bella watched his face change.

"Oh," he breathed. "Oh, *Leah.*"

"What?"

"Later." He didn't take his eyes off the grey wolf. "I'll tell you later."

In the middle of the field, the man rubbed his palms together briskly, as if he'd just come in from the cold and wanted to get started on something.

"Right," he said. His voice carried in the still air the way voices carry over water. "Which one of you's in charge? And don't all put your hands up at once."

No one moved for one long second.

Then Aro stepped out of the Volturi's front rank, unhurried, with Renata gliding behind him so close her hand rested flat against the middle of his back. The black of his cloak swept the snow. He was smiling. His face had the soft, papery translucence of something very old that had been kept out of the light, and his smile creased it like tissue.

"That," Aro said, with every sign of pleasure, "would be myself. Or, more properly, myself and my brothers." He gestured behind him with one long hand without looking, at Caius standing rigid with his lip curled, at Marcus who hadn't lifted his chin. "And to whom do we owe the very great pleasure?"

The man reached into his jacket.

The whole clearing tensed. Bella felt it go through the shield like a shiver, through every one of them under it, forty-odd bodies drawing tight at once. Felix took half a step forward. Emmett, behind Bella, made a sound low in his chest.

The man took out a slim black leather wallet, flipped it open with one thumb, and held it up at shoulder height toward Aro. Something silver inside it caught the flat white light and seemed, at that distance, to shift and turn over by itself.

"Potter," he said. "Unspeakable. Department of Mysteries, on loan to the International Confederation of Wizards." He snapped the wallet shut and put it back inside his jacket. "That's wizards. With a W. Before anybody asks."

Someone on the Volturi side laughed. It was a short, ugly bark of a sound. Caius.

Aro's eyebrows rose in slow, delighted arcs. "Wizards," he repeated, and he said it the way a child says the name of a present it has been hoping for. "How extraordinary."

"Yeah, yeah. Hold your applause." The man, Potter, put his hands in his jacket pockets. "Here's how today goes. You lot"—a tip of his head toward the Volturi—"you lot"—a tip toward the Cullens—"and Scooby and the gang down there"—a glance along the wolves, and a fresh ripple of growling ran down the line and died again—"are about a hundred-odd non-humans standing in a field a few miles from a town full of Muggles, having a staring contest that's tripped four separate monitoring charms and given a bloke in Seattle who reads tea leaves for a living a nosebleed. That's a breach of the International Statute of Secrecy. So."

He straightened. His voice didn't get louder. It got flatter, and every word in it lined up.

"By order of the International Confederation of Wizards, this gathering is to disperse. Everybody turns round. Everybody walks away. Everybody goes home. Today. Now, ideally."

Silence.

Bella had no idea what a Muggle was. She had no idea what the International Confederation of Wizards was, or the Department of Mysteries, or what any of it meant. She found she was holding the shield so hard it creaked, the whole thin skin of it trembling in her mind. The mist piled against it had stopped climbing. Alec had turned his head and was looking at the man in the middle of the field with an expression of mild, polite interest, the way a boy looks at a beetle he hasn't seen before.

Edward's grip on her hand hadn't loosened at all.

Aro tilted his head to one side. The long dark hair moved against his cloak.

"Disperse," he said, tasting it.

And then he laughed.

---

Leah could not have said what was funny.

She could not have said much of anything. The bloodsucker in the black cloak was laughing, a high, pleased, musical sound that carried across the snow, and a hundred other sounds were going on around it—the hiss of breath, the creak of the ice crust, Seth's heart going like a drum somewhere off her left shoulder, the wet little click of Jacob's teeth—and she heard all of it with the perfect, useless clarity of the wolf, and none of it went anywhere. It hit the surface of her and slid off.

The whole world had moved, and she was still trying to find out where it had gone.

A minute ago she had been standing at the end of the line watching the cloaks. She had been doing it for an hour. Dark grey, charcoal, black at the heart of the wedge where the three old ones stood with their dead papery faces. She had been counting them and picking out the ones she'd go for first if it came to it, the big one with the stupid grin, the tracker, and she'd been thinking with the cold, steady part of herself that she was fast, faster than any of them on the pack's side, and that if Jacob broke and ran with the kid she'd run with him and cover his back, because somebody had to and Seth was too young to be the one who didn't come home.

Then the crack.

She had turned her head toward it the way every animal in the clearing had turned its head toward it. She had seen him standing there in the middle of the field in his beat-up jacket with his hands at his sides. And the air had moved, a cold slow drift from the west across the snow, and his smell had come in on it.

Earth. Wet stone. Pine sap, sharp and clean. And under those, so faint that only a wolf would have caught it, a thick dark musk that her body knew before her head did, that made her hackles lift and then lie down again in confusion. *Wolf.* Some other kind. There was none of the pack in it, none of Sam's sharp clean smell or Jacob's or Seth's. Something old and heavy and very, very big.

His eyes had come down the line of wolves. Bright green. They had touched hers for maybe a quarter of a second and moved on.

And the earth had let go of her.

That was the only way she could think of it, afterward, and she'd heard it described that way before, in other people's heads, and she'd hated it every time. Every rope that held her to anything had gone slack in the same instant. Mom. Seth. The pack. The rez. Her father's grave. Sam. The particular hot, bitter, comfortable weight of her own anger, which she had carried for two years like a stone in her stomach and had honestly come to rely on—gone, all of it, cut loose, and for half a heartbeat she had been floating, weightless, held to nothing at all.

Then one rope.

Thick. Steel-hard. From the middle of her chest straight across the snow to the middle of his. And the gravity of the whole planet had shifted and settled down there in the middle of that field, in a pair of scuffed boots, with its hands in its pockets.

*No,* Leah thought. *No, no, no, no—*

*Leah?* Seth, inside her head, bright and startled. *Leah, what's—*

And then she felt him see it. Felt the exact moment it landed in him, her little brother, because that was how it worked, because nothing was private, because everything she was went straight into every other wolf in Jacob's pack like water into sand.

*Whoa,* Seth said. *Whoa. WHOA. Leah—*

*Oh, no.* Jacob. Jacob, who knew exactly what this was because he'd done it himself four months ago, the second he laid eyes on the bloodsucker baby. *No. No no no. Leah, look at me. Look at—*

*Get out,* she snarled at both of them. *Get out of my head. GET OUT.*

*I can't get out, that's the whole—Leah. Leah, look at me.*

She couldn't. She could not take her eyes off him. She could see everything, the dark stubble along his jaw, the faint shine of old wear on the leather at his elbows, the way he stood with his weight settled and even on both feet. She could see that his breath didn't fog in the cold the way a human's should, and she could see a slight shimmer above his shoulders where the heat of him met the frozen air. All of it poured out of her and into them, every detail, every one of them going straight into Jacob and Seth like she was holding up a photograph.

*Is that—* Seth, faintly. *Is he the—did you just imprint on a* wizard*?*

*I don't know what he is.*

*He's a guy. He's an English guy in a field. He has a* badge*, Leah.*

*SHUT UP, SETH.*

*Both of you.* Jacob's voice dropped and thickened and turned into something else, something heavy that came down over her mind like a hand on the scruff of her neck. *Hold the line. Whatever happens. Nobody moves unless I say.*

The weight of it settled on her and stayed. She felt it in her legs. She felt it in her jaw. She stood where she was, because she had to.

In the middle of the field, the bloodsucker had stopped laughing.

"Forgive me," he said, with a little apologetic flutter of his hands. "Forgive me, Mr. Potter. That was unkind. It's only that in three thousand years I have had a great many people come before me and inform me of what I am to do. Kings. Popes. A great many soldiers. I find I have lost the knack of being told." He spread his hands. "And your Confederation, I'm afraid, is quite unknown to us. A rumour, perhaps. Something whispered in old books."

"Yeah," the man said. "That's sort of the point of us."

"Here, among our kind, *we* are the law. We keep the secret. That has been our task since before your Confederation, I suspect, was so much as a twinkle in a magician's eye." He tilted his head the other way. "But I am curious. I confess it. I am very curious. If you would be so good as to give me your hand, I believe we could clear up any misunderstanding in a moment."

He held out his own hand. Pale as paper. Long-fingered. The palm turned up.

The man looked at it.

"I know what your hand does, Count," he said. "Read your file on the flight over. It's about yay thick." He held his finger and thumb a good three inches apart. "So, no. Hands stay where they are."

Something passed over the old vampire's face. It was there and gone so fast that Leah, who was looking right at him, almost missed it. Then the smile was back, wider than before.

"My file," he said softly. "How marvellous."

"This is absurd." The other one, the one with white hair, came forward a step out of the wedge. His voice was thin and sharp and full of contempt. "Brother. He is one man. One. He has a *heartbeat.* We are wasting the morning listening to a performing mortal in a costume."

"It's a jacket, mate. You're the one in a dressing gown."

The white-haired one's lip pulled back off his teeth.

Leah saw the little blonde one move. She didn't move much. She turned her head a quarter-inch away from the shield and toward the man in the middle of the field, and that was all, and Leah knew exactly what it meant, because Bella and Edward had told the packs what Jane could do, what she'd done to Edward in Italy, what it looked like when it hit.

Leah's whole body jerked. Jacob's order held her, hard, but something in her had already lunged forward against it, every muscle, and she felt her claws drive down through the ice crust into the frozen dirt underneath.

*Leah—*

The man's jaw tightened.

That was all. His jaw tightened and a muscle jumped at the hinge of it, once. His shoulders rolled back under the leather like he was shrugging off rain. He turned his head and looked across the snow at Jane.

"Oi," he said. "Pack it in, Wednesday."

The little girl's face went blank with surprise.

Then it went hard. Her eyes narrowed to slits. Her lips peeled back off small, white, even teeth, and she leaned forward a fraction, and the air between her and the man seemed for a moment to tighten, the way air tightens before a storm.

He looked faintly bored.

"I said pack it in. I've had worse off a bloke with no nose, sweetheart. You're not even in the top ten."

Jane's mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

Beside her, her brother's mist had begun to move again. It peeled away from the curve of Bella's shield, where it had been piling up for minutes against nothing, and it drifted sideways across the field in a long, slow, sullen tongue. It slid around the man's boots. It climbed his shins. It rose in a soft grey column to his knees, his waist, his chest, until only his shoulders and his head stood clear of it, and Leah watched his face with her heart slamming so hard it shook her whole body.

He looked down at it.

"Dry ice," he said. "Very *Top of the Pops.*"

Then he looked back up at Alec, and kept looking at him, and nothing else happened at all.

The boy's smooth forehead creased. Just slightly. It was the first expression Leah had seen on his face all morning.

The mist hung there a moment longer. Then it drew back, slowly, reluctantly, slithering away over the snow toward the boy's feet, and the man stood where he'd been standing with his hands in his pockets and snow on his boots.

"Aro."

It was the third one who'd spoken. The one in the middle who hadn't lifted his head all morning, the one who'd looked to Leah like someone had stood a corpse up in a cloak and forgotten about it. He was looking up now. His dull red eyes had a tiny spark of something in them.

The black-haired one turned with a swirl of cloak, his face alight with interest. "Brother?"

"The stranger," the dead one said. His voice was dry as old leaves. "He is bound."

"Bound?"

"To the wolf." The red eyes moved, slow as a clock hand, and settled on Leah. "The grey one. The female." A pause. "It is a minute old. Less."

Leah felt every head in the clearing turn toward her.

Every single one. Vampires on both sides, the old ones and the guard and the witnesses and the Cullens and all the strangers they'd collected. Bella. Edward. And down the line, past Jacob, past the gap between the packs, she felt it when Sam's big black head swung around toward her, and she knew without looking that his dark eyes were fixed on her, and she wanted the snow to open and swallow her whole.

*Oh my God,* Seth whispered.

"I have not," the old dead one went on, still looking at her, "seen one go so deep. Not in all my years." And then, almost to himself, with something in his voice like the ghost of curiosity: "How very strange."

Out in the middle of the field the man's eyes flicked sideways to her. Just for a second. His brows drew together, a tiny crease, as though he'd heard someone say a word in a language he almost spoke. Then he looked away again, back at the old ones.

"Anyway," he said.

The black-haired one was practically glowing. "Mr. Potter. Mr. Potter, you must understand that I cannot simply leave. There is a matter here we have come a great distance to settle. A child." His hand drifted, light as a moth, in the direction of Jacob and the little girl clinging to his back, and Leah felt Jacob's growl climb through the pack-mind like a rockslide. "The law is ours. You have no standing among us. And so we will finish our business, I think, as we came here to finish it." His smile deepened. "And afterward, I should like very much to speak with you at greater length. In Volterra. You would be our honoured guest."

"Can't wait," the man said. "Hard pass."

"*Enough.*" The white-haired one again. He didn't even look at his brother. He snapped his fingers once over his shoulder, a sharp, contemptuous click. "Felix. Bring him."

The big one stepped out of the wedge.

He was huge, as big as Emmett, bigger, the cloak a dark heavy grey that swung around his calves. He was grinning. He rolled his neck as he came, one side and then the other, the way a fighter does walking into the ring, and he came slowly, savouring it, his boots crunching the crust.

"Come along, little wizard," he said. "Let's go for a walk."

Everything in Leah went up like a struck match.

She was moving before she knew it. She was half out of the line, her forelegs locked and her hindquarters bunched and every hair on her spine standing straight up, a snarl ripping out of her so loud and so ugly it hurt her own ears. Jacob's order held her. She felt it holding her like a chain bolted into the ground, felt her own body heave against it, and she didn't care, she *didn't care*, she was going to tear that grinning thing's head off its shoulders and—

*LEAH. HOLD.*

She held. She shook with it. She stood there with her lips peeled off her teeth and drool freezing on her muzzle and she *held*.

The man glanced at her.

"Easy, Lassie," he said. "I've got it."

Then he looked at Felix, who was ten yards away and closing.

"Last chance, Lurch. Turn round and walk off."

Felix laughed and kept coming.

The man took his right hand out of his pocket. He lifted it, not very high. About level with his hip.

He snapped his fingers.

They all burned.

All of them. All at once. Every single cloak in that wedge, from the three old ones in black at the centre down to the palest grey at the very back of the guard, went up in the same instant, as though someone had thrown a switch. The fire was white at the heart and blue at the edges, the colour of a welding torch, and so bright that Leah's eyes slammed shut against it on instinct and the light came through her eyelids anyway, red and stinging. The sound reached her a split-second later: a single deep *whump* that she felt in her ribs, like a gas ring lighting in a giant's kitchen, multiplied by thirty.

A wall of heat rolled across the clearing and hit her in the face.

She opened her eyes.

It was already over. That was the thing. It had taken a second, one, maybe less. The flames were simply gone. Where each cloak had been standing there was a heap of grey ash, slumping, still holding for a moment the rough shape of something that had stood upright—a shoulder, a lifted arm—before it crumbled and fell in on itself with a faint dry hiss. Under every heap, the snow had flashed straight to steam, and each pile of ash sat in its own black ring of wet, bare, smoking earth.

The big one, Felix, had been mid-stride. His ash had gone on without him. It lay sprayed across the snow in a long grey fan, the forward edge of it maybe three feet from the toes of the man's boots.

The smoke came up after.

It boiled up off the field in a thick, rolling, purple column, the colour of a bruise, and it climbed into the cold air and spread and flattened out under the cloud. The stench came with it. Sickly, sweet, cloying, like a church full of incense and something rotting under the floor, and Leah's whole muzzle wrinkled and her eyes ran and she sneezed, hard, and couldn't stop herself.

Small things glittered in the ash. Gold. The heavy pendants the old ones had worn around their necks, and the guards too, fallen where the necks had been, some of them half-melted and running into the dirt like candle wax.

Behind where the wedge had stood, the Volturi's witnesses had frozen.

Thirty or forty of them. Strangers in ordinary coats and boots, men and women with red eyes who'd been brought to watch the Cullens die. For maybe half a second they didn't move at all.

Then they ran.

Every one of them. All at once, in every direction, a blur of bodies too fast for human eyes, and the forest at the far side of the clearing exploded with the sound of their going, branches snapping, snow sliding off the boughs in great soft avalanches, a tree trunk splitting somewhere with a crack like a rifle. The noise went away into the woods in a long, ragged, spreading roar. It got fainter. It was gone.

The man watched them go.

"See?" he said, to nobody in particular. "That's dispersing. Gold stars all round."

He put his hand back in his pocket.

Leah stood with her claws buried in the frozen ground and her heart trying to climb out of her throat and the reek of burnt vampire in her nose, and the first clear thing she felt, the very first one, before the shock or the horror or the plain dumb animal fear of what she'd just seen him do—the first thing was relief.

Relief so huge her legs shook with it. Because the big one hadn't touched him.

She hated it. She hated it so much she could taste it.

The ash was still settling when Stefan understood that it was finished.

It came down out of the purple smoke in a fine grey sift, drifting, turning, as light as the snow it fell on. It settled on the trampled crust where the wedge had stood. It settled on the black wet rings of earth. Some of it came all the way across the field on the cold air and Stefan watched a single flake of it land on the sleeve of his coat, and sit there, and he looked at it for a long time.

He did not brush it off.

Fifteen centuries.

He had imagined this so many times that the imagining had worn grooves into him, the way water wears grooves into stone. In the first hundred years after the fall, when the castles were still smoking in their mountains and the smell of his brothers' ashes was still in his clothes, he had imagined it every night. Later it had become a ritual, a thing he and Vladimir did in the long dull centuries when there was nothing else to do. *How will it look,* one of them would say, and the other would answer. They had argued over the details for a thousand years. Whether Aro would beg. Whether Caius would curse them. Whether it would be fire or teeth, whether it would be quick or slow, whether they would be the ones to do it or merely the ones to watch. Vladimir had always wanted it slow. Stefan had always wanted Aro to understand, for one single second before the end, exactly who had done it and why.

He had never once imagined a stranger in a leather jacket snapping his fingers.

Aro had not understood anything. Stefan had been watching his face. He had been watching it all morning, every small expression, hungry for each one, and at the very last Aro's face had been delighted. Curious. Already planning, already reaching. Aro had died wanting something. He had died without knowing he was dying. There had been no time for him to know.

It had taken one second. Less.

"No," Vladimir said beside him.

He said it very quietly, in the old tongue, the one they had spoken when there were still kings in the mountains and they had been two of them. Stefan didn't turn his head. He didn't need to. He had stood beside Vladimir for so long that he could hear his face.

In the middle of the field, the Englishman had turned around.

He did it without any hurry. He turned his back on the ash and the smoke and the black rings of melt as though there were nothing behind him worth keeping an eye on, which, Stefan supposed, there was not. His hands were in his jacket pockets again. He looked along the Cullens' line, along all of them, the Cullens and the Denalis and the Irish and the Egyptians and the Amazons and the nomads and the two of them standing slightly apart at the end the way they always stood slightly apart. Then down along the wolves. Then back.

"Right," he said. "That goes for you lot, too. Disperse. Means *go away*. In case it's a language thing."

No one moved.

The carefully gathered silence of the Cullens' side held for a long moment. Then Carlisle took a step forward out of it, one step only, his hands open and in plain sight.

"Mr. Potter," he began. "If I might—"

The Englishman lifted one finger without taking his hand out of his pocket. The leather of the jacket tented over it.

"Hang on, Doc. In a sec."

*Don't you DARE.* Her mind came at him like a slap. *Don't you dare tell me how this goes. Don't you dare tell me it'll be FINE, that I'll understand, that it's a GIFT. I heard you. I heard every one of you. Sam, every single day, Jared mooning over Kim, Quil and his baby, YOU—you and your—*

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. He saw it in her head anyway, the way she saw everything in his: Nessie in Rosalie's arms, four months ago, a tiny bloody thing with brown eyes, and the gravity moving.

He didn't have an answer for that. There wasn't one.

And the worst of it, the thing that made his stomach knot up hard and cold, was that he could feel it in her. It ran under every furious word she threw at him, under every snarl, under the screaming. He'd felt it himself, so he'd have recognised it anywhere. A line. Thick as a cable. Running out of the middle of her straight across the trampled snow to that English guy standing in a ring of black melt with a cigarette in his mouth.

And every time she looked at him—and she couldn't stop looking at him—the whole world tipped a little further his way.

*It's okay,* Seth was saying. He was still dancing around her, crouching and bouncing on his front paws like he did when he was nine, and she kept snapping at his face, and he kept letting her. *Leah, it's okay, it's okay, it's—*

*It is NOT okay—*

*Okay, it's not okay, but it's—it's—Leah, he's kind of awesome?*

*SETH.*

*He did the lightning! From the SKY! Nobody can do that, Benjamin can't even do that, and he just—*

*He KILLED the Romanian guy!*

*The Romanian guy jumped him!*

*Both of you, SHUT UP.* Jacob put the Alpha into it, and they shut up. He felt them go quiet, felt the resentment boil off Leah and the guilty little cringe in Seth, and for one blessed second there was nothing inside his head except his own thoughts.

They weren't much help.

Thirty of them. Thirty-something Volturi. Aro and Caius and Marcus and Jane and Alec and Demetri and Felix and all the others, every cloak, every single one, and they were ash. Ash, right there in the snow, steaming, with gold necklaces melting in it. The thing they'd all been waiting for since Alice saw them coming. The thing they'd been dreading for months, preparing for, the thing that was supposed to kill every one of them and take Nessie and burn her. It had walked into the middle of the field this morning, and the English guy had snapped his fingers, and it was over. Just like that. It was over.

Jacob should have been happy.

He'd never been more scared in his life.

Because whatever had just done that to the Volturi was standing forty yards away from Nessie. It had just gone into Bella's head and torn its way through her shield, the shield that had stopped Jane cold, and it had come out again and said *Fuck me sideways* and lit a cigarette with its eyes. And now it was looking at all of them with an expression Jacob couldn't read at all, and it was reaching into its jacket.

His lips came back off his teeth. He couldn't help it.

What came out of the jacket was a mirror.

A little square one, about the size of a paperback, in a battered silver frame. The English guy flipped it open—it was hinged, like a compact—and held it up in front of his face, and tapped the glass twice with the knuckle of his first finger, the cigarette still burning between the other two.

"Sabine," he said.

A pause. The glass fogged. Then it cleared, and from where he was standing Jacob could make out a face in it. It was too small and too far away to see properly. A woman, dark-haired, holding something steaming.

"Potter." The voice came out of the mirror tinny and small, and Jacob had to strain to hear it over the rush of his own blood. It was French, or something like it, and it was very, very tired. "Tell me you are calling to say it is done."

"It's done."

"Bon. Good. Then I can go back to—"

"Well. Bits of it are done."

A long silence from the mirror.

"Which bits."

"Volturi refused to disperse. So, they've dispersed." He tipped the mirror sideways a little, and Jacob realised he was angling it so the woman could see the heaps of ash steaming in the snow behind him. "Bit more thoroughly than they'd planned on."

"*Merde.*" The voice was flat. "All of them?"

"All the ones in cloaks. Witnesses legged it, so I let 'em go."

"There is going to be a smoke column. The Americans are going to see a smoke column."

"There's a smoke column."

"*Merde,*" the woman said again, with feeling. "Fine. Fine. It was authorised. I will do the form. Is that everything?"

"Also, a Romanian jumped me."

"Is the Romanian alive?"

"Nope."

A sound out of the mirror that might have been someone setting a cup down, hard. "Is that everything, Potter?"

"No." The English guy took a drag on his cigarette and let the smoke out of the side of his mouth, away from the mirror, like a polite man would. "Got an unregistered hybrid here."

Jacob stopped breathing.

On his back, Nessie's fingers clenched tighter in his fur. Her heart sped up, just slightly, a tiny hitch in the rhythm, and he felt her lean forward until her cheek was pressed against the back of his neck.

"A hybrid of what?" said the mirror.

"Vampire and human. Cold One, specifically, the local sort. Dad's a vampire, mum was human at the time, mum's a vampire now. Long story, very messy, involves a straw." He looked across the snow, straight at Jacob, straight at Nessie on his back, and Jacob's whole body went stiff as a board. "She's about four months old. Looks about three. Growing fast. Has a heartbeat. Eats, I think. Does a thing where she touches your face and shows you pictures in your head."

"*Four months?*"

"I know."

"That's not—a Cold One cannot—they are *dead*, Potter, the body does not—"

"Yeah, well, apparently the body does. I've seen it. Seen it from the inside, actually." He winced slightly. "Wish I hadn't."

The woman in the mirror said something fast and vicious in French. Then: "Is it dangerous?"

The English guy looked at Nessie for a long moment.

Jacob felt it. He felt that green stare go across the snow and touch the little girl on his back, and his growl came up out of his chest by itself and kept on coming, low and steady and ugly, and he didn't care, he didn't care if the guy snapped his fingers at *him*. He'd take Nessie and run and the guy would have to catch him first.

"She's a toddler," the English guy said finally. "She's got a backpack on. It's bigger than she is."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting off me today." He drew on the cigarette again. The end of it glowed bright orange. "So. The question. Can I not just burn the whole place down?"

The world went white at the edges.

*Burn.*

Jacob was moving. He didn't decide to. His back legs bunched under him, his weight went forward and his head went low, and every muscle in his body was already uncoiling toward the trees, toward the far side of the field, toward anywhere that wasn't here—

*JACOB, NO.*

It was Leah.

She had never, in all the months since she'd come to him, put that kind of weight into a thought aimed at him. It hit him like a wall. He staggered, his claws skidding on the crust, and stopped.

*He won't,* Leah said. *He WON'T. He wouldn't.*

*You don't know that!* Jacob roared at her. *He just said it! You heard him! He said BURN—*

*I know he won't!*

*You've known him for five MINUTES—*

*I KNOW, Jacob!* Her mind was a wreck, a howling storm, fury and humiliation and horror and something else underneath all of it that was calm, that was absolutely certain, that sat in the middle of the storm like a stone. *I know! Okay? I KNOW! I can feel it! He's not going to hurt her! Don't ask me how I know, I just—I KNOW, and I HATE that I know, and if you run he'll think you're a threat, so DON'T RUN—*

Jacob stood there, shaking.

He could feel it. That was the trouble. He could feel it in her, the same way he'd felt the line. The certainty. It went all the way down in her, deeper than the anger, deeper than anything. She knew it the same way Jacob knew, without needing to think about it, that Nessie was asleep when she was asleep, or scared when she was scared, or hungry before she said so. Whatever the guy was, whatever he'd just said, Leah *knew*.

And there was something else.

It came up from the back of his head where the old stuff lived, the stuff that came with the wolf, the stuff that you didn't learn so much as remember. The oldest law they had. The one that didn't bend. *No wolf harms another wolf's imprint.* Not ever. Not for any reason. Not even if you hated them. It was why Paul could tease Quil all he liked about Claire but never so much as raise his voice near her. It was why Sam's pack hadn't been able to touch Nessie once Jacob had imprinted on her, even when they wanted to. It went deeper than the Alpha voice. It went deeper than anything.

Jacob looked across the snow at the guy with the cigarette and the mirror.

He couldn't touch him. None of them could. Not Jacob, not Seth, not Sam or Paul or anyone in either pack. Whatever he did, whatever he said, whatever he decided to do to Nessie—

*Oh, God,* Jacob thought. *Oh, God, Leah.*

*I know,* she said. *I KNOW. You think I don't know?*

Out in the field, the mirror was talking again.

"—absolutely not, Potter. *Non.* What is wrong with you?"

"Just asking."

"You are not *just asking*, you are never *just asking*. The answer is no. You are not to burn anything else. You are not to burn the hybrid, you are not to burn the hybrid's family, you are not to burn the field, you are not to burn the town. You will stay where you are and you will not touch anything and the Registry will come and do the registration. Do you understand me?"

"Hypothetically, though—"

"*POTTER.*"

"Fine. Fine." He sounded faintly disappointed. It took Jacob a second to work out what about, the way it takes a second to work out a word in a language you only half speak. The guy's eyes had left Nessie and gone around the whole clearing, over the ash and the steam and the scar, the wolves milling about, the vampires standing frozen in their line, and his face was the face of a guy who'd come in to do a two-hour job and found the whole house was rotten. "Just thought I'd ask. Would've saved a lot of paperwork."

"It would have saved *you* a lot of paperwork. It would have created a great deal of paperwork for *me*." The voice sighed. "The Registry team is in Vancouver. They will be twenty minutes. MACUSA wants someone on it, too, since it is their soil, so it will be thirty minutes, because they will have to argue about who goes."

"Brilliant. Yanks."

"Be nice to the Yanks."

"I'm always nice."

"You are never nice. Twenty minutes, Potter. Do not move. Do not burn. Do not *smoke* on camera."

"There's no camera."

"There is always a camera. Twenty minutes."

The glass fogged and went blank.

The English guy looked at it for a moment. Then he snapped it shut, slid it back into the inside pocket of his jacket, and took one last long drag on the cigarette. He looked at what was left of it. Then he let go of it and it didn't fall; it just vanished, gone out of his fingers between one blink and the next, smoke and ember and filter and all. As if it had never been there.

"Twenty minutes," he said to the clearing at large. "Make yourselves comfortable."

Nobody moved.

On Jacob's back, Nessie stirred. Her fingers loosened in his ruff, and she sat up, and he felt her small warm hand come up to rest against the side of his neck, flat against the fur.

A picture bloomed in his head. It came softer than the pack-mind ever did, and brighter, and it came with a feeling attached like a ribbon on a present: the English guy, the way Nessie had seen him from up here on Jacob's back. Tall, in his jacket. The bright little spark at the end of the cigarette. The grey ribbon of smoke curling up into the cold. The way he'd said *She's a toddler* and *She's got a backpack on* like he was arguing on her side. And under the picture, the feeling. Curiosity, mostly. Wary, careful, but more curious than scared.

*Who's that man, Jake?*

He couldn't answer her, not like this. He made a soft sound in his throat instead, the one he made when she couldn't sleep.

*He's funny,* said the picture, firmly. *He talks funny.*

From the grey wolf at the end of the line came a strangled whine. Leah had seen it through him. Of course she had.

*Great,* she said bitterly. *Now the baby likes him.*

*Leah—*

*Don't.* She'd stopped snapping at Seth. She'd stopped moving at all. She was crouched low in the snow at the edge of the trees, as far from the field as she could get without actually leaving it, which Jacob knew—because he could feel it, because he felt everything—was exactly as far as the line would let her go. *Just don't. Don't say anything. Don't think anything. Please.*

He didn't. He couldn't think of a single thing to think.

Across the field, Carlisle had started walking.

---

Carlisle walked the way he would have walked into a room where a man was holding a gun.

He kept his hands open and away from his body. He kept his pace slow, a human's pace, and his path straight, so that there could be no question at any point of where he was going or what he meant to do when he got there. Behind him he could hear Esme not saying his name. He could hear Edward murmuring to Bella, low and steady and over and over, a stream of quiet words, and Bella not answering. He could hear Rosalie's teeth.

As he passed the end of the Denalis' line, Eleazar said very softly, without turning his head, "Carlisle."

Carlisle didn't stop. "Yes?"

"I can't see what he is." Eleazar's voice was strained. In the three hundred years Carlisle had known him he had never once heard Eleazar say those words. "I've been trying since he arrived. There's something there. It's enormous. But it won't hold a shape. Every time I look at it, it's a different size." A pause. "Be careful."

"I intend to be."

He walked on, out into the open snow.

The Englishman watched him come. He had his hands back in his pockets. He didn't move, apart from his eyes, which followed Carlisle the whole way. When Carlisle reached the edge of the black star-shaped scar he stopped, there, without stepping onto it. The fused earth at its centre was still giving off a faint wisp of steam. Up close, it smelled like a struck flint.

Up close, too, Carlisle could hear the man's heart properly. Forty-eight beats a minute, strong and even. The kind of resting rate he'd have expected from a long-distance runner, or a man who was very, very calm. The heat coming off him was perceptible even at six feet—he guessed the man's skin would run at well over a hundred degrees—and still his breath didn't cloud in the cold. Carlisle filed that, as he had been filing everything since the crack, in the part of his mind that never stopped taking notes.

The man's hands, when he took one out of his pocket to scratch his jaw, were large and square and entirely unmarked. No scars. No calluses that Carlisle could see. Not so much as a scraped knuckle.

"Doc," the man said.

"Carlisle Cullen."

"Yeah. I know. You're in the file." He tilted his head. "Physician, Forks Community Hospital. Three hundred and something. Nice house. Vegetarian." A beat. "Your lot's file is a lot thinner than Count Chocula's was. Didn't mention any grandkids, for a start."

Carlisle let that pass. "May I ask your full name?"

"Potter. Harry Potter." He said it the way people say the names of their dentists. "And before you ask, no, I'm not going to explain the Department of Mysteries, because I'm not allowed, and the ICW's dead boring, and I'm not the talky one anyway. Registry'll be along in twenty minutes. They're the talky ones. Ask them."

"I don't want to ask about the Department of Mysteries," Carlisle said. "I want to ask about my granddaughter."

The green eyes rested on him for a moment.

"Go on, then."

"You said the words *burn the whole place down*."

"I did, yeah."

"Within earshot of her. Within earshot of her mother, and her father, and the wolf who would give his life for her without hesitating." Carlisle's voice stayed level. He made sure of it. Three and a half centuries of practice went into making sure of it. "So I'll ask you plainly, Mr. Potter. What is going to happen to her?"

The man looked at him a moment longer. Then he blew out a breath through his nose, something that was almost a laugh, and looked away across the field at the russet wolf with the little girl on its back.

"Registry'll want a look at her," he said. "Height, weight. Blood sample, probably, if you'll let them. They'll want to know what she eats and how fast she's growing and what that hand-on-the-face trick does. They'll write all that down in a big book. She'll get a number. Somebody'll want to check in on her once a year, maybe, see how she's getting on. Maybe twice, while she's growing that fast. And that's it." He looked back at Carlisle. "That's all. Nobody's taking her anywhere. Nobody's putting her in a box. And nobody, Doc, is burning your granddaughter."

"Then what did you mean?"

"I meant the paperwork." He gestured vaguely at the ash heaps behind him, the steam, the scar, the whole wrecked white field. "I came out here this morning to tell a bunch of Italians in bathrobes to go home. Should've taken twenty minutes. Instead I've got thirty-odd dead Volturi, one dead Romanian, one unregistered half-vampire toddler, one shield that'd make the Department weep with envy, and two packs of shapeshifters that aren't in the right section of the Registry, and every one of those is a form. Some of them are three forms. I wanted to burn the *paperwork.*" He paused. "And the field, maybe. A bit. On principle."

Behind Carlisle, quite clearly, a girl's voice said, "He's telling the truth."

The man's eyes went past Carlisle's shoulder.

Carlisle didn't turn. He didn't need to. He'd have known Maggie's voice anywhere by now, after these last weeks, light and Irish and very certain.

"All of it," Maggie went on. "Every word. About the little one. He means it."

"Who's that?" the man said.

"Maggie. Of the Irish coven." Carlisle let himself, very slightly, relax. Not much. A degree. "She can tell when someone's lying."

The man's eyebrows went up. "Can she? Handy." He raised his voice a fraction, aimed past Carlisle. "Want a job, Maggie? Pay's rubbish, but there's a pension."

"No, thank you," Maggie said, after a startled second.

"Worth asking."

Carlisle studied him. He had spent three centuries watching faces, a good part of them across operating tables, where faces told you things the body was trying to hide, and this one was harder to read than most. It gave very little away. It looked tired, mostly. Tired in the way that the young doctors looked tired at four in the morning in Accident and Emergency, when they'd been on their feet since the day before and had stopped expecting anything that came through the door to be simple.

"You went into Bella's mind," Carlisle said quietly.

"Yeah."

"By force."

"Yeah."

"Her shield is the one thing she has that's entirely hers. She's never been able to let anyone in. Not even her husband. And you tore it open in front of everyone she's ever cared about." He didn't raise his voice. "That was cruel."

The man looked across at Bella, still kneeling in the snow, Edward's arms around her. Her head was up now. Her hands had come down from her face. She was looking straight back across the field at the man, and the shield was up again over the Cullens' side—Carlisle could tell from the way Edward kept glancing at her, quick and helpless, as though he were listening hard for something that had gone quiet again.

"It wasn't polite," the man agreed. "Shield'll be fine. I felt it go back. It's already knitting." He shrugged. "Wanted to know who the kid was. Wanted to know fast. Didn't fancy forty minutes of your family deciding which bits of the truth I was allowed. So I went and looked."

"You could have asked."

"Could've. Didn't."

Carlisle considered that. He found, to his own faint surprise, that he believed him: that it had been a choice and he knew it had been a choice, and he wasn't going to pretend otherwise. It wasn't much. But it was something.

"And Leah?" Carlisle said.

The man's face did something.

It was brief. A tightening around the eyes, a flicker of the jaw. The look of a man who has just been reminded, in the middle of a crowded room, that he left the gas on at home.

"Don't," he said.

"I only—"

"No, Doc. Seriously. Don't." He took his other hand out of his pocket and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "I've got twenty minutes. Well, eighteen now. And I've got a problem, and I'd like to spend them looking it up instead of talking about it. So if you don't mind."

He held his hand out to his right, at shoulder height, palm up, as though he were waiting for someone to put something in it.

Something did.

There was no sound. There was no flash. One moment his hand was empty and the next it was full of a book: a great, heavy, ugly slab of a thing, as wide as a paving stone and nearly as thick as Carlisle's forearm, bound in cracked brown leather with brass caps on every corner. The weight of it dropped his arm an inch. He let it drop the rest of the way and the book fell into the snow at his feet with a heavy, muffled *whump*, sending up a little puff of white.

Carlisle read the spine before he could stop himself. Gold letters, half rubbed away.

*BONDS OF SOUL AND SHAPE. A Comparative Compendium of Mate-Bonds, Soul-Bonds, Familiar-Bonds, Life-Debts, Blood-Ties, and the Shifter-Ties of the Known Magical World. With Appendices.* And underneath, smaller: *Vol. I. Aardvark Familiars to Kitsune.*

"Vol. One?" Carlisle heard himself say.

"Vol. Two's in Geneva. Hopefully it's the first one." The man flicked two fingers at the snow beside him. A chair appeared there. It was a cheap folding camping chair, green canvas faded almost grey, with a cup-holder in one arm and a split in the seat that someone had mended with silver tape. He sat in it. He hauled the book up out of the snow and onto his knees, where it lay across both his thighs and overhung them on either side, and he opened it somewhere near the middle with a crack of old leather and a smell of dust and something like dried herbs.

Carlisle stood there in the snow and looked at him.

Three hundred years he had spent reading. He had read in the great libraries of Europe, in monasteries and universities, in the private studies of men who were afterwards burned for owning what they owned. He had searched every page he could find for a hint of what his kind were and where they had come from and whether there was anything else in the dark beside them. And he had found nothing. Rumours. Peasant stories. Garlic and crosses. Not one word about a book like this.

A whole world. Libraries of it. With appendices.

"I wonder," Carlisle said, before he could stop himself, "whether there might be a volume concerning—"

"There's about six hundred volumes concerning, Doc. Ask the Registry." The man licked his thumb and turned a page. The paper was thick and creamy and crowded edge to edge with tiny black handwriting and diagrams. "Aardvark familiars. Nope." Another page. "*Animagus*-bonded pairs. Nope." Another. "Basilisk. Why is there a—*why* is there a section on basilisk bonds, who's bonding with a—" He shook his head and turned three pages at once.

From the corner of his eye Carlisle saw that Emmett had sat down in the snow, quite deliberately, cross-legged, with his elbows on his knees and his chin on his fists, like a man settling in to watch a game. Rosalie was standing over him with her arms folded, and she didn't tell him to get up.

On the far side of the field, the grey wolf had crept a few yards nearer.

Carlisle didn't think she'd meant to. She was still crouched low, her belly almost brushing the snow, her ears flat to her skull. Every line of her body said *away*, and yet she had moved forward, a yard, two yards, out of the shelter of the trees. Seth was following behind her at a careful distance, whining softly. And as Carlisle watched, she seemed to realise what she'd done; she froze, and her lips peeled back from her teeth, and she began very slowly to back up again.

The man in the chair turned another page without looking up.

"Doc."

"Yes?"

"You're blocking my light."

Carlisle looked up at the sky. The clouds had closed again, completely. There was no light to block, beyond the thin, flat, shadowless white that lay over everything in the clearing equally.

He stepped aside anyway.

The book smelled like the back room of Flourish and Blotts. Dust and old glue and dried sage. Harry had had it out of the Department's stacks on a long-term loan for three years and had opened it twice, both times for other people's problems, and both times he'd found what he wanted inside five minutes.

He'd been at it for six now.

*Augurey-bonds (prophetic).* No. *Blood-ties (goblin-made).* No, and nobody in their right mind would. *Boggart-imprinting.* That one he read twice, because the heading made no sense, and then he read the paragraph under it and decided it still made no sense and that he'd be raising it with somebody in Volume Two's editorial office. *Centaur herd-bonds.* *Changeling claims.* *Dragon-keeper's ties*, with a woodcut of a man in a singed apron looking smug. No. No. No.

He turned the page.

*Familiar-bonds (reciprocal).*

He slowed down for that one. A two-way bond, both ends live, each party able to feel the other. Formed on sight, sometimes. Lasting for life. He ran his finger down the column of tiny cramped handwriting, and his finger stopped halfway down, at the line that said *the familiar party being, in every recorded instance, non-sentient or partially sentient (see Kneazle, Crup, Owl)*, and he took his finger off the page.

Lassie was a lot of things, by the look of her. Partially sentient wasn't one of them.

He turned the page.

*Fated mates (lycanthropic).* He snorted out loud at that one before he'd even read it, and when he did read it, it said exactly what he'd thought it would say. *Lycanthropes do not form fated or mate-bonds of any kind; reports to the contrary derive entirely from romantic literature of the most unreliable sort. The reader is referred to* Moonlit Hearts *(1887) and its many sequels only as a cautionary example.* Somebody in the margin, in faded green ink, had written *Ha!*

Remus would have laughed himself sick at all this. At the whole morning. At the chair and the book and the pack of wolves gone to pieces over one of them looking at him funny. He'd have sat in the snow next to Emmett with his chin on his knees and laughed till he cried.

Harry turned the page a bit harder than he needed to.

Across the field, the Cullens had stopped pretending they weren't watching him. Carlisle was standing a respectful few yards off to his left, with his hands clasped behind his back and his head tilted, very obviously trying to read the book upside down. Emmett was still sitting in the snow. Bella was on her feet now, with Edward's hand at the small of her back, and she was watching Harry with an expression he'd have called murderous if he'd seen it on anybody who wasn't currently holding a shield over two dozen vampires and a pack of wolves. The rest of them stood about in little clumps, the Irish and the Egyptians and the rest, talking in low voices they thought he couldn't hear. He could hear all of them. The Egyptian boy was still telling anyone who'd listen that it hadn't been him.

He turned to I.

*Imprinting.*

There was a whole entry. He sat up a bit.

*Imprinting (Quileute). Reported among a hereditary shape-shifting lineage of the Quileute people of the Olympic Peninsula, North America. The bond is said to form instantaneously, upon first sight, and to be* directional*—that is, running from the shifter to the object, the object experiencing no corresponding compulsion. The object is in all reported cases non-magical. Strength: considerable. Duration: for the life of the shifter. Purpose: unknown; local tradition suggests it serves the continuation of the line. Insufficient data. See local sources.*

That was it. That was the whole thing. Five lines.

He read it again.

*Directional.* Running from the shifter to the object. The object experiencing no corresponding compulsion.

He thought about the line sitting under his breastbone, heavy and settled, humming faintly like a power cable. He thought about how it had felt, five minutes ago, when the big Volturi bloke had walked at him and the grey wolf had come half out of the line to go through Felix to get to him, and how something at the bottom of Harry had sat up and taken notice and been very, very pleased about it.

*The object experiencing no corresponding compulsion.*

"Thanks for that," he said to the book. "Very helpful. Ten out of ten."

*The object is in all reported cases non-magical.*

He kept going. He went through to the end of the volume, through *Jarvey* and *Jobberknoll* and a short, alarmed section on *Kappa* that seemed to have been written by somebody who'd been bitten by one, all the way to *Kitsune*, which ran to eleven pages and had illustrations, and none of it was what he was looking for. He flipped to the index at the back. It was in a different, smaller hand, faded nearly to nothing. He ran his finger down it.

*Bonds, involuntary.* See Vol. II.

*Bonds, involving more than one form of the same party.* See Vol. II.

*Bonds, reaching the beast.* See Vol. II.

*Bonds, between shifters of differing kinds.* See Vol. II. And below it, in the same green ink as the *Ha!*, someone had added in tiny letters: *(if any).*

"Course it is," Harry said. "Course it bloody is."

He sat back in the camping chair and let the book lie open across his knees, and looked up at the white sky.

He knew what Volume Two would say. He didn't need to send to Geneva for it. Volume Two would have a lot of very interesting entries on a lot of very interesting bonds, and every one of them would be something that happened to somebody else, in a way that made sense, that some wizard in a tower somewhere had written down three hundred years ago. And none of them would be a bond that came in from outside through walls he'd built himself and went through the heart like the heart was a lamp-post, and down through his magic, and all the way to the bottom where the wolf lived. And the wolf reached up and took the rope in its teeth.

None of them would be a Quileute imprint, which was supposed to run one way, landing on a bloke who had something inside him that grabbed the other end and pulled.

There wouldn't be an entry for that. There never was. He'd been a special case since he was fifteen months old, lying in a cot in Godric's Hollow, and in all the years since nobody had ever once written him into a book that was any use.

He let out a long breath through his nose.

"Never anything normal," he said, to nobody. "Not once. Not one bloody time."

When he looked down from the sky, the grey wolf was close.

She'd done it again. He hadn't been watching her, and she'd crept up out of the trees while he was reading, a yard at a time, the way she'd been doing it on and off since he called the Registry. Except this time she hadn't stopped. She was fifteen yards away now, maybe less, out in the open snow on her own with no trees at her back and her brother hanging behind her at a nervous distance, whining. She'd stopped creeping. She was standing up straight. Up close she was smaller than he'd thought—small for one of them, anyway, which put her at the size of a big pony—and lean and rangy, every line of her built for running, her coat a pale, even grey. Her ears were flat to her skull. Her hackles were up in a ridge all down her spine.

Her eyes were on his.

They were dark. Nearly black. And so furious he could feel it coming off her, from fifteen yards, like the heat off an open oven door.

He opened his mouth. He wasn't sure what he'd been going to say. Probably something stupid.

She snarled at him.

It was a big sound. A proper one. It came up out of her chest and ripped out between her teeth, all of them, every single one, bared to the gums, and it went on and on, rising and falling, a long ragged furious tearing noise that said every single thing she couldn't say in that shape. *Get out of my head. Get out of my life. Give it back. Give it back. I didn't ask for this. I didn't ask for YOU.*

And the thing at the bottom of him answered her.

He didn't decide to. That was the thing, afterward, that he'd keep coming back to. He had about a quarter of a second to think *oh, hang on*—and in that quarter of a second the wolf, all the way down at the bottom of the dark, lifted its head off its paws.

The sound came out of his chest.

It was low. It was lower than anything his human throat had any business making. It started somewhere under his ribs and went out into the clearing in a long, slow, heavy roll, so deep it was more something you felt than something you heard. The book on his knees buzzed against his thighs. The canvas of the chair hummed. At the edge of the trees, a whole bough's worth of snow let go of a big Douglas fir and came down in a soft white slide.

It was a rumble from somewhere very old and very big, slow and almost lazy, and what it said, as plainly as if he'd said it out loud, was *there you are*.

Every wolf in the clearing put its ears down.

He saw it happen. He saw it go down both packs like a wave, all of them at once, the russet wolf and the sandy one and the big black one in the middle of the other pack and the silver one that had been laughing, every single head dropping and every pair of ears going flat and back. A couple of the younger ones went straight down on their bellies in the snow.

And the grey wolf's snarl cut off mid-breath.

She stood there with her mouth still half-open. Her hackles were going down. He watched them go. They lay down along her spine all by themselves, the whole ridge of fur flattening out from her skull to her rump, and her tail, which had been stiff and high, came down and down until it was hanging between her back legs. She looked down at herself. She looked at her own tail. Then she looked back at him, and he had never seen an animal look so completely and utterly betrayed by its own body in his life.

The sound in his chest faded away.

The clearing was perfectly silent.

Harry sat very still in his camping chair with his hands flat on the open book. He cleared his throat. He tapped his breastbone with two fingers, once, like a man who'd felt his lunch come back on him.

"Sorry," he said. "'Scuse me." He tapped again. "Must've eaten something wrong."

Nobody said anything.

Behind him he heard Emmett very quietly say, "*Dude.*"

The grey wolf stood in the snow and stared at him. She was shaking again. Her mouth closed slowly. Her ears stayed flat. Something went across her face that he couldn't read and didn't try to, and then she turned on her haunches and walked away from him back toward the trees, stiff-legged and furious, with her tail still tucked and her brother trotting anxiously at her side. She got as far as the treeline.

She stopped there, at the very edge of the trees. She didn't go into them.

He felt it in the line. The exact point where she'd tried to make herself keep walking, and her legs had told her no.

Down at the bottom of the dark, the wolf lay its head back down on its paws. It looked extremely pleased with itself.

*I'm going to kill you,* Harry told it.

It thumped its tail on the stone.

He looked down at the book in his lap. *Kitsune*, eleven pages, with illustrations. He shut it. The covers came together with a heavy flat clap that echoed off the trees, and a little puff of dust came out of the pages and drifted down onto his jeans.

Off to his left, Carlisle cleared his throat. "Mr. Potter. Was that—"

"Indigestion."

"I see."

"Bad sandwich. Airport."

"I see," Carlisle said again, in exactly the same tone, which was the tone of a doctor who had been lied to by patients about where their symptoms came from for three hundred years and had stopped minding.

The first crack came from the north side of the field.

It was followed by a second, and a third, and then a ragged string of them, like a string of firecrackers going off one after another along the treeline. Seven. Eight. Every vampire in the clearing flinched at once. Every wolf came up off its belly.

People were standing at the edge of the trees where no one had been standing a moment before.

A dozen of them, give or take. Wizards. A couple in long dark travelling cloaks with the ICW's silver seal at the throat. Three in ordinary-looking anoraks and walking boots that would have passed in any car park in the Lake District, except that one of them was carrying a clipboard that was writing on itself. Two men and a woman in long tan coats and fedoras—fedoras, honestly, in this decade—with a small gold eagle pinned to each lapel. A thin harassed-looking man holding a silver tray of glass vials in one hand and a tape measure in the other. And at the front of the whole lot, a short woman in a long grey coat with a dark plait over one shoulder and a steaming paper cup of coffee in her hand, looking around the field at the ash and the scorch and the steam and the wolves and the two dozen frozen vampires, and then finally at Harry in his camping chair, with an expression he knew very well indeed.

Sabine had come herself.

"Twenty-two minutes," Harry said, getting up. The book went under one arm. The chair folded itself up with a clatter behind him and vanished. "You said twenty."

"The Americans," Sabine said, "argued." She looked at the black star in the snow. She looked at the heaps of ash, and the gold melting in them. She took a long sip of her coffee. "*Mon Dieu*, Potter."

"I know."

"Where is the hybrid?"

Harry nodded toward the russet wolf. "Toddler on the dog. Big backpack. Be nice, she's nervous. Dad's the one with the face like a smacked arse, mum's the one with the shield. Grandad's the doc. He'll want to talk to you. He'll want to talk to you a lot. He's got a list."

"And the wolves?"

"Shapeshifters. Two packs. Quileute. Local. Need moving into the right bit of the Registry, they've been filed under *Werewolves, Probably* since nineteen-thirty-something."

Sabine's eyes went along the line of wolves. They went all the way to the end, to the grey one standing at the very edge of the trees with her tail tucked and her ears flat and her eyes fixed on Harry like he was the only thing in the world.

Which, Harry thought, as far as the grey wolf was concerned, he more or less was. And she was going to hate him for it for a good long while.

Sabine looked at the grey wolf for quite a long time. Then she looked at Harry.

"Potter," she said slowly. "Why is that wolf looking at you like that?"

Harry tucked the book a bit more firmly under his arm.

"Long story," he said. "Involves a straw."
