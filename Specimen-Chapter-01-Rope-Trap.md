# Specimen

## Chapter One: Rope Trap

---

### I. Aisle Seven

*Forks, Washington — Monday, October 2, 2006*

Seth Clearwater had two lists and a shopping cart with a front wheel that wanted to go left.

The first list was in his mother's handwriting, small and slanted and practical: *milk (2), eggs (3 doz), flour, coffee — the GOOD one, Seth, not the can — dish soap, potatoes, whatever meat is on sale.* The second list was written on the back of a gas receipt in Jacob's handwriting, which looked like it had been done on the hood of a moving car, and said only: *MEAT. Like a lot. Bread. The big peanut butter. Don't let Leah pick anything.*

Leah was in the truck. Leah had declined to come in on the grounds that Thriftway on a Monday afternoon was where hope went to die, and also because she'd run patrol until four in the morning and wanted to sleep with her forehead against the passenger window. Seth had left the engine running so the heater would stay on. Neither of them needed a heater. He'd done it anyway, out of the habit of a kid who'd grown up in a house where you kept your sister comfortable or you heard about it.

He steered the cart past the bakery case, fought the wheel, and won.

It had been three weeks, give or take a day, since Renesmee Cullen was born in a house full of vampires. Three weeks since Bella's heart stopped and started again as something else. Three weeks since Jacob Black had looked down at a baby wrapped in a blanket and every single thing that had been pulling him toward a fight had let go of him all at once, and Sam's pack had been forced to turn around and go home, because you did not touch a wolf's imprint. Not ever. That was the oldest law they had.

Seth had liked most of the three weeks. He knew that made him strange. Leah had told him so roughly forty times. But he liked Edward, and he liked Carlisle, and Esme kept trying to feed him, which nobody else in that house could appreciate, and Bella was still Bella under the gold-red eyes and the stillness. She still said *Seth* the same way. She still laughed at his jokes, even the bad ones. Especially the bad ones.

And Nessie was a baby who could put her hand on your face and show you a picture of what she was thinking. You couldn't hate a house that had that in it.

Leah could. Leah was talented that way.

He loaded the cart with ground beef until the woman at the meat counter asked him, with real concern, whether he was feeding a dog. Seth said, "Kind of," and grinned at her, and she gave him the extra two pounds that had been sitting in the back. He was eighteen since August, six-foot-something and still growing, with shoulders his shirts had stopped forgiving, and he had learned that if he smiled at people in Forks they generally gave him things. He didn't abuse it. Much.

Produce. Potatoes. His mother would want the russets. He was bent over the bin, picking through them like he knew what he was doing, when the smell hit him.

Rain on hot stone. The second before a lightning strike, when the air goes sharp and the hair on your arms stands up. Something green under that, crushed leaves, and under that, coffee.

He straightened up and turned around.

She was at the end of the aisle with a basket hooked over one arm, reaching for something on the top shelf of the dry goods endcap and not quite getting there. Not tall. Dark hair twisted up into a knot at the back of her head and held there with what looked like a pencil, or a chopstick, a slim piece of dark wood with a carved end. A canvas work jacket, tan, too thin for the Olympic Peninsula in October, sleeves pushed to the elbows. Her forearms were freckled and there was a thin silver line of scar tissue across the knuckles of her right hand, like she'd punched a pane of glass once and decided it had been worth it.

In the basket: a box of kosher salt. A second box of kosher salt. A bag of white tea lights. Two dozen eggs. A bottle of cheap red wine. A brick of coffee.

She went up on her toes. Her fingertips brushed the jar she was after, a big glass jar of honey, and nudged it, and it tipped forward off the shelf.

Seth was across eight feet of linoleum before he'd thought about it, and he caught the jar an inch above the floor.

He came up holding it. She'd turned at the sound, or the lack of sound, the way people do when they expect a crash and don't get one, and she was looking at him.

Grey eyes. That was the first thing. A clear rain-cloud grey with a darker ring at the edge.

And then the floor went away.

It didn't go anywhere. He was still standing on it. The cart was still pulling left against his hip. Somebody two aisles over was asking about the price of avocados. But all of it came loose at once: every rope that had ever been tied to him, his mother, Leah, the pack, the Cullens, the reservation, the beach at First Beach in the summer, his father's grave. They stayed tied, every one of them, and stopped being the thing holding him to the ground, and something else was holding him now, and it was her. Only her. A wire run straight through the middle of him and out the other side and tied off on this woman in a cheap jacket with a pencil in her hair, and if she walked out of this store he'd follow her to the end of the world, and he'd be glad about it.

He had seen this happen to other people. He had seen it through Jacob's eyes in the pack mind three weeks ago, and through Quil's, years before, and through Sam's, which was the one nobody talked about. He'd thought he understood it.

He hadn't understood anything.

"Thank you," she said. "That would've been—" She looked at the jar in his hands, then up at him again, and her eyebrows drew in a little. "Are you okay?"

"Yeah," Seth said. "Yes. Hi."

"Hi."

"You dropped your honey."

"I noticed. You caught my honey."

"I did," he agreed. He was still holding it. He made himself hand it over. Her fingers touched his on the glass and she pulled back a fraction, the way people did when they touched one of the pack. They ran hot. A hundred and eight, give or take.

She didn't say anything about it. She looked at his hand for a half second longer than people usually did, though.

"You're new," Seth said.

"Is it that obvious?"

"It's your jacket."

She looked down at herself. "What's wrong with my jacket?"

"Nothing. It's a good jacket. For, like, Arizona." He heard himself and kept going anyway. "It's October. It's going to rain on you for the next seven months. That's a nice-day jacket."

The corner of her mouth went up. It was a crooked smile, more on the left side than the right. "I was told it was a waterproof jacket."

"Who told you that?"

"The jacket. On the tag."

"The tag lied to you."

"Tags do that." She shifted the basket on her arm. "Layla."

"What?"

"My name. It's Layla. You looked like you were about to ask."

He had been about to ask. He'd been about to ask in a much less smooth way than that. "Seth. Clearwater. I'm from La Push. Down the coast. The reservation."

"I know where La Push is. I've been here three weeks, not three hours." She tilted her head. "Clearwater. Like the river?"

"Kind of the opposite. We're the water, and the river's named after somebody else." That didn't make any sense. He decided not to fix it. "Where are you staying?"

Her eyebrows went up a little at that, and he realized how it sounded, and felt the heat climb his neck, which was an accomplishment, given his baseline.

"I mean, which side of town. I'm not— I'm not going to come over. Unless you want me to come over. I could carry stuff. You have a lot of salt."

Layla looked down at her basket, at the two boxes of kosher salt, and something passed across her face, fast, a flicker of caution, like a door being pulled a little closer to shut. Then it was gone.

"I'm renting the blue house on the east end of Calawah Way," she said. "Past the bend. The one with the porch that's falling off."

"The Hendersons' old place."

"Is it? The agent didn't say."

"Mrs. Henderson moved to Sequim after her husband died. Her porch has been falling off since I was ten. There's a step on the left side that'll put you through to the crawlspace."

"I found that step."

"It's a famous step." He was smiling. He couldn't stop. "What do you do? Here, I mean. Nobody moves to Forks."

"I'm doing a restoration job. Old property up past the Sol Duc, out in the trees. The family that owned it died out a long time ago, and the people who inherited it want to know what's still worth keeping before they sell." She said it smoothly, the way a person said a thing they'd said before. "Appraisal, mostly. Cleaning up. There's a lot of junk."

"Huh," Seth said, because he couldn't think of anything up past the Sol Duc but trees and the hot springs and some old logging roads. There probably was a house somewhere. There were houses everywhere out there, rotting into the moss. "That's cool. That's really cool."

"It's mostly dust."

"Dust is cool."

She laughed. It came out of her short and surprised, like she hadn't expected it, and Seth decided right then that he'd spend the rest of his life saying stupid things if that was the noise he got for them.

"I have to go," she said. "My eggs are getting old."

"Your eggs are fine. Eggs last forever."

"Seth Clearwater." She said his name like she was trying it out, deciding how it fit. "It was nice to meet you. Thanks for saving my honey."

"Can I—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you have a phone?"

She considered him. He had no idea what she was seeing. Some huge Quileute kid with three dozen eggs and twenty-two pounds of ground beef, who'd caught a falling jar faster than people were supposed to, and who was currently staring at her like she'd hung the moon personally. It couldn't look great.

She set the basket down on the edge of the potato bin, pulled a pen out of her jacket pocket, took his hand, turned it over, and wrote a phone number across his palm in blue ink. Her fingers were cool. Cool against his skin was everything, though. Cool was most things.

"I don't answer after ten," she said. "And if you call me about my jacket, I'm hanging up."

"I won't call about your jacket."

"Good."

She picked up her basket and her honey and walked away toward the registers, and Seth stood beside the potatoes with his cart pulling left and his hand held open in front of him like he was carrying water in it.

---

Leah was awake when he got back to the truck. She was awake and sitting up straight with her arms crossed, and she watched him come across the parking lot in the drizzle with six bags hanging off each hand, and when he opened the driver's door she looked at his face for exactly one second.

"No," she said.

Seth started loading bags into the back seat.

"No. Seth. Absolutely not. You did not."

"I didn't do anything."

"You did. Look at you. You've got the face." She sounded furious. Under the fury she sounded something else, something older and worse, and he knew exactly what it was, because he'd lived in the pack mind with it for a year. "You've got the *face*, Seth, the stupid face, the one Jared gets, the one *Sam*—"

She stopped.

Seth climbed in and shut the door and sat behind the wheel with the rain ticking on the roof and the heater running for nobody. He looked at the phone number on his hand.

"Her name's Layla," he said.

Leah turned and looked out her window. Her jaw was tight. After a long minute she said, quietly, "Is she nice, at least?"

"She has two boxes of salt."

"What?"

"I don't know. She's nice. She's—" He didn't have the word. He didn't think there was a word. "Yeah. She's nice."

"Great," Leah said to the window. "That's just great." And then, after a moment, almost too low for even him to hear: "Don't let it make you stupid."

He put the truck in reverse. "Too late."

She hit him in the arm hard enough to bruise anybody else, and he laughed, and they drove home to La Push in the rain.

### II. The Step on the Left Side

*Forks — Monday, November 6, 2006*

Five weeks.

That was how long it took Seth to figure out how to tell her, and in five weeks he'd learned a lot of things about Layla Brennan, in the order he'd learned them.

She was twenty-three. She'd grown up in western Massachusetts, in a town she described as "three churches and a feed store," and she'd gone to a boarding school up in the mountains that she talked about in the specific, careful way people talked about places they weren't allowed to describe. She'd been the captain of something there. A club. She didn't say what kind. She had an older brother in New Orleans she called every Sunday and fought with every Sunday. She did the crossword in pen. She sang in the kitchen, loudly and badly, mostly Fleetwood Mac. She made the worst coffee Seth had ever tasted in his life, a black sludge you could stand a spoon in, and drank four cups a day of it, and called it "functional."

She did not own a television.

She owned an owl.

The owl had been the first real weird thing. A big tawny owl that lived in her kitchen on a perch by the window like it paid rent, and looked at Seth with the flat, judgmental patience of something that had seen better people walk through that door. "She's a rescue," Layla had said, too fast, the first time. "Rehab license. I'm not allowed to talk about it." Seth had nodded. He didn't know anything about owls. He knew the owl came and went through a window that was never open when he arrived and was always closed again by the time he looked back. He knew that once, at eleven at night, he'd seen it fly off over the treeline with an envelope in its claws.

He hadn't asked.

There were other things. Her house was always the same temperature, no matter how cold it got outside, and he never once heard the furnace kick on. Her old Ford pickup never needed gas. She'd come back from the property up past the Sol Duc covered in grey dust and smelling like that lightning smell so strong it made his nose sting, with little burns on her fingers, and once with a bandage on her neck that was gone the next day, along with the cut under it. No scab. No mark. And she'd pull that dark stick out of her hair when she thought he wasn't looking and turn it in her fingers while she was thinking, the way other people twirled pens.

He'd fixed her porch in week two. Pulled up the rotten step on the left side, cut a new one out of cedar, sanded it, sealed it. She'd watched him from the doorway with her coffee and said, "You're very handy," and he'd said, "My dad was," and she'd heard something in it and come and sat on the top step beside him without saying anything else, and stayed until the light went.

In week three they'd gone to Port Angeles to see a movie and he'd bought her a raincoat afterward, a real one, a yellow one, in a store by the ferry terminal. She'd stood in front of the mirror in it and laughed at herself and kept it on the whole drive home.

In week four she had put her hand on his arm in the truck and left it there.

He had not kissed her. He'd wanted to every minute of every day for five weeks, and he had not, because Sam had sat him down on Emily's porch the week after the store with a face like a man reading a will and told him: *You tell her. Before anything happens. You tell her what we are and you let her decide. That's the deal. That's the only fair thing there is.*

Jacob had said the same thing, in fewer words, and Jacob's imprint was eight weeks old and could already talk, so Seth figured Jacob had about as much room to give advice as anybody.

So tonight he was going to tell her.

He'd been sitting on her back porch for twenty minutes trying to start.

"You're doing the thing," Layla said.

She was sitting beside him with her knees pulled up inside the yellow raincoat and a mug of the functional coffee between her hands. The yard behind the house ran about forty feet to a wire fence and then the forest started, sudden as a wall, Sitka spruce and hemlock and big-leaf maples that had dropped their leaves in heaps. It wasn't raining. It was going to. The air had the weight to it.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you're about to say something and then you look at the trees instead."

"I'm not looking at the trees."

"You're looking at the trees right now."

He was. He made himself look at her.

"I need to show you something," Seth said. "And when I do, you're going to want to run. And I need you to not run. Okay? Just— don't run. Or run, if you need to. You can run. But I need you to know I'd never hurt you. Ever. Not if somebody held a gun on me. That's the one thing I can't do."

Layla had gone very still. It was a different kind of still from the Cullens'. Theirs was the stillness of statues, of things that had forgotten they used to breathe. Hers was the stillness of a person who'd been in a room with something dangerous before and had learned that moving fast was a bad idea. Her right hand had come off the mug and was resting on her knee, near the pocket of the raincoat.

"Okay," she said carefully. "I don't run."

"Everybody says that."

"I'm not everybody." Her voice was level. "Show me."

He stood up and walked out into the yard. Twenty feet. Thirty. He didn't want to be close to her when it happened. Paul had been standing too close to Emily once. Nobody talked about that one either.

He hooked his thumbs into the waistband of his cutoffs and then stopped, because there was a woman watching, and he was about to take his clothes off in her backyard. "Um," he said. "Could you turn around? For like a second?"

Layla stared at him. "Are you about to *strip*?"

"Kind of! It's part of it! I'll explain!"

She turned around on the step and faced the house. He heard her mutter something that sounded like *this had better be good*.

He kicked out of the shorts and tied them to his ankle with the leather cord, and let the heat go up his spine.

It was always the same. A shiver that came from the middle of him and went out to everything at once. A tearing that didn't hurt, though it should have. The world dropped and then reorganized itself around a body that was bigger and lower and stronger, and smells came up out of the ground like somebody had turned on a light. Woodsmoke from the next house. Rot in the maple leaves. The owl, in the kitchen. Coffee. Layla. Lightning and rain on stone.

*Seth.* Leah's voice in his head, sharp, alarmed. She was out on patrol on the north line. *What are you— oh. Oh, you're doing it. Now? You're doing it now?*

*Get out of my head, Leah.*

*I literally can't, genius.*

*Then close your eyes or whatever.*

"Okay," he tried to say, and it came out as a low huff through a muzzle. He took a breath. He made a small noise, a whine, the least scary sound he could think of.

Layla turned around.

She looked at him for a long time. A sandy-colored wolf the size of a horse, standing in the middle of her yard among the fallen maple leaves, with a pair of cutoff shorts tied to his back ankle. He kept his ears up and his tail low and his weight back, like he'd seen Jacob do around Bella's father. He tried to look friendly. He had no idea if it was working.

Her face did a lot of things. Shock first. Then something sharper, like she was doing math. Then her eyes went over him, all of him, head to tail and back, and lingered on his eyes, and he saw her recognize him in there.

Then she started to laugh.

It was the laugh of somebody who'd been holding a heavy box for a long time and had just been told she could put it down. She put the mug of coffee down on the step beside her. She put both hands over her face. She laughed into them until her shoulders shook.

*Is she okay?* Seth thought. *Leah, is she—*

*How should I know? I'm three miles away. She's laughing at you. That's a good sign. Probably.*

Layla dropped her hands. Her eyes were wet. "Oh, thank God," she said. "Oh, thank *God*. Seth. Oh my God. I've been trying to figure out how to tell *you* for a month."

He tilted his head. It was the only question he could ask.

She reached up behind her head and pulled the dark stick out of her hair. Her hair came down over her shoulders, heavy, and she held the stick up in front of her face for a second, the carved end toward the sky, like she was showing him a card she'd been holding back.

Then she gave it a small, careful flick.

Every light in the house came on at once, kitchen and hall and bedroom, though nobody had touched a switch. The porch light came on. And all through the yard, in the air at about the height of his shoulders, little flames bloomed out of nothing. Dozens of them. A hundred. White tea lights, the cheap kind from the store, floating in the dark at all different heights, their wicks burning steady in the damp air, scattered across the yard and in among the trees like the forest had decided to throw a party.

Seth's ears went flat. Then they came up again, slowly, all the way.

Layla stood up. She came down the steps and walked out across the grass toward him between the floating candles with the stick held loose in her right hand. When she got close she gave it another small turn, and the fallen maple leaves around his feet lifted off the ground and began to circle, slow and then faster, a soft spiral of red and gold going around both of them, whispering.

"Witch," she said. She tapped her chest with the end of the stick. "That's me. That's what I am. That's what the salt was. And the owl. And the property up in the woods. There are curses on that house older than this state, and I'm getting paid an obscene amount of money to take them apart." She was very close now, looking up at him. "You can't tell anyone. Seth. I mean it. There are laws. Big ones. Like, people-come-for-you laws."

*Leah,* Seth thought weakly.

*Yeah,* said Leah, in his head, from three miles away. *Yeah, I saw.*

Layla put her free hand flat on his chest, in the thick fur over his breastbone, where his heart was going like a drum.

"Can you change back?" she asked. "I'd really like to kiss you, and I'm not doing it like this."

---

It took him a minute. Phasing back while your heart was trying to come out of your ribs was hard. He did it behind her shed, and untied his shorts, and pulled them on, and came back around the corner barefoot with his chest still heaving, and she was standing in the middle of her yard in the yellow raincoat under a hundred floating candles, with the leaves settling down around her ankles.

He walked straight to her and she put the stick away in a pocket and took his face in both cool hands and pulled him down, and he kissed her.

It had been five weeks. He'd thought about it for five weeks. He had imagined it a lot of ways.

He'd underestimated it.

When they came apart she kept her hands on his face. She was breathing hard. He was breathing harder.

"So," she said, a little unsteady. "Wolves."

"Wolves," he agreed. "Shape-shifters, technically. Sort of. It's a long story. It's a really long story. There's legends."

"Is it a full-moon thing?"

"No. It's a whenever-we-want thing. Or when we get mad. That's the bad part. You shouldn't make Paul mad."

"Who's Paul?"

"I'll introduce you. You'll hate him."

She laughed again, softer. Then her face changed, and he watched her think. She was always thinking. He loved that about her. He'd loved it from the jar of honey. "Seth. When you said you'd never hurt me. Not if somebody held a gun on you. That's the one thing you can't do." Her grey eyes were steady. "That sounded like a rule."

He took a breath.

He told her about imprinting.

He told it badly. He told her about the store, and the jar, and the floor going out from under him, and the wire through the middle of him. He told her about Sam and Emily, a little, and about Jacob and the baby, which made her eyebrows climb into her hair. He told her it wasn't a choice, and that it was also the most important choice he'd ever make, every single day, because what it made him want was for her to have whatever she wanted. If she wanted him as a friend, he'd be her friend. If she wanted him gone, he'd find a way to be gone. That was the part that was his.

She listened to all of it without interrupting, which he would later learn was the rarest thing in the world.

"So you didn't get a vote," she said at last.

"About the first part? No."

"That's—" She stopped. Her mouth pressed into a line. "That's a lot."

"Yeah."

"That's actually kind of horrifying, Seth."

"I know." He made himself keep looking at her. "But I got a vote on everything after. On the porch step. On the raincoat. On waiting. I got all of those. I picked all of those."

She was quiet for a long time. The candles burned. Somewhere in the house, the owl made a noise like a question.

"Okay," Layla said finally. "Okay. I'm going to need to think about that for, like, a year."

"That's fair."

"But I'm going to do it with you here. If that's all right."

His chest went tight. "Yeah," he said. "Yeah. That's all right."

She pulled him down and kissed him again, shorter this time, and then rested her forehead against his collarbone. "God, you're hot," she muttered. "Like actually. Temperature-wise. It's like hugging a radiator."

"Hundred and eight."

"That can't be healthy."

"It's fine. It's a wolf thing."

"Everything's a wolf thing with you." She pulled back to look at him, and he could see her shoulders come up a little under the raincoat as she remembered something. "Okay. Important. The laws. You can't tell *anyone*. I'm serious. I'm already way out on a branch here. The people I answer to, if they found out a No-Maj — a regular person — if they found out a regular person knew—"

"About that," Seth said.

She went still again.

"So when we phase," Seth said, slowly, "we kind of hear each other. In our heads. The whole pack. Everything we're thinking."

Layla looked at him.

"So Leah already knows," Seth said. "She was out on patrol. She saw the candles. And Jacob's going to know as soon as he phases, which, he phases a lot, so. Probably tonight."

Layla sat down on the grass. She did it all at once, like her knees had stopped taking requests.

"And then," Seth said, because there was no point stopping now, "there's Edward. And Edward kind of reads minds. Like, everybody's, all the time, about a couple miles out. So when I go over there tomorrow—"

"Who," said Layla, very calmly, from the grass, "is Edward?"

Seth sat down beside her. A tea light floated past his ear.

"He's a vampire," he said. "They're all vampires. The whole family. But they're nice. I swear. You're going to love them."

Layla lay back on the wet grass and stared up at the floating candles for a while.

"Seth," she said to the sky.

"Yeah?"

"I am in *so* much trouble."

He lay down next to her. After a minute she found his hand in the grass and laced her fingers through his, cool through hot, and held on.

The rain started about ten minutes later. The candles kept burning through it. She'd done something to them so they would.

### III. Need to Know

*The Cullen house — Tuesday, November 7, 2006*

Leah had seen the whole thing.

She'd seen it from three miles away through her little brother's eyes: the candles, the leaves, the stick that was a wand, the kiss, the *second* kiss, a lot more of the second kiss than any sister should ever have to see, and then later, after he'd come home and passed out on the couch at their mother's, she'd seen it all again when he phased at dawn to run the south line and couldn't stop replaying it. Jacob had phased at six. Jacob had seen it too. The whole pack mind had been a nonstop highlight reel of Layla Brennan for about ninety minutes.

*I'm going to kill him,* Leah had thought, into the link, at Jacob.

*You're not going to kill him.*

*I'm going to kill him a little.*

*Leah.* Jacob had sounded tired. He always sounded tired lately. He wasn't sleeping much; he spent most nights at the little cottage the Cullens had given Bella and Edward, on the floor by the baby's crib, which Leah thought was the most pathetic thing she'd ever seen and which she would also have died before saying to his face, because the look on his face when he did it was something she couldn't stand to look at. *A witch,* Jacob had thought. *A real one.*

*Apparently.*

*We have to tell them.*

*He's going over there anyway. Edward'll get it out of his head before he's out of the truck.*

*Then we'd better be there when he does.*

So here they were, at six in the evening, in the big glass house in the trees, with every light on and the river going by outside in the dark, and seven vampires and a vampire baby and two wolves in human form and one witch in a yellow raincoat standing in the middle of the living room.

Leah stood by the door with her arms crossed. That was her spot. She'd decided that in September. Close enough to the door to leave. Far enough from the Cullens that the stink didn't make her eyes water. The stink was better than it used to be; she'd gotten used to it the way you got used to living near a paper mill. But it was still there, the sweet, chemical, burning edge of it, like bleach poured over rotten flowers.

The witch didn't seem to smell anything. She was standing next to Seth with her shoulders square and her chin up, looking around the room at the people in it, and Leah watched her eyes go to each of them and do something. Measure. That was the word. She was measuring them. Exits, distances, which one was closest to her, which one moved first. Leah had seen Sam do that, and Jacob. She'd never seen a human do it.

*Witch,* she reminded herself. *Whatever that means.*

Edward had known since Seth's truck turned off the highway. He'd met them on the porch with a face like someone had told him a building was on fire somewhere and he couldn't find out which one. Now he stood near the piano with Bella beside him, and Bella had Renesmee on her hip, and the baby was staring at Layla with huge brown eyes.

Carlisle was the one who stepped forward. Of course he was. He was smiling. He looked like a kid on Christmas.

"Miss Brennan," he said, "thank you for coming. I know this can't be easy. You must have questions."

"I have about five thousand questions," Layla said. "I'm guessing you do too."

"A few," Carlisle admitted.

"Can you do something?" said Emmett, from the couch. He was sprawled across most of it with his arm along the back. Rosalie sat at the other end, very upright, not touching him, watching Layla the way she watched anything that came within ten feet of the baby. "Like, a trick. Not to be rude. I just feel like we should see a trick."

"Emmett," said Esme.

"What? Seth's head is full of floating candles. I want floating candles."

Layla looked at him for a second. Then she reached up and drew the wand out of her hair, and her hair came down, and Leah saw every vampire in the room register the motion. Jasper, by the stairs, shifted his weight a quarter inch. Rosalie's hand went to the baby's foot, which was the closest part of Renesmee she could reach from the couch.

Layla pointed the wand at the vase of flowers on the coffee table and turned it in a small, tight circle.

The vase rippled. It was the strangest thing Leah had ever seen, and she turned into a wolf on a regular basis. The glass went soft and folded in on itself, and the flowers drew down into it, and the whole thing shrank and twisted and grew feathers. A second later a small blue bird was sitting on the coffee table, blinking. It shook itself, fluffed up, hopped twice, and took off, and flew one loop around the high ceiling of the room before landing on the top of the piano.

Nobody said anything.

Then the baby laughed. A real laugh, delighted, a bell. She reached her arms toward the bird and kicked against Bella's side.

"Huh," said Emmett, softly.

"That was Esme's vase," said Alice. She was perched on the arm of a chair near the window, and she was staring at a point just past Layla's shoulder with an odd, unfocused look.

"Oh, God, I'm sorry," Layla said, turning to Esme. "I'll change it back. I can change it back. It's still a vase. Mostly."

"Please don't," Esme said. She was looking at the bird. "Leave it a minute."

Carlisle had taken a step closer. "Is it alive?"

"Sort of. For now. It's a transfiguration. It thinks it's a bird. It'll keep thinking that till I end it, or it wears off, and then it'll be a vase again." Layla turned the wand in her fingers, a nervous motion. "There are limits. Rules. Stuff you can't make from nothing. Food's a big one. You can make more of food you have, but you can't make food out of air."

"Damn," said Emmett, sincerely.

"You don't eat," said Rosalie.

"It's the principle."

Alice hadn't moved. "I can see you," she said to Layla, abruptly. "I've been trying since Seth's truck turned in. I can see you, but it's wrong. It's like you're behind wet glass. Or like a radio station when you're driving out of range." She frowned. "I see you for a second, and then there's static, and then I see you again somewhere else. I can't see Seth at all, or Leah, or Jacob. I never can. That's normal for them. But you're—" She shook her head, short and irritated. "You're half there."

"Is that bad?" Layla asked.

"It's new," Alice said, which in Leah's experience was the scariest thing a Cullen could say.

Edward spoke for the first time. "Your thoughts are very loud," he said to Layla.

Layla turned to face him. Leah saw her shoulders tighten.

"I'm sorry," Edward said. "I only mean you aren't hiding anything. Nearly everything you're thinking is right on the surface." He glanced at Carlisle. "There's a school. In the mountains in Massachusetts. A castle. They sort the students into houses when they arrive." His brows drew together. "There's an entire government. Here. In this country. There's been one for three hundred years."

"That's a lot to get from looking at me," Layla said tightly.

"I don't have a choice about it," Edward said, and Leah, who'd never once in her life felt sorry for Edward Cullen, felt something adjacent to it for about half a second. "I'm sorry. I'll try to stay out. It's difficult."

"It's fine." It obviously wasn't. Layla took a breath. "It's fine. It's fair. I've got about five thousand questions about *you*, so. Fair's fair."

"These laws you were thinking about," Jasper said. His voice was mild, Southern, soft around the edges. He hadn't come down off the stairs. "The ones you were worried about on your porch. The people who come for you." He tilted his head a fraction. "Who enforces them?"

Layla looked over at him. Leah watched her decide how much to say.

"The Congress," she said. "It's— think of it as a government. Our government. We have our own. The secrecy laws are the biggest laws we have. Every country's got the same ones. They've been on the books since the sixteen hundreds."

"And when someone breaks them?"

"Depends." Her jaw worked. "Usually they wipe the memories of anybody who saw. There's a spell. Then whoever told gets a fine, or a hearing. If it's bad, they take your wand." She stopped. "If it's really bad there's prison."

"Is this bad?" Carlisle asked gently.

Layla laughed, short and without humor. "I told a werewolf—"

"Shape-shifter," said Seth.

"I told a shape-shifter, who told his whole pack by *thinking*, who told a vampire by *standing near him*, so now an entire family of vampires knows about the wizarding world, and there's a telepath and a fortune-teller in the family. So, yeah. Yeah. It's somewhere on the spectrum."

"You're both magical," said Carlisle, thoughtfully. "Seth. And us. Our kind. Surely that changes the calculation."

"Maybe," Layla said. "I've been thinking about that all day. There's a gray area. The law's about No-Majs. Regular people. You're not regular people. You could make an argument." She looked tired suddenly. "I'd rather not have to make it in front of a judge."

"Then you won't," said Bella.

Everybody looked at her.

Bella shifted Renesmee on her hip. She looked so young still. Even with the eyes. "We've been keeping a secret our whole lives. All of us. All of them have, I mean. For a hundred years. We're pretty good at it." Her mouth curved a little. "Nobody's going to hear about you from us."

"Nobody's going to hear about you from us either," Jacob said, from beside Leah. His arms were crossed too. "Pack doesn't talk."

"Sam's pack might," Leah said.

"Sam's pack doesn't know."

"Sam's pack will know the first time Seth thinks about her near any of them, which is every six seconds."

Jacob shot her a look. Leah shrugged. It was true.

Renesmee had gone quiet. She'd stopped reaching for the bird. She was looking at Layla with total, unblinking attention, and then she twisted in Bella's arms and stretched out one small hand, palm out, toward the witch.

"She wants to show you something," Bella said. "Is that all right? She has to touch you."

Layla looked at the baby. Leah watched something in her face go soft and then go wary again in the same breath. "What's she going to show me?"

"No idea," Bella said. "That's kind of the fun."

Layla stepped forward. Bella stepped forward. Renesmee put her hand flat on Layla's cheek.

Leah saw it land. The witch's eyes went wide and unfocused, and her lips parted, and for about three seconds she just stood there with a baby's hand on her face while the rest of the room watched.

Then she laughed, the surprised laugh that Seth had been playing in the pack mind on a loop since October.

"The bird," Layla said. She looked at Bella, delighted. "She showed me the bird. She wants me to make *another* one."

"Not out of my vases," said Esme. But she was smiling.

The witch made another one. A yellow one, out of a pencil cup. Then a third, a little green one, out of a coaster. Renesmee had all three of them landing on her hands within ten minutes, and Emmett asked if she could make a really big one, like an eagle, out of the TV, and Rosalie hit him without looking, and Carlisle had a list of questions written down on a notepad that he'd produced from somewhere and was asking them one at a time in the kitchen while Esme hovered and Layla drank a glass of water that nobody else in the house could have touched.

Leah stayed by the door.

Seth came over at some point, all lit up. "She likes them," he said. "They like her. Leah. This is good. Right? This is good."

Leah looked across the room. The witch was in the kitchen with Carlisle, drawing something on the doctor's notepad, a circle with lines through it. Her wand was back in her hair. She was laughing at something Carlisle said, and Leah watched her, and watched Jasper on the stairs watching her, and watched Edward by the window with his eyes half-closed, listening to everything in the room at once. Listening to the witch most of all.

She thought about laws that had been on the books since the sixteen hundreds. About people who came for you.

"Yeah," she said. "It's great, Seth."

She didn't say the rest of it. *Nobody has a secret that big for that long by accident. Somebody's guarding it. And now we're on the inside of it.*

She didn't have to say it out loud. Edward looked up from across the room and met her eyes. He'd heard.

He nodded once, slowly.

### IV. Grease

*Charlie Swan's house, Forks — Sunday, November 12, 2006*

Charlie Swan had made a policy.

The policy was this: if it was about Bella, and it was weird, and nobody was getting hurt, he didn't need to know. He'd made the policy in September in his own living room after Jake Black had taken his shirt off and turned into a wolf the size of a pickup truck in front of him, and he'd stuck to it with a discipline he'd never managed on anything in his life, not his diet, not his fishing budget, nothing. Bella was alive. Bella was married. Bella was different — her eyes, her skin, the cold of her hand, the way she was always perfectly still unless she remembered not to be. There was a baby. The baby was Edward's niece, adopted. The baby was growing really fast. Charlie didn't need to know.

Need to know. He'd been a cop for twenty-five years and he'd never really understood how good those words were until now.

It worked pretty well. It worked as long as he didn't think about it too hard at three in the morning.

Sue Clearwater had been coming over to cook for him since August. That had started out as a thing a widow did for a man whose daughter had just gone off and gotten married, casseroles and checking up on him, and somewhere along the line it had turned into something else, something Charlie didn't have a name for and was too old and too scared to put one on. She was standing at his stove right now with her sleeves rolled up, frying chicken in his mother's cast iron, telling her son to set the table and not just stand there.

Seth was setting the table. Seth was setting it for four, and the fourth person was standing in Charlie's kitchen doorway holding a pie she'd bought at the Thriftway, in a yellow raincoat that was dripping on his linoleum.

"Chief Swan," she said. "Hi. I'm Layla. Thanks for having me."

"Charlie's fine." He shook her hand. Firm grip. She looked him in the eye while she did it, which he appreciated. Then she looked at the rest of the room, quick, left to right — the back door, the window over the sink, the hall to the front, the gun belt on the hook by the door — and he noticed her doing it, and filed it away.

He'd met a lot of people in twenty-five years. Most of them didn't clock the exits when they walked into a kitchen. The ones who did had generally been in the military, or in jail, or in the kind of job where people sometimes tried to kill you.

"Seth says you're doing an appraisal," Charlie said. "Up past the Sol Duc."

"That's right."

"Didn't know there was a house up there."

"Not many people do. It's pretty overgrown. It's been empty since the twenties."

"Huh." He took her raincoat and hung it up beside his jacket. "Who owned it?"

"Family called Thackeray. They died out." She smiled. It was a nice smile, a little crooked. "I've got the deed paperwork if you ever want to look at it. Seth said you'd probably ask."

Seth, putting down forks, looked like he wanted the floor to open up under him. Sue snorted at the stove.

Charlie found himself liking her. He didn't want to. His instinct about anybody who walked into Seth Clearwater's life in the year after Harry died was suspicion, deep and protective, and Seth had been walking around for six weeks with a face on him like a lighthouse. But she'd made a joke about his questions before he could ask them, and she'd brought pie, and she hadn't tried too hard. People who were hiding something usually tried too hard.

She was hiding something. He knew that too. Everybody in his life was hiding something lately. He'd made a policy about it.

They sat down to eat. The chicken was good. Sue's chicken was always good. Layla ate like somebody who'd been living on coffee and peanut butter for a month, which, from the look of her, she probably had. Seth ate like Seth, which meant like a combine harvester. They talked about the weather, and the Mariners, and about Layla's brother in New Orleans, and about the boarding school back east that she didn't say much about.

It was nice. It was a nice dinner.

Then Sue got up to put the second batch of chicken in, and the oil in the cast iron had gotten too hot while they were all sitting there talking, and when the cold chicken went in the pan spat and the grease caught.

It went up fast. Grease fires do. A sheet of yellow flame came off the pan and rolled up toward the cabinets, and Sue jumped back with a yelp, and Charlie was already halfway out of his chair reaching for the baking soda, knowing he wasn't going to make it, seeing the curtains over the sink, knowing exactly how a kitchen burned—

Layla was out of her chair before him.

She didn't reach for anything. Her hand went up behind her head and came back down with the stick from her hair, and she pointed it at the stove and said a word, sharp and short, a word Charlie didn't know.

The fire went out.

All of it. All at once. No smother, no steam. One instant there was a two-foot sheet of rolling yellow flame, and the next there was nothing. The grease in the pan lay flat and quiet and black. A little thread of smoke went up from it and that was all. The curtains weren't even singed.

The kitchen was dead silent.

Sue was standing with her back against the fridge and a hand on her chest. Seth had half-risen from his chair with a look on his face that Charlie had last seen on a deer in his high beams on the 101.

Layla was standing in the middle of his kitchen with a stick in her hand pointed at his stove, and Charlie watched the exact moment she realized what she'd just done.

Her face went white.

Charlie looked at the stove. He looked at the stick. He looked at Seth, and at Sue, who did not look surprised, exactly. Sue looked like somebody who'd been waiting for a shoe to drop and had just heard it hit the floor.

He looked at Layla.

"Okay," Charlie said.

Nobody said anything.

"Okay," he said again. He sat back down in his chair. His knees had told him to. He picked up his beer and drank about half of it. Then he set it down very carefully on the table. "Is this," he said, to the beer bottle, "a need-to-know thing?"

Layla's mouth opened. Then it shut.

He looked up at her. She was staring at him, and the stick in her hand had drifted a few degrees toward him. Her fingers were tight on it. Something was going on behind her eyes, fast, like she was standing at a crossroads and doing the math on every road.

Seth said, "Layla," very quietly.

The stick didn't move. Charlie saw it then. He'd seen it on a lot of people over the years, at traffic stops and domestic calls and once in a bank in Port Angeles: the moment somebody stood there holding a thing in their hand and decided whether to use it. She was deciding something about him. He had no idea what. He had a feeling, way down in his gut, that it was something that would make tonight go away. Make it easy.

"Were you about to do something to me?" he asked her. His voice came out level. Twenty-five years. It was good for something.

Layla looked at him for a long second.

"I thought about it," she said.

"What would it have been?"

"I'd have made you forget. The fire. The last five minutes." She swallowed. "It doesn't hurt. You wouldn't have known. It's what I'm supposed to do."

"Huh," said Charlie.

She lowered the wand. All the way. She reached up and put it back in her hair, which took her two tries, because her hands weren't steady.

"I'm not going to," she said. "That's— you're Seth's family. And I'm not going to sit here and eat your chicken and then reach into your head. That's not—" She stopped and pressed her lips together. "I'm not doing it."

"I appreciate that," Charlie said, and found he meant it more than almost anything he'd said in a while.

He picked up the beer again. Drank the rest of it. Thought about his policy.

"So," he said. "Is it need-to-know?"

Layla laughed, a cracked little laugh, and sat back down across from him. "Chief Swan," she said. "I think at this point you need to know."

She told him. Not everything. He could tell she wasn't telling him everything, the same way he could always tell when Bella was skipping parts. But she told him there were people like her. Witches, wizards. That they had their own government and their own laws and their own schools. That they kept themselves hidden, and had for a long time, and that the hiding was the most important law there was. That she'd broken it by standing in his kitchen and putting out a grease fire, and that she'd broken it a little before that, with Seth, and a lot before that, with the Cullens.

"The Cullens know," Charlie said.

"Yes."

"Bella knows."

"Yes."

"Course she does." He rubbed his face. "Course she does. Everybody knows everything except me. That's the policy." He looked over at Sue. "You knew."

"Since last week," Sue said. She'd sat back down beside him. She put her hand on his arm. "I didn't know how to tell you, Charlie. I didn't know if you wanted to know."

"I don't," Charlie said. "I really, really don't." He breathed out. "Is anybody getting hurt?"

"No," Layla said.

"Is anybody *going* to get hurt?"

She hesitated for just a beat too long. "Not because of me. I hope."

He'd take it. He'd taken a lot less.

"Okay," said Charlie Swan. "Okay. Well. That's—" He looked at the stove. At the black pan. At his curtains, which were fine. "Thanks for not burning down my kitchen."

"You're welcome," said Layla.

"Is there any more chicken?"

Sue looked at him for a second like she was going to cry. Then she started laughing, and got up, and turned the heat down under the oil. Seth sat back down. After a minute, Layla laughed too.

They ate the rest of the chicken. They ate the pie. Charlie didn't ask a single other question for the rest of the night, and every person at that table knew it was the hardest thing he'd ever done.

---

Seth walked her out to her truck around ten. It was raining, a light steady drizzle that was more mist than water. She stood by the driver's door with her keys in her hand and didn't get in.

"I almost did it," she said. "In there. I almost did it, Seth. I had the whole spell in my head. I could've done it in my sleep."

"But you didn't."

"I should have." She looked up at the house, at the lit kitchen window, where they could see Charlie and Sue at the sink, doing dishes, standing close. "There's a cop in that house who knows about wizards. You understand that? There's a No-Maj police chief who knows. If anybody from the Congress ever walks through this town and takes one look in his head, I'm done. Wand snapped. Done."

"Nobody's going to walk through this town."

"You don't know that."

"It's Forks," Seth said. "Nobody walks through Forks. Nobody even *drives* through Forks if they can help it."

She leaned her forehead against his chest. He put his arms around her. They stood there in the rain for a while.

"I want to give you something," she said finally, into his shirt.

She dug in the pocket of the raincoat and came out with a cord of braided leather, dark brown, with a single bead strung on it. The bead was silver, about the size of a pea, and when he looked at it closely there were tiny marks scratched all over it, so fine he could barely see them even with his eyes. Little lines and angles. Letters, maybe, in some alphabet he didn't know.

"Curse-breakers work in teams, mostly," Layla said. "In tombs. Old houses. Places where things go wrong fast and you get separated. So you carry these." She pulled down the collar of her shirt. There was a matching bead on a thin chain around her neck. "They're paired. If you bite down on yours — hard, so it breaks the shell — I'll feel it. Mine'll burn. And I'll know exactly where you are, and I can come to you."

"Come to me how?"

"Fast." She tied the cord around his left wrist, snug, and knotted it twice. "You can wear it when you change. I made it stretch. I tested it on a cantaloupe."

"You tested it on a—"

"Don't ask." She held his wrist in both hands. Her face was serious. "It's for emergencies. Real ones. I mean it. Bite it if you need me, and I'll come."

"What if I just miss you?"

"Then call me on the phone like a normal person." She went up on her toes and kissed him. "Before ten."

He watched her taillights go up Forks Avenue in the rain, and went back inside, and Charlie was sitting at the kitchen table with a fresh beer looking at the pan on the stove.

"She's a good kid," Charlie said, without looking up.

"Yeah," said Seth.

"Scared me half to death."

"Yeah."

"You love her?"

Seth looked at the bead on his wrist.

"Yeah," he said.

Charlie nodded slowly, and drank his beer, and didn't say anything else.

### V. Elk

*Forks Avenue — Tuesday, November 21, 2006*

The diner did a decent fry-up and a better pie, and on the fourth morning Harry had settled on the booth in the back corner, the one with the wall behind it and a clear line to the door, the till, and the kitchen pass-through.

He'd have taken that booth anyway. It was the booth the training would have put him in. But it also had the best view of the pie case, so he could tell himself it was about the pie.

"Morning, hon." The waitress was called Darlene. Fifties, reading glasses on a beaded chain, a voice that had been through forty years of Marlboro Lights and come out the other side cheerful. She had his coffee down in front of him before he'd got his coat off. Black. She'd learned that on day one. "Same as yesterday?"

"Same as yesterday, Darlene. And whatever the pie is."

"Marionberry."

"I don't know what that is."

"It's a blackberry that went to college." She wrote on her pad. "Breakfast pie. You know that's not a normal thing to eat."

"It's got fruit in it. It's practically a salad."

She laughed and went off toward the kitchen, and Harry wrapped both hands round the mug and looked out the window at Forks.

It was raining. It had been raining since he got here on Friday, a fine grey persistent rain that didn't so much fall as hang about in the air waiting for you to walk into it. Across the road a log truck went past toward the 101 with its wipers going. The hardware shop was opening. Two kids in hoodies were waiting at the bus stop, sharing a cigarette like it was contraband, which it was, at that age.

Population about three thousand and change. One main road. One high school. One small hospital, where a Dr. Carlisle Cullen worked days and had a reputation for a bedside manner nurses talked about in the car park. One police station, with a chief and three deputies and a single cell that, according to the local paper's police blotter, had last held somebody on Halloween, for urinating on a war memorial.

Four days. He had the town about half mapped. Who opened when. Which cars belonged in which driveways. Which roads went somewhere and which went out into the trees and stopped. The shape of the place, and the rhythm of it, so that when something came along that didn't fit, he'd know.

The legend was simple, because the simple ones held. He was Jim Page, a field contractor for a private conservation trust out of Seattle, up for the winter to do a population survey on the Roosevelt elk in the national forest. He had a business card. He had a clipboard with tally sheets that were half filled in with numbers that would stand up to anybody who knew anything about elk, because he'd spent a night in September learning enough about elk to do it. He had a truck with a trust logo on the door, a fleece with the same logo, and a pair of good binoculars on the dashboard. He was English, which explained the accent, and odd, which explained everything else. People in small towns didn't ask a lot of questions of an Englishman who counted elk. They'd already decided he was eccentric. It did half the work for him.

He didn't hide his face. He'd been told once by a man who'd done this job for forty years that the best disguise you'd ever own was being boring in a place where people saw you every day. So he was boring. He was the elk man. He ate pie for breakfast and tipped well and said *cheers* when Darlene brought the coffee, and in another week he'd be furniture.

Darlene came back with eggs, bacon, hash browns, toast, and a thick wedge of something dark purple on a separate plate, with a scoop of vanilla ice cream going soft on top.

"I didn't ask for ice cream," Harry said.

"You didn't have to." She topped up his mug. "You look like a man who's been sleeping in a tent."

"I have been sleeping in a tent."

"In *November*? Up there?" She shook her head. "You're out of your mind."

"Elk don't count themselves, Darlene."

"Elk don't care if they're counted." She went off again, shaking her head, and Harry started on the pie first, because he was a grown man and nobody could stop him.

It was good pie. Better than good. He'd have to see if she'd sell him a whole one.

The bell over the door went.

He knew who it was before he looked, because he'd seen the cruiser pull into the space out front through the rain on the window, and because Darlene said, "Morning, Chief," in a different voice from the one she used for everyone else, lower and fonder, like he was a nephew who'd been through something.

Charlie Swan. Chief of police. Late forties. Brown hair going grey at the temples, a moustache, the build of a man who'd been fit once and was now fit enough. Grey uniform jacket. Hat in his hand, rain on his shoulders. He looked tired. According to the paper he'd been widowed by divorce, if that was a thing, a long time ago, and his daughter had married into the Cullen family in August, and Harry had read that line in the archive of the *Forks Forum* twice, because if you were going to marry into a family of vampires, it would be useful to know whether your father was aware of it.

Swan sat down at the counter. Darlene put a coffee in front of him. They talked in low voices for a minute about somebody called Sue.

Then Swan turned round on his stool and looked straight across the diner at Harry.

It was a cop's look. Harry had been on the receiving end of a few. This one was an inventory. Clothes, hands, face, the plates on the table. The fleece with the trust logo on it. The booth Harry had picked, back to the wall, eye on the door. That last bit, Harry saw him note, and file.

*All right,* Harry thought. *Good for you, Chief.*

Swan picked up his coffee and came over.

"Morning," he said. "Mind if I—?"

"Go ahead."

Swan didn't sit. He stood at the end of the table with the mug in one hand. Up close he smelled of rain, aftershave, and gun oil. "You're the elk guy," he said.

"Word travels."

"Town of three thousand." Swan's eyes went to the plate. "Pie for breakfast?"

"It's practically a salad."

That got the moustache to move a fraction. "Charlie Swan. I'm the chief here."

"Jim Page." Harry wiped his hand and held it out, and Swan shook it, and Harry let his eyes rest on Swan's eyes, friendly, and went in.

He did it the way he did everything now, without a word or a wand or a breath out of place, the lightest possible touch. The surface of a mind. What was on top. It didn't take more than a second, and nobody had ever felt him do it who hadn't been trained to.

What he got was fog.

He blinked, which was the only sign of it, and covered it by reaching for his coffee.

There was a mind there. It was obviously there. He could feel the shape of it, the size, the way he could feel furniture in a dark room. But it was like trying to read a page through a frosted window. Feeling, colour, mood. Tired. A little worried. Fond of the waitress. Something heavy underneath, about family, about his daughter, a big dense knot of it like a stone in a river. But the words, the pictures, the actual content, were blurred out so badly that Harry couldn't have told you what the man had eaten for dinner last night.

He knew what Occlumency felt like. A wall, or a maze, or a trick of misdirection, and always something a mind did on purpose. Swan wasn't trying to do anything. The fog came with the house.

Interesting.

He filed it. He didn't push. You didn't push a police chief in a diner on a Tuesday.

"Where are you camped?" Swan asked. Cop question, asked like small talk.

"Off Forest Road 29. Up past the Sol Duc turnoff." That was near enough to true that the truck would be seen in the right places. "Plenty of elk up there."

"Plenty of everything up there." Swan drank some coffee. "You hear about the hikers?"

"I did."

"Couple from Olympia. Went up the Hoh River trail on the eleventh and never came out. Search and rescue called it Sunday." Swan's face had shut down a little, into a professional flatness Harry recognised from Auror briefings. "Those trails get bad this time of year. People slip. River takes them."

"Rivers do that."

"Yeah." Swan looked down at his coffee. Then up again. "And we've had some bear sightings. Out past the reservation, up around the national forest. Big ones."

Harry kept his face pleasant. "Bears."

"Big ones," Swan repeated. And something went over his face there, very quick, a flicker of a man thinking about something he'd decided not to think about. Harry couldn't read it through the fog. He could see it, though. "Just be careful out there, Mr. Page. That's all. If you see something big, you stay in your truck."

"I'll keep it in mind, Chief."

"And you call it in."

"I will."

Swan nodded, and looked at him for a beat longer, the inventory look again, and then went back to the counter. He finished his coffee in about four minutes, put some money down, said something to Darlene that made her swat him with a menu, and went out into the rain.

Harry watched the cruiser pull out and turn north.

*Big ones,* he thought. And then, because he'd read the Department's file on this place three times on the flight over, and the file had a section on the Quileute tribe, and their legends, and a footnote about the old treaty line that some of the Department's anthropologists had found in a dusty 1930s Ministry correspondence and been unable to explain: *Well. That's interesting too.*

He finished his breakfast. He finished the pie. He asked Darlene if she'd sell him a whole one, and she said she'd have one boxed up for him tomorrow if he came in before nine, and he left her a tip that would get him the best pie in the case for the rest of his life, and went out to the truck.

---

The truck was a 1979 Ford F-250, black, high off the ground, with a dent in the tailgate and a bench seat that had been reupholstered by somebody with more enthusiasm than skill. He'd bought it for cash off a man in Port Angeles on Friday afternoon. The trust logo on the door was a magnetic decal. The cassette deck worked. That had been non-negotiable.

He put in *Physical Graffiti* and pulled out onto Forks Avenue, and drove the town.

He did it every morning, a different loop each time, the way they'd taught him at the Department's place in the Cairngorms a long time ago: a route that went somewhere that made sense for his legend and doubled back on itself enough to tell him if anything was doubling back with it. Past the high school, where the students were going in. Past the hospital, where a black Mercedes sat in the space marked for staff. Dr. Cullen was in. Past the police station. Out east along the river.

East end of Calawah Way.

He slowed down a bit there. He'd been slowing down there every morning since Saturday.

The road bent along the river. The houses thinned out. There was a blue house past the bend, a small one, old, with a back yard that ran up to the trees. The front porch had been repaired, recently and well. A new cedar step on the left side, still pale against the weathered wood around it. An old green Ford pickup in the drive.

And over the whole property, faint as the smell of rain, a ward.

It was good work. That was the first thing he'd noticed on Saturday, before he'd noticed anything else. Quiet. Well laid. Somebody who'd been taught properly and practised a lot. A standard household set, mostly: a Muggle-repelling charm turned down low so it wouldn't make the neighbours feel odd, a fire ward, a weather charm on the roof. And under those, at the property lines, something sharper. A tripwire. The kind of thing you put up if you'd spent time in places where things came in through doors that weren't doors.

A witch or a wizard lived in that house. A good one.

Which on its own was nothing. There were witches and wizards living quietly in Muggle towns in every county in America, and in every county in Britain, and most of them had never done anything more remarkable than a cleaning charm. He'd put it on his list. He'd come and have a proper look when the net was finished and the town was mapped and he could do it without stepping on anything. He wasn't going to go poking at a stranger's wards in daylight on a residential street because he was curious.

It was on the list. It was third on the list.

He drove past without slowing any further, and turned up the 101, and headed north toward the forest with Robert Plant wailing out of the speakers.

---

He checked six anchors that morning.

The net had taken him three nights. Forty-one anchor points in a rough ring from Neah Bay down the coast past La Push to Kalaloch, inland along the Hoh and the Bogachiel, north up the Sol Duc to the edges of the Elwha, and back around toward Port Angeles. Forty-one points, and between them a lattice of wardlines that ran under the forest floor like fungus, invisible, dormant, tasting the ground.

It was the best thing he'd ever built in the field. It had taken eight years to be able to build it.

He pulled off on a gravel turnout above the Sol Duc and walked about three hundred yards into the trees, to a western hemlock with a root that stuck up out of the moss like a knuckle. Under the moss, cut into the root with a goblin-made blade, were nine runes. Nobody would ever find them. There was a notice-me-not on the tree that would make a dog walk round it.

He crouched and put two fingers on the moss over the runes.

The anchor hummed under his skin, a low steady note like the string of a cello. It was awake. It was listening. Its threads ran off into the dark in four directions, to four other anchors miles away, and through them to all the others. He could feel the whole net from here if he wanted, the way you could feel all your fingers at once if you thought about it.

Nothing. It had caught nothing yet.

He hadn't expected it to. Not this soon.

He stayed crouched there for a minute in the dripping quiet, because he liked it, and because it was the first time in about a week he hadn't had to be anybody.

Eight years. He'd been eighteen when the war ended, and he'd walked out of the ruins of the Great Hall with his own wand mended in his pocket, the Elder Wand on its way back to a white tomb, and a body count behind him and absolutely no idea what to do with the rest of his life. Kingsley had offered him the Aurors. Everybody had assumed he'd take it. He'd assumed he'd take it, for a few months.

Then he'd realised that he didn't understand any of it. Magic. Any of it. He'd been fighting with it for seven years like a man fighting with a pistol he'd found on the floor, and he'd won, mostly on luck and on other people dying for him, and he didn't understand how a single spell he'd ever cast actually worked. Why the words mattered. Why the wand mattered. What was happening in the space between wanting something and having it happen. Nobody he asked knew either, not really. They knew what to do. They didn't know why.

He'd wanted to know why. It had seemed, after everything, like the only thing worth wanting.

So he'd gone and found out. Two years in the library at Hogwarts and the restricted archives at the Ministry, and every library that would let him in after that, and a few that hadn't. A year in Cairo with a goblin warder who'd hated him on sight and taught him everything anyway, because Harry kept coming back. Eight months in a monastery in the Hindu Kush, learning to do without the wand, which was the hardest thing he'd ever done in his life, harder than dying. Runes. Arithmancy. Potions, which he'd been rubbish at for six years at school and turned out to be quite good at once nobody was standing over his cauldron sneering. Wards. Curses. Old magic and new. Legilimency, finally, properly, the thing Snape had tried to teach him with all the patience of a man holding someone's head underwater, and which turned out to be easy once he stopped being afraid of it. Occlumency, which turned out to be easy once he stopped being sixteen.

He'd invented a few things, along the way. That was the bit nobody had expected, him least of all. You couldn't spend that long taking magic apart without starting to see how you'd put it back together differently. A handful of spells. A couple of ward structures. A way of keying an enchantment to a substance instead of a person. This net was one of those.

Saul Croaker had come to see him in the winter of 2002, in a rented room above a chemist's in Edinburgh, and sat in the only chair and looked at the walls, which were covered with Harry's diagrams, for about twenty minutes without saying anything.

Then he'd said, *We'd like you to come and work for us.*

And Harry, who'd been wondering for a year where he was going to find the resources to do half of what he wanted to do, had said yes.

Four years in the Department of Mysteries. He had a desk in a room nobody else could find. He had a budget that didn't appear on any Ministry ledger. He had access to things that had been sealed since before the Statute. He had a licence for all three of the Unforgivable Curses, which every Unspeakable on field service carried and nobody outside the Department knew about. He had, as far as he could tell, more freedom than any other witch or wizard in Britain, on the understanding that he would never talk about any of it with anybody, ever.

The Department, as far as Harry had ever been able to work out, did two things. It found out how the world worked before anybody else did. And when some part of the world turned out to be dangerous, it made sure Britain had already thought of what to do about it, quietly, well in advance, without asking anyone's permission. Half laboratory, half intelligence service. The people on the ninth level had been arguing for three hundred years about which half was the real job. Harry thought it was a stupid argument. You couldn't do either one without the other.

He didn't mind that last bit as much as he'd thought he would. It turned out he'd spent his whole childhood not being able to tell anyone what was really going on. He was good at it.

He took his fingers off the anchor. The hum faded out of his skin.

He went back to the truck and drove to the next one.

---

The file had been on his desk on the first of September.

It was thin, for a Department file. That was how he'd known it was interesting. The thick ones were about things the Department had been studying for three hundred years and still didn't understand. The thin ones were about things they'd just found.

The Department kept an instrument in a room on the ninth level that Harry was not allowed to describe, which measured, roughly speaking, the pressure on the world's secrets. The weight of things that were hidden, pushing against the people who might see them. Most days its needle sat still. On a day in March, at about noon Central European Time, it had kicked so hard it cracked the glass.

The Department had sent someone. Two someones, actually, and they'd found a walled hill town in Tuscany on a festival day, with the streets full of tourists in red cloaks and plastic fangs, and a teenage boy in the shade of a clock tower who'd been about to step into the noon sun and show a thousand people something they were never meant to see. A girl had stopped him. She'd run across the square and thrown herself at him at the last possible second. The two Unspeakables had watched from a café terrace as men in dark cloaks walked the boy and the girl into an alley and through a door in the old walls, and the needle back in London had settled down again.

That was the first part of the file.

The second part was what was under the town.

It had taken the Department six months, and two people it didn't talk about anymore, to put that part together. An old coven. Very old. Three of them at the centre, and a guard around them of perhaps thirty, and a pattern of tourists who went down into the city's underground on organised tours and didn't come back up. Forty or fifty at a time. Every few weeks. For as long as anybody could tell. The Department had found a cistern under the town with the bones of some of them in it.

They were vampires. The Department knew that much, and knew that no catalogue in the building had a page for them. Daylight made them shine. Stakes did nothing. They didn't rot away or turn to ash. They moved faster than any creature in the books. They were harder than stone. And they ruled their kind across the whole world, from that town on that hill, and their kind had been hiding from wizards for three thousand years about as successfully as wizards had been hiding from Muggles.

Croaker's note on the front of the file had been two sentences.

*We are going to have to do something about the Italians. We need to know how they work.*

And under that, in a different ink, added later: *The boy at the clock tower lives in Washington State. Start there.*

So here he was. On foreign soil, in a country whose wizarding government would very much like to know what a British Unspeakable thought he was doing laying a snare net across forty miles of its national forest without a word to anyone, which, as far as Harry was concerned, was entirely their problem. The Department had operated on foreign soil without permission since before America had been a country. It wasn't about to stop now.

The boy's family were the Cullens. Seven of them according to the file and eight by Harry's own count from the ridge, plus a small child with bronze curls that the file didn't mention at all, which he'd noted, and underlined. Gold eyes instead of red. They lived in a big glass house in the trees and worked and paid their taxes and hunted elk, which was a coincidence Harry had found very funny on the plane. The Department's assessment was short. *Anomalous. Possibly cooperative. Higher value alive.* He had a direct instruction not to touch them. He'd been watching them from a ridge a mile and a half off for two afternoons, with a pair of Omnioculars and a Disillusionment Charm and every ward he owned, and he had no intention of touching them.

What he wanted was one of the others. The red-eyed ones. The ones that fed on people.

The file had a section on that too. The Seattle murders, spring and early summer of 2006. Dozens dead, bodies drained. The Muggle police had called it a gang war, and then a serial killer, and then stopped calling it anything at all when it stopped. The Department had read the Muggle coroner's reports and had a much better idea. Something had been happening in this corner of the world. Things like that came to places where there were already things like that. A coven living openly in the woods would draw the others the way lights drew moths. Nomads, the file called them, borrowing a word from somewhere. Passing through. Hunting.

So he'd built a net that would catch the moths, and leave the lights alone.

He'd keyed the whole thing to human blood inside a body that had no heartbeat. Blood moving through a thing that didn't need it to move. Plain human blood would have had every hunter and logger and lost tourist on the peninsula hanging upside down from a tree. It had taken him two weeks of work in the Department, with a scraping of venom from a corpse fished out of that cistern under Volterra, to get the key right, and he was still slightly prouder of it than was strictly professional.

The Cullens didn't drink people. The file said so, and he'd confirmed it in two afternoons on the ridge, watching them come back from the woods with their eyes gone gold and bright. They'd walk through his net like it wasn't there. The others wouldn't.

And when one of the others came along — and one would; one always did — the net would close on it like a fist, and ring a bell in his tent, and he'd go and collect it.

He checked the sixth anchor of the morning a little after one, on a ridge above the Bogachiel in a thin cold rain, and drove back down to the 101 with the cassette on the B-side, and thought about the cistern.

It had had children's bones in it.

He didn't think about it for long. He'd found that was best. He just let it sit there, at the back of everything, where it kept him honest.

### VI. The North Line

*Olympic National Forest, above the Sol Duc River — Saturday, November 25, 2006, 4:11 p.m.*

The light was going.

It went early this time of year, and it went fast, the grey of the afternoon thickening into a deeper grey and then a blue that wasn't really a color so much as an absence of one. Under the trees it was already nearly dark. Leah didn't need the light. She ran the north line by smell and memory and the feel of the ground under her paws, the same as she'd run it two hundred times since September, up from the river along the old logging spurs and over the ridges and down into the creek bottoms where the ferns stood shoulder-high to a human and came up to about her knee.

Seth was half a mile east, running parallel. She could feel him there, in the back of her head, the way you could feel somebody sitting next to you on a couch with your eyes shut.

He was thinking about Layla. Of course he was thinking about Layla. He'd been thinking about Layla for fifty-four days straight, and Leah had been forced to listen to roughly forty-nine of them.

*Can you not,* she thought.

*I'm not doing anything.*

*You're thinking about her hair.*

*I'm allowed to think about her hair.*

*You're thinking about it in a really specific way, Seth. I'm your sister.*

He tried to think about something else. He landed on the Thanksgiving leftovers in their mother's fridge, which was marginally better. Leah snorted and kept running.

Alice had come out to the cottage on Thursday afternoon with the look on her face she got when she'd seen something she didn't like. Leah hadn't been there. Jacob had, and Jacob had phased after, and Leah had gotten the whole thing secondhand through his eyes: Alice standing in the doorway of the little stone cottage with her arms around herself, talking fast. *A man. Alone. Dark hair, red eyes. He's wearing a blue rain jacket that isn't his. He's coming down from the north, along the coast, from Canada. He's been hunting on the way.* And then the part that had made Jacob's hackles go up even in human form: *He's going to cross into the park sometime Saturday. I see him in the trees above the Sol Duc. And then—* and Alice had frowned and shaken her head like a dog with water in its ears. *And then it goes all wrong. It's static. I can't see past it.*

*That's us,* Jacob had said. *That's the pack. You never see us.*

*Maybe,* Alice had said. *Maybe. It doesn't feel like you. It feels like the witch, sort of. Only worse.*

Nobody had known what to do with that. So Jacob had done the only thing they knew how to do, which was put Leah and Seth on the north line on Saturday afternoon and tell them to call him the second they smelled anything.

Jacob was at the big glass house now, in human form, sitting on the living room floor eating the last of Sue's turkey off a paper plate while the baby built towers out of wooden blocks, because Jacob was hopeless. He'd phase the second anybody needed him.

Leah came up over a ridge, dropped into a gully, crossed a creek in one jump without getting her paws wet, and caught it.

She stopped so fast her claws tore up the moss.

*Seth.*

She felt him stop too, half a mile away. *Yeah. Yeah, I got it.*

It was coming down off the higher ground to the north and east. A thin, fresh thread of it, laid across the forest like a wire: that sweet, rotten, chemical stench that burned the inside of her nose and made her eyes water and made every hair on her body stand straight up. Vampire. Not one of the Cullens. She knew all of their scents by now, every one of them, the way she knew the smell of her mother's kitchen. This was someone else.

And under the sweetness, faint and coppery and terrible, blood.

Human blood. Fresh. A day old, maybe two.

*He fed,* Seth thought. His thought had gone very quiet. *He fed on somebody.*

*Yeah.* Leah's lips peeled back from her teeth. *Where are you?*

*Coming.*

*Don't take the creek. Go up over the shoulder. We'll pinch him.*

She didn't wait for him. She was already moving.

---

She caught up to the scent in the next valley and it was so strong now it was like running through smoke. He'd come down this way not ten minutes ago. He'd stopped by a fallen cedar; she could smell where he'd leaned against it. She could smell the jacket, too, nylon and somebody else's sweat and somebody else's deodorant. A man's. A dead man's, probably.

She went up the far side of the valley in three long bounds and at the top of the rise she saw him.

He was about two hundred yards ahead of her, in the open under the high canopy of an old stand of Douglas fir, moving at a walk, which for one of them still looked like a jog. Dark hair, long, tied back. A blue rain jacket, too big for him in the shoulders. He was turning his head left and right, slow, the way they did when they were scenting for something.

He turned his head and saw her.

For one second they looked at each other across two hundred yards of dark forest: a grey wolf the size of a horse on a ridge, and a dead man in a dead man's coat. His eyes were red. Even at that distance she could see it. Bright, fresh red, like a traffic light.

Then Seth came over the shoulder of the hill to the east, sandy and huge and fast, and the vampire's head snapped toward him, and he did the math.

He ran.

*He's running,* Seth thought, startled. *Leah, he's running away.*

*Then chase him, genius.*

She went off the ridge like she'd been fired out of it.

There was nothing in the world like this. Leah would never say it out loud to anybody, but there was nothing. Sam had taken a lot of things from her, and the wolf had taken more, and she hated it, she hated what it had made her and what it had cost her and what it meant about the rest of her life. But this — the ground tearing past under her paws, the air coming in so cold it burned, the scent in her nose and her body stretched out long and low and every muscle in it doing exactly what she asked of it — this, she didn't hate. This was hers. She was the fastest thing in either pack. She was faster than Jacob. She was faster than Sam.

And the vampire was fast, they were all fast, but he wasn't faster than her.

She closed. A hundred and fifty yards. A hundred. He was going downhill, north and east, through the old firs, weaving between the trunks so fast he blurred, leaping fallen logs without seeming to touch them. Seth was coming in on his right flank, pushing him, bending him left. Leah could hear him, the sound of his paws, the sound of his breath, in her ears and in her head at once. *Left, left, I've got the right—*

Eighty yards. She could hear the vampire now too. They didn't breathe when they ran, not really, but his jacket rustled, and his feet hit the ground with little dry cracks like somebody snapping twigs. He looked back over his shoulder and she saw his face. He was scared. Good.

Fifty yards.

The trees opened up ahead. A clearing. Old logging landing, maybe, from back in the seventies, grown up in alder and salal and long grass gone brown with the cold. The vampire burst out into it and Leah was thirty yards behind him and closing, closing, and she gathered herself for the last push. She could already taste it. She was going to take him off his feet at the far edge of the clearing, take him down into the grass, and then Seth would be there, and they would pull him apart, and Jacob would come with a lighter, and that would be the end of him.

Twenty yards.

The vampire was halfway across the clearing.

There was a sound.

It was a strange sound, sharp and deep at once, like a bowstring being let go, or a sapling that had been bent all the way to the ground and then released. *Thrum.* She felt it in her teeth.

The vampire's left leg went out from under him.

It went *up*. That was the only way she could think about it after. Something took him by the ankle and his leg went straight up into the air as though it had been yanked by a rope, and the rest of him went after it, and in less time than it took her to blink he was hanging upside down twelve feet above the clearing with his arms pinned straight at his sides, swinging gently, from nothing at all.

Leah hit the ground with all four paws and dug in so hard she plowed up a furrow of dirt and grass three yards long. Seth came in off the right side and nearly ran into her, scrabbled, twisted, and ended up sprawled half on his side in the salal with his legs going in four directions.

*What,* Seth thought. *What. What. What.*

Leah didn't think anything for a second. She just stared.

The vampire hung there.

He wasn't moving. He wasn't moving *at all*. They never moved unless they wanted to, so stillness wasn't strange on its own; she'd watched the Cullens sit like statues for an hour. But this was different. He'd been running. He'd been mid-stride. And now he was hanging there rigid as a plank, straight as a dropped pencil, with his long dark hair hanging down toward the grass and his face frozen in the middle of a look back over his shoulder that would never finish. His mouth was half-open. His red eyes were open and they did not move. They did not track her. The blue rain jacket had slid down around his chest and his shirt had come untucked and there was a dark stain on it that she could smell from here.

He swung a little. That was the only motion. Back and forth, very slowly, like a pendulum winding down.

Leah looked up.

There was something there. Above his ankle. If she looked straight at it there was nothing, only air and the dark branches of the alders beyond. But if she looked a little to the side of it, there was a line. A faint wavering in the air, like heat coming off a road in August, running straight up from the vampire's boot into the dark until she lost it.

*Is he dead?* Seth had rolled to his feet. He came up beside her, slow, low, his ears pinned. *Leah, is he—*

*They don't die. Not like that.*

*He's not moving.*

*I can see he's not moving.*

She walked forward. She didn't want to. Every instinct she had was telling her to get out of this clearing, to run, to go back over the ridge and howl for Jacob and not come back. But she was the one who went first. She was always the one who went first.

She walked forward through the dead grass until she was directly under him. His face hung about four feet above her head. Up close, the stink was so strong her eyes streamed. She bared her teeth at him. She growled, low, right in his face.

He didn't blink. Nothing in his face changed at all. It was like growling at a photograph.

And under the vampire stink, she smelled something else.

It took her a second. It was all around the clearing, faint, like ozone after a storm. That sharp, crackling, too-clean smell. She'd smelled it a lot lately, through Seth's nose and her own. On Seth's clothes, on his skin, every time he came back from the blue house on Calawah Way. On the witch.

Only this was more. Much more. Layla smelled like the air before a lightning strike. This smelled like standing next to the place the lightning had hit.

*Seth,* Leah thought slowly. *This smells like your girlfriend.*

*It doesn't smell like Layla.* But she could feel him smelling it, feel him check, and she felt him come to the same place she had and go cold. *It smells like... more than Layla. Leah. It smells like way more than Layla.*

The ferns on the far side of the clearing bent flat.

All of them at once, in a ring about ten feet across, as if a wind had come straight down out of the sky and pressed them to the ground. There was a sound like a heavy door being shut a long way off. A soft *whump*. Leah felt it in her ears.

And there was a man standing in the clearing.

He hadn't walked there. He hadn't come out of the trees. One second there was dead grass and flattened ferns and the next there was a man in the middle of them, as if he'd always been there and she hadn't noticed him until now.

He was tall. Six-two, maybe. Broad through the shoulders. Dark hair cut short. He was wearing a charcoal suit, a white shirt with the collar open and no tie, and a long black wool overcoat that came down past his knees. Black leather gloves. Black boots, heavy ones, with mud on them, which didn't go with the suit. He looked about twenty-six, twenty-seven. He had a couple days of dark stubble on his jaw. He had green eyes. She could see that even in the failing light, because they caught what little light there was and held it.

And he was holding a paper coffee cup from the diner on Forks Avenue, with a plastic lid on it, steam coming out of the little hole.

Leah's nose went to work without asking her. Coffee. Wool. Leather. Gun oil, faint. Earth. Pine. Cool stone, like the inside of a cave. And under that, under everything, something that made the fur stand up along the whole length of her spine. She didn't have a word for it. It was something like the smell of the pack mind, if the pack mind had a smell. Something like *wolf*. But it wasn't any wolf she knew. It wasn't anything she knew at all.

He was giving off heat. She could feel it from here, across the clearing, the way she could feel it off Seth, off Jacob. Way too much heat for a man standing in the cold. And his heart—

His heart was going about sixty beats a minute. Slow and steady and bored. He was standing in a dark clearing with two wolves the size of horses, and his heart was beating like he was waiting for a bus.

He looked at the vampire hanging upside down. He looked at Leah, standing right under it with her teeth bared. He looked at Seth.

He took a sip of his coffee.

"Evening," he said.

His voice was English. Like a guy on the BBC. Like somebody from a movie.

He reached into his coat with his free hand. Leah dropped into a crouch, every muscle locking, and beside her Seth did the same. The man didn't seem to notice. He came out with a small black leather wallet, and flipped it open one-handed, and held it up toward them.

There was a badge in it. A gold badge with an eagle on it, and under the badge an ID card with his photograph, and in big blue letters across the top of the card: **U.S. DEPARTMENT OF HOMELAND SECURITY**.

"This situation is contained," the man said pleasantly. "I'm going to have to ask you, please, to vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

Leah stared at him.

*Is he serious,* she thought. *Is he actually serious right now.*

*Leah.* Seth's thought was faint and incredulous. *That's an FBI badge.*

*It's Homeland Security. It says it right on it.*

*It says FBI. Right there. Federal Bureau of Investigation. It's got the little—*

"It says whatever it needs to," the man said, and flipped the wallet shut, and put it away.

Leah went very, very still.

The man didn't look at her. He'd turned his attention back to the vampire. He set his coffee cup down on the flat top of an old stump at the edge of the clearing, carefully, like he didn't want to spill it. Then he walked over until he was standing about six feet from Leah, close enough that she could have had his arm off at the shoulder in less than a second, and he didn't look at her at all. He looked up at the vampire, hanging there with its arms pinned at its sides and its face frozen.

He lifted his right hand and made a little motion with two fingers. A flick. Like he was flicking a crumb off a table.

The shimmer in the air above the vampire's boot went out.

The vampire dropped. Twelve feet, straight down, headfirst. He hit the ground beside Leah with a sound like a bag of cement coming off the back of a truck. The earth jumped under her paws. He lay there on his face in the dead grass, stiff as a board, arms still at his sides, legs still together, not moving. His jacket had ridden up around his armpits.

The man bent down, got one gloved hand in the back of the vampire's belt and the other in the collar of the stolen jacket, and lifted.

He lifted a vampire. A grown man, and a vampire, so heavier than a grown man, by a lot; she'd felt how much they weighed when they hit things. He lifted it off the ground like it was nothing much and swung it up and tucked it under his right arm, horizontal, rigid, the head sticking out in front of him and the boots sticking out behind, the way a guy on the beach at La Push carried a surfboard down to the water.

Then he walked back over to the stump and picked up his coffee with his left hand.

*Leah,* Seth thought, in a very small voice. *He's holding it like a surfboard.*

*I can see that, Seth.*

*He's holding a vampire like a surfboard.*

"It's a bit heavier than a surfboard," the man said. He took a sip of coffee. "And it's a lot less fun at the beach. But I take the point."

The clearing was absolutely silent.

Leah felt her own thoughts stop. All of them. Just stop, like a car going off the road and the engine cutting out.

Then they came back, fast, all at once. *He heard that. He heard that. Seth, he heard—*

"Yes," said the man. "I did."

*Get out,* Leah thought, as hard as she could, as loud as she could. *Get out of my head.*

"Sorry, Lassie." He didn't sound sorry. He sounded mildly interested, like a man checking a crossword clue. "Occupational hazard. I do it to everyone. Be rude to leave you out."

*Lassie?*

"You've got the look." He tipped his coffee cup toward her, a little salute. "You'd pull a kid out of a well and then bite him for being stupid enough to fall in."

Seth made a noise. It came out loud in the clearing, a wheezing huff through his nose, and in Leah's head it was a whole helpless explosion of laughter that he tried to cram down and couldn't.

*Seth. Do not laugh.*

*I'm not laughing.*

*You are laughing.*

*It's a little funny, Leah. It's a little bit funny. What am I?* That last thought went past her, straight at the man. *What am I, then?*

*Seth!*

"You're Balto," the man said.

*Who's Balto?*

"Sled dog. 1925. Diphtheria outbreak in Nome, Alaska, middle of winter, no way in but by dog team. Balto led the last leg. Ran the serum in through a blizzard and saved the town." The man shrugged, which was strange to watch with a vampire under his arm. "Got a statue in Central Park. Take the compliment."

Seth's whole mind went pink with pleasure. *...Cool.*

*Seth, don't be flattered by him!*

*He gave me a statue dog, Leah, I'm a little flattered.*

Leah did the only thing she could think of. She tried to think of nothing. She made her mind into a wall. A big blank grey wall, like the cinder-block wall at the back of the high school, with nothing on it, nothing behind it, nothing at all.

It didn't work very well. The harder she tried not to think of anything, the more things came crowding up behind the wall, the way they always did when she tried not to think. The pack. Jacob. The Cullens. The glass house. The baby. And under all of that, the thing that was always under all of that, the thing that came up whenever she was scared or tired or not paying attention. Sam's face. Sam's face the day he came back. Sam's face looking at Emily.

The man's eyes came around to her and stopped.

He looked at her for a second. Two. Something moved in his face, very slight. A little narrowing around the eyes.

She waited for it. She waited for him to say it, to say the name, to make some joke about it. She knew he'd seen. She could feel that he'd seen. She braced herself for it like a hit.

He didn't say anything.

He looked away from her, back at the forest, and took another sip of coffee, and when he spoke again it was to the trees. "Wall's a decent try," he said. "Most people picture a wall. You'd want to practise, though."

Leah stood there with her teeth bared and her heart going like a hammer, and hated him. She hated him for seeing. She hated him so much it burned.

And a small part of her, a part she would never, ever admit was there, hated him a fraction less for keeping his mouth shut about it.

*What are you going to do with him?* Seth asked. He'd crept up beside her, low, ears flat, eyes on the vampire under the man's arm.

"Ask him some questions."

*He killed somebody.* That was Leah. She hadn't meant to think it at him. It came out anyway. *I can smell it on him. He fed. Yesterday, the day before. He killed somebody.*

"Three somebodies, by my count. A couple from Olympia on the Hoh River trail about two weeks ago. And somebody else this week, across the Strait on the Canadian side, outside Port Renfrew, who hasn't been reported missing yet." The man's voice didn't change at all when he said it. "I know."

*So kill him.*

"Eventually," the man said.

He said it the same way he'd said *evening*. Leah felt the fur along her back come up another inch.

*What are you?* she thought at him. *What the hell are you?*

"Federal," the man said.

*That's not an answer.*

"It's the one you're getting."

He was turning, starting to move, the vampire under his arm and the coffee in his hand, like the conversation was over. Like he'd said his piece and they could go home now, and he'd go wherever he was going, and that would be that.

And beside her, Seth, who had never in his life been able to keep a thought to himself, put it together.

She felt him do it. She felt the pieces go click in his head like a seatbelt. The man appearing out of thin air. The vampire hanging from nothing. The lightning smell everywhere. The wallet that said what it needed to. The *flick*. That little flick of his fingers, which Seth had seen before, dozens of times, on a back porch, in a kitchen, in the living room of the glass house making birds out of vases.

*He's a wizard,* Seth thought. *Leah. He's like Layla. He's a wizard.*

The man stopped.

He'd been halfway through a step. He finished it, and then he stood still, with the vampire under his arm and the cup of coffee halfway up to his mouth, and didn't move for a second. Just stood there. Then he lowered the coffee cup. Slowly. All the way down to his side.

He turned around.

It was the same face. It was the same man, the same suit, the same English voice. But something had gone out of it, or come into it. For the first time since he'd appeared in the clearing, he looked at them like they were something other than furniture. He looked at Seth, and then at Leah, and then back at Seth, and his green eyes were very steady and very cold, and Leah suddenly understood that every single thing he'd done up to this point had been him being *relaxed*.

"Now *that*," he said quietly, "is a word you shouldn't have."

Nobody moved.

"Who taught it to you, Balto?"

Leah felt Seth try not to think about it. She felt him try so hard. She felt him slam into it head-first like a moth into a window, because the only way not to think about a thing is to think about not thinking about it, and the second he tried, there she was. Layla. Layla on the porch in the yellow raincoat. Layla in the grocery store reaching for the honey. Layla's face under a hundred floating candles. The blue house on Calawah Way.

The man's eyes narrowed.

"Blue house," he said. "East end of Calawah Way. New step on the porch." A pause. "Ah."

*Seth,* Leah thought, *stop thinking—*

*I can't, I can't, he's seen her, he's going to go after her, Leah, he's going to—*

*Seth—*

*She's up at the Thackeray place, she's alone, she's right up the hill, he's going to find her, he's going to go right there—*

*Seth, don't—*

But she felt it coming and she couldn't stop it. She felt him think it through in a half-second's panicked arithmetic. *He's already seen her. He knows where she lives. He's going to find her anyway, and she'll be alone, and she won't know he's coming. Better she comes here. Better she comes with her wand up. She's better at this than me. She's better at this than any of us.*

Seth ducked his head and closed his jaws on the leather cord around his left foreleg, and bit down on the silver bead until it cracked.

It tasted like copper and lightning. Leah tasted it with him.

The man watched him do it. He watched the whole thing, and his face did something complicated and tired, the face of a man watching someone back a car into a lamppost in slow motion.

"Oh, you *didn't*," he said.

He set the coffee cup back down on the stump.

### VII. Outclassed

*The Thackeray house, above the Sol Duc — 4:38 p.m.*

The cellar of the Thackeray house had been cursed by somebody who really, truly hated their relatives.

Layla had been working her way down through it for six weeks. The house above was mostly done: a damp, collapsing two-story farmhouse built in 1891 by a wizarding family from Ohio who'd come west for the timber and the solitude, and found both, and then spent thirty years slowly losing their minds in the rain. The upstairs had been hexed in the ordinary paranoid ways, jinxed doorknobs and biting floorboards and a staircase that tried to drop you into a pocket dimension the size of a breadbox. She'd cleared all of that by the end of October. The cellar was the real job. The cellar was where old Absalom Thackeray had sealed his family vault in 1926, two weeks before he died, with a set of interlocking curses so vindictive and so cleverly built that she'd sat on the bottom step the first time she'd seen them and laughed out loud with pure professional admiration.

She was on her knees in front of the vault door now with her lantern hung on a nail and her wand between her teeth, using both hands to draw a counter-sigil in salt on the flagstones, when the bead at her throat went hot.

Hot. A bright stab of heat right in the hollow of her collarbone, like somebody had pressed the end of a lit cigarette there.

She dropped the salt.

She was on her feet with the wand in her hand before the box hit the floor, and she knew where he was. The beacon did that. It was in her head now, like a pin in a map: two and a third miles west-northwest, downhill, in the trees above the river. She could feel him there, on the other end of the line, and she could feel that something was wrong, because he'd bitten it, and he'd never have bitten it unless something was very, very wrong.

She didn't stop to think. Thinking was what she'd do on the way.

She turned on the spot.

---

Apparating to a beacon was a curse-breaker's trick, and it wasn't pleasant. You didn't get to see where you were going. You fixed on the pin in the map and let it pull you, and you came out the other side wherever it was, and you prayed it wasn't the middle of a tree.

Layla came out the other side with a crack like a rifle shot, in long dead grass, in a clearing, in the near-dark.

She saw it all at once. She'd been trained to. You came into a tomb and you had about one second to read the room before the room read you.

A clearing. Old logging landing. Dead grass and alders. Seth, in wolf form, sandy and huge, crouched low in the grass to her left with the broken cord around his foreleg. Leah, grey, to Seth's left, all her teeth showing. And across from them, maybe fifteen yards from Layla, a man.

Tall. Long dark coat. No wand in his hand. A body tucked under his right arm. A *body*, rigid, a man in a blue rain jacket, carried under his arm the way you'd carry a plank of wood. He'd been turning toward the sound of her arrival, and he was looking at her, and his eyes were green, and they did not look surprised at all.

The wolves had their hackles up and their teeth bared, at him.

That was enough. That was all she needed.

She was already casting. She'd been casting since before her feet hit the ground. Her dueling coach at Ilvermorny used to say the best spell was the one that left your wand before you'd finished deciding to throw it, and she'd spent four years on the dueling team and two more on the regional circuit after graduation learning to do exactly that. She didn't say the incantation. She'd stopped saying incantations when she was sixteen.

A Stunner, the hardest she had. A bolt of red light as thick as her wrist that would have dropped a grown mountain troll.

The man lifted his left hand and flicked it away with one finger.

That was all. One finger, the index finger, a little sideways flick, the way you'd brush a fly off your sleeve. The Stunner bent. It bent in midair like it had hit a sheet of glass at an angle, and went screaming off sideways into the trees, and hit an alder at the edge of the clearing and blew a strip of bark off it the size of a car door.

He hadn't drawn a wand.

There was a cold small voice at the back of Layla's head that had kept her alive in a dozen tombs, and it said, very calmly: *He doesn't have a wand.*

She was already throwing the next ones. Three in a chain, a triple, the competition chain she'd won the Northeast Regionals with in 2004. *Expelliarmus* to strip him, *Incarcerous* to bind him, and riding underneath the other two, low and fast where you weren't looking, a Blasting Curse aimed at the ground at his feet. You couldn't block all three. That was the point of a triple. You blocked the high ones and the low one took your legs out, or you went for the low one and got stripped and tied up for your trouble.

The man watched them come.

The Disarming Charm hit the air a foot in front of his chest and stopped. Just stopped, hanging there, a little scarlet star crackling and spitting in the dark. The ropes came hissing out of her wand behind it and he moved one finger again and they went sideways and wrapped themselves twelve times around an old stump at the edge of the clearing, so tight the wood creaked. And the Blasting Curse, the low one, the one nobody ever saw, he didn't even look down at. It went into the ground at his feet and simply wasn't there anymore. No explosion. It went in, and the earth swallowed it, like a stone dropped in a pond.

Then he tapped the hanging Disarming Charm with his fingertip, almost gently, and it came back at her twice as fast as she'd sent it.

She got her shield up with about a tenth of a second to spare. The charm hit it like a fist and her arm went numb to the shoulder and her heels skidded back six inches in the grass.

It had been about three seconds.

Three seconds, and the cold small voice in the back of her head said, very clearly, the thing she would remember for the rest of her life: *You are going to lose this. You were never in it.*

She looked at him. Really looked, the way she'd look at a curse she'd never seen before.

He hadn't moved his feet. He was standing exactly where he'd been standing when she arrived, with his weight easy and his boots planted in the grass. He hadn't put down the body under his right arm. He hadn't said a word. His breathing hadn't changed. She could see it, in the cold, the little clouds coming out of his mouth, slow and regular as a sleeping man's.

She had dueled a lot of people. She had dueled a Wampus house champion who'd later gone on to the Auror program. She had dueled a man in a ruin outside Cuzco who'd been trying to rob her of an artifact and who'd fought dirty and fought to kill. She'd dueled her coach, who'd been ranked in the top hundred in the world in the eighties. She knew what it looked like when somebody was better than her. She'd been beaten before.

This was walking into the ocean.

Her coach had told the team about it once, junior year, late at night after a tournament. Three wizards, he'd said. In the last century. Three, that anybody knew about, who'd fought like that. Who caught spells and turned them over in their hands and put them down wherever they liked, like a child's toys. Dumbledore. Grindelwald. And the British one, the one who'd called himself Voldemort. *You'll never see anybody like that,* her coach had said. *None of you. Not in your lives. They're gone, all three of them. Nobody fights like that anymore.*

She was looking at it.

Seth and Leah went for him.

They went at the same time, from the left, the way she'd seen them move in the pack mind in Seth's memories: Leah high and Seth low, two wolves the size of horses coming off the ground at once, and Layla felt her heart stop, because she knew how fast they were and she knew what those jaws could do to a vampire, and the man was a man, and he was going to die.

The man didn't look at them.

They stopped in the air.

Both of them. Mid-leap, Leah six feet off the ground with her jaws open and Seth just behind her, stopped like they'd run into a wall of glass. They hung there for half a heartbeat. Then something took them both and put them down, hard and flat, on their sides in the dead grass ten feet in front of him. *Put*, the way you'd press a hand down on a puppy's back. And held there. Leah was snarling and fighting it, every muscle in her body straining; Layla could see her claws tearing up the grass and dirt as she tried to get her legs under her and couldn't. Seth was whining. They couldn't get up. They couldn't even lift their heads.

And he still hadn't looked at them.

He was looking at her.

Layla did the only thing she had left, which was everything.

She threw it all. Everything she'd learned in six years of tombs and vaults and cursed houses, every nasty thing she carried for the day she met something she couldn't talk her way past. A Bone-Breaker, low, at his knee. A Severing Charm at his throat, which she had never in her life thrown at a living person and which she threw now without hesitating. A hex of her own, a thing she'd built herself out of an old Incan ward-breaker in a hostel in Lima, which was designed to find the seams in any shield and pull them apart like a loose thread in a sweater. Fire, a gout of it, roaring. A Conjunctivitis Curse at his eyes. She threw them as fast as she'd ever thrown anything in her life, so fast her wand arm was a blur and her shoulder screamed and the air of the clearing was lit up red and blue and white and gold with the light of them.

He took them apart.

He did it with one finger of his left hand, mostly. A flick here, a turn there. The Bone-Breaker went into the ground. The Severing Charm went up into the dark and took the top six feet off a young hemlock, which came down slow and crashing at the edge of the clearing. The fire bent around him in a long curve like water around a rock and went out in the wet grass behind him with a hiss. The Conjunctivitis Curse he simply caught, closed his fingers on in the air, and when he opened his hand it was gone.

And her own hex, the seam-finder, the thing she'd built that nobody else in the world had, he caught too. He held it in the air in front of him for a moment, hanging there and twisting like a little knot of purple light, and he tilted his head and looked at it.

He looked at it with *interest.*

Then he flicked it back at her, and it hit her shield and found the seams and pulled them apart, exactly the way she'd built it to, and her shield came apart in her hands like wet paper.

She was standing there with no shield and her wand arm shaking and her breath coming in gasps, fifteen yards from a man she couldn't touch, and in her head the cold voice was saying *run, run, run,* and she wasn't going to run, because Seth was on the ground in front of him.

His left hand went under the back of his overcoat and came out with a gun.

It was a pistol. Big, square, old-fashioned, dull black steel. A Colt. She knew it, distantly, the way she knew things she'd grown up around. Her grandfather had carried one exactly like it in the Pacific. It was in his hand and pointing at her, and once it was in his hand he stopped using his fingers at all; she threw one last Stunner out of pure reflex and it veered away from him on its own a foot from his face, like a bird that had changed its mind, and he didn't even blink.

He lowered the gun a few inches and shot the ground in front of her.

It was a single shot. A flat, heavy *bang*, much louder than she'd expected, and a tongue of fire out the end of the barrel.

The bullet hit the dirt about four feet in front of her boots.

And the ground blew up.

It went up like a mortar round. A fountain of dirt and rock and grass and roots erupted straight up out of the clearing in front of her, higher than her head, and the shockwave hit her like a truck. It came through her chest and her ears and her teeth all at once. She felt herself leave the ground. She had a long, slow, silent moment in which she was flying backward through the dark with dirt raining around her and her wand still clenched in her fist, which was the only thing she'd done right in the last thirty seconds, and then she hit something soft, ferns, and then something hard under the ferns, a fallen log, and the world went white and then black and then a high, endless, ringing grey.

She lay there.

She was on her back in the ferns. She could see the sky, what there was of it, through the black tops of the trees. It was very dark blue. A few stars. Something warm was running out of her nose. Her ears were ringing so loud it was like being inside a bell. Her ribs on the right side felt like somebody had driven a car over them. She tried to sit up and her body gave a small, firm, and entirely non-negotiable *no*.

She still had her wand. She held onto that.

Dirt was still coming down, pattering on the ferns around her, little clods and pebbles. There was a smell of turned earth and burned powder and something else, something sharp, like a struck match the size of a house.

Through the ringing she heard footsteps in the grass, unhurried. Coming closer.

A shape stood over her against the dark sky. Long coat. The body under his arm, still. The gun was gone; she didn't see where. He looked down at her.

"Right," he said. His voice came to her muffled, like through a wall. It was English. That was the first thing she noticed and she noticed it with a strange detachment, as if she were taking notes on somebody else's life. English. Calm. Not even a little out of breath. "Are we done?"

She didn't answer. She didn't trust her mouth.

"Lovely," he said. "In that case. Shall we try this conversation with words? Instead of spells and teeth?"

He turned around and walked away from her, back toward the middle of the clearing, back toward the stump where — she saw, with that same detached clarity — a paper cup of coffee had been sitting the whole time, untouched, with steam still coming out of the lid.

He picked it up and drank from it.

Somewhere off to her left, the pressure holding the wolves to the ground let go. She heard Leah come up out of the grass with a snarl that turned into a cough. She heard Seth scramble up, and the sound of his paws coming toward her, fast, and then the sound of him changing, that wet tearing noise she still hadn't quite gotten used to, behind a tree, and then bare feet running through the grass.

"Layla. *Layla*. Oh God. Oh God, are you—"

Seth was on his knees beside her in the ferns in a pair of cutoff shorts and nothing else, with his huge hands hovering over her like he was afraid to touch her. His face was wild.

"I'm okay," she said. She wasn't sure she was. It came out thick. "I'm okay. Don't move me. Give me a second."

"You're bleeding."

"Nose. It's just my nose. I'm okay." She lay there and breathed. In. Out. Her ribs hurt on the *in*. "Seth."

"Yeah?"

"Who," she said, very carefully, "the *hell* is that?"

Seth looked over his shoulder, at the man standing in the middle of the clearing with a cup of coffee in one hand and a frozen vampire under the other arm, looking politely off into the trees and waiting for them like a man waiting for a bus.

"I don't know," Seth said. "He says he's federal."

### VIII. Words

*4:47 p.m.*

It took her two minutes to sit up and another three to stand, and she did both of them with Seth's arm around her and her wand in her fist, and the man in the middle of the clearing let her take all five.

He'd put some lights up while he waited. She didn't see him do it. She looked up from the ferns at one point and there were four small points of blue-white light hanging in the air at the edges of the clearing, steady as lamps, enough to see by. Wandless. Wordless. Done while he was drinking coffee. It was such a small piece of magic that she almost didn't register it, and then she did, and it frightened her more than the gun had, because of how little attention he'd paid to it.

Leah had come to stand in front of her. The grey wolf stood square in the dead grass between Layla and the man, with her head low and her lips curled back off her teeth and a growl going in her chest so deep Layla felt it in her own ribs. The man didn't seem to mind. He stood about twenty feet off, the vampire under his arm, sipping his coffee, looking at the three of them the way you'd look at a used car you were thinking of buying.

"Ribs?" he asked.

"Go to hell," said Layla.

"Two cracked, I'd guess. Right side. And you'll be a bit deaf in the right ear till about Tuesday." He shifted the coffee to the hand holding the vampire, somehow, without dropping either, and reached into his coat with the other. Seth went rigid against her. The man's hand came out with a small glass vial and he tossed it underhand across the clearing.

Seth caught it. He didn't even look like he meant to. His hand just went out and closed on it.

"For the ribs and the ear," the man said. "It isn't poison. If I wanted you dead I'd have aimed at you instead of the dirt."

Layla looked at the vial in Seth's palm. Clear glass, stoppered with wax. A liquid inside the color of dark honey, moving slow. No label. No maker's mark on the glass. She pointed her wand at it without taking her eyes off the man and ran the oldest diagnostic she knew under her breath, the one every first-year curse-breaker learned on day one, the one that would light up for any poison or curse or compulsion on the continent.

Clean.

More than clean. The charm came back off the potion with a kind of shine to it. Whoever had brewed it had done it better than anything she'd ever seen come out of a hospital pharmacy. Dittany, and something else, something she couldn't place.

*If I wanted you dead.*

He could have. She'd stood there for thirty seconds with her shield in pieces and he'd put a round in the dirt instead of in her chest. She'd seen what the round did to the dirt.

She took the vial out of Seth's hand, broke the wax with her thumbnail, and drank it.

It tasted of mint and burnt sugar and copper. It went down cold and spread out hot, and she felt her ribs move. Actually move, inside her, a deep grinding click on the right side, and then the pain just went, all of it, so fast she gasped. Her ear popped. The ringing dropped away to nothing, and suddenly she could hear the river, a long way off, and the wind in the hemlocks, and Leah's growl, and her own breath.

"Better," the man said. It wasn't a question.

"Who are you?"

"Page." He drank some coffee. "Jim Page."

"Like Jimmy Page?" said Seth.

Layla turned her head and looked at him.

"What?" said Seth. "My dad had all his records. Zeppelin. All of them. He had *Houses of the Holy* on vinyl, he used to play it on Sundays—" He stopped.

Something went across the man's face. It went too fast for Layla to name it. He'd been looking at Seth, and his eyes had gone a fraction unfocused, the way they had when he was reading the wolves, and then sharpened again.

"No relation," the man said. "Shame. Your dad had taste."

Leah's growl changed pitch. The man glanced at her.

"Yes, Lassie, I'm lying about the name. Course I am." He sounded almost fond. "That's fine. You can still use it. Saves time."

Layla straightened up slowly out of Seth's arm. Her legs held. She kept her wand where it was, down by her thigh, pointed at the ground about three feet in front of the man's boots, which was as close as she thought she could get away with.

"What did you do to that vampire?" she asked.

"Caught it."

"With what?"

"A trap."

She looked past him, at the place where she'd seen the vampire hanging when she came in. There was nothing there now. Only air. But she'd seen the shimmer, in that first half-second, a faint line running straight up from the vampire's boot into the dark. She'd seen it, and she'd been too busy throwing curses to think about what it was.

"That was a ward," she said slowly. "That line. That was a wardline. A live one. You've got a *ward* up out here."

He didn't say anything.

"The Sol Duc road," Layla said. She was thinking out loud now, and couldn't stop. "Last Saturday. Sunday. I was driving up to the Thackeray place and the air felt wrong. On the road, right by the turnout above the river. Like a pressure change. Like before weather. I stopped the truck and got out and walked around for ten minutes and I couldn't find anything and I thought I was imagining it." She stared at him. "That was you."

He tilted his head. For the first time, she thought, he looked at her like she'd said something he hadn't already known.

"You felt that?" he said.

"Yes."

"Huh," said the man. He looked at her a moment longer. "Good for you."

And that was all he said about it, but it was enough. She stood there and did the math, the way she'd do it in a tomb. The turnout above the Sol Duc was two miles from here. Here was ten miles from Forks. He'd said *trap*, but the shimmer had been a single strand of something much larger; she'd felt the shape of it in that first instant without understanding what she was feeling. It was a net. It had to be. And if it reached from the Sol Duc to here, it could reach anywhere. He had laid a ward net across God knew how many miles of national forest, live, keyed and waiting, and she was a professional curse-breaker who did this for a living, and she had been driving across it twice a day for a week and walking across it on foot, and all she had ever felt was a change in the weather.

"Who are you with?" she said.

"I showed your boyfriend my badge."

"You didn't show *me*."

"Would you like to see it?"

Before she could answer he took the wallet out of his coat and tossed it to her across the grass, the same underhand toss as the vial. She caught it left-handed and flipped it open.

It was empty.

Plain black leather, soft and worn. Two halves. No badge. No card. No photograph. Nothing. She turned it over. Nothing on the back. But under her fingers, through the leather, she could feel the charm on it — a thin, intricate lattice of magic, so fine and delicate it was like holding lace. She ran her thumb along it. It was beautiful work. The kind of work you could study for a year. There was no signature on it anywhere, none of the little tells a spellwright left behind like fingerprints, the habits of hand that told you which school they'd learned at or which master they'd learned under. It was as clean as glass.

"It's empty," she said.

"Is it?"

"It said FBI," said Seth, craning to look. "It said Federal Bureau of Investigation. It had a picture of him and everything."

Leah huffed through her nose, sharply. Seth looked at his sister.

"Leah says it said Homeland Security," Seth said slowly.

Layla looked at the wallet. "It shows people what they'd believe," she said.

"It shows people what they need to see," said the man.

"And I need to see nothing."

"Apparently." He held out his hand. After a second she threw it back to him, and he caught it without looking and put it away.

She studied him. She had been studying him since she came out of the ferns, and she made herself do it properly now, the way she'd study a door she didn't want to open. Head to foot.

The suit was a good suit. Charcoal wool, well cut, no visible label. The coat was the same. The boots didn't match the suit; they were work boots, heavy, broken in, with forest mud on them. A man who dressed for a meeting and then walked to it through the woods. The gloves were black leather and told her nothing. He wore no rings. No crest, no house pin, no school anything. She looked at his hands, because hands were where you learned the most about a wizard. Everybody's training lived in their hands. Ilvermorny kids pointed. They jabbed their wands like they were throwing darts. Kids from the French school flourished. Kids from the northern schools cut and stabbed. She'd been able to tell where a wizard had been taught from across a dueling hall since she was fifteen.

This man's hands hadn't done anything a school would have taught them. He'd flicked. He'd tapped. Half the time he hadn't moved them at all.

The gun. An American gun, a Colt .45, the same model her grandfather had carried in the Navy. Which told her he liked American guns, or wanted her to think he did, or had found one in a pawn shop. That was all it told her.

The accent was English. Not posh, she didn't think, though she was no expert. Something rougher underneath it. And accents were free. She'd worked a job in Lima once with a curse-breaker from Boston who could do a perfect Glaswegian when he'd had three beers, and an Irish one when he'd had four.

The magic was the magic. It told her he was the most dangerous thing she had ever seen. It didn't tell her who he answered to.

She looked for anything. Anything at all. A habit, a word, a slip, a mark. She looked for a long time.

There was nothing. There was so much nothing that the nothing itself was the only thing she was sure of: whoever he was, wherever he came from, he had been built — trained, polished, made — by somebody who knew exactly what she would look for, and had taken it all away before he walked into the clearing. She had no idea who that somebody was. She didn't let herself start guessing. Guessing was how curse-breakers died. You saw a shape in the dark and decided it was a door, and you walked through it, and it wasn't a door.

She had nothing.

He'd watched her do all of it, patient, drinking his coffee. When she was finished he said, mildly, "All done?"

Layla didn't answer.

She felt the last thing she tried before she tried it. A reading charm, the quiet kind, the kind you ran on a cursed object to find out who'd made it. She'd never run it on a person. She let it gather at the tip of her wand where she held it pointed at the ground, and started to let it go, slow, low, along the grass toward his boots.

"Don't," he said, without looking at her.

She stopped.

"Right," the man said. He finished the coffee, looked into the empty cup with regret, and tucked it in his coat pocket. "Here's where we are, Miss Brennan."

Her name. He'd said her name. She'd never said it. Seth hadn't said it.

*Seth's head,* she thought, and felt sick.

"You're a curse-breaker," the man said. "A good one. Better than good. That castle in the mountains, and a dueling team after, by the look of that triple of yours, and six years of contract work since. You can feel a dormant ward from a moving vehicle, which I'd have told you was impossible this morning." He said it flatly, like reading a résumé. "You came out here on a job. You met a nice boy in the grocery shop. And you've got a nice boy with no inside voice."

Seth flinched.

"You told the boy what you are," the man said. "Which you'd every right to do; I'd have done the same if a wolf had just turned up on my lawn. The boy told his whole pack, by thinking about you, which he does roughly every six seconds. I'd assume the other pack down on the reservation has it by now as well, or will, the first time he goes near one of them. Then you stood in the middle of a house full of vampires and turned a vase into a budgie. One of the vampires reads minds. One of them sees the future, when she's in the mood."

Layla didn't say anything. Her mouth had gone dry.

"Now, the wolves," the man said, "are a grey area. Magical beings, near enough, if you squint. So are your vampires. You could make an argument. I expect you've been making it to yourself every night for three weeks." He paused. "And then, on the twelfth of November, over fried chicken, you put out a grease fire in Chief Charlie Swan's kitchen. With your wand. In front of him."

Seth's hand closed on her arm.

"Chief Swan," the man said, "is not a grey area. Chief Swan is a No-Maj police officer. Chief Swan is about as black-and-white as it gets."

The clearing was very quiet. Even Leah had stopped growling.

"What do you want?" Layla asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, distantly.

"I'm getting to that." The man shifted the vampire under his arm, a small adjustment, like a man resettling a bag of groceries. "So. As I see it, there are three ways this goes.

"One. I do nothing. I go on my way and you go on yours. And sooner or later, because these things always come out, somebody from your Congress walks through this town, and takes one look in the wrong head, and then they send people. And every mind in that house in the woods, and every wolf on that reservation, and the chief of police, and your Mrs. Clearwater, gets wiped clean as a slate. And you never hold a wand again. And possibly you go somewhere with no windows for a while."

Layla said nothing.

"Two. I tidy it up myself." He said it in exactly the same voice. "Tonight. I'm very good at it. Much better than your Congress. The chief. The pack. The vampires. Balto here." He nodded at Seth. "Clean as anything. Nobody would ever know there'd been something to forget."

"You can't," Seth said. His voice cracked on it. "You can't do that to the Cullens. They're— Edward reads *minds*, you can't just—"

"I can," said the man.

Layla looked at him and couldn't tell if he was lying. That was the worst part. She'd been able to tell when people were lying her whole life, and she looked at him, and she couldn't tell.

"You'd take his memory of me," she said. "Seth's."

"Not all of it. Only the interesting bits." The green eyes went to Seth, and back to her. "You'd be a girl he met in the grocery shop. With a nice laugh. He'd still be stupid about you; I can't do anything about the wolf thing, and I wouldn't try. He just wouldn't know why you've got an owl."

Seth made a sound. It was a low, wordless, awful sound, from deep in his chest. Leah's growl came back, rising, rising.

"Leah," Layla said sharply. "Don't."

The grey wolf stopped. She stood there with every hair on her body on end and her teeth bared and her muscles shaking, and did not move, and Layla had never in her life been so grateful to another person.

The man hadn't moved either. He hadn't even looked at Leah.

"Three?" said Layla.

"Three," said the man. "Nobody hears a word about any of this from me. Ever. Not your Congress, not anybody. You keep your wand. The chief keeps his memories, and his kitchen, and his fried chicken. Balto keeps all of you." He let that sit. "And in exchange, when I knock, you open the door."

"That's blackmail," Layla said.

"Yes," said the man.

He didn't dress it up. He didn't explain. He just said *yes* and stood there and let her look at it.

"For how long?" she asked.

"Till I stop knocking."

"What would you want? Specifically?"

"Help. Information. A favour now and then. A door opened. A conversation with your friends at the big glass house, when it's time for one." He shrugged. "Whatever I ask for, when I ask for it."

"I won't hurt anybody for you."

"I'd never ask you to," he said. "I'm much better at that than you are."

She believed that one. She believed it completely.

Leah huffed, hard, through her nose, and Layla felt Seth tense beside her and turn his head, listening to something she couldn't hear.

"Leah says," Seth said slowly, "that we're going to tell the Cullens. All of it. Tonight. She says you can't stop that."

"Course you are," said the man. "Lassie, the mind-reader's going to have every word of this out of Balto's head before he's got his shoes back on. I'd be disappointed if he didn't. I'm counting on it."

Leah's ears went flat.

"Are you going to hurt them?" Seth asked. "The Cullens?"

The man looked at him. "Do they eat people?"

"No! No. Never. Animals. Deer. Mountain lions, sometimes. Emmett likes bears."

"Then my traps won't touch them," the man said, "and neither will I."

"...Your traps," Layla said. "Plural."

"Plural. Lots." He looked at her. "Don't go looking for them. You won't find them, and they'll notice you trying."

"What are you going to do with that?" She nodded at the vampire under his arm. It hadn't moved the entire time. It hadn't so much as twitched. Its eyes were still open, still red, still looking back over its shoulder at something that wasn't there anymore. She'd been watching it from the corner of her eye since she came out of the ferns, and she was almost certain it was aware. It was in there. It could see her.

"Ask it some questions," said the man.

"And then?"

"Find out what it takes to kill one of these properly."

"You tear them up," Seth said. "And you burn the pieces. That's the only way. Otherwise they put themselves back together."

The man looked at him for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "I know. I'd like to know what *else* works." He smiled slightly. It was the smile of a man looking forward to a long night at his desk. "Thanks, though."

Seth shut his mouth.

Leah's head came up. Her ears went forward, all at once, both of them, and swiveled toward the south. Seth's did the same thing — he wasn't even phased, and Layla still watched his head turn the exact same way at the exact same moment.

"Jake," Seth breathed. "Jake's coming."

"So he is," said the man. He hadn't turned his head. His eyes had gone a little unfocused again, the way they did, and Layla realized with a sick little lurch that he was listening to it too. Inside Leah's head. Listening to the pack. "Big fellow. Russet. About three miles out and coming like a train. He phased when your fortune-teller went quiet, a little under half an hour ago." He blinked, and his eyes came back to the clearing. "He's angry."

"He's our Alpha," Seth said.

"I gathered." The man raised his voice slightly. He was looking at Leah now, past her, into her, and Layla understood a second too late who he was talking to. "Jacob. I'm leaving. You can slow down. You'll want to watch the creek crossing at the bottom of the ridge; the bank's gone soft."

Leah's whole body jerked like she'd been touched with a live wire. Somewhere off in the dark to the south, a long way away, something huge howled. It was a sound of pure fury, and it was getting closer, fast.

"Didn't think so," the man said, unbothered.

He turned to Seth.

"Balto," he said. "Look at me."

Seth didn't want to. Layla could see him not want to. But he looked, because that voice was the kind you looked at.

The man held his eyes. When he spoke again, it was slower, more precise, every word laid down separately like a man setting out cards on a table. Layla understood right away that the words were meant for somebody else. He was talking *through* Seth.

"Mr. Cullen," he said. "Evening. You'll have to forgive me using the boy for this; your phone number's unlisted. I'll be calling on your family when I'm ready. I'll knock. Until then I'd ask you, very politely, not to come looking for me. You won't find me, and I'd take it badly, and I'd much rather we started off friendly." A pause. "And your future son-in-law can slow down too, while we're at it. He's going to hurt himself."

He held Seth's eyes for one more second.

"That's for him," he said. "Not you."

The howl came again, much closer. A mile, maybe less. Layla could hear the crashing in the brush now.

The man turned to her.

"Miss Brennan."

She waited.

"You were good," he said. "Back there. That last hex of yours, the one that pulls a shield apart along the seams. I've never seen anything like it. Hold onto it."

She didn't know what to do with that. She didn't do anything with it. She stood there with her wand pointed at the dirt and her heart going and the taste of copper still in her mouth, and she asked the only question she had left.

"Who do you work for?"

He considered her for a moment. Then he smiled, a real one, crooked and sudden and so completely unlike anything he'd done in the last half hour that it took her a second to recognize it.

"Darlene, mostly," he said. "Diner on Forks Avenue. She does a marionberry pie that'd make you weep. I'm in most mornings, back booth." He nodded at Seth. "Bring him. He looks like he eats."

And he was gone.

There was no crack. There was no sound at all, except a soft *whump*, like a heavy door closing a long way off. The ferns where he'd been standing went down flat in a ring, and the four little lights at the edge of the clearing went out together, and the man and the coat and the vampire under his arm were simply not there anymore.

Three seconds later Jacob came through the trees.

He came like a freight train, a russet wolf even bigger than Seth, bursting out of the alders at the south edge of the clearing at a dead run with brush and dead leaves flying off him. He plowed into the clearing and skidded and spun all the way round, his huge head swinging left and right, his lips peeled back, snarling at nothing. At the trampled grass. At the flattened ring of ferns. At the crater in the ground, four feet wide and two feet deep, still smoking faintly, with dirt and torn roots sprayed out around it in a star twenty feet across. At the hemlock with its top gone, and the alder with its bark blown off, and the old stump at the edge of the clearing with Layla's own conjured ropes still wound twelve times around it, creaking.

He was too late. He snarled anyway. He snarled at the empty dark for a long time.

Leah sat down in the grass. She just sat down, all at once, like her legs had quit.

Seth's knees went too. He sat down hard next to her, in his cutoff shorts, in the cold, and put his face in his hands.

Layla stayed standing. She lit the end of her wand and walked to the crater.

It was a clean crater. She stood at the edge of it and looked down. Two feet deep, a neat bowl blown into the clay, with the dirt still steaming a little in the cold. She'd seen what a Blasting Curse did to earth. This had been bigger than any Blasting Curse she could throw, and it had come from one bullet. One little .45-caliber bullet, out of a gun her grandfather could have carried.

There was no bullet in the crater. No fragments. Nothing.

And she thought of something, and went cold, and turned around and walked back to where the man had been standing when he fired. A gun like that threw its brass when you fired it. Her grandfather had taught her that when she was nine, on a range in the Berkshires. It kicked the empty casing out the side, and the casing went spinning off into the grass, and you had to go and pick it up afterward or the range master yelled at you.

She searched the grass for ten minutes with the light on her wand. She went over it inch by inch while Jacob paced and growled and Seth sat with his face in his hands and Leah watched her, silent.

There was no brass.

There was nothing. There were the marks of his boots in the grass, and the ring of flattened ferns where he'd arrived and the ring where he'd left. There was the stump where he'd set down his coffee. There was nothing else at all. He'd taken the cup. He'd taken the casing. He'd taken the vampire.

He'd left her the ropes on the stump. Those were hers.

Layla stood in the middle of the clearing with her wand lit and looked at nothing for a while.

Then she went and sat down in the grass with the others, and Seth put his arm around her, and Jacob finally stopped snarling and came and stood over the three of them in the dark, his huge sides heaving.

"So," Layla said, after a long time. "That's new."

Nobody laughed.

### IX. Unlisted

*The Cullen house — 6:19 p.m.*

Edward heard them coming from four miles out.

He'd been listening for them since half past four. Since Alice, sitting on the living room floor helping Renesmee build a tower out of the wooden blocks Esme had bought in Port Angeles, had stopped with a block in her hand and gone rigid and said, in a small flat voice, "Oh."

Everybody in the house had turned to look at her.

"The nomad," Alice had said. Her eyes had been wide and blank, looking at nothing. "The one coming down the coast. I had him. I've had him all day. He was in the trees above the Sol Duc, running, and Leah was behind him, I couldn't see her but I could see him running from something, and then—" She'd shaken her head, hard. "And then he just stopped."

"Stopped," Carlisle had said.

"Stopped. When someone dies, it fades out; you can see it coming. This just *stopped*." Alice had looked up at them, and Edward had seen in her mind exactly what she meant, and it had chilled him in a way nothing had in years. A future cut off mid-frame. A film that had run out of reel. "There's nothing after it. There's nothing there at all. It's like he fell into a hole."

Renesmee had put her hand on Alice's cheek, worried, and shown her a picture of the tower falling down, and Alice had laughed shakily and kissed her fingers.

And Jacob, who had been sitting on the floor beside the baby with a paper plate on his knee, had been up and out the front door before Alice finished the sentence, so fast he nearly took it off its hinges, and phased on the lawn, and Edward had caught the first of it through Jacob's mind as Jacob tore off into the forest northward. Not much. Fragments, coming to Jacob through Leah, through the pack: a clearing. A dead man hanging upside down in the air. A tall stranger in a dark coat with a cup of coffee in his hand.

Then Jacob had gone out of range, and there was nothing more, and Edward had stood at the glass wall of the living room for an hour and a half with his family behind him and Bella's hand in his, listening to the forest and hearing nothing but elk.

Now they were coming. Four minds, out of the northwest, moving fast. He knew three of them the way he knew the inside of his own house. The fourth was the witch's, loud and bright and very frightened.

And Seth's mind, coming into range, was screaming.

Seth didn't scream in words. Seth was the most open mind Edward had ever known, a mind without doors, a mind that put everything it had out on the front lawn for anyone to look at, and right now it was playing the last hour and a half over and over in a loop, in full color, with sound. The chase. The snare. The man.

Edward listened to it all the way up the drive.

By the time the truck pulled in — Layla's old green Ford, which they'd walked two miles uphill to the Thackeray place to fetch, with Jacob phased back and driving, because Seth was shaking too hard and Layla had blood on her face — he had seen it nine times, and he stood on the porch to meet them with his whole family behind him, and he didn't need to ask a single question.

"Inside," was all he said.

---

They told it anyway. Edward let them. It helped them, telling it, and it helped his family, who couldn't see what he could see.

Layla told most of it. She sat on the edge of the white couch in the living room with her wand in her lap and a wet cloth that Esme had given her pressed to her nose, though it had long since stopped bleeding, and talked in short flat sentences about the duel. Edward listened to her voice and to her mind at once, the way he always did, and the two of them matched. Every word. She wasn't shading any of it. She was too scared to.

"I've never seen anything like it," she said. "I want to be really clear about that. I'm good. I'm not being modest; I'm good. I've been in fights with people who wanted to kill me, and I won. I've dueled people who were ranked." She took the cloth away from her face and looked at it, and then at Carlisle. "He didn't use a wand. He didn't say anything. He didn't move his feet. He took everything I had and handed it back to me, and he was holding a vampire under his arm the whole time."

"How did he do that?" Emmett asked. He wasn't joking. Edward had rarely heard Emmett's mind so quiet. "Hold one of us like that. One-armed."

"I don't know."

"We weigh a lot, is the thing."

"I know. I *know*. I saw it hit the ground." Layla's hands tightened on her wand. "He picked it up like it was a board."

In her mind Edward saw it again, and in Seth's mind across the room, and in Leah's, from her spot by the door. Three angles. He picked the stranger apart frame by frame, the way he'd pick apart a piece of music, looking for something, anything. Height, build, face. A man in his late twenties, with very short dark hair and a few days of stubble and green eyes. Unremarkable features. A good suit, a long coat. A voice like an English actor's.

He couldn't hear the man's mind, of course. These were memories. Memories didn't come with the minds of the people in them. But Edward had been doing this for a hundred years, and he knew how people *looked* when he could hear them and how they looked when he couldn't, and he had a horrible certainty, watching the man in Leah's memory turn and answer a thought she'd never said out loud, that if he'd been standing in that clearing himself, he'd have heard nothing. The man would have been a blank to him. A wall.

Someone else who could do what Edward did. Someone who could hear, and not be heard.

He'd never met one. Not in a century. Not even Aro.

"English," said Jasper. He was standing behind the couch, near Alice, with his arms folded. His mind was cool and orderly, laying out pieces on a table. "Carries a federal badge that isn't one. Carries an American service pistol. Says he's federal. Calls it a national security operation." He tilted his head. "Military. Or was. The way he stands. Back to cover when he could get it. He never let all of you get on one side of him."

"It doesn't mean anything," Layla said. "Any of it. The accent, the gun. I tried. I looked at everything. He made sure it doesn't mean anything." She looked up at Jasper, and Edward saw in her mind the whole careful inventory she'd made of the man in the clearing — the hands, the coat, the empty wallet, the missing brass — and the blank wall she'd hit at the end of it. "I don't know who he is. I don't know who he works for. I'd tell you if I did. I'd tell you in a heartbeat."

"I know," Edward said quietly. "She's telling the truth. She has no idea."

Layla shot him a look that was half gratitude and half resentment, and he didn't blame her for either half.

"He knows about Charlie," Bella said.

She'd been silent until now, standing near the piano with Renesmee asleep on her shoulder. Her voice was low. Edward felt the shape of what was under it before he heard it, the way he'd learned to read her face since he couldn't read anything else: a new, hard, very cold anger, the kind that only came up in her when someone she loved was standing too close to a fire.

"He knows about my dad," Bella said. "He's using my dad. To get to you." She was looking at Layla. "That's what that was. You said yes because of my dad."

"I didn't say yes," Layla said. "I didn't say anything."

"You didn't say no."

Layla held her gaze. "No," she said. "I didn't."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"There's something else," Edward said.

He'd been holding it back. He'd been holding it back since the truck was four miles out, since Seth's mind had come into range playing it on a loop, and he'd heard it then for the first time, and stopped walking for a second on the porch.

He looked at Seth. Seth looked back at him miserably.

"He left a message," Seth said. "For you. In my— in me. He made me look at him."

"I know," said Edward. "I've heard it."

He'd heard it nine times on the drive up. He was hearing it again right now, because Seth couldn't help it. Seth's memory was holding it up in front of him like a sign.

The stranger, standing in the dark clearing under four small lights with the frozen nomad under his arm. Looking straight into Seth's eyes. Looking *through* them. Looking at *Edward*. With a calm, attentive, faintly amused expression, as if he could see Edward on the other side of the boy's eyes as clearly as Edward could see him.

*Mr. Cullen. Evening. You'll have to forgive me using the boy for this; your phone number's unlisted.*

Nobody had ever done that before. In a hundred and five years, nobody had ever looked at someone and spoken to Edward on purpose through the back of their head. People thought at him sometimes. People who knew. Alice, Carlisle, his family. They'd think things at him on purpose when they didn't want to say them out loud. But nobody had ever *used* him. Nobody had ever taken his gift and turned it into a mailbox and slipped a letter in.

*I'll be calling on your family when I'm ready. I'll knock. Until then I'd ask you, very politely, not to come looking for me. You won't find me, and I'd take it badly, and I'd much rather we started off friendly.*

And the last part, said lightly, as an afterthought, while a russet wolf howled in the distance:

*And your future son-in-law can slow down too, while we're at it. He's going to hurt himself.*

Edward closed his eyes.

He heard Jasper's mind catch it from his face before he said anything. He heard Rosalie's mind go white and then very, very still. Rosalie had been sitting on the arm of the couch nearest Bella, and now she was on her feet without having decided to stand, and her eyes had gone to the sleeping child on Bella's shoulder.

"He knows about her," Edward said. "He knows about Renesmee. He knows about the imprint. He knows she's mine." He opened his eyes. "He told us so. He wanted us to know that he knows."

"He got it out of my head," Seth said. His voice was cracking. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I couldn't stop thinking it, I tried, I tried so hard—"

"Seth." Carlisle's voice was gentle. "It isn't your fault."

"It *is*, though. It's all in here. Everything. All of you. Alice and the visions and Jasper and Edward and Bella's shield and Nessie and the treaty and Sam and— he could have seen *everything*, Carlisle. He was in there for like twenty minutes."

"I know," Carlisle said. "And he would have, from any of us, if he'd caught any of us. It doesn't sound as though there was much you could have done."

"I bit the bead," Seth said. "I brought her there. He was about to leave and I brought her there and he blew her up."

"He shot the ground," Layla said. "Not me. He was very specific about that."

"That's *worse*."

"It's really not, Seth."

Emmett had gotten up off the couch too. His big hands were flexing at his sides. Edward could hear his mind, simple and loud and furious, and he knew what Emmett was going to say before he said it.

"We find him," Emmett said. "Tonight. Jasper and me. Alice can—"

"I can't," Alice said.

Everybody looked at her.

She'd been sitting on the floor by the blocks the whole time, with her knees drawn up and her arms around them. Edward had been half-listening to her mind and it had been doing the same thing over and over, a frantic circling motion, like a person pacing a room. Reaching out. Looking. Coming back empty. Reaching out again.

"I can't see him," she said. "I've been trying since the truck turned in. Since before. I've been trying since he stopped the nomad. I can't see him at all."

"Like the wolves," Jasper said.

"The wolves are different. The wolves are a gap. I can't see the wolves, but I can see the shape of where they'd be. Everything around them bends. The witch is static; she's half there and half gone. He's—" Alice shook her head. Her small face was pinched. "He's nothing. There's nothing to bend around. He's a hole. I look straight at where he should be and there's nothing there at all, and nothing *around* him either, and the nomad's in there with him somewhere and that's gone too. It's like the whole future just has a piece cut out of it with scissors."

Emmett stood there for a second. Then he sat down again, slowly.

"He asked us not to," Edward said. "Look for him."

"He *threatened* us not to," said Rosalie.

"Yes," Edward agreed. "That too."

Carlisle had been listening to all of it with his hands folded in front of him and his face calm, and his mind, as always, was the quietest in the room. Edward listened to it for a while. Carlisle was thinking about London, three and a half centuries ago. About the cellars under the city where his father had hunted the things that hid there. About how there had always been other things in the dark, other hidden places, more of them than anyone knew. He'd always suspected. He'd never been sure.

"He said he'd knock," Carlisle said finally.

"He did," said Edward.

"Then we'll let him knock." Carlisle looked around the room at all of them. "He had a nomad in his hands who has been killing hikers on this peninsula for two weeks. Who might have come here next, to Charlie, to the reservation. Whatever else he is, he took that one off the board. He let Seth and Leah walk away. He let Layla walk away, when by her account he could have done anything he liked. And he went out of his way to tell us, before we did anything foolish, that he'd rather be friendly." His mouth curved, very slightly. "I've known a great many people who'd threaten you in the same breath as they asked you to dinner. Not all of them were enemies."

"And if he is one?" Rosalie asked.

"Then we'll know when he knocks," said Carlisle.

Bella hadn't said anything else. Edward looked at her. She was standing with their daughter on her shoulder and her face set, holding the baby a little closer than she needed to, and her mind was what it had always been to him, since the first day in a biology classroom: a perfect, smooth, unbroken silence. He had a theory about that silence. He'd shared it with her in September. A shield, he'd called it, some instinct of her mind's that kept the rest of the world out. Neither of them knew yet how far down it went.

"Could he hear me?" she asked him. "If he tried. Could he get through that?"

"I don't know," said Edward honestly.

"I'd like to find out," Bella said, very softly, "before he gets anywhere near her."

---

Later, when the wolves had gone home and Seth had driven the witch back to her blue house on Calawah Way, and Esme had put her vase-bird back on the mantel where it had been living for nearly three weeks, and Alice had stopped trying to look and finally just sat in the dark with Jasper's arm around her, Edward went out onto the porch alone.

It was raining again. Light, cold, steady. He stood at the rail and closed his eyes and let his gift go out as far as it would.

Four miles. Five, if he pushed. He heard the deer bedded down in the trees by the river. An owl hunting. Elk on the ridge across the valley, sleepy and slow and dumb. A man in a truck on the 101 headed toward Port Angeles, worried about his mortgage. A woman in a house on the edge of town, putting a child to bed. Charlie, a long way off at the very edge of his range, the way he always was, a dim fogged murmur that Edward could never quite make out. Tired. Watching a game. Thinking about Sue.

Somewhere out there, in all that, was a man who could hear every one of those minds as well as Edward could, and choose which ones to listen to. And who could stand in the middle of all of it and not make a sound.

Edward stood on the porch for a long time in the rain, listening for a silence.

He didn't find one. That was the point. That was the whole point, and he knew it, and he stood there anyway.

### X. Specimen

*Olympic National Forest, off Forest Road 29 — 4:59 p.m.*

Harry came out of the Apparition eleven miles east of the clearing, on a bench of level ground above a creek, under a stand of big-leaf maples that had dropped all their leaves and a pair of Sitka spruce that hadn't.

The tent was where he'd left it. It was hard to miss, if you were him, and impossible to find if you weren't. An old canvas wall tent, olive drab, British Army surplus from the forties with the broad arrow still stencilled faintly on one flap, patched in three places, with a tin stovepipe sticking up out of the roof and a thread of woodsmoke going up into the rain. It looked like the sort of thing a man who counted elk for a living would sleep in. It was meant to.

He stood at the edge of the clearing for a moment with the vampire under his arm and let the wards look at him. Muggle-repellent. Notice-me-not. An anti-Apparition jinx with a hole in it shaped exactly like him. And a fourth one, new this week, wound tight around the whole hollow: the same key as the net, human blood in a body with no heartbeat, set to do something considerably less polite than hang a visitor upside down. They tasted him, and knew him, and let him through.

The vampire under his arm hadn't moved. It wouldn't, till he told it to. But he'd felt it see the tent as they arrived, and he'd felt its mind go quiet and very fast, the way a mind did when it saw a door it didn't like.

"Home sweet home," Harry told it, and ducked through the flap.

---

Inside, the tent was a lot bigger.

The flap opened onto a short passage with a plank floor and a coat rack, and the passage opened onto a kitchen. A proper one. Flagstone floor, a long scrubbed pine table that seated ten, an Aga range in cream enamel that had come out of a farmhouse in Shropshire, and a copper hood above it. The window over the sink looked out on a view of a lake in the Highlands that didn't exist anywhere near here, because he'd lifted it out of a memory and fixed it to the glass. There were open shelves of tins and jars and a rack of cast-iron pans, a fridge that ran on nothing, a kettle that was always hot. A turntable on the dresser by the door, with three crates of vinyl underneath it.

Off the kitchen, through an arch, a library: two storeys of shelving with a gallery and a ladder, and a fire in the grate, and a leather armchair that had taken him four years to wear into the right shape. Down the passage from the library a workshop, a bedroom, a bathroom with a claw-footed tub. And at the end of the passage, two doors. One of oak, bound with iron. And one of plain grey steel, with no handle, and a flight of stone steps going down behind it into the cold.

He went to the oak door first.

The armory was long and narrow and smelled of gun oil and cold metal and the faint struck-match tang of old magic. Racks down both walls, floor to ceiling. Most of what was in them he'd collected in the last five years, from estate sales and auctions and a few men who'd owed him favours and a few who hadn't been in any position to argue. Every one of them had been in his workshop afterward for a while.

A Remington 870 with a magazine tube full of shells he'd packed by hand with rock salt and silver shot and a charm that made them go through shield charms like they weren't there. A sawn-off side-by-side twelve-gauge, his favourite, for reasons he could never quite explain. A Winchester '94 lever-action in .30-30 with runes cut so fine into the receiver you needed a lens to see them, which would put a round through a troll's skull at three hundred yards and tell you, afterward, by the colour of its smoke, what it had hit. A Browning Hi-Power. An MP5. A long, ugly Barrett .50 in a padded case that he'd only had out twice, both times in places that didn't officially exist. Knives on a board: two goblin-made, one silver, one cold iron, one bone.

He didn't need any of it. That was the joke. He could do anything any of these could do with his bare hands and less effort. But a spell could be blocked, if you were good enough, and countered, and argued with. A bullet, enchanted right, was a thing that had already happened by the time anybody knew about it. And there was something about the weight of them he liked. They were honest. Everything in his life was a secret, and a gun was just a gun.

He took the Colt out from the holster at the small of his back and set it on the bench.

It was an M1911A1, made by Remington Rand in 1943 for the U.S. Army. He'd bought it at an estate sale in Leeds four years ago for a hundred and twenty pounds, along with a box of photographs and a tin of medals, from the family of an American airman who'd been stationed in Yorkshire in the war and married a local girl and never gone home. The family had wanted it gone. Harry had taken the photographs too. They were in a drawer in his flat in London. He'd never quite known why.

He'd spent six months on the gun. He'd taken it apart to the last pin and cut runes on the inside of the slide and the underside of the barrel and the face of the firing pin. The magazine held seven rounds and would never be empty again; it drew them out of a small pocket of somewhere else he'd folded into the grip, and made each one as it rose, according to whatever he had in mind when he pulled the trigger. Ball, for ordinary work. Silver, for werewolves. Cold iron, for the things that didn't like iron. An incendiary he used for nests.

And the round he'd put in the dirt in front of the girl, which he'd built in 2003 after Ron spent six months dragging him to a Muggle games shop in Croydon every Saturday afternoon to push little plastic soldiers around a table. The soldiers had carried guns that fired rounds that went into you before they went off. Harry had sat there watching Ron roll dice and thought, *I could make that.*

So he had. It went in, and then it went off. In a person, it didn't leave much. In the ground four feet from the girl, aimed shallow so the blast would go up and out, it had picked her up and put her in the ferns and left her with two cracked ribs and a nosebleed, which was exactly what he'd asked of it.

The brass vanished as it ejected. He'd built that in on the first day. He'd watched her through Lassie's eyes as he left, starting to look for it.

He cleaned the gun, because it hadn't been cleaned since Tuesday and because it was what he did. It took him six minutes. The vampire lay on the flagstones of the passage outside the armory door the whole time, rigid, eyes open, and he didn't look at it once.

Then he holstered the Colt, picked the vampire up again, and went down the stone stairs behind the steel door.

---

The specimen room was cold. He kept it that way. It was the largest room in the tent, a long vault of pale grey stone with a high groined ceiling and light coming from nowhere in particular, and every surface in it had been warded so many times that the air had a faint taste, like licking a battery.

Glass cases lined the long walls. Most of them were occupied. A strip of shed Dementor cloak in a sealed jar, slowly moving. The forelimb of an Acromantula, preserved, as long as he was tall. Something he'd pulled out of a well in Albania that nobody had a name for yet, which he'd named Kevin. A Lethifold sample. A bird's nest made entirely of human teeth, from a house in Norfolk. Notebooks, his own and other people's. A Pensieve on a stone plinth. A long steel table with a channel round the edge and a rack of instruments beside it, silver and goblin-steel and glass.

In the middle of the floor, a circle.

It was twelve feet across. He'd cut it into the stone himself, on the second night, three concentric rings of runes inlaid with silver and cold iron and a thread of something rarer, and in the centre of it a steel frame bolted to the floor, upright, with cuffs at the wrists and ankles and a band for the throat. The cuffs were mostly for show. The circle was what held things.

He carried the vampire into it, stood it upright against the frame, locked the cuffs, and stepped back out across the rings.

Then he let it go.

Not all of it. He'd built the snare's paralysis to come apart in layers, like an onion. He peeled back the top one, the one that held the head and face, and left the rest.

The vampire's eyes moved.

They moved very fast. Left, right, up, down. The vault, the cases, the frame, the circle, the cuffs, Harry. They went over everything in about a quarter of a second and came back to him and stayed there. Its mouth opened all the way, and it made a noise.

No human throat made that sound. It started as a snarl and went up into something higher, a shriek like a circular saw going through a sheet of tin, and it went on and on and filled the whole vault and echoed back off the stone. Its lips peeled back from teeth that were very white and very ordinary-looking. Venom came up in its mouth, clear and thin, and ran down its chin.

Harry waited till it ran out of steam. They didn't need to breathe, but they liked to, and screaming took air. It took about forty seconds.

"Finished?" he asked.

The vampire stared at him. Red eyes. Very red. The red of something that had fed well this week.

"What are you?" it said. Its voice was hoarse from the screaming, and flat, and American, with something old underneath it. Midwestern. "What the hell are you? What is this place?"

"I'm the man asking the questions," said Harry. "This is the place I ask them. You're Abner."

The red eyes went still.

"Abner Gale," Harry said. "Born in Council Bluffs, Iowa, in 1871. Went up to the Klondike in '98 with your brother to dig for gold. Your brother died of scurvy in a cabin outside Dawson City that winter. You didn't. A woman with red eyes found you in a saloon on Front Street in the January and decided she liked the look of you." He tilted his head. "I'm told you've been on the move since."

"How," the vampire said, "do you know that?"

"Same way I know about the Hoh River trail." Harry watched its face. Nothing moved in it. They were good at that. But there were other ways of watching. "And the man outside Port Renfrew on Tuesday night. He had a little girl. Did you know that? There was a car seat in the back of his car."

Nothing.

"No," Harry said. "Course you didn't. Why would you look?"

He went in.

---

He'd skimmed the surface of it in the clearing, picking it up off the grass. It had been enough to get him the name and the hikers and the man at Port Renfrew and a general impression of what he was holding: old, and fast, and frightened, and very hungry.

Now he went down.

It was like nothing he'd ever been in. He'd been in a lot of minds. Human ones, goblin ones, a centaur once, briefly and unpleasantly. A human mind was a house: rooms, some lit and some dark, corridors, a cellar where the things you didn't want to look at were kept. Memory blurred at the edges. Old things went soft. Nobody remembered their sixth birthday the way they remembered yesterday.

This was a library with every light on.

The human part was dim. That surprised him. Twenty-seven years of a life in Iowa and the Yukon, and all of it seen through dirty water: the brother's face in the cabin in Dawson, blue with cold and scurvy, the saloon, the woman, smeared and faded like a photograph left in a window. Then the fire of the change, three days of it, and he could feel every second of that. And after the fire, everything was there. Every single thing. A hundred and seven years of it, and none of it had faded at all. A century of hunting, laid out end to end in perfect order like the pages of a ledger, each one in full colour and full sound. He could have read any of them. He could have read all of them. It would have taken him a hundred and seven years.

And it was *fast*. A human's thoughts ran on one thread. The vampire's ran on five or six at once, side by side, braided. He could feel it trying to think about him and the cuffs and the circle and how to get out, all simultaneously, and underneath that, a steady drone of thirst like a generator running in a basement.

He found it took him about a minute to get the hang of it, which was longer than it usually took him to get the hang of anything. Then he started pulling.

He didn't bother with most of the century. He'd come for specifics. He knew what the Department wanted, and he went looking for it, and for twenty minutes he stood outside the circle in the cold with his hands in his overcoat pockets and his eyes on the vampire's, and the vampire stood in the frame with its face locked and its eyes locked on his, and neither of them moved.

The rules. Every one of them knew the rules. There was only one, and the Italians had made it: *keep the secret.* Hunt as you like, kill as you like, but never let the humans know. Abner had heard it from the woman who made him, in 1899, in a cabin by the Yukon. She'd said it like a prayer.

The Italians. Everybody knew about the Italians. Abner had never been to Italy. But he'd seen them once.

Harry found it and slowed down.

Chicago. February 1962. A rooftop on the South Side, at night, in the snow. Abner had been in the city for a month and so had another one of them, a sloppy one, a nomad called Royce who'd been leaving bodies in the river with their throats torn out and making the newspapers. Abner had been on the roof of a warehouse across the street, watching, when the Italians came for Royce. Three of them, in long grey cloaks. They'd walked across the snow toward him.

A big one, broad as a door. A dark-haired one, lean, who'd led them straight to Royce across a city of four million people without once hesitating at a turning.

And a small one. A girl. She looked about fourteen. Blonde hair, a sweet face, like a picture on a Christmas card.

Royce had turned to run, and the girl had looked at him.

That was all she did. She didn't touch him. She didn't even raise a hand. She looked at him and smiled a little, and Royce fell down in the snow and screamed. He screamed and screamed. He rolled in the snow clawing at his own face. And the girl just kept looking at him, smiling, while the big one walked over and took him apart.

They'd burned the pieces right there on the roof. Abner had watched the purple smoke go up into the snow. Then the girl had turned her head and looked up, across the street, straight at the warehouse roof where Abner was lying flat behind a parapet, and smiled, and the three of them had walked away.

She'd let him go. She'd wanted him to tell people. He'd been telling people for forty-four years.

Harry stayed in the memory for a long time. He went over it five times, the way you'd run a piece of film back and forth looking for a frame. The girl. The look. Royce in the snow.

*No touch,* he thought. *No wand, no word, no gesture. Line of sight.*

Pain, then, that came out of somebody's eyes and went into somebody's head. That made it a mind-gift. And a mind-gift had to get into the mind to do its work.

He'd spent eight years building a mind nothing got into.

He didn't know if that would be enough. He wrote it down in his head as a question, with a box beside it, and drew a line under it.

And under that, a second note, which had come out of the boy's head that afternoon in the clearing, in among all the rest of it: the new one in the glass house, the girl who'd married the clock-tower boy, was something the boy called a *shield*. The mind-reader couldn't read her. He never had been able to, not even when she was human. The family had a theory that gifts slid off her the way rain slid off a roof, and nobody had tested it yet.

Harry underlined that twice.

---

He came out of the vampire's head at about twenty to six and stood for a minute, getting his bearings back. A hundred and seven years of perfect memory left a residue. Everything in the vault looked very sharp for a bit, and then went back to normal.

The vampire hadn't moved. It couldn't. But it had felt him go, and something in its face had changed. It knew what had been done to it. They were quick.

"Right," Harry said. "Thanks for that, Abner. Very useful. Now we're going to do some tests."

"Let me go," said the vampire.

"No."

"Let me go and I'll leave. I'll go north. I'll never come back here. I swear it. I'll never—"

"*Imperio*," said Harry.

He didn't need to say it. He hadn't needed the words for years. He said it anyway, sometimes, for the Unforgivables. It seemed like the least he could do.

The curse went into the vampire like a hand going into cold syrup. He felt it meet resistance at once, more than a human mind would have given him, much more: that braided five-track thinking pushing back, all the threads at once, a hundred and seven years of being nobody's but its own. It was strong. It was stronger than any human he'd ever put under. He leaned on it, steady, the way you'd lean on a door, and felt it give, layer by layer, and then all at once, and go slack.

He let the rest of the snare's paralysis go. All of it. The vampire's body unlocked in the frame, every muscle at once, and it sagged against the cuffs.

"Stand up straight," Harry said.

It stood up straight.

"Raise your right hand."

The cuff only allowed a few inches. The hand came up the few inches and stopped there.

"Tell me your name."

"Abner Gale," said the vampire, in a soft, pleasant voice, like a man answering a census-taker on his porch.

"How many people have you killed, Abner?"

"Four thousand, two hundred and nineteen," said the vampire.

Harry looked at it for a moment.

"Thank you," he said, and let the curse go.

The vampire came back into itself with a jerk that rattled the cuffs. Its eyes went wide. It had felt all of it. Harry watched it understand that it had felt all of it, and done what it was told anyway, and been glad to.

He took out a small notebook and a pencil and wrote. *IMPERIUS: takes. Heavy resistance on entry (est. 3–4× human baseline). Holds once seated. Subject retains full awareness.*

"Next one," Harry said.

He thought for a second about how long.

The girl on the rooftop in Chicago, looking at Royce, and Royce screaming in the snow. That was the Italians' best weapon, if the file and the boy's head and a dead man's memory were all right. The Department was going to send people into a city on a hill to fight her. They'd need to know whether the same thing went the other way. Whether these creatures felt pain from the outside, from magic, the way they apparently felt it from her.

He wouldn't send people up that hill without knowing. Nobody was going to ask him if he'd checked. He was going to check anyway.

"*Crucio*," he said.

The vampire screamed.

It was worse than the first scream. Much worse. It was the same sound, the saw through tin, but higher and rawer, and the body in the frame bucked against the cuffs so hard the steel frame groaned in its bolts and the inner ring of runes on the floor flared white. Its back arched. Its head slammed back against the frame once, twice, and cracked a stone tile behind it. Venom sprayed from its mouth in a fine mist.

Harry counted three seconds and stopped.

The vampire hung in the frame, shaking. It shook for a long time. Venom ran down its chin and dripped onto its dead man's rain jacket.

Harry wrote. *CRUCIATUS: takes. Full pain response. 3 sec. No apparent lasting damage. Recovery rapid.* He looked at the vampire again, and added: *Subject very much did not enjoy it.*

He didn't enjoy it either. He hadn't expected to. He wasn't the sort who did, and he'd met plenty who were, and put most of them in the ground. But he'd been in the man's head, and he'd seen the car seat in the back of the car outside Port Renfrew, and he'd seen four thousand, two hundred and nineteen faces, and he found that it didn't trouble him as much as it might have.

He'd have done it anyway. He knew that about himself, too. He'd stopped pretending otherwise a long time ago.

He left the third one alone.

It was the question the Department wanted answered most. Whether these things could be killed with a word. Whether there was anything in them for the Killing Curse to take, or whether it would go into a body that had stopped being alive in 1899 and find nothing at home. Every other question was a matter of degree. That one was the whole war.

But he only had one specimen. If it worked, he wouldn't have a specimen anymore, and he had a great deal more to do with this one. And if it didn't work, he'd want to know why, and he'd want a second one to try it on with variables changed.

That one could wait for the second specimen. The net was still out there. There'd be another.

---

He did the rest of it by the book.

He took venom. He got the vampire's jaw open under the Imperius again and drew it off the gums with a goblin-silver pipette into a crystal phial, about a teaspoon of it, clear and faintly oily and smelling of nothing at all, which was strange, given how the things themselves smelled. He labelled it and racked it.

He tried a Severing Charm on the back of its left hand at ordinary strength. Nothing. Not a mark. He tried it again at full strength, everything he had behind it, a cut that would have taken the leg off a dragon. It left a fine white line across the skin like a crack in porcelain, an inch long and maybe a millimetre deep, and he bent close and watched the crack close itself back up in about four seconds with a tiny sound like glass being ground under a boot. He wrote that down.

And he tried the one he'd bluffed with in the clearing.

He'd told the girl he could wipe the whole glass house clean. He'd said it to her face and she'd looked at him and not been able to tell. He'd been about seventy percent sure of it at the time. Seventy percent was enough to bluff on. It was not enough to plan on.

"*Obliviate*," he said, and took the clearing out of the vampire's head. The chase. The snare. The wolves. Him.

Then he went back into the library with every light on and looked.

It had taken. Mostly. The hour was gone; the shelves where it had been were empty. But the edges of it were still there, faintly, the way you could still see the shape where a picture had hung on a wall after you'd taken it down. And as he watched, the empty shelf seemed to — he didn't have a better word for it — *strain*. Like the mind was trying to remember the thing it had forgotten, very slowly, by sheer weight of everything else it had never forgotten. He didn't know if it would get there. A week. A year. Never.

*OBLIVIATE: takes, partially,* he wrote. *Unstable. Edges persist. Recommend repeated application & observation. Do NOT rely on.* And under that, because he was honest in his own notebook if nowhere else: *Bluffed girl on this. Would have had to find out the hard way.*

He shut the notebook.

The vampire was watching him from the frame. It didn't know who he was now. He could see that. It had looked at him when the memory went out, and he'd seen it look at him like a stranger, and then — just now, as he wrote — something had flickered in the back of the red eyes. A frown that never quite made it to the face. A piece of a shelf, straining.

"Get some rest, Abner," Harry said. "Big day tomorrow."

He reset the snare's paralysis, all of it, top to bottom, and the vampire locked rigid in the frame with its eyes open.

He went back up the stairs. The steel door shut behind him on its own.

---

He wrote the report at the kitchen table, in the black notebook that had a twin in a locked drawer on the ninth level in London, with a cup of tea at his elbow and a fry-up going on the Aga: six eggs, most of a pound of bacon, a tin of beans, three slices of fried bread. He'd been hungry since about four. He was always hungry these days. He'd stopped thinking about why.

*25 Nov. Specimen acquired 1631 local, sector 7 of the net. Male, apparent age late 20s, actual 135, turned 1899. Full debrief in Pensieve; key extracts below. Unforgivables I and II tested, both take; details attached. III not tested, single specimen, await second. Memory charm: partial, unreliable. Item of interest re: the Italians' gifted guard: see extract 4 (Chicago, 1962). Item of interest re: the local coven: one member reported to be a natural mental shield. Not yet confirmed. If true, value of local coven revised sharply upward.*

He stopped, and thought, and went on.

*Contact made with local magical. Female, American, curse-breaker, early 20s, highly capable. Has breached their Statute with the coven, the wolves (see below), and one No-Maj, the local police chief. Leverage established; cooperation secured for future requirements. No identifying information given. She does not know who we are and will not find out from me.*

*Local wolves: shape-shifters, not lycanthropes. Telepathic among themselves. Legilimency-accessible; they have no defence. Very large. Very fast. Allied to the coven, hostile to red-eyes. Useful.*

*Local coven aware of my presence as of tonight. Message left via a wolf. I will make contact on my own schedule.*

He signed it with the mark he used and closed the notebook and sat back and ate his eggs.

When he opened it again, ten minutes later, there was a reply under his in Croaker's small cramped hand, the ink still settling.

*Good. Watch the Americans. Don't get attached to the locals.*

Harry snorted and closed the notebook.

He thought about the girl, while he ate. Brennan. She was very good. That triple she'd opened with, the Disarming Charm and the ropes and the Blasting Curse coming in underneath, was competition work, and properly done. And the last thing she'd thrown, the seam-ripper, the one that went looking for the joins in a shield and pulled them apart like a zip, was original. He'd never seen it before. It had gone through his outer shield in about a third of a second before he caught it. He'd have to build something for that. He'd enjoy building something for that.

He'd scared her badly. He'd meant to. He'd needed her scared enough to take him seriously and not so scared she did something stupid, and he thought he'd landed it about right.

The leverage would hold. The chief was the hinge of it. A witch could talk herself into a grey area for wolves and vampires. She couldn't talk herself out of a No-Maj policeman, and she knew it, and she'd do whatever she had to do to keep the man's memories in his head where they belonged. That was blackmail, and it would hold, and those were the two things he needed to know about it.

He'd have to have a look at the chief's fog sometime. The boy's head said the fog ran in the family. It said the daughter had it too. In the daughter, apparently, it had grown teeth.

And the boy.

He'd been in the boy's head for twenty minutes in that clearing, and the boy's head was the most open thing he'd ever been inside. No doors. No locks. Everything out on the front lawn for anybody to look at, a girl in a yellow raincoat, a baby who showed you pictures, a sister with a bad wound she wouldn't let anybody touch, a mother, a police chief eating fried chicken, a pack of friends who ran together in the dark. And a man in a kitchen, putting a record on a turntable on a Sunday morning. A big man, with a laugh. *Houses of the Holy*. And the same man, later, on the floor of that kitchen, and an ambulance that hadn't come fast enough.

The boy's father had been called Harry.

He hadn't said anything about that. He wouldn't. He'd filed it, with everything else.

He finished eating, and washed up, and dried the plate and put it away. Then he went over to the dresser by the door and crouched by the crates of records underneath the turntable, and went through them, and found the one he wanted, and slid it out of its sleeve.

He put it on. He set the needle down. He stood there at the dresser for a while with his hands in his pockets, listening to the crackle in the lead-in groove, and then the guitar.

Outside the tent it was raining. Eleven miles west, the girl would be home by now, in her blue house with the new step, with the boy beside her. Further south, a houseful of vampires would be sitting up late in a glass house in the trees, listening to his voice play over and over in a wolf's head. Somewhere on the reservation a big russet wolf would be pacing. Downstairs, a man who'd killed four thousand, two hundred and nineteen people stood locked in a steel frame in the cold with his eyes open and a hole in his memory that he'd spend the rest of the night pushing at.

And all along forty miles of national forest, under the moss, the net hummed to itself in the dark and waited for the next one.

The second track came on. *The Rain Song.* That seemed about right.

Harry turned the lights down, left the record playing, and went to bed.

Darlene opened at six on Sundays. He'd see who came in.
