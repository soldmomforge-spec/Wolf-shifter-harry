# Specimen

## Chapter Three: Yellowfin

---

**The Cullen house — Wednesday, October 4 — 7:41 p.m.**

The telephone rang once, and every head in the house turned toward it.

Carlisle was in the front room with a medical journal open on his knee that he hadn't turned a page of in forty minutes. He set it down. The phone was on the wall by the kitchen door, an old cream-colored thing Esme kept because she liked the sound of it, and it rang a second time while he crossed the room, and he let it ring a third time with his hand resting on the receiver. He knew who it was. He didn't need Alice for that. Nobody else had called this house in a week who wasn't family or a wolf.

Behind him the room had gone completely still. Emmett had stopped mid-sentence. Rosalie, on the stairs with the baby, had stopped mid-step. Jacob, on the floor with his back against the couch and a paper plate of Esme's lasagna balanced on his knee, had stopped chewing. Upstairs, Bella had stopped whatever she was doing, and Edward had stopped beside her.

Carlisle picked up the receiver.

"Cullen residence."

"Doctor Cullen." The voice was English, low and level, with a little roughness at the edges, like a good road with gravel on its shoulder. "Evans. Returning your call."

"Mr. Evans." Carlisle kept his own voice exactly as courteous as he would have with a patient's family at three in the morning. "Thank you for calling back."

"I said I would, when I had something worth saying. Took a bit longer than I'd hoped. Sorry about that."

"Not at all."

"I'll keep it short, Doctor. You'll have people listening and I've got an early start." A small pause, as if he were looking at a list. "Two things."

"Go ahead."

"First. The usefulness of my temporary roommate has run its course."

Carlisle didn't say anything for a moment. In the silence he could hear, very faintly, a kettle beginning to sing somewhere on the other end of the line, and the tick of a clock, and a man breathing slowly.

"Your roommate," Carlisle said.

"The gentleman your friends chased into my snare on Tuesday."

"I see." He did. Every person in the room behind him did. He felt rather than heard Esme's hand go to her mouth. "And second?"

"Second," said the voice, pleasantly, "if you and yours could refrain from burning any further volunteers, I'd be very grateful. Greatly appreciated, honestly."

"Volunteers."

"Anybody who comes onto the peninsula with red eyes. I've a use for them. If one turns up, leave him to the snares. They'll keep him out of mischief till I get there." A beat. "I know it's not your habit. I know you've a bonfire or two to your name. I'm asking nicely."

Carlisle stood with the receiver against his ear and looked at the kitchen door, at the grain of the wood, at nothing.

"Mr. Evans," he said. "What happened to him?"

"I'm not going to go into that, Doctor."

"Is he dead?"

"I'm not going to go into that either."

"You've asked me to hand you every one of our kind who comes through these woods—"

"I've asked you not to burn them," the voice said, without any change in it. "That's all I've asked. What you do otherwise is your business."

"And what you do with them?"

"Is mine."

"I'd like to understand—"

"I'm sure you would," said Evans. Not unkindly. "I'd like a bacon sandwich and eight hours' sleep, and I'm only getting one of them tonight. We'll talk properly, Doctor. You and me. I'd like that, as it happens. But not tonight, and not on the telephone, and not about this."

"Then when?"

"When I've decided what I want from you."

It was so plainly said that Carlisle almost smiled. He'd spent three hundred and sixty years among people who wanted things from him and didn't say so. There was something very nearly restful in a man who did.

"And my family?" he said. "In the meantime?"

"Guest list, Doctor. Same as Tuesday. Nothing's changed. Nothing's going to change unless one of you comes home with red eyes, and I don't expect that." A pause. "The newest one included. She's doing well, from what I hear. Better than most."

The room behind Carlisle went a different kind of still.

"From what you hear," Carlisle said.

"I hear all sorts of things. Good night, Doctor. Mind how you go."

"Mr. Evans—"

The line clicked. The dial tone came after it, flat and steady.

Carlisle stood for a moment longer with the receiver in his hand. Then he hung it up, carefully, on its hook, and turned around.

---

They were all there now. Bella had come down, and Edward beside her with his hand at the small of her back, very lightly, the way he'd touched her for three weeks, as though a newborn might go through a wall if startled and he wanted to be the thing between her and the wall. Alice was in the doorway to the dining room with her eyes half-closed. Jasper was behind her. Esme had come to stand by the piano. Rosalie had reached the bottom of the stairs with the baby on her hip, and the baby was looking at Carlisle with her enormous brown eyes, perfectly calm, the only one in the room who was.

Jacob had put his plate down.

"Well," said Emmett. "He's polite."

"He's killed him," said Esme. Her voice was very quiet. "Hasn't he? That's what *run its course* means."

"Or he's finished with him," said Rosalie.

"Same thing, babe."

"Finished with him could mean he let him go," said Rosalie. "Shipped him off somewhere. Put him in a jar. We don't know."

"He didn't let him go," Edward said.

Everyone looked at him.

"I don't know that," Edward said. "I can't hear him. I'll never be able to hear him. But I've been in Leah's head and Seth's head and I've heard every word that man has said since Tuesday, and he's never once said a thing he didn't mean, or let a thing go he'd got his hands on." He was looking at the phone on the wall. "He didn't let him go."

"*Volunteers*," said Jasper.

He'd come out from behind Alice and was standing with his arms folded, and his face had the faraway look it got when he was moving pieces on a map in his head.

"Plural," he said. "Not *the next one*. *Volunteers*. He's planning on more than one. He's asking us to stop killing nomads because he wants them alive, and he wants them alive because he isn't done. Whatever he was doing to the first one, he wants to do it again." He looked at Carlisle. "Maybe he's not done with the question. Maybe he got an answer and wants to check it."

"Check it how?" said Bella.

Jasper didn't answer that.

Carlisle went back to his chair and sat down and picked up the medical journal and looked at it without seeing it and put it down again.

"He's asking us to stand aside," he said, "while he does whatever he's doing to them. Whatever it is."

"They kill people, Carlisle," said Rosalie. "The ones with red eyes. That one killed a nurse at Lake Crescent. It was on the news. And a hunter from Raymond, they think." She shifted the baby on her hip. "We've burned nomads before. We burned the ones Victoria brought. Nobody in this room cried."

"We burned them to stop them," Carlisle said. "That was all. We didn't keep them."

"Does it matter?"

"I don't know," said Carlisle honestly. "I think it might."

He thought of his father. He didn't often; he'd had three and a half centuries to learn not to. But he thought of him now: a thin, upright, terrible man in black, in the parish of Saint Mary's, standing in the smoke with his Bible open and his eyes shining. His father had not burned people to stop them. His father had kept them first. In a cellar under the church, sometimes for weeks. Asking questions. Writing the answers down in a careful clerk's hand in a book he locked in his desk. Carlisle had been a boy. He'd carried the candles down the cellar steps.

*Volunteers.*

His father had used that word, once, about a woman from Southwark who'd confessed to everything.

"He said Bella's doing well," Edward said quietly. "*From what I hear.*"

Bella's head came up.

"Who's he hearing it from?" said Jacob. He was on his feet now. "Who's he talking to? There's nobody. There's us and there's the pack and there's—"

"Charlie," said Bella.

The word went through the room.

"He doesn't—" Jacob started.

"Charlie's been telling people I'm better," Bella said. Her voice was flat and very controlled, the way it went when she was holding on to something with both hands. "At the station. At the diner. He told Mrs. Newton. He told the pharmacist. He tells everyone, he's so—" She stopped. "He's so happy about it. He says, *she's doing well, she's doing better than anybody thought*. That's what he says. That's exactly what he says."

"*From what I hear*," Edward repeated.

"He's listening to my dad talk about me," Bella said. "And he's repeating it back to you. So you'll know he's listening."

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"Saturday," said Alice, from the doorway.

They turned.

She hadn't opened her eyes. "Charlie's still going. I keep checking. I'll keep checking. Six o'clock, the dock at La Push, Billy with the coffee can, the boat going in, the mist. And the hole in it. Sitting next to Billy." She opened her eyes. "Nothing's changed. Whatever he wants with Charlie, he's still going to go and get it."

"And whatever he wants with Billy," said Jacob.

"I can't see Billy any better than I can see you, Jacob. Billy's always a bit blurry. He's too close to all of you." Alice put her fingertips to her temple. "I'm watching Charlie. Charlie, and the hole."

Jacob looked at the baby on Rosalie's hip, and the baby looked back at him, and put out one small hand toward him without a sound. He went and took her. He held her against his chest, his big hand spread across her back, and she put her palm flat on his cheek, and his face went quiet the way it always did when she did that, as though someone had turned the volume down on the whole world.

Then it hardened again.

"I'll be there," he said. "Saturday. At the beach. I'll watch the boat from the rocks."

"He'll see you," said Edward.

"Then he'll see me," said Jacob. "That's my dad in that boat."

Carlisle looked at him, and at the baby, and at Bella, standing very still with her red eyes on the phone on the wall, and at Esme by the piano, and at all of them.

"Nobody burns anyone," he said quietly. "Not until we understand what he's asking. If a nomad comes, and I pray none does, we drive it off, if it can be driven, and we call him." He paused. "And we find out what he is."

"How?" said Rosalie.

"I don't know yet," said Carlisle. He picked up the journal again. This time he turned the page. "He'll tell us. I think he wants to. He just wants to choose the day."

**The clearing — Thursday, October 5 — 5:02 a.m.**

Emmett went over her hip and hit the ground on his back so hard the turf split under him.

Leah stood over him, breathing hard, her hands still up. Her shoulder ached. Her hip ached. The ground in the clearing was cold and wet under her bare feet and the sky over the treeline had just started to go from black to the dark blue of a bruise, and a vampire the size of a refrigerator was lying in a crater in the grass at her feet, and she had put him there.

Emmett blinked up at the sky. Then he started to laugh.

"Again," he said. "Do that again."

"Get up first."

"I'm getting up." He didn't get up. He lay there laughing, a big booming laugh that bounced off the trees on the far side of the clearing. "Jazz. Jazz, did you see that? She *threw* me."

"I saw," said Jasper.

He was standing at the edge of the clearing with his hands in the pockets of a long canvas coat, the way he'd been standing for the last forty minutes, watching. He hadn't moved much. He'd only spoken when he had to. *Lower. Your weight's in your heels. Turn the hip first, the hand follows the hip. Again.* He spoke the way he probably had to men in the war, Leah thought, flat and patient, the same correction ten times, twenty times, without a flicker of impatience, until her body did it without her.

"She didn't throw you," he said now. "You threw yourself. She put her hip where your weight was going and got out of the way."

"Sounds like throwing to me."

"It's better than throwing," said Jasper. "Throwing takes strength."

Emmett got up. He brushed the dirt off his shoulders, still grinning, and the crater he'd made in the turf was the shape of his back. Leah looked at it and felt something hot and fierce go through her, and it came from the human part of her. The part that had been eleven and had wanted to beat her cousins at arm-wrestling and never could.

She'd been here since four-fifty. Seth had come too, yawning, and was sitting on a stump at the edge of the trees with his knees up, eating a sleeve of crackers he'd taken from their mother's cupboard, watching with his whole face. He'd asked to go next twice. Jasper had said *later* both times. Leah suspected Jasper was saving him for when she was too tired to watch, so she wouldn't have to see her little brother get thrown.

"Again," said Jasper.

Emmett came at her.

He came the way he always came, the way he'd come at the newborns in this same clearing in June, all at once, arms wide, a wall falling over. A month ago she'd have done what she'd always done, which was meet it with her own weight, or phase and meet it with teeth. Now she watched his feet. She'd been watching feet all morning. *Where's his weight. Where's it going. Don't be there.* His left foot came down and his weight went forward over it and she stepped off the line to the right, half a step, not even that, and caught his wrist as it went past her ear.

Turn. Fold. The angle.

She felt the line run from her thumb through the small bones of his wrist and up his arm and lock his elbow, and she felt the exact moment when his own weight, still coming forward, ran into the end of the line. His body went where the line told it. He went down past her onto his knees in the grass with his arm straight up behind him like a man reaching for a branch.

She held it. She didn't lean.

Emmett knelt very still.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. That's—yeah. Okay."

"Pull," said Jasper.

"I'm good, thanks."

"Pull, Emmett."

Emmett pulled. Leah felt it start—a creak, deep in the joint, like ice—and she let go before it finished. Emmett rolled away and sat up in the grass rubbing his wrist, and for once he wasn't laughing.

"That's three times," he said. "Three times since yesterday somebody's done that to me. I'm starting to take it personally."

"Good," said Jasper.

He came out from the edge of the trees, finally, and crouched down in the grass in front of Leah and looked at her feet. Just her feet. He looked at them for a long time, and then he looked up.

"You're good at this," he said.

She didn't know what to do with that, so she didn't do anything.

"You were good at it before," he said. "In the woods. In June. I watched you then. You're fast and you're light and you think while you're doing it, which most of them can't. Paul can't. Jared can't. Jacob can, a little, when he's angry enough." He stood up. "But you were fighting like a wolf in a human body. Now you're starting to fight like a human in one. That's the difference he was talking about."

*He.* Nobody had said the name all morning. Nobody had needed to.

"He said I'm a civilian," Leah said.

"You said."

"He held my hand on a table with his thumb. In a diner. With his other hand he was drinking coffee." She hadn't meant to say it again. It came out of her like it had been coming out of her for two days. "He didn't even look at it. He looked at me. Like it was nothing. Like I was nothing."

"He looked at you," said Jasper, "because he was watching your eyes to see what you'd do next. You don't watch the hand you've already got. You watch the one you haven't." He paused. "That's a man who's done it a thousand times, Leah."

"To who?"

"I'd like to know that too," Jasper said.

Seth crunched a cracker. "Can I go now?"

"Later," said Jasper and Leah together.

---

Jacob came out of the trees at five-forty with the light going gray behind him.

He was in human form, in cutoffs and nothing else, the way he always was now, his hair still wet from the river. He'd come straight from the house. Leah could smell it on him, the sweet sick stink of it under the river water, and under that, faint and clean, the baby. He stood at the edge of the clearing with his hands on his hips and looked at the crater in the grass, and at Emmett rubbing his wrist, and at Leah.

"Huh," he said.

"Yeah," said Emmett. "Huh."

"You did that?"

"Twice," said Leah.

Something went across Jacob's face. She knew him well enough now, after seven months in his head, to read it, and she didn't like what she read, because it was pride, and it was for her, and she didn't know where to put that either. Then it went away and he was just Jake again, tired, too big for his own skin.

"He called," Jacob said.

"Who?"

"Who do you think." He sat down on the stump next to Seth and took a handful of crackers without asking. "Last night. Seven-forty. He called the house. Carlisle talked to him."

Leah went still. "And?"

"And nothing. Two things. That's what he said, *two things*." Jacob ate a cracker. "First: the usefulness of his temporary roommate has run its course."

"His—"

"That's what he said. *Temporary roommate.* Like the leech was sleeping on his couch."

"He killed him," said Leah.

"He wouldn't say. Carlisle asked him straight out. *Is he dead.* He said, I'm not going into that, Doctor." Jacob's voice did a flat, quick, not very good English accent on the last part, and Seth snorted crackers. "And then the second thing. He said if the Cullens could please refrain from burning any further volunteers, it'd be greatly appreciated."

Nobody said anything.

"Volunteers," Leah said finally.

"Any leech with red eyes on the peninsula. He wants them. Alive. He wants the Cullens to leave them for his traps." Jacob looked at the ground. "He didn't say anything about us."

"He doesn't have to ask us," said Leah. "We're civilians."

Jacob looked up at her sharply. Then, slowly, something like a grin pulled at one side of his mouth, and went away again.

"Carlisle asked him what he does with them," Jacob said. "He said, *that's mine*. Carlisle asked him who he is. He said they'd talk properly when he'd decided what he wanted from him. Then he said Bella was doing well, from what he hears, and hung up."

"Bella," said Seth.

"He's been listening to Charlie," said Jacob. "In town. Charlie tells everybody about Bella. How she's better. He's been listening, and he said it back to Carlisle so we'd know." His jaw worked. "Bella went white. Well. Whiter."

Leah thought about the diner. About the dark window, and the man's eyes going to her in it without his head turning. About Charlie's face going soft at her mother's name, and the man cutting his pie with the side of his fork and not looking up.

"He's collecting," she said. "He just—collects. Everything anybody says. Everything anybody does. He puts it all somewhere."

"Yeah," said Jacob.

"Billy?" said Leah.

"I told him. Wednesday night. All of it." Jacob looked at his hands. "He didn't say a word the whole time. Then when I was done, he said, *thank you, son*." Jacob's mouth twisted. "And then he asked me whether I thought the Englishman would want coffee or tea in the thermos. Because he's English."

Seth laughed. Leah didn't. Neither did Jacob.

"He's going," Leah said.

"He's going. I asked him not to. He said Charlie's been his friend for thirty years and he's not going to let him go out on the water with a stranger by himself." Jacob rubbed his face. "And he said he wanted to look at him. With his own eyes. He said, *I've heard about this man from my son and from a vampire and from Sue Clearwater's girl, and I'd like to form my own opinion.*"

"That sounds like Billy."

"Yeah. It sounds exactly like Billy." Jacob stood up. "I'm going to be on the rocks at the river mouth. All morning. Edward says he'll see me. Fine. I want him to see me."

"I'll come," said Leah.

"No." He said it the way he said things now, sometimes, since he'd broken from Sam: with the weight of an order underneath it. "I need you on the line. Nobody's watched the snare line since Tuesday. If he's got something else out there, I want to know. You and Seth take the north."

She wanted to argue. She opened her mouth to.

Then she looked at the crater in the grass, and at her own bare feet, and at Jasper standing quietly at the edge of the clearing with his hands in his coat, who'd spent forty minutes telling her where to put them.

"Fine," she said.

Jacob looked surprised. He looked at Jasper, and then back at her, and didn't say anything.

"Seth," said Jasper. "Your turn."

Seth dropped the crackers and jumped up off the stump so fast he nearly fell over. "Yes. Okay. Yes. Who am I fighting? Am I fighting Emmett?"

"You're fighting your sister," said Jasper.

Seth's face fell. Leah felt something in her chest she hadn't felt in a long time, small and light and almost like laughter, and she didn't let it get to her face.

"Hands up, Balto," she said.

Seth groaned. Emmett lost it completely. Even Jasper smiled.

Jacob stood at the edge of the clearing with the sky going gray behind him and watched his beta put his other beta on his back in the wet grass, gently, three times in a row, and he didn't say anything at all. But he stayed and watched until the sun was up behind the clouds, which he never did, because there was always somewhere else he had to be.

**Field station, Olympic National Forest — Saturday, October 7 — 4:38 a.m.**

Harry woke two minutes before the alarm, the way he always did, and lay in the dark listening to the tent not make any noise.

Then he got up and showered and shaved and put the kettle on, and while it boiled he walked down the corridor to the lab in his socks and looked in through the white door at cabinet N.A. 01.

Merritt Lowe stood in it, upright, the way Harry had left him on Wednesday night, with a binding around him to keep him that way. Gray all over. Matte as an old bar of soap. His eyes were open and filmed, looking out through the glass at nothing. The jars on the shelf in front of him had not moved. Ash. Venom. A gray fingertip. A gray sliver of forearm.

He'd thought he might feel something, looking at it in the mornings. He didn't, particularly. He felt what he'd have felt looking at an engine on a bench that had thrown a rod in the middle of a test: annoyed, and interested, and already thinking about the next one.

He went back to the kitchen and made tea and drank it standing at the counter.

---

The coat was hanging on the back of the kitchen door, where he'd hung it on Friday afternoon after he'd bought it. It was a float coat, the kind men wore on boats so they'd come back up if they went over the side. Newton's Olympic Outfitters had had two left, one in navy and one in a shade of orange that the label called *Safety Blaze* and that Harry privately thought of as *Traffic Cone*. He'd bought the orange one. The Chief had told him not to wear the canvas jacket because the fish would see him coming. The Chief would have nothing whatever to say about a man who'd come dressed as a traffic cone.

He'd done some other shopping on Friday too.

At the Thriftway he'd bought coffee filters and a newspaper and a pack of bacon, and chatted to the girl on register two, whose name was Kimmy and who was saving up to go to beauty school in Tacoma and who thought the English accent was *so cute*, and while he chatted he'd let a bronze Knut drop from his fingers and roll under the bottom shelf of aisle four, between the paper towels and the charcoal briquettes, where nobody would sweep it up until Christmas. At Newton's, while Mrs. Newton went into the back to look for the orange coat in a large, he'd tucked another one behind the rack of fishing flies by the till.

Two coins. Two anchors.

He took the third out of his pocket now and turned it over in his fingers. Bronze. Plain. Warded on both faces so finely you'd need a loupe to see the work. He'd spent eleven months making the set, in a windowless room on the ninth level, with the shelves of the rebuilt Hall of Prophecy humming away through the wall.

It was a joke he didn't tell many people. When he was fifteen he'd run through the Hall of Prophecy with five friends and a dozen Death Eaters behind him and brought the shelves down like dominoes, thousands of them, every glass sphere in the place shattered on the stone. When he'd joined the Department at twenty-one, the very first job they'd given him, before Croaker had even let him near the fourth floor, was helping to rebuild it. Three years. Sweeping up glass. Re-cataloguing. Sitting with the old Seers the Department kept on retainer while they re-recorded what they could remember of prophecies that hadn't come true yet.

Somewhere in the second year of that, with glass in his hair and a seventy-year-old woman in the next chair telling him in a dreamy voice that a man with a red beard would die in a lift, he'd had the thought that became the coins.

If a prophecy could be made, it could be forged.

A Seer saw what was going to happen. That was all the gift was, under the incense and the shawls. She looked down the road and saw the likeliest thing coming along it. The Department's own warding, the kind he wore in a charm on a chain round his neck and had stitched into the canvas of the tent, did the obvious thing: it took you off the road. Made you a hole. Very effective, and very loud, because a Seer who was looking for you would see the hole and know exactly what it meant.

He'd wanted something quieter. Something that, instead of taking you off the road, put a better-looking you somewhere else on it. Anchored the false you to real places, so it sat right in the futures of the people who'd be there. And scrubbed the real you out of the futures of the people you were actually with, so that a Seer looking at them would see them talking to each other and nobody else. No hole anywhere. Just a different, perfectly ordinary morning, with you in it, buying coffee filters.

He'd called it the Fata Morgana. After the mirages sailors saw on the horizon: whole cities floating in the air, ships sailing upside down over the sea. The register had wanted to call it something longer. For once Harry had won.

He'd tested it on everything the Department had. The rebuilt orbs, which had obligingly recorded a prophecy about him buying a pint of milk in Croydon on a Tuesday when he'd been in Inverness. Crystal balls. Scrying bowls. Tea leaves, God help him. A Seer on the fourth floor who had known exactly what he was doing and so proved nothing. He'd even tried it on Sybill Trelawney at a Christmas drinks at Hogwarts, which had proved less than nothing, because she'd looked at him over her sherry and predicted his violent death before Easter, which she'd been doing every year since he was thirteen.

He'd never once had the chance to try it on a real, live, unwarned Seer who was actively looking for him, and who would act on what she saw.

Until now.

*The small dark one,* Gianna had thought, standing in the bakery queue on the Via Porta all'Arco, *who sees what's coming. Aro wants her more than anyone. He'd give the whole guard for her.* Alice Cullen. Harry had been a hole in her road for two weeks. He'd been a hole sitting across from the Chief in the diner, and a hole in a boat, and she would be watching that hole right now, all night, from the big glass house in the woods.

So he'd give her something better to look at. And he'd find out whether a vampire's gift answered to a wizard's lie.

It was a very simple test. If she was fooled, nobody would come to the dock this morning. If she wasn't, somebody would.

---

He dressed in layers: thermal, flannel, a wool sweater with a hole in the elbow that he'd had since before the war and refused to throw away. Jeans. Boots. The shoulder holster went on over the sweater, under the coat. He took the Browning out of it and dropped the magazine and checked it, thirteen rounds, ordinary nine-millimeter, nothing clever in them, and put it back and racked a round and set the safety and holstered it. He didn't expect to need it on a fishing trip. He'd also never once in eight years needed a gun on the day he'd decided not to bring it.

He pulled on the orange coat and looked at himself in the kitchen window.

"Christ," he said.

He left the tent at five-oh-eight.

---

The Chevelle ran up the Forest Service road with its headlights on and the wipers going on intermittent against the drizzle, and came down onto 101 and went north, through Forks, past the dark diner and the dark Thriftway and the dark police station. The Chief's cruiser was gone from its slot; he'd be at Billy Black's already, or on the way. At the junction north of town Harry turned left onto the 110 and drove west toward the coast with the window cracked an inch, and the air that came in smelled of salt, and wet cedar, and the sea.

He'd gone over Billy Black on Friday night with a pot of tea. Billy Black: tribal elder, council member, widower, father of the Alpha of the smaller pack. A man who'd known for most of his life what was in the woods, and had told the stories round the fire to children, and watched those children turn into what was in the stories. A man who'd have been told, by his son, every word Harry had said since Tuesday.

Different man from the Chief. Different lock.

The Chief opened if you made him laugh and kept your hands where he could see them. Billy Black wouldn't open for anything Harry could do with his hands. Billy Black would sit in a boat and look at him and wait for the lie, and when it came he'd know it for what it was and he'd close up for good.

So Harry wasn't going to lie to him. Not much. Not about anything that mattered.

There were three things he wanted out of the day. He'd written them on the back of an envelope the night before and burned the envelope in the sink, out of habit.

The Chief, for Croaker. A proper look, this time. In the boat, with the guards down.

Billy Black's head, for the job. Whatever was in there about the packs, the treaty, the old stories. Everything the Department didn't have, which was nearly everything.

And a test.

He'd thought about the test for a long time, on Friday night, after the tea had gone cold. Then he'd stopped thinking about it and decided.

At the end of the day, he'd leave a mark on Billy Black. A small one. A scent. He'd let a little of the other shape up into his hand, for a second, no more, and put the hand on the old man somewhere friendly, a shoulder, the back of a coat, and then he'd go home. And the wolves would smell it on their elder, on the father of one of their Alphas. A wolf they'd never smelled before. Big. On their land. On their man.

And he'd see what they did. How fast they came. Whether both packs came, or one. Whether they came together or snapped at each other over it. Whether they came looking for him, and how, and what they did when they didn't find him. It would tell him more about how the packs worked under pressure than a month of watching them run their loops in the rain.

That was the test.

And partly—he could admit this to himself, alone, in the dark, in a car, at five-forty in the morning, dressed as a traffic cone—partly he'd do it just to wind them up. The grey one had thought something about him in the diner so loudly that he'd felt it through the shields he'd put up specially not to hear her, like a door slammed two rooms away. The big one, the Alpha, the Chief's daughter's best friend, would go absolutely spare. It was going to be a very long, very loud Saturday afternoon at La Push.

He was, he'd be the first to admit, a bit of a dick.

He'd made his peace with it years ago.

---

At five-forty-six he pulled onto the gravel shoulder a hundred yards short of the sign that marked the reservation line, and turned off the engine, and sat for a moment in the ticking quiet with the third coin in his palm.

Then he went to work.

It took four minutes of fiddly work, like threading a needle in a moving car. He had to hold three places at once in his head: the Thriftway aisle with the Knut under the bottom shelf, the rack of flies at Newton's, and himself, here, in the car. He had to lay a morning down along the road from the coins, a whole ordinary morning, minute by minute, coffee filters and bacon and a newspaper and a box of flies and a wool hat, and the people who'd be there to see it. Kimmy on register two. Mrs. Newton. A man in a Mariners cap who'd hold the door. He had to make it likelier than the truth. And then he had to reach back along the road toward the dock at La Push and take himself out of it, carefully, the way you'd lift a photograph out of a frame without disturbing the glass, so that what was left was a dock, and a boat, and two old friends, and nothing else.

He closed his hand round the coin. It went cold, then hot, then nothing at all.

Somewhere, he supposed, in a big glass house in the woods, a small dark woman with her eyes half-closed had just watched a hole in a boat close up like water.

He started the car.

He crossed the reservation line at five-fifty-one. The sky over the sea was going from black to gray. The road ran down through the trees toward the river mouth, and as it came out into the open at the bottom, he saw the harbor, and the dark water, and the black hump of James Island standing up out of the mist beyond it, and on the boat ramp at the end of the little marina, a man in a wheelchair with a coffee can in his lap, and a police cruiser backing a trailer down into the river.

Harry parked the Chevelle nose out, got out, and walked down the ramp in the orange coat.

**The Cullen house — 5:50 a.m.**

Alice had been looking at the hole for nine hours.

She'd sat down on the floor by the piano a little after nine the night before and hadn't moved since, except once to let Jasper put a blanket round her shoulders that she didn't need. Bella had sat on the stairs most of the night, watching Alice watch, until Edward came and sat beside her. Jacob had left at a quarter to five in his little red car with Edward's spare cell phone in his pocket, because Edward had insisted, and the house was quieter still after that, because the wolf-smell went with him and the blur went with it.

And all night, Alice had looked at Charlie's Saturday.

It came in pieces, the way futures always did, like cards shuffled too fast to read, until she held still long enough for them to slow. Charlie's alarm clock at four-forty-five. Charlie in the kitchen in his long johns, frying an egg. Charlie hitching the boat trailer to the cruiser in the dark with a flashlight in his teeth. The drive. The little marina at La Push, the boat ramp, the black water. Billy at the top of the ramp in his chair with a coffee can of herring in his lap and a thermos wedged down beside his hip. Mist on the river. James Island standing up out of the gray behind it all like the back of a whale.

And the hole.

It stood on the ramp beside Billy. Later it sat in the boat, between Billy and the stern, where a third man would sit. It was the shape of a man, and it was empty, like the place in a photograph where somebody's been cut out with scissors, so that you could see the table through it. Charlie talked to it. Charlie laughed at it, twice. Billy looked at it, a lot, with his face very still.

She'd watched it all night. Every few minutes she'd gone back and checked, the way you'd keep touching a sore tooth. The hole was always there. It had been there at nine, and at midnight, and at three, and at five.

At five-fifty, it closed.

She felt it happen. She'd been looking straight at it, at the boat ramp at six-ten, Billy with his coffee can and Charlie in the river in his waders guiding the boat off the trailer, and the hole standing on the ramp between them, and then—

It filled in. Like water. Like a stone dropped into a pond, except backward: the ripples came in from the edges and met in the middle and the surface went smooth, and where the hole had been there was just the ramp, the concrete, the mist.

Nobody was standing there.

Alice sat up so fast the blanket fell off.

"Alice?" said Jasper, from the couch.

"Wait."

She went forward. Six-fifteen. Charlie had the boat in the water and was wading back to the cruiser to park it, and he looked at his watch, and he looked up the ramp at the empty road, and he said, *Guess your Englishman's not coming, Billy.*

And Billy said, *He's not my Englishman.*

Charlie laughed. He said, *Well, he's not mine either.* He said, *Five more minutes and we go without him.* And five minutes later they went without him. Charlie lifted Billy out of the chair and into the boat the way he'd been doing for years, and folded the chair and stowed it, and pushed off, and started the motor, and the boat went down the river toward the mouth with two men in it and nobody else.

Two men. Talking to each other.

She went forward again. Seven. Eight. Nine. The boat out past the river mouth on the gray swell, a mile off, rocking. Charlie and Billy with their lines in the water. Talking about the Mariners. About Sue Clearwater's fry bread. About Jacob, and how he never came home anymore, and Billy saying *he's got his reasons*. About Charlie's knee. Charlie talked to Billy and Billy talked to Charlie and neither of them talked to anyone else, because there wasn't anyone else. There was just the boat, and the sea, and the two of them.

No hole. Anywhere. Not in the boat, not on the ramp, not on the road.

"He's not going," Alice said. She heard her own voice crack. "He's not there. Jasper, he's not in the boat."

The house moved.

Bella was off the stairs and across the room and crouched in front of her before Alice saw her go. Edward was a step behind her. Carlisle came out of his study. Rosalie came down the stairs with the baby, and Emmett came in from the porch, and Esme from the garden with dirt on her hands.

"Say that again," Bella said.

"He's not in the boat. Charlie and Billy go out alone. Charlie waits for him and he doesn't come and they go without him." Alice was laughing, a little, she didn't know why. "The hole's gone, Bella. It closed up. Five minutes ago."

Bella sat back on her heels. She put both hands over her face. Her shoulders shook, once, twice, with a sound that would have been sobbing if she'd been able to cry.

Edward put his hand on her back.

"Where is he, then?" said Rosalie.

Alice had already gone looking.

She'd never been able to look for him. That was the thing. For two weeks there had been nothing to look for, no future anywhere with his shape in it, only the holes: across from Charlie in the diner, beside Billy on the ramp. She went looking now the way you'd go looking for something in a house where you'd lost it, without any real hope, and he was just—*there*.

There. Clear as anybody. Clearer.

Seven-ten. The Thriftway on Forks Avenue. The automatic doors sliding open. A tall man coming through them with rain on his shoulders, a canvas jacket, dark wool cap, black hair sticking out from under it. Green eyes. He took a basket from the stack. He went down aisle four and stood in front of the shelf with the coffee filters on it for a long time, frowning, as if coffee filters were a problem he'd been set to solve. He took a pack. He went to the cooler and took bacon. Eggs. At the end of the aisle he took a newspaper from the rack and tucked it under his arm.

"He's at the Thriftway," Alice said. "Seven-ten. He's—he's buying coffee filters."

"Coffee filters," said Emmett.

"And bacon. And a newspaper."

She kept going. She went through other people's mornings to get to him. A teenage girl on register two, blond, chewing gum, name tag that said *KIMMY*: her future had him in it. Seven-fourteen, the tall man putting his basket down on her belt, saying something that made her laugh. *Say it again*, Kimmy said. *Say "tomato."* And he said it, and she laughed again, and rang him up. Nine dollars and sixty cents. He paid cash.

A man in a Mariners cap, coming into the Thriftway at seven-sixteen: his future had the tall man in it too, coming out as he went in, holding the door for him. *Cheers, mate.*

Seven-forty. Newton's Olympic Outfitters, the bell over the door. Mrs. Newton behind the counter with her reading glasses on her head. Her future: the tall man at the rack of flies by the till, turning a little plastic box over in his fingers. *Is this one any good for steelhead, Mrs. Newton?* And Mrs. Newton saying it was a pretty good one, and him buying it, and a wool hat, a gray one, and saying something about losing his last hat in a tree.

All of them. Every one of them saw him. Every one of their mornings had him in it, in exactly the same place at exactly the same time, buying exactly the same things, consistent all the way through, the way a real morning was consistent, the way the truth always was.

She opened her eyes and told them.

"Shopping," said Rosalie, when she'd finished. "He stood Charlie up to buy coffee filters."

"He didn't stand him up," said Alice. "He just—decided not to go. Sometime before six. People do. People change their minds."

"Not him," said Edward.

He'd said it very quietly. He was still crouched beside Bella with his hand on her back, and he was looking at Alice, and his face was very still, and Alice knew he'd seen it all. He'd been in her head the whole time, the way he always was, watching the visions play out as she watched them. The ramp. The boat. The aisle. Kimmy. Mrs. Newton.

"It's very tidy," Edward said.

"Futures are tidy when nobody's deciding anything hard," said Alice.

"He decides everything." Edward stood up. "He's been invisible to you for two weeks. Every minute. Wherever he's been. And on the one morning he was supposed to be sitting in a boat next to Charlie, he goes shopping, in town, in the middle of the morning, where you can see him perfectly, and every single person he talks to sees him too."

Nobody said anything.

"Why now?" said Jasper.

"I don't know," said Alice. "Maybe whatever he was using to hide wore off. Maybe he forgot it. Maybe he doesn't care anymore."

"He doesn't forget things," said Jasper.

Alice opened her mouth. She shut it again.

She felt it, then, for the first time: a small cold place under her breastbone, where a minute ago there had only been relief.

"I'm going to go and look at him," said Edward.

"Edward," said Carlisle.

"I'm not going to talk to him. I'm not going to go near him. I'll stand at the end of the aisle and look at him buying coffee filters, and then I'll come home." Edward was already reaching for his keys on the hook by the door. "If he's there, he's there, and I'll owe Alice an apology. And if he isn't—"

"He's there," said Alice. "I'm looking at him."

"Then I'll look at him too," said Edward, and went.

---

She watched him go.

She watched the Volvo come down out of the trees onto the highway and turn toward town. She watched Edward's morning, because she could see Edward perfectly, she'd been able to see Edward perfectly for fifty years. Seven-oh-five, the Thriftway lot. Seven-oh-seven, the automatic doors. Seven-oh-nine, Edward walking slowly down the end of aisle four, past the paper towels and the charcoal briquettes, and looking along the front of the store at the registers.

And there was the tall man, at register two, setting his basket down. Kimmy saying *say "tomato."* Edward standing at the end of the aisle, watching him.

"He's going to see him," Alice said aloud, to nobody. "He's right there. Edward's looking right at him."

At seven-nine, the house phone rang.

Esme picked it up and listened and held it out to Alice without a word, and Alice got up off the floor for the first time in ten hours and took it.

"Edward?"

"There's nobody at register two," Edward said.

Alice stood very still.

"He's right there," she said. "I can see him. He's putting his basket down. Kimmy's laughing. She's asking him to say *tomato*—"

"I'm standing at the end of aisle four," Edward said. His voice on the phone was very quiet and very even. "There is a girl at register two named Kimmy. She's reading a magazine. There's nobody in her line. There's nobody in the store except me and a boy stacking cans of soup in aisle seven and a woman at the pharmacy counter at the back. Nobody's bought coffee filters this morning. I can hear Kimmy thinking. She's thinking about a boy named Tyler and whether he'll call. She isn't thinking about any Englishman at all."

"That's impossible," Alice said.

She could see it. She was *looking at it*. The tall man taking his change. Kimmy laughing. It was right there in front of her eyes, in front of the eyes behind her eyes, as clear and certain as every other future she'd ever seen in eighty-six years, and every one of those had come true unless somebody decided otherwise. Nobody had decided anything. Nobody had changed anything. It was just—not happening.

"Alice," said Edward.

"I'm looking right at him, Edward. You're looking right at him. You're in it. You're standing at the end of the aisle in it."

"I know," he said. "I can see what you're seeing. I'm in your head." A pause. "I'm going to look at something."

She heard him move. She heard, faintly, the squeak of his shoes on linoleum. She saw, in the morning that wasn't happening, the tall man walk out past Edward with his groceries and a newspaper under his arm, and not look at him.

Then she heard Edward crouch down. And she heard, very faintly, through the phone, a small sound. Metal on linoleum. Something small being slid out from under something.

"There's a coin," Edward said. "Under the bottom shelf. In aisle four. Between the paper towels and the charcoal." Another pause. "It's foreign. Bronze. It's got writing on it. I don't—"

His fingers closed on it. She heard that too.

And the Thriftway went out of the vision like a blown candle.

All of it. The tall man, the basket, Kimmy laughing, the newspaper, the door held open for the man in the Mariners cap. Gone, all at once, as if someone had turned off a projector, and what was left in its place was a girl at register two reading a magazine and a boy stacking soup and Edward, crouched at the end of aisle four, holding something small in his hand.

Alice made a sound. She didn't know what kind.

"Alice?" Edward's voice. "Alice, what happened? I heard you—"

"It's gone," she said. "The Thriftway. It's gone. The second you picked that up, he just—it went out."

There was a long silence on the line.

"Then the boat," Edward said.

She didn't say anything.

"The boat's a lie too," Edward said. "Alice. It's the same thing. He's put himself here, in town, where you'd look. And he's taken himself out of the boat." His voice went very flat. "He's in the boat."

"I'm calling Jacob," said Alice.

"I'll do it." The line went dead.

She stood holding the dead receiver. Behind her she could feel them all—Jasper, Carlisle, Esme, Rosalie with the baby, Emmett, Bella still on her knees on the floor—not saying anything. She could feel Jasper trying to send something toward her, calm, steadiness, and she could feel it not reaching, the way heat doesn't reach through glass.

She looked, because she couldn't not.

The boat. Out past the river mouth. Charlie and Billy, talking about the Mariners, about Sue's fry bread, about Jacob. Two men. No hole. It looked exactly like the truth. It looked exactly like every other future she'd ever seen.

She couldn't tell. That was the cold place under her breastbone, spreading. She looked at it as hard as she'd ever looked at anything, and she couldn't tell.

The phone in her hand rang again. She jumped.

"He's in the boat," Edward said. "Jacob's on the rocks by the jetty. He's been there since five-twenty. He watched him come down the ramp at ten to six. He's been in the boat the whole time. Jacob says—" Edward stopped. "Jacob says he's wearing an orange coat. He says he looks like a traffic cone."

Alice put the phone down on its hook. She put it down very carefully, because she could feel her hand wanted to break it.

"He lied to me," she said.

"Alice," said Jasper.

"He *lied to me.*" She turned around. They were all looking at her. "He lied to *it*. To what I see. I've had this for eighty-six years. I've had it since before I can remember anything else. It's the one thing I've never—it's never been *wrong*, Jasper, it's been changed, people change their minds, but it's never been—"

She stopped.

In the vision, still playing, because she couldn't make it stop, it was seven-forty, and there was the bell over the door at Newton's, and Mrs. Newton behind the counter, and the tall man at the rack of flies, turning a little plastic box over in his fingers.

He looked up.

He looked up, away from the flies and away from Mrs. Newton, into the empty corner of the shop beside the door, up near the ceiling, where there was nothing at all. Nothing but a smoke alarm and a cobweb. He looked straight into that corner, the way you'd look into a camera.

And he lifted two fingers to his forehead, lazily, in a little salute.

Then he went back to choosing flies.

Alice sat down on the floor, right where she was, in the middle of the room.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

**La Push marina — 5:52 a.m.**

Billy Black heard the car before he saw it, and he knew what it was before it came round the last bend.

Thirty years of listening to engines come down that road. Charlie's cruiser, which had a whine in the fan belt Charlie had been meaning to fix since the Clinton administration. Harry Clearwater's old Ford, which nobody would hear again. Sam Uley's truck. The Atearas' van. Tourists in rentals, slow and nervous on the curves. This was none of those. This was a big old American eight, run rich, rumbling like a dog in its chest, and it came down the hill toward the marina at exactly the speed limit and turned into the gravel lot and stopped.

Billy watched it in the side mirror Charlie had clamped to his chair for him years ago, as a joke, and that he'd never taken off.

Black Chevelle. Nose out.

Jacob had told him about the nose out.

The driver's door opened and a man got out, and Billy, who had been bracing himself for three days to see whatever it was his son and a vampire and Sue Clearwater's girl had seen, saw a very tall young man in a bright orange float coat.

It was a terrible coat. It was the color of a road cone. It was the color of the life rings on the ferry. It glowed in the gray morning like something radioactive, and the man walked down the ramp in it toward them with his hands in its pockets and his head up, as if it were a perfectly ordinary thing to be wearing at six in the morning on the Quillayute River, and Charlie, standing thigh-deep in the river in his waders with the bow line in his fist, saw it and said, "What in the name of *God*."

"You said not the jacket," the man said.

"I didn't say dress up as a buoy!"

"You'll be glad of it if I go over the side, Chief. You'll see me from the shore."

"We'll see you from *space*."

The man grinned. He had a quick crooked grin that went all the way up into his eyes and made him look about nineteen. Then he turned and looked at Billy, and the grin went down, into some quieter room, and what was left on his face was attention.

He didn't hold out his hand. He stopped two good steps from the chair, which was the distance most people didn't know to leave and Charlie had never once left in thirty years, and he stood with his hands out of his pockets now, by his sides, where Billy could see them.

"Mr. Black," he said. "Harry Evans. Thank you for having me out."

His voice was different from the one he'd used on Charlie. Billy noticed that. It was the same voice, the same English, but there was less in it. Less play. Like a man taking off his hat when he came into a house.

Billy looked at him for a while.

He was big. The man was six-two if he was an inch, and wide, and under the ridiculous coat he stood the way Jacob stood these days, the way Sam stood, with his weight forward over his feet as if the ground were something he'd been told he could leave whenever he wanted. Black hair, messy. Green eyes, very clear. A young face. Older eyes. Clean-shaven, which Billy approved of in a young man and never got anywhere telling Jacob.

Billy held out his hand.

The man stepped forward and took it.

It was hot. Billy had been told it would be. He'd still been holding his breath for it, a little, and when it came, it went through him like a jolt from a fence. He knew that heat. He'd felt it every day since the winter, on his son's skin, on his son's forehead when he'd come home that first night shaking and feverish and frightened out of his mind. He'd felt it on Sam's hands, on Paul's, on Seth's. He'd never felt it on a stranger.

The man let go and stepped back to his two paces. His face hadn't changed.

"Billy," Billy said.

"Billy."

"Charlie says you're English."

"Guilty."

Billy took the second thermos out from beside his hip, the green one, and held it out. "Tea," he said. "Jacob said you'd want tea. I don't know if I made it right. Sarah used to make it. She'd want me to tell you there's milk in it, and if that's wrong I don't want to hear about it."

The man looked at the thermos. Then he took it, and unscrewed the cup, and poured, and smelled it, and drank. He drank it like a man who'd been in a desert.

"It's perfect," he said. He looked up. "Thank you. Tell your lad thanks too."

He didn't look toward the jetty when he said it. He didn't need to. But Billy, who'd been watching his eyes since the man got out of the car, had seen them go there once, as he walked down the ramp, to the black rocks at the end of the jetty where the river met the sea, where Jacob was sitting very still against the boulders in a dark sweatshirt with his hood up. They'd gone there, and come back, and the man hadn't said a word.

"Billy!" Charlie called. "You coming or are you two going to have a tea party?"

---

They went out on the turn of the tide.

Charlie lifted Billy into the boat the way he always did, arms under the knees and around the back, grunting, *you've put on weight, old man*, and Billy said what he always said, which was *you've gotten weak*, and the Englishman folded the chair without being asked and stowed it in the bow under the tarp where Charlie always stowed it, as if he'd seen it done. Charlie started the motor. The boat went down the river between the jetties, past the rocks where Jacob sat with his hood up and didn't look at them, and out through the river mouth onto the gray Pacific.

The swell came under them, long and slow, and the boat lifted and settled. James Island went by on the right, black and steep and crowned with stunted trees, with the mist lying in its gullies. A-Ka-Lat, the old people called it: the top of the rock. There had been a village up there once, a fortress, the dead. There were still things up there Billy didn't talk about.

The Englishman looked at it as it went by. He looked at it for a long time.

Charlie took them out a mile, to the drop-off where the bottom fell away from forty fathoms to a hundred and the bait came up the wall in the mornings. He cut the motor. The quiet came down. Gulls. The slap of water on aluminum. The far-off bark of a sea lion on the rocks.

They fished.

Billy could tell most of what he needed to know about a man in the first hour in a boat.

The Englishman could sit still. He sat so still that after a while the gulls stopped noticing him. He'd never mooched for salmon in his life; that was plain from the first cast, when he let the herring go down too fast and it spun like a top and Charlie said *no, no, no, gentle, like you're lowering a baby into a bath*, and the Englishman said *I've never lowered a baby into anything, Chief*, and Charlie said *well, pretend*. He learned fast. By the third drop he had it. By the tenth he was doing it better than Charlie, and he had the sense not to say so.

And he talked to Charlie.

Billy sat in the middle of the boat with a rod in the holder and a cup of coffee in his hands and listened to him do it. He told Charlie about falling in the Thames at fourteen chasing a hat. He asked about Charlie's knee and listened to the answer. He asked about the Mariners and pretended not to understand baseball so Charlie could explain it to him, at length, with diagrams drawn in the air with a rod tip. Charlie laughed. Charlie laughed more than Billy had heard him laugh since Harry died.

And he kept his hands where Charlie could see them. On the rod. On the gunwale. On his cup. The whole time.

Billy saw that. He'd been told about it. Seeing it was different.

With Billy, the man did none of it.

He didn't tell Billy stories. He didn't make Billy laugh, though once or twice Billy came close, against his will. When he spoke to Billy, he spoke plainly, briefly, as you'd speak to a man you'd been told to respect and had decided on your own that you did. He asked Billy about the island. About the river, and how it moved, and where the channel had been when Billy was a boy. About the fishing, the old fishing, the canoes, the whaling. He listened to the answers the way he'd drunk the tea.

Two different men, in one coat.

It was nearly eight when the Englishman said, "Mr. Black. Can I ask you something for the report?"

"What report?"

"The State wants a folklore section." He was looking at his rod tip. "In the sightings report. Local legends, oral history, that sort of thing. Gives it a bit of colour, apparently. Makes the man in the office feel he's getting his money's worth. Everyone I've asked in town says if anybody knows the old stories about wolves, it's Billy Black."

Charlie groaned. "Oh, here we go."

"Charlie."

"No, no. Go ahead. Tell him." Charlie reeled in and checked his herring. "Tell him all of it. Tell him the whole thing. He'll be sorry he asked by lunchtime."

Billy looked at the Englishman. The Englishman looked back, with polite interest, and waited.

So Billy told him.

He told it the way he'd told it to tourists, and to the anthropology students from the university who came every few summers with tape recorders, and to Bella Swan once, a long time ago, through Jacob. The short version. The one for outsiders. How the Quileute had come from wolves, long ago, and how the wolves had been the tribe's protectors. How there had been enemies in the old days, the Cold Ones, who drank blood and couldn't be killed by arrows. How the wolves had fought them. How, in his grandfather's time, a new kind of Cold One had come, who said they didn't hunt men, and his grandfather had made a treaty with them. *Stay off our land and we won't tell the pale-faces what you are.*

He told it lightly, a little smiling, an old man's story for an English stranger with a notebook.

And partway through, telling it, he felt something.

It was very small. A pressure, behind his eyes, low down, like the feeling in the sinuses before the weather changes. It came when he got to the Cold Ones and stayed while he talked about the treaty. It was painless, and it was there, the way a person standing behind you in a line is there.

The Englishman was looking at him. Looking him in the eyes, quietly, attentively, the way you'd look at a man telling you a story.

Billy kept talking. He finished the story. The pressure went away.

The Englishman nodded slowly. "Thank you," he said. "That's very good."

"It's very *long*," said Charlie, from the stern. "Every time. Twenty years I've been hearing that one." The motor gave a cough and died; it had been idling to keep them on the drop-off. Charlie swore and leaned over it. "Hang on. Fuel line again. Damn thing." He pulled the cover off and stuck his head into it and started tinkering, cursing gently and steadily, and the motor coughed and caught and roared and coughed again, and the noise of it filled the boat.

The Englishman leaned in.

Not much. A few inches. He didn't look at Charlie. He kept his eyes on his rod tip and his voice so low under the motor that Billy, sitting a yard from him, had to strain to hear it.

"And the third wife," he said.

Billy went cold all the way through.

He hadn't said a word about the third wife. Nobody did, outside. Hardly anybody outside the council and the pack had ever heard the third wife's story: how when the Cold Woman came and the wolves were dying, Taha Aki's third wife, who could not change, who had no weapon, took a knife and opened her own heart in front of the Cold Woman so that the smell of her blood would make the thing turn its head, for one second, and give her sons the second they needed. It was the oldest, saddest, truest story they had. It was the one they told at the council fire when the boys first changed. It was not a story for strangers.

"I'll not go into it with the Chief in the boat," the Englishman said, very low. "He's made his choice about all this, and it's a sound one. I respect it. So do you." He let out a little line. "I just wanted you to know that I know. So we can stop pretending, you and me. It's tiring."

Billy didn't say anything. He couldn't have.

"I know what your boy is, Mr. Black," the Englishman said. "He's on the rocks at the end of the jetty, and he's been there since twenty past five, and that's fine. That's what I'd do. I'm not here for him. I'm not here for his friends. I'm not here for the doctor and his family. I'm here for the ones with red eyes. That's all."

"And Charlie?" Billy heard himself say.

The man was quiet for a moment.

"The Chief's a friend," he said.

"Is he."

"He's going to be." He looked at Billy then, for the first time since he'd leaned in, and his eyes were very clear and very green and gave Billy nothing at all. "Nothing's going to happen to the Chief while I'm here, Mr. Black. Nothing. That one I can promise you."

Charlie got the motor going. It settled into a steady idle. Charlie straightened up, wiping his hands on a rag, and said, "There. Should hold. What'd I miss?"

"Billy was telling me about the whaling," said the Englishman, and leaned back, and was just a man in an orange coat with a fishing rod again.

Billy sat very still in the middle of the boat with his coffee going cold in his hands.

He thought about what had just been done to him. He thought about it carefully, the way he'd learned to think about things since Jacob first came home with a fever. The man had told him the truth. Every word. Billy didn't know how he knew that, but he did, the way you know a board will hold your weight before you step on it. The man had sat in Billy's friend's boat and told Billy the truth and let Charlie go on not knowing it, and he'd done it on purpose, in front of Charlie, under the noise of a motor, so that now there were two people in the boat who knew and one who didn't, and the two who knew were Billy and the stranger.

He'd made Billy his partner. In keeping Charlie safe. In keeping Charlie *out*.

And Billy couldn't say a word about it. Not to Charlie. Not ever. Because Charlie had drawn that line himself, around himself, and Billy had promised to keep it, and the man in the orange coat knew that, and had just tied himself to Billy with it like a rope.

With Charlie, he'd used stories and his hands. With Billy, he'd used the truth.

A different key for every lock.

Billy looked at the sea. The swell was coming in long and gray from somewhere a long way off, from the other side of the world, the way it always had. He'd sat in boats on this water his whole life. He'd sat at the council fire and told the third wife's story to boys who were shaking with fever, to his own son.

He'd never been so frightened in a boat in his life.

And somewhere in the back of his mind, very small and very quiet, he was aware of a thing he'd never have said aloud to anyone, not even to Sue: that he liked the man. Against all sense. Against everything his son had told him. He liked him the way you like weather that's going to be bad but honest about it.

The Englishman let out a little more line, and watched his rod tip, and said nothing.

**A mile off La Push — 8:41 a.m.**

Harry let out another foot of line and watched his rod tip nod with the swell, and sorted through what he'd taken out of Billy Black's head.

He'd gone in while the old man told the story. That was the best time, always. A man telling a story he'd told a hundred times had half his mind on the words and the other half off somewhere else, wandering the rooms behind them, and you could walk in after the wandering half and nobody would notice the door. Billy had felt something. Harry had seen him feel it, a little crease between the eyebrows, the look of a man who thinks the weather might be changing. But he'd kept talking. They always kept talking.

It had been a good house to walk through. Old, and orderly, and full. Some rooms Harry hadn't gone into at all.

He went over what he had, the way he'd write it up tonight.

The treaty, first. He had the whole of it now, the real thing and not the tourist version. Nineteen thirty-six. Ephraim Black, Billy's grandfather, and three other men on the beach at the edge of the trees, and five Cold Ones across from them, and the tall fair one doing the talking. Carlisle Cullen, looking almost exactly as he must look now. The terms were short. The Cullens kept off Quileute land. The Quileute kept the secret. And the Cullens would not *bite* a human being. The old men had argued over that word for an hour, and kept it because it covered everything, a killing and anything short of one. Bite one, and the treaty was finished, and the wolves would come. Billy had grown up on that word. It sat in the middle of his head like a stone at the bottom of a well.

Which meant the newest Cullen, the Chief's daughter, had been turned by a bite, and the treaty had very nearly ended because of it. Harry could see the edge of that in Billy's head, raw and recent: a night in September, the packs splitting, a son who'd stopped coming home. He hadn't gone further in. He didn't need to.

The phasing, second. That was the one Croaker would sit up for.

The boys changed when the Cold Ones came near. That was the whole of it. The gene had sat quiet in the tribe for two generations while the Cullens were away, and the moment the Cullens came back, the boys had started burning up with fevers. One, then three, then more. *The more of them come, the more of ours change.* Billy believed it the way he believed the tide. He'd watched it happen, one boy after another, his son among them. Seven in Sam Uley's pack, now. Three with Jacob. And Billy lay awake some nights doing the sums: what happened if more came. A lot more. All at once.

Harry turned that one over for a while. If what lived under the clock tower in Volterra ever came to this coast in numbers, they'd find more wolves waiting for them than had been here when they set out.

That was worth a great deal. He filed it carefully.

Third. The wolves didn't age while they kept changing. They could stop if they stopped. Billy's son might be eighteen for a hundred years if he wanted, and Billy, who would not be here in a hundred years, thought about it every night.

Fourth. Imprinting. Harry had heard the word in the wolves' heads in the woods and hadn't known what it meant. Billy knew. He'd known his whole life. A wolf looked at someone, once, and that was it. Gravity. Whatever she needed, he became. And there was a law, older than the treaty: no wolf might harm the one another wolf had imprinted on. Not ever. Not for any reason. It was the reason, Harry saw now, that the larger pack hadn't come across the river in September and finished what they'd started to do. Jacob had looked at the baby. That was all. And the law had come down between the packs like a wall.

The baby. Harry thought about the baby for a moment, and the Department's unfinished argument about how to count her, and then put her away.

Fifth. The council. Billy. Old Quil Ateara, past ninety, who kept the stories. And Sue Clearwater, who'd taken her husband's seat on it after his heart gave out, and whose daughter had changed for the first time that same day.

Harry had stopped there. He'd backed out of that room the way you back out of a room where someone's crying.

There'd been other rooms. A woman in a car, in the rain, and a telephone ringing in the middle of the night, and twin girls, crying, and a small boy who didn't understand. A wheelchair being delivered. The particular silence of a house after a son phases for the first time and runs out into the dark and doesn't come back until morning.

Harry hadn't gone into any of those either. He'd seen the doors and left them shut.

He'd write up the five. The rest wasn't his business, and it wasn't the Department's, and nobody would ever know he'd seen the doors but him.

---

The Chief was quiet.

He'd got the motor sorted and come back up the boat and sat down on the middle thwart with his rod in his hands, and now he was looking out at the water the way men do on boats when the talking's run out for a while. His face had gone loose. His shoulders had come down from round his ears. He was thinking about nothing much: the fish that weren't biting, the cold, a woman's kitchen with fry bread on the counter.

Harry had been waiting for this for three hours. He'd been waiting for it for a week.

He looked at the Chief. Just looked, the way you'd look at a man across a boat. And went in, lightly, the surface only.

Rain on a window.

It was the same as at the diner. He could see there was a room on the other side. Shapes moving. The suggestion of a kitchen, a woman, light. But it was all running, all smeared, all just out of reach, like looking out of a train window in a downpour. That was odd enough on its own. He'd been in Muggle heads by the hundred and they were all as easy as walking through an open door.

Croaker had said *push*.

So he pushed.

He leaned on the glass, very gently, the way you'd lean on a window to see if it was locked.

And the glass leaned back.

It flexed under him like the skin of a soap bubble. Gave, and gave, and then pushed back, with an even, mild, absolutely immovable pressure, and he felt himself sliding off it to one side, gently, the way a hand slides off a balloon. Nothing was doing it. The Chief didn't know Harry was there. It was simply what the Chief *was*; the glass did it by itself.

Harry came out.

The Chief rubbed his forehead with the back of his wrist. "Huh," he said, to nobody. "Getting a headache."

"It's the glare off the water," said Billy.

"There isn't any glare. It's cloudy."

"There's always glare," said Billy. "You just can't see it."

Harry sat very still, holding his rod, and looked at the sea, and kept his face exactly as it was.

*If it runs in the blood*, Croaker had said.

It ran in the blood. Something did. A thin, mild, accidental version of whatever the daughter had: the thing that had let her stand in the throne room in Volterra with Aro's hand on hers and give him nothing. In the Chief it was hardly anything. A window that rained. A bubble that pushed back. He had no idea he had it, and it had never once been tested by anything stronger than a Muggle trying to guess what he was thinking.

But it was there. And it had come from somewhere. And it had gone somewhere else, and got a great deal stronger on the way.

Harry thought about forty officers on aeroplanes to Tuscany in the spring, and what they'd be carrying in their heads when they went, and whose heads they'd want to be carrying.

He didn't let any of it reach his face. He let out another foot of line and watched the rod tip nod.

He'd write that one up for Croaker alone. Not in the log. In a letter, in his own hand, sealed, by owl. Some things didn't go in the log.

---

At twelve minutes past nine the Chief's rod bent double.

It went down all at once, with none of the nodding and twitching a salmon gives you, the tip slamming into the water, the whole rod bowing into a hoop so deep the butt came up out of the holder and nearly went over the side, and the reel began to scream.

"Whoa," said the Chief, grabbing it. "Whoa—whoa, *whoa*—"

Line went off the reel so fast it smoked.

Harry was on his feet before he'd decided to be.

**A mile off La Push — 9:12 a.m.**

Charlie Swan thought he'd hooked the bottom.

That was his first thought, the stupid one, the one you always have: that the boat had drifted over a rock and the herring had snagged on it and the boat's own drift was dragging the line off the reel. Except the boat wasn't moving and the line was. It was going out so fast the reel was screaming, a high thin metal whine he'd never in thirty years heard come out of that reel, and the rod was bent so far over that the tip was underwater, and the butt was trying to tear itself out of his hands.

His second thought was *sea lion*. Damn sea lion got the herring and the hook with it and now it was heading for Japan.

Then whatever was on the end of the line *turned*, and the rod snapped back for half a second, and then bent again the other way, and Charlie felt it come up through the cork grip into his palms. A shudder. A great slow muscular shake, like a dog shaking water off, except it was a hundred yards down and pulling like a truck.

That was no sea lion.

"*Whoa*," Charlie said. "Whoa, whoa, whoa—"

Evans was on his feet.

Charlie didn't see him get up. One second he'd been sitting on the forward thwart in that god-awful orange coat watching his own rod, and the next he was standing in the middle of the boat, balanced on the swell like he'd been born on it, with his eyes on Charlie's line where it cut down into the gray water at a steep angle and hissed.

"Let it run," Evans said. "Let it run, Chief, don't fight it yet. Thumb off the spool. Let it go."

"It's taking all my line!"

"How much have you got?"

"Two hundred yards. Hundred and fifty. I don't know—"

"Then it's got another hundred to go before it's got all of it. Let it run. It'll stop." Evans turned his head. "Billy. Can you steer us after it?"

"Get me to the stern," said Billy.

Evans bent and picked Billy up.

He just picked him up. Billy was not a small man, and he'd been heavier since the diabetes and the chair, and Charlie had been lifting him in and out of boats for years and knew exactly what it cost his knees. Evans put one arm under Billy's legs and one round his back and lifted him off the middle thwart and turned with him in a rocking boat and set him down on the stern seat next to the motor, with one hand on the tiller, in about the time it took Charlie to blink. Billy didn't even have time to swear at him.

"Follow the line," Evans said. "Keep it off the prop."

"I know how to follow a fish, son," said Billy, and opened the throttle.

The boat came round. Charlie hung on to the rod. Line poured off the reel. And then, as suddenly as it had started, it stopped. The rod straightened a little. The line went slack and then tight again, steady now, heavy, like he was hooked into a sunken car.

"Now," said Evans. "Now reel. Reel, Chief. Pump it up and reel down. Get line back. Every inch you can get."

Charlie reeled.

---

It took him forty minutes.

He'd tell the story so many times afterward that the guys at the station would start mouthing the words along with him. At the time it was just forty minutes of pain. The fish ran, and he let it. The fish stopped, and he pumped the rod up and reeled down, up and down, up and down, his shoulders burning, his forearms locked, his bad knee screaming where he'd braced it against the gunwale. He'd get fifty yards of line back and the fish would take forty of them straight back out again without seeming to notice him. Billy kept the bow on it, swinging the boat round, following the line as it slanted off one way and then another. The swell lifted them and dropped them. Gulls came and wheeled overhead, curious, screaming.

And through all of it, Evans stood in the middle of the boat with his feet planted and talked to him.

"That's it. That's it. Pump and reel. Don't horse it. Let it run if it wants to run. You've got the time, it hasn't. Good. Good. Up and down. You're winning, Chief, whatever your arms are telling you."

He didn't touch the rod. Charlie noticed that, later, when he could notice anything. Any other man Charlie knew would've had his hands on it by the twenty-minute mark: *here, let me spell you, give it here*. Evans didn't. He stood with his hands at his sides and talked, low and steady, like a man talking a jumper down off a ledge, and every time Charlie thought his arms were going to come off at the shoulders, Evans said something that made him want to keep going just to spite him.

"You're doing better than I would. I'd have dropped it in the first minute. I'd be in the water by now. They'd be fishing *me* out."

"Shut *up*, Evans."

"That's the spirit."

Thirty minutes. Thirty-five. The line was coming up steeper now. Straight down under the boat, nearly. The fish had stopped running and started circling, slow wide circles a long way down, and every circle Charlie got a few more turns on the reel.

"It's coming up," said Billy, from the stern. His voice had changed. "Charlie. It's coming up. Look."

Charlie looked.

Down in the gray-green water off the starboard side, a long way down, something flashed. Gold. Then gone. Then again, nearer. A long torpedo of a shape, dark on top and silver-gold below, swinging round in a slow circle under the boat, catching what light there was and throwing it back.

And yellow. Long yellow fins, swept back like the wings of a jet. A row of little yellow finlets down its back toward the tail, bright as paint.

"Oh, my God," said Billy Black.

"What? What is it?" Charlie couldn't take his eyes off it. It was coming up. It was *big*. "What is it, a king? Billy, is it a king?"

"It's a yellowfin," said Billy. He sounded like a man in church. "Charlie. That's a yellowfin tuna."

"It can't be."

"I know it can't be. I've been fishing this water my whole life. There's no yellowfin up here. They don't come north of—they *don't come up here*, Charlie, the water's too cold." Billy was half out of his seat, hanging on to the tiller. "Look at the fins. Look at it. That's a yellowfin."

"Reel," said Evans. "Talk later. Reel."

Charlie reeled.

The fish came up.

It came up out of the gray like a submarine surfacing, rolling, huge, a long fat silver-gold body as long as Charlie was tall, the yellow fins slicing the water, and broke the surface three feet from the side of the boat in an explosion of spray. Its tail hit the hull with a bang like a sledgehammer, and the whole boat lurched, and the rod nearly went out of Charlie's hands, and the fish rolled and thrashed and threw water over all three of them, and Charlie saw its eye, huge and black and furious, and he thought, very clearly, *that thing's going to sink us*.

It went down. It came up again. It hit the boat again. Something in the hull went *crack*.

"Hold it there!" Evans shouted. "Hold it, Chief, keep its head up! Billy, gaff!"

"Gaff's too small!" Billy was waving the little salmon gaff, a hook on a three-foot pole, at a fish that would have eaten it. "It'll take my arm off!"

"Then hold it there," said Evans, "and mind your ears."

Charlie didn't understand. He was hanging on to the rod with everything he had, trying to keep the fish's head up out of the water, and it was thrashing and slamming the side of the boat, and he turned his head to say *what*—

Evans had a gun in his hand.

He'd drawn it from somewhere under that orange coat in one smooth movement, and Charlie, who had been a policeman for twenty-five years and had drawn his own weapon in earnest exactly four times, would remember afterward that he hadn't seen the draw at all. There was no gun, and then there was. A big black automatic with some kind of engraving running down the slide, little marks Charlie couldn't make out, held in both hands and extended over the side of the boat, the muzzle a foot from the tuna's head.

BANG.

The sound was enormous. It went off across the water like a cannon and the gulls exploded upward screaming and Charlie's ears went white.

BANG. BANG. BANG. BANG.

Five shots. Fast, even, like somebody knocking on a door. Every one of them into the fish's head, just behind the eye, in a group Charlie could have covered with his palm. The fish went rigid. It shuddered, once, the whole length of its body, and the yellow fins went stiff and spread. Then it went still and rolled slowly onto its side in the water against the boat, gold and silver, and lay there.

Evans lowered the gun.

He looked at the fish for a second. Then he thumbed the safety on and put the gun away under his coat, and it was gone, the way it had come, and he was a man in an orange float coat standing in a fishing boat again.

The silence afterward was enormous. The gulls were still screaming somewhere up above. Charlie's ears were ringing so loud he could barely hear them.

"Jesus H. *Christ*," Charlie said.

"Sorry," said Evans. "Should've warned you sooner."

"You—you *shot* it. You shot my fish."

"Halibut men do it," said Evans. "Big ones. Bring a hundred and fifty pounds of angry fish into a boat this size and it'll break your legs and stove the hull in. Better it comes aboard quiet." He looked at the crack in the side of the hull, and then at Charlie. "It was going to sink us, Chief. Or break off. Didn't fancy either."

Billy had started laughing.

He was sitting in the stern with the tiller under one arm and the little useless salmon gaff in his other hand, and he was laughing so hard he had to put the gaff down. Big, wheezing, helpless laughter, tears running down his face, his whole body shaking with it. Charlie couldn't remember the last time he'd heard Billy laugh like that. Before Sarah. Maybe before Sarah.

"They do," Billy managed. "They do, Charlie. Halibut men. My uncle Wallace used to carry a thirty-eight in his tackle box. Shot a two-hundred-pound halibut off Cape Alava in nineteen sixty-one and put a hole in his own boat doing it." He wiped his eyes. "Son, you didn't put a hole in the boat."

"No," said Evans. "I'm good with holes."

That set Billy off again.

---

It took all three of them to get it in.

Well, it took Evans. Charlie got the little gaff into its jaw, and Billy held the boat steady, and Evans leaned over the side and got both arms around the fish behind the gills and stood up with it. He just stood up. A hundred and thirty pounds of dead wet tuna if it was an ounce, and he lifted it up over the gunwale the way Charlie would lift a sack of feed, and laid it down in the bottom of the boat between the thwarts, where it filled the whole space from the bow seat to Charlie's boots.

Charlie sat down on the thwart and looked at it.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen come out of the water. The back was a deep, shining blue-black. The belly was silver, with a gold stripe down the side that was already starting to fade. And the fins, the long swept-back fins and the little row of finlets running back toward the tail, were the bright clear yellow of a school bus.

There were five neat holes behind its eye.

Charlie started to laugh.

He didn't mean to. It came up out of him from somewhere around his boots and he couldn't stop it. He sat on the thwart in the rocking boat with his arms aching and his ears ringing and his bad knee throbbing like a drum, looking at the biggest fish he had ever caught or ever would catch, in water where it had no business being, and he laughed until he couldn't breathe.

"Thirty years," he said, when he could. "Thirty years I've been fishing this water. I've never caught anything over thirty pounds out here. Never." He looked up at Evans. "Not once. Not one damn time. And you come out with us *one time*—"

"Beginner's luck," said Evans.

"*Your* beginner's luck. On *my* line." Charlie shook his head. "You're good luck, Evans. You know that? You're a damn good-luck charm."

Evans looked down at him, and for a second something crossed his face that Charlie couldn't read. Then it was gone, and he grinned, the quick crooked grin that made him look nineteen.

"I've been called a lot of things, Chief," he said. "That's a new one."

---

They headed in.

Billy drove. Evans sat in the bow with the wind in his face. Charlie sat on the middle thwart with his feet up on the tuna like it was a footstool, and every so often he looked down at it and started laughing again.

When the laughing had worn off enough to think, he thought about the gun.

He'd known it was there. He'd known since the diner, a week ago, when Evans had shrugged into his overcoat and Charlie had seen the shape of it under the jacket. He'd thought about it most days since. Now he'd seen it.

"That a Hi-Power?" he said.

Evans didn't turn round. "It is."

"Browning."

"That's the one."

"Nice gun."

"Thanks."

"On a fishing trip."

"I've never once needed it on a day I decided not to bring it," said Evans, "and I'd rather not find out what that's like."

It was the kind of thing old cops said. It was the kind of thing *Charlie* said, to rookies who asked why he wore his sidearm to the Fourth of July picnic.

"You got a permit for it?"

"Concealed carry. State of Washington. It's in the glovebox of the car. You're welcome to look."

"In this state they make you a resident for that."

"Then I suppose I must be one," said Evans.

Charlie opened his mouth to say something else, and then didn't.

He'd seen how Evans shot: five rounds in a group the size of his palm, from a moving boat, in about a second and a half. He'd seen state troopers on the range who couldn't do that standing still. He'd seen the man lift Billy Black like a child and lift a hundred and thirty pounds of tuna like a sack of feed. He'd seen him stand in a rocking boat for forty minutes without putting a hand out to steady himself once.

And he'd decided, a long time ago, in a lot of different ways, about a lot of different things, that there were questions he didn't need answered.

*Need to know.*

He looked at the back of Evans's head, at the black hair blowing in the wind, at the ridiculous orange coat.

Harry Clearwater would have loved this. The thought came out of nowhere and sat down beside him in the boat. Harry would have been laughing so hard he fell overboard. Harry would have made Evans tell the story of the shooting four times before they reached the dock. Harry would've said *Charlie, you old fool, you never caught a thing in your life till this English kid came along*, and he'd have been right.

Charlie put his hand on the tuna's cold flank and left it there.

"Good luck," he said, to nobody, under the motor.

He didn't believe in luck. He never had. He was a cop; he believed in evidence. But there were five bullet holes and a hundred and thirty pounds of evidence lying at his feet, in water it had no business being in, on the one morning in thirty years a stranger had come out fishing with him.

He'd have to think about luck.

**The jetty, La Push — 9:52 a.m.**

The first shot came across the water like a door slamming in an empty house.

Jacob was on his feet before the second one.

He'd been sitting on the rocks at the end of the jetty for four and a half hours with his hood up and his back against a boulder and Edward's stupid little phone in the pocket of his sweatshirt, watching a gray boat rock on a gray sea a mile out, and he'd been cold, which he never was anymore, and bored, which he was all the time, and so angry about the phone call at seven-twelve that his hands had been shaking for an hour afterward. *Is he in the boat. Jacob. Is he in the boat.* Edward's voice, very flat. *Yeah, he's in the boat, he's been in the boat since six, he's wearing an orange coat, he looks like a traffic cone, why?* And then Edward telling him, and Jacob sitting there on the rocks listening to a vampire tell him that the man in the orange coat had made Alice see him buying coffee filters in town while he was sitting a yard from Jacob's dad.

And then nothing. Four hours of nothing. The boat rocking. Three dark shapes in it. The swell.

Now: BANG. BANG. BANG. BANG. BANG.

Five. Fast and even and flat, rolling in across the water and bouncing off the cliffs of James Island behind him and coming back again, so that for a second it sounded like twenty. The gulls went up off the island in a white cloud.

Jacob was at the edge of the rocks with his sweatshirt half over his head before he knew he'd moved. He was going in. He was going to phase on the rocks and go into the sea and swim a mile in October water and he didn't care what saw him do it. His dad was in that boat. His dad was in that boat with a man who carried a gun and hung vampires upside down from trees and lied to the future, and somebody had just fired five shots—

He made himself stop. He made himself *look*.

He was human, but his eyes weren't. They hadn't been since the winter. He stood on the wet black rock at the edge of the sea with his sweatshirt round his neck and his heart going like a drum and looked across a mile of gray water at the boat, and he could see it, small but clear, rocking on the swell.

Three men. All three of them standing. Or two standing and one sitting, in the stern. That was Dad. That was the shape of him, the set of his shoulders, his old canvas hat. He was sitting at the tiller. He was—

His shoulders were shaking.

Jacob's heart stopped.

Then the wind shifted, coming off the sea toward the land, and carried a sound with it, very faint, broken up by the swell, the kind of sound only a wolf would have heard across a mile of water. And Jacob stood there with his mouth open and listened to it.

His father was laughing.

He was laughing the way Jacob hadn't heard him laugh in years. Since before Mom. Big, helpless, wheezing laughter, coming across the water in pieces. And under it, Charlie, shouting something, and laughing too.

And the man in the orange coat was leaning over the side of the boat with both arms down in the water, and as Jacob watched, he straightened up, and he was holding something. Something huge. Long and silver and gold and glinting, as big as a man, and he lifted it up over the side of the boat like it weighed nothing and set it down out of sight in the bottom, and the boat rocked with the weight of it, and Charlie's whoop came across the water as clear as a bell.

Jacob sat down on the rock.

He sat there with his sweatshirt round his neck and his heart slowing down and stared at the boat, and after a while he put his face in his hands. He didn't know if he wanted to laugh or throw up. He felt both, a little.

A fish. They'd caught a fish. A huge fish. And the Englishman had shot it.

He shot the fish.

"I hate him," Jacob said into his hands, to nobody. "I hate him so much."

---

The boat came in at twenty to eleven.

Jacob had moved by then. He'd gone back along the jetty and up into the trees above the boat ramp, where the spruce came down nearly to the gravel lot, and he stood there among the trunks with his hood up and watched. The Englishman would see him. Fine. Edward said he'd see him. He'd seen him at six. Jacob had watched him walk down the ramp in that coat and look straight at the end of the jetty for one second, and look away, and he'd known he'd been seen, and he'd stayed where he was. He stayed where he was now.

Word had got round, the way it did at La Push, and by the time Charlie brought the boat up to the ramp there were nine or ten people standing around on the concrete. The harbormaster. A couple of kids. Embry's mom.

Charlie jumped out into the shallows in his waders like a man of twenty and hauled the bow up onto the ramp, and the Englishman got out the other side, and between them they lifted the fish out of the boat and laid it on the concrete.

Somebody said *holy shit*. Somebody else said it louder.

It was a tuna. Jacob knew that much. It was a tuna as long as Charlie was tall, fat and silver and blue-black on top, with yellow fins like it had been dipped in paint, and five neat holes in its head behind the eye. The harbormaster crouched down next to it and touched one of the yellow fins and looked up at Charlie and said something Jacob didn't catch, and Charlie said *I know. I KNOW*, and grinned so wide Jacob could see his back teeth from the trees.

Somebody had a camera. Charlie knelt beside the fish with one hand on it for the photo and his other arm out, waving, *come on, get in here*, at the Englishman.

The Englishman shook his head. He held up both hands. He stepped back out of the frame.

*Of course he doesn't want his picture taken*, Jacob thought.

Then the Englishman turned round, went back to the boat, and lifted Jacob's father out of it.

Charlie usually did that. The Englishman did it like something he'd been doing his whole life, one arm under Billy's knees and one round his back, and Billy said something Jacob couldn't hear and the Englishman said something back, and Billy laughed again, short. The chair was already unfolded on the ramp. The Englishman set Billy down in it, gently, and straightened him in the seat, and for a moment, as he stood up, he left his hand flat on Billy's back between his shoulder blades. One second. Two. The way you'd steady a man in a chair. The way you'd clap a friend on the back.

Then he took it away, and looked up.

He looked straight at the trees. Straight at Jacob. Across thirty yards of gravel and through the spruce trunks and the shadow under them, straight into Jacob's eyes, as if Jacob were standing in the open with a sign over his head.

He lifted two fingers to his forehead. A lazy little salute.

Then he turned away and said something to Charlie, and Charlie pointed at the fish and said something back, insisting, and after a moment the Englishman shrugged, and Charlie got a knife out of the boat and cut a steak off the tuna's flank, a slab of dark red meat the size of a paperback, and wrapped it in newspaper and handed it to him. The Englishman took it. He shook Charlie's hand. He touched Billy's shoulder again, lightly, in passing, and said something, and Billy nodded.

He walked up the ramp in the orange coat with the tuna steak under his arm, and got into the Chevelle, and drove away up the hill.

Jacob waited until the engine had faded. Then he came out of the trees.

He smelled it before he got within ten feet of the chair.

He stopped dead on the gravel.

It hit him the way the leech-smell had hit him the first time, the very first night, in the woods behind the house: a thing his body knew before his head did. Every hair on his arms stood up. His spine went hot. His hands started to shake, the deep fine shake that came up from somewhere under his ribs when the wolf wanted out, and he had to stand there and breathe through his mouth and hold it down with everything he had, because there were ten people and a harbormaster and Charlie Swan on that ramp.

Wolf.

On his father.

Not one of theirs. He knew every wolf on this coast by smell, both packs, every one, the way he knew the voices of his own sisters. This one was a stranger. It was big. It was so big. It was a smell like the inside of a cave, like old forest after rain, like stone that had never seen the sun, and under it, faint, pine and wet granite and coffee and gun oil—the Englishman's smell, the one Leah and Seth had carried back from the woods. But the wolf was on top. Thick. Heavy. Fresh. It was soaked into the back of his father's jacket in a patch the size of a man's hand, and on his left shoulder where the man had touched him in passing, so strong that for a second Jacob couldn't smell anything else.

It said *mine*. That was what it said, to the part of Jacob that was a wolf. It said *I was here, and I touched this, and you didn't stop me*.

"Jacob."

His father was looking at him.

Billy hadn't moved. He was sitting in the chair on the ramp with his hands on the wheels and his face very calm, and he was looking at his son the way he'd looked at him the first night, when Jacob had come home shaking and burning up and not knowing what was happening to him.

"Not here," Billy said quietly. "Take me home."

---

Jacob didn't remember pushing the chair up the road. He remembered the house: the little red house where he'd grown up, the porch, the ramp Charlie had built for the chair. He got his father inside and into the kitchen and then he went straight back out the door and round the side of the house to the back, into the edge of the trees, and he tore the sweatshirt off and kicked off his cutoffs and let it come.

He phased so hard it hurt.

And they were there. Leah and Seth, both phased, two miles north on the snare line where he'd sent them, and the moment he came in they felt it all at once: his father's back, the patch, the smell, the *size* of it, the old stone dark cave smell of it, coming through Jacob's memory like a punch.

Seth said *what*.

Leah said nothing at all for one long second.

Then she said, *That's him.*

*What?*

*That's him. That's what was under the pie.* Her mind was very cold and very clear. *In the woods on Tuesday. In the diner on Wednesday. Under the coffee and the gun oil. Seth smelled it too. Seth. Tell him.*

*Something big,* Seth said, faintly. *Yeah. That's—yeah. That's it. But it was so faint, Jake, it was hardly anything, it wasn't—*

*It's not faint now,* said Leah.

*He put it there,* Jacob said. *On my dad. On purpose. He put his hand on his back and he looked right at me and he—*

*Saluted you,* said Leah. *Yeah. I'm seeing it.* A pause. *Jake. There's something else.*

He felt it before she said it. A shift in the air behind the house. A different smell coming up through the trees from the south, from the direction of the road. Wolves. Familiar. Not his.

*Sam,* said Seth.

---

Sam Uley came round the side of the house on two legs, in cutoffs, barefoot, at eleven fifty-two.

Behind him, in the trees, Jacob could see two big shapes, dark against the darker trunks: Paul, gray-silver, and Jared, brown. They stayed in the trees. They didn't come any closer. Their hackles were up.

Jacob phased back. He didn't bother with the cutoffs for a second; he just stood there in the wet ferns behind his father's house, and Sam stood there, and they looked at each other.

It was the first time they'd been within fifty feet of each other since September.

"There's a wolf," Sam said, "at the marina."

"I know."

"Paul smelled it on the ramp. On the gravel. On a fishing boat. It's not one of mine." Sam's voice was very even. It always was. It was the voice that had told Jacob what he was, the first night, in these same woods. "And it's not one of yours."

"No."

"It's on Billy."

"I know, Sam."

Sam looked at the house. At the kitchen window, where Billy's shape sat at the table.

"Who?" he said.

Jacob pulled his cutoffs on. Then he told him all of it, as fast as he could, standing in the ferns, from the snare on Tuesday to the hand on his father's back. Sam listened without interrupting, the way he always had, his arms folded and his face like a stone.

When Jacob was done, Sam was quiet for a long time.

"He's not one of us," Sam said finally.

"No."

"He's not one of them."

"No."

"Then what is he?"

"Leah asked him," said Jacob. "In the diner. She said *you're human*. He said, *that's classified*."

Something moved at the corner of Sam's mouth: the ghost of a smile from a long time ago, when he'd been the oldest of them and the only one and he'd still known how.

"Let's talk to your father," said Sam.

---

They sat at Billy's kitchen table, the two of them, the way they hadn't sat since before everything: Jacob on one side and Sam on the other and Billy at the end in his chair. Billy had taken the jacket off and hung it on the back of the door, and the smell of it filled the little kitchen anyway. Out in the trees Paul and Jared paced. Somewhere to the north, Leah and Seth were running hard toward La Push; Jacob could feel them coming, faintly, even on two legs, the way you feel a storm coming in your knees.

Billy told them about the boat: the pressure behind his eyes during the story, like the weather changing, and the motor dying, and Charlie bending over it, and the man leaning in.

"*And the third wife*," Billy said.

Sam's head came up.

"That's what he said. Just that. Like he was finishing my sentence." Billy looked at his hands. "Then he said he'd not go into it with Charlie in the boat. He said he knew what you were, son. He said you'd been on the rocks since twenty past five. He said he wasn't here for you, or for the Cullens. He said he was here for the ones with red eyes."

"Nobody outside the council knows about the third wife," said Sam slowly. "Nobody outside the packs."

"I know," said Billy.

"Did you tell him?"

"No."

"Then how—"

"I don't know, Sam." Billy looked up. "I felt something. When I was telling the story. In my head. I thought it was a headache coming on. I don't think it was."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"He was in your head," Jacob said. "Like Leah said. Like with her and Seth in the woods. He was in your *head*, Dad."

"I think so."

"What did he—what's he *got*, then? What did he take?"

"I don't know," said Billy. "Whatever he wanted, I expect." He was quiet a moment. "He said one more thing. When he put me in my chair. He leaned down and said it, just to me, so Charlie wouldn't hear." He looked at Jacob. "He said, *Tell your lad I'm sorry about the jacket.*"

Jacob stared at him.

"I didn't understand it," said Billy. "Then you came across the gravel with your hackles up, son, and I did."

The back door banged open.

Leah came in barefoot in a T-shirt and cutoffs with her hair wet and wild and her chest heaving, and Seth right behind her. She stopped in the doorway. She didn't say hello. She didn't look at Sam. She walked straight to the back of the kitchen door, where Billy's jacket hung, and put her face close to the patch between the shoulder blades, and breathed in.

She stood there for a long moment.

Then she straightened up.

"Yeah," she said. "That's him."

"Why?" said Jacob. "Why would he do that? Why put it on Dad? Why tell us he's—whatever he is? He doesn't tell anybody *anything*. He sat across from you for an hour and wouldn't even tell you his real name. Why *this*?"

Leah looked at the jacket.

"Because he wants to see what we'll do," she said. "He put it on Billy. On the elder. On your dad, Jake. On the one person in La Push both packs would come running for." She turned round and looked at Sam, for the first time, and at Jacob, and at the two of them sitting at the same table. "And look. Here we are. Both packs. Same kitchen. Twenty minutes." Her mouth twisted. "He's counting. Somewhere. He's out there right now, timing us."

Nobody said anything.

"That's one reason," Leah said.

"What's the other one?" said Seth.

Leah looked at the jacket on the back of the door for a long moment, at the patch of dark between the shoulder blades that none of them could stop smelling.

"Because he's a dick," she said.

Billy Black started to laugh again. He couldn't help it. He put his hand over his eyes and his shoulders shook, the same helpless way they'd shaken in the boat, and after a moment, slowly, very much against his will, so did Sam Uley's.

**Field station, Olympic National Forest — 7:40 p.m.**

The tuna steak took four minutes a side in a dry cast-iron pan as hot as he could get it, salt and pepper and nothing else, and it was the best thing Harry had eaten since he'd crossed the Atlantic.

He ate it at the kitchen table with a Rainier, standing the open notebook against the brown sauce bottle, and the quill hung at his shoulder waiting.

"Unspeakable Zero-Seven-Two-One," he said, with his mouth full. "Field notes. Saturday, seventh of October. Nineteen forty local. Not a research log. Nobody died."

The quill wrote it. It did not, he was pleased to see, add anything.

"One. The Fata Morgana."

He'd stopped at the Thriftway on the way home, a little after one, for milk he didn't need. Kimmy had been on register two, chewing gum and reading a magazine. She'd brightened when she saw him. *Oh my God, this guy came in looking for you this morning. Super pale, but really cute? Silver Volvo. He asked if an English guy had been in, and I said not today, and he went all weird and crouched down in aisle four and looked under the shelf? And then he just left.* She'd lowered her voice. *Is he your boyfriend or something?*

Harry had said he wasn't, and bought the milk, and tipped her five dollars on a two-dollar carton, which she'd remember.

"Seer took the bait," he said. "Fully. Mirage held from five-fifty to approximately seven-ten, at which point a member of the family came to the Thriftway to verify by eye. That's the result I wanted: if she's fooled, nobody comes to the dock; somebody goes to the shops. Somebody went to the shops." He took another bite. "Verification defeated it. He found the Thriftway anchor and lifted it; the girl on the till saw him crouch at aisle four at about seven-eleven. Assume that collapsed the Thriftway leg. Newton's leg presumably stood, but by then the game was up." He chewed. "Conclusions: the Fata Morgana works on a live Seer of this species. Duration a bit over an hour against an intelligent target with transport. Weak point's the anchors. Next version, they want burying deeper, or there want to be more of them, or they want to be somewhere a pale young man can't get to with his car keys. Note: the Alpha of the smaller pack was on the rocks at the river mouth from five-twenty, before I cast. Planned. Not a reaction. Discount him."

He wiped his fingers on a tea towel.

"And note that I said hello to the Seer," he added. "In the Newton's leg. Seven-forty. In case anybody in London asks why the Cullens are cross."

"Two. Black, William. Elder."

He gave it the five things. The treaty, and the word *bite*. The phasing, and what brought it on, and what more of the Cold Ones would bring on. The aging. The imprint law, and why the bigger pack had stopped at the river in September. The council, three names.

"Recommend all five go to the Volterra planning cell," he said. "The second one especially. Tell Croaker to read it twice. If the people under the clock tower ever come to this coast in strength, they'll find more wolves waiting than when they set out. I'd want to know that, if I were planning a war." He paused. "There's more in there. Personal. Not recorded."

He didn't say what. The quill didn't ask.

"Three. The test."

He'd left the Chevelle in a logging pull-out a mile up the hill from the marina at eleven-seventeen and gone the rest of the way the quick way, with a Disillusionment on him and a Scent-Dampener over that, and come out on the bluff above the Black house in the wet salal under a Sitka spruce, where the wind came up off the sea into his face and carried nothing of him down the hill. He'd lain there on his stomach in the orange coat, which under the charm was nobody's business, and watched.

"Mark applied eleven-oh-six, marina, to the subject's back and left shoulder. Duration of contact, two seconds and one. Strength: light." He thought about how to put the next part, and put it plainly. "Using the other shape. Hand only. No visible change."

The quill wrote it in the neat impersonal hand and didn't blink.

"Responses. The Alpha of the smaller pack was within thirty yards at the time of marking, in the trees. Detected the mark at approximately ten feet. Very nearly shifted on the ramp in front of ten civilians and the Chief of Police; held it. Credit to him. Removed the subject to the house. Shifted behind the house at eleven thirty-one. The other two of the smaller pack, the grey and the sandy, came south from the snare line at a dead run from eleven thirty-one; on scene at twelve-oh-four. The Alpha of the larger pack arrived on two legs at eleven fifty-two with two of his in wolf form in support. Larger pack detected the mark independently, from the trace on the ramp and the boat, before they'd been told anything. Twenty-one minutes from the first Alpha shifting to the second one knocking on his door."

He drank some beer.

"No hostility between the packs. None. They'd not been within fifty feet of each other in weeks, as far as I can tell, and they sat down at the same table inside the hour because somebody had touched their elder. Cohesion under outside threat: very high. Split's real, but it's shallow; lean on the tribe from outside and it closes like a fist." He paused. "Nobody came looking for me. They thought about it; I watched the big grey one in the trees look up the hill twice. They didn't come. They sat down and talked first. That's discipline. Or it's the old man. Probably the old man."

He'd watched them through the kitchen window, small and far off, for twenty minutes after that. He'd watched the grey one come in last, barefoot, and go straight to the jacket on the back of the door and put her face to it. He'd watched her straighten up and say something. He'd watched the old man put his hand over his eyes, and his shoulders start to shake, and then, after a moment, the big quiet one's.

He hadn't been able to hear what she'd said. He hadn't gone in to find out. He had a fairly good idea.

"Conclusion," he said. "Both packs respond fast, together, and with restraint, to an unknown wolf on their land. They'll be a good deal more careful round me from now on, which is fine. They'll also be talking to each other again, which I'd call a side benefit." He looked at the quill. "Don't write that I meant to do that. I didn't. It's still a side benefit."

The quill hesitated, then wrote *Side benefit*, and stopped.

"Four," Harry said, and then didn't say anything for a moment.

He thought about a window with rain on it, and a soap bubble pushing back, and the Chief rubbing his forehead in a boat and saying *getting a headache*.

"Four's not for the log," he said. "Four's a letter. Strike four."

The quill struck it.

"Five. Requirements. Specimen North American zero-two remains outstanding. Lead's in Seattle from about the first of November. I'll go up for her." He finished the beer. "And I owe the doctor a proper conversation. He's been patient. He'll have heard about the coffee filters by now and he'll be less patient. I'll ring him next week." He thought about it. "Maybe."

He pushed the plate away.

"End of notes," he said.

The quill put the full stop on it and turned toward him, the way it did, waiting to see if there was anything else.

There was one thing. He looked at it for a while.

"Off the record," he said. The quill lifted itself off the page, obediently. "I could've done the test with a bit of hair on a fence post. Same data. Less fuss." He picked up his plate and took it to the sink. "I did it on the old man's back because it'd send the big lad absolutely spare."

He ran the tap.

"And because I'm a dick," he said to the window. "I've made my peace with it."

The quill, which was off the record, said nothing at all. Outside, the rain came down on the canvas he couldn't hear, and somewhere fifteen miles west of him, in a little red house by the sea, a jacket hung on the back of a kitchen door that nobody in La Push would be able to stop smelling for a week.

He thought the cherry pie at the diner on Monday would taste particularly good.
