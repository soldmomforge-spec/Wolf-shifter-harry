# Specimen

## Chapter One: Rabbit Snare

---

**Forks, Washington — Tuesday, October 3, 2006 — 10:06 a.m.**

Charlie Swan parked the cruiser where he could see the whole lot through the diner's front window. That part was habit. Then he sat behind the wheel with the engine ticking and the wipers stopped halfway up the glass, and he didn't get out. That part was newer.

The rain was the October kind, fine and patient, the kind that hung in the air and waited for you to walk into it. It beaded on the windshield and ran in crooked lines down to the wipers. Across the lot, in the back corner beside the dumpster, sat the black Chevelle.

Nose out. Always nose out, so the driver could pull straight onto 101 without reversing. Charlie had noticed that the second day. By the fifth he'd noticed it was never in the same space twice.

He'd run the plate on day three. Washington tags, current. Registered to Grimmauld Holdings LLC, a post office box in Wilmington, Delaware. No wants, no warrants, not so much as a parking ticket. A clean car belonging to a company that, as far as the State of Washington was concerned, belonged to nobody.

Charlie had been a cop for a long time. He knew what that kind of clean usually meant, and none of the meanings were good.

He sat there another few seconds, looking at the Chevelle and thinking about Sunday.

Sunday he'd sat on a white couch in the Cullens' big glass house and watched his daughter cross the room. She'd moved like a dancer. Bella, who'd broken her wrist on a perfectly flat stretch of sidewalk at fourteen. She'd sat down next to him and smiled, and her eyes were brown, but a shade off. Too even. Like brown paint on something that wasn't brown underneath.

And the baby. Edward's niece, they said. Orphaned, adopted, a sad story. Charlie had said sure. The kid had Bella's eyes, exactly Bella's eyes, and she'd looked at him like she knew who he was. Three weeks old, if you believed the story, and she was sitting up on her own and paying attention to the conversation.

Charlie had decided, again, that he didn't need to know.

That had been the deal since the day Jacob Black showed him what he was. Need to know. Charlie had looked at what he'd been shown, and he'd looked at his daughter alive when she'd been close to dead, and he'd made a choice. He'd keep making it, as many times a day as it took.

Which was why a tall Englishman asking questions about wolves sat in his stomach like a bad oyster.

He got out of the cruiser. The rain found the back of his neck right away.

---

The bell over the diner door rang. Coffee and fry grease and something sweet baking in the back. Two loggers at the counter, a retired couple splitting a short stack, the waitress refilling ketchup bottles.

The Englishman was in the back booth. The one facing the door.

He'd taken that booth every morning for eleven days, and the one time somebody else had been sitting in it, he'd taken the stool at the end of the counter where he could see the door in the mirror over the pie case. Charlie had noticed that too.

Today he was wearing a suit. Charcoal, plain, well cut, white shirt open at the throat, no tie. A long dark overcoat hung on the hook beside the booth, dripping onto the linoleum. It was the third time this week Charlie had seen him in a suit, and the other mornings it had been canvas work pants and a flannel shirt and boots that had been somewhere muddy. He was eating cherry pie with a scoop of vanilla ice cream melting off the side, and a mug of black coffee sat at his elbow on top of a folded copy of the *Forks Forum*. It was turned to the police blotter.

He looked up when the bell rang. He'd looked up every time the bell rang, Charlie would have bet money on it.

"Chief." The Englishman lifted his fork in salute. "Come sit. You're making the place look untidy."

Charlie considered the counter. He considered the loggers, who had stopped talking. He walked back and slid into the booth across from the man, and the vinyl sighed under him.

"Morning, Mr. Evans."

"Harry," the man said, the way he'd said it the first time, and the second, and every time since.

Charlie hadn't been able to make himself use it. Harry Clearwater had been in the ground seven months. Harry Clearwater who'd sat in the boat with him every other Saturday for twenty years and talked about nothing, and whose heart had quit on him last spring without any warning at all. Sue still set out an extra plate some nights and had to put it back.

It was a common enough name. That didn't make it easier to say.

"Evans," Charlie said.

The Englishman studied him for a second over the rim of his mug. Then he nodded, like he'd filed something, and went back to his pie.

He was a big man, that was the first thing anybody noticed. Six-one, six-two, wide through the shoulders in a way the suit didn't hide, with heavy wrists and big square hands. Black hair that looked like he'd cut it himself with a knife and given up halfway. Green eyes, a hard, bright green. Mid-twenties, Charlie would have said, if it weren't for something around the eyes that said older. He had a stillness about him when he wasn't doing anything. When he was doing something, eating pie, turning a page, he did it like he was getting the most out of it he could.

The waitress came by with a mug and the pot. "Coffee, Chief?"

"Thanks."

She poured. She looked at the Englishman's plate. "You want another slice, hon?"

"Would it be greedy?"

"It'd be your third."

"Then it'd be greedy." He grinned at her. "Go on, then."

She went off looking pleased with herself. Charlie wrapped both hands around his mug.

"Third slice," he said.

"Don't tell my doctor."

"You have a doctor?"

"I have a woman in London who threatens me with a clipboard once a year." Evans scraped the last of the ice cream off the plate. "She'd have a lot to say about your cherry pie. None of it complimentary. None of it would stop me."

He talked like that, quick and light, and if you didn't listen closely you'd miss that he hadn't told you anything. A woman in London. A clipboard.

"How's the wolf business?" Charlie said.

"Slow." Evans pushed the plate aside and turned the mug a quarter-turn on the newspaper. "Lot of reading. Lot of driving up and down logging roads looking at mud. Your Department of Fish and Wildlife has got very thorough files and not much in them."

"That so."

"Seventeen reported sightings of an unusually large canid on the peninsula between January of last year and last month." He said it like he was reading off a card, except there was no card. "Two of them from hunters, one from a lorry driver—truck driver, sorry—on 101 near Sappho who swears blind he nearly hit something the size of a Shetland pony at four in the morning. The rest from hikers. And then there's the hunting parties."

Charlie didn't say anything.

"You ran those, didn't you? After the hikers went missing." Evans's voice stayed exactly as level as it had been. "Last year, and the year before, after your friend Mr. Forge."

Waylon. Charlie felt it land under his ribs. Waylon Forge, and the hikers after him, and Charlie out in the woods with a rifle and a dozen men from town, hunting animals the size of horses. It all seemed to have happened to somebody else now. Somebody who didn't know what Charlie knew.

"Sorry," Evans said. "That was clumsy of me. He was a mate of yours."

"Yeah," Charlie said. "He was."

"I am sorry."

Charlie looked at him. The Englishman looked back, and it seemed to Charlie he meant it, as far as it went. That was the hard part about him. You couldn't get a grip on where the act stopped.

"We didn't find anything," Charlie said. "Hunting parties. We found a lot of nothing."

"Funny, that." Evans turned the mug another quarter. "All those men with rifles, all those dogs, and not a single track bigger than a Labrador's."

"It's a big forest."

"It is." Evans smiled with half his mouth. "It really is."

The third slice arrived with a second scoop of ice cream that hadn't been on the menu since 1987. Evans thanked the waitress by name—*cheers, Dot*—and she went away pink. Eleven days. He knew Dot's name and her kid's name. He knew Karen Newton's first name, because Charlie had heard him use it in the outfitter's on the fourth day, buying wool socks and a box of shotgun shells and asking whether the Calawah ran high this time of year. He knew everybody's business without seeming to ask about any of it, and he paid cash, every time, and tipped like a man trying to get rid of it.

"You ran my plates," Evans said, through a mouthful of pie.

Charlie took a sip of coffee. "Small town."

"I'm touched, honestly."

"Your car's registered to a company in Delaware."

"Company car." Evans swallowed. "One of the perks."

"Who's they?"

"The people who pay me to look at mud."

"Fish and Wildlife doesn't hire Brits on Delaware shell companies."

"No," Evans agreed cheerfully. "I'd imagine they'd be very upset to hear they had."

Charlie waited. It was an old trick: leave a hole in the conversation and most people fill it with something they hadn't meant to say. Evans let the hole sit there and ate his pie. When the door opened he looked at it, a quick sweep and file, and went back to his pie. Charlie had seen that look on old cops.

"How's your girl?" Evans said.

Charlie put his mug down. "What?"

"Your daughter. Bella, isn't it? Heard she was poorly over the summer. Some sort of bug she picked up abroad, they said. Atlanta, the Centers for Disease Control and so on." He was looking at Charlie with what seemed like plain interest. "Doing better, I hope."

"Who told you that?"

"Small town." Evans held up both hands, fork in one of them. "I don't mean to pry, Chief. Somebody mentioned it at the Thriftway, I think. Whole town seems very fond of her."

"She's fine," Charlie said. "She's recovering."

"Good." Evans nodded. "Glad to hear it."

And he went back to his pie, and that was all. He'd asked it the way you'd ask after the weather. Charlie sat there with his heart going harder than it had any business going, thinking about a white couch and brown eyes that were a shade off, and he couldn't for the life of him tell whether the man across from him had asked to be polite, or asked to watch what Charlie's face did.

He was pretty sure he knew which one he'd have done, in the man's place.

"Where you staying?" Charlie said. "I asked at the motel. And the place out on the 110."

"Camping."

"In October."

"I've got a very good tent."

"Where?"

"Out past the state park." Evans waved his fork vaguely east. "Forest Service land, all properly permitted. I'll show you the paperwork if you like."

"Maybe I'll come by."

"You'd be very welcome." The half-smile again. "Bring a coat. It's damp."

Charlie had opened his mouth to say something else—he wasn't sure what, something about how folks in Forks didn't like strangers asking about their kids—when the Englishman's left hand dropped off the table and into his trouser pocket and stayed there.

Charlie only noticed because he'd been watching the hands. You always watched the hands.

Nothing else moved. Evans's face didn't do anything at all. His fork stayed where it was, a bite of pie on the end of it, ice cream sliding off one side. His eyes went past Charlie's shoulder to the front window, to the gray rain over the lot and the dark line of trees beyond the highway, north and a little east, and stayed there for maybe a second and a half.

Then he put the fork down on the plate, neatly, and took his hand out of his pocket, and reached into his jacket for his wallet.

"Something wrong?" Charlie said.

"Work." Evans thumbed two twenties out of the wallet and tucked them under the edge of the plate. Pie and coffee came to maybe nine dollars, with the third slice. "Sorry, Chief. Duty calls, and all that."

"What kind of duty calls at ten in the morning for a man who looks at mud?"

"Urgent mud." Evans stood up. He took the overcoat off the hook and shrugged into it in one movement, and as he did it Charlie caught something on the left side, under the jacket, high on his ribs. A shape. Could have been a phone. Could have been a lot of things.

He was a cop. He knew what it looked like.

Evans looked down at him, buttoning the coat. "Same time tomorrow? You can buy."

"Like hell."

The Englishman grinned. It took ten years off him. "Worth a try."

The waitress called from behind the counter. "Same time tomorrow, hon?"

"Save me a slice of the cherry, Dot."

"I'll fight people off with a stick."

"That's my girl."

And he went out. The bell rang. The door swung shut on a gust of wet air.

Charlie twisted on the bench to watch him through the window. Evans crossed the lot without hurrying, collar up, hands in his coat pockets, and he didn't look at the rain or the puddles or anything except, once, quickly, the road in both directions. He got into the Chevelle. The engine caught with a deep, rough, eight-cylinder bark that Charlie felt through the glass, and under the engine, for a second before the door shut, a blast of guitar and a high wailing voice. Led Zeppelin. Charlie had owned the record in high school, and his father had made him turn it down.

The Chevelle rolled to the edge of the lot and turned right onto 101. North. Toward the Calawah bridge, toward the long stretch of logging land and second-growth timber that ran up into the foothills.

The opposite direction from the state park and his very good tent.

Charlie watched until the taillights went around the bend. Then he turned back to the forty dollars under the plate and the half-eaten third slice of pie, and he laid the eleven days out in his head end to end. The questions about the wolves. The way the man knew everybody's name. The way he'd asked about Bella.

"Everything okay, Chief?" Dot said.

"Fine." Charlie stood. He left a five on the table for his own coffee, which was more than the coffee cost, and he didn't like that he'd done it, because he knew who he'd done it after. "Thanks, Dot."

Outside, the rain was the same as before. He sat in the cruiser and didn't start the engine. He took out his phone.

He thought about calling Jacob. He didn't know what he'd say. *Some British guy's asking about wolves.* Jacob would get that look, the closed-door look he'd been wearing since he'd started growing six inches a month, and he'd say *thanks, Charlie*, and nothing else, because Charlie had told him he didn't want to know. That was the deal.

He called Billy Black instead. It rang six times and went to the machine. Billy's voice, slow and amused, told him to leave a message.

"Billy, it's Charlie." He looked at the empty corner of the lot where the Chevelle had been. "There's a fella in town. English. Says he's some kind of wildlife consultant. He's been asking about the wolf sightings. About the hunting parties." He hesitated. "He's not—I don't think he's what he says he is. I thought somebody ought to know. Call me back."

He hung up. He sat there a while longer, listening to the rain.

Then he started the cruiser and backed out, and he told himself he'd be back here tomorrow at ten, and he'd buy the man his damn coffee, and he'd watch his hands the whole time.

**North of Forks — the Calawah drainage — 10:19 a.m.**

The forest was wet all the way down.

Leah Clearwater ran the eastern loop at a lope that ate ground without thinking about it, paws silent on the deep litter of needles under the spruce. Rain ran in strings off the hanging moss on the big-leaf maples and pattered on her back, and under her coat she was hot. She was always hot now.

The loop ran from the river behind the Cullens' house, north along the ridge, across the old clear-cut, down into the Calawah drainage and back. She could run it in six minutes when she wanted to. This morning she was going slowly, so she'd have something to do with her legs while her brother talked.

*—and then she put her hand on my face,* Seth was saying, *and it was like watching TV, except the TV's inside your head, and it was the deer Jake caught for her on Sunday, but from her side, like how she saw it. Everything was really bright. Like she was seeing it with the color turned up. Leah, it was so cool.*

*You said.*

*I didn't say the color part.*

*You said it three times, Seth.*

*Well, it was really cool.*

Two miles west of her, on the northern line, Seth was running the way he did everything, flat out and a little sideways. She could feel him in the back of her head like somebody humming in the next room, and the deer from Sunday laid over everything like a photograph on top of a photograph. The kid had shown it to him, so it was in Seth's head, so it was in hers.

Everything was in hers. That was the deal.

She'd thought it would be better, running with Jacob instead of Sam. And it was. It was so much better she hadn't had the words for it the first week. No Sam. No Sam thinking about Emily, Emily's hands, Emily's face, the scars down Emily's face that Sam put there and couldn't stop seeing, the love in it, the guilt in it, all of it pouring through the link every time he phased, so that Leah had spent most of a year living inside the head of the man who'd left her and feeling exactly how much he loved the woman he'd left her for. Her cousin. Her best friend, once.

She didn't have to do that anymore. Now she only had to live inside Jacob's head, which was full of a three-week-old baby, and Seth's head, which was full of whatever Jacob's was full of plus his own boundless enthusiasm for everything the Cullens did.

*You could come in sometime,* Seth said. He'd caught the edge of her thought; he always did. *Esme always asks. She made that lasagna last week, the one with the—*

*I'm not eating anything that comes out of a house that smells like that.*

*It doesn't smell that bad.*

*It smells like somebody boiled a bag of sugar in bleach and then died in it.*

*You get used to it.*

*That's what worries me.*

He let it go. He was good at letting things go. It was the most irritating thing about him.

She came out of the spruce onto the edge of the old clear-cut, where the stumps stood in dead rusted fireweed like gravestones, and stopped, and breathed. Rain. Wet earth. Rot. Elk, a day old, a small herd that had come through the southern edge. Coyote. A truck down on the logging road, a long way off, diesel. The faint permanent sweet stink of the Cullens from the southwest, which was always there now, laid over the whole valley like a stain.

And something else.

Leah's head came up.

It was very faint. A thread. It came from the northeast on the wind that had been blowing down the Calawah all morning, and it was the same sweet stink as the Cullens, but wrong. Sharper. Like the difference between a cut flower and one that's been in the vase a week. Under it, darker, a thicker smell she knew from Victoria's trails last spring, from the Seattle newborns in the clearing in June.

Human blood. Old. A day, maybe two. Carried in the body and breathed out again.

*Seth.*

He'd felt her stop. He was already stopping too, skidding on wet needles. *What—*

She gave it to him. Not words; the smell itself, laid down in the link, and she felt him take it and go still.

*That's not one of ours,* he said.

*Ours.* She let that one go too. Later. *Nomad. It's been feeding.*

*Where?*

*Coming down the Calawah. Northeast. Two miles, maybe less.* She moved. She was across the clear-cut before she knew she'd decided, keeping low, keeping to the edges where the slash would cover her. *It's moving this way. Toward town.*

*Toward the house,* Seth said.

*Maybe.*

*We should get Jake.*

*Jake's on two legs playing with the baby.* He couldn't hear them. Nobody could hear them when they were human; that was the only privacy any of them got. *By the time you run back there and he phases and gets his tail out here the leech'll be halfway to Forks.*

*I could howl.*

*And it hears you and it runs.*

*Leah, Jake said if we found one we were supposed to—*

*Jake said protect the perimeter.* She went down into the trees on the far side of the clear-cut at a run. *This is the perimeter. Come on. It's one. There's two of us.*

He came. Of course he came. She felt him open up into the real run, and she felt the thing that always came with that, the thing she never told anyone because Seth would have been unbearable about it. The two of them moving together in the same head. Just the two Clearwaters. It was the closest thing she had anymore to being part of something that wasn't broken.

*Flank it,* she said. *Stay north. Push it toward the river. I'll take it from the east.*

*Okay.* A beat. *Leah? Be careful.*

*I'm always careful.*

*You're literally never careful.*

She didn't answer that.

---

The scent got stronger in long, uneven jumps as the wind gusted. She ran down into the drainage, where the ground fell toward the Calawah in steep ravines and the trees got old and huge, Sitka spruce and hemlock with roots knuckled up over rocks furred green with moss. The light was dim and gray-green. Water ran everywhere.

The thread of scent became a rope.

She slowed. Low. Belly almost to the moss. Paws placed. Seth was a quarter mile north and to her left, coming down the next ravine over, and she could feel him slowing too, matching her, his nose full of the same stink.

*Fresh,* he said. *Like, really fresh.*

*Shut up.*

She came around the trunk of a fallen hemlock, a nurse log with a row of young trees growing out of its back like a mohawk, and there he was.

He was forty yards away, down the slope. A man, or the shape of one. Dark blond hair to his collar, wet. A brown canvas work jacket with a tear in one shoulder, too big for him across the chest. Jeans black with rain from the knee down. Hiking boots. He was moving across the slope at a walk, unhurried, the way a man walks through his own backyard, and as she watched he stopped and lifted his face and breathed in through his nose. He did it slowly. He was smelling the valley. He was smelling the town.

He was smelling the house, she thought. The Cullens. The baby.

His head came around.

He'd heard something. Her, or Seth, or the rain on her back, she didn't know. His face turned up the slope toward her, pale and smooth and very still, and she saw his eyes.

Red. Bright, wet red, the color of the inside of a fresh cut.

For a second he didn't move. She saw him see her. She saw him understand what he was looking at: a wolf the size of a horse, gray, crouched on a log above him with her lips back off her teeth. She saw something go through him that on a human face she'd have called confusion.

Then he looked past her, to the left, and his face changed, and she knew Seth had come out of the ravine.

The leech ran.

Leah went off the log.

---

A vampire ran like a man running, upright, arms pumping, except that it was a man running at sixty miles an hour through old-growth forest without ever putting a foot wrong. It looked like film sped up. It looked like a mistake.

It was fast.

She was faster.

She'd always been faster. Faster than Paul, faster than Jared, faster than Sam. It was the one thing that was hers. She came down the slope in three bounds, the third one taking her over a creek and a tangle of salmonberry without touching either, and she hit the ground on the other side already at full stretch. The world narrowed down. Trees went by like fence posts. Rain came at her sideways.

The leech went straight down the slope toward the river. He was thinking water, thinking he could lose them in the current, and it was a good thought. Except Seth was already there, coming down the next ravine in a long sandy blur, cutting the angle. Leah felt him see it at the same time she did.

*Turn him,* she said.

Seth turned him. He broke out of the ferns thirty yards ahead and to the left and the leech saw him and swerved right without slowing, and Leah went right with him, and now he was running across the slope, along the valley wall, parallel to the river, with a gray wolf on his heels and a sandy one on his flank.

He threw something. She saw his arm go back without breaking stride and a broken length of branch as thick as her leg came spinning back at her head, and she ducked it and felt it go past her ear like a truck. It hit a spruce behind her and burst into pieces.

*Missed,* she said.

*He's going for the creek,* Seth said.

There was a side creek ahead, coming down a gully to the Calawah, cut deep into the slope between two walls of rock and fern. Too wide for a person to jump. The leech hit the near edge and went across it like it was a puddle, a long flat leap with his coat flaring out behind him, and landed on the far bank in a stand of big-leaf maples.

Leah went over after him. She was three strides behind. Two. She could smell him, the sweet rotten stink of him and the blood under it, a woman's blood, and something in her chest went tight and hot and the world turned red at the edges, and she opened her mouth for the back of his leg as he landed.

He landed. She was in the air behind him.

There was a sound.

She'd think about that sound for a long time afterward. It was like a heavy rope snapping taut, or a bowstring, except bigger, deeper, a sound she felt in her ribs more than heard. Something in the air under the maples went *tight*.

The leech went up.

He went up like he'd been yanked off the ground by a winch. His left ankle went first, straight up over his head, and the rest of him followed, his whole body flipping head-down in a fraction of a second, coat falling down over his shoulders, arms flying out. Leah's jaws closed on air a foot below where his calf had been. She came down on the far bank of the creek in a sprawling, scrabbling crash, all four legs going different directions in the wet moss, and she skidded sideways into the trunk of a maple hard enough to shake water down off every leaf.

She came up snarling.

The leech was hanging in the air in front of her.

He hung upside down from nothing. His left foot was eight or nine feet off the ground, under the long limb of a maple, and there was nothing holding it. No rope. No vine. His ankle was just *up there*, pinned in the empty air as if somebody had nailed it to the sky, and the rest of him dangled from it, right leg bent stiff, arms hanging down past his head. The canvas coat had slid down over his shoulders and hung inside out.

He was turning, very slowly, the way a thing hanging on a string will turn.

He wasn't moving. That was the thing. Leah's hackles were up all the way down to her tail and she was crouched low with her teeth showing, waiting for him to twist, to fold up at the waist and grab for the branch, to do the kind of thing she'd seen them do in the clearing that no living body should have been able to do. He didn't do anything. His arms hung. His fingers hung, half-curled. His face, upside down, was a smooth pale mask.

Only his eyes moved. They rolled and found her. They rolled the other way. Red, and wide, and moving very fast.

He was still turning.

Seth came over the creek behind her and landed in the ferns and stopped dead with a grunt.

*What*, he said. *What. What is that. Leah, what—*

*I don't know.*

*Is that—what's holding him? Is that the Cullens? Did one of them do that? Is that one of Alice's—*

*Alice sees the future, Seth, she doesn't hang people off trees.*

*Then what—*

*I don't know!*

She made herself breathe. She breathed in through her nose, long and slow, sorting it. Rain. Wet maple bark. Moss. Creek water. The leech, very strong, sweet and wrong. And under it, in the air right around him, something sharp and clean she didn't have a name for. Like the air in the yard after lightning had come down in the big storm two winters ago and split Old Man Ateara's cedar. That smell. Hot metal and burnt air. It was everywhere under the maples and nowhere outside them.

*He's not breathing,* Seth said.

She looked. He wasn't. Vampires didn't need to, she knew that, but they did it anyway, all the time, out of habit, to talk and to smell. This one's chest was still. His mouth was a little open, and nothing went in or out of it.

*Somebody did this,* Leah said. *Somebody set this.*

*Like a trap?*

*Like a snare.* She'd seen her father set snares for rabbits, when she was little. A loop of wire on a bent sapling. The rabbit puts its foot in and the sapling straightens. She took a step to her left, slowly, watching the ground, watching the air. Nothing happened. Another step. *It's set for them. It didn't get me. I came over right behind him, I came down right there—*

*Maybe it's only for them.*

*Maybe.*

*Maybe it was a mistake, and it's going to get us next time, and—Leah. Leah, I don't like this.*

She didn't either. She didn't like the smell of lightning on a gray morning with no thunder in it. She didn't like a trap she couldn't see in a forest she'd been running for seven months.

But she knew what to do with him.

*Watch the trees,* she told Seth.

*What are you doing?*

*What do you think I'm doing?*

She went forward. Low. One paw, then the next. The leech's eyes followed her and got wider, which she wouldn't have said was possible. His head hung at about the height of her shoulder. She could reach his neck without even lifting her chin. She thought about the woman whose blood was on his breath. She thought about what that woman's family would have been told: a bear. A cougar. Lost on the trail. She thought about her father, whose heart had stopped the day she phased for the first time, and how there had been nobody to blame for that but her.

She opened her jaws.

*Leah—*

The world cracked open behind her.

It was the loudest single sound she'd heard since the clearing: a flat, enormous *CRACK* like a rifle fired right next to her head, so close and so sudden that every muscle she had fired at once. She was spinning before she knew she'd moved, all four paws leaving the ground, landing facing the other way with her ears flat and her teeth bared and a snarl coming up out of her chest that she couldn't have stopped if she'd tried. Beside her, Seth had done the same thing. They stood shoulder to shoulder in the ferns with their hackles up.

Twenty feet away, in the middle of a patch of sword fern that had been empty a second ago, stood a man in a black suit.

**The Calawah drainage — 10:34 a.m.**

The ground took his ankle.

That was how Merritt would remember it afterward, when he had nothing to do for a long time but remember: the ground reached up and took his ankle. He'd landed clean on the far side of the gully, both feet, in a stand of maples, already turning his weight downhill toward the sound of the river, and something closed around his left boot just above the heel like a fist.

Then the world went over.

He had been a vampire for seventy-five years. In seventy-five years nothing had moved him that he hadn't let move him. He'd been hit by a logging truck on the Bitterroot highway in 1958 and walked away from it with the driver's blood in his mouth. He had never in seventy-five years been *picked up*, and his body didn't know what to do with it. His body did nothing. The forest swung up past his face and the sky swung down under his feet and then the forest was up there, upside down, the trunks hanging from the ground like stalactites, and his head was down among the ferns, and he was swinging.

He told his arms to grab for the branch above him.

His arms hung.

He told his body to fold at the waist, to jackknife up and catch his own ankle and tear it free of whatever had hold of it. His body hung there. He told his lungs to pull air so he could smell the thing that held him, and his chest didn't move.

Nothing moved. Nothing at all, except his eyes.

There was a part of his mind—there had been ever since the change, when the fire had burned the old small mind out of him and left something larger behind—that never stopped doing arithmetic. It was doing arithmetic now. *Ankle held. Rest of the body: no response. No pain. No injury. Pressure at the ankle: even. Height: nine feet at the foot, perhaps two at the head. Rotation: slow, counterclockwise. Cause: unknown.* That part of his mind worked away like a clerk in a burning building, writing things down.

Another part of his mind was screaming.

And another part, a third, was listening to the wolves.

The gray one had crashed down below him and come up with her teeth out. The sandy one came over a second later and stopped dead. Up close they were bigger than he'd thought on the slope; the gray was the size of a draft horse. Her lips were back off her teeth and her eyes were dark and her breath came out of her in steam, and her heart was going like a drum, and so was the other one's, and under the drum of the hearts there was the sound of blood, a great deal of it, hot and fast.

He couldn't smell them. He couldn't pull the air. He'd never in seventy-five years understood how much of the world came in through his nose until it stopped coming in, and the forest around him was suddenly flat and dim, like a photograph of itself.

The wolves were looking at each other.

They looked at each other the way two people do when they're arguing in a room where they can't speak out loud. The sandy one whined; the gray one huffed, hard, and the sandy one shut up. Then the gray one took a careful step sideways, eyes on the ground, and another, and Merritt understood that she was testing the place where he'd been caught. Like a person would.

*Wolves don't do that*, said the clerk.

*They're going to kill you*, said the part that was screaming.

The gray one came toward him.

She came low and slow, one big foot at a time, and her eyes held his upside-down eyes the whole way, and he saw in them a thing he'd seen in a few human eyes over the years, at the end. He saw that she knew what he was and what he'd done. She knew about Sunday. She knew about the trailhead and the girl with the headlamp. She could smell it on him.

He tried to scream. Nothing. His mouth hung open a little, and no air went in, and no sound came out.

Her jaws opened. Her teeth were as long as his fingers. Her breath came over his face in a hot wave and he still couldn't smell it.

The world cracked.

The sound came from behind the wolves and to the left, a flat hard bang like a shotgun going off in a closed room. Both wolves left the ground. They turned in midair, both of them, and came down facing the other way with their backs up and a sound coming out of their chests like engines, and Merritt's eyes went with them, rolling in his upside-down head.

A man was standing in the ferns.

Merritt had been looking at that patch of ground a second ago, and it had been ferns. There'd been a bang, and now there was a man in them, the way a picture is there when you turn the page.

He was tall. Upside down he was hard to measure, but he was a big man, wide in the shoulders, standing very straight. Black suit. White shirt. A thin black tie. A long dark overcoat open over the suit, falling to his knees. Black leather gloves. Dark glasses, in the rain, under the trees, with the light the color of dishwater; water beaded on the lenses and ran off. His hair was black and wet and pointed in several directions at once.

His shoes were black and polished. He was standing ankle-deep in wet ferns and moss and the mud of the creek bank, and there wasn't a speck of mud on them.

And his heart—Merritt could hear his heart. Sixty beats a minute. Sixty-two. It was the heartbeat of a man sitting in an armchair after a good dinner. Two wolves the size of horses were standing twenty feet in front of him with their lips back, and his heart was going sixty-two.

The man looked at the wolves. He looked past them, at Merritt, for about half a second. Then back at the wolves.

He reached into the inside pocket of his coat. The gray wolf's snarl went up a step. The man's hand came out with a black leather wallet, and he flipped it open with his thumb and held it up at shoulder height, face out, toward the wolves. There was a gold badge on one side of it and a card behind plastic on the other.

"Morning, both," the man said. He was English. "Special Agent Evans, Homeland Security. This situation is contained. I'm going to have to ask you to vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

The wolves stared at him.

Two seconds went by. Then the man said, in the same tone, as if he'd been asked:

"Protocol's protocol."

The sandy wolf's ears went straight up. The gray wolf's went flat.

"Yes, Balto," the man said, looking at the sandy one. "I did."

The sandy wolf's mouth fell open a little. The gray one's lips came back further and the snarl in her chest went down into a register Merritt felt in the bones of his face.

"Can't, sorry," said the man to the gray wolf. "You're both thinking at the top of your lungs. It's like trying to have a quiet pint next to the telly when the football's on."

*They're talking to him*, said the clerk in Merritt's head. *He's answering them.*

*That's not possible*, said another part.

*He came out of nowhere with a bang and you're hanging from nothing and you can't breathe*, said the clerk. *Write down what's possible later.*

The gray wolf's head jerked a little, sideways. The man flipped the wallet shut and slid it back into his coat.

"Special relationship, Lassie," he said. "Look it up."

The gray wolf made a sound Merritt had never heard an animal make. It was halfway between a growl and a bark and it had *outrage* in it, the plain human kind, and the sandy wolf beside her made a short wheezing huff through its nose that sounded exactly like a laugh somebody was trying to hold in.

The man tilted his head at the gray wolf.

"Grey coat, long face, spends all her time hauling idiots out of wells." He shrugged one shoulder. "If the collar fits."

The gray wolf's head swung toward the sandy one, sharply, and the sandy one stopped huffing at once, and then its ears went sideways and it looked at the man.

"Sled dog," the man said to it. "Nineteen twenty-five, he ran the diphtheria serum the last leg into Nome through a blizzard when the whole town was dying. There's a statue of him in Central Park. Take the compliment, mate."

The sandy wolf's tail moved. Once. It swept the ferns.

The gray wolf turned her head and looked at the sandy one, and the tail stopped.

Then she looked back at the man, and at Merritt, hanging behind her, and back at the man, and the man took off his glasses.

He folded them one-handed and put them in his breast pocket, and he looked at Merritt.

His eyes were green. Merritt would remember that too, later. Green like the light through the bottom of a bottle.

Then the man was inside him.

There was no other way to say it. There was no pain. There was a pressure, like a thumb pressed lightly on the back of his eyes, and then it was as though somebody had walked into the big echoing house of his mind and started opening doors. Fast. He was *flipping* through them, the way a clerk flips through a file drawer looking for one card. Merritt felt each door open. He felt the man look into it and close it. The highway up the Oregon coast. The motel outside Aberdeen where he'd waited out two days of sun. The rest stop near Elma. The long loop north along the Hood Canal. Port Angeles at dusk. The trailhead.

The girl.

She'd been running. Late, in the dark, with a blue headlamp bobbing on her forehead and gray running shoes, alone on the trail by the lake, and he'd heard her heart a mile off. He hadn't asked her name. He'd never asked any of their names.

He felt the man look at that door. He felt him stand in it for perhaps a second. Then he closed it, quite gently, and the pressure went away, and the man was only standing in the ferns again looking at him.

His face hadn't changed at all.

"Lake Crescent," the man said. He wasn't talking to Merritt. He'd turned his head a little toward the wolves. "Sunday night, about nine. A woman out for a run. Grey trainers, blue headlamp. He's been working his way up the 101 from Oregon." A beat. "He's been busy."

The sandy wolf made a low sound, a whine with something sick in it. The gray one didn't make any sound. Her eyes had gone very flat.

"And as for what I'm going to do with him," the man said, to the gray wolf, as if she'd asked, "I'm going to ask him some questions."

A pause. The gray wolf's head lowered.

"For now," the man said.

The sandy one shifted on its feet. It looked at the man and back toward the south, toward the town, where the sweet stink of the yellow-eyed coven had been lying across the valley all morning, and it whined again, a different whine, a question.

"Your bloodsuckers are on the guest list, Balto," said the man. "The wards let them through. They have done for eleven days."

The gray wolf went absolutely still.

"I've been watching everybody for eleven days," said the man. "It's sort of the job."

Merritt saw the gray wolf's weight go back onto her haunches. He'd been a hunter for seventy-five years and he knew that movement in every animal that made it. He saw her measure the twenty feet of ferns between her and the man in the suit. He saw her decide.

The man was looking at something on the sleeve of his coat. He picked it off with two gloved fingers: a maple seed.

"I wouldn't, Lassie," he said. "You'd make it about halfway."

The gray wolf froze. Something passed over her that Merritt couldn't read, but that he saw land on her like a hand.

The man's face changed for the first time. It went quieter.

"That one's none of my business," he said. "Sorry."

The gray wolf didn't move. The sandy wolf looked at her, and at the man, and back at her, and its ears were all over the place, and it whined very quietly.

The man reached into his overcoat again, a different pocket, and came out with something small and white between two fingers. A card. He flicked it. It turned over in the air twice and came down face up on the moss in front of the sandy wolf's forepaws.

"For the doctor," he said.

Then he turned to Merritt and said, "Right then, Sunshine."

And the fist around Merritt's ankle opened.

He fell straight down, the way a dropped plank falls, and his head hit the rock under the moss with a crack that went through his whole skull and into his teeth, and the rest of him came down after it in one rigid piece and toppled sideways into the ferns. He lay there with his arms still crooked the way they'd been in the air, locked in the shape of a man hanging by one foot, now lying down.

Shoes came into the edge of his vision. Polished black shoes with no mud on them.

The man bent down. A gloved hand closed on the collar of Merritt's coat and another on his belt, and the man straightened up and lifted him.

Merritt's stiff body came up off the ground and swung horizontal, and the man tucked him under one arm, face down, the way a surfer carries a board down to the water, and settled him with a little hitch of his hip. Merritt's head stuck out in front and his feet stuck out behind. The man's arm across his back was as solid as a steel bar.

And it was hot.

Even through the coat and the jacket and the gloves, Merritt could feel it. Every human he'd ever held had felt like a cup of coffee against his skin. This was more. The arm across his back was hot as a man with a fever, hot as a stone left in the sun, and the heat came through the layers of cloth into Merritt's cold body like a hand on a stove, and that was when the screaming part of his mind finally got the upper hand over the clerk, and for a while he didn't write anything down.

From under the man's arm, upside down and sideways, he could see the wolves. The sandy one was staring at the man with its mouth open. The gray one was staring at nothing, or at the man's shoes.

The man looked down at the sandy one and seemed to listen for a moment.

"Longboard, technically," he said.

Then, to both of them, cheerfully: "Off you trot. Tell your Alpha. And tell him the eleven days bit first, Lassie, he'll want to shout about it."

He settled his grip.

"Ta-ra."

The world went into a tube.

That was the closest Merritt would ever come to describing it: every side of him pressed inward at once, as if he'd been shoved into a pipe much too small for him and pulled through it at great speed. His body was granite, and the squeeze took no notice. It went on and on, a second much longer than a second, and in the middle of it, very faintly, he heard the man grunt, like a man lifting something heavier than he'd expected.

Then it let go, and the light changed.

Indoors. The rain had stopped. There was a ceiling, high above, pale and smooth.

The man was walking, and Merritt's view bobbed with it. Scrubbed honey-colored floorboards. A corridor, far too long, doors going away down both sides and lamps in brackets between them, the far end forty or fifty yards off.

They passed a kitchen: a stove, a humming icebox, a record player on a sideboard.

They passed a door on the right with a window of thick glass in it, and through the glass he saw guns.

Shotguns and rifles in wooden racks along the walls. Handguns on green felt under glass. Belts of shells on hooks. Knives. Every barrel and stock and blade he could see had markings cut into it, angular little shapes in rows, like writing in an alphabet he didn't know. On some of them the marks glowed, very faintly, the color of a gas flame turned low.

Then that door was behind them.

They passed a room of books, floor to ceiling, and above its desk a long shelf of identical black notebooks, dozens of them, their spines numbered in white paint.

They passed something he didn't understand at all: a door with no handle, made of what looked like black stone, with a line of the same angular marks running around its frame. The man didn't look at it.

At the end of the corridor a heavy white door swung inward before the man reached it, and closed behind them with a soft thud, and the air went colder and drier on Merritt's eyes.

The room was white, walls and ceiling and floor, a hard white like enamel, lit from no direction he could find. It went back a long way. Along the walls stood cabinets with glass fronts and steel shelves, and on the shelves were jars.

The man walked past them, and Merritt's rolling eyes went over them one after another.

A jar of clear liquid with a hand in it. A gray hand, long-fingered, too many joints in the fingers. A tooth as long as a man's forearm on a velvet cushion, under a glass dome. A jar of something black and thick like tar that moved, slowly, against the glass, as he watched. A row of smaller jars with what looked like bits of skin in them, labeled in a cramped, slanting hand. A big cylinder of glass, taller than a man, with nothing in it but a coil of dark gray mist that drew back from the glass as they passed.

There were empty cabinets too. A lot of empty cabinets. One of them, at the end, stood open, its glass door swung wide, its shelves bare, and above it, on a brass plate on the frame, someone had engraved: N.A. 01.

The man stopped.

In the middle of the white room stood a table. It was steel, long and wide, set on a heavy pedestal bolted into the floor. Its surface was cut with grooves. Circles inside circles, and lines running between the circles, and between the lines the same small angular marks as on the guns, all of it cut into the steel so deep the light lay in the grooves like water.

The man swung Merritt off his hip and laid him on it on his back.

He did it the way you'd lay down something you didn't want to damage. The steel was cold under Merritt's back. He had not felt cold in seventy-five years. He felt it now, or thought he did.

Above him the ceiling was white and very far away. Then the man's face came into it, looking down.

He had a cut on his cheekbone. Merritt saw it and the clerk wrote it down: a small fresh scratch, just below the right eye, beaded with a single bright drop of blood. His own fingernail, the clerk thought. When the man had swung him up off the moss, one of those crooked, frozen hands had gone past his face. The smell would have been extraordinary. Merritt couldn't breathe to smell it. As he watched, the man touched it with the back of one gloved knuckle, glanced at the glove, and the scratch was gone. There was clean skin where it had been, without a line or a mark on it.

The man pulled off his gloves and dropped them on a steel tray. He hung his overcoat and his suit jacket on a hook on the wall, and Merritt saw the gun on his left side, under the arm, in a brown leather holster: a heavy black automatic with the little glowing marks running down the slide. The man left it where it was. He undid his cuffs and rolled his sleeves to the elbows. His forearms were thick and corded, the skin smooth and unmarked.

He loosened his tie.

From a stand at the head of the table he took a long gray quill with a steel nib, held it up, and let go of it, and it didn't fall. It hung in the air beside his shoulder, nib down, and turned a little, like a dog turning to look at its master.

A fresh black notebook lay open on a lectern beside the stand.

Then he looked down at Merritt, on the table, and for the first time, Merritt saw what was in his face when he wasn't putting anything there.

It was the most frightening thing he'd seen in seventy-five years. It was the face of a man about to start the day's work. Patient. Attentive. Interested, the way a mechanic is interested in an engine he's never had apart before.

"Right," the man said.

The quill dipped toward the page.

He'd been on the peninsula eleven days. He'd been on the job since April.

On the feast of Saint Marcus, a retired Obliviator called Muriel Pratt had been eating a lemon ice in the main square of Volterra when, at noon, in the shadow of the clock tower, a pale boy had started to unbutton his shirt and a girl had come sprinting across the square and hit him like a rugby tackle. In the half-second before she shoved him back, the back of his hand had gone past the edge of the shadow into the sun, and Muriel Pratt, who had spent thirty-four years making Muggles forget things, said afterwards that it had gone off like a chandelier. Then two big men in grey cloaks had come out of the shadows, and after them a small girl in a darker one, and they'd led the boy and the girl away. Muriel had written it all down, because she was that sort of woman, and it had gone to the Department because nobody else would have it.

The Department had sent someone to look, and the someone had found the tours: coach parties of Muggles who went into the old keep under the clock tower on a cheap, exclusive guided visit and didn't come out. Two hundred and eleven of them between April and August. On the twelfth of June, one of them had been Bernard Ashby, sixty-eight, retired from the Floo Network Authority, from Hexham, on his first holiday abroad since the war.

That was when Croaker had stopped calling it Muriel's chandelier and started calling it a target.

Most of the rest had come from the receptionist. Gianna was human, she worked the front desk of the keep, and she did her shopping in the town on Thursday mornings. For seven Thursdays running there had been a quiet man behind her in the queue at the bakery on the Via Porta all'Arco, and she had never once noticed him. Gianna knew the names. Aro, Caius, Marcus. The guard: Jane, Alec, Demetri, Felix. And she'd heard the visitors in the spring called the Cullens, and the name Carlisle said in a tone she'd only ever heard used about family.

After that it was airline manifests and a yellow Porsche stolen in Florence that same morning. Seattle. Port Angeles. Forks.

Seven of them, eight now, nine if you counted the baby, and the Department hadn't yet decided how to. They lived in a glass house in the woods and ate deer. The head of the family worked shifts at the local hospital, and his patients' survival figures were so good the hospital's own auditors had twice flagged them as a data-entry error. According to Gianna, he'd lived with the people under the clock tower for decades, a long time ago, and left on good terms.

Croaker had read that line and put his pen down and said, "Don't touch them."

*Asset-potential. More useful alive. Do not engage.* What the Department needed was something it could take apart. A specimen that wasn't anybody's family.

Croaker had asked him the three questions he asked everybody who wanted money for something. *What are you trying to do? Who's tried it before, and why didn't it work? And if you're right, who dies?*

*Find out how to kill them,* Harry had said. *Nobody. Them.*

Croaker had signed the requisition without reading it.

> **PHASE II — MAGICAL RESPONSE.** 11:14.
>
> Objective: test whether standard spellwork—Stunning Spell, Full Body-Bind, Legilimency, et al.—functions as expected.

The snare still had him. It was the part of the job Harry had spent the most time on, and the part he was proudest of, though he'd have died before saying so. The Department register called it *Laqueus Inversus*. Harry called it the Rabbit Snare, and so did everybody else on the fourth floor now, which annoyed the register.

He'd got the idea at sixteen, from a dead man's handwriting in the margin of a Potions book. *Levicorpus.* Hoist the target by one ankle. A schoolboy's joke, and a nasty one. It had taken seventeen failed versions to work out how to nail it to the ground for weeks with nobody holding the wand, make it fire on its own, make it choose what it fired on, and lock what it caught so tight it couldn't blink. Version nine had hoisted a Hereford bull outside Salisbury, and Harry had spent a long afternoon with the farmer. Version eighteen had held a mountain troll upside down in a Wiltshire field for three days in the rain, and on the fourth day the Department had given him the budget for the peninsula.

He'd laid forty-one nodes between the Strait of Juan de Fuca and the Queets River, mostly at night. They were keyed on two things together: a body with no heartbeat, and the resonance of human blood carried inside it. A man could walk through. So could a wolf. So could a cold body that ran on deer. On his second night Harry had sat under the cedars and watched the big one, the one the file called Emmett, walk straight across node six on his way home from a hunt, and nothing had happened, and Harry had written *node six: negative* in his notebook and gone back to his flask of tea.

He touched the specimen's chest and took the snare off from the neck up.

The jaw closed with a click. The throat moved. The specimen dragged in a long whistling breath, his first in forty minutes, and the moment it reached the back of his throat his eyes went to Harry's neck. Then to his wrist.

"Yeah," Harry said. "I know. Bad luck, mate."

Then he took the rest of the snare off, and the grooves in the steel table lit blue. He'd cut every one of them himself, a ring for each limb, a ring for the torso, binding lines between, and they took hold the instant the snare let go. The crooked arms slammed down flat. The bent leg straightened with a sound like a branch breaking under snow. The specimen arched, and the table held him.

"What are you," the specimen said. A flat Western voice, the voice of a man who might once have sold you feed in Montana.

"Busy," said Harry. "Name?"

"Go to hell."

"Lovely to meet you, Go to Hell. Stupefy."

He didn't need to say it. He hadn't needed to say any spell since a year in the Mountains of the Moon with an old woman who'd made him eat left-handed for six weeks until he stopped reaching for his wand to pass the salt. The wand lived in a holster on his right forearm, and he hadn't drawn it on a job in three years. He said the words for the quill.

Red light. The specimen jolted against the bindings and his eyes went dull. At two seconds, they came back.

"Stunning Spell lands. Jolt and a blank of about one point eight seconds. No unconsciousness." Harry thought about it. "He doesn't sleep. You can't knock a man out if there's no *out* for him to go to." He stacked three, fast. The eyes went blank and came back at just under four. "Three Stunners, four seconds. Useful for a gap, and that's all."

He loosened the table's hold on the right arm, on purpose, ready for it. The arm came off the steel in a blur, fingers hooked for his throat from a foot away, and Harry put a Full Body-Bind on it halfway there. It stopped in the air a few inches from his collar. He could feel the cold coming off the fingers. It was trembling, a high fast tremor like a plucked string, the specimen throwing everything in that arm against the spell.

Seth didn't mean to laugh. It came out of his nose in a short hard huff before he could stop it, and then another one, and he felt Leah feel it and he didn't care, because *Lassie*.

The man tilted his head at Leah. "Grey coat, long face, spends all her time hauling idiots out of wells." He shrugged one shoulder. "If the collar fits."

Leah's head whipped round to Seth.

Seth stopped laughing. Instantly. He looked at the man instead, sideways, ears all over the place. *Wait,* he said, *so who's Balto? Like, the cartoon dog?*

"Sled dog," said the man. "Nineteen twenty-five, he ran the diphtheria serum the last leg into Nome through a blizzard when the whole town was dying. There's a statue of him in Central Park. Take the compliment, mate."

Seth's tail moved. He didn't tell it to. It swept the ferns once, left to right, and he felt Leah turn and look at him, and he made it stop.

*Seriously?* she said.

*He saved a whole town, Leah.*

*He's in our HEADS, Seth.* She swung back to the man, and Seth felt her shove the next thought at him like a fist. *What are you going to do with the leech? It killed somebody. I can smell it on him.*

The man took off his sunglasses.

He folded them with one hand and put them in his breast pocket and turned and looked at the leech. His eyes were green. He just looked. He didn't move. The leech hung there under the maple, upside down, turning, with its red eyes rolled all the way round toward the man, and the man looked into them, and something happened that Seth couldn't see. He could feel it, almost. A pressure in the air, like before a storm. The leech's eyes went wide and fixed.

It lasted about as long as it takes to read a page.

Then the man turned his head a little, back toward them, and said, "Lake Crescent. Sunday night, about nine. A woman out for a run. Grey trainers, blue headlamp. He's been working his way up the 101 from Oregon." A beat. "He's been busy."

Seth made a noise. He didn't know he was going to; it came out low and sick. A woman out for a run. He thought of his mom, who went walking on First Beach every morning. He thought of the trail at Lake Crescent, where they'd gone for a picnic one summer when he was little, his dad carrying the cooler.

Leah didn't make a sound. Seth felt her go cold and flat and still inside, the way she went when something hurt too much to feel.

*What are you going to do with him,* she said again. Quieter.

"And as for what I'm going to do with him," the man said, "I'm going to ask him some questions."

*Questions.* Leah's head lowered. *You're going to let him live.*

"For now," the man said.

Seth's head turned south on its own. That was where the house was, the big white house by the river, and Jake on the couch with Nessie in his lap, and Bella, and Edward, and all of them. The smell of them lay over the whole valley like it always did. If this guy had a trap that caught leeches, then—

*The Cullens,* Seth said. *Is he here for the Cullens? Is that thing going to—Nessie, Bella, Esme, if one of them walks through here and it—*

"Your bloodsuckers are on the guest list, Balto," said the man. "The wards let them through. They have done for eleven days."

Leah went absolutely still.

*Eleven days,* she said. *He's been out here eleven days. He's been watching us for eleven days.*

"I've been watching everybody for eleven days," said the man. "It's sort of the job."

Seth felt it before he saw it. In the link, Leah's mind went sharp and quiet and simple, the way it went right before she hit something. *Twenty feet. He's human. Heart's at sixty. Hands are empty. Coat'll slow him down. Two jumps, one if I push. Throat. He won't even get his hands up—*

*Leah, don't—*

Her weight went back onto her haunches. Seth saw it. The man was looking at the sleeve of his coat, at a maple seed stuck to the wool, and he picked it off with two fingers and flicked it away.

"I wouldn't, Lassie," he said. "You'd make it about halfway."

And something came up in Leah. Seth felt it come up through the link, fast and hot and ugly, underneath the plan, underneath everything. *Another one. Another man who walks in and decides how it's going to be and everybody else is just supposed to stand there and take it, like it's weather, like it's nobody's fault—* and under that, for half a second, a picture. Sam. Sam on First Beach in the rain with his hands open, saying *I can't help it, Leah, I can't*, and Emily behind him on the driftwood with her scarred face turned away.

Leah slammed it shut. Seth had never felt her shut something so fast.

The man's face changed. It didn't change much. It went quieter, was all.

"That one's none of my business," he said. "Sorry."

Leah didn't move. In the link there was nothing at all. No thoughts. Just a kind of white, roaring blank, like a TV between stations, and under it something Seth recognized after a second, because he'd felt it in her every day of that first year in Sam's pack.

It was shame.

Seth looked at her, and at the man, and back. He whined. He didn't know what else to do.

The man reached into a different pocket of his coat and came out with a little white card between two fingers. He flicked it. It flipped over twice in the air and landed face up on the moss right in front of Seth's front paws.

"For the doctor," he said.

He turned to the leech. "Right then, Sunshine."

The leech fell.

It just dropped. Whatever had been holding it let go, and it came straight down out of the air like a plank and hit the ground head first with a crack like somebody had dropped a bowling ball on a rock, and toppled over sideways into the ferns, still bent the same way it had been in the air. Arms up. One leg bent. Stiff as a board. The man walked over and bent down and took hold of it by the coat collar and the belt and picked it up.

He picked it up like it was nothing. A full-grown leech, rigid, a body Seth knew from the clearing weighed about the same as a man but was so hard you could break your teeth on it, and the man swung it up and tucked it under one arm, face down, horizontal, with its head sticking out in front of him and its feet sticking out behind.

*He's carrying it like a surfboard,* Seth said, before he could stop himself.

"Longboard, technically," said the man.

He shifted his grip. The leech's stiff arms swung.

"Off you trot," he said. "Tell your Alpha. And tell him the eleven days bit first, Lassie, he'll want to shout about it."

He looked at them both a second longer.

"Ta-ra."

*CRACK.*

Seth flinched so hard he nearly sat down.

The fern patch was empty. The man was gone. The leech was gone. The ferns where he'd been standing were still moving a little, bending back up, and rain was falling into the place where he'd been, and that was all.

---

For maybe ten seconds, neither of them moved. The rain came down. Somewhere up the slope a jay started yelling. The smell of lightning under the maples was already thinning out.

*Leah,* he said finally.

Nothing.

*Leah, are you—*

*Don't.*

*I wasn't going to—*

*He was in my head, Seth.* Her voice in the link was very low and very even, which was worse than shouting. *He was in my head and he looked at—he just looked at it. Like he was reading a menu.*

*Our heads,* Seth said.

*Yeah. Ours.* She shook herself, hard, from her ears to her tail, the way they all did coming out of a river, and water flew off her in a spray. *Get the card.*

Seth looked down at it.

It lay face up on the moss, getting rained on. Thick, cream-colored. A phone number at the bottom with a 360 area code, a Washington number. And above it, in plain black capitals, a name.

HARRY EVANS.

Seth stared at it.

He felt Leah see it through his eyes. He felt her go still again, a different still.

Their dad had been gone seven months. Seth still picked up the phone sometimes to call him, and put it down again. Harry. It was the name on the mailbox. It was the name on the headstone.

*It's a name,* Leah said, after a while. Her voice was rough. *Lots of people have it. Pick it up.*

Seth bent his head. He took the card very carefully between his front teeth, just the edge of it, so he wouldn't put a hole in it.

*Don't drool on it.*

*I'm not drooling on it.*

*You're drooling on it.*

He was a little bit. He held it as gently as he could.

Leah went under the maple.

She went slowly, nose down, right through the place where the leech had been hanging. Seth's whole body clenched. Nothing happened. No rope. No marks on the bark. Just wet maple and moss.

*It doesn't want us,* Leah said. *He said wards. Like a fence. Like the treaty line, except it actually does something.*

*It's a snare,* Seth said around the card. *You said. Like Dad's rabbit snares.*

*Yeah.*

She went to where the man had stood. His smell was strong there, and Seth put his nose down and followed it out from the trampled ferns, one way and then the other, and it went nowhere. It started in the middle of the fern patch and ended there. No footprints in. None out.

*He didn't walk here,* Seth said.

*He came out of the air.* Leah lifted her head. *And then he went back into it.*

*How?*

*I don't know.*

*Leah, what is he?*

*I don't know, Seth.*

He'd never heard her say *I don't know* three times in one morning. He didn't think he'd heard her say it three times in her life.

*Did you smell him?* she said.

*Pie,* said Seth.

*Under the pie.*

Seth thought about it. He didn't want to. *Yeah.*

*What was it?*

*I don't know,* he said. *Something big.*

Leah didn't say anything to that. She turned south.

*Come on,* she said. *Jake.*

---

They ran.

They ran flat out, back across the old clear-cut and down through the spruce, and Seth held the card in his teeth the whole way. He thought about the guy sitting in the dark in these woods every night for eleven days, watching the house, watching Edward and Bella take Nessie down to the river. Watching *them*, running their loops, thinking their private thoughts out loud for anybody to hear.

*He knew which one of us was in charge,* Leah said, beside him, eating ground. *He said tell your Alpha. He knew it wasn't one of us.*

*He knew about the doctor.*

*He knew about everything.*

The house came up through the trees. White, and glass, three stories of it, with the river running gray and fast behind it and the lawn running down to the water. The lights were on, the way they always were on a dark day.

Edward was already on the front porch.

He'd have heard them a mile out. He'd have heard *all* of it, Seth realized, the badge and the snare and *Lassie* and Leah's thing about Sam that Seth hadn't meant to think about again and had anyway. He felt Leah realize it too, and flinch.

Edward's face was very pale, even for Edward. He was standing absolutely still, the way they did when they forgot to pretend, and he was staring at Seth.

The front door opened behind him and Jake came out with Nessie on his hip. She had her hand on his cheek. Her bronze curls were sticking up on one side where she'd been lying on him. She looked at the wolves on the lawn with her big brown eyes, perfectly calm, and Jake looked at Edward's face and then at Seth and Leah and his own face changed.

"What," Jake said. "What happened?"

Seth came up the lawn and put the card down on the bottom step of the porch, very carefully, and stepped back.

Edward came down the steps without seeming to move his feet and crouched and looked at it without touching it. He read it. Seth saw his eyes go across it once, and stop.

"Jacob," Edward said. He didn't look up. "You need to phase. You need to see this yourself."

"See *what*?" Jake said. "Who the hell is Harry Evans?"

---

**The Cullen house — 10:58 a.m.**

Jacob handed the baby to Rosalie, who had appeared in the doorway behind him the way they did, without any sound, and went down off the porch and into the trees at a jog, pulling his shirt over his head as he went.

Leah felt him come in.

It was like a door opening in a room she'd thought was sealed. One second it was her and Seth, and the next Jacob was there, all of him, and before he'd even finished landing on four feet he was reaching for it. She didn't make him reach. She gave it to him in one shove: the scent on the wind, the chase, the snare, the crack. The man in the ferns with his sunglasses on in the rain. The badge. *Protocol's protocol.* The woman at Lake Crescent. *Eleven days.*

She felt it hit him.

*Eleven days,* Jacob said. His mind went hot and red and very fast. *He's been watching the house for eleven days. He's been watching Nessie—*

*He said everybody.*

*Everybody includes Nessie, Leah!*

*I know that.*

She felt him go through the rest of it again, faster, the way you'd rewind a tape, and she felt him hit *Lassie*, and she felt something in him twitch.

*Don't,* she said.

*I didn't say anything.*

*You were going to.*

*I really wasn't.* A pause. *It's kind of—*

*Jacob, I swear to God.*

He let it go. Then he came to the other thing, the thing she'd tried to bury under the badge and the snare and the eleven days and hadn't been able to bury deep enough. Sam on the beach. *That one's none of my business. Sorry.* She felt Jacob see it. She felt him stop on it for one second.

He didn't say anything about it. He went past it like he hadn't seen it.

She'd remember that, later. She'd have to decide what she thought about it.

*Okay,* Jacob said. *Okay. Phase back, both of you. Inside. Everybody needs to hear this, and I want Carlisle.*

*Alice already called him,* said Seth. *I heard her through the window. He's coming.*

---

Leah phased behind the garage, where the gravel gave way to moss, and untied her clothes from her ankle with fingers that still felt too thick, and pulled on the cutoffs and the black tank top and stood there for a second in the rain on two feet with her arms wrapped around herself. She was never cold anymore. She wanted something to hold on to, that was all.

Then she went in.

The smell hit her at the door like it always did, sweet and chemical, burnt sugar and bleach, and she breathed through her mouth and kept walking.

They were all in the big front room with the glass wall on the river. Esme came toward her with a towel, which Leah took because saying no to Esme was like kicking a puppy. Emmett on the arm of the couch. Jasper at the window. Alice in the middle of the floor, very still, eyes half-closed, hands in fists. Rosalie on the couch with the baby, and Bella beside her, close enough to touch, her eyes the same bright newborn red they'd been for three weeks. Edward by the piano, his hand on the lid, looking at the floor.

Seth was already there, in sweatpants and nothing else, sitting on the stairs with his elbows on his knees. Jacob came in behind her, barefoot, pulling his shirt back on.

Headlights swept the glass. A car door. Carlisle came through the front door in his white coat, still with his hospital badge clipped to the pocket, and said, "Alice," and Alice opened her eyes.

"He's gone," she said. "I still can't find him."

"Who's gone?" Carlisle said.

"The nomad." Alice turned to him. "I saw him at twenty past ten. A man. Fair hair. Coming down off the 101 past Lake Crescent into the Calawah. He'd decided to come and look at us. I was about to tell Jacob. Then his future went blurry, which is the wolves; I knew Leah and Seth had found him. And then at ten thirty-four he was just—" She opened her hand. "Gone."

"So they killed him," Emmett said. "Nice."

"No," Leah said.

Every head in the room turned toward her.

"We didn't kill him," she said. "Somebody else took him."

Edward said, without looking up from the piano, "I'll tell it. I heard all of it. It'll be faster."

And he did.

He told it fast and flat and in order, and it was the strangest thing Leah had ever listened to: her own morning coming out of the mouth of a vampire, with nothing left out. The snare. The crack. The badge. *Special Agent Evans, Homeland Security.* He did the voice. He didn't mean to, Leah thought; he just couldn't help getting it right. The flat, quick, amused English voice came out of Edward Cullen's mouth, and the hair stood up on her arms.

*"Your bloodsuckers are on the guest list, Balto. The wards let them through. They have done for eleven days."*

Esme put her hand over her mouth.

When he got to *Lassie*, Emmett made a sound. Rosalie hit him without looking. He stopped making the sound, mostly.

When he got to Sam on the beach, Edward said, "And then he said something to Leah that was private, and he apologized for hearing it," and went straight on to the card. Leah stared at the side of his face. He didn't look at her.

She didn't know what to do with that either. Two of them in one morning.

When he was done there was a silence.

"He's English," Edward added. "Southern England. The home counties, I'd guess, roughed up on purpose. And he can hear thoughts." He finally looked up. "He heard Leah and Seth the way I hear them. Every word. He answered things they hadn't finished thinking."

"Can he hear us?" said Rosalie.

"I don't know."

"Can he hear *me*?" Bella said.

It was the first thing she'd said. Everybody looked at her. She had her hand on the baby's back, and Leah watched her fingers spread across it.

"I don't know," Edward said, more gently. "I can't. Aro couldn't. Jane couldn't. I don't know what he is."

"He's a man," Jasper said from the window.

He'd been quiet this whole time. Now he turned around, and Leah saw the scars on his throat and jaw catch the gray light, dozens of them, little silver crescents.

"He has a heartbeat," Jasper said. "Sixty-two. He's human, or something with a human heart. He moved through space in a way none of us can. He carried a vampire one-handed. And he has a trap laid across this country that none of us has ever seen or smelled or felt, and he says it's been there eleven days." He looked at Emmett. "You hunted the Calawah on the twenty-fourth."

Emmett opened his mouth. Then he shut it.

"Every one of us has been through that ground in the last two weeks," Jasper said. "Every one. He watched his trap let us go. He tested it on us, and we never knew." He looked at Carlisle. "And this morning he made sure we'd find out."

Esme sat down slowly on the arm of a chair.

"He said we were on the guest list," Carlisle said. "That's something."

"Guest lists change," said Rosalie.

Carlisle didn't answer that. Edward had brought Seth's card in from the porch step and set it on the piano, and Carlisle picked it up and turned it over in his long white fingers and looked at both sides.

"For the doctor," he said quietly.

"That's what he said," Seth said from the stairs.

Carlisle read the front again. Leah watched his face, and she saw the exact second he saw the name. He looked up. He looked at Seth, and then at her, and she saw him put it together. Her father had died while the Cullens were gone. Bella would have told them.

"Leah," he said. "Seth. I'm sorry."

"It's a name," Leah said. "Lots of people have it."

She didn't know why her voice came out like that.

"My father hunted witches," Carlisle said, after a while, to no one in particular. He was still looking at the card. "In London, when I was young. He spent his whole life at it. He burned a great many people who were nothing of the kind. When he grew old, I led the hunts for him. That's how I became what I am." He turned the card over once more. "I always believed there were no real ones."

"There's one now," Edward said.

The phone rang.

Everybody jumped except Alice. Esme went and got it. "Hello? Oh—yes, Billy, of course, he's right here." She held it out. "Jacob. Your father."

Jacob took it. "Dad?" He listened. Leah could hear Billy's voice through the receiver, slow and careful, and she heard the words *Charlie* and *English*, and watched Jacob's face go very still. "When?" He listened. "Yeah. No, Dad, I know. I know who he is. Kind of. Yeah. I'll call you back. Don't—just don't say anything to Charlie yet, okay?" He hung up and looked at the room.

"Charlie left a message on Dad's machine this morning," he said. "There's a guy in town. English. Says he's some kind of wildlife consultant. He's been asking about the wolf sightings. About the hunting parties last year." He swallowed. "Charlie said he doesn't think he's what he says he is."

Bella stood up.

She stood up too fast. She was a newborn and she forgot, sometimes; she was standing and the cushion she'd been sitting on was on the other side of the room. Edward was beside her before it landed.

"He talked to my dad," Bella said.

"Bella—"

"He's been in town eleven days and he talked to my *dad*."

"We don't know what he said," Edward said. "Charlie's fine, Bella. Charlie's at the station. Alice?"

"Charlie's fine," Alice said. Her eyes were unfocused, looking at nothing. "Charlie's at his desk. He's eating a sandwich at twelve-fifteen. He's going home at six."

Bella sat back down. Slowly. Rosalie had moved the baby against her own shoulder, out of the way, without anyone seeing her do it. The baby reached out a small hand and touched Bella's cheek and Bella went still, and Leah watched her face soften into whatever the kid was showing her, and the room let out a breath it didn't need.

Carlisle looked at the card.

"I'm going to call him," he said.

"Carlisle," said Rosalie.

"He asked me to. He went to some trouble to ask me to." Carlisle went to the phone on the wall. "And I'd like to know who's holding a member of our kind in a box, in our woods, and why."

He dialled. Leah could hear the rings from across the room. One. Two. Three. Four.

A click.

*"This is Evans,"* said the voice. The same voice. The same flat, quick, amused voice from the ferns, tinny through the receiver. *"I'm either busy or I'm ignoring you. Leave a message and I'll get back to you once I've worked out which."*

Emmett put his face in a cushion. His shoulders shook.

The tone sounded.

"Mr. Evans," Carlisle said. "This is Carlisle Cullen. I believe you left your card with a friend of mine this morning." He paused, and Leah watched him choose the next words. "I'd welcome the chance to talk with you, at your convenience. I think we may have some things to discuss. You have the number." Another pause. "Thank you."

He hung up.

The clock on the wall above the kitchen door said twelve-twelve.

"He was there," Edward said quietly. "He heard it ring. He's sitting somewhere looking at the phone."

"How do you know?" said Seth.

"I don't," said Edward. "I'd have done the same thing."

---

Alice made a sound.

It was very small, a little intake of breath, and she was standing exactly where she'd been standing for the last hour, in the middle of the floor with her fists clenched. But her eyes had gone wide and blank.

"Alice," said Jasper, and was beside her.

"I can't see him," Alice said. "I've been looking for an hour and I can't see him. He's like you." She looked at Jacob. "He's like the wolves, worse; he's like a hole. So I stopped looking for him." Her eyes were moving, fast, side to side, like she was reading. "I started looking for where he isn't. Everybody in Forks. Everybody's tomorrow. If he's going to be somewhere, there'll be a gap in somebody's future, the shape of him. I've been going through them one at a time." She stopped. "I found one."

"Where?" Carlisle said.

Alice turned and looked straight at Bella, and Leah saw Bella's face change before Alice said anything.

"The diner," Alice said. "Tomorrow morning. Five past ten. Charlie's there."

Bella didn't move.

"He's in the back booth," Alice said. "He's sitting facing the door. There's nobody across from him. There's an empty seat across from him and he's talking to it. Charlie's talking to it, and he's listening, and he laughs once, at something it says. Dot's pouring coffee into a cup on the other side of the table, and there's no one holding the cup. There's a plate there. Cherry pie." Alice's voice went thin. "There's a hole in the booth, Bella. It's sitting across from your dad."

The arm of the couch broke under Bella's hand. It just went, with a crack like a branch, and stuffing came out between her fingers.

Edward had her other hand. "Bella. Bella, look at me."

"He's having breakfast with my dad," Bella said. Her voice was perfectly level. "He's been having breakfast with my dad."

"I'll go," Jacob said.

"No," said Edward and Rosalie at the same time.

"Why not? He can't—he's human, he can't hurt me, I'm—"

"You saw what he did to the nomad," Jasper said.

"I'm not a nomad."

"You're Renesmee's," said Rosalie, flatly, "and you don't leave her. Not for this. Not for some stranger who can hear what you're thinking about her and wants us to know it."

"Then Edward—"

"He'll be listening for me," Edward said. "He'll hear me coming before I hear him. I'd be walking in with my thoughts open, and I can't close them. I've never been able to."

"I'll go," Seth said from the stairs.

"No," said Leah.

She hadn't known she was going to say it until she'd said it. She heard it come out and she felt the whole room look at her again, and she let them. She folded the towel Esme had given her, carefully, in half and in half again, and put it on the arm of the chair.

"Not Seth," she said. "He likes him."

"I don't *like* him—" Seth started.

"You wagged your tail, Seth."

Seth shut his mouth.

"Not you," she said to Jacob. "Rosalie's right. You stay with the kid. Not any of you, because he'll hear you coming and Alice can't see what happens. Alice can't see me either, so it doesn't matter who goes, as far as that's concerned." She looked at Jacob. "So it's me. I go in on two legs. I sit at the counter. I drink a cup of coffee. I watch him with the chief. That's all."

"He can read you," said Jacob.

"He can read me on four legs. I don't know about two." She shrugged. "Guess I'll find out."

"Leah—"

"He called me *Lassie*, Jake." She looked at him, and she let him see that she wasn't going to move on this. She didn't know if it was the pie or the badge or the way he'd stood there with his heart going sixty-two and told her she'd make it about halfway. Or the other thing. The sorry. She didn't know what to do with the sorry, and she wanted to look at his face when he didn't have sunglasses on and she didn't have fur, and see if he still meant it. "I want to see his face when I'm standing on two feet."

Nobody said anything.

Jacob looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at the baby, asleep now on Rosalie's shoulder with her fist in Rosalie's hair, and at Bella, holding very still with stuffing in her hand, and at Edward.

"Ten o'clock," he said.

"Five past," said Alice.

Leah nodded. She went out onto the porch and stood there, in the gray noon, looking at the trees. The rain had nearly stopped. Somewhere out there, in eleven days' worth of dark woods, there was a man in a black suit with a vampire under his arm, sitting in front of a phone he wasn't answering.

She'd buy her own pie.
