# Containment

## Chapter One: By Order

*The clearing north of Forks, Washington — January 2007*

---

## I. Edward

Caius wanted the wolves first.

He had wanted it since the Volturi came out of the trees, and in the last twenty minutes the wanting had firmed up into a plan, a neat private thing he kept turning over like a coin in his pocket. The big black one at the end of the line went first, because he was plainly the leader of the larger group. Then the russet one in the middle, the one with the child riding on his back, because killing him would put the child on the ground where Felix could reach her. Caius had watched wolves die before. He remembered the smell of it from a valley in the Carpathians in a century nobody living had a number for. The fur scorching. The high, thin sound they made at the end. He was looking forward to hearing it again.

Edward listened and kept his face still.

He stood at the front of the line with Bella on his left and Carlisle a stride ahead of them both. The snow lay over the clearing like a sheet pulled tight over a bed. It had stuck in the night. Alice had said it would, weeks ago, sitting at the kitchen table with her eyes gone empty and her fingers locked in Jasper's sleeve. *When the snow sticks.* It had stuck. There was a hard crust on it now that would have held a fox, and under the crust the dead brown grass of last summer, and over everything a low white sky that sat on the tops of the trees and gave no light at all.

A hundred yards off, across all that white, the Volturi stood in their wedge.

He knew every one of them by now. He'd been inside each of their heads for the better part of an hour. Felix, enormous and bored, thinking about a woman in Siena. Demetri, whose mind ran on a low steady hum of tracking, touching the thread of every mind in the field one after another, the way a man runs his fingers along the spines of books on a shelf. Heidi, who had come because she was told to. Chelsea, near the back with her husband's hand in hers, feeling for the ties between the Cullens and their witnesses and finding them, to her irritation, too thick to cut. Renata, pressed against Aro's back with one palm flat between his shoulder blades, her small mind narrowed to a single repeated thought: *keep away keep away keep away.* It was all she had ever needed to think.

Alec, patient as frost.

Jane.

Jane had been trying to hurt Bella for forty minutes, and failing, and every failure went into her like a nail. She stood two paces out from Aro's left shoulder with her hood down and her pale hair pinned tight to her skull, and her face was a doll's face, round and sweet and empty, and inside it she was screaming. She had never in her existence met a mind she couldn't set on fire. She had met one now. She was looking at it. She could not stop looking at it.

*What are you,* Jane thought at Bella, over and over. *What are you what are you what are you.*

Bella didn't hear it. Bella couldn't hear anything; that was the point of Bella. But she could feel Jane pressing on the shield, and Edward could feel her feeling it, a faint shiver at the edge of her that he knew the way he knew the sound of her walking down the stairs. She stood very straight. Her eyes, still that new and violent red, were fixed on Jane's face, and her jaw was set, and the whole of her was holding.

She had been holding for forty minutes. She would hold for four hundred if she had to.

Ten yards in front of the Volturi line, a smear of gray-white ash steamed in the snow.

Irina.

Caius had asked her if she still accused the Cullens. She'd said no. She'd been wrong; the child was not immortal; she had seen a little girl on a mountainside and made a terrible mistake. Caius had nodded, very gently. Then Felix and Demetri had taken her by the arms and there had been a sound like a car going into a wall, and a burst of thick purple smoke that smelled like spilled perfume and burning hair, and that was the end of Irina of Denali. Edward had heard her last thought. It had been her sister's name.

Tanya was on her knees in the snow behind Carlisle now. Kate had been held back by Garrett and Emmett both, and it had taken both of them, and there were two black burns on the sleeves of Emmett's coat where her hands had been. Nobody had let go of her since. Garrett stood at her shoulder with his long sandy hair tied back in its leather cord, one hand wrapped around her wrist, and his mind was a pot on the boil. He had fought a war against a king once, in a cold green country across an ocean. He was thinking about it now. He was thinking that some lessons had to be taught twice.

"Brothers," Aro said, in his soft, delighted voice. "Let us confer."

The three of them turned toward one another, slowly, like three old men turning at a graveside. Aro's hands came up and steepled under his chin. His skin was so thin and pale it looked like the skin on boiled milk, and his eyes had the faint white film the very old ones got, as though a cataract had started and changed its mind.

Edward went into him.

Aro's mind was a gallery. It always had been. Long cool rooms full of things he had collected, gifts and memories and faces, all of them lit and labeled and kept. He was walking through those rooms now with his hands behind his back, considering. The empty places on the walls. The places where he would hang what he took today. Alice, first and most of all; he wanted Alice so badly it had a flavor, like wanting water. Edward. Bella, now, more than Edward—*a shield, a shield like that, think of it, Renata is a wall and this child is the sky.* The Egyptian boy who moved the earth. The Amazon who made you see what she wished. Aro stood in his gallery and looked at the empty walls and did a sum, and the sum was a war.

He had decided before they left Volterra. He had decided in Volterra the moment Irina's story reached him. The rest of this morning had been for the witnesses. A trial. A vote. A decision reached in public by reasonable men. *We gave them every chance*, the witnesses would say afterward, in the bars and the dark places where vampires gathered. *We saw it. They were given every chance.*

Marcus was easier. There was almost nothing there. Marcus saw relationships—he had always seen them, the threads between people, their color and thickness and age—and he looked out across the snow now and saw the clearing laced through with them, gold between Edward and Bella, gold between Bella and the child, a thick hot copper cord running from the child to the russet wolf she rode on, and he felt nothing about any of it. He had not felt anything in a very long time. His vote would be whatever Aro wanted his vote to be. He knew that. He did not care that he knew it.

"Minutes," Edward said, very low, so that only Bella and Carlisle would hear it. "Aro wants the vote to look honest. Caius has his order of battle ready. When it goes, it'll go at the wolves."

Carlisle's shoulders didn't move. "Alice?"

"Nothing."

Bella said nothing. He felt her shield tighten, a long elastic pull from end to end of the line.

Then the wolves changed.

He heard it before he saw it. The pack minds were always there at the edge of the noise, two separate rivers of them—Sam's larger group on the right flank, Jacob's on the left—each one braided out of a dozen voices that ran together the way voices run together in a crowded kitchen. Sam's pack was all hunger and readiness and fear held flat. Jacob's was smaller and louder. Seth chattering anxiously to himself about the formation. Jacob underneath everything, steady as a pulse, thinking *south, south, if it goes I'm gone, I run south and I don't stop.* Leah at the far end of the left flank with her hackles up and her thoughts cold and clean and sharp as a filleting knife, ticking off the Volturi one by one: *the tracker, the big one, the twins, the old bastards.*

All of it stopped. Both rivers at once. Every wolf in the clearing lifted its head and turned it to the north.

*Who the hell is that?*

*That's not one of ours. Jake, that's not—*

*Sam—*

*Smells like—*

The wind had come around. It carried the scent of the wolves to Edward every few minutes when it gusted out of the east, rank and wet and hot. Now it brought something else down off the hills at the north end of the field. He caught it a full half-second after the wolves did. Pine. Wet earth. Stone—cold, clean, deep stone, like the inside of a cave. And riding under all of that, very faint, a second smell he knew, because he'd been living alongside it for two years: wolf. Wolf, but wrong. Wolf the way a lion is a cat.

Across the field, Demetri's mind snapped toward the north like a dog on a lead. He reached for whatever it was with the long patient fingers of his gift, and Edward felt him find it and slide off it, find it and slide off it, the way a hand slides off a pane of ice.

*Nothing,* Demetri thought, puzzled, and then, with the first prick of real alarm Edward had heard in him all morning: *I can't hold it.*

Then the engine.

It came from above them. That was what made the whole clearing go still. It was the throaty, ragged, unmistakable bellow of a big old motorcycle opening up, the kind of sound that had no business anywhere in the sky over a snowfield in the Olympic Peninsula, and it came down out of the white cloud ceiling directly overhead and grew. Every head on both sides of the clearing went back. A hundred-odd vampires and nearly twenty wolves, all of them staring straight up into a sky that was full of nothing but the sound of an engine being thrashed by somebody who did not care about the engine.

In Aro's gallery, very far back, a door opened.

Edward saw it the instant Aro did. A memory, old, kept carefully in a room of its own. A man in a dark blue robe in a courtyard in Florence, with rain falling on the stones. Aro's own hand closing over the man's wrist. The taste of what had come up through that touch, which Aro had remembered for four hundred years the way some people remember the first time they drank wine—a whole world, hidden inside the human one like a seam of silver in a rock. Wands. Spells. A government. Laws. *Wizard*, Aro thought, and the word had a shine on it.

The motorcycle came out of the cloud.

It was black and old and very large, with a sidecar bolted on its right side and snow streaming off its fenders, and it came down through the air at an angle like a hawk coming down on a field mouse. It wasn't touching anything. There was nothing under it at all. It dropped through the last fifty feet in a long shallow glide, the engine dropping from a roar to a growl, and its rear wheel hit the snow dead center between the two lines with a crunch and a spray of white, and it slewed sideways and skidded thirty feet with the back end swinging, carving a long gray curve in the crust, and stopped.

The engine idled. It sounded like somebody clearing their throat.

The rider killed it.

In the silence that came after, Edward heard every mind in the clearing try to say something at once and fail.

The man swung his leg over the seat and stood up. He was tall—six-two, Edward guessed, a little more—and heavy through the shoulders and the chest, built like somebody who worked hard with his body for a living and ate well afterward. Black hair that didn't look like it had been introduced to a comb. A brown leather jacket, scuffed pale at the elbows and the seams, over a gray shirt. Dark jeans gone soft with wear. Heavy boots. Mid-twenties. He had on a pair of dark glasses even though there was no sun at all, and he pulled them off now, folded them one-handed, and hooked them into the neck of his shirt.

His eyes were green. Very green, very clear, and they went first, without hurrying, to the Volturi.

Edward reached for him.

There was nothing. A smooth, cool, total absence, like putting his hand out in a dark room for a wall and finding the wall too far away to touch. He knew silence. He'd lived next to Bella's silence for two years. This was a different kind. Bella's had always been a curtain she didn't know she'd drawn. This was a door that somebody had shut on purpose and then stood behind with his shoulder against it.

He could hear the man's heart, at least. Slow. Steady. Sixty beats a minute, maybe less, which was absurd for any human who had just ridden a motorcycle out of the sky into the middle of this. And he could feel the heat coming off him from fifty yards away. The snow under the man's boots was already sagging, going gray and wet. He ran hot. He ran as hot as the wolves.

The green eyes came off the Volturi and moved across the clearing. Over Carlisle. Over Edward, and over Bella, and Edward felt them stop on Bella for a moment and move on. Along the line of the witnesses. Down the left flank, over the wolves, one by one, the way a man reads down a list.

They got to Leah Clearwater, and Edward heard it happen.

It sounded like ice breaking under a skater. A single high crack in the middle of the pack mind, and then a silence where Leah had been—a hole, a gap, as if a radio station had gone off the air—and then a flood. Everything Leah was, all at once, all of it pulled loose from wherever it had been fixed for twenty years and swinging around on its moorings to point, like a compass needle, at the man in the leather jacket.

*Oh,* Leah thought. Just that. And then, in a voice Edward had never heard come out of her, small and appalled: *Oh, no.*

The rest of Jacob's pack hit the wall of it a heartbeat later.

*Leah? What—Leah, what the—*

*Oh my God. Oh my God, Jake, she—*

*No. No way. No. No.*

*LEAH.* Jacob, a roar inside the roar. *Not now. Are you kidding me? Not NOW—*

*Shut up,* Leah thought at them, and there was nothing cold in it now, nothing clean, only a raw and bleeding desperation. *Shut up, shut up, get out, get out of my head, all of you, GET OUT—*

On the right flank, Sam's pack couldn't hear any of it. But they could see Jacob's pack, and Jacob's pack had come apart. Seth had taken two steps toward his sister and stopped. Jacob's head was swinging between Leah and the man and the Volturi, back and forth, his lips peeled off his teeth. Leah herself stood out at the end of the line with all four legs braced wide in the snow like a newborn foal's, shaking from the ears to the tail. Her eyes hadn't left the man's face. They could not.

Edward had been inside Jacob's head on the night Renesmee was born. He knew exactly what he was hearing. He had a bizarre, airless urge to laugh.

The man in the snow didn't seem to notice. His eyes had finished their list, and they went back to the front of the field, to the three ancients, and he cracked his neck, left and then right. The sound of it carried across the clearing.

"Morning," he said.

The accent was English. Southern, a little London in it, a little something rougher underneath that had been sanded down but not all the way. He had a voice like gravel under a tire.

"Sorry I'm late. Traffic."

Nobody spoke.

He reached into the inside of his jacket. Across the snow, the Volturi guard rippled, every gray shoulder shifting a quarter inch, and Felix's mind lit up with pure and simple pleasure. The man took out a leather wallet, the kind a policeman carries, and flipped it open with his thumb and held it up toward the Volturi line, and something lifted off the leather.

It was a seal. A disc of silver light the size of a dinner plate, turning slowly in the air above his hand. There was a device on it, a globe crossed by two wands, and writing that ran around its edge in a script Edward didn't know, and as he looked at it the writing rearranged itself into English and he read it without wanting to. INTERNATIONAL CONFEDERATION OF WIZARDS. And below that, smaller, a name.

"Harry Potter," the man said. "Department of Mysteries. Unspeakable, on secondment to the Confederation." He let the seal hang there a second longer, then closed the wallet, and the light went out. "You lot are in breach."

In Aro's head, the shine on the word *wizard* became a light so bright it whited out the gallery.

"Of the International Statute of Secrecy," Harry Potter went on. "Which, if you're not familiar, is the thing that keeps people like me and people like you out of the six o'clock news. There's a clause in it, seventy-three, if anyone's taking notes. Covers beings. That's you." He glanced along the gray line. "Probably. Some of you are pushing it." He put the wallet away. "Hundred-odd vampires and a pack of wolves the size of carthorses, stood in a field six miles from a town with a primary school in it. Witnesses. Formations. That one's had someone set on fire already this morning." A tip of his head toward the gray-white smear in the snow. "That's a breach. That's a breach the size of a bloody football pitch. So."

He put his hands in his jacket pockets.

"By order of the International Confederation of Wizards, this gathering is over. You'll disperse. All of you. Now."

Ninety minds hit Edward at once.

*Wizard*—the witnesses at the back, the young ones, scattered, frightened, half of them never having heard the word used except as a joke. *Wizards are real? There are—that's—* The older ones in the crowd, the few dozen who were more than a few centuries old, went quiet in a different way. Some of them remembered. *I thought they were gone. I thought they'd hidden so deep—* Over on the Cullen side, Eleazar's mind went very still and very careful. He had been Volturi once. He had served in that court for centuries. He had heard Aro talk about Florence over a glass of something red, and he knew, as most vampires did not, that there was a line drawn somewhere in the dark between their world and another one, and that the Volturi had always been careful to stay on their side of it.

And Eleazar's gift was already reaching for the man, the way it reached for everything. Eleazar felt gifts—the shape and weight of them, how they sat in a person. He reached for Harry Potter and found something there and drew his hand back from it so fast it was almost a flinch. Edward caught the image in his head, half-formed. Like reaching into a pond to touch a fish and finding the whole pond was the fish.

"*Oh*," said Aro aloud, very softly. "Oh, *how* delightful."

He came forward. Three steps, four, out ahead of the wedge, with Renata gliding along behind him like his shadow with her palm still pressed to his back. He was smiling. It was a smile Edward had seen before, in Volterra, in the tower room with the round windows, the day Aro had first taken Edward's hand.

"Forgive me," Aro said. "Forgive me, I am an old man and I so seldom have surprises. A wizard! A true wizard, here, in this cold place." He spread his hands. "Do you know it has been four hundred years since I last had the pleasure? Four hundred years. A gentleman of your kind in Florence—a scholar, a *brilliant* mind—and I have thought of him often. I am Aro." A small, pretty bow. "These are my brothers, Caius and Marcus. And you are welcome among us, Harry Potter. Truly welcome." His hand came out, palm up, pale as candle wax. "Come. Let us speak as friends."

Harry looked at the hand.

"Yeah, no," he said. "I'm all right, ta. I've read your file. I know what that hand does."

The smile didn't move. Behind it, Edward felt something in Aro go cool and alert.

"I'll be honest, mate, I've read all your files," Harry said. "Took me most of the flight. Volterra. The tourist trade. The redhead who goes fishing." His eyes went to Heidi and away again, and Heidi's mind gave a small cold lurch. "The whole thing where you turn up on somebody's doorstep with a crime in one hand and a job application in the other. That one's my favourite. Very slick." He sniffed. "Very *Godfather*."

"You misunderstand," Aro said gently. "We are the law of our kind. We have kept our world hidden from yours, and both from the humans, for longer than your Confederation has had a name. We police our own. We always have."

"Is that what this is? Policing?" Harry glanced around the clearing, at the witnesses packed in the trees, at the wolves, at the ash. "Looks more like Glastonbury from where I'm stood."

"This is a matter between vampires."

"This *was* a matter between vampires. Then you started holding it in a field." He shrugged. "Not my rules. MACUSA's been watching you lot set up camp in their back garden for a fortnight, and they don't like it, and they asked Geneva for help, and Geneva sent me. So I'm helping."

Caius spoke for the first time.

"The Confederation," he said, "has no writ over us."

His voice was like a door creaking in an empty house. In his head there was another memory, rawer than Aro's and much more recent—not a courtyard in Florence but a road outside Prague in the dark, a man in a long coat with a stick of wood in his hand shouting words in a dead language, and Caius taking the stick out of his hand and the hand off his arm. He had enjoyed that. He had enjoyed it more than anything else that year.

"Your Statute is a compact among wizards," Caius said. "We were never party to it. We are not your beasts, to be counted and kept. We were old when your Ministries were mud huts by a river."

"Yeah, I've heard that one," Harry said. "Usually from goblins. Don't care for it from them either."

"You are one man."

"I am, yeah."

"Alone."

"Yep."

"In front of the Volturi."

Harry looked at him for a moment with his head a little on one side. "Is this going somewhere," he said, "or do you just like the sound of yourself? Because I've got a bike I need to get back before the bloke I borrowed it from notices, and he's been dead ten years, so you can imagine he's not in a rush, but I am."

Jane hit him.

Edward saw it start in her head. All the rage of forty failed minutes poured into a single point and driven out across the snow at the man in the jacket like a spike. It was the worst thing Edward had ever seen her do. He'd seen her bring men to their knees in the tower room; he'd felt her do it to him; this was more than that. She put everything she had into it and drove it home and watched his face and waited for him to fall.

Harry blinked.

He looked at her—a small, pale, fierce girl in a near-black cloak, with her hands balled into fists at her sides and her red eyes huge in her face—and his eyebrows went up.

"Was that meant to do something, Wednesday?"

Jane's mind went white.

"Because I've had the Cruciatus off a bloke with no nose," Harry said, "and he meant it a lot more than you do. You want to put a bit of back into it."

She hit him again. Edward felt it go out of her, and felt it reach the place where the man stood, and then he felt nothing at all. It went into Harry Potter the way a stone goes into a lake.

Beside her, Alec's mist had started. Edward could see it in Alec's head before he could see it on the snow—a slow, colorless creep, hugging the crust, feeling its way forward like water finding a slope, the gift that took sight and hearing and touch and left a man standing in the dark without even knowing where his own hands were. It had spent the morning lapping uselessly at the edge of Bella's shield. Now it turned and crawled toward the man in the middle of the field.

"And you can put that away an' all, Damien," Harry said, without looking at it.

Alec's mind hitched.

The mist kept coming.

Aro laughed. It was a lovely laugh, light and fond and amused, like a grandfather watching a child stamp its foot. "Oh, I *like* you," he said. "I find I like you very much, Mr. Potter. Very well. I will be plain, as you are plain." His hand was still out, palm up. "You will come and stand with us. You will give me your hand, and I will see what you are. And then we shall finish our business here, and you shall go and tell your Confederation it was handled. That is the kindest thing I can offer you. I do not think you will be offered a kinder one today."

Caius didn't wait for an answer. In his head, the order had already been given, and the wolves had gone to the back of the queue. *Felix. Demetri. Take him alive if you can. Take his arms off if you can't.*

Felix's mind lit up like a lamp.

"No?" said Harry.

"No," Aro agreed, smiling.

"Fair enough," Harry said, and brought up his right hand, and snapped his fingers.

There was a sound. Edward heard it afterward, again and again, for days—a deep soft *whump*, like a gas burner catching, but a gas burner the size of a house. For a quarter of a second, all thirty-four minds in the Volturi wedge cried out at once, a single white flare of noise in his head so loud and so total it was like staring into the sun. Felix's pleasure, cut off. Demetri's alarm. Heidi, Santiago, Corin. Chelsea, reaching for her husband's hand and finding fire where it had been. Renata's *keep away keep away* running straight on into nothing. Jane, still screaming *what are you*. Alec. Caius, a red bloom of fury and disbelief, *no, no, NO—* The two black cloaks at the very back, the wives, Sulpicia and Athenodora, who had never in Edward's hearing thought of anything all morning except the long cool rooms of the tower and the comfort of being shut inside them. Aro—

Aro's last thought was *Oh.* Surprise, and under the surprise a collector's grief, very pure. *I never got to—*

And Marcus.

Edward would remember Marcus longest of all. In the quarter of a second it took, the gray empty fog in Marcus's mind parted, and there was a woman's face in it. Dark hair. A small, warm, crooked smile. Edward had seen that face once before, in Aro's gallery, in a room Aro never went into. *Didyme.* And what went through Marcus as the fire went through him, plain and enormous and the first thing he had felt in a thousand years, was relief.

Then there was nothing at all.

The Volturi went up in thirty-four columns of white fire, every one of them at the same instant, every one of them from the inside out. Edward saw it with his eyes a heartbeat after he heard it with his mind. Every pillar was whole from the first instant, head to heel. The fire came out of them, through the skin, through the eyes and the open mouths, so hot and so white it had no color at all, thirty-four pillars standing in a wedge on the snow a hundred yards off, and the heat of it hit Edward in the face across all that distance like the opening of an oven door. The snow under the wedge went to water and then to steam in a single explosive hiss. A wall of thick purple smoke rolled up and out and over, choking-sweet, a stink of incense and scorched sugar and something that was very like burning hair.

It lasted one second.

Then the columns weren't there. There was a long, low bank of drifting gray in the air, sinking. There were thirty-four black scorched circles in a wedge-shaped crater of mud and steam where the snow had been. There was a single silver chain, the kind the elders wore, lying in the mud in the middle of it, glowing a dull cherry red and cooling as Edward watched.

And there was silence. The silence where thirty-four voices had been. Edward had never heard anything so loud in his life.

The ash began to come down.

It came down over everything, slow and soft and gray, falling with the snow, onto the shoulders of the witnesses packed at the treeline and onto the wolves and onto Edward's own hands. It was very fine. It was finer than wood ash. It lay on the backs of his hands like powdered sugar and it smelled very faintly, still, of incense.

In the middle of the clearing, Harry Potter put his hand back in his jacket pocket.

"Right," he said. "Anyone else?"

## II. Leah

The ash was landing on her.

That was the stupid thing Leah Clearwater's brain kept coming back to, while the rest of it ran around in circles screaming. It was settling on her back and her shoulders and the top of her head, fine as flour, and it was in her fur now and it would be in her fur for days, and she was going to have to phase back in the woods tonight with dead vampire in her hair. In her *hair.* The smell of it was in the back of her throat like cough syrup, sweet and thick and so wrong her whole body wanted to sneeze it out, and she couldn't, because she couldn't look away from him long enough to sneeze.

Thirty-four of them. She'd counted when they came out of the trees. Jacob had counted, and Seth, all of them, it was the kind of thing you did. Thirty-four cloaks. Now there were thirty-four burnt patches in the mud over there, steaming, and a whole lot of gray stuff in the air, and that was it.

He'd snapped his fingers.

Inside her head, the pack was still going.

*—holy crap, holy crap, did you see—*

*—he just—with his* fingers*, Jake, he just went* snap *and—*

*—Leah, are you okay? Leah? Talk to me. Leah, come on—*

*Everybody shut up.* Jacob. He was trying to sound like an Alpha and he mostly managed it, but underneath it he was as rattled as the rest of them; she could feel his heart going like a drum against her own. *Hold the line. Nobody moves. Seth, eyes on the witnesses. Leah—*

He stopped.

He didn't know what to say to her. She could feel him not knowing. She could feel all of them not knowing, all of them looking at the inside of her head the way you'd look at a house after a tornado's been through, with the roof gone and the furniture out in the yard and somebody's shoes up a tree.

She knew what it was. Of course she knew what it was.

She'd seen it happen to Sam, from inside his head, in the weeks after he first phased back to her and couldn't look her in the eye. She'd seen it a hundred times since, every time he thought about it, every time he thought about Emily. The way he'd been walking up the dirt track to the Youngs' place with his hands in his pockets on a nothing afternoon, thinking about nothing, and Emily had come out on the porch to shake out a rug. And Sam had looked up. And the whole world had come unbolted from the floor and swung around on its chain and come to rest, facing her. Leah's cousin. Leah's *cousin.* She'd been inside Jared's head for it too, and Paul's, and Quil's over a two-year-old in a pink coat, and Jacob's on the floor of the Cullens' living room over a baby with blood on her chin. Every single time it was the same. The ground went. The strings all got cut. Everything you were got gathered up and tied off to one person, and that was it, that was you, for the rest of your life.

She'd hated it. She'd hated it so much she'd built herself out of hating it. Two years of hating it. Two years of being the bitter one, the angry one, the girl who got left, the only she-wolf in the history of the tribe and probably a dead end in her own body—she hadn't had a period since the first time she phased, and she'd figured out what that meant all by herself in her mom's bathroom at three in the morning, and she'd never told a soul, and of course the whole pack knew because the whole pack knew everything. Leah the genetic dead end. Leah who'd never imprint, because what was the point. That was the theory, wasn't it? Imprinting was about the gene. Imprinting found you the one who'd give you the strongest kids. And Leah's body had looked at that whole idea and shut up shop.

She'd made her peace with it. She'd been almost glad.

Then he'd pulled his sunglasses off.

He'd looked down the line at them like a man reading a menu, and his eyes had got to her, and they were green—a stupid, ridiculous, bright green, like a bottle with the sun behind it—and everything Leah Clearwater was had come off its hinges and fallen through the floor.

*Harry Potter*, he'd said, holding up his little floating badge.

Harry.

Her dad's name had been Harry.

He'd been sitting in the kitchen when she phased the first time. She'd been screaming at Seth about something, about Sam, about everything, and she'd felt it come up through her like a fever and she'd gone, right there on the linoleum, and the table had gone over and her dad had stood up so fast his chair hit the wall. She remembered his face. That was all. His face, and then the sound he made, and then he was on the floor holding his chest, and she'd been standing over him on four legs with no way to pick up a phone. They said it was his heart. They said it would've happened anyway. She knew better.

And this guy had ridden out of the sky with her dad's name in his mouth and set thirty-four vampires on fire, and some broken animal part of her had decided, just like that, without asking, that he was the only thing on this planet that mattered.

*No,* she thought at herself. *No. I won't. I'm not doing this.*

Her body didn't care what she thought. Her body stood there in the snow, shaking, and stared at him.

*Leah,* Seth whispered. *Leah, I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.*

She bared her teeth at nothing. *Don't.*

In the middle of the field, Harry Potter turned around.

He did it slowly. He had his hands in his jacket pockets again and he didn't take them out. He looked past the Cullen line to the crowd at the far end of the field—the Volturi's witnesses, the forty or so vampires Aro had dragged along to watch, all of them packed back against the trees with their red eyes huge and their faces like plaster. The wind blew the ash in their direction and a couple of them flinched as it touched them.

"You lot," Harry said. "At the back."

Every one of them went still.

"You're not Volturi. I know that. I didn't do you." He said it the way you'd tell somebody they'd dropped a glove. "You were invited to a show. Show's over. Go home." He paused. "And if I hear a word of this from one of you—if I'm in a bar in Bucharest a year from now and I hear somebody telling it, with the fingers and everything—I'll know which one of you it came from. I'll find you. You won't like it. Go on. Piss off."

They went.

Leah had never seen vampires run scared before. She'd seen them run at things, and away from the pack, and she'd seen the newborns run in the war last spring in a wild screaming tide. This was different. This was forty grown vampires turning around in a mass and *bolting*, crashing through the undergrowth, snapping branches, a couple of them knocking each other flat in their hurry to get into the trees. A woman in a red coat tripped and went down and somebody stepped on her and kept going. A big man in a business suit ran straight through a young fir tree and didn't seem to notice. In five seconds there was nobody at the north end of the field but the snow and the ash and the black scorched patches. She could hear them running for a long time after she couldn't see them. Running south, west, every direction there was, as fast as they could go.

"Lovely," said Harry, to nobody.

Someone on the Cullen side started laughing.

It was one of the Romanians. The two of them had been standing a little apart from everybody else all morning, the dusty ones with the papery skin that looked like it would tear if you poked it, and they'd had a look on their faces since the Volturi showed up like two guys at a boxing match who've put their house on the fight. Now the shorter one had his head tipped back and he was laughing up into the falling ash, a high, cracked, wheezing laugh, like it hurt his chest.

The other one walked out into the snow. He stopped about thirty feet from Harry, and he looked over Harry's shoulder at the black smoking patches in the mud for a long moment, and then he looked at Harry, and he bowed. All the way. Like something out of a movie. Like a duke.

"Fifteen hundred years," he said. His voice was thin and dry. "We have waited fifteen hundred years to see that. I thank you, wizard. I do not know your name for long. But I will remember it."

"Yeah, cheers," Harry said. "Now you can piss off and all."

The Romanian straightened up. He looked at Harry, and Leah watched something go across his face that might have been him thinking about being offended. Then it went away. He smiled with his whole mouth, so his teeth showed, and turned to his friend, who'd stopped laughing.

"Stefan," he said. "Come. We are going home. We have a great deal to tell the walls."

They went off into the trees together without looking back at anybody. Stefan was still giggling to himself.

Harry watched them go. Then he took a breath, and let it out, and turned to the rest of them.

Leah felt his eyes come across the field like a light swinging across a dark yard. Carlisle at the front with his hands half raised. Esme. The big one, Emmett, and the blonde. Edward. Bella, who was standing so stiff she looked like she'd been carved. The Denalis, all three of what was left of them, Kate with Garrett's arms around her from behind now. The Irish. The Egyptians. The Amazons, tall and still with their black braids down their backs. The two who belonged to Jasper. The wolves. All of it. He looked at all of it.

When his eyes got to her, they stopped. Just for a second. It was like being touched.

Then they moved on, and everything in her went after them like a dog on a leash.

"Right," Harry said. "Now you lot."

"Mr. Potter—" Carlisle began.

"I said everyone," Harry said. "Disperse. Means you as well. I'm not doing requests."

"We have no quarrel with you." Carlisle stepped forward, a slow careful step. His voice was quiet and reasonable; it was the voice he used at the hospital, Leah thought, when he had to tell somebody's mom. "None of us came here for this. We came to stand witness for my family. For a child. The Volturi—" He hesitated, looking past Harry at the black patches. "We never intended any of this to go as far as it did. You have my word. All of us will go. Most of these people are guests in my house. But this place is our home. Forks is our home. We live here."

"Then go inside it and shut the door."

"I would like," Carlisle said carefully, "to know what happens next."

"What happens next, Doc, is everyone stops standing in a field." Harry looked along the line. "And then I write a report. And then a lot of people in Geneva read the report and make faces. You'll get a letter."

"A letter."

"Probably. Could be a visit. Depends what's in the report." His eyes moved. "Which depends on what I find."

Garrett let go of Kate.

Leah saw him do it. He'd been holding her from behind with both arms wrapped round her middle, his chin over her shoulder, talking into her ear—she couldn't hear it, but she didn't need to; you could see it in the way Kate's whole body was shaking. And then his arms came away from her, and he stepped round her, and walked out into the open snow toward Harry, slow, with his head up.

"Garrett," Kate said, sharp. "*Garrett.*"

He didn't look back. He stopped twenty feet from Harry. He was a tall man, nearly as tall as Harry, lanky, with his sandy hair coming loose from its tie and a leather coat that looked older than Forks. His eyes were red. He hadn't gone over to the Cullens' animal thing, though he'd been hanging around Kate for a month like a guy who might. Leah had smelled him around the Cullens' place for weeks, and she'd heard him talk—he talked a lot—and she'd decided, grudgingly, that she didn't completely hate him.

"I fought a king once," Garrett said.

Harry looked at him.

"A long time ago. On a cold green hill in Massachusetts, with a borrowed musket, and a good many better men than me." Garrett's voice was quite calm. "He was a long way off, that king. He sent men in red coats to tell us where we could stand and what we could say and what we would pay for the privilege. We told him no. I spent this whole morning telling *them* no." He jerked his head at the black ash. "I'm glad they're gone. I'll say that. I'm glad. But you stand there, son. You stand there and you tell these people—free people, every one—you tell them *disperse*, and *go home*, and *you'll get a letter*, like a magistrate reading out the Riot Act, and I find I've heard that tune before." He smiled. It was a nice smile. "Where I'm from, we have an answer for it."

"Yeah?" said Harry. "What's that?"

"Garrett," Kate said again. "Garrett, don't you *dare*—"

He went.

He was fast. They were all fast, Leah knew that, but she'd forgotten what it actually looked like until you watched one of them come at somebody you—at somebody. He came across the twenty feet of snow in a gray blur, low and straight, his hands up and open, and there was a sound from the Cullen line like a lot of people sucking in a breath at once, and Leah moved.

She didn't decide to. She didn't think about it at all. She was on the end of the line, forty yards away, and one second she was standing there and the next her body had gone like a released spring, out across the snow, flat-out, every muscle she owned screaming one thing. *Get between them. Get between them. Get between—*

She was barely ten yards into it when Garrett caught fire.

It was the same as the others. The same deep soft *whump.* The same light with no color in it. One second there was a tall man in a leather coat in the air with his hands out, and the next there was a pillar of white fire in the shape of him, and the heat came across the snow and hit Leah in the face and singed her whiskers, and she dug all four feet in and skidded and nearly went over. And then the pillar was gone, and there was a long smear of gray ash drifting down onto the snow in the shape of the jump he'd been making, and a little brass button from his coat bounced once on the crust and rolled into a footprint and lay there. Smoking.

Garrett had been about six feet from Harry when it happened. Harry hadn't taken his hands out of his pockets. He hadn't snapped anything. He hadn't even moved his head.

Kate screamed.

It was the worst sound Leah had ever heard. It was worse than the sound her mother had made at the funeral. It went on and on and up and up until it wasn't a sound any more, just a kind of tearing, and Kate was moving, Kate was going for him, and her hands had gone blue-white and crackling with that electric thing she did, little snapping sparks jumping from her fingertips into the falling ash.

Eleazar and Tanya got her. They hit her from both sides at once, Eleazar round the waist and Tanya round the shoulders, and the shock went through them both—Leah could see it go, could see their bodies jerk and lock—and they held on anyway. All three of them went down in the snow in a heap. Kate was still screaming. Her sparks were still going. Eleazar had his face pressed against her back and his eyes screwed shut with the pain of it, and he was saying something through his teeth, over and over, and in the silence after Kate ran out of air Leah heard what it was.

"He has all of us."

Kate went still in their arms. Her sparks guttered.

"Kate. Listen to me." Eleazar's voice was cracked down the middle. "Listen. He has every one of us. I can feel it. I felt it the moment he came down. I didn't—I couldn't understand what I was feeling. I can now." He lifted his head and looked across the snow at Harry, and his face was gray. "He has a hand on every one of us. Every vampire in this field. He has had it since he came out of the sky. Do you understand? He did not need to raise his hand. He never needed to. He could do all of us. All of us. Right now. With a thought."

Nobody on the Cullen side moved.

Leah watched it go through them. It went down the line like a wind going through long grass. The Amazons, who had both come up on the balls of their feet when Garrett went, settled back down onto their heels very slowly, and the tall one, Zafrina, who could put pictures in your head, closed her eyes. The young Egyptian kid, the one who could make the ground move, had dropped into a crouch with his fingers spread on the snow and little cracks running out from under his hands; he lifted his hands off the ground one finger at a time, like a man letting go of a bomb. Emmett had a hand on Rosalie's arm. Edward had a hand on Bella's. Carlisle stood where he'd been standing with his face like stone.

Every one of them was looking at Harry. And every one of them, Leah could see it, was thinking about the inside of their own body. About the venom in it. About how, apparently, it burned.

It was the first time in her life she'd ever seen vampires afraid. It should have felt good.

Harry stood there in the falling ash and looked back at them.

"That's on him," he said. He said it quietly. "I told him. I told all of you. Disperse." He looked at Kate on the ground. Something moved in his face, quick, and then it didn't. "I'm sorry for your loss, love. Truly. But he went for me."

Kate didn't answer. She'd put her face down into the snow.

Leah was still standing where she'd stopped. Ten yards out in front of Jacob's line, alone, on open snow, between the Cullens and the guy. She hadn't moved since she'd skidded. Her legs had locked. She was close enough now to smell him properly under the sweet stink of the ash—and it was pine, and wet dirt, and stone, deep cold stone like the inside of a sea cave at low tide, and under all of it something that made every hair on her body try to stand up. Wolf. Wolf, the way she was wolf. Big. Old.

And she'd run at Garrett. She'd run at *Garrett*, to stop him getting to this guy, when this guy could've—when this guy obviously hadn't needed her for anything. She'd run to protect him. In front of everyone. In front of Sam.

She could feel the whole pack staring at the back of her head.

*Leah,* Jacob said. Very quietly. *What are you doing.*

She didn't know. She didn't know. She wanted to throw up.

Harry glanced at her.

It was barely a glance. A flick of his eyes, down and across, to the small gray wolf standing alone in the snow ten yards in front of the others with her legs braced and her ears flat and her breath coming in hard white puffs. One eyebrow went up a fraction. Like he'd noticed a dog in a car park doing something odd, and filed it, and would get round to wondering about it later.

Then his head turned.

It turned past her, past the Cullens, and found the russet wolf in the middle of Jacob's line, and the little girl sitting up on his back in her white coat with her hands knotted in his fur—and all the loose, tired, careless stuff went out of his face at once, as though somebody had pulled a plug.

And then he wasn't looking at any of them at all. He was looking at Nessie.

## III. Harry

He'd been hearing her since he came down.

Hard not to. A hundred vampires in a field don't make much noise—no breath worth the name, no heartbeat, nothing but the creak of leather and the odd shuffle of a boot in the snow—and twenty-odd wolves make a lot, all of it big and slow, hearts like kettle drums. And in the middle of all that, sitting on the big red one's back like a kid on a pony at a fête, a flutter. Fast. Much too fast. A hundred and seventy a minute, maybe more, a heartbeat like a small bird's, like a wren's. He'd clocked it from the air.

He'd clocked the smell, too, once the wind came round. Blood. Real blood, live, moving. And on top of it, braided right through it, that sweet dry vampire stink. Both at once. In the same small body.

He'd filed it. He'd had other things on.

He didn't have other things on now.

On the way down out of the cloud he'd put a string on every vampire in the field. That was the job, the first thirty seconds of any job like this one: find out what's in front of you and get a hand on it. It was a Department thing, more or less. Old Croaker had spent the best part of a year in a basement teaching him the principle, which had to do with the venom in them and what it was and how it burned, and Croaker had called the finished spell a register, in that dry voice of his, like it was a list of names in a school. Harry thought of it more like a string of fairy lights. Every vampire in the clearing was a little hot point at the back of his head. He could feel all of them. He could feel the Volturi lot going out, all thirty-four at once when he'd closed his hand. He could feel the bloke in the leather coat going out, which he hadn't wanted to do and had done anyway. He could feel the forty from the back of the field getting further away, little sparks heading off in every direction like somebody had kicked a bonfire. He could feel the rest. Twenty-odd of them, stood in a line in front of him, every one now very still.

The wolves weren't on it. Wolves didn't have the venom. He'd have to do something different about wolves, if it came to wolves, and he'd rather it didn't.

The kid wasn't on it either.

She was sitting right in the middle of the vampire line on the back of a wolf, smelling like a vampire, close enough to a dozen of the lit points that he could have reached out with his hand and touched any of them, and on his register she was a blank. Nothing. A little hole in the middle of all the lights, the size and shape of a girl.

He didn't like blanks.

He'd stood in a graveyard once, at fourteen, looking at a bundle of robes in somebody's arms and wondering what was in it, and he'd still been wondering when Cedric hit the grass. He'd made himself a promise after that. He'd never said it out loud. He kept it on jobs like this one. *Know what's in front of you.*

He looked at her.

Three, maybe. Four at a push. Bronze curls coming out from under a white fur hood. A white coat zipped up to the chin. A little black backpack on her shoulders with the straps pulled tight, which somebody had done up for her that morning with a lot of care. Pink in her cheeks with the cold, which meant blood near the surface. Brown eyes. Big ones. They were fixed on his face, and they were not three years old. Nothing about the way she was looking at him was three years old.

She'd seen him burn the bloke in the coat. She'd watched him go.

All right.

He went in.

---

There was a shield in the way.

He'd felt it since he came down, the way you feel a draught in a room. It sat over the whole lot of them, Cullens and guests and wolves together, one big tight dome from end to end of the line, and it was good. The pale little blonde one had been banging on it all morning, and it hadn't so much as dented. He'd felt her do it. He'd felt the other one, the boy, with his fog, lapping at the edge of it like the tide coming in on a sea wall. Neither of them had got through. The shield just gave a bit and came back. Rubber. You could hit it with a sledgehammer for a week and it'd still be there on Monday.

He found the girl at the end of it. The newborn, red-eyed, stood up very straight in the front line next to the bronze-haired one. Her shield. It came off her like a breath.

Hitting it would only make her hold harder.

He went along it.

He went along the inside face of it, quick, the way you'd run your thumb along a seam looking for a loose stitch. Over the doctor. Over the blonde and the big one. It got thinner as it went toward the middle. Over the kid, it was thinnest of all, worn down, like the carpet in the doorway of a busy room. He thought he knew why. Something had been going back and forth through it, over and over, from the inside. Mother and daughter. Whatever it was the kid did, she'd been doing it to her mum all morning, and the mum had been letting her.

He found the thinnest place, and he put the edge of himself against it, and he cut.

There was a way of doing it. That was the thing nobody ever told you when you were fifteen and getting your skull kicked in once a week in a dungeon by a man who hated you. Snape had used Legilimency like a cosh. He came in through the front door and smashed the furniture and stood in the middle of the mess and helped himself. Riddle had been better. Riddle used it like a skinning knife, long and slow and very interested, and Harry had had him in the back of his head for seven years, and he'd learned a lot, most of it without wanting to, about how Riddle did things. After the war, Croaker had taken what was left and spent two years in that basement grinding it to an edge. He was better at it now than Riddle had ever been. Riddle had never needed to be precise. He'd never cared what he broke.

Edge-first, Croaker had said. Narrow. Fast. In and out. Leave the smallest cut you can.

You always left a cut. Croaker had been very clear about that. A shield remembers. You go through one, and it'll close behind you, and it'll heal, and for the rest of its life there'll be a line in it where you went.

The shield parted.

It opened along the edge of him in one long clean line, like a sheet of cling film under a scalpel, and he felt it flinch. Away to his right, the newborn's whole body jerked. Like he'd cut her. He supposed he had.

He went through the cut.

Then he was in the kid.

---

No words.

That was the first thing. He was used to minds with words in them, words lying over everything like a layer of carpet, a constant mutter. The kid had hardly any. She had pictures. Pictures and sounds and smells and the feel of things, the textures, the temperatures, all of it piled up in great heaps to the ceiling and all of it bright—stupidly bright, like the inside of a sweet shop—and all of it moving very fast. Much faster than any three-year-old's head had a right to move. Faster than his own.

And she'd kept all of it. That was the second thing. Every bit. Nothing faded. Nothing filed away and lost. He'd been in a lot of heads and every one of them had been like an attic, things shoved in corners and forgotten. Hers was like a library where somebody had taken every book off the shelf and opened it to the page they were on.

She was frightened. It hit him like a wave—a huge flat shove, every one of her pictures turning toward him at once, like a flock of birds coming at a window. *Out. Out out out out out.*

He didn't let it knock him back. He didn't stop to calm her down either. He hadn't got the time and it wasn't his job. He had questions, and he took them in the order Croaker had drilled into him, one at a time, and he went straight to where the answers were and took them.

*Name.*

A woman's face, very close. Young, brown-haired, white with pain. Brown eyes like the kid's own. Lying on a table on her back, soaked in something dark, saying a word like it was the only word she had left. *Renesmee.* Then the big red wolf, as a lad—tall, brown-skinned, black hair cropped short, grinning down at her in a kitchen. *Hey, Nessie.* A thousand times. Ten thousand. *Ness. Nessie. Nessie-girl.* Then the blond doctor's hand, writing on a chart taped inside a cupboard door. *Renesmee Carlie Cullen.*

Fine.

*Age.*

The same kitchen. A tape measure. The doctor holding it against a wall while she stood still in her socks, every morning, every single morning, and the doctor writing a number, and behind him the brown-haired woman's face—the mother—watching the number go up. Watching it go up too fast. The mother's face doing something careful every morning when the number came. A cake, with candles, and the woman saying *one month*. Another. Another. A big tall Christmas tree in a house made of glass, and everyone singing, and the kid in a high chair already too small for her.

Four months. A bit over. He looked at that one, and turned it over, and set it down, and went on.

*Where from.*

He went back.

Dark. Warm. Wet. Crowded, very crowded, getting more crowded all the time. Sounds through a wall—two voices, always the same two. A low one, soft, a man's, that she liked very much. And a higher one, a woman's, that she liked more than anything in the world, more than anything there was, and she wanted to be closer to it all the time and couldn't. And hunger. Thirst. A thirst so big it was the whole world for a while, a thirst like being on fire, and nothing coming to put it out—

And then something did. Something hot and thick and red, coming down to her through the warm, and the thirst going quiet, and the high voice getting stronger.

Too small. The warm was too small. She pushed. She didn't mean to. She pushed and there was a sound. A crack. A big one, right through everything, like a branch going—and the high voice screaming, screaming—

Teeth. A tearing, all around her. Cold. Light, enormous light. And a face over her, a man's, bronze-haired, wild, with blood on his mouth.

Harry got out of that one.

He got out of it fast, and he took a second he didn't have, in the middle of her head, and breathed. *Son of a bitch.*

He went on.

*Food.*

A cup with a lid and a bendy straw. Red inside. Cold, out of a plastic bag, out of a fridge in a garage. The doctor's hands. Then a forest, at night, very dark to anybody but her, and her sliding down off the wolf's back and running, and a deer, and her teeth in its neck, and the hot rush in her mouth. The wolf watching with his ears down, half disgusted and half proud. Then a plate of scrambled eggs, which she hated. Pushing the plate at the lad. The lad eating them.

*What she can do.*

He didn't need to look for that one. She showed him.

She'd stopped shoving. He felt her stop—felt all that panicky noise of birds go quiet, suddenly, in a way that put the back of his neck up. And then something came at him down the line he'd opened. Down his own line, out of her head into his. A picture.

The bloke in the leather coat. Garrett. Sitting cross-legged on the floor of a house in front of her with a cup of something in his hand, last night, or the night before. Laughing. He had a big laugh. He was singing her a song, a daft old marching song about a man with a feather in his cap, and banging time on the floorboards with his free hand, and she was giggling so hard she'd fallen over.

Then Garrett in the snow, ten minutes ago, as she'd seen it from the wolf's back. In the air. Going white.

She pushed the two of them at Harry together. One on top of the other. As hard as she could.

He felt it land.

He hadn't expected that. That was the honest truth of it. Her gift went through shields; he could see the shape of it now, with it pointing straight at him—she touched you and you saw what she'd seen, and it didn't matter what was in the way. Her mum's shield couldn't stop her. Nobody's could. She'd only ever done it skin to skin. She'd never had the chance to do it down a line somebody else had opened. She'd found it in about a second and a half, and she'd used it. On him. From inside her own head, with him standing in it, she'd reached out along his own spell and stuck a picture in his face of a man laughing and a man on fire.

He shut the line down to a sliver. Then he shut it down tighter.

The last thing she got through before he closed it was her mum's face. Just that. The brown-haired woman looking down at her, very close. Smiling. And underneath it, without words, because she still hadn't got many: *please.*

*Threat,* Harry thought, flat and level, because it was the next question and he wasn't going to skip it.

He went looking. He went quickly through the last of it. The old man with the thin skin this morning, on the snow, putting his cold hand on her cheek, and her showing him everything, everything she had, because her mum had said she could and it might help. A woman with pale blond hair on a mountainside a month ago, looking down through the trees at her hunting, with an expression like the end of the world. Her aunt, the little dark-haired one, the one who saw things, going away in the night and not coming back. A lot of strangers turning up at the glass house one by one, and her going to each of them in turn and touching their faces and showing them who she was, so they'd know. So they'd stay. They'd all stayed.

There wasn't anything under it. No hate. No hunting people. No thirst she couldn't manage. She'd never bitten a human in her life except the one, the first one, on the table, and that had been her mum, and her mum hadn't minded. She was what she looked like. A clever, frightened little girl who'd been alive for four months and remembered every second of it.

What she *was* was the bit he didn't have. She didn't know herself. He found the doctor's voice in there saying a word, *hybrid*, and the brown-haired woman crying in a bathroom with the door shut when she thought nobody could hear. He found the tape measure going up. That was it. That was all a four-month-old had on the subject of what she was. She'd been told she was special, and that she was half, and she'd seen her mum's face when the numbers came every morning.

Not enough. He'd need the mum for the rest.

He came out.

He came out the way he'd gone in, edge-first, back along the line, through the cut in the shield. He did it as clean as he could. There wasn't a gentle way to do it. You went in, you were in, and you'd been in, and that was that. She'd remember him. Everything else she'd kept, every second of four months, every song and every tape measure and every face—she'd keep this too. He'd be in there now with the rest of it. A cold bright line through everything, like a crack in a plate. The day the man came out of the sky and went through her like a blade.

Kids like her didn't get to forget.

He came back into himself, standing in the snow. His own head. His own feet, cold in his boots. The ash coming down on the backs of his hands.

Eleven seconds, start to finish. It felt like a lot more.

He took a breath.

## IV. Rosalie

Renesmee's hand closed on her fingers.

Rosalie had been holding it since the Volturi came out of the trees. She stood at Jacob's right shoulder, close enough that the heat coming off the dog cooked one side of her face, with her arm up and her hand in the child's small warm fist, and she had not let go once. Not when Irina burned. Not when the sky started roaring. Not when the wizard snapped his fingers and the whole north end of the world went white.

Now the fist clamped down on her fingers so hard the bones would have broken if she'd been human, and the world went away.

It always did, with Renesmee. You touched her and she showed you things. Rosalie had stopped being startled by it in the first week. Usually it was small—a bird she'd seen, a smell she liked, *I'm hungry*, *I love you*, *look*—and it came gently, like someone turning your chin to make you look out of a window.

This was different. This came like being thrown down a flight of stairs.

Pictures. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Faster than Rosalie could see them, faster than she could think, pouring through her in a flood, one after another after another—Bella on the table, Jacob in the kitchen, the tape measure, the Christmas tree, the cake, deer, snow, eggs—and every one of them was being *pulled.* That was the horror of it. Renesmee wasn't showing her these things. Something else was going through them. Something was in there with her, inside her, flicking through her life like a man flicking through a filing cabinet, taking out what it wanted and holding it up and dropping it and moving on, and Renesmee couldn't stop it, and Renesmee was screaming inside her own head for it to stop, and what was coming through her hand into Rosalie's was the screaming.

Rosalie saw herself. Saw her own face looking down, from below, from inside a bundle of blanket, in the first minute of Renesmee's life. Saw it held up, looked at, put down.

Then she saw the wizard. From the inside. Through the child's eyes, as the child saw him. A man made of white fire.

It went on for eleven seconds. Rosalie counted every one.

Beside her, Bella had made a sound.

It was a small sound and Rosalie had heard it under the roar in her head anyway: a gasp, short and sharp, the sound somebody makes when a kitchen knife slips. Bella had jerked as though she'd been hit. Her hands had come up off her sides. Her face was turned toward the child, but her eyes were wide and blind and fixed on nothing, and Rosalie knew, without knowing how she knew it, that whatever had come through the shield had come through *Bella* to get there. Had cut her open on the way.

Edward was already moving. Rosalie saw him go—a blur at the edge of her sight, bronze and black, straight at the man in the snow.

Carlisle caught him. Carlisle, who never held anybody, had both his hands in Edward's coat and his whole weight dug in, and his voice came out of him low and very fast. "*No.* Edward. Think. *Think.* Eleazar—"

Edward stopped. His face was the face he'd worn for the three days Bella burned.

Emmett's hand was on her arm. She hadn't felt it arrive. It was squeezing, hard, the way it squeezed when he thought she was about to do something.

She was about to do something.

Then it stopped.

The pictures stopped. All at once, like a tap turned off. Renesmee's hand went loose in hers, and Rosalie was back in the clearing, in the snow and the falling ash, with the dog's heat on one side of her face, and the child on the dog's back made a noise Rosalie had never heard her make.

She was crying.

Renesmee never cried. She had cried once in her life, Rosalie thought—in the first minute of it, on the table, a thin wet furious sound before Rosalie had wrapped her in a towel—and never since. She'd never needed to. She could show you what she wanted. Now she was crying like a baby, a real baby, a high broken frightened hiccupping wail with her mouth open, her face crumpled, her hands pressed to the sides of her head as though she was trying to hold something in. There was blood on her lip. A thin bright line of it from her left nostril down over her mouth, very red against the white fur of her hood.

The smell of it went up into the cold air. Every vampire in the line turned toward it. Nobody moved.

Rosalie reached up and pulled the child down off the dog's back and into her arms, and the dog let her. She held Renesmee against her chest with one hand cupping the back of her head. Renesmee's arms went round her neck and clamped. She was shaking. She was hot as a little stove and she was shaking all over, and she was crying into Rosalie's collar, and her hands kept going to her own head, over and over, as though she'd lost something in there.

Then her hand touched Rosalie's cheek, and Rosalie saw, very small and very sharp, one image. A man's face. Green eyes.

*He was inside,* Renesmee told her, without words. *He was inside me.*

"I know, baby," Rosalie said into her hair. "I know. I've got you. I've got you."

She looked at the wizard over the top of the child's head.

He was standing where he'd been standing. Thirty feet out. He'd closed his eyes. He had his head tipped back a little, toward the white sky, and the ash was coming down on his face and his shut eyelids and he wasn't doing anything about it. His chest went out, slowly, and came back. A long breath. Another. Like a man getting out of cold water.

Then he opened his eyes, and lowered his head, and pinched the bridge of his nose between his thumb and forefinger for a moment.

His hand went into the inside of his jacket and came out empty. He looked at it. Rosalie watched him look at it, irritated, the way a man looks at a pocket where he was sure he'd left his keys. Then he turned the hand over, and there was a cigarette in it. White. Ordinary. Lying across his fingers where nothing had been a second before.

He put it in his mouth. He didn't reach for a lighter. He didn't so much as glance at it. The end of it just caught—a small orange coal blooming in the gray air—and a thread of blue smoke went up from it, and he drew on it long and hard, until the coal glowed, and took it out of his mouth between two fingers and let the smoke go out of him in a long slow stream.

"*Fuck* me," said Harry Potter.

He looked along the line at all of them, at Bella with her blind eyes, at Edward in Carlisle's grip, at Rosalie with the crying child in her arms. He looked tired. He looked, Rosalie thought, almost sick.

"You lot," he said, "have got a seriously fucked-up way of making babies."

For a moment there wasn't a sound in the clearing except the child crying.

Rosalie heard it go into her. She heard it go in, every word of it, and land. She'd wanted a baby since before she died. She'd wanted one with her whole body, every day of every year, for seventy-four years, in a way that had never once dulled; she had watched other women push prams along the street in Rochester and in Chicago and in a dozen small towns and felt it like a blade turning in her stomach. And then Bella. Bella pregnant, Bella dying, Bella bruised black from the inside and too weak to stand, and Rosalie standing over her day and night with her back to the door and her teeth bared at her own family, at Edward, at Carlisle, at the dog—because somebody had to. Somebody had to stand there. Somebody had to hold the cup.

She had held the cup for weeks. She had held Bella's head up off the pillow so she could drink. She had been the one to take Renesmee off the table and wrap her in a towel and stand there in the bloody kitchen holding her while she screamed, the first person in the world ever to hold her, and it had been the best moment of Rosalie's life, all ninety-one years of it.

And this man had walked in out of the sky and gone through it all like a man going through a bin, and then lit a cigarette.

"Say that again," Rosalie said.

Her voice came out very quiet. Emmett's grip on her arm went from hard to iron.

The wizard looked at her. He took his time about it. His eyes went from her face to the child's back and up again.

"Which bit, Blondie?"

"Rose," Emmett said, under his breath. "Rose. Babe. Please."

"You were in her *head*," Rosalie said. "She's four months old."

"Yeah," the wizard said. "I know. I counted." He looked at the end of his cigarette. "I counted a lot of things."

"If you ever—"

"Don't," he said.

He said it quite mildly. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. Rosalie felt it all along her body, all the way through, from the inside out, the same way she'd felt the heat of the Volturi going up, the same way Eleazar had said—*he has a hand on every one of us*—and for the first time since a dark street in Rochester in 1933, Rosalie Hale understood, physically, in her own venom, what it was to be prey. He made no threat. He didn't have to. He stood there smoking. She could feel his hand.

She shut her mouth.

The dog behind her had started to growl.

She'd been hearing it under everything for a few seconds without really listening. A low rumble that she felt more than heard, coming up through the snow into the soles of her boots, so deep it sat in her ribs. Jacob. He'd got to his feet when she lifted the child off him, and now he stood beside her with his head low and his ears flat and every hair on him standing up, staring at the wizard, his lips drawn off his teeth so far she could see the pale pink of his gums. He was shaking with rage.

She'd have let him. She'd have stepped aside. For the first and only time in her life she'd have *encouraged* the dog.

The dog didn't go. Because there was another dog in the way.

The small gray one. Leah. She'd come forward again while Rosalie was looking at the child—she was ten yards out in front of the line, alone on the snow, planted square between Jacob and the wizard, with her legs braced and her head down and her teeth bared. At Jacob. Her own Alpha. Rosalie could see her shaking from here. The fur on her back was standing up in a ridge from her skull to her tail, and the sound coming out of her was a high, thin, frantic snarl like a wire being drawn tight.

The rest of the dogs had come apart.

Rosalie had never particularly looked at the wolves as anything but a smell. She looked now. The line on their left flank, Jacob's lot, had gone ragged. The sandy one, Seth, was turning in circles between his sister and Jacob, whining, his tail clamped down. Out on the right flank, Sam Uley's pack—the bigger group, a dozen or more of them—had broken formation entirely. Some of them were backing up. Two or three were moving forward, hackles up. A big silver one at the front kept lunging out a few feet and stopping and lunging and stopping, snapping at the air in the direction of the wizard, and the huge black one, Sam himself, had gone completely still in the middle of them and was staring across the field at Leah Clearwater. Just staring.

The wizard looked at the gray wolf standing between him and the rest of them. He looked at her for a second. Then he looked at the big silver one dancing back and forth on the right, and at Jacob growling, and at Seth turning his circles.

He put the cigarette back in his mouth.

"What," he said round it, "is going on with the dogs?"

## V. Jacob

The guy wanted to know what was going on with the dogs.

Jacob was going to show him. Jacob was going to show him with his teeth. He was going to go across thirty feet of snow and take that cigarette and the hand holding it and the arm attached to the hand, and he didn't care what happened to him after, because Nessie was crying.

Nessie never cried.

He could smell her blood. That was the worst thing. He'd smelled her blood plenty of times—scraped knees in the woods, a cut finger off the edge of a sardine can, nothing, a kid's nothing—but he'd never smelled it like this, coming out of her nose with her hands holding her head, and it went into him like a hook. She'd been on his back and he'd felt her go stiff, felt every muscle in her small body lock at once, and he'd felt her fingers in his fur clench and clench and clench, and he hadn't been able to do anything. He'd stood there. He'd stood there for eleven seconds with his imprint on his back while some English asshole went through her like a knife, and he'd done *nothing*, and now Blondie had her and she was crying into Blondie's neck and he could still feel the shape of her small hands, gripping.

*I'm going to kill him.* It came out of him into the pack mind so loud it echoed. *I'm going to kill him, I'm going to rip his—*

*NO.*

Leah.

She hit the inside of his head like a truck. Everything she had, all of her, every bit of that hard bitter cold-as-a-January-creek girl he'd been sharing a brain with for months, thrown into the front of his skull at once. And every last bit of it was on fire.

*You can't,* she was screaming at him. *Jake, you can't, you CAN'T, I'll—I won't let you, I won't, I'll—*

He saw himself through her eyes.

It made him sick. It made him sick to his stomach. He saw a huge russet wolf with his head down and his teeth out, shaking, ready to go—and he saw it the way Leah saw it now. As a threat. As the most dangerous thing on the field. As a thing that wanted to hurt *him*—and that word went right down to the bottom of her, *him*, *him*, like a stone going down a well—and she was standing in the way of it, ten yards out on the snow with her legs braced and her teeth bared at her own Alpha, and she would die before she moved. She would die. He could feel it. She didn't want to. She hated it. She hated every second of it. She was standing there hating it with everything in her, hating the guy, hating herself, and she would let Jacob tear her throat out before she let him past.

He knew that feeling. He knew it from the inside. It was the floor going out from under you and the whole world swinging round and coming to rest on one person. It was what had happened to him four months ago on the Cullens' living room floor, when he'd walked in to kill a baby and looked at her instead.

*Jake.* Seth. Small and scared. *Jake, please. Please don't. She can't help it. You know she can't. You know.*

He knew.

He could make her move.

That was the thing. It was right there in his chest, where it had been since the night he broke from Sam. The voice. Ephraim Black's voice, the Alpha thing, the weight that came down on the back of your neck and made your legs do what they were told. He'd only used it on Leah twice. She'd hated him both times. He could feel it now, heavy and hot, sitting under his breastbone waiting to be let out. One word. *Move.* And her legs would go, and she'd step aside, and he'd be across the snow before she could even turn her head.

He gathered it up.

And he thought about Sam.

He thought about the night Bella came back from her honeymoon pregnant, and Sam standing in the clearing behind the Uley house with the whole pack around him, and the voice coming out of Sam like a hammer. *We go tonight. We kill it. We kill her if she's in the way.* And the way it had felt. The weight on his neck, pushing his head down, pushing his legs toward that house. The sheer *wrongness* of it, the thing in him screaming no while his body walked forward. He'd broken it. He'd broken it because he couldn't do it, because no voice in the world was going to make him walk into that house and kill Bella, and it had cost him his pack and his brothers and his place in the tribe, and he'd never once been sorry.

And now here was Leah. Standing in the snow. Screaming at him. And here was the voice in his chest. And she couldn't break it. Not from this, not with her legs already shaking out from under her. If he said the word, she'd move. She'd move, and she'd stand there and watch him go for the one thing on earth that her whole body had just decided was hers, and she'd never, ever be able to stop seeing it. She'd be inside his head forever, watching it.

He couldn't do it.

He tried. He stood there with the word in his mouth, and he tried, and it wouldn't come out.

*Jake,* Leah said. Quiet now. Just his name.

He let the voice go.

It went back down into his chest like a dog lying down. And Jacob stood there on the snow, shaking, with Nessie crying behind him and Leah standing in front of him, and he hated it. He hated everything. He threw his head back and howled at the sky, a long ragged furious sound that bounced off the trees on every side of the clearing and came back to him.

The guy in the jacket watched him do it. He took the cigarette out of his mouth.

"Yeah," he said. "Same, mate."

Paul broke.

Jacob saw it a second before it happened, out on the right flank, in Sam's half of the field. The big silver wolf had been jerking back and forth on the end of his own leash for a minute, lunging and stopping, the way Paul always did when Sam held him and he didn't want to be held. And Sam wasn't holding him. Sam was standing in the middle of his pack like a black stone and staring at Leah, and whatever was in Sam's head right now, it wasn't Paul. It wasn't anything. So the leash was gone. And Paul did what Paul always did.

He went.

He came across the field flat out, low to the ground, a silver streak churning snow up behind him, straight at the guy with the cigarette, his mouth open. Somebody on the Cullen side yelled. Leah spun, turning so fast she nearly fell, her whole body flinging itself sideways to get in front of him. She was too far away. She was never going to make it.

Jacob thought he saw the guy think about burning him, and decide against it.

He just turned his free hand over, palm down, and pushed. Like a man pushing down a lid.

Paul hit the ground.

He hit it from about four feet up, in the middle of his leap, as though somebody had dropped an anvil on the back of his neck. He hit flat on his belly with all four legs splayed out and slid. He slid a good twenty feet across the crust on his stomach, plowing up a long trench of snow in front of his nose, and stopped about three feet from the guy's boots, and lay there. He was trying to get up. Jacob could see him trying, every muscle in his body bunching under his fur. He couldn't get his head off the ground. Something was holding it there.

The guy looked down at him.

"Sit," he said. Then, considering: "Actually, no. Stay."

Paul snarled into the snow. It came out muffled.

"Bad dog." The guy took a drag on the cigarette. "Seriously. Can somebody call off the dogs? And I mean that literally. I'm not being funny."

Leah had stopped dead, ten feet away, staring down at Paul. Her lips were still back. Her whole body was still braced to take a bite out of him if he came up.

Seth phased.

Jacob felt him go—felt him wink out of the pack mind like a light going out—and then there was a skinny fifteen-year-old kid on his knees in the snow behind Jacob, scrambling to untie the cutoffs from round his ankle and get into them, hopping, swearing, ash and snow sticking to his bare back.

The guy glanced over. "Oh, for— Put it *away*, kid."

"Sorry!" Seth got the shorts up. He stood there in nothing else, steaming in the cold, his arms wrapped round himself and his hair full of ash. "Sorry, sorry. Hi. Hi. Okay. You're asking about—about the dogs. About us. I can—um."

"Go on, then."

"She imprinted on you."

The guy looked at him.

"Leah." Seth pointed at the gray wolf, with his whole arm, like a kid pointing at the board in class. "My sister. That's my sister. She's—she imprinted on you. When you took your glasses off. We all felt it. That's why everybody's—" He waved a hand at the whole field. At Jacob. At Sam's pack milling around in the snow. At Paul pinned on his stomach. "That's why."

"She what?"

"Imprinted. It's a wolf thing. It's like—" Seth made a big helpless shape in the air with both hands. "It's like when you see somebody, and that's it, that's them, they're your whole—like the whole world's sort of tied to them now, like you'd do anything, you'd be anything they need. Forever. Like a—like gravity. Kind of." His face screwed up. "It's hard to explain if you're not—"

The guy shut his eyes for a second.

"Son of a bitch," he said. He opened them again. "Like ducks."

Seth blinked. "What?"

"Ducks. Ducklings." The guy waved his cigarette vaguely. "First thing they see when they hatch, they follow it about. Could be a wellie. Could be a dog. Could be some poor sod in a lab coat. Follow it round the pond for the rest of their lives."

Seth opened his mouth. He closed it again. "No," he said. "Kind of. No. I don't—*no.*"

"Right." The guy turned his head and looked at Leah.

She looked back at him.

Jacob felt it go through her. Into the pack mind, raw as a wound: everything she was, braced in the snow, staring up at this guy with his cigarette and his tired face and his eyes like broken bottles. She hated him. She hated him so much it was like a sickness. She had just stood between him and her own Alpha, and she'd have done it again, and she hated that more. She was waiting. She didn't even know what for. For him to say something. Anything. She'd heard a hundred times inside Sam's head what Emily had said when he finally told her. She was waiting for it like a dog waits at a door.

The guy looked at her for a long second.

"Not now," he said.

And he turned away.

Jacob felt it hit her. It went through the pack mind like a slap. Then, underneath the slap, something else—something so twisted up in itself Jacob couldn't make sense of it—that might've been relief. *Good,* Leah thought, savagely. *Good. Good. Don't you dare. Don't you* dare *be nice to me.*

The guy flicked his hand. Paul came up off the snow all at once, as though a weight had been lifted off his back, and scrambled up onto his feet with a yelp and backed off fast, ears flat, tail down, all the way back across the field to Sam. Sam still hadn't moved. He was still looking at Leah. As Jacob watched, the big black head went down—slowly, all the way down, nose almost touching the snow. Jacob didn't know what that meant. He'd never seen Sam do it. He didn't think Leah had either.

She didn't look at him. Jacob felt her not look at him. It was the hardest thing she'd ever done.

The guy had walked away from all of them. He dropped his cigarette butt in the snow, and it hissed, and then he had another one between his fingers, lit already. He walked three steps toward the Cullen line and stopped, and he looked down the row of them—Carlisle, Esme, Emmett, Blondie with Nessie in her arms—and he stopped on Bella.

Bella was still standing where she'd been standing. She'd got her eyes back. Edward had his arm round her and she wasn't leaning on it. She was looking straight back at the guy, and her red eyes were blazing, and Jacob could see her jaw working.

"Right," the guy said to her. "Mum."

Jacob snarled. He couldn't help it. He couldn't move. Leah was still in front of him, and every bone in his body was locked, and all he could do was stand there and growl, low and endless, like a car engine turning over in the cold.

"The kid didn't have the rest," the guy said. "So you're going to give it me."

"Like hell," Bella said.

"Yeah," the guy said. "That's about what I thought."

## VI. Bella

She could feel the cut.

That was what she couldn't get past. It was gone now, closed, the shield whole again from end to end of the line—but she could feel where it had been. A thin hard line running through the elastic of it right above where Renesmee was, like the seam left in skin by a scalpel. When she moved the shield, the line moved with it. When she pulled on it, the line pulled a fraction tighter than everything around it. It ached, the way a scar aches in cold weather. She had a feeling it was going to ache for a very long time.

He'd done that in a second. He'd slipped through the thing that had held off Jane for forty minutes like it was wet paper, and gone into her daughter, and Renesmee had bled.

She did the only thing she could think of.

She pulled the shield in. All of it. She hauled it back off the wolves and off the witnesses and off her family, all that long elastic dome, and wrapped it round herself instead, tight, layer on layer on layer, as thick as she could make it, thicker than she'd ever made it, until it was packed down round her own mind like earth packed round a root. She felt Edward feel her do it. She felt his hand go tight on her arm.

The wizard watched her. He couldn't see it, she was almost sure of that. But he tipped his head a little, as though he'd heard something.

"Don't bother, love," he said.

Then he hit her.

This time he went looking for no seam and no soft spot. There was nothing clever in it at all. He came straight at her, the whole of him, like a truck coming through the front of a house.

She'd thought of the shield as rubber. As something that gave and came back. It had always given and come back; Jane had been hitting it all morning and it had always come back. It didn't come back now. It caved. She felt it go in, all those layers, folding up and crumpling one into the next like the hood of a car going into a wall, and she felt him come through the back of it into her, and she had time for one thought, very clear: *oh God, he isn't even trying to be careful—*

And then he was in her head, and he was tearing it apart.

Her memories went up like birds off a field.

The human ones first. She'd lost most of those. She'd known that since the day she woke up—all of her life before the burning had gone soft and dim, like something at the bottom of a muddy pond, and she'd been trying ever since to hold on to the bits that mattered. He didn't care about that. He reached down into the mud and grabbed handfuls of it and hauled them up into the light, and they came up *sharp*. Sharper than she'd ever had them. Phoenix. The heat, the gravel yard, her mother laughing in the kitchen with her hair up in a clip. The plane. Charlie at the airport in Port Angeles with his hands in his pockets, not knowing what to say. The cruiser. The rain on the cruiser's windshield. The cafeteria at Forks High, and a table at the back, and a boy with bronze hair looking at her as though she'd done something unforgivable.

He went through all of it. He didn't slow down. It was like being turned out of a drawer.

The van, sliding on the ice. Edward's hand stopping it. The meadow, the sun coming out—Edward's skin catching the light, glittering like a field of crushed diamonds—and she felt the wizard *see* that, a sudden sharp check, like a man stopping dead in a corridor. Then on again. James. The ballet studio and the mirrors and the bite, and the fire in her hand. Her birthday. A paper cut. Jasper's face. Edward in the woods, telling her he didn't want her. Months of gray. Jacob in his garage with oil on his hands, rebuilding two old motorbikes for her, laughing, and she felt something in the wizard go briefly, startlingly still, there, at the bikes—and then he was gone again. The cliff. The cold green water. Volterra, and the clock tower, and Aro's hand. The newborns, the war on the mountain. The wedding. A white dress. Her father's arm.

The island.

*Nope,* said a voice that wasn't hers, flat and English and very loud, right in the middle of her mind. *Nope. Not looking at that.* And he went past it so fast that it was gone before she'd felt it start.

He slowed down after that.

He slowed down because this was what he'd come for. She felt him settle into it, the way you'd settle into a chair with a file. A box of tampons in her suitcase on the island, untouched, and the dates. Her stomach, round, overnight. The kicks. The bruises blooming black under her skin, all over her belly and her ribs. The cracked rib. Rosalie, standing over her. Esme with a cool cloth. Carlisle with an ultrasound machine that couldn't see through the membrane. Jacob in the doorway, white with rage and grief. The smell of the blood in the cup—O negative, out of a plastic bag, cold—and the way her nose had told her it smelled *good*, and her horror at that, and her drinking it anyway. Through a bendy straw. Because the baby needed it.

He went through all of it, and she felt him get angrier.

She couldn't tell how she felt that. He didn't think in words much. But it was there, coming off him as he went deeper, like heat coming off a stove. He was angry at the cup. He was angry at the bruises. He was angry at Carlisle's careful voice, and Edward's face at the bedside, and Rosalie with her back to the door. He was angry at all of them. He was angry at *her*.

Then he got to the birth, and she felt him stop being angry and go cold.

She'd hidden this one from herself. She'd done it deliberately. She couldn't remember most of it anyway, but what she had, she'd wrapped up and put in the furthest corner she had and never once taken out. He took it out. The crack of her own spine. The blood coming up out of her mouth. Edward's voice shouting for morphine. The scalpel that wouldn't go in. Edward bending over her with his mouth open. His teeth. Then a tiny weight on her chest, hot and wet and screaming, and Bella saying *Renesmee*, and Renesmee biting her. Then the fire. Two days of it, every part of her, the inside of her bones.

He took that out and looked at it, all of it, for a long, long moment.

And then he went on.

Waking up. The thirst. Her daughter. Charlie in the living room with his hands shaking, and Jacob telling him just enough. A woman on a mountain. Alice, gone in the night. A torn page in a copy of *The Merchant of Venice*, and a name: J. Jenks. A sleazy little office in Seattle. An envelope.

She tried to stop him there.

She didn't even decide to. Something in her lunged in front of the envelope, all on its own, the way a mother steps in front of a car—*not that, not that, you can't have that*—because the envelope was the last thing. It was the only plan she had left. If it went wrong, if this man was what she was afraid he was, then the envelope was how her daughter got away.

He went straight to it.

He didn't even slow down. He found the place where she'd tried to stand in front of something and walked straight through her and picked it up. Two passports. A boy's face and a little girl's. *Jacob Wolfe. Vanessa Wolfe.* The money. The backpack she'd zipped up on her daughter's shoulders herself at five o'clock that morning in the cottage, kneeling on the floor, telling her to hold on tight to Jake and not let go. The plan. *South.*

*Wolfe,* the voice said in her head, dry as dust. *Subtle.*

And then, last of all, he went looking for what Renesmee was, and found Carlisle.

Carlisle in his study, with a blood sample and a microscope and a pad of paper covered with numbers, and the look on his face that he only got when he'd found something beautiful. Explaining it to her, gently, the way he explained everything. *Humans have twenty-three pairs of chromosomes, Bella. We have twenty-five. Renesmee has twenty-four.* A pause. *So does Jacob.* The tape measure on the kitchen wall. The numbers going up every day. Carlisle's careful voice, at night, in the kitchen, when he thought she couldn't hear. *If she keeps on at this rate, she'll be physically mature in perhaps seven years. Possibly less. And after that—I don't know. I don't know, Esme. It could slow. It could stop. Or it could go on.*

*Or it could go on,* Bella had heard, through the wall, and she had gone into the bathroom in the cottage and turned on the taps and sat on the edge of the tub and screamed without any sound.

He took that.

He took it, and he took a long time over it, and then he let go of her all at once and came out of her head the way he'd come in, through the front, and she went down.

Her knees hit the snow. She didn't feel them hit. There was a ringing in her head, very high and very thin, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass, and under the ringing there was nothing. No shield. She reached for it and it wasn't there. She reached again, harder, and she could feel the edges of it somewhere far off, torn, like a sheet ripped on a nail, and when she tried to pull them together they slid out of her hands.

Edward's arms were round her. He was holding her up. He was saying her name, over and over, into her hair, and she could feel him shaking.

"*Bella.* Bella. Look at me. Love, look at me—"

She looked at him. She could see him. That was something.

Behind him, she heard the wizard breathing out a long stream of smoke.

"Right," he said.

She turned her head. He was standing exactly where he'd been, ten yards away. He had a hand pressed over his eyes. He took it away. He looked at her, and then at Edward, and then along the line at the rest of them, and his face was hard and blank and absolutely furious.

"You married a dead bloke at eighteen," he said. "Fine. Your business. And he put a baby in you that broke your back from the inside, and you all sat round a hospital bed in a glass house watching it happen and gave her blood in a *sippy cup*—" He jerked the cigarette at Rosalie. "—because nobody, not one of you, with all your centuries and your money and your *medical degrees*, knew what the hell it was. And when it came out, your fella chewed it out of her. With his *teeth.*" He shook his head. "And then you hid it. And then when you got caught, you sent off for every vampire on the bloody continent to come and stay at your house and meet it. And then you all came and stood in a field six miles from her dad's police station. *Her dad's.* Who's a *copper.* Who doesn't know." He put the cigarette back in his mouth and took it out again. "And you *sparkle.* I saw that too. You actually sparkle. Christ."

"She's my daughter," Bella said.

It came out of her raw. It was the only thing she had left to say.

He looked at her for a moment.

"Yeah," he said. "I know. I saw." Something moved behind his eyes and went away again. "That's about the only bit of this I'm not going to write up as a complaint."

He turned his back on all of them, and took a phone out of his pocket.

## VII. Carlisle

It was a mobile telephone. Carlisle almost laughed.

It was an old one, too, a gray brick of a Nokia with a scratched screen and a stubby aerial, the kind of thing his nurses had carried five years ago and thrown in a drawer since. The wizard flipped it in his hand, thumbed three buttons without looking, and put it to his ear. He stood with his back to the Cullen line, ten yards off, his other hand in his jacket pocket and the cigarette in the corner of his mouth, and the ash kept coming down on his shoulders.

Carlisle didn't move. Nobody moved. Behind him he could hear Bella breathing, ragged, unnecessary breaths, and Edward murmuring to her, and Renesmee's crying gone down to small, exhausted hiccups in Rosalie's arms. He could hear Kate. He could hear Tanya beside her, very low, saying her sister's name, and then Garrett's, and then her sister's again.

He could feel the wizard's hand on him. Now that Eleazar had named it, he couldn't stop feeling it. A faint pressure, everywhere at once, inside, deep, as if someone had laid two fingers on the venom in his veins and was simply resting them there.

"Control," Harry Potter said into the phone. "Yeah, it's Potter. Authentication—hang on." He took the cigarette out of his mouth. "Wellington. Treacle. Four-four-one-nine." A pause. "Yeah. Yeah, I know what time it is in Geneva. Put me through to Ostrowska."

He waited. He looked at the sky. He kicked a little at the snow with the toe of his boot.

"Magda. Hiya. No, I'm fine. Not a scratch. Listen, I'm going to give you the short version and you're going to write it down, all right? Short version." He took a breath. "Volturi are done."

A tinny squawk came out of the earpiece. Carlisle heard every syllable of it, and didn't understand a word; it was a language he'd never heard spoken.

"All of them. Thirty-four. The three old ones, the guard, a pair of wives at the back. Yeah. Yeah, I know. I did warn them. I warned them twice. They had a go." He glanced over his shoulder, briefly, at the black circles in the mud. "Plus one more, after. A nomad on the other side. American. He went for me." A pause. "Yeah. I'll do the form."

He listened.

"Forty-odd witnesses. I let them go, they're scattering. You'll want to tell the European desk and the South American desk to keep their ears open in the usual places, because some of them are going to talk, and it'll get round. Hundred or so on site at the peak. Pack of shapeshifters, local, Quileute. Big. Twenty or so. Native magic, very old, nothing to do with us. Not my problem today." He paused. "No. No sightings. Nearest Muggle settlement's six miles. There's a copper there knows more than he should, but not much more. Leave him."

He listened again. He was nodding a little, absently, the way people do on the phone.

"Right. Now the long bit." He turned around. His eyes went across the line, and stopped on Rosalie, and on the child in her arms. "I've got an unregistered hybrid."

Silence on the other end. Carlisle heard it as clearly as the squawk.

"Vampire father. Human mother—mother's been turned since, three, four months back. The kid was *born*, Magda. Born. Came out of her mum. Female. Four months old, give or take, presents as three or four years. Growth rate's—" He looked at Carlisle. "Fast. Very fast. Their doctor's been measuring her every day. I've got his numbers; I'll send them. He reckons adult in seven years, possibly less, and then he doesn't know." A pause. "Yeah. That's what I said."

Carlisle found he had taken a step forward. He made himself stop.

"Heartbeat. Blood. Body temp runs high. Eats blood, will eat food if you make her. No venom, that I can feel, she's not on the register. Projective telepath, contact, very strong, goes straight through mental shields. Sharp as a tack. Perfect recall. Very attached to the family. Very attached to one of the shifters in particular, there's a whole thing, I'll put it in the report." He rubbed his eyes with his thumb and finger. "Mother's a shield. Mind shield, a big one. Newborn. She's a bit bent out of shape at the moment, she'll be all right."

He listened.

"No. No, I'm not bringing her in." His voice went flat. "Listen to me. I want a containment team *ready.* Ready, and sat on their hands. Being Division, two of your best, people who've done vampires before and lived. A Healer who's done hybrids, if we've got one, and if we haven't got one find me somebody who's done werewolf kids. A mind-Healer. Somebody from Magical Law who can spell 'jurisdiction.' Get them kitted up and sat on a Portkey, and they don't move until I call it." He paused. "Because I said so, Magda. Because I'm the one stood here."

The phone squawked.

"And pull everything the South America desk has got. Brazil, Chile, Peru, anything going back fifty years on vampires and kids. Rumours, folk stories, anything." He looked at the child again. "One-offs don't happen. Not with this. If there's one, there's more, and if there's more, somebody's been making them."

A longer squawk.

"Yeah. Yeah. I'll call in an hour." A pause, and something in his face changed, very slightly, and his voice dropped. "And tell Hermione I'm going to be late. Tell her—yeah. Tell her I'm sorry about the pie."

He took the phone from his ear and snapped it shut and put it back in his pocket.

"Containment," Carlisle said.

The wizard looked at him.

"You said containment." Carlisle kept his voice level. It took more effort than it had taken to keep it level in front of Aro. "She's a child, Mr. Potter. She's my granddaughter. Whatever else she is, she's that. I have watched her every day of her life. She has never hurt anyone. She would never—"

"Yeah, Doc. I know."

"Then—"

"I *know.*" The wizard dropped his cigarette in the snow. It hissed and went out. "I've been in her head. I've been in her mum's. I know more about that kid right now than you do. I know she's never bitten anybody. I know she likes the bloke with the feather in his cap song and hates scrambled eggs. And I know your numbers, because I took them out of your daughter-in-law's head along with everything else, and I know you were stood in your kitchen at three in the morning telling your wife you don't know what happens to her in seven years." He shook his head. "You don't know. I don't know. Nobody on this planet knows. So somebody's going to find out. Either you lot do it, on your own, in a glass house in the woods, with a tape measure, or my lot do it with a hospital and a library and three hundred years of notes on things that aren't supposed to exist." His voice dropped. "And nobody's putting a four-month-old in a cell while I'm on this. That's what *ready* means. That's why I said it."

Carlisle looked at him for a long moment.

"And if you're taken off it?"

The wizard almost smiled. "Then you'd better hope I'm not."

Behind Carlisle, Esme's hand found his. He held on to it.

The wizard turned away, toward the black motorcycle standing where it had skidded to a stop in the snow, and stopped.

The gray wolf was standing beside it.

Carlisle hadn't seen her move. None of them had. She'd been out on the snow between Jacob and the wizard the last time he'd looked, and now she was standing by the front wheel of the motorcycle with her head low and her ears back and her legs braced, and she was staring at the wizard with an expression Carlisle had seen on a great many faces in a great many emergency rooms. Furious. Frightened. Going nowhere.

The wizard looked at her.

She looked back.

Behind them, across the field, the whole of Sam Uley's pack had gone silent. Jacob stood in the snow with his head down. Seth, still in nothing but his cutoffs and ash, had both hands over his mouth.

"You're going to follow me about," the wizard said. "Aren't you."

The gray wolf showed him her teeth.

He looked at her a while longer. Then he sighed, a long gusting sigh that steamed in the cold, and walked over, and sat down sideways on the seat of the motorcycle with his boots in the snow, and took out another cigarette. It lit itself. He didn't look at it.

"Right," he said to no one. "Ducks it is."

And Leah Clearwater lay down in the snow beside the front wheel, with her back to him and her face to the rest of the world, and put her chin on her paws, and kept watch.

The ash came down on both of them. It came down on all of them, on the wolves and the vampires and the black scorched circles in the mud where three thousand years of law had been standing ten minutes ago. Carlisle stood in it with his wife's hand in his and his granddaughter crying softly behind him, and he looked at the wizard on his motorcycle, smoking, waiting for a telephone to ring, and he understood for the first time in his long life that the world had more than one set of rules.

They were going to have to learn the other one. Quickly.
