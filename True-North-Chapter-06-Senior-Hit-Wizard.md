# True North

## Chapter Six: Senior Hit Wizard

---

### I.

The window went gray, and then a paler gray, and then the coat downstairs started talking.

Sue heard it because she was still awake, lying on her back doing sums about ground chuck, and because her house was small and every sound in it came up through the floor. A thin buzz, like a phone set to vibrate on a wooden table. A man's voice, tinny and English, too faint for words. The couch springs groaning as something very heavy sat up all at once.

Then the back door.

She got up and went to her bedroom window in her nightgown with her arms crossed against the cold.

He was in the yard. He stood on the wet grass halfway between the house and the trees, by the burn barrel, in the long gray coat, with the little bronze mirror held up in front of his face in one paw. The fog had come in off the ocean in the night and was lying in the yard in rags, and he was standing in it up to his knees. His back was to the house.

He was listening. She could see that from the shape of him. Both ears pointed forward at the mirror, stiff as fence posts. The tail hanging straight down behind him, not moving. Every so often the tinny voice stopped and he said something short, and the voice started again.

It went on a long time. Five minutes. Eight. Sue's feet went numb on the floorboards.

Then he said something louder, and in the quiet of six-forty on a Sunday morning, with the window open an inch for the air the way she always kept it, she heard it.

"No."

The tinny voice said a great deal.

"Then I'll come and tell him to his face," the wolf said. "Twenty minutes."

He shut the mirror. He stood in the fog a moment longer with his head down, looking at nothing. His tail moved once, low, a short hard stroke like a cat's before it jumps.

Then there was a crack like a rifle going off across the river, and the fog in the yard swirled in a circle where he'd been, and he wasn't there.

Sue stood at the window with her heart going.

Down the hall, Leah's door didn't open. Sue listened for it. Nothing. Her daughter had finally gone to sleep sometime after three, the way Leah always went to sleep after a bad night, all at once and like a stone, and the sound hadn't reached her.

Sue put on her robe and went down.

The couch was empty. The star quilt her mother had made was folded on one end of it into a square, the edges lined up, the corners tucked, neater than Sue had ever folded it in her life. The spare pillow was on top of it. There was a long dent in the couch cushions, and a lot of black fur on them, and more on the rug.

In the kitchen, the light over the stove was on.

She hadn't left it on.

The grocery list was where she'd left it on the counter. *Milk. Eggs (3 doz). Bread (4). Hamburger (lots)*, crossed out, and under it in her own handwriting, *Hamburger (more)*.

And under that, in black ink, in loopy handwriting that slanted hard to the right and had blotted where the pen pressed down:

*Called to Geneva. Back by half eleven. Will wear sleeves.*

*— H. Potter*

Next to *Hamburger (more)*, in the same hand, smaller, somebody had added *(I'll get it)*.

Sue looked at that for a while.

Then she picked up the pen and crossed out *(I'll get it)*, hard, twice, and put the pen down, and put the coffee on, and stood at the counter in her robe while it dripped, and didn't sit down.

*Then I'll come and tell him to his face.*

She didn't know who *him* was. She didn't know what *no* had been the answer to. She knew what a man's back looked like when he'd just been told something on the phone that he was going to have to carry into a room and set down in front of people. She'd seen Harry stand at this same counter with the phone in his hand the day the tribal clinic called about Leah's blood work, and his back had looked like that.

The coffee finished. She poured it.

Outside, the fog sat in the yard where he'd been, and didn't move.

---

### II.

Leah woke up at nine-forty with her face in the pillow and her first thought fully formed, the way her thoughts came after the bad nights.

*He's on the couch.*

Then she smelled the house, and he wasn't.

She knew it before she was all the way awake. The house smelled like coffee and her mother and old hamburger grease and lavender soap, and like him, pine and wet stone and cold creek water, all through the living room and the hall. But that smell was hours old. It had settled into the rug and the cushions the way woodsmoke settles into a sweater. There was nothing new on top of it.

She was out of bed and down the stairs before she'd decided to be. The fourth stair creaked. She didn't care.

The couch was empty. Her grandmother's star quilt was folded on one end of it, square, the corners tucked like a hospital bed.

She stood at the bottom of the stairs in a T-shirt and her cut-offs and looked at the quilt, and something under her breastbone went very cold and very still.

She knew this. She knew exactly this. A boy who'd been in her life every single day for four years, and then one Tuesday he wasn't, and nobody would say where he was, and when he came back two weeks later he was somebody else.

"He's in Switzerland," said her mother from the kitchen.

Leah turned around.

Her mother was sitting at the table in her robe with her coffee and the newspaper, the *Peninsula Daily News*, folded open to the crossword, which she hadn't started. She had circles under her eyes like she'd been punched.

"Geneva," her mother said. "His coat talked at quarter to seven. He went out in the yard and listened to it for ten minutes, and said *no* to it, and then there was a bang and he was gone." She tipped her head at the counter. "He left a note."

Leah went and read the note.

*Called to Geneva. Back by half eleven. Will wear sleeves.*

*— H. Potter*

And next to *Hamburger (more)*, something crossed out so hard the pen had gone through the paper.

"What was that?" Leah said.

"Nothing."

"Mom."

"He offered to buy the hamburger." Her mother turned a page of the newspaper she wasn't reading. "I declined."

Leah read the note again. Then a third time. *Back by half eleven.* She looked at the clock on the stove. Nine forty-four.

The cold thing under her breastbone loosened, a little. Not all the way.

"He said *no*," she said. "To who?"

"I don't know."

"What did he sound like?"

Her mother looked up at her over the newspaper.

"Like your father," she said, "the day the clinic called."

Leah didn't say anything.

She poured herself coffee and sat down across from her mother in her own chair by the fridge, and they sat there together for a while not reading the paper, while the fog outside the window over the sink went from gray to white.

---

Seth came in the back door at ten-fifteen like a load of firewood falling off a truck.

He'd run the Cullens' perimeter all night on Jacob's orders, and he smelled like it, bleach and candy and wet fir and the special stink of a fifteen-year-old who'd been a wolf for nine hours. His collarbone was out of the sling. He went straight past both of them to the fridge and stood in front of it with the door open, eating cold potatoes out of the pot with his fingers.

"Is he here?" he said, with his mouth full. "Is he up? Where is he?"

"Switzerland," said Leah.

Seth turned around with a fistful of potato.

"*Switzerland*?"

"He'll be back at eleven-thirty."

"He went to *Switzerland* and he's coming back by *lunch*?" Seth's face did something amazing. "That's so cool. That's the coolest thing I've ever heard. Can he take me? Do you think he'd take me?"

"No," said Leah and her mother, at the same time.

Seth ate more potato. He was looking at Leah now, and she knew that look. She'd had it pointed at her across this same table for fifteen years. It was the look he got right before he said something that was going to get him killed.

"So," Seth said. "Embry texted me."

"Seth."

"At like two in the morning. On the Cullens' Wi-Fi, which by the way is so fast, Leah, Alice says it's—"

"*Seth.*"

"He just said *DUDE* and *PORCH* and then like forty exclamation points." Seth was grinning so wide she could see the potato. "And then he sent another one that just said *Paul says he said the sex is*—"

"Eat your eggs," said their mother.

"I don't have any eggs."

Their mother got up and got the eggs out of the fridge and put the pan on the stove, very hard, and cracked six of them into it without looking at either of her children, and Seth looked at the back of her robe and then at Leah and mouthed *oh my God*, delighted, and Leah put her face in her hands.

---

At eleven twenty-eight, the crack came in the yard.

Leah was at the sink. She'd been at the sink for twenty minutes, washing three dishes very slowly, because the window over the sink looked out at the yard. She heard the crack and her hands stopped in the water, and she looked up.

He was standing on the wet grass where he'd disappeared, with the fog still lying in rags around his legs. She knew him by his shape first: the height of him, the ears, the tail.

Then she looked at the rest.

The long gray coat was gone. He was wearing black. A black coat, cut close and buttoned all the way up the front with a row of small flat silver buttons, high in the collar, long in the sleeves, the sleeves buttoned at the wrist over the black fur. At his throat, where the gray coat's silver clasp had been, there was a silver pin in the shape of a globe. On the left side of his chest, a narrow badge, silver on black, too small for her to read from the window.

He'd been brushed.

That was the next thing. Somebody had taken a brush to him. The black fur on his face and neck and the backs of his forepaws lay flat and smooth and shone a little in the gray light, like a horse's coat before a show. The burnt tuft between his ears was gone, cut down short to match the rest. Only the singed whiskers on the left side of his muzzle were still the same, short and crisped, with a quarter-inch of new black at the roots. He was carrying a flat black leather case under one arm.

He stood in the yard and looked at the house.

His ears were forward, both of them, stiff and locked there like they'd been nailed. For five days they'd been tipped and cocked and one-up-one-sideways at everything. Now they didn't move. His tail hung straight down behind him and didn't move either.

He crossed the yard. She listened for it, and there wasn't any sound.

Seth was already out the back door.

"Hey! Harry! Hey, you're back, Leah said you were in *Switzerland*—"

"Seth." He stopped at the bottom of the back steps. "Good morning."

Leah, at the sink, went still.

It was his voice. Same pitch, same roughness, the growl still running underneath it like an engine in another room. But the Africa had gone out of it. Every bit. The vowels had all come back from wherever they used to land and lined themselves up straight and flat, and the words came out clipped and level and very English. Like somebody had taken the voice she knew and ironed it.

He sounded like the man in the mirror.

"Is that a *uniform*?" Seth was saying. "That's so cool. What's the badge? Is that—"

"It's a badge." He looked up past Seth at the screen door, where her mother had come to stand with a dish towel. "Mrs. Clearwater."

"Mr. Potter."

His eyes went past her mother, through the screen and the kitchen, to the window over the sink.

To Leah.

He looked at her for about one second. His ears didn't move. His face didn't move. He looked at her the way he'd looked at Thibodeaux's clipboard, probably. The way she'd seen him look at the room at the Cullens' when he walked in, counting doors.

Then he looked back at her mother.

"I need five minutes," he said. "With you and with Leah. Out here, please. Before we go."

Seth looked from him to the house and back. Some of the grin went off his face.

"I'll—I'll get my shoes," Seth said, and went inside past their mother, and Leah heard him stop in the hall, just out of sight, the way he did when he wanted to listen.

Leah dried her hands on the salmon towel. They were steady. She made sure of it.

She went out.

Up close, he smelled of things she'd never smelled on him. Stone floors and old paper and ink. Coal smoke. Some kind of floor polish. And over all of it, on the black wool, faint and sickly and unmistakable, bleach poured over a candy store.

He'd been to the Cullens'. On the way back. Before he came here.

"You stopped at the Cullens'," she said.

"Yes."

*Yes.* In five days she'd never once heard him say *yes*.

"What happened to your voice?" she said.

He looked down at her. His ears stayed exactly where they were.

"Geneva," he said.

---

### III.

He didn't lean on anything.

That was what Leah kept noticing, standing in the wet grass at the bottom of the back steps with her mother beside her and her arms crossed. For five days he'd leaned on everything. Bars, porch rails, pool cues, walls, the Cullens' doorframe, her. He'd sprawled on the grass under the wrong stars like a dropped coat. He'd slouched at her mother's table until Seth's chair broke.

He stood in the yard now like a fence post somebody had driven in with a sledge. Straight. Feet planted. His forepaws were clasped behind his back, she could see the edge of one past his hip, and the leather case was tucked under his arm, and his chin was up, and he didn't move.

"In that room," he said, "I'm the Senior Hit Wizard first."

Neither of them said anything.

"Everything else comes second. I'm going to say some things you'll hate. I'm going to say them the way Geneva says them, because Geneva's who I'm speaking for, and because the people in that kitchen need to hear what they're up against in the voice it's coming in." He looked at her mother, and then at Leah, steadily, one and then the other. "None of it is personal. Some of it is for your own good. You'll hate that part most."

The fog moved in the yard. Somewhere down toward First Beach a gull was screaming.

"What happened in Geneva?" Leah said.

"You'll hear it at noon with the rest of the council."

"Tell me now."

"I'm saying it once."

"Harry—"

"Once, Leah." He didn't raise his voice at all. "In front of the people whose decision it is. Your mother's one of them. You aren't."

Leah stared at him.

Her mother didn't move beside her. Leah could feel her not moving.

"Then why are we out here?" her mother said. "If you're not going to tell us anything."

"So you don't take my side."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good." His eyes went to Leah. "Either of you. When the room turns on me, and it will, you let it. You don't stand up. You don't explain me. You don't say I'm not like that." His ears were locked forward, stiff. "If the council thinks I've got the Clearwaters in my pocket, nothing I say in there will be worth anything, and I need every word of it to be worth something."

"And where do you want me?" Leah said. "Since I'm not anybody whose decision it is."

"With your pack. Next to your Alpha. Wherever Jacob stands."

"Not by you."

"Not by me."

She heard what he was doing. She heard it the way she heard a car downshift on the hill above the house: the gears going, one after another, until she understood where it was going. He wasn't going to look at her in there. He wasn't going to talk to her the way he talked to her. He was going to stand in Billy Black's kitchen in his black coat with his paws behind his back, and she was going to stand next to Jacob, and he was going to look at her the way he'd just looked at her through the kitchen window.

Like a door he was counting.

"Fine," she said.

"Leah."

"I said *fine*."

He looked at her for a second longer.

Then he looked at her mother. "We should go. It's eleven thirty-six. I'd like to be there before Mr. Uley."

Her mother looked at him for a long moment, at the black coat and the silver buttons and the brushed fur and the sleeves, buttoned at the wrist.

"You wore sleeves," her mother said.

"You asked."

"I asked for a shirt with sleeves. I didn't ask for that."

"It's what I've got that's appropriate, Mrs. Clearwater."

Her mother pressed her lips together and went to get her keys.

---

Her mother's truck was a 1987 Ford with a bench seat and a dent in the tailgate from the year Seth learned to drive, and the cab held three people if one of them was Seth and sat in the middle.

It did not hold Harry.

He looked at the cab. He looked at the bed. Then he walked to the back of the truck, put one forepaw on the side of the bed, and stepped up into it without using the bumper, and the whole truck sank four inches on its springs and groaned like a cow.

He sat down in the bed with his back against the cab, his legs straight out in front of him across the ribbed metal, the leather case on his knees. His ears stuck up above the roofline of the cab. Through the little back window, Leah could see the back of his neck, black fur brushed flat over the high black collar.

"Leah," he said, through the glass, without turning his head. "Ride up front with your mother."

She'd had her hand on the tailgate.

She took it off.

Seth climbed into the bed instead, in a hoodie and basketball shorts, and sat down beside him cross-legged, grinning, and started talking before he'd finished sitting. Leah got into the cab next to her mother and slammed the door, and her mother started the truck, and it coughed and caught, and they pulled out onto the road toward the village with a wolf in a uniform sitting in the back like a sack of feed.

Through the back window she could hear them. She didn't try to. She just could.

"—so is it like the FBI? Is it like the CIA? Do you have a gun? Do wizards have guns?"

"No."

"No gun, or no it's not like the FBI?"

"Both."

"What's the badge say?"

"My name and my number."

"What's your number?"

"Four-oh-nine."

"Is that good? Is that, like, low? Low's good, right? Like in the Army—"

"Seth."

"Yeah?"

"Quiet now."

Seth was quiet.

Leah watched the side mirror. From the cab she could see the top half of him over the roofline: two black ears, stiff and forward, cutting through the fog going by like the fins on the back of a boat. They didn't swivel when the truck hit the pothole by the Clearwater mailbox. They didn't turn when a dog came barking down a driveway after the tires. Five days she'd watched those ears go after every sound in every room he'd been in, the clink of a glass, a sneeze in the trees, Grimbald's box scraping the floor behind the bar. He couldn't keep them still if he tried. She'd teased him about it.

He was keeping them still now. She could see it was costing him something, the same way it cost her to keep her face still in the pack mind when Sam thought about Emily. She didn't know what it was costing. She didn't like not knowing.

Leah watched him in the side mirror. Her little brother sat in the truck bed beside the black coat with his knees pulled up and his hands jammed in his hoodie pocket, and his face had changed. He looked at the side of Harry's head for a while, at the locked ears and the brushed fur, and then he looked out at the road going by behind them, the fog, the wet trees, and he didn't say anything else.

Her mother drove with both hands on the wheel.

"He's different," her mother said, after a while, very low, over the engine.

"Yeah."

"Was he like this before? In Canada?"

Leah thought about a wolf leaning on a bar in Atlin eating pineapple pizza and calling it a hate crime. A wolf lying on his back in the grass under the wrong stars with his tail thumping the ground. A wolf in her mother's kitchen with soapsuds in the fur of both his arms, handing her plates one eggshell at a time.

She thought about a wolf standing in a clearing beside a dead man, saying *should've come quietly, boet*, with no expression on his face at all.

"Once," she said. "For about nine seconds."

Her mother didn't ask what had happened in the nine seconds. She drove past the school and the community center and the store, and turned up the road toward the little red house at the top of the hill, where Billy Black's truck was in the drive and Old Quil's grandson's car was parked behind it, and a thin line of woodsmoke was coming out of the chimney into the fog.

---

### IV.

Jacob had asked his dad at breakfast.

He hadn't planned to. He'd planned to do it properly, after the council, when the house was empty, sitting down at the table with coffee the way you were supposed to ask the old men things. But he'd been up since five, and he'd stood in this kitchen at six-thirty making eggs with the woodstove going and the smell of it all through the room, cedar and smoke and old iron, and the smell had been on a stranger last night in the rain, and he'd turned around with the spatula in his hand and said it.

*Dad. The white guy. The trader. The one we got the name from.*

Billy had been reading the paper. He'd put it down.

*What about him?*

*Do you know where he came from? England?*

His dad had looked at him for a long time across the table. Then he'd said, *Who's asking?*

*The guy with Leah.* Jacob had turned the eggs. *He hasn't asked anything. He smells like this kitchen, Dad. Like the stove. I smelled it on him on the Cullens' lawn, and Leah's been smelling it on him for a week. She's got it in a box in her head and she's sitting on the lid.*

His father hadn't said anything for so long the eggs started to burn.

*After the council,* Billy had said finally. And he'd turned his chair around and wheeled himself down the hall to his room, and Jacob had heard the closet door open, and something scrape off the top shelf, and then nothing at all for a long time.

His dad had come back out twenty minutes later and eaten his burned eggs and not said one word about it, and Jacob hadn't asked again.

Now it was eleven forty-three, and his dad was at the head of the kitchen table in his chair with a cup of coffee in front of him and his good flannel shirt on, the blue one, buttoned to the neck. Old Quil was beside him in the hard chair with the high back, the one nobody else sat in, both hands folded on the head of his cane. Quil had driven him over and was sitting on the front steps outside because he was Sam's and this wasn't his to sit in on. The kitchen smelled like coffee and woodsmoke and old man, and Old Quil's liniment, and from the living room through the open doorway, Paul.

Paul was on the couch. Paul was always on the couch. Jacob's sister had come home in August for a week and stayed, and Paul had looked at her across this living room and that had been that, and now Paul was on the couch every day, eating Billy's food and putting his feet on Billy's coffee table. Rachel was in the bedroom with the door shut. Billy had told Paul he could stay where he was. *You're on the list, Paul. You might as well hear it.*

Paul had his arms crossed and his feet on the coffee table and the look on his face he got when he'd been told off and was planning to be told off some more.

*Porch,* Embry had texted. *DUDE. PORCH.* Jacob had read it at three in the morning on the Cullens' back step. Paul had told the whole reservation by breakfast. Paul, by all accounts, had acted out the stage whisper for Embry and Quil four times.

Jacob had heard it secondhand five different ways by eleven o'clock. He didn't know which way was worst.

He heard the truck before he saw it.

Sue's old Ford, coming up the hill in second gear, with something heavy in it. He could hear the springs. He went to the kitchen window and looked out past the dripping eaves at the drive, and the truck came around the bend out of the fog and pulled up behind Quil's car, and he saw what was in the bed.

Seth, in a hoodie, with his knees up.

And beside Seth, sitting with his back against the cab like a guy getting a ride home from a job site, in a black coat, the stranger.

The truck stopped. The stranger stood up in the bed. The truck rose four inches on its springs when he stepped down off the side of it onto the gravel, without using the bumper.

Jacob stood at the window and looked at him.

*He dressed up for us.*

That was his first thought, and it wasn't a kind one. Black coat, cut long and buttoned to the throat. Silver buttons. A silver pin at the collar. Some kind of badge. The fur on his head brushed flat and shining like somebody had gone over him with a curry comb before a parade, and the burnt patch on his head that Leah had put there was gone, cut down. A leather case under one arm. He stood on the gravel by the truck with his back very straight and his paws behind him and his ears pointed at the house, and he didn't look around at anything.

Last night on the Cullens' lawn he'd had a fern in his head and pie on his chin, and he'd waved at Edward.

Now he looked like something that had come to take the furniture.

The cab door opened. Leah got out.

Jacob watched her. She got out of the cab and shut the door and stood there by the front fender with her arms crossed. She didn't go around the truck to him. She didn't look at him. She looked up at the house, at the window, and saw Jacob in it, and her face didn't change. Then she walked up the path and the porch steps and in the front door, past Quil on the steps, without a word.

She came straight through the living room past Paul, who opened his mouth, and shut it again when she looked at him, and came into the kitchen, and stood next to Jacob by the stove.

Right next to him. Shoulder almost touching his arm.

She hadn't stood that close to Jacob voluntarily since the day she'd joined his pack.

"Hey," Jacob said.

"Hey."

"You okay?"

"Fine."

She smelled like him. Still. All over. Under her own rain-and-cedar, the pine and wet stone went all the way down. But she was standing here, next to Jacob, with her arms crossed and her jaw set and her eyes on the back door, and she wasn't standing out there.

"What's going on?" he said, very low.

"Ask him."

"I'm asking you."

"He's not telling me either," said Leah.

Seth came in behind her and stood on her other side without being told, and his face was white.

---

The stranger came up the back steps.

He didn't knock. He stood on the back porch on the other side of the screen door, looking in, and waited. Jacob could see him through the mesh: the black coat, the ears, which didn't fit under the eave and were bent a little sideways by it. The green eyes.

Sue came up the steps behind him and went past him and opened the screen door herself.

"Billy," she said. "Quil."

"Sue," said his dad.

Old Quil said nothing. Old Quil was looking through the screen door at what was standing behind Sue, and his old face, which was mostly wrinkles and a nose, didn't do anything at all.

The stranger ducked and came in.

His ears scraped the top of the back doorframe. They hadn't scraped Sue's last night by accident; Jacob had heard about that too. This time they went flat as he came through, deliberately, and came back up after. He took two steps into the kitchen and stopped with his back to the wall beside the door, where he could see all of them, every door, the window, the hall.

Then his nose moved.

Jacob saw it because he was looking for it. One long, slow pull through the nostrils. The stranger's eyes went to the woodstove in the corner, to the old black iron box with the crack running across the face of its door that his dad had been saying he'd fix since 1994.

The ears didn't move. The face didn't move. The tail, hanging straight down behind him against the black wool, didn't move.

The nose did it once more. Then it stopped.

He looked away from the stove and didn't look at it again.

Jacob, standing right next to the stove with the heat on his back, breathed in too, and smelled it. The stove. Cedar and smoke and hot iron. And six feet away, under the pine and the stone and the cold creek water and whatever the stranger had brought back from Switzerland on his coat, the same thing. Fainter. Like the stove's smell had been washed through a hundred and fifty years of rain and somebody had hung it on a man to dry.

"Mr. Potter," said Billy.

"Mr. Black."

"Coffee?"

"No, thank you."

"Sit down. You're making my kitchen look small."

"I'll stand, Mr. Black. Thank you."

Jacob's dad looked at him for a long moment over his cup. Then he looked at Sue, and Sue sat down in the chair at his left, the one she always took, and folded her hands on the table, and didn't look at anybody.

"Sam's coming," Sue said.

"I can hear him," said the stranger.

Jacob could too. Bare feet on the wet road coming up the hill, walking steady, the way Sam walked when he'd spent the whole night deciding something.

Nobody said anything while they waited. The woodstove ticked. In the living room Paul shifted on the couch and the springs complained. Old Quil sat with both hands on his cane and looked at the stranger, and the stranger looked at nothing, at a spot on the wall over Billy's head, with his paws behind his back.

Jacob had stood in a clearing in June and watched twenty newborn vampires come out of the trees. He'd stood in the Cullens' house three weeks ago while Bella died on a table and came back. He'd never in his life been in a room this quiet.

The front door opened.

Sam came through the living room and stopped in the kitchen doorway. He had a shirt on. A clean one, gray, with the creases still in it from the package. His face was shut tight as a fist.

He looked at the stranger by the back door. The stranger looked back at him.

"Mr. Uley," the stranger said.

Sam's jaw moved.

He came in and stood at the far end of the kitchen by the window, across the table from Jacob, the way he always stood, with his back to the light. He didn't look at Leah. He didn't look at anyone but the thing in the black coat.

Billy set his cup down on the table.

"Well," Billy said. "We're all here."

---

### V.

Sue had sat at this table for two years in her husband's place, and she knew how a council started.

Billy started it. Billy always started it, because it was his kitchen and because Old Quil liked to be the one who finished. Billy would say who was there and why, in his slow voice, looking at his coffee. Then Old Quil would say something that sounded like the weather and wasn't. Then the young men would talk too much, and Sue would let them, and when they were done, the three of them would decide.

Billy put both hands flat on the table.

"Quil Ateara," he said. "Sue Clearwater. Billy Black."

Across the kitchen, by the back door, the wolf's left ear flicked.

Sue was watching for it. She'd told him she would be. She saw it go, once, sharp, the way it had gone at her own table last night, like a horse's ear when a fly lands. Then it was back where it had been, stiff and forward, and nothing else about him had moved.

Beside the stove, Leah's eyes had gone to the ear too. And Jacob's.

Billy didn't see it. Billy was looking at his coffee.

"Sam Uley," Billy went on. "Jacob Black. The Clearwater kids. Paul Lahote, on my couch, with his feet on my table." In the living room, Paul's feet came down off the coffee table with a thump. "And this is Sue's guest. Sue says he has something to tell us. So." Billy looked up. "Tell us."

The wolf took one step forward from the wall. Only one.

"My name is Harry Potter," he said. "I'm a Senior Hit Wizard with the Enforcement Office of the International Confederation of Wizards, in Geneva. I'm here under an assessment warrant issued by the Director of that office. I'm required to tell you that. I'm also required to tell you that nothing said in this room is privileged, and that I'll be writing a report on Wednesday."

The kitchen was very quiet.

Sue had heard him say his name on her back step last night, in the rain, with a fern in his head. *Harry, ma'am. Harry Potter.* The accent had been all over the place. The voice had been rough and a little embarrassed and full of something like a dog that's been told to sit and is trying.

This was the same voice and none of that was in it. He sounded like the man who'd read the will. He sounded like the doctor in Port Angeles.

Old Quil lifted one hand off the head of his cane.

"Sue says you're a wizard," the old man said.

"Yes, sir."

"And a wolf."

"Of a kind."

Old Quil looked at him for a long time, from the brushed black ears down to the black fur on the backs of the forepaws, which were clasped behind him again, and back up.

"The spirit warriors," Old Quil said, "did not wear buttons."

"No, sir."

"Taha Aki's sons are born to this land. They come out of this blood. They don't come from Switzerland in a coat."

"No, sir. I'm not one of Taha Aki's sons."

"Then what are you?"

"Today," the wolf said, "I'm the man Geneva sent."

Old Quil put his hand back on the cane.

The wolf reached under his arm and took the leather case and laid it on the end of the table in front of Billy. He opened it. Inside there was a single sheet of heavy, cream-colored paper, folded once, with a seal on it in dark red wax and a strip of silver ribbon running through the wax. He took the sheet out and unfolded it with his claws, carefully, and laid it flat on the oilcloth facing himself.

"This is a copy of a draft operational order," he said. "It was written in Geneva on Saturday night, on the strength of a memo from a senior officer of the Canadian Ministry, a set of photographs on an American hiking website, and a file on this coast that the Confederation has been keeping since the spring. I saw it for the first time at seven o'clock this morning. I'm required to show it to you. I'm not required to read it to you. I'm going to read it."

Nobody said anything.

He looked down at the paper.

"*Subject,*" he read. "*Unregistered hereditary therianthropic population, Olympic Peninsula, Washington State, North America. Assessed risk to the Statute of Secrecy: severe. Recommended action: remediation.*"

The word went across the table and sat there.

"*Personnel,*" he read. "*Eight Obliviators. Four curse-breakers, Department of Hereditary Bindings. One liaison officer, Magical Congress of the United States of America. Objectives. One. Comprehensive memory modification of the affected community, and of all non-magical persons in the surrounding area with knowledge of the population. Two. Identification of the hereditary inheritance in the affected bloodlines, and its unbinding. Three. Monitoring of the affected community for a period of three generations to confirm the liquidation of the population as a distinct magical category.*"

He stopped.

He looked up from the paper, at the middle of the table, at the place between Billy's coffee cup and the sugar bowl.

"That's the whole of it," he said. "Signed for the Director by his deputy. Countersigned, provisionally, by MACUSA's Department of Magical Security, Washington office."

Sue's hands were flat on the table on either side of her. She didn't remember putting them there.

"What does that mean," said Sam.

His voice came from the window, very low. He hadn't moved. Sue could see the light behind him and his shape against it and nothing of his face.

"It means you'd have gone to bed on Tuesday night and woken up on Wednesday morning not knowing what you are," the wolf said. "You. Him." He didn't point. His eyes went to Jacob, and to Seth, and to the living room doorway, and came back to the middle of the table. "Every one of you who's ever changed. Your families. Your mothers. Your council. Anybody in Forks who's seen something in the trees and wondered. The police chief. You'd wake up with gaps where the last two years had been, and a feeling you'd had a long illness, and nobody would ever tell you what of."

Seth made a small sound.

"Then the curse-breakers," the wolf said. "Whatever it is in your blood that makes you change, they'd find it. It's in there. It's a thread, or something that behaves like one. They'd find it in every one of you with the inheritance, and they'd take it apart."

Sue went cold to the roots of her hair.

*Take it apart.*

She'd sat at her own table at midnight and told him: not one word about taking it apart, not at Billy's table. Old Quil would have a stroke. And here he was at Billy's table with his paws behind his back, saying it in that ironed voice to Old Quil's face, about the whole tribe.

"Thread by thread," the wolf said. "It's slow work. I know how slow, because I've done the work it's built on. When they were finished, the ones who'd already changed would never change again. The ones who hadn't, wouldn't. And every child born on this reservation from next spring on would be born without it." He paused. "Three generations, they'd watch you, to make sure. In three generations, Taha Aki would be a story your great-grandchildren tell the tourists at First Beach, and not one of them would know it had ever been true."

Old Quil's hands had gone white on the head of the cane.

Nobody in the kitchen was breathing. Sue could hear the woodstove tick. She could hear, from the living room, Paul's breath going in and out through his mouth.

"You have no idea," the wolf said, "how close you came to being liquidated."

Sue sat with her hands flat on the oilcloth and thought about the last two years.

*Gaps where the last two years had been.* Two years ago this spring, her daughter had come home from a run on the beach burning with a fever the thermometer at the tribal clinic couldn't read, and screamed at her father in their kitchen, and come apart into fur in front of him, and Harry Clearwater had put one hand on his chest and sat down on the kitchen floor and not gotten up again. That was inside the two years. All of it was inside the two years. The kitchen floor. The ambulance that took forty minutes from Forks. Seth on the porch that night, fourteen, shaking so hard the boards rattled. Seth in the yard a week later, burning, and then not Seth. The funeral. Leah at the funeral with her jaw set and a fever of a hundred and eight under her black dress.

They'd have taken that.

She'd have woken up on a Wednesday morning a widow and not known how. She'd have stood at her husband's grave and wondered what his last day had been like, and nobody on earth would ever be able to tell her, because nobody on earth would remember. Her children would have looked at her across the breakfast table with gaps in them the same shape as hers.

She'd have been grateful, maybe. That was the worst of it. Some nights in the last two years she would have given anything to forget the kitchen floor.

She'd never once thought somebody might offer.

---

Billy spoke first. Sue would remember that: Billy, who'd sat in that chair since the diabetes took his legs, who'd watched his son go into the woods and come out a wolf and had been quiet about it the way he was quiet about everything.

"Who stopped it?" Billy said.

"I did."

"How?"

The wolf looked at the paper on the table. Then he folded it, once, along the crease it already had, and laid it back in the leather case, and closed the case.

"I went to Geneva at seven o'clock this morning," he said, "and I walked into the Director's office without an appointment. I put my name on this assessment. My rank. My record. And an award the British Ministry gave me when I was eighteen, which in that building still means something to a certain kind of man. I told the Director that if a single Obliviator set foot on this coast before my report was on his desk, I'd resign the same day, and I'd take my reasons to every wizarding newspaper in Europe, and that I was the sort of person those newspapers would print." His voice didn't change at all. "He suspended the order."

"Suspended," said Billy.

"It's in a drawer in his desk. If my report on Wednesday says this coast is under control, it stays in the drawer. If my report says this coast is a mess, somebody opens the drawer."

"And if you don't write the report?"

"Then somebody else writes it. Somebody who's never met a wolf who can talk. And they open the drawer."

Billy sat back in his chair.

"You spent something," Billy said slowly, "to get that drawer shut."

"Yes."

"What?"

The wolf didn't answer for a moment.

"Things I won't get back," he said. "That's mine to worry about, Mr. Black. It's not on your table."

"You knew about this last night." That was Jacob, from beside the stove. His voice had come out rough and too loud for the room. "On the Cullens' lawn. In Sue's kitchen. You knew they were going to—"

"No."

"You *had* to know."

"I knew Geneva was nervous. I knew a woman in Ottawa had drawn a circle around a word. I didn't know anyone had put pen to paper until seven o'clock this morning." The wolf's eyes went to Jacob for the first time. "When I found out, I went."

"And came back here dressed for a funeral."

"Yes," the wolf said. "That was the idea."

Jacob's mouth shut.

Leah, beside him, hadn't moved since Billy said his own name. Sue looked at her daughter across the kitchen. Leah's arms were crossed so tight her knuckles had gone pale against her sleeves, and she was staring at the wolf by the back door the way she'd stared at Sam the morning he came back from the woods: like she was looking at someone wearing a face she knew.

The wolf wasn't looking at her. He hadn't looked at her once since they came in.

At the window, Sam moved.

He came away from the light and around the end of the table, slowly, and stopped about six feet from the wolf, between him and the table, with his arms at his sides. The two of them were the tallest things in the kitchen, except Jacob. Sam's shoulders were going under the clean gray shirt.

"So that's it," Sam said. "You came here so we'd thank you."

"I came here so you'd listen," said the wolf.

### VI.

Sam had spent the night not sleeping.

He'd lain beside Emily in the dark with her back against his chest and the rain on the roof, and the stranger's voice had gone around and around in his head like a fly in a jar. *A functioning one. Nose switched on.* And *worth the trade*, which he'd turned over and over until it was worn smooth, and every time he'd felt for the answer, the answer had been *yes*. Yes, she was worth it. Yes, he'd do it again. It came up out of him the way water comes up out of the ground, instant and certain.

And around four in the morning he'd lain there and wondered, for the first time, how much of *yes* was him.

He hated the stranger for that. He hated him more than for the porch.

Now he stood six feet from him in Billy's kitchen, and the stranger stood with his paws behind his back and his ears locked forward and looked at him like he was a form somebody had filled out wrong.

"Listen to what?" Sam said.

"Mr. Lahote," said the stranger.

He didn't raise his voice. He didn't turn his head. He said it at the same level he'd said everything else, toward the living room doorway, and in the living room the couch springs went, and Paul came and stood in the doorway with his arms crossed and his chin out.

"What."

"Last night, from Mrs. Clearwater's porch, I told you to stay off the 101."

"Yeah," said Paul. "I heard. Everybody heard."

"You thought it was funny."

Paul's mouth twitched. He looked at Sam. Sam didn't look back. Paul had told it at the store, and at Emily's back door, and on Jared's front step, and Sam knew because Paul couldn't keep a thing in his head for ten seconds without it falling out of his mouth, and Embry had texted half the reservation before sunrise.

"It was kind of funny," Paul said.

"There's a photograph of you on a corkboard in an office on the fourth floor of the Confederation building in Geneva." The stranger's voice didn't change. "Gray wolf in a clearing off Highway 101, a mile and a half south of the Sol Duc turnoff, in the sun, at two-fifteen on a Tuesday afternoon in July. There's a red pin through it. It's the first thing anyone sees when they walk into that office. I saw it at seven this morning. It's why there's a drawer." He paused. "When I tell you to stay off the fucking 101, Mr. Lahote, it means stay off the fucking 101."

The word sat in Billy Black's kitchen in front of Old Quil and Sue Clearwater like something dropped on the floor.

Paul's face went dark red under the brown. "It was *one time*."

"It takes one time."

"I was chasing a—"

"I don't care what you were chasing."

Paul opened his mouth. He looked at Sam.

"Paul," Sam said. "Sit down."

Paul looked at him for a second longer. Then he went back to the couch, and Sam heard the springs, and heard him not put his feet on the coffee table.

Sam turned back to the stranger.

"My wolves," he said, "are my business."

"Your wolves are on a corkboard in Geneva."

"We've kept this secret a hundred and fifty years." Sam heard his own voice getting bigger and couldn't stop it. "From the whites. From the government. From the Cullens and every bloodsucker that came through these woods before them. We know how to hide. We were hiding before your people had a Switzerland."

The stranger's eyes came to his face.

They were green. Sam had noticed that last night on the porch, in the light. They looked painted on. They were the only thing in the stranger's face that had moved in ten minutes, and they moved now, and fixed on him, and stayed.

"You hid from people who didn't believe in you," the stranger said. "My people hid from people who did."

Nobody said anything.

"They burned us," the stranger said. "Four hundred years ago, give or take. In England, in Scotland, in Germany. Most of the grown witches got away; there's a charm for fire, and anyone with a wand who knew it could stand in the flames and feel a tickle. The ones who didn't get away were the children. The ones who'd done one strange thing in front of the wrong neighbour and didn't have a wand yet, or didn't know the charm. Muggle-born kids whose own parents handed them over. The neighbours who'd been accused of it and couldn't do a thing, and burned anyway." His voice was flat as a table. "After a hundred years of that, my people sat down in a room and signed a law that says no one outside of us can ever know we exist. That's the Statute of Secrecy. It was signed in 1689. Three years later, the Americans had Salem."

Old Quil's eyes had narrowed to slits.

"They're not past it," the stranger said. "None of them. A girl named Ariana Dumbledore was six years old when three Muggle boys saw her do magic over a garden wall in a village in England. That was the eighteen-nineties. The boys went over the wall. What they did to her broke something in her she never got back. Her father went after them and died in prison for it, because the alternative was telling the Ministry what was wrong with her and having her locked up for the rest of her life. She died at fourteen." He didn't look away from Sam. "Her brother was the greatest wizard of the last century. He taught me. He never spoke about her. I found out from a book after he was dead."

The woodstove ticked.

"That's the people you're dealing with," the stranger said. "Four hundred years since the burnings, and my lot are still so frightened that they shoot first and ask questions after. They've earned that fear. Every bit of it. They'll do it to you with a team of Obliviators and a form, and they'll call it remediation, and they'll sleep fine. That's what's in the drawer."

---

"That's easy for you to say."

It came from the stove. Jacob. Sam turned his head.

Jacob was standing with his back to the iron and his arms crossed and his face hot. His shoulders were going. Leah was beside him, very still, and something about her had changed; she'd gone a shade paler, and she was looking at Jacob like she wanted to put a hand over his mouth.

"You walk in here in a uniform," Jacob said, "with your badge and your award and your Chocolate Frog card—"

Leah made a sound.

"—and you tell us how scared everybody is, and how you saved us, and how we should be grateful, and you've never lived one day like we've lived it. Never once had to hide what you are from your own—"

The stranger snarled.

Sam had been a wolf for three years. He'd heard every sound a wolf can make. He'd made most of them. He'd never heard one come out of a man's chest in a kitchen with the coffee on.

It came up out of him low and enormous and all at once, a sound like a truck downshifting on a grade, and his lips came back off his teeth. All the way back. Sam saw every one of them. Long and white and too many, the canines longer than Sam's thumb, and the black gums, and the muzzle wrinkling up in ridges to the eyes.

Every wolf in the room moved.

Sam felt the heat go up his own back like gasoline catching. Jacob came off the stove. In the living room Paul was on his feet, and Seth had stepped back against the wall so hard a picture frame rattled. Leah didn't move. Leah was the only wolf in the room who didn't move.

Old Quil didn't move either.

Sam saw it out of the corner of his eye, and it would stay with him. Eighty-some years old, with a cane, at a kitchen table four feet from a snarling thing that could have taken his head off his neck in one bite. The old man sat with both hands folded on the head of the cane and looked at the stranger's teeth the way he looked at weather coming in off the ocean. Like he'd seen it before. Like he was deciding whether it was worth bringing in the boats.

The stranger jerked his head to the side.

Hard. Abrupt. Like a dog snapping its head away from something it's been told not to bite. His muzzle went toward the window, and his ears went flat to his skull for one second, and the snarl cut off as if somebody had shut a door on it.

He stood like that, with his head turned away, looking at the gray light in the window over the sink. His paws were still behind his back. Sam could see his ribs going under the black coat.

Then he turned his head back.

His ears came back up, slowly, to where they'd been. His lips came down over his teeth. When he spoke, his voice was the same as before, ironed flat, and somehow that was worse.

"For fuck's sake, Jacob," he said. "I gave up more than you can comprehend before I was even a man, so that people like you could live free. I'm not going to stand in your father's kitchen and list it for you."

Jacob's mouth was open. Nothing came out of it.

"And you're right," the stranger said. "I've never lived one day the way you have. I've never been a Quileute wolf. I don't know what it costs. I know what the next part costs, though, so I'll tell you that instead."

He looked at Sam. At Old Quil. At Billy.

"You have no idea how precarious this is," he said. "None. You think you're tucked away at the end of the world on your own land, with your own laws, with a treaty. Do you honestly think MACUSA gives one single shit what treaty you've signed with the Muggle government?"

Old Quil's cane came up an inch off the floor and came down again, hard.

"We are the Quileute Nation." The old man's voice was thin and high and very clear. "Our people signed the treaty at Olympia with the governor in eighteen fifty-five. This land is ours by order of the President of the United States. It has been ours since before there was a United States. No council in Switzerland—"

"Your treaty is with Washington, sir." The stranger didn't raise his voice over him; he waited until the old man stopped, and then went on at the same level. "MACUSA doesn't answer to Washington. It never has. It doesn't recognize Washington in any way that matters to you. It has its own president, its own laws, its own police, and its own prisons, and every one of them is hidden from the government you signed with. To MACUSA, this reservation is a piece of Muggle land with unregistered creatures living on it. That's all. Your treaty is a piece of paper in a Muggle filing cabinet, and nobody in MACUSA has ever read it, and nobody ever will."

"Then let them come," said Sam.

The stranger looked at him.

"In 1926," he said, "MACUSA sentenced two people to death in an afternoon. One of them was their own officer. No trial. They were walked to a room under the building and strapped into a chair over a pool of potion and very nearly executed before anyone asked a second question. They got out by luck." His ears didn't move. "Those were wizards, Mr. Uley. Their own kind. Inside their own walls. You're not inside anybody's walls. You're a wolf in a photograph."

Sam's hands were fists again. He couldn't make them be anything else.

"And if you think that was a long time ago," the stranger said, "my people have had two wars about exactly this in the last hundred years. Back to back. Grindelwald wanted to tear up the Statute and rule over the Muggles for their own good, and he burned half of Europe trying, and the Muggles never even knew why their war got so much worse in 1940. Then Voldemort. He wanted the Muggles under his heel and every witch and wizard with a Muggle parent registered, rounded up, and put on trial for stealing magic. Both wars were bloody. Both wars were about who knows what, and who's on top when they find out." The green eyes didn't blink. "The last one ended eight years ago, at my school, in one night. Fifty-odd dead on the floor of the Great Hall. I knew most of them. Some of them weren't much older than him." His chin moved a fraction toward Seth, against the wall. "So don't tell me what my people would or wouldn't do to keep a secret. I've counted the bodies."

There was no sound in the kitchen at all.

Sam stood there in his clean gray shirt with his fists shaking and looked at the stranger, and the stranger looked back at him, and there was nothing in his face. No apology in it anywhere. No pity, either. He looked at Sam the way Sam had once seen a doctor in Port Angeles look at an X-ray of Emily's face.

Sam wanted to hit him. He wanted it so badly his teeth ached.

He understood, standing there, that he believed every word.

He hated that more than anything else that had happened in two days.

"You want us scared," Sam said.

"I want you careful," said the stranger. "Scared is what you'll be if that drawer opens. By then it won't help."

Out of the corner of his eye, Sam saw Leah.

She was standing by the stove where she'd been the whole time, beside Jacob, with her arms crossed. She was looking at the stranger. Sam had two years of practice not looking at Leah Clearwater, and he did it now out of habit, and so he saw it only sideways: her face, which had gone so still it looked carved.

The stranger hadn't looked at her once. Not when he came in. Not when he read the order. Not when he snarled. His eyes had gone to every person in this kitchen, one after another, and passed over her like she was a cupboard door.

Sam knew what that looked like from the inside. He'd done it for two years. He knew exactly how hard it was.

He didn't want to know that about the stranger. He looked away.

### VII.

Billy Black had a tin on his lap.

It was under the blanket, where nobody could see it, a flat old tobacco tin with the paint worn off the lid down to the metal, and it had been sitting on the top shelf of his closet since his grandmother died in 1971. He'd taken it down that morning after Jacob asked about the trader, and sat on the edge of his bed with it for twenty minutes, and hadn't opened it. Then he'd put it on his lap under the blanket and wheeled himself out to eat his burned eggs, and it had been there ever since.

He could feel the corners of it through the wool against his thighs, where he could still feel anything.

He'd been watching the stranger for half an hour.

He couldn't smell what Jacob smelled. Billy had never phased; the blood had skipped him the way it skipped most men, and gone straight to his son. What Billy had was sixty years of watching men in rooms. Fishermen in the co-op office when the quotas came down. Tribal council when the casino money came in, and when it didn't. Charlie Swan at the kitchen table at two in the morning the night Bella went into the woods and didn't come out. Harry Clearwater, on this porch, the week before his heart stopped, saying *I'm fine, Billy, quit looking at me like that.*

He knew what a man looked like when he'd decided something and was going to carry it whether it broke him or not.

The stranger had his paws behind his back. He'd had them there since he walked in. Billy had watched them. When the old man said *treaty* they'd tightened on each other. When Jacob said *Chocolate Frog*, whatever that was, the claws had come out, all ten, and gone back in. When the stranger snarled at Billy's son in Billy's kitchen, they'd been clenched so hard the black fur on the backs of them stood up.

And the stranger had turned his head away.

Billy had seen a lot of big men lose their tempers in small rooms. He'd seen exactly two in his life turn their heads away in the middle of it. One of them was Sam, the night after Emily. The other one was standing by his back door.

"What do you want from us," said Old Quil.

The old man's voice was dry as a paper bag. He'd had his hands on his cane through all of it. He hadn't looked at anybody but the stranger since he sat down.

"Five things," said the stranger.

"Only five."

"For now, sir."

Old Quil's mouth did something that on a younger man would have been a smile and on Quil Ateara was a warning.

"Go on."

"One." The stranger didn't take his paws from behind his back to count. "No wolf changes within sight of a public road, a trail, a campground, or a beach, in daylight. Ever. Your patrols run in the backcountry. Mr. Uley and Mr. Black draw the routes, and this council keeps the map. You keep it."

Sam's jaw moved. He said nothing.

"Two. Mr. Lahote stays off the 101."

From the living room, a strangled noise.

"Three." The stranger looked at the middle of the table. "Every wolf who's ever changed, living, on a list. Name. Age. Color. Size. The date he first changed. Kept by this council, in this house, written by your hand, Mr. Black. It doesn't leave La Push. It doesn't go to Geneva. Geneva gets a number." He paused. "And when the next boy changes, and he will, and he'll be younger than the last one, he goes on it the same week."

The kitchen went cold.

Billy felt it go. He felt Sue go stiff beside him, and the old man on his other side, and the air coming off Sam by the window turn hot.

"You want a list," Old Quil said, very slowly, "of our children."

"I want you to keep one, sir. So that nobody else ever has to make one."

"That's the same thing."

"The difference is the drawer," the stranger said.

"Our people have had lists made of them before." The old man's voice had gone up and thin. "By the agents. By the schools. By the churches. Names written down by white men in offices, and then the children taken away to Chemawa and Cushman and never—" He stopped. His hands shook on the cane. "You come to this table and ask us to write our own grandchildren down for you."

"For you, sir," the stranger said. "Written down for you. Kept by you. So that the next time a man from Geneva sits in this kitchen, you can put a number on the table and close the book, and he can go home and write that the Quileute council knows exactly what it has and keeps its own house." His eyes didn't leave the middle of the table. "I know what lists have meant to your people. I'm asking anyway. That's for your own good, and you'll hate it, and I'm still asking."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Billy looked at Sue. Sue was staring at her own hands on the oilcloth.

"Four," the stranger said.

"There's more," said Sam.

"Four. A liaison. One officer of the Confederation with this council's leave to come onto this land. Once a year he sits at this table and you give him the number. If anything goes wrong in between, a boy changes in front of a tourist, a photograph turns up, a hiker goes missing, you call him before you call anybody else on earth, and he fixes it before Geneva hears about it."

"You," said Sam.

"If you'll have me."

"We won't."

"Then Geneva picks," the stranger said. "You won't like who they pick."

Sam opened his mouth. Billy looked at him, and Sam shut it.

"Five." The stranger's voice didn't change, but his eyes moved for the first time, from the middle of the table to Jacob. "The Cullens leave Forks by the spring. Dr. Cullen agreed at eleven o'clock this morning, on his own front step. That's my side of the coast, and I'll hold him to it. Their child stays inside their walls until there's a story about her that holds up to a pediatrician and a school secretary."

Jacob had gone gray.

Billy watched it happen. He watched his son hear *leave Forks* and understand what it meant, the way you watch a man step off a curb into traffic he didn't see. Jacob's hands came uncrossed. His mouth opened.

"Leave," Jacob said. "Leave to where?"

"That's between you and them."

"You can't just—she's—I can't—"

"That's between you and them, Jacob," the stranger said. "It isn't on this table."

Jacob looked at him like he'd been hit. Then he looked at Billy. Billy didn't have anything for him. He'd known since the morning Jacob came home and said *her name's Renesmee* that this day was coming, in some shape. He hadn't known it would come in a black coat.

"And the police chief," said Sue.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't spoken since the stranger read the paper. She was still looking at her hands.

"That's six," Sue said. "You said five. But you'll have something to say about Charlie. You said it in my kitchen last night."

The stranger looked at her for the first time since he'd come in the back door.

Billy saw it. One look. Level, flat, nothing in it.

"Mr. Swan is this council's to decide, Mrs. Clearwater," he said. "Within the week. What he's told, and who tells him. If he finds out on his own before you decide, he's mine."

Sue's jaw tightened.

"I'll decide by Saturday," she said.

"Thank you."

"I'm not doing it for you."

"No, ma'am."

Old Quil was looking at the stranger with his old black eyes narrowed so far they'd nearly disappeared.

"And if we say no," the old man said. "To all of it."

"Then I write that you said no, sir. And why. And I write it as fairly as the truth will let me." The stranger's ears didn't move. "And on Wednesday morning the Director reads it, and he opens the drawer, or he doesn't."

"Which will he do?"

"He'll open it."

Old Quil sat back in his chair.

---

"When do you need an answer," Billy said.

"Tuesday night. My report goes to the Director at nine on Wednesday morning, Geneva time. That's midnight Tuesday here."

"Two days."

"Two days, Mr. Black."

Billy nodded slowly. He drank some of his coffee, which had gone cold. He set the cup down. Under the blanket, his hand was resting on the lid of the tin.

"Mr. Potter," he said. "I've been listening to you for half an hour. You want to know what I heard?"

The stranger's eyes came to him.

"Yes, sir."

"A man telling us what he's afraid of."

The kitchen was very quiet.

The stranger looked at Billy for a long moment. His ears stayed exactly where they were. Behind his back, Billy could see the edge of one forepaw, and the claws had come out again, slowly, and stayed out.

"Yes," the stranger said.

"Of them? Or for us?"

"Both, Mr. Black."

Billy nodded again. He'd thought so.

Old Quil lifted his cane off the floor and set it down, once, the way he did when a council was finished with one part and starting another.

"We'll talk now," the old man said. "The three of us. Sam. Jacob. You stay. The rest of you, out." He didn't look at the stranger. "You too. You've said your piece. We'll say ours without you in the room."

"Sir."

The stranger picked up the leather case from the end of the table and put it under his arm. He turned to the back door. He put one forepaw on the handle of the screen.

Billy watched him go, and the tin sat on his lap under the blanket, and he'd meant to wait. He'd told Jacob *after the council*. He'd told himself all morning, sitting on the edge of his bed with the thing in his hands and the lid shut, that he'd wait until this business was done and the room was empty and he could look at the stranger across a table with nobody else in it.

The stranger pushed the screen door open, and ducked, and the gray light came in around him.

"Mr. Potter," Billy said.

The stranger stopped in the doorway with his back to the room.

"My son," Billy said, "says you smell like my kitchen."

Nobody in the room moved.

Billy saw it from where he sat. The left ear. It flicked once, sharp, the way Sue had told him on the phone at eight o'clock that morning it would. Then it went flat to the stranger's skull, and stayed flat for a long second, and came back up.

The stranger didn't turn around.

"Tuesday, Mr. Black," he said.

He went out. The screen door banged behind him on its spring.

Billy sat at the head of the table with his hand on the tin under the blanket and listened to him go down the back steps without any sound at all, and across the wet grass of the yard, toward the trees, and stop.

"Tuesday," Billy said quietly, to nobody.

Leah was already moving toward the front door.

---

### VIII.

Quil was still sitting on the front steps when Leah came out, with his elbows on his knees and his phone in his hand and his face screwed up like he'd been trying to hear through the wall and hadn't quite managed it.

"What happened?" he said. "Paul's being weird. Paul's never weird. Paul's just loud—"

"Ask your grandpa," said Leah, and went down the steps past him and around the side of the house.

The grass along the side of Billy's house was long and soaking. It came up over the tops of Marg's daughter's boots and wet her jeans to the knee. She went past the kitchen window without looking in, past the propane tank, past the open door of the garage where Jacob's Rabbit sat up on blocks with its hood open, the garage where Bella Swan had spent a whole winter falling off motorcycles. It smelled of motor oil and cold concrete and Jacob. She came around the back corner into Billy's yard.

He was at the bottom of it.

Billy's yard ran downhill from the back steps to the trees, fifty feet of rough wet grass with an old chopping block in the middle and a rusted wheelbarrow nobody had moved in years. At the bottom, where the grass gave up and the salal started and the spruce went up dark into the fog, he was standing.

He had his back to the house. His paws were behind his back. The leather case was on the ground at his feet. He was looking into the trees, straight ahead, at nothing, and he was absolutely still. In the black coat, in the fog, at the edge of the woods, he was a tall dark shape standing where the light stopped, outside a house where people were talking about him.

Leah stopped at the top of the yard.

She knew that. She knew exactly that. She'd done it every night for a year, at the edge of the Cullens' lawn, with her arms crossed, in the rain, outside a lit glass wall. She'd never once seen what it looked like from the house.

It looked lonely. That was what it looked like. It looked like somebody who'd decided where he was allowed to stand.

She went down the yard.

The back door opened behind her before she'd gone halfway, and somebody came down the steps fast and light. Seth. He caught up to her by the chopping block and stopped, and looked down the yard at the black coat, and didn't go any closer.

"Seth," Leah said. "Go sit with Quil."

"In a second."

He didn't move. He was looking at Harry's back.

"Harry," Seth said. His voice came out smaller than Seth's voice ever came out. "Was it true? The forgetting thing. All of us. Mom."

Harry didn't turn around.

"Yes," he said.

"And the—the fifty people. At your school."

"Yes."

Seth stood there in his hoodie with his hands jammed in the pocket. He opened his mouth to ask something else. Leah watched him think about it, and decide not to.

"Okay," Seth said.

He turned around and went back up the yard and around the corner of the house toward the front, toward Quil and the steps, walking slower than she'd ever seen her brother walk anywhere.

"He shouldn't have been in that room," Harry said, to the trees.

It was the first thing he'd said that wasn't an answer.

"He's a wolf," said Leah. "It's his room."

"Ja," Harry said, after a moment. "It is."

He didn't say anything else about it. His ears didn't move. But Leah had been watching the back of his head for a while now, and she saw the fur at the nape of his neck lift, very slightly, over the black collar, and lie down again.

Leah went the rest of the way down the yard.

She stopped beside him at the edge of the grass, a couple of feet away, with the salal at her shins and the spruce going up into the fog in front of both of them. She looked at the trees, because he was looking at the trees.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

Behind them, through the kitchen wall, she could hear the council. Old Quil's voice, thin and high. Sam's, low and getting louder. Her mother's, very quiet, cutting through both. She could have heard every word if she let herself.

"Miss Clear—" he began.

"Don't you *dare*."

He stopped.

---

"You didn't look at me once," Leah said.

"No."

"The whole time. Not once. An hour."

"Fifty-two minutes."

She turned her head and stared at the side of his face. He was still looking at the trees.

"You *counted*?"

"Yes."

"Oh my God." She looked back at the trees. Her face was hot again, and it wasn't the good kind. "You counted. You stood in there not looking at me, and you *counted* it."

He didn't say anything.

"You called Jake by his first name," she said. "Everybody else was *Mr.* this and *Mr.* that, and *sir*, and *ma'am*, and you called him *Jacob*, and you said *for fuck's sake* to him in front of his dad."

"Yes."

"You snarled at him. In Billy's kitchen."

"Yes."

"Paul's never going to shut up about it."

"Paul's going to stay off the 101."

She made a noise in her throat that was nowhere near a laugh.

"You said *take it apart*," she said. "In front of Old Quil. Mom told you. Last night. At the table. She said not one word about taking it apart at Billy's table, and you stood there and said it to his face, about all of us."

"Your mother told me not to say I'd take an imprint apart," he said. "I didn't say that."

"You told them you know *how*."

"I do know how."

"You told them they'd do it to us. To *Seth*. Thread by thread." She heard her voice crack and didn't stop. "And the list. You stood there and asked Old Quil to write down his grandkids for you, and you *knew*, you knew about the schools, you knew what he was going to say before he said it, I watched you know it—"

"Yes."

"You made him say it."

"He said it," Harry said. "I asked him anyway."

Leah stood there with her arms crossed so tight her shoulders hurt.

"Are you going to say sorry?" she said. "For any of it?"

He was quiet for a moment. The fog moved in the trees in front of them.

"No," he said.

She'd known. She'd known since the yard at home, since *I'm saying it once*. She'd known since the clearing in Atlin, if she was honest, since he'd stood over a dead man and said *told you twice*. She asked anyway, the way you push on a bruise.

"Every word I said in there was true," he said. "And it needed saying to that room in that voice. If I'd said it the way I talk to you, with my ears doing whatever my ears do and a joke on the end, Sam would have heard the joke. Old Quil would have heard a boy in a coat. They'd have gone home thinking Geneva was a long way off and the man it sent was soft." He didn't look at her. "Geneva's not a long way off. It's in a drawer."

"I know," Leah said.

"Then you know why."

"I know why. I still hate it."

"Good," he said. "You're supposed to."

---

She didn't say anything for a long time.

Behind them, through the wall, Sam's voice had gone up. She heard her own name in it, once, and then Jacob's voice cutting across it, and then Old Quil's cane hitting the floor.

She didn't listen. She looked at the side of his head instead. At the brushed fur, and the ear, stiff and forward, and the high black collar buttoned to the throat.

"Billy asked what it cost you," she said. "In Geneva. You said it wasn't on the table."

"It isn't."

"I'm not at the table."

He was quiet.

"What did it cost you, Harry?"

He breathed out through his nose, long.

"The Director's got my Order of Merlin in his desk," he said. "In the drawer. On top of the order. I took it off my coat and put it down in front of him and told him to hold it till Wednesday. If I'm wrong about you lot, if my report's wrong, or you say no, or a boy changes on a beach in front of a busload of Germans before Wednesday, he keeps it. And my badge with it."

Leah turned her head and stared at him.

"You gave him your *medal*."

"I left it with him."

"Like a—" She stopped.

She thought about a cabin in Atlin. Standing in a doorway with her hand on the frame and a gray coat around her shoulders, saying *so you come back*.

"Collateral," she said.

His head turned.

For the first time since the yard at home that morning, since the kitchen window, since fifty-two minutes in Billy Black's kitchen, he looked at her.

"Ja," he said.

It was one syllable. That was all. But the vowel landed somewhere south of the equator, where it always landed, and it went through her like the first hot water out of a tap in a cold house.

She didn't let her face do anything about it. She was still angry. She was going to be angry for a while.

He was looking at her. And as she watched, something in him came undone, one piece at a time, the way the fog was coming apart in the trees. His ears came off forward. The left one tipped sideways a little, and stayed there, and then the right one followed it. His shoulders came down an inch under the black wool. Behind him, the tail, which had hung dead straight all day like something nailed to his back, moved. Once. A slow short sweep across the wet grass.

He brought his paws around from behind his back, finally, and looked at them like he'd forgotten he had them. The claws were out. He made them go in.

Then he reached up and undid the top silver button of the black coat, at his throat, under the globe pin, and breathed in like a man who'd come up from the bottom of a lake.

"*Eish*," he said, quietly, to the trees.

Leah watched him.

"Are you back?" she said.

"Mostly." His voice was rough. "It takes a bit."

"How long's a bit?"

"Depends how long I was gone." He rubbed the back of his neck with one paw. "Today, I'd say about a sandwich."

She stared at him.

"I haven't eaten since your mum's hamburger," he said.

"You turned down Billy's coffee."

"I wasn't going to eat anything in that room."

"Why not?"

"Because you don't eat at the table of people you've come to tell bad news." He said it plainly, like it was something everybody knew. "Not if you want them to believe it."

Leah thought about that.

She thought about him at her mother's table last night, ten patties, working through them one at a time like a machine at a sawmill. She thought about Billy pushing the cup across. *Coffee?* And the ironed voice. *No, thank you.*

"You're unbelievable," she said.

"Hear that a lot."

It was the first thing he'd said all day that sounded like him all the way through. She hated how much she'd needed to hear it. She looked back at the trees so he wouldn't see what her face was doing.

---

"Billy's got something," she said, after a while.

His left ear flicked.

"Under his blanket. In his lap. The whole time." She didn't look at him. "A tin. Old tobacco tin. I smelled it when I walked in. Rusty metal, and old tobacco, and paper, and the inside of a closet nobody's opened in thirty years. He had his hand on it the whole time you were talking."

"I smelled it."

"What's in it?"

"Don't know."

"Harry."

"I don't know, Leah. I smelled a tin." His ears had gone back a little. "I smelled your Alpha on the Cullens' lawn last night and he smelled like a kitchen I'd never been in. I walked into that kitchen today and it smelled like your Alpha. And the bloke who owns it called it out across the room in front of his whole council, and I've got a feeling about it I don't like, and that's all I've got. A smell and a feeling."

"And a name," said Leah.

He didn't answer that.

"It's a crazy thing to think," she said.

"Ja," he said. "It is."

"I've been sitting on it since Atlin."

"I know. I could see you sitting on it." His tail moved once. "I told you, I don't tell you things I'm guessing at."

"Tuesday," Leah said.

"Tuesday."

Behind them, through the kitchen wall, Old Quil said something sharp, and Sam said *no*, very loud, and Billy said something low that she couldn't make out and didn't try to.

She could hear all of it. If she let herself, she could hear every word. The council of her people, in Billy Black's kitchen, deciding whether to write her brother's name down in a book.

She glanced up at him.

His ears had gone flat. Both of them, all the way back, pressed tight against his skull and held there. He was doing it on purpose. She could see the muscles in the base of them working to keep them down.

"You can hear them," she said.

"Ja."

"And you're not listening."

"It's not mine to hear." He looked at the trees. "That's the one room on this coast that isn't mine. They should get to have it."

Leah looked at him for a long time. At the flattened ears, and the undone button, and the brushed fur already starting to come out of its brushing at the neck. At a man who'd stood in a kitchen for fifty-two minutes and said every hard thing there was to say and not taken back one word, and was now standing at the bottom of the yard holding his own ears down so he wouldn't overhear what they thought of him.

She didn't forgive him. That would take a while. She'd let him know when.

She couldn't flatten her ears. They were human ears; they didn't do anything. So she did the only thing she could think of to drown out the voices coming through the wall.

She started humming.

It was the first thing that came into her head. She knew every word of it secondhand and hated every one, and it came out of her low and flat and completely off-key, the stomping beat and the stupid guitar part and all.

Beside her, his tail stopped. Then it moved again, a long slow sweep through the wet grass, and kept going.

"That's my song," he said.

"Shut up," said Leah. "It's Jake's."

She hummed it anyway, badly, standing beside him at the edge of the trees outside a house where people were deciding things about both of them, with her arms crossed and her jaw set and her shoulder six inches from the black wool of his sleeve.

For two years she'd been the only one who stood out here.

Now there were two of them, and she was still furious, and the fog was coming apart in the spruce, and behind them in the kitchen her people were arguing about whether to write their children down. She hummed louder, so she wouldn't hear it. He held his ears down.

They stood there, and waited for the back door to open.
