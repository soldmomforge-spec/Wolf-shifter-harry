# Live Capture

## Chapter One: Contained

---

### I. Aisle Four

*Forks, Washington — Monday, October 2, 2006*

The list was in his mom's handwriting, which meant half of it was code.

Seth Clearwater stood in the produce section of the Thriftway with a red plastic basket hooked over one forearm and the list held up to the fluorescent lights like it might give up its secrets if he squinted hard enough. Rain ticked against the big front windows. Somebody's wet umbrella dripped in the rack by the carts. The whole store smelled like floor cleaner, bananas, and the damp wool of every coat that had come through the door since seven a.m.

*Eggs (2 doz). Milk. Flour — NOT self-rising. Bacon (lots). Bread x2. Potatoes 10#. Coffee, red can. Something green, Seth.*

The last line was underlined twice.

He grabbed a bag of potatoes in one hand like it weighed nothing, because to him it didn't, and dropped it in the basket. The basket handle creaked. He picked up a head of broccoli, looked at it, and put it in too, mostly so he could tell his mom he had.

He'd been awake for something like thirty hours. Since the baby came, since Nessie, the patrol schedule had turned into a joke nobody was laughing at. Jacob basically lived at the Cullens' now. He slept on their couch, ate their food, and stared at a three-week-old kid who already looked about six months old like she'd personally hung the moon. Which, fine. Seth got it, sort of. Imprinting was imprinting. But it meant the actual running of the actual pack fell to him and Leah, and Leah ran patrols the way she did everything lately, angry and fast and without talking unless she had something mean to say.

His stomach growled loud enough that a lady by the apples looked over.

"Sorry," Seth said. "Long day."

She gave him the look people in Forks gave the La Push boys now, the one that took in the size of him, the T-shirt in October rain, the cutoffs, the bare arms, and decided not to ask. He'd gotten taller again over the summer. He'd turned eighteen in August, which meant his mom had stopped asking where he was going and started asking when he'd be back. He was six-four now, maybe a little more, and still filling out, and he banged his head on things in the house he'd grown up in.

Dairy. Eggs. He did the eggs carefully, because the last time he'd done them fast he'd crushed a carton in his fist without noticing and Leah had laughed at him for a week.

Bacon. He took four packs. Lots was lots.

Aisle four had the coffee and the baking stuff, and it was empty except for a woman standing on the bottom shelf.

She had one sneaker wedged on the lip of the lowest shelf next to the sacks of sugar, and she was stretched up as far as she could go with her fingers just brushing the edge of a box of kosher salt on the very top. Her cart was parked beside her. Seth noticed the cart before he noticed anything else, because it was weird. A ten-pound bag of rock salt. Two boxes of white chalk, the cheap school kind. A bag of tea lights. A carton of eggs. A jar of honey. Coffee. A box of something called Earl Grey. And three more boxes of kosher salt.

"You want a hand?" he said.

"I've got it," she said, still reaching.

"You've got, like, a fingernail on it."

"A fingernail's a start."

The box tipped. She caught it against her palm and wobbled on the shelf, and Seth stepped in without thinking and put a hand flat on the shelving unit above her so the whole thing wouldn't come down. She dropped back onto her heels on the floor and turned around, the salt hugged against her chest.

"Thanks," she said. "See? Got it."

And she looked up at him.

Seth had tried, before, to imagine what it would be like. He'd been in Sam's head when it happened with Emily, and in Jared's, and in Jacob's, three weeks ago, in the Cullens' living room with the blood smell still everywhere. He'd seen it from the inside of other people's memories. He'd thought he understood.

He hadn't understood anything.

It was like missing a stair in the dark. The jolt of it, that drop in the stomach. Except the stair never came. The floor didn't come. Everything that kept him standing on the planet just let go, his mom and Leah and the pack and the patrol routes and the ache where his dad used to be, all of it unhooked, and for half a second he was falling through nothing at all. Then everything caught again. Everything came back. It just came back attached to her.

She had brown eyes with gold in them, the brown of creek water over stones. Dark hair, curly and a lot of it, piled up on her head and held there with a pencil. Freckles across her nose. A raincoat that was too big for her, unzipped, a gray Henley under it with the sleeves pushed up. On the inside of her left forearm there was a scar he'd never seen anything like, thin and silvery and branching, like a fern somebody had pressed into the skin.

She smelled like rain. Like a struck match. Like the air right before lightning.

"You okay?" she said. "You look like you swallowed a bee."

"I," said Seth. "Yeah. Hi. I'm Seth."

She looked at him for a second longer than people usually did. There was something careful in it, like she was measuring him against something he couldn't see.

"Okay," she said slowly. "Hi, Seth. Can you reach the salt? I need three more."

"Three more?"

"I'm a very anxious cook."

He reached up and got three boxes down without stretching. Then, because she was still looking at the shelf like she was doing math, he got two more. She took them and stacked them in her cart with the others, a little white tower of them.

"That's eight boxes of salt," Seth said.

"And a bag of rock salt," she agreed. "I have a long driveway."

"It doesn't snow that much here."

"Then I'll be very prepared." She stuck her hand out. "Layla."

He shook it. Her hand was cold from outside, small in his. She flinched a little, not a big flinch, just her fingers tightening.

"Jesus," she said. "Are you running a fever?"

"Runs in the family."

"That's not a thing that runs in families."

"It runs in mine."

She took her hand back and flexed it, and that careful look came back into her face, deeper this time. Then she let it go, or decided to put it away for later. Seth had grown up with Leah. He knew the difference.

"You're new," he said. It came out sounding dumb. Everything was going to come out sounding dumb; he could feel it. "I mean. I'd know. It's Forks. Everybody knows everybody."

"Moved in two weeks ago. I'm renting the yellow house out past the Calawah bridge."

"The old Hendricks place? With the porch?"

"With the porch," she said. "Which has a step that's going to kill me."

"I can fix it."

It was out before he'd decided to say it. She raised her eyebrows.

"You can fix it," she repeated.

"I'm good with—I fix stuff. For my mom. Gutters, steps. I did the whole back deck at Emily's last summer." He made himself stop. "I could look at it. If you want."

"Do you do this with every woman you meet in the baking aisle?"

"No," Seth said. "Just—no."

She studied him. Somewhere at the front of the store a register beeped. The rain got harder against the windows, a long gray rush of it.

"Thursday," Layla said finally. "Around four. Bring your own hammer, because I don't own one."

"Okay."

"And Seth?"

"Yeah?"

She nodded at his basket. "Your eggs are about to go."

He looked down. His fingers had closed on the carton without his noticing, and the cardboard was creasing in. He let go fast. She laughed, short and surprised, like she hadn't meant to, and pushed her cart off down the aisle toward the registers with the tower of salt boxes swaying.

He watched her go the whole way. He didn't even pretend not to.

---

He got the flour. It was self-rising. He didn't notice until his mom held it up in the kitchen an hour later and said his name in the voice she used when he was nine.

He told her. He couldn't not.

Sue Clearwater put the flour down on the counter very gently and sat down at the kitchen table and looked at him for a long time. Then she got up and hugged him, hard, and when she pulled back her eyes were wet, and she said, "Well. Is she nice?"

"I don't know yet," Seth said. "She bought eight boxes of salt."

"That's not a no," said his mom.

He went out that night at ten to take over from Leah. He stripped in the trees behind the house, tied his cutoffs around his ankle with the leather cord, and let the heat run up his spine and out through his skin, and then he was on four feet in the wet ferns with the whole world gone sharp. The rain was loud. The river was loud. The pack was in his head before he'd even shaken out his coat.

Leah was eight miles north, running the ridge above the Sol Duc. He felt her notice him. He felt her notice *what was in his head*.

It all came out at once. He couldn't help it. That was the thing about the pack. There wasn't a door you could close. The aisle, the salt, the pencil in her hair, the fern-shaped scar, the gold in her eyes, the floor going away under him.

There was a pause in Leah's head. A long, flat, gray pause.

*Oh,* she thought, *you have got to be kidding me.*

*Leah—*

*Eight boxes of salt, Seth. Who buys eight boxes of salt?*

*She said she's an anxious cook.*

*She's a serial killer. She's going to brine you.* But there was no bite to it, or not much. And under it, the way it always was with Leah when she wasn't paying attention to what she let through, there was something else. It was old and tired, and it tasted like Sam's kitchen and Emily's scars and a wedding invitation Leah had torn in half and then taped back together because their mom made her go.

Seth didn't say anything about it. He'd learned not to.

*I'm happy for you,* Leah thought, after a while. It came out stiff, like she'd had to pry it loose.

*Yeah?*

*Don't make it weird.* And then, because she was Leah: *Fix the woman's step. Don't fall through it.*

She peeled off toward La Push to go home and sleep, and her thoughts went small and far away, and then she phased out and was gone, and it was just Seth and the rain and the river.

He ran the long loop south, past the Bogachiel, and he didn't smell a single vampire all night. He hardly noticed. He was thinking about Thursday.

---

### II. The Yellow House

*Monday, November 6, 2006*

The wards rang at 6:14 in the evening, a single clean note under the porch like somebody had flicked a wineglass.

Layla Ashby didn't look up from the stove. She didn't need to. Five weeks of that note had taught her its whole vocabulary. The wards she'd laid around the yellow house were curse-breaker work, the kind of thing she'd learned in Gringotts tomb crews under the Andes and then taught herself to do better: salt lines buried under the gravel drive, runes cut into the bottoms of the fence posts and sealed with a drop of her own blood, a ring of river stones under the porch with the ward-heart chalked on the underside of each one. They didn't keep things out. Keeping things out was loud. Keeping things out told everyone where you lived. Her wards just *listened*, and when something crossed the line they told her, in a language of tones she felt in her back teeth, what it was.

Deer: a soft hum, like breath across a bottle. Raccoons: a scratchy, irritated buzz. The mail carrier, an ordinary middle-aged No-Maj named Doris: a flat, dull tick, the sound the wards made for anybody without a drop of magic in them.

Seth Clearwater: one high clear note, like struck glass, and under it something lower and older that she'd only ever heard once before, in the archive vaults in Lima, when a senior curse-breaker had let her run a test ward across a skin-changer's bone that was four hundred years old.

The first time she'd heard it, the Thursday he came to fix the step, she'd stood in her kitchen with her wand in her hand and her heart going like a rabbit's. Then he'd knocked on the door holding a hammer and a box of nails and a foil-covered dish his mother had sent, and he'd said, "It's fry bread, she says sorry it's not hot," and grinned at her with his whole face. She'd put the wand back up her sleeve and let him in.

That was five weeks ago.

Five weeks of Seth. He'd fixed the step, and then the other step, and then the porch rail, which hadn't been broken until he leaned on it. He'd cleaned her gutters in a downpour without a jacket. He split a cord of firewood in one afternoon while she watched from the window with her coffee going cold, because nobody split wood like that, nobody brought the maul down with that kind of bored, absent ease and never once got tired. He ate everything she put in front of him and then asked, politely, if there was more. He told her about his dad, who'd died in March of a heart attack, and he told it plainly and then went quiet and pulled at a thread on his cutoffs for a long time, and she'd wanted to reach across the table and hadn't. He told her about his sister, who was "kind of a lot," and his mom, who was "the best person alive, seriously," and his friend Jake, who "had a lot going on right now."

He never wore a coat. He was never cold. His skin was so hot she'd once seen the rain steam off his shoulders. He disappeared at night and came back with his eyes tired and his hair wet. Twice she'd heard the howling from up on the ridges, and both times the wards had given the faintest echo of that low old note, miles away, like a struck glass in another room.

She wasn't stupid. She had a pretty good idea.

She'd decided early on not to push. People were allowed their secrets. She had a whopper of her own sitting up her sleeve.

The note under the porch faded. Boots on the steps, the fixed ones. A knock.

"It's open," she called.

Seth came in with the cold, and with rain in his hair, and stopped just inside the door the way he always did, because the kitchen was small and he was not, and he'd learned the hard way about the lamp.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey. I made chili. Too much of it, as usual. Sit."

He didn't sit. That was the first thing.

She turned around with the spoon still in her hand. He was standing there by the coat hooks with his arms folded across his chest, and he looked like somebody about to jump off something high.

"Can you turn the stove off for a sec?" he said. "I need to show you something. Outside."

She turned the stove off.

---

The back field was a quarter acre of soaked grass that ran down to the tree line, where the second-growth fir went up dark and wet and close. It was full dark already. The rain had eased to the fine, drifting kind that didn't so much fall as hang. She'd strung old lanterns along the fence the first week, the kind with a handle and a glass chimney, because she liked them, and she hadn't lit them because a No-Maj would need a lighter for that and she'd kept forgetting to buy one.

Seth walked out to the middle of the field. She followed him to the edge of the porch light and stopped.

"Okay," he said. He turned around to face her. His breath smoked. "Okay. So. You're going to want to run. Please don't run. I'm not gonna hurt you. I'd never hurt you. I'd literally never. You just need to see this, because it's—you need to know, and I don't know how to say it, so I'm just going to show you, and then you can scream if you want, but please just. Don't run."

"Seth."

"Yeah?"

"Do I look like I'm running?"

He looked at her. She'd put her hands in her raincoat pockets, and her wand was in her right sleeve where it always was, and she was fairly sure her face was calm, because she'd spent four years on tomb crews and two on the American dueling circuit before that and she was good at calm. Her heart was going. He probably couldn't hear that. Probably.

He pulled his T-shirt off over his head.

"Okay," she said. "Wow. Not where I thought this was going."

"Turn around," he said, going red. "For like—two seconds. I gotta—the shorts. It's a whole thing."

She turned around. She stood facing the yellow house with its fixed steps and its warped siding and its one lit window, and she listened. A rustle of denim. A long breath out.

Then a sound she felt more than heard, a wet *pull* in the air like a sheet snapping on a line, and a heat at her back like somebody had opened an oven door.

"Okay," said nobody, because nobody was talking anymore. There was just breathing. Big breathing. Deep enough to shake the grass.

She turned around.

The wolf stood in the middle of her back field with its head low and its ears flat back in apology. It was the size of a horse. Bigger than a horse, in the chest. Its coat was sandy brown, darker along the spine, wet and spiked at the shoulders. The rain steamed off its back in a faint white haze. The eyes were Seth's eyes. She'd have known them anywhere, even in that long wolf skull, even set above a muzzle the length of her forearm with teeth in it that could have taken her arm off at the shoulder.

There was a leather cord tied around its left hind leg, with a pair of cutoffs knotted to it.

The wards under the porch rang. That low, old note, loud this time, close, like a bell somebody had hit with a hammer.

Layla started to laugh.

She couldn't help it. It came up out of her chest and wouldn't stop. The wolf's ears went even flatter. It made a sound, a high anxious whine that was so completely *Seth* that she laughed harder and had to put a hand on the porch post.

"Sorry," she managed. "Sorry. I'm not—Seth, I'm not laughing at you. I'm laughing because I *knew*. I knew. The house told me. The first day. You walked up my driveway and the whole house went *bong*."

The wolf tipped its head sideways. One ear came back up.

"Okay," Layla said, wiping her eyes. "Okay. Fair's fair. My turn."

She let the wand drop out of her sleeve into her palm. Ten and a quarter inches, sycamore, with a core of Wampus cat hair, the wand that had chosen her at Ilvermorny when she was eleven and had never once let her down. She didn't say anything. She hadn't needed to say spells out loud since she was fifteen.

Every lantern on the fence lit at once.

They went up in a run, left to right, *pop pop pop pop*, little gold flames standing up behind their glass. She flicked the wand and they lifted off their hooks and rose, a dozen of them, and drifted out over the field in a slow ring around the wolf, bobbing gently at the height of his shoulders. Another flick, and the rain stopped falling on him. It kept falling everywhere else. Over the wolf it hung in the air, ten feet up, a shallow dome of a million suspended drops catching the lantern light, glittering, not moving.

The wolf sat down. Hard. Like its back legs had just quit.

"I'm a witch," Layla said. "Real kind. Wand, broom, the whole deal. There's more of us than you'd think, and we're very, very good at nobody knowing about it." She lowered the wand. The dome of rain stayed where it was. "So. Hi."

The wolf stared at her. Then it stared up at the rain hanging over its head. Then back at her.

Then it shimmered, the air going hot and strange around it, and Seth was sitting naked on his butt in the wet grass with a ring of lanterns floating around him and an expression on his face like someone had just told him Santa Claus was real and also lived next door.

Layla flicked her wand without looking and the wool throw off her couch came sailing out the back door and landed on his head.

"Thanks," said Seth, muffled.

"Uh-huh."

He got it around his waist. He stood up. The lanterns bobbed around him. He looked at them and laughed, a big disbelieving bark of a laugh, and walked through the ring of them toward her, and stopped at the bottom of the porch steps, the ones he'd fixed, so that for once they were almost eye to eye.

"You knew," he said.

"I suspected. Strongly. The house knew."

"The *house*."

"It's a long story. It's a whole branch of magic. I'll show you sometime."

"You're a *witch*."

"I'm a curse-breaker, technically. And a duelist. Ex-duelist. I'm on what you'd call a sabbatical." She swallowed. Up close he smelled like rain and woodsmoke and something wild under it. The steam was coming off his shoulders. "Seth, I just broke a very old, very serious law by showing you that. Like. International. There are people whose entire job is making sure I never do what I just did."

"Is that bad?"

"It's very bad."

"Are you gonna get in trouble?"

"Only if somebody finds out."

He looked at her for a long moment. The lanterns hung behind him. The dome of rain glittered.

"I'm not gonna tell anybody," he said.

And then his face did something complicated.

"Okay," he said. "So. About that."

"About what?"

"There's a thing. With the wolf thing. When I'm—when I'm a wolf, the pack's in my head. All of them. Like, the ones in my pack. We hear each other think. Everything." He winced. "Like, *everything*."

Layla went very still.

"Everything," she repeated.

"Yeah."

"Including the last ten minutes."

"Only if I'm thinking about it when I phase."

"Seth. You are going to be thinking about this for the rest of your natural life."

"Yeah," said Seth. "Yeah, I am."

She stared at him. He stared back. Water dripped off the porch roof between them.

Then Layla put her free hand flat on his bare chest, which was hot as a stovetop under her palm, and leaned in, and kissed him.

He made a startled sound against her mouth, and then he didn't make any sound at all, and his hands came up very carefully, like she was made of glass, and settled on either side of her face. Behind him the dome of rain let go all at once and came down on the lanterns in a long silver hiss, and neither of them noticed.

"This is the dumbest thing I've ever done," Layla said, when she could.

"Yeah?"

"I once picked a seven-layer blood lock in a burial chamber with a jaguar curse sitting on the door. This is dumber."

"Okay," Seth said, grinning so wide it looked like it hurt. "Cool. Can I do it again?"

She let him.

---

The pack, as it turned out, took about forty-five minutes.

Seth phased at a quarter past eleven to go run his loop. He did it in her back field, in the dark, after one last kiss on the porch and a lot of mumbling. Layla stood at the kitchen window with her tea and watched the big sandy shape trot into the trees.

At 11:21 the howling started.

It came from two different directions at once. One voice was deep and furious and came from somewhere east, toward the river, and the other was higher, sharper, and came from the north, and it came out as a long, outraged yell with fur on it.

Layla put her tea down.

"Well," she said to the empty kitchen. "That'll be Jake. And the sister."

---

They came at dawn, in human shape, which she appreciated.

The wards rang twice, the same low old note doubled, and she opened the door before they could knock. Jacob Black was on her porch, enormous, maybe six-seven, cropped black hair and a jaw that looked like it had been set in concrete. Behind him, on the bottom step, stood a woman with Seth's eyes and none of Seth's sunshine: tall, rangy, black hair chopped short, a black T-shirt dress and no shoes, arms crossed. She had the kind of face that had been beautiful for so long it had gotten tired of people saying so and decided to be dangerous instead.

"You're the witch," Jacob said.

"I'm the witch," Layla said. "You're the Alpha. And you must be Leah."

Leah looked her up and down, slow. "Eight boxes of salt," she said.

"They're for the wards."

"Uh huh."

"Seth told you about the salt?"

"Seth tells us about *everything*," said Leah. "Seth is a goddamn open book. I have now seen the inside of your kitchen, your porch, your back field, and your mouth, and I am going to need you to understand that none of that was my choice."

"Leah," said Jacob.

"What?"

He didn't answer her. He looked back at Layla.

"The Cullens want to meet you," he said. "Edward already pulled it all out of my head about four seconds after I walked in the door. Carlisle's—" he made a face, "—*fascinated*. His word. You don't have to come. But if you don't, they'll come here, and you've got a lot of rock salt, but I don't know if you've got enough."

Layla looked at him. She looked at Leah.

"Are they dangerous?" she said.

"Yes," said Jacob.

"To me?"

"No," Jacob said. "Not anymore. Not those ones." He paused. "Seth's already over there. He told them you were coming. He's been talking about you for two hours straight. Even Rosalie's starting to look like she wants to die, and she's already dead."

---

The Cullen house was glass.

That was her first thought, coming up the long gravel drive in the passenger seat of Jacob's Rabbit with Leah folded up silent in the back. The whole back wall was glass, three stories of it, facing the river. It was the kind of house you built when you had nothing to hide from anyone who could possibly see you, or when you were very sure that nobody who could see you would live long enough to talk about it.

There were seven of them in the living room, and a baby.

Layla had read about vampires. Everybody had. Fifth year Defense, the standard American curriculum: the ones who flinched from garlic and running water and the ones who didn't, the Sanguini registry, the treaty protections, the Ilvermorny poster about not inviting pale strangers home. She'd met one, once, a tired old man in Mexico City who'd run a blood bank and wore sunglasses indoors and smelled faintly of wet earth.

These were something else.

They were too still. That was the first thing. They didn't fidget. Nobody shifted weight or crossed and uncrossed their legs or breathed unless they remembered to. They were pale the way marble was pale, and beautiful in a way that made the back of her neck prickle, and every single one of them had gold eyes except for one, a slender brunette standing a little apart by the glass wall with a hand pressed to her own mouth. Her eyes were the dark red of old wine, and she was not breathing at all.

"Bella," said the bronze-haired one beside her, quietly. "You're fine. She smells odd to you. That's all."

"She smells like a *thunderstorm*," Bella said, through her fingers. "Like burned sugar. What is that?"

"Magic, I'd assume," said the blond man in the middle of the room. He stepped forward and offered his hand. "Miss Ashby. Carlisle Cullen. Thank you for coming. I imagine this is a great deal."

His hand was cold and hard as a countertop, and he shook hers very gently, like a man who'd had a lot of practice not breaking things.

"Layla's fine," she said. "And yeah. It's a lot."

Seth was sitting on the arm of the white couch with a little girl on his knee. The size of her threw Layla. Seth had said *baby*, three weeks old, *Nessie*, and the child on his knee looked like a healthy year-old toddler, with a head of bronze ringlets and Bella's brown eyes and a solemn, intent expression no one-year-old had ever worn in history. She was holding Seth's finger in her fist. When Layla came in she let go, and held out both arms, and made a small imperious noise.

"She wants to say hi," Seth said. "It's—she does a thing. Just let her."

Layla crossed the room. The little girl reached up and laid one hand flat against her cheek.

And Layla *saw*: lanterns, a ring of gold lanterns floating in the dark, the rain hanging still above them. It was Seth's memory. She knew that somehow, the way you knew whose handwriting you were reading, but seen and handed back through this child's mind, bright and sharp and full of a wordless, delighted *again, again, do it again*.

Layla sat back on her heels.

"Holy shit," she said.

"Language," said a woman with soft caramel hair, without heat. "Though I quite agree. Renesmee, sweetheart, let her breathe."

"She wants a trick," said Edward. He was watching Layla with an expression she didn't like. It was too focused, the look of somebody listening to a radio station just slightly out of tune. "You're doing something. In your head. It's like listening through a wall."

"It's called Occlumency," Layla said. "Every curse-breaker learns it. Tombs with mind-curses on them are the worst kind. You learn to fold your thoughts up or you walk out thinking you're a pharaoh."

"I can still hear you."

"Bits. You're very loud, by the way. No offense. You're pushing."

He blinked, and the pressure eased. A big curly-haired vampire on the far couch laughed out loud.

"Oh, I *like* her," he said. "Do a trick. Seriously. Do one. Can you turn Rose into a frog?"

"Emmett," said the blonde beside him, who was so beautiful it was almost rude, and whose eyes on Layla were cold as January. "Shut up." She looked back at Layla. "How many of you are there?"

"Enough," said Layla. "More than you'd like. Fewer than you'd think."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you get."

Rosalie's mouth went thin. Edward said, quietly, "She's telling the truth. She doesn't know an exact number. She's thinking of a school. In—Massachusetts? A mountain. And a building in New York with a lot of floors."

Layla glared at him. He held up his hands.

"Sorry," he said. "It leaks."

She took her wand out. Every one of them went still in a different way. The blonde shifted her weight to the balls of her feet. A lean, scarred blond man by the stairs, who'd been watching her since she walked in with his whole body angled toward the exits, went so motionless he might as well have been carved. And Leah, who'd been leaning against the wall by the door, came off it.

Layla tore a page out of a magazine on the coffee table and folded it into a quick, rough bird with her fingers. She touched the wand to it. The paper bird shook itself, flapped twice, and took off, and flew one slow loop around the vaulted ceiling, and came down to land on Renesmee's outstretched hand.

The child laughed, a high pure sound, and the whole room changed.

"Well," said Carlisle softly, "I'll be damned."

"Carlisle," said the caramel-haired woman, Esme.

"I've been alive for over three hundred and sixty years," Carlisle said. "I've met—a great many things. I have never once encountered anything like this. Not a rumor. Not a whisper." He looked at Layla with an expression of pure, delighted hunger that was not, she was fairly sure, about her blood. "The Volturi have never mentioned anything of the kind. And Aro would. Aro *collects*."

"Who's Aro?"

Every vampire in the room exchanged a look. The tiny dark-haired one, Alice, who'd been sitting cross-legged on the piano bench with her eyes half-unfocused like a woman watching television in another room, said brightly, "Someone you should hope never hears about you. I can see you just fine, by the way. You're so clear. Everything's so *clear* with you."

"Is that good?"

"I have no idea," said Alice, cheerfully. "We'll find out."

Leah, by the door, laughed once, short and humorless.

"So witches are real," she said. "Great. Awesome. Vampires, werewolves, a baby that grows like a weed, and now witches. Is there anything that *isn't* real?"

"Leprechauns are real," Layla said. "They're assholes, though."

There was a short silence. Then Emmett laughed so hard the couch creaked, and even Leah's mouth twitched at one corner before she flattened it out again.

---

She told them what she could. She didn't tell them everything. She told them about the Statute of Secrecy, and about the International Confederation, and about how very seriously her people took the question of who knew what. She watched Carlisle's face while she did it and saw him understand, faster than most, exactly what she'd just risked.

"You've done something illegal," he said. "By telling us."

"Very."

"Why?"

She looked across the room at Seth, who was letting Renesmee steer the paper bird around his head like a toy plane and making engine noises.

"He showed me his," she said. "Seemed rude not to show him mine."

Rosalie, of all people, snorted.

---

Five days later, Layla broke the Statute again.

It was a Saturday dinner at the Clearwaters' little house in La Push, with the wind coming off First Beach hard enough to rattle the windows. Sue had made a casserole in a big blue enamel dish that had been her mother's, and Seth, carrying it from the oven to the table with his bare hands because he could, tripped over the dog and dropped it.

The dish hit the linoleum and broke clean into four pieces. The casserole stayed mostly in the bottom one.

Sue made a small sound. She'd lost a husband this year. A dish was a dish.

Layla was already on her knees. She'd drawn without thinking, the way she'd draw on a tripwire, and she touched the wand to the biggest piece and thought *Reparo*, and the four pieces slid across the floor toward each other and fit back together with a soft, clean click. The crack lines sealed. The enamel smoothed. The dish sat whole on the linoleum with the casserole steaming quietly inside it.

The back door opened.

Chief Charlie Swan stood in it with a six-pack of Rainier in one hand and the wind tugging at his jacket. He looked at the dish. He looked at the wand in Layla's hand. He looked at Seth, who froze. He looked at Sue, who closed her eyes.

He turned around and walked back out and shut the door.

There was a pause of about four seconds.

The door opened again. Charlie came in, shut it behind him, set the beer down on the counter, and took his jacket off and hung it on the hook.

"Sue," he said. "This a need-to-know thing?"

"Pretty much," said Sue.

"Then I don't need to know." He pulled out his chair and sat down at the table. He nodded at Layla. "Ma'am. Charlie Swan. You must be Seth's girl."

"Layla," said Layla faintly.

"Nice to meet you." He looked at the dish on the floor, which Seth had not yet picked up. "Son, is that dinner, or is that a lesson in humility for the rest of us?"

Seth picked up the dish.

They ate. Charlie had seconds, and asked Layla how she liked the yellow house, and whether the Hendricks boys had ever fixed the furnace, and he didn't once look at her sleeve. When he left, at nine, he shook her hand at the door, and held it a beat longer than necessary, and said quietly, without looking at Seth or Sue, "Whatever you are. Take care of the kid."

"I will," Layla said.

"Good," said Charlie, and went out to his cruiser.

On the drive home, alone, with the wipers going and the dark trees sliding past, Layla did the math out loud.

"A family of seven vampires. One half-vampire baby. A pack of shapeshifters. Their mom. And a police chief." She drummed her fingers on the wheel. "That's putting the Statute through a woodchipper."

The wipers thumped. The road was empty. Up ahead, the Calawah bridge came up out of the dark, and past it the turn for the yellow house, where the wards were humming softly to themselves under the porch.

Nobody's going to find out, she thought. It's Forks. There's not a wizard for a hundred miles.

She was wrong about that. But she wouldn't know it for two more weeks.

### III. Field Office

*Tuesday, November 21, 2006 — 4:52 a.m.*

Harry woke up because the wolf wanted out.

It did that most mornings now. It paced, somewhere behind his sternum, back and forth in the dark, like a big dog pacing a kennel after it's heard the car door and knows the walk isn't coming. Two months of that. Two months since he'd last let it run, on a wet night in the Brecon Beacons with nobody for twenty miles, and since then he'd kept it shut down tight, because the Olympic Peninsula was full of things with very good noses and he had no intention of leaving his scent all over it.

He lay on his back on the camp bed and looked at the canvas overhead and breathed until it eased off. It didn't ease off much. It never did anymore. He got up anyway.

The bedroom was the smallest room in the tent, which was saying something, because from the outside the tent was a sagging green two-man job from an army surplus shop in Aberdeen with a broken zip and moss on the guy-lines. Inside it was about the size of a decent London flat. He'd done the extension work himself over three winters. He'd done it badly the first time, so that for most of a year the kitchen had been eleven feet longer on Tuesdays. The second version was better. The third was the one he lived in.

He pulled on jeans and a grey T-shirt and went down the short corridor in his socks. The floorboards were oak, salvaged from a Methodist chapel in Wales that had been due for demolition. The rain on the outer skin sounded exactly as close and loud as it would in a real two-man tent, because he'd spent an afternoon getting the acoustics right. A tent that didn't sound like a tent made him itchy.

The kitchen was a galley: two-burner stove, a fridge that ran on a charm instead of a plug, a kettle, a French press, a box of Weetabix that had crossed the Atlantic out of pure stubbornness, and half a cherry pie under a plate. He glanced at the kettle and it started ticking toward the boil.

The map table took up the main room.

Eight feet by five, oak, with the Olympic Peninsula carved into the top. He'd done the carving by hand in two nights at the motel, off the Geological Survey sheets, and then charmed the wood to keep up with the weather. The mountains stood up in the middle in a rough knot. The rivers ran out of them in thin silver lines: Elwha and Sol Duc to the north, Hoh and Queets and Quinault to the west, the Bogachiel and the Calawah threading in toward the cluster of matchbox houses that was Forks. Cloud slid across the wood in a slow grey film. It was raining on the coast. It was raining everywhere.

Over all of it lay the snare.

Forty-one pinheads of blue light marked the anchors he'd sunk in his first ten days on the ground: Cape Flattery, Neah Bay, Lake Ozette, the Hoh visitor centre car park, Kalaloch, Lake Quinault, Hurricane Ridge, a dozen points along the 101, and one in a culvert outside Sequim that he'd had to set at three in the morning lying on his back in a foot of cold water. Between them ran threads as fine as spider silk, criss-crossing the peninsula so densely in places that the carving looked frosted. Every few seconds a pulse travelled down one thread or another, like a heartbeat taking the long way round.

It had taken him four years to build the first one. It had taken ten days to build this one, which was the point of the four years.

He'd spent his first week tuning it, sitting in a parked car on the 101 with a notebook and a pair of Omnioculars, watching the threads shiver every time a pale, gold-eyed figure in an expensive coat walked out of the Forks Outfitters or climbed into a silver Volvo. The ones with gold eyes walked through his web every day, and it shivered, and let them by. It was meant to close on one kind of thing only: the kind with red eyes, the kind that fed on people.

Three weeks in, it hadn't closed on anything.

He leaned on the table edge and looked at the threads. Blue light on a wet green peninsula, and nothing moving through it except the pulses he'd put there himself.

The kettle clicked off. He made coffee and put on a record, because the tent was too quiet with just rain. *Physical Graffiti*, side one. He stood in the kitchen doorway with his mug and listened to "Custard Pie" thump out of a pair of speakers he'd pulled out of a skip behind a Hammersmith music shop when he was nineteen.

Then he took his coffee to the mirror.

---

It hung between two bookshelves in a plain silver frame about the size of a paperback. He tapped it twice, waited, tapped once.

The glass clouded and cleared. A small, sharp-faced woman of about seventy looked out at him from a high-backed chair, cardigan buttoned to the throat, a cup of tea on the arm. Behind her, out of focus, a stone wall and a single lamp. It was coming up on one in the afternoon in London. In the six years Harry had worked for Maud Ellery, he had never once seen her eat.

"Potter," she said.

"Morning, ma'am."

"It's afternoon."

"Not here."

"I'm aware of the time difference." She picked up the tea. "Report."

"Nothing," Harry said. "The lattice is holding. No hits. The Cullens come and go. The dogs run their routes; I've got rough timings on both packs and I've stayed off their bit of coast. Nobody's sniffed me. Nobody's looked twice."

"Three weeks."

"I know."

"Your estimate was two to four."

"And I stand by it. Week four starts tomorrow." He drank some coffee. "You said quiet. I'm doing quiet."

"I did say quiet." Her pale eyes looked at him over the rim of the cup. They'd been looking at Unspeakables over teacups since before his parents were born. "The Board wants your findings by the end of January. That has not changed. If anything it's moved closer."

"How much closer?"

"Close enough that I'm asking." She set the cup down. "The family remains Amber. Hands off. They are worth more to us intact and friendly than they would ever be on your table, and I'll have that understood."

"Understood. Their doctor puts people back together for a living, their tall one reads minds, and their small one sees things before they happen. I'm not daft."

"No one has ever accused you of being daft, Potter. Reckless, frequently." A small pause. "And the resident witch?"

"Still resident. Still a witch. Still minding her own business, as far as I can tell. I clocked her wards the night I got here, gave the house a wide berth, and haven't been back."

"Keep it that way. The Americans are touchy about their soil."

"The Americans don't know I'm on their soil."

"Which is precisely why they would be touchy." She picked the tea back up. "If the Congress finds you—"

"I don't exist. I know." He saluted her with the mug. "I've got my not-existing trousers on and everything."

Ellery looked at him for a long, flat second, the look she'd been giving him since he was twenty and new and inclined to set things on fire in the Department basement.

"Good hunting," she said, and the mirror went dark.

---

He went into town for breakfast because his cover went into town for breakfast.

Harry Evans, independent forestry consultant out of Vancouver, was in Forks on a three-month contract assessing second-growth stands for a timber company nobody had heard of. He kept a room at the Pacific Inn Motel on the main drag, and a black 1970 Chevelle in the motel lot that he'd bought for cash in Tacoma, because a man who walks everywhere in a logging town gets noticed, and a man who drives a car like that gets noticed for the car. He'd chosen the name because it was easy to answer to. Evans had been his mother's name.

He didn't hide his face. There was no reason to. Nobody in Forks had ever seen it. The handful of wizarding photographs that still existed of him were of a skinny seventeen-year-old in round glasses with a lightning-shaped scar, and the Department had quietly arranged, in the years since, that very few new ones were taken. The scar was gone. So were the glasses. He was six-two now and broad through the shoulders, and the last time someone from school had passed him on Diagon Alley, they'd held the door for him and not looked twice.

He Apparated into the trees behind the motel at 6:40. The pop of displaced air was about as loud as somebody shutting a book. That had taken him two years. Most wizards cracked like a starting pistol when they Apparated and thought nothing of it. He'd spent two years taking the crack out, the way you'd take the squeak out of a door, because he was tired of announcing himself.

He walked round the front of the motel, got in the Chevelle, and drove four blocks to the diner, because the man he was pretending to be would.

The diner was the kind of place every small town in America had and every small town in America thought was its own: a long counter, red vinyl stools, booths along the window, a pie case by the register, a chalkboard that said HAPPY THANKSGIVING WEEK in a hand that had given up on the K. It smelled like coffee and bacon fat and wet coats. Half the stools had men on them in flannel and Carhartt, talking about the rain.

"Morning, Harry," said the waitress, who was called Cheryl and had decided on his second visit that she liked his accent. "Usual?"

"Usual, love. And whatever pie you've got that's hot."

"It's Thanksgiving week, hon. It's all pumpkin."

"Then pumpkin. Two slices."

"For breakfast."

"It's got vegetables in."

She laughed and wrote it down. Harry took the stool at the end of the counter by the wall, where he could see the door and the window and the kitchen pass, and found himself two stools down from the chief of police.

Charlie Swan was in uniform, jacket over the back of his stool, working through a plate of eggs over easy and reading the Port Angeles paper folded in quarters. Late forties, moustache, a face that had settled into patience a long time ago. Harry had him in the file. Divorced, one daughter. The daughter had married into the Cullen family in August and then, as far as the town knew, gone away to college, and as far as Harry knew had died and come back in September with red eyes and a baby.

Harry had a rule about Charlie Swan, and it was a simple one. He didn't touch him.

He could have. It would have taken half a second. Surface thoughts were easy on people with no training; you didn't even need to catch their eyes, though it helped. But the man was the father of a Cullen, and the Cullens had a telepath, and Harry had no idea how sensitive that telepath was to someone else's fingerprints on a familiar mind. If he went poking around in the chief's head and the chief went home with a faint headache and a feeling of being watched, and then sat down to Sunday dinner across from a man who could hear thoughts, that was a thread Harry didn't want anybody pulling.

So he drank his coffee and left Charlie Swan's head alone.

"You're the timber guy," Charlie said, without looking up from the paper.

"Guilty."

"Surveying."

"Second growth. Somebody wants to know what's worth cutting in about fifteen years."

"Huh." Charlie turned a page. "You're a ways from home."

"Vancouver's not so far."

"Vancouver's not where that accent's from."

"London, originally. Moved for the trees."

"Huh," Charlie said again. He took a bite of toast. He finally glanced over, a cop's glance, quick and level, the kind that took a picture and filed it. "Lot of new faces this fall."

"Is that right?"

"Seems like it." Charlie looked back down at the paper. His jaw worked for a second, like a man thinking about something he'd decided not to think about. "Town gets real interesting real fast sometimes. Then it goes back to being Forks."

"Sounds like a nice place to live."

"It is," said Charlie Swan, and turned the page.

Cheryl brought the eggs and the pie. Harry ate all of it. He tipped her more than the man he was pretending to be could probably afford, and said, "Cheers, Chief," on his way out, and Charlie lifted two fingers off his coffee mug without looking up.

Harry drove the Chevelle back to the motel, went round behind it into the trees, and stepped out of the world with a sound like a book closing.

---

He spent the morning in the specimen room making sure it was ready, which it had been for three weeks.

It was the biggest room in the tent and the coldest. The floor was flagstone, the walls whitewashed stone that had never belonged to any building; he'd conjured it and then spent a month persuading it to stay. Down the centre ran a long steel workbench with a stone top, scrubbed clean. Along the walls were shelves of jars and boxes, labelled in his own small, square handwriting: a few things from old jobs, a few things from other people's, nothing that would mean much to anybody without the case files. At the far end, set into the floor, was the cell.

It was a circle eight feet across, cut into the flagstones and inlaid with a ring of silver, and inside the silver ring a second ring of runes. It had taken him most of a week to get right. Nothing he'd ever studied in fifteen years of magic had been quite like the things he meant to put in it. Harder than stone. Faster than anything that should be able to move. Cold, and still, and very, very old, some of them. He'd built the circle around stillness, because stillness was the one thing he'd seen them do better than anything alive. They didn't fidget. They didn't breathe unless they remembered to. When they stopped, they stopped all the way, like statues. He'd made the circle hold that stillness in place, the same principle as the snare, and it would hold whatever went into it for as long as he wanted it held.

In theory.

He walked the ring with his hands in his pockets, feeling for any slack in the runes. There wasn't any. There hadn't been any yesterday either.

The armoury was the next room down, and he stopped in it on the way back, the way he always did.

It wasn't large. It didn't need to be. A rack of long guns down one wall: a pump shotgun with a scarred wooden stock, a lever-action rifle older than his grandparents, a sawn-off double-barrel that he kept mostly for sentimental reasons, a long scoped rifle in a case. Blades on the opposite wall, a few of them goblin-made, one of them borrowed from a museum in Prague under circumstances he'd been asked not to put in writing. A workbench with a vice and a set of fine engraving tools. Everything on every rack had runes cut into it somewhere, under a grip or along a barrel or inside a stock, and he had cut every one of them himself, most of them twice, because the first attempt had usually gone wrong.

The 1911 sat in its own place in the middle of the bench, on a square of green felt.

It was a Colt. He'd found it in a pawn shop in Reno five years ago, a plain old service pistol with a worn finish and a story attached that the pawnbroker had clearly made up on the spot. He'd spent fourteen months taking it apart and putting it back together with magic in every piece of it. It had never needed reloading since. What it fired depended on what he wanted it to fire, and what he wanted it to fire depended on what was in front of him. It was the only thing in the armoury he'd ever given a name to, and he'd never said the name out loud to anybody, because it was embarrassing.

He picked it up and checked it the way other men check their watches, and put it in the shoulder holster, and went to get dressed.

---

The suit was black. Off the peg, two years old, from a department store on Oxford Street. White shirt, thin black tie, black shoes that he'd charmed to shed mud, and a long black wool overcoat over the lot of it that hid the holster and the shape of the gun without any help from magic. He'd worn some version of the same outfit on every field job for six years. People saw a man in a black suit and a long coat walk into a place with a badge in his hand and they decided for themselves what he was, and nine times out of ten what they decided was close enough to useful.

He looked at himself in the bedroom mirror while he did the tie. A tall man, black hair that still did whatever it liked no matter what he put on it, green eyes, a face that looked tired around the edges. Twenty-six. He looked about thirty. Eight years of the Department did that.

He'd gone in at eighteen, after the war, because it was the one door in the Ministry where nobody was going to ask him to give a speech. Kingsley had offered him the Aurors and he'd said no; Hermione had told him he was hiding and he'd said yes, probably. He'd gone in thinking he'd stay a year. He'd been very bad at all of it for a long time. Runes. Warding. Arithmancy, which he'd skipped at school and had to learn from first principles at nineteen in a basement with a woman who rapped his knuckles. Potions, which he'd been rubbish at for seven years at Hogwarts and turned out to be all right at once nobody was standing over him. Wandless work, silent work, the mind arts, and the old magic that the Department kept in rooms with no doors. Every one of them had taken years, and none of them had come easily, and every so often, working on one problem, he'd found the answer to a different one and written it down and given it a name. A few of those names were in the Department's restricted catalogue now. One of them had his initials on it, because Ellery had insisted, and he still found that embarrassing too.

He'd stopped meaning to leave after about the third year. Somewhere along the way he'd found out that what he actually wanted was to understand it. All of it. How magic worked, and why, and where the edges were, and what was on the other side of them.

The wolf paced behind his ribs.

"Yeah," he said to it, out loud, doing up his top button. "I know. Not yet."

---

By half three in the afternoon it was getting dark.

He'd spent the hours since lunch at the map table with a notebook, going back over three weeks of thread-pulses and making a list of the places he'd move the anchors if he were starting over. It was busy work and he knew it. Outside the rain had picked up again. Inside, side two of *Physical Graffiti* had finished a while ago and he hadn't put anything else on.

On the carved peninsula, the cloud had thickened over the south, over the Bogachiel and the Hoh. He couldn't see the wolves on the map; he hadn't built it to show them. But by his rough timings, the two Clearwaters would be running the southern loop about now: the light grey one fast out in front, the sandy one following, cutting back and forth across the river valleys in a pattern he'd half-learned from watching their tracks in the mud.

He had a cup of tea at his elbow, going cold, and the last slice of cherry pie on a plate, and a silver cord tied around his left wrist under his shirt cuff, braided from the same stuff as the lattice. If anything closed anywhere on the peninsula, the cord would know first.

He picked up the fork.

Nothing ever happened at half three on a Tuesday.

### IV. Southern Loop

*Tuesday, November 21, 2006 — 3:18 p.m.*

*—and she does this thing where she bites the pencil when she's reading, like she doesn't even know she's doing it, and she's got like nine pencils in that house and every single one has teeth marks—*

*Seth.*

*—and she reads with her feet up on the stove, which can't be safe, right? Like, it's a woodstove. I told her. She said she'd put a charm on her socks. Is that a real thing? Can you put a charm on socks?*

*Seth. I swear to God.*

*What?*

Leah Clearwater put her head down and ran harder, because the alternative was turning around and biting her brother on the ear.

The Bogachiel valley went by in a gray-green smear. Moss on everything. Moss on the moss. Big-leaf maples furred so thick with it they looked like they were wearing fur coats, sword ferns as high as her shoulders, nurse logs rotting into the ground with whole rows of young hemlock growing out of their backs like spines. Rain came down through all of it in a fine, steady hush, and the ground under her paws was soft and black and smelled like a hundred years of leaves. The river was off to her left, high and brown and loud with three days of rain.

She loved this part of the run. She'd never once said so, in her head or out of it. The pack would never let her hear the end of it.

*You love this part of the run,* Seth thought.

*Shut up.*

*You do. You always slow down by the big cedar.*

*I slow down because you're two hundred yards back and I'm supposed to be keeping an eye on you.*

*I'm a hundred and fifty yards back.*

*Run faster.*

He ran faster. She could feel him doing it, the long sandy legs eating ground, the happy loose-jointed lope he'd had since the first week he phased. Seth ran like a golden retriever chasing a tennis ball. Seth did everything like a golden retriever chasing a tennis ball. It used to drive her insane. Lately it just made her tired, the way everything made her tired.

Lately, it had also come with a running commentary about Layla Ashby.

Leah had now seen Layla Ashby from every angle that a sixteen-year-old's-brain-in-an-eighteen-year-old's-body could see a woman from, which was a lot of angles. She'd seen the yellow house so many times she could have drawn it. She'd seen the pencils. She'd seen the fern-shaped scar on the left forearm, close up, in good light, and gotten the story with it, which was something about a tomb in Peru and a curse on a door that Layla had been too slow on by about half a second. She'd seen the lanterns floating. She'd seen the kiss on the porch, in what she was fairly sure was slow motion, because Seth's memory slowed it down every single time he replayed it, and he replayed it a lot.

She'd seen her brother be happy. That was the part she didn't have a good place to put.

She'd tried to hate it. It would have been easier. Imprinting had walked into her life two and a half years ago and picked Sam up out of it like a cat picking up a kitten by the scruff, and carried him off to her cousin, and she'd had to stand there and watch it, and then stand in the pack and *feel* it, from the inside, every day, Sam's love for Emily coming through the link like the smell of somebody else's dinner. She'd hated imprinting with her whole body for so long she wasn't sure she knew how to stop.

But it was Seth. And Layla Ashby was, she'd decided somewhere around week three, kind of okay. She was dry and quick and she didn't flinch when Leah was a bitch to her, and she'd looked Rosalie Hale dead in the eye and said *that's the one you get*, which Leah had replayed in her own head a few times, privately, where Seth couldn't hear. And Layla with Seth was a different animal from Emily with Sam. Emily looked at Sam like he was a gift she'd been handed. Layla looked at Seth like he was a puzzle she'd decided to keep.

*She says I'm a puzzle?* Seth thought, delighted.

*I didn't say anything. Get out of my head.*

*It's a pack, Leah. That's the whole—*

*I know what a pack is.*

She'd let that one through without meaning to. She went back to watching the ground.

They cut across a creek in two strides, splashing, and up the far bank and into the old growth where the trees got big. Real big. Sitka spruce with trunks you could have parked Jacob's Rabbit inside of, if you'd hollowed them out, going up into the rain until they disappeared. The light down here was green and dim, the color of the bottom of a pond. It was going to be dark in an hour.

*Jake's still at the Cullens',* Seth thought. *Nessie learned a new word.*

*How do you know?*

*He phased for like two seconds at lunch to tell me. It's "dog."*

Leah snorted, a big wet huff through her nose. *That's a description. Kid's observant.*

*She said it to Jake.*

*Like I said.*

Seth's laugh in her head was a loose, bright thing. She let him see that she'd almost laughed with him. She was trying.

They ran on. The loop went south along the Bogachiel for another six miles, cut west toward the Hoh, then swung back north along the old logging roads to the edge of Forks, and it usually took them two hours if they did it properly and an hour and a half if Leah was in a mood. It had been quiet for weeks. The Cullens' scent was everywhere, of course, all through the woods east of town, burnt-sweet and sickening; she'd stopped gagging on it last year and these days it just made her nose twitch. But a real one, a stranger, a red-eye, hadn't come through in a month. Not since the redhead's army. Not since the Volturi had come and gone in their long gray cloaks and left that one newborn girl in pieces on a fire.

Leah sometimes thought about the Volturi when she couldn't sleep. The little blond one. The way the Cullens had all gone still when she smiled, like rabbits under a hawk.

She didn't let Seth see that either.

The wind turned.

It came round from the south, off the river, a cold wet gust that bent the ferns and threw rain sideways into her face. And in it, laid over the moss and the mud and the river and the rotting wood like oil on water, was a smell that hit the back of her throat so hard she nearly stumbled.

Sweet. Sickly, cloying, burnt-sugar sweet, a smell like a candy store on fire.

Vampire.

And something else, something under it. Copper. Salt. Bright and red and *recent*.

Leah stopped so fast her paws gouged two long black furrows in the moss.

*Leah?* Seth was there a second later, sliding in beside her, his whole body gone tense. Then he smelled it too, and she felt the hair go up along his back in a long ridge. *Oh, no. Oh, that's—*

*Not a Cullen.*

*No. Nobody I know. Leah, is that blood?*

*It's blood.*

Human blood. She knew the smell. Everyone in both packs knew the smell now. It had been all over the clearing after the newborn fight, and all over the Cullens' living room the night Nessie was born. This was fresher than that and also older than that, a smell that had sat in somebody's mouth and in somebody's clothes, mixed into their skin. The kind of smell you get on you when you've fed recently and messily and haven't bothered to wash.

She put her nose down. The trail ran across the clearing ahead of them, east to west, laid down into the moss so fresh it practically steamed, and it ran toward the river.

*It's close,* she thought. *It's real close. It's like ten minutes old.*

*Should we call Jake?*

*Jake's human. He's at the Cullens'. You want to run back to Forks and knock on the door?*

*No, I just—*

*Then we take it.*

She felt him agree. She felt him get scared too, in the way Seth got scared, which was all at once and then over, like a sneeze. And she felt the thing under both of those, the thing all of them had and none of them really talked about. That old, deep, bone-level *pull*, the one that had been born into their bodies along with the fur and the teeth. A burning, eager wrongness that turned the vampire smell from something disgusting into something you wanted to tear apart with your mouth.

*Go,* she thought, and went.

---

It knew they were coming. She'd have known, if she were it.

The trail went to the river and ran along it, and then it doubled, the way a deer doubles, and she lost it for one second in the spray off a fallen log and found it again on the far side going uphill. It was moving fast, faster than anything on two legs had a right to. But not as fast as her.

Nothing was as fast as her. That was one thing she had.

*Leah, slow down, I can't—*

*Keep up.*

She hit the slope at a dead run and went up it in long, flat bounds, her claws tearing up moss and rotten wood, and the smell got thicker and thicker until she could practically see it hanging in the air, a sweet, sick, rust-colored ribbon twisting off through the trees. Rain whipped into her eyes. A branch smacked her across the shoulder and broke. She didn't feel it.

Then she saw it.

A man. Or something shaped like one. Pale flash of a face looking back over its shoulder, dark hair plastered flat by rain, a big red waterproof jacket flapping open around it, the kind hikers bought at REI, two sizes too big. It was going up the slope between the spruces in a smooth, impossible blur, its feet touching down maybe once every fifteen feet.

Its eyes, when it looked back, were the color of fresh blood.

It saw her. It saw her coming, saw the size of her, and its face did something she'd seen on maybe three vampires in her whole life, which was flat, naked, uncomplicated fear.

Then it put its head down and *ran*.

*Leah!* Seth was behind her, way behind her, a sandy blur coming up the slope. *Leah, it's running, it's running away—*

*Yeah,* she thought. *They do that. Keep up.*

She was gaining. She could hear it now over the rain, the faint scuff of its shoes on wood, the whisper of the jacket. Thirty yards. Twenty-five. It went over the crest of the ridge and down the other side, and she went over after it, and the ground dropped away into a deep fold between two ridges, a narrow valley full of old-growth cedar and devil's club and shadow. The thing went down into it like water poured down a drain. Leah went down after it.

Fifteen yards.

The smell of it was right in her face now, filling her head, and under the smell the copper was so bright it made her teeth ache. Ten yards. She could see the back of its neck. She could see the rain running off it, the way rain ran off stone. She bunched her back legs to jump.

The vampire's foot came down in a patch of moss between two cedar roots.

And it went up.

It went up so fast Leah's brain couldn't make sense of it. One second the thing was running flat out across the valley floor; the next its right foot had shot up into the air like somebody had yanked it on a string, and the rest of it had gone up after, whipping over in a long half-circle with the red jacket flapping, and then it was hanging upside down eight feet off the ground with its arms dangling and its hair hanging down and its face a foot and a half above the moss.

Hanging from nothing.

Leah tried to stop and couldn't, not completely. She skidded, her paws plowing up a wave of wet needles, and went right under it. For a fraction of a second she was looking straight up into its face. Then she was past, slamming sideways into a nurse log hard enough to knock the breath out of her, scrambling up, spinning around with her ears flat and every hair on her body standing straight up.

It hung there.

It didn't move. That was the thing. That was what made the skin crawl all along her spine. Vampires were still, sure. They stood around like furniture. But this was a different kind of still. Its arms hung down in a stiff, unnatural line. Its fingers were spread. Its mouth was half open on a snarl that had stopped halfway. The red jacket had fallen down around its shoulders and hung there, and the rain ran down its upturned face and into its hair and dripped off the ends in a steady patter onto the moss below.

Only the eyes were moving. Red eyes, rolling in a fixed white face, flicking from her to the trees to the sky to her again.

Around its right ankle, if she squinted, she could see something. Not much. A shimmer in the rain, like heat off a road, wrapped tight round the joint and going up from there, straight up, into nothing. Into the air. Into the rain.

Seth came down the slope and nearly ran into the same nurse log.

*Whoa,* he thought. *Whoa, whoa, whoa, what—*

*Don't go under it.*

*What happened? Did you do that?*

*Do I look like I did that?*

*I don't know! I don't know what anybody looks like when they do that!* He circled wide, low to the ground, his ears going back and forth. *Is it dead? Is it—why isn't it moving? Leah, why isn't it moving?*

*I don't know.*

She didn't. She walked a slow circle around it, keeping her distance, her lips peeled back off her teeth. Every instinct in her body was screaming to go for it. It was right there. It was hanging upside down, helpless, a foot off the ground, smelling like somebody's blood. She could have it in pieces in four seconds.

But the air was wrong.

She hadn't noticed it at first under the vampire stink. She noticed it now. The whole little valley smelled—different. Sharper. Like cold stone, like split pine, like the air in a cave. And under that a scorched, prickly, crackling something, not unlike the smell that hung around Layla Ashby's house when she'd been doing whatever she did at her kitchen table with the salt. Not the same. Related, maybe. Second cousins.

*That's magic,* Seth thought, at the exact same second she did. *Right? That smells like—Leah, that smells like Layla's house.*

*Sort of.*

*Did Layla do this?* He brightened instantly, the way only Seth could brighten while standing six feet from a hanging vampire. *She's been doing ward stuff all week. She said she was going to put some up for the pack, like an early warning—*

*Seth.* Leah stopped circling. *Look at it. Does that look like something Layla did?*

He looked.

The vampire hung there. Rain dripped. Its eyes rolled toward Seth, slow, and stayed on him, and in them was nothing but pure animal panic.

*No,* Seth thought, more quietly. *No. She'd have said. She'd have told me.*

*Yeah.*

*So who—*

*I don't know.* Leah's hackles were still up. Her heart was going hard, harder than the run deserved. *I don't like it. Something set this. Something set this, out here, in the middle of nowhere, in the middle of our woods, and we didn't smell it, and we didn't know.*

*Is it a trap?* A thin, unhappy note in Seth's head. *Like—for us? What if there's more?*

She hadn't thought of that. She thought of it now. She looked down at the moss under her paws, the black mud between the roots, and for one ugly second she imagined her own back leg going up into the air the way the vampire's had, her body swinging over, her whole weight hanging off one ankle in the rain.

*Don't move,* she thought. *Don't move your feet.*

*Leah—*

*Just stay where you are.*

They stood there, the two of them, ten feet apart in the deep green dim of the valley floor with the rain coming down and the vampire hanging between them like the world's ugliest Christmas ornament.

The vampire's eyes slid off Seth and went somewhere past both of them, toward the far side of the valley, and widened.

Leah heard it at the same time.

It wasn't much. It was barely a sound at all. A small, soft *thup* of air, the kind of sound a book makes when you close it on a table. If it hadn't been for the rain going quiet around it for a second, like it had flinched, she wouldn't have caught it.

She spun.

Thirty feet away, beside the trunk of a cedar so big the moss on it had its own ferns growing out of the moss, a man was standing where there had not been a man before.

He was tall. That was the first thing. Taller than most people she knew who didn't phase. Broad in the shoulders. He wore a long black wool overcoat, open, over a black suit and a white shirt and a thin black tie, and his shoes were black and shined and completely, impossibly clean, standing in the middle of the deep black mud of the valley floor like he'd just stepped out of a car at a funeral. He had black hair, wet and messy. He wasn't carrying anything. His hands were in his coat pockets.

He looked at the vampire hanging upside down from nothing. Then he looked at Leah. Then at Seth.

Then he took one hand out of his pocket, and in it was a little black leather wallet, and he flipped it open with one thumb, the way cops did on TV, and held it up toward them both.

Something gold flashed in it.

"Afternoon," he said.

### V. Credentials

The man held the badge up a second longer, so they could both get a good look.

Leah looked. A gold shield in a black leather fold, an eagle on it maybe, or something with wings, and lines of small hard lettering underneath that she couldn't get to hold still. Every time she tried to read them, her eyes slid off sideways, like a foot off a wet rock. On the other side of the fold was an ID card with a photo of the man looking bored.

"This situation," the man said, "is contained."

He had an English accent, rough and flat, like somebody who'd grown up in a place with a lot of gray in it.

"I'm going to have to ask the both of you to vacate the premises," he went on, in exactly the tone of a cop asking a crowd to step back from the tape. "As of about forty seconds ago, this is the site of a national security operation. Thanks ever so much for your cooperation. Mind how you go."

He folded the badge shut and put it back in his coat.

Leah stared at him.

Seth stared at him.

The vampire hung upside down between all three of them and dripped.

*Is he serious?* Seth's thought came through high and wobbly. *Leah? Is he for real? Did he just—*

*He just showed a badge,* Leah thought. *To two wolves. In the woods.*

*Is it FBI? I think it said FBI. Did it say FBI?*

*I couldn't read it.*

*Me neither, it kept kind of—*

*Moving. Yeah.*

*Is this, like, a Men in Black thing? Is he gonna do the flashy pen? Leah, if he does the flashy pen, close your eyes—*

"I don't have a flashy pen," said the man.

Seth's thoughts stopped dead.

Leah's didn't. Hers went up a gear. Her whole body went cold, every hair on it lifting, and her lips came back off her teeth on their own and a growl came up out of her chest so low and long she felt it in her own paws.

He'd answered. Seth hadn't said anything. Seth *couldn't* say anything. Seth was a wolf.

He'd answered what Seth *thought*.

The man looked at her the way someone on a train platform listens to an announcement about delays.

"Yes, Lassie," he said. "I can hear you. I can hear the both of you. You're thinking very loudly, and you're thinking at each other, and that makes it a good deal easier."

*Lassie?*

"Lassie," he agreed.

*I'm gray.*

"I'd noticed."

*Lassie's a collie. Lassie's brown and white. I'm not even—*

"Lassie was played by a long line of male dogs, as it happens, so I don't think anyone involved was too fussed about accuracy." He nodded toward Seth. "And that'd make you Balto."

*Balto?* Seth's thought was faint. *Like the cartoon?*

"Like the dog, mate. Real dog. Sled dog. Ran diphtheria serum across Alaska in a blizzard in 1925. There's a statue of him in Central Park. You should feel very honoured."

*How is he doing that,* Seth thought, to Leah, very fast. *How is he—Leah, how is he hearing us, it's supposed to be just us, it's supposed to be the pack—*

"Practice," said the man.

He started walking toward the vampire.

He did it like he was walking across a car park. No hurry. Hands back in his coat pockets. His shoes didn't sink into the mud, Leah noticed, though they should have; the ground there was soft enough that she'd gone in to her first knuckle. He went past Seth at about eight feet and didn't look at him. He went past Leah at about six, close enough that she could have had his arm off at the shoulder before he turned his head, and his eyes stayed on the vampire the whole way.

She caught his scent as he passed. Cold stone. Split pine. The inside of a cave. Wet wool, from the coat. And something else, way down under it, so faint she couldn't hold onto it, that made the fur along her spine stand up for a reason she couldn't name.

*Leah,* Seth thought. *Leah, what do we do?*

She didn't know. She'd never not known before, not since the first time she phased. Vampires you killed. Humans you protected. Pack you ran with. Everything had a slot. This didn't have a slot.

The man stopped under the hanging vampire and looked up at it with his head tilted, the way you'd look at a picture in a gallery that you weren't sure was hung straight.

"Hello," he said to it. "You're a long way from home."

The vampire's eyes rolled down to look at him. Nothing else moved. Up close, Leah could see a tiny, constant tremor in its spread fingers, like a muscle straining against a weight it couldn't shift.

"Name?" said the man.

The vampire didn't answer. It couldn't. Its mouth was frozen half-open on that stopped snarl.

The man waited a second anyway. Then he nodded, like it had.

"Anton," he said. "Lovely. And where've you come from, Anton? Where've you been the last few—" He stopped. Something happened in his face. It wasn't much. A tightening at the corners of his mouth, a flatness in the eyes, there and gone. "Hoh River Trail," he said, quieter. "Saturday afternoon. Two of them. Right."

Leah's growl died in her chest.

Two of them. Saturday. On the Hoh River Trail. Two people, two hikers, out on a Saturday in November in the rain, because people up here did that; she'd done that. She thought of the red jacket. Two sizes too big.

She didn't need to ask whose it was.

"And who sent you out this way?" the man said to the vampire, in the same mild voice. He waited. "Nobody," he said. "Mm. That's a lie, sunshine, but we'll come back to it."

He reached up and took hold of the shimmer around the vampire's ankle with his bare hand, like it was a rope.

"Mind your head," he said, and let go.

The vampire dropped.

It went straight down, headfirst, rigid as a plank, and the man caught it. He put out one arm, without bracing, without stepping into it, and caught it in the middle, across the back, as it fell, and the whole stiff length of it pivoted over his forearm like a seesaw and came round level, and he tucked it up under his arm the way a surfer tucks a board walking down to the water. Feet sticking out behind him. Head sticking out in front. The red jacket hanging down.

Leah had fought vampires. She'd had their arms in her mouth. She knew what they weighed. They looked like people and they weighed like headstones. She'd seen Emmett Cullen lift one end of a pickup truck, once, for a joke, and the vampires she'd taken down in the clearing last summer had hit the ground like sacks of wet cement.

The man didn't even shift his feet.

He turned around with the vampire under his arm and looked at the two of them again.

"Right," he said. "I'll be off, then. You two have yourselves a lovely evening. Mind the snares on your way out; there's a few more about. They won't take you. They're only after his sort." He hitched the vampire a little higher, like it was slipping. "I'd say it was nice meeting you, but I'm not supposed to have met you, so."

*No,* Leah thought.

It came out of her so hard it was almost a bark.

He stopped. He raised his eyebrows.

*That's ours,* she thought at him, deliberately this time, every word shoved out at the front of her head where he couldn't miss it. *This is our land. That thing came onto our land. It killed people on our land. It's ours. You don't get to walk in here in your stupid suit and flash your stupid badge and take it.*

"I think you'll find I do," said the man.

*The hell you do.*

"It's a very nice badge."

*I'll tear your arm off.*

"You're thinking about tearing my arm off," he agreed. "You've been thinking about it since I got here. Twice now you've thought about it in some detail. Very vivid. Lot of shoulder." He tilted his head at her. "Respectfully, Lassie, get in line."

Something in Leah went white and hot and very simple.

*Leah,* Seth thought, *Leah, don't—*

She didn't. She was looking at the man's eyes, and his eyes were green, a dark, clear, bottle green, and they were looking straight back into hers without one single flicker of anything in them that looked like fear. She'd been looked at by a lot of things since she started phasing. Humans, who were terrified. Vampires, who were disgusted, or wary, or hungry. Jane of the Volturi, who'd looked at the whole pack the way a bored kid looks at ants.

This guy was looking at her like she was a dog that had barked at him through a fence. Like he'd heard the bark and filed it and was now waiting, politely, to see if there was going to be anything else.

She held his eyes for one second longer than she wanted to. Then she looked away.

She hated herself for it. She'd hate herself for it for a while.

"Thank you," said the man, and he sounded like he meant it.

*Who are you?* Seth thought. He'd come a step closer, his ears forward now instead of back, his head low. Curious. God, Seth was always curious. *Seriously. What are you? Are you a cop? Are you, like, government?*

"Something like that."

*Are you reading our minds right now? Like right now?*

"I am."

*Isn't that super rude?*

"Very," said the man.

*Doesn't that bother you?*

"You're broadcasting on an open channel, mate. If you didn't want anybody listening, you shouldn't have built a radio." He shifted the vampire under his arm. "Not to worry. I've heard a lot worse in a lot of heads. Yours is mostly about a girl and some pencils."

Seth's ears went flat. Leah would have laughed if she'd had one single ounce of laugh in her.

*Where are you taking it?* she thought.

"Away."

*Are you going to kill it?*

He looked at her. He seemed to think about the question.

"Eventually," he said.

*Eventually?*

"Eventually."

*What does that mean?*

"It means eventually." He turned to go. "Off you pop."

And that would have been it. Leah would think about this a lot afterward, the way you think about the half second before a car accident. That would have been it. He'd have turned around and walked off into the trees with the vampire under his arm, and probably done whatever he did that had made the soft *thup* sound, and been gone. And she and Seth would have stood there in the rain like idiots for a while, and then gone back to Forks and phased out and told Jake, and Jake would have told Edward, and they'd all have stood around the Cullens' big glass living room arguing about it for three hours and never seen him again.

It would have been it, except for Seth.

Because Seth, standing there in the mud with his ears flat and his head low, watching the man walk away with a vampire tucked under one arm like a surfboard, a man who could hear thoughts and could make a vampire fly up into the air and hang there and could catch a quarter-ton of stone with one hand and not even shift his feet, a man who'd come out of nowhere with a sound like a book closing—

Seth thought: *He's a wizard.*

And then, because he was Seth, and nothing ever went through Seth's head just once:

*Holy crap. He's a wizard. Leah, he's gotta be a wizard. Like Layla. Like a real one. That's what the thing on its leg is, that's a spell, that's like the lanterns, that's why it smells like her house—Leah, he's a wizard, he's like Layla, he's a—*

The man stopped walking.

He didn't turn around right away. He stood there with his back to them and the vampire under his arm, in the green dim, with the rain coming down on his shoulders and running off the wool of his coat, and he didn't move at all. For a long second the only sound in the whole valley was the rain on the ferns and the river, way off, and Leah's own heart.

Then he bent down and leaned the vampire against the trunk of the big cedar, propped up stiff and straight and upright like a plank of wood leaned against a garden shed. He straightened up. He brushed something off his sleeve.

And he turned around.

It was the same face. Same wet black hair, same tired eyes. But it was like somebody had switched a light on behind it. All the boredom had gone out of him. All the *off you pop*. He looked at Seth, and then he looked at Leah, slowly, one and then the other, and Leah felt it like a hand on the back of her neck. Like somebody was going through her, room by room, opening drawers.

"Say that again," he said quietly.

*Seth,* Leah thought, *Seth, shut up, shut up, don't think, don't think anything—*

But you couldn't tell Seth not to think something. You couldn't tell anybody. It was the oldest trick in the world. Don't think about the elephant. And Seth, who had spent the last two weeks thinking about almost nothing except Layla Ashby, did exactly what anybody would have done.

It all came pouring out. Leah felt it go, and knew the man felt it too. The yellow house. The ring of floating lanterns. The rain hanging still in the air. Layla's wand in her hand, the length of it, the pale wood. *I'm a witch. Real kind. Wand, broom, the whole deal.* The Cullens' living room, the paper bird circling the ceiling, Carlisle's face. *I have never once encountered anything like this.* The Clearwaters' kitchen, the blue dish lying in four pieces on the linoleum and sliding back together with a soft, clean click. Charlie Swan standing in the back door with a six-pack in his hand, staring.

*Is this a need-to-know thing, Sue?*

The man stood very still in the rain and watched all of it go by.

When it was over, Seth's thoughts had gone small and scared and frantic, a little whimper of *sorry, sorry, sorry, Layla, sorry*.

The man let out a breath through his nose.

"Well," he said. "That's a word you two shouldn't know."

He said it very softly. It was the softest thing he'd said so far. It was also, by a long way, the most frightening. Leah couldn't have said why. Something in the air around him had changed, gone tight and cold, like the air right before lightning hits close.

"And that," he went on, in the same soft voice, "is a lot of other words you shouldn't know, either."

He took one step toward them.

Seth panicked.

Leah felt it happen. It went through her brother like a jolt of current, a white-hot spike of pure fear, every bit of it for Layla, *he knows about Layla, he saw her, he knows where she lives, he knows*, and Seth did the only thing he could think of. He ducked his head down to his own left foreleg, where a braided leather cord was tied snug above the paw, the one Layla had made him two weeks ago, with a smooth black river stone threaded on it that had one white vein running through it like a crack of lightning.

*If you ever need me,* Layla had said, tying it on him in her kitchen with her tongue between her teeth, *anywhere, any time, bite down on that and think of me really, really hard. Don't use it for snacks.*

Seth bit down.

"Don't—" said the man, sharp, one hand coming up.

There was a crack like a rifle shot.

And Layla Ashby was standing in the valley between them, in her too-big raincoat with the hood down and her curly hair coming out of its pencil, with her feet planted wide in the mud and her wand already up and pointed straight at the man's chest.

She didn't ask what was happening. She didn't ask who he was. She took one look at the scene, the man, the wolves, the vampire propped against the tree, and Leah saw her decide, all of it, in less than a second.

Something bright and red left the end of her wand with a sound like ripping cloth.

### VI. Outclassed

Ninety seconds earlier, Layla Ashby had been sitting at her kitchen table with her feet up on the woodstove and a pencil in her teeth, reading a ward schematic for a Hungarian bank vault that a former colleague had sent her for a second opinion, when something reached into her stomach and *pulled*.

She knew what it was before she'd finished feeling it. She'd built it. The lodestone on Seth's leg was half of a pair. The other half was a flat black stone the size of a quarter that she kept in her jeans pocket at all times, and the two halves were bound to each other the way a Portkey is bound to a place: one tug at his end, and her Apparation would lock onto it like a compass needle swinging north. She'd made it for him two weeks ago, after the night of the lanterns. She'd told him not to use it for snacks. She'd half expected him to use it for snacks anyway, at least once, just to see if it worked.

The tug hit hard and ragged and frantic, and under it, coming through the binding the way feelings sometimes did through very good charmwork, was pure, bright terror.

She was on her feet with her wand in her hand before the pencil hit the floor.

She didn't think about it. She turned on her heel in the middle of her kitchen, and the yellow house folded up around her into a hard black squeeze, and then the floor was gone and the world was gone and there was a loud *crack* and she was standing ankle deep in mud in the rain in the bottom of a deep green valley with her wand up.

Training was training. In the first half second, before her conscious mind had caught up, the part of her that had walked into eleven cursed tombs and come out of ten of them on her own feet had already taken the picture.

Old-growth cedar, steep ground, poor light, rain. Seth, in wolf form, ten feet to her left, ears flat, the lodestone still in his teeth. Leah, in wolf form, fifteen feet to her right, hackles up, snarling. A vampire she'd never seen before, red-eyed, propped bolt upright against the trunk of a cedar like a plank, not moving at all. And a man in a long black coat standing between them, one hand half-raised, a step in toward Seth.

Seth was scared. Seth was never scared.

The man was the threat.

She fired.

It was a stunner, but it was *her* stunner, the one she'd spent two years on the circuit refining, and it went out of the end of her wand with a sound like tearing cloth and enough behind it to put a grown troll on its back. It was fast. It was always fast. At twelve feet it was faster than most people could blink.

The man flicked it away.

He lifted his right hand, the one that was already half-up, and brushed two fingers sideways through the air in front of his chest, the way you'd brush a moth off your sleeve. The stunner bent. It bent in midair like a stream of water hitting a thumb, veered off at a right angle, and smashed into the trunk of a hemlock thirty feet away. A piece of bark the size of a dinner plate blew off it in a spray of red sparks and wet splinters.

The man looked at the hemlock. Then he looked at her.

"Evening," he said.

Layla didn't answer. Layla was already moving.

Dueling was a conversation, her old coach used to say. Old man Ortega, in the gym in Albuquerque, with his bad knee and his cigar he never lit. Dueling is a conversation, *mija*, and the one who talks first doesn't win. The one who talks *fastest* wins. You don't throw one spell. You throw a sentence.

She threw a sentence.

Jelly-Legs at the ankles, low and quick and shabby, a nothing spell, a feint, the kind you throw to make somebody look down. Incarcerous behind it, high, at the chest, a tangle of conjured cord that came out of her wand in a hissing gray rope. And, tucked in right behind the ropes where a man looking down at his ankles and then up at his chest wouldn't see it coming until it was on him, a Blasting Curse at the right knee, small and tight and nasty, enough to take the leg out.

It was a three-beat chain. It was the chain she'd won the Western Regional with when she was nineteen. In competition she'd thrown it eleven times and it had landed nine.

The man didn't look down.

He swatted the Jelly-Legs aside with the back of his left hand without glancing at it, and it fizzled into the mud. The ropes hit him in the chest and wrapped, twice around, and then just—*unwound*. They went loose and fell apart into strands and the strands fell apart into gray threads and the threads fell apart into nothing at all, into rain, and were gone before they hit the ground. The Blasting Curse he *caught*, somehow, in the air in front of his knee, in the palm of his right hand, and held it there for a fraction of a second, a fizzing hot knot of orange light. Then he turned his hand over and flicked it sideways, and it went off into a rotten stump ten feet away, and the stump blew apart into a shower of wet red pulp.

His feet had not moved. Not an inch. Not once.

And his hands were empty.

That was what hit her. Not right away. In the next half second, while she was already sliding left to get a tree between them and drawing breath for the next sentence. *His hands are empty.* There was no wand. There'd never been a wand. Everything he'd done, the stunner, the chain, the ropes, he'd done with his bare hands. And he hadn't said a single word.

She knew people who could do wandless magic. Some. A little. Summon a cup across a table, light a candle, make a pencil roll. Ortega could do a wandless Shield Charm, a weak one, and it had taken him thirty years. Wandless magic was a parlor trick. It was a party piece. It was something you did to show off at the end of a long night.

You didn't *duel* with it. You couldn't. Nobody could.

On her right, Leah came off the ground.

She came in fast, faster than Layla had ever seen anything move. One second Leah was a snarling gray shape at the edge of her vision and the next she was a gray blur three feet off the moss, launched sideways straight at the man's throat with her jaws open wide enough to take his head. And on Layla's left, Seth was coming too. He'd dropped the lodestone and he was coming low and hard at the man's legs, all of that sandy bulk behind it, a sound coming out of his chest Layla had never heard him make.

The man didn't look at either of them.

He kept his eyes on Layla. And Leah, in midair, a foot from his throat, *stopped*. Just stopped, like she'd hit the end of a chain, and then something slammed her down out of the air onto the ground so hard the moss splashed. Seth hit the same invisible wall at full gallop and went down on his chest and was pressed flat. Both of them, eight feet apart on either side of him, lay pinned to the valley floor with their legs splayed and their bellies in the mud like something enormous had put one finger on each of them and leaned.

Leah thrashed. Layla saw the muscles bunching under the gray fur, her claws tearing at the moss, her teeth snapping at air. She didn't move an inch. Seth whined, high and furious, and strained until Layla could see his eyes rolling white, and didn't move an inch either.

"Stay," said the man mildly, to the wolves. He still hadn't looked at them.

Then, to Layla: "Shall we carry on?"

Layla came out from behind the tree and threw everything she had.

---

Later, she'd try to remember the order of it and wouldn't be able to. That had never happened to her before. She'd always been able to replay her fights afterward, beat by beat, like film, every spell and every counter. It was half of what had made her good. This one came back in pieces. Flashes.

A Reductor Curse at his chest, at full power, enough to punch a hole in a stone wall. He slapped it. Literally slapped it, open-handed, the way you'd slap a fly against a table, and it went off into the ground at his feet and threw up a fountain of mud that fell back down around him without a speck landing on the coat.

A Severing Charm thrown wide, aimed at the big cedar limb above his head, sixty feet of wet wood that came down through the canopy like a falling bridge. He glanced up at it. It stopped in the air ten feet over his head and hung there, dripping, and then drifted gently to one side and lay itself down across the slope like someone setting down a sleeping child.

A conjured flock, thirty sparrows out of the end of her wand, sharp-beaked, aimed at his face. He looked at them and they turned into rain.

A Confundus and a Stinging Hex in the same breath, one high and one low. Both sent back. The Stinging Hex went straight back down the line it had come in on, at twice the speed, and she got her shield up a quarter of a second before it hit, and it hit like a brick and drove her back two full steps in the mud and left her whole wand arm ringing to the shoulder.

Her own spell. He'd sent her own spell back at her, and it had been stronger coming back than going out.

And all the time, he talked.

"That's a nice one," he said, as the cedar limb settled. "Who taught you that? The angle on it?"

And, a second later, as a curse went past his ear: "You drop your left shoulder before the big ones. Just a touch. Anyone ever tell you?"

And: "Don't do the birds again, love, I don't like the birds."

He sounded like a man commenting on a football match on a pub television, and he wasn't even out of breath. He hadn't taken a step. On either side of him, the two wolves were still pinned flat to the ground, still straining, and he still hadn't looked at either of them once.

Layla's stomach was going cold.

It started in the pit of it and spread outward, a slow, sinking, icy feeling she remembered from exactly one other moment in her life. The tomb under the Andes, the third chamber, when she'd put her hand flat on the door and felt the curse wake up under her palm, and understood, in the space of one heartbeat, that it was older than her and bigger than her and stronger than her, and that she had been wrong about everything.

Losing was the wrong word. You lost to people you could beat on a different day. You lost to people who were better than you. She'd lost plenty of times, on the circuit, to people who were better than her.

She'd never once been anywhere near this.

The names came up in her head without her calling for them. The names they'd taught at Ilvermorny in the History of Dueling elective, with the bad slides on the old projector, the ones even Ortega talked about with his voice gone quiet. Grindelwald. Dumbledore. The British one, the one from the war, Riddle, the one they still wouldn't say the other name of. Accounts of duels that had flattened buildings. Men who'd fought with whole rivers. Witnesses who'd said afterward that it had looked like weather.

Those were the stories. They were the only stories she knew that had ever sounded anything like this.

He was a man in a cheap black suit in the bottom of a wet valley in the Olympic Peninsula, and he was swatting her spells out of the air with his bare hands, and he was *bored*.

She did the one thing she had left. She did the thing that she was actually best at.

She stopped attacking him, and she built a box around him.

She did it fast, faster than she'd ever done it, because if she'd slowed down she'd have had time to be afraid. A seven-point cage, the kind she'd last used in a sealed chamber under a mountain to hold a stone guardian that had already killed two members of her crew. She drew it in the air with her wand, seven fast strokes, seven anchor points snapping down into the mud around him in a ring, and the bars came up between the points in a flash of hard white light, crackling, humming, a cage of pure warding with him standing at the center of it. It had held the guardian for nine minutes. It had held long enough for her to get two people out alive.

It was the best thing she'd ever made.

The man looked down at it. He looked at the bars of light humming in a ring around him. For the first time, he looked interested.

"Oh, that's lovely," he said. "That's really lovely work."

He reached out and touched one of the bars with one finger.

It came apart.

She felt it go. She felt it go the way you feel a stitch go in a seam you sewed yourself. One bar pulled loose, and the anchor point at its base came up out of the mud, and the next bar went, and the next, all the way around the circle, unraveling one after another like somebody pulling the loose end of a knitted scarf. It took about a second. When it was over there was nothing around him but rain.

Layla's breath went out of her in a long ragged rush.

"Seven points," the man said. He sounded thoughtful. "Peruvian base, I'd guess, but you've cut the corners on the north side to save time and tied it off with something American. Old Pueblo work? It's a good cage. If I were a big stone lad with no brain to speak of, it'd have held me for ages."

He looked at her.

"Pity I'm not," he said.

She raised her wand anyway. She didn't know what she was going to throw. It didn't matter. Her hand was shaking. It never shook.

On the ground to her left, Seth made a sound that cut straight through her, a strangled, desperate whine, and she saw his eyes on her, wide and white-rimmed, and saw him strain against the weight on his back with everything he had and not move.

The man sighed.

"Right," he said. "Sorry about this."

He moved his left hand for the first time. It went inside his coat. It came out with a pistol in it.

It was black and squarish and old-fashioned, the kind of gun she'd seen in old No-Maj war movies, and she had just enough time to register that there was something cut into the grip, some fine silver line of rune-work catching the light, before he pointed it at the ground. At the mud. At a spot about six feet in front of her boots.

He fired once.

The sound was small. That was the strangest part, afterward. A flat, sharp *crack*, smaller than her own Apparation. A little spurt of mud where the round went in.

Then the ground came up.

It came up like something underneath it had woken up angry. The whole valley floor in front of her heaved and burst, a slow-motion fountain of black earth and shredded fern and moss and root and stone erupting upward and *outward*, toward her, a wall of it. The sound arrived a fraction of a second later, a deep, punching *whump* that she felt in her chest, in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes.

The blast picked her up.

It didn't hurt, at first. She was just in the air. She was in the air and going backward, fast, her feet out from under her, her arms flung out, the rain and the trees and the dark green sky all spinning past. Her wand was in her hand and then it wasn't; she felt it go, felt her fingers open on their own, and watched it spin away end over end into the dark. She had time to think, very clearly, *Oh, that's not good*.

Then she hit.

She came down on her back in a bank of sword ferns twenty feet from where she'd been standing, and the ferns and the soft wet ground under them took most of it, and what they didn't take went straight through her ribs and out the other side. All the air left her body. White light burst behind her eyes. The world went silent except for a high, thin, endless whine in both her ears.

She lay there.

She couldn't breathe. She tried, and nothing happened, and then something hitched and caught in her chest and air came in, ragged and painful, and she coughed and tasted mud and blood where she'd bitten her tongue. Rain fell on her face. Dirt was still falling, pattering down through the ferns all around her, little clods of it, bits of moss. Somewhere a long way away she thought she heard Seth howling, but the ringing in her ears was too loud to be sure.

She turned her head.

Where she'd been standing, there was a hole in the valley floor about eight feet across, steaming faintly in the rain, ringed with torn roots and black earth. All around it, the moss had been flattened outward in a perfect star.

She'd been six feet from the front edge of it. If he'd aimed at her, there wouldn't have been anything left to land in the ferns.

He hadn't aimed at her.

She understood that, lying there in the wet with her ears screaming. He'd put it exactly where he wanted it. He'd put it where it would throw her and not break her. He'd known how far she'd fly, and where she'd land, and that the ferns would be there to catch her. He'd done the math on her body like she was a problem on a chalkboard, and he'd done it in the half second it took to pull a gun out of his coat.

Her wand was gone. She had no idea where. Somewhere in the dark under the trees.

She lifted her head, slowly. It weighed about a thousand pounds.

Through the drifting rain and the last of the falling dirt, she looked at him.

He was still standing exactly where he'd been. He hadn't moved his feet. The two wolves were still flat to the ground on either side of him, and Seth had stopped straining now and was just lying there with his head up and his eyes on her, shaking. The man put the pistol back inside his coat with a small, neat motion, the way you'd put a pen back in your pocket after signing something. He tugged his coat straight. A clod of earth that had landed on his shoulder slid off, all by itself, and fell to the ground.

He looked across the valley at her, lying in the ferns.

His face was calm. He looked like a man who'd just done a job that needed doing, and was now waiting to see whether there'd be more.

"Now then," he said. His voice carried easily across the clearing, even through the ringing in her ears. He didn't raise it. "Might we lead the rest of this conversation with words, instead of spells and teeth?"

### VII. Terms

The weight came off the wolves all at once.

Layla saw it happen from where she lay in the ferns. Both of them jerked, like dogs coming to the end of a leash that had suddenly been cut, and Seth was up and running before his legs were properly under him. He came across the clearing in four huge strides, and halfway across the air around him went hot and strange and shimmered like the air over a summer road, and he came out of it on two legs, naked, stumbling. He didn't stop. He yanked at the cord on his ankle and got the cutoffs off it and was hauling them on one-legged as he ran, and he nearly fell, and caught himself, and then he was down on his knees in the ferns beside her with his hands on her face.

"Layla. Layla. Hey. Hey, look at me. Are you okay? Are you hurt? Where are you hurt?"

His hands were hot. They were shaking. His face over hers was white under the brown and wet with rain and something that wasn't rain.

"I'm okay," she said. Her voice came out strange; she could barely hear it over the ringing. "I'm okay. Ribs. I think I bit my tongue."

"You flew. You *flew*, Layla, you went like thirty feet—"

"Twenty."

"—I saw you go and I couldn't—I couldn't *move*, he had me, I couldn't—"

"I know. Hey. I know." She got a hand up, somehow, and put it over one of his. "I'm okay."

He looked at her for one second longer. Then he looked up, over her, across the clearing, and his whole face changed. His lip came back off his teeth. A sound came up out of his chest, a low, ragged rumble that she'd only ever heard come out of him in wolf form. The air around his shoulders started to shimmer again.

Layla grabbed his wrist.

"Don't," she said. "Seth. Don't."

"He—"

"*Don't.* Look at me. Not at him. At me." She held on. She made her voice level, the way she'd make it level talking a new crew member down off a panic in a dark corridor. "He'd put you down. He'd put you down and he wouldn't even have to look at you. You saw. You *felt* it. Please. Don't."

Seth's arm trembled in her grip. The shimmer flickered around his shoulders and wavered and finally, slowly, faded out.

He dropped his head. He put his forehead down against her shoulder and breathed, big ragged breaths, his whole back heaving. She held onto him.

Over his shoulder, she watched Leah.

Leah hadn't run to anyone. Leah had come up off the ground in one fluid movement, and shaken herself once, hard, mud flying off her gray coat in a spray, and put herself directly between the man and the two of them. She stood there now with her legs braced and her head low and every hair on her back standing up in a stiff gray ridge. Leah made no sound at all. Layla almost wished she would growl. She stood there, silent, with her eyes fixed on the man, her whole body coiled like a spring.

She didn't attack. Layla saw her think about it. She saw Leah's weight shift onto her back legs and then, slowly, shift back.

Leah had learned something in the last two minutes. They all had.

"Good girl," said the man.

Leah's lips peeled back. The man ignored her.

He was still standing where he'd been, in the exact same spot, which Layla realized now he had not left once since the fight began. He stood with his hands in his coat pockets, looking at the dark under the trees off to his left, where her wand had gone.

He took one hand out of his pocket and held it out, palm up.

There was a whisper in the ferns, a long way off. Something came sailing out of the dark under the cedars, end over end, and slapped into his open hand. Ten and a quarter inches. Sycamore. The handle wrapped in a strip of old leather she'd put on it herself at fifteen, when the wood started getting slippery in her sweaty palms at tournaments.

He looked at it. He turned it over in his fingers, the way a man looks at a tool in a shop he isn't planning to buy.

"Sycamore," he said. "And—Wampus, is it? Bit of a temper on it. Bet it likes a fight." He glanced up at her. "Ilvermorny?"

Layla didn't answer.

"Course it is," he said.

He walked over toward them. Not fast. Leah moved with him, keeping herself between, step for step, her shoulders rolling, and he let her. He stopped about ten feet away, out of the ferns, at the edge of the trampled moss. Then he did something she didn't expect. He crouched down on his heels in the mud, so that his eyes were level with hers, his coat pooling on the ground around him, her wand balanced loosely across his knee.

Up close, in the dim green light, she got her first real look at him.

Mid-twenties. Maybe a bit older; he had the eyes of somebody older. Black hair, a mess, plastered down by the rain. A face that was all angles, unshaven since yesterday, a jaw with a shadow on it. Green eyes, very green, very steady. No scars on his face that she could see. A tired face. A face that looked like it had been awake for a long time, in a lot of places.

She looked for anything else. She looked hard. She'd been trained to look; you didn't walk into a cursed room without reading every inch of it first. A ring. A pin. A tattoo on the wrist. A habit of the hands. The cut of the coat or the shoes. The accent said England, said somewhere ordinary, said nothing else at all; it could have been put on that morning for all it told her.

There was nothing. He was a blank page in a black suit. She went through everything she knew about everyone who did this kind of thing, everywhere, looking for a box he fit in, and came up with nothing at all. She couldn't place him. She couldn't even start.

He held her wand out to her, handle first.

She stared at it.

"Go on," he said. "It's yours. I don't want it."

She took it. Her fingers closed around the leather and the familiar weight of it came back into her hand, and she felt the wand know her, a faint answering hum in the grip, and she nearly cried with relief, and then she understood exactly what he was telling her by giving it back, and the relief went cold.

*I don't need you unarmed,* that was what it meant. *It makes no difference.*

She didn't point it at him. She thought about it. She didn't.

"Thank you," said the man, like she'd done him a favor. "That's better."

"Who are you?" she said.

"Nobody in particular."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you get," he said.

It took her a second. Then her stomach dropped. Those were her words. That was what she'd said to Rosalie Hale in the Cullens' living room, two weeks ago. *It's the one you get.*

He'd gotten it out of Seth's head. He'd been in Seth's head, going through it like a filing cabinet, the whole time.

"What do you want?" she said.

"Right this minute?" He considered. "A cup of tea. And for nobody to try and blow my knee off for at least the next quarter of an hour. I'd take either."

"Why are you here?"

"Fishing."

"For *that*?" She jerked her chin at the vampire, still propped stiff and straight against the cedar on the far side of the clearing, its red eyes fixed on them.

"Caught one, didn't I." He didn't look round at it. "First in three weeks. You'd not believe how slow it's been."

"Three *weeks*?"

"Give or take."

"You've been here three weeks."

"In and around."

She stared at him. Three weeks. Three weeks, in Forks, with her wards humming away under the porch and Seth coming and going up the driveway, and she hadn't had the faintest idea.

"Miss Ashby," he said.

She went very still.

"Yellow house, out past the Calawah bridge," he went on, in the same easy voice. "Wards on the place that hum. Sort of a B-flat, I'd say, if you put your ear to the ground. Very nice work. Lovely, actually. Salt under the drive, and something on the fence posts. Blood?" He tilted his head. "I clocked them the night I got here. Thought: that's a professional. That's someone who doesn't want to be bothered. And I didn't bother you. I gave your house a wide berth for three weeks, and I'd have carried on giving it a wide berth for as long as I was here. Then you'd never have known I existed."

"How do you know my name?"

"It's on your mailbox."

It was. She'd painted it there herself, the first week. ASHBY, in black letters, because Doris the mail carrier had asked.

"Here's the thing, though," said the man, and he shifted his weight on his heels, and the rain ran off his shoulders. "Here's my problem. I gave you a wide berth because I thought you were sat in your nice yellow house minding your own business. And instead—" He looked at Seth. Seth, kneeling beside her, his arm still across her, glared back at him with naked murder in his eyes. "Instead, it turns out you've been busy."

Layla said nothing. There was nothing to say.

"Let's count," he said. He held up a hand. He ticked off fingers. "One family of vampires. Seven of the big ones and one little one. That's eight. A pack of shapeshifters." He nodded at Seth, at Leah. "Three of those in this pack, plus however many in the other one, since you lot can't keep a thought to yourselves and I'd be amazed if it stopped at the border. Their mum, Sue." Another finger. "And Charlie Swan." He paused on that one. "Chief of police. Nice man. Had breakfast next to him this morning, as it happens. He likes his eggs over easy and he reads the paper in quarters." He let his hand drop. "Every one of them knows the word *witch*. Every one of them knows it's real. Every one of them has watched you do magic with their own eyes. And not one of them is a wizard."

"They're not—" Layla started. Stopped. "The Cullens aren't No-Majes. The wolves aren't—"

"Aren't they?" He raised his eyebrows. "Show me the bit of the Statute that covers them, then. Go on. Show me the clause. The one about seven-foot dogs and sparkly blokes with gold eyes. I'll wait."

She couldn't. There wasn't one. No one on the whole of the International Confederation had ever heard of anything like the Cullens or the Quileutes. As far as the law was concerned they were exactly what they looked like.

"And the chief of police," he said, gently, "is very much a Muggle."

The rain came down. Seth was very quiet beside her. She could feel him listening, feel him trying to follow.

"You know what happens next," the man said. "If somebody found out. You know better than I do. Your Congress isn't famous for its sense of humour about this. Never has been. Wasn't so long ago they'd have locked you up for having a cup of tea with a No-Maj, let alone showing him a party trick in his girlfriend's kitchen." He shrugged. "It's different now, mostly. They don't do that anymore. But they'd still take your wand. That's the least of it. They'd take your wand and they'd take your licence, and you'd not work on a crew anywhere in the world again, and you'd do some time, I'd guess, somewhere unpleasant. That's you."

"Fine," said Layla. Her voice shook. She hated it. "That's me."

"And then," he said, as if she hadn't spoken, "they'd come here. They'd come with a squad of Obliviators, and they'd go door to door. They'd start with the police chief. They'd do the mum. Then the wolves. All of them, in both packs, because how would they know where to stop? And then, if they were brave enough or stupid enough, and I'd bet on stupid, they'd go and knock on the door of that big glass house up by the river and try it on the vampires as well. They'd take every memory any one of them has of you. Every one. Down to the studs."

He looked at Seth.

"Everything," he said. "Lanterns. Porch. All of it."

Seth's arm went rigid across her.

"They can't," Seth said. His voice came out hoarse. "They can't—they can't make me forget her. I imprinted. You can't just—it's not—"

"Maybe they can't," said the man. "I don't know. I've no idea what a memory charm does to whatever that is you've got. Neither does she." He nodded at Layla. "Neither does anybody. Nobody's ever tried. Would you like to be the first?"

Seth didn't answer. He'd gone gray under the brown of his skin.

"No," said the man. "Didn't think so."

Layla found her voice.

"You're not American," she said.

"Am I not?"

"You're operating on American soil. Without anybody knowing. You've got a—" she gestured at the vampire with her wand hand, a jerky little motion, "—a whole—*thing*, set up, snares, whatever this is, all over the peninsula, and you just pulled a gun on me and fired it, and I am a citizen of the United States. If anyone's in trouble here—"

"So go and tell them," he said.

She stopped.

"Go on," he said. "Go to your Congress. Walk in the front door in New York and ask for somebody in Law Enforcement and tell them all about me. And they'll say, right, Miss Ashby, and who is he? And you'll say, I don't know. And they'll say, well, what's his name? And you'll say, I don't know. Where's he from? Who does he work for? What's he doing here? I don't know, I don't know, I don't know." He tilted his head. "And then they'll say, well, Miss Ashby, how did you happen to meet this man you don't know anything about, out in the woods, in the rain, at four in the afternoon on a Tuesday? And what were you doing there?"

Layla closed her mouth.

"You'd have to tell them about the dogs, love," he said quietly. "You'd have to tell them about the dogs, and the vampires, and the police chief, and every single thing you've just spent six weeks doing. And by the time they'd finished writing all that down, I'd be gone. I wouldn't even be a rumour. And you'd be the one in the dock."

He was right. She knew he was right. She'd known he was right before he got halfway through. She sat there in the wet ferns with her ribs throbbing and her ears still ringing, and she looked at him crouched there in the mud in his black coat with his tired face, and she felt the shape of the thing he'd built around her, all its walls and corners, and understood it was the same kind of thing as the cage she'd thrown at him. He'd just done it with words. And unlike hers, his was going to hold.

"What do you want?" she said again. It came out flat.

"There we go." He nodded, as if she'd finally got a hard sum right. "Two things. Easy ones. First thing: none of you saw me. Not today, not ever. Not a word to your Congress, not a word to anyone in your world, not a letter, not an owl, not a quiet chat in a pub with an old mate from a tomb crew. I don't exist. You've never met me."

"And the second?"

"Second thing: one of these days, I'm going to ask you for something. You, or him, or her," he nodded at Leah, still bristling between them, "or your mate Jacob, or anybody else in this little circle of yours. I don't know what yet. Might be nothing much. Might be a big ask. When I ask, you find a way to say yes."

"That's blackmail," said Seth.

"Yeah," said the man.

He said it the way he'd have agreed with Seth that it was raining.

"And if I say no?" said Layla.

"Then I make a call I'd rather not make." He shrugged. "And somewhere in an office in New York, a very bored person opens a very boring envelope, and has the most interesting afternoon of their entire career. And then we all find out together what a memory charm does to a wolf."

There was a long silence. The rain filled it.

On the far side of the clearing, the vampire hadn't moved at all. Its eyes were still on them. Layla wondered, distantly, what it was thinking. Whether it was listening. Whether it understood any of it.

"What are you going to do with that?" she said, nodding at it.

The man glanced back at the vampire for the first time.

"Ask it some questions."

"And then?"

"And then it's not anybody's problem anymore." His face didn't change, but something in his voice did. It went a little harder. "It killed two people on Saturday on the Hoh River Trail. A man and a woman. Married, I think. Late fifties. It's wearing his coat." He looked back at her. "In case you were wondering whose side to be on."

Layla looked at the red jacket hanging off the vampire's stiff shoulders. Two sizes too big.

She felt sick. She hadn't known that. She should have known that. She'd come down into this valley and fired at the first thing she saw that looked like a threat to Seth, and she hadn't stopped to think, not even for a second, about the thing that was actually there.

"The family up at the glass house," the man said. "The Cullens. Your telepath's going to have this whole conversation out of the pair of them by suppertime, I'd guess." He nodded at Seth and Leah. "So you can give him a message from me, and save me a trip. Tell him his family's in no danger from me. None. Tell him I'd like very much for it to stay that way." He paused. "And tell him not to come looking. He won't find anything. But he'll annoy me, and I'd rather be friends."

"Friends," said Layla.

"I'm very friendly," he said. "Ask anybody."

He stood up. His knees didn't crack. He brushed off the front of his coat, though there wasn't anything on it, and looked down at her.

"How are the ribs?"

She blinked at him.

"Your ribs," he said patiently. "You came down on them hard. Anything broken?"

"I—don't know."

"Then let him look." He nodded at Seth. "And don't Apparate anywhere till you know. You'll splinch something you're fond of."

Seth was already moving. He'd been waiting for permission without knowing it, Layla thought, and the second he had it he turned his whole body toward her, crouched over her in the ferns, his big hot hands at the zip of her raincoat. "Can I—? I just need to—"

"Yeah. Yeah, go on."

He pulled the coat open. He pushed up the hem of her Henley, gently, so gently, and his breath hissed in through his teeth at whatever he saw there, and he put his palm flat on her side, just under her breasts, and pressed very lightly. She sucked in a breath.

"Sorry. Sorry. Does that—?"

"It's bruised. It's okay. It's bruised."

"There's a—there's a mark, Layla, it's going all purple already—"

"Bruised," she said. "I've had broken. I know broken."

He bent over her and pressed his face into her hair for a second and breathed. His whole attention was on her, now. All of it. There was nothing in the whole valley for him except her, the way there had been nothing in the whole Thriftway except her, that first day. She put her hand on the back of his head and held it there, and looked past his shoulder.

The man in the black coat had turned away from them.

He'd turned toward Leah.

For the whole fight and the whole conversation, Layla realized, he hadn't really looked at Leah. Not properly. He'd talked to her, called her names, answered her thoughts, pinned her to the ground. But he'd kept his eyes on Layla, and on Seth, and on the vampire, and every time Leah had come into his line of sight his gaze had slid over her the way you'd slide your eyes over a piece of furniture that was in the way.

Now he looked at her. Straight at her. And Leah, standing ten feet away in the mud with her hackles still up and her head still low, looked straight back.

### VIII. The Rope

Leah had heard all of it.

She'd stood in the mud with her hackles up and her legs locked and listened to every word, and while she listened she'd thought, as loudly and as vividly as she could, about every single thing she'd like to do to him. She'd thought about his throat. She'd thought about his stupid black coat in strips. She'd thought about the exact sound his arm would make coming off at the shoulder. She'd done it deliberately, on purpose, at the very front of her head, the way you'd shout at somebody through a wall you knew they had their ear pressed against.

He hadn't so much as twitched.

That was almost the worst part. Worse than being pinned to the ground like a bug. Worse than lying there with her face in the moss and her legs splayed, pushing with everything she had and moving nothing at all, while he swatted Layla's spells out of the air like a guy at a barbecue swatting wasps. Worse than the gun. Worse than watching Layla fly.

He'd let her think all of it. He'd heard every word. And he'd just let it wash over him like rain off his coat, and gone on talking about the Statute of Secrecy and memory charms and *a call I'd rather not make*, and called her a good girl.

*Good girl.*

She was going to kill him. She knew it with a flat, perfect certainty, the way she knew which way was north. Maybe not today. Maybe not this year. But she was going to find a way.

And under that, under the rage, in a little cold place way down in her chest that she wouldn't look at directly, she was more afraid than she'd been since the first night she phased.

Because he was right. About all of it. About Layla, about the Statute, about the Obliviators going door to door. About what that would mean for Seth. She'd seen it play out behind her eyes, clear as a movie, while he talked. Her baby brother, the only person left in the whole world who didn't need anything from her except that she exist. Waking up one morning with a hole in the middle of him he couldn't see the edges of, and an imprint pulling at him toward a woman he'd never met. She'd seen what imprinting did to people. She'd lived inside it for two and a half years. She couldn't begin to imagine what it would be like with the other end cut off.

So she'd stood there, and she'd kept her teeth to herself, and she'd let him win.

Now Seth had his face in Layla's hair, and his hands on her ribs, and his whole world had narrowed down to the twelve inches between them, and he'd stopped thinking about anything else at all. Leah could feel it through the pack, the way she always could: the whole of Seth's head gone soft and bright and focused, every thought he had pointed in one direction. The man had stepped back and turned away from them.

He'd turned toward her.

And for the first time since he'd stepped out of nowhere with his book-closing sound and his badge, he looked at her.

He'd looked *toward* her, plenty of times. He'd looked through her, past her, over her. She'd noticed. She'd been noticing all along; she'd been half-glad of it, the way a dog is glad not to be the one the man with the stick is looking at. His eyes had slid off her every time like she was a piece of furniture.

They didn't slide off now. They came down onto her and stayed.

Green. That dark, bottle green. He was going to say something. She could see it. His mouth was already open, a little; he'd drawn breath for it. Something dry and mean, probably. *Lassie.* Something with *Lassie* in it.

Their eyes met.

He didn't say anything.

---

Afterward, she'd try to explain it to herself, and she'd come up with a hundred different ways, and none of them were right.

Leah Clearwater knew what imprinting looked like. She knew it better than anyone alive. She'd seen it happen to Sam from the outside, standing in her own aunt's kitchen with a bowl of potato salad in her hands, and watched his face go slack and stupid and holy while he looked at Emily over her shoulder. Then later, after she phased, she'd seen it again from the inside, through the pack, in his memory, over and over and over: the earth tilting, the bottom dropping out. She'd been in Jared's head for it. She'd been in Quil's head for it, and that one had been the hardest to watch, because Claire had been two years old and what Quil felt for her was something else entirely, something she didn't have a word for, and it had still taken him away. She'd been in Jacob's head for it, in the Cullens' living room, three months ago. She'd been in Seth's head for it in the Thriftway.

She knew exactly what it looked like.

She had never once wondered what it felt like. She'd made a point of not wondering. It was the one door in her whole head that she'd bricked up.

It felt like a rope.

That was the first thing. For all the others it had been gravity, the earth tipping over, the floor dropping out. For her, nothing tipped and nothing dropped. Leah stood on four feet in the deep black mud at the bottom of the valley, with the rain coming down on her back, and she felt something come out of her chest. Out of the very middle of her. It came out of the place right under her breastbone where her wolf lived, where the heat came up from when she phased, and it went across the ten feet of air between them in a straight, hard line.

It went into him.

She *felt* it go into him. Through the black coat and the white shirt and the skin and the bone, deep into his chest, much deeper than she'd have thought a chest went. And it found something there.

There was something there.

She felt it the way you feel a stair in the dark with your foot, a solid, unmistakable shape. Something curled up in the dark at the bottom of him, in a space that should have been too small to hold it. Something with a heartbeat that was not quite his heartbeat. Something that had been lying with its head on its paws for a long, long time, in a place it didn't much like, waiting to be let out.

The rope touched it, and it lifted its head.

And the rope *tied*. She felt it knot itself, right there in the deep dark of him, around that thing, around the *wolf*, a hard, sure, double knot like the knot her dad used to tie on the boat when he wanted it to hold in weather. And then it came back. It came straight back across the ten feet of rain and air into her own chest, the same line, the same rope, and it found her wolf where it lived under her breastbone, and it tied itself off there too.

Both ends. Both directions. Him to her. Her to him. Pulled tight.

Leah's legs nearly went out from under her.

Across the clearing, the man in the black coat took a step backward.

It was a real step. A stumble. The first time his feet had moved since he arrived, without his deciding they should. One shiny black shoe went back into the mud and slid, and his hand came up and pressed flat against the center of his own chest, against his breastbone, hard, like a man who'd just been shot and was trying to find the hole.

His face had gone completely white.

All the boredom was gone. All of it. All the *Lassie* and *good girl* and *off you pop*. All the tired, patient, I've-seen-worse calm that he'd worn through the whole fight like a second coat. It had all been knocked straight off him, and what was left underneath looked about twenty years old. He was staring at her with his eyes wide and his mouth open and his hand on his chest, and he was breathing like he'd been running.

"What," he said, "the *fuck*."

---

It was so quiet in the clearing that Leah could hear the rain on the individual leaves.

Seth had lifted his head. She felt him do it, felt his attention come off Layla for the first time in five minutes, felt the sudden sharp swerve of it in the pack. He was looking at her. Then at the man. Then at her.

He knew. Of course he knew. He'd done it himself, six weeks ago, in the baking aisle. He'd seen the look on her face, and the look on the man's, and he knew.

"Leah?" he said, out loud, in a small, cracked voice. "Leah. Did you just—did he just—"

"Did he just *what*?" Layla. Sharp, confused. She'd pushed herself up on one elbow. "What happened? What was that?"

"He—" Seth's voice broke. "I think he imprinted. On Leah. I think—*Leah?*"

"He's not a *wolf*," Layla said.

But he was. Leah knew it now. She knew it the way she knew her own name. She could feel it, at the other end of the rope. She could smell it too, now. That faint wild something she hadn't been able to put her finger on when he walked past her earlier, way down under the stone and the pine and the wet wool, was right there, right at the front of his scent, as plain and loud as her own. Wolf. Different from hers, and from Seth's, and from Jake's or Sam's. Older, maybe, or from somewhere else, or made some different way. But a wolf. Definitely, absolutely a wolf.

And it was awake. And it was looking at her.

Leah's wolf, the one inside her, the one that was her, the one she'd hated for eight months and fought with and lived with and finally made a cold, tired peace with, came up onto its feet and looked back.

She'd never felt anything like it. Not when Sam kissed her for the first time, at sixteen, behind the Uleys' woodshed. Not when he left. Not the first night she phased, in the yard, with her dad on the ground. Nothing. The whole of her, every hair, every nerve, every inch, was turned toward the man in the black coat like a compass needle swinging north. And she could feel, down the rope, in the dark at the other end, that the same thing was happening to him.

*No,* she thought. *No. No, no, no, no. Not me. Not this. Not this, not this, not me, I don't want this, I never wanted this, this is the one thing I never—*

He flinched.

She saw it. She saw it hit him. Whatever he'd been doing to hear her thoughts, he was still doing it, or he couldn't stop, or the rope was doing it for him now. Her *no* had gone straight down it and into him like a punch, and he flinched from it like a man taking a punch, his head going back a fraction, his hand pressing harder on his chest.

He stared at her. He opened his mouth. He shut it again.

Then he turned away.

It was abrupt and clumsy, nothing like the way he'd moved all afternoon. He turned like a man who'd just remembered he'd left the stove on. He walked straight across the clearing in four long strides to the big cedar, where the vampire was still propped stiff and silent against the trunk with its red eyes rolling. He bent and scooped it up under his arm like a surfboard, without a word, without a glance, the way you'd grab your keys off the table on your way out the door.

He straightened up. He looked back at her one more time over his shoulder.

The look went right through her. It went down the rope, and she felt it land.

His face was still white. His eyes were wide and dark and green and completely, nakedly shaken. He looked, for one second, like he was going to say something.

Then he turned on his heel, and the air folded around him, and he was gone.

There was barely a sound. Less than before, even. A tiny soft *hup* of air, like somebody blowing out a match.

---

The rope didn't break.

That was the first thing Leah understood, standing alone in the mud with the rain coming down. She'd half expected it to. She'd half *hoped*. She'd waited, for a long, frozen second, to feel it snap and come whipping back into her chest, and leave her alone, and leave her empty and normal and pissed off and herself.

It stretched instead. It paid out from the middle of her chest like line paying out off a reel, longer and longer, thinner and thinner, but never breaking, and never going slack. And the other end of it was somewhere. She could feel it. She could feel *where*.

That way.

North and a little east. Up and over the ridges. A long way off, but not so long. Not too long.

She turned her head and looked that way, toward the dark wall of trees on the far side of the valley, and the pull in her chest went *tight*.

"Leah," said Seth.

He was on his feet. He'd stood up from beside Layla without seeming to notice he'd done it, and he was standing there in his cutoffs in the rain, barefoot, his chest heaving, staring at her with an expression on his face she'd never seen on him before. It took her a second to place it. It was the face she'd made at him, six weeks ago, when he came home from the Thriftway.

"Leah, wait. Don't. You don't know where he—you don't know what he—"

She knew where he was. That was the whole problem.

"He's dangerous, Leah, you saw him, you *saw* what he—Leah, just wait, just phase back for a sec and *talk* to me—"

She looked at her brother. She loved him more than anything left in the world. She'd have died for him, any day of the week, without thinking about it. She'd have killed for him. She'd stood still and let a stranger call her a good girl for him, which was harder.

The rope pulled.

Her wolf went.

She didn't decide to go. Afterward she'd want to believe she'd decided. She'd turn it over and over, trying to find the moment where she'd chosen, and she'd never find it. Her legs bunched under her and she went, flat out, in one long low leap out of the mud and away across the valley floor. She went past the steaming crater where Layla had been standing and past the big cedar with the fresh scar on its trunk and into the dark of the trees on the far side. Behind her, very far away, she heard Seth shout her name.

Then she was climbing. Up the wall of the valley, through devil's club and salal, over a nurse log, up and up through the rain toward the ridge, with her claws tearing up the moss and her breath going in and out of her in big, hot, steaming gusts. She'd never run like this. She was always the fastest. She'd never been this fast. The rope in her chest was pulling her up the slope like a winch, and she was running with it and into it and toward it, and the faster she ran, the tighter it got.

She hit the top of the ridge and didn't slow down. The wind hit her there, cold and wet off the ocean, full of rain and spruce and salt and, way, way off, so faint it was barely there at all, so faint she'd have missed it on any other day of her life: cold stone. Split pine.

North and a little east.

She went down the other side like a gray stone rolling downhill.

Nobody was in her head. Seth was human. Jake was human. Sam and his pack were somewhere else, on the other side of the line, in another link entirely. For the first time in eight months, the only voice in Leah's head was her own. And her own voice, running flat out through the darkening woods with the rain in her eyes and a rope through her chest, kept saying the same thing, over and over, like a heartbeat.

*Not me. Not me. Not me. Not me.*

And under it, quieter. So quiet she could pretend for a while she didn't hear it.

*Faster.*

### IX. Open Book

Harry came out of the squeeze in the hollow above the South Fork of the Calawah, twelve miles north and east of the valley he'd left, and his knees nearly went.

He caught himself on the trunk of the big spruce with his free hand. The vampire under his other arm swung forward with the momentum and its head knocked against the bark with a sound like a cricket ball on a bat. He didn't apologise to it.

The hollow was the same as he'd left it. Same nurse log, sixty feet of it, lying across the slope with a row of young hemlocks growing out of its back. Same fern-bank, same carpet of moss, same rain. Same green two-man tent sagging under the spruce boughs with the broken zip and the moss on its guy-lines. The wards hummed around the edges of the clearing at the pitch they always hummed at. Nothing had changed. He'd been gone less than half an hour.

He stood there with his hand on the tree and breathed.

The thing in his chest was on its feet.

Three years he'd had it, and he'd never felt it like this. It had stopped pacing. It was standing at the bars, with its whole body pressed to them, and it was staring south and west toward a valley twelve miles away, and it was *pulling*. The rope ran out of the middle of it, through the bars, through his ribs, out of his chest, and away through the trees in a line he could have drawn on the map table with a ruler. At the other end of it, small and bright and hot and furious, was her.

Moving.

He could feel her moving.

"Right," Harry said, to nobody. His voice sounded odd. "Okay. Right."

He took his hand off the tree. He shifted the vampire to a better grip. He walked across the clearing and ducked through the flap of the tent with it, and the inside opened up around him the way it always did, oak floors and lamplight and the smell of coffee and gun oil and old paper. The record player was sitting silent where he'd left it. The half-eaten slice of cherry pie was on the map table where he'd dropped the fork. The cold tea was beside it.

He'd got up from the table twenty-odd minutes ago, when the cord on his wrist jerked, thinking: *Finally.*

He walked down the corridor to the specimen room, and through the door, and the cold came up off the flagstones at him the way it always did. He carried the vampire down the length of the room past the steel bench and the shelves to the circle at the far end, and stood at its edge, and tipped it upright, and set it down inside the silver ring on its own two feet.

The runes lit.

They went up in a slow ripple round the ring, one after another, a dull silver-blue. The snare's hold on the vampire came loose as the circle's hold took over, he felt the handover happen like one hand passing a rope to another, and the vampire swayed very slightly as it went, and then stopped. It stood in the middle of the circle with its arms hanging and its feet together and the red jacket hanging off its shoulders. Its eyes moved. Nothing else did.

Harry flicked two fingers, and the hold came off its face and its jaw.

The vampire's mouth worked. It made a sound like a gasp, though it didn't need the air.

"Please," it said. It had a faint accent, Czech or Slovak, smoothed over by two centuries of everywhere else. "Please. Whatever you are. I can—"

"Don't go anywhere," Harry said.

He walked back out of the room and shut the door behind him.

---

He went to the kitchen. He didn't know why. He put the kettle on. He didn't need tea. He stood there and watched it start to steam.

His hands weren't steady. He looked at them. He held the right one out flat in front of him and watched the fingers. There was a tremor in them. A fine one. He'd been shot at, cursed, bitten, half-drowned, set on fire twice, and once dropped down a well in Albania with something at the bottom of it that he still had nightmares about, and he could count on one hand the number of times in eight years that his hands had done this.

He put the hand flat on his chest. On his breastbone. Pressed.

She was still coming. Closer now. He could feel it, the way you feel someone climbing the stairs toward a room you're sitting in. He could feel which way she was going and how fast, and he could feel her heart going, or something like her heart. He could feel the shape of what she was feeling, too. It came up the line at him in waves. Rage, mostly. A great deal of rage. Fear, under it. And under that, something that pulled at the rope from her end the same way the thing in his chest was pulling at it from his, and that she very clearly hated.

*Not me. Not me. Not me.*

He was doing nothing to make it happen. It arrived on its own.

The kettle clicked off. He ignored it.

*Think,* he told himself. It was what he'd been trained to do. It was the first thing they taught you in the Department and the last thing they let you forget: when you don't understand something, you stop and list what you know. Leave out what you think, or fear, or want. List what you *know*.

What he knew.

The Quileute wolves imprinted. That was in the file. The Department's file on the Quileutes was four pages long and mostly guesswork, written in the spring by an analyst in London who'd never been within five thousand miles of La Push and had cobbled it together from tribal legend, two anthropology papers, and a long night with a Pensieve full of distant surveillance. There was a paragraph on imprinting. It was a bond. It came on sight. It was permanent, or close enough. It went one way. The wolf was bound to the person; the person was free to feel however they liked about it. *Function unknown,* the analyst had written. *Possibly reproductive selection. Possibly not. Recommend further study.*

It went one way.

This had not gone one way.

He'd felt her rope come out of her chest and come into his, and find the wolf in him, and tie on. He'd felt it. And then he'd felt something come out of *him*, out of the wolf, back down the line into her, and tie on there too. Both ends. Both directions. Like two people grabbing the same rope at the same time from opposite ends and both refusing to let go.

He didn't know why. He had a guess. The guess was that whatever she was, he was something close enough to it that the bond didn't know which end it was supposed to be. He had no way to test that guess. He'd need a lot more than four pages.

Fine. What else did he know?

He knew it couldn't be walled off. He'd tried, in the first half second, in the valley. He'd thrown up every shield he had, eight years of Occlumency, walls he'd built brick by brick against people who'd spent their whole lives learning to break walls down. The rope had gone under them like water under a door. It hadn't even noticed they were there.

He knew he'd run.

He'd picked up the vampire and turned on his heel and run, from a twenty-one-year-old girl standing in the mud on four feet, and he'd done it without thinking, and he hadn't run from anything since he was seventeen.

And he knew that the thing in his chest, which he'd kept locked down for two months, which he'd kept on a short lead for three years, which had done every single thing he'd asked it to do in all that time, including staying quiet, was not going back in its kennel. He could feel it. It was done with the kennel. It wanted to go to her. It wanted to see *hers*. It wanted that more than it had wanted anything since the night he'd first felt it wake up. He'd thought he understood wanting things. He'd wanted to understand magic for eight years, all of it, every corner. He'd thought that was the biggest want he had.

It turned out it was the second-biggest.

He picked up the mug and poured hot water into it and forgot to put a teabag in. He drank some anyway.

He thought about the mirror on the wall in the next room. He thought about tapping it twice, waiting, tapping once. *Ma'am. Slight complication.* He thought about Ellery's face. He thought about what Ellery would say. What the Department would say, when it understood what he was telling it: that there was a woman on the Olympic Peninsula who turned into a wolf, and something none of them had ever seen before had tied her to one of their own. They'd want to understand it. That was what they were for. And the Department was never gentle with the things it wanted to understand.

He'd seen the inside of the jars in the specimen room. He'd filled half of them.

He put the mug down.

He didn't touch the mirror.

---

She hit the outer wards at 4:31.

He felt it on the cord round his wrist and in the soles of his feet, a tremor through the ground of the hollow like a stone dropped into a pond. The outer ring was the simplest of the lot. It turned things. A hiker, a hunter, a curious bear, anything that came within a quarter mile of the tent would suddenly find that it had always meant to go the other way, that it had left something on the stove, that there was a much better trail just over there to the left. They'd go round. They'd never know they'd gone round.

She hit it, and it turned her, and she went round. He felt her go. For about forty yards.

Then the rope in her chest pulled tight, and she stopped, and she turned back, and she hit the ward again. She put her head down and *pushed*.

Nothing had ever pushed on it before. He felt it bend.

He could have held it. He knew exactly what he'd need to do to hold it; he'd built it. One thought and the ring would firm up and harden into a wall she'd never get through if she ran at it till Christmas. She'd circle out there in the rain for an hour, two hours, howling, and eventually she'd give up and go home to her brother, and the rope would stay stretched across twelve miles of forest, and he'd sit in here and feel her hate him down it every day for the rest of the job.

He opened it.

He did it without deciding to, the same way he'd run. One thought, and the ring parted in front of her like a curtain, and she came through it at a dead run and kept coming.

The middle ring was the one that hid the tent. He opened that too.

He went out to meet her.

He'd taken the overcoat off when he came in, and the jacket, and dropped them over the back of the sofa. He was in shirtsleeves, with the tie pulled loose and the holster still strapped on over the white shirt, and the rain soaked through the shoulders of the shirt in about four seconds. He didn't bother fixing it. He walked out into the middle of the hollow, past the nurse log, onto the moss, and stopped there, and waited.

She came out of the trees at the top of the slope.

She stopped dead at the edge of the clearing when she saw him. Gray. Lean. Smaller than her brother by a good bit, long in the leg, built for running more than anything else. Mud to the shoulders. Her sides going in and out like a bellows, steam coming off her back in a white haze. Her ears were flat and her lips were drawn back off her teeth and she was looking at him across sixty feet of rain with her whole body.

The rope went tight. Tighter than it had been all afternoon.

The thing in his chest hit the bars so hard he felt his ribs flex.

He stayed where he was. He kept his feet planted on the moss and his hands at his sides, and he didn't let it out. It took more than he'd have liked. He could feel her wolf looking at him out of her eyes. He could feel it looking for his. He could feel his looking back.

He'd never been looked at like that by anything in his life.

"You found it, then," he said.

It came out rough. He cleared his throat.

She snarled. A long, low, rattling sound.

"Yeah," he said. "Fair."

She took a step forward. Stopped. Her claws dug into the moss.

"I'm not going to hurt you," he said.

He didn't think about it before he said it. It came out because it was true. He realized, half a second later, that it would have come out anyway, whether he'd wanted to say it or not.

She stared at him. Then she turned and went behind the trunk of the nearest spruce, and he felt, rather than saw, the heat come off her, that pull and snap in the air that he'd watched Seth do in the valley. He turned around and looked at the tent. He looked at the moss on the guy-lines. He gave it a decent count of ten.

"You can turn around," said a woman's voice behind him. Low, rough, out of breath. "I'm not naked. We tie our clothes to our legs. It's a whole thing."

"So I gathered," he said, and turned around.

She was standing at the edge of the trees in a black cotton dress that came to her knees and was soaked dark already, barefoot in the moss, with her arms folded hard across her chest. Tall. Five-ten, five-eleven. Short black hair plastered to her head. Brown skin, wet. A long face with high cheekbones and a mouth that looked like it had spent a lot of time pressed into a flat line. Dark eyes. She was shaking, a little. He didn't think it was the cold. He knew it wasn't the cold; he could feel it wasn't, down the rope. She ran too hot for cold.

They stood there in the rain and looked at each other.

"What did you do to me?" she said.

"Nothing."

"Don't—"

"I didn't do anything. I'd tell you if I had." He said it, and then he heard himself say it, and something cold went down the back of his neck.

She heard it too. He watched her hear it. Her eyes narrowed.

"Say something that isn't true," she said.

"What?"

"Just do it. Lie. Say something that's a lie."

He opened his mouth to say *I'm from Leeds*. It was the first thing that came into his head. It was a stupid, harmless, nothing lie; he'd told it to a dozen people in a dozen places. He knew exactly what shape his mouth needed to make.

Nothing came out.

He tried again. *I'm from—*

His throat closed up. It didn't hurt. It just didn't work. It was like reaching for a step in the dark that wasn't there. The words were in his head, all lined up and ready to go, and between his head and his mouth they simply stopped, like they'd hit glass.

He shut his mouth.

"Huh," he said.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she lifted her chin.

"I came here to kill you," she said.

Except she didn't. Her mouth opened on the *I* and stayed there. Her jaw worked. He watched her try. He watched her try twice, three times, the muscles standing out along her neck, and get nothing out at all past the first word. Her eyes went wide and furious, and she clapped one hand over her own mouth like it had betrayed her, which in a manner of speaking it had.

"Huh," said Harry again, quietly.

She took her hand away. "That's—"

"Yeah."

"That's not—that doesn't *happen*. That's not a thing. Seth can lie to Layla. Sam lies to Emily all the time. That's not—"

"Did you come here to kill me?"

She glared at him. She opened her mouth. She shut it again.

"No," she said finally, through her teeth. "I don't know why I came here. I just came."

"Right."

"Did you know this was going to happen?"

"No."

"Has this happened to you before?"

"No."

"Are you reading my mind right now?"

"No."

That stopped her. She looked at him with something new in her face, something careful, measuring.

"Why not?" she said. "You were before. The whole time. You were in my head like it was your living room."

"I know."

"So why not now?"

He thought about it. He found he had to think about it, because the answer had to be true, and he didn't know what it was yet.

"Because I'd rather you told me," he said.

She stared at him.

He hadn't known that was the answer until he heard it come out. He'd been reading minds for a living for six years. He'd been in the heads of murderers and politicians and grieving widows and children and people who were about to die. He'd never once in all that time thought about whether he'd rather they told him. It had never come up.

He'd been lying for a living for eight years, too. Every day. To everyone. Even to the people he cared about most; Hermione and Ron knew him better than anyone alive, and they'd never once heard the real answer to *what are you working on at the moment*. He'd never thought of it as heavy. It was just what the job was. You put it on in the morning like the suit.

He thought of it as heavy now, standing in the rain. He couldn't pick it up. And there was something in his chest that felt very much like he'd been carrying something up a long hill for eight years without noticing, and somebody had just taken it off his back.

He liked it. That was the stupid part. He didn't know what to do with how much he liked it.

"What *are* you?" she said.

"A wizard."

"And?"

"And something else."

"Yeah. I got that. I *felt* that. In the valley, when it—" she gestured at her own chest, at his, a short, savage motion, "—when it *happened*. There's a wolf in you. There's a wolf in you, and it's not like ours, and it was *awake*."

"Yeah," he said. "It's awake now."

"Is that what this is? Because you're a—because you've got a—"

"I don't know. Best guess, yes. But I don't know."

She looked at his chest. At the spot under his breastbone where the rope went in. He could feel her looking at it. He could feel her wolf looking at it, through her eyes, the same way his was looking back.

"Show me," she said.

The thing in his chest threw itself at the bars.

He stood there and let it, and didn't move, and didn't open anything. It took a lot. He kept his hands down at his sides and made himself breathe.

"No," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because I'm not ready. And because if I let it out right now I don't know what it'd do, and I'd like to know that before I find out." He was surprised how even his voice came out. "Not today."

She searched his face. He let her.

"But you want to," she said.

"Yeah," he said. "I want to."

He could say that, too. It came out the same way everything else had. Plain. Like setting a mug down on a table.

"I've kept that side of me on a short lead for the last couple of months," he said. "Since before I came here. I've had a lot of reasons. Good ones. All of them still stand." He looked at her. "I'm pretty sure that's done for now, though."

She didn't say anything. The rain came down between them. Down the rope, he felt something shift in her. He couldn't have put a name to it. Something that had been under the rage and the fear the whole time came up for air, and then went back under, fast, like she'd caught herself at it.

"What's your name?" she said.

"Harry."

He felt it hit her.

It came up the rope so hard and so fast that he took a half step back without meaning to, the second time in an hour, and put his hand on his chest again. Grief. Old grief, eight months old, still raw at the edges. And under it, something far older and worse, a sick, cold, crawling weight, and he didn't need to read her mind to know what that was. He knew what guilt felt like from the inside. He'd lived in it for years.

She'd gone gray under the brown of her skin. She was staring at him like he'd hit her.

"Harry," she said.

"Yeah."

"Of course it is. Of course it's fucking—" She laughed. It was a horrible sound. She put the back of her wrist against her mouth and turned her head away, and stood like that for a second in the rain with her eyes shut.

He waited.

"My dad's name was Harry," she said, into her wrist.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't."

He didn't.

"He died in March," she said. She didn't look at him. She sounded like she was saying it to the trees. "Heart attack. In the yard. I phased. First time. Right there in front of him, I didn't know what was happening, I didn't know what I was, and he—" She stopped.

She didn't have to say anything else. He could see in her face that she knew she didn't have to, and that she could stop there, and that she wanted to say the rest anyway. He could feel her standing on the edge of it.

She didn't say the rest. She let it sit there in the rain between them.

"That's on me," she said instead.

He thought about saying *that's not on you*. He thought about it for a long time. He'd have said it to anyone else. He'd have meant it, too, mostly.

He found that he couldn't make his mouth do it. He didn't know whether it was true, and whatever this was in his chest, it didn't seem to care for the difference.

"I don't know that," he said. "I wasn't there."

She looked at him.

For a long second he thought she was going to come across the clearing and hit him. He'd have let her. Down the rope, he felt her think about it.

Then something in her face cracked, very slightly, at one corner, and she let out a breath that was almost a laugh, a real one this time.

"Everybody always says it's not my fault," she said. "Everybody. My mom. Seth. Sam. *Charlie*. You're the first person who hasn't."

"I can't say it. Apparently."

"Yeah." She wiped rain off her face with the heel of her hand. "Yeah. Apparently."

They stood there.

"So now what?" she said.

He looked back over his shoulder at the tent. At the sagging green canvas and the broken zip.

"Now," he said, "I've got a vampire stood in a circle in there, and a lot of questions to ask it. I was in the middle of asking them when your brother started thinking very loudly about witches." He turned back to her. "I'd like to get on with it."

"You're just going to—what? Go back to work? After *this*?"

"It killed two people on Saturday. It's not going anywhere, and I'd like to know what it's doing here before anybody else gets hurt." He paused. "And I'd like you to come in. If you want."

She stared at him.

"Why?"

"Because you'll want to see what I do with it. Because it came onto your land and killed people on your land and you were the one chasing it." He shrugged. The rain had soaked his shirt through; the holster straps were dark with it. "And because I don't think I could stop you following me in anyway. Not today."

"Is that true?"

"Must be," said Harry. "I just said it."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at the tent. Then back at him.

"That tent," she said, "is about the size of a sleeping bag."

"Yeah," said Harry. "About that."

He held the flap open for her.

### X. Bigger on the Inside

Leah ducked under the flap of a sagging green tent the size of a sleeping bag, and straightened up in a hallway.

She stood there. She didn't move. Water ran off her hair and down her neck and dripped off the hem of her dress onto a floor made of real wooden boards, old ones, dark honey-colored oak with nail holes in it. There was a narrow runner down the middle of the boards, red and worn. Canvas walls on either side, the same green as the outside. And then, about six feet along, the canvas just stopped, and turned into plaster, painted white, with a light switch on it.

The hallway went on for about thirty feet. There were doors off it. There was a framed print on one wall, crooked, of a black car she didn't recognize. Somewhere at the far end she could hear a refrigerator humming.

And over her head, close, loud, drumming, was the rain on the canvas. It sounded exactly like rain on a two-man tent. Like it was right there, a foot above her head. Like if she reached up she'd touch the roof.

She reached up. Her fingers touched nothing. The ceiling was nine feet away and made of wood.

"Yeah," said Harry, behind her, letting the flap fall shut. "Everyone does that."

"What the hell," Leah said.

"Bit of extension work."

"It's a *tent*."

"It's a very good tent."

"It's a *house*. You've got a house in a tent. You've got a—is that a *light switch*?"

"It doesn't do anything," he admitted. "I put it in because the wall looked wrong without one."

She turned around and stared at him. He was standing on the mat inside the flap, dripping, in his soaked white shirt with the gun harness over it and his tie hanging loose around his neck, and he looked about as tired as she'd ever seen anybody look. He also looked, very slightly, like he was waiting to see what she'd think of it. Like a guy who'd had somebody over for the first time.

She didn't say anything about it. She felt it, though. Down the rope.

"Through there's the main room," he said, pointing. "Kitchen's at the end. Loo's the second door on the left if you need it. There's towels in the cupboard by the loo. You're soaked."

"I don't get cold."

"You're dripping on my floor."

"It's your floor. It's a floor in a tent."

"It's a chapel floor," he said. "Welsh. Very old. Go and get a towel, Lassie."

She looked at him.

"Leah," he said, after a second. "Sorry."

She went and got a towel.

---

The bathroom had a real toilet and a real claw-foot tub and a mirror over the sink with a crack in one corner, and a shelf with a razor and a toothbrush and a bottle of some kind of shampoo with a handwritten label. She stood in front of the mirror with a towel over her head and looked at herself.

She looked the same. Same face. Same hair, wet and flat. Same mouth. She'd thought she'd look different. She'd seen Sam, the day after Emily. She'd seen Seth the day after the Thriftway. They'd both looked like somebody had turned a light on behind their faces, like they'd been lit up from the inside.

She just looked tired. And scared. And very, very angry.

And under it, if she looked really hard, something else.

She stopped looking.

There was a sweater folded on the edge of the tub when she came out from under the towel. Navy blue, wool, thick, the kind fishermen wore. She hadn't heard him come in. She hadn't heard the door. It was just there, folded neatly, on the edge of the tub, where it hadn't been a minute ago.

She stood there and looked at it for a long time.

Then she pulled it on over the wet dress. It came down to the middle of her thighs. The sleeves came down past her fingertips and she had to shove them up. It smelled like him. Cold stone and split pine and wool and, way down under it, faint and wild and impossible to miss now that she knew what it was, wolf.

Her wolf sat up and paid very close attention.

"Shut up," Leah told it, under her breath, and went out.

---

The main room was bigger than her mom's whole house.

There was a big leather sofa, scuffed and cracked at the arms, with his black overcoat and suit jacket thrown over the back of it. There were bookshelves, floor to ceiling, two whole walls of them, crammed, books stacked sideways on top of other books, some of them in languages she didn't think she'd even seen written down before. There was a record player on a low cabinet, an old one, the kind with a lid, and a row of records in sleeves on the shelf underneath it. A fireplace. A real one, stone, with a fire going in it that she hadn't smelled from outside. A small silver mirror on the wall between two of the bookcases, plain, about the size of a paperback book.

And in the middle of the room, taking up most of it, a table.

She walked up to it slowly.

It was carved. The whole top of it. It was a map, a real one, cut into the wood in three dimensions, and she knew it before she was halfway across the room, because she'd run every inch of it. The peninsula. The mountains standing up in the middle, the rivers running down out of them, Lake Crescent like a long blue thumbprint, the coast with all its little notches and points. There was Forks, a tiny cluster of houses she could have covered with her palm. There was La Push. There was First Beach, and James Island off it, and the mouth of the Quillayute. She could see the place where the river went past the end of her mom's road.

Clouds were moving over it. Little gray drifts of real cloud, like smoke, sliding slowly across the carved wood from west to east. Over the coast, where it was raining outside, it was raining on the table too, a fine misty drizzle she could see if she bent down close, beading on the carved trees.

And over all of it, like frost on a window, was a web of thin blue light.

She'd never seen anything like it. Threads, dozens of them, hundreds, running every which way over the whole peninsula from little points of brighter light scattered all over the map. North to Neah Bay. South to Lake Quinault. East all the way to Port Angeles and beyond. Every few seconds a pulse of brighter blue went sliding along one thread or another.

Her land. Her whole land, every inch of it, and he'd covered it in this.

"That's the snare," he said, behind her. He'd come in quietly, carrying two mugs. "Forty-one anchor points. You ran into one of them this afternoon. Well. He did."

She didn't answer. She was looking at the coast.

There were points of light all up and down the coast, everywhere. Kalaloch. Ruby Beach. Lake Ozette. Neah Bay. Cape Flattery. Everywhere, except for one stretch, a long gap in the web where the threads ran around the edge of it and didn't cross. From the Quillayute north to the headland and inland about five miles, there was nothing. Not a single point of light. Not one thread.

La Push. The rez. The treaty land.

"You stayed off it," she said.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

"Because it's yours." He set one of the mugs down on the edge of the table, by her hand. "And because I didn't want anything of mine where you'd smell it."

She looked at the mug. It was tea, with milk in it.

"I don't drink tea."

"I didn't know what else you'd drink. There's coffee. There's beer. There's a bottle of whisky somebody gave me in Inverness that I'm saving for a funeral, probably my own."

"Coffee."

He took the tea back and drank from it himself and went off toward the kitchen, and she heard a kettle go on, and then his voice from the doorway, over his shoulder: "There's pie, as well."

"What kind?"

"Cherry. Half of one. Bit of it's been eaten."

"By who?"

"Me. About an hour ago. When I was having a nice quiet afternoon."

Leah's stomach made a noise she hadn't let it make in front of another person since she was twelve. She'd run fifteen miles with no break since lunch, most of it flat out. Her body burned through food like a woodstove burns through kindling, all of theirs did, and she hadn't eaten since a sandwich at eleven.

"Yeah," she said. "Pie."

He came back with a mug of coffee and a plate with what looked like the whole rest of a cherry pie on it, and a fork, and put them both down on the corner of the map table, off the map, on the bit of plain wood at the edge. He didn't say anything else. He went and stood by the fireplace with his tea and let her eat.

She ate the whole thing. She didn't look up. When she was done she put the fork down on the empty plate and sat back, and wiped her mouth on the sleeve of his sweater, and caught him watching her from the fireplace with an expression she couldn't read.

"What?"

"Nothing," he said. "Good pie, that."

"It was okay."

That came out fine. That was true. It had been okay. Mostly it had been there.

He almost smiled. She saw it start and stop.

---

"Who are you?" she said.

She'd been sitting with it since the valley. Since before the valley. Since he'd stepped out of nowhere with a sound like a book closing and flashed his stupid badge. She'd heard him tell Layla *nobody in particular*. She'd heard him tell Seth *something like that*. She'd heard him tell Layla, in that same easy voice, *It's the one you get*.

He couldn't do that to her. Not anymore. She knew it, and he knew it, and she watched him know it.

He put his mug down on the mantelpiece.

He looked at it for a while, like he was thinking about picking it up again. Then he looked at her.

"My name's Harry Potter," he said. "I work for the British Ministry of Magic. The government. Ours. The wizard one."

"You have a *government*?"

"Several. One per country, more or less. Yours is in New York. Your witch is very scared of it."

"Is that who you work for? The—Ministry?"

"Part of it. A department of it. It's called the Department of Mysteries." He said it flatly, like reading a sign. "The people who work there are called Unspeakables."

"Why?"

"Because we're not allowed to talk about it."

"But you're talking about it."

"Yeah," said Harry. "I've noticed."

She waited. She could feel him, down the rope, deciding something. Or not deciding. Finding out what he'd already decided.

"I've never told anyone that," he said. "Not on a job. Not ever. My two best mates know what department I work in, and that's all they know, and they've known me since I was eleven." He shook his head, once. "And I just told you in about four seconds, and I didn't even have to try. And I'm finding I don't mind."

"Why don't you mind?"

"No idea," he said honestly. "Ask me tomorrow."

"What do you do? In the—department?"

"Bit of everything." He thought about it. "Imagine if your CIA and your DARPA had a kid, and nobody was ever allowed to say its name out loud. Half of us are sat in basements trying to work out how magic works. Why it works. Where the edges are. The big questions. What time is. What death is. What happens in somebody's head when they love someone. The other half go out into the world and do things nobody's supposed to know got done." He shrugged. "I do both."

"Which half is this?"

"This is both."

She looked at the table. At the web of blue light lying across her land.

"Why vampires?" she said. "Why here?"

"Because nobody on our side of things had any idea yours existed until last spring." He came over to the table and stood across it from her, with the carved mountains between them. "We've got vampires, in our world. Had them forever. They're nothing like yours. They're sort of sad, mostly. They sit in the dark and drink blood out of bottles and write very bad poetry about it, and if one of them bites someone, we've got paperwork for that. We know what they are. We know how they work."

"And ours?"

"Ours didn't know yours were there. Nobody did. Not for—" He stopped. "As far as we can tell, ever. Then last March, in a town in Italy called Volterra, at noon on a feast day, in a square full of tourists, a young man with bronze hair stood in the shadow of a clock tower and very nearly stepped out into the sun with his shirt off."

Leah's head came up.

"Edward," she said.

"Edward Cullen," Harry agreed. "Bella'd jumped off a cliff, and his sister'd seen it, and he thought she was dead. He wanted to die too, and he'd found a way to make sure. I've seen the memory. Second-hand. One of ours happened to be in the square that day, on holiday, eating an ice cream." He touched the edge of the table with one finger, near La Push. "She didn't see much. A girl running through a crowd. A boy in the shadows. A lot of men in red cloaks. Then all three of them went down an alley and just vanished, and she thought that was odd. She went home and put it in a report, because she's the sort that puts things in reports, and somebody in my department read it, because we're the sort that read reports. And we pulled the thread."

"And what was on the other end?"

He looked at her. Something in his face had gone flat and hard and cold, and she felt it go cold down the rope, too.

"Volterra," he said. "You know about them. You've met some of them."

She had. She'd seen them come across the clearing last summer in their long gray cloaks, after the newborn fight. The little blond girl. The big one. The way they'd looked at the wolves through the trees.

"The Volturi," she said.

"They've been there a very long time," Harry said. "In that town. Under it. Running things. Making the rules for everything like them, everywhere, and killing anything that breaks them. And to keep themselves fed, every few weeks, they bring tourists in. Busloads. A guided tour of the old town. And the tourists go down into the cellars to see the frescoes, and the tourists don't come back up." He was quiet for a second. "They've been doing it for centuries, as far as we can tell. In the middle of Europe. In the middle of *our* world, and nobody on our side had the faintest idea."

Leah stared at him.

"And you're going to—what?"

"My bosses are thinking about it," he said. "Very hard. They'd like it to stop."

"*Good*," said Leah.

It came out of her so hard and fast she surprised herself. She hadn't known she was going to say it. She hadn't known how much she meant it until it was out.

He looked at her for a second. Then he nodded once, slow, like she'd confirmed something.

"Yeah," he said. "That's about where I am too."

"So that's why you're here? To catch one?"

"To catch one and learn everything I can from it. What hurts them. What holds them. What doesn't. What our side of things can do to yours, and what it can't. Because if we're going to go knocking on that door in Volterra one day, I'd quite like to know what's going to open it." He tapped the table. "And there's no good way to study the ones in Italy without them knowing. And the Cullens are off limits."

"Why?"

"Because they're more use to us upright, and friendly, and in one piece." He said it without any softening at all. "A doctor who's been practising for three hundred years. A man who reads minds. A girl who sees the future. If it ever comes to a fight with Volterra, I'd rather that lot were stood on our side of the field than theirs."

"*Useful*," Leah said.

"Yeah."

"That's what they are to you. Useful."

"That's what they are to my department," he said. "I don't know what they are to me yet. I haven't met them. I've watched them buy groceries."

She looked at him across the table. He looked back. She waited to feel him duck it, or dress it up, or slide off it somehow, the way people did. The way Sam did when he didn't want to tell her something. The way everybody did, all the time, without even thinking about it.

He stood there and let her look. He'd told her the ugly version, the true version, the one with no paint on it, and he hadn't flinched from it, and he wasn't going to.

It should have made her angrier. She kept waiting for it to.

Down in her chest, around the rope, something moved that she didn't have a name for.

---

"You know I'm going to phase," she said.

They were still at the table. She'd pulled one of the stools up to it; he'd stayed standing. Outside, she could tell from the sound of the canvas, the rain had gotten heavier, and it was full dark.

"I know."

"Eventually. I have to. I can't just—I'm on the schedule, I've got the morning run tomorrow with Seth, and Jake's going to want to know, and even if I don't run I'll lose my temper at something and it'll just *happen*. And the second I do, they're all going to see it. Everything. All of this. Everything you just told me. The tent. Your name. The Department. Volterra. Jake and Seth, at least, and if Jake phases near the Cullens then Edward gets the whole lot right out of his head, and if anybody from Sam's pack is phased at the same time it goes over the line too." She took a breath. "I can't stop it. I can't hide anything when I'm phased. Nobody can. That's how it works."

"I know," said Harry.

"So why are you telling me?"

He thought about it. She watched him think about it.

"Because you asked," he said.

"That's it?"

"That's it. You asked, and I don't have it in me to say no to you right now, and I'm finding I don't want to." He shrugged. "So your pack's going to know a lot more about me by tomorrow morning than my own Department would be comfortable with. I'll deal with that. I've got some ideas."

"Like what?"

"Like going and having a word with your Alpha before you phase. Face to face, with my mouth, instead of with your head. I'd rather he heard it from me."

"Jake's going to want to rip your throat out."

"I'd expect nothing less," said Harry. "I've just blown up his best mate's girlfriend."

Leah snorted before she could stop it.

He looked at her.

"You don't like her," he said. "The witch."

"I don't *not* like her." Leah frowned. That had come out fine. She tried again, carefully, feeling for the edges. "I like her fine. She's good for Seth. She's smart. She doesn't take crap. I just—" She stopped. She looked at the map. "She's what he got. When he imprinted. She's—*that*."

"And you think you got me."

Leah didn't answer.

She didn't have to. She felt it go down the rope anyway, all of it, in a lump. All of it, the whole ugly tangle she'd been carrying since the valley. *I didn't want this. I never wanted this. I watched it take Sam from me and I swore it would never, ever touch me, and of all the people in the whole world it could have picked, of all the—*

This time he stood there and let it hit him.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "Fair."

"It's not *fair*. None of it's fair." The words came out of her fast and hot. "You know what I thought? When it happened to Sam? I thought, okay. Okay, it's about kids. It's some kind of—gene thing. Breeding. It picks the best match so there'll be more wolves. That's what all the old stories say, more or less, if you read between the lines. I thought, that's why it skipped me. I thought, that's why it'll *always* skip me." She stopped. She hadn't meant to get here. She'd started down the road and the road had kept going. "Because I'm—because it doesn't work. My body. Since I phased. It stopped—doing what it's supposed to do. Every month. It just stopped. I'm a dead end. I'm a genetic *dead end*, I'm the only girl wolf in God knows how many generations and I can't even—" Her voice cracked. "So why would it pick me? What's it *for*? What's the point?"

She heard herself. She shut her mouth so hard her teeth clicked.

She'd never said that out loud. Not to her mom. Not to anybody. The pack knew, because the pack knew everything; they'd all seen it in her head, and none of them had ever said a word about it, and she'd hated them for that, and been grateful, both at the same time. But she'd never *said* it. Never to a person's face. Never on purpose.

She'd opened her mouth and it had come out, with nobody asking and nobody going looking, because she'd wanted, for one second, for somebody to hear it who hadn't stolen it out of her head.

The fire popped. Rain drummed on the canvas a foot above a ceiling nine feet away.

"I don't know what it's for," Harry said.

She looked at him.

"I read the file," he said. "We've got a file on you lot. Four pages. Some bloke in London wrote it who's never been within five thousand miles of here. It's got one paragraph on imprinting, and the paragraph says *function unknown*. He's got a guess about breeding in there, too, and then he crossed it out and wrote *possibly not*." He held her eyes. "Nobody knows what it's for. Not your elders, not my lot, not anybody. And I'd bet you this whole tent that none of them ever saw one go both ways before today, because nobody I've ever heard of has had the bits to do it with. So nobody on earth knows what this is for. You don't. I don't." He shrugged. "I'm going to find out, though."

"How?"

"Same way I find out everything. Very slowly. With a lot of notes."

She stared at him. And then, before she could stop it, something came up out of her throat that was very nearly a laugh.

"You're going to *study* it."

"Course I am. I study everything. It's literally my job."

"Am I going to end up in a jar?"

He went still.

She'd meant it as a joke. Mostly. She felt it land wrong, felt it go down the rope and hit something in him that went cold and hard and very serious.

"No," he said.

It came out flat and absolute. Down the rope, she felt that it was the truest thing he'd said all night. Simpler than a promise. A fact, like *the river runs west*.

"Okay," she said, after a moment.

"Okay," said Harry.

---

He showed her the rest of it after that. Not all of it. Some of it.

The kitchen, which was tiny and messy and had a box of some English cereal she'd never heard of on the counter and a fridge that wasn't plugged into anything. A room full of books and glass jars and bubbling things over little blue flames that smelled like a swamp and a spice cabinet at once, which he said was for potions, and which she backed straight out of with her nose wrinkled. His bedroom, from the doorway: small, a camp bed with army blankets on it, a lamp, a pile of paperbacks, nothing on the walls.

And then a door at the end of a short side corridor, with a heavy iron latch on it.

He stopped beside it and didn't open it.

"Armoury," he said.

"Guns?"

"Among other things."

"Can I see?"

He looked at her for a second. Then he lifted the latch and swung the door open and stood back.

It was smaller than she'd expected. Racks on the walls. Long guns down one side, maybe half a dozen, old wood and dark metal: a shotgun, a lever-action rifle like her grandfather's, a short ugly double-barreled thing, something long with a scope in a hard case. Knives and swords on the other wall, and some things she didn't have names for. A workbench at the back with a vice clamped to the edge and a little rack of tiny, fine tools laid out on a cloth, like a jeweler's.

And everything, every single thing, had silver lines cut into it somewhere. Little marks. Patterns. On the stocks, on the blades, on the barrels, under the grips. Like writing. Like the marks on the old cedar boxes up at the tribal center that nobody alive could read anymore.

"You did all that?" she said. "The—carving?"

"Most of it. Some of it twice."

"The one you shot Layla with." She nodded at the harness still strapped round him, at the dark, squarish grip sticking out of it under his left arm. "That's one of these?"

"That's the first one I ever did properly."

"What does it shoot?"

He thought about how to answer it. She watched him do it.

"Whatever I need it to," he said.

She looked at him for a long time.

"You didn't aim at her," she said.

"No."

"You could have."

"Yeah."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because she's not what I'm here for. Because she was trying to protect your brother, and doing a fairly good job of it, considering. And because I didn't need to." He let the door swing shut and dropped the latch. "If I'd needed to, I would have. You should know that."

"I do," said Leah. And she did. She could feel it. He was telling her what was true, that was all.

He looked down the side corridor, toward the last door. It was a plain door, plainer than the others. Painted gray. No handle on the outside that she could see. The air around it was colder. She could feel it on her shins from where she was standing, coming out underneath it, like a draft from a freezer.

And under the cold, faint, but there, the sweet, sick, burnt-sugar stink of vampire.

"That's where it is," she said.

"That's where it is."

"You said you were in the middle of asking it things."

"I was."

"You said I could watch."

He looked at her. In the narrow corridor, in the lamplight, he looked very tired and very steady and very much like someone who'd been doing this for a long time, and who was about to do it again.

"Before we go in," he said, "you should know a couple of things about how I do this."

"Like what?"

"Like this." He held up one hand. "There are three curses, on my side. Three spells. They were outlawed nearly three hundred years ago, for everyone, everywhere, with a life sentence in prison for using any one of them on another person. They call them the Unforgivables. One of them takes over a person's mind, makes them do whatever you want. One of them causes pain. The worst there is. And one of them kills, instantly, no way to block it and no way back."

Leah said nothing.

"There's a very short list of people who are allowed to use them anyway," Harry said. "Legally. With a piece of paper that says so. I'm on it. Everybody in my Department is."

"Have you?" Leah said. "Used them?"

"Yes."

"All three?"

"Yes."

"On people?"

"Yes."

He kept his eyes on hers when he said it. She waited for him to explain. To give her a reason, a story, a *they had it coming*. He stood there and let her see it.

"Were they bad people?" she said finally.

"Mostly," said Harry.

That was all. *Mostly.* She felt it go down the rope, and she knew it was true, every inch of it, the *mostly* included. Especially the *mostly*.

She thought about the clearing last summer. The newborns, coming across the grass. Her teeth in one of them. The sound it had made. She thought about Jake, after, broken in half on the ground. She thought about the red jacket. Two sizes too big.

"Is it going to scream?" she said.

"Might do," said Harry. "I'll try and keep it to a minimum. I'm there to find out what it knows. But if it won't tell me, and I need to know, I'll do what I need to do, and I won't feel bad about it after. That's who I am at work. I'd rather you saw it than heard about it."

Leah looked at the gray door.

She thought about going home. She could. She could turn around right now and walk back out through the hallway and the sagging green flap and into the rain, and phase, and run. Seth would be in her head inside a mile. Jake, probably, within the hour. She'd tell them everything, because she couldn't not. And then she'd never have to see whatever was behind that door.

The rope in her chest sat taut and steady, tied off at both ends, pulling her nowhere, waiting to see what she'd do.

"Open it," she said.

---

### XI. Where We Left Off

The cold hit her first.

It came out through the door the second Harry pushed it open, a flat, dry, stone-cellar cold that went straight through the wet dress and the wool sweater and into her skin, and Leah's skin hadn't felt cold since March. She stopped in the doorway with her arms wrapped round herself and looked.

It was a long room. Longer than the main room, narrower. Stone floor, big gray flagstones fitted together without mortar. Whitewashed stone walls. Lamps hanging from chains, giving off a hard white light with no flicker in it. Down the middle of the room ran a long steel table with a gray stone top, scrubbed clean, bare except for a notebook and a pen and a wooden rack of little glass vials with cork stoppers.

Shelves down both walls. Jars on the shelves. Boxes. Things in the jars that she looked at once and then decided not to look at again. Most of them were just shapes in cloudy liquid. One of them was a hand, or something like a hand, with too many joints. One of them had a label in small, square, neat handwriting that said *Albania, 2004 — DO NOT OPEN*, and something inside it that was very dark and very still and that she could have sworn, as she passed, turned slightly to follow her.

She didn't look at the shelves again.

At the far end of the room, set into the floor, was a ring.

Silver, inlaid flush into the stone, eight or nine feet across. Inside it a second ring, of marks like the ones in the armory, cut into the flagstones and filled with something that glowed a faint, cold blue. And standing in the middle of the ring, exactly in the center, with its feet together and its arms hanging straight down at its sides, was the vampire.

It had seen her. Its eyes had come round to the door the moment the door opened, and they were on her now. Red, like fresh blood under glass. The rest of it hadn't moved at all. The red jacket was still hanging off its shoulders. Its hands were still spread stiff.

But its face was free. She could see that. She could see its lips move.

"Oh, *no*," it said. It had an accent, something soft and old and Eastern European, worn smooth. "Oh, you brought the dog."

Leah's lip came up off her teeth before she could stop it.

"Manners," said Harry mildly, walking past her down the length of the room.

He didn't look at the shelves either. He went to the end of the steel table, picked up the notebook and pen and a heavy wooden stool from underneath, and carried the stool over to the edge of the silver ring and set it down about three feet outside it. He sat. He crossed one ankle over his knee and opened the notebook on his thigh.

He'd rolled his shirtsleeves up. The holster was still on. He looked, Leah thought, exactly like a guy sitting down to fill in a form at the DMV.

"Right," he said. "Where were we?"

The vampire looked at him. It didn't say anything.

"You were telling me nobody sent you," Harry said. "Back in the valley. Let's start there again, shall we?"

"Nobody sent me."

"That's a lie, Anton."

"I came on my own. I hunt where I like. I was passing through."

"Also a lie." Harry wrote something down. "Third time's the charm. Who sent you?"

The vampire's face moved. It was a strange thing to watch, the face going on its own on top of that frozen body, like a puppet's face on a stick. It smiled. It was a very good smile. It had clearly been practicing it for two hundred years.

"Let me tell you how this goes," it said. "You are a clever man. You have clever tricks. I don't know what you are, but I can see you are clever, and I can see you want something from me. And I have things. I have *so many* things. I have been alive for two hundred and thirty-five years. I know names, places. Things your people would pay a great deal for. Things nobody else alive can tell you." The smile widened. "So. We make a trade. I tell you some of what I know. You let me out of this—" a flick of the eyes down at the glowing ring, "—*delightful* little circle. I walk away, and you never see me again, and we are both happier. Yes?"

Harry looked at it for a moment over the top of the notebook.

"No," he said.

"Then you get nothing."

"I get everything," said Harry. "That's the bit you haven't caught up with yet. I just thought I'd give you the chance to hand it over nicely first."

Something changed in the vampire's face. The smile stayed. Everything behind it went still.

"Last chance," Harry said. "Who sent you?"

The vampire's lips pressed together into a thin, white line.

Harry sighed.

He uncrossed his ankle and set the notebook down on the floor beside the stool. He stayed on the stool and leaned forward a little, with his elbows on his knees, and looked into the circle, and Leah saw his eyes change. Saw them focus. Sharpen. Like he was reading very small print a long way off.

The vampire went rigid. Its eyes went wide.

Harry said nothing. He didn't move. But the vampire's face started to *work*. Its jaw clenched. Its lips peeled back from its teeth. A sound started up somewhere in its chest, low and thin and strained, like somebody trying with everything they had to hold a door shut while somebody bigger leaned on the other side.

Leah realized what she was watching. She'd felt it herself, in the valley, that sensation of somebody walking through her head opening drawers. She'd hated it. She'd hated it with her whole body. But he'd done it to her softly, carelessly, the way you'd flick through a magazine in a waiting room.

He wasn't doing it softly now.

"You're quick," Harry said, conversationally. His eyes didn't leave the vampire's. "I'll give you that. You lot think very fast. It's like trying to read a book while somebody flicks through the pages." A pause. "And there's a lot of book. Two hundred and thirty-five years, you said. You never forget anything, do you? Not one day. All of it, just sitting there."

The vampire hissed through its teeth. Its eyes had gone wild.

"You're hiding it behind other things," Harry said. "Very good. Very fast. You keep putting memories in front of it. A woman in a market. A ship. Snow. A church on fire." He tilted his head. "That's a lot of churches on fire, Anton. You've been busy."

"Get *out*—"

"In a minute."

The vampire made a sound Leah had never heard come out of anything, a sort of shriek through closed teeth, and threw its whole mind against whatever was holding it. She saw it do it. She didn't know how she saw it, but she did, a kind of heave in the air inside the ring, and the runes on the floor around it flared bright blue, and Harry's head went back about an inch, like a man taking a light jab on the chin.

He blinked. He sat back on the stool.

"Right," he said. "Have it your way."

He lifted one hand from his knee, and pointed two fingers into the circle, and said, very quietly, one word.

"*Crucio.*"

The vampire screamed.

It was short. It couldn't have been more than two seconds. Leah would remember it for the rest of her life. The vampire's body didn't move, couldn't move, held stiff in the ring like a statue. All of it went into its face and its voice. Its mouth stretched open. Its eyes rolled up white. And the sound that came out of it was not anything she'd ever heard come out of a vampire, or a wolf, or a person, a high, raw, endless sound that bounced off the stone walls and the jars and the steel table and came back doubled.

Then Harry closed his hand, and it stopped.

The silence afterward rang. The vampire hung in the circle with its mouth open and its eyes unfocused, making a small hitching noise, over and over, like a child that had cried too long.

Leah found she had backed up against the door. She didn't remember doing it. Her hands were flat against the wood behind her. Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

She'd killed vampires. She'd torn them apart with her own mouth. She'd watched them burn. She'd thought about them in the dark, about the clearing, about the newborns and the redhead and the little blonde girl in the gray cloak, and she'd hated them with a clean, bone-deep, born-to-it hatred that had never once in eight months let up. She'd have told anyone who asked that she didn't care what happened to a red-eye. That a red-eye was a thing that killed people and needed to stop.

She still believed that. She found, standing there with her back against the door, that she still believed every word of it.

She also found that her whole body was shaking.

Harry hadn't looked round. He was watching the vampire. He sat on his stool with his hands loose on his knees and watched it hitch and shudder in the ring, and his face was perfectly calm, and down the rope, Leah felt—

Very little. That was what got her. He felt the way a man feels when he's had to use a crowbar on a stuck door, and that was the whole of it. Something had needed doing, and he'd done it, and now he was waiting to see if the door would open.

She'd felt more than that from Sam, cutting up a deer.

"Anton," Harry said quietly.

The vampire's eyes came back into focus. Slowly. They found Harry, and something in them broke open like an egg.

"*Imperio*," said Harry.

He said it almost gently.

It was like watching a light go off in a window. The wildness went out of the vampire's face all at once. The hitching stopped. Its features smoothed out, went blank and soft and easy, like the face of a man drifting off to sleep in a hot bath. Its red eyes went dreamy and unfocused and stayed on Harry with an expression of total, mild, pleasant attention.

"There we go," said Harry. He picked the notebook back up off the floor and opened it on his knee. "Let's try again, shall we? Name."

"Anton," said the vampire. Its voice had gone different. Calm. Pleasant. Faraway. "Anton Vrba."

"Born?"

"Seventeen fifty-two. Near Kutná Hora. In Bohemia."

"Turned?"

"Seventeen seventy-one. In the winter. In Prague."

Harry wrote. The pen made a small scratching sound in the silence. Leah stayed where she was, against the door, and watched.

"Who sent you here, Anton?"

"Demetri," said the vampire dreamily.

Leah's breath stopped.

She knew that name. Everybody in both packs knew that name. He'd been one of the gray cloaks in the clearing last summer, the tall dark one who'd stood at the back with his head up, sniffing the air, and Edward had told them afterward what he was. *The tracker. The best there is. If Demetri has ever caught the tenor of your mind, you can go anywhere on earth, and he'll know which way to walk.*

Harry's pen had stopped too. Just for a second. Then it started again.

"Tell me about it," he said. "From the beginning."

---

Anton Vrba told him.

He told it slowly, the way people tell things in their sleep, with long pauses where his eyes drifted off somewhere and came back. Harry let him take his time. Every so often he asked a question, short and quiet, and Anton answered it and went on.

He'd met Demetri seven weeks ago, in Montreal. By chance, Anton thought. It hadn't been by chance; nothing Demetri did was by chance. Anton had been hunting in the Old Port, and he'd turned a corner and Demetri had been there, leaning against a wall in a long dark coat, smiling, the way you'd smile at an old friend.

Old friends was overstating it. But they'd known each other. Anton had lived in Volterra, once. Forty years, a long time ago. In the eighteen hundreds. As a guest of the household.

"A guest," said Harry. "What does that mean?"

"Many come to Volterra," Anton said dreamily. "To see the masters. To ask for things. To offer things. Some come to ask to join the guard. Most are refused. Some are allowed to stay a while, in the lower halls. To be useful. Aro likes people around him. He likes to have them close. He likes to *know* them." His eyes drifted. "I hoped to be chosen. I had no gift. I thought perhaps one would grow. After forty years, Aro told me kindly that I should go and see the world. He is always very kind."

"And Demetri?"

"Demetri was kind too. Demetri knew me in those years. He has never forgotten me. He never forgets anyone."

"What did he want, in Montreal?"

"He wanted me to go west," Anton said. "To this place. To a small town in the rain, where a family of our kind lives that does not drink from humans. The Cullens. The masters have an interest in them."

Leah's hands curled into fists against the door behind her.

"What kind of interest?" said Harry.

"The masters met them last spring. In Volterra. Edward Cullen came to them asking to die, and Aro would not let him. And there was a human girl with him who knew everything about us, which is forbidden. Aro let them go on a promise. Caius was very angry. The promise was that the girl would be made one of us, soon, or she would be killed." A dreamy, faraway smile. "Caius wants to know if the promise has been kept. Aro wants to know—everything. Aro always wants to know everything. Demetri said, go to Forks. Look. See if the girl is changed. See what else there is to see. Anything strange. Anything new. Then come back to Volterra and tell us, and Aro will be grateful." The smile widened slightly. "It is a very good thing, to have Aro grateful."

"Did you see her?" said Harry. "The girl?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"The dogs," Anton said.

He said it without any heat at all, dreamy and mild, but Leah felt every hair on her back stand up anyway.

"I came from the east," he said. "Over the mountains. Three days ago. I could smell the town from the hills. I could smell the Cullens. And I could smell the dogs, everywhere, all around the edges. The big wolves. Demetri told me there were wolves. He said they killed many of the newborn army last summer. He said to stay away from them. So I did not go into the town. I went around. I went south, to the river, to watch from the forest and wait. I thought I would wait for a sunny day, when the Cullens go out into the woods, and see them from far away."

"And the people on the Hoh River Trail?"

There was a pause. A long one. The vampire's face, under its dreamy blankness, twitched very slightly at one corner.

"I was hungry," it said.

Harry wrote something down. He didn't look up.

"Where are they?"

Anton told him. A side creek, two miles up from the trailhead. A ravine. Under a fallen log. Harry wrote it all down, every word, carefully, and underlined something twice.

Leah closed her eyes.

When she opened them Harry was looking at her. Just for a second. Then he looked back at the vampire.

"Last question for now," he said. "How were you going to tell Demetri what you saw?"

"I was going to go back."

"To Volterra?"

"To Volterra."

"No letter? No telephone? No meeting?"

"No. Go back. Tell them myself. Demetri said that was best. Aro likes to hold your hand. He likes to see it all for himself."

"And if you didn't go back?"

The vampire smiled, dreamy and slow.

"Then Demetri would come and find me," it said. "He knows me. He knows the shape of my mind. He has always known. Anywhere I go, anywhere on the earth, he can close his eyes and know which way I am. It is his gift." The smile grew a little wider. "When I do not come home, he will close his eyes. And he will come."

The room was very quiet.

Harry sat on his stool and looked at the vampire for a long, long moment. His face didn't change.

Down the rope, Leah felt something in him go very cold and very sharp, like a blade coming out of a sheath.

"Anywhere on the earth," Harry said.

"Anywhere."

"In here?"

The vampire looked faintly puzzled, like a man asked a question in a language he only half spoke.

"I don't know what *here* is," it said.

"No," said Harry. "You don't." He closed the notebook. "Neither does he."

---

He did a few more things after that. Leah watched those too.

He got up off the stool and went to the steel table and came back with one of the little glass vials and a strip of cloth. He stepped right up to the edge of the silver ring, close, closer than Leah would have gone, and told the vampire calmly to open its mouth, and it did. He held the vial against the edge of one long white tooth until a few drops of something clear and thick ran down into it, and stoppered it, and wrapped it in the cloth, and wrote on the label. Venom, Leah supposed. She watched his hands while he did it. They were steady. They didn't hurry. They were the hands of somebody who'd done a thousand fiddly, dangerous things with his fingers an inch away from something that could kill him, and hadn't ever gotten used to it, exactly, but had stopped letting it show.

Then he did something she didn't understand at all.

He put two fingers against the side of the vampire's head, just at the temple, lightly, the way a doctor checks for a pulse. And he drew them slowly away.

Something came with them.

It was silver. A thread of it. Fine and bright and shining, like a strand of hair made out of light, or liquid, or both, stretching out from the vampire's temple to the tips of his fingers. It moved slowly. It looked heavy, somehow. Heavier than it should have been. It sagged a little in the air between them, like a strand of something wet.

Harry looked at it with an expression she hadn't seen on him yet.

Interested. Purely, completely, ravenously interested. Like a kid with a new toy.

"Look at that," he murmured. "Look how dense that is."

He caught the silver thread neatly in another of the little vials and stoppered it. It swirled inside the glass like mercury. He held it up to the light and turned it, and watched it move, and then he turned around and looked at Leah.

"Do you want to see?" he said.

"See what?"

"What I just took out of his head." He tapped the vial with one fingertip. "Volterra. The inside of it. From forty years of a vampire who never forgets anything." He paused. "I've got a thing that'll show it to you. Like walking into a room. Nobody'll see you. Nobody can touch you. It's just a memory."

Leah looked at the vial. At the silver stuff turning over in it, slow and heavy.

She thought about the Volturi crossing the clearing in their gray cloaks. The tall dark one at the back with his head up, sniffing the air.

"Yeah," she said. "Show me."

---

The thing was a stone basin, about the size of a birdbath, carved all round the rim with marks like the ones in the armory. He took it down off a high shelf near the door, carefully, with both hands, and set it on the steel table. It was full of something that wasn't water, exactly. It moved like water. It shone like silver, the same pale, cold light as the thread. He uncorked the vial and tipped the memory into it, and the surface swirled and clouded and went still.

"Lean in," he said. "Put your face close to the surface. You'll feel a sort of pull. Let it."

"And then what?"

"And then you're there. I'll be right next to you."

She looked at him.

"I'm not going in there on my own," she said. "If that's what you're about to say."

"Wasn't going to," said Harry. "I'll be right next to you. You've got my word."

That landed. She felt it land, felt it go down the rope and settle in her chest, solid as a stone. He'd said *you've got my word*, and he couldn't lie to her, and so it was true, and it was going to stay true.

She leaned in.

The pull came. It was like falling forward into cold water with her eyes open. Everything went silver, and then dark, and then she was standing on stone, in a place she had never been.

It was a room. A huge round room with a high, high ceiling that disappeared up into shadow, and walls of old pale stone, and slits of windows far up near the top through which a little gray daylight fell in thin, dusty beams. The floor was stone too, worn smooth in a path from the doorway to the far side of the room. There was a drain in the middle of the floor. She noticed the drain.

At the far side of the room, three chairs. Big wooden chairs, high-backed, old as anything she'd ever seen, set side by side on a low step.

There were people in them.

Leah knew what they were the moment she saw them. Her nose told her, even here, even in a memory, where she shouldn't have been able to smell anything. That sweet burnt-sugar stink, so thick in the room it was like syrup.

The one in the middle was smiling.

He had long black hair and skin so white and so thin it looked like paper, like old paper that had been folded and unfolded too many times. His eyes were red, but cloudy, milky, like there was a film over them. He was leaning forward in his chair with his hands clasped and his whole body bright with delight, like a kid on Christmas morning.

On his left, a man with white-blond hair down to his shoulders sat back with his arms folded and a look on his face like he'd smelled something bad. On his right, a man with long dark hair sat slumped against the back of his chair, staring at nothing at all, his face so empty and so tired it made her chest hurt to look at it.

Standing behind the chairs, against the wall, were others. A small blonde girl with an angel's face. A small dark-haired boy next to her who could have been her twin. A huge man, bigger than Emmett. And a tall, lean, dark-haired man with a narrow face, leaning against the stone with his arms crossed, watching the door.

Demetri. She knew it right away. She'd seen him in the clearing.

"Anton!" said the man in the middle chair. His voice was light and bright and full of pleasure, like an old friend's. "Dear Anton. Come, come. Give me your hand."

And she saw herself—no, saw *Anton*, younger-looking somehow, though he couldn't have been, in old-fashioned clothes, a long coat and a high collar—walk forward across the stone and kneel on the low step and hold out his hand. And she saw the papery man take it in both of his own, and close his milky eyes.

She felt Anton's fear, in the memory. She felt it like it was hers. Pure, cold, bone-deep terror, with something sick and adoring wound all through it. He'd wanted so badly to be chosen.

The papery man opened his eyes. He smiled. He patted Anton's hand.

"Forty years," he said gently. "And nothing grows, I'm afraid. Nothing at all. Such a pity. You've been a delightful guest." The smile widened. "Go and see the world, Anton. And remember us kindly."

"Aro," said Harry's voice, quietly, right next to her ear.

She jumped. She'd forgotten. He was standing beside her, shoulder to shoulder, his hands in his pockets, looking at the three chairs with that cold, sharp, interested expression.

"The one in the middle. That's Aro," he said. "The blond one's Caius. The one who looks like he wants to die is Marcus. Behind them, the little girl's Jane, and the boy's her brother, Alec. The big one's Felix." He nodded at the tall man leaning on the wall. "And that's your Demetri."

"He's not *my*—"

"Figure of speech." He was quiet for a second. "First time I've seen them from the inside. Forty years of this lad's memory in that basin. Every room he ever walked into. Every face. Every corridor." Down the rope she felt it again, that blade-cold hunger. "My bosses are going to cry when they see it."

The room began to blur around them. The light from the high windows smeared and ran.

"Hold on," said Harry. "It's skipping."

The round stone room dissolved, and the world tilted, and suddenly there was night and wet pavement and the orange glow of streetlights on old brick, and a narrow street that smelled of river and garbage and cold. A corner. And a tall man in a long dark coat leaning against a wall under a lamp, smiling, with red eyes.

"Anton," said Demetri. His voice was low and pleasant. "It has been a long time. Walk with me. I have a favor to ask, and the masters will be very grateful."

Leah watched him say it. She watched his face. She watched the easy, friendly, terrible way he smiled.

"*Forks*," said Demetri. "In Washington. A little town in the rain. Do you know it?"

Then the memory went silver at the edges, and broke up, and she was falling backward out of it with her stomach left behind somewhere, and her hands hit the edge of the cold steel table and she was back in the specimen room, gasping, with the stone basin swirling quietly in front of her.

She stood there, holding onto the table.

Harry came up out of the basin beside her a second later. He straightened. He looked at her.

"All right?"

"No," said Leah.

That was true. She was shaking again. She'd seen them. She'd seen them up close. She'd seen Aro's papery hands and Caius's sour mouth and that little blond angel face, and she'd stood in their *room*, in the middle of their stone floor, next to that drain.

And she'd seen Demetri, under a streetlight in Montreal, saying the name of her town.

"They're watching them," she said. "The Cullens. They're watching Bella. They sent him to check on Bella." She looked at Harry. "And Nessie. If he'd gotten into town. If he'd seen her. If he'd gone back and told them—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. She knew what Edward had told the pack about the Volturi's laws. She knew what they did to anything new, anything strange, anything they hadn't seen before. She'd seen Jacob's face when he looked at that kid.

"He didn't get into town," said Harry. "He didn't see her. He doesn't know she exists. I checked."

"But they'll send someone else."

"They might."

"And Demetri's going to come looking for *him*."

"Probably."

"*Here.*"

"If he can find him." Harry looked over at the silver ring. The vampire was standing in the middle of it, dreamy and blank, staring into space. "I don't know if he can. I don't know if his gift goes through my walls. Nobody does. Nobody's ever tested it." He paused. "Seems like something worth finding out."

Leah stared at him.

"You want him to come," she said slowly.

He looked at her. He didn't say yes. He didn't have to. She could feel it, down the rope, under everything else, under the cold and the caution and the tiredness. A slow, careful, steady *want*. The same want she'd felt in him when he looked at the silver thread. The same want she'd felt in him, God help her, when he'd looked at her in the valley.

"I want to know if he *can*," Harry said. "And if he can, I'd rather he came here, to me, than went knocking on doors in Forks. And if he comes here—" He shrugged. "Then I'll have two."

"Two."

"Specimens."

"You're insane," Leah said.

"Probably," said Harry. "Do you want to help?"

She opened her mouth to tell him to go to hell.

Nothing came out.

She stood there with her mouth open, holding onto the edge of the cold steel table, feeling the rope taut and humming in her chest, and she realized, slowly, with a sinking, furious, impossible feeling, exactly why nothing was coming out.

She closed her mouth.

Across the table, Harry Potter looked at her, and for the first time since he'd stepped out of nowhere into the bottom of the valley with a sound like a book closing, she watched him smile. Really smile. It was crooked and tired and lopsided, and it pulled up further on one side than the other, and it made him look about five years younger.

"Yeah," he said. "Thought so."

---

### XII. Need to Know

Harry lifted the Imperius at 7:12 by the kitchen clock, and the vampire went back to being a statue in a circle with a pair of furious red eyes, and he shut the gray door on it and left it there.

He went and washed his hands. He didn't need to. He did it anyway, at the kitchen sink, with the hot tap, for a long time.

Leah had gone ahead of him into the main room. When he came back she was standing at the map table with her arms folded over the front of his sweater, looking down at the web of blue light lying over her land. She didn't look round. He felt her know he was there.

He got a notepad off the bookshelf and a biro out of the drawer under the record player, and sat on the arm of the sofa and wrote it out in block capitals. The name of the trail. The name of the side creek. Two miles up from the trailhead, a ravine, a fallen log. Two people. Possibly married. Late fifties. He tore the page off, folded it twice, and put it in his shirt pocket.

"What's that?" said Leah.

"Directions."

"To them?"

"Yeah." He buttoned the pocket. "There's a payphone outside the ferry terminal in Port Angeles. I'll ring it through to the Park Service tonight. Bloke with an accent, out walking his dog, saw something he didn't like the look of. They'll have them out by tomorrow lunchtime."

She turned round and looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because otherwise they'll be under that log till spring," he said, "and somebody's kids are going to spend all of Thanksgiving ringing round hospitals."

He hadn't thought about how it would sound until it was out. It came out plain, the way everything came out with her. He felt her take it in, down the rope. He didn't know what she made of it. He didn't go looking.

"Okay," she said, after a second. Quietly.

"Okay."

He got up off the arm of the sofa and went to the mirror.

---

He tapped twice. Waited. Tapped once.

It was gone three in the morning in London. Ellery answered on the second set of taps anyway, sitting in the same high-backed chair in the same cardigan in front of the same stone wall, as if she'd been sitting there all day waiting. Maybe she had. In six years he'd never once called her at an hour she wasn't awake.

"Potter."

"Ma'am."

"You're calling at three in the morning."

"I am."

Ellery looked at him for a moment out of the silver frame. Then, very slightly, she sat forward.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her.

He told her clean and in order, the way she'd taught him, the way the Department taught everybody. Time of the hit, location, anchor number. The snare's behaviour on capture: closed clean, held clean, no slippage. The handover to the circle: clean. The specimen: male, apparent age thirty, actual age two hundred and fifty-four, Bohemian, no gift. Legilimency: viable, but fast and slippery and hard work; resistance strong. Imperius: viable, once the resistance was broken. He told her how he'd broken it. She didn't blink. He told her about the venom sample.

Then he told her about the memory.

"Forty years," Ellery said.

"Eighteen-forty to eighteen-eighty, near enough. Lived in the lower halls. Walked the whole of the inside, top to bottom, more or less every day for four decades. And he's one of them, ma'am. They don't forget. It's all there. Every room, every corridor, every face. All three of the old ones up close." He paused. "I watched Aro read him. Hand to hand. I watched it happen."

Ellery said nothing for a long moment.

He'd known her for six years. He'd seen her angry, a handful of times; he'd seen her tired, once, in the week after Bode's replacement got himself killed in Cairo. He'd never seen anything on her face that he'd have called pleasure.

He saw something now. It was small. It was mostly in the eyes. It lasted about a second and a half.

"Bottle it," she said. "All of it. Every year he's got. I want it in London by the end of the week."

"There's more."

"Go on."

He told her about Demetri. Montreal. The errand. *Go to Forks. Look. See if the girl is changed.* He told her what Anton had said about coming home, and about what would happen if he didn't.

When he finished Ellery sat back in her chair. She picked up the cup of tea from the arm. He'd have bet any money it had gone cold hours ago. She drank from it anyway.

"The tracker," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You believe he'll come."

"I believe he'll try. Whether he can find his way through my walls is another question. I don't know the answer. Nobody does."

"And if he can?"

"Then he comes to me," Harry said. "And not to the family. That's the important bit. The family's Amber. The family's got a reason to be on our side when the time comes, and they've got no idea yet how much reason. If the old ones in Volterra start sending people round to check on them, that's a problem for us as well as them." He shrugged. "Best place for the tracker to end up is here. With me."

"You want him."

"I want to know if his gift goes through a ward. I want to know if it goes through a Fidelius. I want to know what he can do and how, and the only way to find out is to let him try." He kept his voice level. "Yes. I want him."

Ellery looked at him over the cup for a long time.

"You'll want help," she said.

"I'll want warning, mostly. If you hear anything from inside the walls. Anybody leaving town in a long coat."

"You'll have it." She set the cup down. "And the local complications?"

"Contained."

"Explain *contained*."

"The witch saw me. So did two of the wolves." He gave it to her straight; there was no point doing anything else with Ellery. "The witch has been busy. She's told the vampires, the wolves, the wolves' mother and the chief of police about our world. All of them. In about six weeks."

Ellery closed her eyes, briefly. "The Statute."

"In pieces. Her own Congress would have her wand and her licence and stick her somewhere unpleasant for a few years, and then come and Obliviate half the county. She knows it. I made sure she knows it." He paused. "She's agreed she never saw me, and that she and hers will say yes to whatever we ask of them, when we ask. The wolves have agreed the same. I'll have the family in the same place by the end of the week."

"Leverage."

"Leverage."

"Hm." Ellery studied him. "You've had a busy afternoon."

"I've had worse."

"Is that everything?"

There it was.

He didn't look round. He didn't need to. He could feel exactly where Leah was, standing at the map table twelve feet behind his left shoulder, in his sweater, with her arms folded, absolutely still. He could feel her listening. He could feel her heart.

"Yes, ma'am," said Harry. "That's everything."

It came out easily. That was the strange part, after the last few hours. It came out exactly like every other lie he'd ever told Maud Ellery, smooth and level and without a hitch, like a coin dropping into a slot. Nothing caught in his throat. Nothing stopped it. Whatever had tied itself into his chest in that valley, it didn't care what he told anybody else.

Ellery's pale eyes rested on him. One second. Two.

He held them. He'd been holding them for six years.

"Good hunting, Potter," she said, and the mirror went dark.

---

He stood there for a while, looking at his own reflection in the silver. It looked tired. It looked like it needed a shave.

Behind him, Leah said, "You lied to her."

"Yeah."

"About me."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

He turned round.

She was standing exactly where he'd known she would be, at the corner of the map table, in the blue wash of light from the lattice. Her face was very still. He couldn't read it. He didn't try. He could feel the shape of what she was feeling, though, down the rope: tight and careful and wary, and under it, very small, very quiet, something that might have been afraid to be anything else.

"Because she's my boss," he said, "and she's very good at her job. And her job, and my job, and the whole Department's job, is understanding things. Things nobody else understands. Things that don't make sense." He put his hands in his pockets. "And if I'd told her that there's a woman on the Olympic Peninsula who turns into a wolf, and that something none of us has ever seen before had just tied her to one of her own people, she'd want to understand it."

"So?"

"So the Department's not gentle about understanding things," he said. "It never has been. You've seen the room."

Leah looked past him, down the corridor, toward the plain gray door at the end of it.

"Like you with him," she said.

"Like me with him."

"You said I wouldn't end up in a jar."

"You won't."

"Because you lied."

"Because I lied," said Harry. "And I'll keep lying. To her and to anybody else who asks. For as long as it takes." He looked at her. "That's the only kind of promise I'm much good at. Ask anybody I've ever worked with."

She looked at him for a long time across the map table. The lattice pulsed between them. A slow blue heartbeat taking the long way round.

"You can lie to her," she said. "But you can't lie to me."

"Seems that way."

"That's—" She stopped. She looked down at the map, at the gap in the web where La Push was. "That's a really messed-up thing to find comforting."

"Yeah," said Harry. "I'm finding that too."

---

They had about four minutes.

Leah was the one who felt it first. Harry watched her do it. Her head came up, sharp, like a dog's when it hears a car door three streets over, and she turned toward the tent flap at the end of the hallway, and he felt the jolt of it go through her down the rope.

Then the cord on his wrist twitched, and he felt it too.

Something had hit the outer wards. Something big. Two somethings. They hit it, and it turned them, and they went round. He felt them go. Forty yards. Fifty.

They came back. They hit it again.

They worked it differently from her. They went round, and came back, and hit it again, and went round, circling, testing, the way a wolf circles a fence looking for the gap.

"That's Seth," Leah said. "And—"

She stopped. Her whole face changed.

"And Jake," she said.

"You can tell from in here?"

"I can smell them." She was already moving, down the hallway, toward the flap. "Seth phased. He must've. He went back and phased and Jake was on, and Jake got the whole thing out of his head, the valley, the fight, *you*. He got the—" She stopped dead in the middle of the hallway and turned round and looked at him, and her face had gone pale. "He got the imprint. Seth saw it. Seth *knows*. So Jake knows."

"Yeah," said Harry. "I'd imagine he does."

"He's going to kill you."

"He's going to try and find me first," said Harry. "Which he's having a fairly hard time with, at the moment."

Outside, through the canvas, faint and far off and distorted by the wards and the rain, a wolf howled. It was a big voice. Deep and rough and furious. It went on for a long time.

Leah closed her eyes.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. I have to go out there."

"I know."

"If I go out and phase, he gets it. All of it. Everything. Right now. The Department, Volterra, the room, the—" She gestured back down the corridor at the gray door, a quick, jerky motion. "Everything you just told me. Everything I just saw."

"Then don't phase," said Harry. "Go out there on two feet. And I'll come with you, and I'll tell him myself."

She looked at him.

"You're just going to walk out there," she said. "And talk to him. Jake. The Alpha. With his teeth. While he's like *that*."

"That was the plan."

"The plan you made forty minutes ago."

"Plans have to start somewhere."

Something happened at one corner of her mouth. She flattened it out again fast.

"Jake's different from Seth," she said. "He won't sit there and let you call him names. He'll go for you."

"Then I'll put him on the ground," said Harry. "Same as I did you. And then I'll wait for him to calm down, and then we'll talk."

"You'd do that? To *Jake*?"

"I'd do it to anybody," said Harry. "I'd rather not. That's the truth." He went and got his suit jacket off the back of the sofa and put it on, over the shirt and the holster, and settled the collar. He didn't bother with the tie. He picked up the overcoat. "But I'd rather put him on the ground for five minutes than have him tear my arm off and then feel bad about it in front of you for the rest of his life. He's your Alpha. He's your brother's best mate. I'd quite like him to still be speaking to you in the morning."

She stared at him.

"Lassie," he said, shrugging the overcoat on. "We should go. Before he finds the gap on his own and comes through it at full speed and lands on my head."

She didn't move.

"Call me Lassie again," Leah said, "and I'll take your hand off at the wrist."

He did up the top button of the coat. He looked at her.

"No, you won't," he said.

Her mouth opened.

He watched her try. He watched her feel for it, the way you'd feel in the dark for a light switch. *Yes, I will.* Three little words. He saw her jaw work around them.

Nothing came out.

She shut her mouth. She glared at him. Down the rope, he felt her get furious, and then, underneath the fury, he felt something else. It came up very fast and very bright and he was fairly sure she'd have given a great deal to stop it. It felt, from the inside, very much like the start of a laugh.

"No," Leah said finally, through her teeth. "I won't."

"Didn't think so," said Harry, and held the flap open for her.

---

The rain had eased off to the fine, hanging kind while they'd been inside. The hollow was dark. Dripping. The big spruce, the nurse log, the ferns, all of it silver-black and glistening in the faint light from the open flap behind them. Leah walked out into it barefoot, onto the cold moss, in his sweater and her wet black dress, and stood there in the middle of the clearing with the rain settling on her hair and breathed in.

They'd followed her trail. Of course they had. She'd left one a blind man could follow.

She could smell them. Seth, close, sandy and anxious and running in circles about a quarter mile to the south. And Jake. Jake was everywhere. Jake was the whole south side of the night, hot and huge and angry, crashing through the underbrush along the line of the wards, turning back, crashing through again.

She could smell something else, too, under the wolf smell, faint, like a struck match. Layla. Riding on Seth's back, probably, since she couldn't Apparate with her ribs. Clinging on with both hands and her wand in her teeth. Of course she'd come. Of course.

Behind her, Harry let the flap fall closed. When she looked back the tent was gone. Just a hollow under a spruce, ferns and moss and a nurse log, and a tall man in a long black coat standing in the middle of it with his hands in his pockets, where the tent had been.

He walked over and stood next to her. Not close. A couple of feet.

The rope hummed between them in the dark.

She'd thought about it, while he was talking to his boss. She'd stood there at the map table looking at the gap in the web where La Push was and listened to him lie to that old woman's face, easy as breathing, and she'd thought about it. About the rope. About what it was and what it meant and what it was going to do to her life, starting tonight.

She'd thought about Sam. About Emily. About standing in her aunt's kitchen with a bowl of potato salad watching Sam's face go soft and stupid and holy.

She'd thought about how it had felt, for eight months, to have her whole head torn open every time she phased. To have every thought she ever had dragged out into the light for the pack to pick over. Every bitter thing. Every jealous thing. Every lonely thing. Every thing about her body she'd never told her own mother. Nobody had ever asked first. They took. Because that was how the pack worked, and that was how imprinting worked, and that was how everything worked, when you were a wolf. It took. It took you and it took Sam, and it never asked you first.

And then she'd thought about standing in that lamplit room with the fire going and the rain on the canvas, telling a man she'd met four hours ago the worst thing she knew about herself. On purpose. Because she wanted to.

She'd chosen that. She'd opened her mouth and chosen it. And he'd stood there and taken it, and given her the true thing back, with no paint on it, *I don't know what it's for*, and he'd done that because he couldn't do anything else, and she'd known he couldn't, and it had been the first time in eight months that anybody had heard something out of her that she'd actually decided to give.

She didn't know what to do with that. She didn't know what to do with any of it.

But she knew she wasn't sorry she'd come.

She tried thinking it the other way, just to see. *I'm sorry I came.* She didn't say it out loud. There wasn't anybody to say it to who'd hear. She just thought it, standing in the dark next to him.

It sat in her head like a stone. It didn't fit. It didn't feel like hers at all.

She let it go.

"What happens after this?" she said. "After Jake. After the Cullens. After Demetri, if he comes." She looked sideways at him in the dark. "After the job. What happens then?"

He was quiet for a while. She felt him think about it, down the rope. Felt him look for an answer, and turn over the ones he found, and put them back.

"I don't know," he said.

It was true. She could feel it was true. It was the most honest thing anybody had ever said to her.

"Okay," said Leah.

Out in the dark to the south, Jake howled again. Closer this time. He'd found the edge of the outer ring, the place where it bent, and he was working at it, pushing at it, the way she had.

Harry lifted his hand, palm out, toward the sound. Leah felt the wards open. She didn't know how she felt it. She just did, the way she'd felt the rope pay out in the valley. Something in the dark to the south parted like a curtain.

For about three seconds, there was nothing.

Then Jacob Black came out of the trees at the top of the slope at a dead run, a russet-brown shape bigger than a horse, with the rain flying off his coat and his ears pinned flat back and his teeth bared all the way to the gums. Right behind him, Seth, sandy and frantic, with a small curly-haired figure in a too-big raincoat clinging to his back with both arms round his neck.

Jacob saw them. He saw Leah, barefoot on the moss in a man's sweater. He saw the man standing two feet away from her in a long black coat.

He didn't slow down.

Leah felt the man beside her shift his weight, very slightly, onto the balls of his feet. That was all. His hands stayed in his pockets. Down the rope, she felt him go quiet and still and ready, the way he'd been in the valley before Layla's first spell. And under it, way down deep, in the dark of his chest, she felt something else lift its head and take notice.

It wanted out. She could feel how badly it wanted out. It wanted to meet the big russet thing coming down the slope at it, on its own terms, with its own teeth.

He didn't let it.

He stayed exactly as he was. A man. A tall, tired man in a cheap black suit, standing in the rain with his hands in his pockets, on two feet. Waiting.

"Evening," he called, up the slope, in that flat, rough English voice. "You must be Jacob."

Jacob came on.

"I'm Harry," said Harry. "I think we should have a chat."

And then, because he was apparently physically incapable of not doing it, he took one hand out of his coat, and the little black leather wallet was in it, and he flipped it open with his thumb.

"Don't you *dare*," said Leah.

He looked at her sideways.

Then he shut the wallet, and put it away, and put his hand back in his pocket, and turned back to face the slope with an expression on his face that she would have called, on anybody else, the very edge of a smile.

Jacob hit the bottom of the slope fifty feet away and kept coming, and Leah Clearwater stood on the moss between the two of them with the rain in her hair and a rope through the middle of her chest, tied off at both ends.

She didn't move out of the way.

She found, to her own astonishment, that she didn't want to.

---

*End of Chapter One*
