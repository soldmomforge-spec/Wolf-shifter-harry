# North of Everything

## Chapter Four: Gerald

---

Harry went out first.

He always went out first. Through doors, into rooms, around corners, into the dark at the far end of a parking lot. Leah had stopped noticing it sometime around April, the way she'd stopped noticing the sound of her own heartbeat, and now she only noticed it when somebody else was watching. He ducked under the top of the doorframe and stepped out onto the porch, and she stepped out a half-pace behind him and to his left, which was where he'd put her without either of them ever saying a word about it.

The rain was coming down hard enough to bounce. It hammered the porch roof and poured off the edge in a solid sheet, and the Christmas lights along the rail lit the sheet up red and green and blue and orange, so that looking out at the yard was like looking out through a stained-glass window somebody had left running.

Something opened over her head with a soft, heavy *fwump*.

She looked up. It was an umbrella. A big black one, the old-fashioned kind, with a long wooden shaft and a curved handle the color of dark honey, and black panels stretched so tight the rain hit them like a snare drum. Harry was holding it in his left forepaw, angled forward, so that it covered her completely and him mostly. There had not been an umbrella in her mother's house since Quileute Days in 2003, when Dad's golf one had blown inside out in the parade and he'd stuffed it headfirst into the trash can outside the community center and said, *Good riddance, never trusted it.*

"Where did you get an—" she started, under her breath.

Harry went down the porch steps.

She went with him, because the umbrella went with him, and they stopped together on the bottom step with the rain roaring on the black silk overhead and the gravel shining wet under her boots.

They were right there.

Sam had stopped beside the front bumper of her mother's Subaru, maybe fifteen feet away. Jacob had stopped on the far side of the Rolls-Royce's long black hood, with six feet of wet gravel between him and Sam, the same six feet they'd kept between them all the way up the drive. They were both bare to the waist and barefoot, both soaked, both steaming in the cold like a pair of horses after a hard run. Rain sluiced down their chests and dripped off the hems of their cutoffs.

Jacob looked older. He'd been sixteen when she left, all rage and elbows, carrying a broken heart around in front of him like a tray he wanted everybody to see. Now his face had settled into itself. The hair was short. The jaw was a man's jaw. He was looking at Harry with his mouth a little open, and then at her, and then back at Harry, and she could see him trying to fit the two of them into the same picture and failing.

Sam looked the same.

That was almost worse. The same square shoulders. The same set jaw, the one he got when he'd decided something before the conversation started. The same dark eyes under heavy brows, rain beading on the lashes. She'd spent a year looking at that face from the inside of his own head—watching it in windows he walked past, watching it go soft every time it turned toward Emily. She knew it better than she knew her own.

Out in the trees on both sides of the yard there were eyes. Pairs of them, low to the ground and high up, throwing back the Christmas lights in flat green and gold. A big shape shifted its weight in the alders by the woodpile, and something cracked under it. Something else, closer to the road, was panting.

Then one of the shapes came out.

It was silver-gray, and it came out of the alders at the edge of the drive at a lazy trot, head low, the way a dog crosses a yard it owns. Halfway across the gravel it rose. There was a shudder and a ripple, the wet fur running off it like water off a tarp, and then it was a naked man hopping on one foot in the rain, yanking a pair of cutoffs off a leather cord on his ankle and stepping into them.

Paul Lahote zipped his fly. He didn't look at the porch. He strolled the rest of the way to the Rolls-Royce, turned around, and leaned back against the driver's door with his whole wet bare back flat on the black paint. He folded his arms. He crossed his ankles. He braced one muddy foot against the front tire, and then, finally, he looked up at Harry and grinned.

It was the grin Leah remembered from every patrol she'd ever run with him. The one he wore right before he said the thing he knew would make you want to tear his throat out.

"Leah," said Sam.

She'd been waiting for it.

For fifteen months she'd been sure that if she ever heard him say her name again, something in her chest would go tight and hot and awful, the way it used to. She stood on the bottom step of her mother's porch with her husband's umbrella over her head and her daughter in the house behind her, and waited for it to happen.

The rain came down. Water ran off the ends of her hair and under her collar. Out in the trees a wolf whined, high and short, and stopped.

Nothing happened.

It was his voice saying her name. That was all it was.

"Hi, Sam," she said.

"Are you okay?"

"I'm fine."

His eyes stayed on her a beat longer. They went down to her left hand and stopped there, and came back up. Then they went to Harry, and his weight shifted, a small roll forward onto the balls of his feet that she'd watched him do a thousand times before a fight.

"What," said Sam, "is that."

Harry wasn't looking at him.

Harry was looking at the car.

He looked at it for a long, quiet moment—at Paul's bare back pressed against the driver's door, at Paul's muddy foot on the tire, at the brown water running down the black paint from Paul's shoulders. His ears went back. They went slowly, a notch at a time, the way they'd gone back the morning in Atlin when he walked out to the Ford and found a raven had used the windshield as a toilet.

"Oh," said Harry. "Oh, *dear*."

Leah's head snapped around.

The voice came out of his muzzle, low and rough underneath like always, but somebody had laid about four hundred years of money over the top of it. The vowels had gone long and round and lazy. The consonants had gone crisp as new bills. He sounded like the people in the costume movies her mom watched on PBS on Sunday nights, the ones with the big stone houses and the horses and the women in bonnets, where everybody said *one* instead of *you*. He sounded like a butler who'd inherited the castle. He sounded like he'd been born wearing a little velvet jacket.

"I say," said Harry. "You there. Yes, you."

He lifted his right forepaw and drew a slow, vague circle in the air in Paul's direction with one black claw. The circle took in Paul's wet hair, Paul's bare chest, Paul's cutoffs, Paul's foot on the tire, the whole of him.

"The one with the—" another small circle "—*everything*. Would you be an absolute darling and remove yourself from the motor car?"

Paul's grin stopped moving.

"Only Gerald's just washed it," Harry went on, in the patient tone of a man explaining something to a slow but basically well-meaning child, "and I'd really rather not get poor-people dirt on it. Thank you *ever* so much."

There was a silence in the yard.

It was a very specific kind of silence. Leah had heard it exactly once before, in the cafeteria at Forks High in tenth grade, right after Lauren Mallory's lunch tray hit the floor. Every wolf in the trees had stopped breathing. Jacob's mouth had dropped all the way open. Sam had gone stone still beside the Subaru.

Leah bit the inside of her cheek so hard she tasted blood.

Paul pushed himself off the car.

He did it slowly. He came away from the black paint and straightened up and turned to face the porch, and the rain ran down his face into his eyes and he didn't blink it away. His jaw worked once. A muscle jumped in his neck.

"Who the *fuck*," said Paul, "do you think you are?"

Harry regarded him from under the umbrella.

He took his time. He tilted his long dark head a few degrees to one side. Both ears flicked sideways at once, a small neat motion Leah had learned to read in about the second week, and under the hem of the black coat his tail gave a single short flick against the back of her leg.

"Well, *sir*," Harry said.

He let the *sir* sit there in the rain for a moment, like something he'd set down on a side table to admire.

"Harry Potter-Black." The smallest pause. "Of course."

Jacob made a noise. It came out through his nose. He clapped one hand over the lower half of his face to stop it and was much too late.

Paul stared.

"*Of course?*" said Paul.

"Of course," Harry agreed.

"What the hell does *of course* mean? I've never heard of you."

"No," said Harry, with real sympathy. "No, I don't suppose you have. One doesn't, out here. It's all terribly rural, isn't it?" He looked around the yard—the alders, the sagging gutter, the moss on the roof, the big old Christmas bulbs blurring in the rain—with an expression of polite, interested pity, the face of a man at a zoo reading the little plaque that says *endangered*. "Lovely, though. Very green. Very—" he searched for the word "—*damp*."

Leah turned her face into his shoulder and made a sound that she tried to pass off as a cough.

It didn't fool anybody. His tail flicked against the back of her knee again, twice.

On the driver's door of the Rolls-Royce, where Paul's back had been, there was a big wet smear of mud and pine needles in the shape of a man's shoulder blades. Harry looked at it. His ears went flat with an expression of deep personal suffering. He didn't move a claw.

The smear went away.

One second it was there, and the next the door was black and wet and shining again with the Christmas lights caught in it, red and green and gold. The muddy footprint on the front tire went with it.

"There," Harry murmured, to nobody. "Gerald need never know."

Paul looked at the door. Then at Harry. Then at Sam.

"Did you see that? Did you see what he just—"

"Paul," said Sam.

"He called me *poor*, Sam, he looked me right in the—"

"*Paul.*"

Paul shut his mouth. He didn't move away from the car. He didn't lean on it again, either.

Leah realized she was shaking. Her ribs hurt. She was trying so hard not to laugh that her eyes were watering, and the effort was coming out of her in little jerks against Harry's side, and she knew he could feel every one of them.

Sam had taken a step out from behind the Subaru. He was looking at Harry now, only Harry, with the rain running off his jaw.

"I asked you a question," Sam said.

Harry turned his head and looked down at Leah.

"Darling," he said, in that voice. "Who's this one?"

Leah pressed her lips together until they hurt. "That's Sam," she managed.

"Sam," Harry repeated, the way you'd repeat a word in a language you didn't speak. He looked across the yard at Sam. He looked at him the way Leah had once seen Darlene look at a pallet of frozen hash browns that had come off the truck already thawed. "Sam what?"

"Uley," said Sam, through his teeth.

"Uley." Harry seemed to consult some internal list. "No," he said at last, regretfully. "No, I'm afraid I don't know you."

"I'm the Alpha of the Quileute pack."

"The what, sorry?"

"The *Alpha*."

Harry looked down at Leah again. "Is that a job?"

"Sort of," said Leah. Her voice cracked in the middle. She cleared her throat. "It's sort of a job."

"Does it come with a pension?"

"No."

"Dental?"

"*Harry.*"

"Only one hears such dreadful things about American dental." He looked back across the yard at Sam and gave him a short, polite nod, the kind you'd give a waiter who'd brought you the wrong soup and was clearly having a hard day. "Well. Good for you, Sammy."

Something went across Sam's face, fast.

"Don't call me that," Sam said.

"Mm," said Harry.

"There's two of us," said Jacob suddenly, from the other side of the car. He'd taken his hand off his mouth. His eyes were very bright. "Alphas. I'm the other one."

Harry turned his head and looked Jacob over, boots to hairline, the same way he'd looked over Paul, except Jacob had no boots.

"Two Alphas," Harry said. "In one tiny village. Like the Popes at Avignon."

Jacob blinked. "Like the *what*?"

"Never mind, dear boy. Before your time." Harry's eyes went to the hood of the car, where Jacob's right hand was resting on the black paint. Very lightly. Fingertips only, the way you'd touch a horse you hadn't been introduced to yet.

Jacob saw him look and took the hand away. He didn't stop staring at the car, though. He'd been staring at it, Leah realized, since he came up the drive—at the tall silver grille, the wheels, the doors that hinged the wrong way. Jacob had rebuilt a Volkswagen Rabbit out of junkyard parts when he was fifteen. He'd rebuilt two dirt bikes in his dad's garage for Bella Swan out of scrap and spit. He was looking at the Rolls-Royce the way Seth looked at a stack of pancakes.

"What *is* that?" Jacob said. He couldn't seem to stop himself. "Is that a Phantom? They don't make a Phantom that tall. They don't make anything that tall. That's not a—" he took two steps along the hood, ducking his head to squint at the little silver woman on the front "—that's not a model, I'd know, I've got every brochure they ever—what is it?"

"Bespoke," said Harry.

"Bespoke *what*?"

"Just bespoke."

"Can I look under the hood?"

"Bonnet," said Harry. "And no."

Jacob straightened up. He looked at Harry for a long second through the rain. Then he grinned, and it was the grin Leah remembered from before—before the wolves and before Bella, from when he was a skinny kid running around First Beach with Embry and Quil, lighting things on fire with a magnifying glass and getting yelled at by Old Quil. It went all the way across his face.

"Okay," said Jacob. "Okay. That's fair."

"*Jake*," said Sam.

"What? It's a nice car."

"That isn't the—" Sam stopped. He dragged one hand down his wet face. When he took it away he was looking at Harry again, and his voice had dropped and gone flat, the way it used to go in the pack mind when he'd run out of patience with all of them at once. "You're on Quileute land. You crossed the treaty line in that car twenty minutes ago, and every one of my wolves smelled you do it. Nothing like you has ever come onto this reservation. I want to know what you are. I want to know what you're doing here. And I want to know right now."

"Ah," said Harry. "The treaty."

"Yes. The treaty."

"I adore a treaty." His ears tipped forward with interest. "Who's it with?"

Sam hesitated. It was very small. Leah saw it anyway.

"The Cullens," Sam said.

"The Cullens," Harry repeated. "Those would be the vampires. Leah's mentioned them." He looked down at himself, slowly—from the high collar of his coat with its silver clasp, down the long black front of it, all the way to his bare black footpaws on the wet gravel—as if he were checking. Then he looked back up.

"And am I a vampire, Sammy?"

"Don't call me—"

Harry leaned forward a little, under the umbrella, and lowered his voice to the tone of someone sharing a confidence at a dinner party.

"Do I *sparkle*?"

Jacob turned around and walked three steps away down the drive with both hands on top of his head. His shoulders were shaking.

Leah gave up. She put her forehead against Harry's arm and laughed into the wet wool of his coat sleeve, a long, helpless, wheezing sound, and she couldn't stop it and didn't try. Out in the trees something made a short huffing bark that might have been a cough and might not have been.

Sam's jaw had gone tight enough to crack walnuts. "No," he said. "You don't. You're something else."

"Then I don't suppose your treaty has terribly much to say about me, does it?" said Harry pleasantly. "Thank heavens. I've read a few of these old treaties. The prose is *dire*."

"Nothing like you has ever set foot here."

"Then you're having a red-letter day," said Harry. "You should write it down. Make a little note in your diary." He tilted his head the other way. "Oh, but where *are* my manners."

Behind them, the front door opened by itself.

Leah turned her head. So did everybody else in the yard.

Out of the dark hallway, at about the height a grown man would carry a tray, came one of her mother's blue willow-pattern plates. It came slowly and very steadily, with a paper napkin laid over the top of it, and it drifted out across the porch and down the steps through the downpour—the rain went around it—and settled into Harry's free right forepaw as if somebody had handed it to him.

From somewhere deep in the house, faintly, Leah heard her mother's voice say, "Was that my—"

Harry twitched the napkin off.

The plate was piled with fry bread. Golden and puffed up, still steaming in the cold, with powdered sugar on top. The smell rolled across the yard: fried dough and sugar and hot oil. Leah's stomach, which hadn't had anything in it since a turkey sandwich somewhere over Greenland, made a noise she would have been embarrassed about in any other company.

"Fry bread?" said Harry, holding the plate out toward Sam. "My mother-in-law made it. It's superb."

Sam stared at the plate.

Jacob was at the bottom of the steps before Leah saw him move. He took two pieces. Harry let him. Jacob put one whole piece in his mouth at once and stood there in the rain with his eyes closed, chewing.

"Oh my God," Jacob said thickly. "Sue's fry bread."

"Quite," said Harry.

Paul came around the front of the car. He didn't look at Harry. He didn't look at Sam. He reached out and took a piece off the plate and bit it in half and glared at Harry the whole time he was chewing it.

"Mm," said Harry, approvingly, as if Paul had passed some kind of test. He held the plate out across the yard again. "Sammy? There's one left."

Sam didn't move.

"Suit yourself." Harry took the last piece himself. He held it delicately between two claws of his right forepaw, with the empty plate balanced on the others, and took a small, neat bite off the corner, and chewed, and swallowed, and touched the side of his muzzle with the folded napkin. A little drift of powdered sugar settled into the black fur of his chin and stayed there.

Leah was crying now. Actually crying, from laughing, the tears running down her face and mixing with the rain that blew in under the umbrella.

Jacob had finished his second piece. He was licking sugar off his thumb and looking at Harry with his head on one side.

"Potter-Black," Jacob said. "You said Potter-*Black*."

"I did."

"Black like—"

"The English Blacks," said Harry. "Frightfully old. Frightfully rich. Frightfully dead, the whole lot of them, apart from me." He took another small bite. "I'm the nice one."

"My last name's Black."

Harry looked at him with polite astonishment.

"Is it!" he said. "How terribly common of you."

Jacob laughed. It came out of him like a cough, startled loose, and then he was bent over with his hands on his knees in the rain, laughing at the gravel. Leah hadn't heard Jacob Black laugh like that since before Bella Swan moved back to Forks.

Sam wasn't laughing. Sam was looking at Harry with a small line between his eyebrows, like a man adding up numbers in his head and getting a total he didn't like. His eyes went to Jacob, doubled over. Back to Harry. Then to her.

"Leah," he said. Quieter now. "What's going on. Who is this?"

She straightened up off Harry's arm and wiped her eyes on her sleeve.

"I did tell you," said Harry, before she could answer. "Harry Potter-Black." He gestured with the last of the fry bread, a small courteous wave that took in the porch, the house, the umbrella, and Leah. "The husband."

---

The word landed in the yard.

Leah watched it land on Sam. She'd thought about this moment more times than she would ever admit out loud. Standing in front of Sam Uley someday with somebody else, and watching his face when he found out. In the versions she'd played in her head on the brown polyester bedspread in room seven at Walt's, his face had crumpled.

This time it went still. His eyes dropped to her left hand again, to the gray stone flashing green and gold under the porch lights, and stayed there, and something in his shoulders let go a fraction of an inch, like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Then the stillness came back, harder than before.

"Husband," said Sam.

"Mm," said Harry.

"Since when?"

"July," said Harry. "Under an oak tree. It was lovely. You weren't invited."

Out in the trees came another of those huffing sounds, and then a second one from somewhere else.

Paul had stopped chewing. He swallowed, and looked at Leah, up and down, the way he used to look at her in the pack mind when he was hunting around for the soft place that would hurt the most. Leah felt herself brace for it out of pure muscle memory, the way your foot braces for a stair in the dark.

"Huh," said Paul. "So Leah ran off and found herself a sugar daddy with a tail."

Harry finished his fry bread.

He folded the napkin in half, and in half again, and set it neatly on the empty plate. Then he looked at Paul. His ears had come up straight and still. Under the hem of the coat, his tail had stopped moving.

"Paul," said Harry gently. "Pauly. May I call you Pauly?"

"No."

"Pauly. You may say whatever you like about me. Truly. I'm a very large foreigner in a frankly ridiculous coat, standing in your—" he glanced around "—*garden*, eating your neighbor's baking without having been asked. I'm fair game. I quite understand." A pause. "But you say one more word like that about my wife, and I shall have the dog warden round."

Paul's face went red, and then it went dark. A tremor started in his hands—Leah saw it start, a fine fast shiver like a plucked guitar string—and climbed his arms into his shoulders. His lips came back off his teeth. The rain hitting his skin went up in steam.

She knew that shiver. She'd watched Paul go through it a hundred times, through her own eyes and through his. Thirty seconds, tops, and there'd be a silver wolf the size of a pickup truck standing beside the Rolls-Royce, and the Rolls-Royce would need a new door.

Harry watched him shake with an expression of mild, attentive patience. He didn't step back. He didn't put down the plate.

"*Paul,*" said Sam. "*Back.*"

Leah felt it go out of him.

She'd always been able to feel it. Even now, apparently—no pack, no link, fifteen months of nobody in her head but her—she felt the air change when Sam put the Alpha into his voice, like a drop in pressure right before a storm. It went out of him like a hook on a line, and it hit Paul square in the chest, and Paul jerked as if somebody had yanked a chain bolted to his breastbone. The shaking stopped dead. He took two steps back from the car without deciding to, his face twisting, and stood there with his fists clenched and his chest heaving, glaring at Harry across the hood.

Leah stood very still under the umbrella.

She remembered that. She remembered exactly what that felt like from the inside—the grab, and the way it went straight past every part of you that was supposed to get a vote. She'd stood behind a woodshed in the rain fifteen months ago and felt it buckle her legs.

She looked down at her right hand. At the heavy gold ring with its flat square of green stone, sitting on her finger where it had shrunk itself to fit.

When she looked up again, Sam was watching her.

He'd seen her face. She watched him see it, and watched his eyes go from her to Harry and back, and watched something in them turn over. Worry—real worry, the kind she remembered, the kind he used to have about every single one of them. And under the worry, fear.

She understood all at once what he was thinking. She might have thought it herself, a year and a half ago. Leah Clearwater, who hated everybody, who'd run off into the night rather than share a head with anybody ever again, had come home married to a seven-foot talking wolf in an English accent, and she was standing beside it in the rain giggling like a twelve-year-old. And Sam had lived next door to a family of leeches who could read your mind and see your future and change how a whole room felt just by thinking about it. If half of what Seth had said in the kitchen was true, he'd stood in a field last winter and seen worse.

"Leah," said Sam. "*Come here.*"

It went out of him exactly the way it had gone into Paul. The hook. The line. She felt it leave him and come across the wet gravel at her, and she braced for it the way she always had, her weight going forward onto her toes.

It reached her, and it went through her like a ball thrown at a window somebody had left open.

There was the rain, and the lights, and the smell of fry bread and wet alder and Harry, and the ring on her right hand sitting heavy and quiet on her finger. Inside her head there was nobody but her.

She laughed.

This laugh came out shorter than the others. It had an edge on it.

"No," Leah said.

Sam's face went white.

Beside her, under the umbrella, Harry had gone very still. His ears were up. His eyes were on Sam, and they were green and steady, and there was nothing left in them that looked like a joke.

"Samuel," said Harry softly.

It was the same voice. The long vowels, the crisp consonants, the four hundred years of money. Leah had never heard anything that cold come out of him.

"Did you just try to give my wife an *order*?"

Sam didn't answer.

"In the rain," said Harry. "On her mother's doorstep. In front of me." His head tilted one slow degree. "On a *Friday*?"

---

Sam felt it go out of him and close on nothing.

He had never felt that before. Not once in three years. Not since the first night in the trees behind his mother's house, when he'd come apart into something with fur and teeth and nobody alive to tell him what it was. Not since Jared, shaking on his bedroom floor with a fever of a hundred and eight, when Sam had said *stop* and Jared had stopped. Every time since, whenever he put the weight into his voice, it had landed. Jacob fought it. Paul hated it. Embry used to cry, the first few months, and then go do it anyway. But it landed. You could feel it take hold, the way you could feel your hand close around a rope.

This had gone out across the gravel and found air.

Once before. Only once. The night behind his woodshed, when she went through Jacob and out the other side and the whole pack lurched sideways under him. He had reached for her that night with everything he had in him, and his reach had closed on the place where she'd been.

He stood in the rain with his pulse going in his ears.

She had laughed at him.

He made himself look at Leah instead of the thing beside her.

Her face had changed. He'd known that face since she was fourteen and he was seventeen and she'd thrown a softball at his head at a Fourth of July picnic for calling her *kid*. He'd known it better than his own, once, and then he'd broken it, and then for a year he'd had to look at what was left from the inside. The sharpness had gone out of it. The hollows under the cheekbones had filled. Her hair was long again, heavy and black and past her shoulders, and the last time he'd seen it, it had been hacked off to her jaw with kitchen scissors the week of her father's funeral. She was standing under an umbrella in a coat that had never come from anywhere in Clallam County, with tears from laughing still on her face, and she looked—he went looking for the word, and the only one he could find was *fed*.

He hadn't heard Leah laugh since the summer before he phased.

He'd done that. He knew it. He'd known it every single day since. Emily knew it too. Emily was at home right now with a pan of blueberry muffins cooling on the rack and the phone in her lap, waiting to find out what had come across the treaty line in a car the size of a hearse, and when he got home and told her it had been Leah, Emily would put her hand over the scarred side of her face the way she did when she didn't know what to feel.

He looked at the thing.

It was taller than Jacob. Nobody was taller than Jacob. It stood on two legs like a man, heel to toe, on bare black-padded feet that had no business carrying that much weight across wet gravel without a sound, and he'd been listening for the sound since the door opened. It was black from its ears to its feet. It wore a long black coat buttoned high at the throat with a silver clasp, and it held an umbrella over Leah's head in one long-clawed paw as if this were the most ordinary thing in the world to be doing at six o'clock on a Friday in December on the Quileute reservation. There was powdered sugar on its chin.

And its heart was beating sixty times a minute.

Sam had been listening to that, too. All of them had. A heartbeat was the first thing you heard and the last thing that lied to you. Paul's was up around a hundred and forty and still climbing. Jacob's was jumpy from laughing. Leah's had been skipping like a stone across water ever since she came down the steps. Sam could feel his own in his back teeth.

The thing's was sixty. Slow and even as a clock on a kitchen wall. It had been sixty when it walked out onto that porch in front of twenty wolves. It had stayed at sixty when Paul started to come apart three yards from it. It was sixty now.

He breathed in through his nose. Wet wool. Fry bread. Sugar. Under that, the thing's own smell, which he'd been trying to put a name to since it crossed the line: turned earth, and pine, and something cold and mineral, like the stones at the bottom of the Sol Duc in August. No bleach. No sweet rot. Nothing that belonged to the Cullens or the things that came looking for them.

And under all of that, so faint he'd have missed it if he hadn't spent three years smelling it on himself, a thread of something his wolf knew. Hot and sharp, like the air coming off his boys after a long run. Like the inside of the pack.

He couldn't put a name to it. He didn't like that he couldn't.

"Are you a Child of the Moon?" Sam said.

It was the only thing he had. A white-haired man from Italy with a face like chalk had stood in the snow last January and talked about the Children of the Moon in a voice like a bored history teacher. Werewolves. The real ones, he'd said, as if Sam and his boys were a cheap knockoff. Hunted almost to nothing. The moon was behind the clouds tonight and three nights short of full.

The thing's ears went up.

"A child of Godric's Hollow, actually," it said. "Little village in the West Country. Thatched roofs. A church. Terribly picturesque. You wouldn't know it." The ears flicked sideways. "Is that what you've been standing there trying to work out? How sweet."

"What did you do to her?"

"Just now?" It looked down at Leah, and back up. "Just now I held an umbrella over her." A pause. "She's under an umbrella, Sammy. That's the only thing she's under."

"*Sam*," said Leah.

He looked at her.

"He didn't do anything." Her voice had gone flat and level, the way it used to go in the pack mind on the bad nights, when she'd decided to stop screaming and start being exact. "Nobody did anything to me. I'm not yours, Sam. I haven't been yours since your woodshed."

"I felt it go *through* you."

"Yeah." She turned the heavy gold ring on her right hand with her thumb, once, a small absent movement. It was an old ring, thick and plain, with a square flat green stone in it like something out of a museum case. "Get used to it."

Sam didn't understand that. He could tell he wasn't supposed to.

"Sam," said Paul.

Sam didn't look at him. "Not now."

"Sam." Paul's voice had changed. The heat had gone out of it. "Seth's in there."

"I know Seth's in there."

"No. Listen to me." Paul had come around the front of the car. He was still breathing hard from the almost-phase, his chest going in and out, but his eyes had gone past Sam to the house. "When we were coming in. Before the howl. You were still on two legs back at your place, you didn't hear it. We were right at the tree line." He swallowed. "Seth was in there yelling, man. Loud. We all heard him." He looked at Leah. Then at the thing. "He was yelling he's an *uncle*."

The rain came down.

*Uncle.*

Seth Clearwater had one sister.

And Leah couldn't. Sam knew that. Every one of them knew it. They'd all been inside her head the year she ran with them, and it had been there under everything she thought, morning and night, flat and cold as a stone in a creek. *Dead end.* She'd never said it aloud to any of them. She'd never needed to. It had been one more thing he'd had to carry around with everything else he'd done to her, and he'd carried it, and he'd never once said a word to her about it, because there was no word in the world that would have helped.

A child.

With *this*.

He looked past them at the house.

The curtain in the front window had been pulled all the way back. Sue Clearwater stood in the yellow light behind the glass in her apron, with Seth's big bare shoulder beside her, and she was holding something wrapped in gray against her chest. Holding it the way you hold something you'd step in front of a truck for.

Sam listened.

Under the rain and the lights and Paul's breathing, now that he was listening for it, he heard it. A heartbeat. Tiny. Very fast. Much too fast, fast like a bird's.

He had heard a heartbeat like that once before. In a white field in January, under fifty pairs of red eyes.

And before that, he had seen Bella Swan through Jacob's memory, propped on a couch in that glass house with her ribs standing out like the bars of a xylophone, and a belly that was eating her from the inside out. He'd stood behind his own woodshed in the rain and said *we can't let it be born*. He'd been wrong about that one. In the end he'd stood in the snow beside the leeches for her, him and every one of his boys. But he had been afraid, and the fear was right there again, older than he was, older than the treaty.

"Leah," he heard himself say. "What is it?"

She went still. "What?"

"The baby." His voice came out rough, and he couldn't make it come out any other way. "What is it? Is it like hers? Like the Cullen girl? Did it hurt you? Did it—"

Leah's face shut like a door.

Beside her, the thing's tail had gone completely still.

It let go of the blue willow plate. The plate didn't fall. It rose, smoothly, folded napkin and all, and floated back up the porch steps and in through the open front door, and the door swung shut behind it with a quiet click.

"Samuel," said the thing.

It said it pleasantly. Conversationally. In that same voice, all money and long vowels, like a man at a garden party remarking on the roses.

"I'm going to tell you something now. And then we'll all go back to talking about the weather, which I'm told is dreadful here all year round."

The rain stopped.

It stopped the way a movie stops when somebody hits pause. Every drop in the yard hung where it was—between the porch roof and the gravel, over the long black hood of the car, in the air around Paul's head, an inch in front of Sam's own face. Millions of them. The Christmas lights went into every single one and came back out red and green and blue and gold, so that the whole yard was full of tiny colored lights, hanging there, not moving.

The roar on the roof cut off. The gutters stopped running. In the silence Sam could hear the surf on First Beach a mile away, and his own heart, and somewhere in the trees behind him one of the youngest wolves—Collin, he thought—starting to whine, high and thin, like a puppy shut out in the cold.

The thing's heart was beating sixty times a minute.

"My daughter," it said, "is twenty-seven days old. She is in that house in her grandmother's arms with a little knitted hat on, and she weighs about as much as a loaf of bread. She sneezes when she's surprised. She has never hurt anybody. She is never going to hurt anybody, unless it's Seth, and I gather Seth would let her."

It tilted its head, one degree.

"So. If you, or anybody who takes orders from you, ever comes within a mile of her with that look on your face—" one black claw lifted in a small, courteous gesture "—yes, that one, Sammy, the one you're wearing right now—I shall burn this peninsula to the waterline. All of it. Every tree. Every rock. Every leech, every beach, every one of your lovely damp little roads."

A pause.

"I'll go round Sue, naturally. I'm terribly precise." The ears flicked sideways. "And afterwards I shall send the national park a card."

The drops hung in the air between them, glittering.

Sam stood in the middle of it, with a bead of water stopped an inch from his left eye, red from the bulb behind it, and found that he couldn't swallow. He'd stood in a field across from the Volturi. He'd watched the white-haired one tear the head off a woman who'd come to that field as his own witness. He'd watched Alec's mist come rolling across the snow toward his boys like a tide. He'd been afraid of all of it. But there'd been fifty of them standing in that field together, and every one of them had been afraid too, and he'd been able to hear it, and that had helped.

This thing was standing on Sue Clearwater's front step holding an umbrella over Leah's head, and it was not afraid of anything in the world, and its heart said so.

"Do we understand one another?"

"Yes," said Sam.

"Splendid," said the thing, and the rain fell.

It fell all at once, every drop in the yard at the same instant, a sound like a wave breaking across the roof, and then it was only rain again, ordinary rain, pounding on the gravel and the car and his bare shoulders. The gutters gulped and started running. The whine in the trees cut off.

Sam was soaked to the skin. He didn't feel it.

Under the umbrella, the thing's heart beat on. Sixty. Sixty. Sixty.

It had never moved.

---

*What is it.*

Leah heard it the way she'd heard it the first time. In the middle of her chest, in Sam's voice, with the rain coming down behind the woodshed and nine other minds screaming around her. *The thing she's carrying is a danger to the tribe. We can't let it be born.*

*It.*

She stepped out from under the umbrella.

She didn't plan it. The rain hit her face and her hair and ran cold down the back of her neck, and a half-second later the black silk was over her again, because Harry had moved with her without making a sound. She didn't look up at him. She was looking at Sam.

"*It*," she said.

Sam closed his eyes for a second.

"That's what you called Bella's," said Leah. "Behind your woodshed. Fifteen months ago. 'The thing she's carrying.' 'It.' 'We can't let it be born.'" Her voice didn't shake. She was proud of that, later. "I was in your head when you said it, Sam. I felt you mean it."

"Bella was dying." Sam's eyes were open again. "You saw her. That thing was breaking her from the inside—"

"Yeah, well. Mine didn't." Leah lifted her chin. "Mine's a girl. Her name's Layla. She was born under a full moon in a four-hundred-year-old bed, and the worst thing she ever did to me was kick me in the liver. She's twenty-seven days old, and the worst thing she's done to anybody in the whole world is sneeze on Seth."

"Leah—"

"You don't get to say *it* about my daughter," said Leah. "You don't ever get to say *it*, Sam. Not ever."

Behind her, very quietly, the tail moved once against the back of her leg.

Then Jacob moved.

He'd stopped laughing sometime during the rain. Leah had seen him out of the corner of her eye, standing beside the hood of the Rolls with a million glittering drops hanging in the air around his head, not breathing. Now he came around the front of the car and across the gravel to the bottom of the porch steps, and turned around, and stood on Leah's other side. Facing Sam. Six feet away from him, the same six feet as before, except now the six feet were on Leah's side of the yard.

"Sam," Jacob said. "We did this already."

Sam looked at him.

"We did this," said Jacob. "Last winter. In the snow. Everybody stood in a line for a kid nobody understood, and you stood in it too. I was standing right next to you." Rain was running off his chin. "Don't make me do it again for Leah's."

The two of them looked at each other across the gravel.

Leah couldn't feel either pack anymore. There was nothing in her head but her. But she didn't need to be in their heads to see it—the old thing between the two of them, the thing that had torn open behind the woodshed and never really closed. It was in the set of their shoulders. In the way neither of them blinked.

Sam looked away first.

He looked at Leah. Then past her, at the front window, where her mother was standing in the yellow light with the gray bundle held against her apron and her chin up, looking right back at him through the glass.

Something went out of him. Leah watched it go.

"I wasn't going to—" Sam started.

"You were thinking it," said Leah.

Sam opened his mouth. Closed it. The rain ran down his face.

"Yeah," he said finally. "I was thinking it." He looked at the window again, at her mother, at the bundle. "I'm sorry."

Leah didn't say anything.

He'd said sorry to her more times than she could count, that year. *Sorry, Leah. I'm so sorry. I didn't choose it, you know I didn't choose it.* She'd hated every single one. Every one had come with something stapled to the back of it, a reason, a way out.

This one didn't have anything stapled to it. It just sat there in the rain.

"Congratulations," Sam said. Quieter. "On the—on Layla. Congratulations, Leah."

That one went in somewhere she wasn't braced for, and she stood there with it under the umbrella and found she didn't have the first idea what to do with it.

"Thanks," she said.

Sam nodded once. He turned to Harry, and his face went careful and closed again, the face he used for council meetings and for Old Quil and for telling a fourteen-year-old kid what had just happened to his body.

"The council's going to want to meet you," he said.

"Mom's on the council, Sam," said Leah.

"I know she is."

"Oh, *splendid*," said Harry. The voice was back—every vowel of it, all four hundred years. His ears had come back up. Under the coat, the tail had started a slow, idle sweep. "Do tell them I'd be delighted. I'll bring a cake." He brightened. "Does everybody like a Victoria sponge? Everybody likes a Victoria sponge."

"No," said Jacob and Paul, at the same time.

Harry's ears went straight up. "No?"

"Bad experience," said Jacob. "With a Victoria."

"Red hair," said Paul, from over by the car. "Tried to kill Bella. Twice. Built a whole army of newborns to do it. Edward pulled her head off."

Harry considered this.

"Lemon drizzle, then," he said.

Leah sat down.

She didn't mean to. Her legs just went, the way they had behind the woodshed, except this time it was from laughing so hard there was no air left in her. She sat down on the bottom porch step in the wet and put her face on her knees and shook. Above her, the umbrella came down a couple of feet, smoothly, and kept her covered.

Sam looked at her for a long moment, sitting there on the step. Something moved in his face that she didn't see, because she had her face on her knees. She heard it, though, in the half-second hitch of his breathing.

Then he turned around and walked toward the trees.

"Mind how you go, Sammy!" Harry called after him, cheerfully. "Do buy a shirt! It's *December*!"

Sam didn't turn around. At the edge of the alders, without slowing down, he came apart. A ripple and a shudder, and a black wolf the size of a horse went into the trees where a man had been, and a shredded scrap of denim came fluttering down onto the ferns behind it. A second later it lifted its head and howled, one short flat note, and the forest all around the house answered it with the sound of something very large getting up all at once.

Leah lifted her head off her knees and listened to them go.

Paws. Dozens of them, heavy, crashing through salal and fern. Panting. A branch snapping somewhere toward the road. All of it moving off to the north and east, getting fainter, until it was only the rain again and the surf down on First Beach.

Paul was still standing by the car.

He looked at Harry. He looked at the Rolls-Royce. He looked, for a second, at Leah sitting on the step, and she couldn't read what was on his face, and she found she didn't much care.

"Tell Gerald," said Paul, "he can kiss my ass."

"I shall pass it on," said Harry gravely.

Paul went. He jogged past the hood, close, and as he went by he reached out and touched the black paint with one fingertip, deliberately, the way a little kid touches a stove after his mother says don't. Harry's left ear twitched. Then Paul was at the tree line and through it, and a silver wolf went crashing away after the others, and he was gone.

Jacob was still there.

He stood at the bottom of the steps beside Leah with his arms folded and the rain running down him, and he didn't seem in any hurry to go anywhere. Out in the trees, Leah could smell two wolves who hadn't left with the rest. Embry. Quil. Waiting for Jake.

Harry stood very still under the umbrella for a few seconds, ears turning, one and then the other, following the last of the sounds out into the forest. Then he let out a long breath through his nose.

"Right," said Harry. "That went well."

It came out flat-voweled and clipped at the ends. Johannesburg. The voice that had asked her out on a mountain.

Jacob's head snapped around so fast he must have hurt his neck.

"What," said Jacob, "happened to your *voice*?"

Harry looked down at him. His ears flicked sideways.

"Which one, boet?"

Jacob stared at him. Then he turned around and walked in a small circle on the gravel with both hands pressed over his face, and when he came back around he was laughing so hard he couldn't get any sound out at all. He had to put one hand on the porch rail to stay up. The rail creaked.

"Oh my God," he wheezed. "Oh my God. You're—you're not even—"

"I'm English," said Harry. "Sort of. Born there. Got shipped to Johannesburg when I was four. Went back for school." He shrugged one big shoulder under the coat. "I can do posh. Had seven years of a bloke at school who talked like that every day. And his dad. Mostly his dad."

"*Poor-people dirt*," Jacob gasped.

"Too much?"

"No. No. Oh my God. Paul's *face*." Jacob straightened up and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand. Then his grin faded a little, and he looked at Harry, really looked, from his bare black footpaws to the tips of his ears. "Was any of it real?"

"The bit about the peninsula," said Harry.

Jacob stopped laughing.

"Yeah," he said, after a second. "Yeah. I figured." He glanced out at the yard, at the gravel and the lights and the rain falling on all of it the ordinary way. "You stopped the rain."

"Ja."

"Like, all of it."

"Just the yard." Harry's tail flicked. "Didn't want to show off."

Jacob looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked down at Leah, on the step, with her arms wrapped around her knees and her face still wet from laughing.

"You ran right through me," he said. "That night. You know that?"

"I know," said Leah.

"Felt like getting hit by a train. One second you were there, you were in my head, and the next—" He made a small sound, not quite a laugh. "Took me about a week to stop looking for you."

"Sorry."

"Don't be." Jacob shook his head. Water flew off his short hair. "I got it. Took me a while, but I got it." He looked at the window, where Sue had let the curtain fall and gone back into the room. "You look good, Leah. You look happy. It's weird."

"Thanks, Jake."

"Billy's gonna want to see you. He's gonna lose his mind." He hesitated. "Come by the house tomorrow? Bring—what's her name?"

"Layla."

"Layla," Jacob said, trying it out. He smiled. It was a small smile, softer than the grin. "Bring Layla. And bring him." He looked up at Harry. "We need to talk about the thing north of the lake. Did Seth tell you about it?"

"Some," said Harry.

"Edward thinks it's a leech with something wrong with it. Carlisle doesn't think it's a leech at all. Nobody can catch it. We've been chasing it since October and we've never even seen it." Jacob's jaw tightened. "And there's the girl. In Port Angeles. Did Seth tell you about the girl?"

Harry's ears went flat.

"No," he said.

Jacob looked at Leah, and then at the house. Whatever he'd been about to say, he swallowed it.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Ask Seth tonight. He knows as much as I do."

"Tomorrow," Harry agreed.

"And Nessie's gonna want to—"

"Jake," said Leah.

"—to see a baby, is all, she's never—"

"*Don't push it.*"

Jacob held up both hands. The grin came back. "Okay. Okay. Tomorrow." He started to back away across the gravel toward the trees, and then stopped beside the car, and looked at it one more time. "Seriously, though. Tomorrow. Can I look under the hood?"

"Bonnet," said Harry.

"Under the *bonnet*."

Harry considered him.

"Maybe," he said.

Jacob grinned all the way to the tree line. Then he went into the alders at a jog, and a moment later a huge russet wolf went loping away north through the trees, with a cord flapping on its hind leg and two more shapes falling in beside it—one gray and spotted, one dark brown—and then they were gone too.

The yard was empty.

Leah sat on the step and listened to the rain. Christmas lights. The gutter on the left side gurgling and overflowing where it always had. The surf. Her mother's voice inside the house, saying something low to Seth. And a heartbeat, small and fast, under all of it, coming through the walls. *Small-quick. Small-quick.* Against her breastbone, the pale stone of the necklace pulsed in time with it.

Harry sat down beside her.

He had to fold himself nearly in half to do it. His knees came up almost to his chin, and his tail went down the steps and lay across the gravel in the wet, and he held the umbrella over both of them and didn't say anything.

"Gerald," said Leah.

"Hm?"

"Who the hell is Gerald?"

His ears went sideways. "No idea."

"You made up a guy."

"Kreacher polished it on Thursday," said Harry. "Took him all afternoon. He used a chamois and he wouldn't let me help." He considered. "'Kreacher's just washed it' didn't have the same ring."

Leah started laughing again. She couldn't help it. She put her head down on his shoulder, on the wet wool of his coat, and laughed until her stomach hurt, and he sat there and let her, and his tail went *thump* against the bottom step.

"Poor-people *dirt*, Harry."

"I'll admit that one got away from me a bit."

"The dental plan."

"I stand by the dental plan."

"*The Popes at Avignon.*"

"That's a real thing. There were two of them. It was fourteenth century. It was a whole mess." His ear flicked against the side of her head. "Hermione made me read about it once."

"Sam's *face*." She lifted her head and wiped her eyes. "Harry. You called him Sammy. You called him Sammy like six times."

"Seven."

"You counted?"

"Somebody had to." He looked down at her, and his ears came forward, and the joke went quiet in his face. "You okay?"

She thought about it. Actually thought about it, sitting there on the wet step in the dark with the lights blurring and his coat against her cheek.

"Yeah," she said. "I think so."

"He said congratulations."

"I heard."

"I don't know what to do with that."

"You don't have to do anything with it," said Harry. "You can just leave it there."

She sat with that for a while.

"Leah," he said.

"Mm."

"Who is he?"

She went still against his shoulder. He waited. He didn't push. He never pushed.

"Later," she said. "Inside. After she eats. I'll tell you all of it." She took a breath. "I promise."

"Okay," said Harry.

He stood up, and held out his free forepaw, and pulled her up after him. Then he turned and put that same forepaw flat on the porch post at the top of the steps, the old cedar one her dad had replaced the summer she turned fourteen, and stood there for a moment with his eyes half-closed.

Leah felt it settle. The same way she'd felt it between the gateposts on Dartmoor, smaller, but the same: something going out from under his paw into the posts and the boards and the ground under the house, all the way out to the edge of the alders, and the gravel road, and the trees behind the woodshed. Something old and careful lying down across the yard like a dog across a doorway.

"Same as home?" she said.

"Same as home. Anything cold and dead that walks into this yard without being asked is going to have a very bad night."

"What about the Cullens? They're Seth's friends."

"They can knock from the road," said Harry.

Against her breastbone, the necklace pulsed faster. *Small-quick, small-quick, small-quick.* And from inside the house, thin and furious and rising, came the sound of a twenty-seven-day-old wolf deciding she'd been patient long enough.

Harry's ears swung around toward the door like a pair of radar dishes.

"That's her hungry one," he said.

"How can you tell?"

"It goes up at the end. The cross one goes down." He was already reaching for the doorknob. "Come on."

He closed the umbrella. It folded up in his forepaw with a soft rustle, and then he wasn't holding anything at all, and Leah had been looking right at it.

She followed him inside, still laughing.

---

Seth had been at the window the whole time.

He'd been told to stay inside, so he'd stayed inside. Harry had looked at him with those green eyes and said *don't open the door unless it's me or Leah*, and Seth had nodded, and he'd meant it. He'd stood at the front window with his forehead about an inch from the glass and his hands balled up at his sides, and his mom beside him holding the baby, and he'd listened to every single word.

He could hear through glass fine. They all could. He'd heard *poor-people dirt*. He'd heard *of course*. He'd heard *do I sparkle* and had to bite his own fist. He'd heard Sam say *what is it* and felt his mom go stiff as a board beside him, and then he'd watched the rain stop in the middle of the air, all of it, every drop in the yard hanging there full of Christmas lights, and he'd forgotten to breathe for so long that his mom had reached over without looking and smacked him on the arm.

The door opened. Harry came in first, ducking under the frame, with rain on his shoulders and his ears pointed straight at the baby. Leah came in behind him. She was still laughing. She had both arms wrapped around her own stomach, and her face was wet, and she was laughing so hard she had to lean on the wall in the hallway.

Layla was screaming.

It was a thin, high, furious noise, and it went up at the end like a question. She'd started about thirty seconds ago and she was getting louder every breath, and Seth's mom had been jiggling her and walking her in a little circle in front of the window and saying *shh, shh, they're coming, they're coming*, and Layla had not cared.

Harry crossed the living room in three strides. He stopped in front of Seth's mom and held out his forepaws, palms up.

"May I?"

Mom gave him the baby.

He lifted her up under his chin and bent his head down and put his nose right on the top of her small round head, between her ears, and breathed in. Long and slow. Like he was checking she was all still there.

The screaming dipped. Didn't stop. Dipped.

"Ja," Harry said to her, low. "Ja, I know. I know. Hold on." He turned and held her out toward the hallway. "She wants you."

Leah had stopped laughing, mostly. She came and took the baby and went straight to the couch and sat down and pulled the gray blanket over her shoulder, and did something underneath it, and the screaming cut off like somebody had flipped a switch. In the sudden quiet Seth could hear small, busy, determined swallowing sounds.

He turned around very fast and looked at the Christmas tree.

"Okay," Seth said to the tree. "Okay. Okay." He couldn't hold it anymore. He spun back around. "*PAUL'S FACE.*"

"Seth," said his mom.

"Mom. *Mom.* Did you see Paul's face? When he said poor-people—" Seth tried to do the voice. He put his nose up in the air and pulled his mouth down at the corners and said, "*I'd rather not get poor-people DIRT on it, thenk you EVAH so much*—"

It came out sounding like a cartoon duck with a head cold.

On the couch, Leah made a noise into the back of her own hand. Harry looked at Seth for a long moment with his head on one side. His ears flicked.

"Close," said Harry.

"Really?"

"No."

Seth's mom had gone into the kitchen. She came back out wiping her hands on her apron, and she stopped in the doorway and looked at Harry. Seth knew that look. Seth had been on the wrong end of that look since he was old enough to reach the cookie jar.

"You took my fry bread," she said.

Harry's ears went pink inside. "Borrowed."

"You fed it to Jacob Black."

"Some of it. Paul had one."

"Paul Lahote has eaten at my table every week for two years," said Mom, "and he has never once said thank you."

"He didn't say it tonight either."

"No," said Mom. "I heard." Her mouth did something at one corner. Seth saw it. It went up, about a quarter of an inch, and then she put it back down where it belonged. "Dinner's in forty minutes. I'm making more. Nobody is taking this batch outside."

"No, ma'am."

"Sue."

"Sue," said Harry.

She went back into the kitchen. Seth heard the flour canister come down off the shelf with a thump.

Harry unbuttoned his coat. The silver clasp at the throat first, then the rest, one at a time, and he shrugged it off and hung it on the row of hooks by the front door, where Dad's old Carhartt still hung on the end hook because nobody had ever been able to take it down. The hook he used bent about thirty degrees under the weight and stayed bent. Under the coat he had on a faded gray T-shirt with *THIN LIZZY* across the chest in cracked letters, and the shirt was stretched so tight across his shoulders that Seth could see the shape of the muscle under the fur.

Something in the coat clunked against the wall.

Harry turned his head and looked at it. His ears tipped forward. He reached into the inside pocket, frowning, and took out a little wooden trunk about the size of a deck of cards, with gold letters painted on the lid, and set it on the hall table beside the dish of keys. Seth's mouth fell open all over again.

Then Harry reached in again, and stopped, and took out something else.

It was a basket. A wicker hamper with a lid and two little leather straps, and it was about the size of a matchbox. There was a folded square of paper tied to the handle with string.

"I didn't pack that," said Harry.

"Kreacher," said Leah, from the couch, without even looking up.

Harry looked at the tiny hamper in his paw for a long moment. Then he carried it into the kitchen, and Seth followed him, because Seth was going to follow him everywhere for the rest of the night and maybe the rest of his life, he'd already decided. Harry set the hamper down in the middle of the kitchen table and stepped back.

It grew.

It just got bigger, smoothly, the way a balloon fills up, until it was the size of a footlocker and took up the whole table, and the table legs creaked. Mom stopped with her hands in the dough and looked at it over her shoulder.

It was written in black ink in tiny, spiky, crabbed capital letters that leaned in every direction like a fence after a windstorm.

*MASTER WILL NOT EAT PROPERLY AWAY FROM HOME. KREACHER KNOWS IT. THE WOLF-WOMAN MUST EAT FOR THE HEIR. KREACHER HAS PACKED WHAT IS NEEDED. KREACHER IS CLEANING.*

*— K.*

"Who's Kreacher?" said Seth.

"House-elf," said Harry.

"A *what*?"

"Little guy," Leah called from the living room. "About knee high. Big ears like a bat. Wears a tea towel. Says he's going to iron his hands when he's upset."

Seth looked at Harry. "Iron his—"

"We've talked him out of it," said Harry. "Mostly." He undid the leather straps and lifted the lid.

Harry started taking things out, and Seth stood beside him and watched them come.

A whole roast of beef wrapped in a white cloth, brown and glistening and still hot—Seth could feel the heat coming off it—like it had come out of the oven ten minutes ago instead of five thousand miles away that morning. A slab of raw red meat the size of a couch cushion, wrapped in white butcher paper and tied with string, with *WOLF-WOMAN* written across the paper in the same spiky capitals. Three round loaves of brown bread. A tart in a fluted tin, dark gold and shiny on top, that Harry looked at and said, "Treacle," in a very quiet voice. "Darlene's," said Harry, holding up the pie. "Bumbleberry. Kreacher's been practicing off the recipe card since August. This is the first one he's let out of the house."

*Bramble Jam.*

Harry went still.

He stood there holding the jar in both forepaws, looking at the label. His tail had stopped moving. Seth didn't know what was happening, but he knew enough to shut up.

"That's my gran's writing," Harry said finally. "He's cut the label out of her recipe book and stuck it on." His ears went back. "He's made Gran's jam."

From the living room, there was a short silence. Then Leah's voice, quieter. "Is that the one your dad liked?"

"On everything," said Harry. He set the jar down very carefully in the middle of the table, by itself, away from the edge. "Apparently."

Seth's mom had turned all the way around from the stove. She was looking at the table—the roast, the raw meat, the bread, the tart, the pie, the jar. Then at Harry. Then back at her bowl of dough.

"Well," she said. "I suppose I'll make less fry bread."

---

The trunk went in the living room. Harry set it down on the floor against the wall between the couch and the TV and stepped back, and it grew into a big old steamer trunk with brass corners and *L. S. P-B.* painted on the lid in gold, and it was so big that Seth had to climb over the end of the couch to get past it.

He opened it. Nobody told him not to.

It was full of baby stuff. More baby stuff than Seth had known existed. Tiny sleepers folded into perfect squares. A blanket so soft it felt like fog. A silver rattle black with tarnish. A hot-water bottle in a knitted cover. A jar of something that said *VIOLET POWDER* on it in curly letters, that Leah, when Seth held it up, looked at and said, "Don't ask," and Harry said, "Kreacher," at the same time. And a shelf—an actual little shelf, built into the inside of the trunk—with a stack of cloth diapers on it, folded into squares, so many of them that Seth stopped counting at twenty.

He picked one up off the top and shook it out.

There was a hole in the back of it. A neat round hole, hemmed all the way around with tiny stitches, about the size of a quarter.

Seth looked at it. He looked over at the couch, where Layla had finished eating and was lying in the crook of Leah's arm on her back with her eyes shut and her tiny forepaws curled up against her chest. Under the edge of the gray blanket, the end of a small furry tail stuck out. It twitched in her sleep.

Seth put his finger through the hole in the diaper. It fit exactly.

"She's got diapers," he said, "with a hole for her *tail*."

His voice did something weird on the last word. He didn't care.

"Forty-two of them," said Harry.

"Kreacher made them?"

"Kreacher made them."

"I want to meet Kreacher," said Seth, and meant it with his whole heart.

He folded the diaper back up, badly, and put it on top of the stack, and Harry reached over and refolded it into a perfect square without comment, and put it back.

Seth sat down on the end of the couch beside Leah's feet. He couldn't stop looking at all of them. At Harry, crouched by the trunk, with his tail curled around his footpaws and the Christmas tree lights catching in the black fur. At the baby. At Leah.

"How'd you do the rain thing?" he asked.

"Magic," said Harry.

"Like, real magic? Like with a wand?"

"Sometimes with a wand."

"Where's your wand?"

Harry looked at him. His ears flicked sideways, and the corner of his muzzle went up off a lot of teeth.

"What wand?" he said.

Leah snorted. "He does that," she told Seth. "He's done it since the first night. You'll never get a straight answer. Don't bother."

"Who's Gerald?" said Seth.

"The car," said Leah.

Seth looked out the front window, at the huge black shape sitting in the drive in the rain behind Mom's Subaru, gleaming under the Christmas lights, with the little silver lady on the front leaning into the weather.

"The car," he said.

"The car," said Leah.

"The car's name is *Gerald*."

"Yes," said Harry and Leah, at exactly the same time.

Seth sat back on the couch. He thought about it.

"Awesome," he said.

Leah laughed.

It was a small laugh, short, and it came out through her nose while she was looking down at the baby, and she probably didn't even know she'd done it. But Seth heard it, and something went tight in his chest, because he remembered the last time he'd heard her laugh at nothing like that. It was at the dinner table, the week before Dad died. Dad had told the joke about the priest and the salmon that he'd told a thousand times, the one that didn't even make sense, and Leah had laughed through her nose exactly like that and said *Dad, that's so stupid*, and Dad had looked so pleased.

He looked over at the kitchen doorway.

Mom was standing in it with flour up to her elbows, holding the spatula, not moving. She was looking at Leah too.

Then she saw Seth looking at her, and she blinked, and lifted the spatula and pointed it at him.

"Seth," she said. "Table."

---

There were five chairs at Sue's kitchen table, and one of them was Harry's.

The other Harry. The pine captain's chair at the end by the window, with the arms worn pale where his hands used to rest and the cushion still pressed into the shape of him, because she'd never been able to bring herself to turn it over. Nobody sat in it. Seth didn't. Charlie, the four or five times he'd come for supper, had looked at it and sat somewhere else without being told. Sam had sat in it once, the week after the funeral, and Leah had gotten up from the table and walked out the back door and not come back until two in the morning.

Sue watched the big man come in from the living room with the baby asleep against his chest in a sling Leah had tied for him out of the gray blanket. She watched him look at the table, and at the chair at the end of it. His eyes stayed on the chair for about a second. Then they came to her, and then he went and folded himself down onto the bench under the window instead, with his knees nearly up at his chin and his tail coiled around the bench leg, the same as he had that afternoon.

She didn't say anything. She put the stew on the table.

It was elk, from the quarter Charlie's friend in Sequim had given her in October. She'd made it in the big blue enamel pot that held enough for a church supper, because Seth ate like a church supper all by himself. She set it down next to Kreacher's roast beef, and the bread, and the platter of fry bread, and the sticky purple pie and the dark gold tart and the little jar of jam that nobody had opened, sitting in the middle of all of it by itself. Leah's raw steak went on a cutting board at Leah's place with a knife beside it.

Then she sat down and watched them eat.

She'd been feeding wolves for almost two years. She knew how wolves ate. Seth came home from patrol and ate a dozen eggs standing at the stove, out of the pan. Jacob and Embry and Quil had eaten her out of a week's groceries in one Saturday afternoon in October. She'd thought she'd seen everything there was to see about it.

The big man had four bowls of stew. He had half of Kreacher's roast, sliced thick. He had eleven pieces of fry bread—she counted, because she couldn't help it—and he ate all of it leaning a little sideways on the bench, carefully, so that nothing could drip on the small dark head tucked under his chin. Every so often he stopped with the spoon halfway up and tipped one ear down toward the sling, listening, and then went on eating.

Seth tried to keep up with him. Seth got to three bowls and slowed down and looked at the fourth with real fear.

Leah cut her steak into strips with the knife and ate it raw, one strip at a time, steadily, the way she used to eat cereal before school. The blood ran out onto the cutting board. Nobody said a word about it.

"Mom," said Seth, with his mouth full. "This stew is so good."

"Swallow first."

"It's *so* good, though."

"It's excellent," said the big man. "Thank you, Sue."

The phone rang.

It was the yellow one on the wall by the fridge, with the long curly cord that had been stretched out of shape since Leah was in middle school and used to drag it into the pantry to talk to Sam where nobody could hear her. Sue got up and answered it.

"Hello?"

"Sue. Hey." It was Charlie. She'd known it would be. He had the voice he always had on the phone, like he wasn't sure he was supposed to be calling and was ready to apologize for it. "Listen, I don't want to bother you, I know it's suppertime. But I saw a car go by the station this afternoon, around four. Big black thing, real big, headed out the La Push road. Looked like something out of a mob funeral. I just thought, you know. Make sure everything's okay out there."

Behind her, at the table, three people with very good hearing had gone completely silent.

"Everything's fine, Charlie," Sue said. "That's my son-in-law's car."

There was a long pause on the line.

"Your—" said Charlie.

"Leah's home."

"Leah's—oh. Oh, Sue." She heard him breathe out. "That's great. That's real great. I'm so glad." Another pause. She could hear him working it through, the way he worked everything through, one piece at a time, like a man checking the tires on a truck. "Son-in-law?"

"And a granddaughter."

The pause this time went on so long she thought the line had dropped.

"Huh," Charlie said finally.

"Yes."

"Is he, uh." Charlie cleared his throat. "Is he one of the—you know. Is he one of the ones I don't need to know about?"

Sue looked over her shoulder. The big man on the bench had stopped with a piece of fry bread halfway to his muzzle. His ears were pointed straight at her.

"He's a very tall man from South Africa, Charlie," she said.

"Okay," said Charlie. "Okay. That's all I need to know." She heard him shift the phone to his other ear. "Well. Congratulations, Sue. That's—congratulations."

"Come for Christmas dinner. Meet them."

"I'll bring a ham," said Charlie, and hung up, the way he always did, without saying goodbye, as if he'd used up all his words.

Sue hung the phone back on the wall and turned around.

Leah was staring at her. She had a strip of raw elk halfway to her mouth, and her mouth was open, and she'd forgotten about the elk.

"Mom," said Leah.

"Yes."

"That was Charlie *Swan*."

"Yes, it was."

"Why is Charlie Swan coming to *Christmas*?"

"They're dating," said Seth helpfully, with his mouth full.

Leah turned her head and looked at him. Then she turned it back and looked at Sue.

"Since *when*?"

"Since a while," said Sue, and sat back down, and picked up her spoon.

"He's *Bella's dad*."

"I'm aware of whose dad he is, Leah."

"He's—Mom, he's the *chief of police*. He has a *mustache*. He's—" Leah put the elk down. "Does he even know? About any of it?"

"Jake phased in his living room last year," said Seth. "Like, right in front of him. On purpose. Charlie just sat down on the couch for a while and then he said, 'I don't need to know.' He's on need-to-know."

"Need-to-know," Leah repeated.

"It works really well, actually."

The big man had put his fry bread down. He was looking at Sue across the table with his head tipped a little to one side and his ears forward.

"Sensible man," he said.

"He is," said Sue.

Leah opened her mouth, and shut it, and then put her face in both hands and said something into them that Sue chose not to hear. Seth was grinning. The big man's tail had started a slow sweep against the leg of the bench.

Sue ate her stew. She found she was enjoying it more than she had in a long time.

---

It was the big man who changed the subject. He did it carefully, the way he'd held his coffee cup that afternoon.

"Seth," he said. "Jacob said something outside. About a girl. In Port Angeles."

The table went quiet.

Seth put his spoon down. He looked at his bowl. Then he looked at Sue, and Sue looked back at him, and after a second she nodded.

"October," Seth said. "The twelfth. A girl from Peninsula College. Hannah Reyes. She was nineteen. She was studying to be a nurse." He swallowed. "Some fishermen found her on the bank of the Elwha, below the dam. The paper said it was a cougar."

"It wasn't a cougar," said the big man.

"No." Seth's voice had gone small. "Carlisle—Dr. Cullen—he knows the coroner in Port Angeles. He went and looked. He told Jake about it after." Seth looked at Leah. Then at the sling on the big man's chest. "Her heart was gone. Cut out. Clean, Carlisle said. Like a surgeon."

Nobody said anything.

Sue watched her daughter's hand go out across the bench, without her seeming to know she was doing it, and come to rest flat on the small curved back inside the gray sling.

The big man's ears had gone flat against his skull, all the way down. His tail had stopped.

"Vulk took hearts," he said quietly.

"Who's Vulk?" said Seth.

"The man on the mountain," said Leah. "In Canada. Where we met. The one I bit." Her jaw had gone tight. "He took girls' hearts to keep himself young. He was going to take Kayleigh's. And mine."

"Thirty-one that anybody knew about," said the big man. "Geneva thinks closer to two hundred."

Sue put down her spoon.

"He's dead, Harry," said Leah. "I watched."

"Ja. I burnt him. I burnt his anchor. He's gone." He looked down at the top of his daughter's head. "So it's somebody else. Somebody who learned what he learned. You don't make that kind of magic up on your own. Somebody teaches it to you."

"The trail," said Seth slowly. "That's why the trail stops. It's a *wizard*."

"I think so."

"But how does it just *stop*? We've had six wolves on it at once. It goes into a clearing, and then it's just—not anywhere."

"Apparition," said the big man. "You turn on the spot, and you're somewhere else. Could be a mile. Could be a hundred. To a nose it looks like the trail goes straight up into the air." He picked up a piece of fry bread, looked at it, and put it down again. "Edward said it smells burnt. Like a fuse box."

"Like a lightning strike," said Leah.

"That's what it smells like when somebody pays for magic with blood." His ears were still flat. "It's what Vulk smelled like under his cologne. It's what the clearing smelled like."

Sue looked from one of them to the other. Her son-in-law. Her daughter. They'd stopped looking at anybody else at the table. They were looking at each other, and something was going back and forth between them that she wasn't part of and didn't need to be.

"Tomorrow," the big man said. "I'll go and look."

"*We'll* go and look," said Leah.

His eyes went down to the sling.

"I raised two of you," said Sue.

They both turned and looked at her.

"I raised two of you," Sue said again, "through teething and croup and chicken pox and the both of you turning into wolves in the backyard inside of three months. I can hold one baby for an afternoon."

The big man's ears went back, a little. He didn't say no. He didn't say yes, either.

"Tomorrow," said Leah. "Jake's first. Then we'll see."

"Tomorrow," he agreed, after a moment.

---

Leah ate the rest of her elk without looking up.

Sue let her. She'd learned, the year after Sam, that there were times you asked Leah things and times you didn't, and that if you guessed wrong you got the back door slamming and nothing at all until two in the morning. So she cleared the stew bowls and cut the purple pie and set a piece in front of everyone, and she didn't ask.

The big man didn't either. He waited. He ate his pie in small bites with the fork held in two claws, and every so often he looked at Leah, and then away.

"You said later," he said at last. Very quietly. "Outside."

Leah put down her fork.

She looked at Sue. Sue started to get up from the table—to take the stew pot to the sink, to go and check on something, to be anywhere else.

"Stay, Mom," said Leah.

Sue sat back down.

Leah told it.

She told it flat and plain and in order, the way she used to read her book reports aloud in fourth grade, standing up straight with the paper held in front of her face. Sam and her, from when she was sixteen. Three years of it. Sam going missing for two weeks and coming back huge and burning hot and not able to tell her why. Emily coming to visit from the Makah reservation, her cousin Emily, her best friend, and Sam looking at her once across Sue's front yard.

"It's a wolf thing," Leah said. "Imprinting. You look at somebody and that's it. That's the whole rest of your life. Gravity. She's the only thing holding you to the ground. You'd be anything she needed. Friend, brother, protector, whatever. Nobody picks it. It just happens to you." Her voice didn't change. "He didn't pick it. Everybody kept telling me that. He didn't pick it, it's nobody's fault. I knew. I *knew*." She stopped. "And then Dad died, and I phased. And I had to be in Sam's head. Every day. Every patrol. And every single day, every time he thought about anything, she was right there. Her face. Her hands. How he felt about her. From the inside." She picked her fork back up and put it down again. "A year. Like that."

The kitchen was very quiet. The rain was steady on the roof.

Sue had known all of it. Most of it. She'd watched it happen to her daughter from the outside, a year of it, and been able to do nothing but leave the porch light on. She had never once heard Leah say it out loud.

The big man hadn't moved. His ears were flat. His eyes were on Leah's face and nowhere else.

"I called him Sammy," he said.

Leah let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "Seven times."

"Should've been more."

"Harry."

"If I'd known—"

"If you'd known, you'd have burned his house down."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Ja," he said.

"That's why I didn't tell you."

He looked at her for a long time. Then, slowly, he reached over with one forepaw and covered her hand on the table, the whole of it, claws tucked in so far they disappeared into the fur.

"Can it happen to you?" he asked. "With somebody?"

Sue watched her daughter think about it.

"Maybe," said Leah. "I don't know. Nobody knows anything about me. I'm the only girl." She looked down at their hands. "I'd tell it to go to hell."

His tail moved once against the bench leg.

"And Jacob," he said, after a while, in a different voice. "With the little girl. The one who's fifteen months old."

"He's like her big brother," said Seth quickly. "Honestly. That's all. Whatever she needs. Right now she needs somebody to give her piggyback rides and teach her to skip rocks, so that's what he—"

"Nope," said the big man.

"Thank you," said Leah.

"It's honestly *fine*, though—"

"Seth," said Sue. "Eat your pie."

---

The pie was very good. Sue would have liked to say otherwise, out of principle. She couldn't.

"Who taught your—Kreacher—to make this?" she asked.

"A woman called Darlene. She's got a diner in a town called Tamarack Crossing, up in British Columbia." The big man looked over at Leah. "Leah washed dishes for her. That's where we met. Sort of."

Leah reached into the breast pocket of the big red-and-black flannel and took out a folded piece of paper, soft at the creases, and held it out across the table. Sue took it.

It was an order slip from a restaurant, written on the back in blue ballpoint, in big loopy capitals.

*CONGRATULATIONS HON. YOU EAT FOR TWO NOW, NOT THAT YOU DIDN'T BEFORE. RECIPE IS ON THE BACK SO HE CAN QUIT STEALING MINE. NAME HER SOMETHING WITH A BACKBONE. — D.*

Sue read it twice. She turned it over and looked at the recipe on the other side, the neat small list, *cardamom* underlined.

Then she looked at the small furred head tucked under the big man's chin, asleep, with one silver-tipped ear folded over.

"Layla Sue," said Sue.

"Yeah," said Leah.

"I'd say you did." Sue folded the slip back up along its creases and handed it back. "I'd like to meet this Darlene."

"She'd like you," said Leah. "She'd scare you a little."

"I doubt that."

Leah grinned at her. It was a real grin, quick and crooked, and Sue hadn't seen it in so long that she had to look down at her pie.

She took another bite. It gave her a moment.

"Harry," she said.

His ears came up.

"That name. Black. You said it was your godfather's."

"Sirius," he said. "Sirius Black. His family. Old English family, very old, very rich. He died when I was fifteen. He left me the house, and the name, and Kreacher." His tail flicked. "Mostly Kreacher."

"Were any of them ever out here?"

Sue watched it land. His ears went straight up. Across the table Leah went very still with her fork in her hand.

"There's a story," he said slowly. "In the family books. The Healers found it when they were trying to work out what I was. Some Black, couple of hundred years back, sailed off to the Americas on a fur company ship." He paused. "And came home years later with more than furs. That's all it says. One line."

Sue set her fork down on the edge of her plate.

"Old Quil used to tell a story," she said. "When I was a girl. At the bonfires, after the big ones, the Taha Aki ones, when it got late and the little kids were falling asleep. A pale man who came on a ship with the fur traders. Who stayed with the people for a while." She looked at him. "And then left."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"What else?" said Leah. Her voice was thin.

"I don't remember the rest. I was eight. I was mostly asleep." Sue picked her fork back up. "Old Quil would remember. Or Billy."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Seth's head was going back and forth between all three of them like a man watching tennis.

"Wait," said Seth. "So are you, like—"

"*Seth*," said Leah.

"I'm just *asking*—"

"Not tonight," said Leah. She looked at the big man. "Not tonight, okay?"

"Not tonight," he agreed.

He picked up his fork, and looked at the last bite of pie on his plate for a moment with his ears tipped back, and then he ate it.

---

When the dishes were done—he'd tried to help, and she'd pointed at the bench, and he'd gone back and sat on it—Sue dried her hands and turned around.

"Leah's room is made up," she said. "Fresh sheets."

Leah looked at her. "I've been gone fifteen months, Mom."

"I change them every Sunday."

Leah didn't say anything to that. She looked at the floor.

"Thank you, Sue," said the big man.

"The bed's a twin," said Sue.

He looked down the hall, toward Leah's door. Then down at himself. His knees, folded up nearly to his chin on the bench. His tail, which went most of the way across the kitchen floor.

Sue saw his ears tip forward, very slightly, with interest.

"And don't you dare," she said, "make my house bigger."

His ears went pink on the inside.

"I wasn't going to," he said.

"He was going to," said Leah.

---

After the three of them had gone down the hall—Seth to the little back room off the kitchen that his father had closed in from the porch the year he was born, protesting he wasn't tired, and asleep in about a minute and a half with the door open, snoring—Sue turned out the kitchen light and stood for a while in the dark front hall.

The Christmas lights were still on outside the window. The rain had eased off to a drizzle. The black car sat in the drive behind her Subaru with the colored lights on it, and the little silver lady on the hood had a drop of water hanging off the end of her nose.

By the front door, on the row of hooks, the big black coat hung heavy and long from the one he'd bent. It came down nearly to the floor. Right beside it, on the end hook where it had been hanging since March of last year, was Harry's old brown Carhartt, with the frayed cuffs and the pocket that never closed right and the smell of him still in it if you put your face close enough, which she did sometimes, when the kids were out.

She looked at them hanging there side by side.

She reached up and touched the bent hook, the one holding the black coat. She thought about straightening it.

Then she left it the way it was, and went to bed.

---

Leah's old bed was too short for her.

It had always been too short. She'd hit six feet at fifteen and spent four years sleeping diagonally with her feet hanging off the corner. She'd forgotten. She lay in it at a quarter to two in the morning with her heels on the cold windowsill and the same water stain on the ceiling over her head that had been there since she was nine, shaped a little like Florida, and she didn't sleep.

Harry did.

He'd lain down on the braided rug between the bed and the closet, on his back, flat and still the way he slept every night now, with his arms spread out on either side so he couldn't roll. Layla was asleep in the middle of his chest in the thick black fur, curled up small, rising and falling. The room was ten feet across. His footpaws went out through the open doorway into the hall, and his tail went most of the way down the hall toward the bathroom, and when Seth had come through to brush his teeth he'd had to step over it like a fallen log.

He'd been asleep in under a minute. She'd watched him go. Thirty hours, near enough, since he'd last closed his eyes in Devon, and he'd gone out like somebody had pulled a plug.

The old pocket watch lay open on the rug by his ear. Two small lights pulsed inside it. *Big-slow, small-quick.* Against her own breastbone, under the flannel, the pale stone pulsed with them.

She got up.

She did it quietly, which wasn't hard. She'd been doing things quietly in this room since she was thirteen. She stepped over his arm and opened the closet door and stood looking in.

Her winter coat. The ugly orange one from Fred Meyer that she'd sworn she'd never wear in public, and had worn every day of eleventh grade. A row of shoes on the floor. Her old Thriftway apron on a hook, with *LEAH* on the name tag. And on the shelf above, where she'd always kept it, the coffee can. She reached up and took it down and looked inside. Empty. Somebody—her mother—had wiped it out and put it back exactly where it went.

She put it back too.

That was when she saw the sweatshirt.

It was balled up in the back corner of the shelf behind where the can had been, pushed all the way into the shadows. Gray. Soft with washing. A Seahawks logo on the front, cracked half off.

She knew what it was before she touched it.

She pulled it out anyway and held it in both hands. The smell was old—three years old, faded down almost to nothing—but she'd have known it at the bottom of a lake. Sam. Sam at seventeen, before any of it. She'd stuffed it back there the week Emily came to visit, meaning to burn it in the backyard, and she'd never done it, and then she'd forgotten it was there.

She looked down at the long black shape asleep on her bedroom floor.

*The other one smells like somebody Leah used to know.*

He'd walked down this hall that afternoon to the front door, past her room with the door shut, and smelled it. One old sweatshirt, balled up in the back of a closet, through a closed door. And then he'd walked out onto the porch and called its owner Sammy seven times.

Leah stood in the dark with Sam Uley's sweatshirt in her hands and started to laugh. She did it silently, with her shoulders shaking, the way she used to laugh in church.

She took it to the kitchen.

The kitchen was dark except for the light over the stove and the colored glow from the porch coming in around the edges of the curtain. She opened the cabinet under the sink, and lifted the lid on the trash, and pushed the sweatshirt down into it, under the coffee grounds and the potato peels and the paper napkin from the blue willow plate, and put the lid back on.

Then she stood in front of the fridge.

The receipt was still there. Under the salmon magnet, where she'd stuck it fifteen months ago at one in the morning with rain dripping off her hair. Gone soft and gray at the edges, the way paper goes when somebody touches it every day. Her own handwriting on the back. *Don't let them send anybody after me. I mean it.* Next to it, under a magnet shaped like a Mariners logo, her letter from Whitehorse, four lines, torn along the fold and taped back together.

There was a pen in the coffee mug by the phone. She took it out.

At the bottom of the receipt, under the *—L.*, there was about an inch of blank paper left. She wrote in it, small, pressing hard so it would show.

*Came home. —L.*

She put the receipt back under the salmon. She put the pen back in the mug. Then she went back down the hall in the dark, and stepped over her husband's tail, and didn't get back in the bed.

She lay down on the rug beside him instead.

It was hard, and it smelled like dust and the cedar-chip sachet her mom put in all the closets, and it was better. She lay on her side with her head on his arm. Up close, under her cheek, his heart was going slow and even. Layla's was going fast and light, right above it.

His ear swiveled toward her in his sleep. Without waking up, he moved his free forepaw across the rug until it found her hand, and closed over it, claws tucked all the way in.

On his chest, Layla made a small sound. A soft, rumbling chirrup, deep in her tiny chest, over and over, like a kettle thinking about boiling.

Out in the dark, a long way off—north, toward the river, somebody on patrol—a wolf lifted its head and howled. One long, rising note, all by itself.

Leah listened to it go up into the rain and fade.

Out past the kitchen, her brother was snoring. Down the hall her mother was asleep on her side of the bed. Outside, a black car named Gerald sat in the drive under the Christmas lights, very clean.

Inside her head there was only her. She lay on the floor of the room she'd grown up in and listened to the two of them breathe.

Harry shifted a little in his sleep. His muzzle moved against the top of her head.

"*—ever so much,*" he mumbled. Long vowels. Four hundred years of money.

Leah pressed her face into the rug and laughed until her ribs hurt, without making a single sound.

Then she closed her eyes.
