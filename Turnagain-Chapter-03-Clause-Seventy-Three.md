# Chapter Three: Clause Seventy-Three

The deadfall was where she'd left it.

A big spruce gone over in some old windstorm, twenty yards off the lake road behind the last house, its root plate standing up out of the ground like a wall of dirt and rock with a skirt of dead roots hanging off the bottom. Frost on every one of them. The sky over the far shore of the lake had gone the color of dishwater, and down along the water the first smoke was coming out of the first chimneys, going straight up into air too cold and still to bend it.

Leah went around behind the roots and let go.

It hurt the way it always hurt. The fire went in on itself and the bones came down and the fur went wherever the fur went, and then she was on her hands and knees in the frozen moss with her breath going out of her in big white clouds and steam coming up off her bare back like a horse that had been run too hard. She stayed there a minute. Her shoulders shook. Her fingers dug into the moss and found ice under it.

Then she sat back on her heels and untied the cord from her ankle.

Three hard knots, the way they'd all learned. The grocery bag, gone soft and gray from a week of river crossings. And threaded through the cord next to it, tied off in a neat tight knot she hadn't tied, the little leather pouch.

She looked at it for a while.

It was old. The leather had gone dark and soft the way a baseball glove goes after twenty summers, and the drawstring had been mended once with a different color of thread. It smelled like him, earth after rain and pine and cold stone, and under that like an old drawer in an old house: cedar and dust and lavender.

She picked at the knot with her thumbnail. It came apart at once, as if it had been waiting for her to ask.

Inside, folded in a square of soft gray cloth, was a mirror.

About the size of a playing card. A thin silver frame gone dark in the corners, and a plain silver back with nothing engraved on it, scratched all over with the fine crosshatched scratches of a thing that had lived in a lot of pockets. The glass was perfectly ordinary glass. It showed her a girl with spruce needles in her hair and a rusty brown smear of caribou blood at the corner of her mouth, and dark eyes that looked, to her own surprise, like they'd slept.

She licked her thumb and scrubbed the blood off.

*If you want me, say my name into it.*

She held it in her palm and looked at it. The name was right there. It was sitting on her tongue like a stone she'd picked up off a beach.

Her dad's name.

She wrapped the mirror back up in its cloth and put it in the pouch and pulled the drawstring tight, and got dressed. Black running shorts, the gray T-shirt over the tank top, the five-year-old running shoes that were never, ever going to be dry again. She'd brought them for the walk back into town because they were the only things she owned that she didn't mind losing. The cold came at her bare legs and gave up.

She put the pouch in the pocket of her shorts, where it sat against her thigh, and walked out of the trees.

---

Wes was standing in the window of the motel office with a mug of coffee when she came up the lake road, a big shaggy shape behind glass with a fishing show going on the TV behind him. He watched her come up the shoulder in her shorts at a quarter past six in the morning with frost on the gravel. He lifted the mug about an inch.

Leah lifted two fingers off her thigh, the way everybody up here did.

She showered in four minutes, dragged the brush through her hair twice and gave up, pulled on the jeans and a flannel and the boots and the blue toque with the white pompom, and was across the street and through the back door of the Galena at six twenty-two.

Dot was at the flat-top with six eggs going and a pan of hash browns and the radio on, CBC, a man talking in a calm voice about a mudslide on Highway 37. She didn't look around.

"You're late."

"I know."

"Twenty-two minutes. That's coming off."

"Fine."

Leah tied her apron and stood at the big sink with her hands under the hot tap while it ran cold, then hot. Behind her she heard Dot flip the eggs. Then the spatula stopped.

"You got blood on your ear, Washington."

Leah put her hand up. Behind her left ear, in the soft place under the hair. She felt the stiff dried flake of it there and scrubbed at it with wet fingers and didn't turn around.

"Hunting?" Dot said.

"Something like that."

"Get anything?"

Leah thought about lying. Then she thought about Dot at the end of the Prospector's bar with her rye, saying *huh* like she'd already filed something away.

"Caribou," she said.

The radio man went on about the mudslide. Dot slid the eggs onto two plates and put them in the window.

"No gun, I'd bet," Dot said.

Leah didn't say anything.

Dot looked at her across the kitchen for a long time. Then she reached up and turned the radio up a notch, so that the man was talking a little louder about the mudslide, and went back to her grill.

"Hm," she said.

---

She carried the mirror around for six days.

In her jeans pocket at work, where it bumped her thigh every time she leaned over the sink. On the nightstand in room six at night, face down in its gray cloth, where she could see it from the bed.

She didn't take it out. She thought about it on Wednesday, when the first real snow came down on the peaks across the lake and stayed there. She thought about it on Friday night, lying on the orange bedspread looking at the moose at three in the morning, which was the worst time to think about anything.

She didn't take it out because the second she did, she was going to have to say his name.

And then on Saturday she called her mother.

The pay phone outside the gas station had a skin of frost on the steel hood now. Leah scratched the silver off a fresh phone card and punched in the long string of numbers, the country code, the number she'd known since before she could read.

It rang once.

"Lee?"

Her mother had been sitting next to it. Leah could hear it in her voice. She could see it, eighteen hundred miles south: the kitchen chair pulled over to the wall under the beige phone, Mom in her reading glasses with the *Peninsula Daily News* open on her knee, not reading it.

"Hi, Mom."

"You said next week. It's been a week. I didn't know if — " Her mother stopped. Breathed. "Hi, baby. Hang on. Hang on, he's — he's been sitting by this phone since *Wednesday,* I can't — Seth. *Seth.* Give me a — "

There was a scuffle. Something fell over on the other end of the line, possibly a chair. Then a voice came down the phone so loud that Leah had to hold the receiver away from her ear.

"*LEAH!*"

"Hey, Seth."

"You're in *CANADA?*"

"Yeah."

"Like *real* Canada? With the Mounties and the — the — the bacon?"

"There's one Mountie," Leah said. "She's a woman. She doesn't like me."

He laughed. Her little brother's laugh, the honking, too-loud one that went up at the end like a goose. She hadn't heard it in eighteen days, and it went into her chest like something sharp, and she leaned her forehead against the cold steel of the phone box and closed her eyes.

"I thought you were *dead,*" Seth said.

"Mom told me."

"Like, I felt you go. You know when you're in the bath and you pull the plug, and the water's going down and down and then it makes that noise, that big gross sucking noise at the end? It was like that. And then there was just nothing back there." His voice wobbled, going high. "I thought you drowned in the Sound. I thought a ship hit you."

"A ship almost did hit me."

"*Leah!*"

"It missed."

"Oh my *God.*"

"Seth." She kept her eyes closed. "I'm sorry. I didn't know it'd do that. I didn't think about it. I should've."

He was quiet a second. When he spoke again his voice had come down off the ceiling.

"It's okay," he said. "Jake said it was distance. He said you were too stubborn to die."

"That's what I told Mom to tell you."

"I know. That's what Jake said. You guys said the exact same thing." He seemed to think this was very important. "Hang on." Footsteps. A door. His voice went lower, the way it did when he was about to tell her something he wasn't supposed to. "Okay. Don't tell Mom. Bella threw me into a tree."

Leah opened her eyes. "She *what?*"

"It's fine! It's totally fine. It was like ten days ago. The day she woke up." He was talking fast now, the way he did when he knew she was going to yell. "She broke my shoulder. And my collarbone. Carlisle had to set it. It healed in like a day."

"*Why did she throw you into a tree?*"

"Jake called the baby Nessie."

"Nessie," she said.

"Like the Loch Ness Monster."

"I know what Nessie's like, Seth."

"And Bella *lost* it. She went for his throat. She was so fast, Leah, you have no idea — " His voice went soft with something like awe, which was the most Seth thing she'd ever heard. "And I kind of got in the way."

"Of course you did."

"And she said sorry, like, a hundred times. She's really calm now. For a newborn. Carlisle says she's the calmest newborn he's ever seen."

"Seth."

"Yeah?"

"She broke your collarbone."

"She said *sorry,*" Seth said, as if that settled it. And for Seth, Leah thought, it probably did.

She stood there with the phone against her ear and her free hand jammed in her hoodie pocket, where her knuckles bumped the leather pouch. She thought about her brother lying in the moss behind that glass house with his shoulder caved in. She thought about where she'd been that day. Standing at Dot's sink, eating saskatoon pie.

"Jake's always at the Cullens' now," Seth said. "He's like, *glued.* It's the imprint thing. It's weird, Lee. It's different from Sam and Emily, kind of? It's — I don't know. He just wants her to be okay. He sits there and watches her sleep. He made her a little wooden wolf." Seth hesitated. "Embry's been hanging around. A lot. I think he's gonna come over, to Jake. Quil maybe too. Sam's being kind of a jerk about it."

More voices. Leah felt her shoulders come up around her ears all on their own. Quil, mooning over a two-year-old. Embry, who thought about his mother's cooking and video games and nothing else for hours at a time.

"Great," she said.

"Right? It'll be like a real pack." He didn't seem to hear her tone at all. "So when are you coming home?"

"I don't know."

"Okay," Seth said. Just like that. *Okay.* No pushing, no guilt, no *Mom's been crying,* nothing. He was the only person she'd ever known who could say *okay* like it was a whole sentence. "Is it cool up there?"

"It's cold."

"*Leah.*"

She looked out at the street. The false-front buildings, the Prospector with its half-dead neon, the lake gray beyond the café with the white peaks going up on the far side. The yellow floatplane was gone from the lodge dock with the green roof. It had been gone all week.

"Yeah," she said. "It's cool."

"Cool," said Seth, satisfied.

They talked a while longer. About Mom, who was at Charlie Swan's four nights a week now and kept coming home humming. About Seth's grades, which were bad. About the poker game he'd started with Emmett Cullen, who could stand still like a statue for a thousand years but whose face did things when you dealt him a pair of jacks.

When the recorded voice came on to tell her she had one dollar and twenty cents left, Seth said, "Okay, Mom wants the phone back, she's doing the eyebrows," and then, quickly, before he handed it over: "I'm glad you're not dead."

"Me too, goof."

"Love you."

"Gross," Leah said. And then, because he'd hung onto the line, waiting, the way he used to when he was little and wanted to be the one who hung up last: "Love you too."

---

She walked back to the motel with her hands in her pockets and her head down, and the cold coming off the lake, and it was dark when she got in.

She turned on the lamp with the burnt shade and sat down on the edge of the bed. The heater clanked. Through the wall, the guy in room seven was watching hockey with the sound up. Somebody scored. The guy in room seven said a word Leah's mother would have washed his mouth out for.

She took the pouch out of her hoodie pocket and unwrapped the mirror.

The girl in the glass looked tired. Her eyes were red around the edges, which was the cold, obviously. Her mouth was flat.

"Potter," she said to it.

Nothing happened. The girl in the glass said *Potter* back to her, silently, and went on looking tired.

"*Potter.* Hey." She tapped the glass with a fingernail, which was stupid. "Hello?"

Nothing.

She sat there feeling like an idiot. She thought about putting the mirror in the nightstand drawer with the Gideon Bible and leaving it there when she left, for the next person to find.

*Say my name into it.*

She'd known it wouldn't work. She'd known the whole time she was saying *Potter* that it wasn't going to work, the way you know a door is locked before you try the handle.

She looked at the mirror. She opened her mouth.

It took her three tries. The first time nothing came out at all. The second time it came out as a breath with no voice behind it. She could feel her own heart going. The guy in room seven was yelling at a referee. And she thought about the porch in La Push, and the camera, and *you'd better have her home by midnight,* and she said it.

"Harry Potter."

The glass clouded.

It went soft and gray all over, like breath on a window, and then cleared from the middle out, and it wasn't her face in it anymore.

It was his. Very close, and from slightly below, as if the mirror on his end was propped up against something on a desk. He had a feather quill in his hand, an actual feather, gray and white, and a smear of black ink on his cheekbone just under his left eye, and his hair was doing something that defied description. Behind him was a stone wall with a tall, narrow window in it, and through the window, far off and pink in the last of some sunset that wasn't hers, a mountain with snow on it. A different mountain.

He was looking down at something on the desk in front of him. Then his eyes came up, and focused, and went wide.

"Clearwater."

She didn't say anything.

"That was quick," he said.

"It's been six days."

He blinked. "Is it? Christ." He put the quill down. He looked, for a second, actually rattled, which she filed away. "Sorry. It's — I've been in this room since Tuesday. I think it's Tuesday. There's no clocks. Solveig took the clocks. She says they distract me."

"*Lord Potter,*" said a voice from somewhere off to the side, flat and clipped and very tired. "If that is a personal call, form B-12 is not optional, and I would like it before — "

"Solveig, it's the local."

There was a pause. A long one.

Then, from off to the side, in a different voice, a precise and almost courteous one: "Hello, Leah."

"Hi," Leah said.

"Did he give you a telephone?"

"A mirror."

Another pause. Leah heard a long breath go out through somebody's nose.

"Of course he did," said Solveig Brekke, and there was the sound of a door closing, firmly, and then it was just the two of them.

He looked back at her out of the glass. Ink on his face. In the window behind him, the pink on the mountain was going gray.

"Where are you?" she said.

"Geneva. The Confederation. There's a tower. They keep me in it until I've done my paperwork, like Rapunzel." He held up a sheet of parchment, dense with tiny handwriting, and dropped it again. "Kroll was a hundred and twelve pages. You were eleven. Mostly me telling them to sod off. Solveig made me take out the swearing." He leaned closer to the mirror, and his eyes moved over her face, and his head tipped. Just a little. "You all right? You sound — "

"I'm fine."

"Clearwater."

"I talked to my brother," she said.

He was quiet a second. "Good?"

She thought about Seth saying *okay* like it was a whole sentence. She thought about him lying in the moss with his collarbone broken, and about the glass house, and about a baby she'd never seen named after a sneeze and then a lake monster, and she put all of that back where it belonged, behind the door, because she had not called Harry Potter on a magic mirror in Geneva to talk about the bloodsuckers.

"Yeah," she said. "Good."

He looked at her a moment longer, and she had the feeling he was hearing what she hadn't said, the way he'd heard Kell's portkey before Kell grabbed it. But he let it go.

"Right," he said. "Well. You called. Which means you've a reason for calling, and you've had six days to think about it, so it's a good one." His mouth went up at one corner. "Go on, then."

Leah held the mirror up in front of her face and looked at it and thought about putting it down.

"Third date," she said.

His eyebrows went up.

"Saturday. A week from today. After my shift. On two legs." She ticked it off on her fingers, out of the mirror's view. "In chairs. At a table. Inside. And the food's dead before it gets to me."

"Not even a little bit alive?"

"*Potter.*"

"Dead," he agreed. "Completely dead. Funeral, flowers, the lot." The grin had come out all the way now, lopsided. "Seven o'clock. I'll collect you from the dock."

"Collect me how?"

"It's a surprise."

"I hate surprises."

"You'll hate this one less than most." He seemed to think about it. "Wear something you don't mind getting a bit wet."

"*Why* would I get wet?"

"And it's not a date. Yes. I know." His eyes were very green in the glass. "It's the third one."

She put the mirror face down on the orange bedspread.

She sat there with her hand flat on the silver back of it, feeling her pulse in her fingertips against the metal. The guy in room seven was cheering. Out on the lake, a loon called, long and wavering.

After a minute, through the back of the mirror, very faintly, she heard him laughing.

---

The sun was going down behind the western mountains when she walked out on the dock in a dark green wool sweater of Brynn's, which Brynn had forced on her in the café bathroom after a fight about mascara that Leah had won. The whole lake had gone the color of a peach. There was no wind. The water was so flat that the far shore sat on top of its own reflection, spruce and snow and the green roof of the lodge and the long pink sky, perfectly doubled, upside down.

Across the middle of it, leaving a long V-shaped wake that broke the reflection into a thousand pieces, a canoe was coming.

Red. Old wood and canvas, the paint faded to the color of a brick. One man in it, kneeling in the stern, coming across the lake in long strokes that swung the bow a little left on every stroke and back to the right on the next, so that the whole canoe came toward her in a slow, determined zigzag.

Leah stood at the end of the dock with her hands jammed in the pockets of her jeans and watched him come for a long time.

When he got close enough she could see he was wearing a heavy navy sweater with a rolled collar, the waxed jacket open over it, and the polished brown boots. His hair was wet. So were his sleeves, both of them, to the elbow.

He glided up to the dock, overshot it by about six feet, back-paddled, came in sideways, and thumped the gunwale against the pilings hard enough to rattle the planks.

"Evening, Clearwater," he said, looking up at her.

"You're wet."

"I've been practicing."

"In the lake?"

"Danny had a book," he said with dignity. "It had pictures."

"You fell in."

"Twice. Thursday. The second time was on purpose, I was hot." He held up a hand to her. "Get in. You go in the front. Sit on the seat, not the — no, the seat, yes, that's — "

She ignored the hand. She stood there and looked at the canoe, at him in the stern, at the paddle, at the zigzag wake still spreading out behind him across the whole bay.

"Get out," she said.

"Sorry?"

"Get out of the back. You go in the front."

He looked at her. Then he looked at the paddle in his hand. Then he looked at her again, and something in his face shifted. She saw him put it together: the coast, the reservation, the salt water, everything she'd told him and hadn't told him. Her people had been putting cedar canoes into the Pacific since before there were Englishmen.

"Ah," he said.

He moved forward on his knees, carefully, the canoe rocking. Leah stepped down into the stern in one motion without touching the dock and the canoe barely dipped. She knelt, took the paddle out of his hands, and pushed them off from the pilings.

He sat in the bow facing backward, toward her, which was wrong, and she didn't tell him.

She took them out across the lake in long, quiet J-strokes, the paddle coming out of the water at the end of each one with a little twist that kept the bow dead straight on the green roof. No zigzag. The canoe ran out over the peach-colored water with hardly a sound, just the drip off the blade and the soft chuckle of the water under the hull, and their own reflection going along under them.

Harry watched her paddle for a while without saying anything.

"Who taught you?" he said finally.

"My dad."

He nodded. He didn't say *I'm sorry,* and he didn't make the face. He watched the paddle go in and out.

"He'd have been appalled by me," he said.

"He'd have tipped you in," Leah said, "on purpose," and that got a laugh out of him, the real one, loud, out across the water so that a pair of goldeneyes took off from the shallows by the far shore and went whistling away low over the lake.

---

The lodge was bigger up close than it looked from town. Two stories of logs gone silver-gray with age, a stone chimney at either end with smoke coming out of both, a long deck on posts out over the water, and the green metal roof.

A skinny man was waiting at the end of the lodge dock in a white chef's jacket, with his arms wrapped around himself against the cold. Maybe thirty, Vietnamese, with a thin, worried face and the posture of a man who hadn't slept properly since early September. He watched Leah bring the canoe in without a bump, and his eyebrows went up.

"Oh, thank God," he said. "Somebody who can drive it."

"Leah, Danny. Danny, Leah." Harry climbed out after her, considerably less smoothly. "Danny's my chef. Well. He's Hargreaves's chef. I'm not entirely sure whose chef he is anymore. Possibly the lodge's."

"I'm *my* chef," Danny said. "I'm a free man. Technically." He looked at Leah with naked, desperate curiosity. "Are you the one who eats like him?"

Leah looked at Harry.

"I told him you had a healthy appetite," Harry said.

"He said, and I quote, 'Dinner for two, and she eats like me,'" Danny said. "I have been feeding this man for three weeks. I've ordered more food than my restaurant in Gastown does in a month. The floatplane *pilot* hates me." He turned and started up the dock toward the lodge, still talking, his arms still wrapped around himself. "Hargreaves said Sunday. Then Hargreaves said one more week. Then Hargreaves said the end of the month, and he said a number on the phone, and I paid off my mother's mortgage. *My mother's.* I don't even have a mortgage anymore. I'm here out of — at this point I genuinely don't know. Curiosity. Stockholm syndrome." He held the door open for them. "Risotto's in twenty minutes. Don't touch anything in the kitchen."

---

Inside, the lodge was one long room with a fire going in a river-rock fireplace big enough to stand up in. A moose head over the mantel, a lot better than the moose in room six, with a mild, thoughtful expression. Big windows along the whole lake side, and through them the bay going from peach to violet, and the lights of Turnagain coming on one by one.

At the far end, by the windows, a long plank table was set for two. Somebody had put candles on it. A ridiculous number of candles, in silver candlesticks and glass jars and one brass candelabra with seven branches that looked like it had come out of a church.

Leah stopped and looked at the candles.

"That was Danny," Harry said quickly.

From the kitchen, Danny's voice: "*That was not Danny.*"

"It was a bit Danny."

"I put out *two.* He put out the rest. He said, and I quote, 'More. It's a date. Women like candles.' And I said, Lord Potter, with respect, she's going to think this is a séance."

Leah looked at Harry. Harry looked at the candelabra.

"I've not done this much," he said.

"Done what?"

"This. Dates. Tables. Candles." He made a small, vague gesture at all of it. "I can track a dark wizard across four continents by the smell of his aftershave. I've no idea how many candles you're meant to have."

"Two," Leah said.

He looked at the table. Every candle went out except two, all at once, without a sound, and thin gray threads of smoke went up from forty wicks and hung in the air under the beams.

"Two," he agreed.

---

The risotto was wild mushrooms, chanterelles and something darker Danny called hedgehogs, which he'd picked himself in the woods behind the lodge and wanted her to know about. After that there was a whole baked salmon, and a bison short rib that fell off the bone, and bread still hot from the oven, and a wedge of cheese from Quebec that smelled like feet and tasted like heaven.

Leah ate all of it. She'd been washing dishes since six in the morning, and she ate like it.

Across the table, Harry ate more. Faster. He ate like he'd been living for a week on whatever they slid under the door of the tower in Geneva, which, he told her, was more or less what had happened.

Danny came out of the kitchen with each course and then stood in the doorway with a dish towel over his shoulder and watched them eat it, with an expression of religious horror.

"Where does it *go?*" he said, at one point, to nobody.

"Mind your business, Danny."

"I'm just asking. As a professional. As a scientist of food."

After the cheese, Harry looked at the empty air beside his chair, and a box came down out of it and landed on the table with a thump that made the candles jump.

It was the size of a briefcase. Dark brown cardboard, gold ribbon, the name of a shop in curly script and under it, BRUXELLES. Inside were four layers of little chocolates in fluted paper cups, every one different. Swirls, shells, domes with gold leaf on them, squares with nuts pressed into the top.

"Brussels," Harry said. "As promised."

Leah picked out one of the domes with the gold leaf on top and bit it in half. The shell cracked. Inside was something soft and sweet and nutty that coated her whole mouth, hazelnut and caramel and something darker underneath it, burnt sugar maybe, salt. She closed her eyes without meaning to. When she opened them he was watching her with his chin on his hand.

"It's fine," she said.

"Fine."

"It's okay."

He grinned at her across the two candles.

"What happened to the shop?" she said.

"Sold it back to them. One euro." He ate a square with almonds on top in one bite. "Hargreaves made the kettle noise again. The family sent a card, and another box. It's possible I'll get one every Christmas for the rest of my life. I'm at peace with it."

Leah ate the other half of the dome. She didn't close her eyes this time. It was a near thing.

"Right," Harry said. He wiped his fingers on his napkin, and sat back, and his face went a little more careful than it had been. "Before I forget. I've a question."

"Okay."

"Somebody you know." He held up a hand, as if he was being sworn in. "I'm asking first. As agreed."

Leah went still. She thought about Brynn. About Dot. About Seth, eighteen hundred miles away. "Who?"

"Wes."

She blinked. "Wes?"

"His ice machine," Harry said gravely. "It's been broken since July. I asked. The compressor's gone. He says the part's coming from Edmonton, and it's been coming from Edmonton since July, and it'll be coming from Edmonton when the sun burns out. It'd take me about four seconds."

Leah stared at him.

"You said ask," he said.

"It's an *ice machine.*"

"It's an ice machine belonging to somebody you know. Those were the terms."

"I meant like — like *scholarships.* Like buying people *lives.* I didn't mean — "

"You didn't specify," Harry said. "I'm a bureaucrat now, apparently. I'm very literal. Solveig's rubbing off on me."

She opened her mouth. She shut it. Then she put her hand over her face and laughed into it, helplessly, the stupid hiccuping huff that kept coming out of her lately whether she wanted it to or not, while Danny watched from the kitchen doorway with his dish towel over his shoulder and Harry sat across the table with his chin on his hand, waiting.

"Fix the ice machine," she said, through her fingers.

"Thank you."

"*Don't* fix anything else."

"Dot's walk-in freezer's making a noise — "

"*Potter.*"

"Ice machine," he said. "Only the ice machine. Understood."

---

She asked him about Brynn while Danny was clearing the plates, because she'd been holding onto it for two weeks and it had started to itch.

"She dreamed about your deer."

He didn't say *stag.* He didn't correct her at all. He went quiet and looked at the candles.

"Solveig put it in her report," he said. "Underlined it. Twice. Then she put the report on my desk with that page on top and stood there while I read it."

"How'd it get in her head?"

"The Patronus." He turned his glass of water around on the table. "When I sent it off. I'd only just finished taking the memory out, and she was standing right there, half under, with everything loose. It went past her on its way out, and a bit of it caught. I should've checked. I always check. I didn't." He looked up at her. "Sloppy. She was right."

Leah didn't say anything.

"I can fix it," he said. "It'd be a very small thing. She'd never know."

Leah thought about Brynn on her first day back after the mine, talking about the deer with her eyes gone soft. *It was really pretty. Isn't that dumb?*

"Leave it," she said.

He looked at her.

"It's a nice dream," Leah said. "She thinks it's pretty. Everything else you took. Leave her the deer."

He was quiet for a moment. "All right," he said.

---

After dinner, they went out on the deck.

It was full dark now. The cold had come down hard over the lake, and the stars were out, thick and close, the Milky Way going over from one end of the sky to the other like a smear of chalk. No aurora tonight. The lights of Turnagain lay strung along the far shore, small and yellow, and their reflections lay under them on the black water, perfectly still.

Leah leaned on the deck rail with her arms folded on top of it. The chocolate was still in her mouth. Harry leaned beside her, a foot away, and she could feel the heat coming off him through Brynn's sweater.

"Third date," she said.

"So it is."

"You said the clothes thing was third-date material."

"Did I?"

"On the plateau. I said ours explode. You said, 'What, yours don't?' — no. *I* said that. And you did the thing with your tail. And you said third date." She turned her head and looked at him. "It's the third date."

He looked back at her with that tilt of the head. Then he straightened up off the rail.

"Watch the jumper," he said.

"The *what?*"

He plucked at the front of the navy sweater with two fingers. "Jumper. Sweater. Whatever you lot call it. Watch it. And the boots. And the jacket. Right?"

She looked. Navy wool, the rolled collar. The olive waxed jacket over it, open, with a brass zip and a corduroy collar. Dark trousers. The brown boots, laced and tied in double knots. In the left breast pocket of the jacket, the corner of a purple wrapper poking out.

"Okay," she said.

He stepped back from the rail, into the middle of the deck.

She'd braced for something. The fire. The bones going. She'd watched Seth do it a hundred times, Jacob, Sam, Paul, all of them, the shudder through the whole body and then the tearing, the clothes going to rags. It was a violent thing, phasing. It came out of rage.

There was no sound.

There was a man in a navy jumper standing on the deck, and then there was a fold in the air, the way heat shimmers over a highway in summer, one blink long. And then the deck was full of wolf.

The whole deck. He took up most of it. His black shoulders were up level with her head, and his great black head was higher, and the green eyes were looking down at her from somewhere over the deck rail. His tail swept once, slow, and brushed the far railing. The deck planks didn't so much as creak under him. The log wall of the lodge behind him was a pale blur in the starlight with a black wolf-shaped hole in it.

There were no rags on the deck. There was nothing on the deck at all.

"Well?" he said, in the deep clean voice with the rumble under it.

"Where are your *clothes?*"

"Watch."

The fold again. Shorter this time. And he was standing there on two legs.

Navy jumper. Rolled collar. Waxed jacket, brass zip, corduroy collar. The trousers. The brown boots, laced and tied in double knots. The purple wrapper still poking out of the breast pocket at the exact same angle.

His hair was the same mess it had been before. There wasn't a hair of it different.

Leah stood there with her mouth open.

"Do you know," she said, slowly, "how many pairs of shorts I've lost?"

"Eleven," he guessed.

"*Thirty-four.*"

"That's a lot of shorts."

"I *counted.* My mom used to buy them at the Costco in Port Angeles in packs of six, and the guy at the register asked her once if she was running a *gym.*" She jabbed a finger at his chest. "I have been *caught* running home naked by old Mrs. Penn from the marina, who is eighty years old, and who stood on her porch and told me I had a nice behind, and has told *everybody.* And you just — *where do they go?*"

"They come with me."

"*How?*"

His mouth went up at one corner.

"Fourth date," said Harry.

"You said *third.*"

"For the *clothes.* You asked about the clothes. I've answered about the clothes. They come with me." He spread his hands. "How it works is a separate question. Separate question, separate date."

"That is *not* how dates work."

"It's how mine work. I told you."

Leah looked at him standing there in his ordinary navy jumper. Then she turned around, went back inside, came back out with the briefcase-sized box of Brussels chocolate under her arm, took out a praline, and threw it at his head.

It stopped two inches from his nose and hung there in the air.

He looked at it. Then he leaned forward and ate it out of the air.

"Thank you," he said, with his mouth full.

She threw another one. He caught that one too, with his teeth. She threw three at once, fast and hard, from different angles, and two of them stopped in the air and the third bounced off his forehead and went over the rail into the lake with a small *plip.*

They both looked at the place where it had gone in.

"That one I let through," he said.

"You missed it."

He looked at her. And then, for the first time since she'd met him, he looked like he didn't have an answer ready. His ears would have gone back, if he'd had the right kind of ears. He opened his mouth and shut it.

Leah put the box under her arm and walked back inside, past the candles, past Danny in the kitchen doorway, who had been watching the whole thing with his dish towel clutched in both hands.

"He missed one," she told Danny on her way by.

"I saw," Danny whispered. He sounded almost reverent.

She paddled them back.

He sat in the bow facing her again, the wrong way, with his elbows on his knees, while the canoe ran out across the black water under the stars. Neither of them said much. Somewhere off to the north a loon called and another answered.

When she brought them in against the café dock — no bump — he climbed out first and held the gunwale steady for her, and she let him. She stepped up onto the planks. She stood there with the chocolate box under her arm.

"Dot's doing Thanksgiving," she said.

He looked up from the canoe.

"Canadian Thanksgiving. Monday after next. The ninth. At the café, for anybody who's got nowhere else to go. Wes, the highway guys, the old men. Brynn and her mom." She looked out at the lake. "Dot said, 'Bring your fancy friend. He can carve.'"

"Dot said that," he said.

"Dot said that."

"Not you."

Dot did Thanksgiving the way she did everything else: early, loud, and without asking anybody's opinion.

By two in the afternoon on the ninth of October, the Galena had a CLOSED — PRIVATE sign on the door and every table in the place shoved together down the middle of the room, covered in three different tablecloths and a bedsheet. Two turkeys in the oven. A ham. Wes in a clean flannel shirt with his ponytail combed. The two highway crew guys, who couldn't get home to Terrace because of the mudslide on 37. The three old men in feed caps, three games into the season, still arguing about whether Luongo had been worth losing Bertuzzi over. Ray, behind the counter, which was where he seemed to feel safest. Brynn and her mother, Lorraine, who'd brought a Jell-O salad with marshmallows suspended in it like bugs in amber, and who kept hugging Leah for walking Brynn home that night.

At five past two the bell over the door jingled, and Harry came in with his arms full, and behind him, carrying more, came Danny.

Dot came out of the kitchen with her spatula. She looked at Harry. She looked at Danny, in his white jacket, holding a covered roasting pan and a cooler bag. Her eyes narrowed.

"That's the skinny one."

"Danny Tran," Harry said. "He's a chef. He insisted on coming."

"I did *not* insist," Danny said. "He said, 'Danny, would you like Thanksgiving with real people,' and I said I haven't seen a real person in four weeks except the pilot." He looked at Dot the way one dog looks at another across a fence. "I brought a brussels sprouts thing. With bacon. And a squash thing. I won't touch anything."

Dot looked at the roasting pan for a long, long time.

"What's in the squash thing?"

"Brown butter. Sage. A little maple."

"Hm," said Dot. She jerked her head at the kitchen. "Sprouts go in the low oven. Don't touch my gravy."

Danny went into the kitchen like a man being let into church.

Dot turned to Harry. She reached under her apron and came out with a knife. A long carving knife with a wooden handle worn pale and smooth, the blade gone thin and slightly curved from forty years on a whetstone. She held it out, handle first.

"You carve," she said.

Harry took it. He looked at the blade, turned it in the light from the window. Leah, setting out forks at the end of the long table, watched something change in how he was holding it. His grip settled.

"This is a good knife," he said.

"It was my husband's. Don't wreck it."

When the first turkey came out, Dot set it on a board at the head of the table and stood back with her arms folded, and everybody else stood back with her. The old men stopped arguing about Luongo. Ray leaned on the counter. Brynn's mother had her hands clasped under her chin.

Harry carved it in about ninety seconds.

He didn't hurry. The knife just went where it was going. The legs came off at the joint with a little twist of the blade. The wings. Then the breast meat, in long even slices that lay down on the board one on top of the other like cards being dealt. He turned the bird and did the other side, and left no meat on the bone.

When he was done he wiped the blade on a towel and handed the knife back to Dot, handle first.

Dot took it. She looked at the board. She looked at the turkey, stripped clean down to the frame.

"Huh," Dot said.

Behind the counter, Ray said, "Where'd you learn to do that?"

"Practice," said Harry.

"Practice on what?"

Harry thought about it. "Things that didn't hold still," he said, and went to wash his hands.

They said grace. Dot said it, short, with her head bowed and her eyes open, looking at the turkey. Danny, at the far end, said amen a beat late, a little startled, like he hadn't said it in a long time.

Then they ate. Leah sat across from Harry, between Brynn and an old man named Walter who'd been a hard-rock miner in Cassiar for thirty years and told her about the asbestos in great and cheerful detail.

"Ice machine started working," he said, to the table in general.

"Tuesday. Came out in the morning, there it was, humming away, full of ice. Part never came." Wes picked his fork back up. "Been coming from Edmonton since July. Never came. "Very," Harry said.

"Must've been the cold."

"Must've."

Wes went on chewing. After a while he nodded, once, to himself, and said nothing else about it for the rest of the meal.

Brynn cornered him by the coffee urn after the pie.

Leah watched it from the end of the table, where she was stacking plates. She couldn't hear all of it over Walter. She saw Brynn come up to him, bright and pink-cheeked, with a slice of Dot's saskatoon pie on a paper plate. She saw Harry turn around. She saw Brynn look up at him.

And stop.

It wasn't long. A second. Brynn's face went still, her head a little tilted, her eyes moving over his face as if she was trying to read something written there in small print.

"Have we met?" Brynn said.

Harry looked down at her. His face didn't change at all.

"He's okay."

Dot found her in the kitchen at six, when the sky outside was already going dark and Danny was helping Lorraine Halvorsen wrap leftovers at the counter and the old men were playing crib at the end of the long table.

Dot didn't say anything for a while. She stood next to Leah at the big sink with a dish towel and dried what Leah washed. Plates. The gravy boat. Her husband's carving knife, which she dried very carefully and put away in a drawer by itself.

"You're leaving," Dot said.

Leah's hands stopped in the water.

She hadn't told anybody. She hadn't decided until about four o'clock, sitting at the long table with Walter talking about asbestos and Wes picking his teeth and the rain coming down the steamed-over windows. It had come over her slowly, like the cold off the lake.

"How do you know?"

"You've been looking at everything all day like you were taking pictures." Dot dried a gravy ladle. "I've been doing this forty-five years. People look like that before they go."

Leah washed a plate.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Morning."

"Back to Washington."

"Yeah."

"Your mother'll be glad."

"Yeah."

"The fancy fella going with you?"

"No."

"Hm," said Dot. She hung the towel over her shoulder. She reached into the pocket of her apron and came out with an envelope, folded in half, and put it down on the drainboard next to the sink. Leah could see the edges of bills through the paper. More than she was owed. A lot more.

"Dot — "

"Don't." Dot looked at her over her glasses. "You showed up. You didn't steal. You didn't bring trouble." She seemed to think about it. "Much." She picked up a ziplock bag off the counter. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper, was a wedge of saskatoon pie about the size of a brick. "That'll keep three days if you don't sit on it."

Leah looked at the pie. Her throat had gone tight.

"Job's here," Dot said, "if you come back. I'll be dead before I find another one who doesn't break the plates."

"Thanks, Dot."

"Don't thank me." Dot turned back to the stove. Over her shoulder, gruff: "And don't go off without saying goodbye to that girl. She'll cry for a week and I'll have to listen to it."

He was waiting on the dock.

She didn't know how he'd known. She came out the back door with the envelope in her hoodie pocket and the pie in her hand, and he was standing at the end of the dock with his back to her, looking out at the lake.

It had stopped raining. There was a last thin line of light under the cloud in the west, green-gold, going down. The lake was black and still.

She walked out to the end and stood next to him.

"Dot thinks you're a hitman," she said.

"Dot's very perceptive."

"Wes knows you fixed the ice machine."

"Wes is a man of great discretion. I've decided I like Wes." He looked sideways at her, at the ziplock bag. "Is that pie?"

"It's mine."

"I didn't say anything."

"You looked at it."

They stood there. Out on the water, the first flake came down.

One big flake went past her face, as big as a moth, turning slowly. Then another. Then a dozen, coming down out of the dark and landing on the black water without a sound. Then more, until the air between the dock and the far shore was full of them, falling straight down, slow and soft, and the lights of the lodge across the bay went blurry behind them.

Leah had seen snow before. A few times a winter it came down on the rez and lay on the beach for an hour before the rain washed it off. She'd never seen it like this. The whole sky coming down, quiet, all at once.

"I'm going home," she said.

He didn't move. He didn't say anything right away. The snow was settling on his hair and on the shoulders of his jacket, and melting there almost as fast as it landed, because of the heat in him.

"When?"

"Tomorrow."

"Right." He nodded slowly. He looked out at the snow coming down. "Good."

She turned her head. "*Good?*"

"You came up here to find out what you wanted." He was still looking at the lake. "Sounds like you've found something out."

Leah tried to put it into words, for herself, because he'd hear it anyway the second she did, if he was listening, and she'd stopped minding that about halfway through the third date. She thought about the pumpkin field, the ringing quiet in her head. About Dot saying *Washington* across the kitchen, and Brynn forcing a sweater on her in a café bathroom, and Wes lifting his coffee mug an inch off the window.

"I came up here so nobody would know me," she said. "And Dot knows me. Brynn knows me. Wes knows me, sort of, in a Wes way. And I don't mind it." She put her hands in her pockets. "So I figure I can go home and have people know me there. And it's my head. It's still my head. It's going to be full of guys again the second I get close." She made a face. "But I know it's mine now. I didn't, before."

He was quiet a while.

"That's a good thing to find out," he said.

"Yeah."

"How are you getting there?"

"How do you think?"

"I could have you there in four seconds."

"On my own four feet," Leah said.

He breathed out through his nose, something close to a laugh. "Course."

"I'm serious."

"I know you are." He turned and looked at her properly, finally, with the snow coming down between them. "Where's home, Clearwater?"

She almost told him.

She had the name of it right there, the way she'd had his name on her tongue in the deadfall. The beach, the bluff, the rollers coming in all night. The little house on the edge of the rez with the porch light off. And past it, fifteen miles east through the trees, a glass house that smelled like bleach and burnt sugar, with a baby in it she'd never seen.

She didn't want that here. Not on this dock, with the snow.

"The coast," she said.

"Long coast."

"That's the idea."

He nodded, as if that was fair. As if he'd expected it. He didn't push.

"You've got the mirror," he said.

"I've got the mirror."

"Use it. Or don't." His head tipped. "But I'd like it if you did."

She looked at him. Snow in his hair, melting. Snow on his eyelashes. The green eyes, and the ridiculous jacket, and the mouth that looked like it smirked a lot and was not smirking right now.

She took her hands out of her pockets, and took hold of the front of his jacket with both fists, and pulled.

He came down. He was a lot taller than her and he came down anyway, without any resistance at all, and she kissed him.

It was short. It was hard. She'd meant it to be both. His mouth was hot, hotter than a man's mouth had any right to be, as hot as hers, and he went very still for about a second, the way he'd gone still when she stole his fork. Then one of his hands came up, slowly, and touched the side of her face, his thumb along her cheekbone, and that was all he did. He let her decide how long.

She decided. She let go of his jacket and stepped back.

The snow was coming down between them. He was looking at her with an expression she'd never seen on his face before, and didn't have a name for, and wasn't going to look for one.

"That's not a date," Leah said.

His voice came out a little rough. "What is it?"

"Thanksgiving."

She turned around and walked back up the dock, with the pie in her hand, through the snow.

Behind her, he said, "Clearwater."

She stopped. She didn't turn around.

"See ya later, alligator," said Harry Potter.

The snow came down. The lake took every flake and gave nothing back.

"In a while, crocodile," Leah said, and kept walking.

She didn't hear him go. She didn't hear anything. When she got to the corner of the café and finally let herself look back, the dock was empty, and the snow was already filling in the place at the end where he'd been standing, a dark rectangle going slowly white.

She heard Seth on the fifth day, coming down the Skagit.

She'd crossed the border at the cutline that morning, in the rain, past the little aluminum obelisk with UNITED STATES on one face and CANADA on the other. The gray jay wasn't there. She'd stopped on the American side and looked back up the long shaved strip of hillside going over the ridge into the cloud, and thought about Dot's kitchen, and the lodge with the green roof, and the snow coming down on the black lake. Then she'd turned around and gone down the long gray finger of Ross Lake with the wind behind her.

Below Diablo, where the river came out of the dams that bright fake turquoise and went roaring down its gorge, she felt it.

A nightlight in another room. Small and far off and flickering, like a bulb about to go. Then steadier.

*— Leah?*

She stopped so hard on the trail above the river that she slid.

*LEAH! Leah, is that — oh my God, is that you? Jake! JAKE! It's — *

It came in like a wave coming up a beach. Seth, first, so loud and so bright she flinched from it, his joy pouring into her head like hot water into a cold bath. And behind him, fainter but coming fast, Jacob. Tired, the way Jacob was always tired now, but under the tired something that went loose and glad when it found her.

*Took you long enough,* Jacob thought.

*Hey, Jake.*

And then, behind Jacob, two more.

*Hey, Leah,* thought Embry, cautious, like someone sticking his head around a door.

*Leah! Welcome back!* thought Quil, and right behind the words, with no filter at all, came Claire. Claire on the beach in a pink raincoat, Claire with sand on her face, Claire holding up a shell and saying *Quil, look,* and the great warm helpless flood of it, the imprint, the whole world tipped on its side toward one small girl in a raincoat.

Leah stood in the rain above the Skagit and shut her eyes.

*Oh, for God's sake,* she thought. *When did you two show up?*

*Like last week,* Seth thought. *I told you on the phone! I told you Embry was gonna! And then Quil came too, because Sam was being — *

*Seth. Volume.*

*Sorry.* He wasn't. She could feel how much he wasn't.

It was all there. All of it. The crowd in her skull. Embry wondering if there was any of his mom's fry bread left. Quil thinking about Claire. Seth bouncing off the walls. Jacob somewhere behind them all, lying in his fur on the floor of the glass house, a small warm weight asleep against his ribs, and his whole self bent around it like a hand around a candle flame.

She'd known it was coming. She'd known for five days, the whole way down. She'd told Harry on the dock. *It's going to be full of guys again the second I get close.*

She'd thought she was ready.

His name went through her head before she could catch it. Plain, in words, the way she'd been saying it to herself for a month. *Harry.*

She grabbed it. Too late.

Seth went quiet. All of a sudden, all the bounce going out of him. *Lee,* he thought, very softly. *You thinking about Dad?*

She stood there with the rain running off her muzzle.

Behind the name, she'd caught the rest of it before it could come up. She'd learned how in Sam's pack, a year and a half of holding things down so hard she'd cracked a molar. The dock. The snow. A black wolf in a cottonwood tree. A hand along her cheekbone. It was all there, right behind the door, pressing on it. She held it shut.

And she let Seth have what he'd seen. Her dad. The porch. The boat with the rust streak down the bow.

*Yeah,* she thought. *I was thinking about Dad.*

*Me too, sometimes,* Seth thought. *When I'm running. It's okay.*

It wasn't okay. She'd just lied to her little brother inside her own head, which she'd never once managed in her life. She felt sick with it.

She ran.

Her mother was on the porch.

It was dark when Leah came up through the trees behind the woodpile, the next night, and phased, and dressed with her hands shaking, and walked around the side of the house. The porch light was on. Then she stood up and came down the steps, and Leah walked into her arms.

Her mother was shorter than her. She'd been shorter than her since Leah was fourteen. She held on anyway like she was the taller one, with her face in Leah's shoulder, and she didn't say anything for a long time. She smelled like coffee and lotion and the inside of Charlie Swan's police cruiser.

"You smell like a campfire," Sue said into her shoulder, eventually.

"I brought you pie."

Her mother pulled back and looked at her. At her face. Her hair, which was clean and brushed. The ziplock bag in Leah's hand, with the brick of saskatoon pie in it, squashed flat in its waxed paper, that had been tied to her ankle for eighteen hundred miles and was a good deal older than three days.

"You look different," Sue said.

"I'm the same."

"Mm-hm," said her mother.

On the Saturday after American Thanksgiving, at a quarter to four in the afternoon, Leah was sitting at the island in the Cullens' kitchen eating cold turkey out of a Tupperware container with her fingers, and trying very hard to think about nothing at all.

It was raining. It had been raining since Halloween. The big glass wall at the back of the house was gray with it, the river beyond it high and brown and loud, the cedars dripping. Somewhere in the living room, the TV the size of a garage door had a football game on with the sound low. Somewhere upstairs, someone was playing a piano. Somewhere closer, Emmett was laughing.

The kitchen smelled like a hospital someone had soaked in perfume and set on fire. It also smelled like turkey and sage and cranberries, because Esme Cullen had cooked an entire Thanksgiving dinner on Thursday for a houseful of wolves and Charlie Swan and Leah's mother, none of which any of the Cullens could eat. It had been a strange afternoon. Leah had sat at the far end of the long table across from her mother and Charlie, with the bloodsucker stink in her nose and the food in her mouth, and Charlie had carved the turkey, badly, sawing at it with a knife that wasn't sharp enough, and the whole time she'd been thinking about another turkey, on another table, taken apart in ninety seconds by a man who'd handed the knife back handle first.

She'd kept it to pictures, and held her face very still.

Across the table, Edward Cullen had looked up at her once, sharply, and then away.

So today she was thinking about nothing. She was eating turkey. That was all.

"You know they have plates," said Rosalie Hale from the doorway.

Leah didn't look up. "I know."

"And forks."

"I know."

Rosalie came in the way they all moved, like water poured out of a pitcher, and stood on the other side of the island. She was so beautiful it was boring. The gold hair, the gold eyes, a cream sweater that probably cost more than the Clearwaters' car. She looked at Leah eating turkey with her fingers the way you'd look at a raccoon in your kitchen.

"Esme's going to want that container back," Rosalie said.

"She can have it when I'm done."

"Washed."

"Sure."

"Not licked."

Leah looked up at her for the first time, and very slowly, without breaking eye contact, licked a smear of cranberry sauce off her thumb.

Rosalie's lip lifted off her teeth.

"Hey, Blondie." Jacob came through the doorway behind her with his hair wet from the rain and a toddler on his hip. "Why'd the blonde stare at the orange juice carton for an hour?"

"Don't."

"Because it said *concentrate.*"

"That's not even *new,*" said Rosalie. "You told that one on *Tuesday,*" and took the toddler out of his arms in one smooth motion, like she'd been waiting for him to get close enough. Jacob let her. They'd been doing this for two months, Seth said, the jokes and the snarling and the passing-off. Rosalie had once served Jacob his dinner in a dog bowl with FIDO written on the side, and he'd flipped it into her hair, and she still talked about it like it was the greatest insult of her life, and kept the bowl.

The toddler looked at Leah over Rosalie's shoulder.

Renesmee. Leah couldn't say the name without hearing a sneeze, so she didn't. Eleven weeks in the world, and she looked about two. A mass of bronze curls, the exact color of Edward Cullen's hair. Round pink cheeks. Brown eyes. Bella's eyes, everybody said. Charlie Swan's eyes, Leah thought, because she'd seen them in Charlie's face across the Thanksgiving table and it had made the back of her neck prickle. A heartbeat, fast and light, like a bird's. She smelled like the vampires and like something alive, both at once, and it made Leah's nose itch every time.

She was holding a cup too. A sippy cup, purple, with a lid. Leah knew what was in it. It wasn't juice.

"Leah," said the toddler, clearly, in a voice like a bell. "You came back from Canada."

"Six weeks ago, kid."

"I know." Renesmee considered her with those brown eyes. "You went away on Thursday. At dinner. In your head. You do that."

Leah stopped chewing.

"She means you looked like you were somewhere else," Jacob said quickly. He came around the island and stole a piece of turkey out of Leah's Tupperware. "She talks like that. She's been reading. Carlisle gave her *Peter Pan.*"

"Peter Pan goes away in his head," Renesmee said, satisfied, and drank from her purple cup.

Leah watched her drink it. Six weeks, and it still did something to her stomach. A little girl with pink cheeks and Charlie Swan's eyes, sucking human blood through a purple plastic lid. Expired units from the hospital, Seth had said. *They'd only throw it out otherwise,* he'd said, like that made it better.

*Same old,* Leah thought.

---

It had all been the same old, since she got home. The patrols, mostly, with Seth and Quil and Embry in her head, and Jacob when he could tear himself away from the kid, which was never. Sam's pack on the other side of the line, minding its own business again, held there by the one law no wolf would ever break: you didn't hurt someone a brother had imprinted on. Carlisle with his doctor's voice. Esme cooking for wolves. Alice walking around in a cloud of headache, because she couldn't see the wolves or the kid, and it drove her crazy. Jasper standing in corners. Emmett losing at poker. Rosalie being Rosalie.

And Bella. Bella Swan, who'd been dying on a white couch the night Leah ran, and had a heartbeat, and a dad who sat up waiting for the phone. Bella Cullen now, with red eyes she covered with brown contacts when Charlie came over. Standing so still sometimes it made Leah's skin crawl. Very nice to Leah, in a careful, polite, slightly awed way, as if Leah was something she'd read about in a book. It didn't help at all.

And Edward. Edward heard everything. He'd been in Leah's head every day since she got back, whether he wanted to be or not, and she'd learned to come up the driveway thinking about the most boring things she could find. Her mother's grocery list. The Seahawks' offensive line. The Britney Spears song.

Mostly the Britney Spears song.

She had it going now, low, like a radio left on in another room. She'd discovered that if she ran the chorus on a loop under whatever else she was thinking, Edward Cullen would go out of his way to stay on the other side of the house, and it was the single most satisfying thing she'd learned in six weeks.

She heard the piano upstairs stop, mid-phrase.

A second later Edward was in the kitchen doorway.

He looked at her. He had a thin, tired face for a guy who never slept, and he looked at her now with an expression of real suffering.

"Leah," he said. "Please."

*Oops,* Leah thought clearly, in words, *I did it again.*

He closed his eyes.

"I'm aware there's something you don't want me to see," Edward said quietly. "I'm not trying to see it. I've been making a great effort for six weeks not to see it." He opened his eyes. "But I'd consider it a personal kindness if you'd find a different song."

"No," Leah said.

Rosalie, over by the fridge with the kid on her hip, made a small noise that could, possibly, have been a laugh.

Edward looked at Leah a moment longer. Something moved in his face, faintly curious, the way it had moved on Thanksgiving when she'd thought about the turkey. Then he let it go and turned to Renesmee, and his whole face changed, went soft and open and young.

"Your mother's on her way up from the cottage," he said to her. "She wants to know if you'd like to go and see the river."

"In the *rain?*"

"She says it's very loud today. Very exciting."

Renesmee thought about it. Then she put her small hand flat against Rosalie's cheek, and Leah saw Rosalie's eyes go unfocused for a moment, the way everybody's did when the kid showed them something. Pictures. Thoughts. Whatever the kid wanted you to see, pushed right into your head with a touch.

Rosalie laughed, softly. "Yes," she said. "You can wear the yellow boots."

Leah ate her turkey. She ran the chorus. Under it, very quietly, in the place behind the door where nobody could hear, she felt the small hard weight of the mirror in the front pocket of her jeans, where she'd carried it every day for six weeks and never once taken it out.

She hadn't used it. Every time she'd thought about it, she'd thought about Seth asking *you thinking about Dad?* and Edward Cullen across a table, and four guys in her head every night on patrol. There was nowhere in the world she could say that name out loud without somebody hearing. And he hadn't called her, either. She'd half expected it: the glass going gray at the bottom of her pocket, his voice coming out of her jeans at the Cullens' kitchen island. It never had. He'd left it with her.

*You've got the mirror. Use it. Or don't.*

She put a piece of turkey in her mouth and didn't think about it.

Out in the living room, the football game went to commercial. Upstairs, the piano didn't start again.

And across the kitchen, at the window over the sink, Alice Cullen went suddenly, completely still.

---

Edward heard it go out in Alice's head before she said a word.

He'd been watching her afternoon with her, idly, the way he watched most of Alice's visions: in the background, like weather. Bella coming up from the cottage in the rain. Bella down at the riverbank, her arms curved around an empty space the shape of a child, because Alice had never once been able to see Renesmee. Emmett at the dining room table at five fifteen, losing forty dollars to an empty chair. Esme at five forty, holding out a hand for a Tupperware container nobody was giving her, and making a face at it.

And then, at three fifty-two by the clock on the microwave, all of it went black.

Gone, all at once. The river, the empty arms, the poker game, all of it blinked out together, like a power failure in a city seen from a plane, and where Alice's next two hours had been there was a gray, roaring nothing.

Alice turned from the window over the sink. "Edward."

"I saw."

"Everything after four o'clock. All of it." Her small face was tight with something halfway between a headache and fear. "Like when they all come at once. Like when Jacob walks in."

"Jacob's here."

"Jacob was here at three fifty-one, and it was fine at three fifty-one." Alice put her hand on the edge of the sink. "It's not Jacob."

Edward was already listening.

He went out through the house first, mind by mind. Rosalie by the refrigerator, Renesmee on her hip, thinking about yellow boots. Leah at the island, the chorus of that relentless song going round in her like a carousel, and under it a sudden sharp alertness, because she'd seen Alice go still. Jasper, already rising from the couch. Emmett at the dining table, wondering whether Seth was bluffing. Seth, who was. Jacob in the downstairs bathroom, washing turkey grease off his hands. Esme upstairs, folding laundry. Carlisle in his study, reading about telomeres.

Then out of the house. The trees, the river, a heron on the far bank. Then the drive, three miles of it, winding down through the cedars to the highway, where a log truck went by on 101 in a long hiss of spray. Nearer, nothing. Nearer still —

Footsteps.

On the wet gravel of the drive, a little under a mile below the house. Unhurried. A long stride, a heavy one. And a heartbeat. Slow and strong, a big heart in a big chest, too slow for the stride, as if whoever was walking didn't find the climb any effort at all.

And no mind.

Edward reached for it the way he reached for every mind that came within range, without thinking, the way a man's eyes go to movement. And there was nothing there. No voice. No pictures. Nothing at all, from a living, walking, heart-beating man, as if the space where a mind ought to be had been bricked over.

He had known exactly one silence like that in nearly ninety years of listening.

"Someone's on the drive," he said, pitched low, for the house. "Carlisle."

Upstairs, the laundry stopped. The study door opened. Jasper was at the front windows before Edward finished the word, standing to one side of the glass where he couldn't be seen from outside, with his whole body gone loose and quiet in the way that, a long time ago, had meant men were going to die. Emmett put down his cards. *Finally,* thought Emmett, and grinned.

*Get her upstairs,* thought Rosalie, and didn't move, because Renesmee had turned her head and was looking toward the front of the house with her brown eyes very wide.

At the island, Leah had stopped chewing. The song had stopped too. In its place her mind had gone hard and flat and watchful, the way the wolves' minds went in the second before they ran. *What,* she thought. *What is it. What's coming.*

Edward didn't answer her.

Down at the cottage, Bella had heard him. He felt her start toward the house through the trees, very fast. He let her come.

The footsteps came on. Half a mile. A quarter. The wind was off the river, and it brought the man's scent up through the cedars ahead of him: wet wool and leather, rain on stone, turned earth and pine. Under that, something sharp and dry that Edward couldn't name, like a match a second after it's struck. And chocolate.

Under all of it, faint, something that made the muscles along his back pull tight. Something kin to the wolves' smell, without the wet dog in it. A cousin. Something that lived in the same house.

The man came around the last bend of the drive, under the dripping cedars, and walked out into the open.

Tall, broad through the shoulders. A long charcoal-gray coat to below his knees, collar up, a row of silver clasps down the front, a flash of dark red lining when he moved. Black gloves. Boots of some patterned leather that looked, absurdly, like scales. Black hair, every which way. He was eating a bar of chocolate in a purple wrapper.

It was raining hard. It had been raining hard since Halloween.

It wasn't landing on him.

The rain slid off the air a hand's width above the man's hair and shoulders, as if off a pane of glass, and ran away down an invisible curve to the gravel at his feet. He was walking up the middle of the drive in a downpour, and he was dry.

He came to the foot of the porch steps and stopped, and finished his chocolate, and folded the wrapper in half and put it in his pocket. Then he looked up at the house.

Edward watched his eyes. Green. They went to the front window, where Jasper was standing out of sight behind the frame, and stopped there exactly as long as it took to see him. Then the upstairs window, where Esme had come to the glass. Then the narrow window beside the door, and Edward behind it.

The green eyes held his for a second, flat and steady. Then they moved on.

He'd counted them. Every one, through the glass, in order.

Carlisle came down the stairs and opened the front door. He stood in the doorway in his shirtsleeves, and his mind was calm and attentive and courteous, the way it had been for three hundred years.

"Good afternoon," Carlisle said.

"Afternoon." The man's voice was deep and level. English. *London,* Carlisle thought, with a small jolt of something like homesickness, *or near it. Educated. Recent.* "Doctor Cullen. I'm Harry Potter. International Confederation of Wizards." He looked past Carlisle into the house. "I'm here about the baby."

There was a short silence.

In the dining room, Emmett said, out loud, "*Wizards?*" and started to laugh.

And from the kitchen behind Edward, Leah Clearwater's mind went up like a flare.

The carousel shattered. Everything she'd been holding behind it for six weeks came through at once, so loud and so fast that Edward put a hand on the doorframe. A dock in falling snow. A wolf — a *wolf,* black, enormous, lying on a branch in a gold forest with its paws hanging down. A man in a dark sweater on a deck under the stars, and then not a man at all, and then a man again. A hand on her cheekbone. A voice, the voice on the porch, saying *see ya later, alligator* —

And over all of it, one word, in her own voice, the word she'd never once let herself think in this house:

*HARRY.*

Edward turned his head and looked toward the kitchen.

---

Leah had heard the voice before she knew she'd heard it.

It came through the house from the front door, deep and level and English, and every hair on her arms went up, and the turkey went down the wrong way, and she was on her feet before her brain had caught up. The stool went over behind her.

She went through the living room. Past Jasper at the window. Past Emmett, half out of his chair at the dining table with a fan of cards in his hand. Past Edward Cullen by the door, who was looking at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before and had no time for.

She stopped in the doorway beside Carlisle.

He was standing at the foot of the porch steps in the rain, and the rain wasn't touching him.

The long gray coat. The silver clasps, the red lining, the black gloves, the ridiculous boots. The collar up. The hair. He looked exactly the way he'd looked on the shelf above Turnagain the night he'd come out of nowhere with a crack like a rifle shot, half a chocolate bar in his hand.

He saw her.

She watched it happen. His head came around, and his eyes found her in the doorway, and stopped. And his head tipped to one side. The whole head, thirty degrees, the way a dog's goes when it hears its name from a room it didn't know you were in.

"Well," said Harry Potter. "*Shit.*"

Nobody on the porch moved.

"Clearwater," he said. "Didn't know you were going to be here."

"Didn't know *you* were going to be here," said Leah.

"Clearly." His eyes went past her, up, taking in the porch, the white walls, the glass, the whole enormous house, and came back. "Of all the houses."

Somewhere behind her, Emmett said, very loudly, "Wait. You *know* Van Helsing?"

Leah closed her eyes.

She'd thought it. That was the worst part. She'd stood locked in a stone circle on a mountain in September and looked at that coat and thought *Van Helsing,* and now she was going to have to live with having had the same thought as Emmett Cullen for the rest of her life.

When she opened them again, Harry's nose had wrinkled. His nostrils flared, once, twice. He was looking at the open door and the air coming out of it.

"Christ," he said. "You weren't exaggerating."

"I told you."

"Bleach," he said. "And burnt sugar. And the — "

"Dead thing in the sun."

"Dead thing in the sun," he agreed, and she'd have bet anything he was remembering the same thing she was. A cold rock on a plateau with the sky going green. *They sparkle.* Him laughing so hard his tail thumped the stone.

Behind her, in the kitchen doorway, Rosalie said, in a voice like a blade coming out of a drawer, "Excuse me?"

---

Carlisle held out his hand.

Harry looked at it. He looked at it for a while, a long, plain look, the gloved hands staying where they were at his sides.

"I'd rather not, thanks," he said.

Carlisle lowered his hand. Nothing changed in his face. "I understand."

"Do you?"

"I think so." Carlisle's voice was mild. "You'd like to come in, I imagine. You'll want to see her."

"I'll stay out here." Harry glanced at the doorway again, at the air. "For the moment."

"Then perhaps you'd tell us," Carlisle said, "what the International Confederation of Wizards wants with my granddaughter."

The rain came down on the drive, and slid off the air over Harry's head, and ran away down the invisible curve of it.

"Clause Seventy-Three," Harry said. "International Statute of Wizarding Secrecy. Every magical government's responsible for the concealment and control of the magical beasts, beings and spirits inside its own borders. If one of them comes to the attention of the Muggles, the government answers for it." His eyes went to Carlisle. "Muggles. Ordinary people. Non-magical."

"I'm familiar with the concept," said Carlisle.

"Your lot are beings. Officially. There's a file on you in New York that goes back to nineteen thirty-six. Not a thick one. The Americans mostly leave you alone, because you mostly leave yourselves alone. Then, eleven weeks ago, something started setting off an instrument in a basement in Manhattan."

"It's meant to pick up accidental magic," he said. "Kids who've got it and don't know it yet, so they can be found and sent to school. It twitches every time one of them makes a teacup fly at a birthday party. In September it started going off in Forks, Washington, where there are no wizarding families at all, and it wasn't twitching like a teacup." He looked past Carlisle, past Leah, into the hall. "It goes off every time she puts her hand on somebody's face. A dozen times a day, for eleven weeks. They traced it on Monday. Geneva sent me."

There was nothing from the house. Leah could hear the rain, and the river, and her own heart going.

"And what is it you've been sent to do?" said Carlisle.

"Assess."

"Assess what?"

"Whether she can be kept hidden." Harry said it plainly. "Whether she understands what she is, and who she can't do it in front of. Whether this house is a breach that's being managed, or one that's waiting to happen." He paused. "If she can be kept hidden, I write that down and go home."

"And if she can't?"

Harry didn't answer.

The silence went on long enough that Leah heard Emmett set his cards down on the dining table, very carefully, face up.

"Then I write that down too," said Harry.

---

Jacob came through the house like a truck through a fence.

He came out of the downstairs bathroom with his hands still wet and his face already going dark, because he'd heard every word, and he shoved past Edward and Leah and stopped at the top of the porch steps. He was shaking. The heat was coming off him in waves.

"Who the hell are you," Jacob said, "to *write anything down* about her?"

Harry looked up at him.

He looked at him for a long time. At the shaking. At the way Jacob had put himself between the steps and the door without thinking about it. Harry's nostrils flared, once. His head tilted.

"You're the Alpha," he said.

Jacob went rigid. "How do you know that?"

Harry's eyes flicked to Leah. Just for a second.

Then back to Jacob. "The one who let her go," he said. "Right?"

Jacob opened his mouth. Shut it. He looked at Leah, and something sharp and suspicious went across his face. Leah didn't look back at him. She was looking at Harry.

"That kid," she said, "is Jacob's imprint."

She watched Harry hear it.

He'd been half turned toward Jacob. He went still, the way a person does when a thing they've heard lands on top of a thing they already know, and the two fit together, and the fit is bad. She knew what he was hearing. She'd said it to him herself, on a rock on a plateau with the sky going green. *You look at somebody, and that's it. That's your person. Forever. Gravity moves.*

His eyes went past Jacob. Past Leah. Into the hall behind them, where Rosalie had come out of the kitchen and stopped, with Renesmee on her hip and the purple cup in her small hand.

"On the baby," Harry said.

"Yeah."

He looked at Jacob again. Something in his face had changed, and Leah couldn't read it.

"Christ," he said, quietly.

"So you know what that means," Leah said. "I told you what that means. That's the law. That's the one law. You hurt someone a wolf's imprinted on, and every wolf there is comes for you." She held his eyes. "Both packs. Every one of us." She didn't look away. "Me included."

The rain came down. Harry looked at her for a long moment, standing in the doorway of a house she hated, with her hands in fists at her sides.

"Noted," he said.

"I mean it."

"I know you do," Harry said. "That's why I noted it."

---

Bella came in through the back.

Leah heard the glass door slide and then she was simply there, beside Edward in the hall. Wet dark hair, and the red eyes, bright as brake lights in the gray afternoon. She was breathing, which she didn't need to do, which meant she was afraid. She took Renesmee out of Rosalie's arms without asking and held her against her chest, and looked out the door at the man in the rain.

Harry looked back at her.

He looked at her the way he'd looked at Carlisle's hand. A long, plain look. At her eyes, mostly.

"You're the mother," he said.

"Yes."

"Bella." He seemed to check it against something. "Swan, before. Cullen now." A beat. "You were human in August."

"Yes."

"Married him in August. Human in August. And now you've got red eyes and an eleven-week-old who looks two." He said it without any weight on it, the way he'd said *forty-three counts* on the mountain. "Somebody in New York wrote the wedding down and never followed it up. He's having a very bad week."

"I chose this," Bella said. Her voice was very calm, the newborn calm that Seth had been so impressed by, and under the calm something was clenched tight. "All of it. I chose it."

"I know you did," said Harry. "That's the bit I'm having trouble with."

Bella's chin came up.

"You had a heartbeat," Harry said. "You had a dad who sat up waiting for the phone. You had toast for breakfast and sun on your face and a life that had about seventy more years in it." He tipped his head at her eyes. "And you swapped all of it for a diet of blood. On purpose. You looked at the menu and you picked that."

"Animal blood," said Carlisle quietly.

"Blood." Harry didn't look away from Bella. "I've read about you lot on two planes and a boat. Three hundred years, some of you. Medical degrees lying about this house like other families have dishwashers. Your son's got two, the file says, and never used either. And nobody in this house ever stopped and thought, *maybe toast.*"

"We *can't* — "

"I know you can't. I read that bit too." Harry finally looked at Carlisle. "I'm telling you how it looks from out here. You drain things, and that's dinner. Everything I've ever hunted that lived on blood was something I was sent to stop."

Emmett had come up behind Leah's shoulder, big as a wall. "You got a problem with our diet, Van Helsing?"

"Yes," said Harry.

Emmett blinked.

Then Harry's eyes went to the purple sippy cup in Renesmee's small hand. To the lid. To the dark red that had dried around the spout.

His face went flat. All of it. The same way it had gone flat on the mountain, when he'd held up Kell's ring to the blue light.

"Is that what I think it is."

Nobody answered him.

"It's donated," Carlisle said finally. "Expired units from the hospital blood bank. They'd be destroyed otherwise. She prefers it. We're trying to bring her onto animal blood, and onto solid food, but — "

"She's *two,*" said Harry.

"She's eleven weeks," said Rosalie, from the hall, ice in it.

"She's a child," said Harry, "drinking human blood out of a sippy cup, in a town of three thousand Muggles, and the man who signs it out of the hospital fridge is her grandfather's doctor." He didn't raise his voice at all. "And you lot look at me like I'm the one who's got it wrong."

Leah felt it then.

It came over the porch from behind her like syrup poured over her head. Thick, slow, sweet calm, loosening her fists and making her think, very reasonably, that everybody should sit down and have a nice talk. She'd felt it before. She hated it more than almost anything in the world.

Harry's head turned toward the house. All of it at once, every window, as if he hadn't bothered to work out which one.

"Whoever's doing that," he said, "stop it."

The calm stopped. Like a tap turned off.

Leah breathed out, hard, through her nose. Behind the front window, Jasper had gone still, and Alice had a hand on his arm. Neither of them said anything.

"Thank you," said Harry.

---

"Then there's your father," he said to Bella.

Leah saw it go into her. She saw it go in like a nail.

"Chief Charles Swan," said Harry. "Police chief. Muggle. That instrument in New York has gone off in his kitchen eleven times." He held Bella's eyes. "He's a breach, Mrs. Cullen. He's the biggest one you've got. I haven't decided what I'm doing about him."

"You leave my father *out* of this."

"He's in it. You put him in it."

"If you go anywhere *near* his mind — "

She moved.

Leah had fought them in the clearing last spring and watched Bella every day for six weeks, and she'd never seen anything move like a newborn moves when it means it. One second Bella was in the hall with Renesmee against her chest. The next, Renesmee was in Edward's arms, and Bella was off the porch without touching the steps, coming through the rain at Harry Potter's throat with her lips pulled back off her teeth.

He didn't move.

She stopped.

A foot from him. In the air. Both feet off the gravel, her hands out, her hair flung forward around her face and the rain going straight through it. Her whole body strained. Leah heard something in Bella's arms creak, like stone under a load. She didn't move one inch.

The cold went down Leah's spine like water.

She knew exactly what that was. She'd stood in a cracked stone circle on a mountain with her boot half an inch into the needles, and it had come up through her feet and clamped her ribs and her throat and held her like a fly in amber while the fire broke against it again and again. *It holds a body in the shape it's wearing.* She'd hated Kell for that more than for anything else he'd done.

Behind her the porch boards cracked like gunshots as Edward came off them, Renesmee clutched to his chest —

"*Don't,*" said Harry.

He didn't look at Edward. He didn't do anything that Leah could see. Edward stopped anyway, at the top of the steps, all on his own.

Harry looked at Bella, hanging in the air a foot from his face.

"I haven't decided about your father," he said. Very quietly. "When I do, you'll hear it from me. To your face. Before anybody touches anything." He paused. "That's what you get. That's the most anybody gets from me, and it's a lot more than you're used to from my lot, so I'd take it."

He let her go.

She dropped. She landed on her feet on the wet gravel, light as a cat, and stood there, shaking, and then Edward was beside her, one arm around her and the kid in the other, pulling her back. Bella let him. Her red eyes didn't leave Harry's face.

Leah realized her own hands were shaking. She put them in her pockets. The right one closed on the mirror.

He'd let Bella go. He'd let her go the second he'd said his piece.

Kell hadn't let her go.

---

"It was me," said Jacob.

Everybody looked at him. He'd come down a step. The tremor was still in his arms.

"Charlie," Jacob said. "That was me. I phased in his backyard. I showed him." His jaw was set. "He was going to lose her. He was going to lose both of them. So I showed him. If you want somebody for that, you want me."

Harry looked at Jacob for a while. Then, very briefly, at Leah.

"Didn't hear that," Harry said.

Jacob's eyebrows came down. "What?"

"Your tribe's not in my file." Harry's voice stayed level. "It isn't going in my file. Nobody in Geneva's asking about your people, because I've asked them not to, and they'd rather not have the argument with me." He turned back to the house. "I'm here about the child."

Leah stood on the porch with her hand around the mirror in her pocket and Jacob staring at her, and didn't look at any of them.

She'd told Solveig Brekke on a dock in Turnagain that she wasn't putting Seth in anybody's book. And Solveig had said, *he said you'd say that,* and closed her notebook.

Standing in the rain in front of eight vampires, with every reason in the world to write the whole peninsula down, he'd kept it.

---

It was the kid who broke it.

She'd been watching all of it from Edward's arms. Leah had seen her, out of the corner of her eye, the whole time: brown eyes huge, curls damp, the purple cup forgotten in her fist. Watching her mother hang in the air. Watching the man in the coat.

Now she leaned out of Edward's arms toward him, and reached.

"No," said Edward and Bella at the same time.

Renesmee put her hand on her father's face.

Edward's eyes went somewhere else. Leah watched them go. When they came back, he looked down at his daughter for a long time, and then he looked, of all things, at Leah.

"She wants to show him something," Edward said. His voice was strange.

"Absolutely not," said Rosalie, from the door.

"Rose — "

"*Absolutely* not, Edward, he just — "

"She's asking," Edward said. "She's asking very nicely."

Bella was looking at her daughter. After a moment, her jaw unlocked. She held out her arms, and Edward put the kid into them. She walked forward, human-slow, until she was standing in front of Harry Potter again, close enough to touch. She didn't look at him. She looked at her daughter's face.

Harry didn't move. He looked at the kid.

"Go on, then," he said.

Renesmee put out her small hand and laid it flat against his cheek.

His eyes went out of focus.

It was a second, maybe two. The rain slid off the air over his head, and now over Bella's and Renesmee's too, as if the space he'd made had stretched to take them in. Then his eyes came back. And he laughed.

Short. Surprised. Out through his nose, with a rumble under it, the low huff that Leah had heard come out of her brother a thousand times, and out of herself in a pumpkin field, and out of a man on a dock in Turnagain with his lip pulled back off one long canine.

A chuff.

On the porch behind her, every single one of the Cullens went still.

"Are you?" said Renesmee, in her bell voice. She didn't take her hand away.

"Something like that, sweetheart," said Harry.

Leah's stomach dropped through the porch.

"What did she show you," she said.

He looked up at her over the kid's head. "Ask her."

Renesmee turned around on Bella's hip and looked straight at Leah with her grandfather's brown eyes.

"I showed him your wolf," she said. "When you ran in the river. The gray one." She considered. "And I asked him if he had one. Because he smells like Jacob a little bit. Under the rain."

Every head on the porch turned and looked at Leah.

Leah looked at the sky.

"Clever girl," said Harry, to Renesmee, and took his face very gently away from her hand.

---

He stayed another quarter of an hour, at the bottom of the steps, and he told them how it was going to be.

He'd be in the area a week. Maybe two. He'd come every day, at a time he'd choose, and he'd want to see the child each time, with her mother present. Nobody took her into town. Nobody took her off the property except to hunt, and he'd want to know when. Nobody did anything about Chief Swan until he'd decided. Nobody left the county.

"And if anybody runs," he said, and his eyes went to the front window, to Alice, and stayed there, "I'll know. I'll find you, and it'll go much worse."

"You don't need to threaten us," said Carlisle.

"I'm not threatening you, Doctor Cullen. I'm telling you what happens."

"And at the end of it?"

"At the end of it I'll tell you what I've decided," said Harry. "To your faces. All of you. The same way I told you this." He looked once more at Bella, still standing in the gravel with the kid on her hip, and something went across his face that, on anybody else, Leah would have called pity. "I'd use the time. Teach her who she can touch. Teach her what she can't do in front of people. Teach her fast. That's the best thing you can do for her."

He turned to go. Then he stopped, and looked up at the doorway.

"Clearwater," he said. "Walk me out?"

Leah felt Jacob look at her. She felt Edward look at her. She felt Rosalie and Emmett and the rest of them, and the kid.

She went down the steps.

---

They walked down the drive in the rain.

The cedars closed over them after the first bend, and the house was gone behind them. She was soaked in about ten seconds.

He was dry.

He glanced at her. And the rain stopped landing on her.

The drops coming down out of the cedars bent away a hand's width above her head and fell to either side of her, and the drumming of it on her shoulders was just gone.

"Don't," Leah said.

He looked at her a second longer. Then the rain came back down on her, all at once, cold and heavy, and ran down the back of her neck.

He grinned.

"You didn't mention a baby," he said.

"You didn't mention you're a cop."

"I'm not a cop."

"You're a cop with a wand."

"I'm a — "

"Hit wizard," said Leah. "I know."

They walked. Their feet crunched on the wet gravel.

"When'd you know?" she said. "That it was here?"

"I wondered." He had his hands in the pockets of the long coat. "On the plane. Coast of Washington. Cold ones. Then I thought, she hates them. She told me what they smell like with her lip off her teeth. She'll be nowhere near the place."

"I'm not near the place. I'm in their kitchen. Eating their turkey."

"So I saw."

She walked a while without saying anything.

"The kid," she said.

"I know."

"If you — "

"Clearwater." He stopped walking. He turned and looked down at her in the rain. "I'm going to look at her for a week. I'm going to do my job. I'm going to write down what I see, and I'm going to tell them what it means." His face was plain. "I'm not going to hurt a little girl who asked me nicely if I had a wolf."

Leah looked up at him.

"And Charlie?"

"Haven't decided."

She didn't say anything. She didn't know if she trusted that. Bella hanging a foot off the gravel with her teeth bared. Kell's binding coming up through the soles of her own feet. And then he'd let Bella go, and kept her people out of the book.

She'd have to think about it. By herself. She was getting good at that.

"Where are you staying?" she said.

"Hargreaves bought a house."

"Oh, God."

"In Forks."

"In *Forks?*"

"It's got a garage," Harry said, with some dignity. "Danny's coming down on Tuesday."

Leah stopped in the middle of the drive and laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her the way it always came out of her lately, the stupid wheezing huff, bent over with her hands on her knees in the rain, and he stood there in his dry coat and watched her do it with his head on one side.

When she straightened up, she wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

"You owe me the how," she said.

"I'm on duty."

"You were on duty in Turnagain."

He thought about it. "Fair," he said.

"Fourth date."

"Is it a date?"

"There's a form," Leah said.

His mouth went up at the corner, slow, until the grin came all the way out. "There's always a form."

---

She had patrol at five. She told him that at the bottom of the drive, where it met the highway, with the log trucks going by on 101 in long hisses of spray.

She went into the cedars to strip. When she came out from behind the biggest one, he'd turned around and was standing with his back to her, looking up the highway, with his hands in his pockets. She hadn't asked him to.

She tied the bundle to her ankle. She tied the pouch in beside it, three hard knots. And she let the fire take her.

They hit her all at once.

*LEAH.* Seth, loudest, from the north side of the loop. *LEAH. Leah, was that — I was at the table, I heard the whole — was that HARRY? Like HARRY Harry? The — oh my God. Oh my GOD. I thought you were thinking about DAD.*

*Seth — *

And the door was gone. Six weeks of holding it shut, gone in the second it took her to change, because she'd been so busy watching his face on the porch she'd forgotten to hold anything at all. It went through all of them. The canoe. The candles. The black wolf in the gold tree. The plateau. The snow on the lake. Her fists in the front of his jacket.

*You KISSED him?* Seth's mind had gone up an octave.

*Dude,* thought Quil, from somewhere around La Push. *Is that your boyfriend?*

*He's hot, though,* thought Embry, judiciously. *I mean. Objectively.*

*Leah,* thought Jacob, very tired, from the trees behind the glass house, where he'd phased the second the two of them were out of sight. *Who the hell is that guy?*

*He is NOT my boyfriend,* Leah thought, at all of them, as hard as she could.

Harry had turned around.

He was standing at the edge of the highway in his long gray coat, looking up at her, with the rain sliding off the air over his head. His head tipped.

"May I?" he said.

She thought about it. She thought about four guys in her head, and Edward Cullen three miles up the hill, and a wizard on the shoulder of Highway 101.

It couldn't get any more crowded.

She dipped her head. Once.

*Quil,* she thought, *he is not my boyfriend.*

"Not yet," said Harry.

There was a silence in her head so total she could hear the river.

*He can HEAR you?* Seth thought, in a kind of strangled shriek.

*Seth,* Leah thought, *shut up.*

"Only her," Harry said, to the air. "Only the words. Hello, Seth."

Four wolves lost their minds at once. It was like standing inside a bell.

Harry stood on the shoulder of the road with his hands in his pockets and listened to her think *shut up shut up everybody shut UP,* and the grin came up one side of his face, and the gleam came into his eyes like light at the bottom of a well, his lip lifting off that one long canine.

"See ya later, alligator," he said.

The whole pack was listening. Seth. Quil. Embry. Jacob, in the trees behind the glass house. All of them, in her head, waiting.

She thought about not answering.

*In a while,* Leah thought, *crocodile.*

Seth made a noise in her head like a teakettle.

Harry chuffed. That low short huff through the nose with the rumble under it, the sound her brother made. Then he turned and walked off down the shoulder of the highway into the rain, and around the bend, and she didn't hear him go. There was no crack. There was no sound at all. He was there, and then the road was empty, and a log truck went by in a long hiss of spray.

Leah stood in the ferns at the bottom of the Cullens' drive with four guys yelling in her head.

She'd run eighteen hundred miles to be alone in there. It had never been this crowded in her life.

Somewhere under her ribs, her wolf rolled over onto its back with all four legs in the air.

*Shut up,* she told it.

It didn't.
