# Chapter Four: Bad Moon Rising

The highway south of Turnagain was a gray ribbon of packed snow between two black walls of spruce, and for the first mile Leah ran right down the middle of it, because she could.

There was nothing on it. No trucks, no headlights, no plows. The sun wasn't up yet. The sky over the mountains to the east had gone the color of a bruise healing, yellow at the edges, and the cold was so hard and still that her breath hung behind her in the air like a trail of smoke she was leaving for somebody to follow.

Somebody was.

She didn't hear him. She'd stopped expecting to. She caught the edge of him in the corner of her eye instead, a piece of the dark moving where the dark shouldn't move, pacing her along the shoulder ten yards to her left. His paws came down on the crusted snow and the snow didn't break. She'd watched for it at the sign, and on the hill, and on the long straight stretch past the gravel pit. It never once broke.

*Show-off,* she thought.

"I heard that," said Harry, from the shoulder.

*Good.*

At the bottom of the first long hill the road bent west to follow a river. She didn't. She went straight on into the trees, south by the feel of it, and after a second the dark shape on her left peeled off the shoulder and came after her, and the road went away behind them, and so did the last of the smell of the town. Woodsmoke. Fry grease. Dot's bacon. The lake.

She didn't look back. She'd done enough looking back in the last year and a half to last her.

"Eighteen hundred miles," Harry said, after a while, from somewhere among the spruce. His voice came low and easy through the trees with that engine-rumble under it. "Give or take. Just so I've got it straight. We're running the entire way."

*You said you'd run.*

"I did say that." A pause. "I'd just like it on the record that there's a perfectly good method of getting from here to Washington State that takes four seconds and leaves you feeling slightly sick."

*No popping.*

"No popping," he agreed, without any sign of regret at all, and she heard him laugh, that short chuff through the nose, and for the next six days he didn't mention it again.

---

On the sixth night, a little after one in the morning, she crossed the Skagit flats going the other way.

The pumpkins were gone. The field was turned dirt now, black and wet and stubbled, with puddles lying in the furrows holding pieces of the sky. The hand-painted sign at the corner of the road still said U-PICK OPENS OCT 1. Nobody had bothered to take it down. Out to the east, Mount Baker stood up white against the stars, the same as it had on the morning she'd lain on her back between two rows of pumpkins and laughed until she couldn't breathe.

She slowed down in the middle of the field. She didn't mean to. Her legs did it.

Harry came up on her right and stopped when she stopped. He was steaming. They both were. They'd come down out of the North Cascades that afternoon, over the snow on Ross Lake and down past the dams with the turquoise water gone gray under cloud, and through the dark along the river, and her legs were a long way past tired and into something else, a heavy humming numbness that she knew from the trip north. Six days. Thirteen hours a day, some days more. They'd slept in their fur in the snow on a ridge above the Fraser, and in a burnt forest in the Cariboo with the fireweed gone to white fluff around them, and once, on the fourth night, in the lee of a boulder on a pass so high and so cold that her breath had frozen on her whiskers. He'd lain down on the windward side of her, a wall of black between her and the weather, and she'd let him, and neither of them had said anything about it in the morning.

He'd eaten like a woodchipper the whole way. She'd watched him take a full-grown bull moose down in a swamp near Williams Lake in about four seconds and then look at her with the antlers still twitching and say, "You'll want the liver, I expect. I'll take the rest," and mean it.

Now he stood in the dead pumpkin field with his ears forward and his head tipped, watching her.

"This is the place," he said.

*How do you know?*

"You keep going quiet whenever you think about it. And then you sort of — " he made a small motion with his head — "go *ha.* Very faint. In words. Every time." The tip of his tail moved. "You said *pumpkin* about forty times between here and Canada."

She didn't answer. She was listening.

It wasn't loud. She almost missed it. Way down at the bottom of her own head, under her breathing and the wind in the corn stubble and the far-off grind of a truck on the interstate, there was something. A thread. A faint glow, like a nightlight left on in another room of a big house.

She went very still.

*Seth?*

Nothing came back. Just the thread. Just that faint sense of somebody, a long way off, the way you can tell there's somebody else in a house before you hear them move.

Harry's head had come up. "What is it?"

*The pack,* Leah thought. *It's coming back. I can feel them. Just barely.*

He was quiet for a second. Then he said, carefully, "Do you want to stop?"

She thought about it. She'd thought about it for six days, every mile, lying awake in her fur on the cold ground with him breathing on the other side of her. She'd thought about the pumpkin field and the ringing silence in her own skull and how she'd laughed. And she'd thought about Seth, who'd felt her go out like a light and thought she'd drowned.

*No,* she thought. *Keep going.*

---

Crossing Whidbey the thread got stronger. By Deception Pass it was a murmur. By the time they stood on the bluff above the old fort with the black water of Admiralty Inlet chopping below them and the lighthouse at Point Wilson sweeping its beam across the far shore, it had words in it again, faint and grainy, like voices at the bottom of a stairwell.

*— think that's her? Jake. Jake, is that — *

*— can't tell. It's really — *

*— it's HER, it's totally her, I can feel her, LEAH? LEAH, CAN YOU — *

She flinched. Even faint, Seth was loud.

Beside her, Harry lifted his head. "Something's happened."

*It's my brother,* she thought. *He's yelling.*

"Course he is." He looked down at the water, at the four miles of tide ripping through in the dark. "In?"

*In.*

They went down the bluff and into the inlet together.

The cold hit like a car, the same as before. She went under and came up snorting and pointed her head west and swam. And this time, when the tide grabbed her and started dragging her north toward the Strait, something else grabbed the tide.

She felt it go. The current that had nearly drowned her two months ago went slack around her, the way a rope goes slack when somebody on the other end lets go. Ten yards to her right a huge black head was cutting through the chop, ears flat, green eyes half shut against the spray.

*Are you doing that?*

"Doing what?" said Harry, with his mouth full of Puget Sound.

*The water.*

"Bit," he admitted. "I don't like tides. Took a bad one in the North Sea once. Keep swimming."

She kept swimming. Halfway across, with the lights of Port Townsend getting bigger ahead of her and the voices in her head getting clearer with every stroke, she felt it all the way, the click of something settling back into place, and the house in her skull was suddenly full of people again.

*— LEAH. LEAH. OH MY GOD. LEAH, IS THAT YOU — *

*Yes,* she thought. *Stop yelling.*

*YOU'RE BACK. YOU'RE — where are you? Why are you wet? Are you in the WATER? Leah, are you swimming the inlet at two in the — *

*Leah.* Jacob, lower and slower, from somewhere that smelled, through his nose, like cedar and bleach and burnt sugar. He was at the Cullens'. Of course he was. *Hey.*

*Hey, Jake.*

*You're coming home?*

*For Thanksgiving.*

It was so strange. It was so strange to have them in there again, Seth all bright frantic edges and Jacob heavy and calm and tired, the two of them crowding up against the inside of her skull like people coming in out of the rain. She'd forgotten what it was like. Two months, and she'd forgotten what it was like to have somebody else's feelings pressing on hers like a hand on a bruise. Seth's relief, so big it made her eyes sting. Jacob's — something. Something quieter. Glad, and wary, and underneath that a steady golden hum she'd felt in Sam for a year and a half and recognized instantly: a little girl with bronze curls, asleep on a white couch with her hand in his.

She let it go by. She'd thought about this, too, for six days. You couldn't stop it coming in. You could decide what to do with it when it got there.

*Lee,* Seth said. Slower now. Wondering. *What's that?*

She'd been waiting for it.

She turned her head in the water.

And through her eyes, the way she knew they would, they saw it: the huge black head beside her in the chop, the ears flat, the long muzzle cutting the water, the green eyes, half shut, catching the sweep of the lighthouse. Bigger than her head. A lot bigger. Swimming like he'd done it every day of his life.

The house in her skull went dead silent.

*Leah,* said Jacob, very slowly. *What the hell is next to you.*

She didn't answer right away. She let them look. She let them see the size of him, the black of him, the way the water didn't seem to slow him down. She let them feel what it was like to be swimming four miles of freezing inlet at two in the morning next to something like that, and not be afraid of it.

Then she gave them the line. Just the way he'd said it to her, on the ice.

*That's Harry,* Leah thought. *He's a wizard. He's also a wolf. He's bigger than you. We're dating.*

And then she didn't say another word.

---

For about ten seconds there was nothing in the link at all.

Then Seth started making a noise, a long, rising, wordless sound, half howl and half the noise a teakettle makes, and it went on and on, and underneath it she could feel him up on all four feet somewhere in the trees near the Cullens' with every hair on his body standing straight up.

*A WIZARD? A — what do you mean a WIZARD — Leah — LEAH — what do you mean WE'RE — *

*He's — * Jacob's thoughts had gone jagged. *That's not — what IS that? That's not one of us, Leah, he doesn't smell like — I can't smell him, I can't smell through you in the water, what IS he — how big is he, he looks — Leah, how big is he — *

She didn't answer.

*LEAH.*

She kept swimming.

Beside her, Harry was watching her sidelong with one green eye. "Well?" he said. "How's it going?"

*Seth's making a noise like a kettle,* she thought. *And Jake wants to know how big you are.*

"Did you tell him?"

*No.*

"Good girl," said Harry, and she heard him start to laugh, a low rumble that went right through the water and came up through her own ribs, and somewhere in the woods outside Forks her little brother screamed *WHAT DOES HE MEAN GOOD GIRL* so loud inside her skull that she swallowed half the inlet.

They came up out of the surf under the bluff at Point Wilson twenty minutes later. She stood on the gravel shaking herself, steam pouring off her, Seth still going in her head like a car alarm, Jacob gone ominously quiet. Harry shook too, a great rolling shake that started at his ears and ended at the tip of his tail, and not a drop of it hit her.

"Hundred miles," he said. "Give or take. You want to stop?"

*No.*

"Didn't think so."

They ran the last of it in the dark. Down the length of the peninsula on the old route, Discovery Bay and the dairy pastures and the long black slide of Lake Crescent on their right, the cows lurching to their feet in a panicked lowing wave as two wolves went past where one had gone before. Seth stayed in her head the whole way, asking. Jacob stayed in her head the whole way, not asking, which was worse. She didn't explain anything to either of them.

She'd thought it would feel bad, letting them back in. She'd braced for it for six days.

It felt like coming down a long hill with the brakes off.

At a quarter to five in the morning they came over the last ridge above the Quillayute, and she smelled the sea. Her sea. Cold and gray and full of kelp and salt and diesel from the marina, the cedar smoke from forty chimneys, the wet sand of First Beach, and under all of it, faint, so faint, coffee and fish chowder and her mother's lotion.

*Mom's up,* Seth said, in her head, very quietly. *I told her. She's been up since you hit the water.*

Leah stood on the ridge in the dark with the rain starting, fine and cold, the way it always started here, and couldn't move for a second.

"Leah," Harry said, beside her.

She looked at him.

"Go on," he said. "I'll be right behind you."


---

Sue Clearwater had been sitting at her kitchen table since a quarter past two in the morning, and in that time she'd made two pots of fish chowder, a double batch of fry bread dough, a pot of coffee, another pot of coffee, and up her mind three separate times about what she was going to say to her daughter, and then unmade it.

Seth had come through the back door at ten past two in nothing but a pair of cut-off sweats, soaked to the knees and shaking all over, and stood dripping on her linoleum and said, all in one breath, "Mom, Leah's coming home, she's in the water by Port Townsend, she's swimming the inlet, she'll be here by morning, and there's a guy, and he's a *wizard,* and he's a *wolf,* and he's *bigger than Jake,* and they're *dating,* and she won't tell us *anything else,* Mom, she won't say *one more word* — "

And then he'd turned around and gone straight back out the door and phased in the yard so hard she'd heard the sweats rip, because he couldn't stand to be out of his sister's head for one more second.

Sue had sat down at the table and looked at the salt shaker for a while.

Then she'd got up and made the chowder.

Now it was a quarter to five and still dark, and the rain had started the way it always started in November, not falling so much as arriving, a fine cold mist that beaded on the window over the sink and ran down in crooked lines. The radio was on low. The fry bread oil was hot. She'd got the second pot of chowder going because Seth had come back at half past three, phased out just long enough to stick his head in the door and say, "Mom. Make more. He's really, *really* big," and gone again.

She heard the gravel first. Then the back steps.

Sue put her coffee cup down on the table, very carefully, so it wouldn't make a sound.

The door opened.

Her daughter stood on the back step in the porch light with the rain behind her, in a pair of men's work jeans cinched up with a belt, and a flannel shirt Sue had never seen, and a cheap gray hoodie, and boots, and a knitted blue hat with a white pompom on top, sitting a little crooked on her head.

She was dry. That was the first thing Sue saw, and she didn't understand it until much later. Her daughter had just run a hundred miles through the rain and swum the inlet, and she stood there with the porch light on her and her clothes were dry, and whole, and her hat was on.

Leah looked at her.

Sue had been her mother for twenty years. She'd seen every face that girl had. She'd seen her at three, furious because Seth had been born and was getting all the attention. She'd seen her at sixteen coming home from the fireworks with her mouth swollen and her eyes shining. She'd seen her at nineteen in the hospital hallway with her whole body shaking like a struck wire, with grief, and with the thing that was about to tear its way out of her. She'd seen her every day for a year and a half after that, coming home from patrols with her jaw set so hard Sue had heard it creak.

She'd never seen this one.

"Hi, Mom," said Leah.

Sue got up out of her chair. She didn't remember getting up. She was just across the kitchen, and her arms were full of her daughter, and her daughter was hot as a stove the way they all were now, and smelled of rain and spruce and salt water, and something else, something like cold stone, and she was holding on, too. Holding on hard.

"You ate the chowder," Sue said into her hair.

Leah made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else. "It was good chowder."

"I made two more pots." Sue pulled back and held her at arm's length and looked at her. Thinner in the face. Browner, if that was possible, in November. And the hat. Sue reached up and touched the pompom with one finger, and it bounced. "Oh, Lee."

"Don't."

"I said I'd pay money."

"*Mom.*"

Sue let her go. And then she looked past her daughter's shoulder at the back step, where somebody else was standing in the porch light, waiting, with the rain coming down on his hair.

---

He was big. Seth hadn't been lying about that. He filled the doorway when he came through it, his shoulders nearly brushing the frame on both sides. He was in a waxed jacket gone dark with rain over a cream sweater, and dark trousers, and leather boots polished to a shine that had no business on a gravel drive on the reservation at five in the morning. His hair stood up in every direction. His eyes were green as the moss on the north side of a cedar.

He stopped just inside the door and wiped his boots on the mat. Both of them, carefully. Then he looked at her.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said. English. Very English, the kind of voice Sue only ever heard on the Sunday programs with the big houses. "I'm terribly sorry about the hour."

Sue looked at him for a while.

"Sue," she said.

"Sue," he agreed. "Harry."

She'd known it was coming. Seth had told her that too, at half past three, in the doorway, in a different voice — *Mom, his name's Harry. Like Dad.* She'd thought she was ready.

She looked at her daughter. Leah was looking back at her, and her face had gone very still, braced, the way it went before a fight.

Sue thought about her husband on the porch with his camera and his coffee, telling Sam Uley how deep the water was off First Beach.

"Well," she said. "Sit down, Harry. You must be starving."

Leah's face did something complicated. Then it settled.

He sat.

---

She fed him.

She'd fed Quileute wolves for a year and a half. She'd fed her son, who'd gone from a skinny kid who wouldn't eat his vegetables to something that could clear a whole pot of spaghetti standing up at the stove. She'd fed Jacob Black, and Paul, and Embry, and Quil, sometimes all at once, the spring the newborns came, when Sam had them running double shifts and they'd come through her kitchen like locusts through a wheat field. She knew what a wolf could eat.

She'd never seen anything like this one.

He ate the first bowl of chowder and three pieces of fry bread in the time it took her to sit back down. She filled the bowl again. He ate it. He said *thank you* every time she put it down, and then he ate it, all of it, and when it was gone he sat there with his hands on either side of the bowl, waiting, without asking, the way a well-trained dog waits by its dish.

The fourth time she filled it, Leah, who was on her own third bowl, said, without looking up, "He'll eat the whole pot, Mom."

"There's two pots."

"He'll eat both."

Sue looked at him. He looked back at her, and his mouth went up a little on one side, and she saw a tooth. A long one. A canine, white and sharp, sitting in a row of very good teeth like it had every right to be there.

She'd seen that tooth before. On her own son. On her own daughter. On her husband's best friend's boy and on Sam Uley and on every one of the boys who'd come through her kitchen. Never on two legs.

"Is that right," she said.

"I made a promise," Harry said. "Before we left. Your daughter had rules."

"What rules?"

"I'm not allowed to buy anything. For her, or for you, or for Seth. Not one thing." He counted on his fingers. "I'm not allowed to punch Sam. Not even recreationally. We had to run the whole way." He looked down at the empty bowl. "And I'm to eat whatever you make. All of it. I'm not to say no to seconds, and I'm not to say no to thirds."

Sue looked at her daughter.

Leah was eating her chowder and very carefully not looking at anybody.

"That's a good rule," Sue said slowly.

"It's my favourite of the four," said Harry.

She got up and filled the bowl again.

---

Seth came in at twenty past five.

He came in the way he'd come in at ten past two, through the back door, soaked, in a pair of shorts he must have had tied to his ankle, and he didn't stop when he saw them at the table. He didn't even slow down. He crossed the kitchen in two strides and picked his sister up out of her chair, all of her, chowder spoon and all, and held her off the floor against his chest, and buried his face in her shoulder, and stood there.

Sue watched her daughter's arms come up around her brother's neck. Leah's eyes were shut. The spoon was still in her hand.

"You jerk," Seth said into her hoodie. His voice cracked in two places.

"I know."

"You absolute *jerk.*"

"I know, Seth."

He set her down. He kept one hand on her shoulder like he thought she might vanish again. Then he turned around, slowly, and looked at the man sitting at their kitchen table.

Harry looked back at him.

Seth was fifteen, and six foot four, and he'd grown even since September; Sue could see it in his wrists. But he looked at the man at the table with his mouth a little bit open, and for a second he was eight years old again in the front row at the county fair, watching the man with the snakes.

"Hi," Seth said.

"Hi," said Harry. "You must be Seth."

"Are you really a wizard?"

"Yes."

"Like — a *wizard* wizard? Like with a wand?"

"Sometimes."

"Can you do something? Like right now? Can you do a — "

Harry looked at Leah.

Leah, without looking up from her chowder, said, "No."

"Sorry," Harry said to Seth. "She says no."

"*Leah* — "

"How big are you?" Seth said, swinging back to Harry. "In fur. You looked so big, through her eyes, in the water, I couldn't tell, Jake couldn't tell, he's been going *nuts* about it, he wants to know how big — "

Harry looked at Leah again.

"No," said Leah.

"Sorry," said Harry.

Seth made a noise like the noise Sue had heard come out of the radiator in January of 1994, right before it burst. He looked at his mother. "*Mom.*"

Sue had been watching all three of them, and she'd had about two hours' sleep, and she'd made four batches of fry bread, and her daughter was home in a hat with a pompom on it. She picked her coffee up and drank some.

"Sit down, Seth," she said. "There's chowder."

---

At six o'clock, with the first gray coming up behind the trees and the rain still coming down, Harry stood up from the table and asked if he could wash the dishes.

"Sit down," Sue said.

He sat down.

Leah looked at him like she'd never seen anyone do that before, and then she looked at Sue, and then she looked at the table.

"Where's he sleeping?" Sue said.

Leah's ears went red. Sue saw it go, from the lobes right up under the edge of the blue hat, and she didn't know her daughter's ears could still do that. She'd thought Sam had burned it out of her.

"There's the couch," Sue said, mildly.

"He won't fit on the couch," said Leah.

"Then he can fit on the floor."

"He's not going to fit on the *floor,* Mom. Have you *seen* him?"

"He's not sleeping in your room," Sue said. "Your brother's sleeping in your room."

Seth went scarlet. "I *told* you not to tell her — "

"Wouldn't dream of it," said Harry, before anybody could say anything else. He stood up, and pushed his chair back under the table, and picked up his jacket off the back of it. "Sue. Thank you. That was the best chowder I've had in my life, and I've had chowder in Boston." He looked at Leah. "I'll sort something."

"What does *sort something* mean?" said Seth.

"Ask your sister."

"*LEAH* — "

But Harry had already gone out the back door, and Sue heard the screen bang behind him, and then nothing. No feet on the gravel. No car door. Nothing at all.

She looked at her daughter.

"Mom," Leah said. "Don't."

"I didn't say a word."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say," said Sue, "that he wiped his feet." She got up and took his bowl to the sink. "Both of them."

---

She found them at a quarter to eight, when she went out back with the coffee can of scraps for the compost.

The rain had let up. The yard was gray and dripping and smelled of wet cedar and woodsmoke from the Blacks' chimney down the road. And in the lee of the woodpile, on the soaked grass where her husband used to split kindling, there was a wolf.

She'd seen wolves in that yard before. Her own children. Jacob. Sam, once, a long time ago, when he'd come to apologize and couldn't make himself be a man for it.

She'd never seen one like this.

It was black. Black as the inside of a well. It lay curled on its side with its nose tucked under its tail, the way a sled dog sleeps in snow, and the curve of its back came up higher than the top of her woodpile, and the woodpile came up to her shoulder. Its sides rose and fell, slow and huge. The rain was beaded on its coat in silver drops that didn't soak in.

And curled into the hollow of its belly, between its great forelegs, was her son. Sandy-gray and gangly, in his fur, snoring, with his head on one of the black paws like it was a pillow and his tail tucked over his nose.

The black wolf opened one eye. Green.

It looked at her for a long moment over Seth's head, and she looked back at it, with the coffee can of potato peelings in her hand.

Then it closed the eye again, and sighed out through its nose, a long gust that stirred the wet grass, and went back to sleep.

Sue stood on the back step for a while.

Then she went back inside, and put the can down by the door, and started another pot of coffee.

"Hm," she said, to nobody, and found she was smiling, and let herself.


---

Leah woke at half past eight in her own bed for the first time in more than two months, and lay there looking at the ceiling.

It smelled like Seth. Her pillow, her sheets, the whole room, a big dumb fifteen-year-old-boy smell of sweat and wet dog and the body spray he thought nobody could tell he wore. There was a Mariners cap on her dresser that wasn't hers and a pile of his socks in the corner. On the nightstand, propped against her lamp, was the one photograph she'd left behind: her and Seth and Dad on the boat, the summer she was twelve, all three of them squinting.

He'd been sleeping in here with that picture for two months.

She lay there until she trusted her face again. Then she got up and pulled on her jeans and her flannel and went to the kitchen, where her mother was standing at the window over the sink with a cup of coffee, looking out at the backyard.

"They're still out there," Sue said, without turning around.

Leah came and stood beside her.

The rain had stopped. The yard was gray and dripping, and in the lee of the woodpile, on the soaked grass, the biggest wolf she had ever seen lay curled like a sled dog in the snow, and her little brother was asleep in the curve of its belly with his head on one enormous black paw.

Seth was snoring. She could hear him through the glass.

"He came out at about six," Sue said. "Your brother. I watched him. He walked right up to it and stood there, and it opened its eyes and looked at him, and it lifted that leg up off the grass, like a door. And Seth just lay down." She drank her coffee. "Like he was five."

Leah didn't say anything.

"Your brother doesn't lie down next to just anybody, Lee."

"I know, Mom."

"Hm," said Sue, and handed her a mug.

---

Jacob came up the gravel at ten to nine, on foot, in cutoffs and nothing else, with the rain beaded on his shoulders and steam coming off him in the cold.

Leah smelled him from the porch: woodsmoke and motor oil and wet dog, the way he'd always smelled, and over all of it, laid on thick, the bloodsucker stink, bleach and burnt sugar. It had sunk into him. Into his hair and his skin. He smelled like he'd been sleeping in that house for two months, because he had.

He stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at her.

He looked older. That was the first thing. Older around the eyes, in some way she couldn't have explained. And easier. The thing that had sat on his shoulders all through September, that had bent him nearly double by the river with the glass house yellow behind him, was gone. He stood in her mother's yard in the cold like a guy who'd put something heavy down and was still getting used to not carrying it.

She knew what had done that. She'd felt it in the link at two in the morning, that golden hum. She found she didn't want to hate it as much as she'd planned to.

"Hey, Lee," Jacob said.

"Hey, Jake."

"You look good."

"I know."

He laughed. It surprised him; she saw it surprise him. Then his eyes went past her, past the corner of the house, to the woodpile, and the laugh went out of his face like a light switching off.

He walked around the side of the house without saying anything. She went after him.

The black wolf was awake. It lay exactly where it had been, with Seth still snoring against its belly, but its head was up off the grass now and its ears were forward, and it was watching Jacob come across the yard with an expression of mild, polite interest, like a man on a park bench watching somebody else's dog.

Jacob stopped about ten feet away.

Leah watched him take it in. The way she had, in the cottonwoods. The length of the body along the grass. The depth of the chest. The width of the skull. The paw under Seth's head, bigger than Seth's head. She watched him do the math. She watched him get to the end of it.

"Huh," said Jacob.

The wolf looked at him a moment longer. Then, very gently, it slid its paw out from under Seth's head, set Seth's face down on the grass, and stood up.

It took a while. There was a lot of it to stand up. It unfolded off the grass one piece at a time, front legs and then the long black back and then the hindquarters, and when it was all the way up it stood beside the woodpile with its head a good two feet above the top of the cordwood, and looked down at Jacob Black from a height that Jacob Black, in two legs or four, had not had to look up to since he'd phased.

Jacob looked up.

"Morning," said the wolf, in a deep, clean, very English voice with an engine running somewhere under it. "You must be Jacob."

Jacob's mouth opened.

On the grass, Seth woke up all at once, rolled over, saw Jacob, saw the wolf standing over him, and scrambled up onto all four feet with his tail going so hard his whole back end wagged.

Leah didn't need to be in the link to know what was going on in there. It was all over his face. *Jake, look at him, I slept on him, he's so big, he TALKS when he's in fur, he said "budge up" — *

Jacob ignored him. He was still looking up.

"What are you," he said.

The black wolf turned its head and looked at Leah.

"No," said Leah, from the corner of the house.

"Sorry," said Harry, to Jacob. "She says no."

Jacob turned around and looked at her. "*Leah.*"

"Jake."

"He's — he's *bigger* than me. He's — what is he? Is he one of us? He doesn't smell like one of us. He smells like — " Jacob's nose worked. "Like dirt. Like rocks. Like a match. Like that guy in Canada you were thinking about, with the ring — "

"That's right," said Leah.

"*What* is he?"

"I told you last night," Leah said. "He's a wizard. He's a wolf. He's bigger than you. We're dating." She came down off the step. "That's all you get."

Jacob stared at her for a long time.

Then he turned around, and pulled the cord off his ankle, and phased, right there on the grass, and stood up on four feet, russet and enormous, bigger than any of them, bigger than he'd been in September, and went nose to nose with the black wolf.

He came up to about its eyes.

Leah didn't need the link for that one either. It was right there in the set of his ears: *Oh, COME ON.*

She sat down on the back step and laughed until she couldn't breathe.

---

At half past nine, after Jacob had phased back and gone round the yard twice in his cutoffs swearing under his breath, and Seth had eaten six pieces of leftover fry bread standing up, and Harry had changed back on the grass in front of all of them — in his waxed jacket and his cream sweater and his polished boots, perfectly dry, not a blade of wet grass on him — and Jacob had looked at the boots and said "How — " and Harry had said "Ask Leah," and Leah had said "No," and Jacob had gone round the yard a third time, Harry came and sat down on the back step beside her.

"I need to ask you something," he said.

"What."

"It's about your rule." He had his elbows on his knees and his hands loose between them. "I've bought something. It's for me. I need somewhere to sleep, and your mother's right, I don't fit on the couch, and I'm not sleeping in your room, and Seth seems to have adopted me but I can't spend every night in the garden or the neighbours will talk." He looked down the yard at Jacob, who was still glaring at him. "The thing is, where I want to put it, there'd be money involved. And the money would go to people you know. So I'm asking."

Leah turned her head and looked at him.

"What did you buy," she said.

"A boat."

"How big a boat."

He considered. "Bit big."

"*Harry.*"

"Eighty-eight metres," said Harry. "Bit under three hundred feet. Hargreaves found it in Seattle. It belonged to a software chap in Bellevue who got divorced and wants it gone before his wife's lawyers find it. It left the dock at seven this morning. It should be here by about four." He held up a hand before she could open her mouth. "It's for me. I'm going to sleep on it. You're not allowed to stay on it, or your mother, or Seth, unless you want to, in which case I'll probably not stop you, but I'm not *giving* it to anyone. It's mine. That's the rule. I checked."

Leah closed her mouth.

"The thing is," he went on, "I'd quite like to anchor it off your beach. And that's your tribe's water. So I'd want to ask your chief, properly. And I'd want to pay a mooring fee. And I wanted to ask you first, because that's money going to people you know." He looked at her sideways. "So. May I?"

She looked at him for a long time.

From the middle of the yard, Seth, who had been listening with a piece of fry bread halfway to his mouth, said very faintly, "Three hundred *feet?*"

"Billy," Leah said finally. "You ask Billy. He's on the council. And it's not a cent more than what's fair."

"Who decides what's fair?"

"Billy." She stood up. "And me."

---

Billy Black was on his porch in his wheelchair with a blanket over his knees and a cup of coffee in both hands, the way he was every morning, watching the road. He watched the three of them come up it — Leah in front, and Harry beside her with his hands in his jacket pockets, and Seth trailing behind with his mouth still slightly open — and he didn't say anything until they'd stopped at the bottom of his ramp.

Then he looked at Harry for a long time.

"So you're the wizard," Billy said.

Harry inclined his head. "Mr. Black."

"Jake was in here at three in the morning talking about you. Couldn't sit still. I haven't seen him like that since he was twelve and Embry beat him at arm wrestling." Billy drank his coffee. "Leah."

"Billy."

"You look good."

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"Everybody's right." His eyes went back to Harry. "What can I do for you, son?"

Harry asked. He asked it plainly, the way he'd asked Leah, standing at the bottom of the ramp with his hands out of his pockets now. A boat. His. Eighty-eight metres. He'd like to anchor off First Beach, outside the river mouth, clear of the fishing boats and well clear of the island. He'd keep it out of everyone's way. He'd pay whatever the council thought was right.

Billy listened to all of it without changing his face at all.

"Eighty-eight metres," he said at the end.

"Yes."

Billy looked at Leah. "What's that in feet?"

"Two eighty-eight," said Leah. "And change."

Billy was quiet for a long time. A truck went by on the road behind them and slowed down to look and kept going.

"November's rough out there," Billy said. "You'll drag your anchor. You'll end up on the rocks under the island."

"I won't," said Harry.

Billy looked at him. Harry looked back. Something went between the two of them that Leah didn't catch, and then Billy looked down at his coffee.

"The school's roof," Billy said. "Tribal school. It's been leaking since ninety-one. Council's been trying to find the money for a new one for fifteen years. We keep getting it patched." He looked up at Leah, not at Harry. "That's fair, for a winter. I think."

Leah opened her mouth.

She thought about the tribal school. About the bucket that sat in the corner of her fourth-grade classroom every November to catch the drip from the ceiling tile with the brown stain on it, and how Mrs. Jackson used to say they'd get it fixed next year. About Seth, two years later, in the same classroom, with the same bucket.

She shut her mouth.

"That's fair," she said.

"Done," said Harry, and held out his hand.

Billy looked at it. Then he set his coffee down on the arm of his chair and leaned forward and shook it.

"Welcome to La Push," Billy Black said. "Don't hit the island."

Behind them, Seth made the kettle noise again, very quietly, and had to go and sit down on the bottom of the ramp.


---

By four o'clock half the reservation was on First Beach.

Nobody had organized it. Nobody had to. Billy had told Old Quil, and Old Quil had told his daughter-in-law at the store, and the store was the store, and by the time the light started to go there were people standing all along the high-tide line among the bleached driftwood in their rain jackets with their hands in their pockets, and kids running up and down at the edge of the surf, and three pickups parked on the bluff with their engines running for the heat. Somebody had brought a thermos. Somebody had brought a camera. Billy sat at the top of the beach in his chair with a blanket over his knees and Jacob standing behind him with his hands on the grips, and Sue sat on a log beside them with her hood up, and nobody said what they were waiting for.

Leah stood at the waterline with her hands jammed in her hoodie pocket and her pompom on and the wind in her face, and didn't look at anybody.

Harry stood beside her, eating fry bread out of a paper bag.

Her mother had given him the bag on the way out of the house. He'd said *thank you, Sue,* and looked at it like it was a medal.

"You're going to eat that whole bag," Leah said.

"I've been told I'm not allowed to say no." He offered it to her. She took a piece. "Here she comes."

She came around the headland to the north a little before half past four, with the last of the light behind her, and everybody on the beach went quiet at once.

She was white. That was the first thing. White as a gull, long and low and sharp at the bow, three decks stacked up one on top of another like a wedding cake, with dark glass all along the sides and something on the top deck that Leah realized, after a second, was a helicopter pad. Her lights were on. Every one of them, from the waterline to the radar mast, so that she came down the coast in the November dusk lit up like a hotel that had gone for a swim. She was so big that for a long time she didn't seem to be getting any closer, only bigger, until all at once she was right there, off James Island, with the swell breaking white on the rocks behind her and her engines rumbling across the water like distant thunder.

On the high-tide line, somebody said, "Holy *shit.*"

It was Seth.

Then the anchor went down. Leah heard the chain run out, a long iron roar that went on and on. And the ship swung slowly on it, head to the swell, and settled.

And then the sea went flat.

Leah saw it happen. Everybody did. The swell had been coming in all afternoon in long gray ranks, eight and ten feet, the way it always did in November, breaking on the island and the reef and the beach in long white walls. Around the ship, in a circle a hundred yards wide, it stopped. The water inside the circle went smooth as a pond. The swell came in from the west and reached the edge of the circle and simply lay down, the way a dog lies down when it's told, and went around, and picked itself back up on the far side, and kept coming.

The ship sat in the middle of her own little lake with her lights on and her reflection hanging under her, upside down, perfect.

Up at the top of the beach, Billy Black turned his head very slowly and looked at the man standing next to Leah at the waterline.

Harry ate another piece of fry bread.

"Told him I wouldn't hit the island," he said.

---

Sam's pack came down the path from the road at a quarter to five.

Leah smelled them before she heard them. She'd have known them anywhere, every one of them. She'd lived in their heads for a year and a half. Jared, cedar and the cinnamon gum he always chewed. Embry, motor oil and the cheap shampoo his mom bought in bulk. Quil, sweet and milky, like the baby powder off the kid he spent his whole life carrying around. Paul, sweat and too much body spray and the sharp hot smell of a temper always about two degrees off boiling.

And Sam.

Rain and cedar and the woodsmoke from Emily's stove, and under that, so familiar it went into her like a hook, Sam.

She didn't turn around.

They came through the driftwood in a loose wedge, the way they always moved, Sam out front, in cutoffs and nothing else in the cold, all of them huge and brown and steaming. People on the beach got out of their way without being asked. They stopped about twenty feet back from the waterline.

Leah turned around.

He looked the same. That was the stupid thing. She'd thought he'd look different, after two months. He looked exactly the same. The same wide shoulders and the same short hair and the same face, older than it should be, and the same eyes. Dark and steady and tired. Looking at her.

"Leah," Sam said.

"Sam."

"You're back."

"For Thanksgiving."

He nodded slowly, like that was an answer he had to turn over before he could put it anywhere. Then his eyes went past her, the way they'd been wanting to since he came down the path, to the man standing beside her with a paper bag of fry bread.

He'd been tracking that scent all day. She could see it on him. On all of them. They'd come across it in her mother's yard and on the road to Billy's and they'd followed it, and it had smelled like nothing any of them had ever smelled. Wolf, and not. Dirt and rocks and a struck match. And the tracks. Leah had seen them herself, in the mud by the woodpile, that morning. Every one of them bigger than her two hands laid side by side.

"Who's this," Sam said.

Leah had been waiting two months to answer that question. She'd been waiting her whole life, maybe.

"That's Harry," she said. "He's a wizard. He's also a wolf. He's bigger than Jacob. We're dating."

And then she didn't say anything else.

The silence went on for a long time.

Behind Sam, Jared let out a long, low whistle. Quil's eyes had gone to the ship, and back to Harry, and back to the ship. Embry said, faintly, "Is that — is that his *boat?*"

"It is," said Harry, pleasantly.

"*Dude,*" said Quil.

Sam didn't say anything. He was looking at Leah. She'd seen that look on his face before. She'd seen it from the inside. It was the look he got when he was trying very hard to think about something and not let himself think about something else.

Paul laughed.

It wasn't a nice laugh. Paul didn't have a nice laugh. It came out of him short and ugly, and he stepped up level with Sam, and looked Harry up and down, the boots and the jacket and the hair, and curled his lip.

"Bullshit," Paul said. "A *wizard.* Sure. What is he, some rich white boy she picked up on the side of the road?" He looked at Leah. "Guess you had to go all the way to Canada to find a guy who didn't already know what you were like, huh, Leah? Bet he doesn't know about the — "

He didn't get any further.

He didn't get any further because Harry had finished his fry bread, and folded the top of the paper bag over twice, neatly, and put it in his jacket pocket, and turned his head and looked at Paul. He kept his feet where they were and his hands in his pockets, and looked at Paul the way he'd looked at Kroll across the circle on the mountain, flat and green and patient, like a dog at a rat hole.

Paul started to shake.

Leah knew it from the inside. She'd felt it come up in Paul a hundred times, that tremor that started in the hands and ran up the arms into the shoulders, the heat boiling up under the skin. He was going to phase. Right there, on First Beach, in front of half the tribe, in front of the kids at the edge of the surf. Paul never could hold it. He was going to go off like a bomb.

"Paul," said Sam. "Stand down."

The voice dropped.

It went down into a register under hearing, a note that came up out of the sand through the soles of her boots and into her bones. It was a sound older than any of them. She'd felt it on her own body more times than she could count. She'd felt it bend her spine and close her throat and pin her flat on the forest floor while the thing in her chest screamed. *Don't think about Emily around me, Leah.* Like you could order a woman to stop bleeding.

Paul locked solid. Mid-shake. Every muscle in his body seized and held, and the heat went out of him like water out of a cracked jar.

And Leah flinched.

She couldn't help it. She wasn't Sam's anymore; the voice had no hooks in her. Her body didn't know that. Her body heard that note and went small, all on its own, her shoulders coming up and her chin going down, the way it had every time for eighteen months. It lasted half a second.

Harry saw it.

She felt him see it. He didn't look at her. He didn't need to. His head just turned, a quarter inch, toward Sam, and tipped over to one side, the dog-tilt, slow, the way he'd tipped it in the cottonwoods when he'd heard something he couldn't place.

"Ooo," said Harry. "*Spicy.*"

---

Every head on the beach turned.

Sam looked at him. "What?"

"That." Harry took his hands out of his pockets. His eyes had gone strange — unfocused, a little, the way Dot's went when she was doing sums in her head, looking at something about two feet to the left of Sam's ear that nobody else could see. "Do it again."

"What?"

"The voice. Anything. Go on. Tell him to back up."

Sam stared at him. Then, slowly, without taking his eyes off Harry, he said, "Paul. Back up," and the note came up out of the sand again, and Paul took two stiff steps backwards like a puppet.

Harry watched it the way Leah had seen him watch Kroll's hexes come at him, the way a man watches a card trick to see where the card goes.

"Right," he said. "Got it."

He talked fast. He talked the way he'd cooked, without stopping to look at his own hands.

"There's a line running out of you to each of them. Four here — and two more up the road somewhere, I can feel the ends going off into the trees. Like guy-ropes on a tent. Old. Older than my sort of magic by a long way. That's the stuff your stories are made of, I'd guess. The chief who left his body. When you drop your voice like that, you put weight on the rope, and the rope goes straight down into the bit of them that's wolf, and the wolf does what it's told, and the boy's got to go along with it whether he likes it or not. The voice is just the label. It tells the wolf whose hand's on the other end." He tipped his head the other way. "It's lovely work, honestly. Whoever made it knew exactly what they were doing. Only there's no lock on it. Nothing at all. Anybody who can see the rope can pick it up."

Sam's face had gone very still. "What does that mean?"

"It means this," said Harry.

He looked at Paul.

"Paul," he said. "Sit."

His voice dropped. The same note. The same impossible sound from under the sand, the one that belonged to the chief who'd gone into the wolf, the one that had only ever come out of an Alpha's mouth in all the history of the tribe. Leah felt it go by her like a truck going by on the highway, close enough to stir her hair, and touch nothing.

Paul sat.

His legs went out from under him all at once, like somebody had cut the strings, and he sat down on the wet sand of First Beach, hard, in front of the whole reservation, with his knees up and his hands flat on the ground on either side of him and his mouth hanging open.

Nobody on that beach made a sound.

A gull went over, crying. The surf came in and went out. Up on the bluff, one of the pickups ticked as its engine cooled.

Then Embry Call fell over.

He just went, straight over sideways into the driftwood, and lay there on his back with both hands over his face making a noise like a seal. Quil grabbed Jared's arm and bent double. Jared had both hands over his mouth and tears running out of his eyes. Up at the top of the beach, Leah heard Jacob say, very clearly, "Oh my *God,*" and Billy start to cough into his coffee, and Seth make the kettle noise so loud a woman by the pickups turned around to see if somebody was hurt.

Sue Clearwater, on her log, said, "Hm."

Paul sat on the sand and looked up at Harry with an expression Leah would treasure for the rest of her natural life.

"Huh," said Harry, looking down at him with mild interest. "Works."

"*Let him go,*" said Sam. His voice had gone rough.

"Already have." And he had. Leah could see it; Paul's shoulders had come down an inch, the way a dog's do when you take your hand off its collar. "Up you get, Paul. Sorry about that. I had to know if it worked."

Paul got up. He got up slowly, and his face was dark red under the brown, and every muscle in him was shaking again. But it was Sam's *back up* still holding him now, and he couldn't do anything with it except stand there and shake.

"You — " Paul said. "You — "

"Yeah," Harry said. "I know."

Sam took a step forward. Just one. He stopped close enough that Leah could smell Emily's woodsmoke on his skin. He was looking at Harry, and Harry was looking back at him. Sam had a few inches on him, standing on two legs. Harry didn't seem to have noticed.

"Don't you ever," Sam said, very low, "do that to one of mine again."

"I won't," said Harry. He said it simply. "It was the once, to see if I could. I'll not do it again." His eyes went to Leah for half a second, and came back. "And never on her. Not ever. Not for anything."

Sam's jaw moved. "She's not mine anymore."

"No," Harry agreed. "She isn't."

---

Sam looked at her then.

He looked at her for a long time, standing on the beach in the last of the light, with the ship lit up behind her on her flat circle of water and the wind off the ocean blowing the pompom on her hat. She didn't know what he was seeing. She'd lived inside his eyes for a year and a half, and she didn't know. She'd never been on this side of them before. Not since the day Emily walked into the kitchen.

"You look good, Lee," Sam said.

She thought about all the things she could say to that. She'd had a hundred of them, once. She'd rehearsed them in the shower and on patrol and lying awake at night with his voice in her head. She'd had them sharpened to points.

She found she didn't want any of them.

"I know," Leah said.

Out on the water, something white was coming in toward the beach through the surf, fast, its engine buzzing. A launch. A little open boat with a canopy, the size of a pickup truck, with a man in a white jacket at the wheel, slewing through the breakers and running its nose up onto the sand ten feet from where they stood.

"That's my lift," said Harry. He looked at Leah. "Want to see it?"

"Yeah," she said.

"Anybody else?" He turned to the beach at large. "There's room. There's a hot tub, apparently. The software chap left a lot of very bad paintings."

Seth was already halfway down the beach. Embry had stopped making the seal noise and was sitting up in the driftwood. He looked at Quil. Quil looked at Sam.

Sam didn't say anything.

Embry and Quil looked at each other. Then they got up and came down the beach together, not running, not quite.

Paul didn't move. Jared stayed beside him. Sam stood where he was.

Leah climbed into the launch over the bow. Harry came after her, and then Seth, and then Embry and Quil, wading out knee-deep in the November surf without seeming to notice it, and the man in the white jacket backed them off the sand and swung the bow around toward the lights.

She looked back once.

Sam was standing at the waterline in the dusk, alone, with his hands at his sides, watching them go. He got smaller. The beach got smaller. The people along the driftwood turned into a long dark line against the trees, and up at the top of it, her mother lifted one hand, and Leah lifted one back.

Then she turned around, and faced the ship, and didn't look back again.


---

Edward heard Leah Clearwater coming from three miles away.

He'd always been able to. Her mind had a particular sound, in the months he'd known it: a high, hard, narrow sound, like wind through a crack in a window. Bitter. Precise. Exhausting to be near. Jacob's thoughts were loud and Seth's were bright and Sam's were heavy, and Leah's had always been the one he'd tuned out first, as a man in a crowded room tunes out the person complaining at the next table.

The sound that came through the trees from the west on Saturday afternoon a little after two was a different one entirely. It was loud and clear and very deliberate, and it said the same thing over and over in the same flat tone, like a child reciting a times table:

*Hi, Edward. This is Harry. He's a wizard. He's also a wolf. He's bigger than Jacob. We're dating. Hi, Edward. This is Harry. He's a wizard —*

Edward stopped with one hand on the back of the white couch.

Behind the chant, faint, the way you can hear music under a conversation, there was a second set of thoughts in range. Embry Call, on patrol for Sam, near the treaty line, and Embry's mind had gone high and thin with disbelief.

*— they're hopping it. They're just — they're running right ALONG it, and every couple strides she hops over, and then back, and then over, and the big one's doing it too, he's doing it in time with her, like it's — like it's hopscotch — oh my God, he just stopped and SAT on it. He's sitting ON the line. He's looking at me. Sam's going to lose his MIND — *

And then, a moment later, Embry again, strangled: *He waved. How does a wolf WAVE —*

"Edward?" said Bella, from the far end of the couch.

She had Renesmee on her lap, and Jacob on the floor at her feet with his back against the couch and his head tipped back against Bella's knee, half asleep. Renesmee was looking at her father with her head on one side. She'd caught it from his face. She caught everything.

"Leah's coming," Edward said. "With — " He stopped. He listened again, harder, for the second mind. The one Leah was chanting about. He reached for it the way he reached for any mind within range, without effort, the way a man turns his head toward a sound.

There was nothing there.

He knew silence. He had Bella's silence, the smooth blank wall that had driven him half mad for two years and that he had come, with time, to love. This was different. Where a mind should be, running along the treaty line beside Leah Clearwater's, there was a door.

Heavy. Plain. Oak, if a door in a mind could be oak. Shut.

He pushed on it. It didn't move. It felt like the door of a house where somebody was home and had decided, on balance, not to answer.

"With what?" said Jacob, from the floor, opening one eye.

"With the wizard," said Edward slowly.

On the floor, Jacob made a noise in his throat that was almost a growl and almost a laugh. "Yeah," he said. "Good luck with that."

---

They came across the river at twenty past two.

The whole family was out on the back lawn by then. Carlisle and Esme by the glass. Rosalie on the steps, with her arms folded, already furious. Emmett beside her, grinning like a boy at a circus. Alice and Jasper a little apart, Alice with her eyes half closed and her head tilted, frowning.

"I can't see them," Alice said. "Either of them. Leah, I never could. But him — " Her frown deepened. "It's like the dogs. He's a hole."

"He's amused," said Jasper quietly. "Whoever he is. He's very, very amused."

Leah came out of the trees on the far side of the river first, gray and lean and fast, and went across the water in a long flat leap and landed on the lawn and stood there with her sides going. Then the trees on the far bank moved, and the second wolf came out.

Edward had seen the black wolf through Jacob's eyes that morning, and through Seth's, a hundred times, in a hundred replays, each more indignant than the last. Jacob's memory had shrunk it a little, out of pride. Seth's hadn't. In the flesh, coming down the bank to the edge of the river in the gray afternoon light, it was exactly as big as Seth had said, which was bigger than anything that size had any right to be, and it made no sound at all.

It gathered itself on the far bank and jumped.

The river was thirty feet wide there. The wolf went over it without seeming to try, and landed on the lawn beside Leah, and the grass didn't so much as bend under it. Its paws came down and the earth took the weight the way water takes a leaf.

Rosalie said a word that Esme did not approve of.

The black wolf looked up at the house. Its eyes were green. They moved along the glass, along the family, from face to face, taking each of them in without hurry. They stopped on Edward.

And from behind the oak door, very clearly, in a voice Edward had never heard before, a single thought came through. Pleasant. English. Deliberate.

*Knock knock.*

Edward stood very still on the lawn.

He had read the minds of human beings for eighty-eight years. He had never once had one hand him a thought. He'd taken them. Overheard them. Eavesdropped on them by the thousand, on buses and in classrooms and in hospital waiting rooms, whether he wanted to or not. Nobody, in eighty-eight years, had ever opened a door a crack and passed a thought through it to him on purpose, like a note under a desk.

"Who's there?" Edward said aloud, before he could stop himself.

The black wolf's mouth opened.

"Harry," it said. Out loud. In the same English voice, deep and clean, with something like an engine idling underneath it.

Emmett's grin had gone so wide it looked painful.

"Harry who?" said Edward, faintly.

"Harry up and let us in," said the wolf. "It's bloody freezing out here."

Emmett sat down on the steps and laughed until the glass behind him rang.

---

Inside, they changed back. Both of them. On Esme's pale wool rug, in the middle of the great white room, one after the other, and when they stood up they were dressed. Leah in jeans and a flannel shirt and a knitted hat with a pompom, which she had clearly been wearing as a wolf. The man — Harry — in a waxed jacket and a cream sweater and boots that had been polished within the hour, with his hair standing up.

Edward had heard Leah Clearwater curse her torn clothes in his hearing perhaps two hundred times since the spring. He looked at her hat. Then at Harry. Then he listened to Leah's mind, and found it had moved on from the chant, and was thinking, very loudly, and with tremendous satisfaction, *Yeah. That's right. Eat it, Cullen.*

Carlisle came forward with his hand out. "Welcome. I'm Carlisle Cullen. This is my wife, Esme. I don't believe — "

"Harry." The hand was shaken. "Thanks for having us. Sorry about the river. And the line." He glanced at Leah. "We were on the line a bit."

"So I heard," said Carlisle, with the faintest twitch at the corner of his mouth. He was looking at Harry the way he looked at a new journal article. Edward could hear the questions stacking up behind his eyes, neat and orderly. *Body temperature. Mass. That tooth. The clothes —*

"No," said Harry.

Carlisle blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"You can't have a blood sample." It was said kindly. "You were going to ask."

Carlisle's mind went perfectly blank with surprise, which in eighty-eight years Edward had seen happen perhaps twice. Then it filled up again, and the thing it filled up with was delight.

"I was," Carlisle admitted.

"Everyone does," said Harry. "There's a woman in London who cries about it."

---

Emmett waited a full four minutes before he came across the room.

He'd heard from Seth. He'd heard about the poker, and the villa, and Seth had told him in great detail about the beach and Paul, and Emmett had spent the whole of the previous evening making Seth tell it again, and then again, while Rosalie sat in the corner looking at the ceiling. His mind had been bright and clear and simple for twenty hours, and it said one thing, which was: *Him. I want to try him.*

"Seth says you won a villa at cards," Emmett said.

"In Sofia. There was a Bulgarian." Harry looked at him. "I don't like to talk about it."

"I don't play for houses." Emmett put his elbow on Esme's dining table — the long dark walnut table that nobody in the house had ever eaten at — and held up his hand. "Arm wrestle me."

Rosalie said, "*Emmett.*"

Harry looked at the hand. "What are we playing for?"

"My Jeep."

"I don't want your Jeep."

"Then what do you want?"

Harry thought about it. Edward listened to the door, and the door told him nothing, and he found that he was leaning forward slightly, as everyone in the room was leaning forward slightly.

"If I win," Harry said, "the lot of you come out to the boat one night this week. I'll cook. You sit at the table and pretend to eat it."

The room went quiet.

"The boat," said Edward, "is anchored off First Beach."

"It is."

"In Quileute waters. The treaty — "

"Forbids you setting foot on Quileute land," said Harry. "Yes. Leah told me." He tipped his head. "Is a boat land?"

Edward opened his mouth. He found he had nothing to put in it.

In Leah's head, very clearly, he heard: *Billy is going to LOVE this.*

Carlisle had a hand over his mouth. Esme was looking at Carlisle. Jacob, from the doorway, said, "Oh, *hell* no," and Leah said, "Jake, sit down," and Jacob, after a moment, astonishingly, sat down.

"Fine," said Emmett, delighted. "Deal. And if I win?"

"You can have my boat," said Harry.

Leah made a choking noise.

They clasped hands. Edward watched Emmett's mind as it happened, because Emmett's was the only one of the two he could watch. He saw Emmett set his feet, and brace, and lean in with the weight of three hundred pounds of marble and seventy years of being the strongest thing in any room he walked into. He saw Emmett's mind go from delight to surprise to a kind of wild, incredulous joy, like a man who has spent his whole life swimming in a pond and has just been thrown into the sea.

The hands didn't move. For three seconds, four, five, they didn't move at all. Emmett's arm was trembling. Harry's wasn't. Harry was looking at Emmett with mild interest, the way he'd looked at the river before he jumped it.

Then Harry's hand went over, slowly, the way a door swings shut, and put the back of Emmett's hand down on the walnut.

The table broke.

It broke in half, straight down the middle, with a crack like a rifle shot, both halves folding inward and crashing to the floor. Emmett went down with it. He lay on his back on Esme's floor among the wreckage and laughed so hard the chandelier swayed.

Esme stood in the doorway of the kitchen with her hands over her mouth.

"I'm so sorry," Harry said to her, getting up off his knees and dusting walnut splinters off his sleeve. He sounded as if he meant it. "That was entirely my fault. I'll replace it."

"Please don't," said Esme faintly.

"It's no trouble." He took a little mirror out of his jacket pocket, the size of a playing card, and looked into it. "Hargreaves. A walnut dining table, please. Long. Eighteenth century if you can, nicer than the one I just broke. For a Mrs. Esme Cullen, Forks, Washington." He glanced at the two halves of the old one on the floor. "And the tree it came from, if anyone knows where that is."

A tired English voice came faintly out of the mirror. "Yes, my lord."

"Don't call me that."

"Yes, my lord," said the voice, and the mirror went dark.

Edward had never in his existence heard his daughter think anything as loudly as she thought it then.

She'd been on Bella's hip by the stairs through all of it, silent, watching with her great dark eyes, her bronze curls standing out around her face. She looked, at ten weeks old, like a child of perhaps a year and a half; she understood like a child of eight; and her mind, which was the only mind in that house besides Leah's that Edward could hear without trying, had gone from curiosity to wonder to a single, huge, shining want.

She reached out from Bella's arms toward the man brushing walnut off his sweater.

Bella looked at Edward. Edward looked at Jacob. Jacob was already on his feet.

"Nessie — " Jacob said.

But Harry had seen the hand. He looked at the little girl for a moment, with his head tipped. Then he came across the room, and stopped in front of Bella, and bent until his face was level with Renesmee's.

"Hello," he said. "I'm Harry."

Renesmee put her palm flat on his cheek.

Edward saw what she showed him, because he saw everything she showed anyone. The black wolf coming over the river, huge, silent. Its back, broad as a bed. Herself, on top of it, small, in her blue coat, with her hands in the fur. Trees going by. Wind. And then the word she used when she wanted something very much and was being very polite about it, the word Bella had taught her: *please.*

Harry stayed bent with her hand on his cheek. Edward couldn't hear what he thought. He saw his face change, though. Something came into it at the edges that hadn't been there for the table or for Emmett or for Carlisle. Some old thing.

"I've got a godson who asks me that every weekend," Harry said to her, quietly. "He's eight. He's still in one piece." He straightened up. "You'll have to ask your mum."

Every head in the room turned to Bella.

"Absolutely not," said Rosalie.

"Bella — " said Jacob.

"Edward?" said Bella.

Edward listened. He listened to his daughter's mind, which was all *please* and wind and fur. He listened to the door, which said nothing. And he listened to Leah Clearwater's mind, which was saying, with the patience of someone who had been in his head enough times to know exactly how he worked, *He let an eight-year-old ride round a garden on him every weekend for two years. The kid's fine. His gran nearly had a stroke. The kid's fine, Edward. Let her go.*

"Slowly," Edward said, after a long moment. "On the lawn. Where we can see."

"Slowly," Harry agreed.

---

He changed on the lawn and lay down flat on the wet grass like a dog waiting to be patted, and Bella set Renesmee on his back, between his shoulders, with her small hands buried to the wrists in the black fur. Then he stood up.

Renesmee's mind went white.

That was the only word Edward had for it, then or later. Pure light. Delight so total it had no shape at all. The ground had dropped away from her and she was higher than she'd ever been, higher than her father's arms, higher than Jacob's back, and the thing under her was hot and moving and enormous and silent, and she laughed. Out loud. A high, clear peal of laughter that rang across the lawn and off the glass, and every one of them heard it, and Rosalie, on the steps, put her hand on her chest.

The black wolf walked. Then it trotted, slowly, in a wide circle around the lawn, smooth as water, so smooth the child on its back barely swayed. Jacob phased in the middle of the grass with a bang of torn denim and ran alongside it, russet and huge and frantic, his nose an inch from Renesmee's dangling foot the whole way round.

On the second circle, something else came out of the air beside them.

Silver. White. Moonlight gone solid. It poured out of nowhere and folded itself into the shape of a stag, a great antlered stag made of light, and ran beside the black wolf with its hooves making no mark in the grass, tossing its head.

Renesmee screamed with joy.

Edward stood on the lawn and listened to his daughter's mind fill up with a silver stag running beside a black wolf under a gray November sky, and couldn't have moved if the Volturi themselves had walked out of the trees.

Beside him, Leah Clearwater had her hands in her hoodie pocket and was watching Jacob run alongside with his ears pinned flat, and was thinking, with enormous and uncomplicated pleasure, *Look at his FACE.*

Edward found, to his own very great surprise, that he was smiling.

Charlie Swan came to dinner at six, the way he did most Saturdays now, and Seth watched him meet the wizard.

Charlie came in the front door in his flannel and his jeans with a six-pack of Rainier under one arm and his mustache damp from the rain, and said, "Sue," and kissed Seth's mom on the cheek in a way that made Seth want to go and live in the woods, and then he turned around and saw the man sitting at the kitchen table, and stopped.

Harry stood up. "Chief Swan."

"Charlie." Charlie shook the hand. Seth watched him do the cop thing, the thing he did to everybody, the up-and-down. Boots. Hands. Face. The boots took him a while. "You'd be the fella with the boat."

"I would."

"Coast Guard called the station this afternoon." Charlie sat down slowly and put the beer on the table. "Said there's a three-hundred-foot yacht anchored off First Beach in the middle of a November swell, and the water's flat as a millpond for a hundred yards all round it, and could I tell them why."

"Could you?" said Harry, with interest.

"No." Charlie cracked a beer. "So what line of work are you in, Harry?"

"Enforcement. International."

"Like Interpol?"

"Bit like Interpol." Harry considered. "More forms."

Charlie looked at him for a long moment over the top of the can. Then he looked at Sue, at the stove, with her back to all of them.

"Sue," said Charlie.

"Need to know, Charlie," said Seth's mom, without turning around.

Charlie was quiet. He looked at Harry. He looked at Leah, who was sitting at the end of the table in her hat, eating a roll, watching him with an expression Seth recognized from the first time she'd seen him on the beach at La Push in a bathing suit, when she was eleven, which was the face of somebody enjoying herself enormously at somebody else's expense.

"Right," said Charlie finally. "Well. I don't need to know." He drank some beer. "Good to have you home, Leah."

"Thanks, Charlie."

"Pass the potatoes," said Charlie, and that was that.

---

Charlie left at eight for the game at Billy's. Seth's mom went to the door with him, and they stood out on the porch for a while talking low, and Seth turned the TV up so he wouldn't have to hear any of it.

The news was on. Channel 4 out of Seattle. And halfway through, between a story about a mudslide on 101 and the weather, there it was. Seth sat up on the couch so fast he knocked the remote on the floor.

It was taking off. A plane bigger than any plane had a right to be, white with a blue tail, two whole decks of windows running all the way down the side of it, lifting off a runway somewhere in the sun like a building that had decided to fly. *— latest round of route-proving flights,* the anchor was saying, *as the troubled European jumbo prepares for certification, nearly two years behind schedule — *

"That's the A380," Seth said, to the room. "That's — Leah, look. That's the A380."

Leah, at the table, didn't look up from her pie. "Okay."

"It's the biggest passenger plane ever built. It's bigger than a 747. It's got two full decks the whole way down. It can take like eight hundred people if they cram 'em in. Nobody's even got one yet. Singapore's supposed to get the first one, but they keep pushing it back because the wiring's all screwed up — "

"How do you know all that?" said Harry.

He was on the floor in front of the couch with his back against it, with his boots off and his socks on the rug, which Seth's mom had told him he could do, which she had never told anyone in Seth's whole life. He had a plate of pie on his knee. It was his third plate of pie.

"Discovery Channel," Seth said. "They did a whole special. They build the pieces all over Europe and ship them to France, and then they float them up a river on barges and drive them through these tiny little villages in the middle of the night 'cause they're too big for anything else. The wings are as big as a basketball court. Each wing." He looked at the TV, where the plane was coming in to land now, slow and huge, its wheels coming down in a long row. "You could put showers in it. Bedrooms, even. That's what they said on the special."

Harry watched the plane land on the TV for a while, with his fork in his pie.

"Huh," he said.

At the table, Leah put her fork down.

"Harry," she said.

"Mm?"

"*Harry.*"

But he'd already taken the mirror out of his pocket. The little one, the size of a playing card, with the old silver around the edges. He looked into it the way you'd look out a window to check the weather.

"Hargreaves," he said.

Seth leaned over the arm of the couch. In the glass there was a face. A man's face, long and pale and gray-whiskered, with a pair of half-moon glasses on the end of his nose and the expression of somebody who had been woken up at four in the morning for the third time this week and was beyond surprise.

"My lord," said the face.

"Sorry. Is it early?"

"It is a quarter past four in the morning in London, my lord. I am entirely at your disposal."

"Brilliant. Quick one. That big aeroplane. The Airbus. The double-decker one."

There was a pause.

"The A380," said Hargreaves.

"That's the one. Can I have one?"

The pause was longer this time.

"My lord," said Hargreaves, very carefully, "the A380 has not yet been certified. The first delivery is scheduled to go to Singapore Airlines in the autumn of next year. Airbus is approximately two years behind schedule. There is a queue of airlines from here to Dubai."

"Get me a place in the queue, then. Near the front."

"The list price is in the region of three hundred million United States dollars, my lord. Before the interior is fitted. A private interior would add a great deal, and take eighteen months at the very least."

"Is that a lot?" said Harry.

Seth's chest stopped working.

"*Harry,*" said Leah.

Harry lowered the mirror and looked at her across the room. Seth looked at her too. She was sitting at the table with both hands flat on either side of her plate and her face going through about six things at once.

"You said," Leah said. "You *promised.* Not one thing. Not for me, not for Mom, not for Seth — "

"It's for me," said Harry.

"He's sitting *right there* — "

"It's mine." Harry looked perfectly serious. "I'm buying it for me. I want an aeroplane." He tipped his head toward the couch. "Seth's allowed to sit in it. You said I couldn't *buy* him anything. You never said anything about him sitting in my things."

Leah opened her mouth.

Seth's mom had come back in from the porch at some point. She was standing in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded, looking at the four of them: her son hanging over the arm of the couch with his mouth open, her daughter at the table, and a twenty-six-year-old man on her living-room floor in his socks, holding a mirror with an Englishman in it.

"Mm-hm," said Sue Clearwater.

"*Mom* — "

"I didn't say anything, Lee."

"Shall I proceed, my lord?" said Hargreaves, from the mirror, in the voice of a man who had already proceeded.

"Yes, please. And Hargreaves?"

"My lord."

"Can it have a hot tub?"

There was a very long silence from London.

"I shall enquire," said Hargreaves, "of Toulouse."

"Lovely. Thanks. Oh — and the table. The walnut one."

"In hand, my lord. Along with the tree."

"Don't call me that."

"Goodnight, my lord," said Hargreaves, and the mirror went dark.

---

Nobody said anything for a while.

On the TV the weather was on. Rain. Rain all week, rain through Thanksgiving, highs in the forties. Seth didn't hear any of it. He was sitting on the couch staring at the man on the floor, who had gone back to eating his pie.

Three hundred million dollars. Before the inside.

He tried to make the number mean something, and couldn't. It just sat there being a number, like the distance to the sun.

"Can I," Seth said. His voice cracked. He started again. "Can I sit in the cockpit?"

"Course," said Harry, through pie. "It's my aeroplane. You can sit anywhere you like."

"Can I sit in the *hot tub?*"

"If they can fit one."

Seth made a noise. He didn't mean to. It came out of him without his permission, high and long, and his mom said "*Seth,*" and Leah put her face in her hands.

Then Leah got up from the table. She walked across the living room, and bent down, and took hold of the front of Harry's sweater in her fist, and pulled. He came up off the floor like he weighed nothing, plate and fork and all, and she took the plate out of his hand and put it on the coffee table and towed him by the sweater out through the kitchen, past their mom, and out the back door, and the screen door banged behind them.

Seth sat on the couch.

He could hear them out there in the yard, in the dark. Leah's voice, going up, saying *you* and *loophole* and *three hundred MILLION* and *my brother is going to have a STROKE.* Harry's voice, lower, saying something Seth couldn't make out.

And then Leah laughing.

The real one, the one that came all the way up from the bottom of her and went on and on, wheezing, the one Seth hadn't heard since before Dad. Since before any of it. He'd thought it was gone. He'd thought Sam had taken it, along with everything else, the way you think a song's gone when the station stops playing it.

He sat on the couch with his heart going and listened to his sister laugh in the backyard in the dark.

His mom came and sat down next to him. She didn't say anything. She just sat, and after a while she put her hand on the back of his neck, the way she used to when he was little, and they listened to it together until it stopped.

"Mom," Seth said.

"Mm."

"Is it okay if I really like him?"

His mom was quiet for a long moment, looking at the dark window.

"I think," Sue said, "your sister's going to have to get used to that."

When she could breathe again she let go of his sweater.

They were standing by the woodpile in the dark, in the wet grass where he'd slept that morning with her brother curled against him. The kitchen window threw a yellow square across the yard. Through it she could see her mother sitting on the couch with her hand on the back of Seth's neck.

"Three hundred million dollars," Leah said.

"Before the interior."

"With a *hot tub.*"

"If Toulouse says yes." He was looking down at her with his head tipped and his hair wrecked where she'd towed him through the screen door. "You did say *buy.* For *him.* I was very careful."

"You're a lawyer."

"I've been near a lot of lawyers. It rubs off."

"You're a rich asshole."

"Yes," said Harry, the way he'd said it at the Prospector, with his fork in Dot's pie.

She looked at him for a long time.

Then she said, "Run."

---

She phased in the yard, clothes and all, and went over the fence at the bottom of the garden without looking to see if he'd follow. She didn't need to. Halfway down the slope through the dripping salal she caught the edge of him on her left, a piece of the dark moving where the dark shouldn't move.

She checked her head on the way down. Out of habit.

Quiet. Seth in his skin, on the couch. Jacob in his, at the glass house, with a sleeping kid on his chest. Nobody in fur but her. The house in her skull was empty, every room, the lights off. She could feel the doors to the others, the way you can feel where the walls are in your own bedroom in the dark. She knew they were there. She knew they'd open the next time any of them phased.

For tonight, it was hers.

They came out of the trees onto First Beach at a dead run and she didn't slow down. The tide was out. The sand was hard and wet and gray in the dark and it went on for a mile, and she took it flat out, the gear past fast, the wind screaming, the surf a white blur on her right and the driftwood a pale blur on her left, and he was there, the whole way, pacing her, silent, the hard sand taking his weight without a mark.

At the south end, under the bluff, she wheeled and went straight into the sea.

The cold hit like a car. It always would. She came up snorting and swam, and the big ranks of the swell came at her out of the dark, eight feet, ten, and the first one lifted her and dropped her, and the second one —

The second one lay down.

It came at her and flattened, the way the swell had flattened around the ship that afternoon, and went past her like a dog going past on a leash. So did the third. A black head came up out of the water beside her, ears flat, eyes half shut.

*Are you doing that?*

"Bit," said Harry. "I still don't like tides."

The ship sat in her circle of flat water with her lights low, a few windows yellow, a figure in a white jacket on the bridge who looked down at two wolves swimming toward the stern at nine o'clock at night and very professionally looked away. There was a platform at the back, right at the waterline, teak, wide as Sue's kitchen. Harry went up onto it in one surge and turned and stood there streaming, and she came up after him, and they shook, both of them, and none of his hit her.

He went up a flight of white stairs, and then another, and then another, and she went after him, her claws clicking on teak that probably cost more than her mother's house, past windows full of dark furniture and very bad paintings, all the way to the top.

It was the helicopter pad. A big flat circle with a white H painted on it, open to the sky, and the whole Pacific black and moving all around it outside the flat ring of water, and the lights of La Push scattered along the shore, and the dark hump of James Island off to the north with the surf breaking white at its foot.

He lay down on the H.

She looked at it. She looked at him lying on it, enormous and black and wet, with his chin on his paws.

*You're lying on the H.*

"It's my H," said Harry.

She lay down beside him. Close. Closer than on the plateau. She put her side against his side, and felt the heat of him come through his wet fur and hers, and his breathing, slow and huge, going in and out against her ribs.

---

A little after six the clouds broke.

They broke in the east first, over the mountains behind the town, a long tear in the gray with stars in it. Then more stars. Then the tear widened and kept widening, and the wind dropped, and out over the black shoulder of the Olympics, low, something came up.

It was the moon. What was left of it. A paring so thin it was barely there, a single silver line curved like a fingernail, the last of it before the new moon. It came up slow over the ridges with the stars around it and hung there over La Push, over the dark houses and the river and the boats in the marina, very small and very sharp.

Harry lifted his head and looked at it.

And then, low, deep in his chest, with the engine-rumble under it, he started to hum.

She knew it from the second note. From the bottom of her stomach. From the floor of a truck cab and the wheelhouse of a boat with a blue hull and a rust streak down the bow and a radio with salt on the grille.

He was terrible. He was off on the second note and he never got back on. He came in late on the part her dad always came in early on, and he hummed the bit her dad always shouted, and the whole thing came out of him like a cello with a cold, rolling across the helipad and out over the flat black water toward the shore. He kept going. He hummed the whole thing through, start to finish, under the thin moon, and he didn't stop for her, and he didn't ask if it was all right.

She didn't want him to.

When he got to the end he started it again.

*You sing like him,* Leah thought.

He stopped humming.

"Terribly?" he said.

*Yeah.*

He was quiet for a moment. Then he put his chin back down on his paws, right next to hers, and started again from the top, just as badly, maybe worse, and she shut her eyes.

She thought about her mother's hand on Seth's neck. About Paul on the sand with his mouth open, and Sam alone at the waterline, getting smaller. About a little girl with bronze curls screaming with joy on the back of a black wolf with a silver stag running beside her, and Jacob's face. About a three-hundred-million-dollar aeroplane her brother was going to be allowed to sit in. About the tribal school roof and the bucket in the corner of her fourth-grade classroom.

About four more days to Thanksgiving.

She'd thought, coming home, that the house in her head would be too full. She'd braced for it for six days, every mile. She'd braced for poor Leah and Sam's Leah and all of it, waiting for her on First Beach like the tide.

It was full. That was true. It was full of people. Her mother. Her brother. Jacob. Dot, eighteen hundred miles north, frying bacon in rows. Brynn. A boy named Teddy she'd never met whose hair changed colour when he was cross.

And a very large, very wet, very rich asshole on a helicopter pad in the middle of a sea he'd told to lie down, humming her father's favorite song so badly that a gull on the radar mast above them took off and flew away toward the island in protest.

She found she didn't mind.

She went to sleep there, in her fur, with her chin beside his and the old moon going up over the mountains, on top of a boat she wasn't allowed to be given, with the song going on and on above her head. Nobody in her head but her.

She didn't check.
