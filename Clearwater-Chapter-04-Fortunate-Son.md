# Chapter Four: Fortunate Son

She woke because something was frying.

For a few seconds she didn't know where she was. Cedar under her belly, smooth and dry. Old smoke in rock. The sea going in and out somewhere below her, loud, the way it had been loud through her bedroom window every night of her life until she was nineteen. Then bacon. A lot of bacon. Coffee. Butter going brown in a pan. And threaded through all of it, earth after rain, pine, cold stone.

Leah opened her eyes.

The coals in the big hearth had gone gray on top and orange underneath. The amber lights along the walls had turned themselves down to almost nothing in the night, and nobody had touched them. Through the ragged opening in the rock the sky was the flat pale gray of a Washington morning, and the wind came in as far as the edge of the opening and stopped there.

Over the fireplace, the moose regarded her from its lake.

The rug beside her was empty. There was a dent in the pile the size of a small car, slowly springing back.

Harry was in the kitchen.

He was in human form, barefoot on the planks, in jeans and a black T-shirt so old the logo on the back had cracked into a hundred white islands. A dish towel hung over one shoulder. He had a cast-iron skillet going on the black range and another one beside it, and on the shelf above the sink sat a small silver radio she hadn't seen last night, tuned to a classic rock station out of Seattle, playing Led Zeppelin.

He was singing along.

He knew every word. He hit none of the notes. He had a spatula in one hand and was conducting the hood of the range with it, and when the song got to the part about Mordor he did a sort of shuffle on the planks that made the bacon jump.

Leah lay on the rug with her chin on her paws and watched him do it for about a minute.

Then she got up, stretched — front legs, then back legs, a long shudder down her spine that ended in her tail — and padded past him toward the door on the left.

He glanced over his shoulder. "Morning, Clearwater."

She didn't answer. She hadn't let him in, so she couldn't, and she wasn't going to bark at a man holding bacon.

"Your bag's on the bed," he said to her back. "Didn't open it. Didn't touch anything in it. Put it down and left."

She shouldered the door open.

The room behind it was small and dry and smelled like split cedar. A bed with a heavy wool blanket in red and black stripes. A chest of drawers. A lamp, already on. A slot of a window cut through the rock at head height, with the sea in it, gray on gray. Her Mercantile duffel sat at the foot of the bed, zipped, exactly where he'd said.

She nosed the door shut behind her, and let the fire go.

---

When she came back out she was in jeans and the flannel and wool socks, with her hair yanked back in an elastic and the picture of her and her dad on the boat standing on top of the chest of drawers behind her, where she could see it from the bed.

The radio had moved on to AC/DC. Harry had moved on to eggs.

"You sing like a seal giving birth," Leah said.

"Thank you." He cracked two eggs one-handed, the way Dot did, and only got a little shell in the second one. He fished it out with a corner of the first shell without looking. "Sit. Breakfast. Proper one."

She sat at the long table. He put a mug of coffee in front of her, black, and then a plate, and then a bottle.

The plate had a sandwich on it. Thick white bread, toasted on one side, with what looked like half a pig folded between the slices. The bottle was squat and brown with a red-and-gold label and a picture of a building with a clock tower on it.

"What's this?"

"Bacon butty."

"A *what?*"

"Bacon sandwich. Proper English one. You put the brown sauce on." He tapped the bottle. "HP Sauce."

Leah looked at the label. Then at him.

"Named after me," Harry said.

"No it's not."

"No, it's not," he agreed, and sat down across from her with a plate holding four of them. "Houses of Parliament. Still. I like to think."

She put some on. It was tangy and dark and vinegary, like ketchup that had been to college and come back with opinions. She ate the whole sandwich in five bites and he slid another one across the table without being asked.

"You had a visitor," he said, while she was eating the second one.

Leah stopped chewing.

"About three," he said. "Something cold came up to your line north of the river and stood there. The ward told me. I went down."

She swallowed. "You went down. Alone."

"You were asleep."

"You should have woken me up."

"I thought about it." He ate a third of a sandwich in one bite. "You were snoring. It seemed rude."

"I don't snore."

"You snore like an outboard motor," Harry said. "It's lovely. Very regular. I could set my watch by it."

She kicked him under the table. He didn't move his leg, so it was like kicking a fence post, and it hurt her more than him, and he didn't even have the decency to look pleased about it.

"Who was it?" she said.

"Bronze hair. Gold eyes. Very pale. Very well dressed for three in the morning in the rain." He reached for the sauce. "Edward, I assume."

Her lip came up off her teeth before she could stop it. "What did he want?"

"To have a look at me. He's had the whole business out of your brother's head, and your Alpha's, since we came over the water. He'd been standing at that line every night since, apparently, waiting for something to come out of the trees so he could see it with his own eyes." Harry shook brown sauce onto his bacon. "So I came out of the trees, and he saw it."

"As a wolf."

"As a wolf."

"What did he do?"

"Went very still." Harry considered. "They do that, I gather."

"Like statues."

"Like a cat that's just seen a bigger cat." He took a bite, chewed, swallowed. "Then he tried to have a look inside my head."

Leah put her sandwich down.

"And I let him have a look at the front step," Harry said. "Bit of a welcome mat. I thought he'd enjoy it." He picked up his coffee. "I thought of that song of yours."

She stared at him. "You didn't."

"Start to finish. Twice. With the key change."

"There's no key change."

"There is the way I sing it."

The laugh got out of her before she could catch it. It came up out of her chest like it had in the pumpkin field, a sharp bark, and she clapped a hand over her mouth and glared at him over it.

"He put two fingers to his forehead," Harry went on, unruffled, "and said, very politely, that he would be grateful if I'd stop. And I said I'd be grateful if he'd stay on his side of the line until somebody invited him over it. And he said that was the treaty. And I said lovely, then we agree. And he said welcome to Washington. And I said thanks very much, and went back up the hill." He drank his coffee. "Took about four minutes. Very civilized. You'd have been proud of me."

"Did he sparkle?"

Harry's face fell. "It was three in the morning, Clearwater. It was pitch black. It was *raining.*"

"So no."

"I've never been so disappointed in my life."

She laughed again. She didn't bother covering it this time.

He finished his fourth sandwich and wiped his hands on the dish towel and looked at her across the table, and something in his face went businesslike.

"Right," he said. "Lesson one."

---

Lesson one was a sock.

A single gray wool sock, one of hers, out of the bag of them she'd bought at the Mercantile the same day as the phone card. He held it up between two fingers.

"Your change throws everything outward," he said. "I watched you do it at Liard, from behind, while I was admiring the moss. Heat and mass, all at once, from the middle out. Like a bomb. Anything on you that isn't you goes with it." He turned the sock over. "Mine folds in. Everything comes along because there's nowhere for it to go. So I'm not going to try to make you change like me. I'm going to try to make your clothes stop minding."

"My clothes."

"Start small. One sock." He held it out. "Put it on."

She looked at him. Then she took the sock, pulled off the one she was wearing on her left foot, and put on the one he'd handed her.

He looked at her foot. Nothing happened that she could see. Then she caught it, faint, sharp — the struck-match smell, there and gone, like somebody had lit a match in the next room and blown it straight out.

"There," he said. "Go and change. Come back with it or without it."

She went into her room and shut the door. She stripped, all of it, except the one gray sock on her left foot, and stood there on the cedar planks feeling like an idiot. Then she let the fire take her.

It went the way it always went. Heat from the middle. Her bones letting go. The room getting smaller as she got bigger. She stood on four legs and looked down at her left forepaw — no, her left hind, she'd put it on her foot — and there was nothing on it but fur.

*Figures,* she thought.

She let the wolf go again. The heat drew back in. The fur went in. She was standing on two bare feet in the middle of the little cedar room, naked as the day she was born.

Except for one gray wool sock.

On her left foot. Pulled up to the ankle. Not a stitch out of place.

She stared at it for a long time.

Then she got dressed, every piece of it, and went out into the kitchen, and put her left foot up on the bench beside him without a word.

Harry looked at the sock. He looked at her face. The corner of his mouth went up.

"Progress," he said.

"It's one sock."

"It's one more sock than you had yesterday."

"At this rate I'll have pants by Christmas."

"Ambitious," Harry said. "I like it." He picked up his empty plate, and hers, and took them to the sink. "Tomorrow, two socks. Then we'll see about a shirt. I want to see how it does with cotton before I try anything with elastic in it. Elastic's a nightmare. Ask anybody."

She sat on the bench with her socked foot up, watching him wash the skillets, and thought about every pair of shorts she'd ever exploded. Every shirt. The tarp off the woodpile. And Paul Lahote's face the morning the cord snapped on the fence.

One sock. It was a stupid thing to feel that way about. She felt it anyway.

"Right," Harry said, drying his hands. "Pie."

"It's nine in the morning."

"Every film I've ever seen about America," he said, "has got a diner in it. With pie. And a waitress who calls you hon, and a bloke at the counter in a hat, and coffee that comes round again before you've asked. And I've been in this country a full day, Clearwater, and I've not had any of it."

"You've been in this country a full day because you *ran* here."

"Exactly. And now I need a car."

"We could run to Forks. It's fifteen miles."

"We could. Americans drive." He took the bigger mirror out of his back pocket, the one with the plain black frame and the crest she couldn't read. "When in Rome."

He held it up. "Hargreaves."

The glass clouded and cleared.

The man who looked out of it was thin and gray and somewhere in his fifties, with a narrow, careful face and half-moon glasses and his tie loosened two inches. Behind him she could see a slice of dark paneled wall, a window with rain on it, and a gray London sky going toward dusk. Stacks of paper. A green-shaded lamp.

"My lord," said Hargreaves.

"Hargreaves. Evening. I need a car."

There was a pause.

"Which one, my lord?" Hargreaves said.

Leah looked at Harry. *Which one.*

"Not one of mine," Harry said. "An American one. Here. Washington State, the coast."

"Specifications?"

"Big. Black. Loud. Old." He thought about it. "Back seat a wolf could lie down in."

Hargreaves's face didn't move. Not a muscle. Leah had played poker with Embry Call, and Embry couldn't do that.

"By when, my lord?"

Harry glanced at Leah. "Lunch?"

From the glass came a long, thin, rising sound, like a kettle somebody had left on in another room.

Leah put her hand over her mouth.

"Delivery address," Hargreaves said, when the sound had stopped.

Harry looked around the cave. At the hearth, and the moose, and the sea through the rock.

"My mom's," Leah said. She gave it to him. The road, the number, the mailbox that said CLEARWATER in her father's block capitals with the second R falling off.

Hargreaves wrote it down with a fountain pen. "Very good, my lord."

The glass went dark.

Harry put the mirror back in his pocket. He looked enormously pleased with himself.

"He's going to quit," Leah said.

"He's been going to quit since I was seventeen," Harry said. "His father was going to quit on my grandfather. It's a sort of family tradition. Shoes on, Clearwater. Leave that sock where it is. I want to see how it does over a whole day."

---

Sue Clearwater hadn't slept.

She'd lain on her back in the bed she'd shared with Harry for twenty-three years and listened to the rain start up around two and quit around four, and she'd thought about the name. *Harry.* Said out loud, on her own porch steps, by her daughter, about a stranger holding a pie. She'd known it was coming. Seth had told her the morning before. It hadn't helped.

At six, Seth came in through the kitchen door off patrol with his cut-offs on the right way round for once, soaked to the skin, and ate eleven pancakes standing at the stove and told her everything at once with his mouth full.

A cave. Dad's cave, up on Qw'aatilla, only it was huge now. A fireplace you could stand up in. Rugs. A real kitchen. Lights that just floated in the air, Mom, they just *hung* there. Two bedrooms. A hot spring, inside the rock, like an actual hot tub.

And a moose.

"A moose."

"A painting of a moose. Over the fireplace." Seth stuffed half a pancake in. "It's got really messed-up legs."

Sue had decided not to ask.

"She was happy," Seth said, around the pancake. He'd stopped chewing. "In her head. Before she fell asleep last night. I was on the north end of the line and she phased and I could feel her, and she was just — happy, Mom. Like, all the way down. I don't think I ever felt her like that. Not since before Dad."

He'd gone up to bed after that. Sue had stood at the sink for a while and not done anything.

So she knew where her daughter was. Up on the headland, in Harry's place, asleep in her fur in front of a fire. And she knew where the man was, more or less, which was wherever her daughter was.

At seven, Billy called.

"Emily says the weather's breaking," Billy said. "Clear tonight, maybe tomorrow. Last dry spell before the winter rains, she figures. She wants a fire on First Beach."

"Tonight."

"Tonight." A pause on the line. "Sam's coming by at nine for the folding tables. Quil's already here. You should come by too."

He didn't say why. He didn't need to. They'd known each other forty years.

---

Billy's house was small and red and needed paint, and his kitchen was the size of a closet, and Sam Uley filled most of it.

He was standing by the counter when Sue came in, with his arms folded and his shoulders against the cabinets, too big for the room the way all of them were too big for every room now. He'd cut his hair short again. He looked tired. He'd looked tired for two years.

"Sue," he said.

"Sam."

Billy was at the table in his chair with a cup of coffee in both hands. Old Quil Ateara sat by the window with his cane between his knees and both hands folded on top of it, small and dry and very old, his eyes half closed, the way he sat when he was listening to everything. Rachel Black was at the stove frying eggs in a T-shirt with a university logo on it, and when Sue came in she lifted the spatula in greeting and went back to her eggs with the face of somebody who had no intention of leaving the room.

Sue sat down across from Billy. Rachel put a cup of coffee in front of her without asking.

"So she's home," Billy said.

"She's home."

"How is she?"

Sue thought about her daughter in the wet grass with her hair down her back, and the way she'd held on, stiff, then not.

"She ate four bowls of chowder," Sue said. "He ate four too."

Billy's mouth twitched. "Jake says he's something to see."

"Jake says he's bigger than Jake."

"I heard." Billy turned his cup in his hands. "Never thought I'd hear Jake say that about anybody and sound happy about it."

"I want them at the fire tonight," Sue said. "Leah and Seth." She set her cup down. "And him."

Rachel's spatula stopped scraping.

Sam didn't move off the counter. He didn't unfold his arms. But Sue had watched him grow up from a skinny little boy whose father walked out on him, and she saw his jaw go tight.

"Good," said Billy. "I want to meet him."

By the window, Old Quil opened his eyes. "A wolf," he said, "that speaks with a man's voice."

"So Seth says."

"I'd like to hear that," Old Quil said, "before I die." And closed his eyes again.

"No," said Sam.

He said it quietly. Sam never raised his voice in the elders' houses. He'd been brought up right, by his mother, and by Billy and Harry and Old Quil after his father walked out.

"With respect," Sam said. "Billy. Sue. The fire's for the tribe. The stories are for the tribe. We don't know him."

"Leah knows him," Sue said.

"Leah's known him five weeks." Sam unfolded his arms and put his hands flat on the counter behind him. "He's not Quileute. He's not even American. Jake says he's a — " he stopped, like the word tasted wrong — "wizard. That he killed a man up there, some kind of old — thing, in front of her. That he's got more power than Jake can measure. That he can hear what you're thinking if he wants to."

"Jake says he can hear what you're thinking if you let him," Sue said.

"And we're supposed to take his word for that."

"We're supposed to take Leah's."

Sam looked at the floor. "He's living with her in a cave on Qw'aatilla," he said. "Sue. You don't know what he — "

"Bella Swan," Sue said, "sat at that fire."

Sam's head came up.

"Last spring." Sue kept her voice level. "You let Billy tell the story of the cold ones to a girl who was engaged to one of them. She sat right there on the log with her hand in Jacob's and heard every word, and then she went home and married a Cullen. Don't tell me who can listen to the stories, Sam."

"That was different."

"It was," Sue agreed. "She was marrying the people we were made to fight. This man brought my daughter home."

Sam flinched. It was very small. She saw it anyway.

"Leah came home on her own," he said.

"Yes. She did." Sue picked up her coffee. "And he came with her. So he comes to the fire."

"Sue — "

"I'm not asking, Sam."

The kitchen went quiet. The eggs spat in the pan. Rachel had given up all pretense of cooking and was standing with the spatula in her hand watching them like it was the best TV she'd seen in months.

Sam looked at Billy. Billy drank his coffee. He looked at Old Quil. Old Quil's eyes were closed, and he might have been asleep, except that the corner of his mouth was turned up.

Then Sam looked back at Sue, and she saw what was under it. She'd seen it before, at Harry's funeral, when Sam stood at the back of the crowd and didn't come up to the grave. Guilt, old and worn smooth with handling. Worry. And something else, further down, that he'd never in a hundred years say out loud, that had to do with a girl he'd once bought a ring for living in a cave with a stranger.

Sue had hated him once. For a long time. She'd hated him so much she couldn't be in the same room as Emily's mother at the Thriftway. Then Leah phased, and Sue had found out what imprinting was, and she'd stopped hating him and started feeling something closer to pity, which was worse, for both of them.

She set her cup down.

"You've been looking after Leah from inside her head for a year and a half," she said, more gently. "You don't have to anymore. You don't get to."

Sam held her eyes for a long moment. Then he looked down at his hands.

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

"Good," said Billy. "Put me close to the fire. My knees are killing me."

"I'll want to see the wolf," said Old Quil, without opening his eyes.

"Ask him nicely," Sue said.

Sam pushed off the counter. He picked up the folding tables from where they leaned against the wall, two under each arm, like they were made of cardboard. At the door he stopped.

"I'll tell the others," he said.

"Tell them to behave."

"Paul — "

"Paul's never behaved a day in his life," said Billy.

"I'll handle Paul," Rachel said, and flipped her eggs.

Sam looked at her. Then at Sue. Then he went out, and the screen door banged behind him, and they heard his truck start.

Billy waited until it was gone down the road. Then he looked across the table at Sue with an expression she'd known since they were both kids on First Beach, before either of them got married, back when Harry Clearwater was the loudest boy on the rez and Billy was his shadow.

"You enjoyed that," Billy said.

"A little," Sue admitted.

"Harry would've loved it."

"Harry would've told him to go jump off James Island."

"Harry *did* tell him that," Billy said. "Twice. When he was twelve. Little punk kept letting the air out of his tires." He drank his coffee. "So. What's he like? The other one."

Sue thought about it. About boots set side by side on her mat. About four bowls of chowder and *I'm sorry about the name, I gather it's a bit much.* About a man looking at her daughter across her kitchen table like somebody who'd decided to walk a long, hard road to see where it went.

"I'll let you decide," she said.

At the stove, Rachel laughed out loud.

Sue finished her coffee and drove to Port Angeles, because Emily would need potato salad for twenty and the Thriftway never had enough mayonnaise. She meant to walk up to the headland when she got back and tell her daughter herself. She never got the chance. At five past twelve, in the Safeway parking lot, her cell phone rang. It was Marge Atterson from across the road, calling to say there were two flatbed trucks outside Sue's house unloading cars, and one of them had a Bentley on it, and was Sue aware.

---

Leah heard the trucks three miles off.

She and Harry had walked down off the headland along the top of First Beach and in through the back of the rez, and they'd been sitting on her mother's porch steps for twenty minutes by then, him eating an apple out of the bowl on Sue's kitchen table and her with her left foot up on the step below, looking at the sock. It was still on. She'd walked two miles on it in her boot. It hadn't even bunched.

Two diesel engines downshifting on the hill above the school. Air brakes. A long hiss.

"That's us," Harry said, and threw the apple core into the woodpile.

They came around the bend one behind the other, two flatbed trucks with Washington plates, each one carrying something long and low under a fitted gray cover strapped down at all four corners. The first one pulled up at the end of Sue's gravel. The second one pulled up behind it, half on the road.

Across the street, Marge Atterson's screen door opened. Marge came out onto her porch in her slippers and stood there with a cordless phone held to her ear, not even pretending.

The driver of the first truck was a heavyset man in a Mariners cap with a clipboard. He climbed down and looked at the house, and the mailbox with CLEARWATER on it in hand-painted capitals, and the woodpile, and then at Harry.

"Potter?"

"That's me."

"Sign here. And here. Initial here, here, and here." He watched Harry sign. "Fella at the shop in Tacoma said to tell you she's got her original three-twenty-seven, rebuilt, and he cried a little bit when I loaded her. Swear to God. Grown man."

"Did he," Harry said, with interest.

The driver went up on the flatbed and undid the straps and pulled the cover off.

It was a car.

It was long. Longer than her mother's Ford by a couple of feet. Black, the kind of black that went down a long way, so the gray sky moved around in the paint like clouds in a pond. Four doors and no post between the windows, so the whole side of it was one long clean line of glass and chrome. A grille like a mouth full of teeth. Three taillights on each side at the back, red as coals.

The driver let down the ramps and backed it off the truck. The engine turned over with a sound Leah felt in her back teeth before she heard it, a deep, rough, rolling grumble, like something big clearing its throat in a cave. He parked it in front of the woodpile and got out and handed Harry the keys.

Harry stood there with the keys in his hand and didn't say anything.

He walked around the car. Slowly. All the way around, once, the way Leah had seen men walk around a horse at the county fair. At the front fender he put out his hand and laid it flat on the paint.

"Hello, gorgeous," he said to it.

"It looks like a hearse," said Leah.

"She," said Harry, "looks like sin."

"*She?*"

"Everyone calls their car she. It's the law."

"It's not the law."

"It is in my house."

"Which house?"

"All of them," Harry said, and opened the driver's door and looked in at the long black bench seat like a man looking at a featherbed after a week in a ditch.

The second driver had climbed down by then. He was younger, skinny, with a goatee, and he'd already pulled the cover off his own load. Underneath it sat something low and silver and fat-wheeled, with a winged B on the nose, gleaming on the back of a flatbed on a gravel road in La Push in front of a house with a blue tarp on part of the roof.

"Guy in London said bring both," the skinny driver said. "In case the first one wasn't suitable."

Harry looked at the Bentley over the roof of the Impala.

"Send it back," he said.

"Back where?"

"Wherever Bentleys go when nobody wants them. Tell Hargreaves I said thank you, and no."

The skinny driver looked at the Bentley. Then at Harry. "Man, you know what this thing *costs?*"

"No idea," Harry said.

"More than every house on this road," Leah said.

Harry looked up from the car.

He looked at the road. At Marge Atterson's house with the moss on its roof, and the Hoffmans' with the boat on blocks in the yard that hadn't been in the water since 1999, and her mother's, with the tarp, and the paint coming off the porch rail where her dad used to sit. Something went across his face and was gone.

"Right," he said. "Back it goes."

The skinny driver shook his head and started strapping the cover back on. Across the street, Marge Atterson said something into her phone that Leah could hear perfectly well, and Leah turned around so Marge could see her face, and Marge went back inside.

---

Harry had the hood up before the trucks were out of sight.

He stood with one hand on the edge of it, looking down into the engine like he was reading a letter. Leah came and stood next to him. It was a lot of metal and black hoses and a big round air cleaner on top like a pie tin, and it smelled like oil and new rubber and gasoline.

"Three-twenty-seven," Harry said. "Four-barrel carb. New plugs. New belts. Somebody's swapped the gearbox out for a better one." He reached in and touched something. "Somebody loved her."

Leah looked at him sideways. "You know engines."

"A bit."

"Where'd a lord learn engines?"

"My best mate's dad had a Ford Anglia," Harry said. "Little blue thing. I spent a lot of time around it when I was twelve. Long story." He let the hood down gently and pressed it shut with the flat of his hand. "It could fly."

"Cars don't fly."

"That one did. Then it went feral and lived in a forest for six years." He went around to the driver's side. "Get in, Clearwater."

"I'm not riding in a car that might go feral."

"She won't go feral. She's American. She's got far too much self-respect." He opened his door. "Front seat. Before I change my mind and make you lie down in the back."

She got in the front.

The bench seat was black leather, cracked a little at the seams, and the dashboard was a long sweep of chrome and black with round dials in it and a radio with push buttons and, underneath it, bolted in, a cassette deck somebody had added later. The steering wheel was huge and thin. It smelled like old leather and Armor All and somebody's pine-tree air freshener, long gone, still faintly there in the headliner.

Harry got in beside her, and the whole car rocked on its springs. He put the key in. He sat there for a second with his hand on it.

Then he turned it, and the engine woke up under them with that same deep rolling grumble, and he put both hands on the wheel and laughed out loud.

---

They took the La Push road east through the trees, fourteen miles of it, two lanes with no line down the middle, curving along above the river with the moss on the alders and the ferns going rusty on the banks. He drove with one wrist hooked over the top of the wheel and the other arm along the back of the seat behind her, not touching her, and the car took the curves like a boat taking swells.

At the gas station on the edge of Forks he pulled in and went inside and came out five minutes later with a paper bag. He dumped it on the seat between them.

Cassette tapes. A dozen of them. Faded cases, cracked plastic, a yellow sticker on every one that said 2 FOR $3.

"Bargain bin," he said, extremely pleased. "I bought the whole bin. Kid at the till looked at me like I'd robbed a church."

Leah picked through them. *Led Zeppelin IV. Back in Black. Night Moves. Boston.* Something called *Leftoverture* with a cartoon on the cover. *Hotel California.* And at the bottom, with a crack across the case, *Willy and the Poor Boys.* Creedence Clearwater Revival.

She held it for a second.

"House rules," Harry said, pulling back onto the highway. "Driver picks the music, shotgun shuts her cakehole."

Leah turned her head slowly and looked at him.

"Say that again," she said.

He glanced over at her. He looked at her face for about a second.

"Driver picks the music," he said, "and shotgun is very welcome to share her views. At any time. On any subject."

"That's what I thought."

She opened the Creedence case and popped the tape in on side two, and hit play.

A hiss. A drumbeat like somebody kicking a door down. Then the guitar, and John Fogerty yelling at the top of his lungs about senators' sons and millionaires' sons and flags and draft boards, and the old tape deck bringing it out tinny and loud and a little warbly from forty years of being left in hot cars.

Harry listened to the first verse. She watched him get to the chorus and work out what it was about.

"Oh, very good," he said.

"Thought you'd like it."

"I'm not a millionaire's son. Technically I'm a — " he worked it out — "billionaire's grandson. Different tax bracket entirely."

"It's about rich guys," Leah said. "Whose kids never have to go fight."

He didn't answer straight away. He drove. The trees went by. The chorus came round again.

"Yeah," he said. "Well. Some of us went."

She looked at him. He was watching the road, both hands on the wheel now. His face hadn't changed at all. That was how she knew to file it.

Then he reached over and turned the volume knob all the way to the right, and sang along with the rest of it at the top of his lungs, in a voice like a garbage disposal, and got every single word right and every single note wrong.

Her father used to sing this one on the boat. Off Cake Rock, with the lines out, and her mother's sandwiches in the cooler. He'd sung it exactly that badly.

Leah rolled her window down and put her arm out in the wet air and let him.

The diner sat on the main drag in Forks between the hardware store and a real estate office, and Leah had worked there for two years.

She hadn't thought about that until he pulled into the lot. She'd been thinking about pie. She'd been thinking about how he'd look when he ate it. "I used to work here."

"No," she said. "It's got the best pie in Clallam County."

"Leah Clearwater," she said. "Well, look what the cat dragged in."

"Hi, Maxine."

"Yes."

"With the pie," she managed.

He picked his fork back up. "Don't make me choose," said Harry, and finished the marionberry.

*Sam wasn't happy about it.*

The road went up behind the rez off the highway, a gravel track through second-growth hemlock and old clear-cuts gone to salmonberry and fireweed, switchbacking up the ridge in long loops with the ruts full of rainwater. Leah hadn't been up it in years. She knew it anyway. The washout at the second bend. The big stump at the third, where somebody had carved a face in it with a chainsaw a long time ago. The ditch at the fourth.

She sat in the back seat with her arms folded along the top of the front seat, and watched her little brother drive.

"Ten and two," Harry said. He was riding shotgun with his arm along the back of the bench and his knees up against the dash, perfectly relaxed. "Hands at ten and two. Like a clock."

"Like this?"

"Like that. Now give her a little bit. Gently. She's a lady."

"Like Mom's car?"

"Your mum's car is a Ford," Harry said. "This is a lady."

Seth gave her a little bit. The Impala rolled forward over the gravel with that rough grumble coming up through the floor, and Seth's grin got so big Leah could see it from behind, in the way his ears moved.

"Okay," he said. "Okay, okay, okay — "

He gave her more. The back end slewed on the wet gravel and the car started to swing toward the ditch, and Seth yelped. Harry didn't move his arm off the seat. He reached over with his other hand and put two fingers on the bottom of the wheel, and the car straightened out like somebody had grabbed it by the scruff.

"Easy, kid," Harry said.

"Sorry. *Sorry* — "

"Don't be sorry. Be slower." Harry took his fingers off the wheel. "Brake. Gently. Like you're stepping on a cat you don't want to wake up."

"That doesn't make *sense.*"

"It makes perfect sense. Try it."

Seth tried it. The car slowed and stopped, rocking on its springs, at the bend with the carved stump. He sat there with his hands at ten and two, breathing hard, and then he started to laugh, high and cracked and stupid with joy.

Leah sat in the back and didn't say anything.

Her dad's truck had been an '84 F-150, white once, with rust in the wheel wells and a cracked windshield and a stick shift that ground in third. It had smelled like Copenhagen and coffee and fish scales, and there'd always been a bait cooler in the bed. He'd sat in the passenger seat with one hand braced on the dash and the other over his eyes, peeking through his fingers, and yelled *Clutch, Lee. Clutch. CLUTCH* — and when she stalled it he'd laughed so hard he'd had to get out and walk around.

This car didn't smell anything like that truck. It smelled like leather and Armor All and pine and stone. The road was the same road.

"You okay, Lee?"

"Fine." She cleared her throat. "Go on. You're blocking traffic."

"There's no traffic."

"There's a raccoon," Harry said, pointing. There was. It was sitting in the ditch, looking at them with deep suspicion. "He's late for something. Off you go."

Seth went.

"What do you actually do?" he said. "Like, for a job."

"Do you have a supervisor?"

"I did this morning. I'm not certain I still do."

"Paul's gonna be a jerk tonight," he said.

Paul Lahote had been in Leah Clearwater's head for a year and a half, and he could say with total confidence that it was the worst place on the reservation.

Worse than the inside of his uncle's truck, which smelled like dead elk. Worse than detention. Worse than the first night after he phased, when he'd come apart in his mom's backyard and put his new paw straight through the doghouse. Leah's head was cold and sharp and full of broken glass, and you couldn't walk through it without cutting yourself, and she *wanted* you to. Every patrol. Every hunt. Sam this, Emily that, every one of you is pathetic, I hope you choke. Twenty-four hours a day. You'd be running the line at four in the morning thinking about nothing, about a sandwich, and she'd think something so mean about your mother that you'd trip over your own feet.

Then she'd gone over to Jacob, and it went quiet. Paul had thrown a party. An actual party. In his head, at least. Jared had come.

Now he was dragging a driftwood log the size of a canoe up First Beach toward the fire with the sun going down, and Sam had come back from Billy's that morning with a face like a slammed door and told them all she was coming. With the guy.

*Nobody starts anything,* Sam had said. *If he starts something, nobody finishes it. You come and get me.*

Paul had thought that was a weird thing for Sam to say. Sam didn't usually tell them to come get him. Sam usually told them to handle it.

He dropped the log by the fire. Sparks went up. The fire was going good now, a big one, built tall the way Billy liked it, and the driftwood was doing the thing it did, burning blue and green along the edges from the salt soaked into the grain, so the flames looked like something out of a chemistry class.

Everybody was there. Billy in his chair, close to the fire with a blanket on his knees, and Old Quil next to him in a camp chair, wrapped up like a burrito. Sue on a log with a thermos cup in both hands, looking at the road. Emily at the folding tables with the food — a pot of chili the size of a garbage can, hot dogs, buns in plastic bags, Sue's potato salad, a whole sheet pan of the blueberry muffins she made that were as big as your head. Jared and Kim sitting on a log with their shoulders touching, being disgusting. Quil walking up and down the tide line with Claire on his shoulders, letting her steer him by the ears. Embry and Seth hauling another log. Collin and Brady had strung up the volleyball net between two stripped poles of drift they'd jammed into the sand, and they were arguing about whether it was straight.

Rachel was standing by the fire with her hands in the back pockets of her jeans.

Paul went and stood next to her, because that was what he did now, whenever she was anywhere. He'd stopped fighting it about a month ago. It was a lot less tiring.

Sam stood on the far side of the fire with Emily, not doing anything. Just standing there with his arms folded, looking at the trees above the parking lot.

"You think she'll actually come?" Jared said, from his log.

"Sue said," said Embry.

"Sue said she'd *tell* her."

"She'll come," Sam said, without looking around.

Then Paul heard it.

An engine, up in the trees on the road above the lot. Big. Not a truck. A deep, rough, rolling grumble that got louder, and on top of it, coming through the trees and down over the beach and out across the water, loud enough to make the gulls on the sea stacks take off: music.

A drumbeat. A guitar. A man yelling his head off.

Billy Black's head came up like somebody had called his name.

On her log, Sue put her hand over her mouth.

Paul looked at them. "What — "

"That's Creedence," Rachel said. She'd gone quiet. "That's Harry's — Leah's dad. He used to drive down here every summer with the windows down playing that. Every single time. My dad told him he'd go deaf and he said good, then he wouldn't have to listen to Billy." She was watching the trees. "I haven't heard it down here since I was a kid."

The car came out of the trees and down into the lot. It was long and black and low and mean-looking, like something out of a movie about bank robbers. It pulled into the space at the end, nearest the path, and stopped. The music cut off in the middle of a line.

The doors opened.

Paul had been expecting a monster.

Seth had gone around the whole rez saying it — *he's bigger than Jake, he's BIGGER than JAKE* — and Embry had said it to Paul, and Jared had said it to Paul, and Paul had spent all afternoon figuring that whatever got out of that car would be something you'd have to duck to get through a door.

The guy who got out of the driver's side was maybe six-two. Six-two, six-three. Big through the shoulders, sure. But Paul had three inches on him, easy. He had on an old brown leather jacket and jeans, and his black hair was sticking up all over like he'd been electrocuted. He looked like a guy in a car commercial. The kind where the guy drives the truck through a river for no reason.

Paul felt better. A lot better.

The guy went round to the back door of the car and started pulling out white cardboard boxes. Stacking them up. Six. Seven. Nine, in a tower that went up to his chin.

And Leah got out of the other side.

Paul stopped feeling anything at all.

She was laughing.

Leah Clearwater was standing in the parking lot at First Beach in a flannel shirt with her hair down, laughing. Out loud. With her head back. At something the guy had said over the roof of the car. It was a big, loud, honking laugh, like a goose, and Paul had been inside her head for a year and a half and he'd heard her think every single thing a person could think, and he had never, not once, not one time, heard that sound.

Not out loud. Not in there.

"Is that," said Jared slowly, "*Leah?*"

"Dude," said Embry.

"Whoa," said Quil, with Claire on his shoulders.

"Shut up," said Paul.

They came down the path through the drift logs toward the fire. The guy had the pie tower. Leah was walking backward in front of him, talking, poking him in the chest every few words with one finger, and he said something back, and she shoved him with her hip — not a little shove, a real one, a Leah one, the kind that had put Paul through a wall once — and the guy took it like a tree takes wind. He leaned. The pie tower leaned with him. Seven, eight, nine, the top box sliding off the edge into the air.

Leah caught it one-handed without looking. Put it back on top. Said something.

He said something.

She laughed again, the goose laugh, and punched him in the arm.

Paul looked across the fire at Sam.

Sam was standing exactly where he'd been. His arms were still folded. He hadn't moved at all. But Paul had been in Sam's head too, and he knew what it looked like when Sam's whole body went still like that, the way a dog's does right before it decides whether to bark.

Emily was looking at Leah with her mouth a little open. Her scarred cheek was lit orange by the fire on one side and gold by the sunset on the other.

They got close. Paul smelled him.

Turned dirt after rain. Pine needles. Wet rock, like the inside of a cave. All of it real strong, wolf strong, the way the pack smelled to each other. And under it something else, something dry and sharp and electric that made Paul's lip want to come up off his teeth on its own. He'd never smelled anything like it. And the guy ran hot. Paul could feel it from ten feet, coming off him like off a woodstove. Hotter than Paul. Hotter than Sam.

The guy stopped by the fire. He looked around at all of them over the top of the pie boxes — Billy, Old Quil, Sue, Sam and Emily, Jared and Kim, Quil with Claire, the kids by the net — and then his eyes came round to Paul and stopped there.

Green eyes. Bright green, in the firelight.

He smiled at Paul. Just a little. Just one side of his mouth.

And Paul saw his tooth.

The canine on that side was long. Way too long. White and curved and sharp as a fishhook, sitting there in a row of perfectly normal teeth like it had every right to be there.

Deep down inside Paul, where his wolf lived, his wolf took one look at that tooth and lay down flat on its belly with its ears pinned back.

Paul hated it. He hated it so much his hands shook.

"Evening," said the guy, to everybody. English. Real English, like the guy in the James Bond movies. "Harry. Brought pie."

---

Everybody was looking at her. Leah had known they would be. She'd known it in the car, and on the porch, and in the diner with her fork in a slice of pie she couldn't taste. She'd thought it would feel like walking into the café in Turnagain on the first morning in her shorts, every head turning.

It did. It also felt like walking out onto a stage after the lights were already on.

Her mother got up off the log first. She came around the fire and put her arms around Leah, quick and hard, and said into her hair, "You played it."

"He picked the car," Leah said. "I picked the song."

Sue held on a second longer than she needed to. When she let go her eyes were wet, and she didn't do anything about it, and she turned and took the top three pies off Harry's tower like she'd been doing it all her life.

"Emily," Sue said. "Where do you want these?"

Emily came and took three more. Her eyes went from the pies to Harry's face and back, and then to Leah, and stayed on Leah for a long moment, and Leah looked straight back at her.

Then Billy rolled his chair forward over the packed sand, two hard shoves on the wheels, and stopped in front of Harry, and looked up at him.

Billy had gotten old. Leah hadn't noticed it before she left. His hair was more gray than black now, and his hands on the wheels were spotted, and his face had gone deep-lined around the eyes. He looked up at Harry for a long time with no expression at all.

"So you're the other Harry," Billy said.

"Afraid so, sir."

"Harry Clearwater was my best friend for forty years," Billy said. "He couldn't sing worth a damn."

"Neither can I."

"Nobody's worse than Harry."

"He's worse," Leah said.

Billy's eyebrows went up. "Prove it."

Harry looked at him. Then he cleared his throat and sang the first line of the chorus they'd come down the hill to. It came out of him like a garbage truck backing up. Down the beach, the gulls that had just landed on the rocks took off again.

Billy winced all over. "Jesus," he said. "Okay. You're worse."

He was grinning. Leah hadn't seen Billy Black grin since the funeral.

"Bring that pie over here," Billy said. "And sit where I can see you. I want to look at the guy who's got Sue Clearwater overruling Sam Uley in my own kitchen."

"Billy," said Sue.

"What? It was great. I'm gonna tell everybody."

Old Quil hadn't moved from his camp chair. He sat with his blanket up to his chin and his cane between his knees, and he was looking at Harry from under his eyelids the way he looked at weather coming in off the water.

Harry went over and crouched down in front of him so their eyes were level. Leah watched him do it.

"You're the wolf who speaks," said Old Quil.

"Too much, mostly, sir."

"I would like to hear it."

Harry glanced along the beach. Quil was coming up the tide line with Claire on his shoulders, and Claire had both her fists in his hair and was yelling *giddyup* at the top of her lungs.

"Another night," Harry said. "If that's all right. There's a little one here. I'd hate to frighten her."

Old Quil looked at him for a long time. Then he looked at Claire. Then back.

"Another night," he agreed, and closed his eyes.

Harry stood up.

Sam was there.

He'd come around the fire without Leah hearing him, which shouldn't have been possible, and he was standing three feet away with the firelight on one side of his face. He was taller than Harry. Four inches, maybe. Broader through the chest. He'd been the biggest thing on the reservation for two years until Jacob had his growth spurt, and he stood like it.

Leah's heart was going like a hammer. She didn't let it get to her face.

Sam held out his hand. "Sam Uley."

Harry took it.

Leah watched the handshake. She watched the tendons stand up in the back of Sam's hand, and his knuckles go pale, the way they did when he was holding onto something he meant to hold onto. She watched Harry's hand, which didn't do anything at all. His face didn't do anything either. He looked at Sam the way he'd looked at the bacon that morning, interested and patient.

"Sam," Harry said. "Thanks for having us."

"Sue's call."

"So I gather." Harry let go. "Appreciate it anyway, Sammy."

The air went out of the whole beach.

Nobody had called Sam Uley *Sammy* since he was nine, when a kid from Forks tried it once at a Little League game and went home with a fat lip and a note. Leah had been eight, in the bleachers with a snow cone. She remembered the note. She remembered Sam's face on the bus after.

Sam's face did exactly the same thing now.

"It's Sam," he said.

"Course it is," said Harry.

Leah turned around very fast and looked at the ocean. She made a noise. She tried to turn the noise into a cough. It didn't work. Across the fire, on her log, Sue was looking down into her thermos cup with enormous concentration, and her shoulders were moving.

When Leah turned back around, Sam had gone back to Emily. He was standing with his arms folded again, looking at the fire, and a muscle was jumping in his jaw.

And Emily was standing in front of her.

Up close, the scars were the first thing. They always had been. Three long lines down the right side of her face, from the hairline to the jaw, pulling the corner of her eye down and the corner of her mouth with it, old and silvery and puckered. Leah had seen them for the first time in the hospital in Forks, the day after. She'd thought about them every single night for a year and a half, in Sam's head, with Sam looking at them from the inside. She knew their shape better than her own face.

She looked at them now and they were her cousin's face. That was all. Emily's face, with the scars on it, and the firelight, and a smear of flour on her chin she didn't know was there.

"Hi, Lee," Emily said.

"Hi, Em."

"You look good."

"I've been eating."

Emily laughed. It came out surprised, and a little wet. Then she stepped in and hugged her, quick, the way you'd touch a stove to see if it was hot, and Leah stood there and let it happen, stiff for a second. Then she lifted one hand and patted her cousin twice on the back. Two pats. Like she was burping a baby.

Emily stepped back. Her eyes were bright.

"Your hair got long," she said.

"There's no hairdresser in Turnagain."

"Where?"

"Canada," said Leah. "Long story."

Emily looked over Leah's shoulder at Harry, who was being handed a hot dog on a stick by Seth, who was looking at him like a golden retriever looks at a tennis ball. She looked at the pie boxes stacked on the end of her folding table.

"Nine pies?" Emily said.

"I asked her first," said Harry, from behind Leah.

Emily blinked at him. Then at Leah.

"Inside joke," Leah said.

Emily looked at her for a second longer. Something moved in her face, slow, like she'd just worked out a word problem and didn't like the answer, or did, and couldn't tell which. Leah had inside jokes now. Leah had inside jokes with somebody.

"Okay," Emily said softly. "There's chili."

---

There was chili. There were hot dogs, three packages of them, and a sheet pan of blueberry muffins as big as softballs, and Sue's potato salad, and somebody had brought a bag of those little powdered donuts, and there were the pies.

Harry ate.

Leah had seen him eat a caribou. She'd seen him eat three burgers at the Prospector and four plates of turkey at Dot's and six slices of pie that afternoon. She still had to stop and watch.

He ate four hot dogs off sticks while standing up, talking to Billy about fishing. He sat down on the log next to her with a bowl of chili and ate that, and then another one, and then he picked up a fifth hot dog and looked at it in the firelight with deep suspicion.

"Clearwater," he said.

"What."

"What is this."

"It's a hot dog."

"It's a sausage that's given up." He turned it over. "Look at it. It's the color of a traffic cone. It's an insult to my entire country. In England we'd have it arrested."

"So don't eat it."

"I didn't say I wouldn't *eat* it," Harry said, and ate it in three bites.

Across the fire, Collin and Brady were counting out loud.

"Six," Brady said.

"That's seven," said Collin. "He had one when Seth gave it to him."

"Seven," Brady breathed.

Harry reached into the cooler at his feet without looking and pulled out two cans and tossed them across the fire. Collin caught his. Brady fumbled his and caught it on the second try against his chest. Root beer.

"Lads," Harry said. "Very impressive. Don't count my hot dogs. It's rude."

"Sorry," said Brady. Then: "How big are you? Like — as a wolf?"

"Big enough."

"Bigger than Jake?"

"I've not met Jake," said Harry. "Not in person. I'm told he's very tall."

Brady and Collin looked at each other with their mouths open.

Paul had been watching the whole thing from across the fire with his arm around Rachel and his face getting darker and darker, and now he leaned forward with his elbows on his knees.

"So, Leah," Paul said. "You go to Canada and bring back a souvenir?"

Leah turned her head and looked at him.

She didn't think about it. It came up out of her the way it always had, from the same cold place, flat and fast as a knife coming out of a drawer.

"Say one more word, Lahote," she said, "and I'll tell Rachel what you were thinking about in the shower last February."

Paul's face went the color of the sand.

Rachel turned her head slowly and looked at him. "What were you thinking about in the shower last February?"

"*Nothing.*"

"Paul."

"It was *nothing,* it was before you even — "

"Before I even *what?*"

"Clearwater," said Harry beside her.

She turned.

He was holding up one of Emily's blueberry muffins between his thumb and finger like a man presenting the crown jewels. His face was completely serious.

"What is this," he said.

"It's a muffin."

"It's the size of my head."

"Emily makes them like that."

"I think I'm in love," Harry said.

"You said that about the *pie.*"

"I'm a man of large appetites, Clearwater. I contain multitudes."

And Leah laughed.

It got away from her the way it had been getting away from her for six weeks. The big, loud, stupid goose honk, right out of the middle of her chest, with her head back. She reached over and took the muffin out of his hand and bit about a third of it off the top, and chewed, and gave the rest back to him, and he looked down at the wreckage of it in his hand with an expression of deep personal betrayal, and she laughed harder.

When she could see again, every single person around the fire was looking at her.

Jared had a hot dog halfway to his mouth. Embry had sat down on the sand without seeming to notice he'd done it. Quil had stopped walking with Claire on his shoulders, and Claire was pulling on his ears, and he wasn't moving. Paul had stopped arguing with Rachel. Rachel had stopped arguing with Paul. They were both just looking.

Sam was looking at the fire. He was the only one.

"What," said Jared, very quietly, to nobody, "is *happening.*"

On the log beside him, Kim put her chin in her hand.

"She's flirting," Kim said.

"Leah doesn't flirt."

"Jared," said Kim, with great patience. "She's *flirting.*"

Leah felt her face go hot. Hot from the inside, which never happened to her, not even after a run.

She turned on Kim. Opened her mouth to say something that would take Kim's face off.

Beside her, Harry took a big bite out of what was left of the muffin and said, with his mouth full, "She's quite bad at it, honestly. You should've seen her at the hot springs. Nearly drowned me."

Leah closed her mouth. She looked at him.

She punched him in the arm, hard. He didn't move. It was like punching the side of the car.

She laughed again anyway.

---

The sun went into the ocean. For a minute after it was gone the whole sky over the water stayed lit, orange going to pink going to a deep clean violet, and the sea stacks stood up black against it, and the fire burned blue and green at the edges. Then the color started to drain out from the top down.

Quil had finally let Claire down off his shoulders. She'd found a pink plastic tiara in Emily's bag, and put it on him, and now she was making him walk up and down in front of the fire wearing it while she clapped. He did it. He did it with total dignity, seventeen years old and six foot four with a pink tiara on his head, and when she got tired of clapping and held her arms up he scooped her off the sand and settled her against his chest, and she put her face in his neck and yawned.

Leah was watching Harry.

He'd gone still. He'd been halfway through a slice of pecan pie on a paper plate, and the fork had stopped on the way to his mouth, and he was looking at Quil and Claire. At the air between them.

His face had gone flat. The way it had gone flat on the mountain at the old mine, when he'd held up Kroll's ring to the blue light and counted.

"Clearwater," he said, very low.

"What?"

"What's that?"

"What's what?"

"Between them." He didn't take his eyes off the space between Quil's chest and the little girl's head. "There's a line. Thin. Like fishing line. Gold. Comes out of the middle of his chest and goes into her. It's pulled tight." He watched Claire shift in Quil's arms. "It moves when she moves. He doesn't. He just — goes with it."

Leah looked. She couldn't see anything. Quil, and a sleepy kid in a fleece jacket, and the fire.

"You can see it," she said.

"If I look the right way. Like heat coming off a road."

"That's the imprint," she said.

He didn't say anything. He turned his head slowly and looked around the fire. At Jared and Kim on their log. At Paul and Rachel. At Sam, standing behind Emily with one hand on her shoulder.

"All of them," he said.

"All of them."

"Sam's is thicker." His voice had gone very quiet. "It's been there longer. It's — " He stopped. "Your brother?"

"No."

"And you."

Her heart did something. "You tell me."

"Nothing," Harry said. He looked at her, finally. "Not a thread on you. Not a thread on me. I know. I looked."

"When?"

"At the mine. The second I saw your face. Again at the bar. Again at Liard, after." He put the fork down on the plate. "Again on your mum's porch last night. I look every time, Clearwater. I'd have told you."

She looked at him for a long moment in the last of the light.

*I checked,* she'd told Jacob on the Skagit flats. *Every time.* She hadn't known he'd been checking too. She hadn't known that was a thing you could do from the outside.

"Okay," she said. It came out rough.

"Okay," he agreed.

"Best thing that ever happened to me," said Paul.

They both looked up.

He'd come around the fire. Of course he had. Wolf ears. He was standing over them with his hands in the pockets of his cut-offs and his chin out, and Rachel was right behind him with her arms folded and an expression on her face like she'd come along mostly to see what happened.

"Imprinting," Paul said. "You're asking about it. It's the best thing that ever happened to me." He jerked his head at Rachel. "You see her and that's it. That's everything. Everything else just — goes. You'd do anything for her."

"Anything," Harry said.

"*Anything.*"

Harry looked past him at Rachel. "And if she'd said no?"

"She didn't say no."

"I said no for a month," said Rachel.

Paul turned around. "That was different."

"It was a *month,* Paul." Rachel looked at Harry. She had her father's eyes, dark and dry and amused. "He'd have been whatever I wanted. That's how it works. Friend. Brother. Guy who mows my lawn. If I told him to go away, he'd have gone, and been miserable for the rest of his life, and smiled about it every time he saw me." She shrugged. "So I had a choice. Technically."

Paul looked hurt. "You love me."

"I do," Rachel said. "Mostly. Shut up."

Harry was quiet. Everybody near the fire had stopped pretending not to listen. Jared and Kim. Embry, sitting on the sand. Quil, standing with Claire asleep on his chest in her tiara, his face gone careful. Sue and Billy and Old Quil. Sam, behind Emily, very still.

"Where I come from," Harry said, "there's a kind of magic that ties one creature to another. The one who's tied does whatever the other one needs. Can't help it. Can't want to stop. Gets ill if they're sent away. Feels grateful for it."

"So?" said Paul.

"So we call it a house-elf bond." Harry turned his paper plate around in his hands. "I've got one. Inherited him along with a house, years ago. Little old elf called Kreacher. My mate Hermione's been at me to free him for nine years, and every time I bring it up he threatens to throw himself off the roof." He looked up at Quil. At the thin gold line Leah couldn't see. "The bond looks like that. Same color. Same knot, right in the middle of the chest. Runs one way."

Nobody said anything. The fire popped.

"There's nastier ones," Harry went on, in the same level voice. "Ones people put on other people, on purpose. I've cut a few of those off in my time. In a cellar outside Bucharest, once, on nine girls. Those look like that too." He breathed out through his nose. "Honestly, mate? It looks like a slave bond. It looks so much like one I'd have arrested somebody for it in Romania."

Paul's hands came out of his pockets.

Across the fire, Sam took his hand off Emily's shoulder.

Leah felt it go through all of them at once, the way it would have gone through the pack link if they'd been phased: Paul's temper coming up hot, Jared standing up off his log, Embry's head coming around, the kids by the net going quiet. Every wolf on First Beach turned toward the man on the log with the paper plate, all at the same time, like dogs hearing the same whistle.

Harry didn't stand up. He sat there with his pie.

"I'd never hurt her," Quil said. He said it quietly, holding Claire. His voice shook a little. "Ever."

Harry looked at him. "I know you wouldn't, mate," he said. "That's the bit that worries me. You couldn't."

Quil opened his mouth and didn't say anything.

"I chose Sam," said Emily.

She'd come around the fire. She was standing beside Paul with her hands in the pockets of her jacket and her chin up, and the last purple light from the sky was on the scarred side of her face.

"I want you to know that," Emily said. "Whatever it looks like to you. I said no to him. For a long time. And then I chose him."

Harry looked at her.

Leah watched him look. She watched his eyes go to the scars, and stay there, and read them. Three long lines. Hairline to jaw. Something with claws, and a lot of weight behind it, and a temper. She watched him put that together with *I said no to him for a long time,* and get the answer, and she watched his face go very, very still.

He looked past Emily at Sam.

Sam looked back.

Nobody on the beach moved. The surf came in and went out. Down by the net, Brady had his hand clamped over Collin's mouth.

Leah reached over and put her hand on Harry's forearm. On the old leather. She could feel the heat of him through it, and the arm under it, gone hard as a fence rail.

"Recreationally," she said quietly.

For a second he didn't move. Then he breathed out, long and slow, and the arm under her hand let go.

"Right," he said.

He looked back at Emily. "Then good," he said. "I'm glad. That's what counts."

Emily held his eyes a second longer. Then she nodded, once, and went back around the fire to Sam.

Harry looked around at all of them, all the wolves standing up and staring at him with their fists closed. He shrugged. A big, loose, lazy shrug, all the way up to his ears.

"Look," he said. "It's real. I can see it's real. It also looks like a lead on a dog. But hey. Not my magic. Not my beach." He picked his fork back up. "Whatever floats your boat, man."

And he ate the rest of his pie.

There was a long silence.

"Did he just," said Jared.

"He did," said Kim.

Leah didn't hear the rest, because she was laughing again.

She couldn't help it. It was the shrug. A year and a half. A year and a half she'd thought it, every night, in every one of their heads — *it's a leash, it's a leash, he didn't choose her, it chose him, and nobody gets to say so* — and every one of them had heard her think it and called it bitter. Called it Leah being Leah. Jealous, sour, poor Leah, who couldn't get over it.

And this guy had looked at it for about four minutes and said it out loud at a council fire with his mouth full of pecan pie. And then shrugged. And then asked for seconds.

She laughed until she had to put her forehead down on his shoulder.

"He's not wrong, though," said Embry, from the sand.

Quil looked down at him. "*Embry.*"

"I'm just saying." Embry hadn't imprinted. He was the oldest of Sam's wolves who hadn't. He was looking at the fire with a funny expression. "It's kind of a lot. When you say it like that."

Nobody answered him.

Across the fire, Sam had sat back down on the log beside Emily. He was looking at Leah with her forehead on another man's shoulder, laughing so hard she was shaking. Leah could feel him looking.

She didn't lift her head.

---

"*Volleyball,*" Brady yelled, about ten minutes later, because he was thirteen, and because the silence around the fire had gone on long enough that somebody had to do something about it.

Seth was on his feet before Brady finished the word. "Leah and Harry are with me."

"Fine," said Paul, too fast. "Jared. Embry. Brady."

"That's four against three," said Collin.

"I'm with Seth," said Rachel, and walked across the sand and stood next to Leah with her arms folded.

Paul stared at her. "*Rach.*"

"I want to see what he does," Rachel said.

Collin climbed up on the cooler to referee. Quil sat down on a log with Claire asleep against his chest, still wearing the tiara. Sam stayed where he was, next to Emily, and didn't say anything. The elders turned their chairs.

The sky was almost gone. There was a strip of dark green left low over the water and then nothing, and no moon, and the fire threw an orange circle out across the sand that just about reached the net and stopped.

"I can't see a thing," said Rachel.

"Fair," said Harry.

He didn't do anything. Leah was watching him and he didn't move his hands or his face. But lights came up out of the dark over the net, one after another, small and amber, a dozen of them, and hung there in a long loose line ten feet off the sand, like somebody had strung porch lights between two poles that weren't there. The sand under them went gold. The net threw a shadow.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Huh," said Billy finally.

"He does that," said Sue.

Old Quil opened one eye, looked at the lights, and closed it again.

---

Leah hadn't played since high school. It didn't matter. Wolves didn't play volleyball the way people played volleyball. Jared served the first one so hard it hissed. Seth dug it out of the sand. Rachel got a hand on it. It came off her fingers high and wobbling toward the back of the court, a lost ball, the kind you watch drop.

Harry was already under it.

He set it without looking. Leah didn't see where he'd put it. She didn't need to. She was already in the air, because she'd known where it would be the same way she'd known where he'd be on the plateau with the caribou, and she came down on it with her whole arm and drove it into the sand at Paul's feet so hard it buried itself.

Paul looked at the hole.

"One-nothing," said Collin from the cooler.

It went like that.

It shouldn't have. They'd never played together. They'd never played anything together except *first one to the top buys.* But every time the ball came over the net she knew where he was, and he knew where she was, and it was like the hunt and it was like Liard and it was like the dock, and they talked the whole time, nonstop, through every single point.

"Clearwater. Left."

"I *know* left."

"You were drifting."

"I don't drift."

"You drift like a boat with no anchor. It's very distracting."

"*You're* distracting."

"Thank you."

"That wasn't a compliment — "

"Sounded like one — "

"*Ball,*" said Rachel, and Leah got it, and Harry put it up, and she put it down on top of Embry's head.

"Sorry," said Leah, not sorry at all.

"Four-one," said Collin.

On the other side of the net, Jared was looking at the two of them like he was watching a car crash in slow motion.

Paul was getting red. He started hitting harder. He hit a spike straight at Rachel's face to see what she'd do, and Harry's arm came across in front of her and the ball bounced off his forearm with a sound like somebody slapping a side of beef and went straight back over the net into Brady's stomach. Brady sat down.

"Where'd you learn to *play?*" said Brady, from the sand.

"Didn't," said Harry. "I played a different game. On brooms."

"*Brooms?*"

"Long story. I was the one who catches the little thing."

"What little thing?"

"Serve, Lahote," Leah said.

It went to fourteen-all. The lights hung over the net. Everybody around the fire had stopped talking and was just watching — Billy leaning forward in his chair with his elbows on his knees, Sue with her thermos forgotten, Emily with her chin on Sam's shoulder, Kim with her chin in her hand and a look on her face like she was watching her favorite soap opera. Even Sam was watching.

Paul went up for the big one. He went up high, higher than he should've been able to, and came down on the ball with everything he had.

Leah went up to meet him.

She got both hands on it at the top of the net, square, a wall, and the ball came off her palms like it had been shot out of a cannon. It hit Jared in the forearms on the way back across. It went off his forearms at an angle nobody could've planned, and up, and back, and over the far end of the court, and over the fire's circle, and over the dark beach, and came down in the surf forty yards out with a little white splash.

The backwash took it. Leah watched it ride out past the first line of breakers, a pale dot bobbing in the dark water, going away.

"I got it!" said Seth.

He was already kicking off his shoes. He had his shirt half over his head and was jogging toward the water, because the Pacific in October was fifty degrees and none of them felt cold anymore and he'd fetched a thousand balls out of that surf in his life.

"Sit, kid," said Harry.

"It's fine, I don't get cold — "

Harry was already walking.

He went down the beach. Not fast. Hands in the pockets of the leather jacket. Down out of the firelight and the amber light, across the dry sand and onto the hard wet sand where the waves had been, and into the foam.

He didn't stop when he got to the foam.

The water came up the beach in a long white sheet and slid in under his boots, and he kept walking, and his boots stayed on top of it. He walked out over it. Out onto the dark water, where the next wave was coming in, a big one, standing up black and glassy in the dark, curling at the top.

He walked up the front of it.

Up the face of the wave like it was the side of a hill. Over the top as it broke, the white water pouring down behind him. Down the back. Two of the amber lights had come loose from the line over the net and drifted after him, bobbing along a few feet over his shoulders like lanterns somebody was carrying for him, so they could all see him going out there.

Another wave. Up, over, down.

He got to the ball out beyond the second line of breakers, where the swells rolled big and smooth. He bent down and picked it up out of the water. He shook it off. He tucked it under his arm.

He turned around and walked back.

One hand still in his jacket pocket. Over the swells. Up and over the breakers from behind. Across the white water sliding back down the sand. Onto the beach.

He walked up into the firelight and stopped at the edge of the court.

His boots were dry. His jeans were dry, all the way to the ankle. There wasn't a drop of salt water on him anywhere.

Nobody on First Beach said anything at all.

The fire popped. The surf went out. Somewhere in the dark the gulls were complaining about something on the sea stacks.

On Quil's chest, Claire woke up. She lifted her head off his shoulder with her tiara crooked over one eye and looked at Harry, and pointed at him with one fat little finger.

"Again," Claire said.

Harry tossed the ball to Collin on the cooler. Collin didn't catch it. It bounced off his chest and rolled away across the sand.

Harry looked around at everybody. At Paul with his mouth hanging open. At Jared. At Seth, standing at the edge of the water with his shirt in his hand. At Brady and Embry and Rachel and Kim. At Billy and Sue and Old Quil, who had both eyes open now. At Sam.

"What?" Harry said.

Nobody answered.

"Why's everybody looking at me like that?"

"You," said Paul. "You just — you walked. On the — you *walked* on the — "

"Oh," said Harry. "Right."

He seemed to think about it.

"Yeah. Bit of trivia for you, since we're all friends now," he said. "Jesus was a wizard."

And his hair started to grow.

It came down out of its mess in long brown waves, down over his ears, down past his jaw, all the way to his shoulders. A beard came in on his face, short and neat and brown. The leather jacket rippled and ran and went white, and kept going, down past his knees to his ankles in a long white robe with a blue sash across it. His boots were sandals. And behind his head, very softly, a ring of gold light came up, round as a dinner plate, so that it lit up the hair from behind and threw a glow down onto his shoulders.

He lifted both his hands a little at his sides, palms out.

It was every Sunday-school picture Leah had ever seen. It was the painting in her grandmother's hallway, the one with the lamb. It was standing on First Beach next to a volleyball net, under a string of floating porch lights, with sand on its sandals.

Kim made a sound like a teakettle. Emily put both hands over her mouth. Old Quil's eyebrows went all the way up into his hairline, which Leah had never seen happen in twenty years.

And then Harry turned around.

He turned to Leah. He bent down, unhurried, robes and all, and picked a hot dog bun out of the open bag on top of the cooler. He held it up in the gold light. He broke it in half, slowly, with great solemnity, the way a priest would.

He held one half out to her.

"And for you," he said, in a deep and holy voice, "only the best of my body."

Leah looked at the bun.

She looked at him. At the beard, and the halo, and the sandals, and the green eyes, which were exactly the same and were waiting.

She took the bun.

"Amen," said Leah, and bit it.

First Beach came apart.

Paul sat down in the sand where he stood, and then lay down flat on his back and stayed there, wheezing. Jared bent over with his hands on his knees. Seth sat down in the wet sand at the edge of the water with his shirt over his face. Brady and Collin were holding onto each other. Rachel was crying. Kim was crying. Emily had turned around and was standing with her back to everybody and her shoulders shaking. Embry was lying on the ground making a noise like a seal.

Billy Black laughed so hard his chair started to roll backward toward the fire, and Sue had to lunge and grab the handles.

"Lord help us," said Sue, holding onto the chair, tears running down her face.

The gold ring faded out from behind his head. The robe went brown and cracked and turned back into leather. The hair pulled back in and stood straight up off his scalp like it always did. He stood there in his jacket and his jeans and his dry boots with the other half of the bun in his hand.

"Lord Potter, technically," said Harry, and took a bite.

Leah was laughing so hard she couldn't stand up. She bent over with her hands on her knees next to Rachel and laughed until her stomach hurt, the goose honk, over and over. And through the tears, across the fire, she saw Sam.

He was sitting next to Emily with his elbows on his knees. He wasn't laughing. But the corner of his mouth had gone up, on one side, and stayed there, and he was shaking his head slowly at the sand.

"Wait," said Brady, when he could talk. "Wait. For *real?*"

Harry chewed his half of the bun. He swallowed.

"Water into wine," he said, "is second-year Transfiguration. Bit fiddly with the bouquet. Loaves and fishes is Gamp's Law. You can't make food out of nothing — it's one of the five exceptions — but you can make more of it if you've got some to start with. Textbook. My mate Hermione did twelve inches of parchment on it when we were fifteen. Got a hundred and twelve percent."

"Out of a *hundred?*" said Collin.

"Out of a hundred."

"And the walking?" said Embry, from the ground.

"Showing off," said Harry. "Bit of a charm. Fourth year, if you can do it in a gale. He clearly could."

"And the — " Rachel wiped her eyes. "The coming back from the dead thing?"

Harry didn't answer straight away.

He looked at the fire. It was only a second. Less. He stood there with the bread in his hand and looked at the blue and green flames along the driftwood, and his face went somewhere a long way from First Beach, the way it had gone at Liard right before she'd stopped him.

"That one's harder," he said.

He took another bite of the bun.

"Takes three days," he said, chewing, "if you do it properly."

Then he grinned, and the canine showed, and the moment closed over like water.

Leah straightened up. She looked at him across the sand in the amber light.

She filed it. Next to the scars that weren't there. Next to Trudel's missing voice, and *some of us went*, and a leather jacket that smelled faintly of a big dog. The file was getting thick.

He caught her looking. His eyebrows went up.

"Fourth date?" she said, quietly, under all the noise.

"Fifth," said Harry. "At least."

---

Nobody told stories that night. Billy said they could try, but nobody would hear a word over Paul, who kept starting to laugh again out of nowhere and having to walk down the beach by himself.

The fire burned down to a long red bed of coals with blue running through it. Emily packed up the chili. Kim and Jared left first, then Collin and Brady, walking backward up the path so they could keep looking at Harry until the last possible second. Quil carried Claire up to his truck asleep, tiara and all.

Leah went down to the water.

She stood at the edge of the wet sand where the foam ran up and stopped an inch from her boots, and looked out at the dark where the ball had gone. Behind her, up at the fire, she could hear Harry. He was sitting on the log between Billy's chair and Seth with his elbows on his knees, and Billy was telling him about the time her dad had hooked a sixty-pound king off Cake Rock and been towed halfway to James Island in a rowboat. Seth was doing the sound effects.

She heard Sam coming. He didn't try to be quiet about it.

He stopped beside her. Not close. Six feet. He put his hands in his pockets and looked at the water too.

For a while neither of them said anything.

"He's right," Sam said.

Leah didn't turn her head. "About what?"

"The leash." Sam was quiet. "I used to tell myself it wasn't my fault. Imprinting. I didn't choose it."

"You didn't."

"No." He breathed out. "But I chose what I did after. I chose to use the voice on you. *Don't think about Emily around me.*" He said it flat, the exact words, the exact way. "I chose that every time I said it. Nothing made me."

Leah stood very still.

"I'm sorry, Lee," Sam said.

Out loud. In words. On First Beach, in the dark, with the surf coming in.

She'd heard him think it a thousand times. Every night for a year and a half, under everything else, like a radio left on in another room. *Sorry, sorry, Lee, I'm sorry.* He'd never once opened his mouth and said it.

She looked at the water for a long time.

"Okay," she said.

He waited, like he thought there'd be more. There wasn't. She'd said what she meant. He seemed to understand that, after a minute.

"He makes you laugh," Sam said.

"Yeah."

"I never heard you laugh. In there. Not once."

"There was nothing funny in there, Sam."

He took that. He nodded slowly. Then, carefully, the way a man walks out onto ice: "Is it — "

"No," Leah said. "He looked. I looked. It's just me."

Sam let out a breath she hadn't known he was holding.

"Good," he said, and meant it, and she could hear that he meant it. Then he turned and walked back up the beach to Emily, who was waiting at the top of the path with the chili pot in her arms.

Leah stood at the edge of the water by herself.

She did the thing. Reached around inside her own head, every corner of it. Seth wasn't phased. Jacob was somewhere in the trees across the river, human, holding a baby. Nobody was in there. Nobody had been in there all night. Every wolf on First Beach had watched her laugh until she cried, and not one of them knew what she'd been thinking while she did it.

They'd have to guess.

Boots on the sand behind her. Earth after rain, pine, cold stone, old leather.

"All right, Clearwater?"

"Yeah," she said. "I'm all right."

"Want to go home?"

*Home.* The cave on Qw'aatilla, with the fire and the moose and the brass fish on its nail, and both doors open.

"Yeah," she said.

At the top of the beach Harry stopped and dug in his pocket and tossed the keys underhand to Seth, who caught them against his chest and looked down at them like they were made of gold.

"Lot to your mum's," Harry said. "Five miles an hour. Headlights on. If there's a scratch on her in the morning, kid, I'll know."

"*Seth Clearwater,*" said Sue.

"Ask your mother," said Harry.

Sue looked at her son, and at the keys, and at the long black car up in the lot under the one streetlight. She looked at Harry. She looked at Leah.

"Five miles an hour," Sue said. "I'm riding with you."

Seth whooped so loud the gulls went up off the sea stacks for the third time that night.

Leah and Harry walked south down First Beach in the dark, along the hard sand at the edge of the tide, toward the black shape of the headland at the end with the arch in it. The fire got small behind them. The amber lights from over the net drifted along overhead for a while, then went out one at a time.

Halfway down the beach she shoved him sideways into the surf.

He went. He walked along on top of the foam beside her with his hands in his pockets, his boots dry, looking at her with his head tipped to one side.

"Show-off," Leah said.

"Amen," said Harry.

She laughed. The goose honk. It went out over the water and back along the beach in the dark, all the way up to where a few wolves were still standing around the coals, and they turned their heads and listened to it go, and didn't know why.

She liked that a lot.
