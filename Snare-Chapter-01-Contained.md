# Snare

## Chapter One: Contained

---

### I. Aisle Six

*Forks, Washington — three weeks after Renesmee*

The list was written on the back of an electric bill in his mother's handwriting, and the third line said *the GOOD peanut butter*, with *GOOD* underlined twice.

Seth Clearwater was nineteen years old and could run down a deer on a mountainside without breathing hard, and his mother still underlined things for him.

He pushed the cart past the bakery case at Thriftway with one hand and held the list in the other, squinting at it under the fluorescent tubes. He'd been awake for something like thirty-one hours. Jacob had been pulling doubles since the birth, which meant Seth had been pulling doubles, which meant Leah had been pulling doubles and letting everyone in the pack mind know exactly how she felt about it in a looping internal monologue that had a lot of words in it his mother wouldn't let him say in the house.

Three weeks. Three weeks since Bella's heart had stopped in that upstairs room in the Cullen house and started again as something else. Three weeks since Jake had walked down the stairs ready to kill a baby and had instead gone stupid and soft in the face and stayed that way. Three weeks since Sam's pack had come through the trees at night and turned around again because you didn't hurt an imprint. Ever. It was the oldest law they had.

Seth had been thinking about that a lot. About how it had hit Jake. About Sam with Emily, Jared with Kim, Quil with little Claire. It looked like getting hit by a truck that loved you.

*Eggs, three dozen.* He got four. *Bread.* He got three loaves. *Ground beef x5*. He looked at the case and got eight, because he'd seen his mother's face at dinner the night before when the serving dish came back empty and Leah was still reaching for it.

*Cereal, whatever's on sale.*

Aisle six.

He came around the end-cap too fast. Thriftway had built a pyramid of Folgers at the head of the aisle, big red cans stacked about chest high, with a cardboard sign on top that said FALL INTO SAVINGS and had a cartoon leaf on it. His cart's front wheel had a wobble. The wobble caught the bottom row.

He heard the can go before he saw it. Then the next one. Then the whole left face of the pyramid leaned out like it had decided something.

He moved without thinking about it, which was the only way he ever moved fast. The cart went one way and he went the other. He caught a can with his right hand and one against his chest with his forearm and trapped a third against the shelf with his hip, and two more hit the linoleum and rolled, and one dropped past his shoulder straight toward the head of the woman standing on her toes at the top shelf.

It stopped.

It did. He saw it. For half a second, maybe less, a three-pound can of Classic Roast hung in the air about four inches above a mess of dark curls, perfectly still, the way a frame freezes when the DVD player skips. Then it dropped, and the woman turned and caught it in both hands like she'd been expecting it the entire time.

Seth stood there with coffee in his arms.

"Well," the woman said. "That's one way to say hello."

She had an accent that went slow in the middle of words, and she looked at him. She had brown eyes with something lighter in them, a ring around the pupil the color of strong tea held up to a window. Freckles across her nose, too many to count, and a little white scar through her left eyebrow. Her hair was piled up on her head and held there with what looked like a pencil, except the pencil was dark and carved. A green sweater with the sleeves shoved up past her elbows. There was a burn scar on the palm of her left hand, old and shiny, shaped like a leaf.

And the floor of Thriftway let go of him.

That was the only way he'd ever be able to explain it, later, to Jake, who'd nod, and to Leah, who wouldn't. Everything that held him down came loose. His mother. Leah. The pack. Jake's voice in his head. The ground. Every cord in him that ran out to something and anchored him to it snapped, all at once, and for one long awful second he was floating in the middle of aisle six with nothing tying him to the planet at all.

Then they all came back. Every one. And every one of them ran to her.

His mother was still his mother. Leah was still his sister. He'd still die for Jake. All of it stayed. All of it ran through her first now, like she'd become the center of the map and everything else got measured from where she stood.

He realized he was holding his breath. He let it out.

"Hi," he said.

The woman raised the scarred eyebrow. "Hi."

"I'm— sorry. That was me. The cart. It's got a wheel." He was still holding the coffee. He didn't know what to do with it. "Hi."

"You said that."

"Yeah." He set two cans carefully on the shelf, label out, and then turned the second one so the label matched the first, for some reason. "I'm Seth."

"Layla." She looked at the can in her hands, then up at him. Her mouth did something at one corner. "You catch a lot of coffee, Seth?"

"Not usually. That was a first." His face was hot. His face was always hot, wolves ran at a hundred and eight, but this was a new kind. "Are you okay? It didn't— it didn't hit you."

"No," she said. "It didn't."

She held his eyes a beat longer than she needed to. Then she looked away, up at the top shelf, where she'd been reaching. "You don't happen to know if this town sells coffee with chicory in it."

"With what?"

"That's a no." She sighed. "Pas de chicorée. Évidemment." She held up the can of Folgers like it had personally insulted her family. "Where I'm from, this is what you give people you don't like."

"Where are you from?"

"Louisiana. Outside Baton Rouge, then all over. Here, now." She put the can into a basket hooked over her elbow. The basket had one onion, a bag of rice, a bottle of hot sauce, and a box of kitchen matches. "Three weeks."

Three weeks. Seth did the math before he could stop himself. She'd moved to Forks the same week Renesmee was born. The same week the whole world had gone crazy. He'd been running the perimeter around the Cullen house nineteen hours a day and the whole time she'd been here, in town, buying rice.

"The diner's got okay coffee," he heard himself say. "Still no chicory. It's better than that, though. And they've got pie. The marionberry's really good. Do you know what marionberry is? It's like a blackberry but—" He made himself stop. "It's good."

"I'll keep it in mind."

"I could show you. Where it is. The diner." The diner was on the main road through town, it was in the most visible place in all of Forks, nobody had ever needed to be shown where it was. "Or anything. If you need anything. Like if you need help moving stuff. I'm strong. I mean, I can lift stuff."

She looked at him. The corner of her mouth went up again.

"You're sweet," Layla said, and the word went into him and lodged there like a fishhook. "I'm out on Calawah Way. The yellow house with the bad porch step."

"I can fix a porch step."

"I bet you can." She shifted the basket on her arm. Then she bent, picked up the two cans of Folgers that had rolled under the bottom shelf, and put them back on the pyramid's ruined face, one on top of the other. "Nice to meet you, Seth."

"You too. Yeah. Bye."

She walked away down aisle six. She didn't look back. At the end of the aisle she reached up, as if she were scratching her head, and touched the pencil-thing in her hair, and Seth smelled it then, under the floor wax and the bakery and the bleach they used in the meat section. Ozone. Like the air on the beach right before a lightning storm rolls in off the water. Cedar under that. Rain on a hot road.

He stood in the aisle long enough that a lady with a cart full of cat food said, "Excuse me, hon," in the tone that meant she'd said it once already.

---

Mrs. Pattison at register two had known his mother for thirty years. She rang up the ground beef and the eggs and the three loaves of bread and looked at him over the scanner.

"You okay, Seth? You look like you got hit with a two-by-four."

"I'm good," Seth said. "I'm really good."

"Your mom said you've been working nights."

"Yeah."

"Well, get some sleep." She bagged the peanut butter. The good kind. He'd almost forgotten it. He'd had to go back. "You look feverish."

He carried six bags out to his mother's truck in one trip and put them in the back and then stood in the parking lot in the drizzle with the keys in his hand. The yellow house on Calawah Way. The bad porch step. He knew the street. He'd run it a hundred times on patrol, along the back of the lots where the trees came down to the river.

He didn't remember starting the truck. He remembered pulling into his mother's driveway in La Push forty minutes later, which was twenty minutes longer than the drive took, and he remembered putting the groceries away, and then he remembered the screen door banging behind him as he went out the back and across the yard and into the trees.

He couldn't stand still. That was the whole reason.

He tugged his shorts off and tied them to his leg with the leather cord, and he let it come up through him the way it did now, easy, a shiver that started in the spine and kept going until his body caught up. The world went loud and sharp-edged. The rain had a hundred smells in it. Then the minds came on, the way they always did, like walking into a room where the TV's already playing.

Leah was on the north line, running. Jacob was lying on the Cullens' back lawn, half-asleep, watching Renesmee through the glass wall upstairs; Seth could feel the soft, idiot fog of contentment coming off him like heat off a stove.

*Hey,* Seth thought. *Hey, guys.*

He felt Leah's attention swing toward him the second before she went through his last hour like somebody flipping through a photo album. Aisle six. The cans. The can that stopped in the air. Brown eyes with the ring of tea in them. Freckles. The floor letting go.

Leah stopped running.

*Oh,* she said. *Oh, you have got to be kidding me.*

On the Cullens' lawn, Jacob's head came up off his paws.

*Seth?* he said. *Did you just—*

*Yeah,* Seth said. He sat down in the ferns behind his mother's house with the rain coming down through the cedars onto his back and couldn't stop his tail from going. *Yeah. I think I did.*

### II. The Bad Porch Step

*Calawah Way — five weeks later*

The roux was the color of a penny, and Layla Devereaux had been stirring it for thirty-five minutes.

She didn't stop. You didn't stop. Her grandmother had told her that when she was eight, standing on a chair in the kitchen in Port Allen with the window open and the river smell coming in. *You stop, it burns. It burns, you start over. Ain't no magic for it, chère.* Which had been a joke, because her grandmother could have stirred it with a flick of her wand from the next room and had never once done it, on principle. Some things you did with your hands.

So Layla did it with her hands. A wooden spoon her grandmother had carved. A cast-iron pot she'd carried in her trunk through four countries and six tombs. Flour and oil and patience, and the light going out of the sky outside the kitchen window while the rain came down on the Calawah valley the way it had come down every single day since she'd gotten here, steady and grey and unbothered.

Eight weeks in Forks. She'd come here because a realtor in Port Angeles had shown her a listing for a yellow two-bedroom on an acre with a river at the bottom of it and a price that said nobody else wanted it, and because she'd looked at a map of the Olympic Peninsula and seen how much green there was, and how far that green was from the nearest wizarding settlement of any size, and she'd thought: *good*. Nobody here would know her. Nobody would know what had happened in the tomb outside Chachapoyas, or what had happened to Teo, or why she didn't take contracts anymore. She could stir a roux and listen to the rain and let the scar on her palm stop aching.

That had been the plan.

The plan had not accounted for Seth Clearwater.

She added the trinity, onion and celery and bell pepper, and the pot hissed and sent up a smell that went straight to the back of her skull and turned on every light in her childhood. She stirred. On the windowsill, a little brass bell she'd hung there the first night chimed, once, softly.

Somebody had crossed the ward line at the bottom of the drive.

She didn't look up. She knew what it was. She'd known for five weeks what it was, every time. The bell rang for anything with a heartbeat bigger than a raccoon's that came up her property, and it rang one note for human and a different note for not. She'd laid that ward the first night with her shoes still muddy, because six years of tombs teaches you to do that before you unpack your toothbrush.

The bell rang the second note. It had always rung the second note for him.

Footsteps on the porch. Heavy. He was a big boy. He stepped over the bad step, which he had fixed on the third day she'd known him and which was still called the bad step because he'd decided that was funny. Then a knock: two, a pause, one.

"It's open," she called.

The door opened. Rain and cold air came in, and him. Seth filled the kitchen doorway, and he had to duck a little under the frame, which he did every time without noticing. He was wearing cut-off jeans and a grey T-shirt and nothing else, in the first week of November, on the Olympic Peninsula, in the rain. He was carrying a paper sack in one hand and a bunch of grocery-store flowers in the other.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey yourself." She kept stirring. "What's in the bag?"

"Mom sent bread. She made it this morning. And she said to say thank you for the pralines, she's never had them, and she wants to know how you make them so she can stop eating mine." He put the bag on the counter. He held up the flowers, a cone of cellophane with orange mums and some carnations dyed a blue no carnation had ever been. "These are from me."

"Those are from Thriftway."

"They're from me *at* Thriftway." He looked around for somewhere to put them. "Do you have a vase?"

"Under the sink. There's a mason jar."

He found it. He filled it at the tap, very carefully, and put the flowers in it, and arranged them, and then set them in the middle of her little table, where she'd already laid out two bowls and two spoons. He stood back and looked at it and nodded like he'd built something.

She watched him do it and did not let her face do anything.

Five weeks. He had come by on the second day with a toolbox and fixed the step and then sat on it for two hours talking to her about tide pools while she pretended to be busy in the yard. He'd come by on the fourth day with a cooler of smoked salmon from his mother. He'd come the sixth day and the seventh. He'd walked her down to the river at the bottom of the lot and shown her where the steelhead would come up when the rain got serious in December, and pointed out an otter slide in the bank, and told her the Quileute word for it, and then admitted he'd made the word up. He'd never once tried to kiss her. He'd never once touched her except by accident, and every time it happened—a hand on a mug, his shoulder against hers on the porch swing—she'd felt the heat coming off his skin, a dry fever-heat like a cast-iron pan on low.

A hundred and eight, she'd guessed. Maybe more. A man with that temperature should have been in an ICU with his brain cooking. Seth Clearwater ate four cheeseburgers at a sitting and went swimming in the Pacific in October.

She'd checked the moon, of course. The first week. She'd sat on her porch on the night of the seventh of October, under a full moon that had for once made it through the cloud, with her wand in her lap and a Wolfsbane recipe she didn't have the ingredients for running through her head. Seth had walked up her drive at nine o'clock with a pizza. Two pizzas. He'd eaten one and three-quarters, and he'd looked up at the moon once and said it was pretty, and that was all.

So Seth was something else. Something with no moon in it.

And there were the others. The tall pale family people in town talked about, the doctor and his wife and their many beautiful adopted children, who'd left town recently or hadn't, depending on who was telling it. The girl who'd gotten sick. Layla had seen the doctor once, in the pharmacy, back in her first week, and every hair on her arms had stood up. She'd gone home and sat in her kitchen and told herself that this was a small town and she was jumpy and she'd spent too long underground with things that wanted to kill her. She'd been half right.

Sanguini, she'd thought, standing in the pharmacy. Except vampires didn't walk around in daylight, however cloudy, and they didn't look like that, carved and still. And they didn't buy cough drops for their patients and chat with the pharmacist about her grandson's baseball team.

She had a lot of questions about the town of Forks. She'd been very careful not to ask any of them out loud.

"It smells amazing," Seth said.

"Twenty more minutes. Sit down. You're making the kitchen small."

He sat. The chair complained. He put his elbows on the table and his chin in his hands and watched her stir.

"What's it called again?"

"Gumbo."

"I know it's gumbo. I mean the brown part."

"The roux."

"Roo," he said, trying it out. "Like the kangaroo."

"Spell it in your head the French way and you'll get close." She added the stock, a cup at a time, and the pot stopped hissing and went thick and dark. She put in the sausage. She put in the chicken. She put the lid on and turned it down and leaned on the counter and finally looked at him straight.

He was looking at her the way he always did. Like she was standing in the one bright spot in a dark room.

"How old are you, Seth?"

He blinked. "Nineteen. Twenty in March."

"Mm."

"Why?" Then his whole face went worried. "How old are you?"

"Twenty-four."

"Oh." He thought about it. "Is that a problem?"

"Ask me after dinner," Layla said.

---

They ate at the little table with the ugly blue carnations between them, and he had three bowls, and he told her his sister had called her the Thunderstorm for the first week, because of how she smelled.

Layla put her spoon down. "How I *smell*?"

He went red to the ears. "I mean. You smell nice. Like. Ozone. Like right before lightning." He stared into his bowl. "I have a really good nose."

"Apparently."

"That came out weird."

"It did come out a little weird."

"Leah says I should just stop talking sometimes."

"Leah," said Layla, "might be wise."

He laughed. He had a big, loose, uncomplicated laugh, and he did it with his whole body, and the table shook. She found she was smiling at her gumbo.

Leah. She'd met Leah once, in the second week, outside the diner, a long-limbed young woman with cropped black hair and a face like a shut door. Leah had looked at Layla the way you'd look at a dog that had wandered into your yard and might bite your little brother. She'd said *hey* and nothing else. Then she'd looked at Seth and something had passed between the two of them without either of them saying a word, a whole conversation in the set of a jaw. Layla had seen people do that before. Married couples. Twins. Teams that had gone into the dark together too many times.

She'd thought about that a lot afterward too.

After dinner the rain eased off to a drizzle and they took coffee out onto the porch. It was Folgers. She'd made her peace with it by doctoring it to within an inch of its life with cream and sugar and a pinch of cinnamon. The porch light was off. The only light came from the kitchen window behind them and a sodium lamp on a pole out on Calawah Way, a hundred yards off, buzzing orange through the wet.

They sat on the swing. It complained louder than the chair had.

"After dinner," Seth said.

"What?"

"You said to ask you. After dinner." He was looking at the yard, not at her. His hands were wrapped around his mug. "So I'm asking. Is it a problem?"

Layla looked at him for a long moment. Big hands. A face that hid nothing at all. Rain in his hair.

"No," she said. "It's not a problem."

He turned and looked at her, and she put her mug down on the porch boards and leaned over and kissed him.

He went very still. For a second she thought she'd misread the whole thing, the whole five weeks, and then his mug was on the boards next to hers and his hand was on the side of her face, and it was like putting her cheek against a sun-baked stone, and he kissed her back the way he did everything else: all the way, with his whole heart, without a single defense.

When she sat back, her pulse was going hard enough to feel in her teeth.

"Okay," Seth said, a little hoarsely. "Okay. Wow."

"Wow," she agreed.

He laughed, kind of breathless, and put his forehead against hers. Then he sat back too. Something went over his face, a cloud over a field. He took a breath.

"There's something I've gotta tell you," he said.

She waited.

"It's kind of— it's gonna sound crazy. Okay? Like, the craziest thing you've ever heard. And I'm not supposed to tell people. Like, ever. But you're— it's different for you. There's a rule. You're allowed to know." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Okay. So. The Quileute. We've got these old stories. About how we're descended from—"

"Seth."

"—from wolves, and when there's danger—"

"Seth." She put her hand on his arm. The heat came up through her palm. "My house hums when you walk up the steps."

He stopped.

"It what?"

"Hums." She pointed at the kitchen window, at the brass bell hanging inside it. "That bell rings when something with a heartbeat crosses the bottom of my drive. One note for a person. Another note for anything that isn't. It's rung the second note for you every single time for five weeks."

Seth stared at the bell. Then at her.

"You've got a," he said, "a *bell*."

"I do."

"A magic bell."

"Mm-hm."

"That knows."

She reached up to the back of her head and drew out the long dark stick that held her hair. Her curls came down around her shoulders. She held it up between them in the dim orange light, and he looked at it, and she watched him understand that it wasn't a pencil and had never been one.

"Cypress," she said. "Thunderbird tail feather. Twelve and a quarter inches. Made by a woman named Shikoba Wolfe's great-great-granddaughter in a workshop in Montana, and I've had it since I was eleven years old."

She flicked it, lightly, at the two lanterns hanging on hooks at the corners of the porch. They had been dark since she'd moved in. She'd never bought oil for them.

They lit. Small clean flames, white at the heart, burning on nothing.

Seth made a sound she'd never heard a grown man make.

"I'm a witch, chère," Layla said. "The real kind. The kind with a government and laws and schools and a whole world folded up underneath this one where you'd never look." She turned the wand in her fingers. "I'm not supposed to tell people either. Ever. There's a law about it, a big one, and a lot of very serious people whose job is enforcing it. So that's where we are."

Seth looked at the lanterns. He looked at the wand. He looked at her.

"The coffee can," he said. "At the store. It *stopped*."

"It was gonna hit me in the head."

"You did that."

"I did that."

"I thought I imagined it. I've been thinking about it for five *weeks*." He stood up. The swing rocked back and hit her calves. He walked to the edge of the porch and turned around and walked back, and his hands were moving like they wanted to grab something. "Okay. Okay. Can you— are there— is there, like, a broom? Do you fly on a broom?"

"I have a broom. It's in the shed. It's mostly for sweeping."

"*Mostly*."

She laughed. She couldn't help it. "Sit down before you break my porch."

He sat down. He was grinning so hard his face had to hurt. Then he stopped grinning, and his eyes went somewhere inward for a second, and when they came back to her they were serious.

"Can I show you mine?" he said. "Like— I mean my thing. The wolf thing. I'd have to go in the yard. And I'd have to take off my— I'll go behind the shed."

"Show me," Layla said.

He went down the steps, over the bad one, out into the drizzle. He went around the side of her little shed. She heard the denim come off. She heard a strap being knotted. Then she heard a sound she'd heard once before, in the Carpathians, on a contract that had gone wrong in a completely different way: a sound like a big sheet of canvas being ripped down the middle, with a crunch inside it, wet and quick.

The bell on the kitchen windowsill rang the second note so hard it jumped on its hook.

What came around the side of the shed was a wolf. It was the size of a horse. Bigger than a horse, maybe, in the chest. Sandy fur, lighter on the belly, darker down the spine, thick and wet and steaming faintly in the cold. Long legs. A long muzzle. Ears up. Huge dark eyes that were his. Exactly his. And around its right foreleg, a leather cord with a pair of cut-off jeans knotted into it.

It stopped at the bottom of the porch steps and looked up at her.

She'd faced a lot of things in the dark. Inferi in a sunken cenote in the Yucatán. A curse on a jade mask in Guatemala that had tried to wear her face. Teo's body, at the end, on the floor of that tomb. She had a good body for fear; it went very cold and very clear and steady when she needed it to, and it had kept her alive.

It went cold now, for one second. Then it went the other way, completely, and she started to laugh.

The wolf's ears went down. It looked at her like she'd hurt its feelings.

"No," she said. "No, baby, I'm not laughing at you. *Look* at you." She came down the steps. She came right down to the bottom of them, and the wolf lowered its enormous head until its eyes were level with hers, and she put out her hand with the leaf-shaped scar on the palm, and it pushed its nose into it. Wet and hot as a kettle. Its breath smelled of gumbo. "Look at you. Oh, you're beautiful. You're so *big*."

The wolf huffed. Its tail swept the wet grass behind it in a slow, enormous arc.

Then its ears snapped up and its head turned, sharp, toward the dark at the back of the lot where the trees went down toward the river, and she saw its whole body change. Something was talking to it. Something she couldn't hear.

### III. Party Line

*The north line, three miles out*

Leah Clearwater was eleven miles into her shift and in a mood about it when her brother phased in her head and brought a girl with him.

She'd been half-expecting it. That was the worst part. She'd been half-expecting it for five weeks, since the day he'd sat in the ferns behind the house with his tail going like a windshield wiper and his head full of freckles. Every time he phased since then he'd come in with that girl stuck to him like a burr. Layla laughing at something. Layla's hands in the dirt of her stupid garden. Layla in a green sweater. It had been five weeks of riding along in her brother's head while he fell off a cliff in slow motion and called it flying.

That was imprinting. She'd watched it from the inside before. She'd watched Sam's mind go to Emily every waking second, and she'd been there in the pack mind with him while he did it, and she'd learned to let it wash past her like cold water, because the alternative was drowning.

With Seth she'd decided to just be annoyed. Annoyed was easier.

So when he phased in tonight she braced for gumbo and porch swings and the specific feeling of a kiss, which—*nope*, she skipped that, she shoved past it so hard she almost tripped over a log—

—and landed on a stick of carved wood in a freckled hand, and two lanterns lighting themselves on nothing.

Leah stopped running.

She stood in the middle of a game trail in the dark with her ears straight up and her heart going and she went back through it. All of it. The bell in the window. *One note for a person. Another note for anything that isn't.* The wand. *Thunderbird tail feather. Twelve and a quarter inches.* The coffee can at the store, hanging in the air above a head of dark curls, which Leah had seen a hundred times in Seth's memory and had decided, every time, that he was remembering wrong because he'd been dazzled. *A government and laws and schools and a whole world folded up underneath this one.*

*Seth,* she said. *Seth. Is that woman holding a WAND?*

*Leah! Hey!* He was so happy. He was so happy it came through the bond like a radio turned all the way up. *Did you see? Did you see the lanterns?*

*I saw the lanterns.*

*She's a witch! Like a real one! She's got a broom!*

*She said it was for sweeping.*

*She said MOSTLY.*

Another mind came on. Jacob, groggy, from somewhere near the Cullen place; she could smell the river on him, and the sickly-sweet burn of vampire coming through the glass, and under that, the one smell in the world that made Jacob's whole mind go soft, Renesmee asleep upstairs.

*What,* Jacob said, *is going on.*

*Seth's girlfriend's a witch,* said Leah.

*Is that a figure of speech?*

*No.*

There was a pause while Jacob went through it, the way she had. She felt him do it. She felt him sit up on the Cullens' lawn.

*Holy crap,* said Jacob.

*Right?* said Seth.

In the backyard on Calawah Way, the witch had her hand on Seth's nose. Leah could feel it through him, small and cool and steady. She could hear her voice. *Look at you. Oh, you're beautiful.*

Leah breathed out through her teeth.

*Okay,* she said. *Okay. Nobody panic.*

*Nobody's panicking,* Seth said.

*I'm telling myself, Seth.*

She started moving again, a lope, then a run, cutting southeast through the second-growth toward the river. The rain came down through the hemlocks. She could smell the Calawah, cold and full, and the town beyond it, woodsmoke and wet asphalt and the fryer at the diner.

She was thinking fast. She'd always thought fast; it was one of the things that made her good at this, whatever Paul used to say. *She lit fire on nothing. She stopped a falling can with her brain. She's got a law, and a government, and people who enforce it.* The Cullens had a government too. Leah had been in Edward's head enough through Jacob to know what the Volturi were. Old. Italian. Big on rules. Very big on what happened to people who broke the rules about secrets.

And the witch had just broken her rules, sitting on a porch swing, for Seth.

*Jake,* she said. *The leeches.*

*Yeah,* said Jacob. *I know.*

*Edward's gonna pick it out of your head the second you walk in the house.*

*He's probably picking it out of my head right now, I'm on his lawn.* Leah felt Jacob look up at the dark glass wall of the Cullen house. *Yeah. There he is.*

Through Jacob's eyes she saw the pale shape standing at the window on the second floor, very still, looking down. Edward Cullen's face had an expression on it she'd never seen there before.

He looked interested.

*He wants to meet her,* Jacob reported, a second later. *Carlisle does too. Tomorrow night.*

*Great,* said Leah. *Love that. Let's take Seth's new girlfriend to meet the vampires. What could go wrong.*

*She's not afraid of them,* Seth said. *I can tell. I mean, she knows about them. Kind of. She thought the doc was a different kind of vampire, she said, she saw him in the pharmacy, she's got a word for it. Sangweeny.*

*Seth, I need you to stop saying words.*

*Okay.*

She came out of the trees at the bottom of the yellow house's lot, where the grass went down to the river. The rain had nearly stopped. The porch at the top of the lawn had two little white flames burning at the corners, and in the light of them her brother stood with his head down and a woman with her hair loose and a stick of wood in one hand stood next to his shoulder.

The woman turned her head. She looked straight down the lawn into the dark under the trees, right at where Leah had stopped.

She couldn't see anything. It was pitch black down here, and Leah was grey, and she'd been doing this since the spring. She hadn't made a sound.

The woman said, "You must be Leah."

Leah didn't move.

"He talks about you," the witch said. "All the time." She tipped her head to one side. "You can come up. I don't bite."

*She doesn't,* Seth said helpfully.

Leah stood under the trees for a long time. Then she walked out onto the wet grass and up the lawn, slow, with her head low and her ears forward, and stopped ten feet from the bottom of the porch. She put her nose up and breathed in.

Ozone. Cedar. Rain on a hot road. And something sharper under it, like a match just struck. Leah had smelled a lot of things in eight months. Vampire, which was rot and sugar and a cold that got up into your sinuses. Human, which was salt and soap and fear. Wolf. She'd never smelled anything like this.

The witch looked at her. She didn't look away and she didn't look down. She had a scar through one eyebrow and another on her hand and she held that wand like it was part of her arm.

"Okay," Layla Devereaux said. "So I'm guessing that's how you all talk. In your heads." She looked from one wolf to the other. "That's a lot of people in your head, Seth."

*Just three,* Seth said, though she couldn't hear him. *Mostly.*

"And I'm guessing," the witch went on, "that your sister doesn't like this one bit, and that somewhere out there is somebody else who's listening, and that by tomorrow I'm going to be meeting some people."

Leah looked at Seth.

*Did you tell her that?*

*No! She's just smart!*

Leah looked back at the witch. The witch looked back at her. And then, slowly, because she couldn't think of anything else to do with it, Leah dipped her head. Once.

"Yeah," Layla said quietly. "That's what I figured."

She looked down at the wand in her hand for a second, and something went across her face that Leah, who knew a lot about keeping things off her face, recognized right away.

The witch was scared, and the fear on her face had nothing to do with the two wolves on her lawn.

It was for whoever was going to find out she'd done this.

### IV. The Glass House

*The Cullen house — the following night*

Edward heard her four miles out.

He'd been listening for her since dusk, standing at the glass wall on the second floor with his daughter asleep against his shoulder, her breath a damp, steady pulse against his collarbone. He'd heard Seth's truck come off the highway and onto the long unmarked drive, and Seth's mind with it, loud and bright as a struck bell, and Jacob's alongside, and Leah's, sour and alert, riding in the bed of the truck in human form with her arms crossed against the cold. He'd heard all three of them the way he always heard the wolves, a jumble of instinct and noise.

And then a fourth mind, in the passenger seat, that he had never heard before.

*—left at the fork, he said, two miles of gravel, no lights, it's a long drive to have nowhere to run to, Devereaux, you've done worse, Chachapoyas was worse, nothing is worse than Chachapoyas, stop it—* and then, abruptly, in French, a little prayer of her grandmother's, mumbled under the breath of her thoughts the way some people hummed. *—Sainte Marie, mère de Dieu—* And then back to English, and a list. A list of things: the lay of the drive, the distance to the river, the likely number of them in the house, the probable reach of a Shield Charm against something that moved the way Seth had said they moved. A list of exits. A list of spells in an order. She was arranging her mind the way a soldier laid out a kit.

It was the cleanest, fastest mind he'd heard in years. It had a texture to it unlike anything he knew, unlike human thought, a sort of brightness at the edges, as if each idea came with a faint charge on it.

He listened to her think about him. She'd built a careful little picture of the Cullens from Seth's descriptions, and the picture was wrong in a number of affectionate ways. She imagined Esme as taller. She imagined Edward as somehow more sinister, and she was considering whether the business about him reading minds was a figure of speech.

Then the truck came round the last bend, and the house's lights came through the trees and fell across the windshield, and Edward heard her see it.

*Big,* she thought. *Lot of glass. Glass is a choice, if you're a thing that doesn't need to be afraid of anything coming in.* And then: *Okay. If he can really hear me, he can hear me now.*

And the mind closed.

It did it all at once. He'd never felt anything like it. One instant her thoughts were running bright and fast in front of him like water over stones, and the next they'd been folded away behind something smooth and heavy, a door swung shut on its hinges. He could still tell she was there. There was a faint pressure where she was, a hum, the way you can feel someone standing on the other side of a wall. But the words were gone.

He went very still at the window. Renesmee stirred against his shoulder and made a small cross noise and settled.

"Edward?" said Bella from the doorway behind him.

"She shut it," he said.

Bella came to stand beside him. She moved like what she was now, eight weeks into it, every motion clipped a little too precise. Her eyes were still the vivid crimson of a newborn's, though Carlisle swore he could see the first amber coming in at the rims.

"Shut what?"

"Her mind." He turned and looked at his wife. "I could hear her. She thought, *if he can really hear me, he can hear me now*. And then she closed it, like a book."

Bella's mouth parted.

She had spent two years being the only mind in the world he couldn't read. He saw that thought arrive in her face, and he saw her decide not to say it, and he loved her for it.

"Is it like me?" she asked anyway, after a moment.

"No. With you there's nothing. Silence. With her, I can tell there's someone behind the door." He looked out at the drive. The truck had stopped. Four doors opened; there were only two doors. Leah vaulted over the side of the bed. "She learned how to do this. Somebody taught her."

From downstairs, Alice called up, in a voice pitched for no human ear, "Edward, I've been trying all day and I can't *see* her."

He went down with Renesmee in his arms and Bella beside him.

Alice was sitting on the bottom stair with her knees pulled up and her fingertips pressed to her temples, which she only did when something was really bothering her. Jasper stood behind her with one hand on her shoulder. The rest of them were in the great room: Carlisle by the fire, which they kept lit when there were guests; Esme beside him; Emmett sprawled on the white sofa with his boots on it; Rosalie standing behind the sofa, as far from the front door as the room allowed and directly between that door and the stairs Renesmee had just come down.

"The wolves are different," Alice said. "The wolves I just can't see, they're blank, they're holes. Nessie I can't see. Her, I can see. I can see her coming up the walk right now. And then I try to see past that, what happens next, if she says anything, if she does anything—" Alice spread her fingers in front of her face. "It smears. Like a watercolor someone left out in the rain. It's the worst when I try to see her do—*whatever* it is she does. It just runs."

"Interesting," said Carlisle quietly.

"It's *rude*," said Alice.

Somebody knocked on the front door.

---

Seth came in first, ducking his head under the lintel he had no need to duck under, beaming at everyone. Jacob came in behind him and went straight past all of them to Edward and held out his arms, and Edward, after a moment, gave him Renesmee, because there was no point in doing anything else. Leah came in third and stood with her back against the wall beside the door and her arms still folded and her face set like concrete.

And between Seth and Leah, Layla Devereaux stepped into the Cullen house and stopped on the threshold and looked at them all.

She was small. Edward had expected her to be bigger, somehow, from the inside of Seth's head. Five-five, perhaps. Slim, in jeans and a dark canvas jacket that was soaked through at the shoulders, with a tangle of dark curls pinned up on her head and the wand through it, plain as a pencil. Freckles. A white scar through one eyebrow. Her heart was going quick and steady, a hundred and four, a hundred and six, and she smelled—

Every one of them smelled it at the same moment. He saw it go round the room. Emmett's nostrils flared. Rosalie's head came up.

She smelled of blood, of course; she was human, or human enough. But there was something laid over the blood, something he'd never once in a century scented on any living thing. A struck-match sharpness. Ozone. The air in the high country in August an instant before the lightning.

She smelled like weather.

"Hi," said Layla.

Nobody said anything. Then Esme moved, because Esme always moved first. She crossed the room at a human pace with her hands open and stopped a polite distance off and smiled.

"Hello. I'm Esme. Please come in out of the cold. Would you like something to drink? We don't, but we keep things for our guests. There's coffee. Or tea."

Layla looked at her for a long second.

"Is the coffee Folgers?"

Esme blinked. "I— I believe it's from a roaster in Seattle."

"Then I'd love some," Layla said, "thank you."

And that was how it began.

---

She told them very little, in the end, and Edward came to understand by the end of the first hour that she was doing it on purpose.

She sat in the armchair nearest the door, the one Carlisle had offered her, with a mug of coffee held in both hands, and Seth on the floor beside her chair with his back against its arm. She answered questions. She asked them. She was polite, and wry, and a little funny, and when Emmett asked her if she could turn him into a frog she looked at him for a long moment over the rim of the mug and said, "I could. I'd want a reason," and Emmett grinned so wide his face nearly split and said, "I'll come up with something," and Rosalie hit him on the back of the head.

And every time a question came near the edges of something—where she was from, exactly; where she'd trained; who governed her people; how many of them there were—she smiled and turned it aside.

"There's a lot of us," she said, when Carlisle asked. "Everywhere. More than you'd guess. We hide. We've hidden for three hundred years, give or take, and we've gotten very good at it. That's about as much as I'm comfortable saying."

"Of course," said Carlisle. He was sitting forward in his chair with his elbows on his knees and his fingers steepled, the way he sat at his desk with a difficult chart. Edward could hear his father's mind, and it was alight. Three hundred and some years of curiosity had come awake all at once. *A whole hidden people. Their own laws. Their own medicine, surely. What else is out there that I've walked past without seeing?* "You mentioned that you'd thought I was something else. When you saw me in town."

"I did." She turned her mug a quarter turn. "We have vampires. Where I come from. We call them vampires, anyway. Pale. Night-walkers. The sun hurts them. They sleep in the day, they drink blood, they're—they live alongside us, more or less, with a lot of rules and a lot of paperwork. I knew one, a little, when I was at school. He came to a party." She looked at Carlisle across the room, at his face, which in the firelight looked like something carved for a cathedral.

"And how do we compare?"

"He was a person with a sickness." She said it plainly. "You— forgive me, Doctor. You're like a statue that got up. I saw you in the pharmacy, and every hair on my body stood up, and I went home and told myself I was imagining things. Seth says your skin's hard as granite. He says you don't sleep. He says you don't die, unless somebody pulls you apart and burns the pieces, and that your bite makes more of you." She shook her head slowly. "I've never read anything about anything like you. Nobody I know has. Our libraries are very old and very big, and I've spent a lot of time in them, and there's nothing."

"Nothing at all?"

"Old stories. There's always old stories. Stone men in the mountains. Cold women who walked in the snow." She shrugged. "Every culture's got those. I always thought they were about our vampires, garbled. Now I'm not so sure."

Carlisle sat back. Edward heard him think, very clearly, *two kinds. Two kinds of vampire, in two worlds, that have never met each other. Or that have, and no one remembers.*

"You said your people have laws," said Jasper, from where he stood at the foot of the stairs. It was the first time he'd spoken. He'd been watching her the whole time the way he watched everyone, and Edward had been listening to him do it: *Fear, held down hard. Curiosity, a lot of it. Something underneath, old, heavy. Grief. Somebody died. She's very good at not showing it.* "About secrecy."

"Yes."

"And you've broken them."

The room got quieter.

Layla looked at Jasper. She didn't look away.

"Yes," she said.

"What happens," said Jasper, "if your people find out?"

"To me? Depends who finds out, and how much they care, and what kind of mood they're in." She was speaking more slowly now. "They'd want to wipe your memories. All of you. Seth and Leah and Jacob and their whole tribe, probably, if they thought the tribe knew. They have people who do that for a living, and they're very good at it. They'd want to do something about me. Take my wand, maybe. Lock me up, maybe." She took a sip of coffee. Her hand was perfectly steady. Edward could hear her heart, and it was not. "My country's a lot stricter than most about this. They used to have a law that said you couldn't so much as be friends with a person like Seth. It's gone now, the law, but the feeling's still there. They'd take a dim view."

"Could they?" said Rosalie. It was the first thing she'd said too. "Wipe our memories. Could they even do it? To us?"

Layla looked at her. "I don't know," she said honestly. "I've never heard of anybody trying."

Rosalie's eyes went to Renesmee, who was sitting on Jacob's knee on the floor by the fire with her bronze curls tumbling down her back, playing with a little wooden horse that Esme had carved her. Edward heard Rosalie's thought, sharp and cold as a blade: *And if they try on her?*

"Your people," Rosalie said. "What would they do about her?"

Layla followed her look to the child.

"My people," she said, "would care a whole lot more about me than about her. She's a baby."

"She's not exactly a—" Rosalie began, and stopped.

Renesmee had put down the horse. She was looking across the room at the stranger in the armchair with her mother's brown eyes, very wide and very interested. Then she got up off Jacob's knee, the way she did now, in one smooth motion that no eight-week-old should have been capable of and no two-year-old either, and walked across the room on the pale floor in her little stockinged feet, straight to Layla's chair.

Jacob made a sound. Rosalie made a sound. Edward put out a hand, not to stop her, only to be ready, and Bella was suddenly at his shoulder.

Layla looked down. Renesmee looked up.

"Hi," said Layla softly.

Renesmee reached up and put her palm flat against the witch's cheek.

Edward saw it all at once, the way he always did when his daughter used her gift and he was near enough to the mind she touched. He saw the picture Renesmee pressed into Layla's head: two little flames burning at the corners of a porch, white at the heart. Renesmee had gotten it from Jacob, who'd gotten it from Seth through the pack, and had been told about it at bedtime with sound effects. It came with a question folded into it the way Nessie's pictures always did, a bright, simple, overwhelming *how?*

And for one second, under the child's hand, the door in the witch's mind fell open.

Edward didn't mean to look. He saw anyway. It came through Renesmee's touch like light through a crack. A tomb, deep and wet, carved stone gone green. A young man with a narrow brown face and a gold tooth laughing at something she'd said. A hiss of something from the dark. A jade mask on a stone shelf, smiling. The young man on the floor, after, his eyes open, and her own hands on him, and her own voice saying his name over and over—*Teo, Teo, Teo*—and the scar on her palm, new, the shape of a leaf, burning.

Edward turned his face away.

When he looked back, the door was shut again, and Layla was smiling at his daughter with her eyes very bright.

"You want to see?" she said.

Renesmee nodded solemnly.

Layla reached up and drew the wand out of her hair, and her curls came down. Every vampire in the room tensed. Jacob was halfway to his feet. Leah had pushed off from the wall.

Layla held the wand loosely, tip down, and made a little circling motion with it, and murmured a word Edward didn't catch.

Lights came out of it. A dozen, then twenty, then more: tiny drifting points of cold blue fire, each the size of a firefly, spilling out of the end of the wand and rising into the air of the great room. They rose slowly, turning, toward the high ceiling, and drifted there, and some of them came down again and wandered around Renesmee's head like curious moths. One landed on the end of her nose. She went cross-eyed looking at it.

Then she laughed. It was the first time any of them had heard her laugh out loud with a stranger. It went up through the room like the lights.

Emmett said, very quietly, "Oh, *come on*."

Bella's hand found Edward's and squeezed it hard enough to crack a human's bones.

Renesmee put both hands up and tried to catch a light. It slipped through her fingers like smoke and kept on drifting. She turned and looked at her mother, and then at Jacob, and then at Rosalie, delighted, demanding they look.

"They don't burn," said Layla. "You can touch them. They'll go out in about an hour. My grandmother used to make them for me when I couldn't sleep."

She looked across the room at Rosalie over the top of Renesmee's head. Rosalie looked back. Something in Rosalie's mind, some tightly wound wire, slackened by a few degrees.

"Okay," Rosalie said quietly. "Okay."

---

It was late when Carlisle asked the last question, and by then most of them had drifted. Renesmee had fallen asleep on the rug under a slow tide of blue lights with Jacob curled beside her like a dog at a hearth. Emmett and Jasper had gone out to hunt, Emmett protesting all the way to the river that he wanted to stay and watch more magic. Alice sat on the stairs, still frowning at the space in front of her face where Layla's future kept running like wet paint.

"We have laws too," Carlisle said. "Ours are simpler than yours, I think. There's one law, really. Keep the secret. And there are those who enforce it."

"Seth told me a little," Layla said. "The Italians."

"The Volturi." He said the name the way he always did, with a sort of tired fondness that had been wearing thinner every year for three centuries. "I lived with them once, a very long time ago. They're old. They're powerful. And they are very serious about what happens to humans who know what we are."

"What happens?"

"The humans die," Edward said, from the window. "Or they're changed."

Layla looked over at him. It was the first time she'd looked straight at him all night.

"And a witch?"

"We don't know," said Edward. "They've never met one. As far as we know."

"As far as you know."

"Aro," said Edward, "is the eldest of them. He has a gift. If he takes your hand, he hears every thought you've ever had. All of it. Everything you remember, everything you know, everyone you've ever met."

Layla was silent a moment.

"Then nobody shakes his hand," she said.

"He's touched me," said Edward. "Once. In the spring."

He watched her take that in, and turn it over, and see the edge of it. He saw her see it, the way he'd seen it himself an hour ago, standing at the window, listening to her mind shut. *I know about you now. If Aro ever touches me again, he'll know too.*

"So the less you know," Layla said slowly, "the better."

"I think so. Yes."

She nodded. She put her mug down on the side table, very gently.

"That's the first thing anybody's said tonight that made complete sense," she said.

---

On the porch, at the end, with Seth already halfway to the truck and Leah leaning on the tailgate with her arms folded and her eyes on the dark, the witch stopped at the top of the steps and stood a moment in the cold.

Edward was inside, by the glass. He'd been listening to the hum behind her closed door all night. Now, as she stood there, he felt the door open a crack. She'd forgotten him, for a moment, or thought herself out of range.

One thought came through it, small and very clear, said to herself, the way people say a thing over and over until it sounds true.

*Nobody's coming. Nobody knows. Nobody is ever going to know.*

Then she went down the steps to the truck, and Seth held the door for her, and they drove away.

### V. Need to Know

*La Push — the following Sunday*

Charlie Swan had been a police officer for twenty-three years, and he knew what it looked like when somebody was about to do something to him.

He'd seen it on a meth cook in a trailer off the Hoh Road in '94, half a second before the guy went for the shotgun under the couch. He'd seen it on a drunk outside the Pacific Tavern at closing time. He'd seen it on Renée once, near the end, when she was standing in the kitchen in Forks with Bella on her hip and her car keys already in her hand.

It was a small thing. A kind of stillness. The eyes going flat while the person behind them made a decision.

He saw it now, on the face of a twenty-four-year-old girl from Louisiana standing at Sue Clearwater's stove with a smoking pan of fish behind her and a stick of wood in her fist.

---

It had been a nice dinner up to then.

Sue had been doing Sunday dinners since her husband died. She'd started them that first awful spring, Charlie suspected, so the house wouldn't be quiet on the one night a week he'd always been home, and she'd kept them going, and somewhere along the way Charlie had started coming. He brought beer. He brought a pie from the diner if he remembered. Sue fed him, and he sat at the end of the table that had been Harry Clearwater's and didn't think too hard about that, and the kids came and went, Seth and Leah and their friends, eating like locusts.

Lately it'd been harder. Lately there was a lot Charlie didn't think about.

He didn't think about Bella, who'd been very sick and was better now and was living out at the Cullen place, cold as river ice, with her eyes a color the contact lenses didn't quite hide. He didn't think about the baby, who was Edward's niece, adopted, and who was growing faster than any baby had a right to. He didn't think about the afternoon in October when Jacob Black had taken his clothes off in Charlie's front yard and turned into a wolf the size of a horse.

*Need to know*, Jacob had said. Afterward. Shivering in a pair of shorts. *It's on a need-to-know basis, Charlie. You don't need to know. You just need to know she's okay.*

So he'd decided he didn't need to know. It was easier than he'd have guessed. You just stopped asking. There were a lot of things in the world that a man didn't need to know, and Charlie had worked his whole life in a place where people kept their business their own.

So he was sitting at Harry Clearwater's end of the table on Sunday night with a cold Rainier, listening to Sue and Seth's new girlfriend talk about something called a *roux*, and thinking about nothing much, when the fish pan caught.

It happened fast the way grease fires do. Sue had turned around to grab the plate, and the girl, Layla, had been nudging the burner up, and the oil had gotten too hot and found the flame. There was a *whump* and a sheet of orange went up off the pan, two feet high, right up toward the cabinets and Sue's curtains. Sue shouted. Seth was halfway out of his chair.

And Layla Devereaux pulled that stick out of her hair, the one Charlie'd figured was some kind of hippie hair accessory, and pointed it at the pan and said something in a voice like a slap.

The fire went out.

It went all at once, like somebody had turned off a TV, and in its place there was a puff of cold white vapor that rolled off the stovetop and spilled down the front of the oven and across the linoleum and broke against Charlie's boots. He could feel the cold of it through the leather. The pan was rimed with frost. The fish was frozen solid in the oil.

The kitchen was dead quiet. The radio on the windowsill was still playing the Seahawks postgame show.

Layla turned around with the stick in her hand, and she looked at him, and Charlie saw it. The stillness. The eyes going flat.

Seth said, "Layla—"

"Whatever you're thinking about doing, kid," said Charlie, "I'd rather you didn't."

She didn't move.

"I've had a hell of a year," he said. "I've already got a lot of things I don't think about. I'd like to keep the rest of what's in my head where it is, if it's all the same to you."

The stick came down an inch. Then another.

Sue was looking at the girl. Sue Clearwater sat on the Quileute tribal council and had buried a husband and watched both her children turn into something out of her grandmother's stories, and Charlie had never once seen her look scared of anything. She didn't look scared now, exactly. She looked like a woman waiting to see which way the wind would go.

"Layla," Sue said. "Honey."

Layla breathed out. She put the stick back in her hair. Her hand, Charlie noticed, was shaking a little now, and it hadn't been a second ago.

"My name's Layla Devereaux," she said, "and I'm a witch, Chief Swan. That's about all I can tell you."

Charlie thought about that.

"A witch," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Like with the—" He made a vague gesture that was supposed to mean pointy hats and cauldrons and cats and didn't really mean anything.

"Kind of," Layla said. "Not much like that."

"Huh." He took a long drink of his beer. He set it down. He looked at the frozen fish. "Is any of it going to hurt anybody?"

"No, sir."

"Seth?"

"No," said Seth. "No, Charlie. Not ever."

Charlie looked at the kid's face. He'd known Seth Clearwater since the boy was born. He'd been at the hospital in Forks the night Sue had him. Seth had never once in his life been able to tell a lie with that face.

"Then I don't need to know," Charlie said. "Do I."

Layla looked at him like nobody had ever said anything like that to her before.

"I guess you don't," she said.

"Good. Good." He picked up the beer again. "Sue, is that fish gonna be any good now?"

Sue looked at the pan, and at him, and something happened at the corner of her mouth that might, on a less strong-minded woman, have turned into hysterical laughter.

"I'll make more," she said.

---

He left around ten. He said goodnight to Sue at the door, and did not kiss her on the cheek, which he'd been thinking about doing for months, and went out into the rain toward the cruiser.

Seth and the girl were sitting out on the front steps under the porch light, side by side, close enough that the rain coming off the eave didn't reach either of them. Charlie went past them with a nod. Layla nodded back. She had something in her hand, a little thing, a bead or a stone, pale and round, and she was holding it up to the light and turning it between her fingers. As he went by, he saw her touch the tip of the stick to it, and the bead flared once, silver, and went dark.

He didn't ask. He got into the cruiser and shut the door, and the windows were cracked an inch to stop them fogging, and he sat a minute with the engine running and the wipers going, because he was tired, and because he was a cop and some things you couldn't stop noticing just because you'd decided not to.

"Leg," the girl was saying.

"Which one?"

"The one with the strap. Where you tie your shorts."

Seth stuck out one long leg on the steps. There was a leather cord around his calf, under the cut-offs. Charlie had seen all the La Push boys wearing those lately and hadn't wondered why. The girl bent and threaded the bead onto the cord and tied it off in a knot that looked like it'd survive a hurricane.

"So you just—bite it?"

"Bite it. Hard. Hard enough to crack it. It'll take your teeth fine, wolf or not, I made sure." She sat back. "It'll pull on me. Right here." She put her fist under her breastbone. "And I'll know where you are, and I'll come."

"Like, right away?"

"Right away."

"What if you're in the shower?"

"Then you're gonna get a wet, very angry witch with no clothes on, and that's your problem." She pushed him with her shoulder. He didn't move an inch. "Seth. I mean it. After the other night. After all that about the Italians. If something comes for you that you can't handle, you don't be brave. You bite down."

"I can handle stuff."

"Seth."

"Okay," Seth said. "Okay. I promise."

"Say it again."

"I promise, Layla."

Charlie put the cruiser in reverse. He backed out of Sue's drive onto the road and drove home to Forks through the rain with the wipers going and the heater roaring.

About a mile out of La Push he noticed that the rain had stopped hitting the windshield. All around the car it was coming down in sheets, he could see it bouncing off the hood, on the road in the headlights, but it didn't touch the glass. It sheeted off a few inches above it like it had hit a pane of something.

He turned the wipers off. He drove the rest of the way home like that, in a little dry pocket in the dark, and he didn't think about it at all.

### VI. The Gin Trap

*Forks — two and a half weeks later*

The pie was pumpkin, because it was the Tuesday before American Thanksgiving and the woman behind the counter had told him with real conviction that nobody in Forks ordered anything else this week, and Harry Potter had decided on the spot that he was not going to be the man who ruined that for her.

He ate it at eight in the morning with black coffee in the back corner booth of the diner on the main road, with his back to the wall and the door in the corner of his eye and the kitchen pass-through behind the counter in the other. It was very good pie. The crust had lard in it. He could tell. He'd had an argument about lard in pastry once with Molly Weasley that had lasted most of a Christmas, and he'd lost, and he'd been right.

"How is it?" The waitress, whose badge said MAXINE and who was about sixty and had forearms like a stevedore, topped up his coffee without being asked.

"Criminal," said Harry. "Should be illegal, a pie like that. You lot ought to be arrested."

"Well, the Chief's right there, you can turn us in." She nodded toward the counter. "More cream?"

"Go on, then."

She went. He drank his coffee and looked, without looking, at the Chief.

Charles Swan, forty-one, Chief of Police, Forks, Washington. Nineteen-hundred-odd words in a manila folder in Harry's tent, a passport photograph and a newspaper clipping about a fishing tournament, and a single line at the bottom of the last page in Croaker's small, square hand: *Father of Isabella. Possibly aware. Do not engage.*

He was a tidy, quiet man of medium height with a moustache that had gone out of fashion before Harry was born and was never coming back. He was in uniform. He sat at the counter on the same stool he'd sat on each of the four mornings Harry had been coming in, and he ate the same thing, two eggs over easy and bacon and wheat toast, and he read the *Peninsula Daily News* folded into quarters. He did not look at Harry.

He'd looked at Harry once, on the first morning. One glance, from the door, the length of the room. It had gone across the booth and taken in the lot in a second and a half. Then Swan had sat down and read his paper and never looked again.

Harry had been looked at like that before, by people who were very good at their jobs. He had not gone into Swan's head. He'd thought about it, the first morning, the way you think about scratching an itch, and then decided against. Some people felt it when you went in. Not many, and never knowing what they felt, but a man with a cop's nose for when something was off would carry the feeling round all day afterward like a stone in his boot, and he'd remember the face he'd been looking at when he first noticed it. Harry was in Forks for a while yet. He'd rather be the English fishing bore in the corner than the bloke who'd given the Chief of Police a headache.

Besides. There were rules about that sort of thing, even for him. Not many. Some.

Swan folded the paper, put down a ten, said, "Thanks, Max," and got up. On his way to the door he stopped beside Harry's booth.

"You're the steelhead guy," he said.

"Guilty," said Harry.

"Maxine says you're staying out the Sol Duc somewhere."

"Cabin up past Sappho. Belongs to a friend of a friend."

Swan nodded. He looked at Harry's coat, which was a waxed-cotton Barbour gone soft at the cuffs and shiny at the elbows, and at the flannel shirt under it, and at the cup of coffee and the pie, and at Harry's hands round the cup. He took his time doing it, and he didn't pretend he wasn't.

"Kind of early for winter fish," Swan said.

"So I'm discovering."

"Who told you to come in November?"

"Bloke in a pub in Bristol," said Harry, "who I now suspect has never been further west than Swindon."

Swan's moustache moved slightly. It might have been a smile. "Bogachiel's your best bet, early," he said. "Below the hatchery. Couple weeks yet."

"Cheers. I'll give it a go."

"Mm." Swan put on his hat. He looked down once more, at Harry's boots under the table, a pair of brown leather Red Wings, and then back up. "Good luck," he said, and went out the door into the rain. The little bell over the door rang behind him.

Harry watched him through the window, crossing the lot to the cruiser.

*He looked at the boots,* he thought. *Twice.*

The boots had been sitting in a box in a shop in Port Angeles nine days ago. They had never once been in a river. They had a scuff on the left toe from where he'd kicked a rock on purpose in the car park outside, and he had thought, at the time, that would do.

It hadn't done. Swan had looked at them and seen new boots on a man who said he'd come five thousand miles to stand in cold water.

"Sloppy," said Harry, aloud, to his pie.

"What's that, hon?" said Maxine, passing.

"Nothing, love. Talking to myself. Could I get a slice of that to take away?"

---

The car was a grey Ford Taurus from the Hertz desk at Sea-Tac, and Harry had hated it on sight, and had come in the two weeks since to hate it with a settled, considered, almost spiritual conviction.

It was the right car. That was the trouble. It was exactly the car a moderately prosperous English fishing bore would hire at the airport, and it vanished into any car park in America, and nobody had ever looked at one twice in its life. It had a CD player. That was the most interesting thing about it. It had the turning circle of a canal boat and the personality of a filing cabinet and it smelled, permanently, of someone else's air freshener. Back in London, in a lock-up garage under the railway arches in Vauxhall, there was a black 1969 Mercury Cougar that he'd rebuilt with his own hands and a great deal of magic over two summers, and he thought about it every time he turned the key in the Taurus, the way you'd think about a girl you'd left behind.

He drove east out of Forks on 101 with *Physical Graffiti* in the CD player and the wipers on the second setting, and he turned it up when "Kashmir" came on, because a man had to keep his standards somewhere.

The road ran along the Sol Duc. On his right the river came and went through the trees, high and fast and the color of old jade. On the left, the forest went up, endless, second-growth Douglas fir and hemlock and Sitka spruce with moss on everything, hanging in sheets off the branches and lying in great wet green carpets over the deadfall, and the rain coming down through all of it like it had been coming down for ten thousand years and saw no reason to stop. He'd been in a lot of forests. This one had the most water in it of any he'd known, outside a swamp. It got into the car. It got into his coat. It got into his tent, despite three separate charms against it, and lay on the canvas of the ceiling in fine beads every morning.

Eleven miles out, past the turn to Sappho, he pulled into a gravel turnout above the river where the anglers parked and got out.

Nobody about. It was a Tuesday morning in November and nobody with any sense was out here. He walked down off the gravel to where an old steel guardrail ran along the edge of the drop to the river, and he crouched by the third post from the end, where the rust had eaten into the base, and he put two fingers to the earth there.

The ward was where he'd left it. He felt it hum under his fingertips like a cello string that somebody, somewhere, was very softly bowing.

Under the soil at the base of the post, a hand's depth down, was a disc of Welsh slate the size of a saucer, cut with forty-one runes in a spiral and blooded with three drops of his own. There were a hundred and thirty-eight more like it, scattered across the north end of the Olympic Peninsula in a pattern it had taken him nine nights to lay and three months of planning in London before that. They sat under guardrails and culverts and the footings of logging bridges and the bases of particular trees, and on the beach at Kalaloch under the drift-logs, and in the riverbeds of the Hoh and the Bogachiel and the Queets, and at the bottoms of four roadside mileage markers along 112 out toward Neah Bay.

The outer ring was a tell-tale and nothing else. It watched every road and river and ridge-line by which a thing might walk into the west end of the peninsula, and it reported back. It told him what crossed. It did not stop anything.

The inner ring was the trap.

It ran in a loose, broken circle through the forest round Forks and La Push, eight or ten miles across, strung tree to tree and anchored on the slate discs and on the deadwood and on the bones of the land itself. He thought of it as a snare-line, the way an old keeper in a Cumbrian valley would string wire through the bracken for rabbits, and he'd named it the way he'd named most things he'd made: after the oldest, ugliest, most effective thing it reminded him of. The gin trap.

It had taken him two years to build the first one. He had built the first one in an empty warehouse in Wapping and caught himself in it eleven times before it worked properly, and he had built the second in a valley in the Carpathians, and caught a Lethifold in it, which nobody on record had ever caught alive. He had sat up with that Lethifold for three nights in a box of light while it tried to eat him and failed. It was in a jar now. Part of it.

This one was the biggest he'd ever laid. It was also the fussiest. Most wards were blunt instruments. They were built to keep a thing out, or keep a thing in, or tell you when a thing went by. A gin trap had to *choose*. It had to know the difference between what it was meant to catch and every other living thing that walked through it, deer and elk and hikers and the occasional black bear and the odd idiot from Seattle on a mountain bike, and it had to let all of those go by without so much as a tingle in the soles of their feet, and then close round the one thing it was meant for like a fist.

He'd keyed it to blood.

He had no sample of the creature itself; nobody did, which was the point of the whole bloody exercise. But he knew from the Volterra material, and from what the Department had been able to piece together since the spring, that whatever these things were, they ate people. They drank blood, human blood, and they were full of it. And so the snare-line tasted, very faintly, the blood in everything that crossed it, and it was looking for one thing: human blood, a great deal of it, recently drunk, inside a body that had no heartbeat.

That let through the deer. It let through the hikers. It let through the bears. It let through the Cullens.

The Cullens drank from animals. That had been in the file too, with a question mark after it, and he'd confirmed it himself on the second night, lying under a Disillusionment Charm on a ridge above the river behind their house at three in the morning and watching the big dark-haired one wrestle an elk to the ground in the shallows and drain it in about eleven seconds. Their eyes were gold. They had no human blood in them. The gin trap would let them walk past it without a twitch.

It would let through the big dogs too, the ones from the reservation, which he had also watched from that ridge, and which had made him sit up and think for a good long while. Whatever those were, their blood ran very hot and very human, and very much alive.

It had not been in the file. Nothing about the wolves had been in the file at all, except for a line from a hiking forum on the Muggle internet, printed off and stapled to the back by someone in the Department's anomalies office with a sense of humor: *Saw a WOLF up by Hoh River trail must have been 7 ft at the shoulder no joke.*

He'd taken a good long look at them. He'd thought about it. And he'd decided they weren't his problem. They were local, they patrolled, they had a fixed range and a fixed routine and they were plainly, obviously hunting the same things he was. They hadn't come anywhere near his tent. As far as he was concerned the wolves were a bit of local wildlife that happened to be on his side, and he'd leave them be until they gave him a reason not to.

He stood up and wiped his fingers on his trousers. Down below, the Sol Duc went roaring past over the rocks.

There was one other thing in Forks that wasn't in the file, and he thought about it now, standing above the river, because he'd been thinking about it on and off for two weeks.

There was a witch in town.

He'd felt her wards on the third night, driving slowly through Forks after midnight with his window down. A little yellow house out on Calawah Way, set back on a lot that ran down to the river, with a line of protection round it that you'd only have spotted if you were the sort of person who spent his evenings building things like the gin trap. It was American work. He could tell from the shape of it, the way the runes sat: Ilvermorny trained, almost certainly, then a lot of fieldwork on top. Tomb-work, maybe. Curse-breaker's habits. Tidy, layered, clever, with a mean little sting in the outer layer for anyone who tried to unpick it. Good, solid work. He'd seen better perhaps twice in his life.

He had driven past and not slowed down and not gone back.

There was a fair chance she was nothing at all. Witches lived in the middle of nowhere for all sorts of reasons. Some of them wanted quiet. Some of them were hiding from something, or somebody, and he had no particular interest in finding out what. She wasn't on any list he'd been given. She hadn't noticed him, as far as he could tell, which meant that his wards were as quiet as he'd meant them to be. And a British Unspeakable operating on American soil without so much as a postcard to MACUSA had very good reasons not to go knocking on doors and introducing himself to the locals.

So he'd filed her. *Witch, Calawah Way. Competent. Not a target. Avoid.* He'd thought she'd stay filed.

He got back into the Taurus and turned *Physical Graffiti* back on and drove up the valley toward the tent.

---

The tent stood in a hollow on a bench of land above the north fork of the Calawah, a mile and a half from the nearest forest road, under three hundred-foot hemlocks so close together that the ground beneath them was nearly dry. It was a small, faded green canvas affair of the sort you'd find in the back of any Scout hut, with one pole, a sagging ridge, and a flap that didn't close quite right. Anybody walking through the hollow would not have seen it. Anybody who did see it would have remembered, all at once and very urgently, something they'd left on the stove at home.

Harry ducked under the flap.

Inside it was a good deal bigger. It had taken him a winter to get the space right, and Hermione had redone two of the extension charms for him when she'd seen them, because she said looking at his arithmancy gave her a migraine. He'd let her. She'd been right.

The front room was a kitchen and a sitting room in one, low and wide, with a stone floor and a black iron range and a scrubbed table and a battered leather armchair and a record player. A kettle was already starting to sing, because he'd charmed it to the tent-flap. He put the pie on the table and hung up the Barbour and put the kettle on properly and stood by the range a minute, feeling the dry heat of it come into his hands.

Through the next door, the operations room. A long table with a map spread on it that took up all of it, eight feet by five, of the north end of the Olympic Peninsula, drawn by hand in his own ink over three nights. The roads were on it, and the rivers, and the towns, and the reservation at La Push. And on it, faint as pinpricks of starlight, a hundred and thirty-nine tiny points of white glowed where the slate discs lay, and between them the threads of the gin trap ran in a thin silver line through the green, like a single hair laid on the paper.

Nothing moving on it. He'd have known.

On a corkboard at the end of the room: the file. Photographs, mostly. A grainy still from a Muggle tourist's camcorder of a plaza in Tuscany, with a clock tower, and red cloaks in a crowd, and a pale young man in an open shirt at the very edge of an archway's shadow, one bare forearm and the point of one shoulder just out in a band of noon sunlight, catching it like a jewel-cutter's tray. Another photograph, sharper, of the same young man, three weeks later, getting into a silver Volvo in the car park of the hospital in Forks. Edward Cullen. A list of names under that. *Carlisle. Esme. Alice. Jasper. Rosalie. Emmett. Edward.* And then, underneath, with a question mark: *Isabella Swan?* And then a line from Croaker, written in green ink across the bottom of the board, that had been there since the brief.

*CULLENS: PRESERVE. Value alive > value as specimens.*

Through the next door: the library, which was just books and shelves and a lamp and the worn armchair's twin, and his notebooks. There were fifty-three of those now, black, the hardback sort you could buy in any stationer's on the high street, filled edge to edge in his cramped handwriting. Eight years of them. He didn't go in.

He went to the armory instead, because he'd be needing it soon, and he liked to look.

It was the smallest room in the tent and the most carefully made. Stone walls, and a heavy oak door with a lock on it that would have taken a team of goblin curse-breakers a fortnight to open. Inside, racks. A Lee-Enfield No. 4 rifle from the second war, with a line of tiny runes inlaid in silver all the way down the woodwork of the stock, which he'd had off a dead man in a bog in Donegal in 2003, and had since taught to put a round through a quarter-mile of ward and come out the other side. A sawn-off double-barrelled twelve-bore, with a box of shells beside it he'd packed himself, rock salt and powdered silver and a little crushed asphodel for anything that might be a ghoul. Four knives of goblin-wrought silver in a row, which had cost him a debt he was still paying. A shelf of small, plain tin canisters, each sealed with a thumbprint of red wax, each holding enough Fiendfyre to take the top off a hill; he had never yet opened one, and he was quite looking forward to the day he had to.

And in a holster on its own hook, by the door, where he could put his hand on it without looking: the Colt.

It was a 1911, a Government Model, made in Hartford, Connecticut in 1943, and it had been pointed at him once, in an alley behind a spice market in Marrakesh, by a man who had been paid a great deal of money to kill him and had fallen short in the attempt. Harry had kept the gun afterward, as a souvenir, the way some people keep a ticket stub.

Then he'd spent most of a winter on it.

He'd never told anybody exactly what he'd done to it, not even Hermione, mostly because Hermione would have wanted to read his notes and then argued with him about them for a month. But the magazine in it never ran dry, however many times he fired, and the rounds that came out of it were whatever he needed them to be when he squeezed the trigger. He could put a round of nothing much through a man's coat and drop him like a sack of wet sand without breaking the skin. He could put a round through an engine block. If he wanted to, he could put one into the side of a building and take the side off.

He took it down off the hook now and checked it out of habit and holstered it under his arm in the soft leather rig he'd had made for it in Jermyn Street. Then he went through to the last room.

---

The specimen room was cold. He kept it cold. It was long and narrow and lit with a hard, clean, blue-white light, and the floor and the walls were stone, and down the middle of it ran a pair of steel tables with gutters along their edges.

Down one side, shelves. Jars, mostly. A great many jars, every one of them labelled in the same cramped hand as the notebooks. An Inferius's left hand, in a solution of his own making, which still flexed its fingers sometimes in the night and scratched faintly at the glass. A ribbon of something thin and black and soft as velvet, folded over on itself: the Lethifold from the Carpathians, or as much of it as he'd needed to keep. Six of an Acromantula's eyes, each the size of a billiard ball. A Grindylow in a tank that he'd caught in Hampshire and meant to let go and hadn't. A phial of something silver that wasn't a memory and wasn't a potion and that he hadn't decided on a word for yet.

And on its own at the end of the shelf, on a square of black velvet in a case of glass sealed with three different charms: a fang, as long as his forearm, curved and pale and pitted, with a single dry brown stain still running along its groove.

He'd pulled that one out of his own arm, once. He'd been twelve. He had kept the rest of the basilisk where it was, at the bottom of the Chamber, but he had gone back for that tooth when he was twenty-two, with a lantern and a hacksaw and a great many complicated feelings, because it was the most dangerous substance he'd ever personally encountered and he'd wanted to know why.

Down the other side of the room, the cells.

There were four. Each of them was eight feet on a side, and walled on three sides with dressed stone and on the fourth, facing the room, with a single sheet of something that looked like glass and wasn't, etched in its depth with a lattice of runes so fine it made the light shimmer faintly when you looked through it. Three of the cells were empty. In the fourth, something grey and shapeless and very cold sat in the corner, where it had sat since a mine in the Pennines in February. It was what had been left on the ground after a Dementor had come apart. He was fairly sure nobody else had ever kept one. He didn't know yet what it was, and he'd been trying for nine months, and every time he went into that cell the room got a little colder and the lamps dimmed a little and he dreamed about his mother that night.

He stood in front of the first empty cell.

There was a card on the glass at eye level, in a little brass frame. He'd written it in London, and slipped it in, and had looked at it every day for two and a half weeks.

*SPECIMEN 1*
*Vampire (unclassified; "Volterra" type)*
*Collected: ———*

He touched the card with one finger. Then he went back into the front room, and made his tea, and took down the mirror.

---

It was an old shaving-glass, the size of a paperback, in a tortoiseshell frame. He sat in the leather armchair with his tea on the arm and held the glass on his knee, and said, "Croaker," and waited, and watched his own face cloud over and go dim. He looked tired, he thought. He'd been out every night for nine nights laying slate in the rain, and he hadn't shaved since Sunday. Green eyes, dark hair that needed cutting, a face that was beginning to take on the look of a bloke who'd spent too long in a tent.

The glass cleared. A different face looked out of it.

Saul Croaker was somewhere north of a hundred and thirty and looked it, in the way a very old tree looks it, gnarled and hard and wholly unbothered by weather. He had very little hair left. He had a long, deeply lined face like an Easter Island statue that had been left out in the rain, and a pair of pale, colorless, terribly patient eyes. He had been an Unspeakable for a hundred and ten years. He had worked, for most of them, on time. Harry had once asked him, in the pub, how long he'd been at it, and Croaker had said, in perfect seriousness, *Depends where you start counting.*

"Potter," said Croaker.

"Morning, sir. Evening, for you, I suppose."

"Quarter past six. I've a meeting with the Minister at seven. What have you got?"

"Nothing in the bag yet," said Harry. "Line's set. Every scrap of it. I walked the last of the outer ring last night out at Neah Bay and the lot's live. Checked the Sol Duc anchor this morning. Clean as a whistle."

"And the locals?"

"Cullens are where you'd expect. Haven't been into town since I got here, apart from the doctor, and he's on nights at the hospital. They're keeping to the house and the hills. There's more of them than were in the file. There's at least one I'd not got a name for. Small, dark hair, young-looking. Moves like the rest of them. Eyes red." He thought about the ridge above the river, at three in the morning, and a slim, small figure in the shallows below him, hunting with a kind of fury the others didn't have. "Red, but going over. Orange round the rims. Feeding off animals, same as the others. If I had to bet, I'd say she's new. And I'd say she might be the Swan girl."

Croaker considered this.

"Swan," he said. "The one from Volterra."

"Could be. Her dad's been telling the town she's been ill. Clinic somewhere out east. That's the story." Harry drank some tea. "I'll know more when I know more."

"And these animals of yours."

"Dogs," said Harry. "Wolves. Whatever. They're about. Big as a Mini Cooper, a few of them. Run a patrol round the Cullen place and down to the reservation, regular as clockwork. I keep my distance. They keep theirs. They're hunting what we're hunting."

"You've no idea what they are."

"Course I've got an idea. I've got loads of ideas. I just haven't got a sample." He put the tea down. "They're not on the brief, sir. I'm not going to go poking a pack of wolves the size of a lorry with a stick in their own back garden for the fun of it. They aren't bothering me. I'll leave them alone."

"For now."

"For now."

Croaker looked at him out of the glass for a long moment. He did that. Harry had learned not to fill the silence.

"The Minister," Croaker said finally, "wants a date."

"Course he does."

"He's been shown the Volterra material. All of it. The tour groups." Croaker's voice did not change. "Four hundred and twelve, Potter. That's how many tourists we've traced into that palace on that woman's tours since the spring, through that door off the Piazza dei Priori. Four hundred and twelve in, and we have yet to find one of them come out. That's the ones we've traced. Germans. Japanese. A girls' school from Lyon on a choir trip. Every one of them Muggle. Every one of them in a country that has a Ministry that should have seen it, and didn't, for three thousand years, and still doesn't."

"I've seen the file, sir."

"I know you've seen the file. I'm telling you what the Minister said to me when he saw it. He said he wants it stopped. I told him we can't stop what we don't understand. He asked me what we need. I told him we need to know how they die." Croaker leaned closer to the glass. "So I'm asking you. When do I get my specimen?"

"When one walks into my snare."

"And if none does?"

"Then I'll go and find one," said Harry, "and walk it in."

Croaker grunted. It was, from Croaker, almost an endorsement.

"Mind the Americans," the old man said.

"I always mind the Americans."

"You're a tourist."

"I'm always a tourist."

"You're a tourist with three Unforgivables on a Department warrant, on foreign soil, without a scrap of notification to MACUSA, setting snares for an unknown species in the backyard of a coven we have formally designated as an asset." Croaker's pale eyes didn't blink. "If anything goes wrong over there, I've never heard of you. The Minister's never heard of you. The Department has never heard of you. Your own mother's never heard of you."

"She's dead, sir."

"I know, Potter. It was a figure of speech."

"Always nice when you check in."

"Find me my specimen," said Croaker, and the glass went dark.

Harry sat with it on his knee a minute longer. Then he put it back on its shelf beside the range, and cut himself a slice of the pumpkin pie he'd bought for later, and ate it standing up at the table, looking through the door at the map.

The Department of Mysteries had two ends. Everyone who'd heard of it at all knew the first one: the Brain Room and the Veil and the Hall of Prophecy, the long silent laboratories under Whitehall where old men and women with ink on their fingers spent fifty years on a single question about the nature of time. That was where the work went in the end. The other end was smaller. It had no room of its own and needed none, and it sent people out into the world to find the things the long silent laboratories needed. Things nobody else had. Things nobody else knew about. It found out what was possible before anyone else did, and then made very sure it stayed the only one who knew.

Croaker had put it to him that way once, in the Leaky Cauldron in the spring of 1999. Harry had been eighteen and fresh out of the war and trying very hard not to sit with his back to the door. He'd turned down Kingsley twice already. Auror training. It would have been the obvious thing, and everyone had expected it, and he'd stood in the Atrium of the Ministry looking at the new fountain and known, very clearly and very quietly, that he did not want to spend the rest of his life chasing dark wizards down alleys. He'd done enough of that. He wanted to know *why*. Why the Elder Wand. Why the Stone. Why he'd stood in a white place that looked like King's Cross and talked to a dead man and come back. Why his mother's love had kept a Killing Curse off him when he was one, and what the Veil was, and where Sirius had gone. Why magic worked at all, and how far down it went.

He'd said all of that to Croaker, more or less, after three pints. Croaker had listened without a flicker. Then he'd said, *We'd like to know too, Mr. Potter. Very much indeed. You walked into the Death Chamber at fifteen and walked out again. You walked into death itself at seventeen, by every account, and walked back out of it. We would very much like to know how.*

It had been nearly eight years. Two of them in the Department's archives, which went down nine floors below the bit anyone saw, reading until his eyes gave out. A winter in Uzbekistan with an old woman in a mud house outside Khiva who'd never in her life held a wand and who'd taught him to do without his. Eighteen months, on and off, with a family of goblin ward-smiths in a forge under the Mendips, who'd taught him that every ward ever made was an argument with the world, and that you had to win the argument. A year at the Veil. He didn't talk about the year at the Veil. Half a dozen things in the notebooks that nobody had ever done before, and which nobody else knew he'd done. The gin trap. The Colt. A way of closing his mind so that no Legilimens he'd ever met could find the door, never mind the handle. Three or four spells he'd made from nothing, which he hadn't named yet, and might never.

And a lot of other things in the notebooks. Things he'd done because the job had needed them done, or the work had. He'd used the Cruciatus Curse on a man in a cellar in Antwerp for nine minutes, because the man had known where a stolen Time-Turner was, and three children would have died if Harry hadn't found it by morning. He'd put the Imperius on a Romanian customs official to get a crate through Constanța without opening it. He'd killed four people, since the war. All four with the curse that was supposed to be unforgivable. All four had been trying to kill him at the time, or someone else, and he'd felt about the same about all four of them afterward, which was nothing much. The Department's warrant covered all three curses and always had; it had never answered to Magical Law Enforcement, and it never would. Kingsley had banned the Unforgivables for his Aurors. He hadn't banned them for Harry. He couldn't.

It didn't keep Harry up at night. Most things didn't anymore.

He finished the pie. He washed the plate. He went and lay down on the camp bed in the corner of the library with his boots still on and his hand on the Colt under his arm, and slept like a dropped stone.

---

The ring woke him.

It was a heavy, plain gold band on the third finger of his right hand, and it was hot, the way a coin gets when you've held it too long in a fist.

He was on his feet and in the operations room before he'd entirely woken up. The map lay on its table under the hard light. It was quarter past two in the afternoon by the clock over the door; he'd slept longer than he meant to. On the paper, the hundred and thirty-nine white pinpricks glowed. Up in the northeast corner, where the Sol Duc came down out of the mountains past the Sappho turn, one of them had gone red.

Then the red moved. It came off the anchor in a smooth fast line through the forest, heading west, toward the coast, toward La Push, toward Forks. He watched it cover three miles of map in the time it took him to take three breaths.

*There you are.*

It was fast. It was very fast. He'd seen the Cullens run, ranging and circling through the hills after their elk. This one went in a straight line through the timber, travelling, the way a man in a hurry walks down a street. A nomad, then. That had been in the file too, a word the Department had borrowed from somewhere: *nomads*. The ones that didn't live in a family in a big glass house and drink elk. The ones that went where they liked and ate who they liked.

He stood and watched it come.

He didn't go after it. Not yet. He could have. He could have Apparated into the forest in front of it and stopped it there and then, the old-fashioned way, the hard way. But he hadn't spent nine nights in the rain laying the inner ring to throw it away now. He wanted to see the trap work. He wanted to see it close by itself, on its own terms, on a thing that didn't know it was there. That was the experiment. That was half of what he'd come for.

The red thread came on, steady, west-southwest. It would hit the inner ring a mile or two north of the Quillayute, if it held its heading. Ten minutes, perhaps. Less.

He went to the armory and took the badge wallet off its hook, and put it in his inside pocket. He went to the wardrobe in the corner of the library. He took off the flannel and the jeans and the Red Wings.

When he came out again he was in a charcoal-grey suit, single-breasted, cut close by a tailor on Savile Row who'd made it for him as a favor and asked no questions about the reinforcing charms worked into the lining. A white shirt. A narrow black tie. Black Oxford shoes with a polish on them that no mud on earth would stick to. A long black wool overcoat over the lot, to the knee, collar up. Thin black leather gloves. The Colt was under his left arm, a clean, familiar weight. A pair of dark glasses in his breast pocket, which he would almost certainly not need, it being the Olympic Peninsula in November, but which he liked to have.

He looked at the map.

Two more points of light had appeared on it, very close behind the red one. These were a hot, live, coppery gold, the color he'd keyed the trap to show for anything that crossed it running at the heat of those dogs from La Push, and they were moving like the red one moved, and they were *chasing* it.

"Oh," said Harry. "Now that's handy."

The red thread swerved. It went north, hard, and then back west, and he watched the two gold points cut the corner on it like sheepdogs on a stray, the way they'd been doing it, he suspected, for years. They were driving it. They didn't know they were driving it toward anything. But they were.

It hit the inner ring a mile and a half north of the Quillayute, at twenty-three minutes past two.

The silver thread went bright as a lit fuse along an inch of the map. Then it pulled in on itself, all along that inch, like a drawstring. The red light stopped dead.

On Harry's finger the ring went from hot to cold in an instant, the way it did when the trap had closed on something and held.

He looked at the two gold lights that had stopped beside it, and at the red one between them, and at the time.

"Right," he said, to nobody. "Showtime."

He turned on his heel.

### VII. Lassie

*North of the Quillayute — 2:09 p.m.*

At nine minutes past two that Tuesday, while a man in a tent above the north fork of the Calawah slept on a camp bed with his boots on and a hand on the gun under his arm, Leah Clearwater smelled the bloodsucker.

She'd been running the day line with Seth since noon. It was the dead shift. Nothing came through in the daytime; the leeches who didn't live in glass houses liked the dark, same as everybody's grandmother said, and on a grey wet Tuesday in November the woods north of the river had all the excitement of a waiting room. She'd been running it anyway. She ran it every day. She ran it because last fall a red-haired leech had come through these trees looking for Bella Swan and left hikers dead up by the Hoh, and because this summer a whole army of newborns had come over the mountains, and because there was no such thing as a dead shift. There were just shifts where nothing had happened yet.

And Seth was in her head.

Seth had been in her head for nineteen years, one way or another, and since the spring he'd been *in her head*, and she was used to it. She was used to the way his mind went everywhere at once like a dog let off a leash. She was not used to this. For seven weeks it had been the witch. Every hour, every minute, every thought he had took a little detour through the yellow house on Calawah Way and came back with something stuck to it. The witch's hands. The witch's laugh. The witch on her knees in her garden this morning planting garlic, of all things, in the rain, with mud on her jeans and her hair falling down, singing something in French under her breath, when Seth had stopped by on the way to patrol—

*Seth.*

*Sorry.*

*I can smell the garlic, Seth. I can smell it in your head. That's not a thing that should be possible.*

*She says it's for the winter. You plant it in November and it comes up in—*

*I don't care when it comes up.*

*—June, she says—*

She was about to tell him exactly where he could plant the garlic when the wind turned.

It came down off the ridge to the north, out of the timber above the river, wet and cold and full of the smell of spruce and rotting nurse-logs and elk. And under all of that, threaded through it like a strand of wire through wool, there was the other smell. The one that went straight up into the top of her skull and lit it on fire.

Sweet. Sickly sweet. Like a rotten peach left in a hot car. Like bleach. Like ice. It got into her sinuses and burned there, the way it always did, and every hair on her body stood straight up from her nose to the end of her tail, and her lips came back off her teeth before she'd decided anything.

Seth stopped dead beside her. She felt it hit him.

*Is that—*

*It's not them,* Leah said.

She knew every one of the Cullens by smell now. She'd had to. Months of running their perimeter and lying on their lawn; she could tell Rosalie from Alice at a mile, and Edward from Emmett at two, and Bella, the newest, who still smelled faintly of Bella underneath, which was its own special kind of awful. This one was older than any of them. Rank. And there was something with it, under the sweetness, coppery and thick and fresh.

Blood. Human blood. A lot of it, and not long ago.

*Red-eye,* Leah said. *Nomad. It ate something on the way in.*

*Somebody,* said Seth quietly.

*Yeah.*

She didn't wait. She didn't phase to tell Jacob, who was human and asleep on the Cullens' couch with Nessie on his chest, and she didn't stop to think about it. She went. Up the slope, through the salal, flat out, belly to the ground, and Seth came with her a half-length behind on her left shoulder, the way they'd done it a hundred times.

---

She was the fastest wolf in either pack, and she knew it the way she knew her own name. Jacob was bigger and Sam was steadier and Paul was meaner, but none of them could catch her, and on a good day, on clean ground, with her blood up and the stink of a leech in her nose, she could run down nearly anything that walked.

The leech saw them coming at about four hundred yards.

She saw it see them. It was crossing a clearing on a long open slope where a logging company had clear-cut sometime in the eighties and the second-growth was only thirty feet tall. A man-shape, thin, moving the way they moved, all one smooth line like water poured downhill. It was wearing somebody's jacket. A bright red Gore-Tex shell, the kind the hikers bought at REI in Seattle, too big in the shoulders. She could smell the person it had belonged to all over it.

It stopped in the middle of the clearing and turned its head and looked at them.

Red eyes. She could see them from four hundred yards. Bright, wet, ruby red.

Then it ran.

*Left,* Leah said. *Take the left. Don't let it get to the river.*

*Got it.*

They split. She went straight up the middle of the cut, flat out, and the stumps went past her like fence posts. Seth peeled off to the left in a long curve to get between the leech and the water. If it got to the Quillayute, it could go in and come out anywhere downstream and they'd lose the scent for an hour. If they could keep it on the land, keep it running north, toward the hills, they could run it down. It was fast. They were all fast. But it had been eating and travelling, and it was alone, and it was scared.

She could smell that too. The fear. They didn't sweat, leeches. But fear did something to them anyway, sharpened the sweet smell like a struck match sharpens the air.

It went north. It went into the old growth on the far side of the cut, where the trees were enormous and the ground under them was dim and soft and silent with moss, and she went in after it a hundred yards behind and closing.

It was a good runner. She'd give it that. It knew how to use trees. It went up a trunk and across a limb and down another, and doubled back once, trying to throw her, and she just cut the corner on it and it lost twenty yards. Seth came in from the left again and turned it, and it veered north and east, and she drove it, and he drove it, and it went where they wanted it to go.

Fifty yards.

Thirty.

Its hair was long and lank and dirty blond. It had a narrow face, young, gaunt, and it kept looking back over its shoulder, and every time it did she could see its red eyes going wide.

*Mine,* Leah said. *I've got it.*

*Leah, wait—*

She gathered herself to take it. She'd done this before. You hit them from behind, high, at the shoulder, and you bore them down, and you bit through the neck before they could get their arms around to you, and it was like biting through cold marble, and the sound it made was a sound she'd never forget.

Twenty yards. She was coming off the ground—

And there was a flash in the air in front of the leech like the sun catching a fishing line.

It happened faster than she could see it. One second the leech was running. The next second something silver had come up out of the moss around its ankle and closed on it like a fist, and the leech's legs went out from under it, and then it was off the ground entirely. It went up. It went up backward, yanked off its feet and hauled up into the air by one ankle with a sound like a bowstring, and then it was hanging there, upside down, twelve feet off the forest floor, its long dirty hair dangling, its arms down at its sides, swinging gently from a line of light that ran up from its ankle to a limb of an enormous hemlock thirty feet above.

Leah hit the ground under it with all four feet and slid ten yards in the moss before she stopped.

Seth came in beside her, skidding. They both stood there, panting, hackles up, staring up.

The leech hung there and turned, slowly, on the end of its line.

It wasn't moving. That was the thing. That was the worst thing about it. Leeches moved all the time, even when they were standing still, they twitched and flickered and made tiny adjustments, and this one hung there stiff as a fence board. Its arms were pressed down flat against its sides. Its legs were together. Its head was tipped back. Only its eyes were moving. They rolled in their sockets, down toward the two wolves under it, red and wide and terrified, and back up toward the line on its ankle, and back down again.

The line hummed. It hummed like a power line in the rain. It was silver, and very thin, and it glowed a little in the dim green light under the trees, and where it was looped round the leech's ankle it was pulled so tight that the bright red Gore-Tex had puckered.

*What,* said Leah, *the hell.*

*Is that Layla?* Seth said. His mind was going a hundred miles an hour. *Is that a Layla thing? Is that a witch thing? Did she—*

*How would Layla—*

*I don't know! It's magic! It's GLOWING!*

Leah looked at the leech. The leech looked at her. Its mouth was open a little. She could see its teeth.

Twelve feet. She could make twelve feet. She could make twelve feet easy, from a standing start, take it by the throat and bring it down and the line could take the rest of its leg for all she cared—

She bunched her back legs under her.

*Leah—*

There was a crack like a gunshot ten feet away, and a man was standing in the moss.

---

He was just there. One instant there was empty moss between two hemlock trunks, and the next he was standing on it as if he'd been standing there all afternoon. She'd have heard anyone coming through those trees. She'd have smelled him a mile off.

Leah spun to face him with her whole body low and her teeth bare and a growl coming up out of her chest that she could feel in her own ribs. Seth was beside her doing the same.

The man looked at the two of them. Then he looked up at the leech. Then he looked back at them.

He was tall. Six-two, maybe. Broad through the shoulders. He was wearing a suit. An actual suit, dark charcoal grey, with a white shirt and a skinny black tie, and over it a long black coat that came to his knees, with the collar up against the rain. Black leather gloves. Black shoes, shined. She looked at the shoes. They were standing in the wet moss of the Olympic rainforest a mile from the nearest trail, and there wasn't a speck of mud on them.

His hair was black and cut short. He hadn't shaved in a couple of days. He had green eyes. Very green; she noticed it the way you'd notice a car in a parking lot that's been painted an odd color.

And he smelled—

She breathed in, hard, through her nose, the way you do when you can't believe what you're smelling.

Pine. Earth. Cold stone, like the inside of a cave. Coffee, a little, and something sweet like baking. And under all of it, so faint she thought she'd imagined it, something that made the fur along her spine stand up for a reason that had nothing to do with leeches. Something with fur on it.

He was the farthest thing from a leech she'd ever smelled. His heart was beating, slow and steady, sixty beats a minute. She could hear it. It was the slowest heart she'd ever heard on a man standing ten feet from two wolves the size of horses.

He reached into the inside of the black coat.

She almost went for him then. Every muscle in her body was ready for it. But he did it slow, slow enough that she could see what he was doing, and what came out was a little black leather wallet. He flipped it open with one thumb and held it out at arm's length toward the two of them, at about the height of their eyes, the way cops did on TV.

"Afternoon," he said.

He was English. He had an English accent, like out of a movie, a little rough at the edges.

"This situation is contained," the man said. "I'd ask you both to please vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

Leah stared at the badge.

There was a gold shield in the wallet, and a card with a photo on it, and some printing. She could see it as clear as anything. FEDERAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION, it said, in blue letters, with an eagle. It looked exactly like the one that FBI agent had shown Charlie the time that kid from Port Angeles had gone missing two summers ago.

*Is he serious?* she said. *Is he actually serious? He's showing his badge to two wolves?*

*It says Homeland Security,* Seth said, sounding dazed.

*It says FBI.*

*No, it says Department of Homeland Security. It's got the eagle thing and the—*

"It says whatever you need it to say, Balto," said the man. "That's rather the point of it."

Leah's mouth shut.

Seth's mouth shut.

The man flipped the wallet closed and tucked it back into his coat. He looked at the two of them with an expression of mild, patient interest. Like a guy at a bus stop.

*Did he just,* Leah said, very slowly, *answer you.*

*Leah,* said Seth. *Leah. I think he can hear us.*

"Loud and clear," said the man. "Both of you. All at once. It's like being on a party line, you two. You do know you talk over each other?"

*Who the HELL,* said Leah, *is this guy?*

"Bloke with a badge, Lassie," said the man. "Do keep up."

There was a silence in the pack mind like the silence after a car crash.

*He called you Lassie,* said Seth, faintly.

*I heard him.*

*He called you LASSIE.*

*I HEARD HIM, SETH.*

The man had already looked away from them. He was looking up at the leech, the way you'd look at a fish you'd landed, judging the size of it. The leech's red eyes rolled down toward him and fixed on him, and Leah saw something in them she'd never seen in a leech's face before. It was afraid of the wolves. It had been running from the wolves for two miles. But it hadn't looked at the wolves like that.

"Hello, you," the man said to it, quite softly. "Aren't you a beauty."

He raised his left hand, palm out, in its black glove, and snapped his fingers.

The silver line went out like a light.

The leech dropped. It came down headfirst, stiff as a plank, straight down toward the moss, twelve feet, and Leah had time to think *it's going to hit and come up swinging* before the man took one step forward and caught it.

He caught it by the front of the stolen jacket with one hand, in midair, like catching a dropped towel. And then, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, he turned it in the air so it was horizontal, its feet one way and its head the other, and tucked it up under his right arm, the way Leah had seen a hundred surfers at First Beach carry a board down the sand.

It stayed rigid. It didn't bend. It lay there along his side under his arm with its arms pinned flat and its legs together and its red eyes rolling, as stiff as if it were carved out of wood.

Leah stared.

She'd fought leeches. She'd fought a lot of them. She knew what they weighed. They looked like people, slim and light, and they weighed like rock. They weighed like statues. Picking up a full-grown leech was like picking up a refrigerator. Emmett Cullen, who was the biggest of them, had once told Seth he weighed about as much as a small car.

The man was holding a refrigerator under one arm like a surfboard, and his shoes had not sunk into the moss by so much as a quarter inch.

"Right," he said. "Well. Lovely to meet you both. Off you trot."

---

*That's ours,* said Leah.

She didn't think it to him. She didn't think it to anyone. It came out of her the way growls came out of her, from the bottom of her chest, and she stepped forward with it, one paw, and her lips peeled back.

*We kill those. This is our land. This is OUR LAND, and that thing came onto it with blood on its jacket and we KILL THOSE—*

"Not this one, Lassie," the man said, without looking at her. "This one's spoken for."

She took another step. Seth was next to her, trembling. She could feel him not wanting to and doing it anyway.

The man turned his head and looked at her.

He kept both hands where they were. He looked at her, with those green eyes, from about eight feet away, and raised one eyebrow.

"Don't," he said.

She stopped.

She didn't know why. She hated that she didn't know why. She was a wolf the size of a horse with teeth that could go through the hide of a leech and she had stopped dead in the moss because a guy in a suit had raised his eyebrow at her, and every bit of her that was a wolf was telling her to go, *go*, and some small cold part of her at the very back that was just Leah, that had spent eight months watching things and learning what was dangerous, was telling her very quietly to stand still.

*He can't do anything to us,* she said to Seth. *He's one guy.*

"I'd rather not find out if you're right," said the man. "It'd be a terrible shame to have to find out. I've nothing against you. I've been watching you lot run round the hills for a fortnight and I've quite enjoyed it. You're very good." He shifted the leech a little higher under his arm, like a man hitching up a bag of shopping. "So let's not, eh?"

*A FORTNIGHT,* Leah said.

*What's a fortnight?* said Seth.

*Two weeks. It's two weeks, Seth. He's been watching us for two weeks and none of us smelled him.*

"Mm," said the man. "Sorry about that."

Something had changed in Seth. She felt it, the way you feel the floor shift when somebody heavy walks into a room. She'd been so busy with the man and the eyebrow and the leech that she hadn't been listening to her brother, and when she turned her attention to him she found him somewhere else entirely. He was back in that one instant. The crack in the air. The man standing on the moss where there'd been no one.

The crack.

Seth had heard that sound before. A lot. Leah went after the memory and saw it as he saw it: the backyard on Calawah Way, a week ago, in the dusk, and Layla in a green sweater, laughing, turning on her heel on the grass with her wand up, and vanishing with that exact sound, *crack*, and then another *crack* from the far end of the lot by the river, and Layla standing down there, waving. *It's called Apparating. Don't you dare tell my mama I showed you, I wasn't even allowed to do it till I was seventeen.*

And the silver line. And the badge that said whatever you needed it to say.

The thought went through Seth's head as clear and loud as if he'd said it out loud in an empty church.

*He's a wizard. He's a wizard like Layla.*

The man stopped.

### VIII. Balto

*Same place — 2:26 p.m.*

He'd been about to turn away, with the leech under his arm and his free hand coming up like he was going to snap his fingers again and vanish, the way Seth had seen Layla do a hundred times now, and he didn't. His hand stayed where it was, halfway up. He stood there in the moss with the rain coming down through the hemlocks and pattering on the shoulders of his black coat, and for one long second he didn't move at all.

Then he turned his head and looked at Seth.

Seth had been looked at a lot. He'd had Sam look at him, when he'd first phased, and the old men on the council, and Edward Cullen, who looked at you like he was reading your whole life off your face. That first time in the Thriftway, Layla had looked at him like she was trying to figure out what he was, and she'd gotten there.

He'd never been looked at like this. The man's eyes had been interested, before, mildly, the way you'd be interested in a dog in a park. Now they'd gone very still and very bright and very focused. They went into him. Seth felt them go in. He could feel them, somewhere at the back of his skull, like a finger pressed against the inside of his head.

"Now that," the man said quietly, "is something you shouldn't know, Balto."

His voice had changed. It was softer. It was a lot worse.

He took one step toward Seth. Just one. The leech under his arm stared at the treetops.

"Who told you that word?"

Seth tried not to think about her.

He tried really, really hard. He tried so hard that he did the thing you always do, where somebody tells you not to think about a pink elephant and suddenly there's nothing in your whole head but the pink elephant, and there she was. Layla. Layla in the garden this morning with the garlic and the rain in her hair. Layla on the porch swing. Layla lighting the lanterns with a flick. Layla's wand. Layla's yellow house. Calawah Way.

He felt the man see it. Seth didn't know how he knew. He just did.

"Ah," said the man. "The house on Calawah Way."

Seth's heart stopped.

*He knows,* he said. *Leah, he knows where she lives, he KNOWS—*

*Seth, calm down. Seth—*

*He's been watching for two weeks, he knows where she LIVES—*

He'd seen the man catch the leech. He'd seen him carry it like it weighed nothing. He'd seen the eyebrow stop Leah dead, Leah, who didn't stop for anybody, Leah who'd once growled at Sam in front of the whole pack. He'd felt that finger in the back of his skull. And this man knew where Layla lived, and he'd gone soft and quiet, and Seth thought of her alone in her garden in the rain on her knees in the mud with the garlic, and the man walking up her drive in his long black coat, and her bell in the window ringing, one note, and her turning round—

*If something comes for you that you can't handle,* she'd said, on Sue's front steps, with the rain coming off the eave. *You don't be brave. You bite down.*

*I promise, Layla.*

He'd rather she came here, ready, with her wand up and him and Leah next to her, than have this guy find her on her own.

He didn't think about it any more than that. He couldn't have. He just did it, the way he'd done everything that mattered in his whole life, all at once and without stopping. He dropped his head and twisted and got his right foreleg up to his mouth and found the little hard round bump on the leather cord under the fur and bit down on it.

It cracked between his back teeth like a hazelnut.

There was a taste like a battery on his tongue. A tiny flash of silver light came out between his teeth. And somewhere very far away, he felt something go *tug*, like he'd pulled on one end of a long string and somebody had felt it on the other end.

The man was looking at him.

"What've you got there?" he said. Then, as Seth lifted his head with the taste of lightning still in his mouth: "Oh, you didn't."

The air cracked open six feet to Seth's right, and Layla came out of it with her wand already up.

### IX. Outclassed

*Same place — 2:27 p.m.*

The hook went in under her breastbone while she had a pawn-shop ring in a pair of tweezers.

Layla had been at the kitchen table with a jeweler's loupe in her eye since lunch, picking at a cheap little hex someone had worked into a silver signet she'd found in a junk shop in Port Angeles. It was a nasty, petty, beautiful little piece of work, a jinx that would have made the wearer's fingers go numb a little more every day until the hand was useless, and some long-dead witch had hidden it under the engraving so cleverly she'd almost missed it. She'd been enjoying herself. She'd had the radio on. She'd been thinking about Seth, and garlic, and what she'd make for dinner.

Then the bead cracked, five miles away, and something took hold of her by the middle of the chest and *pulled*.

She didn't decide. Her body knew what that tug meant before her mind did. She was out of the chair with the loupe still in her eye and the wand out of her hair, curls tumbling, and she'd turned on her heel on the linoleum and followed the line the bead had thrown her without seeing the kitchen go. Tight. Black. The pressure all round, squeezing her ribs to a point, and the line pulling her down it like a fish on a reel.

She came out of it in the rain with her wand up and her feet already set.

Forest. Old growth, dim and green and dripping, enormous trunks, deep moss. Seth to her left, in his wolf shape, sandy fur dark with rain, head low, the end of the leather cord on his foreleg dangling where the bead had been. Leah beyond him, grey, every hair on her standing up, lips back off her teeth.

And ten feet in front of them, a man in a long black coat with a vampire under his arm.

She saw it all in one picture. She'd been trained to. Six years underground, where the thing you didn't see in the first half-second was the thing that killed you. The coat. The suit under it. The gloves. A tall man, broad, dark-haired, unshaven, with eyes that were already turned toward her. He'd been looking at the place she'd appear before she'd appeared. And under his right arm, held flat along his side like a plank, a thin blond man in a red hiking shell with his arms clamped to his sides and his eyes, his red eyes, rolling toward her and away.

A vampire. One of *theirs*. A stone-man from the old stories with fresh blood in his eyes. Held up off the ground, rigid, helpless, by a man in a suit, with her Seth eight feet away with his hackles up.

She had no idea what any of this was. She didn't need one. Seth had bitten the bead.

She put a Stunner straight at the man's chest.

It was a good one. It was the best she had. She'd been the Ilvermorny dueling champion two years running and the North American junior titleholder at seventeen, and she'd put a Stunner through a Shield Charm in competition more than once, and this one came off the tip of her wand bright red and fast as a bullet and on a dead-straight line for the center of his coat.

He lifted his left hand and flicked it away with one finger.

That was all. He crooked his first finger, in its black glove, and touched the air in front of him with the back of it, the way you'd brush a fly off your sleeve. And the Stunner bent. It changed direction all at once, as if it had struck a wall at an angle, and went whipping off sideways into the trees, and hit the trunk of a hemlock thirty feet off with a sound like a gunshot. Bark exploded off the tree in a spray of red-brown splinters as big as her hand.

Layla's whole body went cold.

---

She knew, then. She'd remember that, afterward. She'd remember that she knew it inside the first two seconds, and that everything after that was just her body and her training refusing to believe it.

You didn't deflect a Stunner with your finger.

You couldn't. It wasn't a thing that could be done. You blocked a Stunner with a Shield Charm, which needed a wand. You dodged it, if you were fast. You met it with a counter-curse, which needed a wand. Some old masters, people who'd been dueling for a century, could turn a spell aside with their wand in a little twist, a parry, like a fencer; she'd seen it done at an exhibition in Boston by a hundred-and-ten-year-old Brazilian woman, and it had been the most beautiful thing she'd ever watched, and the woman had been sweating afterward.

This man didn't have a wand in his hand. He hadn't spoken. He hadn't even looked at the spell. He'd been looking at her.

Seth and Leah moved.

They went at the same instant, from two sides, the way she'd seen them move in Seth's memories, low and fast and terrible, two wolves the size of horses closing on one man from left and right with their mouths open. She didn't even have time to scream at Seth to *stop*.

The man didn't look at them.

They stopped in midair. Both of them. Six feet off the ground, at full stretch, legs out and jaws wide, they hung there for a fraction of a second, exactly the way that can of Folgers had hung over her head in aisle six, and then something she couldn't see took them both and put them down. Hard. Flat on their bellies in the moss, side by side, a dozen feet apart, legs splayed out, chins pressed into the ground. Like a hand had come down out of the sky and pinned two kittens to the carpet.

Leah snarled. It was an enormous sound. She heaved against whatever held her, and Layla saw the muscles stand out under her fur along her shoulders and haunches, and the moss tore under her claws, and she didn't move an inch.

Seth whined.

"Stay down, Lassie," the man said. He said it the way you'd talk to a dog that kept trying to get up on the sofa. His eyes stayed on Layla the whole time. "You're all right, Balto. Nobody's hurt. I'm being very gentle." He tilted his head. "Afternoon. You'll be the witch. Bit rude, that."

He was English. Of course he was English. He had an English accent, a little rough, London maybe, and a face she'd never seen before in her life.

*Lassie,* Layla thought. *Balto.*

He could hear them. He could hear the wolves' minds. Whatever he was, he could hear them, and he'd been listening, and now he knew everything Seth knew.

She went to work.

---

She didn't repeat herself. That had been the whole of her style, in the dueling halls. Teo used to call it *throwing the kitchen*. Never two spells the same, never two from the same family in a row, never the same angle twice, until the person across the floor from you ran out of answers.

Ropes first. *Incarcerous*, hard and low, the cords coming out of her wand like striking snakes, to take his legs.

He crooked a finger. The ropes turned round in the air like a school of fish changing direction and came straight back at her, and she had to Vanish them six inches from her own face.

A Blasting Curse, then, at the ground at his feet, not at him, not near the thing under his arm, to throw him off balance. *Confringo*. Orange fire.

He tipped his hand up. The curse went up. It went straight up, as if it had hit a ramp, into the canopy forty feet overhead, and burst there in a ball of flame and smoke, and a hemlock limb as big as a canoe came crashing down through the branches, spinning. He stepped half a pace to the left without looking up. It hit the moss where he'd been standing hard enough to shake the ground under her feet.

He shifted the vampire a little higher under his arm.

She tried the tomb trick then. The one Teo had taught her, in a camp outside Cuzco, with a bottle of pisco between them and the stars out over the mountains. A Stunner wrapped inside a Disillusionment so it went invisible, and behind that, folded up inside it like a seed in a fruit, a Reductor Curse on a delay, so that if you blocked the first layer the second one opened up on your shield and blew it apart from inside. *A matryoshka, chica. A doll inside a doll. Nobody looks inside the doll.*

She'd never once seen anybody get through it clean.

She threw it.

He put up one fingertip, and the invisible thing she'd thrown stopped on the end of it, a foot in front of his chest, and hung there.

She could see it. She shouldn't have been able to see it, it was Disillusioned, but he was doing something to it and it shimmered into sight like heat-haze, a knot of tangled light spinning slowly on the tip of his finger. He looked at it. He looked at it the way she'd been looking at the pawn-shop ring an hour ago, through a loupe, interested.

"Oh, that's clever," he said.

Then he took it apart.

She watched it happen. He did something with his finger, a tiny turning motion, and the outer layer of the curse came away like peel off an orange. The Disillusionment drifted off in a ribbon of light and went out. The Reductor came out of the middle of it, a small black seed, and he flicked it away sideways into the wet ground where it burst harmlessly and threw up a little fountain of moss and mud. And then there was just the Stunner left, sitting on his fingertip, red and bare. He turned his hand over and *sent it back*.

She threw herself flat. It went over her head close enough to stir her hair, and hit the tree behind her, and the tree rang like a bell.

She came up on one knee with the wand already moving and something cold and sick in her belly that she recognized from Chachapoyas, from the moment she'd seen the jade mask on the shelf start to smile.

---

She'd read the accounts. Everyone who dueled had read them; you read them the way young baseball players read about Babe Ruth. She'd read the reports out of Paris in 1927, of a rally in a graveyard, of a ring of blue fire that killed every Auror who crossed it. She'd read the testimony from the duel in 1945, the one people still called the greatest ever fought, two men in a field in Austria for three hours, and the old witches and wizards who'd watched it from a hillside saying there'd been moments they couldn't tell which of the two was doing what to the sky. She'd read the reports in the international press ten years ago about a night in the British Ministry, a headmaster and a man the British were too frightened to name, and how the statues in the Atrium fountain had got up and walked. She'd read that the man they wouldn't name could fly. No broom. Just fly.

Every account she'd ever read of magic like this belonged to one of three names.

All three of them were dead.

The man in the black coat was standing in the moss with a vampire under one arm and two wolves pinned to the ground on either side of him, taking her curses off his fingertip and handing them back. He wasn't even breathing hard. He hadn't moved his feet more than half a step since she'd arrived.

She threw a dozen hemlock cones at him. She'd Transfigured them on the ground at her feet in the half-second after she came up, a dozen little brown cones into a dozen steel darts as long as her finger, and she Banished them all at once in a spread at his chest and legs, and she put a Stinging Hex behind them for good measure.

The darts stopped a foot from his coat and hung there, all twelve, in a neat little cloud. The Stinging Hex he swatted into the trees with the back of his glove.

Then the darts dropped into the moss like dead bees.

She tried the ground. She'd learned it in a tomb in Oaxaca, a Zapotec trick, old, older than wands: you cursed the earth a man stood on, and you made the roots of things take hold of him. She put her free hand flat on the moss and *pushed*, and all round the man's black shoes the forest floor heaved, and roots came up out of the ground, a dozen, two dozen, pale and thick as her wrist, twisting up to take his ankles.

He looked down at them.

They lay back down. They went back into the earth one after another, slowly, like dogs that had been told to heel.

She tried *Expelliarmus*, and felt like an idiot even as she did it, because he didn't have a wand to take. It hit him in the chest and did nothing at all. He laughed. It was a short, real, surprised sound.

"Nothing to take, love," he said. "Bless you, though."

---

Somewhere in there, she couldn't have said when, the vampire stopped being under his arm.

She saw it the next time she looked. He'd let go of it, and it hadn't fallen. It was floating beside him at the height of his hip, perfectly level, perfectly still, stiff as a board in its stolen red jacket, its red eyes rolling. It hung there in the rain like a coffee table somebody had forgotten to put legs on. Now he had both his hands free.

She had a curse left. She'd been holding it back.

She'd learned it in the dark too, the last year, before Teo. A thing off a stone lintel in Peru, cut there six hundred years ago by somebody who'd wanted grave-robbers very badly dead. She'd copied it out to learn how to break it, and in learning to break it she'd learned how to make it. It was black. It was a filthy, ugly, killing thing, and it would turn a man's blood to stone in his veins, and she had never once cast it on a living thing.

She felt it there behind her teeth. She felt her wand hand want it.

Seth whined again, on the ground, ten feet away, pinned in the moss, looking at her with his enormous dark eyes.

She let it go.

She put up a Shield Charm, the biggest she had, because the man had just reached into his coat with his right hand while his left kept on idly batting away the Jelly-Legs Jinx she'd sent at him, and what came out from under his arm was a gun.

A pistol. Big, square, blued steel, the kind her grandfather had kept in a drawer in Port Allen under his socks. A Muggle gun. For one tiny furious second, she was almost insulted.

Then she understood that he hadn't used one single spell on her yet. Not one. He'd only been sending hers back. And now he was going to use this, a No-Maj's gun, because he wanted to, because he could, because it was funny, and that frightened her more than anything else had.

*Protego*. Silver-blue and solid, a curved wall of force in front of her from the ground to above her head. She'd stopped a charging Erumpent with that shield once, in a training paddock in Wyoming. It would take a bullet. It would take a dozen.

He tipped the barrel down past the shield, almost lazily, and fired into the ground about three feet in front of the base of it.

There were two sounds. The first was the gun, a big hard *bark*, flat and loud in the wet air. The second came a fraction of a second after it, from the ground, from the spot where the round went in, and it was enormous. It was a sound like a bomb going off under a parking lot. The earth in front of her shield came up. It came up in a fist, a geyser of moss and black dirt and stones and splintered roots, and the force of it went under the bottom edge of her shield where the shield met the ground and picked her up.

She went backward through the air. She saw the canopy go by overhead, green and grey and spinning, and the rain falling down toward her out of it. She had time to think, very clearly, *that was a .45, that was one little .45 into the dirt,* and then she hit something that gave, and something else that didn't, and the world went white and rang like a struck bell.

---

She lay in a thicket of salal twenty feet from where she'd been standing, on her back, with leaves in her mouth and her ears singing one long high note. Dirt was coming down out of the sky onto her face. Little clods of it, and pebbles, and bits of moss, pattering on the leaves round her like the start of a hailstorm.

She couldn't breathe. She lay there and tried. Her chest wouldn't do it. Then it did, all at once, a great whooping gasp, and the air came in and her ribs on her left side lit up like someone had put a hot iron to them, and she rolled half over and coughed and spat out dirt and blood. She'd bitten her tongue.

Her hand was empty.

She looked at it. She looked at her empty right hand in the leaves, the fingers still curled round nothing. She hadn't let go. She'd never in her life let go of her wand. Six years in tombs, and a wolf-pit in the Carpathians, and a sunken cenote full of Inferi, and she had never once opened her hand. The blast had simply taken it.

The ringing in her ears was going down. Through it, she could hear the rain. And something else. A whining, very high and frantic. Seth.

She got her elbows under her. She got her head up.

There was a hole in the ground where she'd been standing. A crater, a yard across and two feet deep, steaming faintly in the rain, with black earth thrown out round it in a ring, and the moss scorched and torn back in a circle twenty feet wide. The hemlock nearest it had lost all the bark off one side.

And beyond the crater, exactly where he'd been the whole time, the man in the black coat stood with the gun in his right hand pointed at the ground and the vampire floating level at his hip. His shoes were still clean. There was dirt on the shoulders of his coat, a scattering of it, and he brushed it off with the back of his left glove, and then he held that hand out, palm up, into the air.

Her wand came out of the ferns on the far side of the clearing, where the blast had flung it, and floated across the crater through the rain, and settled into his palm.

He looked at it a second. Cypress. Thunderbird tail feather. Twelve and a quarter inches. Then he slid it into the inside pocket of his coat, beside the badge. He holstered the gun under his arm.

On the ground beside him, Seth was straining so hard against whatever was holding him that his whole body shook, his eyes fixed on her, whining. Leah had stopped fighting. She lay flat in the moss with her ears back and her teeth bared and her pale eyes moving from Layla to the man and back.

The man looked over at Layla in the salal.

"Right," he said. "Reckon we might try this with words now? Fewer spells." He glanced down at the two wolves. "Fewer teeth."

Nobody answered him.

"She's fine, Balto," he said, more quietly. "Cracked a rib, I should think. Bit her tongue. Nothing worse. I'd know." He looked at Seth for a moment. Then he made a small motion with two fingers, as if he were lifting something. "Go on, then."

Seth came up off the ground like he'd been fired from a gun.

He was at her in two bounds. He didn't even slow down; he came crashing into the salal and stopped over her and put his huge hot nose against her face, her neck, her hair, her chest, snuffling, whining, shaking, and she put both arms round his neck and held on and felt the heat of him come into her, and she thought she might cry and didn't.

"I'm okay," she said into his fur. "I'm okay, baby. I'm okay."

He whined.

"I know. I know you did. You did right."

Across the clearing, Leah was still flat on the ground.

"You I'm less sure about, Lassie," the man said. "Give it a minute."

Leah's lip curled back further.

Somewhere to the south and east, a long way off, very loud, a wolf howled. It was a big sound, deep, and it went on, and it carried through the rain and the trees like a siren.

The man looked up toward it with an expression of resigned interest.

"Oh, good," he said. "More of you."


---

### X. Terms

*Same place — 2:34 p.m.*

Jacob Black phased at two-thirty-one to take over the day line from Seth, and walked straight into the middle of a war.

He'd been asleep on the Cullens' couch. Nessie had been asleep on his chest, both small hands fisted in his T-shirt, breathing in that quick light way she had, and Bella had come and lifted her off him at quarter past and said, *you're late, Jake*, and he'd groaned and rolled off the couch and gone out the back and across the lawn to the trees, yawning, stripping his shirt off as he went.

He'd phased, and both of them were there, and both of them were *screaming*.

Leah never screamed, and she was screaming now, without any words in it at all. She was lying in the moss with something pinning her down that she couldn't fight and couldn't see and her whole mind had gone white and hot as a struck magnesium flare. And Seth. Seth was standing over his imprint in a thicket with his nose in her hair and his whole mind just one word, over and over, *Layla Layla Layla*, and under it, in flashes, everything. A leech hanging from a line of light. A man in a suit with a badge. *Lassie.* *Balto.* A Stunner bent off a fingertip like light off a mirror. Two wolves stopped dead in midair. Layla's spells going one after another into the man and coming back out of him. A gun. A crater. Layla going backward through the air like a doll somebody had thrown.

Jacob had been on the Cullens' back lawn. Ten seconds later he was a mile into the trees and still accelerating, and the howl came out of him without his deciding anything at all.

He ran it in six minutes. Eleven miles. He'd never run like that in his life.

---

He came into the clearing like a train coming out of a tunnel, and stopped so hard his paws tore up the moss for twenty feet.

He saw it all at once, the way you see a car wreck. The crater, still steaming. The bark blown off one side of a hemlock. A limb the size of a canoe lying broken in the moss. Layla Devereaux sitting up in a thicket of salal with leaves in her hair and blood at the corner of her mouth, holding on to Seth's neck. Leah, flat on the ground on the far side of the clearing, pinned like a butterfly to a card.

And the guy. Standing in the middle of the whole thing in a long black coat, with his gloved hands in his pockets, and a leech floating beside him in the air at the height of his hip like somebody had laid it on an invisible table.

The guy looked at Jacob. Jacob looked at the guy.

Every single thing in him that was a wolf said *kill*. The leech, first, because it was a leech, a red-eye, and it had human blood in it that he could smell from here. Then the man. The man who'd done that to Leah. The man who'd done that to Seth's girl. Jacob could feel the Alpha in him coming up through his chest like a fist, the thing he'd been born with and had finally taken up the night he walked away from Sam's pack, and it wanted this guy's throat.

"Clifford," said the man. "Lovely. You'll be the one in charge, I expect."

Jacob's lips came off his teeth. A growl came out of him that he felt in his own back legs.

"Sit," said the man.

Jacob did not sit.

"Fine. Stand, then." The man didn't seem bothered either way. "You can talk furry if you like. I can hear you perfectly well. But the lady can't, and she's got a stake in this, and I'd rather not have to translate. Up to you."

*He can hear us,* said Seth, from the thicket. *Jake, he can hear everything. He heard everything in my head. He knows about Layla. He knows about the Cullens. He knows about NESSIE, Jake, I'm sorry, I couldn't stop thinking—*

And in that instant, like a light switching on, the only thought in Jacob's head was Nessie.

He didn't mean it. He couldn't help it. Her face. Her bronze curls. Her small hot hands on his face, showing him things. The way she'd laughed under Layla's floating lights. The way she grew, every day, a little more, so that every morning she was someone new. Every bit of him that he'd ever been before her had gone to her the moment he'd looked at her, and she was there now at the front of his mind, and he knew, he *knew* with a sick lurch, that the man in the black coat could see her.

The man's face changed.

It was a small thing. Jacob almost missed it. Something went across it, quick, and was gone.

"Your girl," the man said. His voice had gone quieter. "The little one. With the curls."

*If you go near her,* Jacob said, and the words came up out of the bottom of him, *I will find you. I don't care what you are. I don't care what you can do. I will find you if it takes the rest of my life and I will—*

"Mate," said the man. "Calm down. I don't do children." He said it flatly, a plain fact, like telling somebody the time. "Not ever. She's got nothing to fear from me. You've my word on it."

Jacob stared at him.

He didn't know why he believed it. He did, though. Something in the way the man had said it. It had come out too fast and too flat to have been thought about first.

He stood there in the moss for a long moment with his hackles up and the rain coming down on his back.

Then he turned and walked behind the nearest hemlock, and let the wolf go.

---

The cold hit him like a slap. It always did, the first second, even running hot. He untied the cut-offs from his leg and pulled them on with shaking hands and stood there behind the tree a second longer with his palms against the bark, breathing.

Then he came out.

He was six-foot-seven in his bare feet. He had about forty pounds on the guy in the coat, and four or five inches, and he was standing in the rain in a pair of shorts in November steaming like a racehorse, and the guy looked at him with the same calm, slightly amused interest he'd had when Jacob was a wolf the size of a horse. As if the difference didn't come to much either way.

"Better," the man said. "Thanks."

"Let her up," said Jacob.

The man glanced over at Leah. "She'll go for me."

"She won't."

"She absolutely will. I can hear her thinking about it. She's got quite a detailed plan." He tilted his head toward Leah. "It involves my femoral artery."

*Leah,* said Jacob, in his head; he couldn't, not now he'd phased, but he looked at her, and she looked back at him with her pale wolf eyes, and he put everything he had into the look. All the Alpha in him. *Don't.*

Leah's lips came down off her teeth. Slowly.

"There we are," said the man, and made a little lifting motion with two fingers.

Leah came up off the ground. She stood up very slowly, the way you get up off a floor after somebody's knocked you down, shaking out each leg. She didn't go for him. She came round the long way, wide, keeping well clear of him and the floating leech, and went to stand next to Jacob, her shoulder against his hip, her head low. She was shaking all over. He put his hand in the fur on her neck and left it there.

In the thicket, Seth had already gone behind a tree. He came out in his cut-offs, running, and dropped onto his knees in the salal next to Layla and put his hands on her face, and her shoulders, and her side, and she hissed and caught his wrist.

"Ribs," she said. "Left side. Don't."

"Are they— is it—"

"It's cracked. It's fine. I've had worse." She was looking past him, at the man. Her face was white under the freckles and very still. "Help me up."

Seth helped her up. She stood. She was swaying, a little, and leaning hard on Seth's arm, and she had leaves in her hair and dirt down one whole side of her and blood on her lip, and she looked across the steaming crater at the man in the black coat like she'd have taken his eyes out with her thumbs if she'd thought it would do any good.

She'd figured out it wouldn't. Jacob could see her figure it. He'd seen people figure that out before. He'd seen Bella figure it out, about Edward, a long time ago.

"Who are you?" Layla said.

"You can call me Bonham."

"Is that your name?"

"It's the one I'm giving you."

"Who do you work for?"

"Next question."

"MACUSA?" She was watching his face. "The Confederation? Are you private? Are you— a bounty man, a collector, a— are you some kind of—"

"You keep guessing," said Bonham, "and I'll keep not answering, and we can do it all afternoon if you like. It's a nice rhythm. But it's starting to rain properly, and I've a pie at home."

"A *pie*."

"Pumpkin. From the diner. It's Thanksgiving week, apparently nobody orders anything else." He took his hand out of his pocket and gestured at the leech floating beside him. "Also, this one won't keep."

---

"What do you want with it?" said Jacob.

Bonham looked at the leech as if he'd forgotten it was there.

"Him? Research."

"That thing kills people."

"He does. Did." Bonham glanced down at the floating figure. Its red eyes rolled up toward him and stayed there. "Couple in a Subaru at a state campground outside Aberdeen. Two nights ago. They'd stopped on their way to the coast. He took the son's jacket after." He reached out with one gloved finger and flicked the collar of the red Gore-Tex shell, and the leech's eyes rolled again. "He's had a look at a few towns on the way up here. He liked yours. He was going to stop for a while."

Jacob's stomach turned over.

"How do you know that?"

"Same way I know your girl's name's Renesmee and you call her Nessie and her mum hates it," said Bonham. "Same way I know your doctor works nights, and the little dark-haired one sees what's coming, except she can't see you lot, or the baby, and from what Balto's heard she's having a terrible time with Miss Devereaux. Same way I know there's one of them who can feel what you feel, and one who can read your mind, and one who keeps everyone else out of her head, which I'd very much like to meet." He looked at Layla. "Same way I know that two and a half weeks ago this young lady sat on her porch swing on Calawah Way and pulled a wand out of her hair and told a nineteen-year-old wolf that she was a witch. Real kind. Government. Laws. Schools. *A whole world folded up underneath this one where you'd never look.*" He paused. "That's a nice line. Balto remembers it very fondly. He's had it on a loop in his head the whole time we've been talking."

Seth's face went brick-red.

Layla went whiter.

"And then, the next night," said Bonham, "she sat in an armchair in a glass house in the woods in front of a coven of a species nobody in your world or mine has ever so much as put a name to, and told them most of the same. And put on a light show for the baby. And the Sunday after that, she put out a grease fire in Mrs. Clearwater's kitchen in front of the Chief of Police of Forks, Washington, and didn't take his memory afterward, though she thought about it, and he saw her think about it, and told her not to." He looked at her steadily. "I'd have done the same, for what it's worth. He's a good man. He looked at my boots."

"Your *boots*?" said Layla.

"Long story."

The rain had picked up. It was coming down through the hemlocks properly now, steady and hard, rattling on the salal and hissing on the hot earth of the crater. It was running off Jacob's shoulders. It was running down Layla's face. Around the man in the coat it came down and stopped an inch above his shoulders and ran off sideways round him, like he was standing under glass.

"So here's where we are," Bonham said.

He said it plainly. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to.

"International Statute of Secrecy. Sixteen eighty-nine. Your lot and everyone else's. Breach of the Statute, Miss Devereaux, three counts that I know about, and I'd say there's a good few more if I went digging. The pack. The council on the reservation, I should think, by now; I'd be very surprised if Mrs. Clearwater hasn't told the old men. The coven. The Chief." He held up one finger for each. "Your government's not known for being relaxed about that sort of thing. Your government's quite famous for it, in fact. They used to lock people up for having a No-Maj to tea."

"They don't anymore," Layla said. Her voice was very steady. Jacob, standing six feet away, could hear her heart going like a hummingbird's.

"They would for this," said Bonham. "They'd send a team of Obliviators up here so fast your head would spin, and they'd do the reservation and the town and the council and Mrs. Clearwater, and Charlie Swan, God help him, and Balto here wouldn't remember his own girlfriend's name when they were done. Whether they can do the coven is another question. I'd love to know the answer to that one, scientifically. But they'd have a very interesting time finding out, and I don't think your vampire friends would let them try twice." He shrugged. "And then they'd come for you."

Nobody said anything.

"Right now," said Bonham, "there's exactly one person off this peninsula who knows what you did, Miss Devereaux. That's me. I'd like to keep it that way. I'm guessing you'd like that too."

Layla looked at him for a long time across the crater.

"What do you want?" she said.

"Nothing today."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I might ring," said Bonham. "And when I ring, you'll pick up." He looked round the clearing, at all of them, Layla and Seth and Leah and Jacob. "All of you."

"We don't work for you," said Jacob.

"Course you don't, Clifford. Wouldn't dream of it. You'll just be extraordinarily helpful."

"And if we say no?"

"Then someone comes who isn't me," said Bonham. "And they don't ask."

---

Jacob looked at the guy for a long time.

He'd known people like this. Not many. He'd known Sam, back at the start, when Sam had been the only one, and had walked around carrying the whole weight of the tribe on his back and didn't let anyone see it. He'd known Jasper Cullen, a little, now, enough to see how Jasper stood in a room, how he always knew where the doors were. Jasper had been a soldier in a war older than the state of Washington.

This guy stood like that. He'd stood in the middle of the Olympic forest and taken everything Seth's witch had and handed it back to her, and pinned two wolves to the ground without looking at them, and caught a leech in a trap made of light, and his heart had stayed at sixty beats a minute the whole time. And now he was standing there in the rain that wouldn't touch him, talking about pie.

And he could have killed all of them. Jacob understood that. It came to him slowly, the way cold comes up through your feet when you stand in a river. He could have killed Layla with that gun, as easy as anything, and he'd shot the dirt in front of her instead. He could have done anything at all to Leah and Seth while he had them pinned. He hadn't. He'd been careful. He'd been *gentle*, like he'd said.

He'd still put a crater in the ground where Seth's girl had been standing.

"What do we get?" said Jacob.

Bonham's eyebrows went up. He looked, for the first time, like he was actually interested.

"Go on."

"You want us to be helpful. Fine. What do we get?"

"Ah." Bonham nodded slowly. "Now we're talking." He took his other hand out of his pocket. "What you get is this. You've a line round this end of the peninsula that you run every day and every night, the three of you and the other lot. You've been running it since the spring. You've had red-eyes come through it before. You will again." He glanced at the floating leech. "From today, nothing with red eyes walks onto this end of the peninsula without ending up hanging from a tree the way this gentleman did. I've strung the whole of it. Every road. Every river. Every ridge. Ten miles round the town and your reservation both. Anything that's been drinking people walks in, it doesn't walk out. You'll have a great deal less running to do."

"And the Cullens?"

"Walk straight through it. It doesn't so much as tickle them. Tickles nothing with gold eyes. I made sure." He looked at Jacob. "I'm not here for them, Clifford. They're no use to me in a jar. Tell them that. You'll have to tell them anyway; the mind-reader'll pick this out of your heads by teatime. Tell them I've no quarrel with them. Tell the doctor I'd like a word, one of these days. Not today." He paused. "And tell the mind-reader that if he fancies a look round inside my head, he's welcome to try. Once."

"Why once?" said Seth.

"Because he won't want to try twice," said Bonham.

"And the Chief?" Layla said quietly.

"Nobody tells the Chief anything." For the first time Bonham sounded faintly emphatic. "He's had a hard enough year. And he's already looked at my boots twice."

"Your *boots*," said Layla again.

"I said it was a long story."

"What happens to that?" said Leah.

They all turned. She'd phased. Nobody had seen her go behind the tree, and she was standing beside Jacob in her cut-offs and a black tank top, shivering, with her arms wrapped round herself and rain plastering her short black hair to her head. She jerked her chin at the floating leech. Her face was white and furious and absolutely steady.

Bonham looked at her.

"Do you really want to know, Lassie?"

Leah held his look for five full seconds.

"No," she said.

"Good girl."

Leah's whole body went rigid. Jacob tightened his hand on her shoulder.

"Don't call me that," she said through her teeth.

"Fair enough," said Bonham. "Lassie it is."

---

He took Layla's wand out of the inside pocket of his coat.

He held it a second, looking at it. Then he tossed it, end over end, across the crater, and it turned twice in the air and dropped neatly into Layla's open hand as if it had been aimed.

She looked down at it. Her fingers closed on it.

"Why give it back?" she said.

"Because you'll want it," said Bonham. "There are worse things than me in the world, Miss Devereaux. You've met a few. And because it wouldn't make a blind bit of difference."

Layla's jaw tightened. She didn't lift the wand.

"You knew where I lived," she said. "Before. Before Seth thought it. You said *the house on Calawah Way*. Like you already knew."

"Since my third night here." Bonham nodded. "Lovely wards. Tidy. The sting in the outer layer's a nice touch. I left it alone." He looked at her a little longer. "Most people would've missed you. I'd have liked to keep missing you. You'd no business being in this, and now you're in it. I am sorry about that."

He said it like he meant it. Jacob couldn't tell if he did.

"How do I reach you?" Layla said.

"You don't," said Bonham. "I'll reach you."

He looked round the clearing once more. At the crater, still faintly steaming in the rain. At the hemlock with its bark blown off. At the canoe-sized limb lying in the moss. At the four of them standing there, two shivering in their shorts, two in clothes torn and filthy. At the ring of moss torn up round where Leah and Seth had been pinned, where their claws had dug in.

"Sorry about the mess," he said. "Be seeing you."

He put one gloved hand on the leech floating beside him.

He turned on the spot, and there was a *crack* like a branch breaking, and both of them were gone.

---

The rain came down.

Nobody moved for a while. The four of them stood there in the clearing in the hemlocks with the water running down their faces and the crater hissing softly between them, and listened to the silence where he'd been.

It was Seth who spoke first.

"He called me Balto," Seth said. "The whole time. I don't even know what that is."

"It's a sled dog," said Layla. Her voice was quiet. "A famous one. There's a statue of him in Central Park."

"Oh," said Seth. He thought about it. "That's kind of nice, actually."

"He called me *Lassie*," said Leah.

"Yeah," said Jacob. "We know, Leah."

"He called me Lassie and he called me *good girl* and he pinned me to the ground like a— like a—" She stopped. Her teeth were chattering. Jacob didn't think it was all from the cold. "Jake, I couldn't *move*. I couldn't move at all. I tried so hard I tore something in my shoulder and I couldn't move."

"I know."

"I've never not been able to move."

"I know, Leah."

Layla was standing very still with her wand in her hand, looking at the place in the moss where Bonham had stood. There was no mark there. Nothing. He'd stood there through all of it and the moss under his feet wasn't even pressed down.

"He left nothing," she said.

Jacob looked at her.

"Not one thing I can use." She said it slowly, like she was going down a list. "He's English. That's nothing. Anybody can do an English accent; I can do an English accent. His badge was blank. To me. Just black leather and a feeling like I ought to believe it. Somebody put a Confundus on it so fine I could barely see the seams, and that's nothing either, there's a hundred people in the world could do that and they could be anywhere. The suit's a suit. The gun's a gun. He never said a spell. He never used a wand. He never once said a single word that came from a school or a country or a government or a— *anything*." Her hand was trembling now, a little. "He knew about MACUSA. He knew about the Statute. He knew the year. Everybody knows the year. He knew about Ilvermorny, I think, the way he looked at my wards, the way he talked about them, but he'd only need to have seen one of us before to know that." She shut her eyes. "Whoever he works for, they built him so he wouldn't leave anything behind. And he didn't."

"Does it matter?" said Seth. "Who he works for?"

"Yes, chère." Layla opened her eyes. "It matters a whole lot."

"Why?"

She looked at the crater for a long moment.

"Because somebody sent him," she said. "And I'd sure like to know who sends a man like that to a place like this to catch one vampire."

Jacob looked down at the moss where the leech had hung. At the faint silver mark, already fading, that the line of light had burned into the bark of the hemlock thirty feet up.

*Nothing with red eyes walks onto this end of the peninsula without ending up hanging from a tree.*

"We have to tell the Cullens," he said.

"Yeah," said Leah. Her teeth were still chattering. "Edward's going to love this."

### XI. Specimen One

*The tent above the Calawah — 2:51 p.m.*

He came out of the Apparition in the hollow under the hemlocks with the vampire floating beside him at hip height and his left hand flat on its chest, and stood a moment in the rain, listening.

Nothing. The hollow was quiet. The tent sat in the middle of it, sagging and green and forgettable, and the creek went on at the bottom of the slope over its stones. No wolves. He'd have heard them. They'd have been loud about it.

He ducked in under the flap and the vampire came in after him, steered by two fingers, and he walked it through the front room past the range and the leather armchair and the pie on the table, and through the operations room, where the map lay on its table with a single red point burning a mile and a half north of the Quillayute, and through the library, and into the cold blue light at the end.

The cell door was the glass itself. He touched two runes at the edge of the pane and it went soft as water, and he walked the vampire through it and laid it on the stone floor on its back, and walked out again. The glass hardened behind him with a sound like ice forming on a pond.

He stood and looked at it.

It lay there in the middle of the cell in its stolen red jacket and its dirty jeans, rigid, arms at its sides. Its eyes were still rolling. Up at the ceiling, at the stone walls, at the shimmer of runes in the glass, at him.

"Welcome home," Harry said.

He took off the overcoat and hung it on the hook by the door. He took off the gloves one finger at a time and put them in the coat pocket, where they touched Layla Devereaux's wand-shaped absence and the badge. He took off the suit jacket and hung that up too, and loosened the tie, and rolled his shirtsleeves to the elbow, and unbuckled the shoulder rig and hung the Colt beside the coat. Then he took the brass card-frame off the glass, and a pen out of his shirt pocket, and wrote, leaning on the pane:

*Collected: 21.xi.06, 14:23. N. of Quillayute R. (gin trap, inner ring, anchor 77). Condition on capture: intact.*

He slid the card back into the frame.

Then he pulled a stool over from the steel table and sat down on it in front of the glass, with his elbows on his knees, and took the hold off the vampire's face.

---

It came back all at once. He watched it happen. One moment the face on the floor was a mask, and the next, everything above the neck was moving, the mouth and the jaw and the brow and the eyes, all at once, too fast, flickering through expressions like someone riffling the pages of a book.

"*Let me go,*" it said.

Its voice was very beautiful. They all had beautiful voices; that had been in the file. It was a tenor, with something of the American middle in it, flattened vowels, long ago. It filled the cell like music.

"No," said Harry.

"You don't know what you're doing. You don't know what I *am*."

"I'm getting a fair idea."

"You don't— *listen to me*. You have no idea what you've— there are rules. There are *laws*. There are people who'll come for this. Do you understand me? There are people who will come for you, for *this*, who'll tear this whole country apart to—"

"Oh, I do hope so," said Harry.

It stopped.

Its eyes went to him and stayed there, red and bright and wet. He watched it look at him properly, for the first time. At the rolled sleeves and the loosened tie and the stool and the man sitting on it with his elbows on his knees, quite comfortable, in front of a cell in a stone room at the end of a tent, with a basilisk's fang on a shelf behind him in a glass case and an Inferius's hand flexing its fingers in a jar.

"What are you?" it said.

"Ask me something else."

"What do you want?"

"Everything," said Harry. "Shall we start?"

He went in.

---

He'd been inside a lot of minds. Human minds, which were rooms full of fog and furniture you kept tripping over, everything half-remembered, half-made-up, a bit of last Tuesday tangled up with a bit of the summer you were nine. Goblin minds, which were cellars, cold and orderly and full of locked boxes. The Lethifold, which had been nothing at all, just hunger, black and flat as the bottom of a well. Seth Clearwater's, an hour ago, which had been like stepping into a field on a sunny day with a dog running round and round you, barking.

This was different. He'd expected different. He hadn't expected this.

It was all *there*.

Every memory he touched was perfect. Sharp as cut glass, bright as noon, every detail in it, nothing faded, nothing fogged. He touched the night before last, and it was as clear to him as if he'd stood in it: the state campground outside Aberdeen, the rain, a blue Subaru Outback with Oregon plates parked by the fire ring, a man and a woman in their fifties under the open hatchback with mugs of something hot and a little camp stove. The woman laughing at something. The man's hand on her back. The smell of their blood under the rain, coming across the dark at the vampire like the smell of bread out of a bakery door. He could see every leaf on the alder over the fire ring. He could see the pattern on the woman's mug. He could see, very clearly, everything that happened next.

He made himself watch all of it. That was the job.

Then he went further back, and it was the same. A truck stop in Idaho, in August. A woman hitchhiking outside Billings. A house in Reno, a family, in 1971. Every one of them perfect. Every face. Every sound. Seventy-five years of it, laid out in rows, like a gallery where every painting was a window and every window was open.

And behind them, at the far end of the gallery, a long way down, the one place where it all went soft.

The human years. He found them almost by accident, in the dark at the back, and they were the first thing in this whole mind that was like a human memory, blurred and broken, half of it gone. A dust storm. A brown sky in the middle of the day. A farmhouse in the panhandle of Oklahoma with the windows taped and a mother coughing in the next room. A name, said by that mother, hoarse and full of love, that the creature on the floor had not thought about in seventy-five years. *Danny.* Daniel Pruett. Twenty-two years old. Spring of 1931.

And then a woman on the road outside Guymon, at dusk, in a long dark coat, with red eyes. And then fire.

Harry hadn't known about the fire.

It was the worst thing in the mind. Worse than the campground. It was a memory of three days of burning, every second of it laid down as perfect as the rest, and he went into the edge of it for half a second and came out again with his heart going hard and the taste of ashes in his mouth. *Three days*, he thought. *Every one of them goes through that. Every single one.* The venom. The change. He made a note to write it down. He made a note to remember that he hadn't wanted to.

Then he went looking for what he'd come for.

---

It took him a long while. The mind was enormous and clean and there was no fog to hide anything in, but there was a great deal of it, and the creature fought him. He felt it fight. It had no shields, nothing a wizard would recognize, but it could *move*, inside itself, faster than any human mind he'd ever been in, and every time he reached for a memory it tried to snatch that memory away and throw a different one in front of him, like a card sharp palming an ace. It was clever. It was quick. It was seventy-five years old.

He was a good deal quicker.

He found the law, first. The one law. Everyone knew it; it was laid down in this mind like bedrock, like the first thing a child learns about fire. *Keep the secret.* Never let them know. Never hunt where it will be noticed. Never leave a body that can be found. And behind the law, the ones who kept it. A feeling came first, before any name. A cold place at the very bottom of the creature's mind where it kept all the things it was afraid of, and in the middle of it, like a stone in a riverbed, a single word. *Volterra.*

He went down into the cold place.

Stories. Mostly stories. Things told by other nomads round fires in the backcountry, in the long empty hours, in the night. He went through them one after another like a man going through a filing cabinet.

*The brothers.* Three of them. Old, older than the oldest city anyone could name, sitting on thrones in a stone room under a hill in Italy.

*The guard.* A girl with a child's face, Jane, who could look at you and make you burn worse than the change. A boy, her twin, who could take every sense you had away until you were floating in black. A tracker, Demetri, who could find anyone, anywhere, anywhere on earth, if he'd ever once brushed against their mind; and a great hulking one who did the tearing. The creature had seen the tracker once. Chicago, 1987. A vampire who'd been careless in a hospital. He'd watched from a rooftop three blocks away as a man in a dark cloak walked into an alley and walked out again ten minutes later with ash on his hands, and it had been the only time in seventy-five years Daniel Pruett had been truly afraid.

*The immortal children.* An old story. Older than any of the others. Babies. Little ones. Turned, once upon a time, by vampires who'd loved them or wanted them, and who never grew, who couldn't learn, who couldn't be taught or controlled, who'd destroyed whole villages in their tantrums. The brothers had wiped them out. Every one. And every coven that had made one. Burned them all, in a great fire, centuries ago. It was the one thing that would bring the guard down on you faster than breaking the secret. Faster than anything.

And in the middle of all of it, coming up again and again, from every nomad and every fire, the one story every one of them told and the one that this creature had been most afraid of, sitting at the very heart of the cold place like a spider in the middle of its web.

*Aro.* The eldest. The one who touched you.

*If he takes your hand, he knows. He knows everything. Every thought you ever had. Every face you ever saw. Every word anyone ever said to you. He takes your hand, and you're an open book, and he reads you cover to cover in a second, and then he knows everything you know.*

Harry sat very still on his stool.

He'd heard that before. An hour ago. In a dog's sunny head, in a memory the dog had been half embarrassed by and half thrilled about, of a firelit room in a glass house and a pale young man at a window saying, in a voice like a cello, *Aro is the eldest of them. If he takes your hand, he hears every thought you've ever had.*

And then, quieter: *He's touched me. Once. In the spring.*

He came out of the vampire's head.

---

He sat for a while on the stool in the cold blue light, with his elbows on his knees, and looked at nothing.

On the floor of the cell, Daniel Pruett lay with his face working, and said, hoarsely, "Get out of my head. Get *out* of my *head*—"

"I'm out," said Harry.

"What did you— what did you *do*—"

"Had a look round." He got up off the stool. "You've a lovely memory. It's very tidy in there. You should see mine."

He went to the steel table and took one of the goblin-silver knives off the roll where he'd left it that morning, and a little glass phial with a stopper, and a notebook, the fifty-fourth, still mostly empty, and he went back through the glass into the cell.

---

The next hour was work.

He wrote it all down as he went. That was the job too.

*Skin.* He drew the edge of the goblin silver across the back of the creature's right hand, hard, the way you'd draw a diamond across glass, and it went across with a sound like a nail on slate and left a faint white line, as fine as a hair, that closed up as he watched. Goblin silver would cut steel. It had once cut him a door through the side of a Gringotts vault in Zurich. *Hardness: extreme. Goblin silver marks it; does not penetrate under hand pressure. Self-repairs in seconds. Recommend: Fiendfyre; basilisk; Severing Charm under test. Do NOT recommend: bullets, standard blades, Muggle anything.*

*Venom.* He put a hand flat on the creature's forehead and held its head still and prised its jaw open with two fingers of magic, against a strength that would have snapped the arm off a gorilla, and the creature snarled at him without moving and snapped at the air an inch from his wrist. He held the phial to the edge of its teeth. The venom ran out of the gums into the glass, clear and thin, like water, with a smell of rotten sweetness that went up the inside of his nose and stayed. He stoppered it. *Venom: clear, low viscosity. Odour: sweet, decay. Do not get any on you, idiot.*

*Light.* He'd wondered about this since the plaza in Volterra. He stood back and held up his hand and called up a light he'd taken eleven years to get right, a knot of white-gold fire the size of a tangerine that burned with the same spectrum as noon on a clear day in June. *Lumos Solem*, people called the little version of it, the school-girl version. This was the full one. He let it rise to the ceiling of the cell and hang there.

The creature's skin caught it. Its face, its neck, the backs of its hands, the strip of belly where the jacket had ridden up: all of it lit up at once as if somebody had flung a fistful of crushed diamonds into a spotlight. Little points of light everywhere, prisms of it, a thousand, a million, scattering off the skin and dancing on the stone walls and the glass. It was the plaza in Volterra. It was exactly the plaza in Volterra.

He looked at it for a long moment, with his face lit up in a thousand colours.

*Conjured sunlight reproduces the effect,* he wrote. *It's the light, then. The spectrum. Doesn't need the sun. Could be useful. Could be very useful. Ask Thought whether it's on the surface or in the cells.*

He put the light out.

He wrote one more line under it, and underlined it.

*How they die: the wolves know. Teeth, then fire. Ask the wolves.*

Then he closed the notebook and walked back out through the glass, and it hardened behind him, and Daniel Pruett lay on the stone floor of Cell One and watched him go with his red eyes.

"That's it?" the creature said. Its beautiful voice had gone thin. "That's *all*?"

"For today."

"What happens tomorrow?"

Harry hung the knife back on its roll. "Tomorrow," he said, "I find out what else you've got in that tidy head of yours. And the day after that, some very old people in a very quiet building a long way from here find out what I found out. And after that, Danny, it's out of my hands."

The creature flinched at the name. He watched it flinch.

"Nobody's called me that," it said, "in seventy-five years."

"I know," said Harry. "I've seen."

He turned off the hard blue light at the door, and left it lying in the dark.

---

He made tea. He made it properly, the way he'd been taught in a kitchen on Privet Drive at the age of six by a woman who'd hit him with a wooden spoon if the bag stayed in too long, and he stood at the range with the mug in both hands while the heat came up through the iron into the soles of his shoes.

Then he cut a slice of the pumpkin pie and ate half of it standing up.

Then he took the mirror down off its shelf.

"Croaker," he said.

It took longer this time. Nearly a minute. When the glass cleared it showed a different room behind the old man's head, dark wood and green lamplight, and Croaker was wearing a dinner jacket that looked older than the Ministry building.

"Potter. I'm at the Minister's table."

"Then I'll be quick, sir. I've got your specimen."

Croaker didn't move his face. He never did. But he put down something out of frame, a glass, and the click of it was very small and very distinct.

"Intact?"

"Intact. Male. Seventy-five years since it turned, near enough. Snare took it clean, inner ring, north of the river, at twenty-three minutes past two local. Paralysis held the whole way in. It's in Cell One now."

"Preliminaries."

"Skin's harder than anything I've got short of the fang. Goblin silver marks it, barely. It heals in seconds. Venom's clear, sweet, nasty. Phial of it on the shelf for the Thought people. And the light thing from the plaza, the glitter, that's the spectrum. I did it with a conjured noon and got the whole show."

"Mind?"

"Perfect recall," said Harry. "Every memory sharp as the day it happened, right back to the day it turned. Bit past that, blurred. Legilimency works. It fights, quick as anything, but it hasn't got shields, not as we'd know them. It's a library with no locks on the doors." He drank some tea. "I've had a look at their law. It's what we thought. One law, mostly. Keep the secret. And a family in Italy keeping it. The old man, the brothers, the guard. I've got names for six of the guard and what they do. You'll want it in a Pensieve. I'll send it Thursday."

"And how they die?"

"Teeth and fire," said Harry. "The wolves do it. Tear them apart and burn the pieces. I watched it in their heads. That's step one. Whether anything of ours does it quicker, that's step two, and I'll need more than one of them to find out properly."

"Then get more than one."

"Working on it, sir."

Croaker was quiet for a moment.

"You said the wolves," he said. "You've been in their heads."

"Had to be. They chased it into my snare. Two of them. Then they had a lot of opinions about who it belonged to." Harry put the mug down. "They're people, sir. Lads and a girl from the reservation, who turn into wolves the size of a lorry when there's one of these things about. They share a mind when they're furry. All of them. Like a party line. And they've been killing this lot for the best part of a year."

"Hostile?"

"Not to us. Not now."

"Explain *now*."

Harry told him. He told him about the witch on Calawah Way, and the bead, and the duel in the clearing. He kept it short. He told him the witch was good. He told him she'd broken the Statute half a dozen ways in under three weeks for a lad she was in love with, and that the wolves knew, and the coven knew, and the Chief of Police knew a bit. He told him what he'd said to her in the clearing, about the Obliviators and MACUSA, and what she'd said back.

"She'll keep quiet," Harry said. "They all will. She knows what MACUSA does. And the wolves want the red-eyes off their land more than they want me off it. I gave them that."

"MACUSA?"

"Doesn't know I'm here. Won't."

"Does the witch know who you are?"

"She knows a bloke called Bonham with a nice coat."

Croaker grunted.

"And the coven?" he said.

"They'll know all of it by tonight," said Harry. "One of them reads minds, sir. Few miles' range. The wolves'll walk into his house and he'll have the whole afternoon out of their heads before they've shut the door. Another one of them sees what's coming. Bits of it. Can't see the wolves at all, can barely see the witch. My guess is she can't see me." He thought about it. "There's one who feels what you feel, and pushes it about. And one who keeps the mind-reader out of her head, which is going to be the most interesting thing on this whole peninsula if it's what I think it is."

"The Swan girl," said Croaker.

"Yes. She's one of them now. It was her, at the river. Turned this autumn."

Croaker was silent again.

"Anything else?" he said.

There was a notebook on the table at Harry's elbow. The fifty-fourth. It was open where he'd left it, and on the page he'd scrawled, in a hurry, at the side of the tent before he'd taken off the coat, a few lines from inside Jacob Black's head while the boy stood in the rain with his hackles up.

*CHILD. Born early Sept? Half-human — heartbeat. Grows. Fast. Bronze curls. Shows you pictures with her hands. "Nessie." The big one would die for her. They all would.*

Harry looked at the page for about as long as it would take to read it.

Then he shut the notebook, and put his hand flat on the cover.

"No, sir," he said. "That's the lot."

"Good work, Potter."

"Enjoy your dinner, sir."

The glass went dark.

---

He sat in the leather armchair for a long time with the mirror face-down on his knee.

The rain came down on the canvas overhead. The range ticked. Through two doorways and down the length of the library, he could just see the edge of the cold room, dark now, and the faint shimmer of the runes on the glass of Cell One.

He thought about the gallery in Daniel Pruett's head. About every face in it being perfect, and every word anyone had ever said to him, and every story told round a fire. And about the one story that sat at the bottom of the cold place in that mind like a spider in the middle of a web.

*If he takes your hand, he knows everything you know.*

And a pale young man at a window in a glass house, in a memory a wolf had been half embarrassed by: *He's touched me. Once. In the spring.*

He set it out in his head the way he'd been taught to, one piece after another, like laying slate.

Edward Cullen had been inside Aro's hand once. Aro had let him walk out of Volterra. He'd done it because he wanted something, and from what Harry had seen of the old man in Daniel Pruett's memories, Aro always wanted something, and always came back for it.

Edward Cullen now knew there were witches. He'd sat across a firelit room from one. By tonight he'd know there was a man on the peninsula catching his kind in traps made of light, and he'd know that man's face, and his voice, and the name he'd given, and the gun, and the snare-line, and the tent, roughly, and the crater in the moss. He'd have it all out of four heads by teatime.

And the Cullens had a child.

A child who grew. A child who wasn't in any of the stories the nomads told round their fires, except one. The oldest one. The one that brought the guard down on you faster than anything. *Little ones. Turned. Who never grew, who couldn't be taught.* Any vampire who saw that girl from a distance, without knowing what she was, would see exactly that. And then they'd go to Volterra. Because that was what you did.

And Volterra would come here.

They'd come for the child. They'd come to the glass house in the woods. And at some point, sooner or later, the old man would put out his hand to Edward Cullen, smiling, the way the stories said he smiled, and take it.

And in one second, Aro would know everything.

He'd know there was a whole world folded up underneath the one he'd ruled from his stone room for three thousand years. He'd know there were witches and wizards, millions of them, in every country on earth, hiding. He'd know there was one in a yellow house on Calawah Way. And he'd know there was a man in a black coat with an English accent catching his kind in the Olympic forest and taking them away to study, and he'd start to wonder who'd sent him, and why.

Every advantage the Department had in this, they had because Aro didn't know they existed.

The Cullens had stopped being an asset at twenty-six minutes past two that afternoon, when a nineteen-year-old wolf had thought one word too loudly in a clearing. They were now the single biggest risk to the Statute of Secrecy on the face of the earth. And they had no idea.

"Bollocks," said Harry, to the empty tent.

He sat with it a while longer.

Then he thought about the other side of it. He made himself. That was the job too.

If they came, they'd come *here*. A long way from Volterra, from its stone walls and its tunnels and its three thousand years of knowing every inch of the ground. Here. To the Olympic Peninsula. To a forest he'd spent nine nights in the rain stringing with wire. To a snare-line keyed, very precisely, to one thing and one thing only: human blood, a lot of it, inside a body with no heartbeat.

Every one of the guard in Daniel Pruett's memories had red eyes.

Croaker wanted a date.

Harry put the mirror back on its shelf. He picked up the notebook and the plate with the other half of the slice of pie on it, and went through into the operations room, and stood over the map. A hundred and thirty-nine white points of light. The thin silver hair of the inner ring. The town. The reservation. And east of the town, on the river, in a clearing in the trees, a single small mark he'd made with a pencil on the first night: a square, for the glass house.

He looked at it for a long time, eating the pie.

He was going to have to go and see the Cullens.

Not today. The mind-reader would need a night to finish going through the wolves, and to tell the others, and to be properly frightened, and to talk himself out of it, and to be frightened again. Tomorrow, then. Or Thursday. Thursday was Thanksgiving. The Americans would all be at home with their families. He could bring a pie.

He'd need to talk to the doctor. He'd need to talk to the one who kept people out of her head, because if she could keep the mind-reader out she might be able to keep the old man out too, and that would change everything. He'd need to find out how fast the child was growing, and who'd seen her, and who hadn't. And he'd need to find out whether a Cullen would sit across a table from a man who'd just strung their whole end of the peninsula with a trap and listen to him say *I'd like to help you, and I'd like you to help me, and if your old man in Italy ever comes for your little girl, I'd like very much to be here when he does.*

He finished the pie.

Down at the end of the tent, in the dark, past the shimmer on the glass, Daniel Pruett lay on the stone floor of Cell One and stared at the ceiling with his perfect memory, waiting.

There were three empty cells beside him.

"Get comfortable, Danny," Harry said quietly, to no one at all. "You're going to have company."

He washed the plate. Then he went to bed with his boots on.
