# Snare

## Chapter One — Contained

---

### PART ONE: SETH

**Forks, Washington — three weeks after Renesmee Cullen was born**

The list was in his mother's handwriting, and it had been folded so many times on the drive in from La Push that the crease had eaten whatever word came after *eggs*.

Seth Clearwater stood in the produce section of the Thriftway with a red plastic basket hooked over his forearm and squinted at it. *Eggs. Milk (2% — NOT whole, Seth). Bread. Something potatoes. Coffee. Onions.* The something could have been *russet*. It could have been *red*. It could have been a swear word. His mom had written it with the phone pinned between her ear and her shoulder, Charlie on the other end, the kitchen radio going on about the Mariners finishing last in the division again.

He picked up a ten-pound bag of russets. Then a bag of reds. Then he put both in the basket, because whatever he bought would be gone by Tuesday anyway. Everything in the Clearwater house was gone by Tuesday. It had been that way since Leah phased, and worse since he had, and somewhere along the line his mom had quit asking how two people could eat like eleven and started buying flour in sacks big enough to sit on.

Rain came down the front windows in long gray sheets. It was the second Saturday in October, a little after four, and the light outside had already quit for the day. Inside it was fluorescent hum and the smell of wet coats and floor wax and the coffee grinder by the bakery, and threaded underneath all of it, faint and sweet and stuck to the inside of his own nose, the smell of the Cullen house.

He'd been there most of the morning. He went most mornings now. Three weeks since the baby — since Renesmee, since *Nessie*, a name Bella still hadn't forgiven Jacob for — and the big glass house on the river smelled sugary enough to make his eyes water. He was getting used to it. Three weeks ago he'd stood in that house and watched Jacob walk upstairs meaning to do something terrible, and he'd watched him come back down looking like somebody had hit him with a truck made out of sunlight. Seth had gotten used to that, too. He'd gotten used to Bella with red eyes and a careful way of holding a coffee mug she didn't drink from. He'd gotten used to the baby who looked at you like she was reading your file.

He was a fast learner. Everybody said so.

He hooked a carton of eggs, checked them for cracks the way his mom did, and came around the end of the cereal aisle at the exact moment a shopping cart came around it from the other direction.

The cart caught him low on the hip. It didn't hurt. Nothing much hurt anymore. He turned to say *sorry*, which was what he said whether or not it was his fault, and the whole store tipped sideways and stayed that way.

He knew what it was. He knew what it was before he'd finished turning his head, because he had lived inside Sam's memory of the first time Sam saw Emily, and Jared's, and Quil's, and three weeks ago he'd had a front-row seat inside Jacob's head while Jacob's entire life pivoted on a baby's brown eyes. He'd felt it secondhand so many times that the real thing should have been familiar.

Secondhand hadn't prepared him for any of it.

Something under his ribs went tight, a line drawn from the middle of him to the middle of her, and every other line that had been holding him in place — his mom, the pack, the rez, the beach, the stupid basket on his arm — let go at once and came back hooked to a different anchor. He felt the floor through his sneakers. He felt his own heartbeat. He felt the earth turning under the store, and for one long, stupid second all of it was turning around a woman in a tan canvas jacket with rain in her hair.

"Hey," she said. "You okay? I clipped you pretty good."

Her hair was a dark copper color, pulled into a braid that was losing the argument with the weather, little wet pieces stuck to her temples and her neck. Freckles across her nose and the tops of her cheeks. Gray eyes, gray like the river stones at First Beach after a wave pulled back off them. She was maybe five-six, wiry under the jacket, and she had a thin white scar across the back of her right hand, straight as a chalk line, running from the knuckle of her first finger to her wrist.

Seth realized he was holding the eggs against his chest like a football.

"Yeah," he said. "No. Yeah. I'm good. Hi."

"Hi," she said, slowly.

"I'm Seth."

"Okay." One corner of her mouth moved. "You want to let go of those? You're kind of squeezing them."

He looked down. One of the eggs had a crack in it now, a hairline running up the shell. He put the carton in his basket very carefully, on top of the potatoes, and then took it off the potatoes because his mom would kill him, and set it on top of the bread, and then realized the bread would be flattened by the eggs, and then gave up and just held the basket.

She watched all of this with the expression of someone watching a nature documentary about a large, harmless animal.

"Sorry," he said. "About the cart."

"I hit *you*."

"Sorry anyway."

That got a real laugh, short and surprised out of her, like she hadn't been planning on it. She pushed a wet piece of hair off her forehead with the back of her scarred hand. "Okay, Seth. No harm done."

He looked at her cart, mostly because he needed somewhere else to put his eyes before he did something really embarrassing. Six cylinders of Morton salt, the blue kind with the girl and the umbrella. Two bottles of red wine, cheap. A bag of oranges. A box of Lucky Charms. A coil of copper wire from the hardware side of the store and a box of plain white beeswax candles, the tall kind people bought for power outages. A jar of whole cloves.

"That's a lot of salt," he said.

"I'm brining something."

Her heartbeat jumped on *brining*. Just a little, just one skip, the way a car shifts gears. Seth had been listening to heartbeats for a year now and to Bella's absence of one for three weeks, and he'd gotten good enough at it that sometimes he forgot other people couldn't hear.

"What are you brining?"

"A ham." She paused. "A big ham."

He nodded seriously. "That's a big ham."

"It's a really big ham."

"Like, a whole pig."

"Like a whole pig, Seth." She narrowed those river-stone eyes at him and her mouth did the thing again, the almost-smile. "Do you work here? Is this a loss-prevention thing?"

"No! No. I'm just — I'm getting stuff for my mom." He held up the list as evidence. "She wrote 'something potatoes' and I don't know what the something is, so I got both."

"Very thorough."

"I'm from La Push. Down the coast." He pointed, which was pointless, because they were inside. "I haven't seen you before. Are you new? You're new."

"I'm new." She leaned on her cart handle. "I moved in about a month ago. I'm renting a place out past the Calawah. Off the 101, the gravel road with the green mailbox."

Seth knew the green mailbox. Everybody knew the green mailbox. "The Hendricks place?"

"I don't know who Hendricks is."

"Old guy. Died. Before my time. That house has been empty for like six years. The roof—"

"Leaks." She nodded. "Yeah. I handled it."

"You fixed the roof?"

"I handled it," she said again, mild, and something about the way she said it made the back of his neck prickle. Her heartbeat was steady that time. Whatever she'd done to the roof, it was true.

"If you need help with anything out there," Seth said. "Like — firewood, or the porch, the porch is pretty bad, or the gutters. I'm good with gutters. I'm good with most things. My friend Jake fixes cars. I could get Jake. If your truck—"

"How do you know I have a truck?"

"Everybody out past the Calawah has a truck."

She considered that and seemed to accept it. Then she put out her hand. "Layla."

He shook it.

She didn't let go. Her fingers tightened on his, and her gray eyes came up to his face, sharper now, all the documentary-watching amusement gone. "Jesus. You're burning up."

"I run hot."

"You run *hot*? Seth, you're—" She turned his hand over in hers, palm up, like she was going to read it. Her thumb pressed the inside of his wrist, right over the pulse. "That's a fever. That's a real fever. You should be at urgent care, not buying potatoes."

"I feel fine. It's a family thing. Like — metabolism."

"Metabolism," Layla said.

"We all run hot. My sister, too." True. "My cousins." Close enough to true. "Doctor says it's normal for us."

Her thumb stayed on his wrist one second longer than it needed to. Seth felt the pad of it like a brand. Then she let him go, and her hand went into the pocket of her canvas jacket and stayed there, curled around something long and narrow that made the fabric stand out in a line. He watched her shoulders settle. He couldn't have said what had changed in her face. Something in it had switched from *friendly* to *paying attention*, and it had happened while she touched him.

"Layla," he said. "Like the song?"

The almost-smile came back. "My dad was a big Clapton guy."

"My dad liked Creedence. He used to—" The sentence ran out from under him. It still did that, a year and a half later. He could be fine for weeks, and then a word would come out of his mouth in the wrong order and his dad would be standing in the middle of it, singing "Proud Mary" off-key in the kitchen with a spatula. "He liked Creedence," Seth said again, quieter.

She heard the past tense. He watched her hear it. She didn't say *I'm sorry*, and she didn't make the face people made. She said, "Good taste," and let it go, and Seth thought that might have been the nicest thing anybody had done for him in a month.

They ended up in the same checkout line, because there were only two open and the other one had Mrs. Stanley with a cart full of coupons. Layla went first. Seth watched the cashier, a junior from Forks High named Katie he half knew, scan six canisters of salt without blinking. Layla paid cash. Her wallet was old brown leather, the edges worn pale.

Seth paid with his mom's twenty and got back four dollars and change and stuffed it in his jeans. By the time he got out the sliding doors, Layla was already halfway across the lot in the rain, bags hooked over both wrists, heading for an old green Ford with Minnesota plates and a dent in the tailgate.

"Hey!" he said, and jogged after her. The rain was cold on his face and hissed a little where it hit the back of his neck. "Hey — Layla."

She turned with her keys in her hand. Rain was running off the brim of her hood.

"Can I see you again?" he asked. "Like on purpose?"

She looked at him for a long moment. He was six-foot-five and soaked and holding a grocery bag with ten pounds of potatoes in it like it weighed nothing, and he knew exactly how he looked, because Leah told him at least twice a day.

"Forks is three blocks long, kid," Layla said. "You'll see me."

"I'm not a kid. I'm eighteen."

"Congratulations." She opened her truck door. "I'm twenty-two. That's a lot of years for someone with a fever."

"I don't have a fever."

"Get your temperature checked, Seth." She swung up into the cab. Then she leaned back out, just her face and the wet copper braid. "It was nice to get hit by you."

"You hit *me*."

"Sorry anyway," she said, and shut the door.

He stood in the rain and watched the green Ford pull out onto Forks Avenue and turn north, toward the 101 and the Calawah and a house with a roof that wasn't supposed to be fixable. He stood there until he couldn't hear the engine anymore, which was a long time, because he could hear an engine for about two miles now if he wanted to.

The line in his chest went with her. It didn't break when the truck went out of sight. It just stretched, easy as anything, and stayed attached.

"Oh, man," Seth said to the parking lot. "Oh, *man*."

**La Push — that evening**

Leah heard him coming from the road.

She heard the truck first — their mom's old Ford, the one with the busted muffler Jacob kept promising to fix — and then the door, and then the wet thump of Seth's sneakers on the porch steps, two at a time, and she knew from the rhythm of it before he'd even gotten the screen door open. She knew her brother's moods by his feet. She had lived inside his head for a year, and before that she'd changed his diapers.

He was happy. He was so happy he was practically bouncing up the stairs.

Leah put her pen down on the community college catalog she'd been marking up and waited.

The screen door banged. Seth came in sideways with three grocery bags in each fist and his hair plastered to his skull, dripping on the linoleum, and set the bags down on the counter so carefully that she knew something was wrong with the eggs.

"Hey," he said.

"You cracked the eggs."

"One egg." He started unpacking. Bread, flattened. Milk — the right milk, at least. Two bags of potatoes. He didn't look at her. "Mom at Charlie's?"

"Mom's at Charlie's." Leah watched him put the potatoes in the fridge. Then take them out and put them in the cupboard. Then stand there holding the cupboard door. "Seth."

"Hm."

"What happened?"

He turned around. He had their dad's face, mostly — the wide jaw, the eyebrows that did all the work — and right now that face was lit up from the inside like a jack-o'-lantern.

Leah's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

"No," she said.

"Leah—"

"No. Don't say it."

"I imprinted," Seth said.

She'd known. She'd known from the stairs. It still hit like a door slammed on her hand.

The kitchen was very quiet. The fridge hummed. Rain ticked on the window over the sink, the window their dad had put in the summer she was nine, standing on a stepladder with a level and cursing in a way he thought she couldn't hear. A pot of their mom's stew sat on the back burner on low, and the smell of it filled the room, venison and onions and bay leaf. Leah looked at it. It was easier than looking at Seth's face.

She thought about Sam's face. She didn't want to. It came anyway, the way it always came: Sam in the doorway of Emily's house two years ago, looking at Emily with that same lit-up lantern expression, while Leah stood in the yard in a sundress she'd bought for him, and understood before anybody said a word that she'd stopped existing. That nothing she was or did or wanted had counted for anything once the universe had made its choice for him.

Then she'd phased, and she'd gotten to live inside his head while he loved someone else. For months. Every patrol. Every thought.

*Imprinting is a leash*, she thought. *It's a leash and they call it a soulmate.*

Seth was still standing there, holding the cupboard door, waiting for her to say something.

Leah picked up her pen. Put it down. "Who is she?"

"Her name's Layla." He said it like a word in a language he'd just learned. "She just moved here. She's renting the Hendricks place."

"The Hendricks place doesn't have a roof."

"She fixed it."

"Is she Quileute?"

"No."

"Makah?"

"She's from Minnesota. I think. She has Minnesota plates."

"Of course she does," Leah said. She heard how she sounded. She didn't try to fix it. "How old?"

"Twenty-two."

"Seth."

"I'm eighteen! I'm allowed to—"

"I know how old you are. I was there." She pushed the catalog away. "Does she know you exist?"

"She hit me with a shopping cart."

Leah closed her eyes.

"It was an accident," Seth added. "She said sorry. Then I said sorry. It was kind of a whole thing." He finally let go of the cupboard and came over to the table and pulled out the chair across from her and sat in it backward, arms folded on the top rail, chin on his arms. He was dripping on the floor. Their mom was going to lose her mind. "Leah. It's — I didn't think it'd feel like that. I've seen it. I've seen Jake's, I've seen Sam's—" He winced. "Sorry."

"Don't."

"I know. Sorry. But I've seen it a bunch of times, from inside, and I still didn't know. It's like the whole planet moved over three feet."

"I know what it's like," Leah said. "I've been inside Sam's head every day for two years. I know exactly what it's like."

Seth was quiet. When she opened her eyes he was looking at her with that open, stupid, kind expression that made people want to tell him things, and she hated it, and she would have taken apart anybody who tried to change it.

"You don't have to be happy for me," he said.

"I know I don't." She rubbed her face. Her hands were hot. Her hands were always hot. A year ago her hands had been normal and she'd had a fiancé and a plan, and her body had been on the same schedule as every other twenty-year-old woman's body on the planet. Now she was six feet tall and ran at a hundred and eight degrees and hadn't had a period in eleven months, and there was a word for what that meant, and the word was *dead end*. The magic didn't hand out imprints to dead ends. The magic picked for the bloodline. It had picked Emily for Sam. It had picked a half-vampire infant for Jacob. It had picked some salt-buying white girl from Minnesota for her baby brother.

It had never picked anything for Leah Clearwater, and it never would. Most days she was glad.

"What's she like?" she made herself ask.

Seth's face did the lantern thing again. "She's funny. She's — she said 'sorry anyway' back to me. She's got this scar on her hand, like a cut, really straight. And she knew. When she grabbed my hand, she felt how hot I was and she got—" He frowned. "She got careful. Like, all of a sudden. She put her hand in her pocket and held something."

"Pepper spray."

"I don't think so. It was long."

"A knife."

"I don't think it was a knife." He scratched his jaw. "And she lied about the salt. She said she was brining a ham. Her heart jumped."

"Maybe she's a serial killer."

"*Leah*."

"Maybe she's salting the earth. Maybe she's a slug enthusiast." Leah stood up and took the stew off the burner, because it was starting to stick and because she needed to be facing the stove instead of facing him. "You're going to tell Jacob."

"Yeah. Tonight. When I phase." He hesitated. "Leah, I'm not gonna — I'm not gonna tell her. About us. Not yet. I want to—" He stopped.

"You want her to actually like you first," Leah said to the stew. "If she's going to."

"Yeah."

"Good." She banged the ladle on the side of the pot, harder than she needed to. "Smart. Make her pick you on her own before the magic picks her for you."

He didn't answer that. When she turned around, he'd gotten up and was putting the eggs in the fridge, and she could see the one cracked shell from across the room, a thin dark line running up it like a hairline fracture.

"Get a towel," she said. "You're dripping on Mom's floor."

**The following weeks**

Forks was three blocks long. Layla had been right about that.

The first time Seth saw her again on purpose, it was the following Thursday, at the diner on Forks Avenue, and he hadn't been looking for her, exactly. He'd just driven past four times. On the fourth pass he saw the green Ford parked out front with its dented tailgate, and he parked behind it, and went in, and she was sitting at the counter with a cup of coffee and a slice of blackberry pie and a thick paperback with the cover folded back so nobody could see it.

He sat down two stools away.

She didn't look up from her book. "You drove past four times."

"Three."

"Four, Seth."

"Four," he admitted.

She turned a page. "Did you get your temperature checked?"

"Yes," he said, which was true. Carlisle had checked it on Tuesday, mostly out of curiosity, and written *108.9* in a little leather notebook and said *fascinating* in that way he did. Seth had not mentioned to Carlisle that the reason he'd asked was that a girl in the Thriftway had told him to.

"And?"

"Normal. For me."

"What's normal for you?"

He opened his mouth. Shut it. She glanced at him sideways over the edge of the book, and he got the strong, specific feeling that she already knew the number, and was waiting to see if he'd say it.

He ordered pie instead. Two slices. The waitress, Cheryl, had known him since he was eight and didn't blink.

They talked for an hour. Seth would think about that hour a lot over the next few weeks, lying on his back on his bed at night with the window open and the rain coming in, and what he'd remember most was how many questions she asked and how few she answered. She asked about La Push. She asked about the tribe, about the elders, about the old stories — *the really old ones, the ones nobody tells tourists* — with a kind of hungry precision that made him careful. She asked about the forest. She asked whether anybody he knew ever felt anything strange out in the woods past the river. Like a hum. Like the trees were listening.

"That's a weird question," Seth said.

"I'm a weird girl."

"What do you do? Like for work?"

"Estate appraisals." She took a bite of pie. "Antiques. Old things people find in their grandmother's attic and want to know what they're worth."

Her heart skipped on *appraisals*. A small skip. Enough.

Seth thought about Leah's voice in his head: *Make her pick you on her own.* He thought about all the things he couldn't tell her yet. He decided, sitting there with blackberry on his fork, that if he was going to have secrets she couldn't ask about, then she was allowed to have some, too.

"Cool," he said, and meant it.

She looked at him for a second like he'd surprised her. Then, without any warning at all, she reached across the empty stool between them and put two fingers on the inside of his wrist again. Light. Clinical. Her eyes went unfocused, the way people's eyes went when they were counting.

"A hundred and eight," she said. "Give or take."

Seth's fork stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Good guess," she said, and took her hand back, and went back to her pie.

"How did you—"

"Everybody's got something, Seth." She turned a page in her book. Her heart was steady as a clock. "Eat your pie."

The week after that, he fixed her porch step.

The week after *that*, he fixed the gutters, and the porch railing, and the latch on the woodshed, and then ran out of things on the outside of the house to fix and started looking hungrily at the inside. She let him into the kitchen on a Sunday in early November with the rain coming sideways off the Pacific, and handed him a beer, and told him not to touch anything in the living room.

The Hendricks place was small and old and should have smelled like six years of mildew, and it didn't. It smelled like woodsmoke and coffee and cloves, and something sharp and clean underneath that he didn't have a word for. Like the air before lightning. The roof didn't leak. He'd seen from the yard that there were whole sections of shingles missing on the north slope, gaps you could have dropped a cat through, and when he stood in the kitchen and listened, the rain hit those gaps and stopped. Just stopped, a few inches above the bare boards, and slid away sideways like it had hit glass.

In the corner of the living room, there was a wooden box about the size of a breadbox, wrapped three times in copper wire, sitting inside a ring of salt. Six canisters' worth.

It was whispering.

Seth heard it from the kitchen doorway. Low and scratchy and continuous, like a radio left on between stations, and every few seconds it said a word. He couldn't make out what the words were. He didn't want to. Every hair on his arms stood straight up, and something low in his chest, where the wolf lived, pulled its lips back off its teeth.

"Layla," he said carefully. "What's in the box?"

She was at the stove with her back to him. She didn't turn around. "Work."

"It's talking."

That did make her turn around. She looked at him with a spatula in one hand and an expression he'd never seen on her before, wide-eyed and still. "You can hear it."

"Yeah."

"From over there."

"Yeah."

"What's it saying?"

"I don't know. I don't—" The wolf in his chest was still showing its teeth. "I don't like it."

"No," she said. "Nobody does. It's a nineteenth-century music box from an estate sale outside Tacoma, and the woman who owned it locked her husband in the cellar for thirty-one years, and some of that is still in it." She said this the way someone else might have said *the transmission's shot*. "It's fine. The salt holds it. Don't step over the line."

"I wasn't gonna."

"I know." She turned back to the stove. Her shoulders were tight. "Most people can't hear it, Seth. Most people walk past it and get a headache and go home and yell at their kids and never know why."

He thought about the heartbeat skip on *appraisals*. He thought about the roof. The thing in her pocket at the Thriftway, long and narrow. Her fingers on his wrist, counting. *Everybody's got something.*

"I'm not most people," he said.

"No," Layla agreed, very quietly, to the eggs in the pan. "I've been noticing that."

They ate at her kitchen table with the rain coming down and the box muttering in the next room. Afterward she walked him out onto the porch he'd fixed, and they stood under the eave in the cold with the woods black and dripping all around the clearing, and he could hear her heart going fast and she could probably see his breath steaming more than it should have.

She put her hand flat on his chest. Right over the place where the line went in.

"I need to tell you something," she said. "And I can't."

"Okay."

"That's it? Okay?"

"I've got one of those, too," Seth said.

Her hand stayed where it was. He watched her decide something, and then decide against it, and then step back, and he let her go, even though every part of him that wasn't the wolf wanted to follow her inside and the wolf wanted it twice as much.

"Drive safe," she said.

"I'm not driving," he said, before he could stop himself.

She looked at the old Ford parked by the woodshed, which was his mom's, which he had in fact driven here. Then she looked back at him.

"I'll come back for the truck," Seth said, and walked off into the trees, and didn't look back, and phased the second he was far enough in that she wouldn't hear the sound his clothes made coming apart.

*Smooth*, said Leah in his head, from fifteen miles away. *Very smooth.*

*Shut up. Are you on patrol?*

*Somebody has to be. You're busy getting your heart broken by an antique dealer.*

*She's not an antique dealer.*

*No*, Leah thought, and he felt her turn it over, the box and the salt and the rain stopping in midair over the missing shingles. *She's not.*

**Five weeks after the Thriftway**

He told her on a Thursday, because he couldn't stand it anymore and because Jacob, who was so happy these days it was disgusting, said *Go for it, man, she's yours, she gets to know*, and because Layla had stopped pretending she didn't know there was something to tell.

He walked her out into the trees behind the Hendricks place at dusk. The rain had let up for once, and the light under the spruces was gray-green and thick as water. Their breath hung in front of them. Somewhere off to the north he could hear the Calawah running high with a week of rain in it.

"So," Layla said. She had her hands in her jacket pockets. Both of them. He knew by now that one of those hands was wrapped around the long narrow thing she always carried. "We're going into the woods. At night. Alone."

"It's not night yet."

"Seth."

"Okay. Okay." He stopped in a little open space between three big cedars, where the ground was soft with needles. He turned to face her. He'd practiced this. He'd practiced it in the shower and in the truck and to Jacob, who'd laughed at him for twenty minutes straight. None of the practice versions came out. What came out was: "There's this old story."

"I like old stories."

"I know you do." He took a breath. "The Quileute — we're descended from wolves. That's the story. The spirit warriors. Taha Aki, he could leave his body and go into a wolf's, and then later he couldn't come back, so he and the wolf became the same thing, and then his sons — they could do it, too. Turn into wolves. To protect the tribe."

"From what?"

He'd been ready for *That's a cute story*. He hadn't been ready for *From what*, asked in that same flat, interested voice she used for the music box.

"From the Cold Ones," he said. "Vampires. Different from — I mean, I don't know what you think vampires are. These ones are cold and hard and they don't have heartbeats and they smell sweet, like, way too sweet, and they're really fast and really strong, and they drink blood. Mostly people. Some of them drink animals instead. Those ones are okay. Mostly."

Layla said nothing at all. She was standing very still.

"And when the vampires come back," Seth went on, because now that he'd started he couldn't stop, "the wolves come back, too. The spirit. It's in the blood. It gets switched on. It got switched on for me last year. For my sister, too, and Jake, and a bunch of guys. That's why I'm a hundred and eight degrees. That's why I could hear your box. That's why I—" He couldn't figure out how to say the next part. He decided not to. "I'm gonna show you. Okay? Don't run. I'd never hurt you. I'd never, *ever* hurt you. But don't run, because it's — running is a thing that makes it harder."

"Seth," Layla said. Her voice was very calm. "Show me."

He stepped back. Then three more steps, to give himself room. He'd tied a pair of cutoffs to his ankle with a leather cord before he came, because Jacob had said, *Dude, do not get naked in front of her before you get naked in front of her*, which was gross but fair. He looked at her one more time — copper braid, gray eyes, hands in her pockets — and let go.

It was fast. It was always fast. Heat ran up his spine like a match dragged down a strip, and his body came apart and went back together bigger, and his shirt and jeans went to pieces with a sound like a sheet tearing, and then he was standing on four feet in the cedar needles with the whole forest pouring in through his nose — the river, the spruce, a deer two hundred yards east, the woodsmoke from her chimney, and her. Cloves and coffee and that clean lightning smell, stronger now, and her heartbeat, going fast but even.

He was big. He knew he was big. He was sandy-colored and shaggy and his shoulder came up to the height of her head, and he made himself stay very still and keep his ears forward and his mouth closed.

Layla looked at him for a long time.

Then she let out a breath that steamed in the cold.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Yeah. That tracks."

In Seth's head, a long way off on the patrol line, Leah thought: *That's it? 'That tracks'?*

Layla took her right hand out of her jacket pocket.

She was holding a stick. That was his first thought. A stick about thirteen inches long, dark brown, polished, a little thicker at one end, with a pattern carved into the handle that looked like feathers. She held it the way Jacob held a wrench, like it was the most natural thing in the world to have in your hand.

"My turn," she said. "Don't freak out."

She turned the stick in a little circle, barely a flick of her wrist, and said a word Seth didn't know.

The rain stopped.

It had started up again a few minutes ago, a fine cold mist sifting down through the cedar boughs, and now, all at once, in a circle about twenty feet wide around the two of them, it simply wasn't there. He could see it still falling outside the circle, silver in the gray light. Inside, the air was dry. He could hear the mist hitting something above them, something invisible, with a sound like sand on a tent.

Then Layla flicked the stick again, and lights came out of the end of it.

Little ones. Dozens of them. Gold and blue, the size of marbles, rising out of the tip like sparks off a campfire, except sparks went up and went out, and these went up and *stayed*, drifting outward into the branches over their heads and hanging there like somebody had strung the cedars for Christmas. They lit her face from below. They lit his fur. They lit the drops of water stopped and sliding on the invisible roof over their heads.

*Leah*, Seth thought. *Leah. LEAH.*

*I see it*, Leah thought back, and for once in her life she didn't sound sarcastic. She sounded like somebody who'd been punched. *I see it. I'm seeing it. Seth, what the hell.*

*Get Jake.*

*Jake's not phased. He's at the Cullens'. Seth, what is she?*

"I'm a witch," Layla said, out loud, as if she'd heard. She hadn't. She was just answering the question his whole huge wolf face had to be asking. "That's the word. It's not a great word. I've never liked it, honestly, but it's the one we use. There's a lot of us. Not a lot a lot. But a lot. All over. We have schools and laws and a government and a newspaper, and we're really, really not supposed to tell anybody." She swallowed. Her hand on the wand was shaking a little, he saw. Just a little. "I'm a curse-breaker. That's the real job. People find bad old things and I take them apart before they hurt anybody. The box in my living room. That's what I do. I used to duel, too. Competitively. I was pretty good." She laughed, a little high and strange. "I'm breaking about nine laws right now, Seth."

He phased back.

He didn't decide to. It just happened, heat running the other way down his spine, and he was on his knees in the cedar needles, human and naked and shivering in a way that had nothing to do with cold. He fumbled the cutoffs off his ankle and dragged them on, and then he stood up, and she was right there, close enough to touch, with the little gold lights hanging over both of them and the rain hissing on the invisible roof.

"Nine laws," he said.

"Give or take."

"For me."

"I wasn't going to let you be the only one standing out here with no clothes on," Layla said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Metaphorically."

He kissed her.

Or she kissed him. He'd never be sure, afterward, and he'd never care. Her hands were cold on his face and her mouth tasted like coffee, and the stick was still in her right hand, pressed flat against his cheek. He could feel it humming, a tiny vibration like a phone on silent. The line in his chest that had been stretched across the whole town for five weeks went slack and easy all at once, like a rope that had finally been tied off at both ends.

When they came apart, the lights were still hanging in the cedars.

"So," Layla said, breathless, with her forehead against his chin, because that was as high as it reached. "Vampires."

"Yeah."

"Some of them are okay. You said. Mostly."

"The Cullens. They live out on the river. They don't eat people." He hesitated. "There's a baby. Kind of. It's a long story."

"I want to meet them."

"Seriously?"

"Seth, I take apart cursed objects for a living. Something under this whole forest has been humming at me since the day I drove in, and I just watched my boyfriend turn into a horse-sized dog." She pulled back far enough to look up at him. Her eyes were very bright. "I want to meet *all* of them."

*Boyfriend*, Seth thought.

*Oh my God*, Leah thought, from fifteen miles away. *Oh my God, he's going to cry.*

He wasn't going to cry. He was pretty sure he wasn't going to cry.

**The Cullen house — three days later**

Leah stood with her back to the big glass wall and her arms folded across her chest, and watched a witch float Emmett Cullen up to the ceiling.

It was a Sunday afternoon, gray and dripping, and the Cullens' living room was so crowded that even the vampires had run out of places to stand. Carlisle was by the piano with Esme's hand in his. Edward stood behind the white sofa, which was where Bella sat with the baby in her lap; Rosalie perched on the arm of it like a guard dog in a cashmere sweater. Alice was cross-legged on the floor directly in front of Layla, close enough that if she'd wanted to, she could have taken the witch's throat out before the witch blinked. Jasper stood against the far wall, near the stairs, his face tight and careful the way it got when a room had too many feelings in it. Jacob had the armchair. Seth had the floor beside Layla's feet, and he'd been grinning so hard for forty minutes that Leah was starting to worry about his face.

And Emmett was going up.

He'd volunteered. Of course he'd volunteered. He'd stood in the middle of the room with his arms spread and said, *Okay, witch lady, do your worst*, and Layla had looked at Seth, and Seth had shrugged, and Layla had pointed that stick at Emmett's chest and said something that sounded like Latin. And now three hundred and fifty pounds of grinning, granite-skinned vampire was rising off the hardwood like a parade balloon, slow and steady, past the level of everybody's heads, until his hair brushed the ceiling twenty feet up and he hung there with his arms still spread, rotating gently.

"This," Emmett announced from the ceiling, "is the greatest day of my life."

"Your *wedding* was the greatest day of your life," said Rosalie.

"Which one?"

Rosalie bared her teeth at him. Emmett laughed so hard he started to tip over sideways, and Layla corrected him with a little twitch of her wrist without even looking up, the way you'd steady a cup.

Leah watched the wrist.

That was the thing she couldn't get past. The floating was insane, but she'd been living inside an insane world for a year — she turned into a wolf; her brother turned into a wolf; she'd stood in this house three weeks ago and smelled a baby that was half vampire — and her brain had a place to put *insane* now. It was a big room with a lot of shelves. Floating went in there fine.

What she couldn't put anywhere was how *easy* it was for Layla. Layla did it like she'd been doing it since she was eleven, which, she'd explained in her flat Midwestern way an hour ago, she had. There was a school. It was in Massachusetts on top of a mountain. There were *houses*, named after animals, and there was a government in New York with a name that sounded like an allergy medication, MACUSA, and there was a law. One big law, all over the world, signed in 1689. *Don't tell.* Don't tell the people who can't do it. Don't let them see. Don't let them know. Hide.

Leah knew a thing or two about hiding. The tribe had been doing it for a thousand years.

"Bring him down slowly," Carlisle said, and there was a note in his voice Leah had never heard before, something like a little boy's voice in a church. "Please. Esme likes that floor."

Layla brought Emmett down slowly. He landed in a crouch, bounced to his feet, and immediately said, "Can you make me a wand? Can vampires have wands? Edward, can vampires have wands?"

Edward had been very quiet. Leah had noticed. He'd been standing behind Bella with one hand on her shoulder, and his gold eyes had been fixed on Layla's face the whole time with a look of intense, almost painful concentration, like a man reading a book in a language he only half knew.

"I don't know," Edward said. "She doesn't know, either." He tilted his head at Layla. "You don't. You're wondering the same thing."

Layla's head came around fast. "That's twice now."

"I'm sorry. It's difficult to turn off."

"No — it's—" She stared at him. "You're not using a wand. You're not even making eye contact. There's no spell."

"There's never a spell," Edward said. "I just hear it."

"Everything?"

"Everything you're thinking, while you're thinking it." He paused. "You're thinking about a teacher. Someone who taught you a discipline for closing your mind. You weren't very good at it, she said. Too impatient." Another pause. "You're thinking that you should have practiced more."

Layla sat back on the edge of the sofa and pressed her lips together, and for the first time since she'd come into the house, she looked rattled. Leah felt an ugly little flicker of satisfaction at that and then felt bad about it, and then felt annoyed that she felt bad about it.

"There's a name for what you do," Layla said. "In our world. We call it Legilimency. But it's a skill. It's hard. You have to learn it, you need eye contact for it, mostly, and it's—" She waved her hand. "It's rare. It's really rare. And what you're doing isn't that."

"No," Edward agreed. "I don't think it is."

"I have about a thousand questions," Layla said.

"I know."

"Can you *stop* that?"

"Not really," Edward said, with something that was almost an apology, and Bella laughed, a bright bell-sound in her new voice, and reached up and squeezed his hand.

Carlisle let go of Esme and came around the piano. He moved slowly for him, which was still faster than any human; he moved like he was trying not to startle a deer. He crouched down so his gold eyes were level with Layla's.

"I was born in London," he said. "In the sixteen-forties. I don't know if Edward's mentioned it — I don't know if anyone's mentioned anything about any of us. My father was an Anglican pastor. A zealous one." His mouth moved, wry and old. "He spent his life hunting the evils of the world. Vampires. Werewolves. *Witches.* He burned a great many innocent people, in his time, Miss Kincaid. And not one of them, I think, was ever what he said they were."

Layla looked at him for a long time.

"No," she said. "Probably not. We were pretty good at not getting caught, by then. There's a spell for it. If the fire was real, you'd just — make it cool. Like a tickle." She hesitated. "There's a story about a witch who liked it so much she got herself caught forty-seven times. Wendelin the Weird. It's a story we tell first-years."

Carlisle closed his eyes.

When he opened them again, Leah saw something in his face that she'd only ever seen in the faces of very old men at the end of very long funerals. A kind of relief. A kind of grief. Three hundred and sixty years of carrying something, and someone had just handed him the receipt.

"Thank you," he said quietly.

"For what?"

"For telling me it was all a lie."

On Bella's lap, the baby made a little sound and held out both her hands.

Renesmee was eight weeks old and looked like she was going on two. She had Bella's brown eyes and a riot of bronze curls and a face so beautiful it made Leah's teeth hurt, and she talked in whole sentences in a little bell voice when she felt like it, which wasn't often, because she didn't have to. She had something better.

"She wants to show you something," Bella said. "Is that okay? She just — she touches you. It doesn't hurt."

Layla glanced at Seth. Seth nodded, eager, beaming.

Layla leaned in. Renesmee put one small pale hand flat against her freckled cheek.

Leah watched Layla's eyes go wide and blank. She knew what the witch was seeing. She'd been on the other end of it a dozen times: a picture poured straight into your head, clear as a movie. Probably Emmett floating. Probably the little gold lights, which Seth had been replaying in the pack mind for three days straight like a kid with a new song.

"Oh," Layla whispered. "Oh, she's — oh. That's so — what *is* she?"

"Ours," said Rosalie, flat and final, from the arm of the sofa.

Layla looked up at her. Then she laughed, short and startled, and nodded once, like that was a perfectly good answer. Renesmee took her hand back and smiled the slow, wise smile that made Leah want to check her for a second set of teeth.

Then the front door opened.

Every vampire in the room was already turning before the knob had finished going around. Leah heard the car in the drive in the same instant they did — she'd been tuning it out, she realized, because she knew the engine so well. Big old Ford sedan. Police issue. Heavy feet on the porch, the creak of a gun belt, the jingle of keys.

"Bells?" said Charlie Swan, in the doorway. "I brought those—"

He stopped.

He was holding a grocery bag. Leah could smell what was in it: a rotisserie chicken and a carton of the off-brand vanilla ice cream he always brought for the baby, who couldn't eat it, but liked to watch him eat it. He was in uniform. He'd come straight from the station.

And he was looking at the ceiling. At the cluster of little gold and blue lights that Layla had hung up there an hour ago and forgotten to take down. They were still drifting gently against the beams, like dust in sunlight, glowing.

Then he looked at Layla. At the polished stick in her hand.

Layla moved.

It was very fast — fast for a human, anyway. She came up off the sofa with the wand already rising, and her face had gone completely blank and professional. Leah had seen that face before, on cops and on Sam and once on her own father, the day he'd put down a dog that had been hit on the highway. It was the face people made when they were about to do something they didn't like because the rules said so.

"I'm sorry," Layla said to Charlie, very quietly. "This won't hurt. Ob—"

"*No.*"

Edward didn't shout. He didn't need to. He was simply standing between the wand and Charlie, somehow, with no visible movement in between, and his gold eyes had gone flat and hard.

And from the sofa came a sound Leah had never heard Bella Cullen make before. It came up from the bottom of her chest, low and rolling, a sound like a big cat in the dark. Bella was on her feet. She had the baby tucked into one arm against her shoulder and her red eyes were fixed on Layla's wand, and her lips had pulled back from her teeth.

The room went dead silent.

Jasper, by the stairs, had straightened off the wall. Leah felt a wave of something come off him, heavy and slow, like a weighted blanket dropped over the whole room. Calm. Fake calm. She hated it when he did that. She fought it on principle.

"You were going to take his memory," Edward said to Layla, still in that quiet voice. "That's what that word is. You were going to reach into his mind and take the last ten minutes out."

"Yes," Layla said. Her wand hadn't lowered. Her face hadn't changed. "That's the law."

"He's her *father*."

"I know who he is." Layla's eyes flicked past Edward's shoulder to Charlie, and then to Bella, crouched and snarling with the baby on her shoulder, and then to Seth, who'd come up off the floor and was standing frozen halfway between the two of them, his face stricken. "I know," she said again, softer.

"Somebody," Charlie said from the doorway, in a voice that was trying very hard to sound like a police chief's, "tell me what the hell she was going to do to my head."

Nobody answered him.

Leah watched Layla's wand. It was the only thing in the room worth watching. The tip of it was trembling a little — not much, less than a leaf on a still day — and Leah could see the decision going back and forth behind the witch's eyes. The law. The world she came from, the whole hidden thing, three hundred years of *don't tell*. And on the other side: a room full of people Seth loved, a vampire with her teeth out, a man standing in a doorway holding ice cream for a baby who couldn't eat it.

Layla lowered the wand.

She did it slowly. All the way down, until it pointed at the floor. Then she put it back in her jacket pocket and took her hand out empty and held it up, palm out, so everybody could see.

"Okay," she said to Charlie. Her voice was shaking. "Okay. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, Chief Swan. I wasn't — I'm not going to do anything to you."

Bella's growl stopped like a switch had been flipped. She straightened up, and the baby on her shoulder reached out and patted her cheek with one small hand, and Leah saw Bella's face crumple for half a second into something almost human before it smoothed back into marble.

Charlie looked at Layla for a long moment. Then at the ceiling, at the little lights. Then at Emmett, who gave him a cheerful thumbs-up. Then at Bella.

"Is this," Charlie said, "a need-to-know thing?"

Bella let out a breath she didn't need. "Yeah, Dad. It's kind of a need-to-know thing."

"Do I need to know?"

"Probably not?"

Charlie nodded slowly. He set the grocery bag down on the side table by the door, very carefully, like it might explode. He took off his hat. He crossed the room in a straight line to the armchair Jacob had just vacated, and sat down in it heavily, and put his hat on his knee, and looked at the floating lights on the ceiling.

"Okay," he said. "I'm gonna sit here for a minute."

"Take your time, Charlie," said Carlisle gently.

"Nobody touches my head."

"Nobody's touching your head," Layla said. "I promise."

Charlie looked at her. His mustache moved. "You Seth's girl?"

"Yes, sir."

"Huh," said Charlie, and that was all he said for a long time.

Leah watched Seth go to Layla and put his arms around her from behind and rest his chin on top of her head, and she watched Layla lean back into him like a tree leaning into the wind, and she watched her brother's face. That face. That stupid, open, lit-up face.

She turned and slid the glass door open and went out onto the back deck, and nobody stopped her. She stood at the rail with the river running loud and brown below and the rain coming down on the trees, and she breathed in the cold until her lungs hurt.

The world had gotten bigger. In an afternoon. There were schools on mountains and governments in New York and laws written down in sixteen-eighty-something, and somewhere out there were people who could hold the rain off with a stick and hang lights in the air with a word, and who'd been hiding the whole time, right alongside the vampires and the wolves, in the same rain, in the same woods.

There was a whole world out there where nobody had ever heard of Sam Uley.

Leah Clearwater stood on the Cullens' deck and thought about that for a long time, and wasn't sure, when she finally went back inside, whether it made her feel better or worse.

### PART TWO: THE FEDERAL MAN

**The Sol Duc foothills, twelve miles east of Forks — two weeks later**

The map woke him at twenty to six, same as every morning, by not doing anything.

Harry lay on his back in the dark and listened to it not do anything. Down the short corridor from his bedroom, in the room he called the war room because calling it anything else would have meant admitting it was a study, there was a table eight feet long with a relief map of the Olympic Peninsula raised out of its surface in pale grey wood. Mountains, rivers, the long bite of Hood Canal, the scalloped Pacific coast from Cape Flattery down to the Queets. Running across the map in thin silver lines were the wards. Forty-one anchor stones. An outer line across the southern neck of the peninsula from Grays Harbor to the canal. A second line up the coast, done the hard way, a mile at a time, over the four worst nights of rain he'd seen since Scotland. And two tight inner rings around Forks itself, because whatever came onto this peninsula would come here eventually. That was what thirteen days of watching the Cullens had taught him about vampires: they went where other vampires were, the same way blokes in a strange city went to the pub with the most cars outside.

When something crossed one of those lines, the map would chime, a single clear note like a struck glass, and the place where it had crossed would light up red.

It hadn't chimed in thirteen days.

Harry got up.

The bedroom was small and plain because he'd built it that way, a bed and a trunk and a hook for his coat and nothing on the walls. The rest of the tent was a different story. From outside, if you'd somehow got past the Muggle-repelling charms and the notice-me-not and the three layers of ward behind those, you'd have found a faded green canvas two-man tent in a hollow under the Sitka spruces, with a fire ring in front of it and a camp chair with a split seat. Inside it went down two flights and back further than the hill it was dug into, and he'd stopped counting the rooms somewhere around the third year.

Kitchen first. It always was. The kitchen was the one room in the tent that looked like a kitchen should, a long scrubbed table and a cast-iron range that ran hot all day, copper pans on hooks, a window over the sink that looked out on a view of a Cornish cove he'd charmed in at two in the morning one night when he was twenty-two and drunker than he should have been. The cove was showing a grey, choppy early afternoon. He'd never got around to fixing the time zone on it.

He put the kettle on. Then he put the coffee on as well, because he'd been in America for thirteen days and something about the place made you want both. Three eggs, four rashers of bacon, two slices of bread fried in the fat. He ate standing at the counter, looking at the cove, and thought about nothing much.

Then he went to look at the map, because looking at it was a habit, and he'd learned a long time ago that the habits that kept you alive were the boring ones.

The silver lines lay quiet across the grey wood. Down on the western edge of the inner ring, near the river, a little cluster of nine faint gold pinpricks glowed steady, the same as every morning. He'd written them into the ward as exceptions in his first week, after three nights lying on his belly in the wet salal across the river from that big glass house with a pair of Omnioculars and a notebook. Nine signatures. Seven adults who'd been adults for a very long time, one who'd been one for about two months, and one small something that had a heartbeat and probably wouldn't have tripped a ward keyed to vampires anyway. He'd added her because he didn't know what she was, and when he didn't know what something was he put it on the list.

The exceptions were belt and braces. The ward itself was keyed to cold flesh carrying human blood. That had taken him eleven days of work in Department basement Lab Four last spring, and it was the only reason the Cullens could walk out of their own back door without ending up hanging upside down from a spruce tree. They ate deer. They ate elk. Once, from what he'd seen through the Omnioculars, a black bear that had very badly wanted to be left alone. The ward didn't care about deer.

The ward cared about the other kind.

Harry looked at the map a while longer. Then he went to the corner of the room where a slate the size of a paperback book sat on a small iron stand, and he picked it up. Its twin was in a locked drawer in an office in London, nine floors under Whitehall, and the man who held the key to that drawer was the only person on earth who knew which of the Department's people was standing on the Olympic Peninsula this morning. Harry turned the slate over. Chalk letters had come up on it overnight in a narrow, upright hand.

*Status. Working group convenes 3 Jan. Tuscany needs numbers. — C.*

Harry took the stub of chalk from the stand and wrote underneath it, in his own blocky capitals: *FISHING.*

He thought about it, then added: *YOU'LL GET NUMBERS.*

He put the slate back on its stand.

Tuscany. That was what they'd started calling it in the Department, on the rare occasions anybody called it anything, because the man who ran the Department had a sense of humour that ran about as deep as a bootprint and because *Volterra* was a word nobody wanted to see written down. Harry had read the file sixty or seventy times. It was a slim file to start with and a very thick one now. It had started, of all things, with a holiday.

Last March, on the feast of Saint Marcus, an Italian wizard from Bologna named Ercole Fanti had taken his wife and two daughters to Volterra to see the festival. He'd been standing in the Piazza dei Priori at noon, eating a pastry, when a young man in a white shirt walked out of the shadow of the clock tower toward the sunlight, and a girl came running across the square shouting, and the young man's bare chest had caught the light for about half a second before she knocked him backwards into the dark. Fanti told his Ministry that the boy's skin had lit up like a disco ball. His Ministry wrote it down and forgot it, the way ministries did. A clerk in the Italian liaison office, who owed Harry's Department a favour, did not forget it. Six weeks later the file was on a desk nine floors under Whitehall, and nine weeks after that there was a team in Tuscany.

What the team found was a castle with an old family in it that had been running guided tours for longer than there had been tourists. A pretty woman who led groups of thirty or forty visitors at a time into a stone hall under the clock tower, and an exit count of zero. The team had sat on the city for four months. They had counted. The numbers had gone to the Minister in a sealed envelope, and the Minister had read them and been sick in his own wastepaper basket. Harry had liked him better for it.

So: Tuscany. A working group. A strike, eventually, against something that had been eating people in the middle of Europe for three thousand years without a single wizard noticing. And nobody in the Department knew how to kill one, beyond fire, and nobody knew whether the curses worked, and nobody knew what their venom did, or whether the gifts the team had picked up rumours of — the one who read minds by touch, the little blonde one who made men scream with a look — would go through an Occlumency shield or round it. Nobody knew anything. The Department hated not knowing things more than it hated almost anything else on earth.

Which was why Harry was standing in a hole in a hill in Washington State, on soil where he had no legal right to be, waiting for a vampire to walk into his trap.

He went down the stairs.

The library came first, three walls of books floor to ceiling and the fourth wall taken up by a single long shelf of notebooks. Plain black covers, bound in cheap Muggle card, numbered on the spine in white paint. One through sixty-three. Number one was on the far left, its spine bent and soft from handling, and if he took it down and opened it he'd find his own handwriting from September of 1998, cramped and slanted, an eighteen-year-old's handwriting: *Basic ward theory, from Hermione's list. Book 1 of 14. Don't skip the arithmancy this time.*

He didn't take it down. He knew what was in it. He knew what was in all sixty-three, roughly, the way you knew the inside of a house you'd lived in for a long time. Notebook nine was where he'd finally got wandless summoning to hold for longer than a second. Notebook fourteen was the year he'd spent in the Department's Time Room learning things about causality that he still didn't like to think about too hard. Twenty-two through twenty-six were the Death Chamber, and he didn't open those very often. Thirty-one was where he'd first written down the ward that was now strung across the peninsula, in a version that had snared a stray dog, an Auror trainee, and himself, before it had ever snared what he'd built it for. Forty was the gun. Fifty-two was the year he'd realized he wasn't using his wand anymore, and had stopped carrying it, and had put it in a drawer.

Eight years. Sixty-three notebooks. A few thousand nights of not sleeping, a few hundred of bleeding, and something on the order of twenty spells in the Department's restricted register that hadn't existed before he wrote them down. He didn't think about the number often. When he did think about it he mostly thought about how much was still left.

Past the library was the workshop, and past that, the armory.

He stopped in the armory doorway, because he always did. It was a narrow room with a stone floor, lit by a row of plain glass globes. Down one wall were the long guns in a rack: a pump-action shotgun with the stock cut down and a pattern of runes burned into the wood, which he kept loaded with rock salt, for the things rock salt was good for; a lever-action rifle with a brass receiver he'd bought in a pawn shop in Laramie and spent four months reworking; something larger and uglier in a case he hadn't opened since Bucharest. Down the other wall were blades. Goblin silver, mostly. A short sword that had belonged to a man who didn't need it anymore. And on a felt-lined shelf at shoulder height, by itself, an empty holster-shaped space where the 1911 lived when he wasn't wearing it.

He took the gun down off its peg by the door, where he'd hung it last night, and checked it out of habit, though he knew exactly what condition it was in. Old Colt, an Army model from before the Second World War. Blued steel, worn silver at the edges. Grips of yellowed ivory that he'd carved himself, the long way, over a winter, with a pattern of runes so fine they looked like grain in the bone. It didn't have a magazine in it. It didn't need one. That had been the first half of notebook forty. The second half had been working out how to tell it, with a thought, what he wanted the next round to do when it arrived, and that had taken a lot longer.

He slid it into the shoulder holster and went on to the last room.

The specimen room was cold. He kept it that way, a few degrees above freezing, with a charm on the door that sealed it like a jar. Shelves lined three walls, steel and glass, and on them were labelled vessels in neat rows: acromantula venom in a dozen grades of purity; a basilisk fang in a velvet-lined box, its tip black; a hank of silvery Veela hair under a bell jar; a frozen cross-section of Inferius tissue the size of a dinner plate, sealed in ice that would never thaw; a jar of Erumpent fluid with a paper label on it in his own handwriting that read *DO NOT SHAKE*, underlined three times, because a junior Unspeakable named Pruett had once shaken it.

In the centre of the room, in a circle cut into the stone floor and filled with lines of hammered silver, stood the box.

He'd built it for this job. Eight feet on a side and ten high, walls of conjured glass three inches thick, layered in the conjuring with seven different wards he'd tested against every kind of strength he could get his hands on, from a mountain troll to a Hungarian Horntail on a bad day. There were rings set into the floor inside it. There was a drain. There was a steel table, bolted down, with restraints of goblin-wrought chain. There was nothing inside it at all.

Harry stood and looked at the empty box for a while.

Then he went back upstairs and got dressed.

---

He wore the suit into Forks every day. That was the job.

Black, single-breasted, a cut that hung right over a shoulder holster. White shirt. A narrow black tie. A dark wool overcoat over the top, because it rained in Forks the way it rained in Manchester, steadily and without apparent interest in whether you minded. Black boots that looked like shoes from the ankle up, because no shoe on earth was going to survive the Olympic National Forest. A pair of sunglasses with plain dark lenses in the breast pocket. He'd thought about an earpiece and decided it would be overdoing it.

He'd got it wrong in Bucharest, his first year in the field. He'd gone in quiet, in old jeans and a Muggle anorak, keeping his head down, and by the end of the first week the whole city knew there was a quiet foreigner in old jeans keeping his head down, because a man trying not to be noticed is the most noticeable thing in a small town. Every bored housewife and off-duty copper had taken one look and started a story about him.

So in Forks he'd done the opposite. He'd parked a black Chevrolet sedan he'd paid cash for in Port Angeles on Forks Avenue at noon on his first day, and walked into the diner in a black suit and sat down in a booth by the window and ordered lunch. He'd done it again the next day, and the next. He was polite to the waitress. He tipped twenty percent. He read the *Peninsula Daily News* and did not discuss what he was doing in town, and by the fourth day the whole of Forks had decided he was from the government — Fish and Wildlife, or the Forest Service, or the EPA, there'd been some business last year with a mill upriver and the water — and once they'd decided, they stopped looking. In a town of three thousand people, the man nobody can place is the man everybody watches. The man everybody has decided about is furniture.

Harry liked being furniture. Furniture got to sit in the window and watch.

He parked the Chevrolet on Forks Avenue at five past twelve. He went into the diner. Cheryl — fifty-something, reading glasses on a beaded chain, a smoker's laugh she'd kept long after she'd given up smoking — saw him come in and pulled a mug off the rack before he'd got his coat off.

"Agent," she said, which was what she'd started calling him on day six, as a joke, without his ever having said the word.

"Cheryl."

"Pie's cherry today. Blackberry's gone. Some kid from the rez came in yesterday and ate half of it."

"Cherry's fine."

"Cherry's always fine," she agreed, and poured his coffee, and went.

He sat in his booth. Through the rain-streaked window, Forks went by in its slow grey way: a logging truck downshifting at the light, two kids in Spartans hoodies, an old man walking an older dog. On the corkboard by the door, under a flyer for a church rummage sale and another for a lost cat, someone had pinned a sheet of white paper with a photo on it. Harry had read it the first day. He read it again now, out of habit. *MISSING. DANIEL OKAFOR, 34. LAST SEEN OCT 28, HOH RIVER TRAIL. 6'1", 190 LBS. WEARING RED NORTH FACE JACKET, GREY PACK.* In the photo, Daniel Okafor was grinning on a mountaintop somewhere with his arm around a woman whose face had been cropped out, his red jacket zipped to the chin.

Three weeks, now. Harry had read the ranger reports at the Hoh station. They'd stopped looking on the twelfth day.

The pie came. It was good pie. It was very good pie, in fact; he'd developed a fairly serious relationship with the pie in this diner over the last thirteen days, and he wasn't proud of it. He ate the first forkful looking out of the window at the rain.

On the third finger of his left hand there was a plain silver ring that was tied to the map in the war room, and it was cool and quiet against his skin, the way it had been for thirteen days.

He ate the second forkful.

**The Calawah, south fork — that afternoon**

The smell hit Leah a mile out, and she was running before she'd finished thinking about it.

*Seth.*

*I've got it. I've got it. Leah — that's fresh. That's like, ten minutes.*

It came down the wind from the southeast, across the south fork of the Calawah, through a stand of second-growth hemlock where the ground was all moss and fallen trunks and the light came down green. Sweet. That was always the first thing. Sickly, cloying, the kind of sweet that got into the back of your throat and made your stomach turn over — rotting flowers and bleach and burnt sugar, all at once. Under the sweet, worse, there was blood. Old blood, on the vampire's breath and in its clothes and under its fingernails. Human. Leah would have known it anywhere.

*It's not one of the Cullens*, Seth thought, which was stupid, because obviously it wasn't. The Cullens smelled sweet, too, but clean. Like candy in a sealed jar. This was candy that had been left in a dead man's pocket.

*No kidding*, Leah thought, and stretched out.

The forest came at her in pieces. Moss, a log, a creek a yard wide, a slope of loose shale, a log again. Her paws hit and lifted and hit. She was small, for a wolf — smaller than Seth, smaller than all the boys — and lighter, and faster than any of them. She'd been the fastest in Sam's pack, and she was the fastest in Jacob's, and on bad days, which was most days, running was the only thing that made her head go quiet. The wind roared in her ears. The sweet stink pulled at her nose like a hook.

Seth was a hundred yards to her right and a little behind, crashing through a stand of vine maple like a bulldozer. She could feel him in the back of her head the way she always could, a second set of eyes, a second heartbeat. Jacob's mind was dark — he was human today, down at the Cullens' with the baby, like every day. It was just the two of them. It had been just the two of them on most patrols for three months.

*There*, Seth thought.

She saw it a half-second after he did, through a gap in the hemlocks, a flicker of red against the green. Moving fast — vampire fast, a blur that might have been a man if you saw it out of the corner of your eye in bad light. It was going east. Then it caught their scent, or heard them, and it stopped, and for one half-second Leah got a look at it, a clear look, framed between two trunks thirty yards away.

Male. Lean. Long brown hair in a ponytail. Jeans. Bare feet, black with dirt. A red jacket, a nylon outdoor jacket, two sizes too big, the sleeves pushed up over its white forearms. Red eyes, bright as brake lights in the green gloom. It looked at her. Its lips pulled back.

Then it ran.

*It's running*, Seth thought, surprised.

They did, sometimes. The smart ones. A Cold One alone in the woods with two wolves coming at it could count. They could fight one wolf and maybe win. Two, they were a coin toss. Most of them had heard stories, from other vampires, about the wolves on this coast, and the stories hadn't been nice ones.

This one turned and went west and north, uphill, flat out.

*Flank it*, Leah thought. *Push it to the river. It won't want to swim with us on it.*

*On it.*

Seth peeled off to the right. Leah went straight up the middle. They'd done this before, a dozen times, in the year since the redhead and her army — they'd done it in their sleep, in training runs, in Sam's head and then in Jacob's. Herd it. Pin it. Close.

The vampire was fast. It was very fast, faster than most of them, and it knew how to run in the woods; it went over logs and through brush without slowing, and once it went straight up the side of a big Sitka spruce and along a limb and dropped thirty feet on the other side, and Leah had to go around. She lost twenty yards. She got them back on the downslope. Seth was coming in from the right in a long curve, and she could feel through his head what he could see, the red jacket flashing ahead of him, the vampire's head turning back to look at them.

It saw where they were pushing it. It cut left, hard, away from the river, toward a long gentle rise thick with sword fern and old nurse logs grown over with moss.

*Got it*, Leah thought. *It's boxed. There's a cliff on the other side of that rise. It'll have to turn—*

The vampire hit the top of the rise and stopped in midair.

That was what it looked like. One second it was going full stride, bare feet a blur, over the top of the rise. The next, something caught it by the right ankle and *yanked*.

Leah saw it happen in the fraction of a second it took. A line of light flashed across the forest floor, ankle-high, running in both directions farther than she could see. Silver-blue, thin as a fishing line, there and gone. The bit of it under the vampire's foot didn't go. It whipped up around the vampire's ankle like a lasso and snapped tight, and then it went *up*, straight up, as if someone ten feet above them had hauled on a rope. The vampire's legs went out from under it. Its body swung up and over in a long arc, the red jacket flapping down over its head, and it came to a stop hanging upside down from nothing, ten feet off the ground, bobbing very slightly, like a deer carcass hung up to bleed.

Then it went still.

Completely still. Leah had seen vampires stop moving before. They could stand like statues for hours. This was something else. The vampire's arms hung straight down toward the ferns, fingers spread and rigid. Its mouth was open. Its whole body had locked up with the look of someone frozen halfway through a scream, and the only thing on it that moved at all was its eyes. Red eyes, rolling and darting, upside down.

Leah hit the brakes so hard she tore up a yard of moss.

Seth came out of the ferns to her right and nearly ran into her.

*What*, he thought. *What. Leah. What is that.*

*I don't know.*

They stood there, side by side at the bottom of the rise, panting, hackles up. The vampire hung above them and turned very slowly in the rain, a quarter turn one way and back. Leah could see the line around its ankle now that she knew to look. Faint, silver-blue, a kind of shimmer in the wet air, wrapped twice around the bare white ankle and running up into the empty space above it, where it ended at nothing. Just stopped, ten feet up, attached to the air.

She smelled it. It didn't smell like anything natural. Ozone, like the air after a lightning strike. Something underneath that she almost knew. Cloves? Not cloves. Something clean and sharp and—

*That's like Layla*, Seth thought. *Leah. That smells like when Layla does stuff.*

Leah's fur went flat against her spine and then stood all the way up again.

She started to circle. Slowly. Keeping her eyes on the vampire, because whatever had it might let go, and if it let go she wanted to be the first thing it saw on the way down. Seth went the other way around without being told. The vampire's red eyes tracked them. Its body didn't so much as twitch.

*Is it dead?*

*They don't die from hanging, Seth.*

*I know. I mean — is it, like, a trap? Like Layla said there's spells for—*

The crack came from behind her.

It was a sharp, flat sound, like a whip, or a branch snapping in a hard freeze. Leah had heard it before. She'd heard it three or four times now, in the two weeks since the Cullens' living room, every time Layla turned on the spot in the clearing behind her house and vanished and came back. Leah's body was spinning before her head had caught up, ears flat, teeth bare, a snarl tearing up out of her chest.

There was a man standing in the ferns.

He was tall — taller than Charlie, shorter than Seth, maybe six-two — and broad through the shoulders in a way that the overcoat didn't hide. Black overcoat, open, over a black suit. White shirt. A narrow black tie. Dark glasses, in the rain, under a cloud ceiling so low it was practically sitting on the treetops. Black hair, wet, sticking up in back like he'd never in his life won an argument with a comb. Clean-shaven. Late twenties, maybe. He was holding a crumpled paper napkin in his left hand, and as Leah watched, he balled it up and stuck it in his coat pocket.

He smelled like coffee. He smelled like *cherry pie*. He smelled like gun oil, and wet wool, and under that, like pine needles and cold stone and rain on a creek bed, and under *that*, very faint, something she couldn't place at all, something that made her hackles go up and down at the same time and her brain throw out the word *dog* and then throw it away again. No sweetness. None. Not a vampire. And there was a heartbeat in him. Slow and steady as a clock.

The rain hit his shoulders, and steam came off them.

Just a little. A thin haze, curling up off the black wool where the cold rain landed, the way it came off Seth's bare back when he phased out on the beach in winter. Leah stared at it.

The man looked at her. Then at Seth. Then up at the vampire, hanging there with its red jacket over its face, and back down at the two of them.

He reached into the inside pocket of the overcoat with two fingers, slowly, the way you'd reach around a nervous dog, and came out with a black leather wallet, and let it fall open. There was a gold badge on one side. An eagle. An ID card on the other. Leah only got a quick look at it before he flipped it shut again, but the words across the top were big enough: DEPARTMENT OF HOMELAND SECURITY.

"Afternoon," the man said.

He had an English accent. A real one, rough at the edges — nothing like the posh guys in movies.

"This situation is contained," he said. "I'd ask that you please vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

Leah stared at him.

*Is he*, Seth thought, slowly, *talking to us?*

*He's talking to us.*

*We're wolves.*

*I'm aware we're wolves, Seth.*

The man put the badge away. He walked past them — straight past Leah, close enough she could have taken his leg off at the knee, with his hands in his coat pockets and his eyes on the vampire — and stopped underneath it, and looked up. The vampire's red eyes rolled down to look back at him.

"Nice jacket," the man said to it. "Bit big on you."

He took his right hand out of his pocket and made a little gesture, two fingers, like a man snapping off a thread. The silver line around the vampire's ankle let go.

It dropped straight down, rigid as a board. The man stepped half a pace to one side and caught it — one arm under its middle, just like that, three hundred pounds of stone-hard dead weight — and swung it round and tucked it under his left arm, horizontal, the way surfers at First Beach carried their boards up from the water. Its bare feet stuck out behind him. Its head stuck out in front, ponytail dangling. Its arms still hung down stiff, fingers spread.

He shifted it to get a better grip and turned around to face them.

Leah snarled.

*Okay, no*, she thought. *No. Nobody walks off with a leech on our land. I don't care who he is.*

"Your land's about six miles that way, Lassie," said the man, and pointed west with his free hand. "This bit's the national forest. I checked."

Leah's snarl died in her throat.

On her left, Seth made a sound that she'd never heard a wolf make before, a high, cracked whine, like a dog that had just been stepped on.

*Did he*, Seth thought. *Leah. Did he just—*

"Yes, Balto," the man said. "I did."

Leah's whole body had gone cold, which was a thing she hadn't felt in a year.

She looked at him. He looked back at her through the dark glasses, with the vampire tucked under his arm and rain running off the end of its ponytail, and he didn't look worried. He didn't look much of anything. He looked like a man waiting for a bus.

*Get out of my head*, she thought at him, as hard as she could.

"Then stop shouting," he said, and hitched the vampire up a little higher.

*He can hear us*, Seth thought. *He can actually hear us. Like Edward. Leah, he's like Edward—*

"Not a lot like Edward," the man said. "Bit more effort involved, my end. Not much."

Leah's lips peeled back off her teeth on their own.

*You're in our heads. You're in our heads and you're just — standing there.*

"Well, I'm not going to sit down. The ground's wet."

*Who are you?*

"Federal."

*Federal* what*?*

"Just federal."

*He's FBI*, Seth thought, a little wildly. *It said FBI on the badge. I saw it, Leah, it said Federal Bureau of—*

*It said Homeland Security, Seth.*

*No, it said FBI—*

*It said DEPARTMENT OF HOMELAND—*

Leah stopped thinking. She turned her head, very slowly, and looked at the man, and he looked back at her, and one corner of his mouth went up. Just a little. A small, private, crooked thing that didn't go anywhere near his eyes, because his eyes were behind the glasses.

"Says what it needs to," he said.

The back of Leah's neck crawled.

She made herself breathe. In, out, through the nose. The stink of the vampire under his arm. Coffee and cherries. Pine and stone and rain on a creek bed and the thing underneath that her brain kept trying to call *dog*. She got her paws under her and set her weight. She was faster than him. She'd been faster than everything she'd ever chased. If she went for his legs while Seth went high—

"Don't," the man said, mildly. "Honestly. Don't. I've had a nice lunch."

*What do you want with it?*

He glanced down at the vampire under his arm as if he'd forgotten it was there. Its red eyes rolled up to look at him and rolled back.

"Above your pay grade, Lassie."

*Call me Lassie one more time.*

"What's that, Lassie?" he said. "Timmy's fallen down the well?"

Seth made a noise. It was a short, strangled huff, and it took Leah a second to recognize it as her brother trying very hard not to laugh, and she hated him a little, and she hated the man in the suit a lot.

"Right," the man said. "Lovely chatting. Go on, off you pop. Run home and tell Jacob there's one less of these on the peninsula and he's welcome to send me a fruit basket."

*He knows Jake's name*, Seth thought. *How does he know Jake's name, he's — oh my God, that crack, Leah. That crack when he showed up. That's the sound Layla makes. That's apparating. He apparated. He's not a fed, or — he's a wizard. He's like Layla. He's a—*

The man stopped.

He'd been turning to go, the vampire under his arm swinging round with him like a plank. He stopped halfway through the turn and stood there with his back three-quarters to them, perfectly still, rain dripping off the hem of the overcoat.

The forest was very quiet. Leah could hear the rain on the sword ferns, a million tiny taps. She could hear the creek at the bottom of the slope, the hiss of water on wool, the man's heart. Slow and steady. It didn't change. Nothing about him changed. He just stood there for one second, two, three, and Leah had the strangest feeling that somewhere behind those dark glasses, very quickly, a great many doors were being opened and shut.

Then he turned his head and looked at them.

Before, he'd looked at them the way you looked at a pair of dogs in somebody's yard. Now he looked at them one at a time, and he took a long time over each. At Seth first, all the way down and back up. Then at Leah. He didn't move anything except his head, and Leah, who'd stared down vampires twice her weight and once a whole army of newborns in a clearing at dawn, felt the fur all along her spine try to lie down flat.

"Now," he said quietly. "You shouldn't know that."

*Seth*, Leah thought, very fast and very calm. *Shut up. Shut your brain up. Think about baseball. Think about anything.*

*I'm trying! I'm trying, I'm not thinking about anything, I'm not thinking about—*

And of course he was. Of course he was, because he was Seth, and Leah watched it pour out of him across the pack mind like a dam giving way: the Thriftway, the cart, copper hair and gray eyes and six canisters of salt. Blackberry pie. A box muttering in a ring of salt. The cedars behind the Hendricks place with the rain stopped overhead and the little gold lights hanging in the branches. *Nine laws, give or take.* The Cullens' living room. Emmett on the ceiling. The baby's hand on a freckled cheek. Charlie in the doorway with his grocery bag, and a polished walnut wand coming up, *I'm sorry, this won't hurt*, and Edward between them, and Bella's growl, and the wand going slowly down.

All of it. In about two seconds. Leah watched it go and couldn't stop it.

The man in the suit stood in the rain and watched it go, too.

His face didn't change. When it was finished he let out a breath through his nose, slow, and said, softly, almost kindly: "Oh, Balto. Oh, mate. You *have* been busy."

Leah stepped in front of her brother.

She did it without thinking. One long stride to the left, so that she was between Seth and the man with her head low and her ears flat and every tooth in her mouth showing. The snarl that came out of her was the ugliest sound she'd ever made.

The man looked at her. Then he looked past her, at Seth, and he tilted his head a little, as if he'd heard something.

He had. Leah heard it, too, because it came across the pack mind loud and panicked and desperate: *Layla. She said if there's ever magic trouble, if it's ever something we can't — she made me the stone, she said bite down, she said she'd come—*

The leather cord around Seth's right foreleg. Leah had seen it a hundred times in the last five weeks, wrapped twice round above the paw, the cord he tied his cutoffs to. And tied into it, alongside the cutoffs, a flat gray disc of river stone the size of a quarter, with a mark scratched into one side that looked like a lightning bolt and a key at the same time.

*Seth*, Leah thought. *No—*

"Go on, then," said the man in the suit.

They both looked at him.

He'd turned all the way round to face them now. He stood with his feet apart in the ferns and the vampire still under his arm, and his head was tipped very slightly to one side, and his voice was easy.

"Go on," he said. "Let's meet her."

*Don't*, Leah thought. *Seth, don't. He* wants *you to—*

Seth bit down.

She heard the stone crack between his back teeth, a sharp little *tik* like a walnut shell. Then, from nowhere, a feeling went through the air of the clearing like the moment before a thunderclap — her ears popped, her fur lifted — and a smell like lightning and cloves bloomed out from her brother's foreleg and was gone.

*What did you do?*

*I called her*, Seth thought. He sounded sick. *I called her, Leah, I had to, he—*

*You called her to* him*?*

The man in the suit wasn't looking at them anymore. He'd turned to the big western hemlock behind him, and he leaned the vampire up against it, upright, propped against the trunk the way you'd lean a board against a garage wall, its bare feet planted in the moss and its red eyes rolling. He patted it twice on the shoulder. Then he stepped back into the open, into the middle of the little clearing at the top of the rise, and straightened his tie, and put his hands in his overcoat pockets, and waited.

He didn't have to wait long.

Layla had a pair of jeweller's tweezers in her right hand and a dead man's pocket watch in her left when her chest caught fire.

She was at her kitchen table with her dragonhide gloves on and a loupe screwed into one eye, and she'd been working on the watch for three days. Silver, 1880s, hunter case, from an estate in Aberdeen. Whoever had owned it had wanted very badly for time to stop, and it had, more or less, for everyone who'd worn it since. Two of them had died in their sleep with the watch in their hand, eighty years apart, both at 4:11 in the morning. The curse was wound into the mainspring like a hair caught in a zipper, and she was halfway through teasing it out when the stone at her throat flared so hot she could smell her own skin.

She'd made the stones in October, the week after the cedars. Two flat discs cut from the same river rock out of the Calawah, so that they'd remember being one thing, and paired with an old tracing charm her grandmother had used to find lost cattle in Minnesota winters. One for Seth, tied into the cord on his leg. One for her, on a leather thong around her neck. *If there's ever magic trouble. If it's ever something you can't handle with your teeth. Bite down, and I'll come.* He'd laughed at her. He'd tied it on anyway.

He'd bitten down.

The tweezers hit the table. The watch hit the table. The loupe fell out of her eye and rolled. Her hand was already going for the wand on the table next to her, and her body was already turning, and the stone was pulling at her the way a hook pulls a fish — south and east, a few miles, through the trees, *there* — and she didn't stop to think, didn't stop to take off her gloves, didn't stop for anything. She turned on her heel in the middle of her kitchen and let the stone take her.

Darkness. Pressure. The feeling of being squeezed through a pipe much too small. Then air, cold and wet, and the smell of hemlock and moss and rain, and her boots hitting soft ground.

She came out of it the way she'd been trained to, low and moving, wand up, eyes going everywhere at once. That had been drilled into her for two years on the circuit, by a man named Ostrowski who'd had three fingers on his left hand and no patience on either. *You come out of a jump, you assume the room's already shooting at you. You don't look. You* read*.*

She read.

A slope of moss and sword fern, a little clearing at the top of a rise, heavy timber all round. Rain. Seth — the big sandy shape of him, she'd know him anywhere — fifteen feet to her left, and Leah beside him, small and gray and every tooth showing. Both alive. Both standing. Both with their hackles all the way up.

Against a big hemlock trunk to her right, propped up like a mannequin, stood a vampire.

Not a Cullen. She'd met all of the Cullens now. This one was lean and filthy and had brown hair in a ponytail and wore a red jacket two sizes too big, and its eyes were red. Bright, terrible red. It wasn't moving. Something was holding it there. It looked at her and its eyes were the only things in it that moved.

And in the middle of the clearing, between her and all of them, stood a man in a black suit.

Overcoat. Dark glasses. Hands in his pockets.

He was the only thing in the clearing that wasn't afraid. Seth was afraid; she could see it in his ears. Leah was afraid, the way Leah was always afraid — under a thick coat of fury. The vampire was terrified. And the man in the black suit was standing in the rain between all of them with his hands in his pockets like a commuter on a platform, and he'd turned his head toward her when she landed, before she'd fully landed, as if he'd known to the inch where she'd come out.

Seth had bitten the stone. Seth had called her. Seth had called her because of *this*.

Ostrowski's voice, in her head: *First spell wins most fights.*

She fired.

It was a Stunner, the hardest she had, a red bolt that left her wand with a crack like a rifle shot and would have put a mountain troll on its back. It went across the clearing as straight as a laser.

The man took his right hand out of his pocket and lifted his index finger and flicked it to one side.

The Stunner bent.

It bent like a pool ball coming off a cushion, a sharp angle in midair three feet in front of him, and went off sideways into the trees and hit a hemlock twenty feet away, and the bark of the hemlock blew off in a starburst of red light and splinters, and the whole tree shuddered all the way up to its crown and showered them with water.

Layla's mind went perfectly white for a quarter of a second.

You didn't do that. That wasn't a thing that was done. You shielded a spell, or you dodged it, or you met it with one of your own and ate the backlash — those were the three options, those had always been the three options, since before there were rules, and every duelist on earth knew it. You didn't *touch* a spell. Spells weren't objects. You couldn't reach out with your bare hand and—

He'd done it with one finger. No wand. She couldn't see a wand anywhere on him. No word. No shield, no flare of light, nothing. He'd just reached out and flicked it away like a fly.

Ostrowski, again: *And if the first spell doesn't win? You don't stop. You never stop. You chain.*

She chained.

She'd been good at this. She'd been better than good. She'd been the Thunderbird House champion three years running at Ilvermorny, and she'd gone straight from there onto the circuit, two years of it, Boston to San Francisco and back again in the back of borrowed cars, and she'd finished her second year ranked eleventh in North America before she'd walked away from it for the money in curse-breaking. Eleventh. Out of four thousand registered. She knew how to chain.

*Incarcerous*, and ropes came whipping out of the tip of her wand, a dozen of them, thick as her thumb, snaking for his arms and legs. Before they were halfway, *Diffindo*, slashed low at his knees. Behind that, *Reducto*, at his chest, with everything she had in it.

He didn't move his feet.

The ropes reached him and fell apart. They came apart in the air in front of him in short neat pieces, as though they'd flown into a blender, and pattered down into the ferns. The Severing Charm he turned aside with the back of two fingers, a little brushing-away gesture, like a man shooing a cat off a counter, and it went off to the left and took the top off a sword fern as cleanly as scissors. And the Reductor Curse — her best one, her hardest one, the one that had put a guy from Atlanta through a plate-glass window in the semifinals at Sacramento — he caught.

That was what it looked like. He put his hand up, palm out, and the curse hit his palm and stopped there, a pulsing blue-white ball of force the size of a grapefruit, an inch from his skin. He looked at it for a second with his head on one side.

"That one's nice," he said.

He turned his hand over and the curse came back at her.

She got a shield up. Barely. *Protego*, and the silver dome of it went up a foot in front of her face, and her own Reductor Curse hit it dead center with a sound like a car hitting a wall. It drove her backward. Her boots skidded through the moss a full yard. Her shield went white and then cracked from edge to edge like a sheet of river ice, and she felt the crack go up her wand arm into her shoulder.

That was when Seth and Leah went for him.

She didn't see them decide to. One second they were standing at the edge of the clearing, and the next they were in the air, both of them, from two different directions, a thousand pounds of wolf between them, coming at the man in the suit from behind and to his left, jaws wide.

He didn't look at them. He was looking at her. He put his left hand out to the side, open, palm down, and lowered it slowly, like a man settling a tablecloth.

Both wolves stopped in midair.

They hung there. For one long, impossible second, Seth and Leah hung four feet off the ground with their legs splayed and their jaws open and their eyes wide. Then they went down. Slowly, carefully, all the way down, until both of them were pressed flat to the moss on their bellies with their legs out to the sides like rugs. Seth whined. Leah made a sound Layla hadn't known a wolf could make, a sound of pure animal rage, and fought it, every muscle in her straining, her claws tearing up the moss, and she didn't move an inch.

He hadn't even turned his head.

"Stay," he said.

Layla had been taught to look for seams. That was the curse-breaker in her, older and deeper than the duelist. Every shield had seams. Every ward, every curse, every piece of spellwork in the world — somebody made it, and anything somebody made had a place where the pieces met. You found the seam and you worked a blade into it and you twisted. She did it without thinking, the way she'd done it ten thousand times on hexed boxes and cursed rings and bad old houses: she let her eyes go soft and *looked* at him, at the magic around him, for the shape of the shield he was holding, so that she could find where it joined.

There wasn't one.

There was no shield. There was nothing around him at all. No wards, no protections, no layered defensive charms like the ones every serious duelist wore under their clothes. He was standing in that clearing in a wool suit with nothing on him but rain.

He'd been meeting every one of her spells *individually*. With his bare hands. While holding two wolves down at the same time. Without looking at them.

Layla knew, then.

She'd known since the first one, really; she'd just been too busy to let it all the way in. Now it came all the way in, and it brought the cold with it, a cold that started in the pit of her stomach and went out into her hands. She knew it because she had been a nerd about dueling since she was nine years old, the kind of kid who read tournament records under the covers with a Lumos, and there was a section in the Ilvermorny archive, in the restricted part of the library on the mountain, that she'd read so many times the librarian had stopped asking her for a permission slip. Accounts of the great duels. Eyewitness statements. Reconstructions.

There had been, in the last two hundred years of written record, three people who fought like this. Wandless when they wanted to be, wordless as a matter of course, catching other people's spells out of the air and handing them back. Three names. Every serious duelist knew them. Albus Dumbledore, who'd beaten Grindelwald in 1945 in a duel that witnesses said had lasted three hours and left a crater. Gellert Grindelwald himself. And the British one, the one they'd called Voldemort, whose fight with Dumbledore in the atrium of the British Ministry in the summer of '96 had been reported in the *New York Ghost* for a week. A witness in that article had said the two of them had fought like men having an argument in a language nobody else in the room knew.

Three names. She'd memorized their duels like scripture.

She was looking at a fourth. In a suit. In the rain. In the middle of the Olympic National Forest.

And he wasn't trying. That was the part that made her hands go cold. He hadn't attacked her once. He hadn't even taken his left hand out of the work of holding two wolves to the ground. He was standing there with his feet in the same place they'd been when she landed, and he was *letting her* — letting her throw everything she had, the way a grown man lets a toddler punch him in the stomach, and she understood, in a very simple and quiet way, that if he decided to stop letting her, she would not have time to know it had happened.

She was terrified.

She attacked anyway. She didn't know what else to do. Seth was on the ground. She threw fire — *Confringo*, a blasting curse that would have taken the front off a house — and he put two fingers up and pinched it out in midair like a candle wick, and the smoke curled up off his fingertips. She dropped her wand-tip to the forest floor and threw her whole weight into a transfiguration, the ground under his feet heaving up into roots, a dozen of them, thick as her wrist, grabbing for his ankles and calves. He lifted one foot out of them, then the other, like a man stepping out of a pair of boots, and the roots went gray and dry and crumbled.

"Your left foot leads," he said, conversationally, as she was already moving for the next one. "When you're about to go big. You plant it. Bad habit. Somebody'll have that off you one day."

She went big.

He took his right hand out of the air and put it inside his overcoat.

She saw it come out. It came out so fast that it was just *there*, the way the man himself had just been there when she'd landed — a pistol, a big old-fashioned automatic, blued steel gone silver at the edges and grips the color of old piano keys. Layla had grown up in Minnesota with a father who hunted, and she knew a gun when she saw one, and she knew that a gun didn't matter. Bullets didn't go through a Shield Charm. Everybody knew that. Every witch and wizard alive had grown up being told that No-Maj weapons were—

He pointed it at the ground six feet in front of her and fired once.

The sound was enormous. It was much too big for the gun. It was a sound like a door slamming in the sky, and the bullet hit the dirt between her boots and his and the dirt *exploded*.

The forest floor went up in a geyser six feet high, moss and soil and rock and fern, and the force of it came off the ground in a wall. Her shield was up — she'd got it up, she'd got it up the instant she saw the gun, because some small cold part of her had known — and the blast hit her shield and went straight through it like a fist through wet paper, and her shield came apart around her in silver shards, and the wall of force picked her up off her feet and threw her.

She went backward through the air. She saw the gray sky, very close. She saw a hemlock bough. She hit the ground, and bounced, and hit it again, and rolled, and fetched up hard on her back against the side of an old nurse log thirty feet from where she'd been standing, buried in sword fern, with dirt and moss raining down on her and no air at all in her lungs.

Her ears were ringing. A high, steady, endless whine.

Her hand was empty. Her wand was gone.

She lay there. She couldn't have moved if she'd wanted to. She stared up at the hemlock boughs and the rain coming down through them, and dragged at the air, and waited for her lungs to remember how. Her nose was running. She tasted copper. Somewhere very far away, under the whine in her ears, she could hear Seth howling — a high, horrible sound — and Leah snarling, and the rain.

Then the howling stopped. And through the ringing, very clearly, as though he were standing right beside her, which he was not, she heard the man in the suit say:

"Right. Now that everyone's had a go." A pause. "Might we conduct the rest of this conversation with words instead of spells and teeth?"

It took her a minute to sit up.

He let her have it. That was the thing she'd remember about it later. He didn't hurry her. When she finally got an elbow under herself and then a hand and then pushed up so her back was against the nurse log, with the moss soaking through her flannel shirt and her dragonhide gloves still on her hands and blood running out of her left nostril, he was standing exactly where he'd been. The crater in front of him was about four feet across and a foot deep. It was still steaming faintly in the rain.

He'd put the gun away. Her wand was in his hand.

He was turning it over, slowly, looking at it the way she'd look at a nice piece of antique silverwork, holding it lightly by the middle. Somewhere in the last minute he'd summoned it to himself without her noticing.

"Black walnut," he said. "Thunderbird tail feather. Thirteen and a quarter, give or take." He ran a thumb along the carving on the handle, the feather pattern her grandmother had paid extra for. "Lovely bit of work. American makers do a nice line in temper. Wouldn't have thought it'd handle that much load without kicking."

"Give it back," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ringing ears, flat and far away.

"In a minute."

Seth and Leah were still on the ground. Still pinned, flat to the moss on either side of him. Seth's eyes were fixed on her and he was whining in a long, steady, continuous note, like a kettle, and every muscle in his huge body was straining toward her, and he wasn't moving. Leah had stopped fighting. She lay with her chin in the moss and her lips back off her teeth, perfectly still, and her eyes were on the man's face and nowhere else.

"Who," Layla said, and had to stop, and spit blood into the ferns, and start again. "Who the hell are you?"

He thought about it. Or pretended to. "Page," he said.

"Page."

"Agent Page."

"Like Jimmy Page."

"Never heard of him," said the man, with a perfectly straight face.

She stared at him. He stared back at her through the dark glasses. Neither of those things told her a single thing, and she realized, sitting there in the wet with her blood in her mouth, that she was looking for things to tell her. She couldn't stop. It was the curse-breaker again. She was hunting for seams.

English accent. That was the only thing he'd given her. A real one, she thought, rough at the edges, nothing she could place by region. And that was worth exactly nothing, because she'd once watched a guy from Phoenix do an Irish accent for an entire tournament weekend on a bet, and there was a potion for faces and a charm for voices, and anyone who could do what he'd just done could sound like anybody he wanted. No wand, so nothing she could trace to a maker. No insignia. No ring, except a plain silver band on his left hand with nothing on it. No ward signature she could read, because he wasn't wearing any. The suit was off a rack. The badge he'd shown the wolves — she'd caught a glimpse of it in Seth's head in the instant before the stone took her, a gold eagle — could have said anything. The gun was old American and meant nothing. He'd said three words about her wand and they'd been the words of someone who knew wands, which was everyone.

She had spent years learning to read the people who made cursed things from the things they made. She could look at a hexed music box and tell you which decade it was made in, which kind of person had made it and roughly how angry they'd been. She'd watched this man for two full minutes do the most extraordinary magic she had ever seen in her life, and she could not tell one single thing about him.

That frightened her more than the gun had.

"Layla Kincaid," he said. "Born Saint Paul. Ilvermorny, Thunderbird House. Two years on the circuit. Ranked eleventh, which I'd believe. Curse-breaker. Moved into a rented house out past the Calawah in September and charmed the roof on the first night, before you'd even unpacked, which was the right call. Been making eyes at young Balto here for seven weeks and stepping out with him for two. Lovely." He tipped her wand toward her like a pointer. "And about two weeks ago, on a Sunday afternoon, in a big glass house on the Calawah, you levitated a vampire, hung lights on the ceiling, and told eight vampires, three wolves and something that's about ten weeks old that the wizarding world exists. Explained the Statute. Explained MACUSA. Answered questions. For about an hour and a half."

Layla said nothing at all.

"Then the town's chief of police walked in carrying a rotisserie chicken," the man went on, "and you put your wand on him, which was correct. And then you took it off him again." He paused. "Which wasn't."

"How," she said. She knew how. She asked anyway.

He tilted his head at Seth. "Your boyfriend thinks in Technicolor."

Layla closed her eyes.

Legilimency. Of course. On a shifter. In wolf form. Wandless, wordless, from ten feet away with no eye contact she could see, which was supposed to be impossible, and was apparently, today, only one more impossible thing on the pile. She thought about the Cullens' living room. Edward Cullen's quiet gold eyes. *You're thinking about a teacher. You weren't very good at it.*

"That's a breach," the man said. "You know it's a breach. You knew it when you did it. International Statute of Secrecy, and your own lot have got all sorts of extra rules on top. MACUSA's a long way from Rappaport, I'll grant, but they've never been what you'd call relaxed about No-Majs. You know what they'd do."

She knew what they'd do. She'd known since the moment her wand went down in the Cullens' living room. She'd been lying awake knowing it for two weeks.

"They'd come," he said. "Team of Obliviators and a couple of Aurors to hold their coats. They'd go to the chief's house first, and they'd take the last fortnight out of his head, and probably a bit more for safety, and they're not always as careful as they ought to be about how much more. Then they'd go to La Push. Ever seen an Obliviation on a shifter, Miss Kincaid? I haven't. I'd imagine it goes badly. And then somebody very senior in New York would sit down with the reports and ask themselves what sort of creature lives in a big glass house on the Calawah and drinks blood and has just been told all about the existence of wizards, and what you do about a thing like that." He shrugged. "And the answer to that one usually involves fire."

She opened her eyes. "And who's going to tell them?"

"Well. That's rather the question, isn't it."

"You?" She laughed. It hurt; something in her ribs was badly bruised. "You'd go to MACUSA? You? Whoever you are, whatever you are, you're standing on American soil. With a gun and no wand and a fake badge and a vampire on a—" she gestured at the hemlock, at the red-eyed thing propped against it — "on a *stick*. They'd want to know who sent that letter. They'd want to know a lot."

"They would," he agreed.

"So you can't."

"Love," he said, quite gently, "I'm not here."

The rain came down.

"There's no record of me on this continent," he said. "Nobody's going to find one. There's a lease on the Hendricks place with your name on it, and a file in the Department of Records in New York with your wand in it, and your circuit rankings, and your mum's maiden name. You exist. I don't." He turned her wand over again between his fingers. "And as for American soil — am I? I'd have to check."

Layla looked at him for a long time.

She thought about Charlie Swan in the armchair, with his hat on his knee. *Nobody touches my head.* She'd promised him. She thought about Seth, lying flat in the moss ten feet away with that long, thin, kettle-whine coming out of him, all his weight pulling toward her. About Bella's face for half a second over the baby's head. About Carlisle, crouched in front of her, saying *Thank you for telling me it was all a lie.*

"What do you want," she said.

"Cooperation."

"With what?"

"With whatever I ask. When I ask." He said it simply. "I'm going to forget I know any of this. All of it. Your little Sunday social, the chief, the whole lot. Nobody in New York is ever going to hear a word from me. In exchange, at some point — maybe next week, maybe next year, maybe never — I'm going to need something. Help. A hand. Somebody who knows how to take apart a bad old thing without setting it off. And when I ask, you're going to say yes."

"Without knowing what it is."

"That's generally how yes works."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll find out how good MACUSA's Obliviators are," he said. "I hear they're thorough."

He said it the way he'd said *Black walnut* — like a fact about the world, one more thing he'd noticed. That was the worst part.

Layla looked at Seth.

Seth looked back at her, and in the big sandy face and the brown eyes she saw every single thing he was thinking, as clearly as if she'd had Edward Cullen's gift. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I called you, I shouldn't have called you—*

"Fine," she said.

"Say it properly, please."

She ground her teeth. "When you ask, I'll help you."

"Lovely." He flipped her wand once in his hand, end over end, caught it by the tip, and held it out handle-first toward her across thirty feet of air. It left his fingers and floated across the clearing, slow and steady, and settled into her gloved palm as gently as a leaf. "Cheers."

She closed her hand around it. It felt like hers. She turned it over and ran her eyes down it, the curse-breaker's look, soft-focus, hunting for anything that hadn't been there before. A tracking charm. A trace. A hook. Anything.

There was nothing. Nothing at all.

Either he hadn't done anything to it, or he'd done something she couldn't see. She sat in the ferns with her wand in her hand and blood on her lip and understood that she would never, ever know which.

The man in the suit took his left hand out of the air.

On either side of him, the pressure holding the wolves came off all at once. Seth came up off the moss like a spring uncoiling, all four feet at once, and he didn't even look at the man. He went straight past him, so close his shoulder brushed the overcoat, and across the clearing to her in three bounds, and skidded down into the ferns next to her and shoved his enormous head into her chest and whined, and whined, and whined.

"Hey," Layla said into his fur. Her voice cracked. "Hey. Hey. I'm okay. I'm okay, buddy."

Harry watched the boy go.

Six and a half feet of sandy wolf, going across the clearing in three strides, past him close enough to brush his coat, and down into the ferns beside the witch with his whole head jammed into her chest like a dog that's been left in the car too long. The witch had her gloved hands in his ruff and was talking into his fur. *I'm okay, buddy. I'm okay.* Her nose was still bleeding a bit. She'd be fine. The round had been the softest thing he'd put through that gun in a year, and he'd shaped the blast to go through her shield and spend itself on throwing her rather than on anything else. A bruise along the ribs, and her pride, and a ringing in the ears till about teatime.

She'd hold. People who'd been on a circuit always held, once they'd lost; they'd had a lot of practice. And the boy would do what she told him. That one was simple.

That left the gray one.

She'd come up off the moss the same instant her brother had, but she hadn't gone anywhere. She'd shaken herself once, hard, from the ears back, and then she'd planted her feet in the place she'd been lying, about eight feet off his left side, square between him and the two in the ferns. Head low. Ears flat. Every tooth she had on display and a sound coming out of her like a diesel engine idling.

She hadn't stopped thinking about his throat in eleven minutes. He'd had the edge of her the whole time, the surface of it, without going looking. She had three different plans for his throat, and she'd been refining all three while he held her down, and one of them was quite good. Under the plans was the anger, and under the anger was something else, older and harder, packed down tight as a fist. He'd brushed against it twice without meaning to and pulled back both times.

She was the problem. She was the one who'd go back to the alpha and the river house and talk. She was the one who'd come looking.

He wanted a better read on her before he left.

Surface skimming was one thing. He could do it to the boy from fifty yards; the boy broadcast like a pirate radio station. This one kept things packed away, and if he wanted what was down there he'd need eyes.

He took off the sunglasses.

He folded them one-handed, the way he always did, and slid them into the breast pocket of the suit. The light under the hemlocks was thin and grey and wet. It took him a second to adjust to it. Then he turned his head and looked at the gray wolf, properly, for the first time.

She was small for one of them. Small next to her brother, at any rate, which was like saying a lorry was small next to a bus. Gray, all over, darker along the spine and paler at the throat, her fur slicked down flat with rain. She was lean in a way the boy wasn't. All the bones of her face showed. And her eyes, set in that long gray wolf's face, were human eyes. Dark brown. Furious. Fixed on his.

He went in.

It was the smallest push. A finger on a door to see if it was latched.

The door opened the other way.

Something came up out of the middle of his chest. Behind the breastbone and down, a long way down, down where the wolf lay. He kept it there. He'd kept it there for years, asleep, and he'd got used to the weight of it the way you got used to a pistol under your arm. And something reached down into that place now, without asking, and took hold.

It was a rope. That was the only word that fit. A rope that ran out of the middle of him, across eight feet of rain and air, and went into the gray wolf's chest, and went down into her, deep, and around something that lay there. He felt it close around something with fur and teeth and a heartbeat, the way you feel a rope go round a post. And then it came back. Back across the air, back into him, down into the dark, and around his wolf, once, twice. And pulled tight.

He felt it knot. He felt it the way you'd feel a fist close on your heart.

The wolf in his chest, which had been asleep for a very long time, stood up.

The whole forest pivoted. Harry felt it go. Every line he'd ever had hold of — the Department, the job, the map, the man with the slate in London, eight years of notebooks and the war before that and the cupboard before that — went slack in his hands all at once and swung round and came back hooked to a new point. The point was eight feet away. It was gray and soaking wet and it was looking at him with human eyes gone wide and blank with shock.

He took a step back. His boot slid in the wet moss. He caught himself.

He put his hand flat on his chest, over the place where the rope went in, and pressed, as if he could hold it closed.

"*What the fuck*," said Harry.

Leah felt it land.

She'd been about to go for him. She'd had it worked out — while he was looking at Seth, a low lunge for the back of the left knee, then up and around for the arm while he was going down. He'd put the sunglasses away and turned his head and looked at her, and she'd seen his eyes for the first time, green, bright, a flat clear green like bottle glass with the light behind it, and she'd felt something push at the inside of her skull, a light pressure like a fingertip.

Then the floor of the world fell away.

She knew. She knew before it finished happening. She had lived inside Sam Uley's head for two years. She had felt this through him a thousand times, secondhand, in every patrol, every thought — Sam seeing Emily for the first time, the ground tilting, gravity changing its mind. She knew exactly what it was. She'd have known it in her sleep.

It wasn't supposed to happen to her. It was never supposed to happen to her. That was the one good thing.

It came out of the middle of her chest, where the wolf lived, and it went across the air to him like a thrown line, and she felt it go into him, deep, way down, and close around something. Something big. Something with fur. Something with a slow, enormous heartbeat like a drum in a cave, a heartbeat that wasn't his human one, and a smell — pine, and stone, and cold creek water, and under all of it the smell her brain had been trying to call *dog* for twenty minutes. Her brain finally got it right.

*Wolf.*

There was a wolf in him. A big one. Bigger than Jake.

And the line came back. Back out of him and across the air and into her, down into her own wolf, who'd gone dead still. It wrapped around her and pulled tight and tied itself off, and she felt it knot in the middle of her like a fist.

Her body stayed frozen in the moss with every hair on end. Like that was where it had always been meant to lie.

And then the whole world went over on its side. Everything. Every line she'd ever had — her mom, Seth, the pack, the beach, the rez, her dad's grave on the bluff, Sam, *Sam*, the old raw place where Sam had been — swung loose and came back around and hooked onto the man standing eight feet away with his hand pressed flat against his chest and his face gone white.

*Leah.*

*You imprinted. You imprinted on* him*. On the — on the* spook*—*

She ran.

She didn't decide to. Her legs did it on their own. When she stopped, she could feel exactly where he was, back up the slope behind her, the way you can feel the sun on the back of your neck with your eyes shut.

*Leah*, Seth thought, very small.

She let go.

The heat ran backward down her spine, and she came apart and went back together smaller, and she was on her hands and knees in the wet cedar duff, naked, in November, shaking all over. She hadn't felt the cold in a year. The shaking came from somewhere else.

Seth went out of her head like a light switching off. It was suddenly very quiet in there. It was just her.

She stood up. Her hair was wet. Rain ran down her bare arms and steamed off her shoulders. She put her hand flat on her own chest, over her heart, the way he'd done, and pressed.

The line was still there. It didn't care.

"No," she said out loud, to the forest. "No. No, no, no."

The forest didn't say anything back.

She stood there for a minute. Maybe two. Then she wiped her face with the back of her wrist, and turned around, and walked back up the slope.

He was standing exactly where she'd left him.

"No," said Seth, and to her surprise, he sounded like he meant it.

Layla had got to her feet, with Seth's arm under hers. She came a few steps closer, limping a little, her wand down at her side but not away. She was looking at the man in the suit the way she'd looked at the music box in her living room. Leah had seen that look on her a dozen times now. Careful and interested and cold all at once, like a surgeon with a scalpel.

She was looking for seams, Leah thought. And she'd just found one.

The man saw her looking. Something in his face closed up, smooth and shut, like a door. He turned away from all three of them and walked over to the big hemlock where the vampire still stood propped against the trunk, red eyes rolling. He bent and got an arm round it and lifted it, and tucked it back under his arm, horizontal, like he'd done before. He hitched it up to get the grip right. When he turned back round to face them, his face was the same face that had cracked into the clearing an hour ago. Bored. Polite. Nobody home.

"Miss Kincaid," he said. "We've an arrangement."

"We do," said Layla.

"Balto." Seth stiffened. "Your friends on the river. Tell them they're not on my list."

"What list?"

"That's the spirit."

Leah looked at the vampire under his arm. At the red jacket. Two sizes too big, bunched up at the elbows and gathered round its skinny white wrists, red nylon, with a black logo on the chest, smeared with mud. It snagged on something in her memory, and she had to chase it for a second before she caught it. A sheet of white paper taped up on the door of the gas station on the 101, where she'd stopped for coffee on her way to patrol every morning for three weeks. A grinning guy on a mountaintop with his arm around someone who'd been cut out of the picture.

"That's the hiker," she said. "That jacket. That's the guy on the flyers."

"Daniel Okafor," said the man. "Thirty-four. Hoh River Trail, twenty-eighth of October."

Leah looked at the vampire. The vampire's red eyes looked at her, and then slid away.

"What are you going to do with it?"

"Ask it some questions."

"It's a leech," Leah said. "It doesn't answer questions."

"Everyone answers questions," said the man in the suit. He said it the way he'd said *Classified*. Simply. "So you'll forgive me, Leah, if I don't lose much sleep over what I'm about to do with it."

She found she didn't. Lose any. Not one second. She looked at the thing in the red jacket and thought about a grinning guy on a mountaintop and she didn't feel a single thing for it except the old, deep, bone-level hate that had come up in her the first day she'd phased and never gone away.

He looked at her one more time.

She felt it coming before it got there, a tug on the line, and she braced for it.

"Don't," she said.

"Wasn't going to," he said.

He turned on his heel.

The crack was the same one as before, flat and sharp, like a branch snapping in a hard frost. The air in the spot where he'd been standing folded in on itself and out again. Then there was nobody there. Just the crater, steaming. Just the rain.

The line in Leah's chest went taut — stretched east, fast, too fast, faster than anything she'd ever chased — and then *down*, strangely, as if into the ground. Then it stopped. It settled. It lay there in her, quiet, running out of her chest and away through the trees, twelve or fifteen miles of it at least, and she knew without having to think about it, the way she knew where her own feet were, exactly where at the other end of it he'd stopped.

She put her hand flat on her chest, over the place where it went in.

"Leah," Seth said quietly. "We have to tell Jake."

"I know."

"He's gonna be in your head the second you phase."

"I *know*, Seth."

Layla was still standing beside him with her wand at her side. She was staring at the place in the air where the man in the suit had been.

"Did either of you," she said slowly, "get a single thing off him? Anything? Where he's from, who he works for, anything at all?"

Seth shook his head.

"He has a wolf," Leah said.

Layla turned and looked at her.

"That's it," said Leah. "That's all I got." She folded her arms tighter. The rain was running off her chin. "He has a wolf, and he likes cherry pie."

**The Cullen house — that evening**

Layla took them back the fast way, which Leah would have voted against if anybody had asked her.

Nobody asked her. Layla just held out both gloved hands, one to Leah and one to Seth, and said, "Hold on, and don't let go no matter what it feels like," and Seth grabbed on right away because he'd done it before. Leah stood there in the rain and looked at the hand for a long moment. Then she took it, because the other option was a fifteen-mile walk barefoot with her brother thinking about her love life the whole way.

It felt like being stuffed through a garden hose. It felt like every bone in her body had been folded in half and then folded again. It lasted about one second and it was the worst second of her life, and then her feet hit the Cullens' front lawn and she let go of Layla's hand and walked three steps and threw up in Esme's hydrangeas.

"Yeah," Layla said, behind her, a little guiltily. "That happens the first time."

Edward was already at the front door.

He'd heard them coming, of course — heard Seth, anyway, who was thinking so loud that Leah could practically hear him herself without being phased. Edward stood in the open doorway with the light behind him and his face had gone very still, and his gold eyes went from Seth to Layla, to the blood on Layla's lip, and then to Leah, wiping her mouth on the back of her wrist next to the hydrangeas.

And stayed on Leah.

She saw him see it. She saw it go across his face — a widening of the eyes, a little shocked tilt of the head — and she knew he had all of it, every second, from Seth's head and now from hers, because she couldn't stop it. She was thinking about it right now. She was going to be thinking about it for the rest of her life. Green eyes, and a rope, and *What the fuck.*

*Say one word*, she thought at him, very clearly. *One. Word. And I'll pull your arms off and feed them to your wife.*

Edward closed his mouth.

He turned around and said, at a perfectly normal volume, into the house: "Carlisle. Everyone. You'll want to hear this."

---

It took Seth half an hour to tell it, and he told it badly, because he was Seth. He went back and forth. He kept getting ahead of himself and then going back to fill in things he'd forgotten, the badge, the sunglasses, the cherry pie, and twice Edward had to cut in quietly and say, *He means this*, and describe what he was seeing in Seth's head, clean and precise as a police report. The whole family was there. Jacob was there, sitting on the bottom step of the big staircase with Renesmee on his knee, and as the story went on his face went from confused, to angry, to something a lot worse than angry.

When Seth got to the part where the man in the suit had held the two of them down on the moss without looking at them, Jacob stood up. He put Renesmee down on the step, very gently, and stood up.

"He *pinned* you," Jacob said. "He put his hands on you—"

"He didn't touch us," Seth said. "That's the thing, Jake. He didn't even touch us. He just—" He held his hand out flat and lowered it, the way the man had. "Like that."

"I'll find him." Jacob's whole body was trembling, a fine high vibration like a plucked wire. Leah knew that tremble. She'd felt it in herself a thousand times. "I'll find him and I'll—"

"No," said Layla.

Everybody looked at her.

She was on the white sofa with an ice pack Esme had brought her pressed to her ribs and Carlisle sitting on the coffee table in front of her, shining a little penlight into one of her eyes and then the other. She waved him off. She looked at Jacob.

"Nobody goes after him," she said. "Not you. Not anybody."

"He *hurt* you."

"He let me hurt myself." She said it very flatly. "Jacob, I need you to listen to me. Edward. Look in Seth's head. Look at the fight. Not at me. At *him*. Look at what he did with his hands."

Edward looked at Seth.

It took a few seconds. Leah watched his face. She saw it change, by degrees, the way you see weather come in over the water. First the concentration. Then a little frown. Then nothing at all — Edward's face going perfectly smooth and still, the way the vampires' faces went when they forgot to look human.

"She's right," he said quietly.

"Edward—"

"She's right, Jacob." He turned his head and looked at Jacob, and Jacob stopped trembling. "He wasn't fighting her. He was — I don't know the word. *Allowing* her. He didn't move his feet. Not once. Not when she threw fire at him, not when she broke the ground open under him. He was taking her spells out of the air with his bare hands and handing them back to her, and holding Seth and Leah down with the other hand, and the entire time he was—" He stopped. "He was *bored*, Jacob. He was talking to her about her footwork."

The room was very quiet.

"There've been three," Layla said into the quiet. "In two hundred years of anybody writing anything down. Three people who could fight like that. I've read every word anybody ever wrote about all three of them. All three of them are dead now, and one of the three was the reason Britain had a war." She took the ice pack off her ribs and looked at it and put it back. "I don't know who he is. I've been trying to figure it out for two hours and I've got nothing. Nothing. English accent, which means nothing. No wand. I couldn't read a single thing on him. I've never not been able to read *something*. I don't know where he's from or who sent him. I don't know if he's government or freelance or something nobody's got a word for. I don't know anything." She looked at Jacob. "Except that if you go after him, he'll kill you. He won't even be upset about it. He'll put you down like a — like a—" She didn't finish.

Jacob sat back down on the step. Renesmee climbed into his lap without a word and put her hand on his cheek, and whatever she showed him made his face crumple and smooth out again.

"He's been here two weeks," Alice said.

She was cross-legged on the floor again, in the same spot she'd sat on the Sunday Layla had floated Emmett. She'd been quiet the whole time. Now everybody turned.

"Thirteen days," Alice said. "Maybe fourteen. There's been something wrong east of town. Up in the Sol Duc country, toward the hot springs. It's like a hole in the weather. I keep looking at the future out there and it just — goes blank. Like when I try to look at—" She glanced at Jacob. "Like when I try to see any of you."

"So he's a wolf," Jacob said. "What, he's a *wolf*? He's some British wolf, some — what, some kind of—"

Seth looked at Leah.

Leah didn't look at anybody. She was standing with her back against the big glass wall with her arms folded, where she'd stood two weeks ago to watch Emmett float. Her clothes were still damp. She could feel the line in her chest running out through the glass behind her and away east through the dark, quiet and steady, as if there were a hook in her ribs and somebody twelve miles off was holding the other end of it and not pulling.

"Leah?" said Jacob.

"He has a wolf," Leah said.

"He *has* a — what does that even mean, he *has* a wolf?"

"I don't know what it means." She heard her voice come out flat and hard. "It means he's got one. In there. And it's bigger than you."

Jacob stared at her. Then, slowly, he looked over at Seth. And Seth, who couldn't hide anything from anybody, who'd never been able to hide anything in his life, went bright red to the tips of his ears and stared at the floor.

"What," said Jacob. "*What?*"

Nobody said anything.

Leah looked at Edward. Edward was looking out the window, at the dark river, as though there were something very interesting about it.

Carlisle, sitting on the coffee table, cleared his throat. "He said we weren't on his list," he said, carefully, the way he said everything, like a man setting down something that might break. "That's what Seth heard. Is that right? 'Your friends on the river. Tell them they're not on my list.'"

"Yeah," said Seth, grateful. "That's what he said. Exactly."

"Anybody who says that," said Jasper from the stairs, "has a list."

"He *trapped* one of us," Rosalie said. She was standing behind the sofa with her hands on its back, and her knuckles had gone even whiter than usual. "Like an animal. Like a — a rabbit in a snare. Hung it up by its ankle. And he knew where we live, and he knew Jacob's name, and he knew where the treaty line is, and he's going to sit up there in his blank spot and do God knows what to it—"

"To be fair, babe," said Emmett, "it ate a guy."

"*I know it ate a guy, Emmett.*"

Bella hadn't said anything at all. She was sitting in the armchair with her hands clasped between her knees and her red eyes fixed on Renesmee, on Jacob's lap on the stairs. Leah knew what she was thinking. Anybody could have known; you didn't need Edward's trick for it. *If he can do that to one of them. If he can walk up to a vampire and hang it from a tree like a deer. What could he do to her.*

"He's not after us," Layla said.

"You don't know that," said Rosalie.

"No. I don't." Layla looked at her. "But I made a deal with him today, and the deal was about you. All of you." She told them, then. The Statute. The Sunday afternoon two weeks ago, and Charlie in the doorway, and the wand that had gone down. What MACUSA did about No-Majs who'd seen things they shouldn't have. What they'd do about the people who'd shown them. What they'd do, very likely, about a family of vampires on the Calawah River once they found out that family had been told everything. *And the answer to that one usually involves fire.* She said it all in the same flat, careful voice, and when she was finished, Carlisle was looking at her with an expression Leah couldn't read at all.

"You did that for us," he said.

"I did it for Charlie," Layla said. "I promised him nobody would touch his head."

Bella got up out of the armchair. She crossed the room in one step, too fast for a human, and then caught herself and slowed down and took the last bit of distance like a person, and she sat down on the sofa next to Layla and took the witch's scraped, gloved hand in both her cold ones and held on to it. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to.

Leah couldn't watch it anymore.

She slid the glass door open behind her and went out onto the deck and shut it again, and stood at the rail in the dark. The rain had stopped for once. The river was running loud and black below, full with a week of it. Way off to the east, over the tops of the trees, the clouds had a faint dirty glow on their bellies from the lights of nothing; there wasn't anything out that way but forest.

The line ran out of her chest and away into the dark toward that glow. Twelve miles. Fifteen. Straight as a surveyor's chain.

The door slid open behind her and shut again. She didn't turn around. She knew the smell.

"I said one word, Edward."

"I haven't said any." He came and stood at the rail beside her, not too close. He'd always been careful about that, around her. She'd never been sure if it was manners or self-preservation. "I'm not going to. It's not mine to say."

"Seth's going to say it the second he phases."

"I know."

"Jake's going to be in my head the second *I* phase."

"I know that, too."

They stood there. The river roared.

"You looked," Leah said finally. "In Seth's head. At him. At — when he was in there. In our heads. Did he take anything? Did he put anything in?"

Edward was quiet for a moment. She could feel him looking, carefully, at her and past her, back through her brother's memory to a clearing in the rain.

"No," he said. "Nothing. I looked very carefully. He went in, and he read, and he came out again. He was—" Edward hesitated over the word. "Tidy. He was very tidy. It's the tidiest thing I've ever seen anyone do in a mind." A pause. "And he didn't stay. Not even in yours. He went the second it happened."

"I know," said Leah. "I felt him go."

Edward was quiet again. Then he said, in a different voice, lower, almost gentle: "Leah. In your memory. When it happened. He—"

"Don't."

"He looks like a man who's been shot," Edward said. "That's all. I thought you should know. It's not only you."

Leah gripped the deck rail so hard the wood creaked.

"Get out of my head, Edward," she said.

"I'm sorry." And he was gone, back through the door, which slid shut behind him without a sound.

She stood there a little longer. The line ran out of her into the dark and lay there, steady, patient as a dog by a door, without pulling at all. That was almost the worst part. She could feel the far end of it — a heartbeat, slow, somewhere under the ground — and every time she let herself feel it, something in her that wasn't her, something with fur and teeth that she'd fought every single day for a year, lay down and sighed.

Jacob's Rabbit was parked at the end of the Cullens' drive, where it always was. The keys were in the ignition, where they always were, because Jacob Black believed with his whole heart that nobody in the world would steal a 1986 Volkswagen Rabbit he'd built himself out of three junkers and a prayer.

Leah stole it.

**The Sol Duc foothills — that night**

The vampire's name was Marek, and it had been telling Harry so for two hours.

It lay on the steel table in the middle of the box, on its back, under the cold white light. The chains of goblin silver ran across its chest, its hips, its thighs, its ankles, its wrists, and each of them was pinned to the table by a runed bolt that went down through the steel and the stone floor and into the hill. Harry had loosened the paralysis from the jaw up an hour ago so that it could talk. It had talked. It had talked a great deal. It had begged, in Polish and then in English, and it had threatened, in English and then in Polish, and for a while it had offered him money, and for a while after that it had offered him things a good deal worse than money. Then it had told him its name, and then it had told him it again, over and over. It was still telling him.

"Marek," it said, from the table. Its red eyes were fixed on the ceiling. "Marek. My name is Marek. I was born in Łódź in eighteen ninety-one. My mother's name was—"

Harry wrote in notebook sixty-four. He was sitting on a stool outside the glass with the notebook on his knee and his overcoat off and his shirtsleeves rolled up and his tie in his pocket, and he'd been writing steadily for two hours.

*Legilimency — viable. Confirmed.* He underlined it. *Subject's mind approx. ten times human speed. Like trying to read a newspaper off the side of a passing train. All of it's there. Recall total. Subject forgets nothing. Every face, every meal.* He stopped. Then he wrote, in smaller letters: *D. Okafor, 28 Oct. 41 others since 1994 that I counted. Probably more. Stopped counting.*

He didn't write the rest. The rest was a woman at a rest stop outside Eugene, and a pair of teenagers in a parked car on a logging road in the Cascades, and Daniel Okafor on his knees on the Hoh River Trail in his red jacket with his hands up, saying *please, man, I've got a daughter*, and none of that was going in the notebook. The notebook was for the Department. The rest went where Harry had always put things like that, which was nowhere.

*Imperius*, he wrote. *Takes.* He'd done it after the first hour, when the begging had started to become tedious: one clean push, and the thing on the table had gone soft and quiet as a sleeping child. *Takes, but slides. Requires sustained attention to hold — estimate 3x human. Subject resisting constantly. Like holding a fish.* He'd let it go again after ten minutes. He'd wanted to see what it did when it came back. What it did was tell him its name.

*Pain response — tomorrow. Need baseline first.*

He looked at that line for a while. Then he wrote underneath it, in the same flat hand:

*Killing Curse — TBD. Last.*

He closed the notebook.

"Marek," said the vampire, to the ceiling. "Marek. Please. I was born in Łódź—"

"I know, mate," Harry said. "I was there."

He got up off the stool and went out of the specimen room, and the door sealed behind him with a soft sound like a jar being shut, and the voice stopped as if it had been cut with scissors.

He stood in the corridor outside with his back against the wall for a minute.

He'd been doing this for eight years. Longer, if you counted the war, and he generally didn't. He'd done far worse things than read the mind of a thing that had eaten forty-two people, and he'd do worse tomorrow, with the Cruciatus, and he wouldn't lose a single minute's sleep over any of it. He knew that about himself. He'd made his peace with it a long time ago, in a cold room nine floors under London, the night he'd signed the Department's book and the ink had gone into the page and he'd felt the oath go into his tongue.

None of that was why he was standing with his back against the wall.

He put his hand on his chest.

It was still there. It had been there all afternoon, all evening, through everything — the jump back with Marek under his arm, the box, the chains, the two hours in that mind like a sewer with the lights on. The whole time, he'd felt it. A line, running out of the middle of him and up through the hill and away west, twelve miles through the dark, to a point he could have walked to blind.

It didn't pull. That was the strange part. He'd been braced for it to pull, all afternoon, the way a compulsion pulled, the way the Imperius pulled. It didn't. It lay there in him and *waited*.

And the wolf was awake.

The wolf had been asleep for a very long time. He'd put it down there on purpose, years ago, and he'd kept it down there, through eight years of notebooks and a few hundred nights of bleeding, the way you'd keep a gun in a safe. And now it was up. He could feel it, the slow, enormous breathing of it, under his ribs. It lay there with its head up, very alert, looking west.

He'd read the Quileute file in his first week. Two pages, compiled from a Muggle anthropologist's notes from the nineteen-twenties and a handful of things he'd picked up through the Omnioculars. On the second page, near the bottom, there had been one paragraph on what the tribe called *imprinting*. *Folkloric. Unverified. Appears to describe a pair-bonding phenomenon among the shifter population. Possibly psychological. Low priority.*

He'd initialled it. He'd moved on.

Nobody had mentioned that it could go both ways. Nobody had mentioned that it could happen to someone who wasn't Quileute. Nobody had mentioned that it would feel like a hand going into your chest and taking hold of your heart and tying a knot in it.

He went up to the war room.

The map lay quiet on the table, silver lines over grey wood. The red light at the top of the rise above the Calawah had gone dark again, the snare reset and ready. On the iron stand in the corner, the slate had a new line of chalk on it.

*Well?*

He took up the stub of chalk.

*CAUGHT ONE*, he wrote. *NOMAD. MALE. TURNED APPROX. 1914. COMMENCING. FULL REPORT TO FOLLOW.*

He looked at it. Then, underneath, he wrote:

*LOCAL COMPLICATIONS. (1) WITCH. MACUSA-REGISTERED. CURSE-BREAKER. COMPROMISED, NOW COOPERATIVE. ASSET.*

The chalk stopped.

He stood there with it in his fingers for a long time. The map lay quiet. The slate waited.

*(2)*, he wrote.

He looked at the number.

There were rules about this. He'd been taught them by a woman with a face like a hatchet in a room with no windows, the first month he'd been in the Department, and he'd taught them himself to the three trainees he'd had since. *You report everything. You report the thing that embarrasses you. You report the thing that compromises you. You report it first. Because the only thing worse than being compromised in the field is being compromised in the field and having London find out from somebody else.*

He'd never broken them. Not once in eight years.

He licked his thumb and rubbed out the *(2)*.

He put the chalk down on the stand and went into the kitchen and stood at the counter with both hands flat on it, looking out of the charmed window at the Cornish cove, where it was coming up on five in the morning and the waves were rolling in black and slow.

"Well," he said, out loud, to nobody. "That's new."

The kettle was on. The kettle was always on. He poured water into the pot and didn't drink it.

The ward chimed.

It was a different chime from the map's. Lower. A soft wooden *tock*, like a knuckle rapped once on a door, and it came from the brass plate above the kitchen doorway. That was the perimeter ward, the close one, the one around the hollow itself. It was keyed to anything with a heartbeat that was bigger than a deer and smaller than an elk and walked on two legs.

Harry didn't look at the plate.

He didn't need to. He'd known for forty minutes. He'd felt the line in his chest get shorter, mile by mile, the whole time he'd been standing in the corridor with his hand on it pretending he didn't feel anything at all. Twelve miles. Then ten. Then six, as a small car with a bad muffler laboured up the forest road toward the Sol Duc trailhead and stopped. Then four, as someone got out and started walking. Then one. Then nothing at all.

He went up the stairs.

---

She was standing at the edge of the hollow, in the rain, with her arms folded.

It had started up again, steady and cold, a soft hiss on the spruce needles. She was in the same cutoffs and black tank top she'd had on that afternoon, and nothing else, not even shoes. Her feet were bare and black with mud to the ankles. Her short black hair was plastered to her head. Rain ran down her bare arms and steamed off her shoulders in the cold, the way it had steamed off his.

She was standing exactly on the line of his Muggle-repelling charm, which ought to have had her turning round and walking back to the car with a sudden vague memory of having left the oven on. She wasn't turning round. She was standing on it like a woman standing on a doormat, staring straight at the spot where his tent stood behind three layers of ward. She couldn't possibly see it. She was staring at it anyway.

He came up out of the tent and stood beside the cold fire ring, and her eyes moved and fixed on him, and he knew that she couldn't see that, either. Not through the notice-me-not. Not with her eyes.

She didn't need to.

"I can feel you," Leah said. "Under the ground. It's like — there's a hole in the hill with a heartbeat in it."

He considered that.

"That's unsettling," he said.

"Tell me about it."

He let the notice-me-not drop.

He didn't do anything to make it happen. He just stopped holding it, for her, the way you'd step aside in a doorway. He saw the moment it went. Her eyes changed focus, and went past him, and found the tent behind him in the hollow: faded green canvas, sagging a little in the wet, a two-man job with a camp chair beside it with a split seat. She stared at it for a long time.

"That's a tent," she said.

"Very observant."

"You live in a *tent*."

"Among other places."

She looked back at him. Up close, in human form, in the dark, she was tall for a woman, nearly his height. Her face was all hard angles and it had a scowl on it that looked like it had been there for a couple of years and was planning on staying. Brown eyes. The same ones that had looked at him out of the wolf's face. She looked at him with them, and the line in his chest drew up tight and then went slack again, and he saw it go through her, too. Her jaw clenched.

"How'd you get here?" he asked.

"Drove. To the trailhead. Walked the rest."

"In bare feet."

"Shoes were in the other car."

"Whose car's this one?"

"Jake's." A pause. "He doesn't know yet."

"Does he know you're here?"

"Nobody knows I'm here." She lifted her chin. "I didn't phase. I'm not going to. The second I do, my entire pack's going to be inside my head, and Seth's already going to tell them everything anyway. I wanted to know what this is before they did."

"And what is it?"

"*You* tell *me*." Her voice cracked like a whip in the wet. "You're the one with the badge. You're the one who knows everything about everybody. You knew my brother's whole life in two seconds. You knew Layla's ranking. You knew where the treaty line is. You've been sitting up here for two weeks watching all of us like we're on TV. So you tell me." She jabbed a finger at the middle of his chest without touching it, right at the spot where the line went in. "What *is* this?"

The rain came down.

There were a dozen ways to answer that. He'd been trained in most of them. He'd trained other people in some of them. *That's not something I'm in a position to discuss.* *I'm afraid that's above both our pay grades.* *Classified.*

"I don't know," Harry said.

She stared at him.

"You don't *know*," she said.

"Nope."

"Some spook you are."

"I've had better days," he admitted.

She made a sound. It might have been the start of a laugh. She cut it off. She looked past him, at the tent, and he saw her nostrils flare, and saw her catch it, coming up faint through the canvas and the wards and the earth. He'd sealed that room. Even so. She'd been hunting them for a year. She'd know it through a mile of rock.

"It's in there," she said.

"Yes."

"The leech."

"Yes."

"What are you doing to it?"

"Asking questions."

"Is it answering?"

He thought about the steel table. About Łódź and Eugene and the Hoh River Trail. *Please, man, I've got a daughter.*

"It's answering," Harry said.

Leah looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, short and hard.

"Good," she said.

The line in his chest did something then. He didn't know what to call it. It went tight and then it went easy, the way a rope does when the weight on both ends finally settles. He saw her feel it too. She put her hand flat on her own sternum, unthinking, and he realized he'd done the same thing, and took his hand away.

"I hate this," Leah said. "I need you to know that. I hate imprinting. I've hated it for two years. I'm not going to — I don't do the thing. The doe-eyes thing. Following you around. Making you muffins. I'm not *Emily*."

"Who's Emily?"

"Nobody. Doesn't matter." Her mouth twisted. "Somebody who makes muffins."

"Right," he said. "Well. For what it's worth, Lassie, I'm not much for doe eyes myself. And I can't bake."

"Stop calling me that."

"I called you Leah," he said. "Once or twice."

"Twice," she said. "You called me Leah twice. And Lassie four times."

He looked at her.

"You counted," he said.

"Shut up."

"You *counted*."

"I said shut *up*." She was scowling at him, ferociously, in the rain. "What's yours?"

"My what?"

"Your name. Your real one. You know mine. You know my brother's. You know Layla's, you know Jake's, you know Charlie Swan's, you probably know my *mom's*. So what's yours?"

There it was.

There were no rules about this one, because there didn't need to be. They'd stopped having to tell him a long time ago. He hadn't told anyone his name in the field in eight years. He'd been Page and Plant and Bonham and Osbourne, in eleven countries, to people he'd shared beds with and people he'd shot. Nine floors down in London, they had his real one in a book, under an oath that would take the tongue out of anyone who spoke it. That was the job. That had always been the job.

The wolf in his chest lifted its head.

"Page'll do," he said.

"It won't."

"It'll have to. For now."

She heard the last two words. He watched her hear them, and turn them over, and decide not to push. Her jaw worked. "For now," she said.

"Best I've got."

They stood there.

The rain came down harder. It drummed on the canvas behind him and hissed in the spruce needles, and ran in streams off the end of her nose, and steamed off her bare shoulders in the dark. She was shivering slightly, he saw, though it wasn't the cold; she didn't feel the cold any more than he did. It was everything else. It had been a very long day for both of them.

He thought about all the rules. He went through them, one after another, the way he'd been taught to, in a cold room in London with no windows. *You don't let anyone in. You don't let anyone near. Nobody sees the inside of the tent. Nobody sees the map. Nobody gets within a mile of the specimen room. Nobody, ever.*

There were six rooms down there she was never going to see. He knew that as well as he knew his own name.

"You're soaked," he said.

"I run hot."

"So I noticed."

She waited.

"Kettle's on," he said.

She stared at him. "*What?*"

"I've got a kettle. It's on. It's always on." He jerked his chin at the tent. "Now you can come in and have a cup of tea, or you can stand out here in the rain for a bit longer and hate me. Your choice."

Leah looked at him for a long, long moment, with rain running down her face and her arms folded so tight across her chest that her knuckles had gone pale.

"Can't I do both?" she said.

"Course you can," Harry said, and turned, and held the flap of the tent open for her.

She came across the hollow in the rain, barefoot, through the mud. She stopped at the flap and looked down into it — at the stairs going down, at the light, at the long scrubbed table and the copper pans and the window over the sink with a Cornish cove in it at five in the morning, all of it opening out under the faded canvas like the inside of a house. He watched her face while she took it in. It didn't change much. Her eyes went wide, and then narrow, and then her mouth tightened at one corner, the way people's mouths did when they'd decided not to give you the satisfaction.

She looked back at him over her shoulder, with one muddy foot on the top step.

He met her eyes. The line between them pulled up tight and went still.

"Wipe your feet," said Harry.
