# Chapter One: Fido

## I. Runoff

The water let go of her somewhere below the thirteen-hundredth level.

Ahsoka Tano came out of the outflow grate on her side, scraping her shoulder along a lip of corroded duracrete, and lay in two centimeters of runoff while the rest of the viaduct roared past overhead in a falling sheet she could feel in her teeth. Her lungs didn't want to work. She made them. One breath, oily and thick, then another, and on the third she rolled to her knees and coughed until something came up that tasted like engine coolant and old pipe.

Above her, a long way above her, the city went on. She could feel it the way she always could on Coruscant, a pressure at the back of the skull, a trillion lives stacked like plates in a cupboard. On a good day it was a kind of music. Tonight it sounded like a crowd that had turned to look at her.

*Snips. Let me help you.*

She pushed the voice away and got her feet under her.

The outflow channel ran along a maintenance shelf, fifteen meters wide, lit at long intervals by sodium strips that buzzed and stuttered. The far wall was a cliff of conduit, pipes the width of a gunship hull bundled with cable and furred with something grey that grew in the dark. The water in the channel moved fast and stank. Things floated in it that she didn't look at closely.

She checked herself the way her master had taught her to check a ship after a hard landing: systems first, then damage. Legs worked. Arms worked. The shoulder she'd scraped bled through the torn sleeve, a thin, steady seep, nothing that needed doing tonight. Her left montral rang where she'd struck it going into the pipe, a high flat tone that made the edges of her vision crawl. Her lekku ached from the cold. Her clothes were soaked through and plastered to her and the left boot had taken on water and squelched when she shifted her weight.

No lightsabers. She knew that. Her hand went to her belt anyway, both sides, and closed on nothing, and for a second the nothing was worse than the cold.

They had taken them in the Temple. Her own master had stood there while they did it. She'd seen his jaw work like he wanted to say something and then she'd seen him not say it.

*That isn't fair,* she thought. *He believed you. He came after you alone.*

*He came after you,* another part of her answered, *and he'd have brought you back.*

She started walking.

---

There was a ladder at the end of the shelf, rungs bolted into a support column, and a hatch at the top that had rusted half-open years ago and never been fixed. She went through it into a corridor that smelled of fried protein and urine and ozone. Light came from a cracked advertising panel at the far end that cycled through three images, a woman holding a glass of something blue, a speeder, a woman holding a different glass of something blue, and in the pauses between, a fourth image that wasn't an advertisement.

It was her.

She stopped in the corridor and watched it come around. They'd used her Temple identification holo, the formal one, where she'd been told not to smile and had smiled anyway, a little, at the corner of her mouth because Master Skywalker had been pulling faces behind the imaging droid. Under it, in Basic and then in Huttese and then in a script she didn't read, ran the words she'd known would be there.

WANTED BY THE GALACTIC REPUBLIC. AHSOKA TANO. TOGRUTA FEMALE. CONSIDERED ARMED AND EXTREMELY DANGEROUS. SOUGHT IN CONNECTION WITH THE MURDER OF LETTA TURMOND AND THE BOMBING OF THE JEDI TEMPLE. REWARD OFFERED FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO CAPTURE. CONTACT REPUBLIC SECURITY OR ANY CORUSCANT GUARD STATION.

Then the woman with the blue drink, smiling like nothing in the galaxy had ever gone wrong.

*Armed.* She almost laughed. She would have given a great deal right then to be armed.

She pulled her wet sleeve down over her hands and kept walking, head lowered, because somewhere in her body an older and colder instinct than training had taken over and knew that standing still in front of your own wanted holo was the kind of thing people remembered.

---

The lower levels at night didn't sleep so much as change shifts. She came out of the corridor into a market street that ran along the bottom of a light well, so deep that the sky was a pale smear far overhead, a rumor of daylight that never reached the ground. Stalls lined both sides under strings of glow-bulbs. A Besalisk was frying something in a vat of oil with three of his four arms and making change with the fourth. Two Twi'leks argued over a crate of droid parts. A skinny human kid with a power coupling under each arm ran past her and didn't look back.

Down here, everybody watched everybody.

She felt the looks as she went, a few of them. A wet Togruta with no coat and no credits and blood on her sleeve was a story, and on these levels stories were currency. She could feel their attention brush over her in the Force, curious, sour, opportunistic, the way she'd once felt a pack of gundarks testing the wind on Ryloth.

She needed clothes. She needed to be dry. She needed to not look like what she was.

A laundry stood at the corner of the next junction, a hole in the wall with industrial drums churning behind a mesh gate and a hand-lettered sign that promised SAME-CYCLE SERVICE NO QUESTIONS. The proprietor was a Toydarian asleep on his own counter with his wings folded and his snout in a cup. Along the back wall hung finished orders in clear wrap, coveralls mostly, the dull grey-green of maintenance crews, each one tagged with a number.

She stood across the street and looked at it for a long time.

The Jedi didn't steal. That was a rule so basic it had never needed to be written down. She had been a Jedi since she was three years old.

She'd also been accused tonight of blowing up her own home and murdering a prisoner with her mind, and every corridor of that military base on the way out had become one more reason for them to believe it. She thought about Admiral Tarkin's face, mild and attentive, and the way he'd said *the evidence speaks for itself* as if evidence were a person who'd come to him privately.

She crossed the street.

The mesh gate was locked with a mechanical bolt. She reached into it with the Force and drew the bolt back a centimeter at a time, so slowly that it didn't click. The Toydarian snorted into his cup. She slipped in, took the smallest coverall she could find off the rack along with a hooded work cloak in the same drab color, then stood for a moment holding them with her heart going fast.

There was a jar on the counter beside the sleeping Toydarian with a few loose credit chits in it for tips.

She didn't have anything to put in it. She stood there anyway, ridiculous, a fugitive in a laundry at two in the morning, trying to think of what to leave. In the end she pulled one of the small decorative clasps from the end of her torn sleeve, a silver thing Padmé had given her for a reception two years ago, and set it on the counter beside the jar.

Then she let herself out and drew the bolt home behind her.

---

She changed in the alcove of a dead turbolift, fast, shivering, peeling off the wet layers and stuffing them into a refuse chute. The coverall was too big in the shoulders and too long in the leg and smelled of industrial cleanser. She rolled the cuffs. The cloak was better; its hood was deep and loose enough to settle over her montrals without crushing them, though the tips pushed the fabric up into two points she couldn't do anything about. Her lekku she tucked down inside the coverall's collar. In the dark, from the back, she might pass for something else for a few seconds.

A few seconds was all anyone ever got anyway.

Her padawan beads hung down along her right lek. She found them with her fingers and held them.

She could take them off. Tuck them away in a pocket where nobody would see the Jedi in her. Her thumb ran over the small smooth beads, counting them without meaning to. Each had meant something once, a trial, a lesson, a day.

She left them where they were and pulled the hood lower.

---

The plan, if it was a plan, came to her somewhere around the time her teeth stopped chattering.

She couldn't stay on Coruscant.

She'd thought, at first, running from the base with Anakin's voice behind her, that she could stay down here and find the truth herself. Find whoever had killed Letta. Find whoever had built the bomb. Walk back up into the Temple with the answer in her hand and watch their faces change.

But down here she had no friends and no credits and no way to talk to anyone without them tracing it. She had no access to records. Every holo-board between here and the surface had her face on it. The Coruscant Guard would be pulling every camera on every level, and the reward on that board would turn every other being in this market into a pair of eyes. She'd seen it already: the Rodian at the noodle stall who'd looked at her twice. The Gran who'd stopped talking to his friend and started talking into his wrist.

Off-world, there were a few people who owed her. There was Lux, who might listen. There were Onderonians who remembered her. There were a hundred planets where nobody had ever heard of Letta Turmond and wouldn't care if they had.

And there was Barriss.

The thought surprised her with how much it hurt. Barriss was here, on Coruscant, in the Temple, probably still in the healers' wing helping the wounded from the bombing. Barriss would believe her. Barriss would know what to do, would sit across from her in that calm, solemn way and turn the problem over until she found the edge of it. If Ahsoka could get a message to anyone, it would be Barriss.

But a message to Barriss was a message to the Temple, and the Temple had let Tarkin take her.

*Later,* she told herself. *Get somewhere safe. Then reach out. Then think.*

Off-world, then. Which meant a ship. Which meant a port. The big civilian spaceports up top would be crawling with the Guard. There were freight yards all along the edge of the Works, though, where cargo came down from orbit on heavy lifters and went out again packed into haulers whose captains asked about weight and payment and nothing else. She'd been there once, years ago, chasing a smuggler with Rex. The place had been all noise and steam and crates stacked six stories high, a maze you could get lost in.

A maze you could hide in. A maze where somebody, for the right reason, might let a stowaway into a cargo hold and forget about her until they reached the Mid Rim.

She didn't have the right reason. She didn't have any reason at all except her hands and whatever she could do with them. That would have to be enough.

---

It took her the rest of the night to get there.

She walked when she could and climbed when she had to. Twice she took a public lift, keeping her face down and her hood low, standing at the back among workers going on shift who smelled of sweat and machine oil and paid her no attention at all. Once a patrol of Coruscant Guard troopers passed through a junction she was crossing, four of them in their red-marked armor, and she turned smoothly into the doorway of a cantina and stood just inside it with her heart going like a speeder engine while they went by. She didn't breathe until their boots had faded.

They'd simply been walking, an ordinary patrol on an ordinary night. That was the worst of it, how normal they'd looked, how much like every patrol she'd ever stood beside.

She thought about Rex. Rex would be looking for her by now, if they'd told him. Rex would do it properly, the way he did everything. He'd hate every minute of it.

She thought about her master and didn't let herself think about him for very long.

By the time she reached the Works, the smear of sky far overhead had gone from dull orange to dull grey, which was what morning looked like this far down. The freight yards announced themselves before she could see them, a vibration that came up through the soles of her boots and into her shins: heavy lifters dropping from orbit on columns of repulsor thrust, cargo haulers booming as they took off, the endless grinding thunder of loader droids moving containers from one stack to another.

Then the corridor she was in opened out, and she was standing on a gantry looking down into the Kessra Freight Terminal.

It was enormous. She'd forgotten. The terminal occupied the bottom of a shaft that went up and up into the haze, two kilometers across, ringed with landing platforms at a dozen different levels like the gills of some colossal fish. On each platform sat a ship or the space for one: bulk haulers with their bellies open, rusted tramp freighters, a long sleek corporate transport with its logo painted over. Between the platforms, filling the floor of the shaft, stood the stacks. Cargo containers piled six, eight, ten high in rows and blocks, with narrow canyons between them where loader droids crawled on treads and crews in grey-green coveralls very much like hers moved back and forth with datapads and hover-dollies. Steam vented from the deck in a dozen places. The whole thing smelled of hot metal and fuel and fried food from the stalls along the mezzanines.

And it was busy. Hundreds of people at least. Thousands of crates. Nobody looked up at a Togruta on a gantry with her hood down over her face.

Ahsoka let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the viaduct.

*All right,* she thought. *All right. Find a ship.*

She went down.


## II. The Stacks

The first captain she tried laughed at her.

He was a Devaronian with one horn snapped off short and a hauler called the *Brass Ox* sitting on Platform Nineteen with her cargo doors open, and when she came up the ramp and asked, quietly, if he took passengers, he looked her up and down from the cloak's pointed hood to the rolled cuffs of her stolen coverall and laughed so hard he had to put a hand on the hull.

"Passengers," he said. "Sweetheart, I take ore. Ore doesn't eat, doesn't talk, doesn't steal from my galley. You got credits?"

"I can work."

"You can work." He wiped his eyes. "Doing what?"

"Whatever needs doing."

His gaze sharpened on her then. It moved to her hood, to the shapes of her montrals under it, and something calculating came into it that she liked much less than the laughter. She felt it shift in the Force, a small greedy turning.

"Off my ramp," he said, quite pleasantly, and she went.

The second was a Sullustan woman with a tramp freighter half-loaded with machine parts, who didn't laugh but didn't look at her either, only said "No," and kept on with her inventory. The third was asleep. The fourth had a protocol droid on the ramp who informed her that Captain Jessarin did not entertain inquiries from unaccredited persons and that she should apply through the terminal's employment office during standard business hours, a list of which could be found—

She walked away from the droid while it was still talking.

By the time the grey light overhead had brightened to something like full morning, she'd been to nine platforms and been refused at all nine, and her legs had started to shake in a way that had nothing to do with fear. She hadn't eaten since the base. She couldn't remember the meal before that. The fried-food smell from the mezzanine stalls had become a kind of torture, sweet and greasy, and twice she caught herself standing still, staring at a vendor's grill as though it might come to her if she looked long enough.

*Focus.*

She found a quiet corner between two stacked containers, sat down with her back to the cold metal, closed her eyes, and breathed.

Master Plo had taught her this, when she was very small and the Temple had been too loud. You couldn't make the noise go away. You could make yourself very still in the middle of it, like a stone in a stream, and let it go around you. She let the terminal go around her: the boom of the lifters, the grind of the loaders, the voices in a hundred languages. She let her own hunger go around her, and her fear, and the ache in her shoulder.

In the stillness she reached out.

The Force was thick here, crowded with life. She skimmed over it, not trying to read anyone, only feeling the shape of the place. Fatigue. Boredom. A sharp spike of anger from a dockworker who'd dropped something on his foot. Contentment from someone eating. It was ordinary, all of it, the morning feelings of a working port, and she let herself rest in that for a moment.

Then, at the edge of it, something else.

Attention. Several points of it, small and cold and focused, moving in a way that ordinary people didn't move. Spreading out. Taking positions.

She opened her eyes.

---

It was the Devaronian. She was almost sure of it, later, though she never found out. Some calculation about the reward and whether it was worth his time had come out in favor, and he'd made a call, and the call had gone to somebody who'd been sitting in a cantina on the mezzanine waiting for exactly that kind of call. Because they came fast. Faster than the Guard could have.

She got up and walked, not hurrying, keeping to the canyon between the stacks. Left at the next intersection. Right. The attention followed her, patient, keeping pace, closing. She felt one of them above her, up on the containers, moving along the tops of the stacks with the light sure tread of someone who'd done it before.

She went right again and found a dead end.

A loading bay, cut into the bottom of a block of stacked containers, maybe twenty meters deep and fifteen wide, with an open cargo lift at the far end that had been switched off and a few pallets of sealed crates sitting in the middle of the floor waiting for a loader. Overhead, the stacks went up six containers high on three sides. The only way out was the way she'd come in.

She turned.

There were five of them.

The first one to step into the mouth of the bay was a human, older, solidly built, with a clipped grey beard and a long coat that had once been expensive. He had a blaster pistol in his right hand, held low and easy along his thigh, and a stun pistol holstered across his belly in a cross-draw rig. His eyes were very pale and very calm.

Behind him and to his left came a Trandoshan, huge and green-grey and scaled, wearing a flak vest over bare arms and carrying a scattergun across his chest. His nostrils flared wetly as he tasted the air. To the right came a Weequay with a face like old leather and a pair of blaster pistols low on his hips, and behind them a Nikto, the horned, red-skinned kind, with a long shock-staff in one hand and what looked like a net launcher strapped to his forearm.

The fifth she felt before she saw: up on the containers to her left, four stories up, a Rodian lying flat with a long rifle laid across the lip of the stack. His snout twitched. His big dark eyes didn't blink.

"Ahsoka Tano," the human said. His voice was gravel and politeness. "Rask Tolliver. These are my associates. I wonder if we could have a conversation."

"I don't know who that is," she said.

"Of course you don't." He smiled a little. "You're a dockworker. You've come down here to look for a job and you've lost your way. It happens. These stacks are very confusing." He lifted his free hand, slowly, and pointed with two fingers at her hood, at the twin peaks of fabric that her montrals pushed up. "Do you know, I think the Republic would be very interested to meet a dockworker who happens to be a Togruta female of about the right size and shape, who happens to have walked in here from the direction of the Ventrix lifts, at about the time that a Jedi fugitive might have reached them, if she'd come up out of the Zone Thirteen outflows."

Ahsoka said nothing.

"And who," Tolliver added mildly, "is wearing a coverall from a laundry two levels down that reported a theft this morning. The owner's quite upset. Somebody left him a silver clasp." His smile grew a little. "Very honest of her."

Behind him, the Trandoshan made a noise that might have been laughter, a hissing rattle deep in his throat.

"I didn't do it," Ahsoka said.

"I'm sure."

"I didn't. Any of it. Somebody set me up."

"That," said Tolliver, "is going to be a very interesting thing to explain to the Republic. Truly, I wish you luck with it. I really do. But the Republic doesn't pay me to determine guilt. It pays me to deliver. Alive is preferable, they tell me, so alive is what we'll aim for." He cocked his head. "You haven't got your lightsabers, have you? I heard they confiscated them."

She said nothing again, which was its own answer, and saw him register it.

"That makes this much easier," he said. "Gorvash."

The Trandoshan stepped forward.

---

She moved first.

She didn't have a blade, and she didn't need one to do what Master Plo had taught her about weight and balance, so she reached out into the Force and pulled the pallet of sealed crates between them sideways, hard, a shove that sent half a ton of cargo skidding across the deck straight into the Trandoshan's shins. He went over with a roar. The Weequay's hands came up off his hips full of blaster and she was already moving, darting left, toward the containers on that side, because the Rodian's rifle was up there and the closer she got to the base of his stack the less angle he'd have on her.

The first stun bolt from the Weequay went past her ear, blue rings, a crackle of ozone. The second hit the container wall beside her and spread across it in a web of light. She went flat, rolled, came up, and threw the Force at him the way you'd throw a punch, and he went backward off his feet into the Nikto behind him and they both went down in a tangle.

*Four seconds,* she thought, very clearly. *You have four seconds before they get organized.*

She ran for the mouth of the bay.

Tolliver didn't move. He didn't lift the blaster. He simply stood there, in the center of the bay's opening, watching her come, and at the last instant, when she was almost close enough to put a Force shove through him, he drew the stun pistol across his belly with his left hand and fired it from the hip.

She'd felt it coming. She was already turning. The bolt took her a glancing blow across the right side, low, at the ribs.

It was like being kicked by a bantha. Her whole right side went dead and then went white with pain, every muscle on that half of her body clamping at once, and she came down on one knee on the deck with her teeth together so hard she tasted blood. Her vision fizzed.

She heard the hiss and thump of the net launcher behind her.

The net hit her across the shoulders and wrapped. Weighted cords swung around her body and the mesh tightened as it touched, and then the charge went through it, and she screamed.

It didn't last long. A second. Two. The Nikto had it set to pulse. When it stopped she was on her side on the deck with the net wound around her arms and her cloak and one montral, the hood pulled half off, the cords cutting into the side of her face. Her right side was still not hers. Her left hand had cramped into a claw.

Boots came toward her across the deck. Several pairs.

"There," Tolliver said, above her. He sounded almost regretful. "That wasn't necessary, was it?"

The Trandoshan's face appeared upside down in her vision, blotting out the light. He breathed on her. His breath smelled of raw meat. He reached down with one clawed hand and turned her face toward him by the chin, and she felt his claws dig in under her jaw and saw his yellow eyes go narrow with something far uglier than professional interest.

"Pretty," Gorvash said. "Little Jedi. Gorvash gets points for Jedi."

"Gorvash gets paid in credits like the rest of us," Tolliver said. "Off."

The Trandoshan held on another second, to show he could. Then he let go and straightened.

Ahsoka lay on the deck and breathed and tried to feel her right hand. The fingers twitched. That was something. She reached for the Force and found it there, of course, it was always there, but her concentration was in a dozen pieces, scattered across the pain, and every time she tried to gather it the net pulsed, low, a warning tingle through the cords, and it scattered again.

"Pell," Tolliver said. "Call it in. Tell the Guard we have her secure at Kessra, Bay Forty-Four East. Tell them we'd like the reward transferred before we hand her over, not after, and tell them we'll want it in writing."

"They're gonna love that," the Weequay said. He was rubbing the back of his head where he'd hit the deck. He looked at her with no love at all.

"They'll pay it. They want her too badly not to. Ixxa, stay up top. Drang, keep that net live."

The Nikto grunted and adjusted something on his forearm, and the cords hummed against her skin.

Ahsoka closed her eyes.

*Get up,* she told herself. *Get up. Get up, you have to get up.*

Her body didn't answer. Above her, the stacks went up and up into the grey light, and very far overhead a heavy lifter drifted down from orbit on a column of blue thrust, slow and enormous, carrying freight to people who had never heard of her and never would.

She thought, quite clearly and without any self-pity at all: *This is how it ends, then. Sold for a reward in a loading bay by a man with nice manners.*

And then, from the mouth of the bay, a voice she didn't know said, "Morning."


## III. Breakfast

Eleven minutes earlier, Harry had been eating noodles.

The stall was on the east mezzanine of the Kessra terminal, wedged between a parts dealer and a booth that sold hydrospanners and luck charms from the same tray. It had four stools and a counter, and the woman who ran it was a Pantoran with a burn scar up one arm who never smiled and never got an order wrong. Her noodles were thick and hand-pulled and came in a broth so hot and salty it made his eyes water. He'd been eating here on and off for six years. It was the only reason he ever took a contract that ended in this part of the Works.

He was on his second bowl. She'd already put the third on to boil without asking.

Below the rail, the stacks spread out in their grid of grey and rust and faded company paint, and the noise came up off them in a single long rolling sound that he'd stopped hearing years ago, the way you stopped hearing the sea. Lifters, loaders, voices. Somebody swearing in Huttese two rows over. Somebody else laughing.

He ate and thought about nothing much.

The Gran had gone easier than he'd expected. A bookkeeper for a Nar Shaddaa loan outfit, who'd skipped with a datacard full of somebody else's numbers and a lot of somebody else's credits, and who'd thought the best place to hide from the Hutts was the middle of the busiest city in the galaxy. It wasn't a bad thought. It had taken Harry four days. The Gran had cried when Harry found him, all three eyes, and then tried to bribe him with the credits he'd stolen, and then cried again, and then come along quietly. Harry had handed him over an hour ago to the Guild's agent in the Works, a fat, unhurried Kubaz named Thrennik who kept an office above a shipping firm on the north side and smelled of the insects he ate out of a jar on his desk.

Thrennik had paid him. Then Thrennik had pushed a datapad across the desk with one long finger.

"New posting," he'd said, in his whistling, wet Basic. "Republic. Very big reward. Very hot. You want, Harry?"

Harry had looked at it. A holo of a Togruta girl, orange and white, with blue-and-white-striped montrals and big serious eyes and the corner of her mouth turned up a little, like she'd been trying not to laugh. Jedi Padawan. Seventeen standard years old. Wanted for the bombing of the Jedi Temple. Wanted for the murder of a prisoner in Republic custody. Escaped from military detention the night before.

He'd read it twice. Then he'd pushed it back.

"No."

"Many credits." Thrennik had rubbed his fingers together, a gesture he'd picked up from humans and used constantly. "Many, many."

"Ja, I can read."

"You don't like Jedi?"

"I don't like it," Harry had said, and then he'd left, and he still couldn't have said exactly what it was he didn't like, except that it was a girl of seventeen with that face, and that the Jedi he'd met in this galaxy had been a lot of things, stiff and secretive and too sure of themselves, but none of them had ever struck him as the type to blow up their own house.

He'd been in this galaxy twenty-two years. He'd stopped keeping count of most things a long time ago, birthdays included; they'd stopped doing anything to him around the time he'd stopped being able to die. But in twenty-two years he'd learned that when a posting came through with a number that big attached to a kid, somebody very high up wanted that kid gone very fast, and in his experience, very fast was what people wanted when they were worried about questions.

Not his business.

He finished the second bowl. The Pantoran took it away and put down the third without a word.

He'd been thinking, lately, that nothing much was his business. Contracts came and contracts went. Skips skipped and got found. The war went on in the HoloNet, far off and endless, battles with names he forgot by the next week. He fed Luna. He fixed the ship. He took jobs because they were there and because doing nothing made him restless in a way that got worse every year, a low, grinding itch under the skin. He'd started to suspect it would keep getting worse.

He was picking up his chopsticks for the third bowl when he heard the stun bolt.

---

Stun bolts had a different sound from regular blaster fire. Higher. A sort of buzzing snap. Most people in the Works wouldn't have picked it out of the noise. He picked it out the way he'd have picked out his own name.

Then a second. Then a thump, heavy, a body-weight thump.

He put the chopsticks down.

The breeze in the terminal ran, most mornings, from the big intake vents on the west wall toward the exhaust stacks on the east, which meant everything that happened on the floor of the stacks rode up past the east mezzanine on its way out. He'd chosen this stool for the noodles. He'd kept it because of that breeze. He turned his face into it now and breathed in through his nose, slow.

Fuel. Hot metal. Fried protein from the next stall over. Under that, coming up from the canyons between the stacks: ozone, sharp and fresh, the kind a stun charge left. Trandoshan, that musky reptile stink, very strong, very close to the ozone. Weequay. Nikto, which always smelled faintly of sulphur to him. A human, male, older, expensive cologne over gun oil. Rodian, higher up, on the tops of the containers, where the breeze caught it cleaner.

And Togruta.

He knew the smell. There'd been a Togruta trader on Ord Mantell who'd sold him spare parts for three years running, and a pair of Togruta hunters he'd shared a camp with once on Kashyyyk. Their skin smelled dry and clean, like sun on long grass, with something sharp underneath it like a cut rind of citrus. This one smelled like that. It also smelled like old water, sewer water, the kind that came down out of the outflows from the upper levels with everything the city didn't want in it. Like industrial laundry soap. Like blood, a little, fresh. Like fear, which had a smell all its own, flat and metallic.

And under all of it, faint, so faint he had to close his eyes and breathe again to be sure, a thread of incense.

He'd smelled that incense four times in twenty-two years. Once on a Jedi Knight who'd sat two tables down from him in a cantina on Ord Mantell for an hour, watching the door. Once on a dead one, on Ryloth. Once on a robe somebody had tried to sell him on Nar Shaddaa. Once on a Jedi Master who'd come aboard a transport he'd been riding on, three years ago, and spent the whole flight meditating with his eyes closed. Whatever they burned in that Temple of theirs, it got into everything. It got into skin. You could wash it out of clothes but you couldn't wash it out of somebody who'd breathed it every day of her life.

He heard the hiss-thump of a net launcher. Then a high, hard scream that cut off short.

He put some credits on the counter. More than the noodles. The Pantoran looked at the credits and then at him.

"Keep the bowl hot," he said. "Back now-now."

She said nothing, which from her was a yes.

---

Bay Forty-Four East was a hundred meters from the east mezzanine and eight meters down.

He went over the rail.

People did that sometimes, in the Works. Jumped. It wasn't unheard of. But they mostly did it from a lot higher up, and they mostly didn't land on the top of a cargo container two stacks over with less sound than a cat makes coming off a chair. He came down on the balls of his feet and bled the weight away into the metal with a touch of the push he'd been using so long he barely thought of it as magic anymore, and the container didn't so much as tick under him. Then he was moving along the tops of the stacks, quick and low.

The Rodian was lying flat on the edge of a container four stacks up on the left side of the bay, rifle laid across the lip, eye to the scope. He didn't hear Harry go past behind him on the next stack over. Harry didn't stop. Not yet.

He went down the back side of the block, dropping from container to container, and came around into the canyon at the mouth of the bay on foot, and walked in.

He took it in as he walked. Five of them, the Rodian included. Tolliver, who he knew by reputation and had seen once across a room on Nal Hutta: an independent, no Guild ticket, with a crew that took Republic postings and Hutt postings and anything else that paid and had a habit of bringing marks in damaged. The Trandoshan, big, scattergun across his chest. The Weequay, two pistols, rubbing the back of his head. The Nikto, shock-staff in one hand, net launcher on the other forearm with its control lit green.

And on the deck in the middle of them, wrapped in a stun net, the girl.

She was smaller than he'd expected. The holo hadn't given a scale. Her hood had been pulled half off and one montral stuck out through the mesh at an angle that looked painful, blue and white stripes, the tip of it scraped. There was blood in a thin line along her jaw where something with claws had held her. Her eyes were closed. Her chest moved fast and shallow. The coverall was grey-green, three sizes too big, rolled at the cuffs.

He came all the way in and stopped about five meters short of the group.

"Morning," he said.

They turned. He watched them do it. The Weequay's hands went back to his pistols. The Nikto swung the shock-staff around. The Trandoshan's nostrils flared, tasting him, and Harry saw the yellow eyes go narrow with something like confusion; whatever he smelled like to a Trandoshan, it apparently didn't add up.

Tolliver turned last and slowest, blaster down along his thigh, the stun pistol in his other hand.

"Good morning," Tolliver said. He looked Harry up and down. Harry let him. A tall human in a battered brown leather jacket over a dark shirt, dark trousers, scuffed boots, a heavy blaster on his right hip that he'd last fired eighteen months ago. Black hair that needed cutting. No armor. "Can I help you?"

"You were making a lot of noise," Harry said. "I was eating."

"My apologies." Tolliver's eyes went to the patch on the left breast of Harry's jacket: a small, worn, stitched sigil, the Guild's. They stayed there a second and came back up. "This is a Republic posting. The Guild has no exclusive on it."

"Didn't say it did."

"Then I'm not sure what we have to discuss."

Harry looked past him at the girl. Her eyes had opened. They were blue, very blue, and they were looking right at him, sharp even through the pain, the way a cornered animal looks at a door that somebody has just opened. Calculating, trying to decide what kind of new trouble he was.

"Take the net off her," Harry said.

The Nikto laughed, a croak through his breathing apparatus. Tolliver didn't.

"I don't think so," Tolliver said.

"She's not going anywhere. You've got five guns on her. She can barely move. That thing's still cooking her."

"That thing," said Tolliver, "is the only reason a trained Jedi isn't currently putting my associates through the walls. With respect, friend, I don't know you. And I don't take instructions about my marks from strangers who walk in off the street because they didn't like the noise."

"Harry," Harry said.

"What?"

"You said you don't know me. I'm Harry."

There was a short pause. The Weequay looked at Tolliver. Tolliver looked at Harry. Something moved behind his pale eyes, some memory turning over, a rumor heard in a bar somewhere, and Harry watched him decide that whatever the rumor was, it didn't matter, because there were five of them and one of Harry and the reward was on the deck at his feet.

"Well, Harry," said Tolliver, "it's been a pleasure. Now I'd like you to turn around and walk out of here, because in about ten minutes the Coruscant Guard is going to arrive, and I'd hate for them to find you standing here and get the wrong idea."

Harry looked at the girl again.

"Hey," he said to her. "You do it?"

Her eyes widened a fraction. Then narrowed. Her voice came out cracked and rough. "No."

He breathed in. Under the ozone and the Trandoshan and the cologne, her scent hadn't changed. Fear, flat and metallic. Pain. Exhaustion so deep it had its own sour edge. He knew the smell of a lie. People's bodies told on them; their sweat changed, sharpened, went acrid. He'd spent twenty-two years learning to read it on a hundred species.

There was nothing acrid on her. Not a trace.

"Right," Harry said.

"Pell," Tolliver said, without turning his head. "Show the gentleman out."

The Weequay drew.

He was fast. Weequay often were. His right hand came up full of blaster, red light already flaring in the emitter, and he fired from the hip at Harry's chest at five meters' range, the kind of shot you didn't miss.

Harry's wand was in his hand. It had been in his sleeve when he walked in, and then it was in his hand, and he swung it short and hard from the wrist like a man playing a ball off his hip. The bolt hit it a hand's width from the tip with a sound like a hammer on a bell and went off at an angle into the side of a container, where it left a glowing, spitting crater the size of a fist.

Everyone stopped.

The Weequay stared at his pistol. Then at Harry. Then at the crater.

"Ag, man," Harry said, and lifted the wand.


## IV. Tear

He had hit a blaster bolt with a stick.

Ahsoka lay in the net and stared at him. Her right side still burned and twitched, and the cords still hummed against her skin, and her head was full of the high flat ringing from her montral, but none of it mattered for the space of that one heartbeat, because she had watched it happen and she still didn't believe it.

A lightsaber could deflect a bolt. That was what lightsabers were for, or one of the things. A blade of plasma met a bolt of plasma and turned it. Jedi trained for years to do it. She'd trained for years to do it. She had never once heard of anybody doing it with a piece of wood.

It was wood. She was sure of that. Pale and slightly crooked and no longer than her forearm, held loosely in a big hand. There was nothing in it, no emitter, no cell, no kyber crystal humming in a housing. A stick.

And when he'd swung it, she had felt something.

She'd been trying to read him since he walked in. She'd been trying to read all of them, the way she'd been taught, looking for the shapes of their intentions in the Force: Tolliver's cool greed, the Trandoshan's hot, ugly appetite, the Weequay's resentment, the Nikto's boredom, the Rodian's patience up on the stacks. They were all there. They were all easy.

He had been hard.

Where he stood, the Force went quiet. She didn't know a better way to put it. It was like the stillness at the mouth of a cave on a hot day, where the air coming out of the dark is suddenly, shockingly cold. The Force flowed up to him and around him and seemed to slow there and pool, and when she'd tried to reach into the pool to see what was in it, her attention had slid off it the way her fingers would slide off ice.

When he'd swung the stick, something in that cold still place had flexed.

Then he lifted it again, and she felt the Force tear.

---

There was no other word. She would try to find one later, lying awake, and she never would.

It was as if the Force were cloth, a great fine sheet of it stretched through everything, through her, through the deck and the stacks and the five people standing around her and the air between them, and something had put its claws into the cloth and *ripped*. She felt it go. She felt the edges of it part with a sound that had no sound, a long, shuddering, fibrous shriek, and she felt the whole sheet of it jerk and recoil around the tear, the way skin jerks back from a cut.

Light came out of the end of the stick, a hot orange flash, and hit the Weequay in the chest.

Pell went backward off his feet as if a speeder had hit him. Something inside him burst. Fire came out of him, out of his chest and his mouth and the place where his chest had been, and he hit the deck four meters away in a sprawl and rolled once and didn't move again, and smoke came up off him in a thick, greasy column that smelled of burned meat and cooked hide.

Ahsoka's whole body clenched against the Force itself. The tear was still there, hanging in the air where the bolt of light had passed, raw and open, its edges fluttering, and she could feel the Force around it shivering, trying to close it and not knowing how.

High on the stacks, the Rodian fired.

She felt it, the brief bright spike of his intent, and in the same instant the man with the stick turned his head and looked straight up at the place where the Rodian was lying, and swung the stick up and around.

The second tear was worse than the first, because she was ready for it and it didn't matter. It went through her like a hook. The Rodian's bolt struck the deck where the man had been standing a half-second before, and the man wasn't there anymore, he'd taken one long step to the left, and the light from his stick went up the side of the stack like a thrown spear. It hit the lip of the container under the Rodian's rifle.

The top of the stack came apart.

The container's lip and roof peeled open in a ball of orange fire and black smoke, and the Rodian went up with it, end over end in the smoke, and came down in pieces. One of the pieces hit the deck two meters from Ahsoka's head with a sound she would remember. His rifle came down after it, spinning, and clattered and skidded away. Bits of hot metal pattered down all around her like rain.

"*Gorvash!*" Tolliver shouted.

The Trandoshan was already bringing the scattergun up from across his chest, roaring, his whole body coming around behind it. He was so close. Two meters from the man, maybe three. A scattergun at that range would take a human apart.

The man flicked the stick sideways at him, a short, flat, economical cut, as if he were knocking a fly off a table.

The tear was thin this time, thin and long and very sharp, like a paper cut in the Force, and somehow that was worse too; it went in deep and kept going.

The scattergun came apart in Gorvash's hands. It came apart in a long clean diagonal line, the barrel going one way and the stock the other, and the line didn't stop at the gun. It went on through the Trandoshan, from his left shoulder down to his right hip, through the flak vest and the scales and everything under them, and for a second nothing else happened. Gorvash stood there holding two halves of a gun. His yellow eyes went down to look at himself. He opened his mouth.

Then the top part of him slid sideways off the bottom part, and the rest of it came down all at once, and Ahsoka turned her face against the deck and shut her eyes.

She heard the Nikto yell something in his own language. She heard the net launcher's control chirp, a rising electronic whine, and knew without looking what it meant, that he'd thumbed it from pulse to full, and she had just time to think *oh no* before the net lit up around her.

It was worse than before. Much worse. She arched in the cords. Her heels drummed on the deck. She couldn't hear anything but her own blood and the shriek of the charge and something that she realized after a moment was her own voice.

Through it, very faintly, she felt a fourth tear.

Then the charge stopped.

She lay on her back, gasping, the net hot and smoking against her skin, and blinked up at the light. Drang the Nikto was lying across from her on his side with his shock-staff in two pieces beside him and the forearm with the net control on it lying a meter farther off, by itself. The rest of him was in more than one piece too. There was a lot of red, very bright, spreading across the deck in a thin sheet toward her.

Four tears. Four gaping wounds in the Force, hanging in the air of the loading bay in the places where the light had gone, their edges ragged and trembling. She could feel all of them at once, like four knives held to her skin.

*Who are you?* she thought. *What are you? What* is *that?*

Then a hand grabbed the back of her net, and her head was jerked up, and the cold round muzzle of a blaster pressed in hard against the base of her left montral.

---

"*That's enough!*"

Tolliver had hauled her half upright by the cords of the net, one-handed, and was holding her in front of him, crouched behind her, his blaster jammed into the soft place at the root of her montral where she could feel her own pulse beating against the metal. She could feel him too, now, his body pressed to her back, the heat of him and the shaking. His breath went in and out against her lek, ragged, fast. The calm was gone. He smelled of sweat and cologne and terror.

The man with the stick stood about six meters away in the middle of the bay among the bodies, and looked at them.

He hadn't moved since the Nikto. He stood with his weight even on both feet and the stick down at his side in his right hand, loosely, like a man holding a pen. Smoke drifted across between them from what was left of Pell. His face, which Ahsoka now saw clearly for the first time, was young, a human face, square-jawed, clean-shaven, with untidy black hair falling over his forehead and very green eyes. He didn't look angry. He didn't look like anything at all.

"Drop it," Tolliver said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Drop the — whatever it is. Drop it now, or I swear I'll put a bolt through her head, I swear on my mother, I'll do it."

"Republic wants her alive," the man said. His voice was a deep baritone with an accent she didn't know: flat, clipped vowels and consonants with a soft roll at the edges of them, unhurried. "You said."

"I'll take half for dead! I'll take nothing! Drop it!"

The man looked at Tolliver. Then he looked at Ahsoka.

She stared back. She didn't know what she was trying to say. *Don't*, maybe. Or *help*. Or *what are you*. She found that her eyes had filled up and she was furious about it.

He lifted his left hand, the empty one, the one without the stick. He lifted it maybe ten centimeters, palm down and fingers loose, as if he were going to scratch the side of his own neck. Then he closed it into a fist and turned it, a short, sharp twist of the wrist.

Behind her, Tolliver's neck broke.

It made a sound like a green branch over a knee. Wet and sharp. She felt it through her own back where he was pressed to her, a sudden violent jerk that went through his whole body and stopped. The blaster dropped away from her montral. It hit the deck by her knee and lay there. Tolliver's hand let go of the net. For a moment he stayed crouched behind her, propped against her back, and then he slid sideways and fell over and lay on the deck with his pale eyes open and his head turned much too far around on his shoulders, looking at the ceiling.

Ahsoka knelt there in the smoking net. She didn't breathe.

The Force was silent.

That was what she couldn't make sense of, kneeling there. She'd been waiting for the tear. She'd been braced for it. A man had just reached out across six meters of empty air and broken another man's neck without touching him, and she'd seen Jedi do that, seen the dark side do it, seen Ventress and Dooku and worse lift people off their feet and choke the life out of them. She knew what it felt like when someone used the Force to kill. It felt like a hand closing on the inside of your own throat. It felt like rot.

This had felt like nothing. The closed fist, the twist, the crack, and the Force perfectly still around all three, as if nothing at all had happened. As if whatever he'd done had come from somewhere the Force couldn't see.

---

The man put the stick away.

She didn't see where. One second it was in his hand and the next it was gone, back into his sleeve or a pocket or wherever it lived, and he was walking toward her across the bay, stepping over Gorvash's legs on the way without looking down. He didn't hurry. His boots made almost no sound on the deck. She noticed that the way she noticed everything right now, in a disconnected, glassy way: the Trandoshan had made the deck shake when he walked, and this man was at least as tall and almost as broad and the deck didn't seem to know he was there.

She tried to get up. Her legs folded. She tried again, and got as far as one knee, with her hands still tangled in the cords in front of her.

He stopped about two meters away and crouched down on his heels so their eyes were level.

Up close he was even bigger. Broad through the shoulders and chest, long-armed, with big, square hands, the skin of them smooth and unmarked. His jacket was old leather, cracked at the elbows, with a small patch stitched on the breast that she recognized after a moment: the Bounty Hunters' Guild. There was a blaster on his hip that he hadn't touched. He smelled, from two meters away, of smoke and noodle broth and something underneath both that reminded her of the high forests on Kashyyyk, resin and cold stone.

He looked into her eyes.

And she felt him come in.

It was very light. Almost nothing. Like the brush of a fingertip down the edge of a stack of flimsi. She felt the top of her mind ruffle, the surface of it, the thoughts that were loudest right now, *I didn't do it I didn't do it Letta in the cell, Letta clawing at her throat, Master's hand reaching out to her on the pipe, Snips, let me help you, the drop, the cold, the water*, and all of that flickered as if someone were turning the pages.

She'd been trained for this. Every Jedi had. Every Jedi learned from childhood to guard her mind, to know the feel of another presence pressing on it and to close against it. She'd practiced against Master Plo in the Temple for years, his presence pressing patiently at the edges of her mind until she learned to hold it out.

This came from that same cold, still, quiet place she'd felt around him, and it moved through her thoughts as lightly as if it belonged there.

She slammed shut.

She did it with everything she had left, which wasn't much, and she was expecting him to push back. Master Plo always had, gently, testing. Instead the touch was simply gone, withdrawn instantly, the way a hand comes back from a hot stove, and he was crouched there in front of her looking at her face with a small line between his eyebrows.

"Sorry," he said.

"*Stay out of my head.*" It came out shaking. Everything was shaking.

"Ja, okay." He held up both hands, open, palms toward her. Empty. "Okay."


## V. Strays

She had good shields. He'd give her that.

Harry stayed crouched on his heels with his hands up and let her glare at him. Her eyes were wet and furious and her whole body was trembling in the net, small fast tremors running through her from the stun charge, and she was still on one knee, still trying to get the other foot under her.

He hadn't gone deep. He never went deep without a reason, and he'd only wanted the top layer, the thoughts sitting right at the front where anybody shouting them would have them. It was a habit by now, like checking a room's exits; a quick look at the surface of somebody told him whether they were planning to stab him and usually whether they were lying about whatever they were saying out loud.

The surface of her had been loud.

*I didn't do it.* That first, over and over, like a fist hitting a door. Then pictures, bright and jumbled and sharp-edged the way fresh memories were. A cell, white light. A thin human woman with her hands at her own throat, clawing, her heels kicking against the floor, and nobody's hands on her. The girl's own voice, shouting for a guard. Then running. A corridor full of clone troopers, red-marked armor. A sewer pipe, a huge one, the mouth of it open over a drop, and a man standing at the edge of it, a human, tall, dark-haired, scar through one eye, one gloved hand reaching out to her: *Snips, let me help you.* The drop. The cold.

And behind all of that, sitting underneath it like a stone, grief. Some big building on fire. Bodies on stretchers. Clone troopers and people in robes. A place she'd called home.

None of it was what he'd have expected to find in the head of somebody who'd bombed that building. People who'd done terrible things had a particular kind of noise on their surface, in his experience, a hard bright self-justifying chatter or else a careful, practiced blankness. Hers was all bewilderment. Like a kid who'd come home and found the locks changed.

He put that together with what his nose had already told him, and it came out the same both ways.

"Okay," he said again, mostly to himself.

She'd got her other foot under her. She came up off the deck in one jerky movement, tangled in the cords, and swayed, and stayed up. She glared at him from inside the net with her hood half off and blood on her jaw.

"Hold still," he said.

"Don't you *dare*—"

He looked at the net and cut it.

There was no need for the wand for something that size. He just thought about the cords coming apart, and they did, all at once, along a dozen lines, and the whole net slithered off her in pieces and pooled around her feet with a wet, dead sound. The control box on the Nikto's severed forearm across the bay fizzed once and went dark.

She flinched as if he'd hit her. Her whole body jerked, and she looked down at the net around her feet, and then back up at him with an expression he couldn't immediately read, closer to the way people looked at him when he'd just said something in a language they didn't speak.

"You did it again," she said.

"Did what?"

She didn't answer that. She was backing up, very slowly, a step at a time, her hands up in front of her with the fingers spread in a way he'd seen on Jedi before, toward the dead-end wall of the bay. Her eyes never left him. Her right foot dragged.

He stayed where he was and let her go. He could hear her heart from where he crouched. It was going like a hummingbird's.

"Your right side," he said. "Stun bolt?"

"What do you care?"

"You're dragging the foot."

"I'm fine."

"Ja, you look it." He stood up, slowly, so she could see him do it. She stopped backing. Her hands came up a little higher. "It'll wear off in an hour. Two, maybe. Your jaw needs cleaning."

She touched her jaw. Her fingers came away red, and she looked at them as if she hadn't known.

He looked around the bay. Smoke from the Weequay and the burning container lip overhead, rolling up into the shaft. The Rodian's rifle lying by itself in the middle of the deck. Pieces. A lot of blood. Somewhere off to the north, past the stacks, he could hear voices beginning to rise, people who'd heard the explosions and were deciding whether to come and look, and further off than that, the long falling wail of a terminal security siren starting up.

Tolliver had told the Weequay to call the Guard, and the Weequay had done it, and before long they'd come down on this bay with a gunship and a lot of rifles. Then they'd take the girl back to wherever the woman in the white cell had died, and somebody, sooner or later, would decide that the easiest thing all round was if the girl didn't make it to whatever passed for a trial.

He thought it, anyway. He'd seen enough to think it.

He'd also killed five people in the last ninety seconds, and she'd watched him do it, and now he was standing between her and the only way out of the bay. He could see exactly how that looked from where she was standing.

"I can take you in," he said.

She went white under the orange of her face. Actually white, the color draining out of her cheeks until the white markings over her eyes stood out stark against it. Her hands came all the way up and he felt the air in the bay shift and thicken around her, gathering, the way it did around Jedi when they were about to throw something, and even through the shaking, even half-dead on her feet, she set herself with her bad foot back and her chin down.

"*Try it.*"

He stopped. Ran it back.

"Ag, no. Ja. That came out wrong." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Take you in. Like a stray. Food, a bunk, a shower. Off this rock."

She stared at him.

"I'm not a stray," she said finally.

"You're wearing a stolen laundry coverall that smells like a sewer."

"It *was* in a sewer. Well, I was. It wasn't. It was in a laundry." She stopped, and closed her eyes for a second, and he watched her pull herself together by main force. When she opened them again they were hard. "I don't know you. You just killed five people in front of me. You're Guild. There's a reward on me big enough to buy a starship. And I'm supposed to just—what, walk out of here with you because you asked nicely?"

"Ja," Harry said. "That was the idea."

"*Why?*"

Footsteps, outside the bay, coming from the west, fast, a lot of them, heavy boots striking the deck in step, the way only soldiers or droids ran. Over them, from somewhere above, the deep pulsing thrum of repulsors and the whine of a gunship's engines spooling down to hover.

Then the smell.

Plastoid. Armor polish. Gun oil. And men, a dozen of them at least. He'd smelled clone troopers before, plenty of times, over the last however many years of the war, and he'd never once got used to it. Every man has his own scent the way he has his own face. These men had one between them. A dozen copies of the same smell, the same sweat, the same skin, running toward him in step, and something in the back of his head that wasn't entirely human had always found that deeply, quietly wrong.

And with them, out in front, something he didn't have a name for. Dry. A little like old paper, a little like cloves. And under it, faint, that thread of temple incense.

"Ask me later," he said.

"What? No. I'm asking you *now*—"

"Your people are here."

She heard it then too. He watched her hear it. Her montrals tilted, both at once, a small, involuntary movement toward the mouth of the bay. Her face changed. Whatever she'd been gathering in the air around her went out of it, like breath, and for a second she just looked her age, seventeen and filthy and exhausted and very, very frightened.

Then she straightened, and put her chin up, and turned to face the mouth of the bay.

Harry turned too, and went and stood beside her, about an arm's length off. She glanced at him sharply, sideways. He kept his hands in his pockets.

The clone troopers came around the corner of the stacks at a run.


## VI. Fido

They were Coruscant Guard. She knew it before she saw the red.

Twelve of them came around the corner of the stacks in a wedge, rifles up, the long DC-15s, moving the way clones always moved, every man covering every other man, and they spread across the mouth of the bay in a line so neat it might have been drawn with a rule. Their armor was white with crimson markings on the helmets and shoulders and chests. The sergeant in the center had a crimson stripe down the middle of his helmet and a pauldron on one shoulder. The muzzles of twelve rifles found her and the man beside her and stayed there.

"*Nobody move!*" the sergeant shouted. "Hands where I can see them! Hands! Now!"

Ahsoka lifted her hands.

She didn't have a choice about it. Her body did it on its own, the way it had been trained to do around men in that armor her whole life, only with them on her side. Her palms came up empty and open to shoulder height and stayed there, trembling, and she looked down twelve rifle barrels at twelve identical T-shaped visors and could not find a single face she knew.

Beside her, the man called Harry kept his hands in his pockets.

"*Hands!*" the sergeant bellowed at him.

"Ja, I heard you," Harry said, and took them out of his pockets and held them loosely at his sides, out where everyone could see them. The sergeant's rifle twitched, and for a second Ahsoka thought he'd fire, and then he didn't.

Behind the line of troopers, somebody stepped through.

---

Ki-Adi-Mundi.

He came between two of the troopers with his lightsaber already in his hand and already lit, its blue blade held low and to the side, humming. He was very tall, taller than Harry by a head, most of it skull: the long, high, tapering dome of the Cerean cranium rising up and back from his forehead, smooth and pale and faintly veined, a fringe of long white hair at the back and a white moustache and short beard framing a stern mouth. His robes were brown and plain. His eyes went over the bay once, swift and thorough, the way she'd seen them go over a battlefield map on a holotable: the Weequay, burned black, still smoking. What was left of the Rodian. Gorvash in two pieces. The Nikto and his separated arm. Tolliver with his head on backward.

She saw him take it in. She saw it go into him, all of it, and settle there, and she saw the line of his mouth go flatter.

Then his eyes came to her and stopped.

"Ahsoka Tano," he said.

"Master Mundi."

It came out hoarse. She cleared her throat. She'd known him her whole life, at a distance, the way younglings knew all the Council: a tall, grave, reserved presence at the far end of the Council chamber, a voice that always made sense of things, a man she'd once watched carry two wounded troopers off a field on Geonosis without being asked. She'd never once spoken to him for more than a minute at a time.

"You will come with us," he said. His voice was even and grave. "Now, please."

"Master Mundi, I didn't do it. I didn't kill Letta Turmond and I didn't bomb the Temple. Somebody—"

"Then you have nothing to fear from a fair hearing."

"*Fair?*" It came out of her too loudly and cracked in the middle. "They gave me to Tarkin. You gave me to Tarkin. The Council stood there and let the Republic Navy take a Padawan of the Order and put her in a military cell, and then a prisoner died in front of me and there was nobody in that room but me and her. Tarkin doesn't want a hearing. He wants a firing squad."

Some of the troopers shifted. Only slightly. She saw it.

Master Mundi's face didn't change. But she felt something move in him, in the Force, a small heavy turning, like a stone rolling over in deep water.

"The Council," he said, "is aware of the Admiral's enthusiasm." He paused. "That is a matter for the Council. You are a matter for me. Running has done you no good, Padawan. Five more beings are dead." His eyes went past her to Harry and stayed there. "Is this the reason?"

"He—" She didn't know how to finish it.

"You," Master Mundi said to Harry. "Who are you?"

"Harry."

"Did you kill these beings?"

"Ja."

"All of them?"

"Ja."

Master Mundi looked at him a long moment. Ahsoka watched his eyes narrow very slightly, and she knew what he was doing, because she'd done it herself ten minutes ago. He was reaching. Feeling for the shape of this man in the Force, his intentions, his nature, the shape of the threat. She watched the faint crease come between Master Mundi's brows, watched him reach a little further, watched the crease deepen.

She knew exactly what he'd found. The cold, still place. The pool his attention slid off.

His grip shifted on the hilt of his saber.

"Then you will also come with us," he said, "and you will explain how." He lifted his free hand without looking back. "Sergeant. Stun them both. Carefully."

"Sir." The sergeant thumbed a control on the side of his rifle. Down the line, twelve rifles chirped. "Setting for stun. On my mark—"

"Can I ask you something?" Harry said.

He said it to Master Mundi. He said it in a tone of mild interest, as if they were two strangers waiting for the same shuttle, and he was looking at Master Mundi with his head tilted very slightly to one side. Looking, Ahsoka slowly realized, at a point some way above his face.

"You may surrender," Master Mundi said.

"Ja, no, that's a different thing. This is a question." Harry squinted. "Has anybody ever told you that you've got a head like a bellend?"

Silence.

It went on a long time. The whole bay seemed to listen to it. Smoke drifted between them. Somewhere above, the gunship's engines hummed.

"A what?" said one of the troopers, from the end of the line.

"Tip of a—" Harry nodded downward, briefly, at nothing in particular, and then back up at the long, smooth, pale, gently-veined dome of the Cerean skull rising above the Jedi Master's face. "You know. Look at it."

The trooper looked at it.

Several of the troopers looked at it. Ahsoka saw their helmets tip, very slightly, one after another down the line, up toward the top of Master Mundi's head. Then she saw one of them, second from the left, go very still. Then his shoulders started to shake. A sound came out of his helmet, compressed and tinny through the vocoder, like a boot being slowly stepped on.

"*Trooper,*" the sergeant said.

"Sorry, Sarge. Sorry. Sorry—"

Ahsoka stared at Harry. She was aware that her mouth was open. She was aware, in some distant part of her that had been trained in diplomacy and protocol since she was three years old, that she was standing in the middle of a massacre, being arrested for murder by a member of the Jedi High Council, and that a total stranger had just told that member of the Jedi High Council, in front of a squad of the Coruscant Guard, that his head looked like the end of a—

Master Mundi had not moved. Not one muscle. His face was perfectly calm. Only the blade of his saber, held low beside him, trembled a fraction, and in the Force she felt something come off him in a single hard, clipped, frozen wave that was the closest thing to outrage she had ever felt from a Jedi Master.

"*Seize them,*" he said.

The troopers came forward.

Ahsoka stepped back. She didn't decide to. She stepped back the way she'd stepped back from the edge of the pipe, without thinking, because twelve rifles were coming at her and there was only one direction left to go, and she stepped back into Harry, into his side, and his hand landed on her shoulder.

It was hot. She noticed that. Hot like a fever, right through the coverall, the heat of it spreading into her shoulder in a wide flat band.

She could have pulled away. She had just enough left to do it.

She didn't.

"Fido," Harry said.

---

The world closed on her like a fist.

She'd once pulled a starfighter out of a dive over Ryloth so hard she'd grayed out, the force of it pressing her back into the seat until she'd felt her eyes flatten in their sockets. This was that, from every direction at once. Everything squeezed. Her ribs, her skull, her lungs. The air was gone. She couldn't breathe. She was being pulled through something very narrow, too narrow, a tube with walls she couldn't see, and her montrals—

*Her montrals.*

They were sensing organs. She'd lived with them her whole life and mostly forgot about them, the way she forgot about her ears, but they were always working, always feeling out the shape of the space around her, the walls and floor and the bodies moving in it. Now the space around her had folded in on itself, crushed down to nothing, and the montrals screamed. They were being pressed flat. There was no other way to describe it. Pressure built behind her eyes and in the long hollow curves of the montrals until she thought they'd crack.

And the Force.

She'd felt five tears in the bay. This time she was *inside* one. She was being dragged through it, through the long ragged rip in the fabric of everything, and the Force howled around her as she went, and for one long instant she understood that she was going a very great distance, much further than across a bay, much further than across a city. She felt Coruscant go.

It went all at once. The trillion-voiced roar, the stacked lives, the music she'd lived inside since she was three years old, the constant enormous murmur at the back of her mind that she'd forgotten was even there, cut off as though a door had slammed in an empty hall.

Then the long dark pull, on and on.

Then deck plating, hard under her knees.

---

She threw up.

There wasn't much. She hadn't eaten. What came out was thin and sour and burned her throat, and she knelt there on all fours on the cool metal floor heaving until nothing more came and then heaving some more anyway. Her montrals throbbed in long, slow, sickening pulses. Her eyes streamed. Her whole body was shaking again, harder than before.

Somewhere close by, something was making a noise.

She'd never heard a noise like it. A sharp, hoarse, repeated cry, *rah, rah, rah-rah-rah*, high and urgent and full of an emotion she couldn't place, and behind it the rapid clicking patter of something with claws running on a metal floor. It came closer, then stopped, behind some kind of barrier, and went on crying out. *Rah. Rah-rah.*

"Ja, ja, it's me," said Harry's voice, above her. "Wait. *Wait.*"

The crying stopped. It changed into a high thin whine, then quiet.

Ahsoka stayed on her hands and knees and breathed.

Something was wrong. Something besides the pain and the sickness and the montrals. It took her a few breaths to work out what it was.

It was quiet.

The ship hummed, the way ships did, systems and fans and the faint tick of metal settling. The quiet was in the *Force*. She'd lived on Coruscant almost all her life, and even when she'd been off-world, on campaigns, aboard a cruiser or in some jungle or desert or ice field, she'd always felt the absence of Coruscant as a kind of shape, and gone back to it like coming home. And she'd been there ten seconds ago. In the bay. In the middle of all of it. Now she reached for that great murmur and found nothing. Where it should have been, there was only a soft, slow, green kind of quiet. Wind. Trees, many trees, enormous and patient. Small lives in the trees and in the grass. The bright, close, intent presence of some animal, very near, behind a door, practically vibrating with impatience.

And the man. That cold, still pool, standing right beside her.

She lifted her head.

She was in a ship's hold, or a common area: a square space with a low ceiling of exposed conduits, worn deck plating, a padded bench along one wall with a table bolted in front of it, a short corridor leading forward and another leading aft. The light was the soft yellow of a ship on low power. It smelled of machine oil and cooking and something else, something animal and dry and slightly dusty that she didn't recognize. There were short black and white hairs on the floor beside her hand.

And there was a viewport. A big one, set into the hull above the bench, and through it she could see sky.

It was evening, or nearly. The sky was a deep clear blue-green, darkening toward the top of the viewport, with a few stars already out in it, and low on the horizon two moons hung one above the other, one large and pale gold, one smaller and pinkish. Under the moons were trees. Huge, dark, feathery trees, the tallest she'd ever seen, their tops swaying in a wind she could hear faintly through the hull, and a long slope of silver-grey grass running down from the ship toward them.

No city. No towers. No traffic lanes. Not a single light anywhere.

She stared at it. Her mind refused it. She'd been standing in the bottom of the Kessra Freight Terminal on Coruscant, in the morning, with twelve troopers coming at her and Master Mundi's saber in her eyes, and it had been less than a minute ago. Less than *half* a minute.

"Where are we?" Her voice came out a croak.

Harry crouched beside her. He'd crouched the same way in the bay. He looked at her, and then at the mess she'd made on the deck, and flicked two fingers at it without any particular expression, and it was gone. Just gone. The deck was clean. She felt the tiny tear, a pinprick in the quiet.

"My ship," he said.

"Where's your ship?"

He held out his hand, palm up, toward the aft corridor. A cup came flying out of it, a plain metal cup, upright and steady, and landed in his hand without spilling a drop. It was full of water. He held it out to her.

"Not on Coruscant," he said.


## VII. Residue

At that same moment, a very long way off, Ki-Adi-Mundi's grip closed on nothing.

He'd reached for them as the troopers moved. It had been the obvious thing to do and he'd done it without thinking, the way he'd have caught a falling cup: a firm, gentle hold of the Force around both of them, the girl and the man, to keep them where they were while the stun bolts did their work. He'd felt the Force gather in his hand. He'd felt it close.

And there was a sound like a whip cracked beside his ear, and the space inside his grip was empty.

The troopers fired. Four of them, maybe five; he'd given the order and their fingers had already been moving. The blue rings of the stun bolts went through the air where the man and the girl had been standing and struck the far wall of the bay, splashing across the container doors in webs of light that crackled and faded. A gust of air rushed in to fill the place where two bodies had been, stirring the smoke, spinning a little whirl of ash and grit across the deck.

Then nothing. The smoke settled. The light faded from the wall.

"*Cease fire!*" The sergeant had his rifle up and was turning, sweeping the bay. "Cease fire! Where— Spread out! *Spread out!* Check the stacks, check the lift, check the tops—"

The squad broke apart. Troopers ran for the cargo lift at the back of the bay, for the corners, for the containers on either side. Two of them went up the side of a stack with their ascension cables. Somebody was shouting into a comlink about the perimeter. Somebody else was swearing, steadily, in the flat Kamino-accented Basic they all shared.

Ki-Adi-Mundi stood very still in the middle of it, his lightsaber humming in his hand, and listened to the Force.

---

The tear was still there.

He'd felt it open. He'd been inside the edge of it, almost, with his hand around them: a rip in the Force so violent and so sudden that he'd felt it in his teeth, in the twin hemispheres of his brain, in the long hollow chambers of his skull. It had opened in the space where the man stood and the man had gone into it, and taken the girl with him, and it had closed behind them.

It was still closing. He could feel it now, a vertical seam in the air where they'd been, puckered and raw, the Force on either side of it drawing slowly together the way the lips of a wound draw together in a bacta tank. Slowly. Far too slowly. As though the Force were not sure, entirely, how it was meant to heal.

And there were others.

He turned his head and looked, though looking with his eyes was beside the point. The bay was full of them. A long ragged one, hanging in the air between the place the man had stood and the body of the Weequay. Another going up the side of the stack at a steep angle, to where the burned lip of the container still dripped molten metal. A thin one, very straight, very fine, that ran through the place where the Trandoshan had died, and another like it through the Nikto. Smaller ones, pinpricks, where the net lay in pieces.

Five wounds in the Force. Six, counting the one he'd just watched open.

He had felt the Force disturbed many times. He had felt it darken, sour, run thick with fear and anger and grief. He had felt it bend around the Sith like light around a dying star; he had stood within fifty meters of Count Dooku and felt that cold, controlled, magnificent darkness pull at everything near it. He'd read the reports of the Nightsisters' magick on Dathomir: a green, slithering, ancient wrongness, and by every account it moved *through* the Force. It drank from the dark side. It was still part of the same great current.

He had never once felt the Force *cut*.

He let his lightsaber's blade die. The silence where its hum had been seemed very loud.

"Sir." The sergeant was back, at his elbow. Brask, his name was, a senior sergeant in Commander Fox's battalion, a careful, competent man whose helmet carried a long blaster scorch across one cheek that he had never bothered to paint over. "Nothing on the stacks, sir. Nothing on the lift. Nothing on scanners. No thermal, no movement, no residual heat. They just..." He seemed to hear what he was about to say and stopped. "They just stopped being here, sir."

"I know, Sergeant."

"Sir, with respect, how is that—"

"I don't know, Sergeant."

Brask was quiet for a moment.

"Yes, sir," he said finally.

---

Mundi walked the bay.

He went slowly, the way he'd been taught as a Padawan on Cerea to walk a field after a raid, looking at everything twice. His mind divided itself as it always did, the right and left hemispheres of his brain each taking a thread and running with it, and he let them. On one side, observation. On the other, inference. He'd spent his life trusting that the two would meet somewhere in the middle.

The Weequay first. He knelt beside the body. It had been thrown four meters, perhaps five, and had come down on its back, and the chest was gone: burned open from the inside, ribs splayed outward, the edges of the wound charred and crusted. The smell was very bad. It looked as though something had ignited in the middle of the man's body and pushed outward.

"Medic," he said.

One of the troopers came over, the white-and-red of his armor marked with the medic's symbol on one shoulder, and crouched opposite him with a scanner and ran it over the body without being asked.

"No blaster residue, sir," the medic said after a moment. "No explosive compound. No accelerant. Nothing chemical I can read at all." He frowned behind his visor, or so Mundi guessed from his voice. "There's heat damage consistent with a thermal detonator at very close range. But there'd be baradium traces. There's nothing."

"Thank you."

The Trandoshan next. Mundi did not kneel. He stood over the two halves of Gorvash and looked at the line where they'd separated, which ran diagonally from the left shoulder to the right hip without a waver in it, as straight as a cut made with a surveyor's laser. It went through the flak vest. It went through the hide and the bone. On the deck beside the body lay the two pieces of the scattergun, and the same line ran through those, through the durasteel of the barrel housing, and he bent and looked at the cut face of the metal and saw it was bright and smooth as a mirror.

"Edges are open, sir," the medic said quietly, beside him. "Uncauterized. No striations." He paused. "I've never seen anything this clean. Flesh or metal."

"Nor have I," Mundi said.

The Nikto was the same. The Rodian was a matter for the droids with the scoops.

Then Tolliver.

He'd recognized the man, in the moment of coming into the bay; Rask Tolliver, independent bounty hunter, with a long sheet in the Coruscant Security files and a longer one on Corellia. He was lying on his side near the dead-end wall with his face toward the ceiling and the rest of him toward the floor. The medic ran his scanner over him and was quiet for longer this time.

"Cervical spine's broken, sir," he said. "C1 and C2. Rotational. Clean break." He moved the scanner up and down. "No bruising on the neck. No pressure marks. No grip marks. Nobody had their hands on him."

"No," Mundi agreed.

"You think it was the Force, sir?"

Mundi didn't answer. He knelt down beside Tolliver and laid his hand flat on the deck a few centimeters from the dead man's head, and closed his eyes, and reached.

Death left marks on the Force. Every Jedi knew that. Every death was a small ripple, a brief turning of the current as the living Force released one being into the cosmic, and a violent death left a deeper mark, a bruise, an echo that lingered sometimes for hours, sometimes for years. He had walked battlefields where the echoes were so thick he'd had to shield himself to think. He'd walked into rooms where someone had been strangled with the dark side and felt the hand of it still there in the air, as clear as a fingerprint.

He reached into the place where Rask Tolliver had died, not ten minutes ago, violently, in terror, and found—

The ordinary ripple of a life ending. That was there. Faint and clean and fading, like any death.

And nothing else.

No echo of a hand. No trace of a reaching, dark or light. No bruise where the violence had been done. The Force around Tolliver's broken neck was perfectly calm, perfectly smooth, as if the man had simply lain down here and died of old age.

Mundi stayed there on one knee for a long time with his hand on the deck.

On the left side of his mind, very calmly: *A being has been killed with a power that touched the Force at a distance in a manner identical to the Force's own use. That power left no echo in the Force. Therefore it did not pass through the Force. Therefore it was not the Force.*

On the right, just as calmly: *There is nothing that is not the Force. The Force is in all living things and binds the galaxy together. There is nothing outside it.*

The two thoughts met in the middle, as they always did, and for the first time he could remember, they did not fit.

---

He stood. His knees ached. He was suddenly very conscious of his age.

The troopers had finished their sweep and were standing in twos and threes around the edges of the bay, waiting for orders, talking low on their helmet channels the way they did when they thought officers weren't listening. The Force carried emotion whether he wished it to or not, and from the cluster nearest him came a particular kind of tight, suppressed hilarity that he recognized, after a moment, with a cold sinking feeling, from a great many years of watching young men in barracks.

Second from the left. The trooper who'd laughed. His shoulders were shaking again.

"*Hollis,*" Sergeant Brask said, very low and very hard, without turning his head.

"Sorry, Sarge."

"You will be."

Mundi looked away. His eyes fell on the side of the container nearest him, where the scorch from a stun bolt had left a patch of blackened metal, and the metal around the scorch had been polished to a dull sheen by years of loader droids scraping past. In it, faintly, he could see a reflection: a tall figure in brown robes, white-bearded, the long pale dome of his skull rising above the face.

He did not look at it. He was a Master of the Jedi Order and a member of the High Council, and he had more serious things to consider.

He looked at it for perhaps one second.

Then he turned his back on it and unclipped his comlink from his belt.

---

Master Windu answered on the first chime. His face flickered up out of the comlink, small and blue and grim, the lines around his mouth very deep.

"Report."

"We located Tano at the Kessra freight terminal, as the informant said." Mundi kept his voice level. "She'd been taken by a group of bounty hunters. Independents. By the time we arrived, they were dead."

Windu's eyes narrowed. "She killed them?"

"No. A human male killed them. He identified himself only as Harry. Guild affiliation. He killed five beings in the space of perhaps two minutes. When I attempted to take them both into custody, he—" Mundi stopped.

"He what?"

"He took her."

"Took her where?"

"I don't know."

There was a short silence on the line. Windu did not like silences that he hadn't put there himself, and Mundi watched him decide whether to fill this one.

"Ki-Adi. Took her *where*?"

"I don't know, Mace. They were standing in front of me, within the reach of my hand. I had them both held in the Force. There was a sound, and they were gone, and the Force was—" He searched for a word. He was conscious of the troopers nearby, and lowered his voice. "Torn. Literally. It has been torn open, in several places, around the bodies, and where he vanished. I can feel the wounds. They are still closing."

Windu's holographic face did not move. But his eyes did.

"Sith?"

"I don't believe so."

"Nightsisters?"

"I considered it. No. Their magick draws on the dark side. It moves through the Force. This..." Mundi breathed out. "This does not move through the Force at all, as far as I can tell. I could not read him, Mace. I reached for him and I could not find him. The Force went to him and around him and stopped at the edge. As though he were—" He stopped again.

"Were what?"

He had been going to say *dead*.

That was the thing that had been sitting in the back of his mind since he'd first stepped into the bay and turned his attention on the man and felt it slide off him like water off glass. He'd felt something like it before. Decades ago. In the Halls of Healing, sitting beside a dying friend in the last hour, when the old Cerean had finally let go and Mundi had felt the Force around the bed go still, at the very instant of passing. A pause. A threshold. A great silence holding its breath at the edge of a door.

The man had felt like that. Standing there in his old leather jacket with his hands at his sides, with his ridiculous remark, with his strange accent. He had felt like the moment of death.

"Not alive in the way the Force expects," Mundi said finally, which was true and which explained nothing, and he saw Windu register both of those things.

"And Tano?"

"That is the other matter." Mundi turned, and looked at the seam in the air where they'd vanished. It was narrower now, a little. Still raw. "I had her presence in my awareness when they went. I was holding it, and it was cut off, Mace. Between one breath and the next. I have been reaching for her since. I cannot find her anywhere in the city. I do not believe she is on Coruscant."

"That's not possible. Every port in the sector is locked down. Every lane has a Guard checkpoint. Nothing's left atmosphere in the last hour that we haven't scanned."

"I'm aware."

"Is she dead?"

Mundi closed his eyes.

"I don't believe so," he said. "I believe I would have felt it."

The silence on the line was longer this time.

"Come back to the Temple," Windu said. "I'll convene the Council. Master Yoda will want to hear this from you directly." He paused, and some of the iron went out of his face, not much. "Skywalker's on his way to you. He was searching the sector north of you. I couldn't stop him."

"I would not have tried."

"No." Windu's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "Ki-Adi. You're sure? About all of it?"

"I am sure about what I saw," said Mundi. "And about what I felt. I am sure of nothing else at all."

The hologram went dark.

---

Skywalker came in fast, the way he did everything, on a borrowed Coruscant Security speeder bike that he brought down into the canyon between the stacks at a speed that made two of the troopers jump back, and he was off the bike and running before it had fully stopped. He came into the bay at a dead run and pulled up short at the sight of it, and Mundi watched the bodies go into him one by one exactly as they'd gone into Mundi himself. The Weequay. The Trandoshan. Tolliver.

Skywalker's face went grey.

"Where is she?" He came across the bay toward Mundi in four long strides. "Master Mundi. Where's Ahsoka? They said you had her. They said you had her *pinned down*—"

"She is not here."

"Then where—"

"I don't know."

Skywalker stared at him. Mundi had always found him difficult to read; there was so much in him, all of it so close to the surface and all of it moving at once, and the young man's emotions came off him in the Force in waves so strong that being near him in a crisis was like standing too close to a fire. Fear came off him now. Raw, enormous fear.

"Did she do this?"

"No."

Some of the grey went out of Skywalker's face, not all.

"Then who—"

"A bounty hunter. A human. He called himself Harry." Mundi paused. "He took her with him."

"Took her? Took her where? How? Which way did they go? Did anybody get a—"

"They vanished, Skywalker. In front of me."

Skywalker shut his mouth.

Then he shut his eyes. Mundi felt him reach. It was characteristically clumsy, characteristically enormous: Skywalker reached out into the Force the way another man might throw open every window in a house at once, sending his awareness rushing out across the whole of the city in every direction, searching. Mundi felt it sweep through him and past him. He felt it go out and out, through the stacks and the Works and up through the levels, into the upper city, across the districts. He felt it hunt.

He knew what it would find, because he'd already looked.

Skywalker opened his eyes. They were wide.

"I can't feel her," he said. His voice had gone quiet. "Master Mundi. I can't *feel* her. I've always— Since she was fourteen, I've always known where she was, roughly. Even on the other side of a battlefield. Even when she was taken on Wasskah, I could feel her, a little, like she was very far away. I can't feel her at all."

"No."

"Is she—"

"I do not believe she is dead." Mundi heard himself say it for the second time in ten minutes and found it sounded no more convincing than it had the first time. "I believe I would have felt that. I believe *you* would have."

Skywalker looked at him. Then at the seam in the air, the place where Mundi had been standing when he'd grasped the empty air. He went over to it slowly and stood in front of it, and Mundi saw him feel it, the raw edges of it, the slow, uncertain closing, and saw him flinch.

"What *is* that?"

"I don't know."

"What *was* he?"

"I don't know that either."

Skywalker turned back to him. The fear in his face had hardened into something else. Mundi had seen that hardening before, in this young man, and had never once liked where it went.

"Then I'll find out," said Skywalker. "He's Guild. The Guild keeps records. I'll find out who he is and where he's taken her and I'll get her back."

"Skywalker."

"What?"

Mundi looked at him for a long moment. There was a great deal he might have said. That the Council would decide how to proceed. That Skywalker had let his attachment to his Padawan cloud his judgment from the beginning. That a man who could kill five armed beings in two minutes without leaving any mark on the Force, and step from one side of the galaxy to the other with a Padawan under his hand, was not a man to be found by kicking in the doors of Guild offices.

He found that he did not have it in him to say any of it.

"Be careful," he said instead.

Skywalker stared at him, as if he'd expected an argument and didn't know what to do without one. Then he nodded once, sharply, and turned and went back toward his speeder bike at a run.

Mundi stayed where he was.

Around him the bay went on with its business: the troopers cordoning off the canyon, the medic recording images of each body, the clean-up droids rolling in on their treads. Somebody had found a fire suppressor and was spraying foam onto the burning lip of the container high above. Foam pattered down onto the deck like snow. Sergeant Brask was speaking quietly and at length to Trooper Hollis, by the lift, in a tone that suggested Hollis would be spending the coming week in the waste reclamation sector.

The seam in the air where the man had stood was almost closed now. Almost. Mundi went back and stood in front of it, and after a moment he lifted his hand, slowly, and put his fingers into the place where it had been.

It was cold there. A small, deep cold, like the air over still water at night.

He stood with his hand in the cold for a long time. He thought about the man's eyes, which had been very green, and had looked at him across the bay, before the remark, with a calm and slightly bored attention that Mundi now realized had not, at any point, contained the slightest concern about twelve rifles and a Jedi Master. He thought about the Force sliding off him. He thought about Rask Tolliver's neck, and the perfect calm around it. He thought about a door, and a threshold, and a silence holding its breath.

For most of his long life, Ki-Adi-Mundi had believed that the galaxy, however dark it became, could be understood. That the Force was the sum of all things, and that a patient and disciplined mind, given time, could know any part of it.

He took his hand back out of the cold and looked at his fingers.

They were trembling. Very slightly. He watched them until they stopped, and then he turned and went to find a transport back to the Temple, and he did not once in all that long ride look at his reflection in the viewport.


## VIII. Somewhere Quiet

She didn't take the cup.

She stayed where she was, on her hands and knees on the deck plating, and looked at it, and at the hand holding it, and at him. He didn't push it on her. He set it down on the deck between them, about halfway, and straightened up and stepped back, and went and sat down on the far end of the padded bench under the viewport with his elbows on his knees, as if he had all the time in the world.

Behind a closed door in the aft corridor, the animal whined. A long, thin, rising note. Claws scratched once on metal.

"*Wait,*" Harry said, without raising his voice. The whine stopped.

Ahsoka got up.

It took two tries. Her right leg was still unreliable from the stun bolt, and her head swam as soon as it came up off the deck, and her montrals were still throbbing in long slow waves from whatever he'd done to them. She put out a hand and found the bulkhead and leaned on it until the room stopped tilting. Then she stood with her back to the wall and looked around properly.

Two ways out of the room. The aft corridor, with the closed door and the animal behind it, and beyond that, she guessed, the engines and the boarding ramp. The forward corridor, short, ending in a hatch that stood half-open on darkness and the faint glow of instrument panels. A cockpit. A ladder set into the wall beside the forward corridor going up, probably to a turret or a top hatch. The bench, the table. A wall locker. A rack of tools.

Nothing she could use as a weapon closer than the tool rack, which was three meters away and on his side of the room.

She looked at the cup.

She was so thirsty. She hadn't noticed until she saw the water, and now it was the only thing in her body, a dry burning in her throat and the back of her mouth, worse for the sourness of being sick. She'd been in the outflow. She'd swallowed some of it. She hadn't had a drink since the base.

She bent, slowly, keeping her eyes on him, and picked it up.

It was cold. Beads of condensation stood on the outside of it. She lifted it to her nose and smelled it, and it smelled of nothing, the clean, flat, metallic nothing of ship's water. She reached into it with the Force, which was absurd—you couldn't feel poison in the Force, it didn't work like that—and felt only water.

He was watching her do it. He didn't say anything.

She drank it all in one go. Then she stood there with the empty cup in her hand, breathing hard, and he lifted one finger off his knee, and the cup was full again. Just full. The water was simply there, cold and clear, to the brim. She felt it happen, the pinprick in the Force like a needle going through cloth.

She stared at the cup. Then at him.

She drank that too.

---

"Where is this?" she said when she could talk.

"Told you. My ship."

"Where is your *ship*?"

"Somewhere quiet."

"That's not an answer."

"Ja," he said. "It is."

She pushed off the wall and went forward, toward the cockpit, keeping as far from him along the room as she could, and he didn't move to stop her. He didn't even turn his head. She went through the half-open hatch into the dark and the instrument glow and stood there for a second while her eyes adjusted.

It was a small cockpit, two seats, old. The upholstery on the pilot's chair had been patched with tape. The consoles were a mix of three different manufacturers, Corellian mostly, with additions bolted in where something had died and been replaced with whatever was to hand. Through the canopy, the same blue-green sky. The same two moons. The same immense dark trees, closer now, and below them the long silver-grey slope of grass going down to a line of black water she hadn't seen from the common room, a lake or a wide slow river, with the moons in it.

She found the navicomputer and woke it.

It took her a minute. The interface was customized in ways she didn't follow, and there were displays she didn't recognize at all, a cluster of small dials on the right side of the board whose readouts were written in a script she'd never seen. But a navicomputer was a navicomputer. She called up current position.

The system had no name. The nav listed it by a survey catalogue designation, a string of letters and numbers, nothing else. No population. No traffic control. No listed settlements. Mid Rim. She pulled the galactic map up around it and zoomed out and out until she could see where she was.

She stood there and looked at the distance between the little blinking marker and Coruscant for a long time.

It was a long way. She could do the arithmetic. With a good hyperdrive, and good lanes, and nothing going wrong, it was the better part of two days' travel. With the routes and lane-jumps she'd have to take to get there and back, more.

It had taken them less than ten seconds.

She put both hands flat on the console and leaned on them and closed her eyes.

*It isn't possible,* she thought, very clearly. *Nothing can do that. Nothing in the galaxy can do that.*

The trees swayed outside the canopy. A wind she couldn't feel moved through the grass in long silver ripples. The quiet pressed in on her from all sides, the soft green living hush of a world almost nobody had ever stood on, and somewhere very, very far off on the other side of all of that quiet, on the other side of a distance it would take her two days to cross, Master Mundi was standing in a bloody loading bay with his saber in his hand, staring at the place where she'd been.

She opened her eyes and looked at the comm panel.

It was a standard unit. Old, a little battered. Long-range transceiver, HoloNet relay access, encryption module. She could tell from the status lights that it was live.

She could call the Temple. She could call her master's personal frequency, which she'd had since she was fourteen and knew the way she knew her own name. She could tell him where she was. He'd come. He'd come in the *Twilight*, with Rex, as fast as the hyperdrive would carry them, and he'd—

He'd take her back.

He'd do it gently. He'd argue for her. He'd stand in front of the Council and in front of Tarkin and shout. And none of it would matter, because when he was done shouting she'd still be back in the white cell, and somebody would still have killed Letta Turmond with a power that only a Force-user could have, while the only Force-user in the room was Ahsoka, and the evidence would still be speaking for itself.

Her hand was resting on the comm panel. She didn't remember putting it there.

"Comm works," said Harry's voice behind her.

She spun. He was standing in the hatchway, one shoulder against the frame, not quite inside the cockpit. His eyes were on the lake beyond the canopy.

"Call who you want," he said.

"What?"

"Anybody you like. It's live. It's encrypted, but whoever's on the other end'll be able to trace it if they're any good." He shrugged one shoulder. "Up to you."

She stared at him.

"You'd let me call the Jedi Temple," she said. "From your ship. And tell them exactly where we are."

"Ja."

"They'd come here."

"Probably."

"With an army."

He seemed to think about that.

"Probably," he agreed, and went on looking at the lake.

She looked down at her hand on the comm. She didn't know how long she stood there. Long enough for the light outside the canopy to change, for the gold moon to climb a little higher above the trees. He didn't move. He didn't say anything else.

She took her hand off the comm.

---

When she came back into the common room he'd moved to the galley end of it, by the aft corridor, and had his back to her. He was doing something at a counter she hadn't seen before, a fold-down shelf by the wall with a cold-box under it. She heard a lid open. Then the smell hit her: meat, raw, and something cooked and starchy, and her stomach turned over twice, once in hunger and once in nausea, so hard that she had to stop and put a hand on the table.

She sat down on the bench. She hadn't meant to. Her legs did it.

She sat on the end nearest the cockpit, with the corridor at her back, and watched him.

He was very big. She noticed that again, now that the adrenaline was going out of her and she could see him without the bay full of bodies around him. He had to duck his head a little under the conduits in the low ceiling. His shoulders took up most of the width of the narrow galley space. He moved around in it as if he'd done it ten thousand times, reaching for things without looking, his hands going straight to them. The leather jacket was old and scuffed at the elbows, and the sleeves were pushed up a little, and his forearms were bare and smooth and unmarked.

Five people. She'd watched him kill five people. She kept coming back to it. She'd been at war since she was fourteen; she'd seen more death than most beings saw in a lifetime, and she'd caused her share of it, droids by the hundred and organics too, when there'd been no choice. She knew what it looked like when someone killed in a rage, and in fear, and in cold blood. She'd been trying, since the bay, to fit what she'd seen into one of those.

She couldn't make it fit. He'd done it the way he was getting food out of the cold-box now. Without hurry, without fuss, without any sign at all that it had cost him anything.

And then he'd turned around and told Ki-Adi-Mundi that his head looked like—

She put her face in her hands. Her face was hot. A sound came out of her that she didn't recognize, high and strangled, and she realized with horror that it was the beginning of a laugh. She choked it off. She sat with her face in her hands and her shoulders shaking, and she wasn't sure, for a moment, whether she was going to laugh or cry or be sick again, and in the end she didn't do any of them. She sat very still and breathed until it passed.

When she looked up, he'd half turned and was looking at her with something in his hand, a package of something wrapped in flimsi.

"You okay?"

"*No.*"

"Fair."

He turned back to the counter.

---

"Why are you doing this?"

It came out of her flatter and harder than she'd meant. She didn't care. She sat at the end of the bench and stared at the back of his head.

He didn't stop what he was doing. He'd taken the package out of the flimsi, a slab of red meat, and was cutting it up on a board with a long knife, and she watched the blade go down, down, down through the meat in neat even strokes.

"Why help me?" she said. "You're Guild. You're a bounty hunter. That reward's enough to buy a ship better than this one. You could have let Tolliver have me and gone back to your breakfast. You could have taken me in yourself. You could have taken me in *now*, you could have flown me straight to Coruscant, or, or done whatever that was, and handed me over and been rich. So why? What do you want?"

He finished the cut he was making. Put the knife down. Turned round and leaned back against the counter with his arms folded and looked at her.

"You didn't do it," he said.

"How do you *know* that?"

"I know."

"Because you went in my head."

"Partly."

"And the rest?"

He tapped the side of his nose, once, with one finger, and didn't say anything else.

She stared at him. She didn't know what that meant. She was fairly sure she wasn't going to find out.

"That's it?" she said. "I'm innocent, so you killed five people and kidnapped me across half the galaxy?"

"You're innocent," he said, "and you're a kid, and the whole government's hunting you. The army. The Navy. Your own lot. Every bounty hunter on Coruscant with a blaster and an overdraft." He shrugged. "I'm not going to stand there and watch a kid get run down by a government for something she didn't do. Not when I can do something about it."

He paused.

"Plus," he said, "I've been looking for shit to do."

She opened her mouth. Then closed it.

"I'm not a kid," she said finally.

"Okay."

"I'm not. I've been a commander in the Grand Army of the Republic since I was fourteen. I've led troops. I've fought at Christophsis and Ryloth and Geonosis and Mon Cala and a dozen other places you've probably never—"

"Okay," he said again, and she heard that it meant exactly the same thing as the first time, and that nothing she was going to say would change it, and she shut her mouth and glared.

He looked back at her without much expression. Then he turned round, picked up the knife, and went on cutting.

---

"What are you?" she asked, a while later.

"Hungry."

"That's not—" She stopped herself. Breathed. "What did you *do* back there?"

"Got you out."

"With what? How? What was that thing you had, that stick, how did you hit a *blaster bolt* with it? What did you do to those men? How did you break Tolliver's neck without— What did you do to the *Force*? You tore it. Do you know that? Every time you did something you tore it, I *felt* it, and Master Mundi felt it too, I saw his face. And then you just— and we— and now we're—" She waved a hand at the viewport, the trees, the two moons. Her voice was rising and she couldn't stop it. "We were on Coruscant! We were on *Coruscant* ten minutes ago!"

He'd stopped cutting. He was looking at her over his shoulder.

"Ja," he said.

She waited.

He didn't say anything else.

"That's it?" she said. "*Ja?*"

He seemed to think about it. Then he nodded, once, and went back to the meat.

She sat there with her mouth open.

"What does Fido mean?" she demanded.

"Hm?"

"Fido. You said Fido. Right before. What does it mean? Is it a word? Is it a command? Is it some kind of code, is it—"

He shrugged.

She waited again. She waited a long time.

Nothing.

"You," she said, "are the most irritating being I have ever met in my entire life, and I have met *Hondo Ohnaka*."

He didn't seem to know who that was. He also didn't seem bothered.

"And you were in my *head*."

"Top of it."

"Don't do it again."

"Okay."

"I mean it. If you ever go in my head again without asking, I'll—" She stopped, because she couldn't think of anything she could do to him that he'd care about, and he seemed to understand that too, because he turned around and looked at her properly for the first time in a while.

"I won't," he said. "Without asking."

It was said plainly, the way he said everything. She looked at him for a long moment, searching his face for the lie, and didn't find one; not that she'd have known how to find one in that cold, still pool he carried around with him.

"Fine," she said.

---

She was shivering.

She noticed it at the same time she noticed him noticing it. The coverall was still damp in places from the outflow and the laundry cleanser in it had started to smell sharp and chemical as her body heated it, and under that, now that she was sitting still in a small closed room, she could smell the sewer. On herself. In the folds of the cloth and in her skin. It was disgusting. She'd been living inside it for so many hours she'd stopped being able to tell.

He looked her over the way she'd seen quartermasters look over a trooper who'd come back from a bad patrol, totting up what needed replacing.

"Those smell like a sewer," he said.

"They *were* in a sewer. I told you."

"Ja, I could tell." He looked at the coverall a moment longer, as if measuring it. Then he looked at the bench beside her and twitched two fingers of his left hand.

There were clothes on the bench.

They hadn't been there and then they were. No flash, no sound. She felt it, the little tearing pinprick in the Force, and when she turned her head they were sitting on the padding beside her hip in a neat folded stack. Dark trousers, close-woven, in a charcoal so deep it was almost black. A long-sleeved tunic of some soft dense fabric the deep red-brown of rust. A short, fitted jacket over that, dark grey, with a high collar and a deep hood that had been cut, she saw when she reached out and touched it with one finger, in a shape that would sit around and over montrals instead of crushing them. Underthings. Thick socks. On the deck beside the bench, a pair of soft, close-fitting boots, dark brown, the leather supple and new.

She picked up the tunic. It was real. Heavy in her hand, faintly heated, as though it had been lying in the sun, the weave tight and soft. It smelled of nothing at all, not even of newness.

She looked at the boots. Then she picked one up and turned it over and looked at the sole, and then held it against the bottom of her own foot, without quite knowing why.

It was exactly her size.

"How did you—" she started.

"Fresher's second door on the left." He'd turned back to the counter and was scraping the cut meat into a bowl. "Lock's on the inside. There's hot water. There's a medkit under the sink; clean that jaw."

"Harry."

"Hm."

"*How did you know my size?*"

He picked up the bowl. He seemed to consider the question for a moment, with the air of a man who'd been asked something reasonable that he had no intention of answering.

"Need to feed the dog," he said.

"The *what*?"

But he was already going, the bowl in one hand, down the aft corridor toward the closed door. She heard him say something low, and heard the door slide open, and there was a scrabble of claws on metal and the strange high hoarse cry again, *rah, rah,* right on top of him, and his voice saying, "Ja, ja, I know. I know. Sit. *Sit.* Good girl." And then the door slid shut and the sounds went muffled.

Ahsoka sat on the bench in her stinking coverall, holding a boot that fit her exactly, and stared at the closed door.

*Dog,* she thought.

She didn't know what a dog was.


## IX. Hot Water

The fresher was the second door on the left, exactly where he'd said.

It was tiny, the way ship freshers always were: a sink, a toilet, a mirror, a shower stall barely big enough to turn around in, everything in brushed grey metal with a drain in the middle of the floor. It was also clean. Very clean. There was a stack of thick towels on a shelf over the toilet and a bar of something on the sink that smelled faintly of resin. On a hook on the back of the door hung a man's dark shirt, big enough to make a tent.

She locked the door. Then she stood with her back against it and her eyes shut and the folded stack of clothes held to her chest with both arms, and just breathed for a while.

It was the first time she'd been alone since they'd come for her in the Temple.

That had been — she tried to count it and couldn't. Days. It felt like weeks. Since the hangar went up and the dust came down and she'd run through the smoke with Master Skywalker toward the screams. Since the investigation and the nano-droids and Jackar Bowmani's widow. Since the cell, and Letta, and the guards, and the corridors, and the pipe, and the cold black water. Every hour since then someone had been with her or after her or looking at her face on a holo-board. Even in the underlevels, in the dark, she'd felt them.

Here there was nobody. Just the hum of the ship, and her own breath, and through the bulkhead, very faintly, the sound of a man's voice saying something low and patient, and a strange soft scrabble of claws.

She put the clothes down on the closed lid of the toilet and peeled the coverall off.

---

It was worse than she'd thought.

The net had left a lattice of welts across her shoulders and down both arms, narrow raised lines of angry red-violet in a diamond pattern where the cords had pressed in and the charge had gone through. Her right side, from the bottom of her ribs to her hip, was one huge dark bruise where the stun bolt had taken her, mottled and tender. Her knees were scraped raw from the outflow grate. Three parallel cuts ran along the line of her jaw on the left side, where Gorvash's claws had held her; they'd stopped bleeding but the edges were puffy and hot. Her left montral had a long shallow scrape near the tip. Her palms were cut. There was dried blood in the creases of her knuckles and black grime under every nail.

She looked at herself in the mirror for a long moment and didn't recognize anything about the girl in it except the markings.

Then she got into the shower and turned the water on, all the way hot, and stood under it.

It was real water, not a sonic. It came down on her head and her montrals and her shoulders in a heavy, steaming stream, and it hurt, on the welts and the scrapes, and she didn't care. She stood with her head down and her palms flat against the wall and watched it run off her into the drain, grey at first, then brown, then grey again, and finally, after a long time, clear.

She used the bar of resin-smelling soap on every part of herself she could reach. Twice. She scrubbed under her nails with her own claws until they were clean. She stood with her lekku held forward over her shoulders under the stream and let the water run down them, the way she'd done as a youngling when she'd come in muddy from the training gardens and Master Plo had sent her off to wash with a look of grave, patient long-suffering that had made her laugh.

And then, standing there, without any warning at all, she started to cry.

It came up out of her like being sick had come up, all at once and without permission, a great heaving wrenching thing that bent her over in the stall with her forehead against the wall. She didn't try to stop it. She couldn't have. She cried for Letta, who'd been terrified and stubborn and had wanted to tell her something before somebody closed an invisible hand around her throat. She cried for the dead in the Temple hangar, the Jedi and the clones and the workers she'd never known. She cried for the five men in the loading bay, which made no sense, because they'd been going to sell her, but she'd watched them come apart in pieces and she cried for them anyway. She cried for her master's hand reaching out to her on the edge of the pipe, and for the fact that she hadn't taken it, and for the fact that she still didn't know whether she'd been right.

She cried because she was seventeen and she'd done everything they'd ever asked her and they'd let a Navy admiral take her anyway.

It went on for a long time. Then it stopped.

She stayed bent over in the stall with her forehead against the cool metal and the water running down her back, emptied out, breathing slowly. After a while she straightened up. She turned the water off. She stood in the steam and listened to the ship.

Through the bulkhead, the low voice had stopped. The clicking claws had stopped. The ship hummed. Far off, outside the hull, the wind went through the trees.

*All right,* she thought. *All right. What now?*

She didn't have an answer. But she found, standing there in the steam, that she could ask the question without her chest closing up. That was something.

---

The medkit was under the sink, as promised. It was a good one, better than the standard issue in most civilian ships: a full roll of bacta strips, antiseptic, stim-shots, a field cauterizer, a skin-sealer, a small vial of something labeled in that same unfamiliar script she'd seen on the cockpit dials, which she didn't touch. She cleaned the cuts along her jaw with antiseptic, hissing through her teeth, and laid a bacta strip along them. She did the scrape on her montral, the worst of the welts, her palms, her knees. By the time she'd finished, the bacta had begun to bite, a cool, faintly itching numbness, and some of the ache had gone out of her.

Then she dressed.

Everything fit. She'd known it would, after the boot, but she still stood there in front of the mirror and checked each piece as she put it on, the way she might have checked a strange weapon for traps. The trousers sat at her waist and fell straight to her ankles without bunching. The tunic's sleeves stopped exactly at her wrists. The fabric was soft against the welts. The socks were thick. The boots went on as if they'd been made on her feet and broken in for a month.

She put on the jacket last. She lifted the hood and settled it over her head, and it went around her montrals. It had been shaped for them, the deep cowl of it curving up and back to make room, and it sat there, not pressing anywhere, not pulling, the folds falling in a way that disguised the shape of them almost completely. From the front, in shadow, she might have been anyone. A human woman. A Zeltron. A Mirialan. Anyone at all.

She stood and looked at the stranger in the mirror for a long time.

The stranger had blue eyes and orange skin and white markings on her face, and a fresh bacta strip along one side of her jaw, and nothing whatsoever about her that said *Jedi*. Except, when she pushed back the hood again: the beads. Her padawan beads, hanging down along her right lek, damp from the shower.

She lifted her hand and touched them.

She thought about Master Mundi's face in the bay. *You will come with us. Now, please.* The flatness in it. The distance. She thought about the Council chamber, the ring of chairs, the faces turned toward her the day they'd let Tarkin take her, and her master standing at her side and saying, over and over, that she was innocent, and nobody listening.

She thought about taking the beads off.

She didn't. She let her hand fall and pulled the hood back up over them, and turned away from the mirror, and unlocked the door.

---

Harry was sitting on the deck of the aft hold with his back against a crate and his legs stretched out in front of him, watching Luna eat.

He'd fed her in the same place he'd always fed her, in the corner by the ramp housing where her blanket and her bowls lived, and she was doing what she always did with dinner, which was inhale it. Nerf, raw, cut into chunks, a scoop of grain he'd cooked and cooled that morning, a raw egg from the market on Dorvalla broken over the top. He'd made her sit before he put it down. She'd sat. He'd made her wait. She'd waited, trembling all over from nose to tail, staring at him with her mismatched eyes, one blue and one brown, with an intensity that would have burned a hole through durasteel. He'd made her wait four seconds, because she'd been good, and said *okay*, and now she was on her third pass around the bowl, chasing the last fragment of egg yolk into the corner with her tongue.

The bowl scraped across the decking. She followed it.

He watched her and thought about not very much.

He thought about the Guild. Thrennik would have the news by now. Five dead in Bay Forty-Four East, one of them Tolliver, and a big human with a Guild patch and an accent nobody could place walking off with the girl in front of a Jedi. Thrennik wasn't stupid. There weren't many men on Thrennik's books who'd fit that, and only one who'd been in his office that morning and turned the posting down. The Guild's view of independents like Tolliver was low, and nobody at the Guild would cry for them. Its view of members who took a Republic bounty and then ran off with it was something he'd find out about later.

He thought about the Jedi with the head.

He'd seen the man's face, in the second before he'd taken the girl out. Calm. Grave. Not a muscle out of place. And the blade of that saber twitching just slightly in his hand. Harry had felt him reaching, too, earlier, a pressure like fingers pressing on a window from the other side, and felt it slide off. He'd felt the Jedi feel it slide off.

Probably the remark hadn't been necessary.

He'd enjoyed it, though.

He thought about the girl standing in his cockpit with her hand on the comm. She'd stood there a long time. He'd meant it; if she'd made the call, he'd have dealt with whatever turned up. But he'd been glad when she took her hand back. He'd been gladder than he'd expected.

Luna finished. She licked the bowl one final time, very thoroughly, all the way round the rim, to make sure. Then she licked her own nose. Then she came over and lay down beside his leg with a long, gusty sigh and put her chin on his knee and looked up at him with the brown eye and the blue one.

He scratched the base of her ear. She leaned into it.

She was a border collie, mostly. Black and white, with a white blaze down her face and a white chest and four white feet, as if she'd stepped in paint, and a long plumed tail with a white tip. Medium-sized, light-boned, built for running. Her coat was thick and soft on her chest and shorter on her face, and she shed on everything he owned. She had been with him six years.

Around her neck sat the collar. Plain dark leather, well worn, with a small silver tag on the ring at the front. He'd made it the first winter he'd had her, after she'd gone off the end of a loading ramp on Ord Mantell chasing a scurrier and landed wrong. There were runes worked into the inside of the leather, small and close, where they didn't show. Nothing that hit her got through it. Nothing ever had.

He ran his thumb along the edge of the leather, out of habit, checking. Still sound. It always was.

Down the corridor, the fresher door hissed open.

Luna's head came up off his knee.

Her ears went up. Both of them, high and sharp, swiveling forward toward the sound. Her nostrils worked. He watched her take in the smell, the new one, the stranger: soap and bacta and Togruta and everything under them. He watched the whole of her go still and intent and focused, the way she went when a shaak came over the brow of a hill on Dorvalla, or a scurrier in a cargo bay, or, once, memorably, a Hutt.

She got up very slowly, low to the ground, one paw at a time.

"Luna," Harry said.

Her eyes stayed on the corridor.

He sighed, and got up, and followed her.


## X. Luna

The thing was in the corridor.

Ahsoka stopped in the fresher doorway with one hand still on the frame and looked at it, and it looked back at her.

It was about the size of a young nexu, but built nothing like one. Four legs, long and fine-boned. A narrow head with a long tapering muzzle and a black nose at the end of it, wet and shining. Two ears, triangular, that stood straight up from its skull and then folded over at the very tips. A tail, long, plumed, held low behind it. And fur. Fur all over it, thick and soft-looking, black across the back and the head and white in a broad stripe down the middle of its face, white across its chest and down its legs, as if it had walked through a puddle of paint.

It was crouched. Low to the deck, its belly almost touching the plating, its front legs bent and its weight forward, utterly still. Its head was level with its shoulders. Its eyes were fixed on her.

One of them was brown. The other was a pale, startling, icy blue.

She'd faced the Zillo Beast. She'd faced Ventress. She'd stood in front of Cad Bane with her lightsaber lit and not looked away. None of them had ever looked at her like this.

She reached out to it in the Force, carefully, the way she'd reach for any strange animal, and found focus. Total, consuming, burning focus, a bright fierce narrow beam of attention pointed straight at her as if she were the only thing in the galaxy that had ever existed or ever would. It had a great deal of energy, all of it coiled up and held. And under the focus, something else, quick and light and bright as sunlight, that she couldn't immediately name and that kept trying to bubble up through the stillness and being pushed back down.

Behind it, in the corridor, Harry stood with his hands in his pockets.

"What," Ahsoka said carefully, without moving, "is that?"

"Luna."

"What *is* Luna?"

"Dog."

"What's a dog?"

Harry looked down at the creature for a moment, as if considering the question. Then he looked back up at Ahsoka.

"That," he said.

She stared at him. The creature stared at her. Nobody moved.

"Is that Fido?" she asked.

Something happened at the corner of Harry's mouth, very briefly.

"No," he said. "That's Luna."

---

The dog moved.

It came forward a step, still low, still crouched, placing each paw down slowly and deliberately on the deck plating with a faint click of claws. Then another step. Its eyes never left her face. Ahsoka stayed exactly where she was in the doorway and felt her own body going still to match it, the way she'd go still in front of a predator, every muscle locking.

"Harry," she said, out of the side of her mouth.

"Hm."

"Is it going to bite me?"

"No."

"Are you sure?"

"Ja."

The dog came another step, and then another, and then it was right in front of her, and it stretched its long neck out toward her and smelled her. It smelled her boots first, very thoroughly, both of them, its nose working in quick, short, snuffling bursts. Then her knees. Then her hand, hanging at her side, which she held perfectly still while the wet cold nose went over the back of her knuckles and between her fingers and across the bacta strip on her palm. It smelled the bacta for a long time. Then, without any warning at all, it licked her.

She jumped. She couldn't help it. The tongue was hot and wet and rough and very quick, one long swipe across the back of her hand, and she jerked the hand up and held it against her chest and stared down at the dog.

The dog stared back up at her. Its tail, which had been held low and still behind it, moved. Just once. One slow sweep, side to side.

The bright, quick thing she'd felt under its focus bubbled up a little higher.

"It licked me," Ahsoka said.

"She does that."

"*Why?*"

Harry seemed to consider that too. "Likes you, probably."

"It doesn't even know me."

"Doesn't take her long."

The dog had begun to move again. It went past her, slowly, sideways, along the wall of the corridor, its eyes on her the whole time, and she turned her head to follow it, and it kept going. It went around behind her. She felt it there, at her back, between her and the fresher, and she turned to face it and it moved again, very smoothly, keeping just at the edge of her vision. It was circling. It went all the way round her once, in the tight space of the corridor, and ended up on her left side, slightly behind, and stopped there, crouched, watching.

She took a step away from it, to the right.

It moved too. Quickly this time, a short darting rush, and was suddenly on her right side, slightly behind, crouched again, and she found that without meaning to she'd stepped forward instead, toward the common room. It moved left. She stepped forward again. It flowed across behind her to the right, low, quick, and she stepped forward again, and then she was out of the corridor and into the common room, and the dog was between her and the corridor, and it pressed its nose very lightly against the back of her calf.

She took two steps toward the bench.

Then she stopped and turned around and looked at it.

"Why," she said, "is it *pushing* me?"

"She's herding you."

"*Herding* me?"

"Ja."

"Like a, like a nerf? Like a bantha? I'm not *livestock*."

"She knows that," Harry said. "She doesn't care."

The dog was watching her. Waiting. Its eyes moved between her and the bench, back and forth, as if to say: *There. That's where you go. Go on.*

"Make her stop."

"Luna," Harry said. "That'll do."

The dog stopped. Immediately. All at once, the whole low, coiled tension went out of her, and she straightened up from the crouch and stood on four legs like an ordinary animal, and her tail came up from behind her and began to wag, steady and slow, side to side. She looked at Harry. Then at Ahsoka. Then she trotted the last two meters to the bench, hopped up onto it, turned round once, and sat there, at the end of it, looking at Ahsoka with an expression that was so obviously expectant that Ahsoka felt it in the Force like a tug on her sleeve.

*Well? Come on, then.*

Ahsoka looked at Harry.

He'd come into the common room behind them and was standing at the galley counter, already pulling things out of the cold-box. He didn't seem to be paying any attention.

She went and sat down at the other end of the bench.

The dog looked at her. Then it got up and walked along the bench toward her, the padding sinking under its paws, and lay down beside her with its chin on its forepaws and its body pressed along the side of her thigh. She could feel the heat of it through the new trousers. It sighed. A long, deep, contented sigh that went all the way through its body and out of its nose, and then it closed its eyes.

Ahsoka sat very still and looked down at it.

After a while, slowly, she put her hand on its back.

The fur was the softest thing she'd ever touched. Softer than a tooka's. Thick and dense and very slightly coarse on the top, where the black of it was, and underneath, when her fingers sank into it, a layer of down so fine it was like touching smoke. The body under it was lean, ribs close under the skin, and she could feel the dog's breathing, slow and deep, and under that the quick light patter of its heart.

The tail thumped twice against the bench.

She'd met a thousand species. She'd studied thousands more in the Temple archives, in xenobiology, in creature-handling, in the long patient lessons on the varieties of life the Force ran through. She'd seen tookas and nexus and krayt dragons and kaadu and acklays, and massiffs, which were the nearest thing, if a massiff were soft and small and had ever once in its life wanted to be friends with anyone. She'd never seen anything like this. Nothing in the archives. Nothing on any planet she'd visited. Nothing anyone had ever mentioned.

*Dog,* she thought again. The word meant nothing to her.

She ran her hand down the dog's back, slowly, from the shoulders to the base of the tail, and the dog made a small soft groaning noise in its throat and pushed back against her hand. She did it again.

At the galley counter, Harry was cutting things up. He didn't turn round.

---

A little later, the dog got up, hopped down off the bench, and trotted off down the aft corridor without a backward glance. Ahsoka felt absurdly, unreasonably abandoned. She'd just started to work out whether that was ridiculous when the dog came trotting back with something in its mouth.

It was a ball. A small, slightly squashed, battered ball, dark blue, with a seam running around it, and the dog brought it straight to her and put it down on the deck at her feet and sat in front of it and looked up at her.

Ahsoka looked down at the ball. Then at the dog.

The dog looked at the ball. Then at Ahsoka. Then at the ball again.

The tail swept back and forth across the deck.

"What does it want?" she asked.

"Throw it," said Harry, without turning round.

"Throw it where?"

"Anywhere."

She bent down and picked it up. It was wet. Very wet. She held it between two fingers and looked at it with distaste, and the dog's whole body tightened, the focus coming back into it so hard that she felt it in the Force like a plucked string. Its eyes were on the ball. Only the ball. Nothing else in the room, nothing else in the galaxy, existed.

She moved the ball to the left. The dog's head moved to the left.

She moved the ball to the right. The dog's head moved to the right.

In spite of everything, in spite of the day she'd had and the five dead men and the welts on her arms and the bounty on her head, Ahsoka felt the corner of her mouth twitch.

She threw it. Not hard. Underarm, down the aft corridor, a gentle toss that would have carried it maybe six meters before it bounced.

The dog went.

It went from sitting to running so fast it seemed to skip the space between, a black-and-white streak, claws scrabbling and skittering on the smooth deck plating for purchase. The ball bounced once, twice, and went skipping on through the open hatch at the end of the corridor into the aft hold, and the dog went after it at full speed, flat out, ears back and tail streaming, and at the hatch it tried to turn.

It didn't turn. The deck plating was too smooth and it was going too fast. Its legs went out from under it sideways and it slid, scrabbling, through the hatchway, and slammed side-on into a stack of cargo crates on the far side of the hold.

The crates were big. Durasteel, banded, the heavy kind used for machine parts. The stack was four high. The dog hit the bottom one at full speed and the whole stack lurched, and swayed, and the top two came down.

Ahsoka was on her feet. She had her hand up and was reaching with the Force before she'd even thought about it, but she was too far away and too slow, and the crates came down with a tremendous echoing crash of metal on metal, right where the dog was. One of them landed square on it. She heard the impact. She saw it. The crate bounced, came down on its corner, tipped over and lay on its side.

The sound rolled away through the ship.

Ahsoka stood frozen with her hand out.

There was a pause.

Then, from underneath the edge of the fallen crate, a black-and-white head appeared. Then the rest of the dog. It squirmed out from the gap between two crates, shook itself vigorously from nose to tail so that its ears flapped, looked around, spotted the ball where it had rolled into a corner, pounced on it, and came trotting back down the corridor toward Ahsoka with the ball in its mouth and its tail going round in circles.

It dropped the ball at her feet. It sat. It looked up at her.

There wasn't a mark on it. Not a limp. Not a whimper. Not a hair out of place.

Ahsoka lowered her hand very slowly.

The crate had weighed two hundred kilograms if it weighed one. She'd heard it hit. She'd felt it hit, in the Force; she'd felt the moment of impact, and the moment of impact had felt *wrong*. There'd been a small, tight, cold knot in the Force right there where the dog was, around its neck, and the crate had come down on it and simply stopped, as though it had struck a wall that wasn't there. As though something had caught it.

She looked at the dog's neck.

There was a band of leather around it. Dark, worn, plain. A small silver tag hanging from a ring at the front. She'd seen it before without registering it, the way she'd seen the bench and the table. Now she reached toward it with the Force, very lightly, and felt the knot there again. Small and tight and cold and still, exactly like the stitches she'd felt in the air of the loading bay after the tears had closed. Exactly like the man.

She looked across at Harry.

He hadn't turned round. He was standing at the galley counter with his back to her, stirring something in a pan. He hadn't even looked up at the crash.

Ahsoka looked at the back of his head for a long moment.

Then she looked down at the dog, who was sitting at her feet with the wet ball in front of her, gazing up at her with the brown eye and the blue one, the tail sweeping slowly back and forth across the deck behind her.

*Again,* said everything about her. *Again. Again. Again.*

Ahsoka picked up the ball and threw it again. Softer this time. And shorter.


## XI. Dinner

He cooked enough for six people.

She watched him do it from the bench, with the dog lying across her feet, because the dog had decided after the ninth throw of the ball that that was where it lived now. The pan on the galley's heating element was the size of a shield. Into it went meat, a great deal of it, cut into thick strips; then some kind of root vegetable, orange and fibrous, sliced; then onions or something like onions, and a handful of dark leaves, and spices from four different jars, and the smell that came up off it filled the whole ship and made her stomach clench so hard that she had to press her arm against it.

Some of it he did without his hands.

She noticed that slowly. The knife on the board behind him had gone on cutting by itself. It went up and down through a second pile of roots, steadily, neatly, while he stood at the pan with his back to it stirring with a wooden spoon. A jar lifted off a shelf and tipped a measure of something red into the pan and went back to the shelf. A drawer opened, and two plates and two sets of utensils came out of it and laid themselves on the table in front of her, one setting on her side and one on the far side, and the drawer shut.

Each one was a pinprick. Tiny tears in the Force, one after another, opening and closing, the way stitches open and close in fabric under a needle.

She'd been feeling them all evening. She was beginning, horribly, to get used to it.

"You're not hiding it," she said.

"Hiding what?"

She gestured at the knife.

He glanced back at it, as if he'd forgotten it was there. Then he went back to stirring.

"Why would I hide it?" he said.

She didn't have an answer to that. She sat with it a while. Every Force-user she'd ever known had been careful about what they did in front of other people. The Jedi, because the Order had rules about it and because using the Force casually in front of the uninitiated made them uneasy. The Sith and the Nightsisters and the rest, because power was something you kept in reserve and showed at the right time. She'd never seen anyone use whatever this was the way he did. As though it were nothing. As though it were the same as having hands.

"There's bread," Harry said. "In the cold-box. If you want it."

She got up, carefully, extracting her feet from under the dog, who groaned. She found the bread. It was a round flat loaf, dense and dark, wrapped in a cloth. She brought it to the table and stood holding it.

"Is this—" she started.

"Market on Dorvalla. Last week."

"Oh." She put it down. She felt foolish. She'd been about to ask if he'd made it out of nothing like the clothes.

---

He served it in the pan.

He put the whole pan down in the middle of the table on a folded cloth, with a big spoon in it, and sat down across from her, and gave her the spoon first. She looked at him. He nodded at the pan.

She took some. Then, because he didn't say anything, she took more. Then she sat and looked at her plate for a second, the meat and the roots and the dark leaves glistening in their sauce, the steam coming up off it, and something in her chest tightened very hard and she had to wait a moment before she could pick up her fork.

It was the best thing she'd ever eaten. She knew that was the hunger. She didn't care.

She ate the first plate without speaking and without looking up, fast, faster than was polite, faster than she'd eaten since she was a youngling in the refectory racing her crèche-mates to seconds. She tore pieces off the dark bread and dragged them through the sauce. She burned her tongue and didn't stop. When she looked up, finally, with her plate empty, he'd already finished his first plate and was halfway through his second, and he pushed the pan an inch toward her without comment.

She took more.

She managed most of a second plate before she had to stop. She sat back against the bench with her hands on her stomach, feeling it stretched tight and full for the first time in days, and watched him finish the pan.

He finished the pan. All of it. Then he scraped the bottom of it with the last of the bread. Then he got up and went to the cold-box and came back with a slab of cold meat and a knife and sat down and began to eat that, too, in thin slices, as though he were only just getting started.

The dog had come to sit beside him, upright and very polite, gazing up at the meat with total devotion. Every so often he cut a sliver off and dropped it without looking, and the dog caught it in the air with a snap.

"Do you always eat like this?" Ahsoka asked.

"Ja."

"How do you—" She stopped herself.

He looked up at her.

"Never mind," she said.

---

"I want to see the news," she said, a while later.

He looked at her across the table for a moment. Then he reached into the inside of his jacket and pulled out a datapad, thumbed it on, and slid it across to her without a word.

She'd thought he might argue. She found that she'd half-wanted him to.

The datapad was a cheap civilian model, scratched and old. It was already open on a HoloNet aggregator. She pulled up the Core feeds and found it immediately, because it was the top of every one of them.

CORUSCANT. *Jedi fugitive escapes again in deadly freight-yard incident.*

The holo showed the mouth of Bay Forty-Four East, from a distance, behind a security cordon. Coruscant Guard troopers moving back and forth. Smoke still rising faintly from the stacks. A reporter, a Zabrak woman in a sharp blue suit, standing in front of it, talking fast and earnestly.

"... *five beings confirmed dead this morning in what Republic Security is calling a violent confrontation between the fugitive Padawan Ahsoka Tano and a group of independent bounty hunters attempting to bring her into custody. Sources within the Coruscant Guard say that Tano was assisted by an unidentified male accomplice and that the pair escaped the scene despite the arrival of Republic forces and a member of the Jedi High Council. The Jedi Order has declined to comment. Meanwhile, Admiral Wilhuff Tarkin, who has led the Republic's investigation into the Temple bombing, released the following statement within the last hour—*"

The holo cut. Tarkin's face. Lean and grey and composed, standing at a lectern somewhere with the Republic crest behind him.

"*Today's tragic events,*" Tarkin said, in his clipped, precise Core accent, "*only confirm what the evidence has already made abundantly clear. Ahsoka Tano is a danger to the Republic and to every citizen of it. This morning alone, she has been linked to the deaths of five more beings. I call upon the Jedi Council to cooperate fully with this investigation, and I call upon any citizen with information regarding her whereabouts to come forward. The Republic will not rest until she faces justice.*"

Ahsoka put the datapad down on the table.

She sat and looked at it. Tarkin's face had frozen in the middle of a word. His mouth was a little open. His eyes were very calm.

"Five more," she said.

She heard her own voice and it sounded far away.

"*Five more.* They're putting the bounty hunters on me. They're putting *your* bounty hunters on me."

"Ja," Harry said. He'd stopped eating.

"I didn't—" Her voice cracked. She stopped and started again. "I didn't kill anybody. Not Letta. Not the people in the hangar. Not them. I never touched them. You did that. And now they're— He's standing up there in front of the whole galaxy and saying—"

"He'd have said it either way," Harry said.

She looked up at him.

"If I hadn't come," he said, "you'd be in a cell. Or dead. And he'd be saying it about the cell, or saying it about you being dead." He shrugged. "Man like that's going to say what he likes. The bodies are just what he's saying it about today."

She stared at him.

"That's not—" she began, and stopped, because she'd been about to say *that's not comforting*, and she realized he had only been telling her how it was.

She looked back at Tarkin's frozen face. After a while she reached out and turned the datapad face-down on the table.

---

The datapad chimed.

It chimed again, a different, sharper tone, and a small light flashed on the edge of it. Harry reached across without hurry and turned it face-up and looked at it, and she watched his eyes move as he read.

"What is it?"

He turned it round so she could see.

It was a Guild bulletin. She recognized the sigil at the top, the same one stitched on his jacket. Below it, her own face, the same Temple holo they'd used on every board on Coruscant. Below that, text.

*POSTING UPDATE. Target: TANO, Ahsoka. Republic contract. Status: ACTIVE. Reward: increased (see attached). NOTE: Target believed to be traveling with accomplice, human male, approx. 1.9m, dark hair, green eyes, possible Guild affiliation (unconfirmed). Accomplice is considered EXTREMELY DANGEROUS. Accomplice is responsible for the deaths of five licensed and unlicensed hunters. Approach with extreme caution. Republic requests any information on accomplice's identity and methods.*

She read it twice.

"They doubled it," she said. She looked at the figure in the attachment and felt slightly faint. "They more than doubled it."

"Ja."

"Because of you."

"Probably."

"That's—" She looked up at him. "That's *you*, on there. Your description. *Possible Guild affiliation.* They'll know. Won't they? Somebody'll know it's you."

"Thrennik knows already, I'd guess."

"Who's Thrennik?"

"Kubaz. Guild agent in the Works."

"And he'll tell them? The Republic?"

Harry considered this. "He'll sell it to them," he said. "Eventually. When the price is right."

"And then they'll come after you. Bounty hunters, and the *Republic*, and the Jedi. All of it. Everything that's after me will be after you."

"Ja," he agreed, and reached across the table and took the last heel of the dark bread, and began to eat it.

She sat and watched him eat the bread.

"Doesn't that *bother* you?"

He finished his mouthful. He seemed to give the question some honest thought.

"Pass the salt," he said.

She stared at him for a long moment. Then, without quite knowing why, she picked up the salt and passed it to him.

---

Later, when the pan was clean and the plates had washed themselves in the galley sink while he stood beside it drying them with a cloth, and the dog had gone back to lying across her feet, she said, "What happens now?"

He was putting the plates away. He didn't turn round.

"Now you sleep."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow you decide."

"Decide *what*?"

"What you want to do." He shut the cupboard and turned round and leaned against the counter. "You want to go back, I'll take you back. Coruscant. The Temple. Wherever. Walk you right up to the front door, if you like."

"Just like that."

"Ja."

"And if I don't want to go back?"

"Then you don't."

She looked down at the dog. It looked up at her, its tail moving lazily against the deck.

"I want to find out who did it," she said slowly. She hadn't known she was going to say it until she did, and once it was out she knew it was true. "Who killed Letta. Who built the bomb. Who set me up. Somebody did. Somebody who could choke a person from across a room without touching them, which means a Force-user, which means—" She stopped. She didn't want to finish that thought. "I want to find them. I want to know. And then I want to walk into the Council chamber and put it in front of them, all of it, and make them look at it."

Harry looked at her for a while.

"Then we find out who did it," he said.

"*We?*"

He shrugged.

"Told you," he said. "I've been looking for shit to do."

She opened her mouth to say something. She didn't know what. Something sharp, probably. Something about how she didn't need help, and didn't know him, and didn't trust him, which was all true.

She closed it again.

---

The cabin he gave her was the one off the starboard side of the common room, a narrow space with a bunk and a fold-down shelf and a small round viewport, and a door with a lock on the inside of it.

"Locks from in there," he said, standing in the doorway. "Nobody can open it from out here."

"Not even you?"

He looked at her. He didn't answer, and she understood, with a small cold drop in her stomach, that the honest answer was probably *no, not even me*, and that the honest answer to the next question, *could you open it anyway, if you wanted?*, was almost certainly *yes*, and that he wasn't going to say either one of them out loud.

"Nobody's coming tonight," he said instead.

"How do you know?"

"Nobody knows where we are."

He stepped back out of the doorway, and she shut the door, and locked it, and stood with her back against it listening to his footsteps go away down the corridor toward the cockpit. She could barely hear them. A man that size, on deck plating, and she could barely hear them. Then she heard the creak of a chair and the faint thump of boots going up onto a console, and silence.

She sat down on the bunk.

From inside the sleeve of the new jacket, carefully, she slid out the knife.

She'd taken it off the galley counter while he'd had his back to her, putting the plates away. It was a short kitchen blade, sharp, with a plain grip. She'd palmed it and slid it up her sleeve in one movement, the way Rex had once shown her on a long boring night on a transport, and she was almost sure he hadn't seen.

Almost. She thought about the way he hadn't turned round when the crates fell on the dog. She thought about the way he'd known she was standing at the comm without looking. She thought about the nose, and the finger tapping on the side of it.

She put the knife under the pillow anyway.

---

She lay on her back on the bunk in the dark and didn't sleep.

The ship was quiet. The fans had wound down to their night setting, a low soft hush. Through the small round viewport above her she could see a slice of the sky, deep blue-black now and thick with stars, more stars than she'd ever seen from Coruscant, and the edge of the gold moon. The wind had dropped. When she held her breath she could hear, very faintly through the hull, something that might have been water lapping at a shore.

After a while there was a scratching at the door.

She sat up, hand going to the pillow. The scratching came again, light, patient. Then a small whine.

She got up and unlocked the door and opened it a crack.

The dog was sitting in the corridor. It looked up at her with its mismatched eyes, pale and dark in the dim light. Its tail moved.

"What do you want?" Ahsoka whispered.

The dog stood up, walked past her legs into the cabin as though it had been invited, turned round twice on the floor in the narrow space between the bunk and the door, and lay down across the threshold with a long sigh. It put its chin on its forepaws. It faced the door.

Ahsoka stood and looked down at it for a while.

Then she shut the door, and locked it, and got back onto the bunk, and lay down on her side facing the dog. In the dark she could just make out the white of its chest and the white tip of its tail and the faint gleam of the silver tag on its collar.

She let her hand fall over the edge of the bunk. After a moment, a cold wet nose found it, and pushed it once, and then the dog settled again with its head under her palm.

---

She reached out into the Force.

She did it slowly, carefully, letting her awareness go out from her like water spreading across a floor. The dog first, right there under her hand: bright and close and drowsy now, the intense focus all gone soft and slack, only the steady comfortable glow of an animal that was fed and comfortable and lying where it wanted to be. Then the ship around her, the hum of it, the faint residual flicker of the machines.

Then the cockpit. And him.

She still couldn't read him. She didn't try very hard. The cold, still place sat there at the front of the ship like a deep pool in a forest, quiet, unmoving, and her attention slid around the edges of it and couldn't find a way in, and after a moment she let it go and went on past it, out through the hull.

Outside, the world opened up. The grass, a great slow silver sea of small green lives. The trees, enormous and ancient and patient, their roots going down and down into the dark soil, their crowns rustling with things that slept and things that hunted. The lake, cold and deep, full of slow-moving shapes. Something large walking along the far shore, a grazer, unhurried. Something small and quick in the grass near the ship. The wind. The water. The two moons pulling gently at the lake.

It was beautiful. She'd been so busy being afraid she hadn't let herself feel it until now.

And then, as she let her awareness spread farther, out to the edge of the clearing and beyond, she found something else.

It was faint. So faint that at first she thought she'd imagined it. But it was there: a line, running through the grass and the trees at a distance of perhaps two hundred meters from the ship, in a great wide circle all the way around it. She followed it with her mind. It went on and on, unbroken, around the whole clearing and the lake shore and back again to where she'd started. And all along it, at intervals, she felt them. Small, tight, cold knots in the Force. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Exactly like the one on the dog's collar. Exactly like the stitches she'd felt in the air of the loading bay as the tears had closed.

A ring of them. Around the ship. Around her.

She lay very still in the dark with her hand on the dog's head and felt it for a long time. She didn't know what it was. She didn't know what it did. She was quite sure that if she asked, he wouldn't tell her.

She found she wasn't afraid of it. She turned that over too, and couldn't work out what it meant.

---

Last of all, she reached for Coruscant.

She knew it wouldn't work. It was two days away in the fastest ship she knew. But she reached anyway, out past the ring of cold knots, out past the moons, out into the great dark between the stars, as far as she could go, looking for one presence. A bright, hot, restless, enormous presence that she'd known since she was fourteen, that she'd felt on the other side of battlefields and on the other side of planets, and that had always, always been there when she looked for it.

Nothing. Just the dark, and the stars, and her own small voice echoing in it.

He'd be looking for her. She knew that without needing to feel it. He'd have reached for her the moment he heard, the way he always did, throwing his whole self out across the city like opening every door in a house at once. And he'd have found nothing. Not where she was, not how she was, not whether.

She thought about him standing in that loading bay, or on some rooftop in the upper city, reaching and reaching into the place where she'd been.

*I'm alive,* she thought, into the dark, as hard as she could. *I'm alive, Master. I'm safe. I'm— I'm somewhere quiet.*

She knew it wouldn't reach him.

She sent it anyway.

Then she lay in the dark on a stranger's ship, on a world without a name, with a stolen knife under her pillow and a creature the galaxy had never heard of asleep under her hand, and listened to the water on the shore until, sometime past the setting of the gold moon, she slept.

---

In the cockpit, Harry sat with his boots up on the console and watched the moons go down over the lake.

The datapad lay face-up on his knee. The Guild bulletin was still open on it, the girl's face, the line of text underneath. *Republic requests any information on accomplice's identity and methods.* He'd read it three times. He'd been thinking, on and off, for the last hour, about what the Jedi with the head would be telling his Council right about now, and what the Council would make of it, and how long it would take before somebody with more sense than Tolliver came looking.

Not long, probably.

Down the corridor, behind the starboard cabin door, he could hear two things breathing. One of them slow and deep and even, the long sighs of a border collie who'd had a big dinner and nine throws of a ball and was now on guard duty. The other lighter, quicker, catching every so often. Then, gradually, after a long while, slowing. Evening out.

He listened until it was steady.

Then he took his boots down off the console, turned the datapad off, and sat in the dark for a while longer, looking at the place where the gold moon had been.

Then he got up and went to see if there was any of that cold meat left.
