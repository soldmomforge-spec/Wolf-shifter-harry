# Need to Know

## Chapter Two: Both Ends

---

## Part One: The Absolute Law

---

### I. Leah

*The north fork of the Calawah — Monday, November 20th, 6:44 p.m.*

*Ask him?* Jacob said. *Ask him* yourself*? Leah, I don't even know where he* is*. Nobody does. Seth can't remember. I can't remember. I've been trying for five hours and every time I get close my head goes somewhere else, it's like trying to pick up a fish with oven mitts—*

*Yeah,* Leah thought. *It does that.*

She went down the last of the slope in long loose bounds, through sword ferns that came up to her chest, and hit the river at the shallow place below the split boulder. The water came up cold around her legs and her belly. She went through it without slowing and came up the far bank and shook, once, hard, nose to tail, and the spray went off her in a sheet. The leather cord around her left hind leg had her cut-offs and her tank top knotted into it, soaked through for the second time that day. She didn't care. She kept going.

South. A little east. Toward the highway, and the long drive, and the white house with all the glass.

*Where are you going?* Seth said. He was a sandy blur at the back of her head, standing in the trees at the edge of the Cullens' lawn with his ears up, and Jacob beside him, huge and russet and bristling. They'd phased out there twenty minutes ago to wait for her. She could see the house through their eyes, the long white face of it, every light on, and through the big front window the shape of Carlisle bending over a kitchen stool with a woman sitting on it.

*There,* Leah thought. *Where you are.*

*Not home?*

*Layla's there, right?*

*Yeah. Carlisle's doing her back.*

*Then there.*

*Why?*

She thought about it while she ran. It was a fair question. Two years ago, two weeks ago, two hours ago, she'd have gone home. She'd have run the long way round to the reservation and phased in the trees behind her mom's house and gone in the back door and up the stairs and shut her door and put her face in her pillow and stayed there until somebody made her come out. That was what you did, when something happened to you. You took it somewhere nobody could look at it.

She didn't feel like doing that.

She checked. She actually checked, running, the way you'd check your pockets for your keys. She went looking for the part of her that wanted to hide, and it wasn't where she'd left it.

*Because everybody's going to want to talk about it,* she thought. *And I'd rather get it over with.*

That was true. She could feel Seth hear that it was true. The pack mind was good for that much.

*Lee,* Seth said carefully. *Are you okay? Like, actually?*

*Yeah.*

*Like* actually *actually?*

*Seth.* She went up a cutbank in two jumps and through a stand of alder and down the other side. *Look in my head. What do you see?*

He looked. She felt him look. She let him. She held still for it in the middle of her own skull, the way you'd hold still for a doctor with a light, and she felt her little brother go through her, carefully, poking at things. The ridge. The rope of light. The man on the slab with his hands in his pockets. The green eyes, and the cables going, all of them, all at once. Sam's line going slack and sliding out of her. The tent. The hallway that went on too long. The blue fridge. A man at a range with his back to her, frying eggs, in a white shirt with the sleeves rolled up, and the smell of bacon fat. The bench in the dark. Her face in his hand.

And the wall. The big smooth gray wall in the middle of the afternoon where the other half of it was.

*You're—* Seth stopped. *You're happy.*

*I wouldn't go that far.*

*No, you are. Lee. You're, like, calm. I haven't felt you calm since—* He stopped again. He didn't finish. He didn't have to. *Since before.*

*Huh,* she thought. *Yeah. Maybe.*

*She's not calm,* Jacob said. *She's in shock.*

*I'm not in shock, Jake.*

*You imprinted on a guy who pinned you to a rock with his* brain*. Who shot at Layla. Who's got a vampire in a hole in his back room and a—a* thing *in your head that we can't see through. That's shock. That's textbook. Your dad used to—* He broke off. Even in the pack mind, she felt him flinch. *Sorry. Harry used to do the first aid course at the tribal center. Every summer. He said people in shock act calm.*

*I'm hungry,* Leah thought. *Do people in shock get hungry?*

*You ate six eggs! I saw it! Half a loaf!*

*I ran twenty miles since then.*

*That is not—*

*Is there food at the house or not?*

There was a pause.

*Esme made lasagna,* Seth said. *For Layla. And me. There's like half of it left.*

*Then I'm coming to the house.*

---

She kept the line with her the whole way.

She didn't have a choice about it, but she noticed she wouldn't have chosen anything else. It ran out of the middle of her chest and away behind her, northwest now, back up the hill and across the river and into the big trees, and it didn't get any thinner the further she went. It stretched. It hummed. She'd thought, the way you think things before you know anything, that it would hurt to go away from him. In Jacob's head, the first week, going home from the Cullens' at night had felt like having a tooth pulled through his ribs, every single time, and Sam's head had been the same, worse, all that first summer, Sam lying awake in his little house behind the Ateara place with his whole body pointed down the coast toward Makah.

It pulled. It pulled the way a dog pulls on a leash when it wants to go and look at something. It pulled and it pulled and she kept running the other way, and every time it pulled she knew exactly where he was.

He was in his kitchen. He'd gone back inside. He was standing at the big black range with both hands flat on it, and there was music, she could feel it more than hear it, a thump coming down the line like a heartbeat through a wall. Something with a lot of drums. He'd put the kettle on.

And under him, down at the bottom of him, the thing behind the locks was lying with its chin on its paws and its eyes open, facing her.

She could feel it watching her go. It had seen her face in his hand. It knew she'd come back. It knew it the way she knew the river was going to be cold, and it was lying there in the dark at the bottom of him in a kind of huge contented patience, the way an old dog lies on a porch in the sun. Waiting. Not worried at all.

Her own wolf was the same. That was the strange part. She'd been running inside it for two years and she'd never once felt it like this: loose and easy and light, running like it was running home. It had been a hard, angry, fast animal for two years, the fastest of all of them, because the faster it went the less it had to feel. It ran just as fast tonight, with its head up and its ears loose.

It kept turning its head as it ran. Back over its shoulder. Up the line.

*Lee,* said Jacob. *Your—your wolf's doing a thing.*

*I know.*

*It keeps looking back.*

*I know, Jake.*

*It's* wagging*. Leah. Your wolf is wagging its* tail*.*

*Mind your own business.*

*I literally can't! That's the whole point of the pack mind!*

She came out of the trees onto the highway at the bend south of the Cullens' turnoff, and crossed it, two lanes of wet blacktop shining in the dark with nobody on them, and went up the bank on the other side and into the trees again. A truck came round the bend a few seconds after, a logging truck going home empty, its headlights sweeping the trees where she'd been. She was already a quarter mile into the dark.

*Leah,* said Seth. *Can I ask something?*

*You're going to anyway.*

*Is he—* She felt him try to work out how to say it. *Is he going to hurt you?*

She thought about it. She actually thought about it, which was more than she'd have done for anybody else, because Seth had sat in the ferns on the ridge that afternoon holding a woman who'd been thrown twenty feet by one bullet and he had a right to ask.

She thought about the man on the slab with the vampire in the air behind him and her brother and her pinned flat on the rock. She thought about the crater. She thought about the black oak door, and the round room, and the jars, and the gray wall in the middle of her head where something had closed over what happened in there like a hand.

She thought about him standing in the dark on the bench with his hand flat on the side of her face, and the locks in his chest groaning, and him saying *Not today. Sorry. Not today.*

*No,* she thought.

*How do you know?*

*Because he can't lie to me,* Leah said. *And I asked him.*

That was half true. She hadn't asked him, not in those words. But she'd been in him all afternoon, in the kitchen and the hall and the round room, and she hadn't needed to ask. She'd felt the answer the way you feel the floor under your feet. Seth felt the half that was a lie, because Seth felt everything, and then she felt him feel the other half, the floor, and stop worrying about the first half.

*Okay,* he said. *Okay.*

*He can't* lie *to you?* Jacob said. *What does that mean, he can't lie to you?*

*It means what it says.*

*Since when is that a thing? That's not a thing. Imprinting doesn't do that.*

*Tell it that.*

---

She came out onto the edge of the Cullens' lawn at three minutes past seven.

Jacob and Seth were waiting in the trees by the garage, and they both came toward her as she came out of the dark, and stopped. Jacob was the biggest of the three of them, by a long way; he always had been. He stood a little under seven feet at the shoulder, russet and black-muzzled and thick through the chest. Seth stood a full head shorter than she did, all long legs and big feet and ears he hadn't grown into, still filling out. He'd keep growing for a while yet, Carlisle said. He'd probably end up bigger than her. Not this year.

They looked at her. She looked at them.

*Hi,* Seth said.

*Hi.*

Jacob didn't say anything. He was looking at her the way you look at a car somebody you know has just driven into a lake, very carefully, all over, for the damage.

*I'm going to phase,* Leah said. *Don't watch.*

*We've all seen—* Jacob started.

*I said don't watch.*

They both looked away. She went behind the big cedar at the corner of the garage, the one that had a branch missing from the night in June when Emmett had wrestled Paul into it, and let go.

It was easier than it had been that morning. She noticed that. Usually phasing back felt like getting shoved into a box a size too small, every bone folding and every muscle pulling in at once. Tonight it was quick. The wolf went back down under her ribs the way a dog goes back to its bed, turned round once and lay down, and she was standing barefoot in the wet needles in the dark, a woman again, freezing and dripping and with her hair in her eyes. She got the cord off her ankle and her clothes out of the knot and wrung them out and put them on wet. The tank top stuck to her. The cut-offs were cold enough to make her gasp.

She stood there a second with her arms crossed, steaming.

Northwest, a long way off, at the other end of the line, she felt him go still at his range. Just for a second. Like he'd felt her change and stopped what he was doing to feel it better.

Then he went back to his kettle.

She went across the lawn.

---

Edward was on the porch.

Of course he was. He'd have heard her coming a mile out; he'd have heard all three of them, Jacob and Seth in the trees and her in the river, a long time before she got close, the way he heard everything. He was standing at the top of the steps in a gray sweater with his hands in his pockets and the light from the hall behind him, and he was looking at her with an expression on his face that she'd never seen there before.

She'd seen Edward Cullen look a lot of ways. Mostly she'd seen him look sorry. He'd looked at her for two years like she was something sad he'd walked past on the side of the road.

Tonight he was looking at her like she was a word in a language he'd thought he spoke.

She stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"What," she said.

"There's a wall in your head."

"Yeah."

"I can hear everything else." He said it slowly, like he was checking each word. "Every bit of today. The ridge, the man, the tent. Your brother at the river. The fry-up. I can hear every bit of it as clearly as I've ever heard anything, and then in the middle there's—" He took one hand out of his pocket and made a shape in the air with it. A flat thing. A pane of glass. "I've been listening to minds since 1918. I've heard the inside of Aro's head. Bella's is silent. Silent's one thing; I've had two years to get used to silent. This is a room in the middle of a house, with the door shut, and something standing in front of it."

"Something's standing in front of it," Leah agreed.

"What?"

"His." She came up the first step. "Don't touch it."

Edward looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, through the open door, she could hear the house. People not breathing, which was what a houseful of vampires sounded like when they were all listening at once. Layla's heart, quick and a bit ragged. Seth's and Jacob's, coming up behind her across the lawn in human form now, she could hear their bare feet on the wet grass. And a little heart going fast as a bird's in the big room at the back.

"I couldn't touch it if I wanted to," Edward said quietly. "I tried. In the first second. I'm sorry; I couldn't help it. It was like putting my hand flat on a stone at the bottom of a river in January." He paused. "It was very old, Leah. Whatever that is. Older than us."

"Yeah. I got that."

"Did he put it there?"

She thought about how to answer that. She found, standing on the step, that she had to think about it, because the first answer that came was *no* and the second was *yes* and she couldn't say either one out loud without it sticking in her teeth.

"Sort of," she said. "It came with him."

Edward's face didn't change. Edward's face never changed. But she watched him put that away somewhere careful.

"Esme's kept some lasagna warm for you," he said, and stood aside.

---

The big room was full.

They were all there. She'd known they would be. Esme in the kitchen doorway with a dish towel in her hands. Alice and Jasper by the glass wall at the back, Alice with her arms wrapped round herself and a pinched look on her little face, like a headache. Rosalie at the bottom of the stairs, on the second step, so she'd be taller than everybody. Emmett on the floor by the piano with his long legs stuck out. Bella on the white couch, with the kid on her lap; the kid was in pajamas with ducks on them, which seemed insane to Leah, a vampire baby in duck pajamas, and she was wide awake, sitting straight up with both hands on her mother's arm, staring at the door.

And in the middle of all of it, on a kitchen stool by the island, Layla.

She was sitting backwards on the stool, straddling it, with her arms folded on the counter and her chin on her arms. She had her shirt off. She had a black sports bra on and nothing else from the waist up, and Carlisle was standing behind her with a little glass jar in one hand, and her whole back, from her shoulder blades to the top of her jeans, was one bruise.

Leah had seen a lot of bruises. She'd seen Jacob after the newborns had broken half the bones down his right side. This was something else. It was the color of a thundercloud, black and purple and a sick yellow-green at the edges, and it went right across her back in a long band, the shape of the cedar log she'd hit. You could see the line of it. You could see where the bark had been.

And as Leah watched, Carlisle dipped two fingers in the jar and drew them down the middle of it, and where his fingers went, the black went purple and the purple went pink.

Leah stopped walking.

"Huh," she said.

"Murtlap essence," said Carlisle, without looking up. His voice was the voice he used when he was having the best day of his life and trying not to show it. "Seth ran to her house and fetched it. It's a pickled tentacle. From a—sea anemone, apparently. Of some kind." He drew his fingers down again. Pink. "Layla says it's the most common household remedy in her world. They give it to children for scraped knees. Leah, I've watched it heal what should have been a three-week hematoma in about forty minutes. I've got no mechanism for it at all." He dipped his fingers again. "None. It's wonderful."

"He's been saying that for forty minutes," Layla said into her arms. "*It's wonderful.* I've got a bruise the size of Nebraska."

"It's a wonderful bruise."

"Thank you, Doctor."

Layla turned her head on her arms and looked at Leah.

She looked bad. Not just the back. Her face had gone gray under the brown, and there were shadows under her eyes like thumbprints, and her hair had come out of its braid and was stuck to her neck. But her eyes were sharp. They went over Leah the same way they'd gone over the room the first time she'd walked into this house, fast, all at once, the way a cop's did. Feet, hands, face.

"You okay?" Layla said.

"Yeah."

"He didn't—"

"No."

Layla looked at her a second longer. Then she nodded and put her chin back down on her arms.

"Okay," she said.

That was all. Leah found she liked her more for it than she'd liked her for anything yet.

She went into the kitchen.

Esme had the lasagna out before she got there. It was in a big blue baking dish, and there was about half of it left, and it was still hot. Esme put a fork in it and handed her the whole dish without a word, the way Esme did things, like she'd been feeding wolves her whole life and had stopped bothering with plates a long time ago. Leah took it. She stood by the fridge with the dish in one hand and the fork in the other and ate.

It was good. It was really good. It had sausage in it.

She ate about a third of it standing there before she noticed that the whole room was watching her.

Nobody said anything. Jacob and Seth had come in behind her; she could feel them in the doorway. Jacob had his arms crossed. She could feel him from across the room like you'd feel a space heater, the heat of him and the anger coming off it, and she could feel him building up to something. He'd been building up to it since the ridge. He'd been building up to it all the way through the pack mind and across the lawn, and now he was standing in the kitchen door with his arms crossed watching her eat lasagna out of a dish with a fork, and she could hear his heart going.

She ate another bite.

"Seriously?" Jacob said.

"What?"

"You're *eating*?"

"I told you I was hungry."

"Leah." He came into the kitchen. He came all the way in, right up to the island, and put both hands flat on it, and Layla, on her stool, lifted her head and leaned a little bit out of his way. "Leah. What the *hell*."

"Jake—" Seth said from the door.

"No. No, I want to know. I want her to say it." He was looking right at her. His eyes were very bright. "How could you imprint on *him*? Out of everybody. Out of every single person on the whole *planet*. Him?"

Leah put her fork down in the lasagna.

"He pinned us to a rock," Jacob said. "Me and—not me, *you*, you and Seth, he put you flat on a rock with his *brain* and you couldn't move. I felt it. I felt it in Seth's head after. He walked in and took your kill and called you a dog's name and went through Seth's head like a burglar and then he *shot* Layla—"

"He shot the ground," said Layla, into her arms.

"He shot the ground near Layla! She flew twenty feet! Look at her back! And then he walked off with a vampire under his arm like a—like a surfboard, and he's got it in a back room, Leah, in some kind of—I saw it, in your head, before the wall, there's a *circle*, and *jars*, there's a room full of jars with *stuff* in them that's *moving*, and then he went in there and did something to it and we can't even see what because there's a—a wall in your head—" He stopped. He took a breath. "And then you came out of all that and he made you *eggs*?"

"Six eggs," said Emmett, from the floor by the piano. Everybody looked at him. "What? That's a lot of eggs."

"I don't get it," Jacob said. "I don't. I've been trying all afternoon. You're the—you're *Leah*. You hate everybody. You hate *me* and I'm nice to you. You've hated every single person who's ever walked into this house. And he walks in and pins you to the ground and calls you *Lassie*, and you—" He threw his hands up. "How could you imprint on *him*?"

The kitchen was very quiet.

Leah looked at him across the island.

She'd been waiting for it. She'd been waiting for it since the river. She'd felt it coming up through the pack mind, from him, all that hot hurt angry confused feeling with no place to go. A month ago, a week ago, this morning, she'd have met it head on. She'd have gone for his throat. She'd have said the meanest thing she could think of and then gone out the back and phased and run until her legs gave out.

She still wanted to say the meanest thing she could think of. She just found she wasn't angry while she said it.

"Are you serious right now?" Leah said.

"Am I—yeah! Yeah, I'm serious—"

"*You* want to talk to *me* about who I imprinted on." She put the lasagna dish down on the counter. "You. Jacob Black. You want to stand in this kitchen and have that conversation with me."

Jacob's mouth opened.

"Okay," said Leah. "Let's have it."

"Leah—"

"No, let's. Let's do it. Let's talk about imprints." She came round the end of the island. She took her time about it. "Let's talk about yours, Jake. Do you remember yours? Because I do. I was there. I was in your head. I've watched it about four hundred times, because you can't stop thinking about it, so every time I'm on patrol with you I get to watch it again, like a movie I didn't buy a ticket to." She stopped about three feet from him. "You walked into this room with your teeth out to kill a baby. And the baby looked at you. And gravity moved. That's how you tell it, right? *Gravity moved.*"

"That's—"

"A baby, Jake. A newborn. She was about three minutes old. She was still covered in her mom's blood. Half of it was on the *ceiling*." Across the room, Bella made a small sound; Leah didn't look at her. "Your imprint is a *literal baby vampire hybrid*. Half bloodsucker, half human, born by her mom's husband chewing her out of her mom's stomach. That's who you looked at and fell in love with. Okay? That's yours. Look at her, she's cute, I don't care. It's still *weird*, and I'm not taking imprint advice from a guy who imprinted on a newborn half-leech in duck pajamas."

"She wasn't in duck pajamas then," said Seth.

"Not *helping*, Seth."

"Sorry."

Jacob had gone a dark red, all the way up from his collar to his hairline. "That's different."

"Oh, it's different."

"It is different! It's—Nessie's—she's going to grow up, she's—it's not like that, it's not *that kind of*—you *know* it's not, you've been in my head—"

"I know what it's like. I know exactly what it's like. You're her big brother and her dog and her best friend and one day maybe something else and you'll be whatever she needs you to be forever, and you'd die for her, and that's beautiful, Jake, I mean it." She meant it. She could hear herself meaning it; so could everybody in the room. "And it's still a baby vampire hybrid. And you still walked in here and looked at her and *she* happened to *you*. So don't you stand in this kitchen and look at me like I drove into a lake. Because if you really think about it, if you really, actually sit down and think about it, mine is the best one."

"The—*best*?"

"The best." She held up one finger. "A. He's not a baby. He's a grown man. He's older than me. He's got a job. He's got a—a fridge, Jake, he's got a fridge and a record player and his own place, which is more than anybody else in this pack has, including you, because you sleep on *their couch*." A second finger. "B. He's human. His heart beats. I can hear it. He eats cheeseburgers. He bleeds, probably. He doesn't smell like bleach and dead flowers. No offense," she added, to the room in general.

"Some taken," said Rosalie, from the stairs.

"C." Leah held up the third finger, right in Jacob's face. "He's a wolf."

The kitchen was silent.

"He's also a wolf," Leah said. "Somehow. I don't know how. I don't think *he* totally knows how. But he's got one. You saw it. You saw it in Seth's head and you saw it in mine. It's down in the bottom of him and it's bigger than you, and it took one look up the line at me and grabbed hold and *pulled back*. Out of every imprint in the whole history of the tribe, every single one, Taha Aki's and Ephraim's and Sam's and Jared's and Quil's and yours, mine is the only one that's ever imprinted *back*." She dropped her hand. "So yeah. He's not a baby, he's human, and he's a wolf. One, two, three. Mine's the best one. Take that."

Jacob stared at her.

"And another thing," Leah said.

"Oh my God," said Emmett happily.

"Do you think I *chose* this?" She took the last step, right up into Jacob's face, and looked up at him, because he was a lot taller than her and she'd never once in her life let that stop her. "Is that what you think? You think I was standing on that rock thinking *ooh, he's cute, I'll have that one*? That I *picked* him? Out of a catalogue? I was about to go for his throat, Jake. I was about two seconds off it. I looked up at him to see where to bite and the whole world went." She held his eyes. "Did you choose Nessie?"

Jacob didn't say anything.

"Did you? On that couch? With your teeth out? Did you stand there and think it through?"

"No," Jacob said, after a long moment. Very quietly.

"No," said Leah. "Neither did I."

She turned round and went back to the counter and picked up her lasagna, and her fork, and ate a bite.

It was still really good.

Behind her, nobody said anything for about five seconds. Then the kid on Bella's lap reached up and put her hand flat on her mother's cheek, and Leah, without looking, heard Edward laugh. It came out of him quick and surprised, like a cough.

"What?" said Bella.

"She wants to know," Edward said, "what a hybrid is. And whether she's a good one."

"Oh, baby," said Bella. "You're the best one."

"Hey," said Leah, with her mouth full. "No. Mine's the best one. We just went over this."

Across the island, on her kitchen stool, Layla Quinn put her face down in her folded arms, and her shoulders started to shake, and it took Leah a second to realize she was laughing.

Jacob stood in the middle of the kitchen with his hands hanging at his sides. He looked at Leah eating. He looked at Seth in the doorway, who was very carefully not smiling. He looked at Bella and the kid on the couch, and at Edward behind them with his hand over his mouth, and at Emmett, who was grinning at him from the floor like Christmas had come early.

"I hate all of you," Jacob said.

"Yeah," said Leah. "Welcome to my life."

### II. Edward

*The Cullen house — 7:21 p.m.*

Leah Clearwater's mind had always sounded to Edward like a radio left on in an empty room.

He'd heard it for two years, on and off, whenever she'd come within range: the tight bitter hum of her, running in the same groove over and over, and under the hum, never quite out of earshot, the other station. Sam. Sam's face, Sam's voice, a beach at night, a dirt road, a kitchen in Makah. A song she couldn't turn off. He'd learned to tune past her the way you learn to tune past traffic. It was the only courtesy he'd been able to offer her, and she'd never known he was offering it.

Tonight the other station was gone.

He'd noticed it on the porch and he noticed it again now, standing behind the couch with one hand on Bella's shoulder, watching Leah eat lasagna out of Esme's blue dish with her hip against the fridge. The groove was gone. The hum was gone. He listened for Sam the way you'd listen for a clock you'd stopped hearing years ago, and found a quiet place where Sam had been, smooth and empty as a riverbed after the water's gone down.

And running out of the middle of that quiet place, away to the northwest, a line.

He couldn't hear the far end of it. The man at the end of it was twenty miles off, and Edward's range, on a good night, with the wind right, was four. But he could hear Leah *feel* the far end. She was doing it constantly. Every few seconds, in between bites, in between the things she was saying to Jacob, she'd check it the way a woman checks a sleeping baby on a monitor, and Edward caught the edges of what she found there. A kitchen. A kettle coming to the boil. Music, felt more than heard, a drum. A man standing at an iron range with his hands flat on it, and under the man, down at the bottom of him—

Something lying in the dark with its eyes open.

Edward had felt that in Seth's head this afternoon, secondhand, a memory of a memory, and it had been unsettling. Felt in Leah's mind, live, it was something else. It was the size of it. A human mind had a shape. A vampire's mind had a shape, faster and harder and with more rooms. Edward had heard the Quileute wolves, and their minds had a shape too, a human shape with something old and wild folded into it like a second skin. This thing at the end of Leah's line had no shape he recognised. It was a weight. It bent her mind toward it the way the moon bends water.

And in the middle of all of it, the wall.

He hadn't lied to her on the porch. He'd tried it once, in the first second, out of a reflex he'd had for eighty-eight years and wasn't proud of, and it had been like pressing his palm flat to a stone at the bottom of a river. Since then he'd only looked at it. He couldn't stop looking at it. It sat in the middle of her afternoon like a pane of frosted glass, cold and gray and seamless, and through it he could see nothing at all. A black oak door. Then glass. Then, three hours later, Leah coming back out through the black oak door into a room with a skylight and a motorcycle, and then glass again where whatever had happened in between should have been.

He'd been reading minds since 1918. He'd never seen a mind with a locked room in it.

He was very good at not letting things show on his face. Bella still felt it go through him. He felt her hand come up and cover his on her shoulder.

"What?" she murmured, too low for anyone but the family to hear.

"I'll tell you," he murmured back. "In a minute."

He was listening to the wall. Something about it had been bothering him since the porch.

He went back through what he had. Jacob and Seth had come in a few minutes ago with the pack mind still fresh in their heads, the run back, Leah crossing the river, Jacob shouting at her. He'd heard their memories of it as they came up the lawn, bright as a photograph. And in their memories of the pack mind there was the wall too, the gray place they'd crashed into, the way they'd felt it: a blank, smooth fog the size of an afternoon.

It had been bigger.

He went back through their memories again to be sure. In Jacob's head, an hour ago, the wall had taken in the kitchen at the tent, or a part of it; a long piece of conversation at a big table; a man's voice saying something and then nothing. In Seth's, the same. Jacob had run into it and bounced off and run into it again, the way Jacob ran into most things, and every time he'd hit it, it had been the same size and the same shape. A whole stretch of the afternoon, gone gray.

In Leah's head, now, the kitchen was clear.

He could see it. The long table. The blue fridge. A man standing by the range with a bottle of beer in his hand, in shirtsleeves, with the rain coming down on the canvas roof a long way up, saying *Ask me something. Anything. Ask me my name.* And Leah, standing there in her wet cut-offs, saying *What's your name?* And the man opening his mouth to say one name, and feeling it stop in his teeth, and saying another.

The wall had moved. Sometime between the river and the porch, it had pulled back from the kitchen, the way frost pulls back off a window when the room warms up, and sealed itself again around a smaller space. A tighter one. Around the black door. Around a few things said at the table about work, and about a stone, and about a word, *strike*, that he could see the edges of and nothing else. Around the round room.

Around something about Renesmee.

He went very still.

It was only an edge. He could see where it was by the way Leah's thoughts went round it. Every time she looked across the room at the couch, where his daughter was sitting on Bella's knee with her hands on Bella's arm and her eyes wide, Leah's mind went toward something, and walked into the glass, and stopped. And every time she stopped, there was a feeling that came off her, through the wall, the only thing that came through it. A feeling he knew very well, because he'd felt it himself every single day since the morning in September when Bella had opened her new eyes.

*Not her. Not on my watch. Not ever.*

That was what came through the glass. In Leah's mind, about his daughter, a girl Leah had called a half-leech to her face in this kitchen not ten minutes ago. Fierce as a wolf at a den.

He stood behind the couch with his hand on his wife's shoulder and didn't let any of that reach his face.

*Later,* he told himself. *Find out later. Find out what she can't say.*

What he said, out loud, into the quiet that had come down after Jacob's *I hate all of you*, was the thing he could see.

"His name is Harry Potter."

---

The room turned to look at him.

Leah turned slowest. She had the fork halfway to her mouth. She put it down. She looked across the kitchen at him with her dark eyes very narrow, and he heard her think, clearly, *Get out of my head, Cullen*, and then, a half second behind it, sharper: *Wait. How did you—that was behind the—*

"It's not behind the wall anymore," Edward said.

"It was an hour ago." She said it out loud. "I felt it. In the pack. Jake ran right into it."

"An hour ago it was. It isn't now." He looked at her, and not at the glass in her head; he tried to give her that. "It's moved. It's smaller. It's only around some of it now. Around his work, I think. Whatever he does. Whoever he does it for. His name's outside it." He paused. "You were thinking about him telling you. In his kitchen. When he couldn't get the other name out."

Leah stared at him. He heard her go back to it herself, check, find the memory, find it whole. *Huh.*

"Harry," said Seth slowly, from the doorway. "His name's Harry?"

"Harry Potter," Edward said again.

On her kitchen stool by the island, Layla Quinn went absolutely still.

He heard it before he saw it. He'd been keeping half an ear on her mind since she came in this afternoon, the way you'd keep half an ear on a piano in another room, because he'd never stop finding it extraordinary: the low organ-note under her thoughts, the hundreds of words hanging in their rows. For four hours tonight it had been tired and sore and shaken, and under the shaking it had been working, the way her mind always worked, turning over the man on the ridge like a stone in her hand. *English. American badge. A gun. Wandless. Silent. Who. Who. Who.*

Now every word on every hook in her head swung at once.

Esme had made her a mug of tea. She'd been holding it in both hands on the counter in front of her for the last twenty minutes without drinking it. Her fingers opened. The mug went off the edge of the counter.

Emmett caught it about four inches off the floor. He'd been on the other side of the room by the piano. He was crouched by the island with the mug upright in his hand before most of the room had seen him move, and he hadn't spilled a drop, and he looked down at it, pleased with himself, and looked up at Layla, and stopped looking pleased.

Layla had both hands flat on the counter. Her face had gone a color Edward had only ever seen on people in the ER who'd just been told something about their own X-rays.

"Say that again," she said.

"Harry Potter," Edward said.

"Say it *slower*."

"Har-ry. Pot-ter."

She looked at him. Then she looked at Leah. Then she looked at Seth, who was looking back at her with an expression of growing alarm, and then she looked at nothing, at the middle distance over the sink, and laughed.

It was an awful laugh. It came up out of her in a single high crack and stopped. She put her hand over her mouth. She took it away. She laughed again, and this time it went on a little longer, shaky and breathless, and Carlisle, standing behind her with the jar of Murtlap in his hand, put the other hand very gently on her shoulder, the way he would have in the ER.

"Layla?" said Seth. "Babe? What—"

"No," said Layla. "No. No, no, no. Oh my God."

"What?"

"I threw a Stunner at Harry Potter." She said it to the sink. She said it like a woman reading her own name off a tombstone. "I threw a Stunner at—I threw *roots* at Harry Potter. I tried to take the Boy Who Lived out at the knees with a Bludgeoning Hex." She put both hands over her face. "I threw my *Disjunction* at him. My own spell. And he said it was *clever*. Oh my *God*."

"Who," said Leah, very flatly, "is the Boy Who Lived?"

Layla took her hands off her face and looked at her.

Edward heard it go through her head. Something like disbelief, at first, that there could be a person anywhere who didn't know. Then, close behind it, an embarrassed little correction: *No. Of course. Of course she doesn't. None of them do. How would they?* And then something careful, the curse-breaker's carefulness, picking up a heavy old object and deciding which end to hold it by.

"Okay," Layla said. "Okay. Um."

"Take your time," said Carlisle.

"Okay." She sat up on the stool. Carlisle took his hand away. She didn't seem to notice she was still in her sports bra with a bruise the size of a car door across her back; nobody else seemed to notice either. "So in Britain. In the seventies. There was a—there was a war. Our kind of war. Wizards. There was a dark wizard over there, the worst one in about a hundred years, maybe the worst ever. He called himself Lord Voldemort."

"*Lord*?" said Emmett. "Like a—like *lord* lord?"

"He made it up. His real name was Tom Riddle. He was a—it doesn't matter what he was. He was really, really bad. He wanted to rule everything. He wanted to wipe out Muggle-borns, wizards with non-magic parents, and then probably the Muggles. Non-magic people. You. He had an army. He killed a lot of people. Families. He killed whole families." She swallowed. "People over there were so scared of him they wouldn't say his name. They still won't, a lot of them. My grandmother wouldn't, and she was in Washington State."

"And the boy?" said Carlisle quietly.

"In 1981," said Layla, "on Halloween night, he went to a house in a little village in the west of England. There was a family hiding there. A young couple. James and Lily Potter. And a baby." Her voice had gone very even. "He killed the father in the hall. He killed the mother in the nursery, in front of the crib. And then he pointed his wand at the baby and used the Killing Curse."

Esme, in the kitchen doorway, made a very small sound.

"The Killing Curse," Layla said, "always works. That's what it's for. There's no shield for it. There's no counter. You can't block it, you can't heal it. If it hits you, you die. It's always been like that. Every time. Every single time in the history of magic." She looked at the counter. "Except once. It hit the baby, and it bounced. It went back the way it came and hit Voldemort, and destroyed him. Or nearly. And the baby was left in the wreck of the house with his parents dead and a cut on his forehead shaped like a lightning bolt." She paused. "He was fifteen months old. And everybody, everywhere, in every magical country in the world, has known his name since the next morning. I knew his name before I knew how to read. Every kid I grew up with did. Harry Potter. The Boy Who Lived."

Nobody said anything.

On the couch, Renesmee had both hands up against Bella's cheek. Edward heard what she was showing her mother: a house in the dark, a crib, a green light, fuzzy and shapeless because she didn't know what a Killing Curse looked like and had made it up out of Layla's words. And under it a question. *Was he scared?*

"He was a baby, sweetheart," Bella said, very softly. "I don't think he knew to be."

"And then what?" said Seth. He'd come all the way into the kitchen now. He was standing beside Layla's stool with one big hand on the counter next to hers, not touching it. "He grew up?"

"He grew up," Layla said. "And Voldemort came back."

"Came *back*?"

"It's—yeah. It's a long story. Nobody in America really knows all of it. The British are very weird about it. But he came back, somehow, when Harry Potter was a teenager, and there was a second war, and it was worse than the first one. They took over the government. They took over the *school*. And in May 1998, at Hogwarts, at the school, in Scotland, there was a battle. A big one. Hundreds of people. And at the end of it—" She stopped. "At the end of it, Harry Potter walked out into the middle of the Great Hall in front of everybody and dueled Voldemort, and killed him."

"How old was he?" said Carlisle.

"Seventeen."

The word sat in the room.

Edward heard it land in everyone. In Jasper, by the glass wall at the back, it landed somewhere old and cold, somewhere that remembered Galveston and the long years of the southern wars, a boy soldier at seventeen with a rifle. In Rosalie it landed and was turned over and put away with her mouth shut. In Emmett it lit something boyish and enormous. In Carlisle it landed in the place where he kept all the children he'd failed to save in three hundred and sixty years, and stayed there. In Jacob, sixteen when he first phased, seventeen when he'd stood on the field in June with the newborns coming out of the trees, it landed with a small, unwilling click of recognition.

In Leah it landed and went straight down the line.

Edward felt her do it. Felt her reach down the rope, without meaning to, toward the man standing at his range twenty miles off, as if she could put her hand on his shoulder from here.

"There's a thing," Layla said. "In dueling. It's stupid. It's a bar thing. Everybody who's serious about it has this list. My coach at school, Calloway, she used to make us memorize the duels. The great ones. The ones people still argue about. And she said there were three names in the twentieth century that belonged on the list, and nobody else. Dumbledore. Grindelwald. Tom Riddle." She laughed again, short. "Tom Riddle's on the list. Voldemort's on it. Calloway said he was the third best duelist of the century and the worst human being. And Harry Potter killed him at seventeen. He's the one who closed the list."

"And then?" said Edward.

He knew already. He'd heard her get there in her head a moment before she said it.

"And then he disappeared," Layla said. "There's pictures of him in the papers after the war. The British ones, the *Prophet*. Skinny kid. Glasses. Messy hair. He looked about fourteen. There's pictures of him at the trials, after, the Death Eater trials, testifying. And then about '99, nothing. He stopped being in the papers. He stopped being anywhere. People said he'd died of something the war did to him. People said he'd gone to live in a monastery in Tibet. People said he'd gone mad. The *Quibbler* said he'd gone to the moon to fight—I don't know, something, the *Quibbler*'s a weird paper." She looked at her hands. "Calloway thought he'd just quit. Burned out. She said you can't do what he did at seventeen and ever want to pick up a wand again." She was quiet a second. "I thought she was probably right."

"He didn't quit," said Leah.

They all looked at her.

She was standing where she'd been standing, with the lasagna dish on the counter by her elbow and her arms crossed over her wet tank top. Her face hadn't moved. In her head, Edward heard the ridge go past again, fast: a man on a slab of granite catching a spell in his bare hand and closing his fingers on it, and steam coming off his palm.

"He didn't quit," Leah said again. "Whatever he did. He didn't quit."

"No," said Layla. "No, I don't think he did."

"Excuse me," said Esme.

She said it quietly. She was still in the kitchen doorway with the dish towel in her hands; she'd been twisting it the whole time Layla talked, round and round, and Edward, who'd heard Esme's mind every day for eighty years, had heard what she was thinking about. A house in a village, a crib, a mother in front of the crib. Esme didn't think about her own son very often anymore. She'd taught herself not to. It had taken her a long time.

"Leah," Esme said. "Edward said—when you asked him his name. In his kitchen. And he said—" She stopped. "I'm sorry. I don't mean to pry. Edward, you said a cupboard."

Edward hadn't. He'd only thought it. Esme had been married to Carlisle for too long and lived with Edward for too long; she'd learned to read the edges of his face the way he read everybody else's head.

Leah's eyes went to Esme. She didn't say anything for a second.

Then she did. She said it flat, the way she said most things, like she was reading it off a card.

"I asked him where he was from. He said a cupboard under the stairs in a house in Surrey. Number four, Privet Drive. He said he lived in it until he was eleven." She paused. "The cupboard. Not the house. Well, both, he said."

Esme's hands stopped twisting the towel.

"His aunt and uncle," said Layla, very quietly. "It was in the papers. One of the British ones, after the war, did a big story. *The Boy Who Lived: The Untold Childhood.* He was sent to live with his mother's sister. They were Muggles. They didn't—" She stopped. "The story said it wasn't good. I didn't know it was a—I didn't know about a cupboard."

Nobody said anything. Esme folded the towel, once, twice, carefully, into a square, and put it down on the counter, and went out through the back of the kitchen toward the dining room, and Carlisle, after a moment, set down the Murtlap jar and went after her.

Edward didn't follow them with his mind. He'd learned to give them that, too.

Seth broke the silence. Seth always did.

"So he's like," he said, "a famous wizard who grew up in a closet and beat up the worst bad guy ever when he was a junior in high school. And then disappeared. And now he's in a tent on the Calawah with a vampire in his basement."

"Yeah," said Layla.

"And Lee imprinted on him."

"Yeah."

Seth thought about it.

"That's *so cool*," he said.

"Seth," said Jacob, from the far side of the island.

"What? It is! Jake, it is! It's like—it's like if Lee imprinted on—on—" He looked around for help. "Who's a really famous guy?"

"Superman," said Emmett promptly.

"Superman! It's like if Lee imprinted on Superman, if Superman grew up in a closet and then shot at my girlfriend."

"At the *ground* near your girlfriend," said Layla and Leah at the same time, and then looked at each other, and Layla's mouth twitched.

Jacob put both hands over his face.

"Who does he work for?"

Rosalie said it. She hadn't come down off the second stair. She'd stayed where she was all through Layla's story, with her arms crossed and her face cool and her mind working, and Edward had heard her mind working, because Rosalie's mind worked the way his did when it mattered: fast, practical, without sentiment, straight to the thing that might hurt them.

"A famous British wizard," Rosalie said. "With an American badge. On American soil. Who doesn't want to be found. Hunting vampires. Who's he hunting them *for*?"

Every head turned back to Edward.

He shook his head. "It's behind the wall."

"All of it?"

"All of it. Whatever he does, whoever he does it for, why he's here. Anything he said to her about it. Anything she saw in that back room." He hesitated. He didn't say the rest. He didn't say *and something about our daughter*, because he didn't know what it was, and Bella was holding Renesmee on her knee with both arms, and he could feel Bella's shield, the invisible elastic edge of it that she'd only just learned to stretch, flickering at the edges of the room the way a candle flickers in a draft. "The wall's around his work. That's all I can tell you."

"Layla," said Carlisle.

He'd come back in. Esme hadn't; Edward could hear her upstairs, in the room that had been Edward's before he'd moved to the cottage with Bella, standing at the window. Carlisle was by the island again. His face was as kind and as calm as it always was. Edward heard him under it, careful and grave.

"Layla," Carlisle said. "If he grew up to be what he was meant to be. What would he be?"

"An Auror," said Layla immediately.

"Which is?"

"Like—like a cop. A magical cop. Like a federal agent and a cop and a soldier all at once. MACUSA has them, in America. The British have them at the Ministry. That's what everybody said he'd be. It was in all the papers, after the war: *Harry Potter to join Auror Office.* Like it was a done deal. Like it was his destiny or something."

"Is that what he is?"

Layla opened her mouth.

She closed it again.

Edward heard her think it through. He heard her do it the way she'd stood in front of doorframes in Cuzco with a lamp and a magnifying glass, one mark at a time. *An Auror carries a wand. Every Auror I've ever met carries a wand, and he didn't take his out once. An Auror's a cop. A cop goes through channels. A British Auror on American soil checks in with MACUSA first, always, or it's a diplomatic incident. A British Auror doesn't carry an American badge. A British Auror wouldn't touch a gun if you paid him. A British Auror would have arrested me. Under British law. For telling Charlie. He'd have had to. He didn't. He made a deal instead. Like a man who doesn't have to answer to anybody.*

*Like a man who isn't anywhere.*

She came round to the end of it, and he heard her stop. He heard her come up against nothing, the way you'd come up against the end of a hallway in the dark, a smooth blank wall with no door in it.

"No," Layla said slowly. "No, I don't think so. I don't know what he is. He doesn't fit." She looked at her hands on the counter. "It's like I said on the ridge. It's like reading a door where somebody's scratched off every glyph. Every single thing about him that should tell you something, doesn't. On purpose." She shook her head. "I don't think I'm ever going to find out unless he tells me. And I don't think he tells people things."

"He told *her*," said Jacob, nodding at Leah.

"Yeah," said Layla. She was looking at Leah too, now, with that sharp working look. "Yeah, he did."

Leah ate a piece of lasagna.

"And it came with him," Layla said. "You said. The wall. Then it's something he's carrying." She was quiet a moment. "That makes sense, actually. If it's a binding. A big one. An old one. I've seen bindings like that on—" She stopped. "On people who weren't supposed to talk. On objects. A binding like that, when it first goes on something new, grabs everything near what it's protecting. It's dumb. It's like a dog in a strange house; it doesn't know which room is its room yet, so it lies across all the doors. And then, after a bit, it figures it out. It settles. It lets go of what it doesn't need. Like a cast drying." She looked at Leah. "Did it hurt? When it settled?"

"I didn't feel it."

"No. You wouldn't. It's on him. It's just—" She made a vague shape with one hand. "Using you. Like a shelf."

"Thanks," said Leah.

"I'd love to look at it," Layla said. "If you'd let me. I wouldn't touch it, I'd just—"

"No."

"No," Layla agreed, after a second. She'd heard it too, Edward thought. Heard the floor under it. "No. You're right. That's his."

There was one more mind in the room he hadn't listened to properly, and he made himself listen to it now.

Alice had been quiet the whole time. She was by the glass wall at the back, next to Jasper, with her arms wrapped round herself and her chin down, and her mind was doing what it had been doing since a little after noon, when Seth had first come up the drive with the witch on his arm and blood on his wrist. Looking. Looking and looking. Reaching out into the hours ahead, the way she always did, the way she couldn't not, and finding in front of her a stretch of blur so large and so dense that it made her temples ache.

*I can't see him,* she thought, and caught Edward's eye across the room. *I can't see him at all.*

He tipped his head, very slightly. *Go on.*

*The wolves blur. You know they do. They always have. Seth, Jacob, Leah, I lose them a bit, I lose the edges. It's like trying to see through a window with rain on it. But I can see* round *them. I can see what happens to us when they're there.* She frowned, and her small sharp face went sharper. *When Leah came across the lawn, I looked at tomorrow. Just to check. And her future went out. All of it. The second she came in the door. And not just hers. Everything near hers. Like somebody dropped a sheet over half the board.*

*Because of him.*

*I think so. He's in it. He's in all of it. Wherever he is, it's just—gone. And I can't see round him, Edward, the way I can with the wolves. There's no round. There's nothing.* She was quiet for a second. *I looked for him this afternoon. After Seth came back. I thought, if I can't see him, maybe I can see what he decides. Just one thing. And I got a—a coin.*

*A coin?*

*A coin. In the air. Turning over. Over and over. That's all. Just a coin in the air, and then nothing.* Her mind went small and unhappy. *He's doing it on purpose. Isn't he. He knows about me.*

Edward thought about the wall, and what was behind it. Somebody had sent this man here, across an ocean, into a town full of vampires, and that somebody knew enough to know there was a girl in the Cullen house who could see what you decided.

*Yes,* he thought back. *I think he does.*

Alice looked at him for a long moment through the room full of people. Then she turned her face against Jasper's arm, and Jasper, without looking down, put his hand over her hair.

Edward looked at Leah.

She'd finished the lasagna. She'd scraped the dish. She was rinsing it out at the sink with her back to all of them, steaming a little in her wet clothes, and in her head the rope ran away northwest, humming, and at the other end of it a kettle had boiled and a man had poured it and was standing by his range with a mug in his hand. Waiting for it to cool. Drinking it, after a while, black. Feeling, at the end of the rope, her, washing a dish in a vampire's kitchen.

He was listening to her. Twenty miles off, at the other end of that line, Edward was suddenly quite certain of it. He was listening to her the way Edward listened to everybody.

The thought did something very cold to the back of Edward's neck.

### III. Leah

*The Cullen house — 7:48 p.m.*

She dried the dish and put it in the rack and turned round, and found they'd moved on without her.

That was the first thing she noticed. They'd done the thing people did, the thing she'd watched the council do at the tribal center a hundred times when somebody brought them a problem: they'd heard the story, and been sad about the cupboard, and now they were standing round the big room in little groups deciding what to do about it. Carlisle had gone to stand by the fireplace with his hands behind his back. Rosalie had come down off her stair. Emmett had got up off the floor. Jasper had come away from the glass wall with Alice still under his arm, and he was talking low to Rosalie, and Jacob had his back to the kitchen and his arms folded and his feet planted wide like a guy getting ready to argue with a referee.

"—still one man," Rosalie was saying. "A famous one. Fine. Famous isn't bulletproof."

"He made a bullet do *that*," said Emmett. "Rose. Did you see the hole? Seth showed Edward the hole. It was like six feet across."

"So he's got a big gun."

"It was a *little* gun! That's the point! It was a tiny little old gun and it made a hole like a—"

"I don't care what kind of gun it was."

"I want to arm-wrestle him," Emmett said. "I'm just saying. If we're making a list. I want that on it."

"We're not making a list," said Carlisle.

"Everybody sleeps," said Jasper quietly.

He said it to Rosalie, not loud, the way he said most things. He had a slow southern voice that sat in the back of his throat, and when he used it for war it went slower and softer, and Leah had heard it used for war on the field in June when the newborns came. Everybody had listened to him then. They were listening now.

"Everybody sleeps," Jasper said. "Everybody eats. Everybody's got somewhere they keep the things they can't stand to lose. That tent of his, wherever it is. The room in the back. He's one man in one place, and he can be anywhere he likes in a second, and that's a problem. But one man in one place has to come home sometime."

"He doesn't come home," said Seth from beside Layla's stool. "He just *appears*. Like, out of nothing. You don't hear him."

"Then we find where he appears to."

"Leah can find it," said Jacob.

He said it without turning round. He said it to the room, like she wasn't in the kitchen behind him with a dish towel in her hand.

"She was there," Jacob said. "She walked straight in. Seth can't remember where it is, I can't remember, Layla says it's wards, fine, but Leah got through. So Leah can take us."

"Take you where?" said Carlisle.

"Up there. All of us. Tomorrow." Jacob shrugged his big shoulders. "We go up there, and we talk to him, and we make it pretty clear he's not welcome on this coast anymore, and he packs up his tent and takes his vampire in a jar and goes back to wherever—"

"Jacob," said Carlisle. "We don't know that he's a threat."

"He pinned my pack to a rock!"

"Your pack and Layla were trying to kill him at the time."

"Leah was trying to kill the *vampire*—"

"And he stopped her," said Carlisle, very mildly, "from killing the vampire. Which he'd caught. Which he'd evidently gone to some trouble to catch. And then he let her go unhurt." He looked at Layla. "And you, too. When he could have done otherwise. Couldn't he?"

Layla, on her stool, didn't say anything for a second.

"He could've killed me," she said. "In the first second. Before I knew he was there. He could've done anything he wanted with me." She pulled Seth's flannel shirt off the back of the stool and put it round her shoulders, careful of her back. "He didn't."

"There you are," said Carlisle.

"So he's a nice guy," said Jacob. "So he's a nice guy who tortures vampires in a basement and goes through people's heads and blackmails your—"

"We don't know what he does in his basement," said Edward.

He said it from behind the couch, where he'd been standing all this time with his hand on Bella's shoulder. He said it quietly. He hadn't looked at Leah once since he'd said Harry's name, and she'd noticed, because she'd been watching him not look at her. He'd been looking at the kid.

"We don't," Edward said. "None of us. It's behind the wall."

Jacob turned round then. He looked at Leah over the island.

"Then tell us," he said. "Lee. Come on. You were there. What's he doing up there? What's he *want*?"

And Leah stood in Esme Cullen's kitchen with a dish towel in her hand and looked at all of them and understood, all at once, that none of them had got it.

Not one. Not Carlisle with his hands behind his back and his reasonable voice. Not Rosalie, who thought famous wasn't bulletproof. Not Jasper, working out where a man slept. Not Jacob, who was going to march up a hill tomorrow with his chest out and tell him to pack up his tent. Not even Seth, who thought it was cool.

They were talking about him like he was a nomad who'd wandered in off the highway. A problem. Something that had come onto their land that you could make leave if you stood in a big enough line and showed enough teeth.

And they were talking about her like she'd got a crush.

"You're not getting it," Leah said.

"Getting what?"

"Any of it." She put the dish towel down. "You're standing here making plans like he's a guy. Like he's some leech from Canada we can run off. You saw him through Seth's head with your face in the moss, Jake. That's all you saw. A guy in a suit on a rock."

"I saw what he did—"

"You saw what he did with his hands in his pockets." She came round the island. "You saw him hold me and Seth flat on a rock without looking at us, and hold a vampire in the air behind him, and take everything Layla had and give it back to her, at the same time, and you think we're going to walk up there tomorrow and *make it pretty clear he's not welcome*."

Nobody said anything.

"I was in him," Leah said. "Do you get that? I was *inside* him. All afternoon. I *still am*." She put her hand flat on the middle of her chest, where the rope went in. "He's at the other end of this. Right now. He's in his kitchen. He made tea. He's drinking it black, standing up, because he doesn't sit down when he's thinking. He's got the record on, the one with the drums. And he's listening."

"Listening to what?" said Rosalie.

"To me," said Leah. "To this. To you." She looked round at all of them. "He can hear this. Right now. Through me. Whatever you say in this room tonight, you're saying it in front of him."

There was a silence in the big white room so complete she could hear the kid's heart going like a bird's on the couch.

Behind the couch, Edward closed his eyes.

"I thought so," he said quietly. "I wasn't sure. I thought so."

"So if you're going to plan something," Leah said, "go ahead. But he's in the room."

---

Jasper was the first one to move. He did it very slowly. He took his arm off Alice and turned his head and looked toward the northwest, toward the dark glass at the back of the house and the night beyond it and the twenty miles of black forest on the far side of that, the way you'd turn your head to look for somebody who'd come into a room behind you.

Then he looked back at Leah.

"Can he hear me think?" he said.

"No. Just me. Just what comes through me." She shrugged. "I think. I don't know. It's been about six hours."

"But you don't know."

"No."

He nodded slowly. She could see him file it. It went in somewhere behind his eyes, neat and cold, the same way she'd watched things go in behind Harry's eyes in the round room that afternoon, and the thought came to her, uninvited and very clear, that the two of them would understand each other a lot better than anybody in this house was going to like.

"How," said Carlisle.

He'd come away from the fireplace. He had his doctor face on, interested and careful. "Leah. How? I've heard Jacob describe it. I've watched it with Renesmee for ten weeks. I've never heard of an imprint carrying—that. Thought. Hearing. At a distance."

"Ours don't," said Jacob, low.

"His does," said Leah. "Mine does. It goes both ways." She looked at Jacob. "I told you. In the pack. You weren't listening. You heard *both ways* and you thought it was a nice thing to say. Like a card. It's both ways, Jake. Everything you've got for Nessie, all of it, the gravity and the cables and the *I'd die for her*, he's got for me. Right now. And I've got it for him. And on top of that, I can feel him from twenty miles off like he's standing next to me, and he can feel me, and he can hear what I hear, and he can't lie to me." She paused. "And I can't lie to him."

"At all?" said Bella. It was the first thing she'd said in a while.

"At all. We tried. In his kitchen. I tried to tell him I was fine and it stuck in my teeth. He tried to tell me his fake name and his mouth just—" She made a shape with her hand, a door shutting. "Said the real one."

Layla had turned round on her stool to look at her. Her eyes were very wide.

"And you know what the worst part is?" said Leah. "Out of all of it?"

"What?" said Seth.

"I can see him."

She heard how it sounded. She said it anyway, because it was the truest thing she had and it was the thing none of them were getting.

"That's what nobody tells you about imprinting," Leah said. "Nobody. They tell you about the gravity. They tell you it's like the whole world gets cut loose and there's only one thing left. They never tell you it makes you *see*." She looked at Jacob. "Sam saw Emily better than anybody ever saw her in her whole life. Better than her mom. I know. I was in his head for two years watching him see her. Every little thing. You see Nessie better than anybody in this house. Better than *him*." She tipped her head at Edward, without looking. "Don't make that face, Edward, he does, you know he does."

"I know he does," said Edward quietly.

"That's what it does. It makes you see. Inside and out. All the way down." She put her hand on her chest again. "And I can see *him*. Better than you lot are ever going to see him. Better than Layla can, with all her spells. Better than whoever he works for. Better than he sees himself, maybe. I can see what he is."

"Then tell us," said Jacob. Quieter now. "Lee. If you can see him. Tell us what he is."

She opened her mouth to tell them.

She was going to tell them about the round room. About the circle, and the vampire on its back in the dead woman's coat. About the man at the edge of the silver with his hands in his pockets, saying the names of spells out loud in a flat voice like ingredients off a card, and the Stunners going flat on the vampire's chest like water off a wall. About the word he'd said quietly, the only spell she'd heard him say all day, and what the vampire's face had done after. About the gray phone, and the voice like a kindly old uncle on Christmas morning, and the—

Something closed on her throat.

It came up from somewhere under her collarbone, cold and heavy and very old, and laid itself across her windpipe, gently, the way a hand lies on a shoulder, and the words stopped against it like water against a dam, and she stood in the kitchen with her mouth open and nothing coming out of it.

She tried again. She pushed. *There's a room.*

The hand tightened. Just slightly. Just enough.

She shut her mouth.

They'd all seen. She could tell. They'd all watched her open her mouth and try to say something and stop like somebody had thrown a switch, and Jacob had taken a step forward without meaning to, and Seth had come off the counter, and Layla had gone very still on her stool.

"Lee?" said Seth.

"I'm fine." She put her hand on her throat. Under her fingers there was nothing at all: skin, her pulse going hard. The cold thing was under that. It had let go as soon as she stopped pushing. It sat there now, under her collarbone, quiet, patient, like a dog lying across a door. "I'm fine. That's—" She swallowed. "That's his. That's the wall. It's on my throat too."

"It stopped you," said Layla. Her voice was very soft. "It physically stopped you."

"Yeah."

"From telling us what he does."

"Yeah."

"Leah," said Layla. "Do you understand what that means?"

"Yeah," said Leah. "I do. Do you?"

She looked round at all of them.

"Whatever he's got, whatever this thing is that sits on his throat so he can't tell people things, it was supposed to sit on his throat when he told *me*," she said. "It didn't. He told me *everything*. In his kitchen. Every single thing he's not supposed to tell anybody, ever. He felt it not go off. He stood there with his hand on his chest waiting for it to choke him and it didn't do anything. You know why?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Because it looked at me and thought I was him. That's what he said. It's built to keep him from telling anybody who isn't him. And it doesn't think I'm anybody else."

Nobody moved.

"So whatever he is," Leah said, "to whoever sent him here, whatever that is, I'm in it now. I'm on the inside of that wall. And so's he. So when you talk about walking up there tomorrow and running him off like a nomad, you're talking about him. And you're talking about me." She let her hand drop from her throat. "That's what you're not getting. That's the size of it."

The big white room was so quiet she could hear the rain starting up again on the glass.

---

It was Jasper who broke it. Of course it was Jasper.

"Then she's leverage," he said.

He said it to Carlisle. He said it in the same slow soft voice he'd used for everybody sleeping, reasonable, almost gentle, the voice of a man pointing out where the high ground was. Leah could see he meant nothing by it. He was doing what he'd been made to do, a long time ago, in a war in Texas, and what he'd been made to do was look at every piece on the board and work out which ones mattered.

"If it binds him the way it binds her," Jasper said, "then she's the one thing in the world he can't afford to lose. If he turns out to be a threat to this family, that's the only lever anybody's going to get on him. I'm just saying it. Somebody ought to."

The room went cold.

Leah felt it go through her. She felt the wolf come up under her ribs, fast, every hair on end, and she felt, a long way off at the other end of the line, something else come up too. The thing at the bottom of the man in the kitchen. It came up off its paws in the dark behind its three locks with its lips coming back off its teeth, and the man in the kitchen put his mug down on the range very carefully and stood still.

She didn't move. She didn't let either of them move.

"Say that again, Jasper," she said.

Jasper looked at her. He had very old eyes, under the gold. He'd been in a lot of rooms where somebody said something like that in that voice, and he knew what it meant.

"I'm not threatening you, Leah," he said.

"You're standing in a room with me talking about using me as a hostage against my imprint," Leah said. "While he's listening. You know that, right? You just heard me say he's listening. You said it anyway." She tipped her head. "That's either really brave or really dumb."

"Jasper," said Alice, under her breath.

"It's a fact," said Jasper. "That's all."

"It's a fact he just heard you say." Leah looked at him steadily. "And you know what's on the other end of this line right now? Because I do. I can feel it. It's standing up."

Jasper didn't say anything for a moment.

Then, very slightly, he inclined his head. It was the oldest, most formal little movement she'd ever seen a person make, something out of a hundred and fifty years ago, a man in a gray coat on a porch in Texas.

"I withdraw it," he said.

At the other end of the line, she felt the man in the kitchen let his breath out. She felt the thing at the bottom of him stand there another long moment with its lips back. Then, slowly, it lay down again. It didn't put its head down.

"Jesus Christ," said Emmett, to nobody in particular.

---

Jacob had been standing there through all of it with his arms crossed. She'd felt him the whole time, like a stove at her back. She could feel what he was feeling without the pack mind, without anything; she'd known him since he was a baby. He was scared. He was scared and he hated being scared and when Jacob hated being scared he did the thing he'd been doing since he was sixteen and first phased, the thing he'd learned off Sam: he got big. He got loud. He took charge.

"Okay," Jacob said. "Okay. Okay. Everybody stop."

They stopped.

He uncrossed his arms. He came into the middle of the room, between the couch and the island, and stood there in his cut-offs with his bare feet on Esme's white rug, and Leah watched him do it. Watched him put on the alpha like a coat. He'd gotten good at it. He'd been bad at it for a long time and he'd gotten good.

"Here's what we're doing," Jacob said. "Tomorrow. First light. Leah takes us up there. Me and Seth. Emmett and Jasper, if you want to come, you can come. Carlisle, you come and do the talking, because you're good at it. We go in to talk. Nobody starts anything. We tell him, nicely, he's got till the end of the week to finish whatever it is he's doing, and then he packs up and he goes. Off the peninsula. Off the coast. He can go do his wizard stuff in Oregon." He breathed in. "And Leah."

She waited.

"Until then," Jacob said, and she heard it come into his voice, the thing under his voice, the thing that came up out of the old blood when he needed it and went into you like a hook, "you stay away from him. You don't go up there alone. Not tonight, not tomorrow, not until we know what—"

"Jake," Leah said.

She said it quietly. It stopped him anyway.

"You know how this works, right?" she said.

He blinked. "What?"

"This. Imprinting. You know how it works. You're the alpha; you're supposed to know." She came out from behind the island, slowly, and came to stand in front of him on the white rug. "So help me out, because I can't remember. What was that one law?"

"What law?"

"That one law. The big one." She tipped her head and looked up at him. "The one that stopped Sam. Back in September. When he was planning to come in here with the whole pack in the middle of the night and do what he was planning to do." She didn't look at the couch. She didn't have to. Everybody else in the room did. "What was that one called again?"

Jacob's face went still.

She watched it land. She watched it go all the way through him, from the top of his head to his bare feet on the rug, and come out the bottom.

She'd been there, that night in September. She'd already left Sam by then, her and Seth both, to run with Jacob. But she'd been in Sam's head long enough, that summer, to hear him lay it out a hundred times: the Cullens were making something, something new, something nobody understood, and when it came it would be in the house on the hill, and it had to die before it grew. And on the night it came she'd stood in the trees round this house in the rain with her brother, waiting for Sam's pack to come through the woods, and they'd come. And then Jacob had walked out of the house and told Sam what he'd done in there, with his teeth out, looking at a baby, and she'd watched every single wolf in Sam's pack stop dead in the rain at the edge of the lawn. Like a wall had gone up in the middle of the forest.

The oldest law they had. Older than the treaty. Older than Ephraim. A wolf did not harm the one another wolf had imprinted on. Ever. Not for any reason. You could fight, you could hate each other, you could split the pack in two. But that one, you didn't cross. It was the only law that Sam, at the end of everything, with his whole pack behind him and the rain coming down and a monster in the house on the hill, couldn't get round.

It was the only reason the kid on the couch was alive.

"The absolute law," said Jacob. Very quietly.

"That's the one," said Leah. "Thanks."

"Leah—"

"No wolf hurts another wolf's imprint. That's how it goes, right? Ever. For any reason." She said it plainly, the way her dad used to say it at the bonfire when she was little and sitting on his knee. "That's what kept Sam out of this house. That's what's had Paul and Jared and all of them standing at the treaty line for ten weeks with their tails between their legs looking at Nessie and not able to do a thing about it. You hid behind that law, Jake. You stood behind it with her in your arms and dared them. And I stood there with you. I left Sam's pack to stand there with you. Because it was right." She took a breath. "Well. It's my turn now."

Jacob didn't say anything.

"He's my imprint," Leah said. "Harry. That law's sitting on him now, the same as it's sitting on her. You can't touch him. Seth can't touch him. Sam can't, Paul can't, Embry can't, not one wolf on this whole coast can lay a tooth on him and you know it, because if they could, she'd be dead." She lifted her chin and looked past Jacob at the rest of the room. At Jasper, by the glass. At Rosalie, at the foot of the stairs. At Emmett with his big arms. At Edward, behind the couch. "And if anybody *else* in this house goes up that hill tomorrow and tries anything, then every wolf on the coast is bound to stand between you and him. The same way they'd stand between Sam and her. Because that's how the law works. Ask Jake. He wrote the book on it."

The rain ticked on the glass.

"That's—" Rosalie started.

"That's the law Jake hid behind," said Leah. "Yeah. It cuts both ways. Turns out."

Jacob was staring at her. His mouth had come open a little. She could see him trying to find the hole in it, the way he'd try to find the hole in a fence, running up and down the length of it with his nose to the ground. And she could see him not finding one. There wasn't one. He knew there wasn't. He'd checked that fence himself, every inch, in September, on the worst night of his life, and it had held.

"And don't bother with the voice," Leah said, a little more gently. "You were about to. I heard it start."

Jacob's jaw tightened.

"You know what happens when an alpha tries to order a wolf off their imprint," she said. "Or tries to make them hurt one. You know exactly what happens. You were standing right there when Sam tried it on you." She held his eyes. "You walked out on him over it. You broke his whole pack in half rather than do it. And you were *right*."

The fight went out of Jacob all at once. She watched it go. His shoulders came down. His hands, which had been in fists at his sides, opened. He stood there on the white rug in the middle of the Cullens' living room looking at her, and for a second he looked like a kid she'd known since he was in diapers, who'd been carrying something way too heavy for way too long.

"I wasn't going to make you hurt him," Jacob said.

"I know."

"I was just—" He pushed both hands back through his hair. "Lee. I don't know what he is. None of us do. He's got a *vampire* in a *jar*. I was just trying to keep you—"

"I know what you were trying to do," Leah said. "I'm telling you, you can't. Not this one."

He looked at her a long time.

"Okay," he said finally.

"Okay?"

"Okay." He dropped his hands. "You're right. You're—God, I hate it, but you're right. I'd have killed anybody who tried that on me in September. I'd have killed *Sam*." He breathed out. "So, okay. Nobody goes up there."

"Nobody goes up there," Leah agreed.

"But if he hurts you—"

"He won't."

"*If* he hurts you, Leah, I don't care about the law, I'll—"

"He *won't*, Jake." She said it without any heat. She just said it, the way you'd say the sun was going to come up. "He can't. Think about it for one second with your actual brain. It's both ways. If you can't hurt Nessie, if Sam couldn't hurt her, if nobody in the whole world could make you hurt her even if they put a gun to your head—what makes you think he can hurt *me*?"

Jacob opened his mouth. He shut it again.

At the other end of the line, a long way off, the man in the kitchen had stopped breathing. She felt him do it. She felt him stand perfectly still by his range with his mug forgotten, listening, and she felt something come down the line from him that she didn't have a word for. Something like a man finding out the floor's still there after he's stepped off a ladder in the dark.

She didn't look at it too hard. She'd look at it later.

---

"Then," said Carlisle, into the quiet, "we ask him here."

Everybody looked at him.

He'd come all the way into the middle of the room. He was standing next to Jacob now, with his hands behind his back and his fair head a little bowed, the way he stood when he was about to say something the family wasn't going to like but was going to do anyway.

"Openly," Carlisle said. "As a guest. Here, to our door, since he hasn't invited us to his. Where he can see we've got nothing up our sleeves." He looked at Leah. "If he'd come. Would he come?"

"Yeah," said Leah.

"You're sure."

"He's curious about you. He's curious about all of you." She looked round the room, at the glass and the white walls and the seven pale faces and the kid in her duck pajamas. "He's going to want to look at you like you're in jars."

"Lovely," said Rosalie.

"He won't put you in one," Leah said. "Probably."

"*Probably*?"

"I'll ask him," said Leah. "Tonight. I'll ask him to come up in the morning."

"How are you going to ask him?" said Seth. "You don't even have his phone number. Do wizards have phones? Layla doesn't have a phone."

"I have a phone," said Layla, into her hands.

"You have a phone that's in a drawer and doesn't have a battery in it."

"That's still a phone, Seth."

"I don't need his number," Leah said.

She didn't explain. She didn't think she needed to. Half the room had worked it out already; she could see it on their faces. The other half worked it out a second later, and she watched Rosalie's mouth go thin, and Emmett grin, and Bella, on the couch, put her hand very gently over the kid's ears, as if that would help.

At the other end of the line, the man in the kitchen picked his mug back up off the range.

She felt him take a long drink. She felt him put it down. And she felt, very clearly, coming down twenty miles of rope through the dark, something that might have been a laugh, if laughs could travel. Something that said, without any words at all, *Yeah, all right. Go on, then. Ask me.*

*Tomorrow,* Leah thought, toward him. *Morning. Their house. Be nice.*

No answer came down the line in words. None needed to.

She felt him hear it.

---

Layla and Seth left at eight-thirty. Seth drove her truck, because her back was too stiff to sit up straight, and Layla lay down across the bench seat with her head in his lap and her eyes shut, and Leah stood on the porch and watched the taillights go down the drive, and felt her brother through the walls of the truck the way she'd always felt him. Happy. Worried. Happy. Driving with one hand and the other one in Layla's hair.

Edward came out behind her.

She didn't turn round. She'd known he would. He'd been waiting all evening, she thought. Waiting for everybody else to be busy.

"There's something behind the wall," he said. "About my daughter."

"Yeah."

He came and stood beside her at the porch rail. Out in the dark, the rain was coming down soft and steady on the lawn.

"Is she in danger?"

She opened her mouth to answer him, and felt the cold thing under her collarbone lift its head. Only lifting. Letting her know it was there.

She shut her mouth and thought about it.

"I can't tell you," she said. "It won't let me. I'm sorry."

"Leah—"

"Ask him." She looked at him. "Tomorrow. Ask him. He can tell you. He gets to decide. I don't." She paused. "And I think he'll tell you. I think he wants to."

Edward was very still beside her.

"She's fine," Leah said. "Right now. Tonight. She's fine."

"How do you know?"

Leah looked out at the dark. Northwest. Twenty miles off, at the end of the line, a man was rinsing a mug in a sink in a kitchen that was too big for the tent it was in.

"Because if she wasn't," Leah said, "he'd already be here."

She went down the porch steps, and across the wet lawn to the trees, and behind the cedar with the branch missing. She didn't look back. She felt Edward stand on the porch behind her, a long time, after she'd phased.

## Part Two: The Window

### IV. Harry

*The Bogachiel road — 8:52 p.m.*

The Explorer was where he'd left it, nose out in the logging turnout under the dripping alders, with a fir cone on the windscreen and the Chandler on the passenger seat. Nine hours. Nobody had touched it. Nobody came up the Bogachiel road on a Monday in November unless they'd got lost or were looking for somewhere to dump a fridge.

He stood beside it in the dark for a minute with his hand on the cold wing and listened down the line.

She was running. She'd left the Cullens' at twenty-five to nine and phased behind the garage and gone off through the trees toward the coast at a pace that would have got her a speeding ticket on the 101, and she was about halfway home. He could feel the ground going under her. Wet needles, a creek, a stretch of road she crossed in two bounds. He could feel her not thinking about him, carefully, the way you don't look at a cake on a counter.

He'd been listening to her for two hours. He'd stood in his kitchen with a mug of tea going cold and listened to her stand in a vampire's kitchen and tell a roomful of them what he was. He'd heard her tell them about the oath. He'd heard the oath close on her throat and felt it from twenty miles off like a hand on his own windpipe, and it had taken him a second to remember it wasn't on his. He'd heard the soldier say *leverage*.

He'd heard her say *the absolute law*.

He'd heard her say *what makes you think he can hurt me*.

He'd put the mug down after that. He'd stood by the range for a while.

He took his hand off the car and got in and started it, and drove it six miles round by the highway and up the forest service road on the far side of the Calawah to the gravel pull-off he used, a mile and a half below the tent. Harry Evans parked there most nights. There were bootprints in the mud of the trail going up from it, Harry Evans's bootprints, a photographer's, a man who walked up into the trees every evening with a tripod and came back down every morning with the light. He'd laid them himself, every one, in September.

He locked the car. He put the keys in his pocket. He turned on the spot.

---

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — 9:14 p.m.*

The ridge was black and wet and smelled of scorched earth.

He came out of the turn onto the granite slab where he'd stood that afternoon, and stood there again, and let his eyes adjust. They adjusted quicker than they used to. That had started with the other thing too. He could see the shape of the hemlocks against the sky, and the pale smear of the slab under his boots, and twenty feet off, the darker black of the crater.

He didn't light his wand. He didn't light anything. He stood still for a full minute and listened and smelled and let the place come up under his attention.

Rain, coming down soft. The river a long way below, loud in the gorge. A deer, half a mile off up the ridge, heading away from him. Owls. Nothing on two legs within five miles. Nothing cold within ten; the lattice would have told him, and the ring on his finger was cool.

Then he looked at the magic.

It hung over the ridge like smoke after a bonfire. His own work was gone; the sinks had eaten it as it happened, the snare and the hold on the wolves and the shot and the Apparition, the way they'd been eating everything he did on this peninsula for sixty-six days. What was left was hers. The witch's. Layla Quinn's. Every spell she'd thrown at him was still there in the air in the dark, drifting, a long gold smear from the far side of the slab where she'd Apparated in to the nurse log where she'd landed. The Stunners. The ropes. The Blasting Curse that had taken the boulder apart. The roots, which had left a residue in the moss like a stain. The Disjunction, which had left almost nothing, because it was a spell for taking things apart and it had taken itself apart on the way out, which was clever, and which he noted.

And over all of it, like a scar in the air at the far end of the slab, the place where she'd come in. Fourteen miles in one jump, frightened, with no destination, following a bead. It had torn a hole in the fabric of the ridge the size of a barn door, and the hole was still there, ragged at the edges, closing very slowly, the way a hole in water closes.

To anybody with the right instrument, from anywhere within about forty miles, the ridge above the Bogachiel had lit up at eleven fifty-two that morning like a firework.

He'd known that on the ridge. He'd known it at the time. He'd had a vampire in the air and two wolves on the ground and a witch throwing curses, and he'd decided not to stop and clean up after her because there'd been other things to think about.

Then the other things had got a great deal bigger.

"Right," he said quietly.

He started with the snare.

It was in the slab under his feet, one of the two hundred and eleven: a river stone the size of a fist, carved and buried in September in a hole under the granite he'd made with a borrowing charm. He knelt and put his palm flat on the rock over it. It was spent. It had fired once and done exactly what it was built to do and it was sitting there now in the dark like a mousetrap with the bar down. He re-set it. It took about forty seconds. He felt the knot come back up in the lattice, a bright new point on the web, and the web settle round it.

Then he stood up and went looking for the American.

---

He'd found it in September, in his second week, walking the peninsula with his pack of stones. He'd found all of them, eventually. There were four on the peninsula: one at Lake Crescent, one up the Elwha, one on the coast near Kalaloch, and one on a bald ridge three miles northeast of where he was standing, in a concrete vault the size of a garden shed with a solar panel on the roof and a white sign on the door that said PACIFIC NORTHWEST SEISMIC NETWORK — UNIVERSITY OF WASHINGTON — SCIENTIFIC EQUIPMENT — PLEASE DO NOT DISTURB.

He Apparated to it. He stood outside the door in the rain and looked at the sign and thought, as he'd thought in September, that you had to admire it.

The vault was real. The seismometer in it was real. It was a broadband instrument bolted to a concrete pier sunk into bedrock, and it was so sensitive it could feel the waves breaking at La Push twenty miles off, and it sent what it felt down a cable and up into the sky to a university in Seattle where real graduate students looked at it and wrote real papers. And bolted to the same pier, round the back, out of sight of anybody who wasn't looking very hard, there was a brass cylinder about the size of a vacuum flask, engraved with a bald eagle holding a wand in its talons.

MACUSA had been piggybacking on Muggle seismic stations since the nineteen-sixties. Somebody very sensible in New York had worked out that the best place to listen for magic was in the middle of an instrument built to listen for everything else. Any magic big enough to matter made a pressure in the world. A seismometer couldn't feel it, but the bedrock it sat on could, and a brass cylinder bolted to that bedrock by a woman in a Woolworth Building basement with a very good ear could feel what the bedrock felt.

He'd routed his whole lattice round these four in September. He'd built his sinks with them in mind. He'd been careful.

The witch hadn't known they were here. Why would she? She'd been fourteen months off the job, and in her own grandmother's town, and frightened.

He opened the vault door with a touch. He went in. It was dry and cold inside and smelled of concrete and electronics, and the seismometer sat on its pier under a padded cover like a cake under a cosy, humming to itself. He crouched behind it and put his hand flat on the brass.

The recorder was a simple thing. They always were, the good ones. It listened, and when it heard something it wrote it down, and once every twenty-four hours, at midnight Pacific time, it sent what it had written to Seattle. He'd spent an afternoon in September learning to read it without its knowing. He read it now.

*11:51:58. Pressure event. Sharp. Local. Bearing 214. Range 4.6 kilometres. Multiple components. Classification: active spellwork, sustained, two or more practitioners probable.*

*11:52:31. Pressure event. Severe. Bearing 214. Range 4.6 kilometres. Classification: Apparition, long-range, unshielded.*

And under that, in a different hand, the recorder's own idea of a priority flag. A little brass star. *Refer.*

It hadn't sent yet. It would send in two hours and forty-one minutes.

He crouched there with his hand on it and thought.

He could wipe it. It would take him a few seconds. And a recorder that had run clean for forty years and then sent Seattle a blank day with a hole in it the exact shape of a wiped log would get a great deal more attention than one that sent Seattle a bang. A woman in a basement in New York would notice a hole. That was what she was there for.

He could break it. He could make it look like the solar panel had failed, or a mouse had got in. And then somebody would come and fix it, and while they were fixing it they'd ask why it had stopped working on the one day it had something to say.

He could leave it alone. And at midnight it would send Seattle a little brass star that said two wizards had been fighting on a ridge in a national park at noon on a Monday, and one of them had Apparated in from fourteen miles off without bothering to hide it. And somebody in Seattle, who according to Croaker was already asking questions in places where questions got overheard, would get very interested.

Or he could give it a reason.

He took his hand off the brass. He went outside and stood in the rain and looked up.

The sky was low and solid and black, the cloud sitting on the tops of the ridges. It had been storming on and off along the coast all week. There'd been lightning on Thursday night out over the strait; he'd watched it from the bench above the Calawah, sitting on a log with a beer and Gary for company.

These recorders didn't like lightning. Nobody in MACUSA would have put it in a manual, but he'd sat in the vault in September and read back through three years of this one's log, and every big strike within ten miles had come up on the brass as a pressure event. Sharp, local, severe. There was something in lightning that magic recognised. Wizards had known that for as long as there'd been wizards. You brewed certain potions in a storm. Certain wands were made from things that lived in one. The witch's own wand had a feather in it from a bird that was supposed to make the weather.

The machine couldn't always tell the difference. It wrote *probable* and it put a little brass star by it and it let a human being decide.

He was going to give the human being something to decide about.

---

He went back to the ridge.

He stood on the slab and looked up at the cloud for a long time, feeling it. It was the hardest thing he knew how to do with weather, and he didn't do it often. It was easy to make rain worse. It was easy to make wind. Lightning was a different animal. The charge was already up there; there was always some up there, in a sky like this. He had to find it, and gather it, and make it want to come down in one place. It was like trying to get a cat to come to you by thinking at it.

It took him nearly six minutes. He felt the cloud start to pull together over the ridge, the charge running through it from the bottom up, a pressure in his teeth and the back of his neck, every hair on his arms coming up.

He chose the hemlock.

It was a big one, at the edge of the slab nearest the crater, a hundred and twenty feet of tree that had been standing on that ridge since before Lewis and Clark. The witch's Stunner had taken a branch off it that afternoon about twelve feet up. He felt a bit bad about it. Not very.

He stepped back off the slab, a long way back, into the trees on the far side. He made sure of the shield. He made sure his own sinks were holding over himself, because the next bit was going to be very loud, and he needed every bit of it to read as weather.

Then he let go of the cat.

It came down out of the black so bright that for a second the whole ridge was white and flat and shadowless, every needle on every tree, every drop of rain hanging in the air like it had been stopped, and the sound came with it: the sky torn in half directly overhead, a noise so big it went through his shield and his coat and his ribs and shook his teeth. The hemlock split from the top to the ground in a line of white fire and came apart down the middle like a peeled banana, and both halves stood there for a second, steaming, and then the left half went over, slowly, into the crater, with a long tearing groan and a crash he felt through the soles of his boots.

The thunder went rolling off down the valley. He listened to it go. It went down the Bogachiel and out over the flats toward Forks, bouncing off every ridge on the way, and he thought that in about thirty seconds everybody in town who was still awake would look up from their television and say *that was close*.

He came out of the trees.

The ridge was full of smoke and steam, the rain hissing on the burning halves of the hemlock, the crater full of shattered timber and smoking needles. He walked round the edge of it. He walked down into it. He crouched at the bottom, where the round had gone in that afternoon and turned the ground into a fist, and he put his hand flat on the burnt soil and pushed heat down into it. A lot of heat, in a narrow line. When he took his hand away the sand under the topsoil had fused into a twisted gray tube about the length of his forearm, glassy and rough, branching like a root.

Fulgurite. Lightning glass. Any geologist who came up here with a shovel would find it and nod and put it in a bag.

He did two more, smaller, at the edges of the crater. Then he went to the nurse log where the witch had landed and burned a long black streak down the moss on its side, the way a ground current would run along a wet log after a strike. Then he went round the ridge and took the last of her residue up out of the air with his hands, carefully, a fold at a time, like a man taking down a wet sheet off a line, and fed it down into the strike, into the hot black place in the middle of the crater where the lightning's own pressure was still ringing, so that the two of them ran together and you couldn't tell where one stopped and the other began.

He left the barn-door hole where she'd Apparated in. He couldn't close it. Nobody could; you could only wait. He tucked the edges of it under the strike as best he could and hoped.

When he was done he stood on the slab in the rain and looked at it all.

It'd hold up to a tired analyst at two in the morning in Seattle who wanted to go home. *Lightning. Precursor event at noon, probable static discharge or ball lightning, see attached strike data, 21:20 hours, confirmed visual, confirmed thunder, fulgurite on site.* It'd hold up to a man with a shovel.

It wouldn't hold up to a curious one. It wouldn't hold up to somebody who came up here on foot and stood on this slab and felt the hole.

He looked at the hole for a while. Then he Apparated back to the vault and crouched behind the seismometer and put his hand on the brass, and read it, and found what he wanted.

*21:20:07. Pressure event. Severe. Bearing 214. Range 4.6 kilometres. Classification: atmospheric discharge, probable. Correlate 11:51:58.*

The little brass star was still there by the noon entry. He hadn't expected it to go. But the machine had drawn its own line between the two, which was the best he could do.

"Good lad," he said to it, and patted it, and left.

*The tent — 10:09 p.m.*

He looked in on the specimen first. Habit.

Casimir Wolski lay on his back in the middle of the four silver rings exactly as he'd been left, rigid, in the dead woman's red jacket, with his black hair fanned out on the stone and the blackened stub of his little finger lying on the flagstone where it had been when Harry closed the circle. The eyes came round to him as he opened the black oak door. They followed him across the room and round the edge of the silver and back. There was nothing in them that hadn't been there at two o'clock. There was quite a lot of it.

"Evening," said Harry.

The eyes didn't change.

He checked the rings. He checked the shelf with the fang, back in its jar under three new seals. He checked the jar with the fingertip in it, which hadn't moved. He stood at the edge of the circle a minute longer, looking down.

"There was a woman called Dana Whitcomb," he said. "You won't remember her. You'll have had a lot of Danas."

The eyes didn't change. He hadn't expected them to.

He went out and closed the door and put his hand on it until it went *clunk*.

---

He went into the kitchen and put the kettle on, and stood at the range, and thought about Croaker.

*Anything else?*

*No, sir. Nothing else.*

He'd said it in the hall at twenty to six with his back to the kitchen doorway, while she sat at the table on the other side of it listening. He'd known he was going to say it about two seconds before he said it. He'd thought about the room on Level Nine with the brains in the tanks and he'd thought about her in it, and it had come out of his mouth as easy as breathing. He'd lied to his superior officers before. He'd lied to Kingsley more than once, for Kingsley's own good. He'd lied to the Minister. He'd lied to most of the Wizengamot at one point or another, and to every journalist who'd ever got hold of his name.

He'd never lied to Croaker. Not once in eight years.

It had never come up. Croaker didn't ask questions he didn't need the answers to, and when he asked one, he'd generally already worked out the answer and was asking to see whether you'd give it to him straight. It was a test you took every day on Level Nine whether you knew it or not. Harry had taken it every day for eight years. He'd passed it every day for eight years.

Until twenty to six this evening.

The kettle started to tick on the iron.

The old man had taken him in at nineteen. He'd been a boy with a dead godfather and a dead headmaster and a dead friend for every year he'd been at school, and a lot of nightmares, and a skill set nobody else in the Ministry knew what to do with. Kingsley had wanted him in the Aurors. Everybody had. It had been in all the papers. Harry had sat across a desk from Kingsley in the summer of '98 and listened to him explain what an Auror's career would look like, and it had looked like more war. It had looked like spending the rest of his life chasing what was left of Voldemort's people round the back roads of Britain until one of them got lucky.

Croaker had come to see him a week later. At the Burrow. In the garden. He'd stood by the gnome-infested hedge in his gray robes looking like a bald undertaker, and Molly had stood at the kitchen window with her wand in her hand, and Croaker had said, without any preamble at all: *Mr Potter. You walked into the forest knowing you were going to die. You came back. Nobody in the history of magic has done that. You don't know how you did it. Neither do I. Would you like to find out?*

He'd said yes on the spot.

He'd been finding out for eight years. Croaker had given him everything he'd asked for and quite a few things he hadn't known to ask for. Uganda. Mumbai. The Veil, and the Time Room, and the room with the brains, and the locked room he still wasn't allowed in. The old man had never once asked him to be anything except what he was. Never once asked him for a speech, or an autograph, or a smile for the *Prophet*. Never once, in eight years, told him a lie.

The kettle came up to the boil. He took it off.

He'd lied to him about the most important thing that had happened to him since the forest.

He stood there with the kettle in his hand and found he didn't like that at all.

The mirror was on its nail by the telephone.

He took it down. He stood in the hall with it in his hand, looking at his own face in it, and down at the bottom of him, under his breastbone, behind the three locks, something stood up.

He felt it come up off its belly. He felt it put its weight against the first lock, not hard, the way a big dog leans against your leg when it wants you to stay where you are. He felt it look at the mirror through his eyes.

And growl.

It went right through him. Low and long, a sound he felt more than heard, a vibration in his ribs and the back of his teeth. The thing at the bottom of him had never made a sound before. It had lain there for two months and looked at him and put its head down. It had stood up for the first time this afternoon on the ridge. Now it was standing in the dark behind its locks with its lips back off its teeth, looking at the mirror, and growling at it.

At what was on the other end of it.

"Oh, give over," Harry said.

The growl went on.

He knew what it was thinking. It didn't think in words, but it didn't need to; it was in him and he was in it and he could feel the shape of what it wanted the way you feel the shape of a stone in your boot. London was the place that had sent him here. London was the place with the room with the brains in the tanks. London was where you took things that were interesting and put them in jars and found out how they worked. He'd said so himself, in his own kitchen, to her face. *They'd have you in a jar, Leah.* And it had been there, at the bottom of him, listening, when he said it.

And now he was going to ring London and tell them about her.

"He's not going to put her in a jar," Harry said.

It didn't believe him. He felt it not believe him. It leaned on the lock a little harder.

"He's not. He's an old man in a basement. He's never put anybody in a jar in his life." He thought about that. "That I know of."

It growled.

"I'm not lying to him," Harry said. "I'm not doing it. I've done it once, and I'm not doing it again, and I'm certainly not going to sit here and keep doing it every Friday in a written report for the next God knows how long. You want me lying to him? You want me spending the rest of my life looking over my shoulder at my own boss? Because that's how people get found out. That's exactly how. You lie once and then you've got to keep lying and then one day somebody pulls the right thread." He shook the mirror at nobody. "You want to keep her safe? Then we don't lie to the one man in London who could actually keep her safe. We tell him. And then we see."

It stood there behind the locks, growling, for a long moment.

Then, slowly, it stopped. It stayed on its feet at the bottom of him with its head low and its ears flat, watching the mirror. Waiting to see.

He supposed that was the best he was going to get.

He held the mirror up.

"Croaker," he said.

The glass went gray, and then dark, and then it was the room again. The small wood-panelled room, the green-shaded lamp. It was twenty past six in the morning in London and the old man was exactly where he'd been at twenty to two, behind exactly the same drifts of paper, writing. He might not have moved. Harry had never been entirely sure he did.

There was a cup of tea at his elbow this time. That was the only difference.

"Potter," said Croaker, without looking up. "Twice in one night. I'm honoured."

"Sir."

"It's twenty past six."

"I know, sir."

"Is it the specimen?"

"No, sir. The specimen's fine."

"Then it's something else." The pen moved. "Go on."

Harry stood in his own hallway with the mirror in his hand and the thing at the bottom of him watching through his eyes, and said it.

"I lied to you, sir."

The pen stopped.

It stopped in the middle of a word. Harry watched it. He watched the old man's hand stay exactly where it was, on the paper, with the nib touching it, for a long moment. Then Croaker put the pen down, very precisely, alongside the paper, square to the edge of it, and looked up out of the mirror with the tap-water eyes.

"Go on," he said.

"When you asked if there was anything else. At twenty to two. I said no. There was."

"Yes," said Croaker. "I know."

Harry opened his mouth. He shut it again.

"You said it was the rain," said Croaker. "You did eleven days in a ditch outside Kampala in 2001 in the long rains, waiting for a Nundu, and you never once mentioned the weather. You told me it was the rain. You're the worst liar on Level Nine, Potter. It's one of the more useful things about you." He picked up his tea and drank some of it. "I've been sitting here for four and a half hours wondering whether you'd ring."

"You could've rung me."

"I could. I didn't." He put the cup down. "I find you learn more about a man from what he does with four and a half hours than from anything you could ask him. Go on. What was there?"

Harry told him.

He did it in order, the way he'd have done a report. He found he couldn't do it any other way. The two wolves on the ridge. The chase, the snare. The witch. He'd mentioned the witch at twenty to two only as *a local*; he gave Croaker the rest of it now: who she was, what she'd thrown at him, the Disjunction, the deal he'd made with her on his own account. Croaker's face didn't change at the deal. It didn't change at the Disjunction either, though Harry thought he saw the old man's left eyebrow move a sixteenth of an inch.

Then he got to the grey wolf.

He said it plainly. There wasn't another way to say it. He'd looked her in the eye, and something had happened, something the locals called imprinting, and it had happened both ways. He told Croaker about the line. He told him about not being able to lie. He told him about the oath.

That was when Croaker's face changed.

It didn't change much. Nothing in that face ever did. But Harry had worked under him for eight years, and he watched the old man go still all the way through, in a way he'd seen exactly once before, in 2003, when a junior researcher had come up from the Time Room to say that one of the hourglasses had started running backwards on its own.

"The stone," Croaker said.

"Didn't go off, sir. I told her everything. The Department. What we do. Volterra. The word *strike*. It sat there and listened and didn't touch me." He paused. "And then she went home, and her pack went into her head—they share minds, when they're wolves, all of them, it's how they work—and the stone reached down the line from me into her, twenty miles off, and walled it all up. In her head. Before they could see it."

Croaker said nothing for a long time.

"From you," he said finally. "Into her."

"Yes, sir."

"At twenty miles."

"Yes, sir."

"And it holds."

"It holds, sir. She tried to tell them about the specimen room tonight, out loud, and it closed her throat. Gently. Like it does mine."

Croaker looked at him out of the mirror. Then he looked down at the papers on his desk, at nothing in particular. Then he looked back up.

"That stone," he said, "is older than the Ministry. It's older than the Department. It's older than the Statute and the Confederation and every law we've got. It was old when the Romans found it. I've had my hand on it every day for thirty-five years, Potter, and I couldn't tell you the first thing about how it decides what it decides." He was quiet. "If it's looked at that girl and decided she's you, then she's you. I'm not going to argue with it. I'd lose."

Harry let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

"There's more, sir."

"I'd assumed."

He told him about the phone call.

He told him about putting Casimir under the Imperius and ringing Volterra on a gas-station phone and getting Gianna on the line, *Pronto*, and the old man on the other end of it, and every word Casimir had said, and every word Aro had said back. He told him he'd made the call off his own bat in about four minutes without authorisation, because the alternative was letting the specimen tell Aro something that Aro mustn't know. He told him that the Department's entire operational picture of Volterra now rested, in part, on a lie he'd told Aro through a puppet, and that if Aro ever took the puppet's hand in the spring and looked, he'd find out it had been a puppet.

"He won't take the puppet's hand in the spring," Croaker said.

"No, sir."

"Because the puppet's going to burn."

"Yes, sir."

"And what's the something Aro mustn't know?"

Harry had been waiting for that one. He'd known it was coming since he'd taken the mirror off its nail.

"I'm not going to tell you, sir," he said.

Croaker looked at him.

"It's staying out of my report," Harry said. "I won't lie to you about it. It's there. I'm telling you it's there. What it is stays with me."

"Is it a threat to the Department?"

"No, sir."

"To the operation?"

"No, sir. If anything, the opposite."

"To you?"

Harry thought about a little girl in duck pajamas on a white couch putting her hand on her mother's face. He thought about her through a pair of Omnioculars on a ridge in October, holding on to a russet wolf's fur with both fists, laughing.

"No, sir," he said.

Croaker was quiet for a while.

"You'd tell me," he said, "if that changed."

"The second it changed."

"Hm," said Croaker.

That was all. Hm. He picked up his tea and drank some more of it and put it down. Harry had seen men on Level Nine break into a sweat under that *hm*.

"Anything else?" said Croaker.

"One thing, sir."

"Go on."

"I'm compromised." He said it straight out. He'd been going to work up to it. There wasn't any point. "You should pull me. Send somebody else. Mbeki, or Cartwright. Cartwright's done vampires before, she did the Lyon nest in 2004. I'll hand over the specimen, the lattice, the keys, all my notes. I'll brief whoever you send." He took a breath. "Because if it comes down to the Department or her, sir, it's her. Every time. I can't help it and I wouldn't if I could. And you should know that about the man you've got in the field."

The hallway was very quiet. The rain drummed on the canvas a long way up. In the kitchen, the kettle ticked as it cooled.

At the bottom of him, the thing behind the locks had gone absolutely still.

Croaker looked at him out of the mirror for so long that Harry started to wonder whether the connection had frozen.

"Are you finished?" said Croaker.

"Yes, sir."

"Sit down."

"Sir?"

"You're standing up. You've been standing up this whole time. I can hear it in your voice. Sit down, Potter."

There was a little wooden bench under the telephone. Harry sat down on it.

"Do you know," said Croaker, "how many Unspeakables have rung me at six o'clock in the morning to tell me they'd lied to me?"

"No, sir."

"One."

Harry looked at him. "Who?"

"Me," said Croaker. "In 1971. To my predecessor. A man called Ashworth-Hale. I'd lied to him on a Tuesday about something I'd done in Prague, and I lay awake all Tuesday night, and at six on Wednesday morning I went down to his office in my dressing gown and told him." He picked up his pen, looked at it, put it down again. "He didn't sack me, either. He said much the same as I'm about to say to you. I didn't understand it for about twenty years."

Harry waited.

"Here's what you are, Potter," said Croaker. "You're placed. You are, as of this afternoon, the only person in the Department, and quite possibly in the world, with an open line into a community of shapeshifters that nobody in our world has known existed for three thousand years. A line they can't lie down, and you can't lie up. A line that the oldest binding in this building has looked at and approved. You're sitting in the middle of a nest of the only vampires in existence we've marked as allies, with a population of cold ones passing through on their way to and from Canada that you can snare at your leisure, and a curse-breaker in your pocket who invents her own spells." His mouth did something that might, on another man, have been a smile. "You are exactly where you need to be. I couldn't have placed you better if I'd tried. I didn't try. That's what worries me about it."

"Sir, the girl—"

"I'm getting to the girl." Croaker folded his hands on the desk. "Every other man on Level Nine, I'd have recalled for a quarter of what you've just told me. Do you know why?"

"Because it's a security risk."

"Because they'd never have told me. They'd have sat on it. They'd have put a nice tidy report on my desk on Friday, and another one the Friday after, and they'd have lied to me in every one, very well, and in about eighteen months I'd have found out by some other route and had to have them taken apart in a room downstairs. You rang me at six in the morning to tell me you'd lied to me. To tell me you'd choose a girl over your oath, and to ask me to replace you because of it." He held Harry's eyes out of the glass. "That's the whole reason I will never get rid of you, Potter. You're the only one of them I've never had to wonder about. I'm not about to start."

Harry sat on the little bench in his hallway and didn't say anything at all.

"Now," said Croaker. "The arrangement."

"Sir."

"You can't stay on that coast as a ghost. Not if you're going to be there long. And you're going to be there long. You'd be there a while whatever I did; between your cold ones and your shapeshifters and your witch and whatever it is you're not telling me, that coast has thrown up more unexplained magic in sixty-six days than the whole of Wales has thrown up in a decade." He pulled a sheet of the grey paper toward him off the drift. "So you won't be a ghost. As of—" he glanced at something Harry couldn't see, a clock on the wall, "—six twenty-eight this morning, Greenwich, you're the Department's liaison to the Quileute people of the Pacific Northwest, and to such other local magical and quasi-magical populations as may come to your attention in the course of your duties. It's a standing post. It's open-ended. It'll be in the Department's records, and in no other records whatsoever." He was writing. "It won't stop the Americans hanging you if they catch you. But a liaison is a great deal easier to explain to MACUSA than a ghost, if it ever comes to explaining. Which it had better not."

"Sir—"

"Speaking of whom." Croaker didn't look up. "The noise in Seattle."

"I've seen to the ridge." Harry told him about the brass recorder and the lightning and the fulgurite. Croaker listened without comment.

"It'll hold for a day or two," he said when Harry had finished. "It won't hold for a week. Somebody'll come. They always come."

"I know."

"Be there when they do. Be somebody else when they do. And keep the witch out of their way." The pen scratched. "Is that everything?"

"The girl, sir."

Croaker stopped writing.

"Yes," he said. "The girl." He looked up. "Does she have a name?"

"Yes, sir."

"Don't tell me it."

Harry stared at the mirror.

"I can't be made to write down what I don't know," said Croaker. "I can't be made to say it in a room with a black stone in it, and I can't have it taken out of my head by anybody who might one day take an interest in a young woman who can walk through your wards and fool our oath. I don't know her name. I'm not going to know it. You'll refer to her, in anything you send me, as your local contact. And if anybody on Level Nine ever so much as asks you what her name is, you'll send them to me." He went back to his writing. "And I'll deal with them."

"Sir," Harry said. His voice wasn't steady. He cleared his throat. "Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me. Write me a report. Friday." The pen moved. "Including the tooth."

"Yes, sir."

"And Potter."

"Sir."

Croaker didn't look up. He went on writing, in his small spidery hand, on the grey paper, under the green lamp, and he said, in exactly the same voice he'd used for everything else:

"Whatever it is you keep in the cellar."

Harry went very still on the bench.

"Let it out once in a while," said Croaker. "Things kept in cellars go off."

The mirror went dark.

---

He sat on the bench in the hall for a long time with the mirror in his lap.

He'd never told anybody. He'd never told Croaker. He'd never told Hermione, or Ron, or Ginny. He'd done it on his own in 2003 in a place nobody knew about, and he'd come back with it and never said a word, and he'd built the locks himself and checked them every morning, and in three years nobody in the Department had ever looked at him twice.

Croaker had signed off on his medicals every year.

Croaker had read his expense claims, which had gone up by about a third the year he started eating like a horse.

Croaker had sent him to a coast full of shapeshifters.

"Christ," Harry said, out loud, to the empty hallway.

At the bottom of him, behind the three locks, the thing in the dark had stopped growling. It had been listening. He could feel it had been listening to every word, the way he'd been listening to her for two hours this evening down the line, and it had heard the old man say *don't tell me it*. It had heard him say *I'll deal with them*. He could feel it turning that over, in the slow huge way it turned things over, like a big animal sniffing at a stranger's hand.

Then it lay down.

It lay down behind its locks with its chin on its paws, the way it had for two months. Except it kept its eyes open, and it kept them pointed southwest, toward the coast, toward a little house he'd never seen, with a woodshed out back and a dented Ford in the drive, where a woman was lying on her back in a narrow bed in the dark with her eyes open.

He hung the mirror back on its nail.

He went to bed.

---

### V. Leah

*The Clearwater house, La Push — 11:41 p.m.*

The ceiling of her bedroom had a water stain on it shaped like Oregon.

She'd been looking at it for two hours. She knew it better than she knew Oregon. It had been there since she was eleven, since the winter the gutter over the back porch froze and backed up and her dad got up on a ladder in a rainstorm swearing at it in Quileute, and her mom had said they'd paint over it in the spring, and the spring had come and gone eight times and nobody had. Oregon had a long bulge on its eastern side that went down into a point, like Idaho had been squeezed out of it. There was a darker patch near the top that she'd decided when she was twelve was Portland.

She'd come home at five past nine and phased behind the woodshed and come in the back door. Her mom had been in the kitchen. Her mom had looked at her across the kitchen, over a pot of something on the stove, for about five seconds without saying anything, and then said, "There's hot water. Go and stand in it." So she'd gone and stood in it, for twenty minutes, until it ran cold, and then she'd put on a T-shirt of her dad's that came down to her knees and the gray sweatpants with the hole in the knee, and come in here, and lain down on top of the covers.

She'd been lying there ever since.

She wasn't tired. That was the thing. She'd been up since five. She'd run the eastern loop and chased a vampire for six miles and been pinned to a rock and run twenty miles to a tent and twenty miles back and stood in the Cullens' kitchen and told Jacob Black where he could put his alpha voice. She'd eaten six eggs and half a loaf and most of a lasagna. She ought to have been asleep before her head hit the pillow.

She lay there with her eyes open, looking at Oregon, and the line in her chest pulled.

It pulled northeast. Up through the ceiling and the roof and the wet black sky, across the river and the highway and twenty miles of forest, up a hill, to a little green tent with moss on the guy lines. She could feel the exact place it went. She could have got out of bed and walked there in the dark with her eyes shut.

And at the end of it, he was awake too.

She could feel that as well. He'd gone to bed. She'd felt him do it, a while after he'd come back from wherever he'd gone: some ridge, some weather, a sound like the sky tearing that she'd heard all the way down here, a long roll of thunder off to the east at twenty past nine that she'd heard through the bathroom wall over the noise of the shower. Then a long time in his hallway, talking to somebody. An old man. She'd felt the shape of the old man come down the line through him: dry and patient and very, very sharp, like a stone with an edge on it. She'd felt Harry stand in his hall and say things to the old man that made her sit up in bed. She'd felt the thing at the bottom of him growl.

Then she'd felt it go quiet. Then she'd felt Harry sit on something hard for a long time without moving.

Then he'd gone to bed. And he was lying in it now, in the dark, in a narrow bed in a little room like a soldier's, on his back, with his eyes open. Same as her.

She turned on her side, away from the window. She shut her eyes.

The line went on pulling.

She turned on her other side, toward the window. She opened them again.

"Oh, come *on*," Leah said, out loud, to Oregon.

---

The thing nobody had said, about Sam, was that you got used to it.

She'd lain in this bed a lot of nights the last two years with the wire in her chest running down the coast to the little house behind the Ateara place. Every single night. Two years of nights. The wire had been there when she went to sleep and there when she woke up. It had been there when she was angry and when she was sad and when she was so tired she couldn't feel her own legs. You got used to it the way you got used to a bad knee. It was just how things were. It hurt when it rained.

She went looking for it now. She couldn't help it. She went looking for it the way you'd put your tongue in the hole where a tooth used to be.

It wasn't there.

She went all the way down the coast in her head to the little house, and there was nothing running there. Sam was in it. She knew Sam was in it; she could have told you which room. He'd be asleep on the left side of the bed with his arm over Emily, the way he slept, the way she'd watched him sleep in his own memories ten thousand times. She could think about him. She could see his face. She could think *Sam* as loud as she wanted, *Sam Uley, Sam, Sam*, and it was just a name. A guy she'd gone out with. A guy who lived down the road.

It didn't hurt.

She lay there and waited for it to hurt. She was sure it would. She was sure, any second, it would come back, all of it, like a wave coming back in after it goes out, the way it always had. She lay there with her eyes on the ceiling and every muscle braced for it.

It didn't come.

The other line hummed on, running the other way. Northeast, up the hill, into the tent.

And down at the bottom of him, at the far end, the big thing in the dark lifted its head, and looked at her up the whole length of the line, from twenty miles off, as if it had felt her go looking for Sam and wanted to know why.

*Nothing,* Leah thought at it. *Never mind. Go back to sleep.*

It didn't go back to sleep. It kept looking.

---

There was a knock on the door.

It was a very small knock. Two knuckles, twice. Her mom's knock. Her mom had been knocking on her door like that since Leah was thirteen and had first started locking it, and it had never once been louder, even the nights it should have been.

"Yeah," said Leah.

The door opened. Her mom stood in it in her bathrobe with the light from the hall behind her. She didn't turn the light on in the room. She stood there a second, looking at Leah lying on top of the covers in the dark in Harry Clearwater's old T-shirt, and then she came in and shut the door behind her and sat down on the end of the bed, by Leah's feet, the way she used to when Leah was little and had a nightmare.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Billy called," said her mom. "About nine-thirty. While you were in the shower."

"Jake called Billy."

"Jacob called Billy from the Cullens' telephone. Billy said Jacob was very upset. Billy said Jacob wouldn't stop saying a word." Her mom paused. "The word was *Leah*."

Leah didn't say anything.

"And then Seth called," said her mom. "From that girl's house. About ten. He said I should come and knock on your door. Around now. He said you'd be awake."

"Seth's a snitch."

"Seth's a good boy," said her mom. "Seth said you'd say that."

They sat there. The rain was coming down on the roof, soft and steady, and somewhere out past the trees, down at the bottom of the reservation, the sea was going in and out on First Beach. Leah could hear it if she listened. She could always hear it.

"Billy said you imprinted," said her mom.

"Yeah."

"On a white man."

"He's English."

"Billy said that, too. Billy wasn't sure if it made it better or worse." Her mom smoothed the bathrobe over her knees. "Billy said he was a—" She stopped. Leah watched her try to find the word, and decide not to say it, the way her mom decided not to say lots of things. "Billy said he was something like the girl. Like Layla."

"Kind of."

"Kind of."

"He's like Layla if Layla was—" Leah tried to think. There wasn't anything. "A lot."

Her mom was quiet.

"Is he good to you?" she said.

Leah opened her mouth to say *yeah*, and then didn't, because she wasn't sure that was true. She didn't know if it was true. She'd known him for about twelve hours. He'd called her Lassie. He'd pinned her to a rock with her face in the moss. He'd made her eggs. He'd stood at the edge of a silver circle with his hands in his pockets and done things in a round room that she couldn't have told her mom about even if the cold thing under her collarbone let her.

He'd put his hand on her face in the dark and told the thing at the bottom of him *not today*, and it had listened.

"I don't know," Leah said. "I don't think he'd say he was good. I don't think he'd say that about himself."

Her mom looked at her.

"But I can't lie to him," Leah said. "And he can't lie to me. That's—it does that. Ours does. It's different from the others. We tried, in his kitchen. It sticks. Right in your teeth." She looked up at the ceiling. "So whatever he is, I'm going to know. All of it. I already know a lot of it."

Her mom didn't say anything for so long that Leah turned her head on the pillow to look at her.

Her mom was smiling. Barely. It was in the corners of her mouth, in the dark.

"Your father couldn't lie to me either," she said.

"Mom."

"He tried once. In 1983. Before you. He bought a share in a fishing boat with Jimmy Ateara without telling me, eleven hundred dollars, which was all the money we had in the world. And he came home and I asked him where he'd been, and he opened his mouth to tell me he'd been at the tribal center, and he just—" She laughed, very quietly. "His face. He went red right up to his hair. And he said, *Sue, I bought half a boat*. It was a terrible boat. It sank in 1986."

Leah stared at her.

"He never tried again," her mom said. "Not once in twenty-three years. He used to say there was no point. He said I'd have known anyway." She smoothed the bathrobe again. "It's not the worst thing in the world, Leah. Not being able to lie to somebody. It saves a lot of time."

Leah looked at her mom sitting on the end of the bed, in the dark, in the bathrobe she'd had since before Seth was born, and something came up in her throat that she had to swallow twice to get back down.

"What's his name?" her mom said.

Leah thought about not telling her. It came and went in about a second. There wasn't any point in that, either.

"Harry," she said.

Her mom went still.

She went completely still, on the end of the bed, with her hands on her knees. Leah watched it happen. She watched her mom's face, in the light from under the door, stop moving, every part of it, the way a deer stops in a road.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Oh, no. I didn't—*

She hadn't thought about it. That was the stupid thing. Twelve hours, and she'd heard the name a hundred times in her own head and said it out loud in the Cullens' living room and heard Edward say it and Layla shout it, and she'd never once put it next to the other one. The other Harry. The one whose T-shirt she was wearing. The one who'd bought half a boat. The one who'd been in this house on a night last spring, when she'd phased for the first time screaming in the yard. Who'd maybe seen it, and maybe not; she'd never know. Whose heart had stopped in the next room, and who'd been dead on the kitchen floor before the ambulance got past Forks.

"Mom," she said. "Mom, I didn't—I'm sorry, I didn't even—"

"No," her mom said. "No. Don't be sorry."

"I didn't think—"

"Hush." Her mom put her hand on Leah's ankle, through the sweatpants. Her hand was warm and dry and she held on tight. "Hush, baby. It's just a name."

She held on to Leah's ankle. Leah lay there and let her. Neither of them said anything for a long time.

"It's a good name," her mom said finally. Her voice was a bit rough. "It's a good, plain, sensible name. Your grandmother picked it out of a Sears catalogue. She liked the man in the picture, wearing the work boots." She was quiet a second. "Your father would have thought it was very funny."

"Funny?"

"Oh, yes. He'd have laughed for about a week." Her mom took her hand off Leah's ankle and wiped her eyes with the back of it, quick, the way she did everything. "He'd have said, *Well, she always was a daddy's girl.*"

"*Mom.*"

"He would have!" Her mom was laughing, sort of, and crying, sort of, both at once, very quietly, there in the dark at the end of the bed. "He'd have said it at the dinner table, in front of everybody. Right in front of the boy. And you'd have thrown a fork at him."

Leah put both hands over her face.

"You'd have missed," her mom said. "You always missed."

---

Her mom left at a quarter past twelve.

She stood up off the end of the bed and stood there in the dark a moment, looking down, and then she bent over and kissed Leah on the forehead, which she hadn't done since Leah was about fourteen, and Leah let her.

"Bring him to dinner," her mom said at the door.

"Mom, I don't even know if he—"

"Bring him to dinner, Leah. Sunday. I'll make the salmon." She paused with her hand on the doorknob. "Does he eat?"

Leah thought about the fry-up. Six eggs, for her. Eight for him, standing at the range, before she got there. The plate he'd cleared at the diner, in Seth's memory of Layla's memory of the cheeseburger smell on him on the ridge.

"Yeah," she said. "He eats."

"Good," said her mom. "I don't trust a man who doesn't."

She went out. The door shut. Leah heard her go down the hall, and the bathroom light click on, and the tap run, and the bathroom light click off. She heard her mom's door close.

Then she lay there in the dark and listened to the house.

It was a small house and she knew every sound it made. The fridge in the kitchen, humming. The clock on the wall in the hall, which was five minutes fast and always had been. Seth's empty room next door, which made no sound at all, which was a sound of its own; he was at Layla's, on the couch, with Layla asleep on her stomach across his legs because she couldn't lie on her back, and he was awake too, she thought, she'd have bet money. Stroking Layla's hair. Thinking about his sister. The rain on the roof. The sea, a long way off.

And the line.

Northeast. Humming.

At the far end of it, he hadn't moved. He was still lying on his back in his narrow bed with his eyes on the ceiling. She could feel him, the shape of him, like a hand feels the shape of a cup in the dark. She could feel he'd heard. Every word. Her mom, the name, the boat, the Sears catalogue. Her dad on the kitchen floor. She could feel him lying there with it, very still, the way he'd stood by his range earlier tonight with his mug forgotten while she told Jacob about the absolute law.

She could feel he didn't know what to do with it.

That made two of them.

*Go to sleep,* she thought, up the line. *Harry. Go to sleep.*

He didn't.

Neither did she.

At ten to one she was still lying there, looking at Oregon. At ten past one she got up and went to the window and stood there with her forehead against the cold glass, looking out at the dark yard and the woodshed and the black wall of the trees beyond it, with the rain running down the outside of the window in long wiggling lines. At a quarter past one she got back into bed. Under the covers this time. She pulled them up to her chin and lay on her side and shut her eyes and breathed slowly, in and out, in and out, the way the school counselor had made her do in eleventh grade after her dad, and counted.

She got to two hundred and six.

At two hundred and seven, at the far end of the line, twenty miles off, she felt him get up.

---

### VI. Harry

*The tent — Tuesday, November 21st, 1:16 a.m.*

He'd been lying there since twenty to eleven.

The room was small. He liked it small. It had a bed, a chair, a hook on the back of the door, and a shelf with six books on it that he'd read so many times the spines had gone white. No window. There was no outside for a window to look at; the room was folded into the inside of a two-man ridge tent somewhere in the middle of four nested expansion charms, and the nearest real night was forty feet away on the other side of the canvas. He'd put up a lamp on the wall that he kept on low. He'd never been able to sleep in proper dark. He'd stopped trying to work out why around 2001.

He lay on his back with his hands on his chest and looked at the canvas of the ceiling.

Two hours and thirty-six minutes.

At the bottom of him, behind the three locks, the thing in the dark lay with its chin on its paws and its eyes open, pointed southwest.

He'd heard the mother come in.

He hadn't meant to. He hadn't meant to listen to any of it. He'd lain down and shut his eyes and tried to do the thing he did on jobs when he needed to sleep and couldn't, the counting thing, the breathing thing, the trick an old Healer in Mumbai had taught him for shutting your mind like a door. It had worked on him for five years. It had worked on him in a ditch outside Kampala and on a cot in a basement in Minsk with a dead man in the next room.

It didn't shut on the line.

So he'd heard the knock. Two knuckles, twice. He'd felt her say *yeah* to it. He'd felt the bed dip at her feet as somebody sat down on the end of it, and he'd felt Leah lie there in the dark looking at a water stain on her ceiling while a woman with a voice like a slow river told her that Billy had called.

He'd heard *Is he good to you?*

He'd heard her say *I don't know. I don't think he'd say he was good.*

He'd lain there and thought: *no. He wouldn't.*

He'd heard about the boat. Eleven hundred dollars and a fishing boat and a man going red right up to his hair. He'd heard *What's his name?*, and Leah say it, and he'd felt, down twenty miles of line, a woman on the end of a bed go so still that Leah's whole body had gone cold with it.

And then, because Leah's head was open to him like a door left on the latch, he'd found out why.

He'd seen it before she'd finished thinking it. A kitchen. This house, the house he'd never been to. A kitchen with a yellow lino floor. A night in the spring. A girl screaming in a yard, in a voice that wasn't a voice. A man in the next room with his hand on his chest. A man on a yellow lino floor. A man called Harry.

He'd lain there with that.

He'd had a lot of things done to him by his name in his life. He'd been famous for it before he could walk. He'd had it shouted at him in the Great Hall by four hundred people at once, and whispered at him in corridors, and printed on the front of every newspaper in Britain, and carved, once, into a desk in the Hogwarts library by a second-year Hufflepuff with a crush. He'd had it on a gravestone in a churchyard at Godric's Hollow, under his father's. He'd been called it by a dying man in a forest, kindly, and by Voldemort, coldly, and by Dumbledore, on a platform at King's Cross that wasn't King's Cross, as if it were a question.

He'd never had it belong to somebody else first.

He'd never been the second Harry in a house.

He'd lain there and thought about a man he'd never met and never would, who'd bought half a boat, and couldn't lie to his wife, and laughed at the dinner table. Who'd have thought it was very funny. Who'd have said *Well, she always was a daddy's girl*, in front of everybody, right in front of the boy, and had a fork thrown at him.

*Right in front of the boy*, the mother had said. Meaning him. Meaning a man she'd never met, sitting at her dinner table. Like there'd be a dinner table. Like it was settled.

*Bring him to dinner. Sunday. I'll make the salmon.*

*Does he eat?*

He'd lain there with that, too.

At ten to one she'd got up and gone to the window. He'd felt the cold glass on her forehead. At a quarter past one she'd got back into bed, under the covers this time, and started counting. He'd lain there and counted with her. He hadn't meant to do that either. He'd got to two hundred and six.

*Go to sleep*, she'd thought, a while ago, up the line. *Harry. Go to sleep.*

She'd said his name like it was a word she was getting used to the taste of.

At two hundred and seven he got up.

---

He dressed in the dark, by the low light of the lamp. Jeans. A flannel shirt. Wool socks, his boots. The shoulder rig, from its hook on the back of the door, with the Colt in it; he'd never gone outside on a job without it and he wasn't starting tonight. The waxed jacket over the top.

He didn't think about why he was getting dressed. He didn't think about what he was going to do once he was. He just did it, the way you get up in the night for a glass of water, without deciding to.

He went down the hall in the dark. Past the library with the fire banked to embers in the grate. Past the kitchen, the blue fridge humming. Past the telephone, and the little bench under it, and the mirror on its nail. He looked at the mirror as he went by.

*Whatever it is you keep in the cellar.*

He went up the three wooden steps and out through the flap.

It was raining. Of course it was. A soft fine rain, hardly more than a mist, hanging in the dark under the spruces and beading on everything. The river was loud below the bench. The wards sat round him in the dark like a dog asleep across a doorway.

Gary was on the canvas by the door. He'd moved about four inches since this evening.

"Don't," Harry said to him.

Gary didn't.

He walked out into the middle of the bench, away from the tent, onto the moss under the biggest of the spruces, and stood there.

He put his hand flat on his chest. Low and to the left. Where it lived.

---

He'd built the locks in September, in this clearing, the night before he laid the first stone.

He'd sat on that log over there with a bottle of whisky he didn't drink and thought about it for a long time, because there was nothing about it in any book. Nobody had ever written down how to lock up something like this. Nobody had ever needed to; nobody had ever had one. So he'd made it up, the way he'd made up most of the things that mattered, out of three other things he did know how to do.

The first lock was the scent. He'd braided it into the Occlumency, into the shields he'd been building in his own head since he was fifteen, so that whatever the thing gave off went in under the shields and stayed there, folded small, like a letter in a locked drawer. That was the one that had kept a pack of wolves with noses like bloodhounds from smelling it on him in a petrol station for two months. That was the one that had slipped, a little, on the ridge, when the grey one had stood next to him breathing in hard.

The second lock was the body. That one was old work, borrowed and bent: the first half of a Human Transfiguration fixative, the kind they used on St Mungo's long-stay wards to stop people with bad curses changing shape in their sleep. He'd taken it apart and put it back together pointing inward. It held the bones where they were. It held the skin on.

The third lock was the will.

That was the one he'd made up out of nothing. That was the one that sat on the thing itself, on the part of it that wanted, and said *no*. Every morning for sixty-six days. *No. Lie down. Not today.* He'd built it out of the same stuff he used to throw off an Imperius, which was the only thing he'd ever found that could hold a want down by main force. It was heavy and cold and it took effort, every single day, a little, like holding a door shut against a wind.

He'd leaned on all three of them this afternoon, on the ridge, when the line went in. He'd leaned on them in front of the tent, when she came. He'd leaned on them in this clearing four hours ago with his hand on her face and every one of them groaning, and they'd held.

He stood there in the rain with his hand on his chest.

*Let it out once in a while*, said a dry old voice in his head. *Things kept in cellars go off.*

He opened the first lock.

It went with a feeling like a breath let out. He felt the scent come up out of the drawer where he'd folded it and go out of him into the night. He smelled it himself, for the first time in two months. It came off him like heat off a road. Something under the pine and the coffee and the cold stone, something big and wild and old, the smell of the place in the woods where a big animal has slept, the smell of the dark under the trees before there were people.

At the bottom of him, the thing behind the locks lifted its head.

He opened the second lock.

That one hurt, a bit. It always had, the few times he'd done it. It was like taking off a cast. Everything in him that had been held in place for two months went loose all at once, every bone, every joint, every muscle, a long shivering slackness through his whole body, and he stood there swaying a little in the rain with his hand on his chest, feeling himself go soft at the edges like a candle near a fire.

The thing at the bottom of him stood up.

He felt it stand. He felt it come up onto all four feet behind the last lock, behind the will, the heavy cold door he'd been holding shut every morning for sixty-six days, and stand there, very still, with its head up. It had pushed on the ridge and pushed in front of the tent and pushed in the clearing at half past six with her face under his hand, and every time he'd held, and every time it had gone back down.

It stood there now with its weight off the door, waiting to see what he'd do.

"Yeah," Harry said quietly. "Yeah, all right."

He opened the door.

---

It came up through him like a diver coming up through deep water.

That was the nearest he'd ever got to it, the three times he'd done this before: a long rising rush from somewhere very far down, cold and dark and pressing, and then the surface, and the light, and the air. It came up through the middle of him and filled him and kept coming. It went out along his arms and his legs and up his spine. It went into his teeth. It went into his eyes.

And he went with it.

He felt his spine lengthen first. It was the strangest part, every time; he felt it go long, longer, a long rolling stretch like a man waking up after a hundred years, and his weight went forward off his heels and down. His hands met the moss. He felt them change on the way down, the fingers drawing in and the palms thickening into pads, and the moss was suddenly very far away and then very close. His shoulders went wide and deep. His chest went deep. The rain on his back stopped landing on canvas and wool and started landing on fur, thick and heavy and close, and he felt each drop land, every single one, a tiny cold tap all down the length of him.

His face went long. His ears went up.

And the night came in.

It came in all at once, through his nose, so hard it was almost a sound. Spruce, a thousand different spruces, every one its own smell, old and young and dying. The river, which smelled of snowmelt and granite and salmon that had died upstream a month ago and were still coming down. Wet earth, rotting wood, ferns, fungus, a thousand kinds of fungus. An elk that had crossed the bench at dusk. A marten in a hollow log forty yards away, asleep. The tent, which smelled of canvas and bacon and gun oil and him. The slug.

And under all of it, faint, threaded through the rain from the southwest like a single bright string, her.

He stood there and breathed it.

He stood on the bench in the rain on four feet and breathed in and looked at the night, which had gone every shade of gray there was, sharp and clear, every needle on every branch. The tent was very small. It came up to about the middle of his chest. Gary was on the canvas by the flap at about the height of his knee, if he'd still had knees, gleaming.

He looked down at himself.

Black. He'd always been black. A deep flat black from his nose to the end of his tail, the black of the bottom of a well, with a dark charcoal under-layer down close to the skin where the rain hadn't reached. He lifted one forepaw out of the moss and looked at it. It was bigger than his whole human hand spread out. Bigger than a dinner plate. The pads on the underside were black and thick and cold, and the claws, when he spread the toes, came out of the fur a long way, curved and dark.

He put it back down. It didn't make a sound.

He turned his head and looked at the biggest of the spruces, the one he'd camped under for sixty-six days. There was a branch on it he'd hung his towel on most mornings, a long dead lower limb that stuck out at about the height of the top of his head. It was a little under the height of his shoulder now.

He'd watched the russet one through the Omnioculars in October, from a mile off, standing on the Cullens' back lawn with the little girl on his back. He'd measured him by the porch rail. A little under seven feet at the shoulder, give or take. Bigger than any wolf had any right to be. Bigger than a shire horse.

He stood a head above that. Near enough. And from his nose to the end of his tail he'd have gone close on twelve feet.

He didn't know why. The first time, in 2003, he'd been smaller than this. It had grown. Every year it had grown, a little, the way he'd grown four inches after he was eighteen, the way his magic had grown, as if every bit of him were taking its time to come in. He'd stopped trying to work out why round about the same time he'd stopped comparing himself to anybody.

He felt like one thing. He'd been afraid of two, the first time, in 2003, standing in the wet heather on a hillside. He'd thought it would be like the Imperius, or like Voldemort in his head, somebody else at the wheel. Every time, the hands on the wheel had been his own. It was him. It had always been him. It was the part of him he'd kept in the cellar, and now it was up the stairs and in the kitchen and standing in the doorway with its ears up, and it was him, and he was it, and for the first time in sixty-six days there wasn't a single thing in him pulling in a different direction from anything else.

All of it pointed southwest.

All of it.

He put his nose up into the rain and found the string of her, and went.

---

He went through his own wards without slowing down. They opened for him. They knew him; he'd made them. They opened round him like water round a fish and closed behind him, and he was off the bench and down the slope through the big trees, flat out, with the rain coming at his face.

He'd never run like this.

The first time, in Scotland, he'd been too frightened to do more than walk. The second, in Wales, in 2004, he'd gone round a hill once at a trot and come back and locked it up again for two years. The third, in a forest in Romania in 2005 on a job nobody had asked him to do, he'd run for about a mile and then stopped, because he'd come over a ridge and seen a village down in the valley with its lights on and been suddenly, coldly certain that if anybody in that village looked up and saw him he'd have to do something about it.

He'd never just run.

He ran now. Down through the spruces to the river. Across the north fork in one long bound from a boulder on one side to a gravel bar on the other, twenty feet of black water going by under him. Up the far bank and through the old-growth on the other side, through ferns that came up to his chest and brushed his flanks and sprang back behind him, and he didn't make a sound. That was the thing he noticed, after a while, the thing that stopped him thinking for a second. He was the size of a horse and a half and he was going through the forest at forty miles an hour, and he didn't make a sound. His feet came down where his feet wanted to come down and he felt the ground under each one before it took his weight, every twig, every stone, every root, and he put his weight on it like pouring water into a glass, and nothing cracked. His hind feet came down in the marks his forefeet had left. Nothing on the ground behind him said he'd been there except a line of prints in the moss.

West. Then southwest. Over the ridge between the Calawah and the Sol Duc, through a stretch of clear-cut where the stumps stood up out of the slash in the dark like gravestones, and down into old trees again on the far side. The Sol Duc, wider and louder; he crossed it at a riffle where the water came up to his chest and went past him cold and hard as a shove. Across the highway, two lanes of empty blacktop, in one jump. Through a field of wet grass where four elk lifted their heads and stood absolutely still and watched him go by and didn't run, because they couldn't work out what they'd seen.

The string of her got stronger. It got brighter. He could smell the sea now, under her, salt and kelp and cold.

He came down off the last of the high ground toward the coast at twenty-five past one, and smelled the other wolves.

---

There were two of them, on the line where the reservation started, a mile or so inland, running a slow patrol in the dark. He'd known they'd be there. He'd had them on the lattice for two months, the western pack, seven or eight rust-coloured dots around the reservation, and there were always two out at night on that boundary. He'd watched them go round and round it like the hands of a clock.

He'd never smelled them like this.

They smelled like her. Like her and not like her. Like the same family, a lot of cousins, all with the same thing underneath: the old wild smell from the bonfire stories, the smell of something that had been here before the people. And on top of that, each of them, their own. One was younger, a bit sour, a bit bored. The other was older, heavier, sadder. Coffee and wood smoke and motor oil and a woman's shampoo, and under it all something that had been sitting in the same place a long time, like a stone in a shoe.

He knew who that was. He'd been inside Leah's head for twelve hours. He'd watched her watch that man sleep ten thousand times in her own memory of his memory.

He went still in the trees.

He thought about it for about half a second. Then he did what he'd have done on a job, which was to make himself not there. He wrapped the Disillusionment round himself from his nose to his tail, close, the way you'd pull a cloak round you, and felt the rain start to go round him instead of landing on him, and he folded his scent down small again, under the shields, for a minute, with the drawer left open. And he went on, slower, in a long curve to the south that would take him past the patrol line at a decent distance.

He didn't go far enough. He realised that when he was halfway round. The older one had changed direction. It was coming down the line toward him, not fast, nose down, the way you'd walk over to look at something you'd half seen in the corner of your eye.

He stopped. He stood absolutely still in the dark under a big cedar, with the rain sliding round him like water round a stone, and let it come.

It came out of the trees about forty yards off. A wolf, black, bigger than the grey one, smaller than him by a fair bit; he'd have put it at about the russet one's size. It stopped at the edge of a little clearing with its head up and its ears forward, and looked straight at the cedar he was standing under, and breathed in. Long and slow.

Harry didn't breathe at all.

The black wolf stood there for a long moment with its nose working. He could see its nostrils flaring in the dark. He could see it trying. It had smelled something; it couldn't find it again. It smelled pine, and cedar, and wet stone, and that was all there was to smell, because that was all he was letting there be.

And because he was standing forty yards from it, and because its mind was as open as the grey one's had been on the ridge, and because he couldn't help himself, he looked.

It was a big, heavy, tired mind. It had a lot in it. Most of it was a woman with three long scars down one side of her face, asleep in a bed a mile away with one hand under her cheek. And a lot of it was the same thing it had been thinking about every single night for two years, round and round, a rut worn so deep in it you could have lost a cart in it.

But tonight there was something new on top of the rut. It had come in at about half past nine. A telephone call. An old man in a wheelchair, Billy, ringing round the council the way he did when something happened, and saying a sentence that the black wolf had been turning over ever since.

*Leah imprinted.*

And what the black wolf thought about it, standing there in the rain at the edge of the clearing with its nose up, was so simple and so loud that Harry heard it without even trying.

*She's free. Thank God. Oh, thank God, Lee. Thank God.*

The black wolf stood there a moment longer. Then it shook its head, a long shuddering shake that sent the rain off it in a spray, and turned, and went back up the line the way it had come, toward the younger one, slow and heavy, with its head down.

Harry stood under the cedar.

He found he'd put his ears back flat. He found his lips had come up off his teeth, a little, all on their own. He found that the thing he was, the whole of him from his nose to his tail, wanted very much to go up that line after the black wolf and put it on its back in the mud and stand over it until it understood something. He didn't know exactly what. Something about thanking God. Something about two years. Something about whose fault it had been that a girl had had to be set free in the first place.

He stood there and let himself want it, for about three seconds.

Then he put his ears back up and went on, south, round the patrol line, the long way.

It wasn't his business. She'd tell him if it was. She'd tell him, he thought, quite loudly.

---

He came down through the last of the trees above the village at twenty to two.

It was a small place. He'd seen it from the lattice, a cluster of dots that weren't wolves, but he'd never been down here; Harry Evans did not go near the reservation. Houses along a few roads near the mouth of a river. A school. A store with one light on over the door. The marina, with the boats rocking at their moorings in the dark. And beyond all of it, the sea, huge and black and loud, coming in on the long curve of First Beach in lines of pale foam.

The string of her went straight into the middle of it, to a little house near the edge of the trees on the inland side.

He went down to it the back way, through the trees.

It was a two-storey house, small and square, with gray siding that needed paint and a porch on the front. A woodshed out back, with a chopping block in front of it and an axe in the block. A clothesline with nothing on it. A rain gauge on a post. A dented old Ford Taurus in the drive with a crease in the back door. A kitchen window with a light left on over the stove. And upstairs, at the back, facing the trees, a window with no light on in it at all.

He stopped at the edge of the trees, at the bottom of the back yard.

He let go of the Disillusionment. He didn't know why. He just let it go, and felt the rain start to land on him again, every drop, all down his back.

He sat down in the wet grass.

He sat the way a dog sits. He'd never done that before, either. He just did it, without deciding, his back end going down and his front legs straight and his tail coming round over his feet, and he sat there at the bottom of the Clearwaters' back yard in the rain, forty feet from the house, and looked up at the dark window.

He didn't know what he was going to do now.

He'd got up and got dressed and come twenty miles through the dark without once thinking about what he'd do when he got here. He'd been an Unspeakable for eight years. He didn't go anywhere without knowing what he'd do when he got there. He had plans for his plans. He had a plan for what he'd do if MACUSA kicked down the tent flap in the next ten minutes, and a plan for what he'd do if the Volturi came over the mountains, and a plan for what he'd do if the Department turned on him, and every one of those plans had a plan behind it.

He had no plan for this. He was sitting in somebody's mum's back garden at twenty to two in the morning as a wolf the size of a shire horse and then some, looking up at a window.

He looked up at it anyway.

On the other side of it, at the end of the line, about forty feet away now and nearly straight up, she'd stopped counting.

### VII. Leah

*The Clearwater house — 1:41 a.m.*

She'd felt every bit of it.

She'd lain there under the covers with her eyes shut and her count gone to nothing and felt him get up, twenty miles off, and get dressed. She'd felt him walk down his long hall in the dark and go out through the flap into the rain. She'd felt him stand on the moss under the big trees with his hand flat on his chest.

And then she'd felt him open the first lock.

It went through her like somebody had opened a window in a hot room. Something came up the line from the far end, faint and wild and huge, a smell she couldn't smell from twenty miles off but could feel, the ghost of a smell, the one she'd caught on the ridge under the coffee and the pine and lost again. It came up the line and into her chest and her own wolf, curled under her ribs, lifted its head so fast she sat up in bed.

Then the second lock. She felt him go loose at the far end, all over, every bone, and sway.

Then the third.

She'd felt that one go like a door coming off its hinges. She'd felt the thing at the bottom of him come up, all of it, up out of the dark through the middle of him, huge and cold and rising, and she'd felt him go with it. She'd felt him come down onto four feet in the moss twenty miles away. She'd felt the rain land on his back.

She'd been sitting up in bed with both hands pressed flat to her chest, breathing like she'd run up a hill.

And then he'd started running.

She'd felt every step. That was the thing she couldn't get over, after; she'd felt every single step of it, down through the trees to the river, over the river, up the other side, west, southwest, toward her, toward her, the line getting shorter and shorter and the weight at the end of it getting heavier and heavier, the way a bell gets louder as you walk toward the church. Her wolf had been on its feet under her ribs the whole time. It had been on its feet and pacing, round and round in the dark under her heart like a dog at a door when it hears the car in the drive.

She'd felt him stop, a mile or so inland, at the edge of the reservation. She'd felt him go still and small. She'd felt something cold come down the line from him, sharp and sudden, like the edge of a knife, and then go away again. She didn't know what that was. She'd find out.

Then he'd come on. Slower. Round the long way.

And then he'd stopped.

Close. Very close. So close the line hardly felt like a line anymore; it felt like a hand on her. Like somebody standing just on the other side of a wall.

She was sitting on the edge of her bed now, in her dad's T-shirt and her sweatpants, with her bare feet on the cold floor. She hadn't turned the light on.

She could feel exactly where he was. She could have pointed. Down and to the left. The bottom of the back yard. Sitting.

*He's sitting,* Leah thought. *He's sitting in the yard.*

She got up off the bed. She went to the window.

---

It was dark in the yard. There was a light on over the stove in the kitchen downstairs, and some of it came out through the kitchen window and lay in a long yellow square on the wet grass, and showed her the clothesline and the rain gauge and the corner of the woodshed and the chopping block with her dad's axe still stuck in it from the spring. Past that it was black. The trees at the bottom of the yard were a solid black wall.

She stood with her hands on the windowsill and her face close to the glass and looked down into the black.

Two green eyes looked back at her.

They were at the bottom of the yard, at the edge of the trees, just past where the light ran out. She saw them before she saw anything else. They caught the kitchen light and held it, and threw it back at her, green, the green of a bottle held up to the sun, and they were looking straight up at her window. Straight at her.

They were too high.

That was the second thing she noticed. They were too high up off the ground to be anything she knew. Higher than a man's eyes would have been, if a man had been standing there. Higher than Jacob's, if Jacob had been sitting there in the grass as a wolf. A good way higher.

She stood there with her hands on the sill and stared at them.

And slowly, the way your eyes get used to the dark, the rest of him came up out of it.

He was black. Of course he was black. She'd felt that through the line on the ridge, down at the bottom of him in the dark, before she'd seen anything. He was so black he was a hole in the night, a wolf-shaped piece cut out of the trees behind him, and the only way she could see the edges of him was where the rain caught on his coat and silvered it, a faint gray shine along the top of his back and his shoulders and the long line of his skull.

He was sitting the way a dog sits. Front legs straight, back end down, tail curled round over his feet. Like he'd come over to somebody's house and sat down on the porch to wait for them to come out.

And he was huge.

She'd seen Jacob as a wolf every day for months. She'd stood next to him on patrol and been shoulder to shoulder with him in a fight. She knew exactly how big Jacob was; she knew it better than she knew how big her own house was. She looked at the black wolf sitting at the bottom of her yard and she put Jacob next to him in her head, and Jacob came up to about his eyes.

He didn't move. He didn't do anything. He sat there in the rain at the bottom of the Clearwaters' back yard at a quarter to two in the morning and looked up at her window, and she looked down at him, and neither of them did anything at all for what felt like a long time.

The line between them hummed like a wire in a wind.

Under her ribs, her wolf had stopped pacing. It was standing perfectly still with every hair on end and its ears so far forward they hurt, staring down through her eyes at the thing at the bottom of the yard.

She'd felt her wolf scared plenty of times. It had been scared on the field in June. It had been scared every time it met a vampire. It had been scared on the ridge this afternoon when the weight came down on it and it couldn't lift its head.

Tonight it was looking down at the black wolf at the bottom of the yard the way you'd look at the last piece of a puzzle you'd been doing for a thousand years, turning in your fingers, about to go in.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Okay.*

She went downstairs.

---

She went down barefoot, carefully, on the edges of the steps where they didn't creak. She knew every one. She'd been sneaking in and out of this house since she was fifteen, mostly to meet Sam at the end of the dirt road, and her legs remembered the way.

She went past her mom's door at the bottom of the stairs.

It was shut. There was no light under it. Leah went past it on her toes, and halfway past it she heard her mom's breathing change on the other side of the door, the way a sleeping person's breathing changes when they wake up. It hitched. It went quiet.

Leah stopped in the hall with her heart going.

Then her mom's breathing started up again. Slow and even and deep, the way it went when she was asleep. Too slow. Too even. Like somebody lying in the dark with her eyes open doing it on purpose.

Leah stood there a second. Then she went on, into the kitchen, and through it, and opened the back door as quietly as she'd ever opened anything, and went out.

---

The rain was soft and cold on her face. The grass was soaking. She walked down the yard through the long square of light from the kitchen window and out the other side of it into the dark, and he stood up.

He did it slowly. He'd seen her come out. He'd watched her all the way down the yard. And when she got to the edge of the light, he stood up out of the grass in one long movement, and kept standing, and kept standing, until she had to tip her head right back to keep his eyes in front of hers.

She stopped about ten feet from him.

The heat came off him in the cold. She could feel it from where she stood, the way you feel a woodstove across a room. The rain was landing on his back and going up off it as steam, a thin gray haze all over him in the dark. He smelled—

She breathed in.

He smelled like the ghost she'd smelled on the ridge, all of it, out from under the lock and nothing hiding it. Pine and earth and cold stone, the inside of a cave. And under that, everywhere, filling the whole bottom of the yard, the wild old smell of something that had been in the woods before the people. Something from the stories the old men told at the bonfire when they'd had too much to drink.

Her wolf went up under her ribs on its back legs.

He put his head down.

He brought it down slowly, the way you'd bring your hand down to a dog you didn't know, until his long black muzzle was level with her face, about two feet away. His eyes were on hers. Green. The same green as on the ridge, through the dark glasses he'd taken off. The same green as in his kitchen, across the table, over a beer. Exactly the same, in a face covered in black fur, with a mouth full of teeth she could see the white tips of in the dark.

His breath came out in a long cloud in the cold. It fogged on her face. It smelled of the forest.

"Couldn't sleep," he said.

She jumped about a foot.

It was his voice. His actual voice. It came out of the wolf low and rough, with a growl running under it like an engine idling, but it was *his*, it was the English one from the ridge and the kitchen, every word clean and plain, the *s* in *sleep* coming out sharp as anything.

"You can *talk*?" she said.

"Apparently."

"Like *that*? You can talk like *that*?"

"Bit of a perk." One of his ears went sideways, a quick flick, and she knew without knowing how she knew it that he was trying not to laugh at her. "I didn't know either, the first time. Nearly gave myself a heart attack."

"*None* of us can talk."

"Well," he said. "None of you are me."

She stared at him. He looked back at her with his big head level with hers and the rain coming down on both of them. One of his ears was still sideways.

"Couldn't sleep," she said finally. "You couldn't *sleep*. So you came twenty miles through the woods. In the middle of the night. As a—" She waved her hand at all of him. "As *that*. And sat in my mom's yard."

"Yeah."

"Like a—"

"Like a stray," he agreed. "I know. I was there."

"You could've—I don't know. Called."

"You haven't got a phone."

"I've got a phone."

"You've got a phone in your mum's kitchen on the wall with a curly cord, and your mum's asleep next to it." He paused. "Well. Sort of asleep."

Leah looked back at the house. At the dark window by the back door that was her mom's.

"She heard me," she said.

"She heard you." His ear went back up. "She's lying there with her eyes open doing her breathing on purpose. She's very good at it. Better than you."

"Get out of her head."

"I'm in your head. You heard her." He breathed out, a long fog, and it went over Leah's face and her wet hair and her shoulders. "And you couldn't sleep either."

"No."

"Two hundred and six," he said.

She stared at him.

"You were counting," he said. "I counted with you. I got to two hundred and six." His big head tipped, very slightly. "And then I thought: this is stupid."

She opened her mouth to say something mean. She didn't know what. She'd have found something.

Under her ribs, her wolf hit her from the inside like a shoulder hitting a door.

---

She didn't decide to phase.

She found afterward that that was the only way to say it. She'd been about to say something mean and the next second her hands were on the hem of her dad's T-shirt, pulling it over her head, and she didn't remember telling them to. "Turn round," she said, and heard her own voice come out rough. He turned his whole long head away, round to the side, looking at the woodshed with great interest. She got the sweatpants off. She put them both on the chopping block, next to the axe, and the rain started soaking into them at once and she didn't care at all.

Then she let go.

It was the fastest she'd ever done it. It came up out of her like her wolf had been waiting on the other side of a door with its hand on the knob, and the door opened, and it came straight through. She hardly felt the bones go. She felt the grass hit her hands, and her hands stop being hands. She felt the cold go away, all of it, all at once, into fur. She felt the world come up into her nose like a wave hitting the beach, the rain and the sea and the woodshed and the axe and her mom's flowers by the porch, which were dead for the winter, and the wet earth, and him.

*Him.* Him all over everything. The whole yard was full of him. Up close, as a wolf, with her own nose, it was so much it was almost too much: pine and stone and the old wild dark, and her own family's smell somewhere in it too, like a cousin, like something that had come from the same place a long way back.

The pack mind came on in her head like a light.

She braced for it. She'd been bracing for it every time she phased for two years. Jacob, Seth, the noise, the crowd, everybody in everybody's head—

It was empty.

She stood there in the wet grass on four feet with the pack mind open all the way round her like an empty room, and nobody was in it. Jacob was human. She could feel the dim warm hum of him way off to the east, asleep, on a white couch, with something small and hot curled on his chest. Seth was human, way off to the northeast, on a couch in a blue house with a woman asleep across his legs on her stomach, and he was asleep too, finally, with his hand in her hair.

Nobody was watching.

For the first time since the spring, nobody in the world was in her head but her.

And him, coming up the line, wordless. He couldn't get in the pack mind; she could feel that, he was outside it, the way the Cullens were outside it. But the line didn't care about the pack mind. The line went straight from the middle of her into the middle of him, and now, both of them on four feet, it was so loud it was like standing inside a bell.

She turned round and looked at him.

He'd turned his head back. He was looking at her.

She saw him see her. She felt it come down the line, felt the whole of him, every bit of him from his nose to his tail, look at her standing there in the rain in her mom's yard, a gray wolf, long-legged and narrow and soaked, and go absolutely still.

And then the two of them stopped being careful.

---

In human shape it had taken them twelve hours to get as far as one hand on one face.

It took their wolves about four seconds.

She went straight to him. She didn't think about it. She went across the ten feet of wet grass with her head up and her ears forward and her tail up, and he came down to meet her, his huge head coming down out of the dark, and they met nose to nose in the middle of the yard.

His nose was cold and wet and the breath came out of it hot. She breathed him in. He breathed her in. They stood there with their noses touching, breathing each other, for a long moment, and the line between them went so tight and bright and loud she felt it in her back teeth.

Then she put her nose along his jaw.

She'd never done this. She'd never done it with anyone, not as a wolf; she'd stood shoulder to shoulder with Jacob and Seth in a hundred fights and on a hundred patrols and never once wanted to. She did it now without thinking, the way you'd put your hand out to steady yourself. She put her nose along the side of his jaw, along the line of it, down his throat to the deep fur on his chest, breathing, breathing, finding out every single thing about him there was to find out with a nose, and he stood there and let her, absolutely still, with his head up and his throat bare.

He let her. She noticed that, somewhere in the back of her head where she was still a person. Something that big, that dangerous, the thing that had been in the dark at the bottom of the man on the ridge, standing in her mom's back yard with its head up and its throat bare, letting her.

Then it was his turn.

He brought his head down and round and put his nose behind her ear. She felt the breath of it go in the fur there, hot, and she shivered all over, nose to tail, and her legs went a bit funny. He breathed her in. He went down the side of her neck, slowly, and along her shoulder, and she stood there with her eyes half shut and let him. He found the place behind her ear again and stayed there. She felt his breath go in and out.

She leaned on him.

She didn't mean to. She just leaned, all of a sudden, the whole side of her against the whole front of him, her shoulder against his chest, and he was so big her shoulder came up to about the bottom of his. She felt him take her weight. He didn't shift. He stood there like a tree and let her lean.

She shoved him.

She did mean that. She drew back a little and threw her shoulder into his chest as hard as she could, the way Seth had head-butted her in the stomach every day after school when he was six, the way you'd shove somebody you'd known your whole life. It would have knocked Jacob sideways.

He rocked. Maybe an inch. And then something came down the line from him so bright and so sudden it took her a second to know what it was.

He was laughing.

Inside, down the line. A great big helpless wordless rush of it, the first she'd felt from him, all the way from the bottom of him to the top, and her wolf heard it and went straight up on its back legs inside her and came down and her front end went down onto the wet grass, her elbows right in the mud, her back end up in the air and her tail going like a flag.

She'd seen dogs do that. She'd never once in her life thought she'd do it.

He looked at her down there with her front end in the mud.

Then his own front end came down. Slow, all of it, his huge forelegs folding until his chest was nearly on the grass, his black head down level with hers, his back end up, his tail swinging once. Twice. Like he'd never done it either and was finding out how.

They looked at each other, both of them, with their elbows in the mud of Sue Clearwater's back yard at ten to two in the morning.

She nipped his jaw.

He snapped back. He didn't touch her. His teeth closed on the air about an inch off her ear with a click like a box shutting, and she jumped back, and he came after her, and she went.

---

She went out of the yard and into the trees with him right behind her.

She went the way she'd gone a thousand times, the way her feet knew. Down through the trees behind the houses, across the road, down the bank to the beach. She came out onto First Beach at a dead run and the sand was hard and wet and cold under her feet and the sea was right there, enormous, coming in out of the dark in long white lines, and the noise of it filled up the whole world.

She ran along the edge of it. Right at the edge, where the foam came up and went back, where the sand was so wet her feet splashed. She ran as fast as she'd ever run. She was the fastest of all of them. She'd always been the fastest.

He was right beside her.

She looked over and he was just *there*, at her shoulder, on the sea side, running, his black coat soaked silver with spray and his ears flat back and his eyes on her. She felt him through the line, running. She felt him not trying. She felt that he could have gone past her any time he liked, the way he'd gone past everything else today, and she felt him not do it. She felt him stay at her shoulder, exactly at her shoulder, matching her stride for stride, his big feet coming down in the foam beside her small ones and not making a sound, while she ran flat out.

She ran faster. He stayed at her shoulder.

She cut left, up the beach, hard, to see. He was there. She cut back right, down into the waves, into water up to her chest, cold enough to stop your heart, and came out the other side. He was there. Wet to the ears. Laughing again, down the line.

She'd never run with anybody. She'd run *near* people. She'd run with the pack, in a line, on patrol. She'd run away from people, mostly. She'd been running away from everybody she knew since the spring as fast as her legs would go.

She'd never run *with* anybody.

She ran the whole length of First Beach with him at her shoulder and the sea on their right, past the sea stacks at the south end, black and huge with trees on top bent over by a hundred years of wind, and up the rocks at the end, and onto the trail over the headland, through the dark trees, and down the other side. Second Beach. The big arch in the rock. The stacks standing out in the surf like the backs of something enormous swimming.

And at the far end of Second Beach, up in the bluff above the high-water mark, the cave.

She'd found it the first month she phased. In April. The first week, when she couldn't stand to be anywhere near anybody and she'd run and run and run along the coast at night until she found a place nobody could see her. It was a hollow in the face of the bluff, back under an overhang of black rock, about twenty feet above where the highest tide came. You had to come up a steep scramble of fallen rock to get to it, and from the beach you couldn't see it at all. Inside, the floor was dry sand. There was driftwood in the back, gray and smooth, that some storm had thrown up there a hundred years ago. The mouth of it looked straight out at the sea.

She'd never shown it to anybody. Not Seth. Nobody.

She went up the rocks in four jumps and in through the mouth, and turned round, and stood there in the dry sand with her chest going like a bellows, and looked out.

He came up the rocks behind her. He took two jumps. He had to put his head down to come in under the overhang, and his back brushed the rock, and he stopped just inside, and stood there, dripping, and looked round.

At the sand. At the driftwood. At the black rock of the walls, worn smooth by the wind. At her.

She felt him understand it. She felt it come down the line, all at once, the whole shape of it, the way he'd understood the Disjunction on the ridge in a second and a half: a girl who'd turned into a wolf in the spring, screaming, on the night her dad died, running along a beach in the dark looking for somewhere nobody could find her. Finding this. Coming here, alone, for seven months.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to, and he knew he didn't. He just stood there in the mouth of her cave, dripping, with his head down under the rock, and she felt him hold it, very carefully, the way you'd hold something somebody had put in your hands.

Then he came the rest of the way in.

---

The sand was dry and cold and the rock kept the wind off.

She turned round twice the way she always did and lay down in the place she always lay, at the back, against the driftwood, with her nose toward the sea.

He stood over her for a second. She could feel him not know what to do. Big, black, dripping, too tall for the cave, with his head bent under the rock and his ears flat to clear it, standing there in the middle of the sand like a man in somebody else's kitchen who doesn't know where they keep the cups.

Then he lay down.

He lay down round her. All of him. He came down onto the sand beside her, slow, and curled, and kept curling, until his long black body was round her back and his tail was round her feet and his big head came down on the sand on the other side of her, by her nose. Heat came off him like a fire. She felt it go into her through her wet coat all the way down her back and into her bones.

She put her chin on his foreleg.

It was huge. It was like putting her chin on a log. She felt the bones in it, and the muscle, and the heat, and the big black paw at the end of it lying in the sand a foot from her nose, and she breathed in, and it smelled of him, and she breathed out.

He put his chin down on the top of her head.

They lay there.

The sea came in, out at the mouth of the cave, enormous and black and white, and went out again. The rain had stopped. She hadn't noticed when. There was a gap in the cloud out over the water, and through it, very small, a few stars.

She could feel his heart. It was right there against her back, through his ribs and his fur and hers. Slow and heavy, a deep double knock, like a man in a chair reading a newspaper. Like it had been on the ridge before any of it, before the line, before the green eyes and the cables and everything going.

Hers was going about twice as fast.

As she lay there, it slowed down. Bit by bit. Beat by beat. Until it was going along with his.

Under her ribs, her wolf turned round once and lay down, with its chin on its paws, with a long sigh that went all the way through her. And down the line, at the bottom of him, the big black thing that had come up out of the dark tonight for the first time in two months lay down too, right next to it, so close there wasn't any line left between them at all.

She thought about Sam.

She did it on purpose. She lay there with her chin on Harry's leg and his chin on her head and she thought about Sam, as hard as she could, just to see. Sam on a dirt road at the end of a bonfire night. Sam's mouth. Sam saying her name. Sam on a porch in Makah looking at Emily, and the whole world going.

It was a nice memory. Some of it. It was a memory of a guy she'd gone out with when she was sixteen.

She let it go. It went.

Out at the mouth of the cave, the sea came in.

She was asleep before it went back out.

## Part Three: The House on the Hill

---

### VIII. Leah

*The cave above Second Beach — 7:09 a.m.*

She woke up warm.

That was the first thing. She hadn't woken up warm since the spring. Wolves ran hot, everybody knew that, but she'd been waking up cold for seven months anyway: alone on a rock, alone in this cave, alone in her own bed with the covers kicked off, cold all the way through in a way that had nothing to do with the temperature. She'd stopped noticing. You stopped noticing a lot of things.

She noticed now. She was lying in the dry sand at the back of her cave with her chin on something big and solid and hot, and there was heat all down her back and round her feet and over the top of her head, and she was warm right down to the middle of her bones, and for about five seconds she didn't know where she was or why.

Then she breathed in.

Pine. Cold stone. The old wild smell from the bonfire stories.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Right.*

She opened her eyes.

It was getting light. The mouth of the cave was a long gray slot full of sea and sky, the sea flat and pewter-colored and the sky the same with a line of dirty pink low down at the edge of it where the sun was thinking about coming up somewhere behind her, over the mountains. The rain had stopped. The wind had dropped. The tide was way out; she could hear it a long way off, quiet, breathing.

His chin was still on top of her head.

He was awake. She could feel him being awake down the line, and she could feel the weight of his head on hers, perfectly still, the way you'd hold still with a cat asleep on your lap. He'd been awake a while. She didn't know how she knew that; she just did. He'd been lying there with his chin on her head watching the sea go gray for a long time, not moving, so she wouldn't wake up.

She lay there a minute longer. She didn't move either.

Then her stomach growled.

It was loud. It was really loud. It went off in the quiet of the cave like somebody had started a chainsaw, and she felt the chin on top of her head lift about half an inch, and felt the laugh come down the line before she heard it.

"Morning," he said.

His voice came down through the top of her skull. Low and rough and English, with the growl running under it.

*Shut up,* Leah thought.

"Didn't say anything."

*You were going to.*

"I was going to say it's ten past seven and I'm starving," he said. "Which I am. So that's two of us."

She lifted her head off his leg and looked round at him.

In the daylight he was even bigger. That didn't seem fair. He was lying curled round her in the sand with his head up now, looking down at her, and he filled the back of the cave from wall to wall; his back was against the rock and his tail was across the mouth of it like a draft excluder. In the gray light his coat was so dark it was almost blue at the tips, like a crow's wing, with the dark charcoal fur underneath showing where it parted over his shoulders. There was sand stuck in it. There was dried salt in his ruff from the spray.

His eyes were exactly the same in daylight.

*You didn't sleep,* she thought.

"I slept a bit."

She felt him try to make it sound true. She felt it not be true, quite.

"About an hour," he said. "Between half four and half five. Then a seal came up on the rocks down there and started making a noise like a drain, and I thought I'd keep an eye on it." One ear went sideways. "It's gone now. You slept straight through it. You snore."

*I do not.*

"Like a little outboard motor."

She shoved her head into his chest, hard, the way she had in the yard. He let her. He rocked an inch. She felt him laugh again, down the line, and this time it came out, too, a low huff of breath through his nose that went over her ears.

Then she felt him get serious. Not much. A little. She felt it come down the line from him like the change in the air before weather.

"Your lot'll be waking up," he said. "Your brother. Clifford."

*Who's Clifford?*

"Big red dog." He tipped his head toward the east. "Sleeps on a white couch."

She stared at him. Then, despite everything, despite herself, despite Jacob being in fact her alpha and her oldest friend and the reason she'd left Sam's pack, she felt her wolf's mouth come open in the long loose grin that wolves did instead of laughing.

*He's going to kill you.*

"He'll have to get in line. That soldier of theirs is ahead of him." Harry stood up. He did it slowly, the way he'd done everything in here, with his head down under the rock. "They're expecting us. You told them morning. And if your lot phase and find me curled round you in a cave, I don't think we're going to get a lot of talking done."

He went to the mouth of the cave and stood there a second with the gray light behind him.

And changed.

She'd watched a lot of people change. She'd watched them from outside and from inside. Sam, Jared, Paul, Jacob, Seth, the first time, screaming. Herself, in the mirror on the back of her bedroom door, once, the first month, just to see. It was always the same. It was like watching somebody get hit by a truck, or watching somebody come out of the other side of one. It was violent. It was always violent. The wolf came out of you like a shout and went back in like a slammed door.

He just stood up.

That was what it looked like. The big black wolf in the mouth of her cave stood up onto its back legs, slowly, and as it came up it came in, all of it, smaller and narrower and straighter, like somebody folding up a coat, and there wasn't any tearing sound, and there wasn't any shout, and by the time it was all the way up it was a man. A man standing in the mouth of the cave with his back to the sea and his hair sticking up and sand on his boots.

In his jeans.

In his flannel shirt. In his waxed jacket, with the collar turned up. In his boots, laced. With the shoulder rig under the jacket; she could see the strap of it across his chest where the jacket hung open. He was dry. He was completely dry. There wasn't a drop of seawater on him or a single grain of sand on his clothes. He stood there in the cave mouth and rolled his shoulders, and cracked his neck, and pushed one hand back through his hair, and looked at her.

Leah lay in the sand and stared at him.

*You have got,* she thought, very slowly, *to be kidding me.*

"What?"

*You keep your* clothes*?*

He looked down at himself. He looked back up at her. Something happened at the corner of his mouth.

"Perk," he said.

*I've been tying my shorts to my leg with a* bootlace *for seven months! I've walked home naked through the woods! Twice! In the* rain*! Do you know how many pairs of shorts Seth has—*

"Fifteen," said Harry. "From Newton's. Six dollars each. He was very proud of it." He held up a hand. "I've been in your brother's head, love, I know all about the shorts."

*Your* gun *came with you.*

"It always does."

*That's not fair.*

"No," he agreed. "It's not, really."

She lay there glaring at him. He stood there letting her. Then he turned round and looked out of the cave mouth, east, along the beach, the way they'd come in the dark.

"Where'd you leave them?" he said. "Your clothes."

*On the chopping block. In the yard. By the axe.*

He held out his right hand, palm up, toward the beach.

He didn't say anything. He didn't do anything she could see. He just stood there with his hand out, looking up the beach, for about three seconds.

Then something came over the headland.

She saw it come. A dark flicker at the top of the trees on the headland between First and Second Beach, a mile and a half away. It came down over the trees and out over the sand, low, very fast, faster than a bird, a little tumbling bundle of gray and dark gray flying along about six feet off the beach, straight down the middle of it, past the big arch and the sea stacks, getting bigger and bigger, and came up the rocks to the cave mouth and smacked into his hand.

Her dad's T-shirt. Her gray sweatpants. Soaked through, from the rain, and the chopping block.

He looked at them in his hand. Then he closed his fingers on them, and they steamed. A cloud of it came off them all at once, white, like a kettle, and when it cleared they were dry. He shook them out. They were dry and clean and not even creased.

He looked at her.

"Want them to come with you next time?" he said.

She didn't say anything. She couldn't think of anything.

"Easy enough," he said. "Same idea as your brother's bracelet, roughly. The witch did that one. I'd do it a bit differently. Anything you've got on, it goes where you go and comes back with you. Bit of give in it so it doesn't strangle you on the way." He held the clothes out a bit. "I'll do the lot. Everything you own, if you want. Your shoes. Every pair of shorts in your house. Saves you a bootlace."

She looked at her dad's T-shirt in his hand. It had a hole in the collar where her dad used to chew it when he was watching the Seahawks lose.

*Yeah,* she thought. *Yeah. Okay.*

He did something. She didn't see what. He held the clothes in his hand for a second, and she felt something go into them, something small and careful and neat, the way you'd feel somebody tie a knot. Then he put them down on the sand at the mouth of the cave and turned round, all the way round, with his back to her and his face to the sea.

"Go on, then," he said.

She went back behind the driftwood and let go.

It was as quick as it had been in the yard. Quicker. She was standing in the sand at the back of the cave on two feet with the cold coming in through her skin in about a second and a half, and she came round the driftwood and picked up the clothes and put them on. The T-shirt came down to her knees. The sweatpants had the hole in the knee. They were dry and hot all the way through, like they'd been on a radiator, and she stood there in them with her bare feet in the sand and pulled the collar of the T-shirt up over her nose and breathed in, and it smelled like her dad, still, faintly, under the rain and the sea and him.

"You can turn round," she said.

He turned round.

They stood there in the mouth of the cave in the gray light, about four feet apart, a man in a waxed jacket and a woman in her dead father's T-shirt, and looked at each other.

It was different, on two feet. She'd thought it would be. It was quieter. On four feet it had been so loud between them she couldn't hear anything else, and now she could hear the sea again, and the gulls starting up, and her own heart. The line was still there. It was right there, from the middle of her to the middle of him, humming. But it was quieter, the way a song is quieter in the next room.

And there was all the stuff that wolves didn't have to think about.

She stood there thinking about it. All of it. His hand on her face in the clearing, and the yard, and her nose along his jaw, and lying all night in the sand with his whole body round hers. She felt her face go hot. She felt it go hot right up to her hair, the way her dad's had over the boat.

She looked at him. His ears had gone red.

She'd never seen that before. She hadn't known he could do it. He was standing there with his hands in his jacket pockets and his face perfectly straight, and the tops of his ears were red as anything.

"So," said Harry.

"So," said Leah.

"That was—" He stopped.

"Yeah."

"Our—" He took one hand out of his pocket and made a vague shape in the air, a shape that meant *the wolves*, or *last night*, or *all of it*. "They're a bit—"

"Forward," said Leah.

"Forward. Yeah. That's the word."

"Mine's never done anything like that. *Ever*."

"Mine's never done *anything*." He pushed his hand back through his hair. Sand fell out of it. "I've let it out four times in three years. Every time I've been scared stiff it'd go and eat a sheep. It's never looked at another living thing. Not once. And then last night it took one look at you and it—" He stopped again.

"Played," said Leah.

"*Played*," said Harry, like it was a word in a foreign language. "With its front end in the mud. In your mum's garden."

Leah put her hand over her face.

"I've stood in front of Voldemort," Harry said, to the sea. "I've walked into a dragon's cave in Romania. I've stood in front of the Veil. Nothing in my whole life has been as embarrassing as watching myself do that." He paused. "I'd do it again in a second. That's the worst bit."

She took her hand off her face and looked at him.

He was looking at the sea. The red had gone down his ears onto the back of his neck.

"Yeah," she said. "Me too."

---

They sat in the mouth of the cave for a bit, with their feet hanging over the rocks, and watched the sun come up behind them and turn the tops of the sea stacks gold.

She'd meant to ask him a lot of things. She'd lain awake last night with a list. Where he came from and what he'd done and how many times he'd done it, and the jars, and the gun, and the old man in the mirror. She found, sitting there, that she didn't want to ask most of them yet. They'd keep. He wasn't going anywhere. She could feel that, down the line, as plain as she could feel the rock under her. Whatever else he was going to do, he wasn't going anywhere.

She asked the one that had been bothering her.

"Last night," she said. "When you were coming. You stopped. Up by the line, at the edge of the rez. And something came down the line from you. Something cold." She looked at him. "What was that?"

He didn't answer right away. She felt him think about not answering. She felt him decide not to bother trying.

"There were two of the other lot out on patrol," he said. "On the boundary. I went round them. One of them smelled something and came over to look. Big black one."

She went still.

"Sam," said Harry. "I know. I'd been in your head all day. I knew who it was before I saw it." He was quiet a second. "He didn't find me. I stood under a tree and he stood forty yards off with his nose going, and I listened."

"To his head."

"Yeah."

"What was he thinking?"

He looked at her. He'd told her he couldn't lie to her; she could see him remember it, see him come up against it, the way you'd come up against a step in the dark.

"Billy rang him," he said. "About half nine. Billy rang everybody, apparently. And Sam was standing out there in the rain thinking about it, over and over." He paused. "He was thinking, *She's free. Thank God. Thank God, Lee.*"

Leah sat very still on the rock.

She waited for it to hurt. She waited for it the way she'd waited in her bed last night, every muscle braced. Two years. Two years of Sam's guilt in her head every night, that thick brown sludge of it, *I'm sorry Lee I'm so sorry I never meant I can't help it I'm sorry*. And now Sam standing out in the rain on the boundary thanking God. Thanking God she was somebody else's problem now. She waited for the wave to come back in.

It didn't.

A laugh came up out of her chest, short and dry, and surprised her so much she put her hand over her mouth.

"Of course he did," she said through her fingers. "Of *course* he did. Thank *God*. Like I was—like I was a *dog* he'd finally found a home for."

"That's about what I thought," said Harry.

"What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"I wanted to," he said. He said it very plainly. "I wanted to go up that line after him and put him on his back in the mud and stand on him until he understood something. I'm not sure what. I stood there under that tree for about three seconds with my teeth out wanting it."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because he's yours," said Harry. "Whatever there is to sort out with him, it's yours to sort. If you want me to put him in the mud, you'll tell me. I imagine you'll tell me quite loudly." He looked out at the sea. "And because I'd just come twenty miles in the dark to sit in your mum's garden, and I thought if I turned up with blood on me she might not do the salmon."

Leah stared at him.

"You heard that," she said. "The salmon."

"I heard all of it." He didn't look away from the sea. "The boat. The Sears catalogue. Your dad." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm sorry. About the name. I didn't know."

"How could you know?"

"I couldn't. I'm still sorry."

She looked at him sitting there on the rock beside her with his boots hanging over the edge, and the sun on the side of his face, and the stubble on his jaw. He was looking at the sea very hard.

"Mom said Dad would've laughed for a week," she said.

"I heard."

"She said he'd have said I was a daddy's girl. At the dinner table. In front of you."

"I heard that, too." Something happened at the corner of his mouth. "I'd have liked to have met him. I think."

"He'd have hated you."

"Probably."

"For about a day," said Leah. "Then he'd have taken you fishing."

They sat there.

"Can I ask you one more?" she said.

"You can ask me anything. You know that."

"What is it?" She put her hand flat on her chest. Then she reached out, before she could think about it, and put it flat on his, on his jacket, over the place low and to the left where she'd felt the line go in on the ridge. She felt his heart under it. She felt it go faster. "That. In you. Where'd it come from?"

He didn't answer for a long time.

She felt him think about it. She felt something very big move at the bottom of him, down where the line went in, and turn over, and settle. She felt him go a long way back, years, to somewhere cold and wet and high up with heather on it, and a black sky, and a storm coming, and him on his own. She felt it all come up to the edge of where he could say it, and stop there. And she felt him look at it there for a while.

"I'll tell you," he said finally. "All of it. I'll have to; I couldn't not if I tried. I don't think anybody else in the world's ever going to hear it. But not today." He put his hand over hers on his chest. "Today I've got to go and sit in a house full of vampires and be polite. And I'd like to have had breakfast first."

She felt it come down the line from him. It was true. Every word. And under the words, she felt how big the thing was that he wasn't telling her yet. It was big like the thing at the bottom of him was big. It was something he'd never once taken out and looked at in front of anybody.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. Not today." She took her hand back. "But you're telling me."

"I'm telling you."

---

He took her back by Apparition.

He stood up on the rock at the mouth of the cave and held out his arm, crooked at the elbow, like an old man at a wedding, and said, "Hold on to that. Tight. Whatever happens, don't let go."

"What's going to happen?"

"You're going to hate it," he said. "Everybody does."

She held on.

She hated it.

It was like being sucked down the drain of a bath. It was like being stuffed into a hose a quarter of an inch wide and pulled through it very fast from the other end by somebody who didn't like you. Everything went black and pressing and tight all at once, her eyes squashed back into her head and her ribs squashed in on her lungs and her ears full of a roaring, and she couldn't breathe, and then she could. She was standing on moss with her bare feet, under trees so big the trunks looked like the walls of buildings, with a river roaring somewhere below, and she bent over with both hands on her knees and was sick into a fern.

He held her hair. She hadn't known he was going to. It was short; there wasn't much of it to hold. He held what there was.

"Told you," he said.

She was sick again. Then she straightened up and wiped her mouth on the back of her dad's T-shirt and looked at him.

"Layla said it was like a garden hose," she said.

"Layla's being kind."

"I'm going to kill you."

"Get in line," said Harry, and went to put the kettle on.

---

There was a slug on the tent.

She'd seen it last night in the dark, by the flap. It was still there. It was bigger in the daylight. It was the length of her hand and as thick as her thumb and bright banana-yellow with black spots all over it, and it was stuck to the green canvas about a foot from the door, gleaming, with a long silver trail going back up the side of the tent behind it like a map of where it had been.

She stood in front of it in her bare feet.

"That's Gary," said Harry from inside.

"You *named* it?"

"He was here first." His voice came up out of the tent, out of a long way down. "Don't touch him. He's got a lot going on."

She looked at Gary.

Gary didn't do anything. Gary just sat there on the canvas being yellow.

She went in.

He fed her bacon sandwiches. He stood at the big black range in his jacket with the sleeves pushed up and fried a pound of bacon in about four minutes and put it between slices of bread so thick they were nearly loaves, with brown sauce out of a bottle with a picture of a London building on it, and handed her two. She ate them standing up by the blue fridge. He ate three standing up by the range. Neither of them said anything until they'd finished.

"What's in the back?" she said, with her mouth full.

He knew what she meant. He looked down the long hall, toward the workshop and the black oak door at the end of it.

"Same as last night," he said. "Lying on his back. Not going anywhere."

"Is he—" She stopped. She didn't know what she was asking.

"He's fine," said Harry. "For a given value of fine. I looked in before I went to bed. He's exactly where I left him, and he'll stay exactly where I left him until I take him out, and when I take him out, you're going to be there, and you're going to bring the matches." He wiped his hands on a tea towel. "I promised."

"You did."

"I keep those."

He put the plates in the sink. He put the bacon fat in a jar on the back of the range. He went down the hall to a little door she hadn't noticed last night, and came back with a pair of socks and a pair of boots that were a lot too big for her, and held them out.

"For the car," he said. "It's a mile and a half down to it, and the trail's all roots."

"You've got a car?"

"Harry Evans has a car," said Harry. "Harry Evans is very boring and drives a rented Ford. You'll like him. Everybody does. He tips well."

---

The trail went down through the big trees in long switchbacks, and the boots slopped on her feet the whole way, and at the bottom of it there was a gravel pull-off on a forest road with a brown Ford Explorer parked in it, nose out, with a fir cone on the windscreen and a paperback on the passenger seat.

She got in. She picked up the paperback. It was called *The Big Sleep* and it was falling apart, held together with tape down the spine.

He got in on the other side. He took a CD out of the door pocket without looking at it and put it in the slot in the dashboard. He started the car. Drums came out of the speakers, very loud, a big crashing slow thumping like somebody kicking a door in a stairwell, and a guitar like a saw going through sheet metal, and a man somewhere over the top of it all wailing like a siren.

"Is this the one from last night?" said Leah. "The one you had on? With the drums?"

"Different album. Same band."

"Who is it?"

He looked at her like she'd told him she didn't know what water was.

"Zeppelin," he said.

"Who?"

He looked at her for another second. Then he looked out through the windscreen at the gravel road going down through the trees, and put the car in gear, very carefully, the way you'd put down something you didn't trust yourself not to drop.

"We'll start you on the fourth one," he said. "Tonight. Have you got a record player?"

"My dad had one."

"Good," said Harry. "Good. That's a start."

---

It was eight minutes past eight when they came down off the forest road onto the highway, and turned east, and she put her feet up on the dashboard in his huge boots and looked at him.

"Harry," she said.

"Mm."

"Promise me something."

"Depends."

"You don't get *depends*. You can't lie to me."

"I can say *depends* to you. *Depends* is true." He changed down for the bend. "What?"

"When we get there," she said. "Promise me you won't vaporize anybody."

He drove for a bit.

"Define *vaporize*," he said.

"Harry."

"No, I'm genuinely asking. Is *vaporize* the whole thing, start to finish, a little bit of steam and a scorch mark on the rug? Or does it count if I just—"

"*Harry*."

"Fine." He sighed. "I promise I won't vaporize the entire coven."

"Any of the coven."

He drove for a bit longer.

"Any of the coven," he said.

"Pinky promise."

He took his eyes off the road and looked at her. She was holding her little finger out across the gear stick.

"I'm twenty-six years old," he said.

"Pinky."

"I'm a—" He stopped. She watched him not be able to say whatever the next word was going to be. She watched him remember why. "I'm a grown man with a government pension. I'm not doing a pinky promise. I have never in my life done a pinky promise."

"There's a first time for everything."

"There is *not*—"

"You said you'd do anything I asked."

"I said I'd *tell* you anything you—"

She waggled her little finger.

He looked at it. He looked at the road. He looked at the finger again. She felt him, down the line, go through every single possible way out of this, quickly and thoroughly, the way she'd watched him go through Seth's head on the ridge, and find that there weren't any.

He took his right hand off the wheel and hooked his little finger round hers.

"I promise," he said, through his teeth, "that I won't vaporize any of the coven."

"Or anybody else in the house."

"Or anybody else in the house. *Pinky*."

"Thank you."

He let go and put his hand back on the wheel. The tops of his ears had gone red again.

"With one exception," he said.

"No exceptions."

"One. The big one. Emmett." He glanced at her. "I won't vaporize him. But if he's a twat, I'm putting him through a wall."

Leah thought about Emmett. Emmett, who wanted to be a bear. Who wanted to arm-wrestle. Who'd caught a falling mug from across a room last night and looked so pleased with himself.

"He'll probably like that," she said.

"Then everybody wins."

They drove. The drums thumped. The man on the CD wailed something about a mountain. The highway went up through the wet gray woods toward Forks with the sun coming through the trees in long bars across the road, and she sat with her feet on his dashboard and the paperback in her lap and watched him drive.

"Harry," she said, after a bit.

"Mm."

"The blond one. Jasper. Last night."

His hands didn't move on the wheel. Nothing about him moved. But she felt it come down the line from him, and from the thing at the bottom of him: something very cold that had been lying very still since a quarter to eight last night, and was still lying there.

"I heard him," he said.

"He took it back."

"I heard that too."

"So you're not going to—"

"I promised," said Harry. "Pinky." He changed up a gear. "And he took it back. And he's a soldier, and he said a soldier's thing, out loud, in front of you, knowing I was listening. Which I quite respect, actually." He was quiet a moment. "But if he ever says it again, Leah, I'm going to find out what that lot are like with no arms."

She looked at him.

"That wasn't a joke," she said.

"No," said Harry. "That one wasn't."

They came round the long bend south of town and he put his indicator on and turned up the long drive into the trees.

---

### IX. Edward

*The Cullen house — 8:21 a.m.*

He heard Leah first.

He heard her at the bottom of the drive, three miles out, coming up through the trees, and he knew at once she wasn't on foot. She was sitting down. She was in a car, with her bare feet up on a dashboard in a pair of boots several sizes too big, and her mind was so loud and so calm it took him a second to recognise it. There was music in it. It was coming in through her ears and filling up the whole of her head, enormous, a crashing slow drumbeat like a door being kicked in a stairwell, and a man wailing over the top of it, and Leah Clearwater, who in two years had never once thought about music in his hearing, was sitting in a car thinking *this is actually kind of good* and refusing to say so out loud.

And beside her, behind the wheel, there was nothing.

He'd known there would be. He'd thought about it all night. He'd sat on the floor of the cottage with Bella asleep on the bed behind him, which she didn't need to do anymore and did anyway, sometimes, because she liked it, and he'd thought about the wall in Leah's head and the man it belonged to. He'd thought that a man who could put a wall like that in someone else's mind would have one in his own. He'd been ready for silence. He'd been listening to Bella's silence for two years. He knew what it was like.

Bella's silence was a lack. You reached for her and there was nothing there, the way there's nothing in a room with the lights off. It was soft. It was hers.

What was sitting behind the wheel of that car was more like standing at the edge of a cliff in the dark. There was something there. He could feel the size of it. He could feel it the way he'd felt the thing at the end of Leah's line last night: as a weight, a bend in the air, a place his own mind slid toward and away from at once. But there was nothing coming off it. Not a word. Not a feeling. Nothing at all.

He was standing on the porch. He'd come out when he first heard them, and the others had come out behind him, one by one, the way they had for the witch. Carlisle at the top of the steps. Esme in the doorway. Emmett leaning on the rail with an expression of naked delight. Rosalie by the door with her arms folded. Jasper and Alice inside, at the big front window. Bella in the hall behind him with Renesmee on her hip, because he'd asked her to stay in the house and she'd agreed to stay in the hall, which was Bella's idea of a compromise. Jacob sitting on the top step, human, in his cut-offs and a T-shirt Esme had found him, with his elbows on his knees and his jaw set. Seth and Layla at the bottom of the steps on the grass. They'd come at half past seven, in Layla's truck; Layla had walked up the steps on her own, carefully, without Seth's arm, and said her back was nearly gone, and Carlisle had wanted to look at it, and she'd let him.

He stood there and listened to the car come up the drive. A mile. Half a mile. The music getting louder in Leah's head.

And then, at about a quarter of a mile, somebody spoke to him.

---

*Morning.*

It came into his head the way any thought did. That was the first awful thing. It came in among everybody else's, among Emmett's excitement and Rosalie's cold suspicion and Jacob's grinding unhappy *don't do anything stupid don't do anything stupid*, in exactly the way they did, as if it belonged there. A man's voice. English. Dry. Unhurried.

*You'll be Edward. The one who listens.*

Edward stood very still at the top of the porch steps.

*Don't worry. You'd be in my head, if I let you. I'm letting you have this bit. This bit's for you.* A pause. It was a perfectly ordinary pause, the pause of a man changing gear on a bend. *I'll talk to you like this when I want to. When I don't, you'll get what you're getting off me now, which is nothing at all. Don't bother looking behind it. There's a lot back there and some of it bites.*

He'd been reading minds since 1918.

In eighty-eight years nobody had ever done this to him. Nobody. Alice did something like it; she'd learned, over the years, to think things in his direction, to put a thought on the top of her mind where he'd find it. Carlisle did it sometimes. They were family. They did it because they loved him and because it was quicker than speaking.

This was a stranger, in a car, a quarter of a mile away, who'd looked at Edward's whole gift, the thing that had made him what he was for most of a century, and found the exact edge of it, and picked it up, and was using it to talk to him. Like a telephone. Like a man who'd found somebody else's telephone on a table and lifted the receiver.

*Bit rude, I know,* said the voice. *You'd know all about that.*

The man had been in Leah's head for a day, and before that he'd been in Seth's, and before that he'd been in town for two months. He'd been sitting somewhere in Forks for two months watching all of them, and none of them had known.

*We'll be there in a tick. I've told her I'll be nice.* The car came round the last bend in the drive; Edward could see it now through the trees, a brown Ford, a rented one, with mud up the sides. *I'd like a coffee. Tell your mum.*

Then nothing. The cliff edge. The weight in the air with nothing coming off it.

Edward realised he'd taken hold of the porch rail. He took his hand off it. There was a dent in the wood.

"Edward?" said Carlisle quietly, beside him.

"He'd like a coffee," said Edward.

Everybody looked at him.

"He spoke to me," Edward said. "Just now. In my head. On purpose." He heard how it sounded. He said it anyway. "He'd like a coffee. Black, I'd think."

Esme looked at him for a second. Then she turned and went back into the house, toward the kitchen, without a word.

---

The car pulled up on the gravel at the bottom of the lawn and stopped. The music stopped. The doors opened.

Leah got out of the passenger side.

She was wearing a man's gray T-shirt that came down nearly to her knees, with a hole in the collar, and gray sweatpants with a hole in the knee, and a pair of brown leather boots that were so much too big for her she had to slide her feet along the gravel to keep them on. There was sand in her hair. There was sand on her neck. Edward heard her think *oh, for God's sake* as every single person on the porch looked at the boots, and then he heard her decide not to care, and she came up the lawn sliding her feet with her chin up and her arms folded, and stopped at the bottom of the steps beside her brother.

Seth was looking at her with his mouth open.

"Lee," he said. "Are those his *boots*?"

"Shut up, Seth."

"Where have you *been*? Mom called Layla's at six. She said your bed was empty and your clothes were gone off the chopping block and—"

"*Shut up, Seth*."

Seth shut up. He looked at Layla. Layla looked at the sky, with her lips pressed together.

On the top step, Jacob had gone very still. Edward heard him breathe in, through his nose, hard, the way the wolves did, and heard what he found. Sand. Salt. The sea. Leah. And all over Leah, in her hair and on her shirt and on her skin, worked into her as if she'd rolled in it, that other smell. The wild old one from Seth's memory of the ridge. Jacob had smelled it, faintly, for a second, through Seth's nose yesterday. It was all over her this morning like smoke on a coat.

*Oh,* Jacob thought. *Oh, come* on. *Already?*

And then, half a second behind it, something he'd never have said out loud, something Edward made himself not hear any more of than he had to: *She looks happy. She looks—God. When did she last look like that?*

The driver's door closed.

He came round the front of the car.

---

Edward looked at him. He was aware of every other person on the porch looking at him too, and of every one of their minds doing it differently.

He was tall. A little taller than Carlisle. Broad through the shoulders, and moved like it. Black hair, short, that hadn't been combed. Two or three days of stubble. A plain, straight-featured face, a nose that had been broken once and set a fraction off true. He was wearing jeans and boots and a flannel shirt, and an old waxed jacket over the shirt, open, and under the jacket, on the left, the strap of something. A holster. He'd come up to a house full of vampires with a gun under his arm and hadn't bothered to hide it.

He looked like a man who'd come about the gutters.

Edward had seen men like that before. He'd seen them all his life. He'd seen them in the first war, in the queues at the recruiting offices in Chicago in 1917, farm boys and dock hands, when he was sixteen and wanted to go and his mother wouldn't let him. He'd seen them in diners and garages and lumber yards for eighty years. Ordinary men. He couldn't have picked this one out of a crowd of them.

Except that there was nothing behind his eyes. Except that every man Edward had ever looked at had had a voice in his head, and this one had a cliff.

He stopped at the bottom of the lawn, by the car, and looked up at the house.

He did it slowly. He did it, Edward realised after a moment, the way a soldier looks at a building he might have to go into. The roof. The long glass wall at the side. The door. The windows on the upper floor. The distance from the porch to the trees. Every one of them, on the porch and behind it. His eyes went over each of them in turn without stopping and without hurrying and without any expression at all, and Edward, who couldn't hear a single thing in the man's head, could hear in everybody else's exactly what it was like to be looked at like that.

Rosalie, by the door: *He's counting us.*

Emmett, on the rail: *He's counting us! Oh, that's so cool.*

Carlisle: *He's very calm. He's very, very calm.*

Jasper, at the window, very quietly, in a part of himself he kept for war: *He's already decided which of us goes first. He decided before he got out of the car.*

The green eyes came round to Edward and stopped.

"You'll be Edward," the man said out loud.

His voice was the voice from inside Edward's head. Exactly. It was a strange thing to hear it come out of a mouth.

"Yes," said Edward.

"Thought so. You've got the face for it." He came up the lawn. He walked the way he looked, like a man who'd come about the gutters, in no hurry, with his hands in his jacket pockets, and stopped at the bottom of the steps beside Leah. He looked up at Carlisle. "Doctor."

"Mr. Potter," said Carlisle. "Welcome. I'm Carlisle Cullen. This is my family."

"Harry," said the man. "Harry'll do."

"Harry." Carlisle came down one step. He held out his hand. "Thank you for coming."

Harry looked at the hand for a second. Then he took his own out of his pocket and shook it.

Edward heard Carlisle's mind do something small and surprised. Carlisle's hands were cold; everyone noticed. Carlisle had spent three hundred and sixty years watching people notice. They flinched. They hid it. They said *Oh, your hands are freezing, Doctor*, and laughed, and wiped their own on their trousers afterward when they thought he wasn't looking.

This man's hand was hot. Hotter than a human hand should have been; hot like the wolves. And he took hold of Carlisle's cold one and shook it firmly, once, twice, and let it go, and didn't do anything else at all. Didn't flinch. Didn't notice. Or noticed, and didn't care.

"Your mum's making me a coffee," Harry said. "I hear."

"My wife," said Carlisle. "Esme. Yes."

"Lovely." He looked past Carlisle at the porch. At Emmett on the rail; at Rosalie by the door. At Jacob, sitting on the top step with his elbows on his knees, staring at him. He looked at Jacob for a second longer than he'd looked at anybody else.

"Morning, Clifford," he said.

Jacob's head came up.

"*What* did you call me?"

"Big red dog," said Harry pleasantly. "Lives with a little girl. Very loyal. My cousin had the books. He never read them. I did." He came up the first step. "Don't get up."

---

The window by the front door shivered.

Edward heard it more than saw it, the thin glass humming very faintly in its frame, the way glass hums when a lorry goes past outside. And at the same moment he heard Jasper, inside, at the big front window, reach.

He'd heard Jasper do it a thousand times. He did it without thinking, the way you'd put a hand out to steady something. A room was too tense, and Jasper reached out and put his hand on it, and it went calmer. A man was angry, and Jasper reached out, and he went quiet. Edward had stopped noticing it decades ago. It was just how Jasper moved through the world: a gentle, constant pressure on everybody near him, like a hand flat on a table to stop it rocking.

Jasper reached out toward the man on the steps and put his hand on him.

The man stopped on the second step.

He turned his head and looked through the front window at Jasper. Straight at him, through the glass, past Alice's shoulder. He didn't do anything. He just looked.

"Don't do that," he said.

He said it quietly, at an ordinary volume, the way you'd tell a stranger on a bus they'd dropped their glove. And Jasper, behind the glass, took his hand back so fast Edward heard it in his head like a man jerking his fingers off a stove.

*Nothing,* Jasper was thinking. *There was nothing. I reached for him and there was—it was like putting my hand in a river. Cold. Moving. A lot of it. And then it turned round and looked at me.*

"Sorry," said Jasper out loud, through the glass. His voice was very level.

"That's all right," said Harry. "You didn't know. Now you do." He looked at Jasper a moment longer. "We'll talk later, you and me."

Jasper didn't answer.

Edward heard, in Leah's head, very clearly, a single thought: *He's not done with last night.* And under it, in the same part of her that had been hot with fury on Jasper's account twelve hours ago, something that felt a great deal like satisfaction.

Harry came up the rest of the steps.

---

"Excuse me," said Alice.

She'd come round from the window to the front door. She was standing in the doorway now, beside Rosalie, very small, with her arms wrapped round herself and her pointed face pale and pinched. She looked as though she hadn't slept, which was impossible, and as though she had a headache, which very nearly was.

"Excuse me," Alice said again. "Mr.—Harry. I'm sorry. I just—" She stopped. "I can't see you."

He looked down at her. She came up to about the middle of his chest.

"No," he agreed. "I expect not."

"I've been trying since yesterday. I can't see anything. I can't see anything you're going to do. I can't see anything anybody's going to do near you." Her voice wobbled, very slightly. "I've never not been able to see something. Not like this. Not since I woke up."

"I know," said Harry. "I'm sorry about that. It's on purpose."

He took his right hand out of his jacket pocket. There was a coin in it. A big, old, brownish copper coin, a penny, worn nearly smooth. He held it up between his finger and thumb so she could see it.

"You see what people decide," he said. "That's how it works, isn't it? Somebody makes up their mind and you see where it goes."

Alice stared at him.

"So I don't decide much," he said. "Not where you'd see it." He flicked the coin. It went up off his thumb with a little *ting*, spinning, turning over and over in the air above the porch, catching the gray morning light, and Edward heard Alice reach for it with everything she had, reach for what would happen when it came down, and find nothing. A coin in the air. Turning. Over and over. Nothing after.

He caught it. He slapped it down on the back of his left hand and held it there, covered.

"Heads," he said. He took his hand away. It was heads. "I let this make my mind up. Last second. Every time. You can't see what it's going to do, because it doesn't know either. Nobody does." He put the coin back in his pocket. "It's not personal, love. You're very good. That's the problem."

Alice looked up at him with her mouth open.

*He knows about me,* she was thinking. *He knew about me before he got here. He knew about me before he came to* Forks. *Somebody told him.*

Edward heard her think it, and heard what came after it, and heard her not say it. He thought it too. He'd thought it last night. Somebody had sent this man here, and that somebody knew what Alice could do, and the only people in the world outside this family who knew what Alice could do lived in a city on a hill in Italy.

He looked at the man on the porch. The man looked back at him, mildly.

*Next question,* said the voice in Edward's head. *That's what I'll say when you ask. Might as well save you the trouble.*

---

Emmett came off the rail.

He did it at vampire speed. He couldn't help it; none of them could, when they forgot, and Emmett forgot more than most. One moment he was leaning on the rail at the far end of the porch with his arms folded, and the next he was standing right in front of Harry, about a foot away, huge, grinning, with his hand out.

Harry hadn't moved. He hadn't even turned his head. His eyes had gone to Emmett a fraction of a second before Emmett moved, and stayed on him the whole way, and that was all.

"Emmett," said Emmett, with his hand out. "Hi. Hi. Okay. Two things."

Harry looked at the hand. Then he took it. Edward watched Emmett squeeze, because Emmett always squeezed; Emmett had broken bones in the hands of people he liked, by accident, more than once, before he learned. He squeezed now. Not hard, for Emmett. Hard enough to crush a brick.

Harry shook his hand. Once. Twice. Let go.

Emmett looked down at his own hand. Then he looked up at Harry with an expression of pure, rapturous joy.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Thing one. Can I arm-wrestle you?"

"Later," said Harry.

"*Later*? That's a yes? That's—Rose, that's a *yes*—"

"What's thing two?"

"Thing two." Emmett leaned in. He lowered his voice, which for Emmett meant you could only hear it from the bottom of the lawn. "Can you turn me into a bear?"

Harry looked at him.

On the porch, everybody looked at Harry. Edward heard Layla, at the bottom of the steps, think *oh no*. He heard Rosalie think *Emmett, I swear to God*. He heard Leah think nothing at all, very loudly, in a way that he understood after a moment was Leah trying not to laugh.

"Yes," said Harry.

Emmett's whole face lit up like a sunrise.

"Not today," said Harry.

"But you *could*?"

"I could turn you into a bear. I could turn you into a teapot. I could turn you into a very small bear and put you *in* a teapot." Harry went round him, toward the door. "Not today."

"Rose," said Emmett, turning to follow him with his whole body, like a sunflower. "Rose, did you hear—"

"I heard," said Rosalie.

She was standing in the doorway. She hadn't moved since the car pulled up. She looked at Harry as he came toward her, the way she'd looked at Layla, and at the wolves, and at Bella once, a long time ago: cold, level, and entirely unimpressed.

He stopped in front of her.

"Wipe your feet," said Rosalie.

He looked down. His boots were muddy from the trail. He looked back up at her.

Then he wiped his feet. On the mat. Carefully, both boots, front and back, the way you would in your grandmother's house.

"Thank you," said Rosalie.

"Ta," said Harry, and went in past her.

Edward heard Rosalie's mind, as he went by her, do something it almost never did. It hesitated. It stood there in the doorway with its arms folded for half a second, completely blank, and then it said, to itself, with enormous reluctance: *Well. At least he's got manners.*

---

He stopped two steps inside the front door. The way the witch had, a fortnight ago. The way people did in a house where they couldn't see the exits.

He looked like he'd known where all of the exits were since the bottom of the drive.

He looked round the big white room. The long glass wall at the back, with the gray morning and the river and the trees beyond it. The piano. The white couch. The staircase. Bella, in the hall, with Renesmee on her hip.

He looked at Renesmee.

He didn't look at her long. A second, perhaps. Edward, standing just behind him in the doorway, watched the side of his face while he did it and saw nothing change in it at all. But Renesmee, on Bella's hip, looked back at him. She looked back with her bright brown eyes, very wide, very interested, the way she looked at everything new. And then she did something she hadn't done for the witch, or the wolves, or any stranger who'd ever come into this house.

She smiled at him.

*Relax,* said the voice in Edward's head, very quietly. *I'm not here for her.*

And then, a beat later, so quietly Edward almost didn't catch it: *I'm here because of her. Bit of a difference.*

Edward stood very still in the doorway.

Harry looked away from Renesmee. He looked at the glass wall again, all of it, floor to ceiling, thirty feet of glass looking out at the woods.

"That's a lot of windows," he said, "for a family that sparkles."

Nobody said anything.

"Just an observation," said Harry. He looked round the room once more, and found the armchair by the window. The big one. The leather one, worn, that had been Carlisle's study chair in three different houses, and that for the last ten Sundays had been Charlie Swan's.

He went and sat in it.

He sat down in it the way a man sits down in his own chair at the end of the day: all at once, with a long breath, his legs stuck out in front of him, his muddy boots crossed at the ankle on Esme's white rug. He put his elbows on the arms. He looked round at all of them standing there, in the doorway and the hall and on the stairs, as if he were waiting for somebody to bring him his slippers.

Behind him, through the window, the trees dripped.

"That's my dad's chair," said Bella, from the hall.

He looked at her.

"Is it?" he said.

"Yes."

"Charlie's?"

"Yes."

He thought about it. He looked down at the arms of the chair, where the leather had gone pale from somebody's hands.

"He won't mind," said Harry.

Jacob had come in off the porch behind Edward. He came in fast, past Edward, all the way into the room, and stopped in the middle of the rug, about six feet in front of the armchair, with his fists at his sides. He was shaking a little. Edward could hear him thinking about it. He could hear him thinking about the absolute law, and about Leah, and about how much he would like to pick this man up out of Charlie's chair by the front of his flannel shirt and throw him out through thirty feet of glass.

"Get out of Charlie's chair," Jacob said.

Harry looked up at him from the chair. He took his time about it.

"Make me, Clifford," he said.

The room went very quiet.

Jacob stood there with his fists at his sides. Edward watched him want to. He watched every single part of Jacob Black want to, from his bare feet on the rug to the top of his head. He watched it come up through him like a wave, and he watched it hit the thing Leah had put in front of him last night in this same room, the oldest law his people had, and break on it.

Jacob didn't move.

"Yeah," said Harry. "I heard about your law." He settled back in the chair. "Lovely bit of legislation, that. Very forward-thinking. Somebody ought to send it to the Wizengamot."

"*Harry*," said Leah.

She'd come in behind Jacob. She was standing in the doorway in her dead father's T-shirt and the boots, with her arms folded, and she was looking at the man in the armchair with an expression Edward had never once seen on her face in two years. It was the expression of a woman watching somebody else's dog dig up her mother's garden, and knowing it was going to be her dog from now on, and not minding as much as she ought to.

"What?" said Harry.

"Be nice."

"I am being nice."

"You're in Charlie's chair."

"I like Charlie's chair."

"You promised."

He looked at her. Edward couldn't hear a thing from him; he could hear Leah, and in Leah's head, very clear, there was the inside of a brown Ford on the highway with the sun coming through the trees in bars, and a little finger hooked round a little finger across a gear stick.

"I'm not vaporizing anybody," said Harry. "I'm sitting down. Sitting down's allowed. You didn't say anything about chairs."

"Harry."

He looked at her a second longer. Then he sighed, through his nose, and took his boots off Esme's rug and put them flat on the floor, and sat up in the chair like a man in a waiting room.

"Better?"

"Better," said Leah.

Esme came out of the kitchen with a mug of black coffee. She came across the room and held it out to him, and he took it from her, and looked up at her, and said, "Thank you, Mrs. Cullen," in a voice Edward hadn't heard him use yet. It was quieter, and plainer, and the tone of a boy who'd been taught by somebody, a long time ago, how you spoke to a woman who'd made you something.

"Esme," said Esme.

"Esme," he said. "Thank you."

She looked at him for a moment, with the dish towel in her other hand, and Edward heard her think about a cupboard under the stairs.

Then she went and stood by Carlisle.

Harry drank some of the coffee. He shut his eyes while he did it. He opened them again and looked round at all of them, standing in a loose ring round Charlie Swan's armchair in their own living room like a jury that had been told to wait.

"Right," said Harry. "Who's first?"

---

### X. Jacob

*The Cullen house — 8:34 a.m.*

He couldn't stop shaking.

It was the small kind. He'd had it since he was sixteen, since the first time; it came up from somewhere under his ribs when he was angry and ran out along his arms into his hands, a fine fast tremor like a wire with current in it, and if he let it go on long enough it turned into the other thing. He'd learned to hold it. Sam had taught him to hold it, the first summer, out on the beach at night with his hands in fists, *breathe, Jake, breathe, think about something else*. He'd got good at it.

He was standing in the middle of the Cullens' white rug six feet from a man sitting in Charlie Swan's chair, and he was holding it, and it was taking everything he had.

The man was drinking coffee.

He was sitting up straight now, like Leah had told him to, with his boots flat on the floor, holding Esme's mug in both hands, and looking round the room at all of them over the top of it. Not at Jacob in particular. At everybody. The way you'd look round a room at a party where you didn't know anybody and didn't plan to.

Leah had come and sat on the arm of the chair.

Jacob hadn't seen her decide to do it. She'd been in the doorway with her arms folded telling the man to be nice, and then she'd walked across the rug in those stupid huge boots and sat down on the arm of the chair, next to him, with one leg tucked under her, like it was where she always sat. Like she'd been sitting there for years. The man hadn't looked up at her when she did it. He'd just moved his elbow off that arm of the chair, without looking, to make room, and put it back down a bit further in, so his arm was along the side of her leg.

And she'd let him.

Leah. Leah Clearwater, who'd broken Paul's nose in May for putting his hand on her shoulder. Who hadn't let anybody touch her since the spring without showing her teeth. Sitting on the arm of a chair in her dead dad's T-shirt with sand in her hair, with a strange man's arm along her leg, looking round the room at all of them like she was daring somebody to say something.

Jacob didn't say anything.

He'd smelled it on her from the porch. The sea and the sand and the cold, and that other smell, the wild old one, all over her, in her hair and on her skin, like she'd slept in it. He didn't know where she'd slept. He didn't want to know. He'd watched her come up the lawn in a stranger's boots with her chin up, and he'd seen her face.

He didn't know what to do with any of that.

So he stood on the rug and held the shaking down and waited.

---

Carlisle went first. Of course he did.

He didn't sit. He stood beside Esme by the fireplace with his hands behind his back and his head a little bowed, the way he stood with families in the hospital when the news was bad, and he said, "Thank you for coming, Harry. I know you didn't have to. I'd like to ask you some questions, if you'll let me. And I'd like to tell you some things about us, if you'll let me do that too."

"You can ask," said the man. "Ground rules first."

"Of course."

The man put the coffee down on the little table by the chair, on a coaster, which Jacob noticed because nobody ever used the coasters except Charlie.

"I'm not going to lie to you," he said. "Any of you. About anything." He jerked his chin sideways at Leah, on the arm of the chair, without looking at her. "She's sitting right there. I can't. I've tried. If I try, my jaw locks and the truth comes out instead, and it's embarrassing for everybody. So anything I tell you, you can take to the bank."

Leah, on the arm of the chair, looked at the ceiling.

"But I'm not going to tell you everything," the man said. "When I'm not going to tell you something, I'll say *next question*. When I say *next question*, that's the end of it. Don't push. Don't go round the side. Don't try and get Leah to tell you, because she can't, and you've seen what happens when she tries." He looked round at all of them. "People who push past *next question* have a bad time. That's just how it goes."

"Understood," said Carlisle.

"Go on, then."

"Why are you here?"

"I was sent to catch a vampire," said the man. "One that eats people. Alive, if I could, for study. I caught one. Yesterday. Ten past twelve, on the ridge above the Bogachiel. You know all about it; you've been in Balto's head." He nodded at Seth, who was standing just inside the front door with Layla. Seth went red.

"Who sent you?"

"Next question."

Carlisle didn't blink. He nodded slightly, the way you'd nod at a closed door. "Why study a vampire?"

"Next question."

"Why one that eats people?" That was Rosalie, from the bottom of the stairs, sharp. "Why not one of us? We're right here. You've been watching us for two months, apparently. Why go to all that trouble for some nomad when you could have walked up our drive?"

The man looked at her.

"Because the people I work for," he said, "have looked at your family and decided you're worth more alive than otherwise."

The room went quiet.

"Useful," the man said. "That's the word they used. Useful, and decent, and very probably on the right side when it comes to it. So I was told to leave you alone. And I've left you alone." He picked the coffee back up. "Sixty-six days, I've been sitting in your woods watching you not eat anybody. Hunting deer. Driving very expensive cars very slowly through a town with one traffic light. Going to school, some of you, which I'll never understand as long as I live." He drank. "Very boring. Well done."

"*When it comes to it*," said Edward quietly, from behind the couch.

The man didn't look at him.

"When what comes to it?" said Edward.

"Next question," said the man.

Jacob heard Edward breathe in, which Edward didn't need to do, and saw his face do the thing it did when he was listening hard to something nobody else could hear. Except there wasn't anything to hear. That was the thing that had been crawling up the back of Jacob's neck since the porch. Edward had told them, out there, *he spoke to me*, and then he'd gone white, and since then he'd been standing behind the couch staring at the man in the armchair like a guy staring at a wall with his ear pressed to it, getting nothing.

"And us?" Jacob said.

The man's eyes came round to him.

"The wolves," said Jacob. His voice came out rougher than he wanted. "Were your *people* told about us? Have they got a word for us too?"

"No," said the man.

"No?"

"Nobody told me anything about you. Nobody knew there were any of you. Nobody I work for has the first idea you exist." He looked at Jacob over his coffee. "I worked it out on my own, my first week, when I saw a russet-coloured wolf the size of a horse walk across your mate Carlisle's back lawn with a little girl on its back."

Jacob felt the shaking go up a notch.

"And I didn't tell them," the man said. "And I'm not going to."

"Why not?"

The man didn't answer for a second. He looked at Jacob. Then, for the first time since he'd sat down, he turned his head and looked up at Leah on the arm of the chair, and she looked down at him, and something went between them that Jacob could practically see, like heat off a road.

"Because I'm one of you now, apparently," said the man, looking at her. "Her mum's doing salmon."

---

Jacob opened his mouth to say something about that. He didn't know what. Something.

The man got there first.

"Right," he said, and put the coffee down again. "That's your questions. Most of them. Now I've got something for you."

He looked at Bella, in the hall.

She'd stayed there the whole time. She'd stayed exactly where Edward had asked her to, in the arch between the hall and the big room, with Nessie on her hip, close enough to the stairs to be gone in a blink. Nessie had her arms round Bella's neck. She'd been staring at the man in the armchair since he walked in, with her face against Bella's collar and her eyes huge.

"Bella, isn't it," the man said.

"Yes."

"You'll want to sit down for this. Or hold on to something." He looked at Edward. "So will you."

Bella didn't sit down. Edward came round the end of the couch and went to her, and stood beside her in the arch with his hand on her back.

The man looked at the two of them a moment. Then he started talking.

He did it plainly. He did it the way Jacob's dad told a story at the bonfire when it was one of the bad ones, all the decoration taken off, one fact after another, like laying out stones.

"The vampire your pack chased yesterday," he said, "is called Casimir Wolski. He was born outside Lublin in 1709 and turned in Kraków in 1731. He's been a nomad since 1760. He likes cities and opera and young people, and he's been to Volterra four times." He paused for exactly long enough to watch Edward's face change at the name. "He owes a favour there. An old one. From the eighteen-eighties. To a man called Aro."

Edward had gone the color of paper. Jacob hadn't known he could do that.

"In October," the man went on, "the tracker came and found him in Vancouver. Demetri. And told him Aro wanted his favour back. Here's what Aro wanted. Come to Forks, quietly. Find out whether Carlisle's pet human has been changed yet." He said *Carlisle's pet* in a flat voice, the way you'd read somebody else's handwriting out loud. "Ring home and say. Then come to Italy in the spring, so Aro can take his hand and see for himself."

"The promise," said Bella. Her voice was very quiet. "They said they'd check."

"They said they'd check. And one of the others, the blond one, the one with the face like he's smelled something, has been at Aro about it ever since. So Aro thought he'd send somebody nobody would look at twice. A nomad passing through."

Jacob was shaking. He could feel it in his teeth.

"He came on Sunday night," the man said. "Night before last. He came in from the east, over the ridges, and down on the north side of this house, and stood in your trees behind it at four in the morning. For about an hour."

Nobody in the room was breathing. Not even the ones who did it for show.

"And he heard a heartbeat," the man said. "Fast. Like a bird's. And he smelled something that was blood and not-blood at once, warm and cold together. He'd never smelled anything like it in three hundred years." He looked at Bella. "And he stood there for an hour breathing it in and thinking about how very, very interested Aro was going to be."

The room went cold.

Jacob didn't remember moving. He was just suddenly across the room, between the couch and the arch, between the armchair and Bella and Nessie, with his back to them and his face to the man, and the shaking had gone all the way up into his shoulders and his neck and his jaw, and he could feel his spine starting to go, the long hot pull of it, the wolf coming up under his skin like a fist under a sheet—

And behind him, something went *out*.

He felt it go past him. Over him. Through him. It came out of the arch behind his back like a breath, and spread, and he felt it go round him and round Bella and round the kid on her hip, soft and invisible and enormous, like a soap bubble the size of a room. Bella's thing. Her shield. She'd only been able to stretch it a couple of feet, a month ago. It was halfway across the living room now, and it was round him too. He could feel the edge of it on his skin like the air before a storm.

The man in the armchair had looked up.

He was looking past Jacob, at the arch, at Bella, and his face had changed. All the flat had gone out of it. He was looking at the empty air around her with his eyebrows up and his eyes narrowed, the way you'd look at a bird you'd never seen before land on a fence.

"Where is he," said Jacob. It came out of him as a growl. He couldn't stop it. "*Where is he.*"

The man looked back at him.

"In a circle on my floor," he said, "on his back, missing the end of one finger. He's not going anywhere. He's never going anywhere again."

"Did he tell them?"

"No."

"How do you *know*—"

"Because I was in his head when he rang them."

Jacob stopped.

"I had him ring Aro yesterday afternoon," the man said. "From my back room. On his own phone. About an hour after we caught him." He was perfectly calm. "I held the strings. He said what I told him to say and nothing else. He fought me on it. He wanted very badly to tell Aro about the heartbeat. He tried. I didn't let him."

"What did he say?" That was Edward. His voice had gone very strange.

"He said the girl's been changed since the end of summer. Her eyes are going gold, the animal way, same as yours. The family's as dull as ever. Nothing to report." The man picked up his coffee, looked at it, and put it down again without drinking. "Aro asked if there was nothing else at all of interest, in all that rain. And Casimir said the dogs still run here, and they chased him out of the woods, big stupid things, very tiresome. And he's going south for the winter. Mexico. And he'll be in Italy in the spring."

The room was silent.

"Aro thanked him," the man said. "He said, *You've done me a kindness, old friend. I shan't forget it.*" He looked at Edward, in the arch. "He sounds nice, doesn't he. On the phone."

Edward didn't say anything. Edward looked like he'd been hit in the face with a plank.

"*Tiresome*?" said Jacob.

It was the only thing he could get out. The rest of it was too big. The rest of it was the inside of his chest falling in like a mineshaft. Somebody had stood in the trees behind this house at four in the morning while he was asleep on that couch with Nessie on his chest, and listened to her heart, and he hadn't woken up. He hadn't smelled it. He hadn't been phased. Nobody had been phased. He'd been *asleep*.

"His word," said the man. "Not mine." He tilted his head. "I'd have said *loud*."

---

"Why?" said Jacob.

He heard his own voice crack on it. He didn't care.

"Why'd you do it? You didn't have to. You don't know us. You've got a—a vampire in a jar and your *people* and whatever it is you're here for, you could've let him tell them, you could've just—" He couldn't find the end of it. "Why would you do that?"

The man looked at him for a long moment from the armchair.

"Because I wanted to," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting." He jerked his head at Leah again, without looking. "And it's true. Ask her."

Jacob looked at Leah.

She was sitting on the arm of the chair with her hands in her lap. She looked back at him, and her face was the face she'd had in the kitchen last night when she was telling him about the absolute law: calm, and dead certain, and not even slightly interested in arguing.

"It's true, Jake," she said. "I was there. I watched him do it." She paused. "He held on so hard I felt it from across the room. Like he was holding a truck on a rope. He didn't let go once."

Jacob stood there between the couch and the arch with the shaking going out of him.

He didn't know where it went. It just went, bit by bit, out of his shoulders and his arms and his hands, the way water goes out of a sink. The wolf went back down under his skin. He stood there on the white rug in somebody else's T-shirt with his hands hanging at his sides, feeling Bella's shield still round him like a coat somebody had put over his shoulders, and the kid behind him on Bella's hip with her heart going, fast, like a bird's.

Like a bird's.

He'd have died. If Casimir had told them. If the red cloaks had come over the mountains in the spring. He'd have stood in front of her and died, and it wouldn't have done any good, and he knew it. He'd known it for ten weeks. Every single night on that couch he'd known it.

He looked at the man in Charlie's chair.

"Thank you," Jacob said.

It came out of him very quietly. It was the hardest thing he'd ever said, and it was the easiest, and he meant it with every single part of him from his bare feet to the top of his head.

The man looked at him.

"Don't," he said. "You'll make it weird."

---

"How did you know?" said Edward.

He was still standing in the arch, beside Bella, with his hand on her back. His face had come back a bit. It still looked like somebody had hit it.

"That he'd stood in our trees," Edward said. "What he heard. What he'd planned. How did you know any of it?"

"Next question."

"It's behind the wall," said Edward slowly. "Isn't it. In Leah's head. That's what's behind the wall. Some of it." He looked at Leah. "That's what you couldn't tell me. On the porch."

"Some of it," said the man. "Yeah."

"And you can tell us. And she can't."

"I get to decide what you need to know," said the man. "She doesn't. That's the deal I've got with the thing on her throat. It's the one there is. Nobody asked me." He looked at Edward a moment longer. "And you needed to know that. So now you do."

He turned his head and looked past Edward, at Bella.

"Do that again," he said.

Bella stared at him. "What?"

"The thing you just did. When I said *heartbeat*." He was leaning forward a little in the chair now, with his elbows on his knees, and his eyes were on the air round her, the way they'd been a minute ago. "You put something up. Round her. And round him, and round Clifford, when he came across the room. You pushed it out about twenty feet. I watched it go."

Bella's arm tightened round Nessie.

"You can *see* it?" she said.

"I can see the edges. Like heat off a road." He tipped his head. "That's lovely. That's really—huh." He sounded the way Carlisle had sounded last night about the Murtlap. "I know Occlumency, I've been doing it since I was fifteen. Occlumency's a wall you build in your own head. You've got yours on the outside. And you can *move* it." He looked at her with a bright, interested, intent look that Jacob had seen on exactly one other face in his life, which was Carlisle's, the day Jacob had first let him take his temperature. "Can I have a look? I won't touch. I'll just—"

"No," said Bella.

The man stopped.

"No," said Bella again. She'd gone pale under her pale. "Don't. Please."

He looked at her a second longer. Then he sat back in the chair.

"Fair enough," he said.

He picked up his coffee and drank some of it, and that seemed to be the end of that, and Jacob, who had been braced for an argument, felt something in his chest unclench a quarter of an inch.

"Your dad's got a bit of it," the man said.

Bella went very still.

"Did you know?" He was looking at his coffee. "Not like you. Nothing like. But a bit. I had a go at him in the diner in October. Just a skim, as he walked past my booth. You check the local law; it's standard. Couldn't get in. It was like a door with a big bloke sitting with his back against it." He drank. "One in fifty thousand, maybe. Runs in the family, I'd say."

"You tried to read my *dad*?"

"Once."

"Don't do it again."

"Wasn't planning to." He put the coffee down. "He'd have felt it. He's a good copper. He'd have looked round and I'd have been the only stranger in the room."

Bella looked at him. Jacob watched her decide something.

"Okay," she said.

Behind Jacob, very slowly, the shield came in. He felt it go past him, off him, like a coat being taken off his shoulders, and fold itself back in toward Bella and the kid until it was just round them, a couple of feet, the way it usually was. Bella never let it all the way down these days when Nessie was on her hip. But it was in.

---

The man stood up.

He did it all at once, and it was so sudden that every single vampire in the room moved. Jacob saw them do it. Rosalie took half a step forward from the stairs. Emmett came up off the arm of the couch where he'd perched. Carlisle's hand came out from behind his back. Edward's hand on Bella's back went flat. They did it the way you'd flinch from a dropped pan, without meaning to.

The man didn't seem to notice. He went past Jacob, round the end of the couch, across the room, toward the big glass wall at the back.

Toward Jasper.

Jasper had been standing there the whole time. He'd come in from the front window when the man sat down and gone to stand by the glass with Alice, and he'd stood there with his hands loose at his sides and his eyes on the armchair, and he hadn't said a word. He didn't say anything now. He watched the man come across the room toward him and didn't move at all, except his eyes.

The man stopped about four feet away.

He wasn't as tall as Emmett. He was taller than Jasper by maybe an inch. They stood there in front of the glass wall, with the gray morning and the dripping trees behind them, looking at each other.

"Now," said the man. "You."

Alice took hold of Jasper's sleeve.

"I withdrew it," said Jasper.

His voice was very level. It was the voice from last night, the slow southern one, the one he'd used on the field in June. It didn't shake.

"I know," said the man. "I heard. That's why you've still got both arms."

Nobody moved.

He'd said it the way he'd said *wipe your feet* back to Rosalie, or *later* to Emmett. The way you'd mention the weather. And every single person in the room heard it the same way Jacob did, which was all the way down to the bottom of their spines.

"You said she was leverage," said the man. "In this room, last night, about quarter to eight. Knowing I was listening."

"I did."

"You were right."

Jasper's eyes flickered.

"She is," said the man. "She's the only lever anybody's ever going to get on me. You worked that out in about ten minutes from a standing start, which is good going. So I'll tell you how it works, and you can pass it on." He didn't raise his voice at all. "Anybody who ever reaches for her—you, the old man in Italy, the people I work for, anybody—gets the other end of it. All of it. I don't care who they are or what they think they're doing it for. All of it." He paused. "Write that down somewhere."

Jasper looked at him for a long moment.

"Understood," he said.

"Good." The man held his eyes a second longer. Then something changed in his face, very slightly, at the corner of his mouth. "I liked you last night, for what it's worth. Said it to my face. More or less. Most people would have waited till I wasn't in the room."

And Jacob saw something he'd never seen in ten weeks of practically living in this house. He saw the corner of Jasper Hale's mouth go up. Just a fraction. Just for a second. The way one old soldier looks at another across a bar.

"Most people," said Jasper, "would have."

The man nodded, once, and turned round, and walked back across the room past all of them, and sat back down in Charlie Swan's chair, and picked up his coffee.

Leah hadn't moved off the arm of it. She looked down at him as he sat.

"You said later," she said.

"I said later. This is later."

"You said you'd be nice."

"I was nice. He's got both arms." He drank. "This coffee's very good, Esme."

"Thank you," said Esme faintly.

Jacob stood in the middle of the rug.

He looked at the man in the armchair, drinking coffee, with Leah on the arm of the chair beside him and her leg against his arm. He looked at Leah's face. He thought about the smell on her, and the boots, and her face coming up the lawn. He thought about a heartbeat like a bird's, and a man in a circle on a floor somewhere missing the end of a finger, who was never going anywhere again.

He thought about the absolute law. How it cut both ways.

He went and sat down on the end of the couch nearest the chair. He didn't decide to. He just did. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging between them, the way he'd been sitting on the porch steps when the car came up the drive, and he looked across at the two of them.

After a while, Leah looked back at him.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. He'd known her since he was in diapers.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then, very slightly, she nodded.

### XI. Layla

*The Cullen house — 9:20 a.m.*

The coffee table was glass. Layla had been looking at it for twenty minutes, on and off, and thinking that Esme Cullen was very brave to own a glass coffee table in a house where Emmett lived.

It had held up so far. It had held up through Emmett's elbow going down on it with a crack like a rifle, and the man's elbow going down opposite, and the two of them taking hold of each other's right hands over the middle of it while the whole family stood round in a ring and Seth said *oh my God oh my God oh my God* under his breath beside her like a prayer.

"Ready?" said Emmett.

"Whenever you like."

"On three. One. Two—"

Emmett went on two. Layla saw it. Everybody saw it. He went on two with everything he had, all at once, the way you'd hit a door with your shoulder, and his whole huge body went with it, his shoulder and his back and his legs braced against the floor, and his face did a thing she'd seen on weightlifters on television, every muscle in it pulling toward the middle.

The man's arm didn't move.

It stayed exactly where it was, upright, his hand round Emmett's hand over the middle of the glass, as if Emmett had decided not to push after all. He'd picked his coffee up with his left hand. He drank some of it.

"That was two," he said.

"I know," said Emmett through his teeth.

"You went on two."

"I *know*."

The glass under their elbows had started to make a noise. A very thin, very high whine, the noise a wineglass makes when you run a wet finger round the rim.

Layla stopped watching their arms. She'd watched enough. She let her eyes go loose, the way she did on a job, and looked at the room instead, at the air in it, and felt the back of her neck go cold.

It was everywhere. That was what she couldn't get over. It had been everywhere since he'd walked in the front door: a pressure, faint, in the air of the whole room, like the pressure in your ears on the road over the pass. You'd never notice it unless you were looking. She was looking. And right now it had come in close round the two arms on the glass table, so dense she could almost see it, like heat over a road, holding the man's elbow to the table and his arm upright and the whole of him in his chair as if he'd been bolted to the floor of the house.

The house was holding Emmett off. The man's arm was along for the ride.

"You're cheating," said Emmett.

"Yeah," said the man.

"I can *feel* it. You're—your arm's not even—"

"Nope."

"That's *cheating*."

"I know. You went on two." The man put his coffee down. "Shall we?"

He put Emmett's hand down on the glass.

He did it slowly. Very slowly, so everybody could watch. Emmett's arm went down, and down, and Emmett pushed back against it with everything he had, and Layla heard something in his shoulder creak, a sound like a ship in a storm. It made no difference at all. The back of Emmett's hand met the glass as gently as a leaf landing on water.

And the table broke.

It went in a single long line, corner to corner, with a sound like ice going on a pond, and both halves stayed exactly where they were, held together in their frame, with a crack straight down the middle of them.

Everybody looked at it.

"Sorry, Esme," said the man.

"Oh my *God*," said Emmett.

He'd let go. He was staring at his own hand. Then he looked up at the man in the armchair with an expression Layla had last seen on the face of a six-year-old in Albuquerque watching a hot-air balloon go up.

"Again," he said. "Do it again. Right now. Left hand."

"Later," said the man.

"You said *later* last time—"

"And this was later. There'll be another one." He picked his coffee back up. "I'll come round. We'll do it on the floor. Your mum's got nice furniture."

"She's not my *mum*—"

"Emmett," said Rosalie, from the stairs. "Sit *down*."

Emmett sat down, on the floor, right where he was, beside the broken table, still staring at his hand, still grinning like Christmas.

---

Layla had been working up to it all morning.

She'd been working up to it since the porch, really. Since the car came up the drive and he got out and she saw him in daylight for the first time, without the suit and the long coat and the dark glasses, just a man in a waxed jacket who needed a shave, and she'd thought: *that's Harry Potter.* And her whole body had gone hot and then cold and then hot again, the way it had once at a dueling final when she was eighteen, walking out into the lights.

She still couldn't make herself think of him by it. The name was too big to fit on a man in a waxed jacket who needed a shave. He stayed *the man* in her head, the way he'd been on the ridge.

She'd stood on the grass beside Seth and watched him talk to everybody. She'd watched him tell Jasper Hale about his arms. She'd watched him tell a room full of vampires about a man in Italy who'd sent somebody to listen to their baby's heart, and she'd watched him tell them he'd stopped it, and she'd watched Jacob Black thank him with a face like he'd been cut open.

And she'd stood there with her back not hurting, much, thanks to Carlisle and the Murtlap and a night on her stomach across Seth's knees, and thought: *I threw roots at him.*

She went across the room.

She did it before she could think about it any more. She went past Seth, who said *babe?* and then shut up, and past the broken table and Emmett on the floor beside it, and stopped in front of Charlie Swan's armchair, and looked down at the man in it.

He looked up at her.

"How's the back?" he said.

That threw her. She'd had something ready to say. She'd had about six things ready. None of them were an answer to *how's the back*.

"It's fine," she said. "Murtlap. And Carlisle. And I slept on my front."

"Bit stiff still?"

"A bit."

"Turn round."

She stared at him.

"Go on," he said. "I'm not going to do anything you'd mind. Turn round."

She looked at Leah, on the arm of the chair. Leah looked back at her and shrugged, very slightly. *He's not going to do anything you'd mind*, the shrug said, as plain as if she'd said it out loud. *He can't lie, remember.*

Layla turned round.

She felt him reach up. She felt his hand come to rest flat between her shoulder blades, through Seth's flannel shirt, very lightly. It was hot. It was hot the way Seth was hot, a hundred and something degrees, and she felt the heat go in through the shirt and her skin and down into the muscle where the cedar log had been. And she felt something else go in with it. Something careful and quick and very precise, like a pair of hands going through a knot. It went down the length of the bruise and out along the edges of it and back up the middle, and where it went, the last of the ache went with it.

He took his hand away.

She stood there with her back to him. She rolled her shoulders. Nothing. She bent forward, all the way, the way she hadn't been able to since yesterday. Nothing. Not a twinge. Like it had never happened.

She turned back round.

"That was a healing charm," she said.

"Bit of one."

"You didn't—you didn't *say* anything. You didn't use a—"

"Never do."

"Nobody heals wandless," said Layla. "Nobody. It's—healing's the *hardest*—you have to know exactly what you're—"

"I know a lot of Healers," he said. "I spent a lot of time in hospitals, when I was a kid. I picked some things up." He picked his coffee back up. "You're welcome."

"Thank you," said Layla.

It came out stiff. She heard it. He heard it too; she watched one corner of his mouth go up.

"Go on, then," he said.

"Go on what?"

"Whatever it is. You've been standing on that grass for an hour with your mouth shut so hard I could hear your teeth from the car. Go on."

She looked at him.

"You're Harry Potter," Layla said.

"Guilty."

"You're the—"

"Don't," he said.

She stopped.

He said it the way you'd stop somebody at a door who was about to walk into a room where somebody was sleeping. Very quietly. With his eyes on his coffee.

"I know what I am," he said. "You don't have to say it. Everybody always wants to say it. I've had it said to me in about forty languages. I've had it said to me in Mermish." He drank. "Go on. The other thing."

She swallowed.

"I threw a Stunner at you," she said.

"You threw twelve things at me. I counted."

"Eleven."

"Twelve. The roots were two. You did the moss and then you did a second lot under the slab where I was going to step, in case I stepped. I stepped somewhere else, but it was there." He looked up at her. "That was good. That second lot. I nearly didn't see it."

Layla stood there in front of the armchair with her mouth open.

"I'd like to see the Disjunction written down," he said. "If you'd let me. Not now. Some time. I took it apart on the ridge to give it back to you and I got most of it, but there's a bit in the middle I couldn't follow, where you've turned the Finite core inside out and run it back through itself. I've never seen anybody do that. I'd like to know how you stop it eating the caster."

"It's—" Layla's voice had gone strange. "You run it through a ground first. Through something dead. Stone, usually. It eats the stone."

He went still.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's *clever*."

"That's what you said on the ridge."

"I meant it on the ridge. I mean it more now." He was looking at her the way he'd looked at the air round Bella, with that bright intent interest. "Write it down for me."

"Why?"

"Because when I ring you," he said, "it'll be for work like that."

She looked at him. He looked back at her.

"Who do you work for?" said Layla.

"Next question."

"Is it the Aurors? The British ones? Everybody said—"

He laughed.

It was the first time she'd heard him laugh out loud. It was short and it surprised him, she thought; it came out of him like a cough, and he put the coffee down so he wouldn't spill it.

"Do I *look* like an Auror?" he said.

She looked at him. The waxed jacket. The gun. The muddy boots. Charlie Swan's chair.

"You look like somebody who's never filled out a form in his life," she said.

"I fill out loads of forms. I've got a report due Friday." He picked the coffee back up. "Next question."

She stood there. She did the thing she'd done last night in this same room with every mark she had: she took the gun and the badge and the boots and the accent and the healing charm and the twelve spells counted, and laid them out in a row in her head and looked for the shape. And she came up against it again. The same smooth wall. Nothing on it anywhere. Not a scratch.

"I'm not going to figure it out," she said slowly. "Am I."

"No," he said.

"Not ever."

"Not unless I tell you. And I'm not going to tell you." He said it quite kindly. "Sorry. It's not personal."

"That's what you told Alice."

"Everybody gets the same," he said. "Saves me remembering who got what."

---

The kid had been watching all of it.

Layla had felt her watching, the whole time. Renesmee had been on Bella's hip in the archway for an hour with her arms round her mother's neck, watching the man in the armchair the way she'd watched the blue lights come down out of the air a fortnight ago. Without blinking. With her whole self.

And while Layla was standing in front of the armchair trying to work out what to say next, the kid wriggled.

Layla saw it out of the corner of her eye. Saw Renesmee squirm in Bella's arms, and Bella say *Nessie, no, sweetheart*, very low, and Renesmee put a hand on Bella's cheek. Then Bella went still with her mouth open. And Renesmee slid down out of her arms and onto the floor, and stood there in her duck pajamas, a child who looked about two and a half and was ten weeks old, with her bronze curls down her back. She looked across the room at the armchair.

Then she walked over to it.

Nobody stopped her. Layla saw everybody think about it. She saw Jacob come halfway up off the end of the couch, and Edward take a step out of the arch, and Bella's hand come out after her. Nobody did it. Renesmee walked across the white rug in her bare feet, past Emmett on the floor and the broken table, past Layla, and stopped in front of the armchair, between the man's knees, and looked up at him.

He looked down at her.

He'd gone very still. Layla had watched him for two days now and she'd seen him do a lot of things, and she'd never once seen him go still like that. Like a man who'd had a bird land on his hand.

Renesmee held up her hand. Up toward his face.

"Nessie," said Bella, from the arch. Her voice wobbled. "Sweetheart—"

"She wants to show him something," said Edward. He'd come out of the arch. He was standing behind the couch with both hands on the back of it. "She's been wanting to since he came in. She wants to know if she can."

The man looked up, past Edward, at Bella.

"Can she?" he said.

Layla watched Bella. Watched her stand there in the arch with her empty arms and her shield wrapped round herself like a coat and her face doing ten things at once. She'd been a vampire for ten weeks. She'd been a mother for ten weeks. A man had just told her that somebody had stood in the trees behind her house in the middle of the night and listened to her daughter's heart.

And he'd asked her.

"Yes," said Bella.

The man looked back down at the kid.

"I've got a lot of locks on, sweetheart," he said. Very quietly. Layla had never heard his voice go like that. "Give me a second. I'll open a door for you."

He shut his eyes.

And Edward Cullen staggered.

Layla saw it. She'd never seen him do anything that wasn't perfectly smooth, perfectly controlled, like a dancer. He staggered like a man who'd been shoved. He went back half a step from the couch and his hands came off the back of it and caught it again, hard, and she heard the wood crack under his fingers. His head had snapped round toward the armchair. His face had gone completely white.

It lasted about a second.

Then he let go of the couch, slowly, and stood there with his hands open at his sides, staring at the man in the armchair, and his mouth was open and nothing was coming out of it.

The man had opened his eyes. He looked at Edward across the room.

"Sorry," he said. "Had to open it all to find the right door. That's done. You'll not get that again."

Edward didn't say anything.

The man looked back down at the kid.

"All right," he said. "Go on."

Renesmee put her hand on his face.

---

Layla couldn't see what the kid showed him. Nobody could, except Edward, who was still standing behind the couch looking like he'd been hit by a bus. She could only see the man's face.

It didn't do much. His eyes were on the kid's. And then, after a second, something at the corner of them went soft, and his mouth went sideways.

"Did she," he said.

Renesmee nodded. She kept her hand on his cheek.

"Little blue ones? All over the room? Coming down like snow?"

Renesmee nodded again. Harder.

"That was the witch," he said. "That was Layla. She did that for you." He tipped his head toward Layla, standing beside the chair, without taking his eyes off the kid. "Nice, weren't they."

Renesmee pressed her hand flatter on his face. Layla could see her concentrating, her little forehead creased.

The man listened. Then he laughed, very quietly, through his nose.

"Can I do lights," he said. "That's the question, is it?"

Renesmee nodded.

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he looked up, past her, at Bella in the arch, and at Edward, and at Jacob half up off the couch, and Layla saw him ask them. With his eyebrows. Just that.

Bella nodded.

"Better than lights," he said to the kid. "Watch."

He held his right hand out, palm up, beside her, at about the height of her head.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have a wand. Layla was standing two feet from him and she was watching his hand and she was watching his mouth and she was watching the air, the way she'd watch a doorframe, and there wasn't a word or a movement or a single thing to tell her what he was doing. One second his hand was empty.

The next second it was full of silver.

It poured out of his palm like smoke, like mist off a river at dawn, bright, so bright it lit up the whole of that end of the room and threw everybody's shadows up the white walls. It poured out and up and gathered itself, and Layla knew what it was before it had finished, because she'd been doing it herself since she was fifteen and she'd taught it to other people. And she stood there with every hair on her arms standing up.

A Patronus.

Wandless. Silent. Corporeal. In about a second. In a quiet room on a gray morning with nothing to be afraid of. Without even looking at it. He was looking at the kid.

It came together in the air and dropped to the floor, and it was a stag.

A big one. Bigger than any deer Layla had seen in the woods round Forks. Silver-white and shining, with a great spread of antlers that nearly touched the ceiling, and it stood there on the white rug in the middle of the Cullens' living room and lifted its head and looked round at all of them, and the light came off it like the light off a full moon on snow.

Renesmee took her hand off the man's face and stood there in front of it with both hands over her mouth.

The stag looked down at her. It lowered its head. It lowered it all the way, slowly, until its silver nose was level with her face, and she took her hands off her mouth and reached up and put both of them on its nose. They went through, a little, the way your hand goes into mist. Silver light ran up her arms to her shoulders and into her curls.

She laughed. Out loud. It went round the room like a bell.

Behind Layla, Bella made a sound.

Then the stag lifted its head and turned, and walked. Slowly, round the room, the way you'd walk a child round a garden. Past Emmett on the floor, who'd gone completely silent with his mouth open. Past the broken table. Past Carlisle and Esme by the fireplace, and Esme put out her hand as it went by, without seeming to know she'd done it, and the light ran over her fingers. Past Rosalie on the stairs, who didn't move but whose eyes followed it all the way round. Past Jasper and Alice at the glass. Past Seth by the door, who said *whoa* very softly. It went all the way round the room, and the kid followed it, step for step, with her hand on its silver flank.

And halfway back across the rug, in front of the armchair, it changed.

Layla saw it happen. Everybody did. The stag shivered. All of it, all at once, the way a reflection shivers when you drop a stone in a pond. The antlers went first. They went to mist and blew away. Then the whole shining shape of it ran together like wax and came apart and came back, lower, longer, leaner, all in about a second.

And there was a wolf standing on the rug.

A silver one, bright as the stag had been, shining in the middle of the room. Long-legged and narrow. Deep-chested and fine-boned, built for running, with a long sharp narrow face and big ears standing up. It stood there for a second in the middle of the floor, looking round. Then it trotted, light, straight to the armchair, past the man in it, and sat down on the rug in front of Leah Clearwater, and looked up at her.

Leah stared at it.

Nobody in the room said anything.

Layla looked at the silver wolf sitting on the rug, and at Leah on the arm of the chair, and she didn't need anybody to tell her. She'd seen that wolf. She'd seen it on the ridge, yesterday, coming off the granite with its teeth out, gray and fast and furious. She'd seen it a dozen times through Seth's eyes, in the stories he told her. Long legs. Narrow face. Big ears.

"That's—" said Leah. Her voice had gone very small. "That's me."

The man in the armchair was looking at the silver wolf.

He was looking at it the way Layla imagined you'd look at your own front door after somebody had painted it a different color in the night. Completely still. Not blinking.

"That's new," he said.

"Patronuses change," said Layla.

She hadn't meant to say it. It came out of her the way things had been coming out of her for six weeks, since a boy in a store told her not to buy the yellow jacket. It was the curse-breaker in her, the bit that couldn't see a thing without saying what it was.

"They change sometimes," she said. "Not often. It's rare. It happens after something—big. Something that changes the person. Usually when somebody—"

"*Don't*," said the man.

Layla shut her mouth.

He hadn't looked away from the wolf. But the tops of his ears had gone red. Bright red. All the way round. She watched it spread down onto the back of his neck.

"Usually when somebody what?" said Leah.

"Later," said the man.

"Usually when somebody *what*, Layla?"

Layla pressed her lips together. She could feel her face trying to do something and she wouldn't let it. She looked at the ceiling. She looked at Seth, by the door. Seth was looking from the silver wolf to his sister to the man in the chair and back, and she watched him work it out, slowly, the way Seth worked most things out, and watched his eyes go huge.

"*Layla*," said Leah.

"It's his Patronus," said Layla, to the ceiling. "He can tell you."

"I'm asking *you*."

"And I'm not telling you." She risked a glance down. The man in the armchair was still staring at the silver wolf with his ears on fire. "He'll tell you. He can't *not* tell you. You can ask him tonight."

Leah turned and looked down at the man beside her.

Layla watched her do it. She watched Leah look at the side of his face, at his ears, at whatever she could feel coming down that line between them. And she watched Leah's own face change. She watched it go from furious to puzzled to something else, very slowly, and then she watched Leah Clearwater, who by Seth's account had hated every human being on the Olympic Peninsula for two years, go as red as he was.

The silver wolf sat on the rug in front of them both with its ears up, looking from one to the other, perfectly content.

Then it faded. Slowly. Like a light going down at the end of a day. It faded until it was just a shimmer on the rug, and then nothing, and the room was a gray morning room again, white walls, glass, a broken table.

Renesmee stood in the middle of the rug where it had been, with silver still fading out of her curls. She turned round and looked at the man in the armchair. Then she walked back over to him and climbed up into his lap.

Just like that. Like she'd been doing it all her life. She climbed up onto his knee in her duck pajamas and sat there, and leaned back against his chest, and put her thumb in her mouth.

He sat there with his hands in the air on either side of her, like a man who'd been handed something very valuable and very breakable and wasn't sure where to put his hands.

"Er," he said.

"Put your arm round her," said Bella, from the arch. Her voice had gone thick. "She likes that."

He put his arm round her.

Renesmee sighed and settled, and shut her eyes.

---

Layla went back to Seth by the door, after a while.

She stood beside him and he put his arm round her, and she let him, and they stood there together and watched the room. Esme had gone to get more coffee. Carlisle had sat down on the couch, at last, as if his legs had decided for him. Rosalie had come down off her stair and was standing by the broken table looking at the man in the armchair with the sleeping child on his knee, and Layla couldn't read her face at all.

Edward was still behind the couch. He hadn't moved since the stag. He was looking at the man in the armchair.

"For one second," Edward said. His voice was very quiet. "When you opened it. Before you found the door. I heard you."

The man looked up at him over the kid's curls.

"I said sorry."

"I know. That's not—" Edward stopped. He started again. "I've heard a lot of minds. I've heard Aro's. I've heard minds that were a thousand years old. I've never—" He stopped again. "What *was* that?"

The man looked at him for a long moment.

"Me," he said.

Edward didn't say anything else.

---

"What happens now?" said Carlisle.

He was sitting on the couch with his elbows on his knees, the way Jacob had been sitting on the step. He looked tired, which Layla knew was impossible, and he looked older, which was impossible too.

"Now," said the man, "I'm your neighbour."

"For how long?"

"For good, I'd think." He shifted the sleeping kid a little higher on his chest, carefully. "The people I work for rang me this morning. Well. I rang them. As of half six this morning their time, I'm here long-term. Open-ended. I'm going to be up that hill on the Calawah for a long while." He glanced sideways at Leah, on the arm of the chair. "I'd have been here anyway, I think. Now it's official."

Leah didn't say anything. Layla saw her hand, on her knee, close into a fist and open again.

"And what do you want from us?" said Rosalie.

"Not a lot." He thought about it. "I'd like to talk to the Doctor about venom. Properly. Sit down with him for a few afternoons and pick his brains. He's been walking round with it in him for three hundred and sixty years and he's a medical man; he'll know things nobody's ever thought to ask." He looked at Carlisle. "If you'd be willing."

"I'd be glad," said Carlisle. He said it slowly. Layla watched him mean it.

"And I'd like you to keep doing what you're doing. Keep not eating people. Keep the kid out of sight. Keep your noses clean." He looked round at all of them. "And if anybody comes over the mountains in a red cloak, or anybody you don't know turns up in the trees behind your house at four in the morning, you tell me. Straight away."

"How?" said Rosalie. "You haven't got a phone. Or you've got a phone none of us has the number for. How are we supposed to *tell you*?"

The man didn't answer. He didn't need to. He just turned his head and looked up at Leah, on the arm of the chair, and Leah looked down at him, and everybody in the room watched it happen.

"Oh," said Rosalie. "Right."

"Through her," said the man. "Tell her. Tell Clifford, if he's phased, and he'll tell her. Tell Balto. They'll all hear it, and she'll hear it, and I'll hear it." He looked back at Rosalie. "I'll hear it about a second after you say it. Wherever I am."

Rosalie looked at him for a long time. Then, very slightly, she nodded.

"One more thing," the man said. He turned his head and looked at Bella. "Ring your dad."

Bella blinked. "What?"

"Ring Charlie. Ask him to come up. On his lunch, or whenever he can." He shifted the kid again. "Somebody's going to come up this road in a day or two. A woman, probably, or a man, in a long grey coat, with a badge that's hard to read, asking about the lightning on Monday night. I'd like a word with him before they do."

Layla went cold all over.

She knew who wore long gray coats and had badges that were hard to read. She'd known since she was eleven years old. Every witch and wizard in America knew.

"MACUSA," she said.

The man looked over at her, across the room.

"Probably," he said.

"They're coming *here*? Because of—because of the ridge? Because of me?" She'd taken a step forward without meaning to. Seth's arm came with her. "Because I Apparated in like an idiot without even—they'll find out. They'll find out about Charlie, and about all of you, and—"

"They'll find," said the man, "a curse-breaker with bees, who came up here to rest after a bad year, and has never told anybody anything." He held her eyes across the room. "They'll find a perfectly normal police chief who knows nothing about anything. They'll find a family of very boring doctors who keep themselves to themselves. And they'll find a lightning strike on a ridge above the Bogachiel at twenty past nine on Monday night, with fulgurite in the crater and a tree split top to bottom." He paused. "I've been through it already. Last night. All of it. They'll go home."

Layla stood there with Seth's arm round her.

"You did that," she said. "Last night. You went up there and—"

"Made it rain a bit harder," said the man. "Yeah."

"For me."

"For the deal." He looked at her a moment longer. Then, very slightly, his mouth went sideways. "And because I liked your Disjunction. I'd hate to have to wait fifteen years for you to write it down."

Layla didn't say anything. She couldn't.

She stood there by the door with Seth's arm round her and looked at the man in Charlie Swan's chair, with Charlie Swan's sleeping granddaughter on his chest and Leah Clearwater on the arm of the chair beside him. And she thought about her grandmother on the back step, at nine o'clock every night, for forty years, keeping her secret. Alone. With her bees.

*Leave them be,* Gran had written. *The cold ones and the hot ones both.*

Gran hadn't known about this one. Nobody had.

Across the room, Bella had taken her phone out of her pocket. She stood in the arch looking down at it for a second. Then she pressed a button and held it to her ear.

"Dad?" she said. "Hi. No, everything's fine. Listen—can you come up to the house? On your lunch?" A pause. "There's somebody here who'd like to meet you."

Another pause. Longer.

Bella looked across the room at the man in the armchair.

"Yeah," she said into the phone. "Yeah, Dad. Kind of. I think it's that kind of thing."

### XII. Charlie

*The Cullen house — 12:09 p.m.*

He knew the car.

He knew it before he'd got the cruiser all the way round the last bend in the drive. A 2004 Ford Explorer, mud-brown, Washington plates, a rental sticker from Sea-Tac in the corner of the back window. Mud up the sides to the door handles. A fir cone in the wiper. It had been parked round the side of the diner, nose out, in the same spot, most days at about half past eleven for nine weeks, and Charlie Swan had been a cop for twenty-three years, and he noticed cars.

He pulled the cruiser in beside it and turned the engine off and sat there.

*English,* he thought.

Doreen called him that. Everybody at the diner did by now. A big friendly loud Englishman with a camera round his neck who'd turned up in September and taken the back booth like he'd paid rent on it, and ate a cheeseburger every day in about seven bites, and Doreen's pie with his eyes shut, and tipped twenty dollars on a twelve-dollar check. Took pictures of moss. Showed them to anybody who'd stand still. A photographer. A book about the rainforest, for some publisher in England.

Charlie had run the plates in his second week. You did. A stranger turns up in a town of three thousand and stays, you run the plates. They'd come back clean. Rental, Hertz, Sea-Tac, a Mr. Harry James Evans of London, England, with a UK passport and a six-month visa and no wants and no warrants and not so much as a parking ticket. Clean as a whistle.

Charlie had sat in his cruiser outside the diner that day with the printout on his knee and thought: *too clean*.

He hadn't done anything about it. There hadn't been anything to do. A man was allowed to be clean.

He sat in the cruiser now, on the gravel in front of the Cullens' house, and thought about Monday.

Monday, eleven-forty. The Englishman getting up out of his booth all of a sudden, leaving a twenty on the table, half a slice of pie still on his plate. *Got a call. Light's coming good up the Bogachiel. Save me a slice for tomorrow.* Charlie had been sitting at the counter with his back to him. He'd heard it. He'd thought: *what call? He hasn't got a phone out.* He'd thought: *since when does that guy leave pie?*

Monday night, nine-twenty. Sitting in his own kitchen with the game on and a beer, and the whole sky going off like a bomb, out east. Thunder that rattled the windows. He'd gone out on the back step and looked, and seen the glow on the clouds over the ridges, up the Bogachiel, and thought: *that's close*.

Tuesday, eight in the morning. A call from the ranger station at Bogachiel. Lightning had hit up on the ridge. Split a big old hemlock top to bottom. Nobody hurt, no fire to speak of, too wet. Just letting you know, Chief, in case you get calls.

Tuesday, eleven fifty-two. His daughter, on the phone, in the voice she'd had since August, the new voice, very clear and very careful. *There's somebody here who'd like to meet you. Yeah, Dad. Kind of. I think it's that kind of thing.*

And here was the Englishman's car.

Charlie sat there with his hands on the wheel.

He'd made a decision, ten weeks ago, on his own front steps. Jacob Black had taken his shirt off in Charlie's front yard and turned into something the size of a horse, and Charlie had sat down on the steps and stared at it, and then he'd decided. He'd decided he loved his daughter more than he needed to know what had happened to her. He'd decided he'd rather have her, whatever she was now, and his granddaughter, whatever *she* was, than have answers. He'd said it out loud. *Need to know.* And he'd kept to it. Ten weeks, every Sunday, in that big leather chair by the window, with his coffee, he'd kept to it.

He'd kept to it two Sundays ago, when Maeve Quinn's granddaughter had lit the whole room up with little blue lights coming down out of the air like snow, and he'd sat there in the chair with his mouth open and said *maybe I need to know that one*, and then gone home and never asked.

He got out of the cruiser.

---

The front door opened before he got to it. Esme. Esme always did that, opened the door before you knocked, and he'd stopped asking himself how she knew.

"Charlie," she said. "Come in. There's coffee."

"Thanks, Esme."

He went in.

The first thing he saw was his granddaughter. She came across the room at him at a run, the way she did, too fast, a lot too fast, a bronze-haired blur in a pair of pajamas with ducks on them. He got down on one knee just in time to catch her, and she hit him in the chest like a sack of flour and threw both arms round his neck, and put one hand on his cheek.

He'd never got used to it. He didn't think he ever would.

She showed him a deer.

A big deer, all silver, shining, walking round this room. Antlers up to the ceiling. Her own hands on its nose with light running up her arms. And then it changed. It went to mist and came back as a wolf, a silver wolf, long and skinny, and the wolf went and sat down in front of—

Charlie looked up over her curls.

The Englishman was standing up out of the leather armchair by the window.

Out of Charlie's armchair.

He'd been sitting in it. Charlie could see the dent in the cushion. He'd been sitting in it and he was getting out of it now, unhurried, and he was looking across the room at Charlie, kneeling there on the rug with his granddaughter round his neck. He didn't have the camera. He had a waxed jacket on and a flannel shirt and a pair of jeans, and he hadn't shaved in a couple of days, and under the jacket, on his left side, there was the strap of a shoulder holster.

Charlie looked at the holster. Then he looked at the face.

The face was the same as at the diner. Same face, same eyes, very green. It was a different face, too. The diner face was the face of a man who wanted to tell you about the light on the Hoh at seven in the morning. This one looked back at Charlie across the room with no expression at all, quiet and level, the way a man looks at you across a table in an interview room when he's done this a lot more times than you have.

"Chief," said the Englishman.

The voice was different, too. The same accent. Rougher. Less of it.

Charlie stood up, slowly, with Nessie on his hip.

"English," he said.

---

There was a girl sitting on the arm of the chair.

He'd thought it was one of the Cullens for a second. It wasn't. Dark skin, short black hair cut ragged, a long narrow face. Sand in her hair. Sitting on the arm of the chair the Englishman had just got out of, with one leg tucked under her, in a pair of gray sweatpants with a hole in the knee and a pair of man's boots about four sizes too big.

And a T-shirt.

A gray T-shirt, a man's, too big for her, that came down nearly to her knees. With a Seahawks logo on the front, faded nearly to nothing from a thousand washes. And a hole in the collar.

Charlie looked at the hole in the collar.

He knew that hole. He'd watched it get made. February the fifth, this year, Super Bowl XL, Seahawks and the Steelers, in Harry Clearwater's living room in La Push with a cooler of Rainier between them on the floor and Sue in the kitchen pretending she wasn't listening. Fourth quarter. Hasselbeck's pick. Harry had got the collar of his shirt in his teeth and chewed on it like a dog with a rope and hadn't let go till the final whistle, and they'd lost, and Harry had sat back on the couch with a mouthful of wet T-shirt and said *well, there's always next year, Charlie*.

Ten weeks later he'd been dead on his kitchen floor.

"Leah," Charlie said.

Sue's girl looked at him from the arm of the chair. She'd seen him see the shirt. He watched her see it.

"Hi, Chief," she said.

Charlie didn't say anything for a second.

"Charlie," said his daughter, from the arch behind him. "Dad. This is—"

"I know who he is," said Charlie. "He eats Doreen's pie."

"Guilty," said the Englishman.

"Harry Evans. London. Photographer."

"That's me."

"You're not a photographer," Charlie said.

The Englishman thought about it.

"I take very good pictures of moss," he said.

Charlie looked at him. He looked at the girl on the arm of the chair, in her dead father's Seahawks shirt, and at the man standing next to her, and at the space between the two of them, which was about a foot. Which was nothing. Which was the kind of space Charlie had last seen between two people in a parking lot in Port Angeles in 1985, when he'd been young and stupid and Renée had been younger and stupider and neither of them had been able to stand more than a foot apart for about a year.

"His name's Harry," said Leah.

Charlie looked at her.

"Not Evans," she said. "That's for the car. Harry Potter." She held his eyes. "I know, Chief. I know."

Charlie stood there in the middle of the Cullens' living room with his granddaughter on his hip and didn't say anything at all for a long moment.

"Harry," he said finally.

"Yeah."

"Your dad," said Charlie, "would've laughed himself sick."

Something happened at the corner of Leah Clearwater's mouth. It was the first time Charlie had seen it happen since the spring.

"Mom said he'd have laughed for a week," she said.

"Sue's being generous," said Charlie. "A month. Minimum." He shifted Nessie on his hip. "He'd have told everybody at the bait shop."

"He'd have told everybody at the *tribal council*."

"He'd have put it in the *newsletter*."

Leah laughed. Out loud. It was short and it surprised her, he could tell; she put her hand over her mouth. And the Englishman standing beside her turned his head and looked down at her while she did it, and Charlie, who'd interviewed something over four thousand people in twenty-three years and could tell you most of what they were thinking from their faces, saw what went across his.

He'd seen that look before, too. On Jacob, looking at the kid in his arms. On his own daughter, in the hospital in Phoenix, the spring before last, when she'd woken up and seen the Cullen boy sitting by her bed.

He'd seen it on Harry Clearwater, looking at Sue, at their wedding, in 1982, when Charlie had been nineteen and the best man and dropped the ring.

Charlie looked at the Englishman. The Englishman looked back.

"Need to know?" said Charlie.

The Englishman looked at him a second longer.

"Need to know, Chief," he said.

Charlie let out a breath he'd been holding for about ten weeks.

"Thank God," he said.

---

The Englishman stood aside from the chair.

"Yours, I think," he said.

Charlie looked at the chair. He looked at the dent in the cushion. Then he went over and sat down in it, with Nessie on his knee, and it was still holding somebody else's heat, and that was strange, and he decided not to think about it. Esme put a cup of coffee into his hand. Black, two sugars. Nessie leaned back against his chest and put her thumb in her mouth.

The Englishman didn't sit. He leaned against the window frame beside the chair with his hands in his jacket pockets. Leah stayed on the arm, on the other side. The rest of them were round the room, the Cullens, the Black kid on the end of the couch with his elbows on his knees, Seth Clearwater by the door with Maeve's granddaughter. All of them watching.

"There's a broken table," said Charlie.

"That was me," said the Englishman.

"Emmett," said Charlie.

"That was also me," said Emmett happily, from the floor.

Charlie drank some coffee. He didn't ask.

"Somebody's going to come and talk to you," the Englishman said. "Tomorrow, maybe. Thursday at the latest."

"Who?"

"A woman, I'd think. Could be a man. Fifties, probably. Long grey coat. Very polite. They'll have a badge that's hard to read. You'll look at it, and the words'll sort of slide off, and you'll think you saw *Federal* something, or *Department of* something, and you won't be able to say what. They'll be very interested in Monday."

Charlie held his coffee.

"Monday," he said.

"The lightning. Up the Bogachiel. Monday night, twenty past nine."

"There was lightning up the Bogachiel Monday night."

"There was," the Englishman agreed. "Split a big hemlock top to bottom up on the ridge. You heard the thunder."

"Whole town heard the thunder."

"Right. So when they ask, that's what you tell them. Lightning. Big strike, Monday night, ranger station rang you Tuesday morning. Hemlock. No fire, too wet."

"And if they ask about Monday morning?"

The Englishman's eyebrows went up, very slightly.

"Monday morning," said Charlie. "Eleven-forty. You left half a piece of pie on your plate. You said you'd had a call. You hadn't had a call; you didn't have a phone out. You said the light was coming good up the Bogachiel." He drank. "Twenty-three years, son. I notice when a man leaves pie."

The room was very quiet.

The Englishman looked at him for a long moment. Then something happened at the corner of his mouth. It was the diner face, for a second, just a flicker of it, the big friendly loud Englishman, and then it was gone again.

"The whole week's been stormy, Chief," he said.

Charlie looked at him.

"Lightning," he said.

"Lightning."

"All week."

"Thursday night out over the strait. Monday noon up the Bogachiel, a bit, early. Monday night, the big one. Very unsettled weather we're having." The Englishman tipped his head. "You might mention the ranger station. Rangers are very reliable witnesses. Nobody ever asks a ranger anything twice."

Charlie drank his coffee.

"And if they ask about anything else?"

"You're a small-town cop," said the Englishman. "You don't know anything about anything. You've got a nice quiet town with one traffic light and a lot of rain. Some of the kids down at the reservation are on steroids, probably; everybody says so. The Cullens are very nice, very quiet, very boring. Dr. Cullen does good work at the hospital. Maeve Quinn's granddaughter's come up to sort out the old lady's house and keep her bees going. There's an English photographer who eats too much pie."

"That's all true."

"That's why it'll work."

Charlie thought about it.

"Are they going to hurt anybody?" he said.

"Not if everybody's boring."

"I'm the most boring man in Washington State."

"I know," said the Englishman. "That's why I wanted a word with you, and not with anybody else."

Charlie snorted into his coffee.

"One more thing," the Englishman said. He'd come off the window frame. He crouched down beside the chair, on his heels, so his face was level with Charlie's, and Charlie found himself looking straight into those green eyes from about two feet away. "They might try and have a look in your head."

Charlie went still.

"They don't need their hands," the Englishman said. "Just—a look. Like I did, in October. Sorry about that, by the way. You check the local law. You won't know they're doing it. You'll feel a draught, maybe. A bit of pressure, here." He touched his own temple. "Like a headache coming on."

"I felt that," said Charlie slowly. "In October. At the diner. I thought it was the coffee."

"That was me. I didn't get in." The Englishman held his eyes. "You've got a door in there, Chief. A good one. You were born with it. I couldn't get past it without leaning on it, and if I'd leaned on it you'd have known. Your daughter's got the same door, only she's built a house round it." He paused. "If you feel the draught, think about something. Something boring. Something you feel strongly about. Fill the room up with it. Lean on the door from your side."

"Like what?"

"What do you feel strongly about that's boring?"

Charlie thought about it. It didn't take long.

"The Mariners' bullpen," he said.

The Englishman looked at him.

"Seventy-eight and eighty-four," said Charlie. "Last in the division. Again. And they've got a closer who can actually close, for once, Putz, and they keep getting him the ball down three runs in the eighth because the middle relief's a bunch of—" He stopped. "You don't know what I'm talking about."

"Not a word," said the Englishman. "That's perfect. Think about that."

"For how long?"

"Till they go away."

Charlie drank the last of his coffee.

"Okay," he said.

The Englishman stood up. He looked down at Charlie in the chair for a second, with the kid on his knee. Then he put his hand out.

Charlie looked at it. Then he shifted Nessie to his other arm and shook it. The hand was hot. Hot like Jacob's, like the La Push boys'. He'd been noticing that about the La Push boys for ten weeks and not asking.

"Thanks, Chief," said the Englishman.

"Don't mention it," said Charlie. He didn't let go. He held on a second longer, and looked up, past the Englishman, at Leah on the arm of the chair. Then back. "Ever."

The Englishman's eyebrows went up.

"Her dad was my best friend," said Charlie. He said it quietly. He said it the way he'd say a thing at a traffic stop that he wanted remembered. "For thirty years. I was the best man at his wedding. I dropped the ring." He held the hand. "You understand me?"

The Englishman looked down at him.

"Yes, sir," he said.

Charlie let go.

*Forks Diner — Wednesday, November 22nd, 11:44 a.m.*

She came in at a quarter to twelve.

Charlie was on his stool at the end of the counter. Doreen had poured his coffee. Marion had brought his pie out of the back herself, blackberry, the last slice off the morning's pie, the way she did on Wednesdays. He'd taken the first bite.

The bell over the door went, and the rain came in, and with it the smell of wet wool. He didn't turn round. He looked in the mirror over the coffee machine, the long one with the Coca-Cola logo in the corner, where he'd been looking at the door from this stool for nineteen years.

A woman. Fifties. Gray hair cut short, like a man's. Steel-rimmed glasses. A long gray coat, wool, good wool, down past her knees, dark with rain across the shoulders. Sensible shoes. A leather bag on a strap. She stood just inside the door and her eyes went round the room the way a cop's did. Exits. Hands. Faces.

They went to the back booth, and rested there, and moved on.

They came to Charlie at the counter and stopped.

She came and sat down on the stool next to him.

"Chief Swan?" she said.

"That's me."

"Ruth Abernathy." She took a slim wallet out of her coat and opened it on the counter between them, beside his pie, and he looked at it.

There was a badge in it. Gold, an eagle. And a card, with her picture on it, in the same glasses, and some printing.

He looked at the printing. The words went sideways. Not much. Just a little, like a word in a dream, or a sign seen through rain on a windshield. He looked at it hard and got *United States* and something underneath that might have been *Department* and might have been *Interior* and wouldn't hold still. He felt his eyes try to slide off it and made them stay.

*You'll think you saw* Federal *something,* said a voice in his head. *And you won't be able to say what.*

"Interior," said Ruth Abernathy, and put the wallet away. "We look after some of the monitoring equipment up in the park. Seismic stations, mostly. One of ours had a bit of a fright on Monday."

"The lightning," said Charlie.

"You heard about it."

"Whole town heard it." He ate a bite of pie. "Monday night. About nine-twenty. Rattled my windows. Ranger station at Bogachiel rang me Tuesday morning. Big hemlock up on the ridge, split top to bottom. No fire. Too wet."

"That's right." She was watching him. She had gray eyes behind the glasses, pale and sharp. "Monday night. Our equipment registered that, yes." She paused. "It also registered something earlier. About noon. On the same ridge."

Charlie chewed his pie.

"Whole week's been stormy," he said.

"Has it?"

"Thursday night, out over the strait. You could see it from the highway. Monday noon there was some up the Bogachiel, early, I think. Before the big one. Monday night, the big one." He drank some coffee. "Very unsettled weather we're having."

"You saw it? On Monday? At noon?"

"Didn't see it. I was in here." He nodded at the counter. "Eating pie."

She looked at him.

And Charlie felt the draught.

He felt it exactly the way he'd felt it in October and thought it was the coffee. A pressure. Right in here, behind his eyes, at his temple, like a headache coming on. Like somebody had opened a door at the far end of a long hall in an empty house and a cold breath of air had come down the hall and was pushing, very gently, on a door that was shut.

He thought about the Mariners' bullpen.

He thought about it hard. He thought about seventy-eight and eighty-four and last in the West again, and J.J. Putz throwing ninety-seven with nobody on base in the ninth of a game they'd already lost. He thought about the middle relief. He thought about every single blown lead since April, in order, and he got angrier with every one, and he filled the whole of the inside of his head up with it, wall to wall, like water filling a room, and he sat on his stool with his fork in his hand and leaned on the door from his side.

The draught pushed.

He thought about the bullpen.

The draught pushed a little harder. He thought about Jarrod Washburn's ERA.

Then it stopped.

Beside him, Ruth Abernathy put two fingers to her own temple and pressed, briefly, with her eyes shut, the way you'd press on a headache.

"Are you all right, ma'am?" said Charlie.

"Fine." She opened her eyes. "Long drive. From Seattle. In this." She looked at him for another moment, with the pale sharp eyes, a little narrowed, the way you'd look at a lock that hadn't opened when you'd been sure you had the right key. "Anything else unusual, Chief? On Monday? Anybody you didn't know in town?"

"It's Forks, ma'am," said Charlie. "Everybody's somebody I know."

She turned on her stool and looked round the diner.

He watched her in the mirror. He watched her look at the Petersons at the window table, and old Mr. Weber at the counter with his paper, and the two loggers in the booth by the door. He watched her look at the back booth.

The English photographer was in it. Of course he was. He'd been in it since eleven-thirty, in his flannel shirt and his waxed jacket, with his camera on the table beside his plate and a stack of photographs spread out in front of him, six-by-fours, glossy, a few dozen of them. Moss. Green moss and yellow moss and red moss and moss on a log and moss on a rock and moss on a sign that said HOH RIVER TRAIL. He was holding one up for the girl across the table from him, and talking, loud, waving his other hand. *—and that's at seven in the morning, see, that's the thing, at seven the light comes down through the canopy at about a thirty-degree angle and it does this, look at that, look at the* green—

And the girl across from him was Leah Clearwater.

She was wearing her own clothes today. Jeans, a black hoodie. She had her chin on one hand and she was looking at the photograph he was holding up with an expression of perfect, total, stone-faced boredom. And with her other hand, without looking, she was reaching across the table with his fork and eating his pie off his plate.

He was letting her.

He went on talking about the light. She went on eating his pie. He didn't look at the pie. She didn't look at the photograph.

Ruth Abernathy looked at them for about three seconds.

Then her eyes moved on, past them, to the kitchen pass and the pie case and the door to the restrooms, and came back round to Charlie, and Charlie, watching it all in the mirror over the coffee machine, saw that she'd seen exactly what she'd been meant to see. A big loud English tourist with a camera, boring a local girl to death about moss while she stole his dessert.

She turned back round on her stool.

"Well," she said. "Thank you, Chief. You've been very helpful."

"Don't mention it."

She took a card out of her coat pocket and put it on the counter beside his plate. It was white and plain and had her name on it, and a phone number with a 206 area code.

"If you think of anything," she said. "Or if the weather does anything else interesting."

"I'll call."

She stood up, and buttoned her coat, and went out. The bell rang. The rain came in. The door shut.

Charlie watched her in the mirror all the way across the parking lot to a gray sedan that he'd have bet a month's pay was a government rental, and watched her get in, and sit there behind the wheel for a long moment, with both hands on it, looking at the diner. Then she started the car and pulled out onto Forks Avenue and turned north, toward the highway, toward Port Angeles, toward Seattle.

He looked down at the card on the counter.

The phone number was gone. The name was gone. It was a plain white card with nothing on it at all.

Charlie looked at it for a while.

Then he put it in his shirt pocket, behind his badge, and picked up his fork.

"Who was that?" said Doreen, coming down the counter with the pot.

"Park Service," said Charlie.

"What'd she want?"

"Asking about the lightning."

"Oh, that lightning." Doreen topped up his coffee. "Wasn't that something? Rattled every dish in my kitchen. My cat went under the bed and wouldn't come out till Tuesday." She went on down the counter with the pot. "Hey, English! You want more coffee?"

"Please, Doreen," said a loud English voice from the back booth. "You're an angel. Marry me."

"Get in line, hon."

Charlie looked in the mirror over the coffee machine.

In the back booth, the Englishman had put his photographs down. He'd stopped talking about moss. He was sitting back against the vinyl with his coffee cup in his hand, and he was looking straight up the length of the diner, past the loggers and the Petersons and old Mr. Weber, into the mirror. At Charlie.

He lifted his cup. About an inch.

Across the table from him, without looking up, Leah Clearwater ate the last bite of his pie.

Charlie Swan looked at the two of them in the mirror for a long moment. A man named Harry, in the back booth of the Forks Diner, where Harry Clearwater had sat with Charlie on a hundred Saturday mornings after fishing, arguing about the Seahawks with his mouth full. And Harry Clearwater's girl, across the table, eating his pie.

He didn't know what the man was. He didn't know who he worked for, or what he did up there on the Calawah, or why the words on a woman's badge had slid off sideways like rain on a windshield, or where her phone number had gone. He didn't know what had happened on the Bogachiel ridge at noon on Monday. He didn't know what the silver deer was, or the silver wolf, or what had broken the Cullens' coffee table. He didn't know what was in the Clearwater girl's face these days that hadn't been there since the spring.

He was never going to know. He'd decided that ten weeks ago.

He picked up his own cup and lifted it. About an inch.

Then he turned back round on his stool and ate his pie.

It was very good. It always was.
