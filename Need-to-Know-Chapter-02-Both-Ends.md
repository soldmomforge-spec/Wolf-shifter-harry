# Need to Know

## Chapter Two: Both Ends

---

## Part One: The Law

---

### I. Leah

*The north fork of the Calawah — Monday, November 20th, 6:41 p.m.*

*Ask him yourself.*

She threw it into the pack mind and kept running, and for about three strides nobody said anything at all.

Three strides was a long time in there. She'd been living in the pack mind since the spring, and in all that time she could count on one paw the number of times it had gone quiet. Sam's lot had never shut up. Jacob's lot was smaller and nicer, and it still didn't shut up. There was always somebody thinking about food, or a girl, or a fight, or the time Paul fell off the cliff at First Beach. Right now there were two of them in there with her, Jacob and Seth, and both of them had stopped dead in the middle of a thought, the way you stop when you put your foot down for a step in the dark and the step isn't there.

Then Jacob came back, loud enough to make her ears ring from the inside.

*Ask him? Leah. I don't even know where he* is*.*

*Northwest of you.*

*That's a whole forest!*

*Yeah,* Leah agreed. *Big one.*

She came down off the bench under the spruces at a long easy lope, the river loud below her in the dark, and went down the bank and into the water without slowing. The north fork was up to her chest where it ran past the big split boulder, the one that looked like a broken heart, and it was so cold it hurt her teeth. She went through it. She came out the far side streaming and shook herself once, hard, nose to tail, and kept going.

Behind her the line went back up the slope and through the trees and in through the flap of a little green tent, and down a slate hallway, and into a kitchen. She didn't have to think about it to know that. It was just there, the way her own tail was there. At the far end of it a man was standing at a cast-iron range with both hands flat on the metal. A kettle was starting to tick as it heated. A record was going round on the player in the corner, the second side, loud, too far off for her to hear, and she could feel him listening to it, the way you can feel somebody in the next room tapping their foot.

He'd been with her. For the first mile. She'd felt him come along, riding quiet behind her eyes, feeling the river and the cold and her paws on the ground, and she'd let him, because she couldn't think of a reason not to. Then the pack had come on in her head like floodlights over a field, and something cold had woken up under her ribs and closed over half of her afternoon, and she'd felt him go still at the other end of the line. Then she'd felt him leave. He'd pulled back out of her head the way a man steps out of a room so the people in it can talk.

She knew what the cold thing was. She'd known as soon as she touched it. She could see behind it fine; it was her own afternoon, it was all right there where she'd left it, the kitchen and the jars and the round room and the phone and the old voice laughing on the other end. Every bit of it was hers to look at. Nobody else could.

*His,* she thought. Quietly. Down at the bottom, where she hoped the boys wouldn't pick it up. *That's his. He put that there.* Then, a second later, more fairly: *No. That put itself there. He didn't know it could.*

She'd felt how much he hadn't known. It had come down the line from him like a draught.

*Leah,* Seth was saying. *Lee. Are you okay? Did he hurt you? Did he do anything to you?*

*I'm fine, Seth.*

*Your head's got a hole in it!*

*It's a wall. Sort of.*

*It's a big grey thing! I can't see past it! Jake can't see past it! There's all this stuff and then there's just—* She felt him poke at it. It was like feeling somebody press a thumb against a bruise she didn't have. *—grey! What is it? Did he do something to you? Did he put something in your head?*

*Seth.*

*Because if he did, Layla can take it out, she said she takes things apart for a living, she said she can find where something hooks into a person and—*

*Seth. Breathe.*

He breathed. She felt him do it, a long ragged gulp of air, and she realized he was in human form, not phased at all; he was hearing her because Jacob had phased and Seth had phased right after, the second her howl went up from the bench, and they'd both been sitting there in her head ever since she'd changed outside the tent. She could see where they were through their eyes if she bothered to look. She bothered. The Cullens' lawn, dark, the long white house all lit up behind them like a ship. Jacob at the bottom of the porch steps. Seth halfway across the grass toward the trees, as if he'd been about to come and get her and had only just remembered he didn't know where she was.

Seth was huge and sandy and he was shivering, and the night was about forty-five degrees, which for Seth was a heat wave.

*It doesn't hurt,* she told him. *Okay? It doesn't hurt. I don't even feel it, unless I go looking. It's just there.*

*What* is *it, though?*

She thought about how to say it. She tried to think *it's an oath*, and nothing came. The words just weren't in her mouth. They went soft and blurry halfway up, the way your own phone number goes sometimes when somebody asks you for it too fast. She tried again, *it's his job*, and that went the same way.

*Huh,* she thought.

*What?*

*Nothing. I can't tell you. It won't let me.*

There was a pause in the pack mind about the size of a parking lot.

*It won't let you,* Jacob said.

*Nope.*

*Leah, that's the creepiest thing I've ever heard. And I hang out with vampires.*

She didn't answer that. She came up out of the river valley onto a logging spur, mud and gravel and old ruts full of rainwater, and crossed it in two strides and went into the second-growth on the other side, the young hemlocks packed in close as matches in a box. She knew where she was now. She'd hunted through here last winter. The Calawah main stem was about four miles south. The Cullen house was another six past that, up the long slope from the north side.

She turned toward it without thinking about it.

*Where are you going?* said Jacob.

*Where are you?*

*Here. The house. Everybody's here. Carlisle's had Layla on the couch for six hours, he looked at her back, she's black and blue from her neck to her—* Jacob stopped, delicately. *Anyway. She's okay. Nothing broken. Carlisle said whoever threw her must've known exactly how hard to throw.*

*He did,* said Leah. *He checked.*

*He* checked*?*

*On his way over. To her. After. He said nothing was broken. He said she'd want a hot bath and six aspirin.*

The pack mind went quiet again. In the quiet, very small, she heard Seth think: *That's exactly what Carlisle said. Hot bath. Ibuprofen, though.*

*Close enough.*

*Leah,* said Jacob. *Come here. Just—come here. Okay? Come to the house. We need to talk about this.*

*I'm coming.*

*Right now.*

*I said I'm coming, Jake. Look where I'm pointed.*

He looked. She felt him look through her eyes, at the trees going past and the ground coming up and the direction of the light, such as it was, the faint grey glow of Forks low on the clouds to the southwest. She felt him work out where she was and how far.

*Twenty minutes,* he said.

*Fifteen.*

*You're tired.*

*I'm not tired.*

She wasn't. That was the strange thing. She should have been. She'd run the eastern loop that morning, and run down a red-eye along the top of the ridge above the Bogachiel, and been held flat on a rock with her face in the moss by something she couldn't see for what had felt like an hour. She'd fought it until her shoulders had burned and two of her claws had split. Then she'd run twenty miles by feel through country she'd never seen. She'd stood in a round room full of jars and watched a man take a vampire apart with words. She'd eaten six eggs. She should have been dead on her feet.

She felt like she could run to Canada.

She felt light. That was the only word she had for it. She'd been carrying something around in her chest for two years, heavy and sour, like a stone you swallowed, and she'd stopped noticing the weight of it the way you stop noticing the sound of the fridge. And now it was gone. She kept reaching for it, the way you reach with your tongue for a tooth that's been pulled, and there was nothing there. Just a smooth place. Just room.

There was a turn coming up, where the old game trail she was following forked. Right went on south toward the Calawah. Left went west and down, toward the coast, toward the reservation, toward a little house on a dirt road with a porch and a yard full of flowers.

Every time she'd come through here in two years, she'd felt the left fork pull at her. Every single time. She'd felt the cable in her chest go tight and point down that trail like a dog pointing at a bird, toward Sam, and she'd taken the right fork anyway with her teeth set, hating herself, hating him, hating the trail.

She came up to the fork at a dead run and took the right without thinking about it, and was fifty yards past it before she realized what she'd done.

She slowed down. She stopped.

She stood in the hemlocks with her sides going and her breath smoking and looked back at the fork. At the left-hand trail, dark and wet, going away down the slope.

Nothing. She waited for it, and it didn't come. There was no pull. There was a trail in the woods, and she knew where it went, and she didn't care where it went. She could think about Sam, she found. She could think about his face. She could think about Emily on the porch, and Emily's scars, and the baby they'd been trying for since the summer that Emily thought nobody knew about. She could think about all of it, and it was like looking at pictures of somebody else's family in a stranger's house.

She thought: *Oh.*

She thought: *I can't believe it was that easy.*

And underneath that, quietly, so quietly she almost missed it: *Two years and a guy with a gun. Real easy.*

*Lee?* said Seth. *Why'd you stop?*

He'd felt it. Of course he had. He was in her head. She felt him look where she was looking, at the left-hand trail, and she felt him understand, slowly, the way Seth understood things, all at once at the end.

*Oh,* he said. Very softly. *Oh, Lee.*

*Don't.*

*I'm not. I'm not doing anything.* A pause. *It doesn't—it doesn't pull anymore? At all?*

*No.*

*Not even a little?*

*Seth.*

*Sorry.* Another pause. Then, in a completely different voice, like a kid on Christmas morning trying very hard to be quiet about it: *That's good, right? That's good. Lee, that's* so *good.*

She didn't say anything. She turned around and went on south, and after a minute, without meaning to, she found her tail was up.

---

The boys didn't stay quiet for long. Boys never did.

Jacob had been going through her head the whole time she'd been running, the way you'd go through a house after a break-in, room by room, trying to work out what was missing. She could feel him doing it. She let him. There wasn't anything she could do about it anyway, and there was a mean part of her, a part that had been in charge for two years and wasn't quite ready to hand over the keys, that wanted to watch his face when he found the good stuff.

He found the tent first.

*Leah, that's a—that's a* hallway*. That's a hallway inside a tent. That's like fifty feet of hallway.*

*More.*

*How—*

*Magic, Jake. Wizard. Keep up.*

*It's got a* bathtub*.*

*Claw feet.*

Then the kitchen. The big table. The blue fridge, humming. Him at the range, in his shirtsleeves, with the gun under his arm, cracking eggs one-handed into a pan of bacon fat. She heard Seth's whole head light up.

*He made you* eggs*?*

*Six.*

*He made you six eggs?*

*And bacon. And bread fried in the bacon. It was really good, Seth. It was really, really good.*

*Layla doesn't make me eggs,* Seth said, sounding betrayed. *Layla makes me sandwiches.*

*Layla made you three sandwiches the first day,* Leah said. *I watched you think about it for a week.*

Then Jacob found the workshop. The motorcycle with the sidecar. The racks of guns through the narrow door. She felt him slow down there. She felt him look at the guns for a long time. Jacob didn't like guns. Billy had a deer rifle in the back of the closet that nobody had fired in ten years, and Jacob had never once touched it, because, he'd told her once in the pack mind on a long dull night on the northern loop, he didn't need to. *I've got teeth. Why would I need that?* And now he was looking at a wall of them hanging up neat as tools in a garage, belonging to a man who didn't need them either, and had them anyway, and kept them oiled.

*Why does a wizard need guns?* Jacob said.

*Ask him.*

*I'm asking you.*

*You saw what one of them did to the ground,* said Leah. *You tell me.*

Then he found the round room.

She felt him go into it the way she'd gone into it, through the black door with the iron bands, and stop. She felt him see the jars. All of them, floor to ceiling, round and round. The grey hand curling and uncurling against its glass. The skull with too many teeth. The flat black thing on the high shelf that looked back at you. She felt the fur on Jacob's back go up from his ears to his tail, there on the Cullens' lawn, twenty miles away; she felt it through his skin like it was hers.

And then he found the circle in the middle of the floor, and the thing lying on its back in it, rigid, in a red rain jacket. Its eyes rolling up at the ceiling.

*He's got the red-eye,* Jacob said. *Leah. He's got it in a—what is that? A cage?*

*A circle.*

*In his* tent*?*

*In his back room.*

*It's* alive*.*

*For now.*

She felt Jacob try to go further. She felt him try to see what had happened in the round room after the man in the suit had crouched down at the edge of the silver and said *afternoon* to the vampire, and she felt him hit the grey and slide off it. The grey was all over that part. There were bits of it she could still show him. The vampire's face coming alive on a body that didn't move. A little blue lick of fire on a pale hand, and the skin going dark under it like a stone in a campfire. Her own hands, gripping the seat of a wooden chair. Then grey. Then a long flat stretch of grey, from somewhere in the middle of all of that right through to the black door closing behind them.

*What happened in there?* said Jacob.

*Stuff.*

*What kind of stuff?*

*The kind I can't tell you about.*

*Did he hurt it?*

She thought about it. She thought about the scream that had come bouncing round and round off the jars.

*Yes,* she said.

*Good,* said Jacob, at once, with his teeth in it. And then, slower, a second after: *Did* you*?*

*No. I just watched.*

*And you're—okay with that? You're okay with whatever he—*

*It ate a dental hygienist from Olympia, Jake. Her name was Dana. She stopped to take a picture of the light on the river and it let her finish taking it.* She ran. *Yeah. I'm okay with it.*

He didn't say anything for a while.

She came down through a long stretch of alder and devil's club, the big leaves slapping wet against her shoulders, and out onto the bank of the Calawah main stem, wide and grey and fast in the last of the light, and went in. Deeper here. She swam a few strokes in the middle where her paws couldn't find the bottom, the cold going right through her coat to the skin, and then the bottom came up and she was out the other side and climbing.

Seth had been quiet. She'd felt him being quiet. She'd felt him go through the same stuff Jacob had, slower, and stop on different things. The kitchen, mostly. Him at the range. Him leaning in the doorway of the kitchen with a beer in his hand, saying something she couldn't show Seth because it was grey. Him pressing the heel of his hand into the middle of his chest. Him on the bench in the dark outside the tent, with the rain on his shoulders, reaching up to put his hand on the side of her face.

She hadn't been sure she wanted Seth to see that part. Now he'd seen it, and she found she didn't mind that much.

*He shot Layla,* Seth said finally. Very quietly. Like it was something he'd been carrying around for hours and had only just found the right place to put down.

*He shot the ground.*

*That's what Layla said!*

*Layla's smart.*

*He threw her twenty feet, Lee.*

*Her own shield threw her twenty feet. Her shield held. That's why she went. He told me.* Leah felt the shape of it as she said it, a thing she'd picked up in the kitchen without even trying, the way you pick up a song on the radio. *If he'd wanted to hurt her, he'd have shot her. He's got a gun that can turn a rock into gravel. He shot the dirt in front of her feet, Seth. He wanted her to stop. She stopped.*

*He went in my head.*

*Yeah.*

*He went through it like—he took stuff. Lee, he took stuff out of my head and I couldn't stop him, and I bit the bead, and—*

*I know.*

*—and then you* imprinted *on him?*

There it was. She'd felt it coming since the bench. She'd felt it coming up through Seth like a bubble in a bottle, getting bigger and bigger on the way to the top.

*Seth,* she said. *You imprinted on a witch in a camping store.*

*That's different!*

*How?*

*She didn't* shoot *anybody!*

*She threw a blasting curse at a guy she'd never met. Without asking him a single question. In front of you.*

*He had a dead guy under his arm!*

*He had a dead* vampire *under his arm. A vampire that I chased up that ridge so I could take its head off. Which you were helping with.*

There was a long, outraged pause.

*That's not fair,* said Seth.

*Nothing's fair,* said Leah. *Welcome to it.*

But she felt the corner of her mouth try to come up, in there, behind her teeth. And she felt him feel it, and get confused about it, because he couldn't remember the last time he'd felt her do that in the pack mind and mean it.

---

Jacob held it in for another three miles, which for Jacob was some kind of record.

She was coming up the long slope from the north side of the Cullen place, the slope where, last night at four in the morning, a nomad in a dead woman's jacket had stood in these trees for an hour breathing in the smell of a heartbeat. She knew that because she'd seen it in a round room, through a man's eyes, as he lifted it out of the vampire's head like a fish out of a stream. She wondered, in passing, if Jacob had found that part. She felt for it. Grey. Of course it was. *The child*, the cold thing had said when it closed, as clear as a word on a label. She couldn't show him that the red-eye had been here. That it had stood right where she was running now, close enough to the house to hear the kid's heart, and that the old one in Italy had sent it.

She tried, once, just to see. She tried to think, clear and simple, at Jacob: *Jake, it was here last night. It was in these trees.*

Her throat closed.

Wolves had throats of a sort, and she had one, and it closed. A hand came down out of nowhere on the front of it, cold and gentle and completely certain, and pressed, and the thought just stopped. Nothing of it came out in the pack mind at all. She ran on with her breath coming short and the cold hand lying there across her windpipe. Resting. Patient. Like it was asking if she was sure.

She stopped trying. The hand lifted off.

Twenty miles behind her, at the other end of the line, something jerked.

She felt it. A man standing in a dark place, outdoors somewhere, with rain on his face, going suddenly stiff all over. She felt him feel the hand on her throat. She felt him feel it like it was his own hand, and hate it, more than she'd felt him hate anything all day, more than the vampire, more than the old voice on the phone. And she felt one word come down the line from him, very clear, very low, the way a hand lands on your arm.

*Don't.*

*I wasn't,* she thought back. *I'm not. I just wanted to see.*

She didn't know if he heard. He didn't say anything else. But she felt him stay there, at the other end of the line, for a while, outside her head, waiting. Like a man who's heard a crash in the kitchen and is standing at the bottom of the stairs, listening, to make sure nobody's hurt.

Then she felt him go back to whatever he was doing, out in the rain.

*Leah,* said Jacob. *What was that?*

*Nothing.*

*Something just—you just went all—*

*Nothing, Jake.*

And that was when he lost it. She'd known he would. She'd felt it coming for miles, building up behind everything else he'd been thinking, the jars and the tent and the guns, the way rain builds up behind a dam. And now it came over the top.

*Him?* Jacob said. *HIM? Leah. Out of every single person on this whole entire planet. Seven billion people. You looked at a guy who put you face down in the dirt, who held Seth down, who threw Layla into a log, who walked up to us in a* suit *and a pair of* sunglasses *and called you* Lassie*—and you* imprinted *on him?*

*Six and a half billion.*

*WHAT?*

*There's six and a half billion people. Seven's a few years off.*

*Leah, I swear to God—*

*Say it to my face, Jake.* She came up over the last rise. Through the trees ahead of her she could see the lights of the house, all the long glass wall lit gold, the lawn dark and wet in front of it, and two shapes on the grass she didn't need her eyes for. *I'm right here. Say it to my face.*

She went in under the cedars at the edge of the lawn. She stopped behind the biggest one, and let go.

The change went through her the way it always did, the tearing feeling, the world going small and loud and then small and quiet, the cold coming in through her skin all at once instead of through a coat. She was on her knees in the wet needles under the cedar, human, steaming, breathing hard. Her clothes were on her left ankle where she'd tied them outside the tent, the cut-offs and the tank top rolled up tight in the cord. She pulled them on. Wet, still. They'd been wet since one o'clock in the afternoon. She found she didn't care about that either.

She stood up.

The pack mind was gone, the way it always went when she changed back, like a door swinging shut on a room full of people talking. Inside her head it was quiet. Just her, for the first time since six o'clock.

And the line. The line didn't go anywhere when she changed. It ran out of the middle of her chest away to the north-west, and down, and at the far end of it she could feel him, faint and steady, a man in a dark place with rain coming down on him, working at something with his hands.

She put her hand flat on her chest where it went in. She didn't know she was doing it until she'd done it. She took her hand away again.

Then she walked out from under the cedar, barefoot, across the Cullens' lawn, toward the light.

### II. Jacob

*The Cullen house — 7:02 p.m.*

He'd phased back on the porch steps about two minutes before she came out of the trees, because he'd figured if he was going to yell at her he wanted to do it with his mouth.

He'd been standing at the bottom of the steps since, in his cut-offs, in the rain, with his arms folded across his chest so tight his own ribs hurt, and Seth on the grass ten yards off between him and the tree line like a kid waiting for the school bus. Behind him the house was lit up gold from end to end. Every window. He didn't have to turn around to know who was standing in them; he could hear them, the little sounds they didn't bother hiding around him anymore. Emmett, leaning in the open front door, cracking his knuckles one at a time, slow, because he thought it was funny. Rosalie on the stairs inside, not moving. Edward on the porch right above him, so still that if Jacob hadn't been able to smell him he'd have thought the porch was empty. And through the big glass wall to the left, Bella, holding Nessie on her hip, with Nessie's hand flat against the window and her face pressed up to the glass, watching the trees with her mouth open, because Nessie had heard Jacob howl two hours ago and had wanted to know what was wrong, and nobody had been able to tell her.

He'd told her it was fine. He'd said, *It's just Leah being Leah.* She'd put her hand on his face and showed him a picture of Leah as a wolf, grey and skinny and mad, the way Nessie always pictured her, and then a big question mark made out of Jacob's own worried face, and he'd had to go outside.

Now he stood at the bottom of the steps and watched Leah walk out from under the cedars.

She was barefoot. She was in the same cut-offs and black tank top she'd had on at lunch, and they were soaked, and her hair was plastered flat to her head in short wet spikes, and there was mud to her knees. She came across the lawn toward him in the light from the windows, not fast and not slow, and the steam was coming up off her shoulders in the rain the way it came off all of them, and she was looking at him.

He'd been ready. He'd had a whole speech. He'd been working on it for about three hours, since one o'clock, since the ridge, since he'd come running up out of the trees and found a crater in the moss and Seth holding Layla against his chest in a fern patch and Leah gone, *gone*, twenty miles away with her head full of a rope and something at the bottom of a stranger lifting its head to look at her. He'd gone over it again while she was running, while he was walking through her afternoon like a burglar, through a hallway inside a tent and a kitchen with a blue fridge and a round room full of things in jars that had made every hair on his body stand up. He'd had it all lined up, point by point, like Billy lining up beer cans on the fence for target practice.

He looked at her face, and the speech went somewhere.

It was her face. Same nose, same mouth, same sharp chin that she stuck out at people when she was about to bite them. But something was off about it, and it took him a couple of seconds to work out what, and when he did, it scared him worse than the jars had.

She wasn't angry.

He'd known Leah Clearwater his whole life. She'd babysat him, once, when he was seven and she was ten, and she'd made him eat a raw egg on a dare and then told his dad. She'd been mad at the world, a little, the way older kids were, for as long as he could remember. And for two years, since the night Sam disappeared and came back changed and wouldn't tell her why, she'd been angry all the time. Every day. In the pack mind and out of it. It was in the way she stood, the way she walked into a room like she was expecting to get hit and planned to hit back first. Her mouth had a shape it made. Her shoulders had a place they lived, up around her ears. He'd stopped noticing it, the way you stopped noticing that the sky was grey.

Her shoulders were down.

She walked up the lawn to the bottom of the porch steps and stopped about six feet from him and looked at him with her arms hanging loose at her sides and the rain running down her face, and her face was calm. Tired, maybe. Wary. Calm, though. Like somebody had come along in the night and taken a big stone out of the middle of her, and she was still standing a little funny from the lack of it.

"Hey, Jake," she said.

That did it. The speech came back all at once, and it came out all wrong.

"*Him?*" Jacob said.

She raised her eyebrows.

"*Him*, Leah? *Him?*" He unfolded his arms. He didn't know what to do with them, so he pointed, at her, then off at the trees, north-west, at twenty miles of forest and a man somewhere out in it in a suit. "How could you—out of everybody—how could you imprint on *him*?"

"That's what you went with?" said Leah. "You had twenty minutes."

"Fifteen."

"You had fifteen minutes and that's what you went with."

"He put you face down in the dirt!"

"Yeah. I was there."

"He held Seth down on a rock! Seth's *seventeen*—"

"Eighteen," said Seth, from the lawn.

"—Seth's *eighteen*, and he held him on a rock, and he went into his head and went through it like—like a guy going through a dresser drawer—and he threw Layla twenty feet into a log, and he blackmailed her, Leah, he's *blackmailing* her, he's going to call her up one day and make her do God knows what. He stole our kill. He walked up to us in a *suit*. In sunglasses. In the *rain*. He called you *Lassie*."

"I know what he called me."

"He's got a bloodsucker in a *jar* in his *back room*!"

"A circle," said Leah.

"*Whatever!*" His voice cracked. He hadn't meant it to. It went up and broke in the middle like it hadn't since he was fifteen, and he heard Emmett, behind him in the doorway, make a small delighted noise, and he didn't care. "He's—we don't know *anything* about him! We don't know who he works for, or what he's doing here, or what he wants, or what he *is*—you saw that thing in him, Leah, at the bottom, we *all* saw it—and he walked into our woods and took your head apart like he owned it, and there's a *wall* in there now, a big grey nothing where half your afternoon used to be, and you just—you just looked at him and *went*—"

He ran out of air.

He stood there in the rain at the bottom of the Cullens' porch with his chest going up and down and his hands out at his sides, open, like he was asking her for something. He didn't know what. He didn't know what he wanted her to say. He knew what he was afraid of, which was that he'd look at her and see the thing he'd seen in Sam, and in Jared, and in Paul, and in himself, three weeks ago, in a mirror in the Cullens' downstairs bathroom at four in the morning. That gone look. That look of somebody who'd been picked up and put down in a place they hadn't chosen, and liked it there, and would never, ever leave.

He looked at her and he saw it.

He also saw her get angry.

He'd almost been glad. For half a second. It was the first thing about her face all night that he recognised.

Then she came up the last six feet between them, fast, barefoot on the wet grass, and stuck her finger in the middle of his chest.

"*How could I?*" Leah said. "*How could I?*"

"Leah—"

"No. No, you shut up. You had your turn. You had fifteen minutes and you used them to come up with *how could you*." She jabbed him. It hurt. She had a finger like a tent peg. "How could I imprint on *him*? Jacob Black. Look over your shoulder."

He didn't. He didn't need to. He knew who was in the window.

"*Look*," said Leah.

He looked. Nessie had both hands flat on the glass now. Her eyes were huge. Bella was holding her and staring out at the lawn with her mouth in a thin straight line, and the brown contacts were out, and her eyes were gold, and very hard.

"At least mine," said Leah, loud enough for the window, loud enough for the house, loud enough, probably, for Forks, "isn't a literal *baby*. At least mine isn't a *vampire*. At least mine isn't a literal baby vampire hybrid who was born ten weeks ago and is already reading *chapter books*."

There was a short, terrible silence.

Inside the house, someone dropped something. Jacob heard it hit the floor and bounce. It sounded like a magazine.

"No offence, kid," Leah said to the window, a little quieter.

Nessie, behind the glass, tilted her head.

"Leah," Jacob said. "That's—"

"*True*," said Leah. "It's *true*. You want to do this? Okay. Let's do this. Let's line them up. Let's line up every single imprint in this whole stupid tribe and look at them, since you want to talk about *how could you*. Sam's a grown man and he imprinted on my cousin, who was going to be my bridesmaid, while he was still with me. Quil imprinted on a two-year-old. *Two.* She still eats crayons, Jake. Jared and Paul got lucky, and good for them, I guess. Seth got a witch. In a camping store. Who throws blasting curses at strangers and asks questions later." She didn't look round at her brother; Jacob saw Seth open his mouth on the lawn and then shut it. "And you. You were standing in this house with your teeth out, about to kill a newborn baby because she nearly ripped her own mother in half on the way out. And she looked at you. And you went. And now you sleep on a couch with her on your chest every night like a guard dog. And everybody thinks that's *sweet*."

"It *is* sweet," said Emmett, from the doorway.

"Shut up, Emmett."

"Shutting up."

Leah turned back to Jacob. She was still holding up her finger. She folded it down and held up her hand, and stuck the finger up again, like she was counting for a kid.

"So, if you really think about it," she said, "mine's the best one. Out of all of them. Mine's the *best*. Because, A." She held the finger in his face. "He's not a baby. He's a grown man. He's got a job and a car. He's got a *bathtub*."

A second finger.

"B. He's human. Mostly. He's not a vampire, he doesn't drink blood, he doesn't sparkle, he's got a pulse. He eats eggs. Six at a time. He's got a *beer fridge*."

A third.

"And C." Her voice dropped. Jacob didn't think it was on purpose. It went low and rough in a way he'd never once heard it go in two years of sharing a head with her. "He's also a wolf."

The rain came down on the lawn.

"So take that," said Leah.

Jacob stood there with her three fingers about an inch from his nose and found he didn't have anything to say. Behind him he heard Emmett very quietly say *oh, she got you*, and Rosalie, on the stairs, very quietly say *Emmett*, and the sound of a hand landing on the back of a big head.

"We don't know that," Jacob said finally. It came out weaker than he wanted. "That he's a wolf. We don't know what that was. That thing. At the bottom. It could have been anything. It could have been something he—I don't know. Caught. Stole. Some kind of magic thing he keeps in there, like the jars."

"It's a wolf," said Leah.

"You don't know that."

"Jake." She dropped her hand. "It looked at me. Down the line. It looked right up out of him at me and it *knew* me. And mine knew it. I felt mine look at it. I felt her look up from where she's been lying in the dark on her own for two years and *see it*." Her jaw worked. "You know what mine did?"

He didn't say anything.

"She wagged her tail," said Leah. "Mine. My wolf. She's never done that in her life. Not for Sam. Not for you. Not for Seth, and she loves Seth. She saw that thing at the bottom of him and she wagged her stupid tail." She looked at him. "Don't tell me what it is, Jake. I know what it is."

He didn't tell her.

He stood there and he thought about the rope. He'd seen it in Seth's head on the ridge, he'd seen it go into the guy in the suit and keep going, down, through those three things like locks, into the dark. He'd seen the thing at the bottom lift its head. He'd felt how big it was, the way you feel a truck go by in the dark on the highway from the wind it makes. He'd tried for six hours to make it into something else. Some kind of trick. Some kind of spell.

He'd known. The second he'd seen it. Every hair on his body had known.

"And it went both ways," he said. He hadn't meant to say it out loud.

"Yeah," said Leah.

"Ours don't go both ways."

"No," said Leah. "They don't." She looked at him for a long moment. "Did you choose her?"

He stared at her.

"Nessie," said Leah. "When you looked at her. On the floor in there, with the doc trying to get Bella's heart going and Blondie holding a baby covered in blood, and your teeth out. Did you choose her? Did you think about it? Did you stand there and go, okay, out of six and a half billion people, I think *this* one?"

"No," said Jacob.

"Did Sam choose Emily?"

"No."

"Did Seth stand in Newton's Olympic Outfitters with fifteen pairs of shorts and think it over?"

"Leah, that's not—"

"Then what makes you think *I* chose *this*?"

She said it quietly. That was what got him. She'd been yelling, and then she wasn't, and the last bit came out so flat and quiet that he almost didn't hear it over the rain.

"Do you think I'd have *picked* this?" said Leah. "Do you really think, if I got to pick, I'd have walked up to the guy who just pinned me to a rock and gone, *yeah, him*? I was going to tear his throat out. I was standing there with my teeth out, telling him to come on, *try it now that I can move*. I was going to go for his face." She stopped. "And he took his glasses off. I don't even know why. And I looked back at him because I was too mad to look anywhere else. And that was it. That was all it took. That was all I did, Jake. I looked at a guy."

Jacob didn't say anything.

"So don't ask me how I could," said Leah. "You of all people. Don't you dare."

She stepped back. She folded her arms across her chest, the way he had, and stood there in the rain with her bare feet in the grass and looked at him.

And he thought about the Cullens' living room, ten weeks ago. The smell of blood so thick he could taste it. Rosalie with the baby, with the thing, he'd thought of it as a thing, the thing that had killed Bella. Walking across the room to it with his teeth out and his whole body shaking, so sure, so completely sure what he was there to do. And then the thing had looked at him. And there'd been a second. One second. And then there'd been no thing. There'd been her, and there'd been nothing in the whole wide world he wouldn't do for her, and he hadn't chosen it, not any of it, and he'd have chosen it a thousand times over if anybody had given him the chance.

Behind the glass, Nessie had taken one hand off the window and put it on Bella's cheek. Bella's face changed. Jacob didn't need to see the picture. He knew what she'd be asking. *Is Leah sad? Is Leah okay? Why is Jake making that face?*

"No," Jacob said, finally. "I don't think you chose it."

"Good."

"I just—" He pushed his hands back through his wet hair. "Leah. He's *dangerous*. You saw what he did. You saw what he *is*. He could've killed all three of you on that ridge without even taking his hands out of his pockets."

"Yeah," said Leah. "He could've."

"So why didn't he?"

She looked at him for a long time.

"Because he didn't want to," she said. "He doesn't kill things he doesn't have to."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I can't lie to him," said Leah. "And he can't lie to me."

The porch creaked above him. Edward had moved. Just a little. Jacob felt him do it; he felt Edward go still in a different way, sharper.

"What?" said Jacob.

"We can't lie," said Leah. "To each other. We tried. In front of his tent. He tried to tell me his name was Harry Evans, which is what it says on his passport, and his mouth wouldn't do it. It just stopped. And then it said his real name instead, like somebody else was working his jaw. And then I tried to tell him I was fine, and I couldn't. I told him I was freaking out." Her mouth twisted. "Which I was. So."

"That's not how imprinting works," said Jacob slowly.

"Yeah," said Leah. "He said that too."

"So everything he told you—"

"Was true. All of it. Every word." She looked at the house, at the windows, at the people standing in them. "That's what I'm saying. He didn't kill us because he didn't want to. Because he doesn't. Because he only does what he needs to, and he didn't need to. Because he had what he came for. He told me. He couldn't *not* tell me."

"What did he come for?"

She opened her mouth. Jacob watched her open it.

Nothing came out.

He watched her try. He watched her jaw work, and her throat move, and her eyes go wide and furious, exactly the way they'd gone, he realized, when she'd been held face-down on the granite fighting something she couldn't see. Her hand came up to her own throat. It stayed there. She stood on the Cullens' lawn with her hand at her throat and her mouth half open, and nothing came out of it, and the rain came down.

Then she shut her mouth and dropped her hand.

"That," she said, in a perfectly normal voice, "is the wall."

Seth had come up the lawn. He was standing right behind her now, very close, close enough that if she fell over backward he'd have caught her. He was looking at the back of her neck like he wanted to put his hand on it and didn't know if he was allowed.

"Lee," Seth said. "What's his real name?"

"Harry," said Leah.

Seth went completely still.

Jacob did too. He felt it go through him, through his chest and his stomach, a weird hollow drop like missing a step in the dark. He couldn't help it. He looked at Seth. Seth was looking at the back of his sister's head.

Leah didn't turn around.

"Yeah," she said. "I know."

Nobody said anything.

Jacob had been at the funeral. He'd been sixteen. He'd stood at the back of the crowd at the cemetery on the hill above the reservation with his dad's chair in front of him, in the rain, in a suit of Billy's that was too short in the arms, and watched Charlie Swan stand at the front with his hat in his hands, staring at the coffin like he couldn't work out what it was doing there. Charlie's best friend. Charlie's fishing buddy, thirty years. They'd taken the boat out every summer weekend since before Bella was born. Sue Clearwater had stood at the head of the grave with her hand on Seth's shoulder, and Seth had been shooting up so fast that week his wrists stuck out of his sleeves, and Leah had stood a little apart from both of them with her face like a locked door, and she hadn't cried. Not once. Not that whole day. Jacob had found out later, much later, in the pack mind, that it was because she'd phased for the first time the night before, in the yard, screaming, and her father had seen what she was, and grabbed at his chest, and gone down in the next room while she was still out there in the dark.

Harry Clearwater. He'd made the best smoked salmon on the reservation and he'd laughed like a seal barking and he'd called Jacob *Jake the Snake* until Jacob was twelve.

"His name's Harry," Seth said. Small.

"I know what his name is, Seth."

"Lee—"

"I *know*." She still didn't turn round. "I noticed. Okay? Out in front of his tent, when he said it, I noticed. I didn't say anything. I didn't think about it. I didn't let myself. And then I didn't think about it all afternoon. And now you're all thinking about it, and I'm thinking about it, and it's just a *name*, Seth, it's a really common name, there's probably a million of them, and it doesn't *mean* anything." Her voice was steady right up to the very end, and then it wasn't, and she stopped.

Seth put his hand on the back of her neck.

She let him. She stood there and let him, with her arms folded and her chin up and her eyes on Jacob, and Jacob looked back at her and thought, for the first time in two years, *she's going to be okay*. He didn't know where it came from. He didn't know if it was true. It came up out of the middle of him anyway, the way the wolf came up, from somewhere older than thinking.

"Harry what?" said Jacob, carefully. "What's the rest of it?"

Leah opened her mouth.

He saw it happen again. He saw her get halfway to it, and stop, and her hand come up to her throat.

"*Stop*," said Seth. "Lee, stop, don't. It's hurting you. Don't."

"It's fine." Her voice came out squashed and strange. "It just *sits* there."

"Then don't make it do anything else."

She let go of it. She shut her mouth. She shook her head, once, hard, like a dog coming out of water.

And on the porch above them, Edward Cullen said, very quietly, in a voice Jacob had never heard him use before, a voice like a man reading a name off a stone:

"Potter."

Leah's head snapped up.

"Harry James Potter," said Edward.

Inside the house, on the long white couch where she'd been lying on her stomach for six hours under a heating pad with a mug of Esme's tea going cold on the floor by her hand, Layla Quinn sat bolt upright and said, loud enough for the whole house, loud enough for the lawn, loud enough, probably, for Forks:

"*What?*"

### III. Edward

*The Cullen house — 7:09 p.m.*

He hadn't meant to say it.

That was the truth, and Edward had been alive long enough to know that it was the kind of truth that didn't excuse anything. He'd been standing at the top of the porch steps for twenty minutes listening to Leah Clearwater's mind come toward the house through the woods, and then come out of the trees, and then stand on the lawn shouting at Jacob, and he'd been listening the way he always listened, because he couldn't do anything else. And when she'd put her hand to her throat the second time, and he'd heard what was in there pressing on her, he'd opened his mouth before he'd finished deciding to.

He'd wanted it to stop. That was all. She'd been trying to say a word and something was holding her by the throat until she quit, and he'd been able to read the word, so he'd said it for her.

Now he stood at the top of the steps in the rain with the name hanging in the air and every mind within a mile turned toward him at once, and he thought, with the small cold clarity that came to him in moments like this: *That was a mistake.*

Then he thought: *No. It would have come out. She'd have kept trying.*

He'd been reading Leah for two years, on and off, at the edges of things. Across the treaty line. From the trees around the house when the wolves had come to stand guard over Bella last winter. From the far side of a clearing in June, with the newborns coming out of the trees. Her mind had always been the same: a tight, bitter, grinding hum, like a motor running too hot, and under it, going round and round in a groove worn so deep it never came out, a man's name. Sam. Sam. Sam. She'd never stopped thinking it. Not for a minute, not in two years. He'd heard her think it while she fought. He'd heard her think it in her sleep, once, curled up in wolf shape at the edge of the lawn at dawn.

He'd heard her come out of the trees tonight, and the name was gone.

He'd actually turned his head. Like someone hearing a clock stop. He'd stood there on the porch and listened for it, and listened, and it simply wasn't there. The groove was there, worn smooth and empty, like the place on a floor where a heavy piece of furniture has stood for years and been taken away. And the motor had stopped running hot. What was left was quiet, and tired, and wary, and very clear, the clearest he'd ever heard her. Like a pond with the mud settled.

And across the middle of it, the wall.

He'd seen it in Seth and Jacob first, of course. He'd been in their heads all afternoon, both of them, poking at it from inside the pack mind the way you'd poke at a bad tooth, and he'd seen it the way they saw it, as a grey smooth blank, a fog where things should have been. He'd thought, when he first heard about it, that it would be like Bella. Bella's mind, to him, had always been a silence. A perfect, smooth, closed nothing, like a stone at the bottom of a river. He'd spent a year listening to that silence. He knew every inch of it.

This was different.

Bella's silence was hers. It came from inside her. It was shaped like her. This had been put there. He could feel the edges of it, the place where it met Leah's own mind and the two didn't quite match, the way you can feel a patch on a coat. It was cold. That was the first thing. It was very cold, a dense, dead, stony cold he associated with very old places, and it was heavy, heavier than anything he'd ever felt in a living mind, as if somebody had set a slab of granite down in the middle of her and walked away.

And it hummed.

He'd heard something like it once before. Two weeks ago, in his own living room, in the head of a woman with a scar through her eyebrow. That deep note under Layla Quinn's thoughts, the organ playing one low chord in a cathedral; the structure, she'd called it later, when he'd described it to her and she'd gone pale and then laughed. *That's just magic, Edward. That's what it sounds like when you've got it.* The wall in Leah's head had the same kind of note under it. But where Layla's was alive, and moving, and changing all the time like weather, this one didn't move at all. It was one note, very deep, very old, held for a very long time. It sounded like something that had been made once and had never needed to be made again.

It sounded like a promise. A promise somebody had made with their hand on something that would outlast them.

And around the edge of it, cut into it, the way letters are cut around the rim of a coin or into the lintel over a tomb, there were words.

He hadn't been able to read them at first. They'd been dim, like an inscription worn down by rain. He'd stood on the porch and listened to Leah shout at Jacob about baby vampires and bathtubs and beer fridges, and he'd been aware, at the very edge of his hearing, of something said over and over in a voice that wasn't hers. Like a ward. Like a name muttered under the breath. He couldn't make it out.

Then she'd tried to say it out loud. The first time.

And the wall had *moved*. It had tightened. He'd heard the deep note under it rise, very slightly, like a man shifting his grip; and as it did, the words around its edge had come up bright, all at once, the way a seal comes up clear when you press it into hot wax. Three words. The same three words, going round and round the rim of it, the name the promise had been made in.

He'd read it the way he read anything. He'd had no choice about that either.

And then she'd tried again, and he'd watched the wall close its hand on her throat a second time, gentle and certain and absolutely without pity, and he'd heard Seth say *it's hurting you*, and he'd opened his mouth.

"Potter," he'd said. "Harry James Potter."

---

Leah was looking up at him from the bottom of the steps with her face gone white.

"How," she said. "How did you—"

"It's written on it," said Edward. He kept his voice low. "The wall. Around the edge. Like the name on a seal. I can't see behind it. I can't see anything that's in there, any more than they can." He nodded at Jacob, at Seth. "But it's signed. Whatever it is, it was made by someone, and it carries his name. When you tried to speak, it came up very clearly." He paused. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to take it from you. You were hurting yourself."

She stared at him. He heard her think, very fast and very clearly, all at once: *signed, it's signed, he swore it on a rock in a room with no windows, of course it's got his name on it, of course it does, you'd sign your name to a thing like that, they'd make you*—

—and then the thought ran into the wall and stopped dead, the way a car stops when it hits a tree. The deep note shivered. He heard it shiver. And she jerked, very slightly, at the bottom of the steps, as if someone had touched her.

He didn't know what she'd been about to think. He knew it had been close to something.

He put it away. Carefully. In the place where he kept things he'd come back to later.

"Okay," said Leah. Her voice was flat. "Okay. Well. That's his name, I guess."

"What's *going on*?" said Layla Quinn, from inside the house.

---

They went inside, because Esme came out onto the porch and said, very gently, that it was pouring and the children were standing in it and she had a pot of soup on, and nobody had ever in Edward's memory managed to argue with Esme about soup.

Layla was sitting up on the white couch in the long room, very straight, with one hand braced on the cushion and her face pale and furious. She'd sat up too fast. He'd heard the bruises on her back register it, a hot white bloom of pain right across her shoulder blades, and heard her decide not to care. Carlisle was already beside her, saying something about lying back down, and she wasn't listening to him. Her mug of tea was lying on its side on the floor. Esme picked it up without a word and took it away.

Seth went straight to her. He always did. He folded himself down onto the floor at the end of the couch and put his back against it and his head near her knee, and she put her hand on his hair without looking at him.

Leah came in last. She stood just inside the front door, dripping on Esme's floor, with her arms folded and her chin up, the way she stood in rooms full of vampires, as if she'd been invited somewhere she didn't want to be and intended everyone to know it. But it was a thinner version of the old stance than he was used to. It was the posture, held up out of habit, with less underneath it.

Layla was staring at Edward.

"Say that again," she said.

"Harry James Potter," said Edward.

He watched it go into her.

He'd been expecting a reaction. He'd heard how fast Leah's thoughts had gone when he'd said it on the porch; he'd heard Jacob's go blank and Seth's go blank, because the name meant nothing to either of them. He'd assumed, without thinking much about it, that it would mean something to the witch.

He hadn't expected it to mean this much.

Layla's mind didn't so much react to the name as fall over. It went in all directions at once. A rush of pictures, flicking past so fast he only caught them at the edges. A small square card with a picture on it of a thin dark-haired boy in round glasses, and the picture *moved*, the boy turned his head and looked out of the card and then walked out of the frame. A newspaper, a fat black headline, *THE NEW YORK GHOST*, and under it in letters three inches high: *YOU-KNOW-WHO DEAD. BRITISH BOY ENDS WAR.* A girls' dormitory at night, a stone room in mountains, a dozen girls in pyjamas crowded round one bed reading the paper by wandlight. A classroom. A tall, straight, white-haired woman at a blackboard, drawing chalk lines across it, two lines meeting in the middle. *Disarming Charm. Killing Curse. Point of contact. Here.* Tapping the chalk on the board, hard. *The luckiest duel in recorded history, ladies and gentlemen, and don't any of you ever try it.*

And then, cutting across all of it, the ridge. Today. Her own wand going end over end up into the rain. And a hand reaching up out of nowhere to take it out of the air.

"Oh my God," Layla said.

She put both hands over her face.

"Layla?" said Seth. He was on his knees. "Layla, what—"

She started to laugh.

It came out of her behind her hands, high and cracked, and it hurt her; Edward heard every breath of it go across the bruises on her back. She bent forward over her knees and laughed until she couldn't breathe, and then kept going, and Seth knelt there with his hands out like he wanted to hold her and didn't know where it was safe to touch.

"I threw a Blasting Curse," Layla said, from behind her hands, "at *Harry Potter*."

She laughed harder.

"I threw a *Disjunction* at Harry Potter. I threw *roots* at him. I tried to take his *legs out*." She took her hands away from her face. Her eyes were streaming. "He picked my Disjunction up off his fingers and said *oh, that's clever*. Harry Potter said my spell was *clever*. And then he gave it back to me and it took my shield off and I got thrown into a *log*."

"Layla." Carlisle had his hand on her shoulder. "Breathe. Slowly."

"I'm breathing. I'm breathing." She wiped her face on the back of her wrist. Her hands were shaking. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. It's—you don't understand. None of you understand. You don't know who that is."

"No," said Carlisle gently. "We don't. Tell us."

She looked at him. She looked round the room, at all of them, at Emmett leaning on the piano with his eyebrows up, at Rosalie on the stairs, at Jasper by the kitchen door with Alice beside him, at Bella with Nessie on her hip. At Leah by the door. She looked at Leah longest.

"Okay," she said. "Okay."

She told them.

She told it badly at first, too fast, tripping over the beginning of it, and then she slowed down and told it properly, the way Edward suspected she'd once told it to younger witches at a summer camp where she'd taught duelling. He listened to her say it and listened to her think it, and the two ran side by side, almost word for word, which was how he knew she believed every syllable.

There'd been a wizard in Britain. A dark one. The worst any of them had seen in a hundred years. He'd called himself something that people over there wouldn't say out loud, even now, even years after he was dead; they called him *You-Know-Who*, or *He-Who-Must-Not-Be-Named*, and Layla said both names with an odd flat twist to her mouth, like a woman repeating something she'd been taught as a child and grown out of. His real name, the one he'd been born with, had been Tom Riddle. He'd fought a war in the seventies. He'd killed a lot of people, witches and wizards and Muggles. Whole families.

"Muggles?" said Emmett.

"Non-magic people," said Layla. "Us. You. Well. Not you." She waved her hand. "People like Charlie."

He'd gone to a house one night in 1981, Halloween, to kill a baby. He'd killed the baby's father at the door and the baby's mother in the nursery, and then he'd turned his wand on the baby, a fifteen-month-old in a cot, and used the Killing Curse.

"Which nobody survives," said Layla. "Nobody. There's no counter. There's no block. You can't shield it. It just kills you. That's what it's for. In the whole history of magic, nobody had ever survived it."

The curse had rebounded. Nobody knew how. The dark wizard had been destroyed, or nearly, and the baby had lived, with a scar on his forehead shaped like a lightning bolt, and the war had ended overnight. And the whole wizarding world, everywhere, had known his name by the next morning.

"The Boy Who Lived," said Layla. "That's what they called him. Like a title. Every kid in America knew it. There were books. There was a—my cousin had a *lunchbox*."

"A lunchbox," said Jacob.

"With his face on it. A cartoon. A little baby with a lightning bolt." She laughed again, shakily. "It was so stupid. It was such a stupid lunchbox."

"And then?" said Carlisle.

And then he'd grown up. And the dark wizard had come back, when the boy was fourteen, the way some of them do, the way she'd seen things come back out of the ground in Mexico that should have stayed down, and there'd been another war. Three years of it. The British papers had stopped printing anything but the dead. And in May of 1998, at the school in Scotland where the boy had grown up, there'd been a battle. Hundreds of people. Students. Teachers. Families. And at the end of it, in the great hall of the school, in front of everybody who was still standing, the boy and the dark wizard had fought. Alone. In a circle, with everybody watching.

"He was seventeen," said Layla. "Seventeen. The other one was seventy-one years old and he'd been the most dangerous wizard alive for half a century. And they each threw one spell." She held up her hands, fists, and brought them together in the air in front of her, slowly, until the knuckles touched. "Riddle threw the Killing Curse. Harry Potter threw a Disarming Charm. *Expelliarmus.* It's a first-year spell. It's the first combat spell they teach you. It knocks the wand out of the other person's hand. That's all it does." She opened her fists. "They met in the middle, and the Killing Curse went backward, and Tom Riddle died on the floor."

Nobody in the room said anything.

"That's who that is," said Layla. "That's Harry Potter."

Edward heard the image of the tall white-haired woman come up in her again, stronger, sharp as a photograph. *The luckiest duel in recorded history.* Chalk tapping on the board. *There's a list, ladies and gentlemen, there are three names on it, and Tom Riddle was the last of them, and he died on the floor of a school to a first-year spell thrown by a seventeen-year-old boy. Draw your own conclusions about luck.*

And then the ridge, again. Her wand in somebody else's hand, turning, as if he'd picked a pencil up off her desk.

"He took my wand," said Layla quietly. "Today. Did you see?" She was looking at Seth. "He caught my wand. Right out of the air. I didn't even see him do it. That's twice in his life he's taken a wand off somebody in a duel, that I know of. And one of those times it was the—" She stopped. She swallowed.

"The guy on the list," said Seth softly. He'd heard about the list. Edward heard him remember her telling him, on the walk down off the ridge, in a voice he hadn't liked.

"Calloway always said it was luck," said Layla. "My teacher. She made us study that duel. Every year. She said it was the single luckiest moment in the history of the craft and that anybody who thought it was anything else was a fool." She was quiet for a second. "I'd like to see her stand on that ridge."

"So he's—" Emmett had been holding it in. Edward could hear exactly how hard. "So he's *famous*. Like, wizard-famous."

"Emmett," said Rosalie.

"No, I'm asking. Like, how famous? Is he, like—" Emmett waved one big hand. "Wizard Elvis?"

Layla looked at him.

"If Elvis," she said, "had killed the man an entire country was too frightened to name. When he was seventeen. In front of everybody. And then walked off the stage and never came back."

"Never came back?" said Carlisle.

"He disappeared." Layla shrugged, and winced. "After the war. A year or two of pictures in the papers, him at memorials, at the trials. He was going to be an Auror, everybody said so. That's their version of—law enforcement. Dark wizard catchers. Everybody said he'd be the youngest Head of Aurors in history. Then he just—stopped. Stopped being in the papers. Stopped being anywhere. Nobody knew where he went." She shook her head slowly. "There were rumours. People said Africa. People said he was dead, or hiding, or sick. My roommate at Ilvermorny swore up and down he was running a pub in Cornwall under a fake name."

"Is he?" said Jacob.

Layla turned her head and looked at Leah.

So did everybody else.

Edward listened to Leah. Her mind had gone very still, standing by the door, dripping. She was thinking about a slate hallway and a cast-iron range. About a man at the range in his shirtsleeves with a gun under his arm, cracking eggs. She was thinking that he'd looked like somebody who'd been on his own for a long time, and that she didn't care about any of it, the lunchbox or the list or the duel in the great hall; that the only part she cared about was *a fifteen-month-old in a cot*, and the mother killed in the nursery, and the father at the door. She was thinking about a cupboard under some stairs, somewhere called Surrey, which he'd told her about in front of his tent when she'd asked where he was from. She was thinking, very quietly, *he didn't tell me any of the rest of it. He didn't tell me about the war. He told me about the cupboard.*

And every time her thoughts went near anything that had happened after that, after the kitchen door, the wall hummed.

"He doesn't run a pub," said Leah.

"What's he doing?" said Jacob.

She looked at him. She didn't even try to answer. Edward heard her not try, heard her look at the question and the wall standing behind it and decide it wasn't worth her throat.

"Ask him," she said.

---

"I've seen him," said Alice.

She'd been quiet all evening. Edward had heard her being quiet; it was one of the loudest things in the house. She was standing by the kitchen door beside Jasper, with her arms wrapped around herself, and her mind was a fast bright unhappy flicker, the future coming and going in front of her in pieces, none of them holding still long enough to look at.

"Since September," said Alice. "I didn't know it was him. I didn't know it was anybody. There's been a man. An Englishman. I'd see him for a second at a time, out of the corner of everything. Getting out of a brown car in front of the diner. Standing in the rain on the Hoh road with a camera. Ordering pie." She hugged herself tighter. "And every single time, right before I saw what he was going to do next, he'd take a coin out of his pocket. A penny. A big old copper one. And flip it. And the second it went up in the air—" she opened her hand, "—nothing. Blank. Like a television with the plug pulled. And then I'd see him again a day later somewhere else, and he'd flip it again, and it'd go blank again."

"You never mentioned it," said Jasper. He sounded thoughtful.

"I thought he was just indecisive." Alice laughed, not happily. "I see a lot of people who can't make up their minds, Jazz. There was a woman in Port Angeles last year who changed her mind about a sofa forty times in one afternoon. I thought he was that. A tourist who couldn't decide where to eat."

"He decides by coin," said Jasper slowly, "at the last possible second. So the decision's never his. It's the coin's." He was looking at nothing, at the middle distance, the way he looked when he was seeing a battlefield laid out in front of him like a map. "So there's nothing for you to see. There's no decision to make. Just a penny in the air."

"That's what it looks like," said Alice.

"That's tradecraft," said Jasper. "That's what that is. That's somebody who knew there was a seer in this town and built a habit around it. Every day. For two months." He turned his head and looked at Edward, and Edward heard the rest of the thought before Jasper spoke it, cold and neat and very precise, a soldier's thought: *He knew what Alice could do before he got here.*

"How?" said Rosalie, from the stairs. She'd heard it too, in the silence, in the shape of it. "How would he know that? Who would've told him about Alice?"

Nobody answered.

Edward stood very still by the piano.

He was thinking it. He didn't want to think it and he was thinking it anyway, the way he always did, in perfect order, one step after another. There were very few people in the world who knew what his sister could do. The family. The wolves, now. Bella. A handful of nomads, over the years, who'd come through and seen and gone on their way.

And three men in a tower in Italy, who'd stood in a room with red marble floors last spring and watched Alice walk in beside him, and one of whom had held his hand and seen everything in his head, everything, every thought he'd ever had about her, about all of them. One of whom had looked at Alice afterward the way a collector looks at a painting he intends to own.

He looked at Leah.

She was looking at the floor. Her face was perfectly blank. And under it, behind it, at the wall in the middle of her mind, the deep note had come up. It was humming. He could hear it straining. It sounded like something leaning very hard against a door.

Nobody had said *Italy*. Nobody had said anything at all. Rosalie had asked who would have told him about Alice, and somewhere behind the wall, without meaning to, Leah had flinched toward an answer.

He didn't push. He didn't need to. He took what he'd heard, the hum, the strain, the sound of the door, and put it in the same place as the other thing, the thing she'd been about to think on the porch steps. A room with no windows. A rock. *They'd make you.*

He'd look at it all later. Alone. With Carlisle.

"Does it matter?" said Bella.

Everyone looked at her. She was standing in the middle of the room with Nessie on her hip, and Nessie had both arms round her neck and was looking at Leah with an expression of grave, intense interest. Bella's gold eyes were steady.

"Does it matter how he knows?" said Bella. "He knows. He's known for two months. He's been in town, eating pie at the diner, two miles from my dad, two miles from *her*." She didn't need to say who. Her arm tightened round Nessie. "And he never came near us. Not once. Alice saw him on the Hoh road and at the diner and never once on our drive. He could have, any time. He knew where we were. He knew what we are. He could've come up here the first week." She looked at Leah. "He didn't."

"That we know of," said Rosalie.

"That Alice can see," said Bella. "Which is everything that matters."

"With a coin," said Rosalie. "With a *penny*, Bella. He's been making Alice blind with a penny for two months. So she couldn't see him if he'd been standing in our kitchen." Rosalie came down two steps. Her face was beautiful and hard as a blade. Her mind was running very fast, and almost all of it, Edward heard, was Nessie. "So let me see if I've got this right. The most famous wizard in the world. A man who, according to Layla, killed the worst dark wizard in a century with a first-year spell when he was seventeen. A man who walked through Layla like she was a child, and Layla is very good—I've watched her—and held down two wolves at the same time, and caught a *vampire* in some kind of magic snare out of thin air, and carried it off under his arm like a—a *surfboard*." She came down another step. "He has a vampire in a cage in his back room. He has a deal with Layla he can call in whenever he wants. He knows what Alice can do, and he's been hiding from her for two months. He knows where we live. He's been in Seth's head and Leah's head and he's seen *everything*. Our house. Our names. *Charlie.* Her." She didn't look at Nessie. She didn't have to. "And we don't know where he lives, or who he works for, or what he wants, or what he's going to do with any of it."

She stopped on the bottom step.

"And our solution to all of that," said Rosalie, "is apparently that Leah *likes* him."

Edward heard the room go cold around Leah. He heard Leah's own mind come up off the floor and narrow down to a point, very bright, very sharp, like a blade drawn.

He heard her think: *Oh, you have* got *to be joking.*

The voice she thought it in wasn't quite hers. The accent was wrong. It had come from somewhere else and lodged in her head and she'd used it without noticing. The shape of the words was English.

He found, a little to his own surprise, that he was looking forward to what came next.

---

### IV. Leah

*The Cullen house — 7:26 p.m.*

"*Likes* him," said Leah.

She came away from the door. She hadn't decided to; her feet just did it. She walked into the middle of the long white room, barefoot on the pale floor, leaving wet prints, and stood there with a vampire on the stairs in front of her and a vampire at the piano and a vampire by the kitchen door and two more on the couch and one with a baby on her hip, in a room that stank of bleach and burnt sugar and flowers left too long in a vase, and she didn't care about any of it.

"Likes him," she said again. "That's what you think this is."

Rosalie looked at her down that perfect nose. "What would you call it?"

"I'd call it you weren't listening," said Leah. "Any of you. You've been sitting here all afternoon going through Seth's head and Jake's head, and listening to Layla, and talking about lunchboxes, and penny tricks, and how famous he is, and how dangerous he is, like he's a—a *problem*. Like he's a thing on a map you can draw arrows at." She turned on the spot, looking at all of them. "You think I've got a crush. You think it's like Seth mooning over Layla at the diner and buying her pie."

"Hey," said Seth, from the floor.

"You *bought her pie*, Seth. Twice."

"It's the *law*."

"Shut up." She turned back to the room. "You want to know what it is? I'll tell you. Right now, right this second, while I'm standing here dripping on your floor, I know where he is."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't mean I know where his tent is," said Leah. "I don't. I couldn't draw you a map. If you put a gun to my head I couldn't tell you which river. I mean I know where *he* is. Right now. He's outside. Somewhere up high, in the rain. He's been out there a while. He's working at something with his hands. He's crouched down. His knees hurt a bit from the cold, the left one more than the right." She heard how it sounded. She kept going anyway. "I can feel his heart. It's slow. It's slower than mine. It's been slow since he got wherever he is. It goes faster when he thinks about me. He's thought about me about forty times in the last twenty minutes, and every single time it went faster, and every single time he told himself to knock it off and get on with it, and every single time he didn't listen."

Somebody on the couch made a small sound. Layla. Leah didn't look round.

"And on the lawn," said Leah. "Just now. When I tried to say his name, and the wall did that." She touched her own throat. She took her hand away. "He felt it. Twenty miles away. Out in the rain. He felt it close on me like it was his own hand doing it, and he *hated* it. And he said *don't*. In my head. One word. Quiet. Just—*don't*." She swallowed. "Same thing happened on the way here. In the woods. I tried to tell Jake something and it shut me up, and he felt it, and he said *don't*, and he stayed. He stood at the other end of me for about a minute, like a guy standing at the bottom of the stairs listening for a crash. Just to check. Then he went back to work."

She turned and looked at Jacob. He was by the fireplace, still in his wet cut-offs, with his arms folded.

"You saw it go both ways," she said. "On the ridge. You saw it. Every single one of ours goes one way, Jake. You hold on. She gets held. That's how it's always been, every story Billy's ever told. That's how it was for Sam. That's how it is for you. You'd die for her," a jerk of her chin at Nessie, "and she's ten weeks old and she doesn't even know what that means yet."

Jacob didn't say anything.

"Mine went into him," said Leah, "and his wolf picked up the other end and tied it off. Round mine. He *grabbed it back*. Both ends. Both ways. I'm held and I'm holding. So is he." She spread her hands. "So, see? Better. Mine's better. It's *stronger*. It's stronger than yours, and it's stronger than Sam's, and it's stronger than anything in any story anybody's ever told round a fire at First Beach. And it's *one day old*."

The room was very quiet.

"He can't lie to me," said Leah. "I told you that. He tried. Twice. His mouth wouldn't do it. And you've got to understand what that means about him, about what he—" She felt it coming. The cold hand, rising. She stepped round it, the way you step round a hole in the dark. "His whole *life* is not telling people things. That's all I can say about it. That's all I'm allowed. His whole life. And he stood in front of his tent and opened his mouth and told me everything. Things he's never told anybody."

She let that sit.

"He let me through his wards. You don't know what that means either. Layla does." She looked at Layla for the first time, and Layla, sitting very straight on the couch with Seth's head against her knee, looked back. "Tell them."

"His wards," said Layla slowly, "were good enough that Seth saw his tent through your eyes, clear as day, and twenty minutes later couldn't remember what valley it was in. I've been trying for six hours. I *still* can't." She hesitated. "If he let you through those, Leah, you didn't walk through a gate. You walked through a wall."

"He didn't even mean to," said Leah. "He told me. I was right at the edge, and it was in my head telling me to turn round, go home, there's nothing up here, and I was going to. I'd forgotten what I was doing. And then he pulled. On this." She put her hand flat on the middle of her chest. "His hand just went out and grabbed it, before he'd decided anything, like you grab a glass going off a table. And all of it, everything he'd built, just opened up for me like I lived there."

She dropped her hand.

"So when you ask what he's going to *do* with what he knows," said Leah, to Rosalie, to all of them, "here's what he's going to do. Whatever keeps me alive. Whatever keeps me *happy*. Whatever I want. Same as Jake would do for her. Same as Seth would do for Layla. Same as Sam does for Emily, every single day, even when it makes him miserable." Her voice was rising. She let it. "And if anybody in this room ever gave him a reason to think they were a danger to me—"

She stopped.

She hadn't meant to stop there. She'd meant to finish it. But she'd felt, as she said it, something go through the line, faint, from the far end. A *pressure*, with no words in it. As if, out in the rain on a hillside somewhere, a man crouched in the wet had suddenly gone still and lifted his head, the way a dog lifts its head when it hears its name said in a hard voice in another room. And under him, under his breastbone, behind three locks, something enormous had opened its eyes.

She felt it look up the line. Toward her. Toward the room she was standing in.

The hair stood up all down her arms.

"*No*," she said out loud, to nobody. Hard. Into the line. "No. I'm fine. Go back to work."

The pressure held for a second. Two. And then, slowly, she felt it ease off. Felt the man at the far end let his breath out and go back to whatever he'd been doing with his hands. Felt the thing under him lower its head, not all the way. It kept its eyes open. It kept them pointed this way.

She looked up.

Every vampire in the room was staring at her. Edward was staring hardest.

"He felt that," said Edward quietly. "Didn't he. Just now. When you said *danger*."

"Yeah," said Leah.

"From wherever he is."

"Yeah."

"And the thing in him," said Edward. "The wolf. It—" He stopped. He was looking at her the way he'd looked at her on the porch, the way you'd look at a page in a language you'd thought you could read. "It *woke up*."

"Yeah," said Leah. "So maybe don't talk about him like he's a problem on a map. Okay? Because I don't think he likes it. And I *know* it doesn't."

---

It should have been enough. She'd have thought it would be enough.

It wasn't, because they were the Cullens, and they'd been surviving for a hundred years by sitting in rooms like this one and thinking things through, and you couldn't make that stop just by yelling at it. She watched it happen. She watched them all go quiet and look at each other and *think*, the way they did, fast, faster than people, and she could almost see the gears.

Carlisle got there first. He'd been standing by the couch with his hand on Layla's shoulder the whole time, and he took it away now and folded his hands in front of him the way he did when he was about to be interesting about something.

"Reciprocal," he said softly. "A reciprocal imprint. Leah, do you understand how remarkable that is? In every account the Quileute have, in every story Billy's ever told me, it's one-directional. A compulsion in the wolf, centred on the wolf, an extraordinary biological and spiritual event, but *one* event. In one person. What you're describing is two separate—" he paused, searching for the word, "—*organisms*, two separate kinds of being, each with something in them that recognised the other at the same moment, and bonded. If his nature is in any way analogous to yours, if the wolf in him is in some sense—"

"Carlisle," said Esme.

"I'm sorry." He smiled, a little ruefully. "I'm sorry, Leah. It's simply that in three hundred and sixty years I've never—"

"It's not a science project," said Leah.

"No. Of course not."

"I'm not a *science project*. I already met one guy today who wanted to put things in jars."

Carlisle's face did something complicated.

Then Jasper.

He came away from the kitchen door. He did it slowly, the way he did everything around the wolves, careful, every movement where you could see it. He had the face he got sometimes, the far-away one, the one that made Leah think about men in old photographs standing beside cannons. The scars on his arms and neck caught the light. There were a lot of them. Leah had never asked.

"She's right that he's dangerous," said Jasper. "And she's right that he hasn't come near us. Both of those things are true." His voice was soft and Southern and completely reasonable. "But the fact is, he knows where we are, and we don't know where he is. That's the whole of it, as far as I'm concerned. Everything else is detail." He looked at Leah. "You can find him."

"I can find him."

"Then that's our advantage. The only one we've got." He was looking past her now, at the long glass wall, at the dark trees, at something only he could see. "We go to him tomorrow, early. He'll be expecting something tonight. Leah leads. The wolves come. Emmett and I come. Edward, so we know what he's thinking before he thinks it." He turned his head. "If Edward *can* hear him. Can you?"

"I don't know," said Edward. "Leah's memory of him is—he seemed very quiet. Very closed. The way Layla describes it, there's an art to closing your mind. He'll have it." He paused. "I'd hear something. I always hear something."

"Then we'll find out," said Jasper. "We go, and we show him how many of us there are, and we sit down with him on his own ground, and we find out what he wants. And we make it very clear what happens if what he wants is any of ours."

"I'm in," said Emmett at once. He pushed off the piano. "Oh, man. I'm *so* in."

"Emmett," said Rosalie. But she'd come down off the bottom step. She was standing behind him with her hand on his arm, and Leah could see her face, and it was the face of somebody who'd been waiting all afternoon for somebody to say *we go*.

"He'll beat you," said Layla.

Everybody looked at her.

She'd stayed sitting up. Leah could tell from her face that every second of it hurt. She had one hand on Seth's hair and the other in a fist on her knee, and she was looking at Jasper with a very odd expression, the one Leah had seen on her at Sue's kitchen table that first day, *Seth says you hate everybody*. Sizing him up.

"I'm sorry," said Layla. "I know you've all—I've seen you move. I've seen how strong you are. But you need to hear this from somebody who's been on the other side of him." She took a breath. "He was holding a vampire in the air behind him, and two wolves flat on the ground, both at once, the whole time, and I was throwing the best things I had at him as fast as I could throw them. And he wasn't trying. I mean that. I've been doing this a long time, I know what trying looks like on somebody. He was *bored*. He was holding three of you down and taking me apart and he was bored." She looked at Emmett. "He'd hold you down too. He'd hold you all down. You'd be lying on his floor in a row like logs, and he'd still have a hand free to make a cup of tea."

"Everyone sleeps," said Jasper quietly.

Leah went cold all over.

She didn't think about it. She didn't need to. It came up through her like the change did, up her spine, all at once. She was across the room before she knew she'd moved, standing in front of Jasper Hale with her face about a foot from his, her lips back off her teeth, a growl coming up out of her chest that had no business coming out of a human throat.

"*Say that again*," said Leah.

Jasper didn't move. He'd been in a lot of fights. She could see it in the way he didn't move.

"I meant it as a fact," he said evenly.

"*You thought about it.*"

"I think about everything. That's my job in this family." He held her eyes. "Saying it in front of you was careless. I apologise for that."

"If you go anywhere near him while he's asleep—"

"Leah." Edward. Right beside her, though she hadn't heard him move. His voice was low. "Leah, look. Look down the line. Is he—"

She looked.

He was. Twenty miles away, on his hillside in the rain, the man at the far end had stopped dead again. And this time he hadn't just lifted his head. He was standing up. She could feel him standing, all the way up, very straight, in the dark. She could feel his heart. It wasn't slow anymore.

And under his breastbone, the thing behind the locks was on its feet. She felt it hit the first lock. She felt the lock groan.

"*No*," said Leah, out loud, through her teeth. "*No. Stop it. Both of you.* It's fine. It's *fine*. It was just words. Sit *down*."

She felt the man at the far end hear her. She felt him stand there in the rain for a long moment with his heart going hard, and then she felt him put his hand flat on his own chest, the way he'd done on the ridge, and lean. On the locks. On the thing behind them. With everything he had. She felt it go down, slowly, growling. She felt it not want to.

She felt him crouch back down in the wet. His heart didn't slow. It stayed fast for a long time.

She stepped back from Jasper.

"Okay," she said. Her voice was shaking. She hated that. "Okay. You see? You see what you just did? I didn't even tell him. I didn't *think* at him. I just got scared, for him, for one second, and he felt it from twenty miles away and he got up off the ground to come and see what it was. And so did the thing in him. It was coming, Jasper. It was hitting the doors. If I hadn't—" She stopped. She swallowed. "That's what this is. That's how serious this is. You say one wrong thing in front of me, and twenty miles away it gets up."

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"I didn't feel that," said Jacob. Very quietly. From the fireplace. "When Nessie gets scared. I don't feel it from twenty miles."

"No," said Leah. "You don't."

"I have to be there. I have to see her face."

"Yeah," said Leah. She turned round and looked at him. "That's what I'm telling you, Jake."

---

Jacob unfolded his arms.

She watched him do it. She watched him come away from the fireplace and walk across the room toward her, slow, and stop about three feet away. He looked tired. He looked a lot older than eighteen. He looked, she thought, like Billy did sometimes at council meetings, when he'd been arguing with Old Quil for two hours and was about to make a decision nobody was going to like.

"Okay," Jacob said. "Okay. I hear you."

"Do you?"

"I hear you, Leah. I get it. It's big. It's bigger than ours. It goes both ways. He'd come for you." He held up his hands. "I get it. But Jasper's right about one thing. He knows where we are. He knows where *she* is." He didn't look round at Nessie. "And we don't know anything about him except what he's let you see. And what he did today, which was put all three of you on the ground. So until we know what he is, and what he wants with us, and who he works for—" he took a breath, "—I don't want you going back up there. Not alone. Not till we've all gone up together and had a look at him."

She looked at him.

"I'm asking," Jacob said quickly. "That's all."

"You'd better be."

"I am. I'm asking."

"You thought about it."

He opened his mouth. He shut it again.

"You thought about it, Jake. I saw your face." She took a step toward him. "You thought about using the voice on me. Right then. *Leah, you're not going back there.* You thought about how it'd sound."

The voice. The Alpha voice. The thing Sam had done to her a hundred times when she'd been in his pack, that had taken her legs out from under her and turned her round and made her do things she didn't want to do in a way that had felt, every single time, like somebody had reached into her chest and grabbed her by the bones. The thing Jacob had sworn, the night he'd broken away from Sam, the night she and Seth had followed him, that he'd never use on either of them. Not ever. Not unless it was life and death.

He'd kept that promise for ten weeks.

"Yeah," said Jacob. Finally. "Yeah. I thought about it."

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to."

"I know you weren't." She looked at him for a long moment. "But I want you to think about something else first. Before you even think about it again. Okay?"

He waited.

"You know how this works, right?" said Leah.

She said it quietly. She said it to Jacob. But she turned her head as she said it and looked round the room, at all of them, at Jasper, at Rosalie, at Emmett with his hands still in fists, at Edward, at Carlisle and Esme, at Bella with the baby on her hip.

"What was that one law," said Leah, "that stopped Sam from doing what he was planning?"

---

Nobody said anything.

She watched it land. She watched it land on Jacob first, because he was closest, and because he'd been there. She watched his face change. She watched him go back, the way she'd gone back on the lawn, to a night ten weeks ago. Sam's pack in the trees around this house in the dark. Eight wolves. Sam out front, black as the night, with his ears flat and his teeth out, there to come through that glass wall behind her and finish what Jacob had come over here to do and then hadn't. There to kill a thing that had been born that day covered in its mother's blood. There to kill her.

And Jacob, in front of the house, in front of the baby. Saying, without words, in the old way, the way they'd all felt it: *you'll have to go through me.*

And Sam stopping. Every one of them stopping. Because they'd felt it come off Jacob like heat. Because they'd known, every wolf in that line, what it would mean. And Sam had stood there in the dark with his teeth out for a long time, and then he'd turned round and taken them all home.

"No wolf," said Jacob slowly, "can hurt another wolf's imprint."

"It's the most absolute law we've got," said Leah. "Isn't it? Older than the treaty. Older than anybody's grandfather. Nobody even remembers making it. It's just *there*. Because the pain would be too much." She held his eyes. "That's how Billy says it. The pain of a thing like that would be intolerable. For the whole pack. You'd kill one of them and you'd feel it come down the line into the wolf that loved them, and you'd feel *him* feel it, and it'd go through every one of you, and it'd tear the pack in half."

Jacob didn't say anything.

"Sam had eight wolves in these trees," said Leah. "He had a *reason*. He was going to come through that window and kill a baby in her crib, and he was *right*, for all he knew, and he had every wolf on the reservation behind him." She jerked her chin at Nessie. "And he couldn't. He couldn't even touch her. Because you looked at her. Because one wolf looked at her, one time, and after that she was off limits to every single wolf there's ever been."

She turned, slowly, so she was looking at all of them.

"So," said Leah. "Jake. You're my Alpha. I'm in your pack. You've got two wolves. Me and Seth." She tilted her head. "What exactly are you going to do about the guy I imprinted on?"

Jacob opened his mouth.

He closed it.

She watched him think it through. She watched him get there, step by step, the way she'd got there on the bench outside the tent, in the rain, with Harry's hand on the side of her face. Her pack couldn't touch him. Not Jacob, not Seth, not her. Not with teeth, not with a show of force, not with a ring of wolves round his tent at dawn showing him how many of them there were. Because if it came to it, if it ever came to it, if he so much as lifted a hand in their direction and somebody went for his throat, it'd come straight back down the line into her. And through her into them. And it would tear them apart.

And Sam's pack. Sam's pack was bound by the same law. Every wolf on the reservation was. Even Sam. *Especially* Sam. He'd have to stand there and take it, the way she'd stood on the edge of things for two years and taken it. Even if he hated it. Even if he thought it was the end of the world.

She felt the corner of her mouth go up.

"Oh," said Jacob. "Oh, *come on*."

"Yeah," said Leah.

"Leah, the law's for, like—little girls. Emily. *Claire*. People who can't—who need—"

"Protecting?" said Leah. "The law doesn't care who it's for, Jake. It covers whoever we look at. Whoever. Nobody ever thought one of us would look at somebody who could put three wolves on their backs without taking his hands out of his pockets, that's all. Nobody ever thought one of us would be a *girl*."

"That's your law," said Rosalie.

Leah turned round.

Rosalie was standing at the foot of the stairs with her hand still on Emmett's arm. Her face was very pale and very cold and very beautiful, and Leah could see in it exactly what she was thinking, because it was the same thing Leah would have been thinking in her place. *Nessie. Nessie. Nessie.*

"It's your law," said Rosalie. "The wolves'. It isn't ours. We never signed anything about imprints."

"Nope," said Leah. "You didn't."

"So we could go up there without you."

"Sure," said Leah. "Go ahead. Go up there without any wolves at all. Find the valley Layla can't remember after six hours of trying. Walk through wards that hide a whole forest from Alice. Walk up to a guy who held two of us flat without looking and caught a vampire in a snare made of *light*." She tipped her head. "And when you come back, *if* you come back, you can come back to a treaty line with a wolf standing on it who knows exactly where you've been. Because she felt every single second of it. From the other end."

Rosalie looked at her.

"She means it," said Edward. Quietly. "Rose. She means every word."

"I know she does," said Rosalie. She didn't look away from Leah. Something went across her face, very fast, and Leah didn't know what it was, but it looked almost like respect. "I'd mean it too."

She let go of Emmett's arm.

"Nobody," said Carlisle, "is going anywhere with teeth."

He said it mildly. He said everything mildly. But it was the voice of somebody who'd lived three hundred and sixty years and run a family of vampires through two world wars and a dozen towns without one of them ever once eating anybody they weren't supposed to, and the room went quiet for it the way rooms always did.

"Leah's right," he said. "And Jasper's right too, and so is Rosalie. We don't know what he wants. But we know a few things." He held up one long pale hand and counted on it, a little the way Leah had, and she wondered if he was teasing her. She decided he probably was. "We know he's been here two months and never come near us. We know he had the chance today to kill three people he had no reason to love and didn't. We know he gave Layla her wand back." He looked at Layla, who nodded slowly. "We know he told her she'd be all right, and how to treat her back, and that he was correct on both counts. And we know that the moment Leah said the word *danger* in this room, a man twenty miles away stood up in the rain to come and help her."

He lowered his hand.

"Those are the actions of someone who might yet turn out to be a friend," said Carlisle. "We'll find out which. So we'll do this the way we'd do it with anyone else who'd come to live in our town and might be one or the other." He turned to Leah. "Would you ask him if he'd come to the house? As our guest. To talk."

Leah looked at him.

"Just to talk," said Carlisle. "He's welcome under our roof. Under the terms any guest is welcome. And if he says no, then he says no."

"Ask him to dinner," said Esme.

Everybody looked at Esme.

"Well, I'm sorry," said Esme, a little pink, which shouldn't have been possible, "but it's the *polite* thing. If a young man's going to come and sit in our house and discuss whether or not we're all going to kill each other, the very least we can do is feed him. Leah says he eats a great deal."

"Six eggs," said Seth helpfully.

"There," said Esme. "I'll make a roast."

There was a pause.

"He'll come," said Layla, from the couch. She sounded very tired. "If you ask him. He'll come. He knows where you live anyway." She tipped her head back against the cushions and shut her eyes. "And for God's sake, don't let Emmett ask him to turn him into a bear."

"I wasn't going to," said Emmett, in a voice that made it very clear he'd been going to.

Leah stood in the middle of the room and felt it go out of her. The thing that had come up her spine at Jasper. The thing that had been holding her straight since the lawn. She felt it drain out through her feet into the Cullens' floor, all of it, all at once, and left her standing there tired to the bone in her wet clothes with her hands hanging at her sides.

"Yeah," she said. "Okay. I'll ask him."

"Thank you," said Carlisle.

Something touched her hand.

She looked down. Nessie was beside her. Leah hadn't heard her come; Bella must have put her down. She was standing on the pale floor in a little blue dress and bare feet, the size of a kid of three, with her bronze curls down her back and her head tipped all the way back to look up at Leah, and one small hot hand was resting on the back of Leah's fingers.

Leah went very still.

She'd never let the kid touch her. Never once. In ten weeks. She'd stood at the edge of the lawn as a wolf, and she'd stood in this room as a girl, and every time the kid had looked at her with those big brown eyes and reached out, Leah had stepped back. She knew what happened when the kid touched you. She'd seen it go through Jacob's head a hundred times. Pictures. Straight into you, through your skin. She hadn't wanted anything going straight into her. She'd had enough of that.

"Can I?" said Nessie. Her voice was clear and high and very serious.

Leah looked over the kid's head at Bella.

Bella's face was very calm. She didn't say anything. She just looked back at Leah, and Leah understood, in a way she didn't have words for, that this was the kid asking, and Bella was going to let the kid ask, and whatever Leah said, Bella would be fine with it.

Leah crouched down. Slowly. Until her face was level with Nessie's.

"Okay," she said. "Go ahead, kid."

Nessie put her hand on Leah's cheek.

It was like the line, a little. That was the first thing Leah thought. A warm push, coming in, gentle and very sure of itself. Then there was a picture in her head that wasn't hers.

A wolf. A grey one, thin and fast and furious, standing at the edge of the lawn in the trees with her ears flat. Leah. The way Nessie saw her. And behind it, somehow, around the edges, a little bit sad. Then the picture changed, and it was still Leah, but standing in this room, just now, with her shoulders down. And then a question. It came with no words, just a feeling, a kid's feeling, bright and simple and enormous. *Are you happy now?*

Leah's eyes stung.

She didn't know what to say. She didn't know how you answered something like that, from a kid, with your face in her hand. She didn't even know the answer.

"I don't know," she said. "Maybe. I think so."

Nessie looked at her, very serious. Then another picture came. Herself, Nessie, on Jacob's back, holding on to his fur, laughing. The way Leah had seen her in a round room, through a man's eyes, through a memory he'd lifted out of a pair of Omnioculars in October and turned over and decided, there and then, crouched at the edge of a silver circle, that no old man in Italy was ever going to put his hand on. And a question again.

*Is he nice?*

Leah opened her mouth.

She thought about *nice*. About a man in a grey suit on a ridge in the rain, flicking curses away like crumbs. About a fang as long as her arm going through a vampire's finger. About a voice on a phone, soft and delighted, *I would so like to see*, and the man crouched over the phone with his face like stone, holding on to a three-hundred-year-old mind with everything he had so that it would say *dogs* instead of *heartbeat*.

She tried, very carefully, to tell the kid that. Just that. Just the shape of it.

The cold hand came up. She felt it rise. She felt it settle across the front of her throat, gentle, patient, asking if she was sure.

She stopped trying.

"He's a lot of things, kid," said Leah. "Nice isn't really on the list." She swallowed. She found some other words, and went round the hand with them, carefully, the way you step round a sleeping dog. "He's on your side, though. Okay? Remember that. Whatever anybody says. He's on your side."

The hand didn't close. It lay there and listened, and it let that go by.

Nessie looked at her for a long moment with her hand on Leah's face. Leah didn't know what the kid could feel through it. Maybe all of it. Maybe nothing.

Then a last picture came. It was a very simple one. Eggs. A frying pan of eggs, six of them, yellow and white. And a big question mark.

Leah laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her like a bark, short and surprised, and Nessie's face lit up like Leah had given her a present.

"Yeah," said Leah. "He made me eggs."

---

She left at twenty to nine.

Seth and Layla were going back to Calawah Way. Carlisle had made Layla promise to take a hot bath and the ibuprofen and to lie on her front all night with a heating pad, and Layla had promised in the voice of a woman who had already decided to take six aspirin instead. Seth had her truck keys. He was going to drive, which he'd done exactly twice before, and Layla was going to let him, which told Leah more about how much her back hurt than anything else had all night.

Leah walked out onto the porch with them. The rain had eased off to a drizzle. The lawn was black and shining, and the trees beyond it were blacker, and somewhere out there, past them, north-west, twenty miles, the line ran out into the dark and down a hill and into a man.

He was moving now. She could feel it. He'd stopped crouching. He'd left the hillside and gone somewhere else, and he was sitting down, indoors, she thought. Something about the air on him had changed. Dry. Closer.

At the bottom of the steps, Layla stopped and turned round.

"Leah," she said.

Seth was already halfway to the truck. He'd stopped too, and was waiting, looking back at them with his hands in his pockets.

"Yeah?"

Layla came back up two steps. It hurt her; Leah watched it hurt her. She stood one step down, so their faces were about level, and looked at Leah with her dark eyes with the gold ring in the middle, and the little white scar through her eyebrow.

"I'm not going to pretend I understand what happened to you today," Layla said. "I don't understand what happened to *me* five weeks ago. I've got a cord in my chest the size of my wrist that I can see with the right spell, and I've stopped trying to take it apart." She was quiet for a second. "I just want you to know. If he ever hurts you. I don't care who he is. I don't care what he did when he was seventeen. I'll find a way."

Leah looked at her.

"He won't," she said.

"I know he won't," said Layla. "I felt him not do it, on the ridge. Every spell I threw at him, he had about six ways to kill me with it, and he picked the one that didn't." She shrugged, and winced. "I'm saying it anyway."

Leah looked at her for a long moment.

"Okay," she said. "Thanks."

"Okay."

Layla went back down the steps. Seth took her arm. Leah watched them walk out across the wet lawn to the old white pickup, Seth slowing his steps to match hers, bending down to say something Leah couldn't hear, Layla laughing and then wincing and then laughing again. She watched him open the door for her. She watched him lift her up into the cab like she weighed nothing, and her let him.

She'd have hated that, two days ago. She'd have stood on this porch watching her brother be happy and hated every second of it, and hated herself for hating it, and gone for a very long run.

She stood on the porch and watched the truck pull away down the drive, its one good tail-light going red and then small and then gone between the trees.

She didn't hate anything.

She went down the steps and across the lawn and into the trees, and behind the biggest cedar she pulled her clothes off and rolled them and tied them to her ankle with the cord, and let go.

Then she ran home, the long way, the way that didn't go anywhere near the turn for Sam's, because she didn't need to prove anything to anybody anymore. And she didn't phase back until she was in the trees behind her mother's house, because Jacob was phased behind her the whole way, quiet in the pack mind, running the north loop by himself. He didn't say a word to her about any of it.

When she peeled off toward home, though, she heard him think one thing, quietly, to himself.

*Bathtub with claw feet. Huh.*

## Part Two: The Liaison

### V. Harry

*The tent, north fork of the Calawah — 6:36 p.m.*

The kettle boiled.

He stood at the range with his hands flat on the iron and let it scream for a bit, because he wasn't ready to take it off. Side two of *Physical Graffiti* was going round on the Rega, Plant and Page halfway into "Houses of the Holy", and down at the bottom of him the thing behind the locks lay with its chin on its paws and its eyes on the south-east wall of the kitchen, where there wasn't a window, where there wasn't anything at all but canvas and charms and twenty miles of dark forest and her.

He took the kettle off. He made tea. He made it properly, out of habit, pot scalded and leaves in and three minutes by the clock over the door, and he poured it into the chipped brown mug with the Chudley Cannons crest on it that Ron had given him for his nineteenth as a joke, and he stood at the range and drank it scalding, and thought about the oath.

He'd felt it go. Out of him, down the line, into her, twenty miles off and running. He'd felt it get there and close. He'd felt the two boys hit it from inside her head and slide off. He'd been in the Department eight years, and he'd taken that oath on the black stone on his first day with his hand flat on the cold of it, and he'd have told you, if you'd asked him this morning, that he understood it about as well as anybody alive who wasn't Croaker. He'd read the charter. He'd read the three monographs on it that existed, all of them restricted, one of them in Old Norse. He'd felt it close his throat in a pub in Belfast.

He hadn't known it could travel.

Nobody had. He'd have bet his pension on that. There was nothing in any of the three monographs about an oath that could look down a bond into somebody else's head, decide she counted as the swearer, and set up shop. Because nobody in the history of the Department had ever had a bond like that to look down. Unspeakables didn't marry, much. The ones who did married carefully, late, and badly, and kept the marriage in one drawer and the work in the other and never opened both at once.

He'd opened both at once by accident on a rock above the Bogachiel and now there was a slab of the Department sitting in the middle of a Quileute girl's head like a safe in a farmhouse.

He finished the tea. He put the mug in the sink.

He'd told her he'd clean up the ridge. He'd said tonight, after she'd gone. He'd said it in this kitchen across the table from her, and she'd looked at him, and he'd felt her file it, the way he filed things. Something he'd said he'd do.

So he'd do it.

He went down the hall to his room and changed. The suit came off and went on its hanger, and the white shirt into the basket, and he put on the other clothes, the ones Harry Evans wore: jeans, a grey flannel shirt, the old waxed jacket that had been his since a car boot sale in Hereford in 2002 and had never once been entirely dry. He kept the shoulder rig on under the jacket. He kept the boots. He took the Nikon off its hook by the door, because Harry Evans never went anywhere without it and because he had a feeling he'd want it, and he hung a cheap headlamp round his neck, because a man out on a ridge at night with no light was a man somebody might remember.

On his way past the workshop he stopped at the black oak door and put his palm flat on it.

Casimir was where he'd left him. Flat on his back in the circle, rigid, the Imperius long since lifted and the circle's hold back on in its place. Harry could feel the shape of him through the door, through the wards, a cold hard knot in the middle of the silver. Furious. Very quiet. Waiting.

"Back later," Harry said to the door.

He went out through the flap into the rain. Gary had moved about four inches since half six. He was on the guy line now, heading somewhere with great determination.

"Don't wait up," said Harry, and turned on his heel.

---

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — 6:49 p.m.*

He came out two hundred yards east of the slab, in the stunted hemlocks below the spine of the ridge, and stood still for a full minute in the dark before he moved.

Rain. Wind off the river valley, coming up the slope from the south. The smell of wet rock and hemlock needles and, very faint now, a long way under it, burnt moss and scorched earth and the hot electric stink of a Blasting Curse gone off against granite. No people. No animals bigger than a vole for half a mile. No cars on the Bogachiel road far below. He let the lattice come up under his attention and checked the whole ridge on it, end to end, and it was empty. No gold. No red. No rust.

He felt for wards that weren't his. Detection work. Sensors. Anything somebody might have left up here since one o'clock that would sit and listen. He felt for it the slow way, in widening rings, the way you'd sweep a room for bugs. If MACUSA had somebody on this coast who'd noticed the morning's fireworks, and that somebody was quick, they'd have had six hours to get a team out here.

Nothing. Just rock and trees and rain and the thin smell of the morning's magic hanging in the air over the slab, like smoke in a room after a party.

He walked up to it.

He put the headlamp on, in case. He stood at the edge of the slab in the white circle of it and looked at what was left.

It was worse than he'd remembered. Most scenes were. In the middle of a thing you saw what you needed and nothing else; it was only afterward, standing in the quiet with a light, that you saw the mess. The crater was the worst of it. Six feet across and three deep, in the moss and the thin soil and the rock under it, two feet in front of where the witch had been standing. The mud he'd blown out of it lay in a black ring all round, thrown in long fans, already half washed back down into the hole by six hours of rain. Twenty feet beyond it, the furrow: a long gouge in the ferns and the moss where she'd gone backward on her shield, ending at the nurse log. The log had a dent in its side the size of a person. The moss was crushed flat in the shape of her.

To the left, the boulder he'd blown apart with her own Blasting Curse. Gravel and fist-sized chunks of granite scattered thirty yards down the slope, a raw pale scar on the bedrock where it had sat for ten thousand years. Up in the hemlock beyond it, a fresh white wound where her first Stunner had taken off a branch the size of her leg. Further down, in the trees, he found what was left of the trunk her Bludgeoning Hex had split.

And everywhere, faintly, hanging over all of it in the rain, her magic.

He could see it without even trying. Her residue. It was gold, mostly, the gold of her, a good clear honest colour like the light through a jar of honey, and it was all over the ridge, smeared across the rock and splashed up the trees and hanging in a fine bright mist over the place where she'd landed. In the middle of it, sharp and pale blue, the shape of her Disjunction, frozen where he'd caught it on his fingers and turned it round. Anyone with the right charm could read that like a signature. Anyone half-competent from MACUSA could stand here tomorrow morning with a wand and a notebook and know that a witch had fought someone on this ridge, hard, for about thirty seconds, and that the witch had made her own spells, and that the spells were very good.

And then they'd go looking for witches on the peninsula who made their own spells. Which would take them about ten minutes.

His own residue was nowhere. He'd built the sinks into himself eight years ago and they took everything he did and drank it down into the stones of the lattice the moment it happened. Standing on the slab, he was a man in a waxed jacket with a camera. If somebody came up here with a revealing charm they'd find a witch fighting the air.

Right.

He crouched down at the edge of the slab and got to work.

---

He did her residue first, because it mattered most.

It was fiddly work. You couldn't just wipe it; residue was like a stain in a carpet, it went into the weave. You had to find the threads of it and draw them out one at a time and feed them somewhere. He fed them to the nearest of his ward stones, which was buried a quarter-mile east along the ridge under a split boulder, and he felt the stone drink each one down and go quiet. He started with the Disjunction, since it was the one that could hang her. It came up off the rock in a long pale blue ribbon and he turned it in his hands, in his head, one more time before he let it go. It was a lovely spell. He'd meant what he said. Somebody had sat at a table for a long time with that one, three years probably, building it a piece at a time. He felt a small sharp regret, sending it down into the stone.

Then the Stunners. The ropes. The roots. The Blasting Curse. Then the Apparition, the big one, the crack she'd come in on from fourteen miles off, which had left a smear in the air on the far side of the slab like a handprint on glass.

He was at it twenty minutes before the first thing came down the line.

It came while he was kneeling on the wet rock drawing up the last threads of her shield, and it came out of nowhere, the way a cramp does, and it was a hand.

A hand on her throat.

He knew it the second he felt it. He knew the shape of it, the cold and the weight and the gentle patient certainty of it, because he'd felt that exact hand on his own throat in a pub in Belfast in 2002. It was the oath. It was his oath, the Department's oath, the thing he'd taken on the black stone. And it was resting across Leah Clearwater's windpipe twenty miles away while she ran, and it was pressing, and she couldn't breathe right.

He went completely still on the rock.

He'd done that. Not on purpose. He'd never in his life wanted anything less. But it was his oath and it was his hand and it was her throat, and every bit of him, from the top of his head right down to the bottom, where the thing behind the locks lifted its head and *snarled*, hated it. Hated it worse than anything that had happened all day. Worse than the vampire. Worse than the old man laughing down the phone.

*Don't,* he said down the line.

He put it there the way you'd put a hand on someone's arm. One word. He didn't know if she'd hear. He didn't know if it worked that way.

*I wasn't,* came back up the line, at once. Clear as a voice in the next room. *I'm not. I just wanted to see.*

The hand lifted off her.

He stayed where he was, on his knees on the slab, for a while. He didn't go into her head. He didn't want to. He just stayed there at his end of the line, the way you'd stand at the bottom of the stairs after a crash, until he felt her breathing steady out, and her stride come back. Then he let his breath out, and found his hands were shaking, and made them stop.

"Right," he said to the rock. "Right."

He went back to work.

---

The hand came again at seven minutes past. Twice in a row, close together.

He felt the first one land, and her push against it, and stop. He felt the second one land, harder, and her push harder, and he heard a voice through her ears, a boy's voice, frightened, *it's hurting you, Lee, stop*, and he was on his feet on the slab with his fists shut before he knew he'd moved.

Then something touched the oath from outside.

He'd never felt anything like it. He stood on the ridge in the rain and felt it, and it took him a long moment to work out what it was, because there was nothing to compare it to. It was like standing inside a vault and hearing somebody on the other side of the door run a finger round the edge of the lock. Slowly. Reading the maker's name stamped into the steel.

Someone had read his name.

Nothing had come out of her head. The oath was holding, fine, solid as a rock. They'd read it off the *outside*. Off the oath itself. As if the thing had his name cut round the rim of it like the words on a coin, which, he supposed, standing there with the rain running down his neck, it probably did. You swore it in your own name, on the stone. The stone took your name. Of course it was written on it. He'd just never in his life met anything that could read it.

He knew exactly who had. He'd been through the boy's head on the ridge at noon; he'd seen the living room, the blue lights, the witch on the floor with the child. He'd seen the bronze-haired one on the carpet with the little girl in his lap, saying *I'm sorry, I can't help it*. He'd known for nine weeks there was a mind-reader in that house. It was in the file. It was in Gianna's head, in the old man's voice, *Carlisle's boy who hears*.

"Course you did," Harry said to the dark. "Course you bloody did."

He stood there and waited for it to go wrong.

It didn't. Nothing came out through the oath. Nothing came back down the line at him but her: a long sharp jolt of surprise, and then something that felt like a woman standing very still on a lawn with her hand on her throat, and then, slowly, something he didn't have a name for, something close to relief.

He breathed out.

The name was out, then. In that house. In those heads. *Harry Potter*, said out loud in a room full of vampires and a witch from New Mexico who'd been fourteen when the war ended and had almost certainly owned a Chocolate Frog card with his face on it.

He found he didn't much care about that, either. He'd thought he would. He'd spent eight years making very sure nobody on earth put that name and that face together. And now he thought about it, crouched on the rock with Layla Quinn's residue dripping off his fingers, and what he mostly felt was tired. And a bit curious what the witch's face had done.

He went back to work.

---

The crater was the problem. Everything else he could make into anything he liked. The crater had to be something.

He'd told Leah it would be lightning. He'd said it across the table without thinking, because it had been a stormy week and because lightning was the only thing he could think of in nature that went bang in the middle of a mountain and left a hole. Now he stood at the edge of it with the light on his forehead and the rain coming down through the beam in white streaks, and thought about it properly.

A strike on open rock left a scar. A pit, sometimes, if the rock was cracked and wet and the water in the cracks flashed to steam and blew the surface out. Spalled stone round the edges, flakes and slabs of it popped loose. Scorched moss. A long furrow, sometimes, where the current ran out along the ground looking for somewhere to go, and boiled the water out of the soil as it went and threw the soil aside. A tree nearby, maybe, with the bark peeled off it in one long spiral from the top to the ground where the charge had come down its wet outside. And, if anybody bothered to look, if a geologist from the university in Seattle came up here one summer with a magnetometer for some reason nobody would ever know, a patch of rock where the bolt had gone in that was magnetised all wrong, pointing every which way, the iron in it scrambled by the current.

You could fake all of that. Most of it you could fake with a wand and a free afternoon.

The magnetism you couldn't. Not properly. Not so a man with a magnetometer would believe it.

He looked up at the sky.

It was low and thick and black, the cloud sitting right down on the ridges, so close he could feel the weight of it on the back of his neck. There'd been thunder off the coast on Thursday and again on Saturday night. The air had that taste in it still, faint, a metal edge, the same taste the boy had smelled on the witch in a camping store.

"Bit flash," Harry said to himself. "Bit flash, mate."

He did it anyway.

It took him about four minutes to find the charge in the cloud and another two to coax it. Weather work was slow and rude and he didn't do it often; it was like trying to herd sheep with your mind. But the charge was there, sitting up in the dark over the ridge in great slow sullen sheets, and it wanted to come down. It always wanted to come down. It just needed somewhere to aim.

He gave it the crater.

He stepped back thirty yards first, behind a hemlock, because he wasn't an idiot. Then he put his hand up in the dark and made a channel, a thin straight line of air from the cloud to the middle of the hole, and he opened it.

The world went white.

There was no gap between the light and the sound. They came at once, a single tearing white crack that went through his chest like a fist and shook the hemlock against his back and lit every needle on every tree on the ridge for one stark instant in perfect, colourless detail. He saw the slab. He saw the crater, and a rope of light standing in it as thick as his arm, pulsing. He saw the rain hanging in the air, every drop, the way the witch had held it in her bee yard.

Then it was dark, and his ears were ringing, and there was a smell of ozone so thick he could taste it on his teeth.

He waited for his eyes to come back. Then he walked over and had a look.

It was perfect. The bolt had gone in exactly where he'd sent it, into the middle of the crater, and found the water in the cracks of the rock and blown a fresh shallow pit in the bottom of the old one, a raw pale star of shattered granite, steaming in the rain. The edges of the crater had spalled. Flakes of rock lay round it like broken plates. The moss round the lip was scorched black for a yard in every direction. And from the crater, running off downslope toward the nurse log, the current had found the furrow the witch's body had made and gone down it, boiling the water out of the ground as it went, scorching the ferns on either side, so that it looked like it had made the furrow itself.

He knelt and put his hand flat on the bottom of the pit. It was hot. The iron in the rock under his palm was all over the place, every grain of it twisted round and pointing somewhere new.

"There you go," he said. "Find that, you bastard."

He did the rest by hand. The boulder he'd blown apart he left more or less as it was, since a strike that size could easily have split a rock next to it, but he scorched the scar on the bedrock and cracked it a little further. The hemlock that had lost its branch he gave a spiral, a long ribbon of bark torn off from the top of the trunk to the roots, and burned the wood underneath. The roots the witch had transfigured out of the moss had gone back to moss when she lost her wand, but he went over the patch anyway, and scorched it. The split trunk in the trees he left, because trees split.

Then he went over to the nurse log, where she'd landed, and stood looking at it.

He found the bead on the ground there.

Or what was left of it. Most of it was on the slab, where the boy had bitten it; he'd find those pieces in a minute. This bit had been thrown clear, a chip of glossy black obsidian the size of his thumbnail lying in the moss at the foot of the log, where it had fallen out of the sandy wolf's fur when he'd gone crashing over to her. And a scrap of leather cord braided with dark thread, snapped. He picked them both up and held them in the light.

He could feel the spell still, faintly. What was left of it. It had been one-use, he could tell; a beacon, and a single jump, keyed to the cord, which was keyed to the boy, which was keyed to her through whatever was in the boy's chest. She'd carved it. She'd sat up all night carving it, he'd bet. You could feel the hours in it. You could feel what she'd been thinking about while she did it, too, faint, like a fingerprint on glass, and he didn't look at that part for long because it wasn't his business.

"Again," Harry said quietly. "Bloody lovely work, again."

He put the chip and the cord in his inside pocket.

He found the rest of the bead on the slab, in four pieces, scattered round a scuff mark. He picked those up too. Then he straightened, and turned round with the light, and saw the claw marks.

---

They were at the edge of the slab, where she'd come down.

She'd jumped for the vampire. He remembered that. He'd come out of the turn with the rope of the snare already in his hand and seen her in the air, a grey shape going up off the moss with her jaws open, fifteen feet to the hanging thing, and he'd stepped into the gap without thinking and she'd had to twist in mid-air not to go into him. And she'd landed on the slab sideways, scrabbling, her claws shrieking on the wet granite, and fetched up four feet from him with her teeth out.

She'd left marks. Four long pale grooves in the granite, each about two feet long, curving where she'd twisted. Then another set, shorter and deeper, where she'd got her feet under her. And further along the slab, over where he'd held her down, where she'd fought the hold with everything she had for longer than he'd thought anything could, a mess of scratches gone into the rock in short furious strokes, the way a dog digs at a door. And in two of those, a dark smear. Rain had thinned it. It was still there.

She'd split two claws, fighting him.

He hadn't known that.

He crouched down beside the marks. He put the light on them. He sat there on his heels for a while, in the rain, not doing anything.

He was going to have to get rid of them. That was obvious. Lightning didn't leave claw marks, and a wildlife biologist who came up here in the spring and found grooves two feet long in solid rock would be on the phone to somebody within the hour. He'd get rid of them, and the blood with them, and then this would just be a ridge where lightning had hit on a Monday in November, and nobody would ever know.

Down at the bottom of him, the thing behind the locks didn't like that at all.

He felt it come up, quietly. It lifted its head and looked out through him at the grooves in the rock, and at the dark smear in the bottom of two of them, and he felt it *want* something, without any words. Something simple and old. The way a dog lies down on a jumper somebody's left on the floor.

"I know," said Harry. "I know. I can't."

It went on looking.

He sat there a moment longer. Then he took the Nikon off his neck and pulled the lens cap and switched the flash on, and leaned in, and took a picture.

The flash went off white in the dark. He wound on. He took another one, closer, of the two grooves with the smear in them. He wound on and took a third of the whole slab, the long curve of where she'd landed.

Then he put the lens cap back on, and put his hand flat on the granite, and smoothed the marks out of it, one at a time, slowly, the way you'd smooth wet sand. The blood he took up last, and he didn't send it to the ward stone with the rest. He didn't know what he did with it. He just held it for a second, the little dark smear of it, and then let it go into the rain.

The thing at the bottom of him put its head back down.

"Yeah," said Harry. "All right. I know."

*7:28 p.m.*

He was nearly done when the pressure came.

This one had nothing to do with the oath. It came down the line from her end all at once, like a door opening on a room where somebody's raised their voice, and the shape of it was her, standing somewhere with her feet planted, saying something in a voice that was rising, and in the middle of it one word that went through him like a nail.

*Danger.*

He caught the edge of it, the way you'd catch your own name said in a hard voice in the next room. *If anybody in this room ever gave him a reason to think they were a danger to me—*

His head came up. So did the thing at the bottom of him.

It came up all at once, wide awake, eyes open, and looked up the line at her. Toward wherever she was. Toward whatever room it was with whatever was in it that had made her say *danger* like that. He felt it look. He felt how far it could see: a direction, a pull, south-east, a lot of cold. He felt it not like what it found. He felt its lips come off its teeth.

*No,* she said. Very clear. Right into him. *No. I'm fine. Go back to work.*

He held still on the slab. He let his breath out. He felt the thing at the bottom of him hear her, too; he felt it hold there for a second, stiff, the way a dog holds when it's been told to sit and hasn't decided yet. Then it lay back down. Slowly. It kept its head up and its eyes open, pointed south-east.

"Right," Harry said. "Right. Back to work."

He went back to work.

*7:41 p.m.*

The second one was worse.

He'd moved on to the last of it: the scorch marks on the hemlock, making sure they matched the spiral. He was standing at the foot of the tree with his hand on the bark when it hit him, and there were no words in it at all. It was pure fear. Her fear. It came down the line like a slap. Fear *for*; he could tell that much. Fear for somebody else. Sharp and hot and total, the way you're frightened when a child runs toward a road.

Fear for him.

Somebody in that room had said something about him. He didn't know what. He didn't need to. He felt her fear for him go through the whole of her like a burn, and he felt her move, fast, felt her face go a foot from something cold, and he felt the growl come up through her chest that had no business coming out of a human throat.

He was on his feet before he knew he'd moved. He was standing all the way up, very straight, in the dark at the foot of the hemlock with his hands open and the rain running into his eyes, and his heart, which had been going slow and steady all evening, was going like a train.

And the thing at the bottom of him hit the first lock.

He felt it go. He felt the whole weight of it come up off the floor of him and hit the lock like a car hitting a gate, and the lock *groaned*, and he felt it start to bend. And behind it the second lock shuddered. And it kept coming. It knew exactly where she was now. It had the line in its teeth and it was pulling itself up it, hand over hand, toward the door, toward the outside, toward twenty miles of forest it could cover in about a quarter of an hour, toward a room with something cold in it that had frightened her.

He let it, for about half a second. He'd think about that later. Half a second where he stood in the rain with his heart going and the lock bending and thought, very clearly, *yes, all right, go on then*.

*No,* said Leah. *No. Stop it.* Out loud, he thought; she'd said it out loud, through her teeth, wherever she was, and it came down the line like a hand grabbing a collar. *Both of you. It's fine. It's* fine*. It was just words. Sit* down*.*

*Both of you.*

He put his hand flat on his chest. The place where the line went in. He leaned on it. He leaned on the first lock with everything he'd got, the way he'd leaned on the Imperius with Aro laughing down the phone, harder than that, and he felt the thing behind it lean back. It wanted out. It wanted her. It wanted whatever had frightened her in its teeth.

*Sit down,* she'd said.

He felt it hear her. He felt it stop.

It went down. Slowly. Growling the whole way, a long low sound he could feel in his own breastbone. It went back down onto its belly at the bottom of him and lay there with its sides heaving. The lock stopped groaning. It was bent. He could feel the bend in it. A dent in the iron where nothing had ever dented it before.

He sat down on the wet ground at the foot of the hemlock, because his knees had stopped wanting to hold him.

His heart didn't slow down. It stayed fast for a long time. He sat there in the rain with his back against a tree he'd just finished pretending lightning had struck, and his hand flat on his chest, and felt her at the other end of the line, standing somewhere with her heart going as hard as his, saying something to somebody in a shaking voice.

"Jesus Christ," he said to the dark.

He sat there till his heart came down. It took about ten minutes.

Then he got up, and finished the scorch marks on the hemlock, and walked the whole ridge end to end one more time with the light, checking. He found a single sticky fingerprint on the side of the nurse log, at about the height of a hand pushing yourself up off the ground. Honey. He took that too.

At twenty past eight he stood at the edge of the slab and looked at all of it.

A ridge in the rain where lightning had come down on a Monday in November. Scorched rock and spalled granite and a pit full of steaming water. A furrow burned into the ferns. A hemlock stripped in a spiral. A boulder split by the heat. Nothing else. No witch. No vampire. No wolves. No Englishman in a suit.

He'd made a lot of scenes into other scenes in eight years. This was one of the better ones.

He turned on his heel.

*Logging turnout, Bogachiel road — 8:24 p.m.*

The Explorer was where he'd left it at a quarter to twelve, nose out under the trees, beaded with rain. Nobody had touched it. He got in and shut the door and sat behind the wheel in the dark with the rain drumming on the roof, and didn't start it.

It was dry in the car. Close. It smelled of old coffee and wet wool and the pine air freshener the rental company had hung off the mirror in September, which he'd never taken down because Harry Evans wouldn't have bothered. He sat in it with his hands on the wheel, the way he'd sat in it at a quarter to twelve, grinning, saying *gotcha* to a red dot on a map in his head.

That was about nine hours ago. It felt like a year.

He sat there and felt her. He didn't go looking. He didn't need to. She was at the other end, twenty-odd miles east now, indoors, sitting or crouching, with something small in front of her. Calmer. Tired to the bone. He could feel the tiredness on her like a wet coat.

Then, all at once, a laugh came down the line.

It came out of nowhere. Short and surprised and rough, like a bark. Like she hadn't laughed in so long she'd forgotten how it went and it had just come out of her anyway, sideways, before she could stop it. He felt it go through her. He felt it go through him. He felt the thing at the bottom of him lift its head at the sound of it, calm this time, *listening*, the way a dog lifts its head when someone it likes laughs in another room.

He didn't know what she was laughing at.

He sat in the dark car on the Bogachiel road with the rain on the roof and his hands on the wheel and found he was smiling. Properly. He could feel it on his face. He couldn't remember the last time he'd caught himself doing that with nobody there to do it for.

He sat there till it faded. Then he started the car.

He drove back through Forks at exactly the speed limit, past the diner with its pink neon going and Doreen behind the counter in the window wiping down the pie case, and out the other side, and north on 101 through the dark, and onto the forest road that ran up the Calawah, and up it eleven miles to the gravel turnaround where he left the car most nights. He walked the last two miles in the rain without a light. He didn't need one.

Gary was on the flap when he got there. He'd made it across the guy line and down onto the canvas and was sitting just to the left of the door, gleaming.

"Don't," said Harry, and went in.

### VI. Harry

*The tent — 9:24 p.m.*

He got a beer out of the fridge and didn't open it.

He stood in the hall with the cold bottle in one hand and looked at the mirror on its nail by the telephone. The plain black frame. The pound-shop look of it. Somewhere on the back, in letters you needed a loupe for, *abandon hope*.

Down at the bottom of him, the thing behind the locks lifted its head.

It didn't like the mirror. He'd noticed that at twenty to six, when he'd taken it down the first time with Leah in the kitchen pretending to read the back of a record sleeve; it had gone stiff all over, the way a dog goes stiff when a stranger comes up the path. He'd put it down to the day. Everything had been strange about the day. It had seemed reasonable that the thing in him would be strange too.

It was doing it again now. Worse. It had come up onto its feet behind the bent lock and was standing there with its head low, looking out through him at the frame on its nail, and he could feel the low steady vibration of a growl in his own breastbone, so faint nobody in the room would have heard it, if there'd been anybody in the room.

He knew why. That was the trouble. He knew exactly why, because whatever it knew, it knew from him.

The mirror was the Department. The mirror was a small panelled room four thousand miles away, and an old man in grey robes who didn't sleep, and behind him a whole building full of people whose job, whose entire reason for existing, was to find out how things worked. Harry knew what they did with things they wanted to understand. He'd done it himself, for eight years, with a clean conscience. He had a round room at the back of this tent full of what he'd done. And the thing in him had stood at the bottom of him and watched him do it, all afternoon, through the specimen room's door: watched him take a three-hundred-year-old killer apart with a fang and a list of questions, and file each piece, neat and cold. It had seen what Harry's hands did to things that were interesting.

And it had heard him say, in his own kitchen, with Leah sitting across the table: *They'd have you in a jar.*

So it didn't want him talking to the mirror. It didn't want him talking to the mirror about anything, ever, if there was the faintest chance any of it would lead back down the line to her. It didn't trust him not to. Why would it? It had been locked in his cellar for two months by a man who could keep secrets from himself.

"I'm not going to tell him about her," Harry said to it.

It went on growling.

"I'm *not*. I'm going to tell him something else."

It didn't believe him. He couldn't blame it. He wasn't sure he believed himself.

He put the beer down on the hall table, unopened. He took the mirror down off its nail.

"Croaker," he said.

---

His own face went away, and the glass went grey, and then dark, and then it was the room. The same small panelled room, the same green-shaded lamp, the same drifts of paper on the desk. Somebody had brought Croaker a cup of tea in the four hours since; it was standing at his elbow, untouched, gone cold, with a skin on it. That was the only thing that had changed. The old man was writing. He didn't look up.

"Potter," said Saul Croaker. "Twice in a night. People will talk."

"Nobody talks to you, sir."

"That," said Croaker, "is rather the point." He finished his line. He put his pen down, square to the edge of the blotter, the way he always did. Then he looked up out of the mirror with the tap-water eyes. "It's half past five in the morning."

"Yes, sir."

"I assume it's important."

"I lied to you."

Croaker didn't move. Nothing in his face moved. It never did; Harry had seen him take the news of three dead Unspeakables in a single afternoon in 2004 with exactly this face, and the news that the Minister wanted to visit Level Nine with exactly this face, and the news that the cafeteria had stopped doing kippers. But his hand had been lying flat on the blotter beside the pen, and Harry watched it go, very slightly, still.

"When?" said Croaker.

"Twenty to six, my time. About four hours ago. You asked me if there was anything else, and I said no." Harry held the mirror steady. "There was. There's a lot else."

"I see."

"And you asked me why I sounded odd, and I said it was the rain."

"I remember."

"It wasn't the rain, sir."

"No," said Croaker. "I didn't think it was." He folded his hands on the blotter. "You've never blamed the weather for anything in eight years, Potter. You stood in a bog outside Kuopio for nine days in 2001 with the Inferi coming up through the peat round your ankles every night, and the only thing you said about the weather in a forty-page report was that the Inferi didn't seem to mind it." He paused. "Go on."

Down at the bottom of Harry, the growl went up a notch. He felt it come up through his chest. It was audible now, faintly. A low, rough, continuous sound under his ribs, like an engine idling in a garage.

He ignored it.

"I'm not going to tell you what the rest is," Harry said.

Croaker looked at him.

"Most of it," said Harry. "Some of it I'm going to tell you now, because it's the Department's, and you should have had it four hours ago. The rest of it's mine. I'm keeping it. I'm not going to tell you what it is, and I'm not going to tell you why, and if you order me to, I'll still not tell you." He took a breath. "That's the bit I rang to say. I'm not going to tell you a story about the weather. You asked me a straight question. I gave you a bent answer. I've never done that to you, and I'm not starting."

For a long moment, Croaker didn't say anything at all.

The growl in Harry's chest had gone very still. He could feel the thing behind the lock standing there, listening, with its ears forward. Waiting to see what he'd say next.

"The part that's the Department's," said Croaker eventually. "Let's have that first."

---

So Harry gave him the phone call.

He gave it to him plainly, in order, the way he'd given him the Stunners and the fire and the tooth. The cheap grey phone in the dead woman's jacket pocket. The single number in it. The Imperius, and how hard it had been to hold. *Pronto*, in Gianna's voice. Hold music, violins. Then the old man.

He told Croaker why Casimir had come to the peninsula: because the tracker had found him in Vancouver in October and told him the master wanted a favour returned. Because a year ago the Volturi had made the Cullen girl a promise, in Italy, the girl who'd been human then and was married to the boy in the photograph now, that she'd be turned or she'd be killed, and they'd said they'd check. Because the one with the face like a bad smell had been nagging about it ever since. Because Aro had wanted it done quietly, by somebody nobody would think twice about, a nomad passing through.

He told Croaker what Casimir had said down the phone, word for word. *The girl is changed. Since the end of summer. The family are as they always were. Nothing to report.* He told him Aro had asked if there was anything else of interest in all that rain, and that Casimir had said *dogs*. He told him Casimir was supposed to go south for the winter and come to Volterra in the spring so Aro could take his hand.

"*I would so like to see*," Harry said. "That's what he said. His exact words. *Come and see me in the spring, Casimir. I would so like to see.*"

Croaker's face did nothing.

"So Aro thinks the Cullens are boring," said Harry. "He thinks his errand's done. He thinks his nomad's gone to Mexico. He's expecting him in the spring. And when spring comes and Casimir doesn't turn up, he'll think what anybody thinks about a nomad who doesn't turn up. And he'll have nothing to make him look over here twice." He paused. "That's the Department's. I made that call off my own bat in about four minutes and didn't run it past you, and I should have, and I'd make it again."

"Why?" said Croaker.

"Why did I make it?"

"You know what I'm asking."

Harry was quiet for a moment. He knew what Croaker was asking. He'd known since the specimen room what Croaker would ask, if he ever told him about the call. *Why did a nomad's report about a coven we've marked as useful matter so much that you Imperiused him on the spot and rang Volterra without asking me?*

*A heartbeat*, the answer was. *In the house of the cold ones. Fast, like a bird's. Blood and not-blood.*

A little girl on a russet wolf's back, laughing, with both fists in its fur.

"That's part of the bit I'm keeping, sir," said Harry.

Croaker looked at him for a long time.

"There's something in Forks," said Croaker slowly, "that you didn't want Aro to know about."

"Yes."

"Something in the Cullen house."

Harry didn't answer. He didn't lie, and he didn't answer.

"And you've known about it," said Croaker, "for some time."

"Nine weeks."

"And it isn't in a single one of your reports."

"No, sir."

Croaker picked up his pen. He looked at it. He put it down again, square to the blotter.

"Will it affect Volterra?" he said.

Harry thought about that properly. He owed the man that. He thought about the child on the wolf's back, and the old man in the middle chair who'd hold out his hand for anything rare enough, and what that old man would do for a creature nobody in three thousand years had ever seen. He thought about the Department's strike, whenever it came, and the room on Level Nine with no name.

"If he ever found out about it," Harry said, "he'd come over here himself to have a look, and he'd bring everybody, and we'd lose the only edge we've got, which is that he doesn't know we exist. So keeping it from him helps. Keeping it from him is the whole point." He paused. "Keeping it from you doesn't change that one way or the other. I'm keeping it from you because I think it's safer with fewer people knowing. I think that includes us."

"Us," said Croaker.

"Yes, sir."

"You think it's safer with the Department not knowing."

"Yes, sir. I do."

The growl in Harry's chest had stopped. He felt the thing behind the lock standing there, very still, with its head up. Listening.

Croaker sat back in his chair. It was the first time Harry had ever seen him sit back. The old man laced his fingers together over his narrow chest and looked out of the mirror at Harry with an expression Harry had never seen on his face before, and couldn't read.

"And the rest of it?" said Croaker. "The part that's *yours*?"

---

Harry stood in the hall of his tent with the mirror in his hand and the unopened beer sweating on the table beside him, and he felt the thing at the bottom of him go stiff all over again. Every hair on it standing up. Its head low. Its eyes on the glass.

*Don't,* it said, without words. *Don't you dare.*

"I'm compromised," said Harry.

Croaker didn't say anything.

"As of this afternoon. About quarter to one." He didn't take his eyes off the mirror. "There's a person. Here. Local. I'm not going to say who. I'm not going to say what. If I told you that, the Department would want to know how it works, and the Department finds out how things work by taking them apart, and I'd stop it. I'd stop you. I'd stop anybody you sent." He took a breath. "That's what compromised means. If it ever comes down to what the Department wants and what's good for them, it's them. Every time. I won't even have to think about it. I'll have done it before I've thought about it."

The growl in his chest had stopped again.

"So you should pull me," said Harry. "Send somebody else. I'll hand over the specimen, and the lattice, and the notes. I'll brief whoever you send. I'll show them how the net works and walk them round the stones. I'll stay out of the way. I just won't do it while lying to you about why. That's all."

He stopped.

Croaker looked at him out of the mirror for a very long time.

"Have you finished?" he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Is something growling, Potter?"

Harry blinked.

He hadn't realised it was loud enough. He listened, and it was. The thing behind the lock had started up again sometime in the last minute, low and rough and steady under his ribs, and it was coming out through his chest the way a cat's purr comes out through its fur, and it was carrying in the quiet hallway.

"Yes, sir," said Harry.

Croaker looked at him a moment longer.

"Hm," he said.

He didn't ask. Harry stood there and waited for him to ask, and he didn't.

Then the old man unlaced his fingers, and leaned forward, and put his hands flat on the blotter, one on either side of his pen.

"Do you know," said Croaker, "what the Department's greatest single cause of attrition is, Potter? Over the last three centuries?"

"No, sir."

"The Veil takes a few. The Time Room takes a few. The brains, the things we bring home from the field, the Americans, the Wizengamot, the Minister; all of them take their share." He paused. "The largest share, by a very long way, is taken by Unspeakables who have nothing they would choose over the Department."

Harry said nothing.

"We take in the cleverest witches and wizards in Britain, and we put them in a building with the most interesting things in the world, and we tell them they may never speak of their work to another living soul outside these walls. And some of them go home every night to someone. A husband. A wife. A child. A garden. A dog. Something on the other side of the door that the work can't have." Croaker's voice didn't change. It never did. "And some of them don't. Some of them stop going home, because there's nothing at home that's as interesting as the work. And they get very, very good. The best I have are always the ones with nothing at home. And then, one afternoon, about ten years in, usually a Tuesday, I find out that one of them has gone down to the Death Chamber on their own, at about four o'clock, with no reason to be there. And stood in front of the arch. And listened to the voices for a little too long."

Down at the bottom of Harry, the thing behind the lock had gone completely quiet.

"I have lost eleven that way," said Croaker. "In forty years. The best eleven I ever had."

"Sir—"

"You came to me at eighteen," said Croaker, "out of a war, having walked into a forest to die and walked back out again. You had nobody at home. You went to Uganda for eight months on my money to learn to do magic without a stick and came back and asked me what was next. You went to Finland and stood in a bog for nine days and came back and asked me what was next. You went to Minsk, and you did what you did there, and you wrote it up in a page and a half, and you were at your desk at eight the next morning asking me what was next." He paused. "I have been waiting for eight years, Potter, for you to find something you'd choose over us. I'd rather begun to think you weren't going to. I'd been quite sure, for about three years now, that I was going to come in one Tuesday and find you standing in front of that arch."

Harry didn't say anything. He found he couldn't.

"You're compromised," said Croaker. "Good. About time."

"Sir," said Harry. It came out wrong. He cleared his throat. "Sir, with respect. You can't run a station with an officer who's told you to your face he'd choose a local over the Department."

"I can run a station with an officer who told me to my face," said Croaker. "That's rather the difference, Potter. You rang me at half past five in the morning to confess to a lie of two words." He picked up his pen again and looked at it. "Do you know how many of the people in this building would have done that? I could count them on one hand. I could count them on one finger." He put the pen down. "That is precisely why I will never get rid of you. Not if you choose your local over me every single day for the rest of your life. Not if you set fire to Volterra by accident. You'll tell me you did it. You'll tell me why, or you'll tell me you won't tell me why. And I will always, always know where I stand." He let that sit. "Do you understand how rare that is?"

"Yes, sir."

"I don't think you do. But never mind." The old man pulled a fresh sheet of grey paper toward him off the top of a drift. "You're where you need to be, Potter. Whatever it is. Whoever. You were right where you needed to be at quarter to one this afternoon and you're still there. I'm not sending anyone. I'm not pulling you."

"The Americans—"

"Are still making noises. I'm aware." Croaker was writing now. The pen scratched. "Given what's come out of that peninsula in nine weeks, you'd have been staying a good while longer in any case. A coven that hunts deer and has a seer in it. A nation of shapeshifters. A nomad on an errand for Aro. And whatever it is you've got that you won't give me." He didn't look up. "If I have to make you the Department's liaison to La Push to keep you there, then that's what you are. As of now."

Harry stared at him.

"Liaison," he said.

"Liaison. Quileute Nation. Olympic Peninsula Station." Croaker wrote. "Station strength, one. Reporting to me. Duration, indefinite." He looked up. "Is that a problem?"

"Is it a *real job*, sir?"

"It is now," said Croaker. "I've just written it down."

"And MACUSA? If they find a British Unspeakable sitting on the reservation with a job title—"

"They won't find a British Unspeakable on the reservation." Croaker signed the bottom of the page with a small, tight, illegible squiggle. "You are still not there, Potter. You are simply not there for considerably longer than we'd originally planned." He set the pen down. "You'll have it in writing by the end of the week. Don't let anybody see it."

Harry stood in his hallway with the mirror in his hand.

At the bottom of him, the thing behind the lock had lain down. He hadn't felt it go. It had just gone, sometime in the last minute, back down onto its belly, and it was lying there with its chin on its paws and its eyes on the mirror, watching the old man in it. The growling had stopped. It looked, as far as he could tell, the way a dog looks at somebody it's decided, provisionally, to tolerate.

"One more thing," said Croaker.

"Sir."

"This person." Croaker's face did nothing. "Are they safe?"

Harry thought about a grey wolf going away into the dark between the spruces, fast as smoke. About a girl in cut-offs standing in the middle of a house full of vampires with her finger in an Alpha's chest. About the oath, his oath, sitting in her head like a safe in a farmhouse, keeping everything he'd ever sworn to keep, keeping it in *her*, so that the only way anybody got it out of her was through him.

"Yes, sir," he said. "Safest person in the world right now, I reckon."

"Good," said Croaker. "Keep them that way." He picked up his cold tea, looked at the skin on it, and put it down again. "Full report by Friday. The tooth, the specimen, the call. Leave out whatever you like. Write *withheld* where you've left it out, so I know where the holes are."

"You'll know anyway."

"I'll know anyway," Croaker agreed. "But it's polite." He paused. "Go to bed, Potter. You look dreadful."

"You first, sir."

"I don't," said Saul Croaker, and the mirror went dark.

---

Harry stood in the hall for a while.

He looked at the mirror in his hand, at his own face in it. He did look dreadful. He looked like a man who'd been up since half five, and snared a vampire, and fought a witch, and been hit by something nobody had a name for on a rock above the Bogachiel, and spent an evening on his knees on that same rock calling lightning down out of the sky to cover it up. He looked about ten years older than he'd looked this morning.

He also looked, he thought, a bit less like the bloke who'd been in the mirror for the last eight years. He couldn't have said how. Something round the eyes.

He hung it back on its nail.

He picked the beer up off the table and opened it with his thumb and drank about half of it, standing in the hall, and then he leaned his back against the wall and slid down it until he was sitting on the slate floor with his knees up and the bottle hanging off his fingers.

"*Liaison*," he said to the empty hallway.

Down at the bottom of him, the thing behind the lock thumped its tail once against the floor of him.

He didn't know it could do that. He'd had it in there for years and he'd never once felt it do that.

"Yeah," said Harry. "Don't get used to it."

It thumped its tail again.

He sat there on the floor and finished the beer, and thought about eleven Unspeakables standing in front of an arch in a stone room at four o'clock on a Tuesday afternoon, listening to the voices. He'd stood in front of that arch himself. More than once. He'd stood there with a clipboard and listened to them. He'd never once thought of himself as somebody who'd stay too long.

He thought about Croaker sitting in that small room for three years, quite sure.

Then he got up, and rinsed the bottle, and put it in the crate by the back door with the others, and went to the library and sat down at the desk by the fire with a pen and a stack of grey paper and started on the report. *Specimen acquired 11:40 a.m., Monday 20th November, ridge line above the Bogachiel River.* He wrote for an hour. He wrote everything. He wrote the Stunners and the fire and the Cruciatus and the Imperius and the tooth, all of it, neat and cold, the way he'd said it out loud for the record. He wrote up the phone call.

When he got to the part where the vampire had stood in the trees behind the white house for an hour in the dark, breathing in something, he stopped, and looked at it for a while, and then wrote *WITHHELD* in capitals, and underlined it, and went on.

When he got to quarter to one on the ridge, and a grey wolf standing ten feet away in the rain with her lips off her teeth, he didn't write anything at all. He wrote *WITHHELD*, and underlined it twice.

At eleven he put the pen down and banked the fire and went to bed.

### VII. Leah

*The Clearwater house, La Push — 9:07 p.m.*

Her mother was sitting at the kitchen table in her bathrobe with a cup of coffee, and the phone was on the table in front of her, the cordless one off the wall, and she was looking at it.

Leah came in the back door barefoot, in her wet cut-offs, with pine needles stuck to her calves and the cord still hanging off one ankle, and stopped on the mat.

Sue didn't look up from the phone.

"Seth called," she said.

"Yeah," said Leah. "I figured."

"From the doctor's house. About an hour ago." Sue turned the phone over on the table, very slowly, the way she'd turn a fish over on a cutting board to see the other side. "He said you'd want to tell me yourself. He said he wasn't going to say anything, because it wasn't his to say." She looked up. "And then he said it anyway. Because he's Seth."

Leah stood on the mat.

The kitchen was small and yellow and too warm, the way it always was, with the woodstove going in the corner and the window over the sink steamed up. It smelled of coffee and fry bread and the cedar-smoke smell that got into everything in La Push from November to April. The clock over the stove said seven minutes past nine. On the fridge, under a magnet shaped like a salmon, there was a photo of all four of them on the boat, the summer Seth was eleven. Her dad at the wheel with his cap on backwards, laughing at something off the edge of the picture, with his mouth wide open. Leah had looked at that photo every day for eight months and made herself not see it.

She saw it now.

"Sit down, Leah," said her mother.

She sat down.

Sue got up, and got a second mug down out of the cupboard, and poured coffee into it, and put it in front of her, and sat back down. She didn't ask if Leah wanted it. She never did. She just put coffee in front of you and assumed. Leah wrapped her hands round it. She didn't need the heat. She held it anyway.

"So," said Sue.

"So."

"Is it true?"

"Yeah."

Sue nodded. She drank some coffee. She looked out the steamed-up window at nothing.

"Seth said a lot of things," she said. "Seth always says a lot of things. He said he's English. He said he's some kind of magic person, like the Quinn girl, only worse. He said he put the both of you on the ground and threw that poor girl into a tree."

"A log."

"A log." Sue nodded again. "He said he's got one of the cold ones in a cage."

"A circle."

"He said you were safe. He said that about four times. He said you were the safest person in the world." She turned the mug round on the table. "He said he thinks the man's in love with you. Already. As bad as you are. He said it goes both ways, whatever that means." She looked at Leah. "He said you looked happy."

Leah didn't say anything.

"I haven't seen you look happy," said her mother, "since you were seventeen."

"Mom—"

"I'm not saying anything. I'm just saying it." Sue drank her coffee. "Billy says there's an Englishman who eats at the diner every day. Doreen told Marion and Marion told Billy's cousin. Big, Doreen says. Dark hair. Takes pictures of moss. Tips twenty on a ten-dollar check and asks for extra pie. Doreen's half in love with him and she's sixty." She set the mug down. "Is that him?"

"Probably," said Leah.

"Hm," said Sue.

They sat there. The woodstove ticked. Out the window, way down the hill past the last of the houses, she could hear the ocean coming in on First Beach, slow and heavy, the way it came in at night.

"What's his name?" said Sue.

Leah had known it was coming. She'd known since the lawn. She'd thought about it the whole way home, twenty miles through the dark, how she'd say it, whether she'd lie, whether she'd say *Evans*, which was what it said on his passport, and was a name she could say without anybody's hand on her throat. She'd decided she'd say *Evans*. It would be easier. It wouldn't be a lie, exactly. It was a name he used.

She opened her mouth to say it.

"Harry," she said.

Her mother went still.

She didn't drop anything. She didn't make a sound. She was holding her coffee in both hands halfway to her mouth, and she just stopped there, with the mug in the air, and sat like that, the way the vampire on the ridge had hung in the snare: everything held, and only the eyes moving. They went to the photo on the fridge. Then they came back to Leah.

Then, very slowly, very carefully, the way she set things down when she was trying not to say something, Sue Clearwater put her mug on the table.

"Harry," she said.

"Yeah."

"Harry what?"

"Just Harry," said Leah. "I can't—there's more. I can't say it. There's a thing. It won't let me."

Sue looked at her for a long time.

"Your father," she said finally, "used to say that he only ever got one thing right in his whole life, and that was the night he walked up to me at the Makah Days dance in 1981 and told me my brother had already said yes." She smiled, very slightly. It was barely there. "My brother had not said yes. My brother had never even met him. He just walked up and said it like it was true, and I believed him for about three dances, and then I found out, and I was so angry I made him drive me home." She turned the mug a quarter-turn on the table. "And then I married him."

Leah didn't know what to say. She'd never heard that story. She'd heard every other story about her dad a thousand times, the salmon, the boat, the time he'd fallen in the river trying to land a steelhead with his bare hands. Not that one.

"He'd have hated this one," said Sue. "Probably. An Englishman. Your father didn't trust anybody from further away than Port Angeles." She looked at the photo again. "Or he'd have loved him. One or the other. There was never a middle with your father."

"Mom."

"It's a name," said Sue. "It's just a name, Leah. There's thousands of them." She picked her mug back up. Her hand was steady. "But it's a good one."

Leah looked down at her coffee.

She found she couldn't see it very well. She blinked, and it came back.

"Is he good to you?" said Sue.

Leah thought about it. She thought about the ridge, and the rock, and her face in the moss. She thought about a kitchen with a blue fridge in it, and a man at the range in his shirtsleeves, and a voice in her head out of nowhere saying *don't*, very quiet, when the cold hand came down on her throat in the woods.

"He made me eggs," she said.

Sue looked at her.

Then her mother laughed. It came out of her short and surprised, like a bark, and she put her hand over her mouth like she was trying to put it back, the exact way Leah did, the exact way Leah had in the Cullens' living room two hours ago with the kid's hand on her face. They looked at each other across the kitchen table with their hands over their mouths.

"Well," said Sue, behind her hand. "That's a start."

---

She went up to bed at half past ten.

She showered first, as hot as the water went, which in that house wasn't very, and stood under it until it went cold and then a little after that, with her forehead against the tile, and let it get the mud and the pine needles and the day off her. Then she put on a T-shirt of Seth's that was too big even for her and a pair of shorts and got into bed in her room at the back of the house, upstairs, under the slope of the roof, with the window that looked out over the yard and the trees behind it.

She lay on her back and looked at the ceiling.

The line was there. Of course it was. It went out of the middle of her chest, out through the wall, through the trees, away north-east across the dark, twenty miles, to a little green tent on a bench above a river. She could feel it the way you can feel your own arm in the dark.

He was sitting down. Somewhere dry and close. There was a fire near him; she could feel the heat of it on one side of him. He was writing. She could feel the scratch of a pen in his hand, slow and steady, the way you can feel somebody tapping a pencil on a desk in the next room through the floor. She could feel him concentrating. Every now and then he stopped, and she felt him stop, and felt him think, and once, about twenty minutes after she lay down, she felt something come off him that was nearly like pain, and then the pen went across the page in a long hard line, twice, and he went on.

She didn't go into his head. She didn't want to. It wasn't hers.

She lay there and felt him write, and after a while, the strangest thing, she found she was listening to it the way you'd listen to rain on a roof.

At eleven, the scratching stopped.

She felt him get up. She felt him do things, small things, walking about, a door, water running. She felt him lie down, somewhere narrow. Then, for a while, nothing. Just the feel of him lying there, twenty miles off, on his back, the same as her.

He wasn't asleep. She could tell.

Neither was she.

---

At half past eleven she turned on her side.

At ten to twelve she turned on her other side.

At midnight she lay on her back again and stared at the ceiling and told herself, very clearly, in words, that she'd run sixty miles today and been flattened onto a rock by magic and gone twelve rounds with a houseful of vampires, and she was going to *sleep*.

Her body didn't listen. Her body was lying in bed in a T-shirt too big for her with every muscle in it humming like a wire.

It was the line. She knew it was the line. She'd felt it in the others, she'd felt it in all of them; she'd felt Jacob not be able to sleep his first week, lying on his bed at Billy's three miles from the Cullen house, with the line from his chest to the baby's crib pulling at him all night like a hook. He'd ended up sleeping on the Cullens' couch by the end of that week. He'd said it was the only way he could stand it.

She'd thought, at the time, that he was being dramatic.

It didn't hurt, exactly. It felt more like being hungry. Like having missed a meal you didn't know you'd been going to eat. The line went out of her into the dark and it was *long*, twenty miles of it, stretched out across the whole black forest and every river in it, and the length of it was the thing she couldn't stop feeling. Every inch of it. She could feel it the way you can feel a rubber band stretched between your fingers, the pull on both ends, the thinness of it in the middle.

And at the bottom of her, in the dark, her wolf was awake.

It had been awake since the bench. She'd felt it when she lay down, curled up with its head on its paws in the place under her ribs where it always lay, and its eyes open. Now it was up. It was walking. Up and down, up and down, the way a dog walks the fence of a yard when there's something on the other side of it. Every time it got to one end, it stopped, and turned its head north-east, and stood there. Then it turned round and walked back.

"Stop it," Leah said to it, out loud, in the dark.

It didn't stop.

She lay there and felt him lie there.

He'd turned over too. She'd felt him do it, at about the same time she had. Onto his side. Then the other. Then his back. He was doing exactly what she was doing, twenty miles away, in a narrow bed in a little room in a tent that was bigger on the inside, and she could feel him doing it, and she could feel that he could feel her doing it, and that made it so much worse.

At twenty past twelve she put her arm over her eyes and thought, at the line, as clearly and crossly as she could: *Go to sleep.*

There was a pause.

Then, faint, dry, from the far end, so clear it might as well have been said into her ear in that rough London voice:

*You first.*

She took her arm off her eyes.

She lay there in the dark and felt her mouth come up at one corner, and she couldn't stop it, and after a second she stopped trying.

*Jerk,* she thought.

Nothing came back. But she felt something on the other end of the line that felt exactly like a man lying on his back in the dark with his arm over his own eyes, grinning.

---

It was about ten to one when it changed.

She'd half dozed, finally, into that grey place before sleep, where you don't know if you're thinking or dreaming. She'd been somewhere on a beach in her head, in the fog, looking for something she'd dropped, and then something went through the line like a pulse of current, and she was wide awake on her back in the dark with her heart going.

He'd got up.

She felt him do it. She felt him sit up in his narrow bed and swing his feet down onto a cold floor and sit there on the edge of it with his elbows on his knees. She felt him think. It came down the line to her as a pressure, like weather building. He was deciding something. Turning it over. She could feel him turning it, the way she'd felt him turn things over in the round room, one side and then the other.

She lay very still.

Then she felt him stand up.

She felt him walk somewhere. She felt him put on clothes. Jeans, she thought. Something heavy, a jacket. Boots. It was such a small thing to feel and she felt every bit of it, the weight of the boots, the stiff cold canvas of the jacket going on over his shoulders, and something about it made her sit up in bed with the blanket in her fists.

She felt him go down a long hall. She felt cold air. Outside. Rain on his face again, lighter now, almost nothing. The river loud below him.

And then she felt him stand there, on the bench above the river, in the dark, with his hands open at his sides, and she felt him put his hand flat on his own chest, right over where the line went in.

And open the locks.

---

She'd never felt anything like it.

She'd felt Seth phase. She'd felt Jacob phase. She'd felt a hundred phases, every one of them, in the pack mind; she knew what it felt like from the inside when another wolf came tearing up out of a person and into the world. This one was happening at the other end of the line, in him, outside the pack mind altogether, and the line was the only way she could feel it, and the line went *mad*.

It went thick. That was the first thing. It had been a rope all day, a rope as thick as her wrist, humming. It went as thick as her arm, her leg, in about a second, and the hum turned into a roar. She felt the first lock open. She felt the second. She felt the third, the bent one, the one with the dent in it, and that one *gave*, all at once, like a rotten gate.

And she felt the thing come up.

Up out of the dark at the bottom of him. Up through where the locks had been. Up the line, the whole huge black weight of it, and out of him into the night. She felt it stand on four feet in the wet moss outside a little green tent. She felt it shake. She felt it lift its head and breathe in, one long breath, all the way down, the first breath it had had of outside air in two months, the cold and the spruce and the river and the rain.

And then she felt it turn its head, and look down the line, south-west, twenty miles, straight at her.

At the bottom of her, her wolf stopped walking.

It stood there in the middle of her with its legs braced and every hair on it standing up, and its ears forward, so far forward they hurt.

Then she felt the thing at the other end of the line *move*.

It went. It went off the bench and down into the dark, fast, impossibly fast, and the line went short. She felt it go short. Twenty miles. Nineteen. Eighteen. Like somebody reeling it in. She felt the river go past it, the north fork, and the big split boulder in the middle like a broken heart. She felt the hemlocks go by in a blur. She felt it come down through the second-growth and across the logging spur and down to the Calawah main stem and *over* it. She felt it go from bank to bank in one leap, and land on the far side without a sound.

That was the strangest part. No sound. She was used to wolves. She knew what a wolf sounded like going through the woods at night, the crash and the thud and the branches. She'd heard herself, Seth, Jacob, Sam, every one of them, a hundred times. She could feel this one going through the forest at a speed that should have been tearing the trees up, and through the line it was *silent*. Like a cat on a carpet. Like smoke.

Fifteen miles. Twelve.

She was out of bed. She didn't remember getting out of bed. She was standing in the middle of her room in Seth's T-shirt with her hands shaking and the line roaring in her chest, getting shorter and shorter and shorter.

Ten. Eight. Five.

She felt it come down out of the hills onto the flat land above the river mouth, where the forest ran out toward the reservation. She felt it cross the road. She felt it slow.

Three. Two.

One.

She felt it come into the trees behind the house.

It stopped.

The line between them, which had been twenty miles long, was about forty yards.

And it hummed. Low and deep and steady, a sound she felt in her back teeth. The roar had gone out of it. It just hummed, all forty yards of it, out of her chest, out through the wall of her room, down across the dark wet yard to the edge of the trees, where something had stopped moving and sat down.

She stood there.

At the bottom of her, her wolf stood there too, very still, with its nose pointed at the window.

Leah walked across the room. She didn't feel the floor under her feet. She stood at the window, at the cold glass, with the ceiling sloping down over her head, and she looked out.

The yard was black. The trees behind it were blacker. There was no moon, just the faint grey of cloud lit from somewhere far away, Forks, or the lights on the docks at the river mouth. She couldn't see anything at all.

Then she could.

Down at the edge of the trees, at the bottom of the yard, where she and Seth had always gone to phase, where she'd stood on the night her father died and screamed and screamed and then come apart into something grey and four-legged and terrified, there were two lights.

Green.

Two eyes. About the height of the porch roof off the ground. Higher. Just sitting there in the black under the trees, perfectly still, not blinking.

Looking up at her window.

### VIII. Harry

*The tent — Tuesday, November 21st, 12:48 a.m.*

He'd been lying on his back for an hour and forty-eight minutes, by the clock on the shelf, and he was fairly sure he'd been awake for all of it.

The room was small, which he liked. A narrow iron bed, made tight, the way the army made you make them, though he'd never been in the army and had picked it up from Fennick in Belfast along with the rifles. A shelf with a clock and a lamp and a paperback. A chair with his clothes over the back of it. A hook with the waxed jacket. That was all. It was the only room in the tent that was the size it looked from the outside, more or less, and he'd done that on purpose. Some nights it helped to sleep somewhere you could touch both walls.

Tonight it wasn't helping.

He lay there in a T-shirt and boxers with his arm over his eyes and felt her lie there.

He'd felt her go up to bed at half ten. Felt her stand under a shower that ran out of hot water in about four minutes and stay under it anyway. Felt her get into a bed somewhere at the top of a house, under a slope of roof, close to a window, with the sea a long way down the hill. He hadn't gone into her head. He'd been very careful about that all evening, since the run. He'd just felt it, the shape of it, the way you'd feel somebody moving about upstairs through the ceiling. And he'd sat at the desk in the library writing *WITHHELD* in a report to the Department of Mysteries, and the whole time, under the pen and the fire and the paper, he'd been listening to her through the ceiling.

Then he'd gone to bed. So had she. And neither of them had slept.

At twenty past twelve, out of nowhere, she'd said *go to sleep* down the line at him, crossly, as clear as if she'd been standing in the doorway.

He'd said *you first* before he'd thought about it.

He'd felt her mouth come up at one corner, twenty miles away, in the dark. He'd felt her think *jerk*. He'd lain there with his arm over his eyes grinning at the ceiling like an idiot for about five minutes.

That had been half an hour ago. He'd stopped grinning.

The trouble was the line. He'd been trying to think about it the way he'd think about anything, cold and clean, an effect with a mechanism, and he kept running into the same thing. It was long. Twenty miles of it, out of the middle of his chest, through the canvas and the charms and the trees and every river between here and the sea. He could feel the whole length of it. He could feel it the way you can feel a muscle you've been holding tensed for too long, a low steady ache, *there* all the time, getting worse the more you notice it.

And he couldn't stop noticing it. That was the other trouble.

Down at the bottom of him, the thing behind the locks was walking.

It had been doing it since about eleven. Up and down, up and down. He could feel its paws on the floor of him. Every time it got to one end, it stopped, and turned its head south-west, toward the sea, toward a house at the top of a hill with a woman in it lying on her back in the dark, and it stood there. Then it turned round and walked back, quietly, the locks untouched. Up and down. Like a dog walking the fence of a yard at night when there's something on the other side of it.

He'd told it to lie down three times. It had lain down three times. Each time it had got up again in about a minute.

He took his arm off his eyes and looked at the ceiling.

*I'm a bit afraid of what happens if I let it out within half a mile of you right now,* he'd said to her, in front of the tent, at about half past one in the afternoon. *So I'd like to not do that today, if that's all right with you.*

He looked at the clock on the shelf. It said twelve fifty.

"Well," he said to the ceiling. "Technically."

The thing at the bottom of him stopped walking.

---

He sat up and swung his feet down onto the cold floor and sat there on the edge of the bed with his elbows on his knees, and made himself think about it.

He hadn't let it out since September. That was the first thing. Two months and a bit. Before that, before this job, he'd let it out every few days, a long run on the Downs or the moors or in whatever empty country was nearest wherever he was posted, because it didn't do well cooped up and neither did he. Then he'd come to a coast full of shapeshifters with noses that could pick out a vampire at three miles, and he'd locked it down tight and kept it down, and it had lain at the bottom of him for sixty-six days with its chin on its paws and its eyes open, angry the way an old dog is angry, quietly.

Today it had stood up. It had hit the locks. The third one had a dent in it now he could feel with his eyes shut.

Two months in the cellar, and then a day like this. He didn't know what it would do when it got out. He'd always known before. He'd always been it, when it was out; there'd never been any gap between them, it had his head and he had its body and they went where they both wanted. But it had never wanted anything this badly before. He'd never felt it want anything the way it wanted the far end of that line.

That was the second thing.

The third thing was her wolf. He'd felt it all night, too. Faint, through her, the grey lean thing at the bottom of her that had looked up the line at him on the ridge and wagged its tail. It was walking as well. He'd felt it start about the same time his had. Up and down. Up and down. Turning its head north-east at the end of every length.

The two of them were walking the same fence from opposite sides.

He sat there on the edge of the bed for a while, thinking about that.

Then he thought about eleven Unspeakables standing in front of an arch at four o'clock on a Tuesday afternoon, listening to the voices. And Croaker in his little room, three years, quite sure.

"Right," said Harry, and got up.

---

He got dressed. Properly. He wasn't doing this in his pants.

Jeans. The grey flannel shirt off the back of the chair. Socks, boots, laced. The shoulder rig, out of habit, and the Colt in it; he didn't go anywhere without it and he wasn't starting tonight. The iron ring on his left hand. The 1971 penny in his front pocket. The waxed jacket off its hook, last. Whatever he went out in, he'd come back in; that was how it worked for him, and he'd been grateful for it every single time for years. There was nothing quite like coming back to yourself on a hillside in Wales in February with your trousers still on.

He went down the hall in the dark. He didn't put the lights on. At the end, by the flap, he stopped, and looked back down the long slate hallway at all the doors. The kitchen. The library, with the fire banked and the report on the desk. The workshop. The black door at the back with a vampire behind it in a silver ring, very quiet, waiting.

"Back later," he said to the dark, the way he'd said it to the black door at half six.

He went out.

---

It had nearly stopped raining. It was coming down so fine now it was hardly rain at all, more a wet weight in the air, beading on the jacket. The river was loud below the bench. Gary was on the flap. Harry didn't say anything to him.

He walked out into the middle of the bench, on the moss, under the big spruces. He stood there with his hands open at his sides.

Then he put his right hand flat on his chest. Over the place where the line went in.

He opened the locks.

He'd thought about how to do it, lying in bed. He'd thought he'd do it slowly. One at a time, the way he'd built them, braided out of his own Occlumency over a fortnight in September, carefully, layer by layer. He'd ease the first one, and let the thing behind it get used to the air, and then the second.

He opened the first one, and the second one went with it.

The third one, the bent one, didn't wait to be asked. He felt it give. Felt it go all at once, like a gate with the hinges rusted through, and the thing behind it came up out of the bottom of him like a diver coming up out of deep water, fast, straight up, the whole black weight of it, and he didn't try to stop it. He let it come. He let go.

The change was the same as it always was. That surprised him, a little, after two months. He'd half expected it to be different. Same as ever: a long hard pull through every bone in him at once, a stretch like waking up and pushing your arms over your head, except it went on, and on, and he went with it. The ground went away and came back. The world went loud.

He was standing on four feet on the moss.

He stood there and breathed.

It came into him all at once, the way it always did, and after two months it nearly knocked him over. Everything. The spruce, and the river, and the moss under him, and the rain in the air. The slug on the flap behind him, a faint clean slick smell like cut cucumber. A vole somewhere under the roots of the nearest tree, very frightened, holding still. The cold, ancient, sugary stink of the thing in the circle at the back of the tent, coming through the canvas and the charms, faint, like a smell from a sealed jar. Elk, a mile up the valley, three of them, bedded down. Woodsmoke from somewhere a long way off. The ocean, twenty miles west, so faint it was hardly there, a cold salt edge on the wind.

And her. On the wind, too, he thought, though it was too far, though there was no way. He'd have sworn it. Pine needles and river water and something like the air before a storm. And under it a smell he hadn't had in his nose since one o'clock that afternoon, standing on a granite slab in the rain with her ten feet in front of him and her lips off her teeth.

He shook himself, nose to tail, once, hard. The rain came off his coat in a cloud.

He looked down at himself, out of habit. He always did, after a long time.

Black. All of it, black, down to the paws, the way it had always been, a dense black outer coat with the wet sitting on top of it in beads and, under that, a thick charcoal undercoat he could feel the cold not getting through. Long in the leg. Deep in the chest. His forepaws on the moss were the size of dinner plates, and he flexed them, and the claws came out of the pads into the soft wet ground, long and pale and curved, and he drew them back in again until they were gone.

He was taller than the lowest branches of the spruce. He'd forgotten that. He'd been walking under those branches for two months without having to duck, and now the nearest one was level with his ear, and the needles brushed the top of his head when he lifted it.

He knew roughly how big he was, in the way you know your own shoe size. He'd never had anything to compare it to on this coast. Through the Omnioculars in October, the big russet one with the child on its back had looked to be about the size of a draught horse, and the sandy one and the grey one a little smaller. He'd guessed, from that, that he was bigger than any of them. He hadn't known by how much.

He didn't care, much. It was a fact. He filed it.

Then he lifted his head and looked south-west.

The line went away from him into the dark. It was so clear now he could almost see it, a dull red thread going off between the trees and down the slope and over the river and away. Thick as a cable. Humming. And the far end of it was sitting up in bed.

He felt her sit up. He felt her hands close on the blanket.

He went.

---

He went the way he always went when he was like this, which was quietly.

That had taken him years. When it first started, he'd gone through woods like a lorry through a hedge. He'd learned, slowly, the way you learn anything, a bit at a time and mostly by getting it wrong; the old woman in the Rwenzori had laughed at him for that too, the first time she'd seen it, and then she'd taken him out at night for six weeks onto the slopes above the camp and made him walk past sleeping leopards until he could do it without waking them. Hind paws in the prints of the front. Toes down first. Weight poured on slow, never dropped. Every step placed, the ground under it felt before it took him. And the rest of it, the bit nobody had taught him, the bit he'd worked out himself: a thin film of magic under each pad, laid down and taken up again a hundred times a second, so the moss didn't crush and the twigs didn't snap and the earth didn't take the weight all at once and pass it on.

He went down off the bench into the dark at a long, flat, loping run, and the only sound he made was his breathing.

The north fork. The split boulder in the middle like a broken heart; he knew it because she'd known it, because he'd felt her go past it at half six. He went over the river in one jump and came down on the far bank on all four paws, and the gravel didn't move.

The second-growth. Hemlocks packed close as matches. He went through it like water through a comb. The logging spur, mud and ruts. The long slope down to the Calawah main stem, wide and grey and loud in the dark. Too wide to clear in one. He cleared it in one anyway, and felt the lattice twitch faintly as he went over, the way it always twitched when he crossed water, a fish turning in a net. He didn't look at it. He didn't need to.

Except that, coming up the far bank, he did.

He brought the lattice up under his mind for a second, without stopping, the way you'd glance at a map on the seat beside you while you drove. Gold, at the white house east of town: seven, and the small bright gold-and-red coal of the child. Rust: three. One in the white house, one moving slowly on the northern loop by itself, one stationary in town, on Calawah Way, outside a blue house where the lattice bent round something old and alive.

And, further west, nearer the coast, where he was going: three more rust dots. Moving. Two of them together, slow, on a long loop through the trees north of the river mouth. One on its own at the edge of the reservation, near the road.

The other pack. The one she'd left. He knew whose it was. He'd seen its Alpha in the boy's head on the ridge, a big black wolf in the dark at the edge of a lawn, ten weeks ago, with his ears flat.

He altered course.

He went north first, then west, then south again, in a long curve that put the wind in his face the whole way and the patrol on the far side of it. He didn't want a fight tonight. He didn't want anybody to know he'd been here. He'd have the rest of his life, it looked like, for the wolves of La Push to find out what he was; tonight he wanted one wolf, and only one, to know.

He crossed the La Push road a mile east of the reservation, at a place where the trees came down to the tarmac on both sides, in one long low bound that didn't touch the road at all. Then he was in the trees north of the river mouth, and the sea was loud now, really loud, close, the long heavy boom of it coming in over the rocks, and the air was all salt.

He slowed.

The line was short. A mile. Less. He could feel her at the far end of it, standing now, in the middle of a room, in the dark, shaking. He could feel her heart. It was going as fast as his.

He came down through the last of the trees above a scatter of small houses, all dark, and stopped at the edge of a yard.

It was a small house. Two storeys, the top one tucked in under the roof. Peeling paint, a colour he couldn't make out in the dark. A porch at the back with a woodpile. A yard of wet grass going down from the trees to the back step, with a washing line across it and an old bucket by the steps. A window upstairs, at the back, under the slope of the roof, looking out over the yard.

The line went straight up to it.

He stood at the edge of the trees.

He could smell her. That was the first thing. All of her. Close now, everywhere, in the grass where she'd walked a thousand times and the trees where she'd phased a thousand times and the back step where she'd sat. He could smell her brother. He could smell the mother, coffee and woodsmoke and something like sage. And, under all of it, faint and old and nearly gone, the smell of a man. Fish, and engine oil, and coffee, soaked into the boards of the back step the way smells soak into wood that somebody has sat on every evening for twenty years. Somebody had sat there a lot. Somebody hadn't sat there for months.

He stood there and looked at the step for a while.

Then he sat down. Right there, on his haunches in the wet grass at the edge of the trees, where the yard began. He folded his tail round his forepaws.

He looked up at the window.

He couldn't see her. The glass was black. He didn't need to see her. He knew exactly where she was, three feet back from it, in the middle of the room, standing very still.

He felt her come to the window.

He felt her look out.

He sat there in the dark at the bottom of the garden, and looked up at her, and didn't move, and waited to see what she'd do.

### IX. Leah

*The Clearwater house — 1:11 a.m.*

She stood at the window with her hand flat on the cold glass and looked down at the two green eyes, and they looked up at her, and neither of them moved.

She didn't know how long. A minute. Less. Long enough for her breath to fog the glass round her fingers and fade and fog again. The eyes didn't blink. They sat there in the black at the bottom of the yard, at the height of the porch roof, a little higher, catching some light from somewhere that she couldn't see, two coins of green at the bottom of a well.

At the bottom of her, her wolf had both front paws up on the inside of her ribs like a dog on a gate.

She took her hand off the glass.

She went out of her room without putting the light on. Down the stairs, keeping to the wall side where they didn't creak, which she'd known since she was twelve and coming in late. Past her mother's door at the bottom, shut, with the sound of her mother's breathing behind it, slow and even, and the old clock on the dresser in there ticking. Through the dark kitchen. The woodstove was down to a red glow. The photo on the fridge was just a pale square. She didn't look at it.

She opened the back door very slowly so it didn't catch on the frame, and went out onto the step.

It was cold. She didn't feel it, much; she never did. The rain had gone to almost nothing, a fine wet hang in the air that settled on her bare arms and her face like breath. The grass was soaked under her feet. Down the hill, past the dark houses, she could hear the sea on First Beach, heavy and slow and enormous.

And at the bottom of the yard, at the edge of the trees, where she'd come apart for the first time on a night in March with her father in the house behind her, something was sitting in the dark, looking at her.

She walked down the yard toward it.

She didn't hurry. She couldn't have hurried if she'd tried; her legs had gone strange, too light and too heavy at once. She walked down through the wet grass past the washing line and the old bucket Seth used for clams, and about halfway there, she started to be able to see it.

It was black.

That was all she could see at first: a darker shape against the dark of the trees, the shape of a wolf sitting up on its haunches with its tail curled round its forepaws, the way a dog sits when it's been told to wait. Then she got closer and the shape kept going. Up. It kept going up. It was sitting, and its head was still above the lowest branches of the alders behind it. It was sitting, and she was standing, and she was going to have to tip her head back to see its face.

She stopped about six feet from it.

She tipped her head back.

It looked down at her.

It was the biggest wolf she had ever seen. She knew wolves. She was one. She'd spent eight months running with the biggest wolves on the coast, every one of them, Sam and Paul and Jared and Jacob, she knew the size of every one of them better than she knew her own hands; she knew exactly where Jacob's shoulder came to on a tree and exactly how high his head was when he stood up straight and looked down his nose at you. She knew Jacob was bigger than Sam now, since the summer, since the baby, a whole hand taller at the shoulder, and that he was the biggest wolf the tribe had ever had, and that he knew it.

If Jacob had been sitting where this one was sitting, his ears would have come to about this one's jaw.

It was black all over. A deep flat black, like the bottom of a well, like the space between stars. The fine rain had settled on it in beads that caught the faint grey light and made it look like it had been dusted with something. Under the black, where the coat parted on its chest as it breathed, there was a darker grey, thick and soft, the colour of charcoal. Its chest was deep. Its legs were long. The front paws, planted in the wet grass, were wider than her two hands spread side by side. It had big ears, up and forward, pointed at her. A long narrow muzzle. And the eyes.

The eyes were his.

She'd seen them on the ridge, behind the dark glasses and then without them, green as a bottle held up to the sun. She'd looked into them for one second and lost everything else she had. They were the same eyes. They were exactly, precisely the same, set in a wolf's face about ten feet off the ground, and they were looking at her.

It smelled like him. It smelled like everything he'd smelled like, on the ridge, under the coffee and the cheeseburger and the pie, the stuff she'd caught for one second and then lost. Earth, turned over after rain. Pine. Cold stone, like the inside of a cave. All of it, a hundred times stronger. Nothing else. No soap. No coffee. Nothing human at all.

And she hadn't heard it.

That was what she kept coming back to, standing there. She'd felt it come twenty miles through the forest in about twenty minutes, she'd felt it come down the hill and across the road and through the trees behind her house, and she had the best ears of anybody in either pack and she'd been standing at the window listening for it with everything she had, and she hadn't heard a single thing. Not a branch. Not a footfall. It had come out of the trees and sat down in the grass forty yards from her bed, a thing the size of a truck, and the grass under it barely looked bent.

Her wolf was going insane. She could feel it. It was trying to climb out of her through her ribs.

The black wolf lowered its head.

It did it slowly. It brought its head down out of the dark, down and down, until its face was level with hers, about three feet away, and she could feel its breath on her. Hot. It smelled of nothing but cold air and the forest. Its eyes were on hers, and very steady.

Then it opened its mouth, and spoke.

"It's Tuesday," it said.

---

She stared at it.

It had his voice. She hadn't known it would talk at all. None of them talked. Jacob couldn't, Sam couldn't, she couldn't; you tried, the first week, everybody did, and what came out was a bark or a whine or a horrible wet growl, because a wolf's mouth wasn't built for it. But this one had opened its jaws and said *it's Tuesday* in his voice, low and rough and London, with a rumble under it like a truck idling, and every word of it came out clear. The *T*, the *s*. She'd heard every letter.

"*What?*" said Leah.

"It's Tuesday," the black wolf said again. Its ears flicked. "I said not today. This afternoon. Outside the tent. *Not today, sorry, not today*." It tipped its huge head a little to one side. "It's ten past one. It's Tuesday. I checked the clock."

Leah stood in the wet grass in Seth's T-shirt and looked at the biggest wolf she'd ever seen in her life, sitting at the bottom of her mother's yard at one in the morning, explaining to her in an English accent that technically it was a different day.

She laughed.

It came out of her too loud. She clapped both hands over her mouth and looked back at the house, at her mother's dark window. Nothing moved. She turned back round with her hands still over her mouth and her eyes streaming.

"You're an *idiot*," she said, through her fingers.

"I've been told."

"You—you ran twenty miles. In the middle of the night. To sit in my yard. To tell me it's *Tuesday*."

"I couldn't sleep," said the black wolf. "Neither could you. I could feel you not sleeping. It was driving me spare." Its ears went back a little, and then up again. "And this one's been walking up and down the inside of me since eleven o'clock like a dog in a kennel. I told it to lie down three times. It wasn't having it." It paused. "Turns out yours was doing the same."

She took her hands down from her mouth.

"You could feel that?"

"Couldn't miss it. Up and down. Up and down. Turning round at the end every time to look at me." The green eyes didn't move off hers. "Two of them walking the same fence from opposite sides. Seemed a bit daft to keep it up."

Leah didn't say anything.

She was looking at his face. At the wolf's face, with his eyes in it. She could see him in there. She didn't know how. It was a wolf's face, long and black and wild, nothing like a man's at all, and she could see him in it as clearly as if he'd been standing there in his waxed jacket with his hands in his pockets: the dry, sideways, careful look; the tiredness; and under it the thing she'd felt on the ridge when his face had gone white, the thing that had come down the line at her all afternoon and all evening and all night, and that neither of them had a name for yet.

"You're bigger than Jake," she said.

"Am I?" The ears went forward. "I wondered. I'd only seen him through binoculars."

"He's going to *hate* that."

"Is he." It sounded pleased.

"He's the biggest wolf the tribe's ever had. He grew a whole hand this summer. He measures himself on a tree behind the Cullens' house. He thinks nobody knows."

"Do they?"

"Everybody knows."

The black wolf made a sound low in its chest. It took her a second to work out it was laughing.

"Your mum's asleep," it said. "First room at the bottom of the stairs. I can hear her clock." It tipped its head toward the trees. "Come out where it's dark?"

She looked at it.

At the bottom of her, her wolf stopped trying to climb out through her ribs, and stood very still, and waited.

"Turn round," said Leah.

The black wolf looked at her for a second longer. Then it got up, all of it, unfolding out of the grass in one long movement until she was looking at its chest, and its head was up somewhere in the alder branches. It turned round, slowly, the way you'd turn round in a small room, and stood with its back to her, facing into the trees, its great black tail low.

She pulled Seth's T-shirt off over her head and folded it, and put it on top of the clam bucket so it wouldn't get wet. Then she put the shorts on top of it. Then she stood in her mother's yard in the dark with nothing on and the cold wet air all over her skin and a wolf the size of a house standing with its back politely turned ten feet away, and let go.

---

It hit her like coming up for air.

That was the first thing. She'd phased a thousand times. She knew how it went: the tearing, the world going small and loud, the stretch through every bone. All of that happened. And then it was over, and she was standing on four feet in the wet grass, and the night came into her through her nose and her ears and her fur all at once, and for the first time since the spring, since the very first time, it felt like coming *home*. Like she'd been holding her breath since March and had just let it out.

Her wolf was right there. Right at the front. It hadn't waited for her.

The pack mind came up around her and it was empty. Jacob had phased out sometime in the last hour; she could feel the cold place where he'd been, like a dent in a pillow. Seth would be in town, human, probably asleep on a couch in a blue house with bees in the yard. Nobody else. Just her, for once. Just her and the dark and the smell of earth and pine and cold stone so thick in her nose that she could hardly smell anything else.

The black wolf turned round.

She'd thought she'd seen how big it was. She'd been wrong. She'd been looking up at it from a girl's height, and a girl's eyes didn't know what they were looking at. Now she was looking at it from her own height, a wolf's height, the height of a man's head at the shoulder, about Seth's size, and she could see it properly. It stood over her. Its shoulder was level with the top of her head. Its head was higher than that, up in the dark, looking down at her. If she stood up straight and lifted her nose as high as it went, she could just about have touched its jaw.

She'd never in her life stood next to a wolf and felt small.

She didn't feel small. That was the strange thing. She looked up at it and every hair on her body stood up, and she felt it look down at her, and she didn't feel small at all.

Her wolf went straight at it.

She didn't stop it. She couldn't have. She didn't want to. It went across the ten feet of wet grass between them in two strides with its head low and its tail high, and stopped about a foot from the black wolf's chest, and stretched its neck out, and put its nose up.

The black wolf brought its head down to meet it.

They touched noses.

She felt it go through the line like a bell struck. All the way down. Into her, and into him, and down into the two of them at the bottom, the grey and the black, and they *knew* each other, in some way older than the pack mind, older than knowing Jacob or Seth from inside their heads; some way from before there were heads to be inside of. She breathed in, and it was him, all of him; she breathed out, and she felt him breathe her in.

He was hot. Hotter than her. She could feel it coming off his face onto hers.

Neither of them moved for a long time.

Then the black wolf moved his head, slowly, down along the side of her face. Along her jaw. Down her neck to her shoulder, breathing her in the whole way, slow and deep, the way you'd breathe in somebody's hair. She felt his breath go through her fur to the skin underneath. She stood very still and let him. When he got to her shoulder he stopped and stayed there with his nose pressed in against her, just breathing.

Then she did it back.

She went along his jaw. She had to reach up for it. Down the side of his huge neck, into the thick black ruff there, where the outer coat was cold and wet on top and the charcoal undercoat underneath was hot as a stove. She pushed her face into it. She breathed. Earth, pine, cold stone. Him. Him. *Him*. Under all of that, very faint, the last thin ghost of the other smells, coffee and gun oil and a dead vampire's sugar stink from the back of his tent, and she didn't care about any of them.

Her tail was going. She could feel it going. She'd never felt it go like that in her life.

His was too. Slow. Back and forth, low behind him in the grass.

She took her face out of his ruff and looked up at him. And then, because her wolf wanted to, because there was nobody to see, because it was one in the morning and she was standing in her mother's yard with the biggest wolf in the world and nobody had ever in her whole life let her be anything except angry, she shoved him.

She put her shoulder into his chest, hard, the way Seth had head-butted her in the stomach when he was six. The way they used to play, as wolves, the first few weeks, before everything.

It was like shoving a wall. He didn't move at all.

He looked down at her.

Then he dropped.

He went down at the front, all at once, forelegs flat out on the grass in front of him and his chest right down on the ground and his back end up in the air with his tail going, his head low, his ears forward, his eyes on her. A play bow. The biggest wolf she'd ever seen in her life was in a play bow at the bottom of her mother's yard at one in the morning, like a puppy, like a dog in a park, asking her to come and get him.

She went for him.

He was gone. He wasn't there anymore. One second he was on the grass in front of her and the next he'd sprung sideways, ten feet, without a sound, and come down light as a cat at the edge of the trees, and turned round, and dropped into the bow again with his tongue out.

She went for him again. Same thing.

She stood there with her sides going and her ears flat and glared at him.

*Cheater,* she thought, as hard as she could.

"Can't help it," he said aloud. Low. The words came out clean, with the rumble threaded under them. "Practice."

*You're huge. You're huge and you're* fast*. That's not fair.*

"Didn't say it was."

She went for him a third time, and this time she didn't go at him; she went past him, into the trees, flat out, the way she ran when she really ran, and she felt him come after her.

---

They went down to the sea.

She didn't decide to. She didn't decide anything. She ran, and the ground took her where she always went, down through the trees behind the houses and across the road, empty and black and shining, and down the bank past the last houses on the bluff and out onto First Beach. The big sea stacks stood out in the surf like black teeth. The sand was hard and wet and grey and went on forever. The sea came in white out of the dark, roaring.

She hit the sand at full stretch and kept going.

She was fast. She was the fastest of all of them. She'd always been the fastest; it was the one thing she had that nobody could take, the one thing the wolf had given her that she'd ever been glad of. Faster than Jacob. Faster than Sam, by a mile. She ran down First Beach in the dark with the wet sand flying up behind her and the surf to her right and nobody in the whole world could catch her, nobody ever had.

He came up beside her.

He showed no sign of trying. He came up on her left side, between her and the bluff, and settled in about six feet off her shoulder, and stayed there. Matching her stride for stride. Just there. A great black shape going along beside her in the dark with his ears up and his mouth open, and his paws hitting the hard wet sand, and the sand barely marking.

She went faster. She went as fast as she'd ever gone in her life.

He stayed right there.

She looked across at him, at the green eyes, and he looked back at her, and she understood, in one long slow sliding moment, that he could have gone past her any time he liked. He could have been at the far end of the beach and back before she was halfway down it. He was going to run beside her as fast as she wanted to go, for as long as she wanted to go, and not one step faster.

Everybody she'd ever run with had been somewhere behind her.

She stopped thinking about it. She just ran.

They went down the whole length of First Beach, to the rocks at the south end where the bluff came down into the sea. She went up over the rocks without stopping, up the headland, up through the salal and the wind-bent spruce on the top, and down the other side onto the trail, and down that, through the dark, to the long curve of Second Beach, and out onto it. It was wilder there. Nobody came at night. The sea stacks stood out in a line in the surf like a wall somebody had started building and walked away from, and one of them had a hole right through it, an arch, and the white water was going through the arch in the dark with a sound like breathing.

She went into the surf.

She hadn't planned that either. Her wolf just went, splashing in up to its chest, into the cold, cold water coming in, and the wave hit her and went over her head, and she came up shaking and snorting with salt in her nose and her eyes. And he was right there beside her, in the water, the next wave breaking against his chest and going round him like a rock in a river, and he put his head down and shoved his nose into the side of her neck, hard, and pushed her over.

She went under. She came up spitting.

She went for him. She went for his foreleg, the way you'd go for a pup's, and she got it, and he let her, and went over on his side in the surf in an explosion of white water, all four legs in the air, with the sea going over him, and she stood over him in the shallows with her teeth in his ruff and growled, a play growl, a pup's growl, a sound she hadn't made since the first week. And underneath her in the water the biggest wolf in the world lay on his back with the waves breaking over his chest and his mouth open and made a sound she could only describe as a laugh.

---

Afterward, she took him to the cave.

She'd found it in April. A month after she'd phased the first time. She'd run, one night, the way she always ran, as far as she could go, because Sam had been thinking about Emily in the pack mind all evening and she couldn't stand another second of it. She'd run down all the beaches one after another until she couldn't run anymore, and she'd ended up at the very south end of Second Beach, where the headland came down into the sea, Teahwhit Head, the big black wall of rock that cut the beach off from the next one. And she'd gone up it, because she didn't want to stop. And about two-thirds of the way up, where the spruce grew out sideways over the drop and the rock was all broken into ledges, she'd found a hole.

It was a cave. A real one, deep, cut back into the headland by some sea that wasn't here anymore, a long time ago. It was high above the tide line, high enough that even the winter storms didn't reach it. You had to know where to put your feet to get to it, and you had to be a wolf. The mouth of it looked straight out west, over the sea stacks and the arch, out at nothing, at the whole Pacific going away into the dark. The floor was dry sand, fine as flour, and it was always dry, even in February.

She'd lain down in it that night and cried for about three hours. As a wolf. She hadn't known wolves could cry. It turned out they had a sound for it.

She'd gone back maybe twenty times since. Always alone. Always at night. Seth knew about it; it had been in her head a hundred times. He'd never come. He knew better. Nobody else had ever been in it but her.

She stood in the mouth of it now, soaked, with sand on her legs and salt in her fur, and looked back at him.

He'd stopped on the ledge outside. He'd come up the rock behind her, all the way up, putting his huge paws exactly where she'd put hers, every step, without a sound. Now he stood out there on the ledge, with the spruce bending over him and the whole black sea behind him, and stayed there, looking at her. Waiting.

She understood that he knew. She didn't know how. He couldn't have seen it in her head; she'd felt him not looking, all night, felt him keep himself carefully on his side of the line. Maybe he could smell it. Maybe it was just the way she'd stopped. But he knew this was hers. He knew nobody had ever been in it but her, and he was standing on the ledge outside in the wet with the wind in his coat, waiting to be asked.

She went to the back of the cave and turned round three times on the dry sand and lay down.

Then she looked at him.

He came in.

He had to put his head down to get through the mouth. He came in slowly, low, the way he'd come down to her in the yard. The cave was big, but he made it small. He filled it up from the floor to the roof. He stood over her for a moment in the dark with the faint grey light from the sea behind him and looked down at her, and she looked up at him.

Then he lay down.

He lay down all round her. That was the only way to say it. He came down onto the sand with his back to the mouth of the cave, between her and the sea, between her and the wind and the rain and the dark and everything in it, and he curled. His body went round her back. His tail came round over her hind paws. His forelegs came round in front of her. And then, slowly, very carefully, as if he was asking, he brought his huge head down and laid it across her neck, his jaw resting on the far side of her shoulders, heavy and hot.

She lay there inside him.

She could feel his heart. Through her side now, through her fur and his, where his chest was against her back. It was slow. It was slow and heavy and steady, a deep double knock, like a man in a chair reading a paper. She could feel it going against her spine.

Hers slowed down to match it. It did that on its own.

The line between them, which had been twenty miles long at midnight, was about a quarter of an inch. The roar had gone out of it, and the hum. It had gone quiet, so quiet she had to look for it to feel it, the way you have to look for your own heartbeat. It just lay there between them, both ends tied, slack and easy, like a rope on a dock with the boat home.

At the bottom of her, her wolf put its head down on its paws for the first time all night, and sighed.

She felt his do the same.

For a long time, neither of them said anything. Outside, the sea came in through the arch with that sound like breathing, in, out, in, out. The rain had stopped. Somewhere a long way out, past the stacks, a buoy was ringing, very faint.

*Is it always like this?* she thought at him. Finally. Quietly. *For you. When you're—like this.*

She felt his jaw move on her shoulders.

"No," he said. Very low. She felt the word through her bones more than heard it. "Never been like this."

*What are you?*

He was quiet for a while.

"Later," he said.

She thought about that. She thought about Seth, on the ridge, telling Layla *later* about a hundred times. She thought about Layla on the Cullens' couch, laughing with her face in her hands. She found she didn't mind *later*. She found, lying in her cave at two in the morning with the biggest wolf in the world wrapped round her like a wall, that later sounded fine. Later sounded like there'd be one.

*Okay,* she thought. *Later.*

His tail moved, once, over her hind paws.

She closed her eyes.

She felt him close his.

The last thing she felt before she went under was the two of them at the bottom, the grey and the black, lying nose to nose in the dark in the place where the two ends of the line were tied off, breathing each other's breath. Done watching. Done walking. Done waiting.

Asleep.

### X. Jacob

*The Cullen house — Tuesday, 6:49 a.m.*

He woke up on the couch with Nessie asleep on his chest and her hair in his mouth, the way he woke up every morning, and lay there for a second in the grey light coming through the glass wall, with her small hot weight going up and down on him as he breathed, and thought: *Leah.*

He didn't know why. He'd been dreaming about something. A beach, maybe. A big black shape in the surf.

He eased Nessie off his chest and onto the cushions, slowly, the way he'd learned, one hand under her head. She made a sound and rolled over and put her thumb in her mouth and went on sleeping. Esme was in the kitchen; he could hear her moving around, a pan, the fridge. Rosalie was upstairs. Edward and Bella were in their cottage in the trees. The house smelled, the way it always smelled in the mornings, like bleach and flowers and coffee Esme made for him and Charlie and nobody else.

He went out the back door and across the lawn in his bare feet and into the cedars, and pulled his cut-offs off and tied them to his ankle, and phased.

He'd meant to run the dawn loop. He'd told himself last night, running the north side alone at midnight with Leah's whole crazy day still going round and round in his head, that he'd get up early and run the dawn loop himself and not ask Seth to do it. Seth had enough going on. Seth had a witch with a back like a bruised plum to look after.

The pack mind came on around him like a light in a dark room, and Leah was in it.

She was asleep.

He'd been in the pack mind with sleeping wolves before. It happened. Sometimes on a long night somebody'd curl up phased in the trees and doze off, and if you phased in while they were out, you'd get them like a radio left on low in another room: a dim hum, bits of dreams going past, a weight. That was what Leah was. A dim hum. Somewhere dry, he thought. She was lying on her side on sand. He could feel the sand under her. He could hear the sea, close, loud, booming into rock and going out again, and the air coming in salt and cold from somewhere in front of her.

And she was being held.

That was the only word for it. He felt it before he understood it. All down her back, from her neck to her tail, there was heat pressed against her, a solid wall of it, rising and falling. Something huge was curled round her from behind. There was weight across her neck and shoulders, heavy and hot, resting there. A head. There was something heavy laid over her hind legs. A tail. And against her spine, slow, deep, steady, going *thud-thud ... thud-thud*, a heartbeat that wasn't hers.

Jacob stood in the cedars behind the Cullens' house with his feet planted and his mouth open.

*LEAH,* he thought.

He hadn't meant to. It came out of him like a shout in a library.

He felt her wake up.

She came up out of sleep all at once, the way they all did, the wolf in her going from nothing to everything in half a second, and her eyes came open. And he saw through them.

Grey light. A cave. The mouth of it in front of her, ragged rock, and through it the sea, the whole Pacific going out grey and flat and endless under a grey sky, and the black sea stacks standing up out of it in a line. One of them with a hole right through it. He knew that rock. That was Second Beach. That was the arch off Second Beach. She was in a cave on the head at the south end. He'd seen that cave in her head before, a hundred times. He'd never been in it. Nobody had.

And in front of her face, lying across the sand, there was a leg.

A foreleg. Black. As black as anything he'd ever seen, a flat dead black like the inside of a closed eye. It came round in front of her from somewhere behind and lay on the sand with the paw at the end of it about a foot from her nose. The paw was huge. He was looking at it through her eyes, from about two inches off the ground, and it was the biggest paw he'd ever seen on anything. Bigger than his. He knew exactly how big his were. He'd put his paw down in the mud next to Sam's on the beach in August, just to see, and his was bigger now, and he'd been so pleased about it that he'd thought about it in the pack mind for a week and Leah had told him to grow up.

This one was bigger than his.

*Morning, Jake,* Leah thought. Thick with sleep. Not even surprised.

*Leah,* said Jacob. *Leah. Is that—*

*Yeah.*

*Is that HIM?*

*Go away, Jake.*

She didn't move. He felt her not move. She lay there on her side in the sand with the weight across her neck and the heat at her back and the sea going in and out, and she didn't even lift her head, and from inside her he could feel how much she didn't want to. She was comfortable. She was so comfortable it came through the pack mind like the smell of bread. He'd never felt Leah comfortable in his life.

Then the weight on her neck moved.

It lifted. He felt it go, slowly, the heavy hot head coming up off her shoulders, and the cold air coming in where it had been. And Leah turned her head and looked up and back, over her own shoulder, and Jacob saw it.

It was looking down at her.

A wolf's head. Black, like the leg. Long in the muzzle, big in the ears. It filled up the whole top half of her sight, up near the roof of the cave, and it was so close to her that he could see every hair round its eyes. And the eyes were green. Green like a bottle with the sun behind it. The same green he'd seen in Seth's head on the ridge yesterday, behind a pair of sunglasses, on a guy in a suit.

The green eyes looked at Leah. Then they moved, very slightly. They went a little out of focus, the way somebody's eyes go when they're listening to something in another room.

Jacob had the most horrible feeling that they were looking at *him*.

"Morning, Jacob," said the black wolf.

---

Jacob sat down.

His back legs went out from under him. He sat down in the wet ferns behind the Cullens' house with his tail under him and stared at nothing, at the inside of the pack mind, at a black wolf in a cave twelve miles away that had just opened its mouth and *talked*.

*It talks,* he thought.

"He," said the black wolf. It sounded patient. "Not *it*. And yes."

*He can hear me. Leah, he can hear me.*

*Through me,* said Leah. She still hadn't lifted her head off the sand. *He can hear everything I hear. In there.* He felt her tap at something in the back of her own head, very lightly, and he felt it too, a weird closed place at the edge of the pack mind right beside her, like a door shut tight with a light on behind it. *He's next to the pack mind. Outside it, on my side. Leaning on the wall.*

*How is he* talking*? Wolves can't talk. We can't talk. I tried for a* week*.*

"Practice," said the black wolf.

*That's not an answer!*

"It's most of one."

Jacob put his head down on his forepaws in the ferns.

He could feel Leah. He could feel her lying there, in the cave, with her head on the sand, and he could feel her being *amused*. It was so strange. It was so strange he almost didn't recognise it. It came off her in the pack mind like light off the sea, bright and sideways and mean and completely, totally happy.

*You knew,* Jacob said to her. *Last night. You knew he'd come.*

*Nope,* said Leah. *I didn't know anything. He just turned up. In the yard. At one in the morning.*

*In your* yard*? At your* mom's*?*

*He sat at the bottom of the garden and looked at my window.*

*Like a* stalker*?*

*Like a guy who couldn't sleep,* said Leah. *I couldn't either. So.*

"Leah tells me," said the black wolf, conversationally, "that you measure yourself on a tree. Behind the house."

Jacob's head came up off his paws.

*LEAH.*

*What?* said Leah. *You do.*

*That was PRIVATE.*

*Nothing's private, Jake. That's the whole point. You think about that tree every time you go past it. You think about the scratch. You've moved the scratch up twice since August.*

"Three times," said the black wolf. "She says."

*I'm going to kill you,* Jacob said to Leah.

*No,* said Leah, *you're not.*

And she got up.

---

She did it slowly. He felt her do it, felt her get her feet under her on the sand and stand, stretching, her back going long, her front legs out, her jaws opening in a yawn so big it creaked. And as she stood up, the black wolf behind her stood up too.

Jacob watched him do it through her eyes. He watched him unfold. He'd been lying curled round her and now he stood, all of him, and Leah turned round to face him in the narrow cave with her nose about a foot from his chest, and lifted her head to look at him, and Jacob saw.

He'd known Leah's height his whole life. Wolf-Leah. He knew where her eyes were. They were about where Seth's were, a little lower; she was Seth's size, long-legged and narrow, built for running, the top of her head about level with a tall man's when she stood up straight. He'd stood next to her a thousand times and looked down at her.

She was looking up at the black wolf, and her eyes were about level with his shoulder.

His *shoulder*. The black wolf's head was up above that, up in the shadow at the top of the cave, looking down at her, and down at Jacob through her, with those green eyes.

Jacob did the maths. He didn't want to do the maths. He did it anyway, the way you poke a bad tooth.

He was bigger than Seth. A lot bigger. A hand, maybe more, at the shoulder. When he stood next to Leah, she came up to about his jaw.

When she stood next to this guy, she came up to his shoulder.

*A head,* Jacob thought, with a horrible sinking feeling. *He's got a head on me. At least a head.*

*At least,* said Leah. She sounded delighted.

*You don't know that! You can't know that! It's a cave! It's got a low ceiling! It's an optical illusion!*

"I could come up to the house," the black wolf offered, "and stand next to the tree."

*NO.*

Leah laughed. Wolves didn't laugh, exactly, they didn't have the mouth for it, but there was a thing they did, a huffing snort, and she did it, right in the black wolf's chest, and Jacob felt her do it, felt how it came up out of her, and stopped being mad. He couldn't help it. He sat there in the ferns behind the Cullens' house and listened to Leah Clearwater laugh, really laugh, in the pack mind, for the first time since the spring, and every bit of being mad went out of him like air out of a tyre.

He hadn't heard her do that since before Sam.

He'd forgotten what it sounded like.

---

"Jacob," said the black wolf.

Its voice had changed. It was lower now. Quieter. The joke had gone out of it.

Jacob looked at him through Leah's eyes. He didn't say anything. He waited.

"About last night," said the black wolf. "At the house. Somebody said something in that room that frightened her. Round about twenty to eight. I felt it. I was twenty miles off on a ridge and I felt it like a slap." The green eyes didn't move. "I don't need to know who. I don't need to know what. I'd just like it not to happen again."

Jacob was quiet for a while.

He thought about Jasper by the kitchen door, with his scars and his far-off eyes. *Everyone sleeps.* He thought about Leah crossing the room before anybody saw her move, and her face a foot from Jasper's, and that growl coming up out of her. He thought about her standing in the middle of the room afterward, shaking, saying *twenty miles away it gets up*.

*It was Jasper,* Jacob said. *He didn't mean it like it sounded. He says stuff like that. He was in a war. A real one, a long time ago. He thinks about what could happen, that's all. He thinks about everything.*

"I know about Jasper," said the black wolf.

Jacob felt cold go down his back.

*How?*

The black wolf didn't answer that. He just looked at Leah, and through her at Jacob, for a long moment.

"If he didn't mean it," he said, "then we'll get on fine."

*And if he did?*

"Then we'll have a conversation," said the black wolf. "Him and me. Somewhere quiet. And after that we'll get on fine." His ears moved. "I'm told I'm invited to dinner."

Jacob blinked. *How do you know that?*

"I don't. Leah's thinking about a roast."

*Esme's making a roast,* said Leah. *Carlisle wants you to come to the house. To talk. Seven o'clock. As a guest.*

"As a guest," said the black wolf. He was quiet for a second, and Jacob could feel him turning it over, the way you could feel weather building out at sea. "Does the guest get to leave again afterward?"

*Yeah,* said Jacob. He said it before he'd thought about it. He said it, and then he thought about it, and he meant it. *Yeah. He does. That's what Carlisle meant. That's how he does things. You're under his roof, you're safe. That's the deal. Nobody touches you.*

He thought about the law, then. He couldn't help it. It came up in him the way it had come up last night, when Leah had stood in the middle of the Cullens' floor and asked it. *What was that one law.* Sam in the dark at the edge of the lawn with his ears flat, turning round and going home.

*And nobody's going to anyway,* Jacob said. *Not any of us. Not ever. You're—* He stopped. He made himself say it. *You're hers. So you're off limits. To every wolf on this coast. That's the law. Even Sam.*

The black wolf looked at him for a long time through Leah's eyes.

"Yeah," he said finally. "She told me."

*She did?*

*I didn't,* said Leah. *Not yet. He's just in my head.*

"I was in her head," the black wolf admitted. "Bit. Sorry. She was thinking about it very loudly." He tilted his huge head. "Protected by tribal law. Like a baby."

*Like* Claire, said Leah, with enormous satisfaction.

*Oh my God,* said Jacob.

---

He stayed phased another five minutes, because he couldn't make himself leave.

He sat in the ferns behind the Cullens' house and watched them through Leah's eyes. He didn't mean to. He told himself to go and run the loop. He didn't go. He watched Leah walk out to the mouth of the cave and stand there on the ledge with the wind off the sea in her fur, looking out at the stacks and the arch and the grey light coming up behind the headland, and he watched the black wolf come and stand beside her, close, his shoulder against hers, looking out at the same thing. Not saying anything. Neither of them saying anything.

He'd seen Sam and Emily like that, through Sam's eyes. On Sam's porch, at night, when Sam didn't think anybody was phased. He'd seen Paul with Rachel. He'd seen himself, in his own head, standing over Nessie's crib.

He'd never seen it from the other side. He'd never seen it go both ways.

He felt Leah lean on the black wolf, just a little, just her weight against his shoulder. He felt the black wolf lean back.

*Jake,* said Leah, without turning round. *You're still here.*

*Yeah. Sorry.*

*Go run your loop.*

*Yeah.* He didn't move. *Leah.*

*What?*

He didn't know what he wanted to say. He sat there in the ferns trying to find it.

*Sam's going to smell this,* he said finally. *On the beach. The paw prints. He runs Second Beach Tuesdays. He's going to find prints in the sand the size of a—*

"There aren't any," said the black wolf.

*What?*

"Prints. There aren't any. I don't leave them." A pause. "Habit."

Jacob thought about that. He thought about a guy in a suit who didn't leave anything, not a scent, not a trail, not a clue. He thought about Layla on the nurse log yesterday, saying *it's like reading a door where somebody's scratched off all the glyphs*.

*Sam's going to find out anyway,* he said. *Eventually.*

*Let him,* said Leah. *Tell him I said hi.*

Jacob sat there for a second longer.

Then he did something he hadn't planned on. He thought it before he could stop himself, the way you blurt something at a funeral. He thought it straight at the black wolf, through Leah, as clear as he could.

*If you hurt her—*

"I know," said the black wolf.

*No, listen. I know I can't do anything. I know about the law. I know I can't touch you. I know you could put me on my back in about a second.* Jacob took a breath. *I'm saying it anyway. If you hurt her, I'll find a way.*

The black wolf turned his head away from the sea. He looked at Leah. Through her, at Jacob. The green eyes were very steady.

"Layla said that to her last night," he said. "On the porch."

*Yeah?*

"Yeah." The black wolf looked back out at the sea. "I'm starting to think she's got a lot of people who'd find a way." His voice was quiet. "Good. She should have."

Jacob didn't know what to say to that.

So he phased out.

He came back to himself on his knees in the cedars behind the Cullens' house, naked and steaming, with the ferns wet on his legs and the house quiet behind him and the grey light coming up through the trees. He sat there for a while. Then he got up and pulled his cut-offs off his ankle and put them on.

He didn't go and run the loop.

He walked round the side of the house, to the back, to where the big Douglas fir stood at the edge of the lawn, a hundred and fifty feet tall and older than the treaty. He stood in front of it and looked at the bark, at the scratch on it, about eight feet up. Four long marks, close together. Old ones under them, lower down, from August, and from September, and from October.

He stood there and looked at it.

Then he looked up. About two feet higher. A head.

"Huh," said Jacob, out loud, to the tree.

He went inside and made himself some coffee, and sat at the kitchen table with it, and didn't drink it, and when Esme asked him what was wrong, he told her nothing was, and found, to his own surprise, that it was mostly true.

### XI. Harry

*Teahwhit Head — 7:06 a.m.*

He felt the russet one go. It was like a radio being switched off in another room; there one second, a big loud worried voice at the edge of her head, and then a click and quiet. Leah let her breath out through her nose beside him on the ledge, long and slow, and leaned a little more of her weight against his shoulder.

They stood there.

The light was coming up behind the headland, behind them, somewhere over the mountains, and it hadn't reached the sea yet. The sea was still grey. The sky over it was grey, a pale clean grey for once, the cloud lifted and broken up and moving off east in long rags. The stacks stood out of the water in their line, black, with birds on them. A long way out, the buoy was still ringing. Below the ledge, the tide was going out from the foot of the headland and leaving the rocks shining.

He was hungry. He'd been hungry since about three. He didn't say anything about it.

He could feel her. He could have felt her from twenty miles off, he'd found that out yesterday, but this was different. She was right there against his shoulder, her coat salt-stiff and still damp from the surf, and the line between them was so short it had stopped being a line at all and was just a kind of heat in the middle of his chest that went over into hers and came back. The thing at the bottom of him, which was the whole of him right now, which had been the whole of him since the bench at ten to one, was quieter than he could remember it. It had stopped walking. It had stopped watching. It was just standing on a ledge in the morning with its shoulder against hers.

*You're thinking about food,* Leah thought.

"I'm always thinking about food."

*You're thinking about bacon.*

"Get out of my head."

*Can't,* she thought, and he felt her mouth try to go up at the corner, inside the wolf, the way it had last night outside the tent. *You get out of mine.*

He didn't try.

---

"I'll change first," he said, after a while. "Then you."

She turned her head and looked up at him.

*Clothes,* she thought. *Mine are on the clam bucket in Mom's yard.*

"I know."

*So I'll be standing in a cave with no clothes on.*

"You won't," he said. "Give me a second."

He walked back into the cave, to the far end, where the roof came down and the sand was dry as flour, and he let go.

He'd half expected that to be hard, too. Two months behind locks, and then one night out, and he'd thought it might not want to go back. It went. He felt it go, the whole black weight of it settling back down through him, down to the bottom, the way a big dog settles onto its bed at the end of a long day. Without any argument at all. He felt it go down and turn round three times and lie down there in the dark with its chin on its paws.

It kept its eyes open. It kept them on her.

He didn't build the locks back.

He thought about it, for about a second, standing there. The braid of them. Three layers, his own work, a fortnight in September. He could have had the first one back up in an hour. He looked down at the thing at the bottom of him, lying there with its eyes on the grey wolf in the mouth of the cave, and thought about putting it back in the cellar.

He left the cellar door open.

Then he was standing on two feet on the dry sand at the back of the cave, in his boots, in his jeans and the grey flannel shirt and the waxed jacket, all of it exactly where it had been on the bench at ten to one, down to the damp on the shoulders of the jacket, and the weight of the Colt under his left arm, and the penny in his front pocket. He flexed his hands. They were hands. He looked at them anyway.

He took off the jacket. Then he unbuttoned the flannel shirt and took that off too, and stood there in his T-shirt in the cold of the cave, and held them both out behind him without looking round.

"There," he said. "I'm facing the wall."

He heard her come in off the ledge. He heard her stop behind him. He heard the tearing sound, the way it went for her, and some breathing, and a muttered word that would have got her thrown out of a pub in Glasgow. Then he felt the shirt taken out of his hand, and a minute later the jacket.

"Okay," said Leah.

He turned round.

She was standing in the middle of the cave in his shirt, buttoned wrong, with the sleeves rolled up about four times and the hem down nearly to her knees, and his waxed jacket over it, hanging open, the shoulders sticking out past her own like she'd borrowed them off a coat hanger. Her legs were bare. Her feet were bare in the sand. Her hair was standing up in salt-stiff spikes all over her head. She had a long scrape down her left shin from the rocks on the way up and a dusting of sand on one cheek.

She looked at him like she was daring him to say something.

He didn't say anything. He found he couldn't, for a second.

"What," she said.

"Nothing."

"You're making a face."

"I'm not making a face."

"You can't lie to me," said Leah.

"Bollocks," said Harry. "Fine. I'm making a face."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she pulled the jacket closed round herself, and walked past him, out to the mouth of the cave, and sat down on the ledge with her legs hanging over the drop and the sea under her feet.

After a minute he went and sat down beside her.

---

The light reached the sea at about twenty past seven.

It came over the headland behind them and went out across the water all at once, the way light does when it finally clears the hills, and the grey went silver and then, in long patches, almost blue. The stacks lit up gold on their eastern faces. The birds on them went up in a cloud and came down again. Through the arch, the sea was white.

They sat on the ledge and watched it. Neither of them said anything for a long time.

"Can I ask you something?" said Leah.

"Yeah."

"On the ridge." She was looking at the sea, not at him. "Yesterday. After you let us up. You came back across that hole in the ground walking on nothing, and I was standing there with my teeth out telling you to come on and try it. And you stopped. And you took your glasses off." She paused. "Why?"

He thought about it. He didn't need to. The answer was right there, and it came out of him the way everything did now, straight, without stopping anywhere on the way.

"I wanted a proper look at you," he said. "You were the only thing on that ridge I couldn't work out."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"The boy was easy," said Harry. "He was an open book. Loud. The witch was good, and she was frightened, and frightened people are easy to read. You weren't either of those. Your head was a closed fist. All edges. And under it there was something I couldn't get hold of. Like a smell on the wind." He shrugged. "So I took the glasses off. To see."

"And?"

"And I saw," said Harry.

She looked at him for a while. Then she looked back at the sea.

"That's a really stupid reason," she said.

"Yeah."

"Two seconds later you'd have had to fight me."

"I know."

"You'd have won."

"I know that too." He looked at the arch. "Glad I didn't have to."

She didn't say anything. But he felt her lean, very slightly, so her shoulder was against his arm through the waxed jacket.

---

She told him about the house.

He'd felt most of it, in pieces, from the ridge. He'd felt her shout at the russet one on the lawn, and her throat close, twice, and the long flat jolt of surprise when somebody up on the porch had read his name off the oath. He'd felt *danger*, and the fear, and her voice down the line telling him to sit. He hadn't known what any of it was about. He'd made a point of not knowing. Now she told him, sitting on the ledge with her bare feet over the drop, and he let her, and didn't go into her head to check any of it.

She told him about the baby vampire hybrid and the bathtub and the beer fridge, and he laughed so hard he had to put his hand on the rock.

She told him about Edward, on the porch, reading the name off the wall like a man reading a gravestone.

"I felt that," said Harry. "On the ridge. Like somebody running a finger round the edge of a lock. Reading what was stamped on it." He shook his head. "I knew there was a mind-reader in that house. I didn't know he could do *that*. Nobody's ever been able to do that. Eight years, there's nothing in any of the books." He thought about it. "I'd like to have a word with him about it, actually."

"He's going to want a word with you."

"Then we'll both have a word."

She told him about the witch on the couch, laughing with her face in her hands. *I threw a Blasting Curse at Harry Potter.* He put his hand over his eyes.

"Oh, God," he said. "The lunchbox."

"She said her cousin had one."

"Everybody's cousin had one. I've seen it. It's *horrible*. It's a baby in a nappy with a scar drawn on with a marker pen." He took his hand away. "There was a cereal, too. In America. Hermione sent me a box. It had a free wand in it."

Leah looked at him.

"Plastic," said Harry. "It lit up."

She told him about Jasper.

She told it plainly, without dressing it up, the way he'd have told it. The far-off eyes. The scars. *Everyone sleeps.* He listened, and felt the thing at the bottom of him lift its head at the name and look at it, and he put his hand on it, inside, and it put its head back down. It kept its ears up.

"He said he meant it as a fact," said Leah.

"It is a fact," said Harry. "It's true. Everybody does." He looked at the sea. "I'd have thought it, in his place. I'd have said it out loud, too, probably, if you hadn't been in the room." He was quiet a moment. "I'll talk to him. He and I'll get on."

"That's what you told Jake."

"I meant it."

And then she told him about the law.

She told him about Sam, and eight wolves in the dark round the white house ten weeks ago, and Jacob standing between them and a newborn baby, and Sam turning round and going home. She told him what she'd asked in the long white room, with all of them looking at her. *What was that one law.*

He'd heard most of it already, in her head, at dawn, when she'd been thinking about it so loudly he couldn't help it. He let her tell it anyway.

When she was done, he sat there for a while.

"So I'm protected," he said, "under the most absolute law of the Quileute wolves."

"Yep."

"Same as a two-year-old."

"Same as Claire," said Leah. "Who eats crayons."

"Same as the baby."

"Same as the baby."

"And if one of them so much as touches me—"

"It comes down the line into me," said Leah. "And through me into all of them. And it tears the pack in half. So they can't." She shrugged in his jacket. "Even Sam. Especially Sam. Sam's going to have to stand there and take it."

Harry sat there on the ledge, an Unspeakable with standing authority and a vampire in his back room and a gun under his arm that could turn a boulder into gravel, and thought about being in the same legal category as a toddler who ate crayons.

He started to laugh.

He couldn't stop. He sat on the ledge above Second Beach and laughed until his ribs hurt, and she sat next to him in his shirt and his jacket and watched him do it, and then she started, too, and the two of them sat there laughing at nothing with the sea going in and out of the arch below them and the birds going up off the stacks.

When he could breathe again he wiped his eyes on the back of his wrist.

"Croaker's going to love that," he said.

"Who's Croaker?"

---

So he told her about the call.

He told her about the mirror, and the old man in it, and the four-hour-old lie, *it's the rain, sir*. He told her what he'd said. *I'm compromised. There's a person. If it ever comes down to the Department or them, it's them, every time.* He told her he'd asked to be pulled.

She went very still beside him.

"You asked him to send somebody else," she said.

"Yeah."

"To take you off it. Off here. Away."

"Yeah."

"Why would you—" She stopped. He felt her think it through, fast. He felt her get there. "Because you'd told him a lie. And you weren't going to keep doing it."

"He's never lied to me," said Harry. "Eight years. Not even the kind bosses tell to be kind. I wasn't going to start on him because I'd got something I didn't want him to know about." He looked out at the sea. "And I thought he'd pull me. Course I did. I'd have pulled me. A man on a foreign posting tells you to your face he'd pick a local over the job? You get him out of there before lunch."

"And?"

"And he didn't." Harry was quiet for a moment. "He said he'd been waiting eight years for me to find something I'd pick over them. He said he'd been sure, for about three years, that I was going to go down to a room in the basement one Tuesday afternoon, and stand in front of something I shouldn't, and listen to it too long." He found he didn't want to say any more about that. He didn't have to. He felt her hear the rest of it anyway, through the line, the shape of it: the arch, the voices, the old man in his little room, quite sure. "He said he wasn't sending anybody. He said I was where I needed to be."

Leah didn't say anything for a long time.

"So you're staying," she said.

"I'm staying."

"For how long?"

"Indefinitely." He felt his mouth go up. "He gave me a job title."

"What job title?"

"Liaison," said Harry. "Quileute Nation. Olympic Peninsula Station."

Leah turned and stared at him.

"You're the *liaison*?" she said. "To *us*?"

"Apparently."

"You put me face down on a rock."

"I'm aware."

"You called me *Lassie*."

"I'm still calling you Lassie."

"You're the *liaison*?"

"It's a very new job," said Harry. "It didn't exist till about half nine last night. He wrote it down on a bit of paper while I watched." He shrugged. "Station strength, one. Reporting to him. Duration indefinite. MACUSA aren't to know. I'm still not here. I'm just not here for longer."

"Did you tell him about me?"

"No."

"Not anything?"

"I told him there was a person. I didn't say who. I didn't say what. I said if I told him, the Department would want to know how it worked, and the Department finds out how things work by taking them apart, and I'd stop them." He looked at her. "He asked one thing. Whether you were safe."

"What did you say?"

"I said safest person in the world, right now."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she looked down at her own hands, in her lap, in his shirtsleeves.

"That's what Seth said," she said quietly. "On the ridge. Yesterday. When Jake was freaking out about where I'd gone. Seth said, *I think she's probably the safest person in the world right now.*"

"Did he."

"I saw it in his head."

"Smart kid, Balto."

"Don't call him that to his face."

"Too late," said Harry. "I already did."

---

They talked about the hand, last.

He brought it up. She wouldn't have; he could feel her not wanting to. He could feel the place in her where it lived, the cold slab of the Department sitting in the middle of her like a safe in a farmhouse, and he could feel her walking round it carefully every time her thoughts got near, the way you walk round furniture in the dark in somebody else's house.

"It hurt you," he said. "Last night. On the lawn."

"It didn't hurt."

"Leah."

"It *didn't*. It was cold. It sat there." She pulled the jacket tighter. "It was like a hand. Like it was asking if I was sure."

"I know what it's like," said Harry. "I've had it on my own throat."

She looked at him.

"Belfast," he said. "2002. Very drunk. Starting to tell a mate something I shouldn't. It closed about that much." He held up a thumb and finger, a quarter-inch apart. "I stopped." He put his hand down. "I didn't know it could go into you. Nobody did. There's nothing about it anywhere. I'm going to find out how it works."

"Can you take it out?"

He thought about that honestly. He owed her that.

"Don't know," he said. "Maybe. I've never tried. I don't know if I want to."

She went still.

"It's the only thing in the world," said Harry, "keeping what I told you in that kitchen safe inside your head. From everybody. The pack. The mind-reader. Anybody who ever gets hold of you and tries to get it out. If it weren't there, every single thing I said yesterday would be sitting in the middle of your brother's head right now, and Jacob's, and the bronze-haired lad's, and through him the whole family." He shook his head. "It does that to your throat, I know. Mostly, though, it's doing it *for* you."

That came out wrong. He felt it come out wrong as he said it, and he didn't take it back, because he couldn't, and because it was true.

"I want it to stop doing that to your throat," he said. "That's all. I want it to let you say what you need to say to your own people about things that are yours. Your family. The kid." He looked at her. "The bit about the kid. I felt you try to tell them. Last night."

"I wanted them to know," said Leah. "That he was here. In the trees. That the old one sent him. That you—" She stopped. She didn't try any further. He watched her not try. "They should know, Harry. They need to know. She's ten weeks old."

He didn't say anything for a moment.

"Then I'll tell them," he said.

She turned and looked at him.

"Tonight," said Harry. "At dinner. The nomad. Who sent him. What he smelled in the trees. What I made him say down the phone instead. The Department bits stay where they are, London and the rest of it; those were never theirs, and I couldn't anyway." He looked out at the sea. "It's theirs. It's their kid. They've got a right to know who's been standing in their garden. I was going to tell them eventually. I'll tell them tonight."

She looked at him for a long time.

"You're going to walk into a house full of vampires," she said, "and tell them you tortured one of their kind in your back room yesterday and made him lie to the scariest vampire in the world on the phone."

"Yeah."

"Over a roast."

"Over a roast," said Harry. "I'm told there'll be a roast."

"You're insane."

"Probably." He felt the thing at the bottom of him stretch, comfortable, in the morning light coming through him. "I'll bring a pie."

---

They walked back.

He'd offered to Apparate. She'd asked him if it was like being shoved through a garden hose, which was what the witch had told her brother, and he'd said it was worse, and she'd said *walk*, so they walked.

They went down off the head the way they'd come up, her first, in his jacket and shirt and nothing on her feet, finding the ledges without looking, and him behind her in his boots, putting his feet where she'd put hers. At the bottom the tide had gone out a long way and left the sand hard and grey and gleaming. They walked up the whole length of Second Beach with the stacks on their left and the sun on their backs. There were prints in the sand from last night, hers, a wolf's, big ones, going down toward the water and the rocks. There was nothing next to them.

"That's creepy," said Leah, looking at it.

"It's habit."

"It's still creepy."

At the north end, the trail went up into the trees, steep and muddy and full of roots, and climbed for most of a mile through big old spruce with ferns under them, and came out at a little gravel car park with a sign about tides on it. Then the road. They walked down the middle of it, because there were no cars. Past the turn for the school. Past the first houses. Past a man in a doorway in his dressing gown with a cup of coffee, who stopped with the cup halfway to his mouth and watched Leah Clearwater walk past his house at quarter past eight in the morning in a man's coat, barefoot, with a large white man in a T-shirt beside her. And kept watching. And turned and said something over his shoulder into the house.

"That's Mr. Ateara," said Leah, without looking round. "He's going to tell his wife. And his wife's going to tell everybody."

"Problem?"

"No," said Leah. She sounded surprised by it. "No. Let her."

They came up the hill to her mother's house from the road, round the side, and into the back yard. The washing line. The old bucket by the step, with Seth's T-shirt and her shorts still folded on top of it, soaked through, a little puddle of rain in the dip of the folds. She picked them up and looked at them and made a face.

Then the back door opened.

---

Sue Clearwater stood on the back step in her dressing gown with a cup of coffee in her hand and looked at him.

She was a small woman. Shorter than her daughter by half a head, broad-shouldered, with grey coming in at the front of her black hair and a face that didn't give anything away it hadn't decided to. Dark eyes. Her son's eyes, he thought, and her daughter's mouth. She looked at him the way he'd been looked at by a Healer in Mumbai once, before she'd taken his eyes out and put new ones in: as if she was going to find out exactly what was wrong with him and had all the time in the world to do it.

She looked at Leah in his jacket. She looked at Leah's bare legs and the scrape on her shin and the sand on her cheek. She looked at the wet clothes in Leah's hand. She looked back at him.

"You'll be Harry," said Sue Clearwater.

"Yes, ma'am," said Harry.

"Hm," said Sue.

She came down one step. She looked up at him from there, which still wasn't very high. She held out her hand.

He took it. He felt her feel it; he saw it in her face, the tiny flicker. Heat. His hands were hot. Hotter than an ordinary man's by a long way. She'd have felt that before, every day, on her son, on her daughter. She'd have felt it on her husband's best friend's boy.

"You run hot," she said.

"So I'm told."

"Hm," said Sue again.

She let go of his hand. She looked at Leah.

"That's not your jacket," she said.

"No," said Leah.

"Where've you been?"

"Out."

"All night?"

"Yeah."

Sue looked at her daughter for a long moment. Harry stood very still and didn't look at either of them, and looked at the woodpile, and felt the thing at the bottom of him lying there with its ears up, listening.

"Well," said Sue. "Come in. Both of you. I've got pancakes."

---

The kitchen was small and yellow and close, with a woodstove going in the corner and the window over the sink steamed up. It smelled of coffee and fry bread and cedar smoke and, faintly, under all of it, the sea. Sue pointed him at a chair at the table without a word. He sat in it. Leah went upstairs, and he heard a door, and water running, and came down five minutes later in jeans and a sweatshirt with her hair wet, and his jacket and shirt folded over her arm. She hung the jacket on the back of his chair and dropped the shirt in his lap. Then she sat down next to him.

Sue put a plate in front of him with six pancakes on it, and a jar of honey with a dipper stuck in it, and a fork.

He looked at the honey. It was dark, nearly brown, in a plain jar with a handwritten label. *Calawah, Sept '05.*

"Maeve Quinn's," said Sue, from the stove. "Last jar. She brought it down to the tribal centre every fall for forty years." She flipped a pancake. "Eat."

He ate.

He ate the six pancakes in about the time it took Sue to make six more, and she put those on his plate without asking, and he ate those too, and on the third lot she stopped and looked at him over her shoulder for a long moment with the spatula in her hand. Then she looked at Leah.

"He eats like your brother," she said.

"Worse," said Leah.

"Hm," said Sue, and turned back to the stove, and poured more batter.

He looked at the fridge while he ate. He couldn't not. There was a photograph on it under a magnet shaped like a salmon. A boat. A family on it, four of them, squinting in summer sun. A small boy with a huge grin and his hair sticking up. A thin dark-haired girl of about fourteen beside him, scowling at whoever held the camera with exactly the face she'd scowled at him with on the ridge. A woman, younger, laughing, with her hand up to keep her hair out of her eyes. And at the wheel, a broad heavy man in his forties with his cap on backwards and his mouth wide open, laughing at something off the edge of the picture.

He'd smelled him on the back step at one in the morning. Fish and engine oil and coffee, soaked into the wood. Somebody who'd sat out there every evening for twenty years. Somebody who hadn't for months.

Sue had seen him look. He saw her see it. She put three more pancakes on his plate and stood there beside the table with the spatula, looking at the photo with him.

"That's my husband," she said. "Harry."

Harry put his fork down.

He looked at Leah. Leah was looking at her plate. She didn't look up. He felt her through the line, a long way down, very still, holding something very carefully the way you'd hold a full cup.

"I'm sorry," he said.

He meant it. It was the only thing he could think of to say, and it was true, and it was nowhere near enough, and he said it anyway.

"He'd have liked you," said Sue. "Or he'd have hated you. One or the other. There wasn't ever a middle with him." She looked at Harry. Her face didn't give anything away. "He didn't trust anybody from further off than Port Angeles."

"Most people manage both, with me," said Harry. "Liking and hating. Usually in that order."

Something moved at the corner of Sue Clearwater's mouth. It was there and gone.

"Hm," she said.

She went back to the stove.

Under the table, Leah's knee came over and pressed against his. Just that. She didn't look at him. She went on looking at her plate, with her face perfectly blank, and pressed her knee against his under her mother's kitchen table, and left it there.

He picked his fork back up.

---

He heard the truck about ten minutes later.

He heard it a long way off, the way he heard everything now, with the cellar door open: an old engine labouring up the hill from the road, a loose exhaust, a cracked tail-light rattling in its housing. He knew the sound. He'd heard it pull into a gravel drive on Calawah Way a dozen times from the other side of town, back when he'd been putting together his file on the only witch in Forks.

Leah lifted her head.

"Seth," she said.

"And the witch," said Harry.

Sue looked at both of them. Then she went to the cupboard and got down two more plates.

The truck pulled into the front, and stopped, and doors went, one and then the other, slower. Feet on the porch. The front door. "Mom? Mom, it's me, Layla's back's really bad, she can hardly turn her head, I said you'd have that stuff you put on Dad's shoulder that time, the stinky green stuff, and also we haven't eaten, and—"

Seth Clearwater came through the kitchen doorway, enormous and sandy-haired and talking, and stopped dead.

He stood there with his mouth still open on the next word. He looked at Harry, at the table, with a fork in his hand and a stack of pancakes in front of him and honey on the plate. He looked at his sister, beside him. He looked at his mother at the stove, with the spatula. He looked back at Harry.

"Morning, Balto," said Harry.

Seth's mouth closed.

Behind him, in the doorway, moving stiffly, with one hand on the frame and her back held very straight, Layla Quinn came into the kitchen and stopped too.

She'd been crying, a bit, from the pain, sometime in the night; he could see it round her eyes. Her face was grey with not sleeping. She was wearing a big man's sweatshirt over her own clothes, the boy's, and her hair was pulled back in a knot that had given up. Her right hand was loose at her side, the fingers a little curled, and he could see, without trying, exactly how far her wand was from her palm. About four inches, up her sleeve, in a leather strap.

She looked at him across the kitchen.

He watched her not reach for it. He watched her think about it, and decide, and not. Her hand stayed at her side.

"Layla," said Harry. "How's the back?"

She looked at him for a long moment.

"I took six aspirin," she said.

"Told you."

"Carlisle said ibuprofen."

"Carlisle's a doctor. I've just been thrown into more logs." He nodded at the stove. "Mrs. Clearwater's got something green, I'm told. For shoulders."

Layla didn't say anything. She stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame and her back held straight and looked at him, at his face, the way the vampire had looked up at him out of the circle, the way everybody had looked at him for about a year after the war, before he'd learned how to make them stop. Like she was looking for something in it. The scar, maybe, though it was long gone. The glasses. The skinny kid on a lunchbox.

"You're Harry Potter," she said.

He glanced at Leah. Leah raised her eyebrows at him over her coffee.

"So I hear," said Harry.

"You took my Disjunction off your fingers," said Layla. "And looked at it. And said *oh, that's clever*. And gave it back."

"I did," said Harry. "Sorry about that. It's a lovely spell."

"You don't have to say that."

"I don't say things I don't have to." He looked at her. "Three years? Building it?"

She stared at him.

"Three and a half," she said.

"Thought so. You can feel the hours in it." He picked up his coffee. "You could've had me with it, if I'd been anybody else. The braid on the back end's the best bit. I've been thinking about it all night. In between other things." He drank. "I'd like to see your notes on it some time, if you'd let me."

Layla Quinn stood in Sue Clearwater's kitchen doorway with her mouth slightly open.

"You picked the wrong day," said Harry, more gently. "I told you that. That's all it was."

Something went across her face, very fast. He didn't go into her head to find out what. He let her keep it.

"Sit down," said Sue Clearwater, from the stove. "All of you. Seth, get her a chair with a back. Layla, sit on the cushion. I'll get the green stuff after you've eaten." She put two plates on the table. "There's pancakes."

---

They sat.

It was a small table. It had been built for four, and there were five of them round it now, and Seth on his own took up most of one side. Layla eased herself down onto a chair with a cushion on it, very slowly, with Seth's hand under her elbow, and let her breath out through her teeth. Leah moved her chair over to make room, and the move put her right up against Harry's shoulder. Sue put the coffee pot in the middle and sat down at the end with her own cup, and didn't eat anything, and watched all of them.

Seth hadn't stopped looking at Harry. He was holding his fork and not using it.

"You came to the house," Seth said finally. "Last night. Lee's head was full of you this morning when I phased in to check on her, before Jake. It was all black. And the sea. You were in the *cave*." He looked at his sister, appalled. "Nobody goes in the cave."

"He did," said Leah.

"You never let *me* in the cave."

"You never asked."

"Because you'd have *killed* me!"

"Yeah," said Leah. She ate a piece of pancake. "I would've."

Seth looked at Harry again. He had a whole lot of things going across his face at once, every one of them out in the open where anybody could see, the way the boy's thoughts had been out in the open on the ridge. Fear, still, a bit. He'd been pinned to a rock. Anger, still, a bit. Harry had thrown his girl into a log. Bewilderment. And, under all of it, coming up slowly, like light through water, something that looked a lot like the face he'd made sitting in his mother's car in a car park in the rain outside Newton's Olympic Outfitters, watching a white pickup with New Mexico plates drive away.

"You're really staying?" Seth said. "Lee said. In her head. You're staying."

"I'm staying."

"Like, for good?"

"For a good while."

"Doing *what*?"

Harry looked at Leah. Leah, chewing, looked back at him with her eyebrows up, waiting to see what he'd say.

"Liaison," said Harry.

Seth looked blank. "To who?"

"You lot," said Harry. "Apparently."

There was a short silence.

Then Sue Clearwater, at the end of the table, put her coffee down very carefully, and laughed. It came out of her short and surprised, like a bark, and she put her hand over her mouth, and her eyes went to the photograph on the fridge, the man at the wheel with his cap on backwards, laughing at something off the edge of the picture.

"Oh, Harry," she said, behind her hand. Harry didn't know which of them she meant. He didn't think she knew either. "Oh, he'd have *hated* you."

"Most people do," said Harry. "Eventually."

"Mom," said Leah.

"Eat your pancakes," said Sue, and wiped her eyes, and poured herself more coffee.

---

He sat there and ate his pancakes.

It was twenty to nine in the morning on Tuesday the twenty-first of November. He had a three-hundred-year-old vampire in a silver circle at the back of a tent twenty miles away, with thirty-one questions still to answer and a dead fingertip in a jar beside him. He had a report on a desk by a banked fire with *WITHHELD* written on it in three places, underlined. He had a lattice of two hundred and eleven stones laid over three thousand square miles of national park, and an iron ring on his finger that would go hot the next time anything red stepped into it. He had a dinner invitation at seven o'clock from a coven of vampires who ate deer, at which, over a roast, he was going to tell them that the oldest and most dangerous of their kind had sent a spy into their garden two nights ago to listen to their daughter's heart. He had a mind-reader to have a word with, and a soldier called Jasper to have a quieter one with. He had a witch across the table who'd built a spell he'd have been proud of, and who'd one day get a phone call from him she couldn't say no to. He had a job title that had been written down on a piece of grey paper at half past five that morning, London time, by an old man who didn't sleep.

*Station strength, one.*

Leah's knee was pressed against his under the table. Seth was telling his mother, with his mouth full, about the time Harry had caught a falling vampire one-handed like a plank, and getting the details wrong. Layla was watching Harry over the rim of a coffee cup with an expression he'd have needed a lot longer to read, and he wasn't going to try. Sue was watching all of them, and saying nothing, and every so often looking at the fridge.

Down at the bottom of him, the thing that had lived in his cellar for two months lay with its chin on its paws in the open doorway, in the light. Its eyes were half shut. Its tail was moving, very slowly, back and forth.

He looked round the table.

He was going to have to tell Croaker, he thought, that the numbers were off.

---

*End of Chapter Two*
