## Chapter Two: Pocket Change

### I. Sunday Service

*The diner on Forks Avenue — Sunday, November 26, 2006, 6:14 a.m.*

Leah hadn't slept.

She'd tried. She'd lain on top of the covers in her room at her mother's house with the window open and the rain coming in on the sill, and looked at the ceiling, and every time she closed her eyes she was flat on her side in dead grass with something she couldn't see pressing down on her ribs like the flat of a giant hand, and her claws tearing up dirt, and a man in a long coat standing over her not even bothering to look at her.

At four she'd given up and gone out the back door and phased in the trees and run. The north line and the east line and the north line again. Seth had been asleep. Jacob had been asleep, finally, on the floor of the cottage. For two hours the pack mind had been hers alone, quiet and dark, and she'd run it until her legs burned and her head was empty.

At six she'd phased back behind the hardware store, pulled on the cutoffs and the T-shirt she'd tied to her ankle, and walked down Forks Avenue barefoot in the rain to the diner, because he'd said *I'm in most mornings, back booth*, and she wanted to see if it was true.

It was true.

He was sitting in the back corner with the wall behind him and the whole room in front of him. A plate of eggs and bacon and hash browns, a second plate with a slab of something purple on it, a mug of black coffee. A newspaper folded in quarters beside his elbow. He wasn't wearing the suit. He had on jeans and a dark green fleece with a logo stitched on the chest, an elk with a circle around it and some words, and the fleece made him look like somebody's cousin who worked for the parks department.

He looked up when the bell over the door went. He didn't look surprised.

"Lassie," he said. "Morning."

Leah stood in the doorway dripping on the mat.

"Shut the door, love, you're letting the weather in," said the waitress, from behind the counter. Fifties, glasses on a beaded chain. She was looking at Leah's bare feet with the expression of a woman who had raised children and seen worse. "You want coffee?"

"Yeah," said Leah. She shut the door.

"Sit down before Darlene thinks I've been stood up," said the man.

She walked down the length of the diner past two old loggers at the counter and a trucker in a booth and slid into the seat across from him. It was a small booth. Her knees nearly hit his under the table. She moved them.

Up close, in the light, he was just a guy. That was the first thing. That was the thing she'd come to find out, really, if she was honest, which she wasn't planning to be. Last night in the clearing he'd been a shape against the dark, a voice in her head, a force that put her in the dirt. This morning he was a man in a fleece eating breakfast. Late twenties. Dark hair cut short, a couple of days of stubble going on three. Green eyes, very green, the kind of green you'd think was contacts on anybody else. A nose that had been broken at least once and set by somebody who knew what they were doing. Big hands around the coffee mug. Big everything. He was taller than she was, sitting down, and she was six foot.

She noticed all of that, and she noticed herself noticing it, and she hated it so much she wanted to put her fist through the window.

*No,* she told herself. *Absolutely not. No.*

He was looking at his pie.

Darlene put a mug in front of her and filled it. "You're a Clearwater," she said. It wasn't a question. "Sue's girl."

"Yeah."

"How's your mom doing?"

"She's okay."

"Good. She's good people." Darlene looked at the man across from her and then back at Leah, and something shifted in her face, a little amused and a little speculative, and Leah decided she'd tear out her own tongue before she gave this woman anything to tell her mother about. "You eating, hon?"

"Yeah. Everything."

"Everything?"

"The big breakfast. Two of them. And whatever he's having." She jerked her chin at the purple slab on his plate.

Darlene's eyebrows went up over her glasses. She wrote it down. She went away.

The man was smiling a little, into his coffee.

"What," said Leah.

"Nothing. You eat like your brother."

"We all eat like that."

"I gathered." He cut a corner off the pie with the side of his fork and ate it. "It's the marionberry, by the way. Darlene says it's a blackberry that went to college."

"I know what a marionberry is. I've lived here my whole life."

"Course you have." He put the fork down. "Go on, then."

"Go on what?"

"You walked down Forks Avenue barefoot in the rain at six in the morning to sit across from me. You've got questions. Ask them before your eggs come; you won't be able to talk after."

She looked at him. He looked back, pleasant and patient.

"Is he dead?" she said.

She kept her voice low. The loggers at the counter were arguing about the Seahawks. Nobody was listening.

"Not yet," the man said.

Something went through her at that. She didn't know what to call it. It came up from somewhere low in her stomach and spread out, slow and hot, and it felt a lot like the moment at the end of a hunt when the leech went down under her and stopped moving. Like the moment she got her teeth in. It felt good.

She'd killed four of them now. Four, since she'd phased. She remembered every one. And every one of them she'd done with her teeth and her claws and her pack around her, and every one had been fast and ugly and over. She'd never once thought about what it would be like to have one of them *alive*. Held. Pinned in place by somebody who wasn't scared of it and wasn't in a hurry.

The thing that had killed those hikers, still alive. Somewhere. With him.

She found she liked that a lot. She didn't know what that said about her. She didn't much care.

"What are you going to do to him?" she asked.

"Things he won't enjoy."

"Good," said Leah.

He looked at her for a second over the top of his mug. Then he nodded, very slightly, like she'd said something he agreed with and didn't need to add to.

"Are you doing it right now?" she asked. "The thing. In my head."

"No."

"How do I know?"

"You'd know." He picked up a piece of bacon. "You've got a tell. You go stiff round the ears."

"I don't *have* ears. Not right now."

"You do in there."

She opened her mouth. She shut it again. She had a feeling, and she didn't like the feeling, that he was telling the truth, and that she *had* gone stiff around the ears, in there, last night, every time.

"Why'd you shut up?" she said.

He chewed the bacon. "You'll have to be more specific."

"Last night. In the clearing." She made herself say it. She was good at making herself say things. It was the only thing she'd ever been good at, before the wolf. "You saw Sam. In my head. I know you did. I felt it. You looked right at it. And you didn't say anything."

He didn't answer right away.

He put the bacon down and wiped his fingers on the paper napkin, slowly, and looked out the window at the rain on Forks Avenue for a moment. When he looked back at her his face hadn't changed much, but something had gone out of the pleasantness. It went more real, somehow.

"Wasn't mine to say," he said.

That was all. He didn't explain it. He didn't make a little speech about it. He just said it, and picked his coffee back up.

Leah sat there.

She'd had a year and a half of people saying things about Sam. To her face and behind her back and inside her own head, in the pack mind, where they couldn't stop themselves. Pity, mostly. Pity was the worst. Paul had made jokes. Jared had been embarrassed for her. Her mother had tried to talk to her about it until Leah had screamed at her in the kitchen and broken a plate. Even Seth, who loved her, thought about it sometimes when he was near her and didn't know he was doing it, a soft sad *poor Leah* in the back of his head that she could hear as clear as a bell.

This man had seen the whole thing in about two seconds, and he'd decided it wasn't his, and he'd put it down.

She didn't know what to do with that. So she did nothing with it.

"You smell weird," she said instead.

"Thanks."

"I mean it. You smell wrong. Last night. Even right now, under the coffee." She leaned in a little, across the table, and breathed in through her nose, and didn't care how it looked. Coffee, bacon, wool, soap, rain. And under it the earth and pine and cold stone, like the inside of a cave. And under that— "You don't smell human."

"I've been sleeping in a tent for ten days."

"You smell like *wolf*."

His fork stopped.

It was a very small thing. It was maybe a quarter of a second, the fork halfway to the pie, not moving. Then it went on and cut the corner off the pie and he ate it.

"I've been round a lot of wet dogs lately," he said.

Leah sat back.

She'd seen it. He knew she'd seen it. He didn't look at her, and she didn't say anything, and the moment went past and the loggers argued about the Seahawks and Darlene came down the aisle with a tray the size of a car tire covered in plates.

"Two big breakfasts," Darlene said, setting them down, "and a marionberry. And I'll bring more coffee. God help us all."

"Cheers, Darlene."

"Don't *cheers* me, you'll make me blush." She went away.

Leah ate. She was starving. She was always starving, and running half the night had made it worse, and she ate both breakfasts and the pie without looking up much. He was right. She couldn't talk while she ate. He didn't try to make her. He read his newspaper and drank his coffee and once, when she reached for the hot sauce, he slid it across the table to her without looking up from the sports page.

It was the pie that got her. The pie was really good.

She was halfway through it when something happened to his face.

She was looking at him when it happened. He'd reached into the pocket of the fleece for something, a wallet she thought, and his hand had stopped. He took it out slowly. It was a small black notebook, a cheap-looking thing with an elastic band around it, and he held it below the edge of the table where nobody else could see it, and opened it, and read something.

She watched all the pleasantness go out of him. She watched it go like water going down a drain. In about one second the man across from her went from a guy in a fleece reading the sports page to the man in the clearing, the one who'd turned around with the vampire under his arm and looked at her and Seth like they were finally worth looking at. Every muscle in his face went still. His eyes moved, reading, and then stopped, and went back to the top, and read it again.

"Bollocks," he said quietly.

He looked at his watch. He closed the notebook and put it back in his pocket.

Then he took out his wallet, a real one, brown leather, and put two twenties on the table under the edge of his plate, which was about three times what the bill would be. He slid his own pie plate, with most of a second slice still on it, across the table to her.

"Have the pie, Lassie," he said. He was already standing up. "I've got to go."

"Go where?"

"Work."

"What kind of work?"

He shrugged into his coat, a canvas jacket hanging on the hook at the end of the booth, and looked down at her. For a second she thought he was going to say something else. He looked like there was something else.

Then he said, "Tell your brother not to bite anything else he can't chew," and walked out.

Leah sat there with a fork in her hand and watched through the rain-streaked window as he crossed the lot to a big black pickup truck, an old Ford, high off the ground, with a magnet sign on the door that had the same elk on it as his fleece. He got in. He didn't look back. The engine turned over on the first try. He pulled out onto Forks Avenue and turned north toward the 101 and drove off much faster than the speed limit.

She watched until the taillights were gone.

Darlene came by with the coffee pot. She looked at the two twenties under the edge of the plate. She looked at the empty booth. She looked at Leah.

"Well," Darlene said. "That's a first."

"What is?"

"He always finishes his pie." She filled Leah's mug. "Something wrong?"

"I don't know," said Leah, and found that she meant it, and that she didn't like it at all.

She finished his pie. It was still good. She was still angry about it.

---

He didn't come in on Monday.

He didn't come in on Tuesday, or Wednesday, or Thursday. Leah knew, because she came in every morning at six-fifteen and sat at the counter and drank one cup of coffee and watched the back booth stay empty, and Darlene stopped asking her if she wanted to order and just started bringing her a piece of pie.

By Friday Darlene had stopped saying anything about it at all, which was worse.

### II. Notice-Me-Not

*First Beach, La Push — Friday, December 1, 2006*

"It's here," said Sam Uley. "Somewhere right in here."

Layla stood on the headland above First Beach in the yellow raincoat with the hood up and the wind trying to take it off her, and looked at the place where he was pointing, and saw nothing at all.

A shoulder of rock covered in salal and moss, jutting out over the beach. A twisted Sitka spruce growing out of it at an angle, its roots wrapped around the stone like fingers. Below, the long grey curve of the beach and the logs piled up along it, bleached white, and the surf coming in, and out beyond the surf, black against the grey sky, the sea stacks and the hump of James Island. The rain was coming in sideways off the Pacific. It was eleven in the morning and it looked like dusk.

"Where?" she said.

"I don't know where. That's the point." Sam's voice was very calm. It was always very calm. Seth had told her once that Sam was the calmest person he'd ever met, and that it was the scariest thing about him, and now that she was standing next to him she understood what Seth had meant. He was huge, bigger than Seth, bigger than Jacob, with the same short hair and the same heat coming off him that she could feel through her raincoat from three feet away, and he stood on the edge of the headland with his hands in the pockets of a pair of cutoff jeans in the freezing rain and didn't seem to feel it at all. He had a grave, heavy, serious face. He looked like a man who'd been asked to carry something a long way and had decided a long time ago not to complain about it.

"We run this headland every night," Sam said. "All of us. It's part of the boundary. And about a week ago Paul noticed that we were all doing the same thing. Every one of us. We'd come up the trail from the beach and we'd go round. Round *that*." He nodded at the spruce. "Every single time. Left side, every time. Nobody ever went right. Nobody ever went near the tree. And nobody could say why. We'd just... not want to." His jaw tightened a fraction. "Wolves don't not want to go places, Miss Brennan. Not on our own land."

"Layla," she said absently. She was looking at the tree.

She'd been looking at it for about thirty seconds now, and she was starting to have the feeling. It was very faint. It was the feeling you got when you were in a crowded room and somebody behind you said your name and you couldn't tell who. A tug at the edge of the attention. A little voice that said *nothing here, nothing here, look at the ocean instead, isn't the ocean nice.*

She looked at the ocean.

Then she caught herself doing it, and her skin went cold all over.

"Oh, you *bastard*," she said softly.

Sam looked at her.

"Not you," Layla said. "Sorry. Him." She dragged her eyes back to the tree. It was hard. It was like walking into a wind. "It's a notice-me-not. A really good one. It makes you stop caring about the tree, so you go around it without thinking. Same as the rest of us."

"You can see it?"

"I can see that I can't see it, which is a start." She pulled her wand out of her hair. Sam's eyes went to it, flat and wary, the same way Leah's always did. "Can you give me some room? Like, ten feet. If this has teeth I don't want you in it."

He gave her ten feet. Then, after a second, he gave her another five.

She stood in front of the spruce in the rain and closed her eyes.

It was easier with her eyes closed. The charm worked on looking; with your eyes shut it had less to grab. She put out her free hand toward the tree, palm open, the way her first master had taught her in a dead man's house in Vermont when she was nineteen, and *felt*.

There.

It was all around the base of the tree. A thin, fine, tightly woven film of magic laid over the roots and the rock, about three feet across. She ran her mind along it the way she'd run her fingers along a piece of fabric to find the weave. She found the weave.

She'd never felt anything like it.

Every notice-me-not she'd ever broken had been built the same way, more or less. They taught it the same way at every school in the world, a basic aversion field, a nudge at the corner of the eye, and the good ones were just tighter versions of the bad ones. This was something else. This had been built from the ground up by someone who'd taken the standard charm apart and looked at every single piece and asked himself why it was there, and thrown most of them away, and rebuilt the rest so they did three things at once. It *offered* your attention something else. It found whatever you'd rather be looking at, the ocean, the sky, the person beside you, and held it up, very gently, so you'd look there instead. And when you did, it closed behind you like water.

It was beautiful. It was so beautiful she stood there in the rain with her eyes closed and her hand out for five full minutes, just reading it, before she remembered she was supposed to be taking it apart.

"Miss Brennan," said Sam, from fifteen feet away.

"I know. I'm working." She wasn't. "Give me a minute."

It took her the rest of the day.

---

She got the notice-me-not off the tree at about four in the afternoon, when the light was nearly gone, and when it came off it came off all at once, like a sheet pulled off a bed, and she found herself looking at the roots of the spruce and seeing them properly for the first time.

There was a stone wedged in among them. A plain grey beach stone, about the size of a loaf of bread, smooth and wet, the kind there were ten million of on the beach below. She'd have walked past it a hundred times. Anybody would have.

She knelt in the wet moss and looked at it with her wand lit and saw the runes.

Nine of them. Cut into the underside of the stone where it sat against the root, so fine she had to put her face six inches from them to make them out. She didn't know them. She'd studied runes for ten years. She knew Elder Futhark and the Younger, the Anglo-Saxon and the Ogham, the Ilvermorny glyph-sets, the Goblin marks, a working knowledge of the Incan ones and the old Egyptian. These were none of those. Or they were all of them. They looked like somebody had taken pieces of five different alphabets and cut them up and stitched them back together into letters that did more than any of the originals could do alone.

And they were *live*. She could feel the stone humming under her hand like a power line.

Sam had come up behind her. He crouched a few feet off, looking at the stone. "That's it?"

"That's it. That's one of them." She swallowed. "I think it's an anchor. For a ward. A really, really big one."

"How big?"

She didn't answer. She was feeling along the stone again, along the threads that ran out of it, and they ran out of it in four directions, down into the earth and away, north up the coast and south along it and east into the forest and one thread that ran straight out to sea and down and went somewhere under the water she couldn't follow. They went on and on. She followed the eastern one with her mind as far as she could, which was a long way, and it was still going when she lost it.

"Big," she said.

"Can you break it?"

"I think so. Maybe." She sat back on her heels. Her knees were soaked through. Her hands were numb. "Sam, I want to be really clear. I've been doing this for six years. I've taken apart curses that were laid down before Columbus. I've never seen anything like this. Not close. This is— it's like somebody built a ward and then built a better ward to sit on top of it and then built another one to watch the first two. It's like looking at a watch with the back off."

"He said they'd notice," Sam said. "If you went looking. Seth told me. He said the traps would notice you trying."

"Yeah."

"Will they?"

Layla looked at the stone. The nine runes looked back at her.

"Probably," she said.

Sam was quiet for a while. The rain came in off the ocean. Down on the beach in the near dark a couple of big shapes moved along the tideline, grey and black, wolves running the boundary. She saw one of them stop and look up at the headland, at the two of them crouched by the tree.

"It's on our land," Sam said finally. "It's on Quileute land, it's on the boundary, and some man nobody's ever met put it there without asking anybody, and it's been making my pack walk around a tree for two weeks without knowing why. I don't care how good it is. I don't care if he can hear us thinking. I want it gone."

He said it the way he said everything. Calm. Heavy. Final.

"He's not here," Sam said. "Leah says he's gone. Hasn't been back to the diner in a week. Nobody's seen him. If he's gone, he can't stop you. If he's not gone—" He shrugged, and it was a big shrug, a mountain moving. "Then he can come and ask me himself why he put a spell on my beach."

Layla thought about the man in the clearing. Standing with a cup of coffee and a vampire under his arm. *Don't go looking for them. You won't find them, and they'll notice you trying.*

She thought about Charlie Swan's kitchen.

"If I do this," she said slowly, "he's going to know it was me."

"Probably," said Sam.

"And he's got something on me. You know that. He's got me over a barrel. He could do a lot of things."

"I know." Sam looked at her steadily. "It's your call, Layla. I'm asking if you can."

She looked at the stone for a long time.

Then she thought about the thing she'd felt three weeks ago, for one second, on the Sol Duc road. The pressure change. Like weather. And how that man had looked at her when she told him, tilted his head, *you felt that?*, like she was a lock he hadn't expected to be hard.

Nobody had ever put a ward on a beach that she couldn't take off it.

"Bring me a thermos," she said. "And a tarp. And a lot of coffee. I'm going to be here a while."

---

It took three days.

She worked through the night on the first night, under a blue tarp Sam rigged between the spruce and two driftwood poles, with a camp lantern and a thermos of Emily Young's coffee, which was the best coffee Layla had ever tasted and made her feel briefly and sharply ashamed of her own. Emily brought it out to the headland herself at nine o'clock, a small woman with long black hair and three long scars running down the right side of her face from her eye to her jaw, faded silver, and Layla looked at the scars and then made herself stop looking. Emily smiled at her like she'd seen her do it a hundred times and didn't mind. She left the thermos and a plate of sandwiches and went back down the trail with Sam's arm around her.

Layla thought about Leah. Then she made herself stop thinking about Leah and went back to work.

The anchor stone fought.

She found that out about an hour into the first night. She'd mapped the first rune, the outermost one, and worked out what she thought it did, which was something to do with holding the threads in tension, and she put the tip of her wand to it and very gently started to unpick it.

The stone bit her.

There was no other word for it. A shock came up out of the rune and through her wand and up her arm like grabbing an electric fence, and it threw her backward off her knees into the salal and knocked every bit of breath out of her. She lay there on her back under the tarp with the rain drumming on it, staring up at the blue plastic, and couldn't move her right arm for about five minutes.

When she could, she sat up and looked at the stone.

It hadn't hurt her. That was the thing she kept coming back to, afterward. It could have. She knew enough about wards to know exactly how much power was sitting in that stone, and it could have stopped her heart. It had picked her up and set her down on her back, hard enough to make the point and no harder.

The same as the gun. The same as the dirt.

*It's him,* she thought. *It's exactly like him.*

She started again. Slower.

By dawn on the second day she'd worked out that the nine runes weren't nine separate things. They were one thing with nine faces, like a crystal, and you couldn't take any one of them out without the other eight noticing and closing up the gap. By noon she'd worked out that the stone was talking to the other stones. All the time. A constant, steady murmur running along the threads in four directions, too fast and too fine to read, like listening to a hundred people whispering in a language you didn't speak. By that evening she'd tried cutting the northern thread, very carefully, a clean slice with her best severing hex, and watched in pure disbelief as the cut end lay there for about ten minutes and then began, slowly, to grow back. It reached out from the stone like a root. It found the other half. It knit.

It had healed itself. It had healed itself in an hour.

She sat under the tarp and laughed until she cried a little, and then she drank the rest of Emily's coffee and started again.

On the third night she got it.

She'd realized, somewhere around three in the morning, that she'd been going at it like a curse-breaker. Like the stone was a lock and she was the key. And that was the mistake. The stone was a living thing, or the nearest a ward came to one, and what it wanted was to *work*. So she stopped trying to open it. She stopped trying to break anything. She took the hex she'd built in Lima, the seam-finder, the one he'd caught in the air in the clearing and looked at with interest and thrown back at her, and she pulled it apart and rebuilt it, in the cold, under the tarp, by lantern light, into something that went looking for the *rhythm* instead. The murmur. The pulse that ran between the stones. And she built it to match the pulse, and hold it, and keep holding it, so that the threads would go on hearing it even after she'd cut them.

Then she cut all four threads at once.

The stone hummed. The threads went slack. The ward on the far end of each one went on listening, and heard the pulse she was feeding it, and thought nothing had happened.

And she reached down with her wand and very gently, one at a time, from the outside in, unpicked the nine runes.

It took her four hours. It was dawn by the time she finished. When the last rune went the stone stopped humming, all at once, like a refrigerator shutting off in a quiet house, and she felt the silence of it go out through the ground under her knees, and she let go of the pulse she'd been holding for four hours and the far ends of the threads felt it go, and she felt them, very faintly, a long way off, *notice*.

Then nothing. Just a beach stone in the roots of a spruce.

She sat down in the wet moss.

Sam was there. She didn't know how long he'd been there. He was crouched just outside the tarp in the grey dawn light with the rain running off his bare shoulders, and he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read.

"Is it done?" he asked.

"It's done." Her voice came out a croak. "It's dead. That one's dead."

"That one."

"There are more." She lifted a hand and pointed east, vaguely, at the forest, at the whole peninsula. "Lots more. It's a net. That was one knot."

Sam looked east for a long time.

"Can you do the others?"

Layla looked at her hands. Her fingertips were raw. There was a burn on the heel of her right palm in the shape of the first rune, where the stone had bitten her on the first night, and it hadn't healed, and she had a feeling it wasn't going to for a while.

"Some of them," she said. "Maybe. The ones near people. The ones near the Cullens. The ones near my house." She swallowed. "Not all of them, Sam. I'd be here till I died."

"That's enough," said Sam.

He held out a hand. She took it, and he pulled her up out of the moss, and she stood there on the headland in the dawn with the dead stone at her feet and the sea going grey to silver behind her and her whole body shaking with exhaustion.

"Thank you," Sam said. He said it very simply, the way he said everything. "It's ours again."

"He's going to know," Layla said.

"Let him," said Sam.

### III. Abandoned Vehicle

*Forest Road 29, above the Sol Duc — Saturday, December 9, 2006*

The call came in at seven-forty in the morning from a deer hunter named Wally Kreig, who'd been driving up 29 before dawn to sit in a blind he'd been sitting in for thirty years, and who wanted the chief to know, at some length, that somebody had left a pickup truck in his turnout.

"*His* turnout," Charlie repeated.

"It's federal land, Charlie, I know it's federal land. I've been parking there since Nixon."

"I'll take a look, Wally."

He'd already known which truck it was going to be before he hung up the phone. He drove up anyway, because that was the job, and because some part of him he didn't want to look at too closely wanted to see it for himself.

It was sitting at the back of the gravel turnout above the river, nose to the trees, the way you'd park if you were planning to walk in from there. A 1979 Ford F-250, black, high off the ground, with a dent in the tailgate. A magnetic sign on the driver's door with an elk inside a circle and the words OLYMPIC ELK POPULATION SURVEY and a phone number with a 206 area code. Two weeks of fir needles on the hood and roof. Two weeks of rain. The tires had sunk an inch into the gravel.

Charlie walked around it twice with his hands in his jacket pockets. Then he put his hand flat on the hood. Cold as a stone. He looked in the windows.

A clipboard on the bench seat, with tally sheets clipped to it, columns of numbers in neat blocky handwriting. A thermos. A pair of good binoculars on the dash. A paper coffee cup from Darlene's, empty. A cassette sticking halfway out of the tape deck. He leaned in close to read the spine. *Physical Graffiti.*

"Huh," Charlie said, to nobody.

He'd had that album. Back in high school. He'd worn the tape out.

He stood there for a while in the cold with the river going by below and the rain ticking on the gravel, and thought about the man who'd parked it. The booth in the back of the diner, the wall behind him and the door in front. Pie for breakfast. *It's practically a salad.* A firm handshake, and green eyes that had rested on his for exactly one second longer than eyes did, and then a blink, quick, like a man who'd reached for a stair that wasn't there.

And then, a week after that, on the Sunday after Thanksgiving, Layla Brennan sitting at his kitchen table at ten o'clock at night with her face white and her hands wrapped around a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking, telling him about it.

*There's a man,* she'd said. *He's like me. Except he's— Chief, he's not like me. He's like nothing I've ever seen. He knows about you. He knows I told you. And he's using it.*

*Using it how?*

*He says if I don't help him when he asks, he'll make it go away. All of it. He'll make you forget. Or he'll tell the people I answer to, and they'll do it, and worse.*

Charlie had sat there and let that go through him. He'd thought he'd be angry. He'd been angry, sort of. Mostly he'd been tired.

*So I'm the hostage,* he'd said.

Layla had looked at him with her chin up, the way she did, and said, *Yeah. I'm sorry. You're the hostage.*

*Need to know,* Charlie had said, after a while. *I guess I needed to know.* And then, because he was a cop, and because she'd told him everything she had, the voice and the coat and the badge that wasn't a badge: *What's he look like?*

*Tall. Six-two, maybe. Dark hair, short. Green eyes. English. He was drinking coffee from the diner the whole—*

*Pie for breakfast,* Charlie had said.

She'd stared at him.

*I had coffee with him on Tuesday,* Charlie had said. *He's the elk guy.*

He thought about that now, standing in the turnout with his hand on the cold hood of the man's truck. He'd stood at the end of that booth and shaken hands with him and told him to watch out for bears.

He'd felt *something*. He'd known it then, in his gut, the way you know when somebody in a traffic stop is about to run. Something about the way the guy sat. The way he'd blinked.

He went back to the cruiser and called the number on the door.

It rang four times and went to a machine. A man's voice, English, pleasant, a little bored. *You've reached the field office of the Olympic Elk Population Survey. We're out counting. Leave a message and we'll ring you back.*

The voice did something to the back of his neck. He left a message anyway. Name, rank, number. *Your vehicle's been sitting in a turnout on Forest Road 29 for two weeks, Mr. Page, and I'd like to know you're all right.* He didn't know why he added that last bit. He meant it, kind of, and he didn't want to.

Then he called Vern Talley, who he'd gone to the academy with and who was a sergeant with the State Patrol now in Olympia, and asked him to run the plate.

Vern called back in twenty minutes. "Registered to a James A. Page. PO box in Seattle. Transferred November seventeenth from a Dale Schroeder in Port Angeles, cash sale, all the paperwork's in order. Insured. Washington driver's license, clean. No wants, no warrants, not so much as a parking ticket."

"Huh," said Charlie.

"What's he done?"

"Nothing," Charlie said. "He left his truck in the woods."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"You want me to dig?"

Charlie looked at the black truck through his windshield. "Is there anything to dig?"

"Not that I can see. Whole thing's clean as a whistle." Vern paused. "Real clean, Charlie. Like, I'd marry this guy's paperwork."

"Yeah," said Charlie. "That's what I figured."

He thanked Vern and hung up and sat there for a while in the cruiser.

Then he got on the radio and called for Jimmy's wrecker out of Beaver, and waited the forty minutes it took him to get there, and watched Jimmy hook the big Ford up and haul it down the mountain, and followed it all the way back into town to the fenced lot behind the station.

It wasn't really his jurisdiction. Forest Road 29 was Forest Service land, and if anybody was going to tow anything off it, it should have been them. He didn't call them. He signed the impound sheet himself, and wrote *ABANDONED VEHICLE* in the box at the top in block capitals, and put the keys, which Jimmy had found taped inside the rear wheel well, in an envelope in his own desk drawer.

If the man wanted his truck back, he could come and ask for it.

At the front desk. In Charlie's building. In daylight.

---

Sunday dinner was at his place again. Sue's idea. Sue had decided, somewhere in the last month, that Sunday dinner at Charlie's was a thing now, and Charlie had decided he wasn't going to argue with her about it, because when he came home on Sundays the house smelled like food and there were people in it, and he'd gone a long time without either.

Seth was there, and Layla. And Leah, which was new.

Leah Clearwater had come to Sunday dinner at Charlie Swan's house exactly once before, the week after her father's funeral, and she'd sat at the end of the table and said four words all night and left before dessert. Tonight she came in behind her mother with her hands in the pockets of her jacket and her face shut like a door, and said "Hi, Chief," and sat down in the same chair at the end of the table, and Charlie said "Leah," and passed her the potatoes, and neither of them made a big deal of it.

Layla had bandages on both hands.

Charlie noticed it when she reached for the salt. White gauze wrapped around the palms, and the fingertips red and raw where they stuck out of it, like somebody who'd been working with rope without gloves. There was a burn on the heel of her right hand, a shiny pink mark in a shape he couldn't make out. She saw him looking and put her hand in her lap.

"I found his truck yesterday," Charlie said.

Seth dropped his fork.

It clattered off his plate onto the floor, and he bent down to get it and hit his head on the edge of the table on the way back up, and sat there rubbing his skull and looking at Charlie with his mouth open. Layla had gone very still. Sue looked from one of them to the other.

Leah, at the end of the table, didn't move at all. But her eyes came up from her plate and fixed on him.

"Where?" said Layla.

"Turnout on 29. Above the Sol Duc. Been sitting there two weeks, near as I can tell." Charlie cut a piece of chicken. "Wally Kreig called it in. Said it was in his spot."

"Was he—" Seth stopped. "Was there anybody—"

"No. Nobody. Truck was locked. Cold. Keys taped in the wheel well." Charlie ate the chicken. "Had it towed down to the station."

"You *towed* it?"

"It was abandoned."

"Chief," said Layla carefully. "That's his truck."

"I know whose truck it is." Charlie put his fork down. "I called the number on the door. Got an answering machine. I ran the plates. He's got a Washington license and a PO box in Seattle and the cleanest paperwork my buddy in Olympia ever saw. And he hasn't been in Darlene's in two weeks. She's still got a whole pie in her freezer with his name on it. He ordered it the week before Thanksgiving and never picked it up." He looked around the table. "So I towed it. If he's gone, it's an abandoned vehicle. If he's not gone, he can come down to the station and get it from me."

"From *you*," Seth said.

"From me."

There was a silence.

"Charlie," said Sue quietly, "is that a good idea?"

"Probably not." He picked his fork back up. "It's my town."

Layla was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read. It was something between alarm and something else, something that looked a little like the way Bella used to look at him when she was about eleven and he'd said something she thought was dumb and brave at the same time.

"He's not gone," said Leah.

Everybody looked at her.

She was still sitting with her fork in her hand and her eyes on Charlie. "He's not gone," she said again. "He left. That's different."

"Two weeks, Leah," said Seth.

"I don't care. He's not gone." She went back to her food. "People who are gone don't tape their keys in the wheel well."

Charlie looked at her for a moment. Then he nodded slowly. He'd been thinking the same thing since yesterday morning. You didn't tape your keys inside your wheel well if you were never coming back. You taped them there if you'd left in a hurry and wanted to be able to find them again.

"His wards are still up," Layla said.

She said it to her plate, mostly. Charlie didn't know what a ward was, exactly. He'd figured out from context over the last month that it was a kind of spell you put on a place, like a lock or an alarm, and that the man had put a lot of them up, and that they were the thing that had caught the vampire in the woods. He didn't want to know more than that. He was trying very hard to stay at need-to-know.

"How do you know?" asked Sue.

"Because I've been taking them down," Layla said. She lifted her bandaged hands off her lap and looked at them. "That's what this is. I've done three. One on the reservation, for Sam. One in the trees behind my house. And one up on the hill behind this house, Chief, about two hundred yards up the slope, in the roots of a big cedar." She looked at him. "He had one of them pointed right at your back door."

Charlie felt the hair go up on his arms.

"I took it out on Friday," Layla said. "It took me two days. I'm doing two more this week, up by the Cullens'. And then I'm done, because I can't do any more. I physically can't. They're—" She stopped. "They're really hard."

"How hard?" Charlie asked.

She thought about it for a while.

"I've broken curses on tombs that killed four people," Layla said. "That were laid down by people who'd spent their whole lives making sure nobody would ever break them. I've never, in my life, worked on anything as hard as the dumbest piece of this man's net." She put her hands back in her lap. "And they're still up. All the rest of them. Running, all over the peninsula. I can feel them. People who leave a place for good take their wards with them."

"Or they die," said Seth. "Maybe something got him. Maybe he's dead."

"Nothing got him," Leah said, without looking up.

"You don't know that."

"Yes, I do." Leah stabbed a potato. "You weren't on the ground, Seth. You were on the ground, sure, but you were too busy crying to look at him. I looked at him. Nothing got him."

"I wasn't *crying*—"

"Seth," said Sue.

Seth shut up.

Charlie looked at Layla's hands, wrapped in white gauze. At the red raw fingertips. He thought about a cedar tree two hundred yards up the slope behind his house, that he'd walked past a hundred times on his way up to the creek to fish, and something cut into its roots, pointed at his back door.

"This guy's going to come back," he said slowly, "and find out you broke his stuff."

"Yeah," said Layla.

"And then what happens?"

"I don't know." She didn't look away from him. "I really don't, Chief."

Charlie thought about that.

"Okay," he said finally. "Then when he does, I'd like to be there."

"Charlie—" said Sue.

"I'm the hostage," Charlie said. "Hostage ought to get a say."

Nobody said anything to that. After a minute Sue reached over and put her hand on his arm, and left it there, and he let her, and they finished dinner.

### IV. The Simple Ones

*The ridge above the Cullen house — Monday, December 11, 2006*

"It's a hole," said Alice. "It's the same as him."

She was standing at the edge of the clearing with her arms wrapped around herself in a coat she didn't need, looking at the old Douglas fir where Layla was kneeling, and her face had the unfocused, unhappy look it got when she was trying to see something and couldn't.

"I can see the tree," Alice said. "I can see the whole ridge. I can see Carlisle standing there holding your lantern for the next three hours, and Edward coming up at two o'clock to bring you a sandwich from the kitchen, which is a lovely thought, Edward, but she's going to drop it in the mud. But right there—" She pointed at the base of the tree, at a place where the bark had grown over in a thick knotted burl. "There's nothing. It's like someone cut it out of the picture with scissors."

"That's it, then," Layla said. "That's the anchor."

"I hate it," said Alice simply, and went back down the hill to the house.

Carlisle held the lantern.

He'd asked to. He'd stood on the porch that morning, when Layla's truck pulled in, and asked if he could watch, with such open, unembarrassed eagerness that she hadn't been able to say no. He'd been a doctor for three hundred years, he'd told her, more or less, and before that the son of a man who'd hunted the things that hid in the dark, and in all that time he had never once been allowed to watch someone like her work.

So now he stood beside the fir in the cold with the lantern held up, perfectly steady, in a way no human arm could have held it for three hours, and watched her with an attention so complete it was almost a weight on her back.

She didn't mind. It was kind of nice. Most people got bored after twenty minutes.

This one was in the burl. Not a stone this time. He'd gone into the living wood, somehow, under the bark, and cut the runes into the heartwood of the tree and let the bark grow back over them, and she didn't know how he'd done that without killing the tree, and she didn't know how he'd done it in a week. Trees didn't grow bark that fast.

It took her most of the morning just to see them.

"May I ask something?" Carlisle said, at about eleven.

"Sure."

"You said, last week, that you'd found what it was looking for. What it was waiting for. The key, you called it."

"Yeah." She sat back on her heels and flexed her hands. They hurt. Everything hurt. She'd been doing this for eleven days. "I worked it out on the second one. The one behind Charlie's house. It took me all night."

"What is it?"

She looked up at him. "You," she said. "Sort of. Well. Not you. Your kind. But only some of you."

He waited.

"It's blood," Layla said. "Human blood. But it's blood in a particular state. It's looking for human blood that's moving through a body with no heartbeat." She held up her raw hands and turned them in the lantern light, the way she'd turn a stone she'd found in a riverbed. "That's it. That's the whole key. A heartbeat with blood in it, that's a human. Or Nessie. It leaves them alone. No heartbeat and no human blood, that's you, and Edward, and Esme, and everybody in the house who's been drinking deer for fifty years; it doesn't even see you. You could dance on it. And no heartbeat *with* human blood in you—"

"Someone who's fed," said Carlisle quietly. "Recently."

"Somebody who's killed," Layla said. "Yeah."

Carlisle was quiet for a long time.

"He built a net," he said finally, "that only catches murderers."

"It catches murderers of one particular species." Layla looked at the burl. "But yeah."

"And it leaves us out deliberately."

"It leaves you out *exactly*. You'd think there'd be some slop. Some margin. There isn't." She shook her head. "It's beautiful, Carlisle. I hate saying it. That key's the most elegant piece of magic I've ever seen. It's like a poem. And I'm sitting here in the mud taking it apart with my bare hands."

"Should you be?"

She looked up at him.

Carlisle was looking at the tree, not at her. His face was troubled. "I wonder," he said slowly, "whether we're right to ask you to do this. Rosalie's right that it's a strange thing to have around the house. A stranger's spell, circling the place where Renesmee sleeps, listening. I can understand Sam's feeling about his beach. And Edward—" He hesitated. "Edward finds it very hard, you know. To be near a thing he can't hear. He's spent a hundred years knowing what everyone around him is thinking. This man is the first person he's ever met who's simply... quiet. And he's left his quiet all over the hillside behind our home."

"I know."

"But I keep thinking of the nomad," Carlisle said. "The one with the hikers. And the one before him, and the one before that. And the young ones in Seattle, last summer. And all of the ones I didn't know about." He looked down at her. "This thing in the tree catches them. Only them. It's been sitting on this ridge for three weeks, keeping out exactly the kind of creature I'd most want kept out of this town. And we're taking it down because we don't like the man who put it here."

Layla didn't answer for a while. She looked at the burl.

"The wolves did fine before," she said. "Jacob says. They've been doing it for seventy years."

"The wolves did very well," said Carlisle gently. "And people still died."

She didn't have anything to say to that. So she went back to work.

---

She got the burl at about four in the afternoon, the same way she'd gotten the others: the matched pulse, the four threads cut in the same instant, the runes unpicked one by one from the outside in while she held the rhythm steady on the far ends of the lines so they'd think nothing had happened. It went quicker than the first one. She'd had practice. It still took six hours, and when it was done she lay down flat on her back in the moss beside the fir and didn't move for twenty minutes, and Carlisle sat beside her and didn't say anything, which she appreciated more than she could tell him.

The second Cullen anchor was down by the river, in a boulder at the water's edge, and she did it the next day, and it took seven hours, and Edward brought her a sandwich at two o'clock and she dropped it in the mud.

That made five.

On the thirteenth, she drove up to the turnout above the Sol Duc to try for six.

She hadn't planned to. She'd told Charlie five, and Sam five, and Carlisle five, and she'd meant it. But she'd woken up at four in the morning on the thirteenth with her hands throbbing and the thought in her head, clear as a bell: *That's the one he checked.*

She didn't know how she knew. She'd felt it, maybe, the first time, three weeks ago, driving up the road. The pressure change. Like weather. That had been the first anchor she'd ever felt, and it had been the only one she'd been able to feel from a moving car. It was stronger than the others. It *had* to be stronger than the others. And if she could get that one, she'd know something. She didn't know what. Something about him.

So she drove up there in the dark, past the empty turnout where the black truck had sat for two weeks, now just tire ruts in the gravel, and walked three hundred yards into the trees with her wand lit until she found the hemlock with the root that stuck up out of the moss like a knuckle.

She knelt down. She put out her hand.

And the anchor looked at her.

That was the only way she could ever describe it, afterward. All the others had been machines. Beautiful machines, the most beautiful she'd ever seen, but machines: they hummed, they listened, they bit if you got careless. This one *looked*. She put her hand out over the moss and felt something under the root turn its attention toward her, slow and enormous and absolutely calm, the way a sleeping animal opens one eye. And beneath it, deeper, under the anchor, under the stone and the root and the moss and the soil, she felt for the first time the thing that all the anchors were tied to.

She understood it instantly, kneeling there with her hand out in the dark and her whole body gone cold. The anchors were only the knots. The net itself was under everything, laid through the bedrock of the peninsula like veins, so deep and so vast and so finely made that she couldn't find the edges of it. She couldn't find *any* of it. She could only feel that it was there, the way you could feel the ocean under a boat at night.

The five anchors she'd spent two weeks killing were the simple part. The snares. The bit that grabbed.

She'd thought she was taking his net apart. She'd been snipping knots off the bottom of it.

She was still kneeling there, with her hand out, when the anchor bit her.

It was nothing like the others. The others had picked her up and set her down. This one put her out like a light. One second she was kneeling in the moss and the next she was lying on her back looking up at the black tops of the trees and the sky going grey through them, and her wand was six feet away, and her whole right side was numb from the shoulder to the hip, and there was frost on her eyelashes.

She'd been lying there for a while. She could tell. The light had changed.

She lay there and thought, with great clarity: *He's not even here, and that's twice he's put me on my back.*

She got up eventually. She found her wand. She walked the three hundred yards back to her truck, very slowly, holding on to trees, and sat in the cab with the heater going full blast until she could feel her fingers again.

Then she drove back down the mountain to Forks and went straight to the Cullens', because it was closest, and walked into the living room in the yellow raincoat with frost in her hair, and every vampire in the house looked up at her at once.

"I'm done," she said. "That's it. Five. I can't do any more. Nobody could."

"You're hurt," said Carlisle, already moving.

"I'm fine. I'm— Carlisle, I'm fine." She let him sit her down on the couch anyway. She was shaking. "Listen. All of you. There's a hole now. In his net. Five anchors gone, all in a line, more or less, from the beach at La Push across town and up here to the river. And the net's still up everywhere else. North, east, south, the whole rest of the peninsula. But there's a hole in it, right through the middle. Right through here." She looked around the room. "About twenty miles across. Coast to foothills. With this house in the middle of it."

"Good," said Jacob.

He was sitting on the floor by the fire with Renesmee in his lap, and he didn't look up. "Good. That's what we wanted. We've been doing this a long time without some English guy's magic trap. We'll do it again."

Layla looked at him for a long time.

"Yeah," she said. "I guess we will."

She didn't know why it made her feel cold. She'd just spent two weeks of her life and most of the skin on her hands doing exactly what they'd all asked her to do. She'd done something nobody else alive could have done. She knew that. She'd beaten five pieces of the best magic she'd ever seen.

She'd taken his locks off the doors, and left the doors open, and she didn't know who'd walk through.

### V. Snow

*The high country above the Bogachiel — Thursday, December 14, 2006*

The snow had come early to the mountains.

Down in Forks it was rain, the way it was always rain, grey and endless. But up here, above two thousand feet, where the old-growth gave way to thinner stands of silver fir and mountain hemlock and the ridges ran up toward the white shoulders of the Olympics, it had been snowing on and off for three days, and the ground was covered in a soft clean layer about four inches deep that squeaked under Jacob's paws.

Nessie loved it.

She was riding on his back, the way she always did when they went up into the high country, with her small hands knotted in the thick fur at the back of his neck and her knees tucked against his shoulders. She'd been laughing for about an hour. Every time a clump of snow fell off a branch she laughed. Every time he jumped a log she laughed. Every time a raven went over, croaking, she laughed and put one hand flat against his neck and showed him a picture of it, the raven, its black wings against the white sky, bright and sharp and perfect, the way she saw everything.

*Raven,* he thought at her, even though she couldn't hear him in here. *Yeah. Good one, Ness.*

She was three months old. She looked about two and a half. She had her mother's eyes, brown, and her father's bronze-colored hair in curls down to her shoulders, and she was the most important thing that had ever happened in the history of the world, and Jacob was aware that he was not entirely objective about this.

Bella was about fifty yards ahead of them, hunting.

He could see her through the trees, a pale shape moving between the trunks, too fast for any human eye to follow. She was on a buck. Jacob could smell it, a big mule deer, panicking, crashing downhill through the snow toward the creek, and Bella was flowing after it like water poured downhill, silent, utterly focused. She'd gotten good at this. She'd been a vampire for three months and she was already better at it than Emmett, and Emmett had been doing it for seventy years.

Jacob kept his distance and kept his nose up.

He liked these trips. He'd never tell Bella that, because she'd get that look on her face, the soft one, the one that made him want to crawl under a rock. But he liked them. Up here there was nobody else. Up here it was just the three of them, and the snow, and the quiet. Up here he didn't have to think about Sam, or the treaty, or the pack, or the Cullens' stink in his nose all day. Or about the English guy who'd been in his head through Leah's eyes three weeks ago, saying his name like he'd been saying it his whole life, *Jacob, I'm leaving, you can slow down.*

He didn't like thinking about the English guy.

He'd been gone nearly three weeks now. Leah went to the diner every morning, which was weird, and nobody said anything about it, which was weirder. Charlie had found his truck. And Layla had spent the last two weeks pulling his traps out of the ground one at a time with her bare hands, and coming home looking like somebody had dragged her behind a car, and now there was a hole in his net that ran right across the middle of the peninsula, and good. *Good.* Jacob had been glad about it. He was still glad about it. This was their land. They'd been guarding it since before there was a Forks, since before there was a Washington, and they didn't need some guy in a suit with a—

The wind shifted.

It came round from the south, up the valley, cold and wet and smelling of snow. And riding on it, faint and sweet and sick, burning in the back of his nose like bleach poured on rotten flowers—

Jacob stopped dead.

Nessie felt it. She stopped laughing. Her hands tightened in his fur.

He swung his head around, south, into the wind. His lips came back off his teeth on their own. Three of them. Three scents, braided together. Strangers. Nobody he knew. And under the sweetness, the copper. Old blood and new blood, human blood, so much of it on them that it was soaked in.

*Leah,* he thought, hard, into the link. *Seth. Leeches. Three. Red-eyes. High country above the Bogachiel, south ridge.*

Leah was there instantly. She was on the north line, miles away, but she was there, in his head, sharp and cold. *Coming. Where's Nessie?*

*On my back.*

*Then don't you dare go after them.*

He wasn't going to. He was already turning, putting his body between the wind and the kid, every muscle locked.

Up ahead, Bella had stopped too.

She'd dropped the deer. It lay in the snow at her feet with its neck at an angle and she was standing over it, perfectly still, with her head turned south. Then she looked back at him. Her eyes were very wide, the gold-red of a newborn coming down off the red, and he saw her see where Nessie was, on his back, and saw her face go blank with terror.

She was beside him in about one second. She didn't take Nessie off his back. She stood in front of them both, between them and the ridge, with her hands open and her teeth bared.

Jacob followed her eyes.

They were on the ridge across the valley. About four hundred yards. Three figures standing in the snow at the edge of the trees, in the open, not hiding, not moving, black against the white.

A small one in front. A woman, dark-haired, in a long sheepskin coat that came down past her knees. Behind her and to the left, a skinny one, a young-looking guy in a leather jacket with his hands jammed in the pockets, rocking on the balls of his feet like a junkie waiting for a fix. And on the right, the biggest leech Jacob had ever seen, nearly the size of Emmett, with a blond beard and a plaid wool coat like a lumberjack's out of an old picture, standing with his arms folded and his head tilted, like a bull looking over a fence.

Even at four hundred yards Jacob could see their eyes. Red. Bright, fresh, feeding red.

They were all three staring at Nessie.

The woman in the sheepskin coat was staring hardest. Jacob watched her face. Vampires' faces didn't move much, but this one did; it moved a lot, very fast. He saw her see the kid on his back. He saw her see the curls, and the size, and how small the kid was, and the way Bella was standing in front of her. And he saw the woman's face go through something like horror, and then, a second after the horror, something else. Something that looked like a woman who'd been walking along a road and found a wallet full of money lying in the snow.

Nessie put her hand against his neck. She showed him a picture: the three of them, on the ridge, small and dark, and the woman's face, and a feeling under the picture that Nessie didn't have a word for and he did. *Bad.*

*Yeah, Ness,* he thought. *Bad.*

"Jake," said Bella, very low. "Take her home."

He didn't move.

"Jacob. *Take her home.* Now."

*Not without you.*

She couldn't hear him. She didn't have to. She looked at him over her shoulder, a fast look, and her face did the thing it did when she'd decided something and there was no point arguing.

On the ridge, the woman in the sheepskin coat raised one hand, slowly, the way a person raises a hand at an auction.

Then all three of them turned and walked back into the trees, unhurried, and were gone.

Bella stood there for a long time with her hands open and her teeth bared at the empty ridge.

*They're gone,* Leah said in his head. She was running, flat out; he could feel the trees tearing past her. *Jake, are they gone? Are you okay? Is Nessie—*

*She's okay. They're gone. They walked off.*

*They* walked *off?*

*Yeah.*

*Which way?*

*South. Back down toward the Hoh. Back the way they came.*

There was a pause in the link, a long one, while Leah ran. He felt her think about it. He felt her go to the same place he'd gone, standing in the snow with the stink of them in his nose, and he felt her go cold.

*South,* Leah said. *Up from the Hoh. Up the middle. Jake—*

*I know.*

*Through the hole.*

*I know, Leah.*

*If the English guy's traps were still up between here and the Hoh, those three would be hanging upside down from a tree right now.*

Jacob didn't say anything to that. There wasn't anything to say.

Bella turned around. She came back to him and put both her hands in the fur of his neck on either side of Nessie's, and pressed her forehead against Nessie's back, and stayed like that for a while. She didn't say anything. She was shaking. He'd never seen a vampire shake before Bella. He'd never seen one do it since.

"They saw her," Bella said into her daughter's hair. "Jake. They *saw* her."

The note came two days later.

It was in the Cullens' mailbox, the big black one at the end of the long drive where it met the highway, on Saturday afternoon. Alice went down to get it, because she'd seen it there, finally, ten minutes before it arrived — she'd been blind as a bat about those three for two days, with Jacob and Nessie in the middle of everything — and she brought it back up to the house holding it by one corner between two fingers like something dead.

It was written on a page torn out of a hiking guide to the Olympic National Park. The page about the Hoh River trail. On the back, in pencil, in a flowing, slanted, old-fashioned hand, like something out of the bottom of a trunk in an attic:

*We saw the child.*

*We know what she is and we know the law, and so do you.*

*We will come to your house on the night of the twenty-first to see her for ourselves. Three of us. No more and no less.*

*If you refuse us, or come for us before then, or loose your dogs on us, we will go to Volterra instead and tell the masters what we saw. You know what they will do.*

*— D.*

They all read it. They passed it around the living room, one hand to the next, Carlisle and Esme and Edward and Bella, Rosalie and Emmett and Jasper and Alice, and then Jacob, who was standing by the fire in human form with his arms folded so tight across his chest his shoulders hurt, and then Leah, who'd come in the front door ten minutes ago without knocking and was standing by it, and then Layla, sitting on the arm of the couch with her bandaged hands in her lap, who read it twice and then handed it back to Carlisle without a word.

Nessie was upstairs, asleep. Seth was up there with her, sitting on the floor by her bed. Jacob had made him.

"The law," said Emmett. "What law? She's not—"

"She looks like one," said Edward.

He was standing by the glass wall, with his back to the room, looking out at the dark. His voice was very quiet.

"From a distance. In the snow. To someone who doesn't know any better. She looks like an immortal child." He turned around. His face was blank and white. "A child who's been changed. Made one of us, before she was grown. Frozen like that forever. They used to happen, a long time ago, and they were—" He stopped. "They couldn't be controlled. They couldn't be taught. They'd kill whole villages in a tantrum. The Volturi destroyed them. All of them. And anyone who made them. And anyone who protected them."

Edward nodded. "Tanya's mother. In Denali. She made one, a long time ago. A little boy. The Volturi came for them both. They made her daughters watch." He looked at Bella. "Anybody who knows the stories knows what happens. And those three know the stories."

"But she's *not* one," said Bella. Her voice cracked on it. "She's got a heartbeat. She's growing. Anybody who gets within ten feet of her can tell."

"Yes," said Edward. "Which is why they want to come here. Within ten feet of her."

Alice had gone very still on the couch, the way she did. Everybody in the room knew what it meant, and nobody said anything, and they all waited. Jacob found he was holding his breath. He didn't know why. Habit.

"If we say no," Alice said slowly, "they go."

She said it to nobody. Her eyes were wide and blank, looking at nothing.

"They go tonight. South, then east. They take a ship out of Seattle. Then it goes very fast. And then—" Her voice went thin. "The meadow. Our meadow, the big one, where we play baseball. It's covered in snow. The snow's stuck to the ground. And they're coming across it in their cloaks. All of them. Aro and Caius and Marcus and the guard and the wives and dozens of others behind them, and they're all looking at us, and—"

"Alice," said Jasper.

She shook her head hard, once, and came back. Her face was white. "That's if we say no."

"And if we say yes?" asked Carlisle.

Alice closed her eyes. She was quiet for a long time.

"Then they come," she said. "On the twenty-first. After dark. Three of them, in that coat and that jacket and that beard. They come up the drive and they come in the front door and they're standing in the living room. Here. I can see that part, mostly. And then it goes to static." She opened her eyes and looked straight at Jacob. "Because you're there. And Leah, and Seth. And Layla. All of you. I can't see what happens after they see her. I can't see anything after that at all."

"We kill them," Jacob said.

The whole room looked at him.

"We kill them," he said again. "That's what happens after. They come in that door, they look at her, and then we kill them. All three. Right here. Leah and Seth and me, and Emmett, and Jasper. They don't walk out of here. Nobody goes to Italy."

"Nobody gets away."

"Somebody always gets away, dog." Rosalie's eyes were black. "You don't know what they can do. You don't know if one of them has a gift. You don't know if they've told somebody else already, or left a letter somewhere, or a friend waiting at the bottom of the hill who goes to Volterra if they don't come back down it by midnight. *I* would have. Any of us would." She looked at Alice. "Can you see that? If we try?"

Alice had already been looking. "No," she said. "Static. You're all in it."

"Then we can't know."

"We can know that if we say no, they go to Italy," Edward said. "We know that for certain."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"We let them come," said Carlisle finally. "We let them see her. We let them see that she's alive, and growing, and what she is. And then we talk to them." He looked around the room. "And we find out what they actually want. Because if all they wanted was to go to Volterra, they'd have gone. They didn't. They wrote us a note."

"They want something," said Jasper softly.

"Yes."

"I watched her face," said Jacob. "The one in the coat. On the ridge. When she saw Nessie." He made himself say it. "She looked like she'd found money."

Carlisle nodded slowly. "Then we'll find out how much."

Layla hadn't said anything the whole time. She was sitting on the arm of the couch with her bandaged hands in her lap and her face grey, looking at the note in Carlisle's hand. Jacob saw Edward look at her, once, and then look away, the way he did when somebody was thinking something loud and private.

"They came up from the south," Layla said.

Everybody looked at her.

"Jacob said. They came up from the Hoh, and they went back toward the Hoh. Up the middle of the peninsula." Her voice was very flat. "Right up the hole. Right up the line where I took the anchors out."

"Layla," said Carlisle gently. "You don't know that."

"Yes, I do." She looked up at him. "There's no other way in, Carlisle. Not for three red-eyes walking around for two days in the high country. Everywhere else, the net's still up. Everywhere else, they'd have been caught." She looked down at her hands. "If I'd left those five stones alone, those three would be hanging upside down in the woods right now. Waiting for him."

"He's not here," said Jacob. "He's gone. They'd have hung there till they rotted."

"Or until he came back," Leah said, from the door.

It was the first thing she'd said since she came in. Everybody turned to look at her, and she looked back at them, arms crossed, chin up, and didn't look the least bit sorry.

"He's coming back," Leah said. "I don't care what anybody thinks. He left in the middle of a piece of pie."

"Leah," said Jacob.

"I'm just saying." She shrugged. "Wouldn't be the worst thing, right about now. Would it."

Nobody answered her.

Jacob looked around the room at all of them. At Bella, with her arms wrapped around herself. At Rosalie, with her black eyes on the stairs where Nessie was sleeping. At Edward, by the window, and Alice, white-faced on the couch, and Carlisle with the note. At Layla, looking at her hands.

He hated it. He hated every word of it. He'd been glad, three days ago, when Layla killed the last stone. He'd said *good* out loud.

He looked at the dark window, out at the forest, and thought about the man in the clearing with the vampire under his arm like a plank. He thought about how fast that man had put Leah and Seth on the ground. How little he'd cared.

*Wouldn't be the worst thing.*

"Fine," Jacob said. "We let them come."

### VI. Solstice

*The Cullen house — Thursday, December 21, 2006, 7:38 p.m.*

The room tasted like a fuse.

That was how Jasper had always thought of it, when he thought about it at all, which was as seldom as he could manage. Other people saw a room. He *tasted* it. Every person in it gave something off, the way a fire gives off heat, and it came to him as a flavor at the back of his throat, or a weight on his chest, or a pressure behind his eyes, and after a hundred and forty-three years he could no more stop sensing it than he could stop seeing.

Right now the living room of the house tasted like the moment before a fuse catches. Copper and sulfur and held breath.

He'd been working on it since six. Since the sun went down at twenty past four and the first snow of the winter began to come down outside the glass wall, big soft flakes falling straight through the dark, the first ones in Forks that had actually stuck. He'd been standing at the foot of the stairs for an hour and a half with his hands loose at his sides, doing what he did. Taking the edge off. Turning things down. A little calm into Esme, who was frightened for everyone. A little steadiness into Bella, who was standing by the piano with her daughter on her hip and so much terror coming off her it was like standing next to a furnace. A great deal of calm into Jacob, who was by the fire in a T-shirt and cutoffs with his arms crossed, and had been on the edge of phasing for ninety minutes, and who would have hated Jasper with all his heart if he'd known how hard Jasper was working on him.

Rosalie he left alone. Rosalie's fear had gone all the way through itself and come out the other side as a cold, clear, glittering fury that Jasper didn't want to touch. Rosalie would be fine. Rosalie would be better than fine. If it came to it, Rosalie would be the first one moving.

Emmett beside her was easy; Emmett was a big warm bonfire of readiness and Jasper had stopped worrying about him in about 1950. Carlisle was steady as stone. Edward was a thin high whine of tension, like a wire about to snap. Alice—

He didn't need to touch Alice. He knew exactly how she felt. She was sitting on the arm of the couch nearest him, and she was frightened, and under the fright there was the restless, circling frustration of somebody straining to see in the dark, and under that there was him. There was always him. He could feel her reaching out for his hand without moving, and he could feel her feel him reach back, and that was enough.

The wolves were in human form. Jacob by the fire. Seth by the stairs, near Jasper, near the baby, a big anxious puppy-smell of fear and resolve so pure and earnest it almost hurt. Leah by the front door.

Leah tasted like a knife.

She always had. Jasper had been in rooms with her since September and he'd never once sensed anything from Leah Clearwater that wasn't sharp. Anger, mostly. Bitterness, so old and so worn in that it had worn a groove. And underneath that, very deep, a grief so heavy that he'd stopped going anywhere near it after the first week, out of something he could only call respect.

Tonight, under all of it, there was something new. It had been there for a few weeks now, faint, and he'd been noticing it without knowing what to call it. Tonight it was strong. She was standing by the door with her arms crossed and her jaw tight and her eyes on the black window, and under the anger and the knife-edge there was something that tasted, very faintly, like *waiting*.

And then the witch. Layla was sitting on the far end of the couch with her bandaged hands in her lap and her wand in her hair, and Layla tasted, tonight, like guilt. Thick and grey and choking. She'd tasted like that for five days.

He gave her a little calm, too. It was the least he could do.

"They're on the drive," said Alice, very softly.

Every head in the room turned toward the door.

"Walking," said Alice. "All three. They're—" She stopped. Her face went tight. "They're thinking about the dogs. If the dogs are inside. Della's telling the other two to keep their hands where they can be seen. The young one doesn't like it." A pause. "Three minutes."

Edward's face had gone blank and sharp. He'd been able to hear them since they turned off the highway. "The young one's name is Ruben," he said. "He's thirsty. He hasn't fed since Tuesday. He's thinking about Seth." His jaw tightened. "And about Layla. About how she smells."

Layla went pale.

"He won't touch her," said Carlisle quietly.

"No," said Edward. "He won't."

Jasper turned up the calm on Jacob a little more. Jacob's jaw was working. He could feel Jacob's whole body thrumming with heat, the wolf right under the skin, pushing.

Three minutes was a long time.

---

They came in out of the snow.

Esme opened the door for them. She'd insisted. It was her house, she'd said, in that soft voice that meant absolutely no discussion, and she'd open her own front door to whoever came to it. She stood aside with her hand on the doorknob and her face perfectly composed and the snow blowing in past her, and the three of them walked into the front hall one after another, stamping snow off their feet out of a hundred years of habit, and stood there.

The smell came in with them. Even Jasper, who'd fed on humans for nearly a century before Alice found him, felt his throat tighten at it. Fresh human blood. A lot of it. It was on their clothes, in their hair, under their nails. They'd fed this week.

The woman came first. Della. Small, five foot two or three, with dark hair cut blunt at her jaw and a narrow, clever, sharp-featured face, in the long sheepskin coat Jacob had seen on the ridge. Her eyes went around the room fast, the way Layla's did, and Jasper tasted her as she did it: a cold, bright, tightly controlled excitement, like a gambler at a table holding a good hand. Greed. A great deal of greed. And under it, carefully held down, fear. She knew what she was walking into. She'd decided the hand was worth it.

Behind her, the big one. Hask, Edward had said in the hour before, in a low voice; he'd heard the name in Della's head on the drive. Nearly seven feet tall. A yellow beard. A plaid wool coat with snow on the shoulders. He ducked his head going under the lintel and straightened up and stood behind Della's left shoulder with his huge arms folded and his face empty. He tasted like almost nothing. Patience. A bovine, endless patience. And loyalty, to the small woman in front of him, deep and simple and total. He'd do anything she told him. Jasper had known a lot of vampires like that. Maria had collected them.

And the young one. Ruben.

He came in last and he came in hungry. Jasper tasted it from across the room before the boy was through the door: thirst, a raw constant itch of it, like a man who hadn't slept in a week. Restlessness. Contempt for everyone in the room, thick and sour. And a hot, mean, eager streak of aggression that Jasper knew very well, because he'd felt it in a hundred newborns in the Mexican wars and once, a long time ago, in himself. Ruben was wiry and dark and looked about twenty, in a leather jacket with the collar turned up, and as he came into the light of the living room and Jasper saw his hands, Jasper's own instincts went cold.

There were scars on the backs of the boy's hands. Crescent-shaped. Pale, hard, gleaming, the way bite scars gleamed on their skin and on nothing else. A dozen at least. More on his neck, above the collar.

He'd fought. Somewhere, for someone. He'd been in an army.

Jasper put a hand very lightly on the newel post of the stairs and kept his face still.

"Well," said Della. She had a dry, light, husky voice with a Southwestern twang in it, Arizona or New Mexico. "This is cozy."

"You're welcome in our home," said Carlisle. He'd come forward, a few steps into the middle of the room, his hands loose and open at his sides. "For the purpose you asked. My name is Carlisle Cullen. This is my family."

"I know who you are." Della's eyes went past him. They skipped over Emmett, over Jasper, over the wolves, and landed on Bella. And on the child on Bella's hip. "So that's her."

Bella's arms tightened.

Renesmee looked back at the strange woman with wide brown eyes. She didn't cry. She didn't hide her face in her mother's neck. She looked at Della with a steady, solemn, assessing interest that was so much like her father's that Jasper felt Edward feel it from across the room.

"Come closer," said Carlisle. "Slowly. You asked to see her. See her."

Della came closer.

She walked across the living room in a dead silence, slowly, a step at a time, with every vampire in the room tracking her and every wolf in the room vibrating with heat, until she was about ten feet from Bella and the child. And she stopped there, and tilted her head, and listened.

Jasper felt it happen in her. The moment she heard it. The fast, light, birdlike flutter of a heartbeat, coming from the little girl on Bella's hip. He felt Della's shock, sharp and bright, and then her nose working, and the smell of the child's blood reaching her, human and not quite human, and a second wave of shock on top of the first. Her eyes went over Renesmee, the flush in her cheeks, the warmth coming off her, the curls that had grown an inch since the ridge a week ago.

"Well, I'll be *damned*," Della said softly.

Ruben made a noise behind her. A sort of laugh. "Told you," he said. "Told you it wasn't one of those. Hasn't got the look."

"Shut up, Ruben."

"She's half," said Carlisle. "Half human. Her mother carried her. She was born. She grows; she's grown since you saw her. She eats, and sleeps, and she'll be grown in a few years, and then she'll stop." He kept his voice very steady and very gentle. "She's no immortal child, Della. She's something else. She's something new. And you have your answer."

Della looked at the child for a long time.

Then she straightened up and turned around and looked at Carlisle, and Jasper felt the shock go out of her, and felt what replaced it, and his stomach turned over.

Pleasure. Bright, cold, calculating, and very pleased with itself.

"I've got *an* answer," Della said. "Sure. And it's a better one than I came in with." She smiled. It was a sharp little smile, and it didn't go anywhere near her eyes. "She's not one of those. So nobody's breaking that law. So Aro won't come out here with his whole parade and burn your house down for it." She tilted her head. "But you know what he *will* do, Doctor? When he hears about her?"

Carlisle said nothing.

"He'll want to see her." Della's voice was almost gentle. "Aro wants to see everything. Anything new. Anything strange. Anything he hasn't got a name for. You know that better than me; I hear you lived with him once. A child born of a human mother. A child with a heartbeat that grows. In a coven of eight, with yellow eyes, living in a glass house in the woods with a pack of trained dogs and a witch." Her eyes flicked to Layla on the couch. "And a mind-reader. And a girl who sees things coming. Oh, he'll want to see her. He'll want to see all of you. He'll come out here with his whole guard just to *look*. And he'll be so very, very grateful to whoever told him first."

"What do you want?" said Carlisle.

"There it is." Della smiled. "I knew you were the smart one."

Jacob had gone so still by the fire that Jasper could feel the effort in every muscle. Jasper poured calm into him like water into a cracked jar and felt it drain out through the cracks as fast as he could pour it.

"This is good country," Della said. "I've been on the move a long time, Doctor. The Southwest is all heat and sun and nobody goes anywhere on foot. California's crowded. Oregon's full of other people's covens. But this—" She spread her hands, a small wide gesture that took in the dark windows and the snow and the forest beyond. "Rain. Fog. Trails up into the mountains full of people who walk off them and get lost every year, and nobody's ever surprised. Logging roads. Little towns where the nearest real police are two hours away. It's *perfect*. And for seventy years nobody's been able to hunt it, because you and your dogs keep everybody out."

"No," said Carlisle.

"I haven't even said it yet."

"You don't need to."

"I'll say it anyway." Della's eyes didn't leave his face. "The three of us. The peninsula. Port Angeles to Aberdeen. We hunt as we please. You keep your yellow-eyed diet, and you call off your dogs, and you let us be. We don't touch your town; we're not fools. We don't come near this house. We'll be quiet as mice." The smile again. "And I forget I ever saw your little miracle. Forever. Not a word to Volterra. Ever."

"*No*," said Jacob.

It came out of him like something tearing. He'd taken a step forward from the fire before he knew he was doing it, and the heat was coming off him in waves; Jasper could feel the air in the room change temperature. Seth by the stairs had gone rigid. Leah by the door hadn't moved, but the knife-edge in her had gone thin and bright and very sharp.

"Jacob," said Carlisle, without turning his head.

"She wants us to let them *eat people*. On our land. In Forks. In La Push. My dad's in La Push, Carlisle. Charlie's in Forks—"

"I know, Jacob."

"Then tell her *no*—"

"I have," said Carlisle. He turned back to Della. "The answer is no."

"Then it's Volterra," said Della. She said it lightly, like a woman naming the time.

"You may find you don't leave this house," said Rosalie.

Everybody looked at her.

Rosalie hadn't moved from her spot by Emmett. She hadn't raised her voice. She was standing there with her hands loose and her face beautiful and blank and her black eyes on Della, and Jasper tasted her and felt the cold glittering fury in her go very, very still.

Della looked at her for a long moment.

"I thought you might say that," she said. "So let me save us all some trouble." She didn't take her eyes off Rosalie. "There's a letter. In Seattle. With a friend of mine, who owes me. Everything I saw on that ridge, and where, and who. If I'm not back in Seattle by the twenty-third to collect it, it goes to Italy." The smile. "So you can kill me, honey. You can kill all three of us. You'll just be doing Aro's postage for him."

Jasper tasted her as she said it. He was very good at tasting people when they lied. Lies had a flavor, faint and metallic, like a cut on the inside of your mouth.

Della's words tasted of almost nothing. Smooth and flat and controlled. He couldn't tell. He honestly could not tell.

He glanced at Edward. Edward's face was frozen, and his eyes were on Della, and he was listening with everything he had.

And Jasper felt Edward's frustration, sharp and bitter. Felt him find nothing. Della was thinking very hard, on purpose, about a letter in an envelope in a drawer in a house in Seattle. Just that. Over and over. The envelope. The drawer. The house. Like a picture held up in front of a window. She'd known about Edward. She'd prepared for him.

The room was silent.

Jasper looked at it the way he'd been taught to look at a battlefield, a hundred and forty years ago, by a woman in Monterrey who'd taught him everything she knew about killing and nothing at all about anything else. Three of them. Ruben would go first; Ruben was already halfway there, Jasper could taste it, the thirst and the aggression wound together and wanting a reason. Hask would go wherever Della pointed. Della would run. Della was a runner. She'd be out of the glass wall before the first blow landed. And Emmett and Rosalie would take Hask, and the wolves would take Ruben, and Jasper would go after Della, and he'd catch her, probably, he was very fast—

And then the letter would go to Italy anyway. Or it wouldn't. And they'd never know.

He looked at Alice.

Alice's face had gone strange.

She'd been sitting on the arm of the couch all this time with her eyes half-closed, reaching, straining, trying to see past the static the wolves made, and he'd been feeling her frustration the whole time like a hand clenching and unclenching. But now something had changed. She'd sat up. Her eyes had come wide open, and they were fixed on Della, and on the two vampires behind her, and the feeling coming off her had gone from frustration to something else, something he'd never tasted from her before in fifty-eight years.

It tasted like shock.

"Alice," he said softly.

"They're gone," Alice whispered.

The room turned to look at her. Della frowned.

"I had them," said Alice. Her voice was thin and high. "Just now. All three. In the static. I had them in bits and pieces, standing here, talking, I had the next few minutes, I had them leaving, or not leaving, or—" She stopped. She was staring at the three of them. "And then they just stopped. All of them. All at once. Like the one in the woods. Like somebody cut them out with scissors."

"What's she talking about?" said Ruben.

"Alice—" said Edward.

"They don't have a future," Alice said. "None of them. Not one second of one."

And somebody knocked.

It came from behind them. From the front hall. Two short, sharp raps of knuckles on wood, the kind of knock you'd give on the door of a friend's office. *Knock knock.*

Every head in the room turned.

There was a man standing in the archway between the hall and the living room, with one hand raised to the doorframe where he'd knocked on it, and snow on his shoulders.

Jasper hadn't heard him come in. Nobody had heard him come in. Eight vampires and three wolves and a witch, and nobody had heard the door, or a footstep, or a breath. He was simply there, as if he'd always been standing in the archway and they'd only just looked.

He was tall. Dark hair, longer than Jasper had heard it described, and a week of dark beard on his jaw. A long black wool overcoat, filthy, torn at the left shoulder so the lining showed, with a dark stiff stain down the collar. Snow in his hair, melting. A cut along his left cheekbone, an inch and a half long, healing pink, that looked about a day old. Heavy black boots crusted with something gray that wasn't snow.

The smell of him came across the room a second after the sight. Woodsmoke. Diesel. Cold, a dry bitter cold, nothing like the wet cold of Forks. Gunpowder. And blood. A lot of blood, most of it old, none of it his.

And his own blood, underneath, from the cut on his face. Human blood. Hot and fresh and alive. His heart was beating. Jasper could hear it from across the room, slow and steady and unbothered, about sixty beats a minute.

Jasper tasted him.

He'd heard about this man for a month. He'd heard about him from three different people's memories and from Edward's report of them, and he'd stood on the porch with Edward that first night and listened to the forest with him, and he'd wondered what this man would taste like when he finally walked into a room. He'd expected something huge. Something cold and enormous, like an iceberg, or something hot and terrible, like a fire.

What he tasted was *tired*.

That was all, at first. A bone-deep, aching, hollowed-out exhaustion, the kind that came after weeks without real sleep. And a dry, low-grade irritation, the kind of feeling a man had when he came home late from a long trip to find the kitchen in a mess. And under that, steadying it, something very still and very flat and very controlled. A held breath. A hand on a door.

That was all. That was everything. Tired, annoyed, and steady.

It was so ordinary that it frightened Jasper more than anything he'd felt from anyone in years.

"Evening, all," said the man.

His voice was English. Low and hoarse, like he hadn't used it in a while.

"Sorry to drop in like this," he said. "I did say I'd knock."

### VII. Two Words

*7:51 p.m.*

Nobody moved.

Jasper felt the whole room seize up at once, the way a body seizes when it steps on something that moves. Shock, from every direction, a dozen different flavors of it hitting him all together. Carlisle's, still and careful. Esme's, frightened. Emmett's, bright and startled, already curdling into a grin he didn't know he was making. Rosalie's, cold. Bella's, a spike of fresh terror on top of the old. Edward's—

Edward's was the worst. Edward was staring at the man in the archway with his whole body gone rigid and an expression on his face that Jasper had never once seen there in fifty-eight years. And Jasper didn't need to read minds to know why. He could taste it. Edward had been listening to the three red-eyes, and to the wolves, and to the witch, and to every one of his family, the whole room roaring in his head the way it always did. And in the middle of all that noise, in the archway, where a man was standing with snow melting in his hair, there was a hole.

Nothing. No thoughts at all. A man-shaped silence, standing in the middle of the loudest room Edward had ever been in.

And from Leah, by the front door, about four feet from the man's left shoulder, something that came through all the shock like a bright clean note through static. Jasper tasted it and almost didn't believe it.

Relief. Hot, fierce, almost savage. And under the relief, something else, low and bright and very unwilling, that Jasper decided he'd think about later, or never.

The man in the archway looked around the room. He did it slowly, without hurrying, the way a man looks round a pub he's just walked into for the first time, getting the lay of it. His eyes went over Carlisle, over Esme, over Emmett and Rosalie and Edward. Over Bella and the child on her hip. Over Jacob, Seth, the witch on the couch.

Jasper felt the man's eyes touch him, briefly. They were very green. There was nothing in them at all.

They stopped on the three red-eyes.

"And you'll be the reason there's a hole in my net," the man said.

He considered that for a second. Then he glanced at the couch, at Layla, who had gone white as paper.

"No. Sorry. That's not fair. Miss Brennan's the reason there's a hole in my net." He looked back at the three of them. "You're just what came through it."

"Who the *hell*," said Della, "are you?"

She'd turned all the way around to face him. Jasper tasted her. All the cold bright gambler's confidence had gone out of her in a second, and what was left under it was fear, raw and fast and very sharp, and calculation running underneath the fear like a motor. She was looking at him and trying to work out what he was, and not getting anywhere, and she knew she wasn't getting anywhere, and that frightened her more than anything.

"Somebody who's had a very long month, love," the man said. "Let's not."

He lowered his hand from the doorframe.

"Right," he said, louder, to the room. "I'll keep this short, because I'm tired and I'm hungry and I've not had a proper wash since about the eighth. Everyone with red eyes, please stay exactly where you are." He paused. "Everyone without red eyes, you're welcome to move about as you like. I'd stay out of the middle of the room, though, if I were you."

Della didn't move.

Hask didn't move. Hask was looking at Della, waiting. He tasted, still, of almost nothing. Patience. Loyalty. A faint, puzzled, cow-like confusion.

Ruben moved.

Jasper felt it coming. He felt it the way you feel a wave coming when you're standing in the surf, the water pulling back from your ankles. He'd been tasting the boy since he walked in the door, the thirst and the aggression wound together, and for a few minutes, while Della talked, it had been wound tight and held down and waiting. And then a human had walked into the room.

A human. Alone. Hot, and alive, with a heartbeat you could hear from across the house. With fresh blood on his face from a cut that had opened again in the cold, a thin red line of it, just there on his cheekbone. In a room full of vampires who didn't drink people, and wolves who'd been told to keep their hands to themselves.

Jasper felt Ruben's thirst go up like a struck match.

He tried. He reached for the boy with everything he had and poured calm into him, a flood of it, enough to put a whole room of newborns to sleep, and it went into Ruben and hit the thirst and vanished like water on a hot stove. He'd felt that before. He'd felt it in the wars. When they were that hungry, and that young, and had fought that much, there was nothing in them left to calm.

"Ruben, *no*—" said Della.

Ruben went.

He went across the room so fast that even Jasper's eyes barely caught it. A blur, low and flat. Twenty feet of floor in less time than it took a human heart to beat once. His lips peeled back. His hands came up, those scarred hands, reaching for the man's throat.

The man's right hand came out of his coat.

There was something in it. Jasper saw it for a fraction of a second as it came up: a stick, long, a foot and a half maybe, pale grey, knotted along its length like a branch that had grown slowly in a hard place. A wand. Jasper had seen Layla's a dozen times now, a slim dark straight thing. This one was old. It looked old the way the mountains looked old.

The man pointed it at Ruben, who was about four feet from him and still coming.

And Jasper felt it.

It came out of nowhere. That was the thing he would remember. One instant the man in the archway tasted of exhaustion and irritation and a still, flat, steady calm, and the next instant, with no rise to it, no build, no warning at all, there was *hate*.

It hit Jasper like a wall falling on him.

It was the purest thing he had ever felt in his life. He had stood in the middle of armies. He had felt the bloodlust of a hundred newborns at once, screaming in their heads. He had felt Maria's cruelty, which had been the coldest thing he'd ever known, and his own guilt, a century of it, which had been the heaviest. He had felt grief, and rage, and terror, and despair, in every shade and flavor there was. He had never felt anything like this.

There was nothing else in it. That was what made it unbearable. There was no fear in it, no anger, no excitement, no doubt. It had no edges where it smeared into anything else. It was hatred the way a blade is sharp. A single, perfect, total desire, from the bottom of the man's soul to the top, aimed at the thing coming across the room at him with its hands out: *I want you dead.*

"*Avada Kedavra*," the man said.

He said it clearly. Two words. Not loud.

Jasper's knees went.

He hadn't felt his knees go since 1863. He hadn't felt light-headed since the night a woman with red eyes found him on a road outside Galveston. He felt both now. The room tilted. His vision went white at the edges, and his hand on the newel post clenched so hard the oak cracked under his fingers, and for one long impossible second he was sure he was going to fall down.

Green light.

It came out of the end of the pale wand and filled the room, a flash of it, vivid and horrible, a green like nothing in nature, and it hit Ruben in the chest in mid-air.

Ruben dropped.

There was no other word for it. One moment he was flying across the room with his hands out and his face twisted, and the next he simply wasn't flying anymore. Everything in him let go at once. He hit the hardwood floor face-first about three feet short of the archway, with a sound like a sack of stones dropped off a truck, and the floor cracked under him, and he slid. He slid the last three feet on his face across the polished boards, limp, with his arms trailing, and stopped against the toes of the man's black boots.

And lay there.

And the hate was gone.

It went as fast as it came. Jasper was still holding onto the cracked newel post, still swaying, his head still ringing, and it was simply gone, switched off like a light, and the man standing over the body in the archway tasted exactly the way he'd tasted ten seconds ago. Tired. Irritated. Steady. As if it had never been there at all.

As if he'd made it, on purpose, out of nothing, for the length of two words, and then put it back wherever he kept it.

The man looked down at the boy at his feet.

"I *had* been rather clear," he said mildly. "No?"

Then several things happened at once.

Della ran.

Jasper had known she would. She was a runner; he'd tasted it on her from the doorway. She went for the glass wall, the big south window that looked out over the river, so fast she was nearly there before Ruben had finished sliding. She didn't care about the glass. She was going through it.

And Hask went for the man.

Hask had been looking at Della, waiting to be told. And Della hadn't told him anything; Della was gone. And something in the big slow patient creature had looked at the boy lying on the floor and at the man standing over him, and come to its own conclusion. Jasper tasted it. Grief. Huge, slow, bewildered grief, like an ox's. And then rage, rising up out of the grief. Hask came across the room like a landslide, nearly seven feet of him, his huge hands out.

The man flicked the wand. Twice.

He didn't say anything this time. He didn't even look, the first time. The tip of the pale wand moved in a small sharp motion toward the south window, like a man flicking water off his fingers, and Della, about two feet from the glass, was no longer there.

Something small and dark hit the window with a tiny *tick* and dropped to the floor.

The second time he looked. He turned his head and looked at Hask, who was about eight feet from him and coming fast, and moved the wand again, and Hask was no longer there either. There was a sound like a marble dropped on a tile floor. Something small and pale bounced twice on the hardwood, *tock, tock,* and rolled, and came to rest about a foot from Ruben's outstretched hand.

The room was absolutely silent.

The man put the pale wand back inside his coat.

Then he stepped over Ruben's body, unhurried, and bent down, and picked up the small pale thing from the floor beside the dead boy's hand. He straightened up and walked across the room, through the middle of eight vampires and three wolves and a witch, none of whom moved a muscle, to the south window, and bent again, and picked up the small dark thing from the floor under the glass.

He held them both up in front of his face, on his open palm, in the light.

They were pebbles.

Two smooth river pebbles, about the size of the end of a thumb. One a dark, dense, slate grey. One paler, a speckled brown and cream, like a thrush's egg.

The man looked at them for a moment. He blew on them, once, gently, the way you'd blow dust off something you'd found on a beach. Then he dropped them into the pocket of his overcoat and patted the pocket twice.

"Lovely," he said.

He turned around and looked at all of them.

Jasper was still holding the newel post. He didn't think he could let go of it. He looked at the man, standing in the middle of the living room with snow melting on his shoulders and a dead vampire on the floor behind him and two living ones in his coat pocket, and he tasted the man again, very carefully, the way you'd touch a stove you'd just been burned by.

Tired. Steady. And a little bit hungry.

"Right," said the man. "Sorry about your floor, Mrs. Cullen."

Esme opened her mouth. Nothing came out. She closed it.

"I'll pay for it," the man said. "Obviously."

### VIII. Something to Take

*7:54 p.m.*

The first sound in the room was Emmett, laughing.

It wasn't much of a laugh. It came out of him in a short, startled, helpless bark, and he clapped a hand over his mouth right after, and stood there by the couch with his eyes huge over his fingers. Edward heard his mind. It was a single word repeated over and over in a tone of pure, appalled delight. *Pebbles. Pebbles. Pebbles. Pebbles.*

Rosalie didn't hit him. That was how Edward knew how badly she'd been shaken.

Nobody else made a sound.

The room was roaring. It always roared, for Edward; it had roared every day of his life since 1918, a hundred voices in his head at once, and he'd long since learned to sort them, to turn some down and some up, the way you'd tune a radio. But he'd never heard it like this. Every mind in the room had gone up to its highest pitch at once and was shouting the same few things in a dozen different voices.

*Dead. He's dead. He's actually dead.* That was Seth, by the stairs, over and over, with a horrified sort of awe.

*Gone gone gone they're all gone thank God oh thank God oh God.* That was Bella. No— Edward caught himself. He couldn't hear Bella. That was Esme.

*What was that green, what in God's name was that green.* Carlisle.

*Can't feel my legs. Can't feel my legs. What was that.* Jasper, still holding the cracked newel post, his mind a white ringing blank like the inside of a bell.

*Oh no. Oh no. He's going to look at me in a second. He's going to look at me.* Layla.

And Leah, by the door, the sharpest and loudest of all, in a voice Edward had never once heard come out of her in four months: *YES.*

And in the middle of all of it, standing in the middle of Esme's living room with snow dripping off his coat onto the floor, nothing.

Edward stared at him. He couldn't stop.

He'd known it would be like this. He'd known since the night in November, standing on the porch in the rain, that this man would be silent to him. He'd seen it in three people's memories. He'd thought he was ready for it. He hadn't been ready at all. It was like standing in a room full of people shouting and seeing one man in the middle of them with his mouth moving and no sound coming out. It was wrong in a way that went right down into his body. His eyes kept going to the man and his mind kept reaching for him, out of habit, a hundred years of habit, and finding nothing, and coming back, and reaching again.

Carlisle moved first.

He crossed the room to the archway, slowly, the way he'd cross a hospital ward toward a bed where something had gone wrong, and knelt down on the cracked floor beside the boy.

Ruben lay on his face with his arms flung out and his cheek against the boards. Carlisle put two fingers on his neck, out of a doctor's habit so old it predated Edward's birth. There was nothing there to feel. There never had been. Then he turned the boy over, gently, onto his back.

Edward heard his father's mind go very quiet.

Ruben's eyes were open. They'd been red, a fresh feeding red, bright as a traffic light. They weren't anymore. They'd gone a flat, dull, cloudy color, like the eyes of a fish on a slab. And the skin—

Edward had looked at the skin of his own kind for eighty-eight years, and he'd never thought about it. It was simply what they were. Hard, and white, and cold, and with a faint quality to it that he'd never had a name for, a sort of depth, as if there were a light very far down under the surface. Like marble with a candle behind it. In the sun it caught fire. In the dark it glowed, just slightly, if you knew how to look.

Ruben's skin had gone dull. Chalky. The light under it was gone. It looked like plaster.

"He's dead," Carlisle said softly.

He said it with a kind of wonder. Edward heard what was under it in his mind, and it was so complicated he couldn't take it all in at once. Grief, because Carlisle grieved every death, even this one. Horror, at the speed of it, and the ease. And under the grief and the horror, rising up through them like something coming up from deep water, something Edward had heard in Carlisle's mind only a handful of times in a century. Something that felt a great deal like joy.

"Actually dead," Carlisle said. "Truly." He looked up at the man standing over him. "What did you do to him?"

"Killing Curse," said the man.

"What does it do?"

The man looked down at him for a moment. He seemed to be deciding how much to say. Edward watched him decide, and couldn't hear a word of it, and it was maddening.

"Takes the soul out of a body," the man said. "Permanently. No mark, no wound, no fuss. Body stays. Everything else goes."

The room went very still.

Edward felt the words land in him. He didn't understand them at first. He heard them, and they went in, and they sat there, and then something in his chest went cold and then very hot.

"And if a body doesn't have one?" he heard himself ask.

The man looked at him.

It was the first time the man had looked straight at him. Edward felt the green eyes settle on his face and stay there. It was the strangest thing he'd ever felt. A man looking at him, *thinking* at him, very clearly; he could see it in the eyes. And none of it reaching him.

"Then there's nothing for it to take," the man said. "And nothing happens. Goes straight through you like light through a window." He tipped his head down at the body. "That was the hypothesis. I'd been chewing on it for about a month. Wasn't sure. Fifty-fifty, maybe."

"And if you'd been wrong?" said Carlisle quietly.

"Then he'd have got his hands on my throat, and I'd have had to do something messier." He shrugged. "I wasn't wrong."

"It found something," said Carlisle.

"It found something," the man agreed.

Edward sat down.

He didn't decide to. He found that he was sitting on the bottom stair beside Jasper, who was still holding the post, and that his hands were flat on his knees, and that he couldn't feel them.

He'd believed it for a hundred years. He'd believed it longer than he'd believed anything. Since 1918, since a bed in a Chicago hospital, since he woke up burning and came out the other side of the fire with no heartbeat and no breath and a thirst like a hole in the world. He'd known it, the way he knew the sky was up. They were damned. They were dead things walking. Whatever had been in him once, the boy who'd been Edward Masen, whatever God had put in him, the fire had burned it out and left a body that moved. It was why he'd left Bella in the woods. It was why he'd refused her for two years. It was why he'd fought her on it with everything he had until the morning she lay on the table in Carlisle's study with their daughter tearing her apart from the inside and there wasn't any choice left. He'd been taking her soul. He'd known he was taking her soul.

And a stranger had just walked into his house and pointed a stick at a vampire and said two words, and the words had gone looking for a soul inside a dead man, and found one, and taken it.

*There was something to take.*

That was Carlisle's mind. It was very quiet. It was the quietest Edward had ever heard it.

*Edward. There was something there to take.*

He felt a hand on his arm. Small, and very strong, and cold. He knew it without looking. It was the only hand in the world he couldn't hear.

He looked up. Bella was standing in front of him with Renesmee on her hip, looking down at him, and her face was doing something he'd never seen it do before. She'd heard. She understood exactly what she'd heard, and exactly what it meant to him, because she'd spent two years fighting him about it. She didn't say anything. She just stood there and held his arm and looked at him, with her eyes very bright.

Renesmee leaned out of her mother's arms and put her small warm hand flat on his cheek. She showed him a picture. It was a picture of his own face, right now, from the outside, and he looked lost.

He put his hand over hers and held it there.

---

"I'll be taking him," the man said.

Carlisle was still kneeling by the body. He looked up. "We burn our dead," he said.

"He's not your dead." The man's voice was mild and completely flat. "He's mine. He's the first one of you anybody's ever killed with a word. I'd like to know what it did to him. Which means I'd like to look at him, which means I'm not burning him on your lawn." A pause. "With respect, Doctor."

Carlisle looked at him for a long moment. Edward heard his mind work through it. It came out, after a while, somewhere very tired.

"He tried to kill you," Carlisle said.

"He did."

"Then I suppose he's yours."

The man nodded once. He drew the pale wand again. Edward watched every one of them flinch at it, even Emmett, even Rosalie. Jasper, beside him on the stair, went rigid all over, and Edward heard him brace for it, the way you'd brace for a blow. It didn't come. The man's face didn't change. Jasper's mind, which had been braced and waiting, went slack with a strange sort of disbelief.

The man pointed the wand down at the body and moved it, a small precise motion like a man signing his name.

Ruben folded.

He drew in on himself, smooth and silent and horribly fast, the limbs going in toward the body and the body going in toward a point, the leather jacket and the boots and the dark hair all going with it, until there was nothing left on the floor but something small and white, about the length of a finger.

A bone. A small, clean, white bone, like a knucklebone off a sheep.

Layla made a sound on the couch. She'd put her hand over her mouth.

The man bent and picked it up. He took a white handkerchief from an inside pocket, wrapped the bone in it neatly, the way you'd wrap a piece of cake to take home from a party, and tucked it back into the same inside pocket.

"That's human transfiguration," Layla said, through her fingers. "On a *body*."

"It is."

"Where did you *learn*—"

"Off a very bad man," the man said. "A long time ago." He patted the inside pocket, once. "Different pocket from the other two. I'll not get them mixed up."

Emmett made a noise again. This time Rosalie did hit him.

Jasper let go of the newel post.

He did it slowly, one finger at a time. There were dents in the oak where he'd gripped it, five of them, in the shape of his fingers. He straightened up. Edward heard his mind, and it was clearer now, the white ringing gone, and in its place a cold careful intensity that Edward recognized from a long time ago, from the years when Jasper had been somebody else.

"What was that?" Jasper asked. "When you said it."

The man looked at him. "You felt that."

"I felt you hate him." Jasper's voice was very quiet. "I felt you hate him more than anything I've ever felt from anyone. Living or dead. For two words." He paused. "And then I felt you stop."

"That's how it works," the man said. "The curse. You've got to mean it. It won't take otherwise. It's the only spell I know of that cares how you feel about it." He considered Jasper for a moment. "So you mean it. And then you stop."

"How?"

The man shrugged. "Practice."

Edward watched Jasper's face. Jasper's face didn't change. But Edward heard his mind, and his mind was thinking, very clearly, in the flat old voice of the soldier he'd been in the wars: *I have known a lot of killers. I have never known one who could do that.*

"Mr. Hale," said the man. "Or Whitlock. Whichever you're going by these days." His eyes stayed on Jasper. "That was you, wasn't it. A minute ago. When I walked in. Something leaning on me."

Jasper went very still.

"Like somebody pouring water on a window." The man's tone was perfectly polite. "Felt it slide off. Thought it was you. I'd rather you didn't, if it's all the same."

"That was meant for him," said Jasper.

"Fair enough. I'd still rather you didn't." The man turned away. "Right. Who's got questions? I can see you've all got questions. I've got about ten minutes of questions in me before I fall over."

"The letter," said Rosalie.

She'd come forward from beside Emmett, and she was standing in the middle of the room with her hands in fists, and her voice was shaking very slightly, which Edward had heard happen perhaps three times in seventy years.

"She said there was a letter," Rosalie said. "In Seattle. With a friend. If she doesn't come back by the twenty-third it goes to Volterra. Everything about Renesmee." Her black eyes were fixed on the man's face. "You've got her in your *pocket*. She's not going back to Seattle. So it goes."

"There isn't a letter," said the man.

Rosalie stared at him.

"There isn't one. She was bluffing." The man put his hands in his coat pockets. "She thought very hard about an envelope in a drawer, for your mind-reader's benefit. Clever girl. She'd have done well in my line of work." His eyes flicked to Edward, then back. "But when I came round that corner, people do a funny thing. Something frightening walks into a room and everybody in it thinks, for about half a second, about the thing they're most afraid of. Can't help it. It comes straight up to the top. And what she was most afraid of, when she saw me, was that she'd meant to write it, and drove round Seattle for a day trying to think of somebody she'd trust with it, and couldn't think of anybody, and came up here without one." He paused. "So she bluffed. There's no letter. There's no friend. Nobody in Seattle knows anything."

Edward stared at him.

He'd been listening to Della for twenty minutes. He'd been listening to her with everything he had. He'd heard the envelope, the drawer, the house, over and over, until he wanted to scream. And when the man walked in — when the man walked in, Edward had turned to look at the silence in the archway, and for one second, two, he'd stopped listening to her. He'd forgotten her. He'd been so stunned by the hole in the room that he'd let her go.

And in those two seconds, this man had walked in out of the snow and read all three of them, and found it.

"You read her," Edward said. "In the second you walked in."

"All three of them." The man's eyes were on him again. "You were looking at me."

Edward didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say.

Rosalie had put her hand over her mouth. She stood there in the middle of the room for a long moment, with her shoulders shaking. Then she lowered her hand. Her eyes were black and wet-looking, though they couldn't be, though none of them had cried in a hundred years. She looked at the man in the filthy coat.

"Thank you," Rosalie said.

The man looked at her. He nodded once.

"Are they alive?" said Bella. "In your pocket?"

She'd turned away from Edward, though she kept one hand on his shoulder. She was looking at the man, and her face was set and white, and Edward didn't need to hear her to know what she was thinking. He could see it. It was the same thing she'd been thinking since the ridge.

"For now," the man said.

"What will you do with them?"

"Ask them some questions."

"And then?"

The man considered her. "And then the Killing Curse gets a sample size bigger than one."

Bella looked at him for a long moment.

Then she nodded.

That was all. She nodded once, short and hard, and Edward watched her do it and felt something go through him that he couldn't name. He'd seen Bella angry. He'd seen her afraid. He'd seen her fight for her daughter's life with everything she had, in a way that had broken his heart and remade it. He'd never seen her look at a man who'd just told her he was going to kill two people and nod.

The man looked back at her. Something in his face changed, very slightly. His eyes narrowed a fraction, and stayed on hers.

Edward watched Bella's head come up. Watched her eyes go wide and then narrow, sharp, the way they did when she felt something she didn't like. Her hand tightened on his shoulder.

"Did you just—" said Bella.

"Tried," said the man.

"And?"

The man looked at her a moment longer. Then he blinked, and the narrowing went out of his eyes, and he looked, for the first time since he'd walked in out of the snow, faintly surprised.

"Huh," he said. "That's new."

He didn't say anything else about it. He turned away. But Edward was looking at Bella's face, and Bella's face had gone fierce and bright, and he knew exactly what she was thinking, because it was what he was thinking too. Whatever it was in her that had kept her silent to him all this time, it had kept her silent to *this* man as well.

---

Layla had stood up.

She was standing by the couch with her bandaged hands at her sides and her face grey, and Edward had been listening to her mind for ten minutes, the same thing over and over, *he's going to look at me, he's going to look at me*, and now the man turned his head and looked at her, and her mind went silent with dread.

"Miss Brennan," said the man.

"Five," Layla said. "I took down five."

"I know."

"And I tried six."

"I know that too." The man's face didn't change. "The sixth one didn't care for you."

"It put me out for forty minutes."

"It does that."

Layla's chin came up. Edward heard her make herself do it. "Sam asked me to. The first one. It was on his beach. On Quileute land. It had his whole pack walking around a tree for two weeks without knowing why, and he wanted it gone, and he had every right to want it gone."

"Then he ought to have come and asked me."

"You weren't here!"

It came out of her louder than she meant. The room flinched.

The man looked at her for a moment, and something went across his face that Edward couldn't read and couldn't hear, and then it was gone.

"No," he said. "I wasn't."

He let that sit for a second.

"I had been rather clear about my traps, too," he said. "As I recall."

"I know," said Layla. Her voice cracked. "I know you were. And those three came through the hole I made, and they saw Nessie, and they nearly—" She stopped. "I know. You don't have to say it."

"I wasn't going to say it." The man was quiet for a moment, looking at her. Then he said: "Five, though."

Layla looked at him.

"Nobody's taken one of those down before," said the man. "I've had them up in four countries. Nobody's ever got close." He paused. "And you did five. In two weeks. With your hands, by the look of them."

Layla didn't say anything. She stood there. Edward heard her mind, and it had gone completely blank.

"They were the simple ones," she said finally. Very quietly. "Weren't they. The anchors. They were the simple part."

"They were," said the man. "You noticed. Good." He looked at her a moment longer. "The one on the hill behind the chief's house, by the way. You'll have thought it was pointed at his back door."

Layla went red.

"It was covering it," the man said. "I'll be putting it back."

He turned away from her.

"And I'll be putting the rest back as well. All five. Tomorrow, the next day." He glanced at Jacob, by the fire, who'd been standing there this whole time in a silence so loud Edward could barely hear past it, a roaring, furious, humiliated, bewildered silence that was just one word over and over: *pebbles*. "Except the one on the beach. I'll find another spot for that. Off the reservation. If your Mr. Uley likes."

"He won't like," said Jacob.

"Then I'll ask him what he would like." The man looked at Jacob steadily. "Where does Sam Uley live?"

Jacob's face closed like a fist. "Why?"

"Because I'd like to meet him."

"No."

"I wasn't asking permission, Jacob." The man's voice was perfectly mild. "I was asking for an address. I can find it myself. I'd just rather not go knocking on doors on the reservation at random; seems rude."

"Where were you?" said Seth.

It burst out of him. He'd been standing by the stairs this whole time with his big hands clenched, and Edward had been listening to his mind going round in desperate circles, and now it all came out in one rush. "You just *left*. In the middle of breakfast. Leah said. You left in the middle of breakfast and you didn't come back for a *month*, and Charlie found your truck, and everybody thought you were dead, or gone, and Layla took down your stuff because Sam asked her to, and then those three came, and—" He stopped. "Where were you?"

The man looked at him.

"Work," he said.

"*Where*, though?"

"Somewhere cold."

That was all he said. He didn't say anything more about it, and Edward, watching him, knew that he never would, not to Seth, not to any of them. There was a gray crust on his boots that wasn't snow. There was a smell on him that wasn't Forks. Edward could have read the whole of it out of any other man's head in a second. He looked at the man and listened as hard as he'd ever listened in his life, and heard the snow falling on the roof, and Seth's frantic heartbeat, and nothing.

"Have you eaten?" said Esme.

Everybody turned and looked at her.

She was standing in the kitchen doorway with her hands folded in front of her and her face composed, the way it always was when she'd decided the most important thing in a room was the thing nobody else had noticed. She was looking at the man in the filthy coat with the healing cut on his face.

"Not since Tuesday," the man said.

"There's lasagna," said Esme. "I made it for the boys. There's far too much of it. There always is."

The man looked at her for a long moment.

"Mrs. Cullen," he said, "I could kiss you."

And Emmett, who had been holding it in for five minutes with both hands over his face, finally lost it entirely, and laughed so hard he had to sit down on the floor.

### IX. Tall, Dark

*The back steps of the Cullen house — 8:31 p.m.*

He ate on the back steps.

He'd asked if he could. He'd stood in Esme's kitchen with a plate in his hand that Esme had loaded with about a quarter of a tray of lasagna, and looked around at all of them, the whole house, crowding into the kitchen doorway and the living room behind it, staring at him, and said, "Would anybody mind terribly if I ate this outside? I've had a month of rooms."

Nobody had minded. Nobody had said anything at all. Esme had opened the back door for him.

So he was sitting on the top step of the wide wooden stairs that went down from the back deck to the lawn and the river, in the snow, in his filthy coat, with the plate on his knees, eating. The snow was still coming down. It was settling on his shoulders and in his hair and he didn't seem to notice. The light from the kitchen came out through the glass behind him and lay across the deck in a long yellow rectangle, and he sat at the edge of it, half in the light and half out.

Leah stood in the kitchen and watched him through the glass for about two minutes.

Then she opened the back door and went out.

She didn't know why. She told herself it was to keep an eye on him. Somebody had to. Jacob was in the living room with Carlisle and the witch, going in circles, and Seth was upstairs with Nessie, and the Cullens were all still standing around the cracked floor where the leech had died like they were at a funeral, and somebody had to watch the guy with two living vampires in his coat pocket. That was all. That was a good reason.

She shut the door behind her. The cold hit her and felt good. She walked across the deck through the falling snow in her bare feet and sat down on the top step, about four feet from him, with her arms around her knees.

He didn't look up. He was eating like a man who hadn't eaten since Tuesday, which, she supposed, he hadn't. Fast and steady and serious, a forkful at a time, without stopping.

His heart did something.

She heard it. She heard everybody's heart; she'd heard her mother's from across the house since she was seventeen. His had been going at about sixty, slow and dull and steady, the whole time she'd been watching him through the glass. And when she sat down on the step, four feet from him, it went once, hard and fast, a single double-time beat, like a man missing a step on a staircase.

Then it went back to sixty.

She looked at him. He went on eating. His face hadn't changed.

"Your heart just did a thing," she said.

"It does that," he said, without looking up. "After a month without a proper night's sleep."

"Uh-huh."

He ate another forkful.

Leah looked out at the river.

She'd said *yes*. In her head, when the leech dropped. She'd said it so loud she'd felt Edward flinch from across the room. She'd felt him turn his head and look at her. She didn't care. She'd meant it.

She'd watched the one with the scars on his hands come across the room at a human being, at *this* human being, who was standing in a doorway with nothing in his hands and blood on his face. She'd been four feet away. She'd been about to phase. She'd felt it come up her spine and she'd been about to let it, inside, in the middle of Esme Cullen's living room, and she hadn't cared about that either.

And then two words.

And the leech had dropped like somebody had cut his strings. Just dropped. Dead before he hit the floor. And the man had looked down at it like it was something the cat dragged in, and said, *I had been rather clear, no?*, in that voice, that dry English voice, like a teacher talking to a kid who'd talked back. And then he'd flicked his stick twice without even looking and the other two were gone, *gone*, little stones on the floor, and he'd picked them up and blown on them and put them in his pocket like change.

She'd been killing leeches for a year and a half. She knew what it cost. She knew how fast they were, and how hard, and what it took to bring one down. The teeth and the claws and the pack all around her, all of them together, and the noise, and the sound of it, like rocks tearing. And the fear underneath, every single time, because one wrong move and they'd have your neck, or your brother's.

He'd done three of them in about four seconds. Standing in a doorway. Tired.

And the thing she couldn't get past, the thing that had been going round and round in her head for half an hour while everybody else stood around being horrified, was how *good* it had felt to watch.

It had felt like the end of a hunt. It had felt like her teeth going in. Only better. Cleaner. Like watching somebody do the thing she'd been trying to do her whole wolf life, and do it perfectly, and not even break a sweat.

She looked at him sideways.

He was scraping the plate. He'd finished the lasagna. He had a week of dark beard and the cut on his cheekbone and snow in his hair and the stain down his collar that she could smell from here, old blood, a lot of it, from more than one person. He looked like hell. He looked like somebody had dragged him through a war backwards.

He was also tall. And broad, through the shoulders, in a way that the filthy coat didn't hide much. And he had a jaw under the beard that somebody ought to have put on a coin, and big hands, and green eyes that had looked at Ruben coming across the room with exactly the same expression they'd used on his lasagna.

*Oh no,* Leah thought. *No. Absolutely not. No.*

She'd been thinking that in one form or another since the diner. Since she'd sat across from him in that booth in the daylight and seen him without the dark and the coat and the terror, and noticed things about him she had no business noticing. She'd been thinking it every morning at six-fifteen, sitting at the counter looking at the empty booth, telling herself she was there to keep an eye out.

She'd been in love once. She'd been in love with Sam Uley since she was fourteen. And then one day he'd looked at her cousin and the whole world had rearranged itself around Emily, and Leah had been left standing outside it, and she'd had to live inside his head every day for a year and a half and *feel* how much he loved somebody else. She'd decided, somewhere in that year and a half, that she was done. With all of it. Men, and wanting, and the whole stupid thing. Because any man she wanted could look at somebody else one day and be gone, and she'd never do that again. She'd rather die.

And here she was, sitting on a step in the snow, looking at a man's hands.

*He's not even Quileute,* some idiot part of her pointed out. *He can't imprint. He can't—*

She cut that off so hard it hurt.

*He smells like wolf,* she thought, instead. Deliberately. Because that was something real, something that wasn't stupid. *Remember that? He smells like wolf. And he blinked at the diner when you said it. And he's never told you what he is, and he never will.*

He set the empty plate down on the step beside him. He looked out at the river for a while.

"Go on, then," he said.

"Go on what."

"You came out here to ask me something. You've been sitting there for five minutes with your jaw clenched. Ask it before I fall asleep on Mrs. Cullen's steps."

She looked at him. "Are you in my head?"

"No."

"How'd you know about the *yes*, then?"

He glanced at her sideways. Just for a second. "What yes?"

"Don't." She glared at him. "When he dropped. I thought it so loud the mind-reader flinched. You looked at me right after. I saw you."

"I didn't have to be in your head for that, Lassie." He looked back at the river. "I was looking at his face. He looked like somebody had shouted in his ear."

Leah opened her mouth. Shut it.

"So you did," he said. "Think it."

"Yeah," Leah said. "I did."

He nodded slowly.

"Good," he said.

That was all. He didn't say anything else. Leah sat there and felt it go through her, the same thing she'd felt in the diner, low and hot. She'd said *good* to him. In the diner. When he said the leech was alive and wasn't going to enjoy the next part. And he'd nodded, just like that, like she'd said something he agreed with.

She hated how much she liked that.

"Why Sam?" she asked.

That made him look at her properly. He turned his head and looked at her for a long moment with his green eyes, very steady, in the light from the kitchen.

"Research," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the true one." He was quiet a moment. "Your pack's three. His is—what, ten? Eleven?"

"Eleven. With Brady and Collin. They're kids. They phased in the summer."

"Eleven. That's a sample size." He looked back at the river. "Three wolves, I can tell you what three wolves are like. Eleven, I can start telling you what wolves are like. How the change works. When it comes. Why it comes. How you talk to each other, in there. How far it reaches. What happens to it when somebody breaks off and starts his own." He paused. "What an Alpha is."

Leah went very still.

"You've had it done to you," he said quietly. It wasn't a question. "The command. The voice. I saw a bit of it in your brother's head, in November, and a bit in yours. He can tell you to do a thing, and you do it. You don't want to, and it doesn't matter. Your body just goes."

She didn't say anything.

"What does it feel like?" he asked.

She almost didn't answer. She'd never told anybody. Not her mother, not Seth. Not Jacob, who knew anyway, because he'd been in her head when it happened. She looked at the snow coming down on the river.

"Like a hand," she said. "Inside. On the back of your neck. And it pushes, and you go. And you're screaming, in there, and you go anyway." She swallowed. "He used it on me once. About Sam and Emily. To make me stop thinking about it, when we were all phased. Because the others couldn't stand it." Her voice had gone flat. "So I stopped. I couldn't think about it. For two weeks. It was still there. It was all still there. I just couldn't *get* to it. It was like being locked out of my own house."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"Jacob broke it," Leah said. "In September. He's got the blood for it. He's Ephraim Black's great-grandson; he was always supposed to be Alpha. He just said *no*. And it let go of him. And then me and Seth went with him, and it let go of us too."

"I'd like to know how," the man said.

"Why?"

He looked at her. "Because a voice that can make somebody do what they're told, against everything in them, with no wand and no word and no spell anybody can see," he said, "is the most interesting thing I've heard of in about five years." A pause. "And because if I knew how it worked, I'd know how to stop it."

Leah looked at him.

She thought about that for a long time. The snow came down. Inside the house, through the glass, she could hear Jacob arguing with Carlisle, and Emmett still laughing about something in the living room, and Esme washing the lasagna tray in the sink.

"Christmas Eve," she said.

He raised his eyebrows.

"Emily does dinner. On Christmas Eve. For the whole pack, every year. Both packs, since September. Everybody. Sam'll be there." She heard herself saying it and couldn't stop. "I'll take you."

He was quiet for a moment.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I know I don't."

"It's his house. And hers."

"I *know* whose house it is." She glared at him. "I said I'd take you. You want to meet Sam? I'll take you to meet Sam. You want to meet him somewhere else, find him yourself, and good luck, because he'll smell you coming from three miles off and the whole pack'll be on you before you get out of your truck." She jerked her chin up. "Or I take you. And they don't."

He looked at her for a long time.

She couldn't read his face. She'd never been able to. But she heard his heart, and it was steady, sixty, slow and even. And she watched him, and he didn't say anything about Sam, or Emily, or what it meant for Leah Clearwater to walk into that house on Christmas Eve with a stranger. He just looked at her. And then he nodded.

"All right," he said. "Christmas Eve."

"Five o'clock. I'll meet you at the treaty line on the La Push road. Where it crosses the river."

"I'll be there."

"Bring your truck."

"Your chief's got my truck."

"Then go get it from him." She stood up. Her legs were stiff with cold, and she didn't care. "And bring something. You don't show up to Emily's with nothing. She'll never say anything, but everybody'll know."

"What does she like?"

Leah stopped.

She stood there on the deck in the snow and thought about it. About Emily, at the stove in her kitchen with her scarred face lit up by the burner, laughing at something Sam said. About all the Christmas Eves, before, when it had been Leah at that stove, Leah helping, and Emily just her favorite cousin who came up from Makah for the holidays.

"Pie," Leah said. "She likes pie."

He looked up at her from the step. Something moved at the corner of his mouth, the very beginning of a smile.

"I know a place," he said.

Leah turned around and went inside before her face could do anything she'd regret.

### X. Sample Size

*Olympic National Forest, off Forest Road 29 — 9:46 p.m.*

The hollow was under four inches of snow, and the tent was under four inches of snow, and the thread of woodsmoke from the stovepipe had stopped twenty-six days ago and the stove inside had gone stone cold.

Harry stood at the edge of the clearing and let the wards look at him. They took longer about it than usual. They'd been on their own for nearly a month, and they'd got particular. Then they knew him, and let him through.

He'd been here for about ninety seconds earlier that evening. Long enough to Apparate into the hollow, drop his bag inside the flap, and put his hand flat on the slate board by the door where the net reported in. Long enough to feel the hole in it, a long dead gap running from the coast across the town to the foothills, and the house of glass sitting in the middle of it. He'd gone straight back out again.

He ducked in under the flap.

The kitchen was exactly how he'd left it. A mug in the sink with a ring of dried tea in the bottom. A plate on the drying rack. The record still on the turntable, the arm lifted and parked, where the auto-return had put it on the morning of the twenty-sixth of November, when he'd come through the flap at a run with forty minutes to make a Portkey and his breakfast half eaten in a diner eleven miles away.

He stood in the middle of the kitchen for a minute and didn't do anything.

Twenty-six days in Russia. Croaker's line in the notebook at the diner had been short, an asset of the Department's blown and running and needing somebody to go and fetch him, and Harry had been the nearest man free. The asset was out. The asset was alive, and in a warm room somewhere a long way from where Harry had found him, and that was all anybody was ever going to hear about it from Harry, including Harry. He'd put it in the box with the rest. He'd write the report in the morning and then he'd close the lid.

He lit the Aga with a look. He put the kettle on. He took his coat off, slowly, because his left shoulder had stiffened up in the cold and the coat had stuck to it, and hung it on the hook by the door, and stood looking at it.

It was ruined. Torn at the shoulder, filthy at the hem, the collar stiff with someone else's blood. He'd liked that coat. He'd had it six years.

He took the two pebbles out of the right-hand pocket and the folded handkerchief out of the inside left, and went down the passage to the steel door.

---

The specimen room was the same as he'd left it, cold and grey and humming faintly with wards. The cases along the walls. The steel table. The circle in the floor.

And Abner Gale, standing in the frame in the middle of the circle, rigid, exactly where Harry had left him, twenty-six days and about four hours ago, with his eyes open.

The eyes moved as Harry came down the steps.

They tracked him. All the way across the room. And Harry, who'd taken the clearing and the snare and the wolves and his own face out of that head with a Memory Charm on the twenty-fifth of November and watched the empty shelves strain afterward, stopped at the edge of the outer ring and looked into the red eyes and saw, without having to go in at all, that the vampire knew exactly who he was.

"Evening, Abner," Harry said. "Remember me?"

He released the head and face. Just that layer.

The vampire's jaw worked. It took a moment. Then, in a voice like a gate on a rusted hinge: "*Yes.*"

"All of it? The clearing? The wolves?"

"Yes."

"When did it come back?"

The red eyes didn't blink. "Eleven days ago."

Harry nodded slowly. He took the notebook out of his back pocket and wrote, standing there, in pencil: *OBLIVIATE (see 25/11): fails. Full recovery of erased material by day ~15. Subject reports total recall. Vampire memory re-knits. Do not rely on memory charms on these subjects AT ALL.*

He underlined the last line twice.

So he'd been right to bluff in the clearing, and he'd been right that it was a bluff. If he'd tried to wipe that glass house clean, it would have held for a fortnight, and then every one of them would have woken up one morning remembering everything, and remembering that he'd done it. He thought about that for a second. About Edward Cullen remembering. About Rosalie Hale.

He wrote: *Bluff in clearing would have failed. Correctly not attempted.* Then he looked at that and crossed out *correctly* and wrote *luckily*, because he was honest in his own notebook if nowhere else.

He reset Abner's face. The red eyes stayed on him.

He went to the bank of cases on the left-hand wall. Two of them were empty; he'd put them aside in November. Small cases, the size of a shoebox, warded glass on all six sides, with a sealed lid. He opened the first, put the dark grey pebble in it, and closed the lid, and felt the wards on the glass take hold. He labelled it in grease pencil: *D. — red. Transf. 21/12/06. Leader.* He opened the second and put the speckled pebble in, and labelled it: *H. — red. Transf. 21/12/06.*

He'd bring them back tomorrow, or the day after. One at a time. Into the circle. He'd ask them questions, the way he'd asked Abner. He'd find out where they'd been and who they'd met and what they knew about Volterra, and how the leader had come to be the sort of creature who'd write a note like that. And then, at some point, when he'd learned everything they had, the Killing Curse would get a sample size of three.

He didn't feel anything much about that. He hadn't expected to.

The handkerchief went in the cold case at the end of the row, the one he'd built for tissue. He unwrapped it on the steel table first and looked at the little white bone lying on the white linen. He thought about Barty Crouch Junior, digging a hole in the grounds at Hogwarts at night to put a bone just like this one in it, a long time ago, and how Harry had found out about that from the man's own mouth, in an office at Hogwarts, under Veritaserum, at the age of fourteen. He'd thought it was the most horrible thing he'd ever heard.

He was twenty-six now. He'd learned the spell in 2002, in the Department, out of a book with a chain on it, because it was the cleanest way to move a body without anybody knowing it was a body.

He wrapped the bone up again and put it in the case and labelled it: *R. — red. AVADA KEDAVRA 21/12/06, 19:52 local. Killed instantly. Body intact, transf. post mortem for transport.*

Then he stood there with the grease pencil in his hand and looked at the label for a while.

*Killed instantly.*

He'd been fifty-fifty on it. He'd told the doctor that, and he'd meant it. Maybe a bit better than fifty-fifty, by the end. He'd had a lot of time to think, the last month, in places where there wasn't much else to do, and he'd gone over it and over it. The Imperius had taken hold of something in Abner. You couldn't put a will under that curse if there wasn't a will there to put under. The Cruciatus had hurt something. And when he'd gone into that vampire's head he'd found a library with every light on, a hundred and seven years of a life, perfect and whole, a person. And he'd never in his life met a person without a soul in it. He'd met one man who'd torn his own into seven pieces, and even that man had kept a scrap.

But a hypothesis was a hypothesis. And he'd been standing in a doorway with a twenty-year-old killer coming at his throat and two words in his mouth, and if he'd been wrong, the green light would have gone straight through the boy like sunlight through a window, and he'd have had about a quarter of a second to think of something else.

It hadn't gone through. It had found something, and taken it, and put him down.

So. They had souls. Damaged ones, maybe. Burnt round the edges. But there. Somewhere in that hard white body, under the venom and the hunger and the hundred years, there was the same thing there was in anybody.

He thought about Edward Cullen, sitting down on the bottom stair like his strings had been cut.

He hadn't thought about that, beforehand. He'd been thinking about the Department, and the Italians, and the boy's hands coming for his throat. He hadn't thought about what it would mean to the clock-tower boy to watch it. He'd seen it on his face, after, and it had been a strange thing to watch. A man finding out something he'd been wrong about for a hundred years, and finding out he was glad.

Harry put the grease pencil down.

He took the pale wand out of his inside pocket and went up the stairs to the armory.

---

It lived in a case of its own, on a shelf at the far end, a long narrow box of black wood lined with grey silk. He laid it in, and closed the lid, and stood with his hand flat on the wood for a moment.

He'd put it back in the tomb in 1998. He'd meant it. He'd stood by the white marble at the edge of the lake at Hogwarts in the middle of the night with Ron and Hermione, and laid it back in Dumbledore's hands, and walked away, and thought that was the end of it.

It hadn't been. In 2001 somebody had tried to break into the tomb. In 2002, somebody else, with a better plan. In 2003, a third time, with a team, and two of the castle's house-elves dead in the grounds the next morning. After that Croaker had come to see him with a face like a closed door and said, very quietly, that the Department had thought about it, and come to the view that the safest place in the world for the most dangerous wand in the world was in the hand of the man it answered to. And Harry, who'd been coming to the same view himself, had gone to Hogwarts one night and opened the tomb and taken it out of Dumbledore's hands, and said sorry, out loud, to nobody, and left.

He hardly ever used it. He didn't need to. There was almost nothing he couldn't do better without a wand than with one, by now. Almost nothing.

The third Unforgivable was the exception. It was the only spell he'd ever met that wouldn't take from him any other way. Not wandless. Not silent. He'd spent the best part of a year in the Department trying, because he hated the idea of a thing he couldn't do with his hands in his pockets, and in the end he'd given up. It wanted a wand, and it wanted the words, both of them, out loud, and it wanted you to mean every letter. It was old. It was older than almost anything. It had rules, and the rules were the rules.

So when he needed it, he used this. It seemed right. It was the Deathstick, after all.

He took his hand off the case and went back to the kitchen. The kettle had boiled.

---

He wrote the report at the kitchen table, with a mug of tea at his elbow and his left shoulder aching and his eyes trying to close.

*21 Dec. Returned to peninsula 1945 local. Net breached in my absence: five anchors removed (local witch, see previous; at request of second wolf pack and local coven), creating a corridor approx. 20 mi across, coast to foothills. Sixth attempted, repelled. Three red-eyes entered via corridor ~14 Dec, sighted coven's child, attempted blackmail of coven (threat: report to the Italians). On arrival I found all three in coven's residence.*

*Result: one KILLED, AK, Elder. Instant. Body intact, retained for study. Two TRANSFIGURED, retained alive for study.*

*AK takes. SOUL HYPOTHESIS SUPPORTED. Post-mortem: loss of subdermal luminosity, eyes cloud. Full workup to follow.*

*Note: Obliviate on these subjects FAILS within ~2 wks. Do not plan on it.*

*Note: coven member Bella (née Swan, the chief's daughter) resisted Legilimency on first light attempt. Natural mental shield appears confirmed. Recommend high priority.*

*Note: coven's mind-reader cannot hear me, as expected. Coven's empath attempted influence on entry; slid off. Coven's seer cannot see me, per the wolves, and apparently cannot see the three red-eyes once I'd decided to act. Same as Nov.*

*Local coven now aware of capability. Assess: shaken, grateful, cooperative. One (the doctor) troubled. One (the mind-reader) very much more troubled, for reasons not tactical.*

*Next: restore anchors. Then second wolf pack. Contact arranged.*

He sat and looked at it for a while. Then he signed it with his mark and shut the notebook.

When he opened it again, there was Croaker's reply under his, in the small cramped hand.

*Then we can kill them.*

And under that, after a gap, as if the old man had sat and looked at his own line for a while before adding anything:

*Good work. The Italians have had three thousand years' practice at not dying. Assume nothing. Get some sleep.*

Harry snorted, and shut the notebook, and put his head down on his folded arms on the kitchen table for a minute, just to rest his eyes.

---

He woke up at half past two with a crick in his neck and the Aga ticking and the tea gone cold.

He didn't go to bed. He got up and made more tea and sat back down at the table and opened the notebook to a fresh page, because he'd woken up thinking about wolves, and when he woke up thinking about a thing he generally didn't get back to sleep till he'd written it down.

*Second pack. Uley, S. (Alpha). 11 per Clearwater, L.*

He sat and looked at that for a while. Then he started a list.

*1. The change. Trigger: presence of vampires (per wolves, per local legend). Mechanism? Genetic, clearly; runs in families; which families, how far back? Other tribes? Ages at first change. What's the lowest? (Two at 13, per L.C.)*

*2. Physiology. Body temp ~108. Healing. Size. Ageing: stops while they keep changing? Starts again if they stop? (Ask Uley. He'll know; he's been at it longest.)*

*3. Pack mind. Range? Line of sight, or none? Does it pass through water, stone? Separate packs, separate minds: what's the boundary made of? Can an outsider listen in (yes; I did). Can an outsider BLOCK it?*

*4. The Alpha voice. Mechanism. Bloodline (Black line; Uley line?). Compulsion acts on the body without the will. Closest analogue: Imperius, but no caster in the usual sense, no wand, no incantation, no detectable spell. Can Occlumency resist it? Can it be broken without a bloodline? Black broke it by refusal. HOW.*

*5. Imprinting.*

He stopped at that one. He sat with the pencil over the page.

He'd seen it from the inside, in the boy's head in November. The grocery shop and the jar of honey and the floor going out from under him. A wire through the middle. He'd seen a bit of it in the girl's, too, and much more of the wound it left behind, in the one it hadn't happened to. He didn't know what it was. A binding of some kind. Old, and very strong, and nothing that anybody had cast. Something in the blood that reached out and tied a knot, without asking, and the knot never came undone.

He'd once spent three months in the Department studying the Unbreakable Vow. This was something like that, and something else.

*5. Imprinting. Binding. Unconsented, permanent, appears to act on both will and body. Analogues: Unbreakable Vow; certain old love-magics (see Room of Love, ninth level). Can it be undone? (Probably not; they'd have found out.) SHOULD it be? (Not my question.)*

He looked at the last line for a long moment. Then he left it.

*6.*

He didn't write anything after the six.

He sat there in the quiet kitchen at three in the morning, with the snow ticking on the canvas overhead, and thought about the diner. About a grey wolf in a T-shirt and cutoffs leaning across the table toward him, breathing in through her nose, not caring what anybody in the place thought. *You don't smell human. You smell like* wolf.

He'd been wondering about that himself, for a while.

He didn't write it down. He drew a line under the five and closed the notebook.

---

He thought about her anyway, while he drank the tea.

There wasn't any point pretending he didn't. He'd felt his own heart do something stupid when she sat down on the step, and he'd felt her hear it, and he'd made a joke about sleep that neither of them had believed. That was a fact, the same as the snow on the canvas and the ache in his shoulder, and he'd been trained to look at facts.

So he looked at it.

He liked her. He'd liked her since the clearing, when she'd tried to think a wall at him and the wall had been the back of a high school, cinder block, grey, absolutely solid and completely useless, and so very much her. He'd liked her in the diner, eating two breakfasts and his pie with the hot sauce, and asking him straight out, with her jaw set, why he'd kept his mouth shut about Sam. He'd liked her on the step. She'd sat four feet from him in the snow in her bare feet and told him what the Alpha voice felt like, a hand on the back of the neck, being locked out of her own house, in a voice he didn't think she'd ever used with anybody. And then she'd offered to walk him into her ex's kitchen on Christmas Eve, and dared him to argue about it.

And she'd thought *yes*. When the boy dropped. Loud enough to make the clock-tower boy flinch. He'd seen it on Edward's face.

He'd heard women say a lot of things to him over the years. Nobody had ever said *good* to him like that before, twice, about the right things.

So. He liked her.

It didn't matter.

She was a source. She was his way into the bigger pack, and through the bigger pack into the most interesting thing he'd found on this continent, and possibly a few things the Department would very badly want to know before it sent anybody up a hill in Tuscany. She was the fastest thing on four legs on the peninsula. She had a mind like a knife and a wound in it she wouldn't let anybody touch. She was about half in love with watching him kill things, which he'd noted, and understood better than he'd have liked to, and was not going to do anything about. And she'd been hurt very badly once, by a man who hadn't had a choice, and had decided she'd rather die than let it happen again.

He was an Unspeakable on foreign soil with two vampires in a glass case and a third in a handkerchief, and a job that could call him away from a diner in the middle of a slice of pie and not let him back for a month.

So he'd keep it where it couldn't do anybody any harm. He'd kept a lot of things there. He was good at it.

He finished the tea. He washed the mug. He put the record back on, the one that had been sitting on the turntable for twenty-six days, and set the needle down, and went to bed with it playing.

He had a truck to collect in the morning. And a pie.

### XI. Impound

*Forks Police Department — Friday, December 22, 2006, 8:04 a.m.*

Sue had called him at ten past eleven the night before.

She'd called from her kitchen in La Push, and she'd been quiet, and careful, the way she got when she was choosing which parts of a thing to say. Seth had come home, she said. Seth was all right. Leah was all right. Everybody was all right. Something had happened up at the Cullens', something bad, or that would've been bad, and it wasn't going to be anymore. And the man was back.

"The elk guy," Charlie had said.

"Yes."

"He do something?"

There'd been a long pause on the line. "He took care of it, Charlie."

Charlie had sat in his recliner in the dark living room with the phone against his ear and the TV on mute, a basketball game going on silently in the corner, and thought about that.

"Okay," he'd said finally. "Need to know."

"Need to know," Sue had agreed softly, and he'd heard her relief, and they'd talked about other things for a while, and then he'd gone to bed and lain there till about two looking at the ceiling.

So when the front door of the station opened at four minutes past eight the next morning and the man walked in, Charlie wasn't surprised. He'd been expecting it since he woke up. He'd been sitting at his desk since seven with a cup of coffee going cold in front of him, and the envelope with the truck keys in it in the top drawer, and his gun on his hip, which was where it always was, and which he'd checked twice anyway before he sat down.

The man had shaved. That was the first thing Charlie noticed. The diner face was back, the one from the booth in November: square jaw, short dark hair, green eyes. He was wearing the elk fleece again, with a brown canvas work jacket over it, and jeans, and the same heavy black boots. He had a white paper box from the diner under one arm and a paper coffee cup in his other hand.

There was a cut along his left cheekbone, about an inch and a half long. It looked about a week old, nearly healed, a thin pink line.

Sue had said it was bleeding last night. Seth had told her. Bleeding, fresh, opened up in the cold.

"Morning, Chief," the man said. "I believe you've got my truck."

Charlie looked at him for a long moment. Then he leaned back in his chair.

"You were gone a while," he said.

"Elk migrate."

"Elk don't migrate in December."

"These ones did." The man came up to the front counter and set the coffee down on it. "Very confused elk."

"Uh-huh," said Charlie.

He didn't get up right away. He sat there and let the man stand at the counter, which was a thing you did, in his line of work, when you wanted somebody to know whose building they were in. The man let him. He stood there with the pie box under his arm and looked around the little station, unhurried. The two empty desks. The bulletin board with the missing-persons flyer for the Olympia couple still pinned to it, curling at the corners. The door to the one cell. The coffee maker. Charlie watched him look at it all and could tell he was seeing everything, the way he'd seen everything in the diner, and filing it.

Then Charlie got up and went to the filing cabinet and took out the impound sheet.

"Abandoned vehicle," he said, laying it on the counter. "Federal forest land. Tow's a hundred eighty. Storage is fifteen a day. Thirteen days." He'd already worked it out last night. "Three seventy-five."

"Fair enough."

The man took out a brown leather wallet and counted out four hundred-dollar bills onto the counter, smooth new ones, and slid them across.

"I don't have change for a hundred," said Charlie.

"Put it in the Christmas fund."

"We don't have a Christmas fund."

"Then you've just started one."

Charlie looked at the bills. He didn't pick them up. He took the envelope with the keys out of his drawer instead and set it on top of the impound sheet, and kept his hand on it.

"Layla told me about you," he said.

"I'd assumed she would."

"She told me what you've got on her." He watched the man's face. It didn't change. "That it's me. That I'm the thing you're holding over her."

"Yes," said the man.

He didn't dress it up. He didn't look away. Charlie had been a cop for twenty-five years and he'd sat across a lot of tables from a lot of men who'd done things, and most of them, when you put it to them straight, flinched, or smiled, or got angry, or started explaining. This one just said yes, and stood there, and waited to see what Charlie would do about it.

"So I'm the hostage," Charlie said.

The man was quiet for a second.

"That's one way to put it," he said.

"What's the other way?"

"You're the reason she says yes."

"Same thing."

"It is," the man agreed. "Yes."

Charlie looked at him for a long time.

"You did it at the diner," he said. "That thing. When you shook my hand. You looked at me funny."

"I did."

"You doing it now?"

"I was."

"Get anything?"

The man considered him. Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "Fog," he said.

Charlie let that sit.

"Bella used to say I was hard to read," he said. "When she was a kid. She'd say she never knew what I was thinking."

"Did she," said the man, and something in his voice changed, very slightly. Like a man who'd been told something he wanted to write down.

Charlie didn't like that. He didn't know why. He took his hand off the envelope.

"Whatever it is you're doing here," he said, "you keep it away from my family."

The man picked up the envelope. He didn't open it. He held it for a second, looking at Charlie across the counter.

"Chief," he said. "Last night, some people came to this town meaning your family very badly. Your daughter, and hers." He paused. "They've gone. They won't be back. You'll have to take my word on that."

"I don't have to take anything," said Charlie.

"No," the man agreed. "You don't."

Charlie looked at him. At the cut on his face that had been bleeding twelve hours ago and looked a week old now. At the pie box under his arm.

"Did you kill somebody?" he asked.

The man looked back at him for a long moment.

"Chief," he said quietly, "you don't want me to answer that."

Charlie thought about it. He thought about Bella, standing in his living room in September with her eyes the wrong color and her skin cold and her face full of love, asking him to just let it be. About Jake, taking his shirt off in the front yard. About a grease fire in his kitchen going out like a light. About a policy he'd made, in his own living room, and stuck to harder than he'd ever stuck to anything.

"No," he said finally. "I guess I don't."

The man nodded.

He tucked the envelope into his jacket. Then he took the white paper box out from under his arm and set it on the counter, on top of the four hundred-dollar bills, and slid it across.

"Darlene had it in her freezer for three weeks with my name on it," he said. "Marionberry. She says it's your favourite."

Charlie looked at the box.

"It is," he said.

"Thought so." The man picked up his coffee. "Merry Christmas, Chief."

"You heal fast," said Charlie.

The man stopped, halfway turned toward the door.

"That cut," Charlie said. "Sue says it was bleeding last night. Looks a week old now."

The man looked at him over his shoulder for a second. Then he smiled, the first real one Charlie had seen on him, crooked and sudden, there and gone.

"Clean living," he said, and went out.

Charlie watched him through the station's front window. Watched him walk around the side of the building to the lot out back, where the black Ford was sitting behind the chain-link with three weeks of grime on its windshield. Watched him unlock it and climb in and sit there for a second. The engine turned over on the first try. Through the glass Charlie could hear, very faintly, the cassette deck kick in, a guitar.

He sat back down at his desk.

He looked at the pie box for a long time. Then he opened it, and got a plastic fork out of the coffee drawer, and ate a piece of it, sitting there at his desk at eight-fifteen in the morning.

It was really good pie.

He was still a little angry about it.

### XII. The Treaty Line

*La Push Road, at the river — Sunday, December 24, 2006, 4:58 p.m.*

The snow hadn't lasted down on the coast. It had turned to rain on Friday night, the way it always did, and washed away, and by Christmas Eve the La Push road was black and shining under the trees and the river beside it was running high and brown and fast.

Leah stood at the side of the road where it crossed the river, in the rain, under the trees, and waited.

She was wearing jeans. And a sweater. A dark red one that had been her mother's, that her mother had given her last Christmas and that she'd never worn, because it was the kind of thing a girl wore when she wanted somebody to look at her, and she hadn't wanted anybody to look at her for two years. She'd put it on at four o'clock. She'd taken it off at four-ten. She'd put it back on at four-fifteen, and looked at herself in the mirror in her bedroom, and said, out loud, *you are pathetic*, and left it on.

She had her hands jammed in the pockets of her jacket. She was not cold. She was never cold. She was standing with her shoulders hunched up anyway.

It was full dark. It had been dark since half past four.

Headlights came round the bend from the Forks side at three minutes to five.

She knew the truck before she could see it. She knew the sound of the engine; she'd heard it pull out of the diner lot on the twenty-sixth of November, and she'd heard it again on Friday afternoon, up on the ridge above the Cullens', when he'd come up there to put back the anchor Layla had taken out of the burl in the old fir. She'd been on patrol. She'd gone and watched him from the trees for two hours, doing it. He'd known she was there. He hadn't said anything.

The truck pulled onto the shoulder beside her and stopped. The passenger window came down.

"Evening, Lassie," he said.

He'd cleaned the truck. That was the first thing. The windshield was clean and the inside smelled of coffee and pine and the soap they used at the car wash in Port Angeles. He was sitting behind the wheel in a charcoal wool sweater and a new coat, a dark one, plain, that looked like it cost money. He'd shaved again. The cut on his cheekbone was gone. She looked for it, and it was just gone, like it had never been there.

On the bench seat between them were two white boxes from the diner, stacked, tied with string.

She got in.

The cab was hot. He had the heater going. She didn't need it and he didn't need it, and he had it on anyway, and she looked at him and didn't say anything about it, and he pulled back out onto the road toward La Push.

The tape deck was playing. Something with a guitar going round and round and a voice up very high over it.

"Seriously?" said Leah. "Zeppelin? To Emily's?"

"Your brother approves."

"My brother likes Zeppelin because my dad did."

The man didn't say anything to that for a moment. He drove. The wipers went back and forth.

"I know," he said.

She looked at him sideways. His face, in the green glow off the dashboard, was quiet.

He didn't turn it off. She didn't ask him to. She looked out the window at the trees going by, black and dripping, and listened to it, and after a while she found she didn't mind.

"Okay," she said. "Rules."

"Go on."

"Don't read anybody."

"I'll try."

"Don't *try*. *Don't.*" She turned in the seat to look at him. "I mean it. Not Sam, not Emily, not anybody. Not the kids. They're thirteen. Not Billy, he's Jacob's dad, he's on the council. Not Old Quil, he's about a hundred. Not my mom. Nobody. You go in there and you sit at that table and you keep your eyes to yourself."

He drove for a moment. "All right," he said.

"Promise."

He glanced at her. "All right," he said again, and she heard that it was different, the second time. "Nobody."

"And don't call Sam anything. No dog names. No Lassie, no Balto. Nothing."

"What about the others?"

She thought about it.

"Call Paul whatever you want," she said.

He laughed. It came out of him short and surprised, the first time she'd ever heard him do it, and it changed his whole face. She looked away fast, out the window, at the trees.

"Paul's got a temper," she said, to the window. "Like, a real one. He phases when he's mad. He'll try to start something. Don't let him."

"I'll do my best."

"Jared's okay. He's got Kim, he's mostly harmless. Embry and Quil are Jacob's friends; they're fine. Quil's got Claire. She's three. She'll be there. Don't be weird about it."

"I wasn't planning to be weird about it."

"Everybody's weird about it." She took a breath. "Brady and Collin are kids. They're going to stare at you. Let them. And Sam—"

She stopped.

The truck went round a long bend. Ahead, through the trees, she could see the first lights of the village, and past them, the dark gap where the land ended and the sea began.

"Sam's calm," Leah said. Her voice had gone flat. "He's always calm. He's going to be calm with you. He's going to ask you questions and listen to every word, and he's not going to raise his voice once, and you're not going to know what he's thinking." She swallowed. "And Emily's going to be nice to you. She's going to be really, really nice to you. Because she's nice. She's always been nice."

The man didn't say anything.

She was looking out the window. She could feel him not looking at her. He kept his eyes on the road and his hands on the wheel, big hands, steady, and he didn't say anything at all, and she waited for him to, and he didn't.

"You all right with this?" he asked finally.

Very quietly. Just that.

"Shut up," said Leah, "and drive."

"Right," he said, and did.

---

Emily's house was at the end of a gravel lane on the edge of the village, a little way back from the beach, where the trees came down almost to the back door. It was small. It had always been small. It had a porch along the front with a rail that Sam had built, and a window box Emily kept full of flowers in the summer, and tonight every window in it was lit, gold, and there were Christmas lights strung along the porch rail, red and green and white, blinking.

There were cars in the lane. Billy's truck. Old Quil's Buick. Her mother's car. Jared's Jeep.

And there were wolves on the porch.

In human form. Five of them, six. Big young men in T-shirts and cutoffs, standing along the rail in the rain with the Christmas lights blinking on their bare arms, laughing about something. Paul. Jared. Embry, Quil. Collin and Brady, gangly and thirteen, leaning on the rail at the end. Through the front window she could see more people inside, moving around. A kitchen full of steam.

The truck came up the lane, and every one of them stopped laughing and turned their heads.

The man pulled up behind her mother's car and turned off the engine. The tape stopped. In the sudden quiet she could hear the rain on the roof and the sea, a long way off, and the blood going in her own ears.

On the porch, she watched it happen. She watched them all catch it at once. The smell of him, coming to them on the wind off the sea, through the closed windows of the truck. She watched Paul's head come up. She watched Embry's nose wrinkle and Jared's eyes go wide. She watched Collin and Brady straighten up off the rail together like somebody had pulled a string. She watched the hair go up on every one of their arms.

*Wolf,* she thought, watching their faces. *They smell it too.*

Then the front door opened, and Sam came out.

He stepped out onto the porch with the light from the house behind him. He was in a T-shirt and jeans. He was barefoot. He came to the top of the porch steps and stood there, and looked at the truck through the rain, very calm, the way he always looked at everything. His face was grave and heavy and patient and gave away nothing at all. Behind him, in the lit doorway, a small figure with long black hair was standing with a dish towel over her shoulder, and the Christmas lights were blinking across the scars on the right side of her face.

Leah's chest went tight.

She sat there in the cab of the truck with her hands in her lap, in her mother's red sweater, and couldn't move.

Beside her, the man reached over and picked up the two pie boxes off the seat. He held them on his knee for a second. He didn't look at her. He looked out the windshield at Sam Uley, standing on the top step in the rain, and Sam looked back at him.

"Right," the man said quietly.

He opened his door and got out.

He stood beside the truck in the rain, in his dark coat, with the two white boxes held in front of him. He didn't go up the steps. He waited. Leah watched him wait. She watched every wolf on that porch watch him, with their hackles up and their noses working and their eyes narrowed, and she watched him stand there and let them.

Sam came down two steps.

"You're the one," Sam said. His voice carried across the yard, very calm. "The one who put a spell on my beach."

"I am," said the man.

"You've got a lot of nerve, coming here."

"I've been told." The man lifted the boxes, very slightly. "I brought pie."

Somebody on the porch laughed. It was Embry; Leah knew it without looking, his high startled laugh, and it cut off fast when Paul elbowed him.

Sam didn't laugh. He stood on the second step and looked at the man for a long time. Leah watched his nostrils flare. She watched him smell it, the same thing she'd smelled in the clearing and in the diner and on the back steps of the Cullens' house in the snow. She watched his calm face, which she'd known since she was fourteen, which she'd loved, which had never once in her whole life given anything away, go very still, and then, just for a second, frown.

"What are you?" said Sam Uley.

The man looked up at him through the rain.

"Hungry, mostly," he said.

"Sam," said Emily, from the doorway. Her voice was soft and very clear. "Let the man in out of the rain."

Sam looked back at her over his shoulder. Something passed between them, the way it always did, the way Leah had watched it pass between them a thousand times inside Sam's head and outside it, and it still felt like a knife going in.

Then Sam stepped aside.

The man didn't move. He turned his head and looked back at the truck. At her. He didn't say anything. He just stood there in the rain, holding the pies, and waited.

Leah opened her door.

She got out of the truck. She shut the door behind her. She walked around the front of it through the headlight glare, in her mother's red sweater, with the rain coming down on her hair and every wolf on that porch staring at her, and Sam, and Emily in the doorway. She walked until she was standing next to the man in the dark coat. And then she kept going, and he fell in beside her, and they went up the steps together.

It was the first time she'd walked up those steps in two years.

She didn't look at Sam as she went past him. She didn't look at Emily. She walked straight through the lit doorway into the warm loud kitchen full of steam and Christmas and the smell of frybread, and felt the man come in behind her with the rain on his shoulders, and heard every voice in the house stop.

"Everybody," said Leah Clearwater, to the whole silent room. "This is Page. He counts elk."
