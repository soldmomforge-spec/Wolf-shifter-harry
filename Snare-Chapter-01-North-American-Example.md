# Snare

## Chapter One: North American Example

---

***Monday, 2 October 2006 — 6:38 a.m.***
***The diner, Forks Avenue, Forks, Washington***

The rain had been coming down since Thursday. It was the fine grey kind that didn't so much fall on Forks as hang in the air and wait for you to walk into it, and at six thirty-eight on a Monday morning it beaded on the diner's front window and turned the Thriftway sign across the road into a smear of red.

Dottie Halvorsen had poured coffee in this building for twenty-two years. In that time she had worked out that you could learn most of what mattered about a man from where he chose to sit.

Loggers took the counter, because the counter was closest to the pot and they had to be up a forest road by seven. The old men took the two-tops by the register so they could argue with whoever was paying. Charlie Swan took the booth by the door, back to the kitchen, eyes on the lot, because he'd been a cop longer than some of his deputies had been alive and it had got down into his bones.

The Englishman took the corner booth at the far end. Cracked red vinyl, a view of the front door, the side window, the hallway to the restrooms and the swing door into the kitchen. Back to the wall. Six weeks running, he'd never once sat anywhere else, and the one morning a pair of tourists from Tacoma had got there first he'd stood at the counter with his coffee and waited until they left.

The bell over the door went at six thirty-nine.

"Morning, Dot."

"You're late."

"I'm one minute late." He shook rain off a waxed canvas jacket the colour of wet tobacco and checked a steel watch on the inside of his wrist, face turned in, the way soldiers wore them in the films. "One minute. I had a moral crisis in the car park about whether I could get away with not shaving."

"You didn't."

"I didn't. Nobody's perfect."

He was tall. Six-two by her eye, and her eye was good; she'd grown up with two brothers over six foot and married a third man who'd lied about it. He was broad through the shoulders and heavy through the arms in the way men got from hauling things for a living, and he moved through the narrow aisle between the tables without bumping a single chair. Dark hair that needed cutting. Green eyes. Thirty at the outside, likely younger. A pair of binoculars hung off his neck on a strap worn pale at the edges, and a notebook with a cracked green cover rode in the jacket's chest pocket. He hung the jacket on the hook at the end of the booth, slid in, and had the menu shut before she'd got to him with the pot.

"Usual?"

"Usual. Two eggs over easy, hash browns, bacon, sausage, wheat toast, short stack on the side—"

"And pie."

"And pie," he said, "because I am a grown man with a job and a car and nobody on God's earth can stop me."

"Marionberry or apple?"

He gave it real thought, which was the thing about him she'd noticed the first week. Most men ordered pie like they were apologising for it. The Englishman ordered pie like a judge passing sentence.

"Marionberry. Heated up. Scoop of vanilla, if Hank's feeling generous."

"Hank's never generous."

"Then tell him it's for the poor foreign bird man and watch his heart grow three sizes."

She poured. He wrapped both hands around the mug and looked out the window at the rain the way some people looked at a fire.

"Lovely weather," he said.

"You're sick."

"I'm from England, Dot. This is a summer's day. Back home we'd be out in shorts having a barbecue and getting sunstroke." He lifted the mug. "Cheers."

She went and put the order in. Hank, who was sixty-one and built like a mailbox, grunted at the pie ticket and slapped a second scoop of vanilla on the plate without being asked, which she decided not to mention.

By the time she came back with the plates, Charlie Swan had come in.

It was a funny thing, and she'd noticed it twice now and filed it away the way you filed the price of gas. The Englishman had said "Morning, Chief," and lifted two fingers off his coffee mug before the bell over the door had rung. Before, by a second at least. She'd looked over and Charlie's hand had still been on the push bar.

Probably he'd seen the cruiser pull in. The window was right there.

"Evans," Charlie said, and took his booth.

"How's the Bogachiel treating you?"

Charlie's face did the thing it did for very few subjects, which was soften. "Water's up. Steelhead won't be in for another month, but I got a nice coho Saturday below the hatchery."

"How nice?"

"Eleven pounds."

"Liar."

"Ten and a half," Charlie admitted, and there was almost a smile under the moustache. "You ever get out there with a rod, or is it all binoculars?"

"All binoculars. They pay me to look up at trees. I'd catch one."

"You probably would."

"I would definitely catch a tree."

She set down Charlie's coffee and his two eggs scrambled, white toast, no butter, because he'd had a cholesterol number in July that had put the fear of God into him. Then she took the Englishman's breakfast down to the corner booth, all four plates of it, and set them out on the table like a hand of cards.

"Christ, that's beautiful," he said, looking at the pie. "Tell Hank I love him."

"Tell him yourself."

"He frightens me."

He ate the way he did everything else she'd seen him do, steady and without waste. He didn't hunch over the plate. Every few bites his eyes went up — door, window, the hallway, Charlie, door — and then back down. A man came in for coffee to go, a pulp-truck driver she knew by face, and the Englishman's eyes went to him and stayed long enough to take in the boots and the hands and then let him go.

The radio on the shelf behind the counter was tuned to the classic rock station out of Port Angeles, which was Hank's one concession to the twentieth century. The Who came on, that long synthesiser opening that went round and round, and she heard the Englishman humming it under his breath between mouthfuls of hash brown. He had it note for note.

"You know you hum," she said, when she came by with the pot.

"I've been told." He held out the mug. "Generally right before someone throws something at me."

"How're your birds?"

"Murrelets." He said it like the name of a difficult relative. "Marbled murrelets. Little fat seabirds. Spend their lives on the ocean, then in summer they fly forty miles inland to lay a single egg on a bare branch two hundred feet up a tree that was old when Shakespeare was a lad. At dawn. In the rain. In the dark."

"Why?"

"Haven't the foggiest. That's what they're paying me to find out." He tapped the green notebook in his shirt pocket. "Season's mostly over. I'm counting stragglers and writing up. Another few weeks."

"Then back to England?"

"Then back to England, where the pie's worse and nobody calls me a poor foreign bird man."

"I never called you that."

"Hank does. I can see it in his eyes."

She laughed, which she didn't do much before eight. Down the room, Charlie had his newspaper folded to the box scores.

"Chief," the Englishman called, not loud. "How's your girl getting on?"

Charlie looked up.

There was a pause, and in it Dottie watched something cross Charlie Swan's face that she couldn't read. There was relief in it, and something else, careful and guarded. All August, when the whole town had known Bella was sick and nobody had known how sick, that face had held nothing but grief.

"Better," Charlie said. "She's home. She's — doing better."

"Good." The Englishman nodded. He held Charlie's eyes for a second longer than the word needed. "That's good, mate. Glad to hear it."

"Yeah," Charlie said, and went back to his paper, and that was all.

Dottie went back for the pie plate. The Englishman had the green notebook out and was writing something in it in a small fast hand, and when he saw her he flipped it shut, without any hurry.

"Bird?" she said.

"Western tanager. Saw it on the wire outside. Late for him, he should be in Mexico by now. Probably got lost." He put the notebook away. "That poor girl of the Chief's. What was it in the end? People keep saying different things."

Dottie topped off his coffee and lowered her voice, because there were some things you didn't say at a normal volume with Charlie Swan twenty feet away.

"Caught something on her honeymoon. Down in South America somewhere. They said it was some kind of rare thing, a virus, I don't know. Dr. Cullen had her up at their house with machines and everything, wouldn't let anyone visit. Charlie was out of his mind. Then she was at some clinic, or she was going to some clinic, and then she was better." She shrugged. "Charlie's been a different man the last two weeks. Comes in, eats his eggs. Doesn't say much, but he never did."

"Cullen. That's the doctor."

"Carlisle Cullen. Best doctor that hospital's ever had, and they know it."

"Heard he's got a big family."

"Five kids. Well, they're grown now. All adopted, all of 'em look like they walked out of a magazine and all of 'em pale as milk. Bella married the youngest boy. Edward." She made a face. "Eighteen and married. I said to Hank, I said—well. Never mind what I said."

"Go on, I'm a foreigner, I'll tell nobody."

"I said either she's pregnant or she's crazy." Dottie glanced down the room and dropped her voice another notch. "And then she got sick, and I felt awful."

"Bit harsh on yourself."

"Esme Cullen came in here one time in four years," Dottie said, because now she'd started, "and ordered a coffee and didn't drink it. Just held it. Left a twenty on a two-dollar coffee."

"Rich people," the Englishman said. "They don't eat."

"That's what I said."

He smiled at her, a short one that went mostly into the eyes, and drank his coffee. Outside the rain thickened. A logging truck went by and threw a curtain of road water over the lot.

"Anyone else new in town?" he said. "I've got survey plots up off the Calawah and the Sol Duc and I'd rather not get shot by some bloke from Seattle who thinks I'm an elk."

"Elk season's not till November. You're safe till then." She thought about it. "Couple hikers through last week. Some fella asking about the road to the Hoh. Nobody stays in Forks on purpose except you."

"I've got a very good reason."

"The birds."

"The pie." He nodded at the radio, where the local news had come on between songs. A woman's voice was saying something about a search being scaled back on the Oregon coast. "You hear about that? Those two from Eugene?"

"Oh, it's awful. Two weeks now. Kids. Up hiking by Cape Perpetua and their car's still in the lot." She shook her head. "They figure they went in the ocean. People don't respect the ocean."

"No," he said. "They don't."

He looked at his coffee for a moment. Then he looked at his watch, inside of the wrist, and took out his wallet.

It was cash. It was always cash. The bill came to seventeen-forty and he put down two twenties, and when she started to make change he held up a hand the way he always did.

"Dot. Please. You've kept me alive six weeks."

"You're gonna go broke."

"I'm on a research grant. They'll never notice." He stood, shrugged into the waxed jacket, and settled the binoculars on his chest. "Same time tomorrow."

"Don't be late."

"I'll be one minute late, out of spite."

He lifted two fingers to Charlie on the way past. Charlie lifted two back without looking up from the box scores.

The bell over the door rang. Through the streaming window she watched him cross the lot to the old Bronco he drove, a seventy-nine, two-tone brown and cream with a dent in the tailgate, the kind of truck that had been on the Peninsula so long it had moss in the window seals. He'd bought it off Jimmy Foss in Port Angeles for cash, Jimmy said. Paid the asking price without haggling, which nobody ever did with Jimmy, and Jimmy had been suspicious about it for a week.

The Bronco started first time. It pulled out east on 101 and was gone in the rain.

Dottie went to clear the booth.

He'd stacked the plates. He always stacked the plates, cutlery on top, napkin folded square. Under the pie plate was a paper napkin with four words on it in the same small fast hand from the notebook.

*Pie was criminal. Cheers.*

She put it in her apron pocket with the others. She had six weeks of them now, in a coffee can at home, and she couldn't have said why she kept them.

It was only later, wiping down the vinyl, that she noticed the seat was dry. He'd come in out of that rain with his jeans soaked dark to the knee, she'd seen them, and he'd sat in that booth for forty-five minutes. The vinyl under her rag was bone dry and held a faint heat, the way a seat did after a dog had been lying on it.

She stood there a second with the rag in her hand.

Then Hank yelled that the eight o'clock rush was starting, which it was, all four of them, and she forgot about it.

***3:09 p.m.***
***Field station, Bogachiel watershed, eleven miles south-east of Forks***

The tent stood in a fold of old-growth on the south side of a creek with no name on any map, under a Sitka spruce so wide at the base that four men holding hands wouldn't have got round it. From the outside it was a two-man ridge tent in faded olive canvas, guyed out to the roots, a bit saggy in the middle, the sort of thing a student might pitch for a fortnight and then leave behind. From the outside it was also invisible to anyone who hadn't been told it was there, and anyone without magic who wandered within a hundred yards of it would suddenly remember an appointment, or the kettle, or a strong and specific need to be somewhere else.

Inside, it was the size of a village hall.

Harry Potter sat at the map table in the front room with a cheese and pickle sandwich in one hand and a pencil in the other, and watched the Peninsula breathe.

The map took up the whole table. He'd drawn it himself over the first ten days, at a scale of two inches to the mile, from US Geological Survey quadrangles and his own walking, and then spent the next three weeks putting the net on it. Silver lines ran over the inked contours like frost on glass: along the ridgelines, down the river valleys, over the passes, through the 101 corridor south of Lake Quinault and up the Hood Canal side, along the whole ragged Pacific shore from Cape Flattery to the Queets. Vampires could swim. He'd learned that from the Seattle file. So the coast was netted as well, and the Strait of Juan de Fuca from Neah Bay to Port Townsend, the lines running a hundred yards out to sea where he'd anchored them on rocks at low tide.

There were two holes in the net, and he'd put them there on purpose.

The first was La Push. The reservation sat on the coast at the mouth of the Quillayute like a thumbprint, and the silver lines bent around it and left it bare. He'd read the treaty in the Quileute's own stories, in a book at the Forks library that nobody had checked out since 1994. The cold ones stayed off the tribe's land and the wolves left the cold ones alone. He had no plans to be the man who broke a seventy-year-old truce by stringing a trap across a tribal border.

The second hole was a ragged oval about three miles across, east of town, centred on a long gravel drive off the highway and a house made mostly of glass.

The Cullens hunted all over the Peninsula, and the oval was only a courtesy. The real exemption was in the lattice itself. He'd keyed it to diet. It read what a vampire had been eating from the traces in its venom and let the deer-drinkers walk straight through. Getting the two samples he needed to tell the difference had cost him nine nights in a Seattle morgue with the case files from the spring's killings, and three more in the woods behind the Cullen place, scraping dried venom off the throats of drained blacktail does. The oval meant that if one of them went for a walk with a temper on, they wouldn't trip anything by accident on their own doorstep.

Eight of them now. Seven when he'd arrived. The eighth had red eyes, which by rights should have set the net off like a fire alarm, and it hadn't, because the eighth had never had a drop of human blood in her life and the red was her own. Isabella Marie Swan. Bella Cullen, now. Nineteen in the middle of September, if anyone had been counting, and he suspected her father had.

And there was the other thing in that house. The thing Charlie Swan had decided, very firmly, at a diner booth this morning, that he was never going to ask about. A baby with his daughter's brown eyes and a heartbeat that ran too fast.

He'd written that down. He hadn't done anything about it. It wasn't his brief.

He took a bite of sandwich and looked round the room.

The front room was the office. Map table, filing cabinet, a corkboard covered in photographs of the Peninsula taken from a broom at three in the morning, a desk with a manual typewriter he used for anything that might end up in front of Muggle eyes. A record player sat on the filing cabinet with *Houses of the Holy* turning on it, the volume low, Plant singing about the hills and far away.

Through the doorway on the left was the kitchen, and past that the bunk room, and past that a bathroom with a cast-iron tub that he'd been told by three separate colleagues was an unreasonable thing to put in a field station. He'd told all three of them where they could put their opinions.

Through the doorway on the right was the armoury.

He could see the near end of it from where he sat. Steel racks along both walls under a plain white light. A Remington 870 with its barrel and receiver cut all over with fine silver runes. A double-barrelled twelve-bore with the barrels sawn back, loaded with rock salt and iron filings, which was a joke he'd made for himself after a job in Louisiana and then kept because it worked. A Webley Mk VI revolver in a felt-lined case, older than his grandfather, the cylinder engraved so closely you couldn't see the steel. An L96 with a scope on it that could see through fog. Crates on the floor, stencilled in his own handwriting: INCENDIARY — HORNTAIL, and LAPICIDA CORE — UNTESTED, and one marked DO NOT, which he knew the contents of and didn't think about much.

Past the armoury was the specimen room. He couldn't see into that from the map table, but he didn't need to. He knew what was in there. A steel table with channels cut into its surface. Shelves of jars. Good light. Cold air. Ready since the first week.

On the desk, beside the typewriter, a black notebook lay open. Its twin sat on a desk in a windowless office nine floors under Whitehall, and anything written in one turned up in the other within a second or two. The last entry was in Croaker's spiky green ink, timed at six this morning, London.

*Planning cell sits 19th. Brass are asking what they're paying for. Can you give me anything?*

Under it, in pencil, Harry had written: *The birds are lovely. Tell Brass to have a biscuit.*

He hadn't sent anything else. There'd been nothing to send. Six weeks of watching a family of vegetarian vampires go to work, go to school, go hunting, get married, have a medical crisis that nearly killed a girl and turned her into one of them, and then all of them shut up in their glass house like a family who'd had a baby, which they had. Six weeks of sitting on a net that hadn't twitched once.

The Department's information on the Volturi ran to two hundred pages now, and most of it was guesswork. They had the photograph from Volterra on Saint Marcus Day, the one that had started all this: a pale boy with no shirt standing in the shadow under the clock tower at noon, a girl in a hooded jacket hitting him at a flat sprint, and three figures in grey cloaks coming out of a side door. A Squib on holiday from Pisa had taken it with a disposable camera and then had it developed, and some clerk in the Italian Ministry's anomalies office had looked at the boy's skin where the sun caught the edge of his arm and sent it to London because nobody in Rome wanted the job. After that it had been six months of quiet work. Tour groups that went into the Palazzo dei Priori and didn't come out. A guide called Heidi who brought them. Records going back further than the Department's own.

They knew where the Volturi lived. They knew roughly how many. They knew almost nothing about what the things *were*, and the planning cell for CLOCKTOWER would not commit one witch or wizard to a fight against a creature nobody had ever had on a table.

So, a table. And a net. And a lot of pie.

He finished the sandwich. He brushed the crumbs off the map with the side of his hand.

At fourteen minutes past three, the map chimed.

It was a small sound, like a fingernail on a wine glass. On the north fork of the Calawah, about six miles from the field station as the crow flew, a single point of red light blinked on in the silver lattice. The lines around it tightened and drew in, the way a snare drew in, and a note in fine silver script wrote itself beside the dot.

*1 subject. Diet: human. Contact velocity 71 mph. Held.*

Two more dots sat just outside the line, moving. Big ones, amber, close together. The map didn't net them, because they weren't vampires, but he'd given it eyes for anything that size moving at that speed.

Harry looked at the red dot for about two seconds.

"Gotcha," he said.

He stood up and went through to the bunk room. The suit was on a hanger on the back of the door where it had hung for six weeks: charcoal wool, single-breasted, a plain white shirt, no tie. A black overcoat over the top. Black leather gloves in the coat pocket. He changed in under a minute, out of the flannel and jeans and into the suit, and laced on a pair of black boots that had cost more than the Bronco. In the inside breast pocket of the jacket was a slim black leather wallet. He checked it was there and didn't open it.

On the way back through the armoury he stopped at the Webley case, looked at it, and left it where it was.

Then he walked out through the front of the tent into the rain, pulled the gloves on, and turned on his heel.

***3:02 p.m.***
***Upper Bogachiel, three miles north of the river***

Ezra Coyle had been dead for a hundred and twelve years, and he had never smelled anything like it.

He'd come up out of the Bogachiel bottomland at an easy lope, a man's pace, because there was no hurry and the forest was very beautiful. Moss on everything, a green so deep it was nearly black. Sword fern to the waist. Bigleaf maples standing in the gloom with their arms full of hanging club moss like old men in shawls. He could hear a varied thrush half a mile off and a black bear turning over a log twice that far, and under all of it the long low roar of the river running high with a week's rain. He could smell elk. He could smell, faintly, from the direction of the highway, the warm salt sweetness of people.

He'd fed two weeks ago, on the Oregon coast. He wasn't thirsty in any way that mattered yet. He'd come north because a woman he'd met in a Portland rail yard in August had told him a story about this place, and he'd wanted to see it.

*There's a coven up on the Olympic,* she'd said. *Big one. Yellow-eyes. Eat animals.* She'd laughed at that, the way they all laughed at that. *And there was a whole army of newborns up in Seattle this spring, a couple dozen of them, somebody's project, and the yellow-eyes went out and took it apart. Every one of them. And then the Volturi came and went and nobody died.* She'd looked at him in the dark of the boxcar with her bright red eyes. *Nobody walks away from the Volturi, Ezra.*

So he'd come to look. To see what kind of coven did a thing like that and lived. To see if there was a place in it for a man who didn't mind a deer now and then if it meant somebody else did the worrying.

He'd crossed the 101 at a place where there was no traffic and come up into the hills, and at two minutes past three, in a clearing full of fallen cedar, the wind changed and he stopped dead.

It was the worst thing he'd ever smelled. Wet dog was the start of it, but there was more under that, something rank and hot and wrong, like an animal that had died and kept walking. It filled his nose and coated the back of his throat and he took an involuntary step back, lip curling off his teeth. He'd smelled werewolves once, the old-world kind, in a slaughterhouse in Chicago in 1931. It hadn't been this.

Then he heard them.

Two of them, upwind, coming fast. Heavy feet on soft ground. Big lungs. Two hearts going like engines.

He turned and they came out of the trees.

He had a quarter-second to look at them, and in that quarter-second he understood that the woman in the rail yard had left something out of her story. They were wolves the way a grizzly was a dog. The bigger was grey, a soft dappled silver-grey, and the smaller was a sandy brown, and the smaller one was the size of a horse. They came out of the ferns side by side with their ears flat and their lips peeled back off teeth like kitchen knives, and the grey one's eyes met his, and they were the eyes of a person.

Ezra ran.

He'd never had to run from anything. That was the thing he would remember afterward, with his perfect memory, every second of it laid out like photographs on a table: the strangeness of it. For a hundred and twelve years he'd been the fastest thing in every room, every alley, every forest. He'd outrun trains for fun. He'd once run from Butte to Spokane in a single night to see a woman, and been bored.

He went north and east up the slope, through the cedar and the fallen giants, leaping logs the size of buses, and the wolves came after him and kept up.

They were *driving* him. Every time he tried to cut west, toward the coast and open country, the grey one was there, a grey blur among the trunks on his left, and he had to break right again. The sandy one ran behind him, so close he could hear the wet snap of its jaws on air. They had done this before. They had done this together, many times. They were steering him the way dogs steered a sheep.

He crossed a ridge. He went down the other side through a stand of hemlock in three bounds and hit a river — a fork of something, white water over black stone, twenty yards wide — and cleared it in one jump, landing on the far bank on the balls of his feet and pushing off again without breaking stride. He heard the wolves hit the water behind him in two great slaps of spray. He heard them come out the other side, shaking, snarling, already closing.

Ahead the ground rose into old-growth again. Huge Douglas fir. A bigleaf maple leaning out over a slot of open ground between two fallen trunks. A clear lane.

He went for it at full speed.

He didn't feel anything when he crossed the line. There was no line. He would go back over it later, in the long dark, frame by frame, and find nothing in the ground or the air or the light to show him where it was. He was running, and then his left ankle stopped.

It stopped the way a rope stops at the end of its length. It stopped dead, in the air, in the middle of a stride, while the rest of him kept going at seventy miles an hour.

The world flipped. His body swung up and over, heels to the sky and head to the ground, and he felt the force of it go through his leg and into his hip and spine and every joint he had, all the speed of his run turned back on him in a single violent jerk. Something gripped him, from the ankle up. It came up through his leg like cold water filling a pipe. Calf, knee, thigh, hip, belly, chest, arms, neck, jaw.

Then he was hanging upside down eight feet above the moss under the leaning maple, and he couldn't move.

He couldn't move *anything*.

He tried. He threw his whole strength at it, the strength that had torn steel doors off railcars and lifted an automobile off a man in 1947 so he could get at what was underneath. Nothing happened. His arms hung down past his head, fingers spread, and he couldn't curl one finger. His jaw was locked half open. His chest was locked half full of air and wouldn't take more or let any go. He didn't need to breathe, he hadn't needed to for over a century, but without breath he couldn't smell, and without smell half the world went dark at once, and that more than anything put the first real crack of panic down the middle of him.

His eyes moved. That was all he had. He rolled them up, which was down, at the forest floor, and then down, which was up, along his own body to his left ankle.

There was nothing there. No rope. No wire. A faint silver shimmer in the air from his boot to the underside of the maple bough, thin as a spider's thread and no brighter, and that was all.

The wolves came up the lane and stopped.

They came at a dead run and then dug in all four feet at once and skidded on the wet moss, and stood. The grey one put her head down and growled, a long rolling sound like a truck idling. The sandy one paced, three steps left and three steps right, under him, its huge head turned up, nostrils flaring. He could hear its heart, fast as a hummingbird's. He could see his own reflection, upside down, in its eyes.

The grey one stepped forward. The hair along her spine was standing up in a ridge. She put her nose out toward the silver thread, very slowly, the way a dog put its nose out toward a snake, and then she flinched back from it and snarled.

*Go on then,* Ezra thought at her, or tried to. *Go on. Get it over with.*

They didn't. They circled. Every few seconds the grey one's ears flicked, and the sandy one's ears flicked a fraction after, as if somebody had said something to them both.

He hung there for what his perfect clock told him was one minute and forty-one seconds.

Then, about fifteen feet away, between him and the river, the air cracked like a rifle shot, and a man was standing in the clearing.

Ezra hadn't heard him come. He hadn't heard a footstep, a heartbeat, a breath. There had been a clearing with rain falling in it, and then there was a man in the rain, and the sound of his arrival was still bouncing back off the trees.

He was tall. He wore a dark suit under a long black overcoat, and black gloves, and black boots, and no hat, and the rain was already darkening his hair and his shoulders. He stood with his hands in his coat pockets. He looked at Ezra first, a steady look, top to bottom, the way a man looked over a used car. Then he looked at the wolves.

Now Ezra could hear his heart. Fifty-eight beats a minute. Slow and even. A sleeping man's heart.

"Afternoon," the man said.

He had an accent. English, and rough round the edges, a voice that had done its growing up somewhere with a lot of brick.

The grey wolf's growl went up an octave. The sandy one stopped pacing and stood very still, head low, ears forward.

"Yeah, I know," the man said, as if one of them had spoken. "Bloke in a suit, out of nowhere. Bit much. Hang on."

He took his right hand out of his pocket. In it was a slim black leather wallet. He flipped it open with his thumb and held it out toward the wolves at arm's length, the way a detective did on television, and Ezra, upside down, caught the flash of gold.

"This situation is contained," the man said. "I'd ask you to please vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

There was a silence. The rain fell on the badge.

The grey wolf's lip rolled back further than Ezra would have thought it could go.

"Because the wolf's a person," the man said, "and the person's got a mouth on her. Sorry. Mind on her. It's hard to keep track."

Ezra's eyes went back and forth between the man and the wolves. The grey one had gone utterly still. The sandy one's head came up sharply.

"Heard you too, Balto," the man said to the sandy one. "You're louder."

He was talking to them. He was *answering* them. The wolves hadn't made a sound except growling, and the man was answering something they'd said. Ezra knew a story like that. Every vampire who'd ever lived any length of time knew that story. A man in Volterra who touched your hand and knew every thought you'd ever had. The yellow-eyed coven was supposed to have one too, a boy who heard everything in a mile.

This man's heart was beating. This man was alive.

"Can't, love," the man said to the grey one. "You're shouting. It's like asking me not to hear a car alarm."

The grey wolf took one step forward. Her weight went down into her front legs.

The man didn't move his feet. He didn't take his other hand out of his pocket. His head turned a few degrees, that was all, and he looked at her.

"You'd get about halfway, Lassie," he said. "Then you'd be hanging up there next to him, and I've only got the one table."

The grey wolf stopped.

The sandy one made a sound. Ezra had been around dogs, before, when he'd been alive and when he'd been dead, and he knew that sound. It was a dog trying very hard not to laugh.

The grey one's head whipped round toward it and she snapped, once, at the air by its ear.

"Hero of Nome," the man said to the sandy one, who had flinched and was now looking at him. "Nineteen twenty-five. Diphtheria epidemic. Six hundred and seventy-four miles of serum relay through a blizzard and he ran the last leg in the dark with the wind knocking him over. Bloody legend, mate. Take the compliment."

The sandy one's tail moved. Once. The grey one's head swung back to the man, ears flat.

"Who I am's above your pay grade," the man said to her. "And frankly mine. What I'm doing with him is my business." He tipped his head up at Ezra. "He's been eating hikers. Two in Oregon a fortnight back. Before that, God knows how many. That makes him mine. You'd only burn him, and that's a waste of a perfectly good specimen."

*Specimen.*

The word went into Ezra like a nail.

The sandy wolf whined, low, and looked at Ezra and back at the man.

The man looked up at Ezra too. It was the first time he'd met his eyes directly. They were green, a hard bright green, and they didn't have anything in them Ezra could hold on to. No hate. No hunger. No pity. A mechanic's interest.

"Ask him some questions," the man said, to the wolf. He waited a beat. "Some of them with words."

He put the badge away.

"The doc," he said to the grey one, who seemed to have asked him something, "the missus, five grown kids, a new daughter-in-law and a very loud wolf who sleeps on their porch. Yeah. I know. Tell your Alpha he's got nothing to worry about from me. Tell the doc that too, while you're at it. He'll want to hear it from you before he hears it from anyone else."

A pause. The grey one's growl had stopped. She was standing very still, staring at him, and Ezra could see her flanks going in and out.

"Because I've had six weeks to do whatever I liked to any of you," the man said, "and I've mostly been eating pie."

He took his left hand out of his pocket.

He didn't wave it, didn't point it, didn't say anything. His fingers moved a little inside the black glove, like a man flicking a crumb off a table.

The silver thread at Ezra's ankle let go.

He fell eight feet head first. He couldn't put out his hands. He hit the forest floor crown first, on a rock under the moss, with a sound like a sledgehammer on a kerbstone, and the rock split in two under his skull and his body toppled over sideways, stiff as a plank, and lay there in the ferns with one arm still pointing at the sky.

The grip hadn't let go. Only the thread had. He was still locked from his jaw to his toes. He lay on his side with his face in wet moss, eyes open, and listened to the man walk over to him. Unhurried footsteps. The squelch of good boots in mud.

The man crouched. Ezra rolled his eyes up and saw a black glove and the edge of a charcoal sleeve with rain on it.

"Sorry, Sparky," the man said. "Gravity."

Then he took hold of Ezra by the belt and the back of the collar and lifted him, stiff, horizontal, a hundred and seventy pounds of a body that could stop a truck, and tucked him under one arm like a surfboard.

Ezra's face was pointing at the ground. He could see the man's boots, and the ferns, and the moss, and a broken rock. He could hear the man's slow heart, very close now, through the wool of the coat.

"Lassie," the man said, over his shoulder. "Balto. Go on home. Tell Jacob."

The world folded.

There was no other word for it, and Ezra had a lot of time later to look for one. The forest pinched shut around him like a fist. Something took hold of every part of him at once, his locked limbs and his cold heavy chest and the inside of his skull, and squeezed, and pulled, as if he were being dragged at enormous speed through a pipe a great deal narrower than he was. He couldn't scream. His jaw was locked. He'd forgotten pain could come from outside a body instead of inside it.

Then it stopped, and the air was dry, and the rain was gone.

"Legilimency," Harry said.

"I don't know that one."

"You will." He leaned forward on the stool, elbows on his knees, and looked into the red eyes from about two feet away. "It's the one where I have a look round inside your head."

The vampire's whole face went still.

"Like the man in Volterra," it said.

"Never met him. Heard about him. He has to touch you, I'm told." Harry didn't blink. "I don't."

He went in.

He'd been in a lot of heads over the last eight years. Muggles, wizards, a goblin once, which he didn't recommend, and a dragon, which he did, briefly, for the view. Every mind had a shape when you went into it. Most human minds were houses: some rooms lit, some dark, doors that stuck, a cellar you didn't want to go down into, and a front room where the person lived most of the time, with whatever they were thinking about right now spread out on the coffee table.

Ezra Coyle's mind was a railway station at rush hour, built inside a cathedral.

It was enormous. That was the first thing. It went up and up into the dark further than any human mind he'd ever been in, and it was lit, all of it, hard and bright, with no dim corners anywhere. The second thing was the noise. He'd been ready for one train of thought. There were nine. Ten. They ran side by side and over each other at once, all at full speed, all perfectly clear: one on Harry's face two feet away, one on the exact position of the door, one on the smell of formalin, one on the sound of Harry's heart, one counting the jars, one replaying the fall from the tree at full speed, frame by frame, over and over. One thinking, very quietly, under the others, *Mama.*

He stood still in it for a second and let it go past him.

Then he started walking.

It was easy to get around. The vampire had no idea how to stop him, no walls, no tricks, no training at all. Everything was where it had been put and nothing had ever faded. Harry reached for 1894 and there it was, on a shelf, bright as yesterday. A boarding house on a muddy street in Butte, a bed with an iron frame, a woman with black hair and red eyes sitting on the end of it in the lamplight, smiling at him. Then three days of fire. He let that one go quickly.

The years after were sharp and close and crowded. The rail yards. The boxcars. A hundred towns. Faces. The faces came in a long line and every one was perfectly clear, and there were a great many of them, and Harry walked past them steadily and counted, because he'd said he would. He didn't stop to look at what happened to them. He'd seen it in the Seattle file. He knew.

The older memories were different. The human ones. They were further back and harder to reach, and when he got to them they were faint, washed out, like photographs that had been left in a sunny window for a hundred years. A small wooden house. A woman's hands in a washtub. A dog. The face above the hands was gone. He looked for it and there was only a pale smudge where it should have been, and around the smudge, a great deal of pain.

He found the woman from the Portland rail yard and listened to her tell the story about the Olympic coven and the Seattle newborns. He found New Orleans, 1923, and the thing in the coffin, and was glad to see it was over quickly.

And he found Montreal.

Nineteen fifty-two. Winter. A narrow lane behind a theatre, deep snow, the light from a single bulb over a stage door. Ezra on a fire escape, three floors up, very still, watching.

In the lane below, a vampire Ezra had known for a month, a loud careless red-haired man called Duncan who'd been killing in the open, was on his knees in the snow. Two figures stood over him. They wore long cloaks, dark grey, the hoods up. One of them was tall and broad and the other was slight, and the slight one had his hand on Duncan's shoulder in a way that looked almost kind.

Neither of them said anything. Duncan didn't fight. That was what Ezra had remembered most, for fifty years: Duncan didn't even try. He just knelt there, shaking, as if he couldn't think of a reason to stand up.

Then the tall one took hold of him, and it was over very quickly, and they burned what was left in an oil drum by the stage door. The smoke came up purple in the light of the bulb, thick and heavy, and smelled sweet, like church incense. The slight one turned his head and looked straight up at the fire escape, straight at Ezra, for a long second. Then he turned away, and the two of them walked out of the lane, and the snow began to cover the drum.

Harry stayed in that one a long time. He went over it twice. The cloaks. The size of the tall one. The hand on the shoulder. The way Duncan had knelt.

Then he came out.

He sat back on the stool and breathed out through his nose. The tent was very quiet. On the table, Ezra was staring at the canvas ceiling with his mouth slightly open, as if he'd been hit.

"How," the vampire said.

"Practice." Harry rubbed his eyes with the heels of his gloved hands. Ten trains of thought at once. He'd have a headache for an hour. "Write this down," he said to the quill.

> **MAGICAL RESPONSE — LEGILIMENCY**
>

"Hero of Nome," the man said to Seth, who had stopped ducking and was staring at him. "Nineteen twenty-five. Diphtheria epidemic. Six hundred and seventy-four miles of serum relay through a blizzard and he ran the last leg in the dark with the wind knocking him over. Bloody legend, mate. Take the compliment."

Seth's tail went. Once. She felt the small dumb glow of it through the link and wanted to bite him.

She dragged her eyes back to the man. Fine. Fine. If he was going to sit in her head, he could answer something.

*Who the hell are you? What do you want with the leech?*

"Who I am's above your pay grade," he said. "And frankly mine. What I'm doing with him is my business." He tipped his chin up at the hanging thing. "He's been eating hikers. Two in Oregon a fortnight back. Before that, God knows how many. That makes him mine. You'd only burn him, and that's a waste of a perfectly good specimen."

*Specimen.*

She saw the leech's eyes go wide when he said it.

*What are you gonna do to him?* Seth asked. He sounded about twelve.

The man looked up at the leech. It was the first time he'd looked it in the eye.

"Ask him some questions," he said. He waited a second. "Some of them with words."

Something went down Leah's spine that had nothing to do with the rain.

He put the badge away.

She made herself think it, clear, at him. *The Cullens. Do you know about the Cullens?*

"The doc," he said, "the missus, five grown kids, a new daughter-in-law and a very loud wolf who sleeps on their porch. Yeah. I know. Tell your Alpha he's got nothing to worry about from me. Tell the doc that too, while you're at it. He'll want to hear it from you before he hears it from anyone else."

*Why should we believe a word you say?*

"Because I've had six weeks to do whatever I liked to any of you," he said, "and I've mostly been eating pie."

*Six weeks.*

He took his left hand out of his pocket. He didn't point it at anything. The fingers moved a little inside the glove.

The thread let go, and the leech dropped straight down onto its head.

It hit a rock under the moss with a crack like a dropped bowling ball, and the rock split, and the leech tipped over sideways onto the ferns, stiff as a board, one arm still sticking up at the sky. It didn't move after that. It couldn't. Whatever was holding it hadn't let go when the thread did.

The man walked over to it. He didn't hurry. He crouched beside it.

"Sorry, Sparky," he said. "Gravity."

Then he picked it up. One hand on its belt, one on its collar, and up it came, and he tucked it under his arm the way the surfers at First Beach carried their boards down to the water. A full-grown leech. Like it weighed nothing.

He looked back at them over his shoulder.

"Lassie," he said. "Balto. Go on home. Tell Jacob."

*How does he know Jake's—*

The air cracked again, and he was gone.

---

The rain kept coming down.

It was the only sound for a long time. Rain on the maple leaves, rain on the ferns, rain on the moss. The river behind them, high and loud. Seth's heart, going fast. Hers.

The leech stink was still there, hanging in the clearing like smoke, but it was already fading. There was the split rock. There was a crushed patch of fern where it had fallen. There was the maple bough, and under it there was nothing. No thread. No prickle in the air. She went up to the place where the thread had been and stood right under it and felt nothing at all.

Seth went to where the man had been standing.

*Two footprints,* he thought. *That's it. Just two. He didn't walk here.*

She went and looked. Two boot prints in the mud, deep and clean, a big man's boots, side by side. Then two more where he'd crouched by the leech, and a trail of four or five between. Nothing coming in. Nothing going out.

She put her nose down into the prints and breathed in.

Rain. Mud. Wool. Gun oil, very faint. And that hole. That nothing.

*He knew my name,* she thought. *No. He knew Jake's name. And he called me—*

*Lassie,* Seth supplied.

*If you think that word one more time I'll take your ear off.*

*Okay.* A pause. *But you have to admit Balto's a lot cooler.*

She nipped him on the shoulder. He yelped and sidestepped and then stood there with his ears up, looking at her, and through the link she could feel what was underneath the stupid jokes. He was scared of the hole in the air. Of a man who stood in the rain with steam coming off him, and could hear them, and had picked up a leech like a surfboard, and had said *I've only got the one table* like it was a joke.

She was scared too. He could feel that. She let him.

*Should we follow him?* Seth said.

*Follow what? There's nothing to follow.*

*Then what do we do?*

She looked at the split rock for a second longer. Then she turned east, toward the highway, toward the long gravel drive and the glass house at the end of it, where Jake was sitting on the floor of the living room, right this minute, letting a three-week-old baby who looked a year old pull his hair.

*We do what he said,* Leah thought. *We go tell Jacob.*

She hated that. She hated every bit of it.

They ran.

---

***3:41 p.m.***
***The Cullen house***
*(Nine miles to the south, Harry was in his specimen room with the vampire on the table, dictating the colour of its eyes to a quill.)*

Edward was waiting on the back porch before they'd even cleared the trees.

Seth saw him through the rain from three hundred yards out, a pale shape on the top step with his hands in his pockets, perfectly still, and knew he'd been listening since about the river. Probably since the first crack of the man arriving. Edward could hear them a long way off when they were thinking loud, and Seth had been thinking about as loud as he'd ever thought in his life.

They pulled up at the treeline. Leah went behind a cedar to phase. Seth went behind another one and let go, felt himself fold back down into his human shape, and stood there for a second shivering in the rain, which he never did. He untied the cutoffs from his ankle and pulled them on.

"Inside," Edward said, when they came up the lawn. His voice was quiet. His face was tight. "Carlisle's on his way. I called the hospital."

"You saw?" Seth said.

"I saw." Edward's eyes went past him to the forest, then came back. "Come in. Jacob needs to hear it from you."

The big glass room at the back of the house was warm and bright and smelled like Esme's cooking, which was a nice smell, and also like seven vampires, which wasn't, but Seth didn't really notice that much any more. Jake was on the floor in front of the couch, cross-legged, with Nessie sitting in the triangle of his legs. She was three weeks old. She looked about a year, maybe more, with a mess of bronze curls and Bella's brown eyes, and she had both fists in Jake's hair and was pulling.

Jake looked up as they came in. Whatever he saw in Seth's face made him go still.

"What happened?"

Bella was standing by the window. She turned. Her eyes were still red, bright red, and Seth had mostly stopped noticing that too. Rosalie was on the arm of the couch right by Nessie, as usual, close enough to grab her. Emmett was sprawled on the other end with the TV on mute, some replay of a Seahawks game. Alice sat halfway up the stairs with her arms around her knees, and Jasper stood at the bottom of the stairs beside her with one hand on the banister, and Alice was staring at nothing.

Esme came out of the kitchen with a plate of sandwiches stacked up like bricks.

"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. Eat. You're shaking, Seth."

"I'm okay."

"Eat anyway."

He took four. He sat on the floor by the coffee table. Leah stayed in the doorway with her arms folded, wet hair dripping down her back, the way she always stood in this house, ready to leave.

"There was a leech," Seth said. "A nomad. Red eyes. We caught the scent up on the Bogachiel and chased him. He ran north. Over the ridge, across the Calawah. And then he hit something in the trees and he just — went up."

"Went up," Jake said.

"Like in a snare. Like a rabbit. Upside down, by his foot, eight feet off the ground. And he couldn't move. Not even a finger. There was this thread thing holding him, all silvery, and it was creepy, man, it made all my fur stand up—"

"Seth." Leah, from the doorway. "Tell him about the guy."

"I'm getting to the guy."

"There was a guy?"

"There was a guy," Edward said quietly. He'd come to stand behind the couch. His eyes were on Seth's face, and Seth knew he was watching the whole thing again, through his head, frame by frame. "He appeared. Out of nothing. There was a sound like a gunshot and a man was standing in the clearing."

"What do you mean, appeared?"

"I mean appeared." Edward's jaw shifted. "He wasn't there. Then he was."

Emmett muted the game harder, somehow, and sat up.

Seth told it. He tried to tell it in order. The suit, the coat, the gloves, the slow heartbeat. The badge, and how it said Homeland Security, it definitely said Homeland Security—

"Fish and Wildlife," Leah said.

"Homeland Security—"

"Both," Edward said. They both looked at him. "It said both. In your memory it says Homeland Security, Seth, with an eagle. In Leah's it says Fish and Wildlife Service. Special agent. Same card. Same photograph."

The room was quiet for a moment.

"So it's a fake," Jacob said.

"It's something," Edward said. "I don't know what. It showed each of them something they'd believe."

Seth kept going. *This situation is contained.* The national security thing. And then the part where Leah had thought *why's he showing a badge to a wolf,* and the man had answered her.

"Out loud?" Bella said.

"Out loud," Seth said. "He answered stuff we were *thinking.* Stuff in the link. He said 'because the wolf's a person.' Then he said I was louder."

He looked at Edward. Edward's face hadn't moved at all.

"He did," Edward said. "I can see his mouth in the memory. He answers every thought, Jacob. He answers them before either of them finishes thinking them."

Jake was very still on the floor. Nessie let go of his hair and looked up at him.

"Like you," Jake said.

"Like me." Edward didn't look happy about it. "Or like Aro."

"He doesn't have to touch," Leah said from the door.

"No."

Then Seth got to the part about the nicknames, and he tried really hard, he did, he tried to just say it like a fact.

"And then Leah thought she'd rip his throat out, and he said she'd get about halfway, and he called her Lassie."

Emmett made a sound like a car backfiring.

He tried to hold it in. Seth watched him try. He put a hand over his mouth and his shoulders shook and then he gave up and fell back on the couch, howling, both arms over his face. Rosalie's mouth had gone very thin and very straight, and Seth could tell she was trying not to smile, which was somehow worse.

"*Lassie,*" Emmett wheezed.

Leah looked at him like she could kill him with her eyes.

"And he called me Balto," Seth said, a little louder, because nobody seemed to be paying attention to that part. "Which is actually cool. Balto was a hero. He knew all this stuff about the serum run to Nome."

"He called you a *sled dog,*" Rosalie said.

"A hero sled dog."

On the floor, Jake was grinning. He couldn't help it. Seth could see him trying to stop, and his mouth kept going up at one side.

"Lassie," Jake said.

"Say it again," Leah said. "I dare you, Jacob."

"I'm just saying it suits—"

"Jacob."

"Okay." He held up a hand. The grin went away, mostly. "Okay. Keep going, Seth."

So Seth kept going. *Who I am's above your pay grade.* The hikers in Oregon. *A perfectly good specimen.* And then the part about the questions, *some of them with words,* and nobody in the room laughed at that.

"He knew about you," Seth said, looking round at all of them. "He said 'the doc, the missus, five grown kids, a new daughter-in-law and a very loud wolf who sleeps on their porch.'"

"I don't sleep on the porch," Jake said.

"You totally sleep on the porch."

"*Sometimes.*"

"He said to tell you that you've got nothing to worry about from him," Seth said. "And to tell Carlisle the same. He said Carlisle would want to hear it from us first. Before anyone else."

"Before anyone else," Jasper said. It was the first thing he'd said. "Meaning there's someone else he could hear it from."

"Meaning he means to tell Carlisle himself," Edward said. "Eventually."

Then Seth got to the end. The leech falling on its head. *Sorry, Sparky.* Picking it up like a surfboard. *Go on home. Tell Jacob.* The crack.

"He knew my name," Jake said slowly.

"He knew everything," Leah said. "He said he'd been here six weeks."

Outside, gravel crunched. A car door. Then Carlisle was in the room, still in his hospital coat with his ID badge on a lanyard round his neck, rain on his blond hair. He didn't say anything. He went straight to Edward, and Edward looked at him, and Seth watched Carlisle's face change as Edward talked to him without making a sound, too fast for Seth to follow, the way they did sometimes.

It took about four seconds.

Carlisle turned to Seth and Leah. "Are either of you hurt?"

"No."

"Did he touch you? Either of you? Did he come close?"

"Fifteen feet," Leah said. "Never closer."

Carlisle breathed out. He sat down on the edge of the armchair by the fire and put his hands together between his knees and looked at the floor.

"Alice," he said.

"I saw the nomad this morning." Alice's voice came from the stairs, light and thin. She hadn't moved. "Coming up the coast. I saw him decide to come and look at us. I was going to tell you tonight. He'd fed recently. He was curious. Then at fourteen minutes past three he went dark." She looked at Seth and Leah. "I thought it was you. You go dark. I thought you got him and that was that."

"We nearly did," Leah said.

"I've been looking for the other one since Edward heard Seth coming." Alice's eyes were wide. "The man. I can't see him. I can't see any of it. I can't see what happens to the nomad. There's a hole."

"Like with the wolves," Jasper said.

"Like with the wolves."

"He isn't a wolf," Leah said flatly. "He doesn't smell like anything. Not like us, not like you, not like a person. Nothing."

"He was hot, though," Seth said.

Everyone looked at him.

"Not like *that.*" His face went red. "I mean he ran hot. There was steam coming off him in the rain. Like off us."

"I saw it," Edward said. "In both your memories. It was coming off his shoulders."

"Maybe he'd been running," Emmett said.

"He just showed up. Out of nowhere."

Nobody said anything for a while.

On the floor, Nessie had gone very quiet. She was looking from face to face, the way she did when the grown-ups got serious, taking everything in with those big brown eyes. Then she reached up and put her small warm hand flat on Jake's cheek.

Seth saw Jake's eyes go unfocused. Whatever she was showing him, it made his mouth twitch.

"Nessie," Jake said. "No."

"What'd she show you?" Bella asked.

"A dog." Jake pulled the hand gently off his face and held it. "From that picture book Esme reads her. The collie on the farm."

Emmett lost it again. Even Esme smiled. Leah turned her face to the wall.

Carlisle didn't smile. He was looking into the fire, and when he spoke he spoke quietly, the way he did when he was talking about something a long way back.

"When I was a boy," he said, "my father hunted witches."

The room went still.

"Witches, and werewolves, and vampires. In London, in the sixteen-fifties and sixties. He and the other pastors would gather a mob and go out into the parishes and find someone. Usually a woman. Usually old, or alone, or strange in some way, or simply hated by the wrong neighbour. And they'd burn her." He turned his hands over and looked at them. "I believed, my whole life, after, that every one of them was innocent. That there was no such thing. That my father had murdered a great many harmless people for nothing."

"Carlisle," Esme said softly.

"I still believe most of them were." He looked up. "I think now I may have been wrong about all of them."

"Great," Rosalie said. "Witches."

"Wizards," Emmett said. "If it's a guy. He'd be a wizard."

"Thank you, Emmett."

"Just saying."

Jasper hadn't moved from the stairs. He was looking at the map in his head, Seth could tell, his eyes gone far away the way Jake's did when he was planning a patrol.

"The trap was on the north fork of the Calawah," Jasper said. "We hunted that valley on Thursday. Emmett and I. We went straight through it. Twice."

"So did I," Bella said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "With Edward. Last week. We were up past the forks."

"It let you through," Carlisle said.

"It let all of us through," Jasper said. "Every one of us. And it took the first one that came along that wasn't us." He looked at Carlisle. "That's a choice, Carlisle. He made a trap that can tell us apart. He knows what each of us is. He knows what we eat."

Rosalie had shifted on the arm of the couch. She'd put one hand down on the back of Nessie's curls, very lightly.

"Then he knows about her," she said.

"He didn't say anything about Nessie," Seth said.

"He didn't have to." Rosalie's eyes were on the window, on the trees. "You said he's been here six weeks. Six weeks. He'll have seen everything."

Jake's arms had gone round Nessie without him seeming to notice. He was holding her the way he held her when one of the Cullens came too close too fast.

"Bella." Edward had turned toward her. "Charlie."

Bella's face had changed. "He talked about someone," she said. "Dad. On the phone, Friday. There's a guy at the diner every morning, Dad said. English. Studies birds up in the old growth. Terrible at talking about fishing. Dad likes him." She swallowed, which she didn't need to do any more and still did. "He said his name was Evans."

"Six weeks," Leah said.

"Six weeks," Bella said. "He's been eating breakfast with my father for six weeks."

Edward was beside her before Seth saw him move. He didn't say anything. He just stood close.

"He said we've got nothing to worry about," Seth said. "He said it twice, basically."

"He also said *specimen,*" Leah said.

"He took a leech that kills people off the Peninsula," Seth said. "That's — I mean, that's good, isn't it? That's what we'd have done."

"We'd have killed it and burned it and gone home," Leah said. "He took it home with him."

Carlisle stood up.

"I'd like to meet him," he said.

"Yeah," Jake said. "So would I." He looked up from the floor, and his face had gone hard, the face he got when he was being the Alpha and didn't like it. "Tomorrow. First light. We go back to that river, and we find out where he went, and I talk to him."

"*We* talk to him," Carlisle said gently. "And we go to talk, Jacob. Only that. He was very clear that he had no quarrel with us. I'd like to keep it that way."

"I want to be there," Edward said. "If he can hear thoughts, I'd like to know whether I can hear his."

"And if you can't?" Jasper said.

Edward didn't answer that.

"If he calls me Lassie again," Leah said from the doorway, "I'm taking his hand off."

"You'll what?" Emmett said. "Fetch?"

Rosalie laughed out loud. Leah went out the back door and slammed it so hard the glass rattled in the frame.

Seth sat there with the last sandwich in his hand. He wasn't hungry any more, which never happened. He looked at the fire, and at Nessie, who had put her head on Jake's chest, and at Carlisle, who had gone to the window and was standing there in his hospital coat with his hands behind his back, looking out at the trees and the rain and the afternoon going grey.

"Carlisle," Seth said.

"Yes, Seth."

"What do you think he's doing to it? Right now?"

Carlisle didn't answer for a long time.

Seth thought about the thread, and the leech's red eyes rolling back and forth, and the man looking up at it like a mechanic looking at an engine. *Ask him some questions. Some of them with words.* He thought about the thing he'd said to Leah, so easy, like a joke. *I've only got the one table.*

He'd thought it was funny at the time.

"I don't know," Carlisle said at last, without turning round. "I don't think I want to know. And I think, very soon, we're going to have to find out."

Out past the glass, the forest went on, mile after mile of it, dark and wet and silent. Somewhere in it a man in a suit was writing everything down.
