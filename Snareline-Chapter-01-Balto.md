# Snareline

## Chapter One — Balto

---

### I. Magically Delicious

**Monday, October 2, 2006 — Forks Outfitters Thriftway, Forks, Washington — 4:12 p.m. PDT**

*Potter, H. — Department of Mysteries, Level Nine, Ministry of Magic, London. 12:12 a.m. BST. Still at his bench.*

---

The list was written on the back of a Clallam County PUD envelope in his mom's slanting capitals, and the last line had been underlined twice.

*SOMETHING GREEN, SETH. NOT A POPSICLE.*

Seth Clearwater stood in the produce aisle of the Thriftway with a red plastic basket hooked over his forearm and considered the celery. Celery was green. Celery was also, as far as he could tell, crunchy water, and he'd been eating like a wolf for most of a year—literally, which was the problem—and he had opinions about food that didn't fill you up.

He put two bunches in the basket anyway. His mom was making meatloaf. Charlie was coming over. Charlie liked celery in his meatloaf, apparently, which Seth had learned the same way he'd learned most things about Charlie Swan in the last couple of months: by being in the kitchen while his mom cooked and not saying one single word about the fact that she was cooking for Charlie Swan.

The misters over the lettuce kicked on with a hiss. He smelled the water before it hit—chlorinated, metallic, city water that had sat in a pipe too long—and under it the bruised-sweet rot of a cantaloupe somebody had stuffed behind the bananas, and under that the floor wax, the meat counter, the burnt coffee in the deli, wet wool, wet Gore-Tex, and wet dog off a guy by the dairy cooler who'd obviously just come in from the woods. Everybody in Forks smelled a little like wet dog in October. Seth smelled a lot like wet dog all year. He figured it evened out.

An old woman in a clear plastic rain bonnet watched him reach for the onions. She was looking at his arms. He was in a T-shirt and cutoffs, and outside the rain was coming down the way it did on the peninsula once fall really got going, gray and steady and patient, like it had a lot of time and nowhere to be. He gave her a smile. She gave him a look like he'd personally insulted her by not having pneumonia, and pushed her cart off toward the canned soup.

*I run hot,* he thought at her back. *A hundred and eight. You should feel my sister.*

Actually, nobody should feel his sister. Leah would bite them.

Onions. Three pounds of ground beef—his mom had written *2* and crossed it out and written *3*, because Charlie was coming and because Seth was Seth. Sage. Bread crumbs. A can of evaporated milk, which he had to ask a stock boy about, because he'd thought it was the same thing as condensed milk and was informed, with some pity, by a kid in a Forks Spartans lanyard that it was not.

He kept drifting while he walked. He did that a lot lately. Three weeks wasn't very long, and a lot had happened in it.

Three weeks since he'd stood in the Cullens' living room listening to Bella's heart stutter and quit and kick and quit again, a sound that had made his own chest hurt. Three weeks since Jake had gone quiet in the pack mind—just quiet, all of him, like somebody had taken the anger he'd been hauling around for a year and set it down very gently on the floor—and then the inside of Jake's head had filled up with a baby and nothing else. Seth had been there for that. In Jake's head, he meant. You couldn't not be.

Now the baby was three weeks old and looked six months, and she'd said her first word at a week—*Momma*—and she grabbed your face with her little hot hand and showed you pictures. Seth had been shown a lot of pictures. Mostly of himself, and of Jake, and once of a deer Bella had caught, from an angle he was pretty sure was Rosalie's arms, which had been a lot, honestly, for a guy who'd just eaten.

Bella's eyes were red now. Bright red, newborn red. She'd been human and his friend and kind of a disaster, and now she was a vampire and his friend and somehow not a disaster at all, which nobody could explain. Jasper said he'd never seen a newborn like her. She'd arm-wrestled Emmett on a boulder out back and broken the boulder and Emmett's whole sense of himself. Edward was playing the piano again. Jake slept on the Cullens' couch and growled at anybody who called the baby anything but Nessie, except Bella, who'd nearly taken his head off for naming her daughter after the Loch Ness Monster, and whom he let call her whatever she wanted, because she was the mom.

And Leah was Leah. Leah had followed him into Jake's pack because she'd rather eat glass than spend another day in Sam's head, and because—Seth was pretty sure—she'd rather eat glass than let her little brother run around with vampires by himself. She'd never say the second part out loud. She said the first part constantly.

Everybody was okay. That was what he kept coming back to, wandering the aisles with his celery. For the first time since Dad, everybody he loved was more or less okay at the same time. Nobody was trying to kill anybody. The treaty was a thing people argued about over dinner, and he was in the Thriftway buying meatloaf stuff so his mom could feed Charlie Swan, who'd been Dad's best friend and who came by on Mondays now.

He thought Dad would've liked that. He thought Dad would've given Charlie hell about it for about a week, and then he'd have liked it.

He got in line.

There was one register open, because it was four o'clock on a Monday in Forks and the Thriftway had never in its life opened two. The guy at the front was paying for a forty-pound bag of kibble and a case of Rainier with a check, which took a while. Behind him was a girl.

Seth noticed her first because she was talking to herself, quietly, in an accent he'd only ever heard on TV.

"—no, that's the one with the—right. Right. Okay."

She had a basket like his. In it: eggs, a gallon of milk, a loaf of the bread his mom called cardboard, a jar of Skippy, a box of tea she was holding at arm's length and glaring at like it had said something about her mother, and a box of Lucky Charms.

She was small—she'd have come up to his shoulder, maybe—and narrow, in a navy raincoat at least one size too big, the sleeves shoved up past her wrists. Her hair was dark brown and rain-dark on top, twisted into a knot at the back of her head with a stick pushed through it to hold it there: dark wood, longer than a pencil, with a little carved knob on the end. Freckles across her nose. She smelled like rain and old paper, and under that, something sharp and clean he didn't have a word for. Like the air right before lightning hits.

The kibble guy tore off his check. The girl stepped up and started unloading.

"Four seventeen," said the cashier, a junior from Forks High named Kaylee something, and popped her gum.

"Right." The girl dug in her raincoat pocket and came out with a fistful of coins and two crumpled singles. She laid the bills on the counter and looked down at the change in her palm. "Right. So this is a quarter."

"Uh-huh."

"And this is . . ." She held up a nickel. "Five?"

"Yep."

"And this one, which is *smaller*"—she held up a dime, pinched between finger and thumb like a piece of evidence—"is ten."

"That's a dime."

"Yes, I've gathered it's called a dime. I'm asking *why*. Why is the ten smaller than the five? Who decided that? Was there a meeting?"

Kaylee looked at her with the bottomless patience of a sixteen-year-old being paid six-fifty an hour. "I dunno."

The girl turned the dime over. "It's got a man on it."

"That's Roosevelt," Seth said, before he'd decided to say anything, and laughed. "And I don't know either. Nobody knows. It's like a national mystery."

She turned around.

---

Seth had seen it happen. That was the stupid part. He'd seen it from the inside more times than he'd ever wanted to.

Sam, first—before Seth had been a wolf, so he'd only gotten it secondhand, in pieces, the memories Sam couldn't stop replaying: Emily in the doorway of Sam's mom's kitchen and Sam's entire life swinging toward her like a gate on a hinge. Jared and Kim. Quil and little Claire, which had been weird until it wasn't, because once you'd been in Quil's head you knew exactly what that was and what it wasn't. Jake, three weeks ago, live.

So he knew the shape of it. He'd figured—on the rare occasions he'd thought about it, because he'd been fifteen for most of a year and mostly what he thought about was food and not dying—he'd figured knowing would make it different when it was him. That he'd see it coming. That it would feel familiar.

She turned around and looked up at him with a little crease of irritation between her eyebrows that was about halfway to a laugh, and her eyes were hazel, more green than brown under the fluorescent lights, and the floor went out from under him.

The floor and everything else he'd ever been standing on.

He would try to explain it later, to Leah, and fail, and Leah would say *I know what it's like, Seth, I've had to watch it a hundred times,* in a voice like a door slamming. But if he'd had the words right then, in the checkout line, he'd have said it was like he'd been held to the ground his whole life by a hundred little threads—his mom and Leah and Jake and the pack and the rez and First Beach and Dad's grave and the smell of the woods after rain and the Cullens, even, the stupid Cullens—and in one second every single one of those threads got picked up and re-tied to a point about two feet in front of him and a foot and a half down. They were all still there. He still loved every one of them. They just all ran through her now.

She was what was holding him up.

He thought, very clearly, in the part of his brain that was still running the store: *Oh, no.*

And then: *Oh. Oh, no, no—oh.*

"Are you all right?" the girl said. The crease had changed shape. "You've gone a bit—"

"Dime," Seth said.

"Sorry?"

"It's a dime. The small one. Ten cents." He heard himself. He kept going, because stopping seemed worse. "So you need four seventeen, and you've got two dollars, so you need two seventeen more, and that's—you've got enough quarters, I think, can I—"

He held out his hand. She looked at it, and at him, and then slowly, with the face of someone deciding whether he was very nice or very strange, she tipped the change into his palm.

Her fingers brushed his.

"Blimey," she said, and pulled her hand back, more surprised than anything. "You're *boiling*. Are you ill?"

"No. I run hot."

"You run *very* hot."

"It's a family thing." He counted out eight quarters, a dime, a nickel, and two pennies and set them on the counter, and his hands were steady, which was amazing, because nothing else about him was. Kaylee rang it in with the expression of somebody who'd be telling this story at lunch tomorrow. "There you go."

"Thank you." The girl was still looking at him. "Sorry—thank you. I've been here six weeks and I still pay for everything with notes and come home with a pocket full of these. Mum says if I fall in the river I'll sink." She stuck out her hand, then seemed to remember the boiling thing, and left it out there anyway. "Layla."

He shook it. Her hand was small and cold from the rain. "Seth."

"Seth." She said it like she was filing it somewhere. "Layla Ashby. We've just moved here. Well—six weeks. From Exeter. That's in England."

"I figured England."

"The accent gave it away."

"The accent, and you hate the tea."

She looked down at the box like she'd forgotten she was holding it. "*This*," she said, with feeling, "is dust in a bag. I don't know what you people have done to it."

"We threw the good stuff in a harbor once. Maybe we never got over it."

That got him a real laugh—short and surprised, like he'd tripped it out of her. Something in his chest did a thing he would think about for the rest of the night.

"You moved here with your mom?" he said. "Is she—what does she do?"

"She's a doctor. At the hospital. A and E—sorry, the ER."

"Oh. So she works with Dr. Cullen."

"You know Dr. Cullen?" Her eyebrows went up. "Mum won't stop talking about him. She says he's the best diagnostician she's ever worked with, and then she goes a bit pink, and I have to leave the room."

Seth tried to picture telling this girl that Dr. Cullen was three hundred and sixty-something years old and that he'd once watched him reset every bone on the right side of Jake's body on a dining-room table. "He's nice," he said.

"Um." Kaylee had her hand on the bagging carousel. "You guys are kind of holding up the line."

Seth looked back. The old woman in the rain bonnet had arrived behind him with a cart full of soup and a face like a closed fist.

"Sorry," Layla said, to everyone, and started shoving things into a plastic bag. The Lucky Charms went in last. She paused with it halfway in and read the box, and her mouth twitched.

"'Magically delicious,'" she read, and snorted, like it was a joke only she'd been told. "Sorry. I've never—what *is* it? Is it cereal?"

"It's marshmallows pretending to be cereal."

"Brilliant." She hefted the bag. Then she looked at him again, and there was a pause where she seemed to be making up her mind about something. "Do you go to the high school? Forks? I've not seen you."

"La Push. The tribal school. Down on the coast."

"Oh." It was a small *oh*, and he couldn't read it, and he wanted to read it more than he'd ever wanted to read anything. "Well. Thanks for the maths, Seth."

"Any time," he said, which was the truest thing he'd ever said to anybody, and she didn't know it, and he watched her walk out through the automatic doors into the rain with her bag on her hip and that dark stick through her hair like a pin through a map.

"Dude," Kaylee said.

He looked down. His basket was still on his arm. He hadn't put one single thing on the belt.

Behind him, the old woman in the rain bonnet sighed.

---

His mom was waiting in the parking lot with the engine running and the wipers going, because Seth had a learner's permit and a body that could outrun a deer, and Sue Clearwater had opinions about which of those things he was allowed to use on Highway 110 in the rain.

He got in. He put the bag in the footwell. He sat there.

His mom looked at him. She had her reading glasses pushed up in her hair and her council face on, the one she'd been wearing since she took Dad's seat, the one that could look at a room full of shouting men and wait until they were done.

"What happened?" she said. "You look like somebody hit you with a two-by-four."

"Mom," Seth said. "I think I imprinted."

The wipers went back and forth. Back and forth.

"On who?" she said finally.

"A girl. In the checkout. She's English. Her mom's a doctor at the hospital with Dr. Cullen. She didn't know what a dime was." He thought about it. "She bought Lucky Charms."

His mom was quiet for so long he started to worry. Then she put her hand over her mouth, and her shoulders shook, and he realized she was laughing—really laughing, the kind he hadn't heard out of her since before the funeral, helpless and wet around the edges.

"Well," she said, when she could. She wiped under her glasses with a knuckle. "Well. At least she's not a vampire."

"Mom."

"I'm happy for you. I am." She reached over and squeezed the back of his neck, the way Dad used to, and then she took her hand back and put the car in reverse and looked over her shoulder so he couldn't see her face. "Your father would have—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

"Yeah," Seth said. "I know."

---

He phased at ten that night, out behind the house where the salal got thick, and Leah was already in there.

He felt her the second the change finished folding him up: Leah running the ridge above the Quillayute at a pace that said she'd been out a while and was in a mood about it, the forest coming at her in a smear of black and green and silver, her paws throwing up mud. And then she felt him, and he felt her feel him, and before he could get a single thought in order she was in his memory of the checkout line like somebody kicking down a door.

*No,* Leah said.

*Leah—*

*No. No. You imprinted. In the Thriftway.*

*Yeah.*

*Over Lucky Charms.*

*She was buying the Lucky Charms. I was buying celery.*

*Oh, well, that's so much better.*

Under the words, he felt her other thing, the one she always tried to keep shoved down where nobody could get at it and never could, because nobody could ever keep anything in here: Sam's face in Emily's kitchen, and Leah on the other side of the door. It went through both of them like cold water and then she slammed it back down so hard he flinched.

*Hey,* Jake said, coming in from somewhere east, near the Cullens'—Seth could feel the river close to him, and a baby's breathing through an open window, and Jake's whole self curled around it. *Congrats, kid.*

*Thanks, Jake.*

*She seems nice,* Leah said grudgingly, after a while. *I hate her.*

He knew she didn't mean it. Mostly.

They ran. The rain had let up and the moon was coming through in pieces, five days off full. Seth ran his loop of the treaty line and thought about hazel eyes and a crease between her eyebrows and couldn't stop, and Leah, who couldn't stop hearing him, said *Oh my God* every four minutes like a clock.

*She had a stick in her hair,* Leah said, around midnight.

*It's a chopstick. Like a hair chopstick.*

*It was not a chopstick. Chopsticks don't have a handle.*

*Why are you even looking at her hair?*

*Because you are, you idiot, you've looked at it about nine hundred times.* A pause, Leah's paws hitting granite, then soft forest floor. *She smelled weird, too.*

*She smelled great.*

*She smelled,* Leah said, *like a fuse box.*

Seth didn't have an answer for that. He kept running. Somewhere in town, a girl from Exeter was eating marshmallows out of a box, and he didn't know which house, and he could feel her anyway, a thread pulled tight between his ribs and the dark.

### II. Things About Seth That Do Not Add Up

**Monday, November 6, 2006 — 418 Spruce Street, Forks, Washington — 3:51 p.m. PST**

*Potter, H. — Department of Mysteries, Level Nine, London. 11:51 p.m. GMT. Reading the Volterra file. Fourth pass.*

---

The list was on page forty-one of Layla's Charms notebook, between a diagram of the wand movement for the Protean Charm and three paragraphs she'd copied out of *Achievements in Charming* about the Fidelius, and she had been adding to it for five weeks.

**THINGS ABOUT SETH THAT DO NOT ADD UP**

1. *Temperature. Has not been measured. Have held my hand an inch from the side of his neck (he thought I was going to kiss him; did not correct him; that is a separate problem) and it was like standing next to Gran's Aga. Muggle normal is 37°C. He is not 37°C.*
2. *Never cold. Has never once worn a coat. It has rained for thirty-four days.*
3. *Eats like a Hippogriff. Ate an entire rotisserie chicken in Port Angeles in the time it took me to find a napkin.*
4. *"Patrol." Says it, then says "work." There is no work.*
5. *Smells sometimes of something sweet and cold. Lilies in a fridge. Strongest after he's been at the Cullens'.*
6. *Is friends with the Cullens, who do not add up either. (See separate list. Dr. C: cold hands, gold eyes, Mum says he never seems to sleep. Mum says this like it's a compliment.)*
7. *Has grown. Is taller than he was on 2nd October. I'm sure of it. I've checked against the doorframe.*
8. *Not a werewolf. Full moon 7th Oct—he was here till nine fixing the porch step and eating our biscuits. Full moon last night—rang at half seven to say goodnight, sounded fine, sounded like he was outdoors. NOT a werewolf.*
9. *Is lovely.*

Number nine had been crossed out twice and written in again both times. Layla looked at it now, sitting cross-legged on her bed with the notebook on her knees and rain ticking against the window, and didn't cross it out a third time.

Biscuit made a small disapproving noise from the curtain rail. He was a little brown owl with an opinion about everything, and he'd come over the Atlantic in a box with air holes and a Muggle-Repelling Charm on the lid, which was illegal in four ways her father had explained at length, and he'd been furious about it for a month. Now he lived on the curtain rail and went out at night to terrorize the local mice and come home with pine needles in his feathers. Barred owls were common on the peninsula, Seth had told her once, pointing up into the dark over Second Beach. Nobody would look twice.

She hadn't said, *That's good, because I've got one in my bedroom.* She'd said, *Oh, how lovely,* and felt like a liar for the rest of the night.

That was the other list. The one she didn't write down.

"I know," she told Biscuit. "I know. Don't look at me like that."

The two pieces of sea glass were lying on her desk on a folded square of flannel, next to her wand and a mug of tea gone cold. They were green, the soft frosted green of old bottle glass, worn smooth by the Pacific; Seth had picked them up off Rialto Beach three Sundays ago and given her the better one and kept the other, and she'd asked to borrow his back and he'd handed it over without asking why, which was another thing about Seth. She'd drilled them both with a needle and a Severing Charm so fine it had taken her nine tries. Then she'd spent nine days on the rest.

A Protean Charm, first—N.E.W.T. standard, Professor Flitwick had said, holding up a Galleon in Charms the year before, *and the less said about the last student who used one to run a secret society under Dolores Umbridge's nose, the better*—and then he'd winked. That linked them. Change one and the other changed with it. That part was easy.

The second part wasn't in any book she had. She'd built it out of the Protean link and an old theory paper on Apparition anchors her father had sent her over the summer, with a note on the front in his small tidy writing that said *Don't try this at home, Lay*, which in her father's language meant *I knew you'd want to read it*. If Seth's piece broke, hers would break with it. And when it broke, it would pull. A thread, out of her sternum, toward wherever his was. And if she was quick, and she didn't think about anything else, she could Apparate down the thread like a bead sliding down a string.

She thought. She'd tested it eleven times with a teacup in the back garden, and the teacup had arrived every time, and only once in two pieces.

She picked up her wand and slid it back through her hair, twisting the knot tight. Cherry and unicorn hair, ten and a quarter inches, slightly springy. Ollivander had handed it to her the August she was eleven and said *Oh, yes. Yes, very good—a wand for someone who will always want to know how things work,* and her mother, a Muggle doctor in a Diagon Alley shop with her purse clutched to her chest, had said *That's her, all right*.

Her mother didn't know about the sea glass. Her mother knew about Seth, and liked Seth, and said *that boy is going to eat us out of house and home* in a voice that meant *please bring that boy round more*. Her mother had worked with Dr. Cullen for eleven weeks now and had stopped going pink about him, mostly. Her mother was on a twelve-hour shift tonight and wouldn't be home till eight.

Layla looked at the list one more time.

The International Statute of Wizarding Secrecy had been signed in 1689. Her father had made her learn the year before she could ride a bike. *Do you know why it exists, Lay? Because the last time we trusted them, they lit fires.* He'd said it like a bedtime story. He worked in the Improper Use of Magic Office, third desk from the window, and he had spent twenty-two years writing letters to teenagers who'd turned their cousins' hair blue in front of Muggles, and he believed in the Statute the way some people believed in God.

She'd thought about it for five weeks. She'd thought about it until she couldn't sleep.

She'd decided. Tonight.

She tore out a page and wrote her mother a note—*Gone to the beach with Seth. Back by nine. Love you. L xx*—and propped it against the kettle. Then she put the sea glass in her raincoat pocket, picked up her mum's car keys off the hook, and went to wait on the porch.

---

He came up the street at a jog at ten past four with his hands in the pockets of his cutoffs and his hair wet, as if it were July.

"You *ran*," Layla said.

"Yeah."

"From La Push."

"It's like fifteen miles."

"That's what I said."

He grinned at her from the bottom of the steps, and she felt it in her stomach, the way she always did, which was ridiculous and also not something she had a list for. He'd definitely grown. He had to duck a little now when he came up onto the porch. "You said you had to tell me something," he said.

"I said I *wanted* to talk."

"Right. Yeah. Me too. I mean—I have to tell you something. I've been meaning to." He rubbed the back of his neck. "It's kind of a big something."

"Funny," Layla said. "So's mine."

They looked at each other.

"You first," he said.

"Absolutely not."

---

She drove. She'd passed her test in Exeter in August, three days before the movers came; whether a British licence counted in the State of Washington was a question she had decided, after some thought, not to ask. Her mum's Subaru smelled like hospital hand gel and wet dog—Seth's fault, the wet dog, and he'd been mortified when she'd pointed it out the first time, which was why she'd never pointed it out again.

He was quiet on the drive down to La Push. Seth was not usually quiet. Seth talked the way other people breathed, about everything—his mum, his sister, his friend Jake, the fish his dad used to catch, a girl named Nessie he talked about like a little cousin, the Cullens, food, more food, a movie he wanted her to see, why Port Angeles was a city but Forks wasn't, a time he'd fallen off the cliffs at La Push on purpose, which he said everybody did, which she had chosen to believe was a Quileute joke. Tonight he sat in the passenger seat with his knee bouncing and watched the trees go by and didn't say anything at all.

By the time they pulled into the lot at First Beach the sun was gone behind the cloud bank out over the ocean, and the light had gone the color of pewter. The sea stacks stood up out of the surf like broken teeth. The big island—A-Ka-Lat, Seth had told her, the Top of the Rock—was black against the last of the sky. Driftwood lay along the top of the beach in heaps, whole trees bleached silver, stacked by winter storms higher than her head.

It was cold. She could see her breath. Seth got out of the car in a T-shirt.

They walked. The sand was dark and hard-packed and wet, and her trainers left prints that filled with water behind her. Seth's feet were bare. He walked on the side nearest the sea, she noticed, the way he always did, like the ocean might try something.

When they'd gone far enough that the car was a pale smudge behind them, he stopped.

"Okay," he said. "So."

"So."

"It's going to sound insane."

"I've a very open mind."

"Like, *really* insane." He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the tree line, where the forest came down black to the top of the beach. "I don't think I can tell you. I think I've got to show you. Is that okay? Will you—just stay here? Right here? And don't freak out. Or, I mean, freak out a little, that's fine, but don't run, okay? Please don't run."

Something in Layla's chest went very still and very attentive.

"Seth," she said. "What—"

"Just—one second."

He walked away from her up the beach, quickly, toward one of the big driftwood heaps. He went around the back of it. She heard, quite clearly, the sound of fabric—of a zip, and denim being pulled off, and then a pause, and then—

Then a sound like a sail filling all at once in a hard wind, a crack and a shudder and a long ripping exhale, and the driftwood pile shifted, and something came around the side of it.

Layla had her wand out of her hair and pointed before she'd thought the word *wand*.

It was a wolf.

It was a wolf the size of a horse—bigger than the horses at her gran's riding school, the shoulder higher than her head—and it was sand-colored, pale gold-brown, its fur rough and thick and already beaded with mist. It had enormous paws that sank into the wet sand. It was looking at her. Its ears were down and back, and its tail was low, and it was looking at her with eyes she knew.

The first thing she thought, in a cold bright voice that sounded like her father's: *Werewolf. The moon was full last night.*

The second thing, faster, in her own voice: *Look at it.* Werewolves weren't that big. Werewolves didn't look like that—they looked like wolves that had gone wrong, all bad angles and long limbs, the pictures in *Fantastic Beasts* made her skin crawl. Werewolves didn't change at will at dusk the day *after* the full moon, with the moon not even up. And a werewolf, transformed, would have gone for her throat by now. This one had put itself down in the sand.

It had lain down. It had stretched out its forelegs and put its great head on its paws, low, slowly, the way a dog does when it wants you to know it's harmless, and it looked up at her from there with Seth's eyes, and whined—a high thin sound, much too small for anything that size.

Her wand hand was shaking. She lowered it an inch.

"Seth?" she said.

The wolf's tail thumped once, heavily, in the sand.

"Oh," Layla said. "Oh, *bloody hell*."

She took a step forward. Then another. Up close it smelled like Seth, like wet dog and cedar and woodsmoke, and the heat coming off it was enormous, like standing at the open door of an oven; the mist was steaming off its back. She held out the hand that didn't have a wand in it. The wolf held very still. She put her palm flat against the side of its face, below the eye, where the fur was short and dense, and it was burning hot, a hundred and eight at least, and it pushed into her hand, gently, the way a cat pushes, and closed its eyes.

"Okay," she said. Her voice cracked. "Okay. Number one, then. That's number one sorted."

The wolf opened one eye.

"Never mind. Go and—you'd better go and change back, I'm about to have a lot of questions and I don't speak wolf."

It got up. It went back around the driftwood. The ripping-sail sound again, in reverse somehow, and then a minute later Seth came out from behind the pile tugging his T-shirt down over his stomach, barefoot, his cutoffs on, a leather cord tied around his ankle that she'd never asked about, his face open and terrified.

"So," he said. "Um."

"How long?"

"Since March. Right before my dad—" He stopped. "Since March."

"Your sister?"

"Yeah. And Jake. And Sam, and a bunch of other guys from the rez, Sam's got his own pack, it's a whole—there's a legend. We're the protectors. Of the tribe. From—" He stopped. He looked at her very carefully. "From vampires. The cold ones. That's what we're for. And I know that sounds—"

"The Cullens," Layla said.

His mouth fell open.

"Lilies in a fridge," she said, mostly to herself. "Cold hands. Gold eyes. Never sleeps. Oh, Mum. Oh, *Mum*." She looked up. "They're vampires."

"They're good ones! They don't—they eat animals. They don't hurt people. Dr. Cullen's never even—he's a *doctor*, he's like the best guy I know, and Bella was my friend before, she's still my friend, and Edward's—" He stopped again, breathing hard. "How did you *know* that?"

"I had a list."

"You had a *list*?"

"I'll show you." She swallowed. "There's something else, though. Isn't there? You said—you had a look. When you said it was a big something. It wasn't only the wolf."

Seth went red to the ears. In the dusk it was hard to see, but she could feel the heat of it from a foot away.

"There's this thing," he said. "It's—we call it imprinting. It happens to some of us. You see someone, and it's like—" He made a gesture with both hands, like gathering something up and setting it down. "You see someone and that's it. Everything sort of ties itself to them. I mean—" He was tripping now, the words coming out in a heap. "You don't have to do anything about it. I'd just be whatever you needed. Like if you just wanted a friend, I'd be the best friend you ever had, I swear, and if you wanted me to go away I'd—" his voice went thin, "—I'd probably try, I'd probably be really bad at it, but I'd try. And it happened. At the Thriftway. With the dime."

Layla stared at him.

"The dime," she said.

"The Roosevelt one."

"You've been in love with me since the *dime*?"

"I mean," Seth said miserably, "it's more like gravity than—yeah. Yes. Since the dime."

The wind came off the water and pulled at her hair. Out past the sea stacks, a gull was making a racket at nothing. Layla stood on the beach with her wand still in her right hand and thought about thirty-four days of rain and Seth fixing the porch step in the dark, and Seth carrying her mum's shopping without being asked, and Seth on Second Beach with his trousers rolled up showing her a sea star the color of a bruise, crouched over the tide pool with his hands hovering over it like it was the most precious thing in the world.

Number nine. Crossed out twice.

"Right," she said. "Fair's fair."

"What?"

"My turn." She took a breath that went all the way down. Her father's voice in her head: *the last time we trusted them*. She lifted her wand. "Don't run."

"Why would I—"

"*Lumos*."

The tip of her wand lit—a clean white light, small and steady, pushing the dusk back a few feet in every direction. It lit up Seth's face from below, and the wet sand, and the gray silk of the driftwood, and his eyes, which went very wide.

She didn't stop. If she stopped she'd lose her nerve. She flicked the light out—"*Nox*"—and swept the wand up and around, the way Flitwick had taught them in fifth year, the movement like drawing a bow, and said, "*Avis*," and the end of her wand burst like a seed pod and birds came out of it. A dozen of them, small and yellow and real-looking, a twittering spray of them that shot up over Seth's head and wheeled and came back around him in a ring, the beat of their wings loud in the cold air. One of them landed on his shoulder. He didn't move. It looked at him, cocked its head, and flew off to join the others, and they went up and up over the beach, gold against the gray, and scattered toward the forest.

Seth watched them go. When he looked back at her, his face was doing something complicated.

"You're a *witch*," he said.

"Shh!"

"You're a *witch*?" he said, at almost exactly the same volume. "Like a *real*—like *Charmed*? Like Sabrina?"

"Please," Layla said, "*please* don't say *Charmed*."

"You have a *wand*. It's the—oh my God, it's the *chopstick*. It's the thing in your hair! Leah said it wasn't a chopstick!"

"Your sister has been looking at my *hair*?"

"She said you smelled like a fuse box!"

"I do *not*—" She stopped. "Do I?"

"Kind of." He was grinning now, enormously, his whole face cracked open with it, the terror gone. "Kind of like right before lightning. I didn't know what it was. I thought it was, like, your shampoo." He took a step toward her. "How long? Since you were born?"

"Since I was a baby, yes. I got my letter when I was eleven. There's a school. In Scotland. Hogwarts." She was talking too fast now too. "And a Ministry, and a whole—there's a whole world, Seth, there's a street in London you can only get into through the back of a pub, and banks run by goblins, and I'm not supposed to tell you *any* of this, I'm not supposed to have told you there's a *word* for it, there's a *law*, it's called the Statute of Secrecy, it's been the law for three hundred years and my dad *enforces* it, my dad is literally the man who—"

He kissed her.

He did it carefully. He did it like a question—he leaned down, and stopped, an inch away, and waited, and she could feel the heat off his face like a stove—and Layla Ashby, seventeen and of age and three hundred years of law on her shoulders, put her free hand on the front of his T-shirt and pulled.

It was brief. It was the first time she'd kissed anyone who wasn't Callum Fisk behind the greenhouses in fourth year, which didn't count. His mouth was hot. He smelled like woodsmoke. When he pulled back, his eyes were shut.

"Was that okay?" he said. "Was that—I didn't ask, I should've—"

"Seth."

"Yeah."

"You can ask me out properly. On a date. Somewhere that's not a beach. If you like." She was holding a fistful of his shirt still. She made herself let go. "As a—I mean, if you're my boyfriend. If that's how it works. With the gravity."

"Yes," he said immediately. "Yes. That's how it works. That's exactly how it works."

"Good."

"Good," he agreed, and he looked so happy she had to look away from it, out at the sea, because it was too much to look at directly.

The moon was coming up. It came over the hills behind the forest huge and orange and a sliver off full, and from somewhere far back in the trees, inland, something howled—a long, rising note that went on and on and broke at the top into something that sounded, incredibly, like laughter.

Seth's face went from happy to stricken in about a second and a half.

"Oh," he said. "Uh. Oh, no."

"What?"

"So, when we phase—when I'm a wolf—the pack can hear what I'm thinking. All of us. At the same time. It's like—everybody's in everybody's head."

Layla looked at him.

"And Jake and Leah were both out," he said. "When I phased. On patrol."

"So they—"

"Saw everything. Yeah." He winced. "Well. Saw up until I phased back. So the birds, no. Your wand, no. But they saw you pull it out. And they saw you *point* it at me, and Leah's going to have a lot to say about the hair thing, and Jake—" the howl came again, closer, unmistakably delighted, "—that's Jake. He's, uh. He's coming."

"Your alpha's coming? Here? *Now*?"

"He's my friend. He's really nice. You're going to like him." Seth hesitated. "And then, um. Probably Edward's going to know. Because Jake's going to go back there, and Edward reads minds."

"Edward reads *minds*?"

"Yeah. Sorry. He's—some of them have, like, extra stuff. Alice sees the future. Jasper does feelings."

"Of course he does," Layla said faintly. "Of *course* he does." She looked down at her wand. She looked at the beach, and the moon, and the black wall of the forest where an alpha wolf was coming to meet her. She'd thought about this for five weeks. She had not, she realized, ever got as far as *afterward*.

Then she dug in her pocket and came out with one of the pieces of sea glass on its cord.

"Here," she said, and held it out. "I made this. For you. It's yours anyway, it's the one you gave me back, but I—I've done something to it."

He took it. He held it up against the moon. It looked like nothing; that was the point. "What does it do?"

"If you're ever in trouble," Layla said, "real trouble—and you fight *vampires*, Seth, so I'm going to assume that's a *when*—you break it. Snap it, crush it, bite it, I don't care. Mine'll break too. And I'll come."

"Come how?"

"Magic," she said, "obviously," and she was pleased that her voice only wobbled a little.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he knelt in the wet sand and tied the cord around his ankle, next to the leather one, in a knot she suspected had been tied around a lot of things in the dark. When he stood up he took her hand—the one without the wand in it—and held it, and his hand was so hot it almost hurt.

Up the beach, at the edge of the trees, something enormous and russet-brown stepped out of the forest and sat down on its haunches in the moonlight, and looked at her, and its tongue lolled out of its mouth in a way that was very hard to read as anything but a grin.

### III. Flame-Freezing

**Monday, November 6, 2006 — The Cullen residence, outside Forks — 6:38 p.m. PST**

*Potter, H. — Department of Mysteries, Level Nine, London. 2:38 a.m. GMT. Asleep at his desk on page 211 of the Volterra file. The page is a list of names.*

---

Carlisle heard the boy coming from four miles out.

It was difficult not to. Jacob Black at a dead run through second-growth forest sounded like a small landslide with opinions. Carlisle sat in his study on the third floor with a monograph on fetal hemoglobin open on the desk in front of him—he had read it three times since September and it had told him nothing about his granddaughter that his granddaughter had not already told him by putting her hand on his face—and listened to the sound get closer: the crash of brush, the long rhythm of a canter that covered twenty feet a stride, the scrabble on the river rocks, the splash. Then a pause at the tree line, and the peculiar rending sound that Carlisle had never quite grown used to, of an enormous body folding itself down into a smaller one.

Downstairs, Edward stopped playing in the middle of a phrase.

"Carlisle," he said, in an ordinary speaking voice, which meant he had already heard everything that mattered from the inside of Jacob's head and was trying to decide how to say it.

Carlisle closed the monograph and went down.

The living room was lit low. Esme was on the sofa with a sketchbook. Rosalie was on the floor in front of the fire with Renesmee, building a tower out of the alphabet blocks Esme had bought in Port Angeles, which Renesmee had learned in an afternoon and now used exclusively as building material, with the air of someone who found them a little beneath her. Emmett was watching a hockey game with the sound off. Alice sat on the arm of the chair where Jasper was reading, her legs crossed, her eyes unfocused; she was looking for something. Bella stood at the glass wall with her palm flat against it, looking out at the river.

Edward had turned on the piano bench. He looked—Carlisle searched for it—*startled*. Which was not a word that described Edward very often, these days or any other.

The front door opened. Jacob came in pulling a T-shirt down over his head, barefoot, wet to the knees, grinning so widely it looked painful.

"Okay," he said. "So. Seth's girlfriend is a witch."

There was a short silence.

"Of course she is," Rosalie said, without looking up from the blocks.

"Like a *witch* witch?" said Emmett.

"Like a wand." Jacob held his hands about a foot apart. "Like a stick, this long. She pulls it out of her *hair*, Leah's been going crazy for a month about the hair stick, and she points it at Seth—Seth phases right in front of her, the idiot, to show her the wolf thing—and she pulls this stick out and points it at him like a gun. Then he phases back and I don't see anything after that, so I go down there, right? And she's standing on the beach holding his hand, and she looks at me—I'm still wolf—and she goes white, and then she goes"—his voice went high and English—"'*Oh, hello, you must be Jake*,' and she lights up the end of it. Like a flashlight. And then a bunch of birds come out."

"Birds," said Jasper.

"Little yellow ones. Out of the stick. They flew off into the woods." Jacob dropped onto the sofa next to Esme, who moved her sketchbook out of the way of his wet knees without comment. "It was awesome. They're coming. Seth's bringing her in her mom's car. She wants to meet you guys. She said, and I quote, 'before I lose my nerve.'"

Renesmee had stopped building. She was looking at Jacob with her head tilted and her bronze ringlets falling over one shoulder. At eight weeks old she looked, by any textbook Carlisle owned, like a child of eighteen months, and she spoke like a child of five, and she thought like something none of them had a word for. She put a block down very precisely on top of another one and said, "Birds?"

"Yeah, Ness. Real ones."

"I want to see."

"Ask her nice. Maybe she'll do it again."

Bella had turned around from the window. "A human," she said. "Coming here."

"She knows," said Jacob. "About everything. Seth told her. She figured out half of it already. She had a *list*."

"That's beside the point, Jacob."

"She smells like lightning. It's barely even appetizing. You'll be fine."

Edward stood up from the bench and crossed to Bella in the way he did now, without seeming to have taken the steps in between, and took her hand. She looked at him, and Carlisle watched something pass between them, and then Bella closed her eyes and nodded and took one long, deliberate last breath, the way she'd been practicing, and held it.

Carlisle did not move for a moment.

*Witch.*

It was a word he had not heard spoken with any seriousness in three hundred years. He had heard it said—in films, in jokes, in a Halloween aisle in a pharmacy—and it had meant nothing; it had meant a green face and a broom. It had not meant anything since London.

It meant something now. He could feel it in the back of his mind like the smell of pitch.

*His father in the black coat, the Bible open in one hand. The crowd in the street outside a house in Southwark, the torches, the thin high voice of a woman inside screaming that she had done nothing, nothing, and his father's voice over it, very calm, reading. Carlisle at sixteen holding a torch because he had been told to. The smell. The ash on his sleeves for days.*

He had believed—all his long life, he had believed—that every one of those women was innocent. He had built a great deal on that belief. On the shame of it.

"Carlisle?" Esme said quietly.

"I'm all right." He wasn't sure that was true, but he made it true enough. "She'll need something to drink, I imagine. It's cold out. Something hot."

"I bought tea," Esme said, rising. "When Ruth started at the hospital. You told me her daughter loathed the tea here, so I found an English shop in Port Angeles and bought a box of something called Yorkshire, and then I didn't know whether that was presumptuous so I never sent it." She was already in the kitchen. "It's presumptuous now. I don't care."

---

The car came up the drive at six fifty-one. Carlisle heard two heartbeats in it before he heard the engine—one slow and enormous and steady, and one fast, quick and light as a bird's, quicker the closer it came.

Seth came in first. He came in the way Seth always came into this house, as if he lived there and was glad about it, and he was holding the girl's hand.

She was small, and wet from the rain, and she had dark hair pulled up into a knot at the back of her head with a wooden stick through it, and she stopped in the doorway with her free hand clenched at her side and looked at them all—seven vampires and a child of uncertain species and a werewolf on a sofa—and Carlisle watched her decide, visibly, not to run.

"Hello," she said. Her voice shook and then steadied. "I'm Layla. I'm—sorry. I'm a witch."

"Miss Ashby." Carlisle stepped forward, slowly, and didn't offer his hand, because his hand was cold and she would notice, and because Jasper had moved without moving to a place between her and Bella and Carlisle did not want to give anyone reason to move again. "You're very welcome here. Your mother is a fine doctor."

That surprised a laugh out of her—short, nervous. "She thinks you're a very fine doctor. She goes pink."

Emmett made a noise like a seal.

"I've made tea," said Esme, coming in from the kitchen with a mug in both hands, and held it out at arm's length with a smile, and Carlisle watched the girl look at the mug and then at Esme's gold eyes and then, very bravely, step forward and take it.

She sipped it. Her eyebrows went up.

"That's *proper*," she said, with something close to shock. "Where on earth did you find that?"

"There's a shop in Port Angeles."

"I've been drinking dust in a bag for twelve weeks." She held the mug in both hands like it was the only solid thing in the room. "Thank you. Really."

Across the room, Edward took a step backward.

It was a small movement, and nobody human would have seen it. Carlisle saw it. Edward's eyes had gone wide and fixed on the air an inch in front of the girl's face. Carlisle knew the look Edward wore for a loud thought; he had seen it in a hundred hospital corridors. This one was new. It was the face of someone who had opened a door in a house he'd lived in for a century and found a staircase going down.

"There's a castle," Edward said aloud.

The girl's head whipped around.

"On a lake," Edward said slowly. "In the mountains. The staircases move. There are—the pictures on the walls are *talking* to you—"

"Stop that," said Layla.

Edward blinked and came back. "I'm sorry."

"Seth said you read minds. He didn't say you narrated."

"I don't usually." He looked, Carlisle thought, genuinely ashamed, and also as if it was taking every ounce of discipline he had not to keep listening. "I'm sorry. I'll keep hearing it, I'm afraid. I can stop saying it. It's—your mind is very *full*."

"It's a lot of revision," said Layla faintly. "I've got N.E.W.T.s in June."

"Newts?" said Emmett.

"Exams." She took another swallow of tea. Then she set the mug down very carefully on the side table and squared her shoulders and pulled the stick out of her hair, and her hair came down around her face, and she held it up so the light from the fire ran along it. It was wood, dark and polished, a little more than ten inches long, carved at the handle. She held it the way a surgeon holds a scalpel. "I suppose you'd all like to see something."

"Yes," said Emmett, Alice, and Renesmee, at once.

---

She started small. Carlisle suspected she always started small.

"*Lumos*," she said, and the tip of the wand lit—a white light, cool and clear, brighter than a candle, casting her shadow and Seth's long across the floor. Renesmee made a sound of delight. Jasper, very slightly, relaxed.

"*Nox*." The light went out. She pointed at a cushion on the floor by the fire, made a little swish-and-flick in the air—"*Wingardium Leviosa*"—and the cushion rose off the floor and floated up to the height of her shoulder and turned slowly in the air.

"Do me," Emmett said.

"Sorry?"

"Do *me*." He was on his feet. "Float me. Come on. Do it."

Layla eyed him. "You must weigh—"

"Rude."

"I was going to say *a great deal*."

"Still rude."

"Emmett," Esme said.

"It's fine," said Layla, and Carlisle heard her heart steady a little, as if this was a thing she understood—a big, loud, cheerful idiot asking her to do something; she'd met them before. "Stand there. Don't move. If I drop you, it's your fault."

She pointed the wand at him and swished and flicked, and said it again with more weight—"*Wingardium Leviosa*"—and Emmett rose two inches off the floor.

He whooped. He threw his arms out like a man on a roller coaster. He rose another inch, and another, his feet dangling, and the girl's wand arm began to shake, a fine tremor that ran up into her shoulder; a muscle in her jaw jumped. She held him there for perhaps four seconds, eight inches off the hardwood, and then said through her teeth, "Right, that's all you get," and lowered him, a little faster than she'd raised him. He landed with a thud that rattled the windows.

"You," she said, breathing hard, "weigh about as much as a *car*."

"Two cars," said Rosalie, and smiled, a real one. It was the first time Carlisle had seen her smile at a stranger in years.

Renesmee was on her feet. She had crossed the room before Bella could reach her, quick and sure on her short legs, and she stopped in front of the girl and looked up at her and held up both arms.

Bella made a sound—a small one, a mother's sound, and with her breath held it came out as no more than a click in her throat. Jacob was already standing. "It's okay," he said. "Bells. It's okay. She just wants to show her something."

The girl looked down at Renesmee and then at Bella and then, questioning, at Seth. Seth nodded. Layla crouched, slowly, until she was eye to eye with the child.

Renesmee put her hand on the girl's cheek.

Carlisle had felt it many times, and he knew what it was like the first time—had watched it go through Seth, and Esme, and Jacob, and watched each of their faces go slack with wonder. It was like being shown a photograph from the inside. He watched Layla's eyes go wide and her breath catch, and he didn't need Edward to tell him what she was seeing, because he could guess: herself, a moment ago, as Renesmee had seen her, the white light at the end of the wand. And then the same image again, brighter, with a feeling attached to it that even Carlisle, without the gift, would have been able to name. *Again.*

"Oh," Layla breathed. "Oh, you clever thing. You put a picture in my head."

"She does that," said Jacob, with enormous pride.

"Do you want birds?" Layla asked the child. "Would you like birds?"

Renesmee nodded.

Layla stood. She swept the wand up and around in a wide curving movement, like drawing a bow, and said "*Avis*," and the end of the wand burst like a seed head and birds came out of it—a dozen, two dozen, small and bright yellow, a twittering, wheeling cloud of them that shot up toward the high ceiling of the living room and circled the chandelier, and Renesmee laughed. She laughed out loud and spun in a circle beneath them with her arms up, and Rosalie had her hand over her mouth, and Alice was laughing too, and Emmett said "*Dude*" in a voice of hushed reverence, and Jasper's eyes tracked the birds the way they tracked everything—as a soldier tracks movement—but he was smiling.

Carlisle looked up at the birds. They were real. He could hear their hearts—twenty-four tiny hammering hearts, where a moment ago there had been none—and smell their feathers, the dry dust smell of a pet shop. They had come out of a stick.

He had been a scientist for three hundred years. He had held human hearts in his hands. He had read every page ever written on the nature of his own condition and found most of it wanting. And he understood that everything he knew about the way the world worked had just acquired a very large footnote.

"*Evanesco*," Layla said, when Renesmee had laughed herself breathless, and the birds vanished one by one, mid-flight, little silent winks of nothing, until the ceiling was empty.

---

They sat. It took a while—there was the matter of where to put a human in a room full of vampires, and the answer, after some delicate choreography that the girl pretended not to notice, was the armchair nearest the fire, with Seth on the floor at her feet and Jacob and the child on the sofa, and Bella at the far window with Edward, still not breathing, watching everything with her red eyes. Esme refilled the tea. Layla answered questions.

She was good at it. Carlisle noted that—she answered precisely, and when she didn't know, she said so, and when she wasn't allowed to say, she said that too, and looked unhappy about it. She told them about the school in Scotland, and the Ministry in London, under the streets, and the Statute.

"Sixteen eighty-nine," she said. "That's when it was signed. It came into force a few years after. Everyone everywhere—all the magical governments—agreed to hide. Completely. From then on."

"Sixteen eighty-nine," Carlisle repeated.

She glanced at him. "Is that—does that mean something?"

"I was forty-nine." He said it simply. He had decided, watching the birds, that there was no point in being anything less than honest with her; she'd been honest with them at real cost. "Or I would have been. I was changed at twenty-three, in 1663. I remember the stories. In London. When I was young they were everywhere. Then—" He thought about it. "Somewhere around the turn of the century, they stopped. I remember noticing. I thought it was that men had become more reasonable."

"We got better at hiding," said Layla.

"My father hunted witches."

He hadn't known he was going to say it until it was said.

The room went quiet. Esme's hand found his.

Layla had gone very still in the armchair. Her heart had picked up again. "Hunted," she said.

"He was a clergyman. In London, in the fifties—the sixteen-fifties. He believed it was his duty. He led the searches. He found—what he believed were witches. Vampires. Werewolves." Carlisle glanced, despite himself, at Jacob, who was looking at him with an odd expression. "He burned them. A great many. I was made to help when I was old enough to hold a torch."

He looked at his hands.

"I have always believed," he said, "that every one of them was innocent. That he burned ordinary women for being old, or poor, or unpleasant to their neighbors. I've built a good part of my life on that."

There was a long silence. The fire popped.

"Did any of them," Layla said slowly, "ever—not burn? Did you ever see one stand in a fire and scream, and then, later—somehow—not be there?"

Carlisle opened his mouth. Closed it.

*A woman in Southwark, at the end of the summer of fifty-four. Tied to the stake in the market, the bundles lit. Screaming—they all screamed—and he'd turned his face away, and when the fire had burned down, his father's men had found nothing in the ashes but the rope. His father had called it the Devil's work, and preached on it for a month.*

"Once," he said.

"There's a charm," Layla said quietly. "The Flame-Freezing Charm. It makes fire feel like—like a breeze. A tickle. Any half-trained witch could do it, even then. They'd let themselves be tied up and scream for show, and then they'd Apparate the minute everyone looked away." She hesitated. "There's a witch in our first-year history book. Wendelin the Weird. She let herself get caught forty-seven times. On purpose. Because she liked the feeling."

Emmett laughed. It was involuntary, and he cut it off fast, and Rosalie didn't hit him, which Carlisle took as a sign of how badly she'd wanted to laugh too.

"So the ones that burned," Carlisle said.

Layla looked at him. Her eyes were very steady, for a girl her age, in a room like this one.

"Were mostly just women," she said. "I'm sorry."

Carlisle sat with that. Esme's fingers tightened on his.

"It's a strange thing," he said at last, "to be told you were right."

---

It was Alice, of course, who broke the silence; Alice could never bear one for long.

"Every afternoon," she said, "at about four, your future goes *blank*." She'd hopped off the arm of Jasper's chair and was perched on the edge of the coffee table now, looking at Layla with frank fascination. "I've been watching you since October—Seth was so *happy*, I wanted to see how it went—and every day, your future runs along perfectly normally, school, studying, your mother, and then at four o'clock *bang*, nothing. Just static. Until about nine. I thought something was wrong with me."

"That'd be him, I think," Layla said, nodding down at Seth.

"It's the wolves," Alice said darkly. "It's *always* the wolves."

"You're welcome," said Jacob.

"Can I ask something?" Layla said. She looked around the room. "Seth's told me—a bit. That you're—that there are rules. That someone's in charge of you. Is there? Like a Ministry?"

Carlisle and Edward looked at each other across the room. It was a short look and a long conversation.

"There are three," Carlisle said carefully. "Very old. In Italy. Their family is called the Volturi. They enforce one law, really—the same as yours. That humans must never know what we are."

"So they're a Ministry."

"With fewer forms," said Edward.

Layla didn't smile. She was looking at the floor, thinking; Carlisle could see her doing it. "Do they know about us?" she said. "Wizards?"

And Carlisle opened his mouth to say *no*, and found that he couldn't.

Because Aro had touched his hand. In Volterra, two centuries ago, a hundred times, and read every thought Carlisle had ever had in the span of a heartbeat. And in the spring, in that room under the city, when Edward had gone there to die, Aro had taken Edward's hand, and Alice's, and read them too. None of them had ever met a witch, so there had been nothing in any of them to find. But Aro was three thousand years old, and in three thousand years he had touched more hands than Carlisle could imagine, and he forgot nothing, and he told no one anything he did not wish to.

"I don't know," he said.

"If Aro knows," Edward said, very quietly, "he has never let a single one of his guard think about it near me. And I've been near a good many of them."

"Do *we* know about *you*?" said Rosalie. She'd picked Renesmee up onto her lap. Her voice was sharp. "Your people. Your Ministry. Do they know what we are?"

"I don't think so." Layla looked uncomfortable. "I mean—we have vampires. They're in books. They're—they're sort of *different*. Ours are sickly, mostly. Pale. They don't like garlic. There's one called Sanguini who gets invited to parties as a sort of curiosity; a teacher of ours used to have him round. I've never heard of one that's—" She gestured at the room, at the marble faces, the gold eyes. "Like this. Nobody I've ever heard of glitters."

"Show her!" said Emmett.

"It's night, Emmett."

"Tomorrow, then. Hey, Layla, tomorrow, if the sun comes out—"

"It's Forks," said Jasper. "The sun won't come out."

"Okay, but who's the toughest?" Emmett leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Like, on your side. Who's your guy? Who's your—" he gestured at himself, "—your Emmett?"

Layla thought about it. Carlisle watched her think, and watched something come into her face that he couldn't name at first—it took him a moment to place it. There was fear in it, and pride, and something older than both: the face of someone talking about a storm she'd lived through.

"Harry Potter," she said.

Across the room, Edward's head tilted.

"There was a dark wizard. The worst in a century, people say. Voldemort. We didn't use to say his name." She said it now, a little defiantly, as if testing whether the ceiling would fall. "He tried to kill Harry Potter when Harry was a baby, and the curse bounced off and destroyed him, and nobody knows why. Then he came back, years later. There was a war. People died—lots of people. And at the end of it, Harry killed him. In a duel. In front of everyone." She paused. "He was seventeen."

"Seventeen," said Jasper.

Carlisle glanced at him. Jasper had been a major in the Confederate Army at nineteen. He had trained boys younger than that for war, and then he'd trained newborns to kill each other in the dark for decades after. Carlisle had never seen the look that crossed his face then, and it was gone in an instant.

"He's on a Chocolate Frog card," Layla went on, a little more lightly, as if she'd heard herself and wanted out of it. "They're sweets. With cards inside. Famous wizards. I collected them when I was little, after the war—everyone did—"

"He keeps walking out of the picture," Edward said.

Layla looked at him sharply.

"Sorry," Edward said. "You're picturing it. A boy with black hair and green eyes. He keeps walking out of the frame and coming back."

"They do that," said Layla, after a moment. "The pictures move. He's twenty-six now. He works for the Ministry. In the Department of Mysteries."

"What's that?"

"Nobody knows." She said it flatly. "That's what it's for. My dad says—" She stopped herself. "My dad says don't make eye contact in the lift."

Seth laughed. Layla didn't. Carlisle noticed that, and filed it away, without at the time knowing why.

---

They left at half past eight, because Layla's mother would be home at nine and there was the matter of explaining why the car smelled of wet wolf. At the door, the girl turned around and looked at them all again, the way she had when she'd come in, and Carlisle saw that her hands were shaking, now that it was over.

"Please," she said. "I'm not supposed to have told you any of this. Any of it. If anyone at the Ministry ever found out—"

"Miss Ashby," Carlisle said gently. "We have kept a secret every day for a very long time. We can keep yours."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and went out into the rain with Seth's hand in hers, and Carlisle stood at the glass wall and watched the car's taillights go down the long drive through the trees until they were gone.

Bella let out her breath at last, behind him, in a long shuddering sigh, and drew in a fresh one, and coughed, and said "*Ow*," and Edward laughed into her hair.

"You did beautifully," he told her.

"She smelled like a thunderstorm in a candy shop," Bella said. "Jacob, you *liar*."

Carlisle didn't turn around. He was looking at his own reflection in the dark glass, and the river behind it, and thinking about the ash on his sleeves. About a woman in Southwark who had sung under her breath when they tied her up, and had not been there when the fire died.

Edward came and stood beside him.

"You're thinking about Volterra," Edward said.

"I'm thinking about Aro." Carlisle was quiet a moment. "About how much he enjoys things he's never seen before."

Edward said nothing. In the glass, Carlisle watched his son's reflection look out into the dark toward the east, toward the sea and the long way round the world to Italy, and he knew they were both picturing the same thing: an old, delighted face, a pale hand held out, palm up, waiting to be filled.

### IV. Belt and Braces

**Monday, November 20, 2006 — Unnamed ridge above the Bogachiel River, Olympic Peninsula — 5:41 a.m. PST**

*Potter, H. — Present.*

---

The rain had been going for eight days. Harry had stopped noticing it on the third.

He stood on the ridge in the dark with water running off the brim of nothing—he'd given up on hats—and down the back of his collar, and laid his palm flat against the trunk of a western red cedar that had probably been a sapling when Cromwell was still a going concern. The bark was soft and stringy and soaked through. Below him the ridge fell away into black, into second-growth hemlock and Douglas fir and a creek he could hear and not see, and beyond that the Bogachiel, running high and loud with eight days of rain.

The line came up the slope behind him. He couldn't see it with his eyes. He'd stopped needing to see things with his eyes in about 2002. He could feel it, though, the way you can feel a tripwire through the sole of a boot if you've stepped on enough of them: a thread of intent pulled taut through the wet dark, three and a half miles of it running down this ridge and across the Bogachiel and up the next ridge south, where it met the line he'd laid on Thursday, which met the one he'd laid on Tuesday, which ran all the way out to the Hoh. North of here it tied into the Calawah lines, and the Sol Duc lines, and the long ugly stretch along the 101 that had taken two days and a near miss with a Washington State Patrol cruiser. From Lake Crescent to the Hoh, coast to the park boundary. Every valley. Every ridge. Every way in.

This was the last anchor.

He took the knife out of his coat—a Fairbairn-Sykes with a blackened blade that had been his for five years and a goblin's for a hundred before that—and cut the rune into the cedar at shoulder height, three strokes, quick and deep. Then he pricked the ball of his thumb with the point, and pressed it into the cut, and held it there.

The line took. He felt it go: the slack come out of it, all the way down the ridge and across the river and away into the dark in every direction, three hundred and some miles of thread pulling tight at once. Somewhere a few miles south a barred owl went quiet in the middle of a call.

Harry took his thumb back and looked at it. The cut had already closed.

"Right," he said to the cedar. "That's you, then."

He dug in his coat again and came out with a glass vial the size of his little finger, stoppered with wax. It held nothing anyone else would have seen—a breath of air off a game trail twelve miles northeast, where something with a cold body had taken down a bull elk on Friday and then gone off up the slope at sixty miles an hour without leaving a single footprint deeper than a quarter of an inch. He'd bottled the air where it had stood. There were seven others like it in a case in the tent, one for each of them; the last he'd only added on Saturday, after he'd got a good long look through the Barrett's scope at the girl with the red eyes.

He broke the wax and held the open vial an inch from the line.

The line slackened. Just there, just for that—the way a dog's hackles go down when it smells someone it knows. Then it drew taut again when he stoppered it.

Good. Eight Cullens, eight exceptions. Anything else that came onto this peninsula with a cold body and no heartbeat and a mass over a hundred pounds and moving faster than any human being could move was going to have a very bad day.

The first version hadn't had the bit about the body being made of meat. The first version had hoisted a Weyerhaeuser log truck thirty feet up into a hemlock on the 101 outside Sappho at two in the morning on Wednesday. That had been a long night. Harry had got the truck down, and the hemlock back up, and the driver—a very nice man named Dale, who'd been on his way to the mill at Port Angeles and was going to be on his way there for the rest of his life with no idea he'd ever stopped—a cup of coffee from his own thermos and a brand-new memory of a deer in the road.

He'd added the bit about meat.

He tucked the vial away and stood a minute longer on the ridge in the dark with his hand on the cedar, listening to the river.

Then he went home.

---

The tent was a two-man ex-army ridge tent in faded olive canvas, the kind of thing a man might buy at a surplus shop in Bremerton if he wanted to camp somewhere without being bothered. It was pitched at the end of a logging spur the Forest Service had decommissioned in 1991 and let the alders take, nine miles up the Bogachiel drainage. Anyone driving the spur—and no one had, in fifteen years, except a family of black-tailed deer and Harry—would have remembered something urgent they'd left at home a quarter mile before they got to it. Anyone who got past that would have seen a clearing with nothing in it. Anyone who got past *that* would have been a problem, and there was a plan for problems, and the plan was in the armory.

Harry came out of nowhere at the edge of the clearing without a sound—that had taken him two years and a broken collarbone, and he still enjoyed it every single time—and crossed to the tent and ducked inside.

It was dry. It smelled of coffee, and gun oil, and the lemon stuff he used on the kitchen counters. The entrance opened onto a short hallway with a coat hook and a boot rack and a floor of wide pine boards, and from there the hall ran back into the hill, past a kitchen, a sitting room, a bedroom, a bathroom with an honest-to-God claw-footed bath, a library, a workshop, and two doors at the far end with heavy locks on them that he kept shut.

He hung his coat on the hook. It dripped. He shook out his hair with one hand and the water came off it in a sheet and went into the boot tray, because he'd told it to.

In the sitting room there was a record player on a sideboard, a proper Technics turntable he'd liberated from a car-boot sale in Croydon in 2003 and rewired to run on nothing at all. He put on *Physical Graffiti*, side one, and turned it up until "Custard Pie" was coming off the walls, and went to make coffee.

The kitchen was small and clean and he kept it that way. There was a gas range that ran on no gas, a percolator, a Muggle fridge that hummed. On the fridge, under a magnet shaped like a Snitch, there was a drawing in purple and orange crayon of a stag and a wolf standing next to each other on a green hill, with a yellow sun in the corner with a face. Underneath, in careful eight-year-old capitals: UNCLE HARRY AND MY DAD.

Harry looked at it while the coffee came up. He looked at the wolf. Teddy had given it a scar across the nose, and tired eyes, and one ear that bent over at the tip; he must have got that from a photograph. Andromeda would have helped.

There were a lot of wolves on this peninsula. Big ones. He'd seen three of them through the scope—a russet one the size of a Clydesdale, a sandy one, a lean grey one that ran like something being chased even when it wasn't—and he'd watched the steam come off them in the rain, and he'd thought about Remus more in the last eight days than in the last two years.

He poured the coffee. He drank it black, standing up. He didn't think about Remus anymore.

---

The library was the room he'd spent the most time on, and it showed. The tent's wards held the shelves at a constant fifty-five degrees and forty percent humidity, and the shelves went up into a dimness that was mostly bluff, and they were full. Arithmancy. Runes—Elder Futhark, Ogham, Ethiopic, the old Babylonian stuff that nobody at the Department believed he could read until he'd made it do something. Wandlore. Alchemy. Ward theory, a whole long wall of it. The complete proceedings of the International Confederation's Committee on Experimental Charms from 1752 onward, which were deadly boring and occasionally contained a genuine piece of insanity a hundred years ahead of its time. Healing. Potions—a lot of potions; that had been a long and humbling road, given where he'd started, and he still had Snape's old Advanced Potion-Making on the shelf there, the copy with the handwriting in the margins, because some lessons you kept.

And at the end of the last shelf, at eye level, eighty-seven notebooks.

They didn't match. The first was a Muggle exercise book, the cheap kind with a cardboard cover, gone soft and brown at the corners. Hermione had given it to him at the Burrow in June of 1998, five weeks after the battle, when he couldn't sleep and wouldn't talk and had spent most of three days sitting in the orchard picking a Lumos apart in his head because it was the only thing that made the noise stop. *If you're going to think about it,* she'd said, *write it down,* and pushed the book into his hands. On the front, in the handwriting of someone who'd just turned eighteen and didn't know anything, he'd written: SPELLS I DON'T UNDERSTAND YET.

It had been a long list. It had taken him a while to realize it was going to be all of them.

Saul Croaker had come to the Burrow in July. Harry had been in the orchard. Croaker had walked down through the long grass in his black robes and his gold half-moon spectacles—he was the only wizard Harry had ever met who looked worse in half-moon spectacles than Dumbledore—and sat on an upturned bucket next to him and watched him make a Lumos go blue, then green, then out, then come back on in the shape of a cube.

"Mr. Potter," Croaker had said, after a while. "Do you know what you are?"

"Tired," Harry had said.

"Interesting," Croaker had said. "You are interesting. You have done several things that ought to be impossible, and you have no idea how you did any of them, and it is eating you alive. I can see it from here." He'd stood up off the bucket and brushed off his robes. "We have the same problem, in my Department. We also have a great many books. Come and see me when you'd like to stop being tired and start being useful."

Harry had gone in the first week of August. He'd been there ever since.

He ran his finger along the spines. Number nine was the eleven months it had taken him to make a Lumos without a wand—just a Lumos, just a light, on a Tuesday in the spring of 1999, and he'd sat on the drawing-room floor at Grimmauld Place and looked at it in his palm and laughed until he cried and then thrown up. Twenty-three was the silent Apparition. Thirty-one had the first sketch of the lattice that had become the snareline, and was mostly wrong. Forty-four had the shield—the one Kingsley's Aurors trained on now and called the Potter Variant, which he'd asked them not to, and they'd ignored. Sixty-one was a bad year. Sixty-two was the year after, which was better, and had the thing in it that had made Kingsley swear in Swahili. Seventy through seventy-six were the Death Chamber, and he didn't open those out here.

Eighty-seven was half full. It had the snareline in it, finished, and his notes on the Volterra file, and a page of questions with nothing written underneath them yet.

*How do you kill one?* Fire, according to the receptionist. *Does Stupefy work? Does anything? Does the Imperius take? What about the Cruciatus? What are their minds like? What are they* made *of?*

*What does the Killing Curse do to something that isn't alive?*

That one he'd underlined.

---

The armory was the first of the two locked doors at the end of the hall.

He let himself in. The lights came up on their own. It was a long, narrow room, and the walls were racked.

The Department had a whole floor of people who believed Muggle weapons were beneath them. Harry had spent three months at Hereford in the autumn of 2001 under a cover story so dull no one at the base had ever asked a second question about it, being taught to shoot by men who did not believe in magic and who were, he'd decided by the end of the first week, the most terrifying people he'd ever met, and that included the Lestranges. He'd come back with a deep respect for the bullet. A spell went where you sent it at the speed of a spell. A bullet went faster, and nobody ever saw it coming, and if you'd taken the time to put something nasty on it first, it carried the nasty a great deal further than a wand could.

He walked down the racks the way some men walk past a row of cars.

The Remington 870, with its barrel cut down and its receiver etched in tight bands of runes, loaded with shells he'd packed himself: dragon's-breath magnesium in a paper hull, with an Incendio folded into every pellet like a letter into an envelope. The Barrett M82, long as a man, with the scope he'd spent most of 2004 on, the one that could see through a Disillusionment Charm and a Mirror Charm and most of an Invisibility Cloak—not his, though; his he'd tested it on, and the Cloak had won, which had pleased him more than it should have. An M4 with a box of rounds jacketed in goblin silver. A Browning Hi-Power, his first, bought from a pawnshop in Aldershot with Muggle money in 2001 and never once failed to fire. A Winchester 1892 lever-action that had no practical purpose whatsoever, which he kept oiled and loaded because he liked it. Knives. A crate of flash-bangs he'd modified to do something to eyes and ears and the inside of minds that he hadn't written up yet because he wasn't sure he was allowed. A steel canister the size of a thermos with a red band around it and a label that said, in his handwriting, GUBRAITHIAN — DO NOT OPEN INDOORS. DO NOT OPEN.

He took the Browning down and checked it, and slid it into the holster under his left arm, and took a spare magazine. Then he stood a moment longer, looking at the 870.

Belt and braces.

He left it on the rack. He was only going to town.

---

The second door at the end of the hall opened onto a room that was colder than the rest of the tent, and brighter.

The floor was poured concrete with a drain in it. The walls were lined with steel shelving, and the shelving was full: jars and cases and boxes, labeled in his writing, each with a date. A scrap of something black and fine as a silk scarf, folded in a lead-lined box that he kept locked, because it was a Lethifold and it wasn't as dead as it looked. A Nundu's whisker in a sealed tube. Three Inferius hands in formalin, one of which still curled its fingers when the light came on. A Veela's feather. An Erumpent horn on a stand, with a card propped in front of it that said EXPLODES. Along the far wall, a heavy oak wardrobe with a padlock on the hasp, which shook itself gently and rattled when he walked in, because there was a Boggart in it and it had heard him.

"Morning," Harry told it. The wardrobe rattled harder.

In the middle of the room there was a steel table with restraints, and a trolley of instruments, and a Sony microcassette recorder.

And past the table, against the back wall, there was the cell.

It was a cube of glass, eight feet on a side, two inches thick, set into the concrete floor on a plinth of black granite he'd cut and carved himself. The glass carried fifteen layers of the Unbreakable Charm, laid one over another like varnish, each keyed to a slightly different frequency of force so that nothing that broke one would break the next. The runes cut into the granite underneath ran in a ring and fed into the walls. He'd tested it with a sledgehammer, and then with a Reductor Curse, and then with the strongest thing he had that wouldn't take the tent down with it, and then he'd stopped testing it, because he'd started to worry about the tent.

It was empty.

There was a card in a little brass holder on the front of the plinth, the way you'd label an exhibit in a museum. It said SPECIMEN 1, and under that, in brackets, PENDING.

"Soon," Harry said, to nobody, and went to call his boss.

---

The mirror was in the workshop, propped against the back of the bench between a disassembled Sneakoscope and a vise. It was a square of old glass in a plain wooden frame, the twin of one that sat on a desk on Level Nine. He'd made them himself in 2003, starting from Sirius's—the same principle, better range, and they didn't crack when you dropped them in a trunk, which had been a design priority.

"Saul Croaker," he said to it.

The glass clouded and cleared. On the other side was an office he knew very well: wood-paneled, cramped, lined floor to ceiling with clocks. Grandfather clocks and carriage clocks and wall clocks and a great brass astronomical thing that showed the phases of the moon, all of them ticking a fraction out of step with each other, so that the room seemed to whisper constantly. In front of them, behind the desk, Saul Croaker looked up over his half-moon spectacles.

He was very old, Croaker—he'd been old when Harry's parents were at school—and very thin, with a long gray face like a heron's and a mind like the inside of one of his clocks. It was twenty to two in the afternoon in London. He had a cup of tea at his elbow. It had gone cold; it always went cold.

"Potter."

"Saul."

"You look wet."

"It's raining. It's always raining. I think it's been raining here since the Flood. I think this is where they stored the extra." Harry pulled up a stool and sat. "Lines are down. All of them. Last anchor went in about half an hour ago."

"Coverage?"

"Lake Crescent to the Hoh. Coast to the park boundary. Every valley and ridge and road for thirty miles around the town." He scratched his jaw; he needed a shave. "Anything that comes in with a cold body and no pulse that isn't on my list goes up a tree. It holds, it locks the joints, it pings me. I go and get it."

"And the list."

"Eight. Dr. Cullen and family. I bottled them off their own game trails. There's a new one, by the way—the newborn, red eyes. The girl from the receptionist's memory. Swan. She was human in March. She isn't now."

Croaker made a note. "And the child?"

"There's a kid," Harry said. "I've seen her twice, through the scope. One of the wolves carries her around like she's made of glass. She's got a heartbeat—fast, like a bird's. She's grown since last week." He shook his head. "I don't know what she is, and I'm not touching it till I do. She's not on the list because she doesn't need to be. The lines ignore anything with a pulse."

"And the wolves."

"Shapeshifters. Native—Quileute, I'd bet money, given where they den. Three that run with the Cullens, at least seven more on the coast who don't. They're enormous, Saul. They run hot. You can watch the rain steam off them." He shrugged. "They patrol the Cullens' land like it's an embassy. They don't know I'm here."

"Avoid them."

"Was planning to."

Croaker took his spectacles off and polished them on a corner of his robe, which was what he did when he was about to say something he'd already said. "The working group meets on the fourth of December. They are tired of reading about these creatures in the memories of a receptionist."

"She had very good memories."

"She had *a receptionist's* memories. Of the front desk. She knows where they keep the stationery. She knows the names of the guard and the color of their eyes and that the old one with the hair likes to have tourists brought down at teatime." Croaker put his glasses back on. "Thirty-one British nationals in Volterra in twelve years who boarded a tour coach and were never seen again. Nine of them ours. The Lowes. The Fitzwilliam boy. You've read the list."

"Page two-eleven," said Harry. "I've read the list."

"Then you know what they want. They want to know what kills these things, and what doesn't. They want to know whether a Stunner will drop one, and how long a Shield will hold against one's fist, and whether the Imperius will take, and what the Cruciatus does to them, and whether you can Legilimens one without coming out the other side screaming. And they want to know"—he paused—"what the Killing Curse does to something that is already dead."

"Underlined," Harry said. "Page one of the book."

"Your authorizations stand. Full latitude on the specimen." Croaker's voice didn't change at all. It never did. "All three, as needed. You know the protocol."

"I wrote half the protocol."

"So you did." Croaker looked at him for a moment. "You'll need more than one, eventually."

"I know. I'll get what comes." Harry leaned back on the stool. "Something will. They pass through. Nomads. The receptionist's memories had a whole category for them: they wander, they feed, they don't stay. Forks has had a few lately. There was something in Seattle this spring—dozens dead, the papers called it a serial killer, and then in June it stopped all at once, and half the missing were never found. Something in these woods ate a string of hikers last autumn that the Rangers put down to bears. They come through here because the Cullens are here, I reckon. Like moths."

"And the Cullens remain Category Two."

"I read the memo."

"Read it again." Croaker said it mildly. "Valued alive. Do not engage without cause. The doctor lived in Volterra for the better part of two decades in the eighteenth century, if the receptionist's gossip is to be trusted, and he knows that castle and those three better than anyone alive or otherwise on this side of the Atlantic. They're more use to us whole and friendly than they are in your cell."

"Saul. They eat *deer*. They're the most boring vampires in the world. One of them's a GP. I'm not going to put a GP in a cell."

"See that you don't."

"And the one who started all this," Harry said. "The bronze one. Romeo."

"Romeo," Croaker agreed, after a moment, and something happened at the corner of his mouth that on another man might have been a smile.

It had been St. Marcus Day. The nineteenth of March, at noon, in the Piazza dei Priori in Volterra, with the whole town in red for the festival and the square packed with tourists. The thing on Level Nine that the Department called the Clamour—a great brass bell in a room no one else had a key to, which rang whenever a large number of Muggles were about to see something they absolutely should not see—had gone off like a fire alarm at eleven fifty-eight Italian time. It didn't care whether the thing about to be seen was a wizard's or not. It just cared that a crowd was about to see it. Two Unspeakables had Apparated into the square with a bucket of Memory Charms at one minute past noon and found nothing: no magic, no breach, just a lot of tourists in red cloaks and a lingering sense that something had happened under the clock tower. One of them had gone back in April, and sat in a café across from an old palazzo for a week, and noticed a pretty young receptionist with a habit of looking at the people coming in and out the way a woman looks at a box of chocolates.

That had been Harry. The receptionist was called Gianna. She'd had an espresso with him at a café in the Via dei Sarti on a Thursday afternoon and told him, without saying a word, absolutely everything.

Including a memory, from March, from the hall upstairs: a young man with bronze hair and a face like a statue's being walked through to the throne room between two enormous men in grey cloaks, with a little dark-haired human girl clinging to his arm. And another, from earlier the same day, of the same young man standing in the shadow of the clock tower at noon with his shirt off, about to take one step forward into the sunlight in front of three thousand people.

"Romeo," Harry said again. "Lives in Forks now. Married the girl. She's the newborn." He shook his head. "I'd quite like to meet him, actually."

"You would *not*."

"I'd like to shake his hand. He's the reason I've got a job this year."

"Category Two, Potter."

"Category Two," Harry agreed. "Hands off. Saul—MACUSA?"

"Still don't know you're there. Keep it that way." Croaker reached for his cold tea, looked at it, and set it down. "If the Americans find a British Unspeakable on their soil with a cell full of their wildlife, there will be a very difficult conversation, and I will be too old to attend it."

"I've been here eleven days. They'd need a map and a torch to find their own arses." Harry stood. "I'm going into town. Time to meet the locals. I'm going to buy the chief of police lunch."

Croaker's eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch. "The father-in-law."

"Chief Charles Swan. Married the daughter off to the Cullen boy in August; it was in the local paper, there was a photo. Nice-looking wedding." Harry picked his jacket up off the back of the stool and shrugged it on over the holster. "If anybody in this town knows about the dogs and the doctor and still sleeps at night, it's him. I'd like to know what he knows."

"Gently."

"I'm always gentle."

"You are *quiet*, Potter. People mistake it for gentle. Once." Croaker picked up his quill. "Report when you've got something in that box."

The glass clouded over, and Harry was looking at his own face.

He needed a shave. He went and had one, with a razor, because some things you did by hand. Then he put on a clean white shirt and the charcoal suit and the black overcoat, and the Browning under his arm, and his wand in its holster along the inside of his left forearm where he could forget about it, and the black leather wallet with the badge in it in his inside pocket—U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Office of Law Enforcement, Special Agent Harry Evans, in a picture he'd had taken at a Walgreens in Tacoma.

He turned the record off on his way out. Then he walked a quarter mile down the logging spur in the rain to where a 1969 Dodge Charger sat under a tarp in the alders, black, with Washington plates and paperwork that would stand up to anything short of a Senate hearing. He'd pulled it out of a federal seizure lot in Tacoma on his second day in the country. The Department had sent him a Ford Taurus. He'd sent the Taurus back.

He pulled the tarp off and folded it and threw it in the boot. He got in. The engine came up rough and loud and settled into a growl that shook the alders, and he sat for a second with his hands on the wheel listening to it, the way you'd sit and listen to a song you liked.

"Right," he said. "Pie."

He pulled out onto the spur, and drove down out of the hills toward Forks.

### V. Need to Know

**Monday, November 20, 2006 — The diner, Forks Avenue, Forks, Washington — 12:22 p.m. PST**

*Potter, H. — Third stool from the end. Marionberry.*

---

Charlie Swan noticed the car before he noticed the man, which he would think about later, because it was the kind of thing a guy in his line of work was supposed to be good at.

It was parked across two spaces at the far end of the lot, nose out, in the rain: a '69 Charger, black on black, with the kind of shine nobody bothered with on the peninsula because the peninsula would just take it off again by Thursday. Washington plates. No dealer frame. Charlie looked at it for a good three seconds on his way in from the cruiser, with the water dripping off the brim of his hat, and thought, *Nobody who works for anybody drives that car*.

Then he went inside, and it was dry and close and smelled like fryer oil and coffee, and he forgot about it, because Rhonda was already pouring his cup.

"Chief."

"Rhonda."

"Patty melt?"

"Patty melt."

He took his usual booth by the window and hung his hat on the hook and sat down with his back to the wall the way he'd been sitting down in restaurants since 1983. The lunch crowd was what it always was on a Monday in November: a couple of guys from the mill in their Carhartts, Mrs. Stanley from the school office with her sister, a table of four retirees from the Methodist church arguing about the Seahawks. And at the counter, on the third stool from the end, a man in a suit.

Charlie looked at him for a while over the top of his coffee.

Big guy. Six-two, maybe a little more, and heavy through the shoulders in a way that the suit had been cut to account for—a good suit, charcoal, the kind you didn't buy in Port Angeles. White shirt, no tie. A black wool overcoat folded on the stool next to him, wet across the shoulders. Black hair cut short and still damp. He was eating pie. He had his left elbow on the counter and his fork in his right hand, and he was eating marionberry pie à la mode like it had personally done something nice for him, and Rhonda was standing across the counter from him with the coffeepot in her hand, laughing.

Rhonda had been working that counter for nineteen years. Charlie had seen her laugh maybe twice.

"—and I said, love, if this is what you lot are hiding up here, I'm never leaving," the man was saying. His voice carried a little, low and clear, with an accent on it that Charlie only knew from the BBC. English. "That's the best pie I've had on this continent. That's possibly the best pie I've had on any continent. I'd like to marry it."

"You can't marry a pie, hon."

"Watch me."

Rhonda laughed again and topped him up and moved off down the counter. The man turned on his stool, a quarter turn, coffee cup in hand, and looked straight across the diner at Charlie as if he'd known where Charlie was sitting the whole time.

He had green eyes. Very green. Charlie, who'd had twenty-three years to get used to being looked at by people who didn't want to be in a room with a cop, felt the back of his neck go tight.

The man picked up his plate and his cup and his coat and came across the room.

"Chief Swan," he said. "Mind if I join you? I won't keep you long. I'll even let you finish your sandwich."

Charlie looked at him. "Do I know you?"

"Not yet." The man set the plate down, and the coffee, and draped the coat over the back of the booth. He took a black leather wallet out of the inside pocket of his jacket and laid it open on the table, turned so Charlie could read it. A gold badge—an eagle and a shield. An ID card. *U.S. Department of the Interior. Fish and Wildlife Service. Office of Law Enforcement. Special Agent Harry Evans.* "Harry Evans. Fish and Wildlife. Out of the Portland regional."

Charlie looked at the badge for a long moment. It looked right. It looked exactly right.

"You don't sound like Portland," he said.

"I'm from Surrey." The man sat down across from him. "My mum was American. Long story. They let me in anyway." He picked up his fork. "Do you mind? It's going to melt."

"Go ahead."

Rhonda brought the patty melt. She looked at Agent Evans and then at Charlie with an expression that said she'd be asking about this later, and went away again. Charlie picked up half his sandwich and didn't eat it.

"Harry," he said.

"That's me."

"Had a friend named Harry." He hadn't known he was going to say it. It was the name—you didn't hear it much anymore. It wasn't a young man's name. "Fished with him every weekend for twenty years."

Agent Evans went still for half a second. His fork stopped on the way to his mouth. Then he set it down on the plate, quite carefully, and looked at Charlie across the table, and something in his face shut off—the grin went out of it, the thing that had made Rhonda laugh—and what was left looked older.

"I'm sorry," he said quietly. "When?"

"March." Charlie put the sandwich down. "Heart attack." Then his brain caught up, and he looked at the man a little harder. "I didn't say he died."

"You said *had*," said Agent Evans. "And you said it like that."

Charlie held his eyes for a second longer. Then he looked away, out the window at the rain, and picked up his coffee.

"Yeah," he said. "Well."

"I'm sorry," the man said again, and meant it, as far as Charlie could tell. Then he picked up his fork again, and when he spoke it was in the other voice, lighter, the one he'd had at the counter. "I'll try to be a credit to the name."

"What's Fish and Wildlife want with me?"

"Wolves," said Agent Evans.

---

Charlie had been a cop for a long time. He'd learned a lot of things in that time that didn't come in the academy manual, and one of them was that the thing your face did in the first quarter-second after a question was the only part of the conversation that couldn't be fixed later. He'd made a career of watching other people's faces in that quarter-second.

He'd never once had to worry about his own. He wasn't a liar. He'd never needed to be.

He felt it go wrong. He felt his eyebrows start up and his jaw go tight and his eyes go to the window—to the tree line across the road, where the rain came down out of the low gray sky into the forest that went all the way to the ocean—and he got it back, but not fast enough, and he knew it, and he knew the man across from him had seen it.

*Jake.* Standing in his front yard in September, in the drizzle, saying *Charlie, you're going to want to sit down,* and then not giving him time to. Jake's clothes going everywhere. The sound. The size of the thing that had been standing on his lawn afterward, russet brown and taller than Charlie's cruiser, with Jake's eyes.

"Wolves," Charlie said.

"Wolves," agreed Agent Evans, cutting a neat corner off his pie. "We've had reports. Going back a while—fall of last year, mostly, then again this spring. Hikers. A couple of hunters. The odd logger. Animals seen in this area that are, according to the people seeing them, the size of horses." He ate the corner of pie. "Which I'm sure is nonsense. People see a big dog in the fog and it's a horse by the time they get to the pub. But we've had enough of them, and they've come from enough different people, that some bright spark in Portland decided someone ought to come up and walk around in the rain and file a report. And that someone was me."

"There's wolves up here sometimes," Charlie said. "Gray wolves. Come down out of Canada."

"There haven't been wolves on the Olympic Peninsula since 1920, Chief. Officially. Last one was shot sometime in the twenties, up the Elwha, I think. We've got a whole file on it." Agent Evans looked at him mildly. "If they're back, that's quite a big deal. Endangered Species Act. Federal habitat. You'd have reporters. People from universities. People in fleeces with clipboards. Hundreds of them. All over the woods."

Charlie thought about people in fleeces with clipboards all over the woods around La Push. He thought about the Cullens' place, out past the end of the road, with its glass wall and its eight people who never seemed to be cold and never seemed to eat. He thought about Bella.

"Nobody wants that," he said.

"No," said Agent Evans. "Nobody wants that." He took a sip of coffee. "Hence me. I'm very quiet. I'm here to find out whether there's anything to find, and if there isn't, I'll write a very boring report and go home, and Portland can put it in a drawer."

"And if there is?"

"Then I'll write a slightly less boring report," Agent Evans said, "and I'll come and buy you another sandwich, and we'll talk about it."

Charlie ate some of his patty melt. It gave him something to do with his face.

"I took some guys out last year," he said, when he'd swallowed. "After the hikers. We had a few go missing out past the—we put it down to a bear. Big one. We went out with rifles, me and a couple of the guys from the department and some of the hunting club. We didn't find anything." That was true. That was all true. "I'll tell you what I told the state. It was a bear."

"A bear," Agent Evans said.

"Big one."

"And the horses?"

"Fog," said Charlie. "Like you said."

Agent Evans looked at him across the table for a long moment. His eyes were very steady. Charlie had the strangest feeling, just then—like a draft going through a room with all the windows shut. Like someone had walked through his house and very politely not touched anything.

Then the man smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, mostly in one corner of his mouth.

"Chief," he said. "I'm a big believer in need to know."

Charlie blinked.

"I work for the federal government. I believe in it more than most. I think it's the single most useful idea anybody ever had." Agent Evans set his fork down on the empty plate, squared it up neatly with the edge. "There's a whole world of things I don't need to know, and I'm very happy not knowing them, and I'm not going to go poking around in your town asking about them. I'm looking for one thing. If it isn't here, it isn't here. Okay?"

Need to know. Charlie had said those exact words to Jake in his front yard in September, with the rain coming down and his hands shaking. *Need to know, Jake. I don't need to know. Okay?*

He looked at the man across from him and couldn't tell whether that was a coincidence.

"Okay," he said slowly.

"Good." Agent Evans reached into his jacket and took out a card and laid it on the table next to Charlie's coffee. Plain white. The Fish and Wildlife logo, the name, a phone number with a 360 area code. "If you hear anything. If anybody comes in to the station with a story. If you see anything big in the road on a foggy night." He paused. "If anyone goes missing out in the woods. Anyone at all. I'd want to know that very quickly."

Something in the way he said the last part made Charlie look up.

"You think something's going to happen?"

"I think there's a lot of woods out there," said Agent Evans, "and a lot of things in them, and I'm a careful man." He picked up his coat. "There's a family out east of town. Big place. The Cullens."

Charlie felt it again—the quarter-second. He held it this time. He held it with everything he had.

"What about them?"

"Nothing at all. They've a lot of acreage out there, that's all, backing on the national forest. If I'm going to be walking around on it, I'd like to ask nicely first." He stood, shrugging into the overcoat. "Your daughter married into them, I hear. Congratulations. August, wasn't it?"

"How'd you hear that?"

"It was in the *Forum*." Agent Evans buttoned the coat. "There was a photograph. Lovely wedding. Small town, Chief. People talk to the man from the government. You'd be amazed."

He took out his wallet—his real one, a beat-up brown leather thing—and peeled off a twenty and a ten and tucked them under the edge of his empty plate. Charlie looked at the money. The pie was four-fifty. The coffee was a dollar.

"For Rhonda," the man said, catching the look. "She's earned it. She laughed at my jokes."

"She doesn't laugh at anybody's jokes."

"Well," said Agent Evans, "then she's earned it twice," and he lifted one hand to Rhonda at the counter, who lifted the coffeepot back at him like a salute, and he walked out of the diner into the rain.

Charlie sat in the booth and watched through the window as he crossed the parking lot—not hurrying, hands in the pockets of his coat, his head bare in the downpour like he didn't notice it was raining—and got into the black Charger, and sat there for a second with the engine idling, and then pulled out onto Forks Avenue going north, toward the 101.

Charlie looked down at the card.

He picked it up. He turned it over. There was nothing on the back.

He thought about the badge, which had been exactly right. He thought about the accent and the suit and the car that nobody who worked for anybody would ever drive, and the way the guy had sat down at his table like he'd been invited, and the way he'd said *when* about Harry Clearwater before Charlie had said one word about Harry being dead. He thought about the draft through the shut-up house. And he thought about Jake in his yard in September, and the sound, and about what Bella's eyes had looked like through those brown contacts the first time he'd seen her after, at the house, holding that baby who wasn't—who was Edward's niece, Edward's brother's girl, that was the story, and Charlie believed the story, because believing the story was the deal.

*If anyone goes missing out in the woods. Anyone at all.*

He put the card in his shirt pocket and buttoned the flap over it.

"Rhonda," he said. "Can I get this to go?"

---

He called Sue first, from the cruiser, with the rain hammering on the roof.

"Some guy from Fish and Wildlife," he said. "Asking about wolves. Big ones."

Sue Clearwater was quiet on the other end of the line long enough that he could hear the radio in her kitchen, the noon news out of Seattle.

"What did you tell him?"

"Bear," said Charlie. "Fog. Nothing."

"Okay." Another pause. "What was he like?"

Charlie thought about it.

"English," he said. "Real polite. Ate a whole piece of pie in about four bites." He looked out the windshield at the empty space at the far end of the lot where the Charger had been. "Said his name was Harry."

He heard her breath go in, small and sharp.

"Yeah," Charlie said. "I know."

Then he called Bella. He called the house, not the cell phone, because Bella's cell phone never seemed to work anymore and the house always seemed to pick up on the first ring, and it was Edward who answered, as if he'd been standing next to it.

"Charlie."

"Edward. Is Bella around?"

"She's right here." A pause. "Is something wrong?"

He told them about the Fish and Wildlife guy. He didn't tell them about the wolves, exactly, because he didn't know how much Bella knew about Jake, and he didn't want to know how much Bella knew about Jake, and the thing about need to know was that it went both ways. He told them a federal agent was in town looking into animal reports and might come by the house to ask about walking their land. He described him. English, big, black hair, green eyes, nice suit. Black car.

The line was very quiet for a moment.

"Thank you, Charlie," Edward said finally. "That's—thank you. We'll keep an eye out."

"Yeah," said Charlie. "Do that."

He hung up. He sat in the cruiser with his patty melt going cold in its foil on the passenger seat and the rain coming down, and he took the card back out of his pocket and looked at it again.

Harry Evans.

He dialed the number on it. It was the only thing he could think of to do.

It rang once.

"Chief Swan," said the English voice, sounding pleased. "That was quick. Did you forget something?"

Charlie hung up.

He didn't call it again. He didn't know how the guy had known it was him. His cell was a department phone on a county account. On caller ID it came up as CLALLAM CO, if it came up as anything at all.

He put the card back in his pocket, and buttoned the flap, and drove back to the station, and spent the rest of the afternoon not thinking about it at all.

### VI. Lassie

**Wednesday, November 22, 2006 — Ridge above the Calawah River, north of the Cullen land — 2:46 p.m. PST**

*Potter, H. — Field installation, Bogachiel drainage. Workshop bench. Rebuilding a Sneakoscope with a pair of tweezers and the radio on.*

---

*—and then she said, "So what is it you lot actually celebrate, is it the Pilgrims? Are we celebrating the Pilgrims? Because in my experience the Pilgrims were awful," and I said—*

*Seth.*

*—I said, it's mostly about the food, and she said—*

*Seth. I swear to God.*

*What?*

*Can you think about something else for one minute? One? I'll time it.*

*Like what?*

*Like anything. Like literally anything else in the entire world. Like the trees. Look at all these trees, Seth. Think about a tree.*

He thought about a tree. It was the tree in Layla's front yard, the big maple, with Layla under it in that dumb navy raincoat, laughing at something he'd said, and Leah felt the whole thing go through her—the leaves, the laugh, the thread pulled tight between her brother's ribs—and she put her head down and ran harder, until the wind in her ears drowned some of it out.

It didn't drown out enough. Nothing ever did.

The ridge above the Calawah ran north and west in a long broken spine, granite under a skin of moss and fern, the trees on it gone thin and wind-bent where the soil got shallow. Leah took it at a lope she could hold for hours. It was raining—it was always raining—a fine cold drizzle that beaded on her coat and ran off, and the forest on either side was a dark wet smear of hemlock and fir and the pale trunks of alders, and it smelled the way it always smelled in late November, like rot and cedar and mushrooms and water. Seth was forty yards behind her and to the left, on the lower trail, because she'd told him to stay there. He was a better tracker than her and he was slower than her and he was her little brother. All three were reasons.

*Tomorrow,* Seth thought, for maybe the ninth time since lunch, *Mom said Layla could come at two. Her mom's on shift till four but she said she'd come after, and Charlie's bringing pie from the diner—*

*I know.*

*—and Billy's coming, and Jake's going to bring Nessie for like an hour, and Mom said she invited Emily and Sam—*

He felt her flinch. He felt it the way she felt it, in her own chest, and he stopped.

*Sorry,* he said. *Lee. Sorry.*

*It's fine.*

*Lee, come on, I know it's—*

*Seth. It's fine.* It wasn't fine. It was never going to be fine, and he knew that, and she knew he knew it, and that was the whole problem with this stupid—*thing*, this hive, this circle of heads with no doors in it, that you could never once in your life just *say* something was fine and have somebody believe you. *Think about a tree.*

He thought about a tree. An actual one this time, a Sitka spruce he'd passed. It was a nice tree. She appreciated the effort.

They ran.

---

There was a man in the woods.

Leah had been thinking about it for days, and she thought about it again now, on the ridge, because she'd hit his scent about a mile back and it was still there under the rain. A man. One man, alone. Gun oil and coffee and wet wool, and something else under that she couldn't place, something like the inside of a cave—cold stone, and pine pitch, and a sharp clean edge on it that made her nose itch. It had turned up on the ridges about ten days ago, all at once, the way scent turned up when someone new came to the woods. And then it had kept turning up. On this ridge. On the one across the river. On the Sol Duc side. Along the highway pullouts, all the way up toward Sappho. Down by the Bogachiel, where nobody went.

Jake said it was a hunter. Elk season, and the guy was being thorough.

Leah had been hunting these woods since she was six, with Dad, before any of this. Nobody hunted like that. Nobody walked every single ridge for thirty miles in a line, like they were drawing a map.

Then on Monday, Charlie Swan had called Mom from his cruiser, and Mom had told them at dinner with her council face on: a fed. Fish and Wildlife. Asking about wolves. Asking about big wolves, and about the Cullens. English, Charlie said. Big guy, black hair, green eyes. Nice suit.

Said his name was Harry.

Leah had been holding a fork, and she'd put it down. She'd gotten up from the table and gone outside and phased in the backyard without bothering to take her clothes off first, and run for two hours, alone, until Seth phased and found her and didn't say anything at all, which was the nicest thing he'd done in a month.

*Harry.* Plenty of men had the name. Old men, mostly; nobody young had it anymore. It was the name of a man who'd been fifty-two and wore a Mariners cap to church and liked his eggs over easy, who'd taught her to gut a fish on the dock at La Push when she was seven. Who'd been standing at the kitchen counter in March, laughing at something on the radio, when Leah's whole body had come apart in front of him in a sound like a car crash, and who had looked at the thing she'd become with his mouth open and put his hand flat on his chest.

She didn't think about that. She never thought about that. She ran, and she didn't think about it, and Seth, forty yards back, very carefully thought about trees.

---

*Leah.*

She'd already stopped.

It came down the wind off the north and slapped her in the face like a hand: sweet, sickly, cloying, so strong it burned the back of her nose and made her eyes water, bleach and rotten flowers and burnt sugar all at once. Leech. And under the sweetness, the other thing, the thing the Cullens never carried—copper and salt and meat. Fresh blood. Human blood. Something on this ridge had been eating people, and recently.

*That's not one of ours,* Seth said.

*No shit.* She had her nose up. North and a little west, coming down off the high ground toward the river. Close—a mile, maybe less. Moving. *Red-eye. You smell that?*

*Yeah.* She felt him go grim, which Seth didn't do often. It always surprised her how much he sounded like Dad when he did. *Yeah. It's fed. Today, I think.*

*Howl for Jake.*

He threw his head back and howled, the long rising note that meant *come*, and it went out over the valley and broke against the far ridge and came back. Somewhere east, at the Cullens' place, Jake would be on the couch with Nessie asleep on his chest. Jake would hear it. Jake would phase.

They didn't wait for him.

---

They took it in a pincer, the way Jake had drilled them, the way Sam had drilled all of them a year ago: Leah up top on the ridge where she could run flat out, Seth below on the slope, pushing up and in. The scent got thicker, and sweeter, and fresher, until it was so strong it was almost a taste, and then she topped a granite hump in a spray of wet moss and saw him.

He was standing in a little clearing in the hemlocks a hundred yards below her, with his back to her. Tall. Long blond hair tied back in a ponytail that went halfway down his spine. A red Gore-Tex shell, brand new, the price tag still flapping off the zipper pull. Jeans. Bare feet—bare white feet, on the moss, in the rain. He was standing very still with his head tipped back, like he was smelling the air.

He turned around.

His eyes were red—an older red than Bella's stoplight newborn color, darker, the color of the inside of a cherry. There was a smear of something dark at the corner of his mouth, and he had a face like a guy on the cover of one of the paperbacks Mom kept in the bathroom, all cheekbones and jaw. He saw her up on the hump. He saw Seth coming up through the ferns below. And he smiled.

Then he took in how big they were.

Leah watched it happen. She watched him measure her, and then measure Seth, and then do the math, and she watched the smile come off his face like someone had wiped it with a rag.

He ran.

---

He was fast. They were all fast, the leeches, faster than anything had a right to be, and this one was fast even for them—he went down the slope through the hemlocks like water going down a drain, not touching anything, not breaking anything, his bare feet barely touching the ground. West. Toward the Calawah gorge.

Leah went after him.

She was the fastest of all of them. Faster than Sam. Faster than Jake, though Jake would never admit it. It was the one thing about being the only girl in a pack of boys who all thought she was a genetic dead end that she'd never, not once, had to argue about. She came off the granite hump in a long flat leap and hit the slope running and *poured* it on, and the forest came apart around her into streaks—green, black, silver, gray—and the ground fell away under her and came back and fell away, and she could hear Seth crashing along below her, slower, and the leech ahead of her, not crashing at all.

*Left,* Seth said. *He's going left—he's going for the gorge—*

*I see it.*

*He's going to jump it.*

*Then so am I.*

The Calawah ran through a slot down here, a narrow gorge thirty feet wide with walls of black rock furred with moss, the river loud and white at the bottom of it. There were places a wolf could clear it. There were places a wolf could not. Leah knew every one of them. She saw the leech hit the edge of the gorge at a dead sprint, a red streak through the trees, and she saw him gather himself and *throw* himself off the edge, out over the gap, arms out—

And then he went *up*.

He went up like he'd been yanked. Like somebody had tied a rope around his ankle and hooked the other end to a truck. One second he was in the air over the gorge, halfway across, and the next his right leg snapped straight up over his head and the rest of him followed it, whipping around in a half-circle with his arms flying out and his ponytail cracking like a whip, and he slammed to a stop thirty feet up under the lowest limb of a big old hemlock on the far side. Upside down. Hanging by one ankle from nothing.

He hung there. He swung once, a little, the way a body swings.

Then he stopped moving.

Leah dug in her claws on the near edge of the gorge so hard she tore up a slab of moss three feet long and went skidding on the wet rock, all four legs locked, and stopped with her front paws at the very lip of the drop and the river roaring white below. Seth came crashing out of the ferns behind her a second later, saw her stopped, tried to stop, didn't, and slammed into her hindquarters hard enough to knock her teeth together.

*Ow—*

*What the hell,* Leah said. *What the* hell*—*

They looked up.

The leech was hanging in the air under the hemlock limb with his arms flung out to the sides and his head down, and his eyes were open—she could see them, red, rolling, darting back and forth—but nothing else moved. Not his fingers. Not his mouth, which was open in a snarl, frozen there. His ponytail hung straight down toward the gorge. The cuffs of his jeans had slid up his shins. His red jacket had fallen down around his armpits and hung there like a flag. There was nothing holding him up. Nothing at all. Leah could see clear sky—gray sky—between his ankle and the limb.

And there was a smell.

It cut through the leech-stink like a knife: sharp and clean and electric, the smell the air made right before lightning. The smell of a fuse box.

*That smells like Layla,* Seth said.

*I swear to God, Seth.*

*Lee, I mean it—that's what Layla smells like. That's the—when she does the thing, with the wand, that's what it—*

*Who's that?* said Leah.

There was a man standing on the far side of the gorge.

---

He hadn't been there. That was the first thing. She'd been looking right at that spot a second before, the strip of moss and fern on the far bank under the hemlock, and it had been empty, and now it wasn't. No sound. No movement. No crack like the one Seth said Layla's thing made when she popped in and out. One second empty, one second a man.

The second thing was the smell. The wind was coming across the gorge toward her, and it brought him with it: gun oil, coffee, wet wool. Cold stone. Pine pitch. That clean edge, like a struck match.

The ridge-walker. The guy who'd been drawing a map of the woods for ten days.

He was big. Six-two, maybe, heavy through the shoulders and chest in a way that the clothes didn't hide. Black suit—an actual suit, out here, in the rain, on the edge of a gorge—white shirt open at the collar, a long black overcoat that hung to his knees and was dark across the shoulders with rain. Black boots with mud to the ankle. His hair was black and cut short, and wet, and his hands were in the pockets of his coat, and he wasn't looking at her. He was looking up, with his head tipped back, at the leech hanging over his head.

"Hello, Fabio," he said.

English.

The leech's eyes rolled toward him and stuck there.

The man looked at him for a few more seconds, considering, the way you'd look at a picture in a museum. Then he lowered his head and looked across the gorge at Leah and Seth.

His eyes were green.

*Charlie's fed,* Leah thought, before she could stop it. *English, big, black hair, green eyes. Nice suit. Harry.*

The man took his right hand out of his coat pocket. In it was a black leather wallet. He flipped it open with his thumb and held it up toward them, across thirty feet of empty air and a white roaring river: a gold badge, an eagle and a shield, catching what light there was. An ID card behind a plastic window.

"Afternoon," he said. He raised his voice just enough to carry over the water. "This situation is contained. I'm going to have to ask you both to please vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

Leah stared at him.

Seth, beside her, sat down. She felt him do it—not on purpose, his legs just sort of went—and his tail thumped once against the moss in complete confusion.

The man closed the wallet and put it back inside his coat. Then he looked up at the leech again, and moved one finger of his left hand, a small flick, like a man flicking a crumb off a table.

The leech fell.

He dropped straight down out of the air, thirty feet, head-first, rigid as a board, arms still out—and stopped. A foot and a half off the ground, he just stopped, and hung there, horizontal now, floating at the height of a man's waist, like a log on still water. The man stepped forward, and bent, and got one arm under the leech's middle, and straightened up with him.

He tucked him under his arm. Sideways. The leech's head forward and his bare feet sticking out behind, his body stiff as a plank, his arms still flung out to either side, his ponytail dangling. The man shifted him a little, hitching him up for a better grip, and settled him against his hip.

Like a surfboard.

Like he was about to walk down to First Beach and paddle out.

The leech's eyes were rolling madly. He didn't make a sound.

*Is this guy for real?* Leah thought.

"Real as it gets, Lassie," said the man.

---

There was a long moment where nothing happened at all.

The river roared. The rain came down. Somewhere off to the east, very faintly, she felt Jake phase—a flare of heat and confusion and *Leah, what's happening*, coming in from a long way off—and she didn't answer him, because she couldn't think of anything except the man on the other side of the gorge, who had just answered a question she had not said out loud.

*Leah,* Seth said, very carefully. *Did he just—*

"Yes," said the man. "Clear as a bell. Like you're shouting down a drainpipe, Balto."

*He can hear us.*

"I can hear you."

*He can hear what we're* thinking*—*

"Still can," the man said patiently, "every word. You can stop narrating, I've got it."

Leah's hackles went up all the way down her spine. She felt her lips come back off her teeth, felt the snarl start somewhere under her ribs and come up through her throat into the air—a sound she'd heard make grown men wet themselves—and the man on the other side of the gorge looked at her with mild interest, like she was a kettle that had started to whistle.

*Get out of my head,* she said. She put everything she had behind it. *Get. Out. Of my head.*

"Get out of my crime scene," the man said, "and I'll call it even."

*Who the hell* are *you?*

"Read the badge."

*I can read a badge, asshole. That's not what I asked.*

"It's what I answered, though." He hitched the leech up again under his arm. "You're not the first person to hear voices in your head, love, I promise. I've met people who'd kill to have it this tidy. Yours come in two—" He stopped, and tilted his head slightly to one side, like he was listening to something far off. "Three. Sorry. Three. There's another one just come in. Hello." He seemed to be addressing the air above Leah's head. "Bit of a temper on you, isn't there?"

*Leah,* Jake was saying, and he was running now; she could feel his paws hitting the ground, feel the trees going by him in a blur, feel him six miles off and closing. *Leah, who is that? Who's talking? Leah, get away from him—*

"Tell him not to bother running," the man said. "He'll only get here in time to miss me."

*This is like Edward,* Seth was thinking. It came out of him in a rush, the way things did with Seth, scared and fascinated at once. *Lee, this is just like Edward, he can do the same—*

"Edward." The man said it slowly, like he was writing it down. "Right."

Leah felt her blood go cold.

*Shut up,* she told Seth. *Seth, shut up, shut up, don't think—*

"Don't blame him, it's very hard not to think. People have been trying for years." The man shifted his weight. He still hadn't taken his other hand out of his pocket. "And you're the one who keeps handing me things, Lassie. I'm just standing here."

*Stop calling me that.*

"Stop thinking about my throat."

Because she had been. She'd been measuring it. The gorge—thirty feet. She could clear it; she'd cleared wider. He was standing ten feet back from the lip on the far side, which gave her a landing. One jump and she'd be on the moss, two strides and she'd have him by the neck, leech under his arm or no leech, badge or no badge, she didn't care, he'd been in her *head*, he had been in her *head*, and nobody got in her head, not Sam, not anybody, not anymore—

"I wouldn't," the man said.

He said it quietly, the way you'd tell somebody the stove was on.

"I watched you come down that slope. You're very quick. You're quicker than any of the others I've seen; I'd put money on it." He met her eyes across the gorge. "But you'd be giving me a reason, and I've had a long week, and I'd much rather not. Honestly. Sit down with your brother."

She didn't sit down. But she didn't jump, either. She stood on the lip of the gorge with her lips back and her hackles up and every muscle in her body cocked like a gun, and she didn't jump, and she hated, she *hated*, that she didn't.

*Leah,* Jake was saying. *Leah, don't. Whatever he is, don't. I'm coming. Four minutes.*

"Three and a half," said the man. "You're making good time."

---

*What's he going to do with it?* Seth thought. He was staring at the leech under the man's arm. The leech was staring back, eyes rolling in his frozen face, wild, begging. *What's he going to do with him? Where's he taking him?*

"Somewhere with better lighting," said the man.

*Is he going to kill him?*

The man looked at Seth for a moment. Something passed over his face too fast to read.

"Not today," he said. "He's been eating people, Balto. You can smell it on him better than I can. Two since breakfast. I'd stop worrying about Fabio." He hitched the leech up one more time, and turned, half a turn, like he was about to walk off into the trees. "Right. That's me. Lovely to meet you both. Stay off the north ridges for a few days, there's a good—"

And Seth thought: *He's a wizard.*

It came out of him clear as a bell, the way things did when Seth figured something out—a bright clean shape of a thought, with Layla right in the middle of it. *He has to be. That rope thing, there's no rope. And he just showed up, he didn't even make the crack noise. And the floating. Layla floated Emmett like that. He's a wizard. He's like Layla—*

The man stopped.

He stopped mid-turn, with the leech under his arm and his weight on one foot, and stood there.

He didn't say anything for a long moment. The rain came down on his bare head and ran off his hair into his collar, and he didn't seem to notice. He stood very, very still on the far side of the gorge, and the loose, careless thing that had been in his face all along, the thing that had let him stand there and trade insults with two wolves like he was waiting for a bus, went out of it. All at once. Like someone had turned off a light.

What was left behind looked at them.

It looked at Seth first. Then at Leah. Then at Seth again. It looked at them for a long time, and Leah, who had looked into the eyes of more than a dozen vampires in the last year, who'd been close enough to smell Victoria, who'd seen the whole of a newborn army come out of the trees in that clearing in June—Leah felt the fur stand up on her back in a way that it had never stood up before.

"Now," the man said, quietly, "that's interesting."

He turned all the way back around to face them.

"You two," he said, "shouldn't know that word. Not the way you just thought it." His voice had dropped. It barely carried over the river. "Not with a *like* on the end of it."

And then she felt him come in.

Before, he'd been listening. She understood that now, too late. Before, it had been somebody standing outside a window, hearing what came through the glass. Now somebody had opened the door. She felt it as pressure right behind her eyes, a cool steady weight, painless so far, enormous and patient and absolutely unhurried, like a hand going into a coat pocket. She felt it go past her. Past her, like she was a door it had walked through, and into Seth.

And she felt what it found.

Layla. On the beach in the moonlight with a wand in her hand. Layla's face in the white light at the end of it. Yellow birds. *Avis*. *I'm a witch*. A castle on a lake. A Ministry under London. *There's a law, it's called the Statute of Secrecy*—

"Oh," said the man, softly, "you're *joking*."

Seth panicked.

She felt it happen a half-second before he did it, and there was nothing she could do. She felt the bottom fall out of him—*he's seen her, he's in my head and he's seen her, he knows her face, he's going to find her*—and she felt his head go down, fast, to his right foreleg, where the sea-glass thing was tied on its cord next to his shorts, and she yelled *Seth, NO—*

He bit down. She heard the glass crack between his teeth.

---

*CRACK.*

The air ripped open six feet to Seth's left like a gunshot, and Layla Ashby was standing on the edge of the gorge.

She'd come from somewhere indoors—she was in sock feet, and a sweater, and pajama pants with clouds on them, and her hair was halfway down out of its knot—and she had her wand up before she'd even finished arriving. She took it all in in less than a second. Leah watched her do it: Seth, crouched and shaking. Leah, hackles up. The gorge. The man on the far side with a vampire under his arm like a surfboard.

She didn't hesitate at all.

"*Stupefy!*"

A bolt of red light came out of the end of her wand and went across the gorge like a flare, straight at the man's chest.

He didn't move his feet. He didn't take his left hand out of his pocket. He lifted his right hand, the one that had held the badge, and flicked two fingers at the spell as it came at him—a lazy little backhand flick, like a man shooing a wasp.

The red light bent.

It veered off a foot in front of his chest like it had hit a curve in the air, and went sideways into the trees, and hit a hemlock thirty yards down the bank with a *crack* and a burst of light. Bark exploded off the trunk. A shower of needles came down. The hemlock shuddered all the way up to the top.

Layla's face went white. She didn't stop. She slashed the wand down and across, hard, with her whole arm in it—

"*Confringo!*"

This one was orange. It came out of the wand like a coal out of a fire, spitting and roaring, trailing sparks, and it went across the gorge so fast Leah barely saw it go.

The man held up his hand, palm out.

The spell stopped.

It stopped in the air about a yard from his face and just *hung* there, a ball of orange fire the size of a fist, roaring and spitting and fizzing like a firework that had forgotten to explode. Sparks came off it and hissed out in the rain. It shook in the air like something trying to get loose.

The man looked at it.

He leaned in, a little, and tilted his head, and studied it, the way you'd study a bug on a windowsill. He turned his palm slightly, and the ball of fire turned with it, slowly, and he watched it turn.

"Blasting Curse," he said. "Nice shape. Good heat." He turned it another quarter-turn. "Bit of a wobble on the back end. You're over-rotating on the slash, it's pulling left. You'd have taken my ear off instead of my face." He looked past it at Layla. "Still. That's the second spell you've thrown at me in four seconds, and the second one would've killed me. I could have you arrested and your wand snapped for attacking an Unspeakable."

Layla went still.

Leah saw it land. She saw the word go into Layla like a knife—*Unspeakable*—and she saw Layla look at him, really look, for the first time. At his face. At his eyes.

All the color left Layla's face at once.

"Oh, God," Layla whispered. "Oh my *God*. You're—"

"Having a very long Wednesday," the man said. "Yes."

He closed his hand.

The ball of fire collapsed. It folded in on itself like a dying star, into a point of orange light and then into nothing, with a soft sound like a candle being pinched out, and a little curl of gray smoke drifted up from his fist and away into the rain.

He lowered his hand.

"Layla Ashby," he said. "Ravenclaw, I'd have guessed, and I'd have been right. Martin Ashby's girl." He paused. "Improper Use of Magic Office. Third desk from the window."

Layla made a small sound.

"Put the wand down, Miss Ashby. Before you do something your father has to write a letter about."

Her hand came down. Slowly. It was shaking so hard Leah could see the tip of the wand trembling from where she stood.

The man looked at her a moment longer. Then he looked down at Seth's leg, at the little crushed glitter of green sea glass caught in the fur, and back at Layla.

"Protean link," he said. "With an Apparition anchor tied into it. Off a bit of beach glass." He was quiet a second. "That's clever. That's not in any syllabus. Who taught you that?"

"N-nobody," said Layla.

"Hm." He looked at her a moment more, and Leah couldn't read his face at all. Then he hitched the vampire up under his arm one last time and looked at the three of them, one after another: Layla, Seth, Leah.

"Right," he said. "Here's how this goes. You three are going to go home. You're going to have a lovely Thanksgiving—I'm told there's pie. And you, Miss Ashby, are going to think very hard about the International Statute of Wizarding Secrecy, and how many people you've explained it to lately, and what they all are." His eyes went past her, briefly, east, toward the Cullens'. "And so am I."

He looked at the wolves.

"Lassie. Balto. Stay off the north ridges for a few days. My lines won't touch you." The corner of his mouth moved. "I might."

And then he was gone.

No crack. No sound. No flash. He didn't turn on the spot, or wave anything, or say anything. He was standing on the bank with a vampire under his arm, and then the bank was empty, and there was nothing on it but a strip of moss and fern in the rain, and two deep bootprints filling slowly with water, and, thirty yards down the bank, a hemlock with a raw white wound blasted into its trunk, still smoking.

### VII. Level Nine

**Wednesday, November 22, 2006 — Calawah gorge — 2:59 p.m. PST**

*Potter, H. — Field installation, Bogachiel drainage, eleven miles south-southwest. Specimen room. Arrived 2:58 p.m. with one (1) specimen.*

---

The first thing Seth did was phase back, because Layla was standing in the rain in her socks and he couldn't hold her with paws.

He did it behind the nearest big fir, fast, so fast it hurt, the change tearing through him like a bad cramp everywhere at once, and he yanked the cutoffs off the cord around his ankle and got them on with his hands shaking and came back around the tree barefoot and shirtless and wet, and she was still standing exactly where she'd been, at the edge of the gorge, with her wand hanging from her fingers, staring at the empty bank on the other side.

"Layla."

She didn't move.

"Layla. Hey." He got to her in three steps. He put his hands on her arms. She was freezing—her sweater was soaked through already, her pajama pants were dark to the knee with wet, her socks were black with mud—and she was shaking so hard he could feel it in his own teeth. "Hey. Hey. Look at me. Are you hurt?"

"He caught it," Layla said.

"Are you *hurt*?"

"He *caught* it, Seth. He caught a Blasting Curse. In the *air*." She looked up at him, finally, and her eyes were enormous. "In his *hand*. And then he told me I was over-rotating."

"Were you?" said Leah, from behind them.

Seth hadn't heard her phase. She came out of the trees in the gray T-shirt dress she kept tied to her leg, barefoot, her short hair plastered to her head, and she looked at Layla and then across the gorge at the bootprints and then back at Layla, and her face was tight and pale.

Layla stared at her.

"*Probably!*" she said, and her voice cracked straight down the middle, and she laughed, a high awful sound, and then she put both hands over her face, wand and all, and kept laughing, and it turned into something else halfway through.

Seth pulled her in. She came without arguing. She was so cold. He wrapped himself around her as much as he could and held on, and felt her shaking go through both of them, and over the top of her head he looked at Leah.

Leah looked back at him. She didn't say anything. After a second, she looked away, at the river.

"Your hand," Seth said, into Layla's hair. He could smell it now. Blood. Not a lot. "Lay. Your hand's bleeding."

She held it out from between them and opened it. In her palm, on its cord, were the pieces of her half of the sea glass, cracked clean through in three places, the edges sharp; she'd been gripping it so hard it had cut her. She looked at it like she'd never seen it before.

"It worked," she said, faintly. "It worked. I wasn't sure it would work on a person."

"You weren't *sure*?"

"I'd tested it on a teacup."

From somewhere close, on the ridge above them, something enormous came crashing down through the hemlocks.

---

Jake hit the near side of the gorge in a spray of moss and mud and skidded to a stop exactly where Leah had, and stood there, huge and russet and blowing hard, steam coming off his back in clouds, his head swinging back and forth across the far bank. His lips were back off his teeth. He was looking for something to kill.

There was nothing to kill. There were two bootprints and a smoking tree.

He stood there for a long time. Then he turned his head and looked at Seth, and Seth could see it in the wolf's face, even without being in his head—everything Leah had shown him on the way here, the man and the badge and the voice and *Lassie* and *Balto* and the moment the man's face had changed.

Jake went into the trees. When he came back he was in cutoffs and nothing else, and he walked straight up to Layla, who had turned around in Seth's arms to look at him, and bent down to look her in the face.

"You okay?"

Layla nodded.

"You sure?"

"No," said Layla, honestly.

"Okay." Jake straightened up. He looked at the three of them, and then across the gorge, and then back at Layla. "Who was that?"

Layla took a breath.

"That," she said, "was Harry Potter."

---

There was a pause.

"Okay," said Jake. "Who?"

Layla laughed again, a short helpless sound. "Right. No. Of course. Of course you don't—" She pressed the heel of her uncut hand against her forehead. "I told the Cullens about him. Weeks ago. Seth was there. Emmett asked who our strongest wizard was."

"The war guy," said Seth slowly. "The—the one who killed the bad one. When he was seventeen."

"Yes."

"*That* was him?"

"Yes." She said it like she still couldn't believe it. "No glasses anymore. And there used to be a scar, here"—she touched her forehead—"there was a whole thing in the *Prophet* about it fading, after. But that's his face, Seth. I've had his face on a card in a shoebox under my bed since I was nine. He used to walk out of the frame every time I looked at him."

"So he's famous," said Leah. "Great. Why is a famous wizard hanging leeches out of trees in the Calawah?"

"Because he's an Unspeakable."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Layla said, and stopped, and visibly tried to work out how to say it to three people who had never heard of the Ministry of Magic until about two weeks ago, "it means he works in the Department of Mysteries."

"You said that," Seth said. "At the Cullens'. You said nobody knows what they do."

"Nobody does."

"So he's like a cop," said Jake.

"He's a lot worse than a cop." Layla's voice was getting stronger. Her teeth were chattering, but the words were coming in order now, like she'd found the end of a thread and was pulling. "Listen. You have—what's it called, the CIA? The spies. The ones who go into other countries and overthrow things. And the NSA—the ones who listen to everyone's phone calls. And the other one, DARPA, the Pentagon one, the mad-science one, they invented the internet and they're trying to build robot soldiers and Mum's got a friend at Johns Hopkins who says they've got a whole program for growing new arms—"

"Okay," said Jake.

"Imagine all three of those," Layla said, "were *one* department. With no oversight. That reported to nobody, not even the Minister, not really. That had a whole floor of the Ministry nobody else is allowed onto. Level Nine. You take the lift down and the doors open and there's a black corridor and a black door at the end of it, and you don't go through that door. Ever. My dad's worked at the Ministry twenty-two years and he's never once been through that door. He told me that when I was little—*don't make eye contact in the lift, Lay, if someone gets in at Level Nine.* I thought it was a joke. I thought he was being funny." She swallowed. "He wasn't being funny."

"What do they *do* in there?" said Seth.

"Research. That's what they say, anyway. Research." Layla laughed again, that cracked little sound. "Time. Thought. Space. Death. Things that don't have names yet. They're supposed to have a room full of prophecies. A room where they study *love*. They don't publish. They don't explain. They just—sometimes things come out of there. A new curse the Aurors start using. A new ward. Some spell that didn't exist last year and now everyone has to learn it for their exams, and nobody knows who invented it, the book just says *Department of Mysteries, 2003*." She looked at Seth. "And every single one of them—every one, that's what people say—is supposed to be a master of everything. Every subject there is. Charms and curses and potions and runes and Arithmancy and Transfiguration and things I've never even heard of. They only take people who are the best in the world at something, and then they make them learn everything else."

"And he's one of them," said Leah.

"He's one of them." Layla shivered, hard. "He's *the* one of them. He walked through that black door when he was eighteen years old, three months after he won the war, and basically never came out. People used to say he was dead. People used to say he'd gone mad. Every year or so there'd be a story in the *Prophet*—someone saw him in Diagon Alley, someone saw him at the Ministry, someone saw him in *Peru*. My dad says—" she stopped, swallowed, "—my dad says there's a ward on the fountain in the Ministry Atrium that he put up in 2002, for a bet, and seven curse-breakers from Gringotts spent a whole weekend trying to take it down and couldn't, and nobody's been able to take it down since, and nobody knows what it *does*. The Aurors train on a shield he made. They call it the Potter Variant. He's supposed to have hated that. Supposedly he fought off three of the old Death Eaters in Knockturn Alley by himself the year after the war, when they came for him, and there wasn't enough left of the last one to put on trial."

The river roared. The rain came down.

"And they're *licensed*," Layla said.

"Licensed for what?" said Jake.

"The Unforgivables." She said it quietly. "There are three curses. They're the worst ones there are. Using one on a person is a life sentence in Azkaban—that's prison—that's an island in the North Sea, it used to be guarded by things that ate your soul. The Imperius Curse. It's—" she groped for it, "—mind control. Total. You'd do anything. Walk off a cliff. Kill your mum. And you'd be *happy* about it. The Cruciatus. That's torture. Just pain, pure pain, as much as the caster wants, for as long as they want; people go mad from it. And the Killing Curse." She looked at the bootprints across the gorge. "Green light. No mark. No counter. No shield blocks it. You just die. Nobody survives it. Nobody has *ever* survived it."

"Except," Seth said slowly.

"Except him," said Layla. "Twice."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Unspeakables can use all three," Layla said. "Legally. On anyone. It's in their charter or their—whatever they have. Everybody knows it. Nobody talks about it. The Aurors had it for a while, in the first war, and it went badly, and they lost it. The Department of Mysteries never lost it." She wiped rain out of her eyes with her sleeve. "That man could have done anything to us. Anything he liked. He could have walked into my head and made me jump into that river. He could have—" Her voice went high and thin. "And I *attacked* him. I'm of age. I'm seventeen. That's two curses at a Ministry official. One of them lethal. In front of witnesses."

"We're not going to tell anyone," said Seth.

"*He* knows, Seth! He's the one who matters! He could have me in Azkaban by tonight! He could have my wand snapped in front of the whole Wizengamot and my dad sacked and my name in the *Prophet*, and he knows my *name*. He knows my dad's *desk*." She was crying now, properly, furious with it. "Third from the window. How does he know that? How does he know *that*?"

"He was in your head," said Leah.

Layla turned and looked at her.

"He was in all our heads. The whole time." Leah's voice was flat. She had her arms crossed tight over her chest, and Seth could see the gooseflesh on them, which he'd never seen on Leah since she phased. "He was answering stuff I didn't say. He knew Jake was coming before I said anything. He knew how far away Jake was. He heard Seth think about you and he went into Seth's head and he *looked*, and I felt him do it, it was like—" Her jaw worked. "I don't know what it was like. Like somebody going through your drawers. He found you in there in about one second. Your name and the beach and the birds and the whole thing. I bet he got your dad's desk out of you while you were still trying to hit him."

Layla had gone white again.

"Legilimency," she said.

"Is that what it's called."

"It's—you can read thoughts. Memories. If you're good enough. Most people need a wand and eye contact and a lot of concentration, and it still only gets you bits and pieces." She looked at the empty bank. "He did it across a river. To three people at once. While he was holding a *vampire*. While he was catching my *curse*."

"Like Edward," said Seth.

"Edward can't help it," said Leah. "That guy can."

---

Jake had been quiet for a long time. Seth knew the look. It was the one he got when he was being alpha, which he hated, and was good at, which he also hated.

"What does he want?" Jake said.

"He said," Seth said, "national security."

"Whose nation?"

"Not yours," said Layla.

Jake looked at her.

"He's British," she said. "He's ours. He's from London, he works for the British Ministry of Magic, he shouldn't even be *here*. There's an American Ministry, MACUSA, it's in New York, it's got jurisdiction over—over all of this." She waved a hand at the gorge, the forest, the whole peninsula. "If he's here without telling them, that's—I don't even know what that is. That's an international incident. But he had a Muggle badge. An American one. He's set up a whole cover. He's not visiting." She looked across the gorge, at the bootprints, at the hemlock limb the vampire had hung from. "And he caught a vampire in a trap that wasn't there. That means he built a trap. He's been building traps. He's been out here building traps in your woods, for—"

"Ten days," said Leah.

They all looked at her.

"I smelled him," she said. "That guy. That's the guy who's been walking every ridge from here to the Hoh since the eleventh. Gun oil and coffee and wet wool and—whatever that other thing is. The cold-rock thing. I told you, Jake. I *told* you no hunter walks like that. He's been out here drawing lines on the whole peninsula like he's laying down a fence, and none of us thought twice."

"You thought twice," said Jake.

"Yeah, and nobody listened to me."

"What's he catching them *for*?" Seth said. "He said he wasn't going to kill it. He said 'not today.' And he called it by a name. He called it *Fabio*. Like it was a—like it was a pet."

"Like a specimen," said Layla.

Seth felt that one go down his back like cold water.

"That's what they do," Layla said, very quietly. "That's what the Department *does*. They find things nobody understands and they take them apart until they do." She looked down at her cut palm, and the broken glass in it. "And he said—when he was talking to me—he said 'think about how many people you've explained it to lately. And what they all are.' And then he looked toward the Cullens'."

Jake's head came up.

"He knows about them," Seth said. "He has to. He asked Charlie about them. On Monday. He—" He stopped. "He said *Edward*. When I thought it. Like he was writing it down."

"He's been watching them," Leah said. "And he's got a trap out there that caught a red-eye and didn't catch any of the Cullens, and the Cullens have been running around in those woods every night for ten days. Jake. You understand what that means? He built it so it *wouldn't* catch them. He knows exactly who they are. He's been sitting out there in the rain with a list."

"So he's not here for them," said Seth.

"*Yet*," said Layla and Leah together, and then looked at each other, startled.

Jake looked east, toward the Cullens' house, toward Nessie. Seth watched him do it, and knew what he was thinking without needing to phase. There was a man out in these woods who could walk into your head across a river and get your girlfriend's dad's desk out of it, who could catch a curse in his bare hand, who collected vampires like butterflies. And about six miles east, in a glass house by a river, there was a little girl who looked two and talked like she was five and was, as far as anybody knew, the only one of her kind in the whole world.

Seth knew exactly how a person who collected things would look at that.

"We're going to the Cullens'," Jake said. "Now. All of us."

"I'm in my pajamas," Layla said faintly.

"You can borrow something of Alice's. Alice'll love it. She's been dying to dress you since you showed up." Jake was already moving. "Leah, run ahead. Tell Edward everything, he'll get it faster out of your head than mine. Seth, you bring Layla. Not phased. Carry her if you have to, it's six miles and she's got no shoes."

"I can Apparate," said Layla.

"Can you Apparate to somewhere you've been once?"

Layla opened her mouth, and closed it. "Probably," she said. "Mostly. All of me would *probably* arrive."

"Seth's carrying you," said Jake.

Leah was already gone, a gray blur into the hemlocks, the T-shirt dress tied to her leg again. Jake went after her a second later. Seth heard them both phase, the double crack of it off through the trees, and then the long rhythm of their running fading east.

He turned around. Layla was standing by the edge of the gorge with her wand in one hand and the broken sea glass in the other, looking across at the far bank, at the two bootprints filling slowly with rain.

"He said it was clever," she said, without turning around.

"What?"

"The pendant. The anchor. He said it wasn't in any syllabus. He asked who taught me." She was quiet for a moment. "Harry Potter said my spell was clever, Seth. And then he told me he could have my wand snapped." She laughed, a small wet sound. "I don't know whether to be sick or frame it."

Seth came up behind her and put his arms around her and his chin on the top of her wet head, and held on, and looked at the bootprints with her.

"Be sick later," he said. "I'll help you frame it."

Then he picked her up—she squawked, and then didn't argue—and turned east, and ran.

### VIII. Specimen One

**Wednesday, November 22, 2006 — Field installation, Bogachiel drainage — 2:58 p.m. PST**

*Potter, H. — Same room.*

---

There was a lurch, and then there was a pipe.

Henrik Aasland had been dead for a hundred and twelve years, and he had been many things in that time—hungry, mostly, and bored, and occasionally afraid—but he had never once been *squeezed*. That was the only word for it. Every part of him at once, from every direction, as if the world had become a tube a great deal narrower than he was and someone had pushed him through it. He could not breathe, which did not matter, and could not move, which did, and for one endless instant there was nothing but black and pressure and a man's arm around his middle like an iron bar.

Then there was light, and concrete, and a smell of chemicals.

He hung under the man's arm like a plank, rigid from his locked ankle to the crown of his head, and the man carried him across a cold bright room past shelves of jars, and Henrik's eyes—which he *could* move—rolled and darted and took in everything at once, the way his kind took in everything.

His kind thought on many floors at the same time. That was the gift of the venom, the one nobody ever spoke of because to them it was simply how a mind worked: a house with a great many rooms and a light on in every one. In one room, Henrik was raging. In another, he was counting the shelves—six, steel, bolted to the wall. In a third, he was reading the labels on the jars and understanding none of them. *LETHIFOLD — DO NOT OPEN. NUNDU (whisker). INFERIUS (L. hand ×3) — still twitches.* A severed hand floated in yellowish liquid and curled its fingers as he passed. Against the far wall, a heavy oak wardrobe rattled on its feet as though something inside it had heard them come in. In a fourth room, Henrik was listening to the man's heart.

It was slow. Slower than any human heart he had ever heard, deep and heavy and very even, like a big drum played by someone in no hurry at all. And the blood behind it smelled wrong. Henrik had smelled ten thousand humans. This man had the copper and salt of all of them, and the sweetness that made venom pool in Henrik's frozen mouth, and under that something else—iron left in a forge, the air after a lightning strike. It smelled like a meal that would burn going down.

In a fifth room, very quietly, Henrik understood that he was not going to leave this place.

The man set him on his feet inside a box of glass. Henrik saw it close around him—eight feet on a side, the walls so clear he could only tell they were there by the shine of the overhead lights in them—and saw the man step back out through one wall as if it were water, and saw the wall be a wall again behind him.

The man looked at him through the glass. Then he moved one finger.

Every joint in Henrik's body unlocked at once.

---

He hit the glass before his feet had finished landing.

He hit it with all of a hundred and twelve years of strength behind his fist and his shoulder and his hip—the punch that had once gone through the engine block of a Ford in Reno. A punch that broke stone.

The glass rang. It rang like a bell, a deep pure note that went up into the bones of his arm and his teeth and hung in the air, and it did nothing else at all. No crack. No mark. His fist bounced off it like a child's off a door.

He hit it again. And again. Left, right, a kick that would have snapped a telephone pole, his whole weight thrown at it from across the cell. The glass rang and rang until the room was singing with overlapping notes, and did not so much as shiver in its frame.

On the far side of it, the man had sat down on the edge of a steel table and taken an apple out of his coat pocket.

Beside him, propped against a rack of instruments, there was a slate—a school slate, like the one Henrik had learned his letters on in Stavanger in 1877—and every time Henrik hit the glass, white numbers wrote themselves across it in a clean slanting hand. *3.8. 4.1. 3.9. 4.4. 4.0.*

"Four point four," said the man, with his mouth full. "That's the kick. Go again."

Henrik went again, for two full minutes by the clock on the wall, until the slate had filled and started over at the top. Then he stopped, because in one of the quieter rooms of his mind he had finally been heard. *Every blow is a measurement. Stop giving him numbers.*

He stood in the middle of the cell with his hands at his sides.

The man held up the apple core and considered it, and then it was gone—not thrown, just gone—and he brushed his fingers off on his trouser leg.

"Thank you," he said. "Very useful. About four tons in the right hand, three and a half in the left. Bit more in a kick. Bit less when you're upset." He tipped his head. "You're quite upset."

Henrik bared his teeth. "Do you know what I am?"

"Roughly," said the man. "That's what we're here to sort out."

---

He had a little machine, a black plastic Japanese thing with a tiny cassette in it, the kind businessmen used for dictating letters. He clicked it on and set it on the table and began to talk.

"Specimen one. Taken 2:51 p.m., twenty-second of November, line nine, Calawah gorge, while being chased by two of the local shapeshifters, the grey female and the sandy male. Adult male. Apparent age thirty. Six foot one. Irises deep red, consistent with a human diet. Name—"

Something touched Henrik's mind.

It was light at first. A pressure, like a hand laid flat against a door. And then it was in, and it was in *all* the rooms. Henrik had never felt anything like it. Every light in the house had been on all along, and someone had walked into every room at once, unhurried, and begun to look around.

He tried to hide. His kind could; you thought about one thing very loudly and put the rest of yourself behind it like a man behind a curtain. He picked a memory—a summer evening in Monterey, a girl in a yellow dress—and shouted it.

The man walked straight past it. Henrik felt him look at the girl in the yellow dress just long enough to recognize what she was for, and step around her into the room behind.

"—Henrik Aasland," the man said into the machine. "Born Stavanger, Norway, 1871. Merchant sailor. Turned 1894 in Valparaíso. Nomadic since. Pacific coast, mostly, Chile to Alaska and back. Two trips to Europe. One"—a pause, and Henrik felt the hand inside him turn something over like a stone—"to Volterra. 1957. Oh, there we are."

The great hall, with the light coming down through high round windows onto the stone. The three thrones. The pale old faces. The guard standing around the walls in their cloaks like statues. Henrik had gone because a nomad in Lisbon had said it was a thing you did once, if you were a hundred or so and had a question you wanted answered. And Aro had come down off his throne with his robes whispering on the stone and taken Henrik's hand in both of his, and smiled, and read him. All of him. Every room.

Henrik had forgotten what that felt like. It had felt a great deal like this.

"The hand," the man murmured. "Any skin. One touch." Then, into the machine: "Specimen corroborates the receptionist on the primary's gift. Contact-based. Total recall of the subject's mind. Guard members—" and Henrik felt them drawn out of him one by one like cards from a deck: a huge dark man with a shaved head; a slender one with brown hair whose eyes slid over you as if he were memorizing the way you walked; a girl, a child, blond, with a face like an angel in a church window, who had looked at Henrik across the hall for two seconds and made him want to crawl out of his own skin. "—confirmed. The big one. The tracker. The little blonde one." He said the last almost gently. "Christ. She can't be more than twelve."

He found the rules next, and listed them as he found them, like a man reading a shopping list. Then the hand turned over the morning.

Henrik had been trying not to think about the morning. So of course he thought about it.

The camp at the end of a logging road up the Sol Duc, in the gray before dawn. A canvas wall tent with a stovepipe. Two men inside in their long underwear, drinking coffee from a tin pot, getting ready to go out after elk. The big one with the beard was called Ray. The older one was Don. They'd been arguing about whether Don's truck would make it back down if it kept raining, and laughing. Henrik had stood in the wet salal twenty feet from the tent wall and listened to them laugh for a long time, because he'd been on the road from Oregon for three days and he was in no hurry.

He felt the man look at what came after. And at what Henrik had done with them afterward: half a mile down the creek, the cutbank where the water had undercut the roots of a big fir, the ferns pulled down over the top.

The hand came out of his head fast, the way you take your hand off a stove. Henrik staggered. He hadn't known he could.

The man sat very still on the edge of the table for a while. He didn't look at Henrik. Then he took a small white card from his coat and a pen from another pocket and wrote, slowly, in small capital letters. Henrik could read it upside down through the glass.

*TWO MEN. RAY & DON. FROM SHELTON, WA. HUNTING CAMP, END OF FOREST RD OFF SOL DUC, ~4 MI ABOVE HOT SPRINGS. GREEN WALL TENT, BLUE FORD F-250. ½ MI DOWNSTREAM OF CAMP, E BANK, UNDER CUTBANK BENEATH LARGE FIR.*

He looked at it for a long moment, and put it in his breast pocket.

"What do you care?" Henrik said. His voice came out strange, too high. "They were going to die anyway. In twenty years. In forty—"

"They've got wives, Fabio," said the man. "Ray's got a little girl. She's six. She drew him an elk on the back of a church bulletin and he had it in his shirt pocket." He looked up through the glass, and his eyes were very green and very flat. "Somebody ought to bring them home. That's all."

He clicked the machine back on. "Specimen confirms two kills, approximately six forty-five this morning, upper Sol Duc. I'll pass it on to the county." His voice hadn't changed at all. "Moving on."

---

He put the lights out next, with a twitch of his hand, until the room was black. Then he said something under his breath, and a sun came up in his palm.

It was sunlight—Henrik knew sunlight, had known it every day for a hundred and twelve years as a thing to hide from—gold and pouring, noon on a clear day, and it came through the glass and hit Henrik full in the face and chest and arms, and his skin did what it always did. It caught the light and broke it. A million tiny points of fire, every color there was, danced across the walls of the cell and the concrete floor and the shelves of jars, the whole room suddenly alive like the inside of a jewel.

There was a pause.

"Oh," said the man, "you have *got* to be joking."

He started laughing.

He laughed for quite a long time. He sat on the edge of the table with a sun in his hand and his head down and his shoulders shaking, and every time he looked up at Henrik glittering in the box he started again. "No," he said. "*No.* That's what he was going to do? In the *square*? Romeo was going to walk out into the piazza at noon in front of three thousand people and—*sparkle* at them?" He wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist. "Oh, that's the best thing that's ever happened to me. That's going in the report. Croaker's going to have a stroke."

He clicked the recorder. "Specimen refracts. Under direct sunlight, or a decent Lumos Solem, the skin produces a prismatic effect. Like cut crystal." A pause. "Like a disco ball. No apparent damage. No apparent discomfort. Entirely cosmetic." He shook his head. "Sweet Merlin."

The sun went out of his hand. The overhead lights came back on.

Henrik stood in the middle of the cell with the last sparks fading off his skin, and in every room of his mind at once he was more humiliated than he had been in a century.

---

The man worked through a few more items on a list in a notebook after that. Henrik would remember that none of it was hurried and none of it was slow. He tried a Stunner through the glass, and Henrik lost six seconds he would never get back, and woke on the floor of the cell to find the man looking at the clock and writing *6 s — stack three or four* in the margin. He measured things. He wrote things down. He spoke into the little machine in the same level voice about all of it.

And then, at twenty past four, he closed the notebook and went to a mirror.

It was propped against a workbench in the next room, through an open door, and Henrik could see a slice of it from where he stood. The man said a name to it. The glass clouded and cleared, and on the other side was an old man with a long gray face and half-moon spectacles in a room full of clocks.

"Potter," said the old man.

Henrik had never heard the name. He put it in a room by itself and locked the door.

"Specimen one's in the box, Saul," the man said. "Nomad. Norwegian. A hundred and twelve years old and a right treat. Four tons a punch, there's a full set of numbers. Stunner gives you about six seconds per hit, so we'll want to stack them. His Volterra memories match the receptionist's on the old one's gift and on the guard. And they *glitter*, Saul. In sunlight. Like a ballroom."

The old man in the mirror looked at him over his spectacles for a long moment.

"I beg your pardon?"

"I'll send you the tape. You'll want to sit down for it." The man rubbed his face. "That's the good news."

"And the bad."

"The wolves know about us."

The ticking of the clocks came through the mirror into the room, a dozen of them, faintly out of step.

"How," said the old man.

"Seventeen-year-old witch. Layla Ashby. Moved out here from Exeter with her mum in August. Martin Ashby's girl—Improper Use of Magic, third desk from the window. One of the shapeshifters imprinted on her, that's what they call it, some sort of soul-bond, and she told him about us because she's seventeen and he's lovely. Then he and his pack walked her into the Cullens' living room and she conjured birds for the baby." The man paused. "And when I picked her out of his head about an hour ago, she Apparated in on an anchor charm she'd built herself out of a piece of beach glass and put a Stunner and a Blasting Curse at me in four seconds."

"Is she in custody?"

"She's in her pajamas in the middle of the woods, scared out of her mind. I left her there."

"Do you want her handled?"

"No." The man said it quickly. "No. I want her left alone. I put the fear of God in her, she'll keep her head down. She's a good kid, Saul. Built an Apparition anchor out of a Protean link at seventeen. Nobody taught her. Somebody at the Department ought to have her name on a list for about five years from now."

"Noted," said the old man dryly. "And the Statute?"

"Shapeshifters and vampires. I don't think the Statute was ever written with them in mind. I'll argue it in front of whoever you like." The man leaned forward. "The problem is the Cullens. They'll have heard by now. They've got a mind-reader in the house, and the wolves will have run straight there. If they spook, they'll run, and we lose the doctor and everything he knows about that castle."

"Category Two, Potter."

"Hands off. I know." The man stood up. "So I'm going to go and knock on their door, before they decide I'm the sort of man you run from."

"You *are* the sort of man you run from."

"That's why I'm knocking." Harry Potter picked his overcoat up off the back of the stool. "I'll report in the morning."

"Potter," said the old man.

The man stopped.

"Bring the doctor something. Manners are cheap."

The glass clouded over.

---

The man came back into the room with the cell, pulling on his coat. He stopped at the plinth under the glass and slid the little card out of its brass holder—SPECIMEN 1 (PENDING)—and took a fresh one from his pocket and wrote on it and slid it back in.

SPECIMEN 1 — H. AASLAND.

He looked up at Henrik through the glass.

"Back in a bit," he said. "Don't go anywhere."

Henrik said nothing.

The man smiled with one corner of his mouth, touched the breast pocket where the white card was, and walked out. The lights dimmed behind him. The wardrobe against the far wall rattled once and went still.

In one room of his mind, Henrik began, very carefully, to look for the edges of the glass. In every other room, he sat in the dark and listened to the hand in the jar curl its fingers, and to the clock on the wall, and to the long silence of a place a very long way from anywhere.

### IX. Manners Are Cheap

**Wednesday, November 22, 2006 — The Cullen residence — 4:44 p.m. PST**

*Potter, H. — In transit. Bottom of the Cullens' drive, on foot, carrying a cardboard box.*

---

It had been dark for fifteen minutes, and Bella had not breathed for nearly an hour.

She didn't need to. That was one of the strange gifts of the last ten weeks: the discovery that breathing was a habit, like biting your nails, and you could stop. She'd stopped when Seth came through the front door with Layla in his arms, barefoot and soaked and white to the lips, and she hadn't started again, because Layla was sitting by the fire now in a borrowed cashmere sweater of Alice's with her bandaged hand wrapped around a mug of Esme's tea, and her heart was still going like a rabbit's, and every beat of it pushed her scent across the room in a fresh wave of something like sugar and rain and struck matches.

Bella held Renesmee on her hip and held her breath and listened to her family argue.

"We go to Denali," Rosalie was saying. "Tonight. We're packed in an hour. Tanya will take us."

"And then what?" said Emmett. "He followed a newborn army's worth of rumor all the way from *London*, Rose. You think he can't find Alaska?"

"I think it'll take him longer."

"He built a trap the size of the *peninsula*," said Jasper. He was standing by the glass wall with his arms folded, and he hadn't sat down since Leah had come through the door an hour and twenty minutes ago and Edward had taken everything out of her head in the space of a breath. "It runs from Lake Crescent to the Hoh. Leah smelled him on every ridge. It took a hundred-year-old nomad out of a dead sprint in mid-air and hung it up like a deer. And it let us pass." He looked at Carlisle. "Every night for ten days, we've been hunting through the middle of it. It let us pass every time. It knows what we smell like."

"Which means he does," said Edward quietly.

"Which means he's had ten days to take any one of us he wanted," said Jasper, "and hasn't."

Carlisle was standing by the fireplace with one hand on the mantel. He'd been quiet for most of it. He'd cleaned Layla's palm and closed it with three neat butterfly strips without seeming to think about it, the way he did everything with his hands, and since then he'd been listening.

"If he wanted us," he said, "we'd know by now."

"*Yet*," said Leah, from the window.

She hadn't moved from it. She stood with her arms crossed over Alice's spare raincoat, which she'd put on over her T-shirt dress with an expression like she was being forced to wear a dead animal, and she was looking out at the dark trees and the drive.

"He said *Edward*," she said, without turning around. "When Seth thought it. He said it like he was writing it down."

"I know," said Edward.

"And he said Lassie," said Leah. "I'm going to kill him."

Bella shifted Renesmee higher on her hip. Her daughter was watching all of it with enormous solemn eyes. She'd been very quiet since Seth and Layla came in. Once, she'd reached out and put her hand on Bella's cheek, and shown her the picture Edward had pulled out of Leah's head and said aloud—a big man on a riverbank, a vampire under his arm like a surfboard—and then a feeling with it. A question. *Who?*

*I don't know, baby,* Bella had thought, uselessly, since only Edward could hear her when her shield was down and her shield was never down. *I don't know.*

She thought about Charlie.

Charlie on the phone on Monday, his voice careful the way it got when he was saying something important while pretending it wasn't. *Some guy from Fish and Wildlife. English, big, black hair, green eyes. Nice suit. Might come by the house.* Charlie, who had sat across a diner table from this man. Who had no idea.

She'd checked, after. She'd called the Fish and Wildlife office in Portland on Tuesday morning in her most bored human voice and asked for Special Agent Evans, and a pleasant woman had put her on hold for a minute and come back and said, *I'm sorry, Agent Evans is in the field this week, can I take a message?*

He was real. He was in their system. Bella still didn't know whether that was better or worse.

"Alice," Carlisle said. "Anything?"

Alice was sitting on the stairs with her chin on her knees and her eyes closed. She opened them and made a face of pure frustration.

"It's the wolves," she said. "It's *always* the wolves. There are three of them in this room. I can see—pieces. I sent Seth and Jake outside for five minutes an hour ago and got almost something." She pressed her fingertips into her temples. "A black car. A tent in the woods, a *small* tent, but it's—it's wrong, it's bigger inside than—I don't know. And a man walking up our drive in the rain. That's all. Then Seth came back in and it all went to static."

"When?" said Jasper.

"I don't *know*. That's the problem. Whenever I get him, it's like—he decides, and I see it, and then he's already *done* it. There's no gap. Most people think about things. He just goes."

"He might come here," said Layla.

Everyone looked at her. She went pink and held on to her mug.

"He said," she said. "At the gorge. He told me to think about how many people I'd explained the Statute to, and what they were. And then he looked this way. And Seth said he'd already asked Chief Swan about you, so he knows where you live." She swallowed. "And he called it a national security operation. My dad would have a word for how someone like that thinks about you."

"What word?" said Rosalie.

"Assets," said Layla.

Nobody liked that. Bella could feel the room not liking it.

Jacob, who had been sitting on the end of the sofa with his elbows on his knees and his eyes on Renesmee the whole time, looked up at Bella. He didn't have to say it. *If he comes near her—*

*I know,* she thought at him, and nodded.

"We don't run," Carlisle said finally. "Not yet. If we run, we tell him we're afraid of him, and then we're something to be chased. I'd rather know what he wants."

"He *told* us what he wants," said Leah. "He wants leeches in boxes."

"He wants *a* leech in a box," said Jasper. "He built it so it wouldn't catch us. That's the part I keep—"

Alice's head snapped up.

"He's here," she said.

---

Every vampire in the room was on their feet. Bella hadn't seen most of them move.

"Where?" said Emmett.

"The drive. Halfway up. Walking. He decided at the bottom—I *saw* it, just now, I saw him decide, he's carrying a—" She stopped. Her face went blank with confusion. "A box?"

"Edward," said Carlisle.

Edward had gone very still beside the piano, with his head tilted toward the front of the house. His eyes had gone unfocused.

"I can't hear him," he said.

"What do you mean you can't hear him?" said Jacob.

"I mean I *can't hear* him. There's a heartbeat coming up the drive—slow, very slow—and there's nothing behind it. Nothing. It's like Bella." He frowned. "No. Wait. There's—"

His face did something Bella had never seen it do in all the time she'd known him. It went slack with disbelief.

"He's *singing*," Edward said.

"He's what?"

"In his head. On purpose. Loudly. It's—" Edward listened. "Drums. A guitar. Somebody screaming about a hearse."

Layla put her mug down very carefully on the side table and stood up, and Seth stood with her.

Footsteps came up the porch steps. Unhurried. Bella heard every one of them: heavy, a big man, boots on wet wood. Then a pause at the top. Then three knocks on the front door. Measured. Polite.

Nobody moved.

"Well," said Esme, after a moment, in a slightly strangled voice, "*somebody* had better answer it."

Carlisle went.

---

He opened the door, and the man on the porch looked past him into the house, and Bella got her first look at him.

He was big—as tall as Edward and much broader, heavy in the shoulders and chest in a way the black overcoat didn't disguise. The rain had soaked his black hair flat and was running off it into his collar, and he didn't seem to have noticed. Suit underneath, charcoal, white shirt open at the throat. Mud on his boots to the ankle. His face was younger than she'd expected and older than it looked. And his eyes, when they went across the room, were very green, and they touched each of them in turn—Carlisle, Esme, Emmett, Rosalie, Jasper, Alice on the stairs, Edward by the piano, Jacob, Seth, Layla, Leah at the window, Bella, and Renesmee on her hip—and moved on, and Bella had the distinct impression of being counted.

He was holding a white cardboard box tied with string, the kind bakeries used. Rain had spotted the top of it.

"Dr. Cullen," he said. English, low, clear. "Evening. Sorry to turn up unannounced. My name's Harry Potter. I work for the British Ministry of Magic, Department of Mysteries." He held the box up an inch. "I brought pie."

Carlisle looked at the box.

"Marionberry," said Harry Potter. "From the diner. I'm told you lot don't eat, so it's for the wolves, mostly. And Miss Ashby." His eyes went past Carlisle to Layla, who had gone rigid. "Breathe, Miss Ashby. I'm not here for your wand."

"Tonight?" said Layla. It came out a whisper.

"Tonight," he agreed. "How's the hand?"

Layla looked down at her bandaged palm and back up at him, and didn't answer.

His eyes moved to Edward. Edward was staring at him from beside the piano with his jaw tight.

"You'll get the whole album if you keep trying," Harry Potter said. "It's *Back in Black*. Bit on the nose, I know. I had it on the way up the drive and it seemed to fit." He considered Edward for a moment, head on one side, and something came into his face that Bella would, later, have to admit was pure delight. "Romeo."

Edward stiffened.

"Shirt off," said Harry Potter. "Clock tower. Piazza dei Priori, noon on St. Marcus Day, in front of three thousand people in red cloaks." He shook his head slowly. "Mate, you set off an alarm in London that hadn't rung since 1945. I've been working on you since March. You're the reason I've got a job this year." He paused. "I'd shake your hand, but I've seen what happens when your lot take hands."

Emmett made a sound like a balloon losing air. Rosalie's elbow went into his ribs hard enough to crack a human's. It didn't stop him.

Edward said nothing at all. Bella could feel the mortification coming off him in waves, and under it, something colder, the thing he'd felt in Volterra, and she moved half a step closer to him without thinking about it.

Harry Potter's eyes came to her.

He looked at her for longer than the others. His head tilted a fraction further. Then he frowned—a small, puzzled frown, like a man trying a key that ought to fit a lock.

"Huh," he said. "You're blank."

Bella said nothing.

"Can't get a thing off you. Not a flicker." He sounded interested. "That's new. I've not run into that before." He looked at her a moment longer. "Mrs. Cullen. Congratulations on the wedding. Your dad's a good man. He didn't tell me a single thing on Monday, and he tried very hard not to think it, either. Tell him thanks for the company at lunch."

Something went through Bella's whole body like a current. She felt her lips come back from her teeth before she'd decided anything at all.

"If you go near my father again—"

"I've no reason to," he said. "He's not on any list. Nobody in Forks is, who isn't in this room." He said it simply, like a fact about the weather, and she believed him, and hated that she did.

By the glass wall, too low for any human ear to catch, Jasper breathed to Carlisle: "He's calm. All the way down. He's already worked out how he'd do every one of us. In order."

"Had a lot of practice, mate," said Harry Potter, without looking at him.

The room went silent.

Jasper's head turned, very slowly.

Bella saw Edward's eyes widen. She saw Alice put a hand over her mouth on the stairs. Jasper had spoken at a pitch none of them would have used if there had been a human within a hundred yards, a breath with words in it, and the man on the porch had answered it as if it had been said across a dinner table.

Harry Potter shifted the pie box to his other hand.

"Right," he said. "Before anyone does anything athletic. Short version." He looked at Carlisle. "Nobody in this house is a target. My government has you flagged as protected—the eight of you, by name and by smell. That's why my lines let you through. Which, as of about two o'clock this afternoon, ought to be the most comforting sentence anybody's said to you all year."

"Where's the one you took?" said Rosalie. Her voice was flat and hard.

"In a box. Alive, in the loose sense." He met her eyes. "He came down the coast from Oregon three days ago, and at quarter to seven this morning he walked into a hunting camp up the Sol Duc and killed two men from Shelton who were getting ready to go out after elk. Ray and Don. Then he hid them under a cutbank and covered them with ferns." He touched the breast pocket of his coat. "In about an hour, the Clallam County Sheriff's office is going to get an anonymous phone call telling them exactly where. That's what I want with him, if you're asking. I want to know everything he is. And I want two families in Shelton to get somebody back to bury."

Carlisle's face changed. Bella saw it—a small, deep shift, like something heavy setting down.

"And the child?" said Jacob.

He'd stood up. He was between the door and Bella, and he was very close to phasing; she could see the tremor running under his skin. Seth was beside Layla with his hand on her back. Leah had turned all the way around from the window.

Harry Potter looked at Renesmee.

He looked at her for a long time. Long enough that Bella's arm tightened around her daughter, and Edward came all the way to her side, and Jacob took one step forward.

Then Renesmee leaned out from Bella's hip, toward the door, and held out her hand.

"Ness—" said Jacob.

"She can come to me or not," said Harry Potter, quietly. He didn't move. "Up to you, Mum."

Bella looked at her daughter. Renesmee looked back, steady, curious, entirely unafraid, the way she looked at everything. *Who?* she'd asked.

Bella stepped forward, slowly, with Edward at her shoulder, until she was just inside the door, an arm's length from the man on the porch. He smelled of rain and gun oil and coffee, and under that of cold stone and pine pitch and something like a struck match, and his blood under all of it smelled hot and strange, and Bella didn't breathe.

Renesmee reached out and put her fingers on the back of his hand.

He blinked.

It was a very small reaction. Bella almost missed it. His eyes widened a fraction, and went unfocused, and came back, and he looked down at the little hand on his with an expression she couldn't read at all.

"Well," he said, after a second, "that's a good trick."

"What did she show you?" said Edward sharply.

"Herself. And him." He tipped his head at Jacob. "And her mum. And the pie." The corner of his mouth moved. "And a question." He looked at Renesmee. "Yes. You can have some pie."

Renesmee smiled at him and took her hand back.

"She went straight through," he said to Bella, low. "Like there was nothing there. I've spent a lot of years making sure there's something there." He looked at the child a moment longer. "I haven't written her down anywhere, Mrs. Cullen. I'd like to keep it that way. I'd suggest you help me."

Bella didn't answer. She didn't trust her voice. She stepped back, and Edward stepped back with her, and Jacob let out a breath that seemed to go on for a long time.

"Lassie," said Harry Potter, glancing past him toward the window. "I'd stop working out the angle. There isn't one."

"Gianna." He said it lightly. "Lovely girl. Front desk. Terrible taste in employers. She's got a whole drawer of gossip about you, Doctor. The Englishman who wouldn't eat. They still talk about you up there, apparently, three hundred years on. The old one with the hair talks about you like a son who went off to the colonies and never writes."

Esme's hand found Carlisle's.

Harry Potter reached into the inside of his coat with his free hand—slowly, deliberately, keeping his eyes on Jasper the whole time, and Bella saw the flash of a holster under his left arm as the coat moved—and took out a photograph. Glossy, eight by ten, a little damp at the corner. He held it up.

It was a long-lens shot, taken from high up, from a rooftop or a tower, looking down into a stone courtyard in bright Italian sunlight. In the shade of a colonnade at the edge of it, a man in a dark robe stood with his hands clasped in front of him, his face turned up toward the sky, as if he were enjoying the weather. Long black hair. Skin like paper. A smile.

Bella heard Carlisle stop breathing.

"My government has a problem with your old friends," said Harry Potter. "They've been bringing tourists up from the square and eating them in the cellar for about two thousand years, and we've only just noticed. Thirty-one of ours in the last twelve. A family called Lowe—mum, dad, two boys. A kid named Fitzwilliam on his gap year." He looked at the photograph, and then at Carlisle. "I'd like to talk about them. With someone who's been inside. I'd like to do it tonight, here, politely, with pie, before anybody in this valley does something that makes it harder." He paused. "You can invite me in, or I can stand out here on your porch, and Mrs. Cullen's—the other Mrs. Cullen's—lovely porch is going to get very wet."

Carlisle looked at the photograph for a long time.

Then he looked at Esme, and at Edward, and at Jasper, who very slightly—so slightly no human would ever have seen it—inclined his head. And at Bella, with her daughter on her hip. And at Jacob, who had his jaw set and his hands shaking and who, after a long moment, looked at Renesmee and then nodded once.

Carlisle stepped back from the door.

"Come in, Mr. Potter," he said. "Please. Sit down."

"Cheers." Harry Potter stepped over the threshold, and the rain came in with him, dripping off the hem of his coat onto Esme's floor. He held the pie box out to Seth, who took it automatically, and looked around the enormous white room, at the glass wall and the dark river beyond it and the piano and the fire. "Nice place. Very bright. Got any coffee? No? Course you don't. What am I saying—"

"I have coffee," said Esme faintly.

He turned and looked at her.

"I keep it for Charlie," she said.

"Marry me," said Harry Potter.

Esme laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, startled and a little hysterical, and Bella saw Carlisle's mouth twitch in spite of everything, and on the stairs Alice made a sound like a hiccup.

Esme went to make the coffee.

Harry Potter sat down on the white sofa in his wet black coat, which made Esme, halfway to the kitchen, wince visibly. He laid the photograph of Aro face-up on the glass coffee table in front of him, squared it neatly with the edge, and sat back, and looked up at the ring of vampires and wolves and one shaking witch standing around him, every one of whom could have killed a man in less time than it took to blink.

He didn't look worried at all.

"Right then," he said. "Let's talk about Italy."
