# North of Everything

## Chapter Five: Walkies

---

Seth woke up because somebody was humming.

It came to him slowly, the way things did when he'd been asleep hard. First the couch, which was too short and had been too short since he was thirteen, so his feet were hanging off the arm at one end into cold air. Then the smell of coffee, which meant his mom was up. Then the gray light, barely any of it, just enough to make the shapes of things in the living room—the TV, the woodstove, the Christmas cards strung on a ribbon across the window. Then the humming.

It was low. Really low. Lower than anybody's voice had any business going, down in the range where Seth felt it in the couch springs as much as heard it. It was slow, and it went up and down in a pattern he didn't know, sort of sad and sort of not, like something you'd sing to a kid in a language you didn't speak anymore.

Seth opened his eyes.

Harry was sitting at the other end of the couch.

He was sitting on it. Seth's first thought, still half asleep, was *that's gonna break.* The couch was older than he was. His dad had bought it at a yard sale in Sequim the year Leah was born, and one of the legs was a stack of paperbacks, and when Paul sat on it the middle cushion made a noise like a dying goose. Harry had sat on the floor all last night to stay off it.

Now he was on it, at the far end, with his back in the corner and one long leg stretched out along the cushions toward Seth and the other footpaw on the floor. And the couch wasn't making a sound. It wasn't even sagging. It just sat there holding up a six-foot-seven wolf with an air of having been spoken to.

He had Layla in his lap.

She was lying on her back along his thigh, on a folded towel, with her head toward his knee and her tiny tail hanging off the other end. She was naked except for her fur. The little gray hat with the ear holes was on the arm of the couch, and the onesie with the tail hole was folded on top of it, and there was a diaper on the floor that had very clearly been changed, rolled up tight and taped shut, which Seth was grateful for.

And Harry was washing her.

He'd bent his head down over her, his ears tipped forward, and he was licking her face. Slowly. One long careful stroke of his tongue from the tip of her blunt little muzzle up between her eyes, along the pale gray stripe, to the top of her head between her folded ears. Then again. Then again, from the side, over one closed eye and along her cheek, smoothing the fur flat. The fur on one whole side of her was squashed and matted, the way Seth's hair got when he slept on it wet, and he was working on that side, a little at a time, against the grain and then with it, so the dark charcoal fur stood up and then lay down and the silver tips caught the gray light.

The whole time, he was humming.

Seth lay there and didn't move.

He'd seen it last night, in the kitchen, when Harry licked her ears. But that had been fast, a couple of seconds, while everybody was talking. This was different. This was the whole thing. He watched Harry finish the face and move to the ears. He did the left one first, the inside of it, very carefully, with just the tip of his tongue, and Layla's ear twitched and folded and unfolded, and then the outside of it, base to tip. Then the right one. Then he lifted her up off his thigh in one big forepaw, his claws curled back out of the way, and turned her over onto her stomach on the towel, and started on the back of her neck.

Layla made the sound.

The kettle sound. The low rumbling chirrup that came out of somewhere deep inside her, over and over, a little louder every time his tongue went down her back. Her tiny forepaws had spread out flat on the towel and her claws—they were so small, they were like grains of rice—were kneading at it, in and out. Her tail had started to curl and uncurl.

The humming didn't change. It went on under the purring, slow and low and patient, and Seth realized after a while that it was the same tune going around and around. Eight or nine notes. Up, and then a long slow step down, and then a turn at the bottom, and back up again.

"What's that song?" said Seth.

His voice came out in a croak. Harry didn't jump. His left ear rotated toward Seth, and that was all, and he finished the stroke he was on, down the length of Layla's spine to the base of her tail.

"Morning," he said. "Something Kreacher sang her."

"Who's Kreacher?"

"Our house-elf."

Seth thought about that. "You have a *house-elf*."

"Ja. He's about two hundred years old. He wears a tea towel. He packed that pie." Harry started on Layla's left hind leg, cleaning down the back of the little thigh to the paw. "That song's his family's. Well, my godfather's family's—Kreacher came with them. He used to sing it to my godfather's little brother. It's about the stars. The family's all named after stars." He licked between the tiny pads of her hind paw, one toe at a time, and Layla's paw spread and curled. "I don't know the words. I only heard it once. I've just got the tune."

"It's kind of sad."

"Ja, it's a bit sad. Most of those old ones are." He moved to the other hind leg. "She likes it, though."

She did. Seth could tell. She'd gone boneless on the towel, completely flat, purring so hard her whole tiny body was vibrating.

Seth sat up slowly. The blanket he'd been under slid off onto the floor. Somebody had put it on him in the night—the star quilt, his grandma's other one, the green and gold one off the back of his mom's chair. He didn't remember getting it.

"Did she sleep okay?" he said.

"On me," said Harry. "From about half two. Like a rock." He nodded down at the matted side of her fur. "That's my chest, that is. She sleeps with her face mashed into it. Comes out looking like she's been through a hedge."

"So that's why you're—" Seth waved a hand.

"Ja. Her mum's still asleep. Didn't want to wake her. Your mum's been up for an hour."

Seth looked toward the kitchen. The light was on in there, and the coffee smell was coming from there, and he could hear the radio, very low. Of course she'd been up for an hour. His mom had been up before six every morning of his life.

He looked back at Harry and the baby.

He wanted to ask about nine hundred more things. He'd gone to sleep with them all crowded up in his head and they were all still there. The werewolf. The fur ship. The humming by the lake, which he'd been trying not to think about since dinner, which had got into his dreams anyway. *Then who's humming?* Leah had said, gray in the face, and Harry hadn't answered her.

He didn't ask any of it. He watched Harry turn Layla over onto her back again and start on her belly, which was rounder and pinker under the fur than the rest of her, and saw her tiny forepaws go up in the air and paddle at nothing, and heard the purring go up a notch.

"Do you do this every day?" he said.

"Every morning. And whenever she cries. And whenever I feel like it." Harry's ears tipped. "Kreacher's got a silver bath he brought all the way from London. He fills it up every morning. Heats the towels. She's never been in it."

"Not once?"

"Once," Harry admitted. "Healer Pye made us. Day three. Layla screamed the house down and peed in it, and Kreacher had to sit down."

Seth laughed. He couldn't help it.

He watched a while longer. Harry was doing her tail now, the last part, starting at the base and working down, and the tail was as long as Seth's finger and fluffed up like a bottle brush, and every time Harry's tongue went down it the fur lay flat and then sprang back up again. Layla's purr was going steady.

And Seth was looking at it, at his niece's tiny tail, when the humming changed.

It just slid. Harry finished the old sad star song, came down to the bottom of it, and went somewhere else without stopping, the way people did when they hummed without paying attention to what they were humming. A different tune. Plainer. It had a long rolling line to it, like a road, and a bit that went up at the end of every line and hung there.

Seth knew it.

He knew it before he knew why he knew it. Something in his chest knew it first and went tight. Then he got there.

A truck. The old blue Ford, the one with the rust hole in the floor on the passenger side where you could watch the road go by under your feet. The radio that only got two stations and one of them was always static. Coming back up from the Quillayute in the dark, with the cooler full of fish in the bed and the heater going and his dad's big hand on the wheel, and his dad singing. Loud. With feeling. And flat. So flat. A full step under the radio the whole way, every word, the same song every single time, because it was his song, and Seth at nine years old in the passenger seat rolling his eyes and secretly singing along under his breath.

Bob Seger. "Against the Wind."

Seth didn't make a sound. He didn't think he did. But Harry's humming stopped, partway through a line, and his ears came around toward Seth, both of them, and he lifted his head off the baby.

He looked at Seth's face for a second.

"That one?" he said quietly.

Seth nodded. He couldn't say anything.

"Your dad's?"

"In the truck," said Seth. His voice was doing something. "Every time. It was, like, the only song he knew all the words to. And he sang it so bad. Like, *so* bad. Mom used to make him stop."

Harry looked at him a moment longer.

"I sing it bad too," he said.

Then he put his head back down, and started on the last inch of Layla's tail, and went back to humming. The same song. From where he'd stopped. Just as flat as Seth's dad. Maybe flatter.

Seth sat on the end of the couch and listened to it with his eyes stinging and didn't stop him.

---

His mom came in from the kitchen at quarter to seven with two mugs of coffee.

She stopped in the doorway. Seth watched her take it in: Harry on the couch, which didn't break; the baby on her back on a towel in his lap, fluffed up and gleaming and purring like an outboard motor; Seth at the other end with the green and gold quilt in a heap on the floor and his face blotchy.

She came over and handed one mug to Seth and held the other one out to Harry. Then she stood there holding it, because Harry had both forepaws full of baby.

"Sorry," said Harry. "One sec."

He picked up the onesie off the arm of the couch. Seth watched him dress her. He'd thought it would be hard, with claws, with those huge forepaws, with a baby the size of a loaf of bread. Harry slid the onesie over her head in one go, fed her tiny forepaws through the sleeves one at a time with the tip of a single claw, threaded her tail through the hole in the back, and did up the snaps between her legs, three of them, *click click click*. Then the little gray hat, ears through the holes. The whole thing took about fifteen seconds. Layla didn't even stop purring.

Then he took the coffee.

"Thanks, Sue."

"Five sugars," said Seth's mom. "I remembered."

"You're a saint."

She sat down in her chair across from them and wrapped both hands around her own mug and looked at Layla for a long time.

"Somebody fixed my gutter," she said.

Seth looked at her. Then at Harry.

Harry was blowing on his coffee. "Huh," he said. "Must've been Charlie. Came back in the night."

"Charlie," said Seth's mom.

"Very handy, Charlie. You said."

"And the puddle?" said Seth. He'd seen that last night, actually, when he went out to pee at three—had stood on the porch in his boxers and looked at the smooth gravel at the bottom of the steps and thought he was dreaming. "At the bottom of the steps. That's been there my whole life."

"Charlie's very thorough," said Harry.

Seth's mom looked at him over her coffee. It was the look. The one she'd given Seth the time he'd told her a raccoon had eaten his history homework. She held it for a while.

Then she looked down into her mug, and Seth was almost sure he saw the corner of her mouth go up.

"And the curtain rod," she said.

"Charlie," Harry agreed.

---

There was a wolf asleep behind the woodshed.

Seth had smelled him last night, and his mom had said who it was at dinner, and he'd forgotten. He remembered when he went to the kitchen window for a refill and looked out. The backyard was white with frost, every blade of grass, and the sky had gone from black to dark blue over the trees, and there, in the lee of the woodshed, curled up nose to tail with one pale ear sticking up off the top of his head like a flag, was Brady Fuller.

Somebody had put a blanket over him.

It was a big one, gray wool, the kind that came in a plastic bag from an Army surplus store. Seth had never seen it before. It was draped over Brady's whole back and tucked in around his paws, and there was frost on top of it, and none at all on Brady.

"Did you—" Seth said, turning around.

Harry was in the kitchen doorway, with Layla against his shoulder. "He kept shivering," he said. "In his sleep. You lot run hot but it's minus two out there." He looked past Seth out the window. "Sam's going to have a word with him about falling asleep on duty, I expect."

"He's gonna get *destroyed*," said Seth, with some satisfaction. Brady had eaten Seth's last ice cream sandwich out of Emily's freezer in October and never apologized.

Harry looked at the frying pan on the stove. Seth's mom had bacon going, a whole pound of it, and a dozen eggs in a bowl beside it waiting to be scrambled. He looked back out the window at the woodshed.

"Sue," he said. "Is there enough bacon for one more?"

"There's never enough bacon," said Seth's mom. "But there's more in the freezer."

Harry nodded. He didn't move. But a few minutes later, when the bacon was done and the eggs were done, a plate lifted itself up off the counter, and six strips of bacon and a heap of eggs and two pieces of toast arranged themselves on it, and it went to the back door. The door opened for it. The screen door opened for it. It went out across the white yard, level and slow, and set itself down in the frost about a foot from Brady's nose.

Brady woke up so fast he stood up under the blanket and got tangled in it.

Seth leaned on the counter and drank his coffee and watched Brady Fuller fight his way out of an Army blanket with his teeth, and then stand there in the frost staring at the plate, and then stare at the house, and then sit down, slowly, and eat everything on it, and lick the plate, and then pick up the blanket very carefully in his mouth and fold it—sort of—with his nose and his paws and lay it on top of the woodpile.

It was the best morning Seth had had in about a year.

---

Leah came out at quarter past seven looking like she'd been dragged backward through a car wash.

Her hair was everywhere. She was wearing Harry's red-and-black flannel and a pair of Seth's old basketball shorts that she must have stolen out of the dryer, and wool socks, and nothing else, and she walked straight past all three of them into the kitchen and got a mug down off the shelf and poured coffee into it and drank half of it standing up at the counter, with her eyes shut, before she said anything at all.

"Morning, love," said Harry.

"Mmf," said Leah.

"She had a good night. Slept on me. I bathed her."

Leah opened her eyes and looked at Layla, on his shoulder, gleaming and fluffed up in her clean onesie, asleep. Her face changed. It went soft for a second, all the way soft, in a way Seth hadn't seen it go since they were kids.

Then she turned around and looked out the window over the sink and saw the Army blanket on top of the woodpile, and Brady sitting next to the empty plate licking egg off his own nose, and the smooth gravel at the bottom of the porch steps beyond the corner of the house.

"Mom," she said. "Did Charlie come back in the night?"

"Apparently," said their mom.

Leah looked at Harry. Harry looked at his coffee.

Seth decided this was the moment.

"So, uh," he said. "Jake called. Like, ten minutes ago, Mom's phone. He's coming at nine. He's bringing Embry and Quil. And probably some of the new guys, because they all heard about the car, and they wanna see the car, and Brady's gonna tell everybody about the bacon, so probably some of Sam's guys too, honestly, because they're gonna be like—"

He stopped, because Leah had put her mug down.

She did it very carefully. She set it on the counter like it was full of something that might go off. And she turned and looked at Harry—at Layla, asleep on his shoulder in the little gray hat with her ears poking out—and Seth saw her face, and he knew that face. He'd seen it on her through the pack mind a hundred times that first year. Every time Sam walked into a room with Emily.

It was the face she made when something bad was coming and she couldn't stop it.

"How many," she said.

"I don't know. Like, five? Six? Ten? It depends who's awake."

"Ten," said Leah.

"Leah, they just wanna see the *car*—"

"They're gonna wanna see *her*."

Seth shut his mouth.

He knew why. He'd figured it out last night, in bed, on the couch, in the dark, with his feet hanging off the end. *Not tonight,* she'd said to Jake on the porch, sharp as anything. And the way she'd gone stiff all over when Seth mentioned Nessie at dinner. And Quil, and Claire. He'd lain there thinking about it until he wanted to throw up.

Nobody had imprinted on a baby on purpose. Jake hadn't meant to. It just happened. Jake had walked into a room and looked at a baby and that was the whole rest of his life.

Seth looked at his niece, asleep on Harry's shoulder, and had a very bad feeling in his stomach.

"I'll tell them," he said. "I'll call Jake back. I'll tell them not to come."

"They'll come anyway," said Leah. "You know they will. Half of them won't hear it, and the other half won't listen, and Paul—" She stopped. She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "I can't keep her in the bedroom all day with the door shut. And I'm not hiding in there with her. I'm not doing that. This is *my* house."

"Then don't," said Harry.

They all looked at him.

He was leaning in the kitchen doorway with Layla on his shoulder and his coffee in his other forepaw, and his ears were up, and he looked about as worried as Seth's mom looked when she was deciding what to make for dinner.

"I'll take her out," he said. "For a walk. Show her round. She's never seen the sea." He took a sip of coffee. "You stand on the porch and tell nineteen boys no as many times as it takes, and she's three miles away with me while you do it, and nobody looks at anybody. And we come back for lunch."

Leah took her hands off her face.

"You were going to the rock," she said.

"Ja."

"I said I was coming. Last night."

"You did."

Seth looked between them. He didn't know what this was. He could tell it was something.

Leah stood at the counter with her arms folded tight across his flannel and looked at Harry for a long time. At Layla. At Harry's ears. At his coffee, which he was holding perfectly steady.

"If you go to the rock," she said slowly, "you take her with you."

Seth's mouth fell open.

"*Leah*—"

"Shut up, Seth." She didn't look at him. "I'm not leaving her here with ten wolves in the yard who haven't imprinted yet. And I'm not leaving her with Mom with ten wolves in the yard either, because Mom can't say no to Embry, nobody can say no to Embry, he does the face." She swallowed. "So she goes where you go."

Harry didn't answer right away. Seth watched his ears. They'd gone still.

"You sure?" he said.

"I watched you walk through a blood mage's curses like they were rain," said Leah. "In Mongolia I watched you stand in front of a dragon with an egg in your other paw. She goes where you go." Her voice went rough at the end. "I need to do this one. Here. On the porch. I need to be the one who tells them."

Harry looked at her for another second.

Then he nodded, once.

"We'll be back for lunch," he said.

"Charlie's coming at one," said Seth's mom, from the stove. "Don't be late."

"Wouldn't dream of it," said Harry.

---

He got ready in about four minutes, and Seth followed him around the house the whole time with his coffee going cold in his hand, because he couldn't stop.

The sling first. It was a long piece of gray cloth, soft as a T-shirt, and Harry wound it around his chest and over his shoulders and knotted it at the back without looking, like he'd done it a thousand times, and made a pocket of it against his chest. Then he took Layla off his shoulder and lowered her in, feet first, so she was curled up against the black fur over his breastbone with her face turned sideways and her ears folded down. Only the top of her head showed, and the little gray hat.

Then he put his forepaw flat over her back and held it there.

Seth felt it. He didn't see anything. It was like the air in the hall went thick for a second, like the pressure dropping before a storm. The hair on his arms went up. And then it was gone.

And so was her smell.

Seth stood there with his nose working. He'd been smelling her all morning—milk and baby and that clean cold wild smell like First Beach in winter that was only hers, and under it, faint, something that was a little bit like Leah and a little bit like Harry and a little bit like nothing he'd ever smelled. It had been all over the house since yesterday. He'd fallen asleep to it.

Now it was gone. Like somebody had switched it off. He could smell Harry—pine and earth and cold stone—and he could smell the coffee and the bacon and the frost coming in under the door, and that was it. Layla was right there, a foot away, asleep on Harry's chest, and to Seth's nose there was nothing there at all.

"Where'd her smell go?" he said.

"Put it away," said Harry. He was doing up his coat. The long black one, over the sling, buttoned to the collar, so that Layla disappeared completely underneath it and there was just a slight bulge at his chest. "Shield on her too. And a couple of other things." He looked down at the bulge in his coat with his ears tipped forward. "Nothing that sniffs gets her. Nothing that listens hears her. Nothing that looks for her finds her unless I want it to."

"Does that work on, like—" Seth stopped. "On the thing. The humming thing."

"Ja," said Harry. Just that.

He said it the same way Seth's mom said *the sun comes up in the east.*

He went to the front door. Leah was there already, in the hall, with her arms folded. She didn't say anything. She put her hand flat on the front of his coat, over the bulge, and held it there for a long moment.

Then she went up on her toes and grabbed a fistful of the ruff at his throat and pulled his head down and kissed him, hard, on the side of the muzzle.

"If you lose her," she said, "I'll kill you."

"Fair," said Harry.

"I mean it, Harry. I will *kill* you. I'll do it slow."

"I know, love."

She let go of him. He opened the door. The cold came in, sharp and clean and smelling of frost and salt and wood smoke from the chimneys down the hill, and the sky over the trees had gone pale, pale gold.

He stopped on the porch and looked out at the yard. His nostrils flared, once.

Then he went down the steps, onto the smooth new gravel where Seth's dad's puddle used to be, and turned left down the road toward the village, with his tail swinging slow behind him.

He was humming again before he reached the end of the drive.

Seth stood in the doorway beside his sister and listened to it go. "Against the Wind." Flat. A full step under. Every note.

Behind the woodshed, Seth saw Brady Fuller get up off the frost, very quietly, and slip into the trees.

---

Brady Fuller was in so much trouble.

He knew it the second he woke up, before he even got the blanket off his head. He'd been phased all night—Sam had told him to stay phased, *watch it, don't engage, don't take your eyes off that house*—and he'd stayed phased, and he'd watched, and somewhere around one in the morning he'd watched with his eyes shut. And the thing about being phased was that you weren't alone in there. Jared had been running the north line at four, and Jared had looked in on him, and Jared had seen exactly what Brady had been seeing for the last three hours, which was the inside of his own eyelids.

*Sam's gonna kill you,* Jared had said, at four, and gone back to the north line, and Brady had slept right through it, and only found the memory waiting for him when he woke up. Along with the bacon.

Now Jared was off and Collin was on, way up by the Sol Duc, and Collin was laughing at him.

*Shut up,* Brady thought.

*He put a blanket on you,* Collin thought, delighted. *Like a dog. Like a horse. Like a little—*

*I said shut up.*

*And then he fed you BACON—*

*Brady.*

Brady's whole body went tight. Sam. Sam had phased in somewhere east, by his house, and was in there with them now, heavy and cold, like a storm front coming in over the water. Brady felt him look through what Brady had been seeing. The frost. The plate. The blanket folded on the woodpile.

*I fell asleep,* Brady thought. There wasn't any point pretending. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry, Sam, I—*

*Later.* Sam's attention was on the road. On the big black shape going down it, tall, on two legs, with a long coat and a long tail swinging behind it. *Where's he going?*

*I don't know. Toward the village. He's got—* Brady sniffed. Then he sniffed again. *He had the baby. I heard Leah say. He's taking her for a walk. But I can't smell her. I can't smell her at all.*

Sam didn't say anything for a second.

*Stay on him,* he said. *Don't get close. Don't let him see you.*

Sam didn't use the voice. He didn't need to. Brady went.

---

Following him was the easiest thing Brady had ever done and also the weirdest.

Easy because he was so *big*. You couldn't lose him. He walked right down the middle of the gravel road and then right down the middle of La Push Road, on the yellow line, with his tail going and his ears up, like he owned it. Brady kept to the trees on the ocean side, in the salal and the salmonberry, fifty yards back, low, and didn't even have to try.

Weird because of the humming.

He never stopped. The whole way down the hill. It was the same song, over and over, and Brady knew it from somewhere, from somebody's dad's truck, and the guy was *so* off-key. Like, physically painful. Brady's ears kept wanting to go flat. And every so often he'd stop humming long enough to say something, in that low rumbly voice with the weird accent, the regular one, not the one from last night—and he wasn't saying it to Brady. He was saying it to the bulge in his coat.

"That's a cedar," he said, by the bottom of the hill. He'd stopped next to one, a big old one by the road with moss all up the north side, and he put his forepaw flat on the trunk. "Western red. Your mum smells like these. So do you a bit. Smell that?" He bent his head down toward his coat. "No? You will."

He walked on. Humming.

"That's a raven. He's having a go at us. Ignore him, they're all mouth."

Humming.

"That's the river. Quillayute. Your grandpa fished in that. Every morning for three weeks one September, with your uncle Seth, and they caught so many fish your gran gave most of them away." A pause. "Kept one jar. We ate it last night. You had milk. It was very good. You'll have some when you've got teeth."

Brady, belly-down in the salal, felt something in his chest go weird and twisty. He didn't know why. He'd known Mr. Clearwater. Everybody had. He'd been at the funeral, way at the back, in his cousin's too-big shoes.

*He's talking to it,* said Collin, in his head, from twenty miles away. *Like it understands him.*

*Her,* thought Brady, before he could stop himself. *It's a her.*

He felt Sam notice that. He didn't know what Sam thought about it.

---

The dogs started at the bottom of the hill.

Brady saw it happen and didn't understand it at first. There was a dog asleep on the porch of the Penn house, a big yellow mutt with one blue eye, the kind of dog that was everybody's and nobody's, that lived on whatever porch had food. Every dog on the rez was like that. They didn't like wolves. They really didn't like Brady; they'd started barking at him the week he phased and never stopped.

The yellow dog lifted its head when the big black shape came down the road. It stared. Its ears went up.

Then it got up, and stretched, and came down off the porch with its whole back end wagging, and fell in behind him.

Then a little black one from the ditch by the Jacksons'. Then two brown ones from under the bleachers by the old ball field. Then that awful three-legged spotted one that bit Seth once. By the time he got to the bottom of the hill where the road flattened out toward the store, he had six dogs behind him, trotting along in a line with their tongues out and their tails going, like a parade. Like ducklings.

He didn't look back at them once. He just kept walking. Humming.

"Got some friends," he said to his coat. "Don't tell your mum. They hate her."

Brady put his chin down in the salal and stared.

---

Lonesome Creek Store was open. It was always open. He went in. The dogs sat down in a row outside the door and waited.

Brady crept as close as he dared, to the corner of the propane cage by the side of the building, and listened through the wall.

"Morning," said Tina Ward behind the counter. Brady knew her. She was his mom's cousin's ex-wife. She'd known him since he was born and she still called him Bradley. "Help you?"

"Morning. Large coffee, please. Black. Five sugars. And—" a pause, like he was looking at the case "—four of those maple bars. And the breakfast burritos. How many have you got?"

"Uh. Six?"

"Six, then. Ta."

"You're the guy with the car," said Tina. "Sue Clearwater's place."

"Ja, that's me."

"Everybody's talking about that car." Brady could hear her ringing it up. "Kevin Sampson drove by twice last night just to look at it. He said it's a Rolls-Royce."

"It's a bit much," said the low voice. "I'll be honest."

"You're Leah's husband."

There was a short pause.

"I am," he said.

"Huh," said Tina. Brady heard the bag rustle. "Well. Tell her Tina says hi. We all thought—well. Tell her hi." Another pause. "And congratulations. On the baby. Is that—" Her voice went funny. Confused. "Did you leave the baby at Sue's?"

Brady realized she must be looking right at his coat. Right at the bulge. And not seeing anything.

"She's with me," he said. "She's asleep. She likes it in here."

First Beach in the morning in December was gray and gold and huge and loud.

The sun had come up behind the hills and the light came down long and flat over the water, catching the tops of the waves. The tide was out. The sand was hard and wet and shiny and went on forever, all the way down to the rocks at the south end, with the big driftwood logs piled up along the top of it like somebody had dropped a box of matches. James Island sat out at the mouth of the river, dark and steep, with trees on top. The sea stacks stood out in the water past it, black against the light, with the surf going white around their feet. Everything smelled like salt and kelp and cold.

He walked straight down to the edge of the water.

Brady stayed up in the drift logs, flat behind a big silver one, and watched him go. The dogs went with him, spreading out across the sand, chasing gulls, splashing in the foam. Then he unbuttoned the top two buttons of his coat.

Brady's ears went up.

He couldn't see her. He was too far away, and the coat was in the way. Brady had swum in it in winter on a dare. Everybody had.

The surf boomed. A gull went over, screaming. The big wolf stood there in the edge of the water with his coat open and his head bent and didn't say anything for a while.

A pause.

"Was that a smile?" Another pause. "That was a smile. Don't do that. Layla. Your mum's going to kill me. Save it for her." The voice had gone rough. "Do it again for her. All right? When we get home. Do it for her first."

Brady's cousin Teo had it on a shirt. He was humming "Back in Black" to a four-week-old baby on First Beach at eight in the morning, with the bass line and everything, *dun, dun-dun*, and his tail was going in time.

*What is he,* said Collin, in Brady's head, very small.

Brady didn't have an answer.

Sam didn't say anything at all.

There was an old man on the bench at the marina.

Old Quil.

Old Quil turned his head.

The humming stopped too.

The school was the last thing.

Brady thought he was heading back up the hill after the marina, back to Sue's. Instead he turned off the road and walked around behind the harbor office, along the gravel lot, toward the tribal school. It was Saturday, and the parking lot was empty, and the buildings were dark. The main building, low and long, and the gym, and the three portables out back on their cinderblocks with the wooden ramps going up to their doors.

The middle portable had a patch on the side. A big square of new plywood, not even painted yet, nailed up over a hole about the size of a garage door. Brady had seen the hole. Everybody had. Dylan Littlesea had been sitting in third period social studies next to the wall when it happened, and the wall had lost.

The wolf walked up to the patch and stopped.

He wasn't humming anymore.

He put his nose close to the plywood, not touching it. He breathed in. Then he turned his head and looked at the trees.

There was a strip of woods behind the portables, alder and spruce, thick, maybe fifty yards deep before it ran up into the hill. Brady was in it. He'd come around the long way, through the salal, and he was lying behind a nurse log about forty yards from the back of the middle portable, right where he could see.

The wolf walked toward the trees.

He walked slowly. His nose was going. He stopped at the edge of the trees, by a big spruce, and breathed in again, and his ears went partway back.

Brady breathed in too.

He didn't smell anything at first. Spruce. Wet. Rotting leaves. A raccoon from last night. Dylan, faintly, all over everything, because Dylan had been coming out here to phase in secret before he knew what was happening to him. And then, underneath, so faint he almost thought he made it up—something sweet. Like flowers. Old flowers. Like the bathroom at his great-aunt's house in Neah Bay.

And under that, something that made the fur on his back stand up.

Rot. And pennies.

"Brady," said the wolf. Not loud. "Come here, please."

Brady stopped breathing.

*Don't,* said Sam, in his head, instantly.

Brady lay there with his heart slamming.

*Sam,* he thought. *Sam, what do I—*

Sam didn't answer right away. Brady could feel him thinking. Feel him weighing it. Feel something else in there, too, something tired and sore that Brady didn't understand and didn't want to.

Brady phased.

It took him a second, it always took him a second, and then he was on his hands and knees behind the nurse log in the cold, naked, gasping, with the wet moss soaking into his knees. And something landed on the log in front of him with a soft *flump*.

It was a pile of clothes. Gray sweatpants. A black hoodie, thick, with a kangaroo pocket. Wool socks. A pair of slip-on sneakers. They were folded. They were dry. They had not been there a second ago.

Brady stared at them.

Then he put them on, really fast, and they fit, everything fit, even the shoes, and he stood up and came out from behind the log.

Harry looked at him. Up close, Brady had to tip his head way back. He was so big. Brady was five-eleven and still growing, and the guy just kept *going*. His eyes were really, really green.

"Uh," said Brady. "Yeah. Thanks. And the—blanket."

"That's the thing," he said. His voice cracked. "From the lake. That's the—that's what Embry—"

Brady breathed in again. He made himself think about it, the way Jared had taught him, sorting it out layer by layer. "The flowers. Like a perfume. Like—" he felt stupid saying it "—like old-lady perfume. Like my great-aunt Bev."

Brady straightened up. He felt sick.

"She?"

Harry didn't answer that. He was looking at the back of the middle portable, at the new plywood patch.

"How many kids go to this school?" he said.

"I don't know. Like, a hundred? More? It's K through twelve."

Brady opened his mouth. Then he shut it.

He thought about the sixth-grade class. About Tyler Penn, who'd grown four inches since summer. About Dylan's little brother Max, who was twelve and had been sent home twice this month with a fever and no other symptoms.

"Ja," said Harry quietly.

"Has Samuel got anybody on this school?" he said.

"No. We do the borders. The treaty line. The roads in."

Brady scrambled after him. The dogs, who had been waiting in a row at the edge of the lot, all got up and fell in behind them.

"Where are you going?"

"North. To the lake." Harry was looking at the road. "There's a rock I want to see."

"Sam said to stay on you."

Harry stopped. He looked down at Brady with his head tipped to one side, and his ears came up, and the corner of his muzzle went up off a lot of teeth.

"Then you'd better get in the car," he said.

"The car's at Sue's. Jake's there. Like, everybody's there."

Harry looked down the road, toward the hill. His ears tipped forward.

Brady heard it before he saw it. A low, smooth hush, coming down the hill. Gravel. Then asphalt. And then around the corner by the store, gliding, enormous, black as the bottom of a well, with the little silver lady on the front leaning into the wind, came the Rolls-Royce.

There was nobody in it.

Brady could see right through the windshield. Nobody. The steering wheel was turning by itself. It came down past the store and past the marina at about fifteen miles an hour, smooth as anything, and pulled into the empty school lot, and swung around in a big slow circle, and stopped right in front of them.

The back door swung open.

"Thank you, Gerald," said Harry.

Brady looked at the car. He looked at Harry. He looked at the empty driver's seat.

"Who's *Gerald*?" he said.

"He's very good with cars," said Harry, and unbuttoned his coat.

The car seat was in the back. Brady stood there by the open door and watched him take the baby out of the sling.

He saw her for the first time.

He didn't know what he'd been expecting. Something weird. Something scary, maybe. Something like Nessie, who looked like a normal little kid and then touched your face and put a movie in your head and made you feel like you'd stuck your tongue on a battery. All the guys in Sam's pack had been talking about the baby since last night. *What is it. Is it a wolf. Is it like him.*

It was a baby.

It was a really, really small baby, with fur. Dark gray, almost black, with silver at the tips, so she shimmered in the light from the open door. A little gray hat with her ears coming out of it. A blunt little face with a black nose and a pale gray stripe down the middle. She was awake. Her eyes were open, a little, blinking. They were green. Bright green, with a ring of gold around the outside.

Her little nose was going. She was smelling the cold. Smelling the car.

Smelling Brady.

Brady stood very, very still.

He knew about imprinting. Everybody knew about imprinting. You couldn't not know, the way Sam thought about Emily, the way Jared thought about Kim, the way Paul had been since he saw Rachel. And he'd known, since about eleven o'clock last night, lying behind the woodshed listening to Leah through the window, why she didn't want anybody looking at this baby. He'd lain there and thought about it and felt sick.

He looked at her.

She looked at him. Blurry. Blinking.

Nothing happened.

Nothing tilted. Nothing grabbed him. The ground stayed where it was. She was a baby, and she was cute, kind of, in a weird furry way, and she had a little bit of drool on her chin, and that was all. Brady let out a breath so big he felt it all the way to his feet.

Harry was watching him. He'd been watching him the whole time. Brady realized it now. Watching his face, with his ears pointed straight at him, and his forepaw resting very lightly on the top of the baby's head, and his eyes steady and green and completely calm.

He didn't say anything about it. He turned and put her in the car seat, and did up the buckles, all of them, and checked them twice, and tucked a gray blanket around her with a little black shield stitched in the corner.

"Front seat," he said to Brady. "Seatbelt."

Brady got in the front seat. It was cream-colored leather and it was so soft he sank into it. The ceiling was full of tiny lights like stars. It smelled like leather and Harry and, very faintly, baby.

Harry folded himself into the driver's seat. He had to put the seat all the way back. He put his forepaws on the wheel.

The six dogs were sitting in a row in the school lot, watching.

"Go home," Harry told them, through the window. "Go on."

They got up and trotted off in six different directions.

Harry pulled out of the lot and onto La Push Road, east, toward Forks and 101. The car didn't make any sound at all. Brady sat there with his hands in the pocket of the hoodie and watched the trees go by and didn't know what to say.

After about a mile, Harry started humming again.

It took Brady a second to place it. Then he did, and he turned his head and stared.

"You're humming 'Highway to Hell,'" he said. "To a *baby*."

"She likes it," said Harry.

Brady looked into the back seat. The baby had her eyes open. Her tiny ears had come up out of the holes in the hat and were pointing forward, at the driver's seat, at the sound. Her little tail was going against the blanket. Back and forth. In time.

"Huh," said Brady.

He leaned back in the seat. He watched the trees. After a while, he noticed he was tapping his foot.

There were six breakfast burritos in a paper bag on the floor by his feet. Harry hadn't touched them.

"Are those—" said Brady.

"Ja," said Harry. "Go on. Leave me two."

The trailhead at the east end of the lake was a gravel lot with room for maybe a dozen cars, a brown Park Service sign, a pit toilet, and nobody.

Harry pulled in at twenty to eleven and parked facing the trail. He turned the engine off. Beside him, Brady had eaten four burritos and was looking at the last two in the bag with a kind of longing that Harry recognized from the inside.

"Those are mine," said Harry.

"I know."

"I'll eat them on the way back." He got out. The cold hit him, clean and sharp, smelling of fir and frost and wet rock and the lake. He opened the back door and leaned in and unbuckled Layla from her seat—every buckle, in order—and lifted her out, blanket and all, and put her back in the sling against his chest. She'd gone to sleep somewhere around Forks, halfway through "Highway to Hell," and she didn't wake up. Her face pushed sideways into the fur over his breastbone. He put his paw flat over her back and checked everything he'd put on her that morning. Shield, scent, sound, sight. All of it sitting where he'd left it.

He buttoned his coat over her.

Then he leaned down and looked at Brady through the open door.

"Stay in the car," he said. "Doors lock. Windows stay up. Nothing gets in—nothing you can hear, nothing you can smell, nothing that wants to walk in your head. I've done the car." He paused. "If anything comes up that trail that isn't me, honk."

Brady's face went a bit pale under the brown. "What if it's something bad?"

"Then honk louder."

"And then what?"

"And then I'll come and sort it out." Harry looked at him. Fifteen. Big hands, big feet, a face that hadn't decided yet what it was going to look like. The hoodie Harry had made him was too big in the shoulders, the way they always were on boys that age. "You did well at the school. Good nose."

Brady opened his mouth and nothing came out.

Harry shut the door. The locks went down, all four, with a soft expensive *thunk*. He put his forepaw on the roof of the car for a second and felt the wards there, humming in the metal, and then he turned around and walked down the trail.

---

It was an old railroad grade. He could see that straight away—too flat and too straight for a trail anybody had built for walking, cut into the side of the hill with the slope going up steep on his right and down steep on his left to the water. Big-leaf maples leaned out over it, bare, with moss hanging off every branch like wet green fleece. Firs behind them. Ferns. Everything dripping. The frost was going in the sun, and the whole forest was ticking and pattering with it.

And on his left, through the trees, the lake.

He'd seen it from the car, from the road on the south shore, but it was different down here. It was right there, a few yards off, through the maple trunks. The water was so clear near the edge that he could see the rocks on the bottom, every one, ten feet down, twenty, and then the bottom just fell away and the color went from green to blue to a blue so dark it was nearly black. There were mountains on the other side going straight up out of it with snow on their tops. No wind. The surface was flat as poured glass.

"That's a lake," Harry told the bulge in his coat. "Bigger than ours. A lot deeper. Don't tell Atlin."

He walked. He hummed. Seger, because it was in his head from the morning, and because every time he hummed it he thought about Seth's face on the end of the couch, and it seemed like a good thing to keep going.

The smell started about a quarter of a mile in.

He'd been waiting for it. It came up on the left, from the lakeshore, faint and then less faint. Violets first. Sweet and powdery and old, the kind of scent that came in a cut-glass bottle with a rubber bulb. Then under it, the rest. Rot. Copper. And that burnt, struck-wire smell that he'd only ever smelled on one man before, on a ridge above Tamarack Crossing.

He stopped humming for a second and breathed it in properly.

Days. Not weeks. Three or four days, the freshest of it, on top of a lot of older layers going back months. She'd walked this grade a great many times. Always the same way. Always ending at the same place.

He started humming again and kept walking.

He thought about the file. He'd read Vulk's file four times in a briefing room in Geneva fourteen months ago, sitting at a table with bad coffee, before he'd ever gone near Tamarack Crossing. Most of it he still had by heart. Born 1863, Brașov. Mother died in childbirth. Father unknown. *Raised by maternal grandmother (name unrecorded). Presumed deceased c. 1880.* That had been all. One line. Nobody had followed it up because there had been nothing to follow up, and because the man himself had been the problem.

Vulk had worn vetiver and sandalwood. Expensive. A man's scent, laid on to cover up what was underneath.

This was violets.

And the tune. Three notes. He'd heard Vulk sing it under his breath in a log cabin by a lake in October, walking a waitress up a mountain on the end of it. He'd assumed it was Vulk's. It had been the kind of thing he'd have made up himself, the way some men made up their own signatures.

He'd been wrong about that. He filed it.

The trail came round a long curve and there was the tunnel.

What was left of it. It had been cut through a rib of rock that ran down to the water, and somewhere in the last ninety years the rock had come down. The tunnel mouth was a heap of rubble and fallen stone and old timbers gone black with rot, with ferns growing out of the gaps and moss over everything, and the trail went up and around it on a narrow switchback over the shoulder of the hill. There was a Park Service sign at the bottom. *TUNNEL CLOSED. USE BYPASS TRAIL.*

On the far side of the tunnel there was a little cove.

He stood on the bypass above it and looked down. The rock ran out into the lake in a hook and made a pool, almost round, maybe a hundred feet across, with a small wooden footbridge going over the narrow neck of it where it opened into the lake. The water in it was a color he'd never seen water be. Blue-green, bright, like something lit from underneath. He could see straight down into it for what looked like a mile.

There was another sign. *DEVIL'S PUNCHBOWL.*

"Ja," said Harry. "That tracks."

The smell went right down the switchback to the edge of the cove. He followed it. Down onto the flat rock at the bottom, by the water, where the tunnel's other mouth had once come out—another heap of fallen stone, smaller, half grown over. And in front of the rubble, right at the water's edge, there was a boulder.

It was a big one. Gray granite, the size of a car, with a cap of moss on the top like a hat and the lake lapping at its foot. It sat in front of the old tunnel mouth like somebody had parked it there.

The smell went straight up to it and stopped.

Harry stopped too.

He stood about ten feet back and looked at it. He didn't go closer. He let his eyes go a bit soft, the way he'd learned to do it, the way you'd look at one of those pictures that turned into a sailboat if you stared long enough.

There it was.

A seam, running down the face of the rock. Fine as a hair. Curved, like the edge of a door. And around it, laid over the stone in layers like the skin of an onion, the wards. He could feel them more than see them, a low dry buzzing in his teeth. A concealment, very good, old-fashioned work, the sort of thing you'd hide a vault behind. A repelling charm under that, the kind that made hikers decide they'd rather look at the footbridge. An alarm under that. And at the center of all of it, holding the seam shut, something older than any charm. It smelled of copper. It was keyed to blood. One person's blood. Hers.

It was a good lock. Built for keeping out wolves, and Aurors, and anybody else who came sniffing.

Harry unbuttoned the top of his coat and looked down at Layla. She was asleep, with her mouth a little bit open and one ear folded inside out.

He fixed the ear. Then he did the coat back up.

Then he went to work.

It took him about a minute and a half. He didn't move. He stood ten feet back from the boulder with his paws folded in front of him and hummed "Against the Wind," flat, under his breath, and took the wards apart one at a time. The repelling charm first, because it was the loudest. Then the concealment, which he leaned on until it let go of the seam, so that from the trail the rock would still look like a rock. The alarm he looked at for a while. It was clever. It was tied into the blood lock, and the blood lock was tied into everything else in there, so that if anybody forced the door, she'd know.

He found where the alarm was listening and put something in front of its ear, the way you'd put a hand over a microphone. Then he took hold of the blood lock and explained to it, slowly and very firmly, that he was family.

It thought about that for a while.

Then the seam opened.

The whole front of the boulder swung inward without a sound, like a door on oiled hinges, and there was darkness behind it, and a breath of air came out that was dry and still and far too mild for December, and smelled of candle wax and violets and old meat.

Harry stood and looked at the dark for a second.

"Right," he said to his coat. "Bit of a walk. Stay asleep. Good girl."

He went in.

It was the tunnel.

She'd made herself a room out of the inside of it. Both ends had come down, ninety years back, and sealed it up like a bottle, and what was left was a long arched space cut through the rock, maybe fifty yards long and twice his height at the top, with the old railroad timbers still holding up the roof in places. Dry. Somebody had made sure of that. The walls were cut stone, black with old soot from the steam engines that had never run through here.

The candles lit when he stepped in.

All of them at once. Dozens. Fat white church candles, in rows along shelves cut into the walls, on the floor along the edges, on an old wooden table in the middle. They came up yellow and still and threw his shadow out long down the tunnel ahead of him, ears and all.

He waited a moment. Nothing else happened. The alarm stayed asleep behind his paw.

He walked in slowly, and looked.

There was a bed against the left wall. A narrow iron one, old, with a white coverlet, very neat. A washstand beside it with a china jug. A rag rug on the stone. A rocking chair. On the washstand there was a cut-glass bottle with a rubber bulb, and he didn't need to go any closer to know what was in it.

On the right wall there were shelves. On the shelves there were jars.

He went and looked at those.

Big ones, most of them, the old-fashioned kind with glass lids and wire clamps. Each one had something dark in it, floating. Each one had a paper label, written in brown ink in a small, tight, slanting hand.

*Elk, cow. 2.X.*

*Black bear, boar. 19.X.*

*Cougar, female. 30.X.*

*Harbour seal. 4.XI.*

Hearts. He knew what they were before he read the labels. Big ones, for the elk and the bear. She'd been testing. Working out how the land up here took to her. What it gave her.

At the end of the shelf, by itself, there was a small glass vial with a cork in it. It was full of dark red, almost black, and it hadn't separated or dried, which meant there was something on it. The label was smaller.

*Call, E. 9.XI. Shoulder.*

Harry looked at it for a while.

Then he went to the table in the middle.

There was a book on it. A ledger, the long narrow kind that shops used to keep accounts in, bound in green cloth gone soft at the corners. It was lying open. He didn't touch it. He bent down and read the page it was open to.

It was a list. Names, in the same brown ink, the same slanting hand. Ages. Dates. Notes.

*Uley, S. 22. Alpha. Levi's line.*

*Black, J. 17. Alpha. Ephraim's line direct. (cf. B—— journal, ff. 38–41.) PRINCIPAL.*

*Lahote, P. 20. Temper.*

*Cameron, J. 20.*

*Call, E. 18. Blood taken 9.XI. Satisfactory.*

*Ateara, Q. 18.*

He went down it. All of them. Names he knew from last night, from Seth and from Leah, and names he didn't. *Fuller, B. 15.* *Littlesea, C. 15.* *Littlesea, D. 13. Changed 14.XII.* Eighteen of them.

And then two more, below the line, set a little apart.

*Clearwater, S. 16. Kind. Easy.*

*Clearwater, L. 20. Female. Gone north IX.06. Rare.*

Somebody had underlined *Rare* twice.

Harry stood over the table and didn't say anything.

The candle flames stood very still around him. Inside his coat, against his chest, Layla shifted in her sleep and made a small grumbling noise and went quiet again.

There was no line for her. He read the page twice to be sure. Then he turned it, carefully, with one claw, and read the next page, and the one after that, which were notes and figures and the phases of the moon. There was no line for her anywhere.

Good.

He turned back to the list and looked at one more thing. At the end of the table, past the ledger, there was a row of jars.

Empty ones. Small. Pint-sized, with glass lids and wire clamps. Lined up neatly along the edge of the table in a single row, like soldiers. Each one had a paper label tied around its neck with string. He didn't have to read them. He read them anyway. *Uley. Black. Lahote. Cameron. Call.*

Twenty jars. He counted them twice.

The last one said *Clearwater, L.*

None of them were small. None of them were waiting for anybody four weeks old. She didn't know.

He stood there for a moment longer. Then he took the ledger off the table and closed it and put it inside his coat, on the side away from Layla.

There was one more thing on the table, under where the ledger had been. A little bundle of paper, tied up with a faded black ribbon. Old paper. Thick, with ragged edges, like it had been cut out of a bound book with a knife. The ink on it had gone brown.

He untied the ribbon with one claw.

It was a journal. Or part of one. Six or seven leaves of it, written close on both sides in a looping old-fashioned hand, with long s's that looked like f's. English. He read the top of the first leaf.

*—and the people at the river's mouth, who call themselves after the wolf, say that their young men become wolves when the cold dead come among them, and I had thought this a tale, until the night of the fourteenth, when I saw it done.*

He read it twice.

At the top of the leaf, above the first line, there was a mark. Inked by hand, small and careful, the way people used to mark their books before bookplates. A shield. A chevron across it. Two stars above, one below. And under it, a ribbon with two words on it, in tiny capitals.

*TOUJOURS PUR.*

He knew those words. They'd been carved over a fireplace in a dark house in London. They'd been on the tapestry in the drawing room, on the doorknocker, on the silver. They'd been in the mouth of a portrait that screamed.

Harry unbuttoned the top of his coat again.

Layla was asleep, in the sling, in her gray hat. And tucked around her, where Kreacher had stitched it into the corner of the blanket with black silk thread, there was a small black shield. A chevron across it. Two stars above, one below.

He looked from one to the other.

"Huh," he said.

He thought about the line in the ledger. *Black, J. Ephraim's line direct. (cf. B—— journal, ff. 38–41.)* He looked at the corner of the first leaf. Somebody had numbered it, in pencil, much later than the ink. *38.*

He turned to the last leaf. Most of it was about the weather. A storm. A broken mast. The ship being made ready to sail. And near the bottom, one line on its own, in ink that had blotted as though the pen had pressed too hard.

*I have asked her father for her, and he has said no; and so I shall ask her.*

The bottom of the leaf had been torn off. There was no signature.

Harry stood in the candlelight and looked at it for a long time. Then he folded the leaves back together, very carefully, along the old fold, and tied the black ribbon around them, and put them inside his coat with the ledger.

He thought about Grimmauld Place. About a man called Mundungus Fletcher, who had walked out of that house in the summer of 1996 with a suitcase full of anything that would sell, and had sold it. Silver. Goblets. A locket. Books. Anything with the Black crest on it.

He filed that too.

Then he turned and looked at the little vial at the end of the shelf. *Call, E. 9.XI. Shoulder.*

He knew what a blood mage did with a sample. He knew what a curse could be laid on, with a bit of somebody's blood and a lot of patience. Embry Call, eighteen, who'd gone to see a doctor about a cut on his shoulder. It wasn't going to stay here.

He knew what would happen when he burned it. The alarm was listening to everything in this room through the blood lock, and that vial was the closest thing in it to her own blood, because she'd been working on it.

He thought about it for about two seconds.

Then he looked at it, and it burned.

---

The fire was white and gold and quiet, and it took the vial in the space of a breath. The glass ran like water. The dark inside it hissed once and went up in a thin red thread of smoke and was gone.

And the alarm woke up behind his paw, and screamed.

He felt it go. A long thin pulse down the length of the tunnel and out through the open door and away across the water, north and west, fast as a thrown stone. Telling her.

Every candle in the tunnel went out at once.

Then the humming started.

It came out of the dark from everywhere. Out of the walls. Out of the stone under his boots and the timbers over his head and the empty jars on the table. Three notes. Up, then down. Then nothing. Then again. Up, then down, then nothing, like a question nobody answered, around and around and around, quiet and patient and endless.

He felt it reach for him.

He'd felt it on the ridge above Tamarack Crossing, faintly, from two hundred meters away, when Vulk was singing it to a waitress. This was much closer and much older and much stronger. It came at the front of his mind like water looking for a crack in a hull. Looking for a way in. Looking for the place behind his eyes that would pick up the tune and start humming it back.

It didn't find one. He'd had people in his head before. A dark lord had lived in there for sixteen years. He'd spent a whole school year learning to close the doors, badly, and the next ten years learning to do it properly. It went over the outside of him like a fly walking on a windowpane.

He checked Layla. He did it first, before anything, with his paw flat over the coat. The sound charm was holding. She'd heard none of it. She was asleep. Her breathing hadn't changed.

Then he started to hum.

The star song this time. The old Black lullaby that Kreacher had sung once, in a cracked voice, on the edge of the bed with her asleep in his arms, that went up and took a long slow step down and turned at the bottom and came back up again. He hummed it low, right down at the bottom of his chest, where he could feel it in his ribs and so could anybody leaning against them.

The two tunes met in the dark.

Hers went around and around. Three notes. His went on, eight or nine of them, slow, and came back, and went on. He didn't make it louder. He didn't need to. He just didn't stop.

After a while, hers did.

There was a silence in the tunnel. A long one.

Then a voice came out of the dark.

It came from the shelf with the jars. From something on it, a small pale thing he hadn't noticed under the candles—a round stone with a hole worn through the middle, the kind you found on beaches, sitting on the end of the shelf next to the empty place where the vial had been. It was an old woman's voice. Thin, dry, careful. The accent was from a long way east of here, and very old, and under it, so faint you'd have to know to listen for it, it was a little bit like Vulk's.

"You are not one of the dogs," it said.

"No," said Harry.

"You smell of them." A pause. He could hear her breathing on the other end of it, somewhere far away. Slow. "And not of them. Something older. Wolf and smoke and—" Another pause. "*Lightning.*" The voice changed. It went still and flat. "You are the one who burned my Casimir."

"Ja," said Harry.

"Potter."

"Ja."

The silence went on a long time.

"He was my daughter's son," said the voice. "I raised him. I taught him the song. I taught him everything he knew." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "He was a greedy boy. He had no patience. One heart, one girl, ten years, run, hide, again. A peasant eating bread one slice at a time. I told him. I told him a hundred times."

Harry didn't say anything.

"There are twenty of them here," said the voice softly. "Did you count? I counted. Twenty hearts that have the old wolf in them, all in one place, all running about in the woods like puppies with no one to tell them what they are. And more to come. Every time the cold ones walk through, more." The breath again. "I have not been hungry in a long time, Mr. Potter. I have been patient for a very long time. I will be patient a little longer. And then I will have a feast, and I will not need another for a thousand years."

Harry stood in the dark with his paw flat over his daughter's back.

"Twenty," he said.

"Twenty."

He waited to see if she'd say it. Twenty-one. A small one. A new one. Something on the wind. He gave her plenty of time.

She didn't.

"Your humming's flat," said Harry. "By the way."

There was a sharp intake of breath at the other end, a long way off.

Harry looked at the stone with the hole in it. It cracked down the middle with a sound like a knuckle, and both halves fell off the end of the shelf and broke on the floor into gravel, and the breath on the other end was gone, and so was she.

He stood there for a second longer in the quiet.

Then he lit the candles again, with a look, because he wanted to see what he was doing, and set about taking the place apart.

---

He burned the hearts first. Elk, bear, cougar, seal. Each one in its jar, in white-gold fire that didn't crack the glass until there was nothing left in it but a little gray dust, and then the glass went too. Whatever she'd learned from them, she could keep. She wasn't getting anything else out of them.

He burned the empty jars. All twenty. He did those one at a time too, starting with *Uley* and ending with *Clearwater, L.*, and he took a little longer over the last one than he strictly needed to.

He burned the bed and the coverlet and the rocking chair and the rag rug. He burned the bottle of violet water on the washstand, and the stink of it went up in a sweet thick cloud and was gone, and the tunnel smelled of candle wax and old stone and nothing else.

He left the shelves and the table, because they'd been cut out of the rock, and stone didn't care.

Then he walked back out through the door in the boulder, into the daylight, and turned around, and looked at the tunnel mouth.

The rubble at the other end had held for ninety years. He made this end match. He leaned on the rock over the inside of the door, gently, with his mind, until the roof decided it had been holding itself up long enough, and it came down. He felt it go through the soles of his boots, a long low grinding settle, deep in the hill. A puff of dust came out around the edges of the boulder.

The door in the boulder swung shut. The seam closed up. He put his paw over it, flat, and when he took it away there was no seam anymore. It was a boulder. Granite, the size of a car, with moss on the top like a hat.

He stood there and listened. Nothing in the hill. Nothing on the wind. Somewhere a long way off, across the lake, a raven.

Then the water in the Punchbowl moved.

---

He heard it before he turned around. A long slow slosh, like something big rolling over in a bath. He turned around.

There were rings on the surface of the cove. Wide ones, spreading out from the middle, from the deep part where the blue went black. Then more. Then something came up through the middle of the rings.

A head. Then shoulders. Then the whole of it, walking up out of the deep water toward the rock as if the bottom were a staircase. Water poured off it. Then another one behind it. Then two more.

Four of them.

They came up out of the Punchbowl onto the flat rock at the water's edge, slowly, dripping, and stood there swaying in a ragged line between Harry and the footbridge.

They'd been in the lake a long time. He could see that. The lake was deep and cold enough to keep things. Their skin had gone pale and smooth and waxy, like the inside of a candle, and their eyes were white, and the water ran out of their mouths when they moved. One of them was a big man in a logger's wool shirt and suspenders and caulk boots. One was a woman in a long coat with a fur collar and a cloche hat still somehow on her head. One was a boy in a canvas jacket, fourteen or fifteen, with the zip done up to his chin. The last one he couldn't tell. It had been in there the longest.

She'd put them in the water to watch the door. Somewhere in the last two months, she'd gone down in the deep part and found them, and woken them up, and left them there. In case.

They turned their white eyes toward him, all four at once.

Harry stood on the rock with his coat buttoned up and his paws folded in front of him.

Inside his coat, Layla woke up.

He felt her do it. A wriggle, and then her small forepaws pushing against his chest, and the little cross grumble that meant she wanted to see what was going on. He unbuttoned the top two buttons. Her head came up out of the sling, gray hat and all, and her ears came up through the holes in it, and her blurry green eyes blinked out at the cold, bright, blue-green water and the four white figures standing dripping on the rock in front of her.

The logger took a step forward. The water ran out of his mouth.

"Ja," Harry said to Layla. "I know. Hang on."

He didn't move. He didn't raise anything.

The fire came up out of the rock under their feet.

It came the way it had come on the ridge above Tamarack Crossing for two dead girls in their hiking clothes. White, then gold. Four columns of it, tall and quiet, wrapping around each of them like arms. It didn't roar. It didn't spread. The moss on the rock beside them didn't even steam. Inside it they stopped walking, all four of them, and stood still, and Harry watched the woman in the cloche hat lift her face up into it, slowly, like somebody stepping out of a dark house into the sun.

Then it sank back down into the rock and went out.

There were four small heaps of pale ash on the flat rock at the edge of the Punchbowl. A breath of wind came down off the hill, the first wind all morning, and lifted the ash, very gently, and carried it out over the blue-green water, and the water took it.

Layla yawned.

It was enormous. Her whole tiny face disappeared into it, pink mouth and pink tongue and no teeth. Then she sneezed. Then she put her face back down into his fur.

"Ja," said Harry. "Bit boring, isn't it."

He stood there a little longer looking at the water. A logger. A woman in a good coat. A boy. He'd tell the Park Service. He'd tell them there were people in the lake who might have someone still waiting to hear. That was all he could do for them now, and he'd do it.

Then he buttoned his coat back up over his daughter's head, and turned around, and walked back up the switchback to the trail.

Halfway up, he noticed he was humming again.

It was Seger. He let it be.

---

Brady did not eat the burritos.

He wanted to. He sat in the front seat of the Rolls-Royce for an hour and ten minutes with the paper bag by his feet and the smell coming up out of it, and he did not eat them, because Harry had said leave me two, and Brady had a feeling about what kind of guy you did and didn't steal burritos from.

He'd heard something, about forty minutes in.

He didn't know what. He'd been sitting with his forehead against the window, looking down the trail into the trees, and something had come up through the floor of the car. It was more like the feeling of a sound. Like when somebody's playing music in a car next to you at a red light and you can feel the bass in your own seat. It had a shape, a little. It went up and then down and then stopped.

And then the car had answered it.

He'd felt that too. A buzz in the door under his hand, in the dashboard, in the leather, all at once, like the whole car had set its teeth. And the other thing had gone away. Just slid off, like rain off a windshield.

Then about ten minutes after that, something had happened in the hill. A long low grinding rumble, deep down, that he felt in his stomach more than heard. A couple of crows had gone up out of the trees down the trail, yelling.

He'd sat there with his hand flat on the middle of the steering wheel, right on the horn, for ten whole minutes after that. He hadn't pressed it. Nothing had come up the trail.

At five to twelve, something did.

Brady heard it first. Faint, through the glass. Then he saw him, coming up the old railroad grade under the maples, tall and black, coat buttoned to the collar, tail swinging slow behind him. Walking, unhurried. Heel, toe. Like a guy walking back to his truck from the hardware store.

Humming.

Brady unlocked the doors so fast he hit his elbow on the window.

"What happened?" He was out of the car before Harry got to it. "What—I heard—the car did a thing, and then the hill did a thing, and—"

"Found her kitchen," said Harry.

He opened the back door and unbuttoned his coat. The baby was awake. She was looking around, blinking, with her ears up through the holes in the little gray hat. Harry lifted her out of the sling and put her in the car seat and did up all the buckles, in order, and checked them twice.

"Her?" said Brady.

"Ja."

Harry shut the back door and came around and got in the driver's seat and held out his paw, and Brady realized what he wanted and handed him the paper bag. Harry ate both burritos. It took four bites. Then he folded the bag up very neatly and put it in the door pocket.

There was a bulge on the other side of his coat. Something square, like a book. And the corner of some old brown paper sticking out of the top, tied up with a black ribbon.

"Listen," said Harry.

Brady listened.

"When we get back, you go behind that woodshed and you phase, and you show Samuel everything you saw today. All of it. The school, the smell, the dogs, Old Quil, all of it." He started the car. "Tell him I said two of his biggest on that school, every night, starting tonight. Tell him I said please." He backed out of the space. "And tell him she's got a list. Twenty names. His is on it. So's yours."

Brady sat very still in the cream-colored seat.

"Mine," he said.

"Fuller, B. Fifteen." Harry pulled out onto the road. "In brown ink. Nice handwriting."

Brady didn't say anything for about a mile.

"What do we do?" he said finally. His voice came out small.

Harry looked over at him, just for a second, before he looked back at the road. His ears were up. His eyes were very green.

"You put two on the school," he said. "I'll do the rest."

He said it the way you'd say you were going to pick up milk on the way home.

Then he started humming again. "Highway to Hell." Flat. In the back seat, Brady heard a small, soft, rumbling chirrup start up, like a kettle thinking about it, in time.

---

They pulled into Sue Clearwater's driveway at twenty to two.

There was a Forks Police cruiser parked behind the Subaru. Charlie Swan was on the porch, in jeans and a Mariners jacket, with a can of Rainier in his hand, sitting on the old swing at the end that Brady had never seen anybody sit on. Sue was in the doorway behind him.

And Leah was sitting on the top step.

She'd been there a while. Brady could tell. She had her arms wrapped around her knees and a mug of something on the step beside her that had stopped steaming a long time ago, and when the car came around the alders she stood up so fast she kicked it over.

Harry got out. He opened the back door. He unbuckled the car seat, every buckle, and lifted the baby out, blanket and all, and turned around.

Leah came down the steps.

Brady thought she was going to run. She came down the steps and across the gravel where the puddle used to be, fast, with her face doing something, and stopped in front of him, and looked down at the baby in his arms.

The baby looked up at her.

She blinked. Her little nose went, smelling. Her ears came up. Her eyes, green with the gold ring around them, went blurry and then went sharp, and found her mother's face, and stayed there.

And then she smiled.

Brady saw it. He was standing by the front fender with his hands in the pocket of the hoodie, and he saw it, plain as anything. Her tiny furry face scrunched up and her mouth opened and the corners of it went up, wide and gummy and lopsided and completely ridiculous, and her tail started going against the blanket.

Leah made a sound. Brady didn't know what kind of sound. He'd never heard Leah Clearwater make one like it.

"She—" Leah said. "Harry. Harry, she just—"

"Ja," said Harry. His voice had gone rough. "She did it on the beach this morning. I told her to save it for you. She's been saving it."

Leah took her out of his arms. She held her up against her face. She was laughing and crying at the same time, and the baby smiled again, right into her nose, and Leah laughed harder and turned around in a circle on the gravel with her.

On the porch, Charlie Swan had gotten up off the swing.

He came to the top of the steps with his beer and stood there looking down at all of it. At Leah turning in circles with a baby. At Brady Fuller by the fender, in a hoodie Brady was pretty sure Charlie had never seen anybody on the rez wear. At the Rolls-Royce. At Harry. Charlie looked at Harry for a long time, up, and up, and Brady wondered what he was seeing. A big guy with a beard, probably. A really big guy.

"So you're the guy," said Charlie.

"I'm the guy," said Harry.

Charlie nodded slowly. He took a sip of beer. He looked up at the corner of the house, at the gutter on the left side, running straight and level along the edge of the roof.

"Sue says I fixed her gutter," he said.

Harry looked at him. "Very handy," he said. "She said."

Charlie looked at the gutter for a while longer. Then he looked back at Harry.

"Yeah," said Charlie. "I did."

Behind him in the doorway, Sue put her hand over her mouth and went back inside.

Leah had stopped turning. She was standing in the middle of the drive with the baby against her shoulder, and her eyes had gone to Harry's coat. To the square bulge, and the old brown paper, and the black ribbon. Brady watched her face change. He watched her look at Harry, and Harry look back at her, and something go between them that Brady didn't get.

"You're late," she said.

"Ja. Sorry. Found a kitchen."

"Later?"

"Later," Harry agreed.

She looked at him a second longer. Then she looked over at Brady, as if she'd only just noticed him.

"Nine of them came," she said. "Jake and Embry and Quil and six of the new ones. I said no nine times." Her mouth twitched. "And then the car started up by itself in the yard and drove away with nobody in it. Right in front of them. Quil screamed."

Brady laughed. He couldn't help it. It came out of him like a hiccup.

"Who's Gerald?" said Charlie Swan, from the porch, to nobody.

Nobody answered him.

---

Brady went around behind the woodshed.

He took off the hoodie and the sweatpants and the socks and the sneakers and folded them, sort of, and put them on top of the woodpile, next to the gray Army blanket. Then he stood there in the cold, and breathed, and let it come.

Sam was there the second he was through. Like he'd been waiting by the door.

*Show me.*

Brady showed him.

All of it. He let it all go at once, the whole morning, from the blanket on his back to the bacon to the dogs. The cedar. The beach—the big wolf standing in the surf with his coat open, saying *Was that a smile?* Old Quil on his bench. The school, and the spruce, and the violets, and *Brady, come here, please.* The car with nobody in it. "Highway to Hell." The trailhead, and the wait, and the car setting its teeth against something that went up and down and stopped. *Fuller, B. Fifteen. In brown ink.*

And the last thing. The thing Brady couldn't stop seeing. A tall black shape coming back up an old railroad grade under bare maple trees, out of the dark of the woods by a lake where something had been hunting them for two months, with his coat buttoned to the collar over his daughter, walking like he was coming back from the store.

Humming.

Sam didn't say anything for a long time.

Brady could feel the others in there too, now. Jared. Collin, way up north. Paul. All of them, quiet, looking at it.

*Two on the school,* said Sam finally. *Starting tonight. Jared and Paul.*

*Paul's gonna be pissed,* said Collin.

*Paul's going,* said Sam. And then, after a moment, quieter, in a voice Brady had never heard him use: *Brady. Did he really say please?*

*Yeah,* thought Brady. *He said tell him I said please.*

Sam didn't say anything to that.

Brady lay down in the frost behind Sue Clearwater's woodshed with his chin on his paws and his pale ear pricked toward the house. He could hear them in there. The kitchen. Plates. Charlie Swan asking if anybody wanted another beer. Leah laughing. The baby, making that kettle noise. And under all of it, low, so low he felt it in the ground more than heard it, somebody humming.

Brady closed his eyes and listened to it.

He didn't know the song. Something slow. Something about stars.

He didn't fall asleep this time. But it was close.
