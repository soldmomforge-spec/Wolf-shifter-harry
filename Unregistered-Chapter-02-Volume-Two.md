# Volume Two

---

Sabine looked at him over the plastic lid of her coffee cup for long enough that Harry started to wonder whether she'd heard him.

"A straw," she said.

"Bendy one. With a lid."

"I am not going to ask."

"You'll have to ask eventually. It's on one of the forms."

She closed her eyes. She kept them closed for a slow count of three, the way she did in meetings when somebody from the Bulgarian delegation started a sentence with *with respect*. Then she opened them again, turned on her heel, and started giving orders.

She gave them in two languages at once, French to the pair in Confederation cloaks and English to everybody else, and she didn't raise her voice for either. The two in cloaks peeled off toward the ash heaps with their wands out and began walking a slow grid across the scorched ground, muttering, little threads of pale light trailing from their wand tips and sinking into the black rings of earth. The three in anoraks headed for the Cullens. The thin man with the tray of vials went after them at an anxious half-trot, the vials chiming against each other with every step and the tape measure in his other hand unrolling and rolling itself back up again like a nervous tongue. The one with the clipboard, a short, round-faced young woman with a bobble hat pulled down to her eyebrows, held it out in front of her at arm's length, and Harry could see the quill clipped to the top of it twitching, already writing down the colour of the snow.

The three Americans stayed where they were at the edge of the trees, in their long tan coats, looking at the field and then at each other.

"Ruth," Sabine said, without turning round.

The one in front came forward: a heavyset woman somewhere in her fifties with a grey bob under her fedora. She walked like a copper, Harry thought. Flat-footed, unhurried, eyes going everywhere. She stopped a polite distance off and looked at the black star in the snow, and the glassy fused dirt at the middle of it, and then up at Harry.

"Potter," she said.

"Investigator."

"We had a guy in the Seattle field office spend twenty minutes this morning bleeding into a teacup."

"I heard."

"He's fine. He's pissed, but he's fine." She looked at the ash heaps. She looked at them for a while. "That's the Volturi."

"Was."

"All of 'em?"

"All the ones in cloaks."

Ruth Calloway breathed out through her nose. It fogged. She looked at him again, and something went across her face, quick and complicated, that Harry had seen on a lot of faces over the years and had never found a good name for. Then it was gone, and she was a copper again.

"I'm gonna need a statement."

"You'll get one."

"Today."

"You'll get one today. After." He nodded past her toward the Cullens. "Start with the kid. Be nice. She's four months old and she's had a morning."

"I'm always nice."

"That's what I say," Harry said, "and nobody believes me either."

Calloway's mouth twitched. She went.

Sabine waited until the Americans were out of earshot—human earshot, which was a joke in this field, but it was the gesture that counted—and then she took Harry by the elbow and walked him three yards off to the side, away from the black star, onto a stretch of trampled snow where there was nothing at all to look at.

He put up the Muffliato before she could ask. He didn't lift a finger for it. It closed round the pair of them like a soap bubble, and he heard the faint, flat hum of it settle into the air on the far side, a sound like a wasp in a jam jar, which was all that any of the sharp-eared bastards in this clearing was going to get out of the next five minutes.

Sabine looked at the shimmer in the air. Then at him.

"Go on," she said. "Say it."

"An Unspeakable."

"Potter—"

"You sent an *Unspeakable*. For *this*." He jerked his head back at the field: the ash and the gold, the Americans in their hats, the vampires stood about in a long uneven line like a bus queue. "A shouting match in a car park. That's all this was ever going to be. Two lots of idiots in a field having a go at each other's mums. You've got a whole floor of Hit Wizards in Geneva, Sabine. A whole *floor*. I've been up there. They've got a pool table. They play Exploding Snap in the canteen and argue about the Harpies' back line and get paid more than me to do it. I've seen the scales. Somebody left the pay scales on the photocopier."

"They do not get paid more than you."

"Danger money. They get danger money. I don't get danger money, because officially I don't exist." He was pacing now, two steps one way and two steps back, inside the bubble. "Send Rasmussen. Rasmussen would've loved this. Rasmussen would've brought a camera and a packed lunch. Send Okonkwo. Send the twins. Send the Spanish bloke with the moustache, the one who did the thing with the kelpies in Lake Geneva—"

"Alvarez."

"Send *Alvarez*."

"Alvarez," Sabine said, "would be standing in that field right now with his own wand at his own throat, waiting for the little blonde girl to tell him which way to push it."

Harry stopped pacing.

"Rasmussen would be screaming on the ground," Sabine went on, quite calmly, and sipped her coffee. "Okonkwo would be blind and deaf and would not know which way was up. The twins would be standing beside the black-haired one, whose hand you would not take, and they would be telling him every secret the Confederation has, and they would be very happy to do it, because by then they would love him. The file named a girl who makes you feel you are burning alive. A boy who takes away every sense you have. A man who reads every thought you ever had with one touch. A woman who ties loyalties like knots and cuts the old ones. A tracker who cannot be hidden from by any charm we know of."

"Chelsea was hiding at the back with her husband. She never even came out front."

"And a Department of Mysteries assessment," Sabine said, over him, "which states, in writing, that in the opinion of the Department no Hit Wizard in the service of the Confederation would keep possession of his own mind for one full minute against the Volturi guard acting together. Your name is at the bottom of that assessment, Potter. You wrote it."

"I wrote it for a *hypothetical*."

"It stopped being hypothetical at four o'clock this morning, when the American desk woke me up to tell me there were a hundred of them in a field in Washington and the little seer in Seattle was bleeding out of his nose." She drank. "I sent the one man who would not need a second man to come and fetch him home afterward. That is all. You would have done the same."

He opened his mouth. He shut it again.

"I was on leave," he said.

"You were on leave *tomorrow*."

"I was on leave *starting* tomorrow. Three days. I had a rod. A proper one. Ron got me a fly rod for Christmas. I was going to go and stand in a river in Ireland in wellies and not catch anything for three days and not talk to anybody."

"You hate fishing."

"I wanted to hate it in peace."

Sabine looked at him over her cup. There was a tired kind of fondness in it, the kind his old Head of Department used to get when Harry came into his office with mud up to his knees. Then it went away, and she lowered the cup, and looked past him at the treeline.

He knew what she was going to say before she said it. He'd been waiting for it since she arrived.

"It is Tuesday," she said.

"I know what day it is."

"The moon is full on Thursday night."

"I know when the moon is, Sabine."

"Two days." She was still looking at the trees. At the grey wolf standing stiff-legged at the very edge of them with her tail clamped between her back legs and her eyes fixed on Harry. "If this—whatever this is, with the wolf—is not finished by Thursday, I do not know what it will do. You do not know. I would bet you a year's salary that no one in the Department knows. It may do nothing at all." She turned the cup round in her hands. "Or things may become—" She hunted for the word in English. She didn't like the one she found. "Weird."

"Weird."

"Strange. *Bizarre*. You have that word? Weird. You know what I mean."

He did know what she meant. He knew it better than she did, and he found that he'd put his hand flat over his breastbone without noticing, over the place where the line came in. Down at the bottom of the dark, something shifted its weight and settled again. He took his hand away.

"Sabine," he said. "Love. Right now, today, this minute, I could not give one single solitary toss if I spend the back half of the week as some furry's wet dream."

Sabine stared at him.

"I do not know what that means," she said.

"Good. Don't look it up."

"I am going to look it up."

"You'll regret it. You'll have to scrub your eyes out with a Scouring Charm." He shrugged. "Anyway, I'm a blast when I'm furry. Ask the kids. Teddy reckons it's the best night of the month. Last time there were six of them hanging off me in the garden at the Burrow and Molly made a cake. With candles. I don't even know what the candles were for, it wasn't anybody's birthday." He blew out a breath. "Thursday's Thursday. It's two days off. There's a lot of field between here and Thursday. I'll deal with it when I get there."

"That is your plan."

"That's my plan."

"That is a terrible plan."

"It's worked so far."

"It has *never* worked," Sabine said. "Not once. I have the files. I have *all* the files." She pinched the bridge of her nose with two fingers, the cup tipping dangerously in her other hand. "*Bon.* Fine. Thursday is Thursday. But you do not leave this peninsula until I say so. And you tell me the moment it does anything. Anything, Potter. If it so much as—"

"Itches."

"If it *itches*, I want an owl."

"You'll get an owl."

"I will get nothing, and on Friday morning I will read about it in the *Prophet*." She lifted her chin at the shimmer of the bubble. "Take this down. I have work to do. And you have—" Her eyes went to the treeline again, and back to him, and something at the corner of her mouth moved in a way he didn't like at all. "—a great deal of reading."

He let the Muffliato go. The hum of it stopped and the cold quiet of the clearing came back in: the scratch of the anorak girl's quill, the creak of the snow under the Registry team's boots, the low murmur of the Irish lot talking among themselves, the steady hiss of the black star cooling behind him. Two dozen vampire heads, which had all very carefully not been turned toward him, very carefully went on not being turned toward him.

He took a breath. He thought about it for maybe a second.

"Kreacher," he said.

The crack was a small one, as cracks went. Smaller than the Registry's had been. It still made half the clearing jump. Somewhere behind Harry a vampire hissed, and a wolf barked once, short and startled, and over by the Cullens a little girl's voice said, quite clearly and with enormous interest, "*Oh.*"

The house-elf stood in the snow by Harry's left boot. He came up to a bit past Harry's knee, and not much past it. He was older than he'd been when Harry first met him, which Harry wouldn't have thought possible; the skin of his face hung in soft grey folds like a melted candle, and the tufts of white hair sprouting from his enormous ears had grown so long and fine they lifted and drifted in the cold air like dandelion clocks. He wore a clean white tea towel knotted at one shoulder, with the Black family crest embroidered on the front in silver thread, and round his neck on a long chain, bumping against his chest, hung a heavy gold locket with a serpentine *S* set into it in green stones.

He looked up at Harry. He looked down at the snow. He looked out across the clearing, slowly, turning his whole body to do it: at the ash heaps and the black rings of earth and the gold glinting in the ash; at the vampires standing in their scattered line; at the wolves.

His nose wrinkled.

"Master," Kreacher said, in a voice like a bullfrog being slowly sat on, "has called Kreacher to a field full of dead things."

"Yeah. Sorry about that."

"*Bloodsuckers.*" The elf's lip curled back off a set of long yellow teeth. "Filthy, cold, unnatural *bloodsuckers*, standing about in the snow like they owns it, and dogs, *dogs*, great stinking dogs the size of carthorses, what would Kreacher's poor Mistress say if she could see—" He broke off. He'd seen the ash properly. He waddled two steps toward the nearest heap and peered at it, at the half-melted gold pendant lying in the grey, and then turned to look up at Harry with his huge watery eyes narrowed. "Master did this."

"I did."

Kreacher considered the ash a little longer. Then he gave a short, decisive nod.

"Good," he said.

"Glad you approve."

"Kreacher's old Mistress always said the Italians would come to a bad end. Wicked, greedy, *Continental* things." He sniffed again. Then he stopped sniffing, and went very still, and his ears came slowly up off the sides of his head until they were standing almost straight. He turned toward Harry. He sniffed once more, deliberately this time, a long slow draw of air with his nose a few inches from the leg of Harry's jeans.

Then he turned his head, slowly, like a compass needle finding north, and looked across the field at the grey wolf by the trees.

He looked at her for a long time. Then he looked back up at Harry.

"Master has—" Kreacher began.

"Don't."

"Kreacher was only going to observe that Master—"

"I know what you were going to observe. Don't observe it."

The elf's mouth closed. His ears stayed up. He did not, Harry noted, look anything like as disapproving as he'd looked about the vampires. He looked thoughtful. He looked, if Harry was being honest with himself, like somebody who'd just been handed a piece of very old and very satisfying gossip about a family he'd been feuding with for a century, and Harry didn't care for it one bit.

"I need a book," Harry said.

Kreacher's ears flicked. "Master has many books."

"I need one I haven't got. Geneva. The Confederation library, the closed stacks, third gallery up, the one with the brass rail. *Bonds of Soul and Shape.*" He held up the volume under his arm so the elf could see the spine. "This is Volume One. I need Volume Two. It'll be next to it on the shelf, or it'll be out on loan to some nosy sod from the Bulgarian delegation, in which case you have my permission to go and get it off him. Madame Oyelaran's on the desk. She'll want a chit."

"Kreacher does not need a *chit*."

"She'll want one anyway."

Sabine, who had been watching all this with her coffee cup halfway to her mouth, sighed. She put her hand into her grey coat and drew out a narrow slip of yellowed parchment. She touched her wand to it without looking, and Harry watched her signature write itself across the bottom in looping black ink and the Confederation's seal bloom underneath it in silver. She held it out.

Kreacher took it between two long fingers, at the very corner, the way you'd pick up a dead mouse by the tail. He held it away from his body and looked at it with his lip curled. Then he folded it once and tucked it into the knot of his tea towel.

"Anything else Master requires?" he said. "While Kreacher is fetching books for Master in *Switzerland*, with *chits*?"

"No. That's it. Cheers."

Kreacher didn't move.

He stood there in the snow with his ears up and his eyes on Harry's face, and he didn't Disapparate, and he didn't say anything.

"What?" said Harry.

"Master has not eaten."

"I've eaten."

"Master," said Kreacher, with deep and terrible patience, "has eaten one half of an egg sandwich from a machine at Heathrow airport at three o'clock this morning, standing up, and has put the other half in his pocket."

Harry put his hand into his jacket pocket. The left one. His fingers met cling film, and something cold and squashed underneath it.

He took it out and looked at it. It was half an egg sandwich. It was a good deal flatter than he remembered.

Off to his left, very faintly, he heard Carlisle Cullen make a small sound at the back of his throat.

"Kreacher will bring food," Kreacher said, and vanished with a crack before Harry could argue, and a little puff of snow went up where he'd been and drifted down again onto the toe of Harry's boot.

Harry looked at the sandwich.

Sabine looked at the sandwich.

"You still have the elf," she said.

"He's still got me, more like." He thought about putting it back in his pocket. He thought about Kreacher's face if it was still in there when he came back. He unwrapped it and ate it in three bites, standing in the middle of the field with the cling film balled in his other fist, and it tasted exactly like an egg sandwich that had spent eight hours and two continents in the pocket of a leather jacket.

Sabine watched him do it. She finished her coffee, turned the empty cup upside down, and it vanished.

"*Mon Dieu*, Potter," she said, for the second time that morning, and went to see about her Registry.

Harry swallowed, screwed the cling film into a tight little ball, and made that vanish too. Then he put his hands back in his pockets and turned round, and found that the man with the tape measure had reached the Cullens, and was bending down, with a great deal of care and the tape held out in front of him like a peace offering, toward the little girl on the back of the russet wolf.

The tape measure got to Renesmee before the man holding it did.

It slid out of his hand by itself, a long ribbon of yellowed cloth printed with little black numbers, and came across the last few feet of trampled snow in a slow wavering line, nosing through the air like the tendril of a vine. It stopped a hand's width from Jacob's muzzle.

Jacob's lips peeled back off his teeth.

The tape measure hung in the air for a moment, considering this. Then it reeled itself back into the man's fist with a fast little *zip*, and stayed there.

"Sorry," the man said. "Sorry. It does that. It's very keen." He held up both hands, the tray of vials in one and the coiled tape in the other, to show that he was a harmless person carrying harmless things. The vials chimed. "Ambrose Pellow. Confederation Registry of Beings. Non-Human and Part-Human, Northern Hemisphere, Western—well, it's a long name. It used to be longer. They took out *Beasts* in March after the centaurs complained." He smiled at Jacob, a nervous, crooked, hopeful smile, and then his eyes went up from Jacob to the little girl on Jacob's back, and the smile changed into something else. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, hello. Look at you."

Bella moved.

She did it before she'd thought about it. One instant she was standing at Edward's side with his hand at the small of her back, and the next she was between the man and her daughter, so close that he took a startled half-step backward and the vials jangled on the tray. The shield flexed in her head and she pulled it in tight around the three of them: herself, and Jacob, and Renesmee.

It hurt to do it. She hadn't expected that. The shield had come back when the Englishman let go of her, it had snapped back over everyone like a rubber band, but it didn't feel the way it had felt that morning. It felt like the skin over a fresh burn, thin and shiny and tight, and every time she flexed it something underneath stung.

She held it anyway.

"Bella," Carlisle said quietly, at her shoulder.

"I know."

"He isn't going to hurt her."

"I *know*." She didn't move. "I heard."

She had heard. Every word. She had heard the Englishman tell Carlisle that nobody was going to burn her daughter, and Maggie say that every word of it was true. She had heard *a number* and *a big book* and *once a year, maybe twice*. She believed it. That was the strange part. She believed it as much as she'd ever believed anything, and her body didn't care. Her body had spent four months getting ready to die for the child on Jacob's back, and it was not about to step aside for a man with a tape measure.

Pellow looked at her. He looked at her for a moment the way people at the hospital used to look at Carlisle when he was about to tell them something difficult, and then he did something she didn't expect. He crouched. He went right down on his heels in the snow, so that his head was lower than hers, lower than Jacob's, and he set the tray of vials down carefully on the crust beside him and laid the tape measure on top of it.

"Mrs. Cullen," he said. "Is it Mrs. Cullen?"

"Yes."

"Mrs. Cullen. I've got two of my own. Six and nine. When my youngest was born she came out blue, cord round her neck, and the Healer took her off to the corner of the room before I'd so much as seen her face, and I very nearly hexed him." He smiled up at her. "I'm not going to take her anywhere. I'm going to ask you some questions. I'm going to measure her, if you'll let me, and weigh her, if you'll let me, and I'd like one drop of blood, one, if you'll let me, and if you won't let me I'll write *declined* and the world will keep turning. That's all I'm here for." He glanced, briefly, past her shoulder, across the field. "He said to be nice. I'm always nice. I'm the nice one."

Bella didn't need to look to know where he'd glanced. The Englishman was standing where he'd been standing, a few yards from the black star, with his hands back in his pockets and whatever he'd been eating apparently gone. His face was turned toward the treeline.

"Edward?" she said.

She didn't take her eyes off Pellow. She didn't need to. Edward was already at her side.

"He means it," Edward said quietly. "He's thinking about his daughter. Rosie. And a cup of tea. And—" a pause, and a flicker of something that in another moment might have been amusement "—he's wondering whether Renesmee has ever seen a Puffskein. He thinks she'd like one."

"What's a Puffskein?"

"I have no idea. It's round. It hums."

Something tight in Bella's chest gave, very slightly.

On Jacob's back, Renesmee had leaned out sideways, holding on to his ruff with one hand, to get a better look at the crouching man. Her curls swung. Bella felt the small warm fingers of her other hand come to rest against her own neck, under her jaw, and the picture came at once, bright and urgent: the elf. The little grey wrinkled thing in the tea towel that had appeared out of thin air beside the Englishman and disappeared again the same way, its enormous ears, its long thin fingers, the tufts of white hair standing up off its ears like milkweed. And the question attached to it, plain as a tug on a sleeve. *What was that? What was it? Where did it go?*

"I don't know, baby," Bella said. "I don't know what it was."

The picture changed. The man in front of them, crouched in the snow. His tray of glass vials. His long yellow tape measure, and the way it had come curling out of his hand. *Can I?*

Bella closed her eyes.

Renesmee had never been afraid of anyone in her life. She had ridden on Garrett's shoulders and pulled Emmett's hair and fallen asleep in Zafrina's lap; she had shown pictures to Amun and to Benjamin and to the two Romanians, who had flinched from her as though she might bite. Bella had loved her for it and been terrified by it in equal measure. She had spent four months teaching her who to be afraid of. And every single person Bella had taught her to be afraid of was lying in a heap of grey ash on the far side of the field, steaming gently in the cold.

She opened her eyes. She let the shield ease, a fraction. Just enough.

"Ask him your question," she said.

Renesmee let go of Jacob's ruff, and before Jacob could so much as shift his weight she had swung one leg over his neck and slid down his shoulder like a child going down a slide, and landed on her feet in the snow beside Bella with the backpack bumping against the backs of her legs. Jacob made a sound. It was a sound Bella had heard him make before, mostly when Renesmee did something on the stairs. Renesmee ignored it. She walked the two steps to Pellow, with Bella's hand never more than an inch from her shoulder, and stopped in front of him, so that for once in her life, with him crouched like that, she was the one looking down.

She put her hand on his cheek.

Bella watched it happen. She'd watched it happen to so many people now. Their faces went blank first, the way faces go when somebody starts talking to them in a dark room, and then whatever Renesmee was showing them began to show on the outside. Surprise. Fear. Love.

Pellow's face went blank. Then it went soft. Then it broke open into a grin so wide it pushed his ears back.

"Oh, that's *marvellous*," he said. "Oh, that's—Ffion! Ffion, come here, she's just shown me—" He laughed. His hand had come up, very carefully, and was hovering a little way from Renesmee's, not quite touching it. "She's shown me a snowman. A snowman with a wolf's tail on it. Did you make that? Did you make a snowman with a tail?"

Renesmee nodded solemnly.

"Jake's," she said.

Behind them, Jacob made a low and long-suffering noise in his chest.

The bobble-hatted girl with the clipboard had come trotting over. Bella hadn't heard her coming, which said something about the state Bella was in. She was very young, younger than Bella had been at her wedding, and her round pink face was the face of somebody holding it together by her fingernails: Bella could hear her heart, quick and light, and could see the way her eyes kept sliding sideways toward Rosalie and Emmett and then jerking back. The quill on her clipboard was scribbling so fast it had begun to smoke.

"She communicates by touch," Pellow told her. "Direct image transfer. Voluntary, I think. Is it voluntary, Mrs. Cullen?"

"Yes."

"Can she do it to anyone?"

"Anyone," Bella said. "Even me."

She heard herself say it, and heard too late how much it gave away. *Even me.* She waited for one of them to ask what that meant.

Neither of them did. Ffion looked down at her clipboard, where the quill had just written something with a flourish and underlined it twice, and said in a small Welsh voice, "Oh, *sound*," and Pellow beamed at Renesmee as if she'd done something terribly clever, which, Bella supposed, she had.

It went on from there. It took twenty minutes. Bella would remember every second of it afterward with the terrible clarity she remembered everything with now, and none of it would seem real.

Pellow asked Renesmee's name, and Bella told him, and he said it back slowly to get it right—*Ren-ez-may*—and Ffion's quill wrote it down. He asked her date of birth, and Bella told him the date, and heard how close it still was. Ffion's quill hesitated, and wrote it down. He asked what she ate, and Bella said *blood, mostly, animal; she'll drink human if we let her, she likes it better, we try not to let her*, and *she'll eat food if she has to, she doesn't like it much, she likes eggs*, and the quill wrote all of that down too. The tape measure came out of his hand again, and Renesmee held perfectly still while it measured her, head to foot, arm span, the circumference of her head, and when it was done she reached up and patted it and it curled briefly round her wrist like a cat before going back into Pellow's fist. He flicked his wand at her once and a little set of numbers appeared in the air above her head in blue smoke, and Ffion copied them down, and Pellow said "*Hm*," and Carlisle, who had been standing silently at Bella's shoulder through all of it, leaned forward with his hands behind his back and said, "Does that include—" and Pellow said, "Bone density? Yes. Would you like a copy?" and Carlisle said "*Yes*," with such naked longing that Pellow laughed out loud.

The blood was the worst part. Bella had known it would be.

Pellow's instrument was a little silver thing off the tray, no bigger than a thimble, with a point on it as fine as a hair, and he held it out on his flat palm for Renesmee to look at before he did anything else. When she put her hand on his cheek again—*what's it for?*—he explained, out loud, so Bella could hear, that it was to find out who she was, all the little bits of her that came from Mummy and all the little bits that came from Daddy, so that if she ever got poorly a Healer would know what to do. Renesmee looked at it for a long moment. Then she held out her finger.

It took a single drop. Bella heard the tiny sound of the skin breaking and smelled the blood, sweet and bright and half like her own, and her throat did nothing at all, because it was Renesmee's, and it would always do nothing at all for Renesmee. A bead of red slid up into the thimble and the silver went faintly pink, and the little wound in Renesmee's finger had closed by the time Pellow set the thimble down.

"Brave girl," he said. "Braver than my son. He bit the Healer."

Renesmee looked pleased with herself.

Then the American came over.

She'd been hanging back until now, the heavy grey-haired woman in the fedora and the long tan coat, watching from a few yards off with her hands in her pockets while her two men went on toward the wolves. Now she came the rest of the way, and stopped beside Pellow, and nodded to Carlisle, and looked at Bella.

"Ruth Calloway," she said. "MACUSA. Magical Congress of the United States. I'm an investigator. I've got a couple of questions, ma'am, for my side of things, and then you can get this little one home and out of the cold."

"She doesn't feel the cold," Bella said.

"Lucky her. I do." Calloway took a small notebook out of her pocket. A real one, paper, with a pencil stuck through the spiral. "It's standard, it's on the form, I ask everybody. Are there any non-magical persons—any regular humans—who know what you are? Or what the kid is?"

Bella didn't move.

She didn't breathe, either, but she didn't need to; she hadn't needed to all morning. She stood there with her hand resting on Renesmee's curls and thought, very clearly, *Charlie*. Charlie in his chair in front of the game with a beer going warm in his hand. Charlie, who had watched Jacob turn into a wolf in front of him and then decided, with a kind of courage she still couldn't quite believe, that he didn't need to know anything else. *Need to know.* Charlie on Christmas Day with Renesmee on his knee, looking into her brown eyes, Bella's old eyes, and not asking.

Edward's hand had closed on her shoulder.

His eyes had gone past Calloway to nothing at all, narrowed on the air a few inches to the left of the investigator's face, the way they went when he was listening hard. Bella saw his jaw set. She felt his fingers tighten, and tighten again.

He bent his head toward her. He didn't move his lips. She barely heard him.

"She's thinking about a memory charm," he said. "They have a procedure. If someone knows, they take it out of them. Out of their *head*." His fingers dug into her shoulder. "She's done it hundreds of times. She doesn't even think about it anymore. It's paperwork to her."

Bella looked at Calloway's pencil, hovering over the page.

She could lie. Maggie would know. Maggie was standing twenty yards off with Siobhan and Liam, and Maggie had heard every word; Bella could see it from the tilt of the girl's head. Maggie wouldn't say anything. Bella was almost certain Maggie wouldn't say anything. But Calloway didn't need Maggie. Calloway was a police officer, and she was watching Bella's face, and Bella had always, always been a terrible liar.

She opened her mouth.

"Ruth."

The voice came from across the field. It wasn't loud. It came over the snow in that flat, carrying way it had, the way voices carry over water.

Calloway turned her head.

The Englishman hadn't moved. He was still standing near the black star with his hands in his pockets, still facing the trees. But he'd raised his voice, just enough.

"Skip that one," he said.

"It's on the form, Potter."

"It's on page four. I've done page four." He did look round then, briefly, and his green eyes went across the field and touched Bella's for a fraction of a second, and went away again. "Write *see Potter*."

Calloway looked at him for a long, flat moment. Then she looked back at Bella. Something moved behind her face; Bella didn't know what. Edward did. She felt his hand ease on her shoulder, slowly, one finger at a time.

"*See Potter*," Calloway said, and wrote it down. She underlined it. "You're gonna write that up too?"

"I'll write it up."

"Today?"

"Today, Ruth."

"Uh-huh." Calloway closed her notebook with a snap. She nodded to Bella, and to Carlisle, and after a moment's thought to Renesmee, who nodded gravely back. "Ma'am. Doctor. Young lady." And she went off across the snow toward the wolves, flat-footed and unhurried, with the notebook back in her pocket.

Bella stood very still.

He'd been in her head. Of course he knew about Charlie. He'd been in there long enough to know about Charlie and the island and the bendy straws and Jacob and all of it, everything she was, and he'd walked out again and left the door hanging off its hinges, and now he'd—

She didn't know what he'd done. She knew what it meant. It meant Charlie would go home tonight and watch the game and drink his beer, with all his memories exactly where he'd left them, including the ones of his granddaughter on his knee at Christmas. And it meant it was because of the man in the leather jacket, who had torn her open in front of everyone she loved and hadn't so much as apologised.

She'd wanted to hate him cleanly. She'd been looking forward to it.

"Edward," she said. "What's she thinking now?"

"Calloway?" Edward's voice was odd. "She's thinking that if Potter says *see Potter*, then it's handled, and it isn't her problem anymore. She's thinking—" He hesitated. "She's thinking she's glad it was him who came. They're all afraid of him, Bella. Every one of them. Their own people. The girl with the clipboard is so frightened of him she's been trying to stand behind Pellow the whole time. But Calloway's glad. She's thinking about something that happened in a war, in Britain, when he was very young. She's thinking about it the way people think about the moon landing." He shook his head slightly. "It's in pieces. I can't get all of it. Something about a man who died twice."

Bella looked across the field.

He was still looking at the trees.

Pellow packed up his vials. He gave Renesmee a card—a stiff little rectangle of parchment with a number on it in silver ink and, in one corner, a moving picture of something round and fluffy and blissful, humming faintly to itself—and told her to keep it somewhere safe, and Renesmee zipped it into the front pocket of her backpack with enormous care. Then he shook Carlisle's hand, and shook Bella's hand without flinching from the cold of it, and went off after Calloway toward the wolves with Ffion trotting at his heels.

Carlisle went after them. To help, he said. Bella suspected it meant asking the man three hundred questions about bone density.

Renesmee climbed back up onto Jacob. She did it without asking, the way she climbed onto furniture, and Jacob stood for it the way he always stood for it, but his eyes followed Pellow all the way across the field and his hackles didn't go down.

For the first time since the crack in the middle of the field, there was nobody standing in front of Bella asking her anything.

She turned to Edward.

He was already looking at her. He'd been looking at her, she thought, for a while. His face was the face she'd seen in the snow when the Englishman let go of her mind and the shield snapped back: blank and stunned and very far away, the face of a man who has been hit hard by something nobody else can see. He'd put it away while the Registry was there. Now it was back.

"Edward."

"I heard you," he said.

She'd known he had. She'd known it from the way he'd held her, kneeling in the snow, with her hands over her face. She'd felt it happen, in the half-second after the shield tore, before the Englishman went through her like a train: the sudden, terrible sense of a door standing open and wind coming in. And she had wanted, for one wild instant before everything else hit her, to turn round and look Edward in the face and *think* at him. *There. There I am. That's what it's like.*

She hadn't had time. The Englishman had been in there by then.

"How much?" she said.

"Eleven seconds." He said it straight away. He'd counted. Of course he'd counted. "From the moment the shield went until the moment it came back. Eleven seconds."

"What did you hear?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"Everything," he said. "All at once. You were thinking about Renesmee. You were thinking *Jacob, run*, over and over, the way you used to think about breathing, under everything else. You were thinking about Alice. About the lightning. About me." He stopped. "About Charlie. You thought about his face if someone told him you were dead. You thought about the island. You thought about the feathers." Something happened at the corner of his mouth, not quite a smile. "You're so *loud*. I always thought you'd be quiet. All these years I imagined it, and I always imagined it quiet. Like a library."

"I don't think it's usually that loud," Bella said. "I don't know. I was scared."

"I know you were." His fingers came up and touched her face, very lightly. "I could hear exactly how scared you were. I've always wanted to know. Every time you've been frightened, every single time, I've stood beside you and guessed. And for eleven seconds I didn't have to guess." He let his hand fall. "And then he was in there with me."

Bella looked at him.

"I could hear him going through you," Edward said. "I still can't hear *him*. Not one word. But I could hear you, and I could hear what was happening to you. It was like listening to someone go through a house, pulling out drawers. Fast. So fast. He went where he needed to go and took what he needed and didn't stop to look at anything else." He frowned slightly, as though that troubled him more than cruelty would have. "But you could feel every drawer."

She remembered every drawer.

"I'd have let you in," she said. "Someday. I'd have learned how. Zafrina thought I could, if I practised. I wanted it to be—" She stopped. "It was supposed to be mine to give you."

"I know."

"He took it."

"Yes."

"I hate him," Bella said, and heard how it sounded, how small and young, and didn't take it back.

Edward didn't argue. He looked out across the field at the Englishman standing alone by the black scar, hands in his pockets, face turned toward the trees, and was quiet for a long moment.

"He also," Edward said finally, "burned the Volturi."

"I know."

"And whatever he just did for Charlie."

"I *know*, Edward."

"You're right," he said. "I keep listing. I've been listing since he arrived. I can't hear him, so I keep listing what he's done, and trying to make it add up to something." He shook his head. "It doesn't add up. None of it adds up."

She looked at the Englishman too. She couldn't help it. Everybody kept looking at him; the whole clearing kept drifting back to him the way a compass needle drifts back to north, however hard anybody tried to look somewhere else. The Registry people did it, glancing over their shoulders while they worked. Rosalie did it.

And he kept looking at the trees.

Bella followed his gaze, and there was the grey wolf, still standing stiff-legged at the very edge of the treeline with her brother hovering a few yards behind her, her eyes still fixed on the man in the field as though she'd been nailed there.

"You said you'd tell me later," Bella said. "About Leah."

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

"She imprinted on him," he said.

Bella had known. She thought she'd known since the dead-eyed Volturi brother said *bound*; she thought perhaps she'd known since the moment Edward said *Oh, Leah* in that awful soft voice. But hearing it was something else. She looked at the grey wolf by the trees. She thought about Leah Clearwater in the Cullens' kitchen a month ago, hunched over a plate of eggs, snarling at anybody who came within three feet of her. She thought about Leah's face the only time Bella had ever seen it without the anger on it, at the bonfire on the beach the night Billy told the story of the third wife, when Leah had looked across the fire at Sam and Emily with her arms wrapped around her knees.

"On *him*?" Bella said.

"On him."

"She's never even *met* him—"

"None of them had ever met them before," Edward said. "You know that's how it works. One look." He looked at the grey wolf again, and his face did something complicated. "I've been in Leah's head for months, Bella. Ever since she followed Jacob. I know her better than she'd ever forgive me for. She hated the imprinting more than anything in the world. She hated it more than she hates us. She thought it was the thing that took Sam away from her. And she thought—" He stopped.

"What?"

"She thought she couldn't. She was sure of it. She's believed for a long time that something in her was broken. That she was a dead end. That it would never happen to her, because there was no reason for it to." He shook his head slowly. "She was relieved about that, in a terrible way. It was the one thing she was sure of."

Bella didn't say anything.

"And there's something else," Edward said, more quietly. "When he made that sound. The growl."

"Indigestion," Bella said, without meaning to.

Edward's mouth twitched, very slightly. "When he made that sound," he said, "every wolf on this field went quiet. Inside. All of them. I've never heard anything like it. It was like watching a room full of dogs when the big dog walks in. Every one of them. Sam. Paul. Seth. *Jacob*." He glanced at the russet wolf, who had gone still at the sound of his own name and pricked one ear toward them. "For about four seconds, nobody in either pack was thinking anything at all. Except—"

"Except Leah."

"Except Leah," Edward said. "Leah was thinking *there you are*."

Bella looked at him.

"In his voice," Edward said. "That's what the sound said, Bella. It came out of him, and she heard it say *there you are*, and something in her answered *yes* before she could stop it." He let out a slow breath. "I've never heard her think anything with less anger in it. Not once in all these months. Then the anger came back, all of it, all at once, and she turned round and tried to walk into the woods, and couldn't."

On the far side of the clearing, the grey wolf turned.

She did it suddenly, pivoting on her haunches the way she had before. Bella saw her brother's sandy head come up in alarm. But the grey wolf didn't try to run this time. She went a few yards into the trees, no further, into the shadow under the first big firs where the snow lay thinner, and stopped behind a thick grey trunk, so that from the field only her tail showed. Then not even that.

Seth whined, high and anxious, and didn't follow.

"What's she doing?" Bella said.

Edward was quiet a moment, listening.

"Something stupid," he said. "I think."

Across the field, the Englishman had turned his head toward the trees as well.

---

The quiet came down on her like a bucket of cold water.

She always forgot about it. Every single time. She'd stand in the trees with the heat rolling up her spine and her bones folding back into themselves, and the last thing on her mind would be the quiet, and then she'd be on two legs in the snow and it would just be *there*. No Jacob. No Seth. Nobody at the back of her skull watching her think. The whole roaring, crowded room of the pack shut off at once, like somebody had slammed a door on a party, and then nothing but her own breath and her own heart and the wind going through the firs.

She used to hate the quiet. Back in Sam's pack, with Sam in her head all day and *Emily, Emily, Emily* running under every thought he had like a creek under a road, the quiet had been the only place she could go to cry without everybody watching. She had hated it for that. She had loved it for that.

She stood in it now with her bare feet in the snow and her hands shaking.

The cold didn't bother her. It never did. She ran at a hundred and eight, they all did, and the snow under her feet felt like nothing much, like a tiled floor. She was shaking anyway. She crouched and fumbled at the leather cord around her left ankle, and her fingers were stupid and clumsy and wouldn't work the knot, and she swore at them out loud, in her own voice, which sounded strange and small after an hour of nothing but snarling. The knot came loose. She shook out the bundle: a pair of cut-off jeans gone soft and white at the hems, and a black tank top with a hole under one arm. She pulled them on. The denim was cold from being tied against the snow, and it was the first thing all morning that had felt like anything.

She stood up.

Through the trees, past the grey trunk she'd put between herself and the field, she could see the clearing. White snow, grey sky. The black wet rings where the cloaks had been, and the ash, and the bloodsuckers standing around in their little huddles. The man with the tape measure walking away from the Cullen kid. And—

She didn't look at him. She made herself not look at him. She looked, instead, very deliberately, at Sam.

He was standing in the middle of his pack on the far side of the clearing, all of him, the big black bulk of him, with Paul on one side and Jared on the other. He'd turned his head toward the place in the trees where she'd gone. Of course he had. He'd been looking at her since the dead-eyed one said *bound*. She'd felt it, even across two packs, even through the noise. She'd felt his eyes on the back of her neck the way she'd felt them for two years: every time she walked into a room he was in, every time she went down First Beach and he and Emily were sitting together on a log, every time she phased in his pack and had to listen to him think about Emily's mouth.

She looked at him now, through the trees. She looked hard. And she went looking for it the way your tongue goes looking for the hole where a tooth got pulled, the soft sore place where something used to be. She knew exactly where it lived. She'd been carrying it for two years. She knew its shape better than her own face. The way her stomach turned over. The way her throat closed. The hot hard ache right under her breastbone that came every time she saw him, that came at night, that came when anybody so much as said his name.

She went looking for it.

It wasn't there.

She looked harder. She stood there in the snow in her cut-offs and stared through the trees at the man she'd loved since high school, the man she'd thought she was going to marry, the man who had walked out of her life the morning he saw her cousin's face and had never once explained it in a way that made any sense. She looked at him and waited for her stomach to turn over.

He was a big black wolf. That was all. A big black wolf in a field. She could look at him the way she'd look at a tree.

All of it. Gone. Two years of it. Every night she'd lain awake hating Emily, and hating herself for hating Emily, wishing she was dead, wishing *he* was dead, wishing more than anything in the whole world that she could just stop. Just *stop*. Wake up one morning and look at Sam Uley and feel nothing. She'd begged for it. She'd prayed for it in the dark, when she didn't believe in anything to pray to. She'd have given anything.

And here it was. And she hadn't even noticed it go.

She'd been too busy, out there in the field, staring at a stranger.

Leah put her hand flat against the trunk of the fir. The bark was rough and cold under her palm and smelled of sap and snow, and she held on to it, and found that she was laughing. It was a bad laugh. It came out of her in short ugly hiccups that hurt her chest, and she couldn't stop it. She stood there and laughed at the black wolf through the trees until her eyes stung.

*There. You wanted it gone. It's gone.*

*Happy now?*

The laugh ran out. She wiped her eyes on the back of her wrist, hard.

The thing was, it was so *stupid*. That was the part she couldn't get round. She'd been so sure. She'd had it all figured out, the one thing in her whole messed-up life she'd been sure of. Imprinting was about the line. Everybody knew it, even the elders, even if nobody said it out loud: it was about babies. Strong kids with the gene. Keeping the wolves going. And Leah had known for months that she was never going to have babies, because she'd stopped bleeding the first month after she phased and she'd never started again. Her body had made a decision without asking her. A dead end. That was what she was. The girl who should never have phased. And the one, single, solitary upside of being a dead end was that this was never going to happen to her. Nothing was ever going to reach into her chest and pull her out of her own life and hand her to a stranger. She was safe. She'd been so sure she was safe.

She'd been standing at the end of the line this morning working out which of the bloodsuckers she'd go for first.

And then one look.

She let go of the tree.

There was something else. She'd known it since the doctor walked out into the field and asked the man his name. She'd heard it from the end of the line, with the wolf's ears, every word, as clear as if he'd been standing next to her. She'd heard it, and felt Seth hear it, felt it land in her little brother at exactly the same instant it landed in her; and then she'd shoved it somewhere she didn't have to look at it, because she'd had about nine other things to scream about at the time.

*Potter. Harry Potter.*

Her dad had been called Harry.

Harry Clearwater. Big laugh. Bad knees. Worst jokes on the rez. Charlie Swan's fishing buddy for twenty years. He'd taught her to gut a fish on the end of the dock when she was six, his big hands over her small ones on the knife. He'd been standing right in front of her the first time she phased, the very first time, when her body came apart at the seams without any warning at all, and his heart had stopped.

Two years ago. Nearly.

Her mom still said his name sometimes, in the kitchen, when she forgot. *Harry, can you—* And then stopped.

Now everyone was going to say it. All the time. Her mom was going to have to say it. Seth was going to say it every day, probably, in that stupid bright puppy voice of his. *Harry did the lightning. Harry's got a book. Harry this, Harry that.* And Leah was going to have to stand there and hear it, over and over, with that rope tied through the middle of her chest, pulling.

She stood there a while longer in the snow.

Then she turned round and walked out of the trees.

Seth was still waiting at the edge of them, where she'd left him. His sandy head came up so fast when he saw her that his ears flopped. He whined, high and questioning, and danced a step toward her, and then a step back.

"Stay," she said.

He whined again. Louder.

"I said *stay*, Seth. I mean it." She didn't look at him. "I'm not running off. Where would I go?"

That stopped him. She felt him stop. He couldn't hear her anymore, not with her like this, but he didn't need to; he was her brother, he'd known her his whole life. He sat down in the snow with a soft *whuff*, and stayed.

She walked out into the field.

She felt it go across the clearing ahead of her. A ripple. Heads turning. One after another this time, all the way across, the bloodsuckers and the wizards and the wolves, as they noticed the tall girl in cut-offs and a black tank top walking barefoot across the snow in the middle of January. One of the Registry women in an anorak stopped with her wand halfway raised and gaped at her. Somebody in Sam's pack barked. Over by the Cullens, the big one, Emmett, said "Oh, *hell* yes," with deep satisfaction, and somebody shushed him.

She didn't look at any of them.

She looked at him.

It was easy. That was the worst part. She'd braced herself to drag across the field one step at a time with every muscle locked, the way she'd dragged herself back to the trees an hour ago. Every step was easier than the one before, like walking downhill. Her feet wanted to go faster. The rope ran out of her chest across the snow, pulling, steady and patient, just *there*, the way the ground was there under her feet; and the closer she got the less it pulled and the more it just sat. Settled. Like something lying down.

She hated it. She hated it so much.

He'd watched her come out of the trees. He watched her all the way. He was standing a couple of yards from the black scar where the Romanian had been, with the huge old book tucked under one arm and his other hand in his jacket pocket, and he didn't move as she came. His face didn't do anything, either, until right at the end, when she was close enough to see the stubble on his jaw and the green of his eyes and the place on his collar where the leather had worn pale; then his eyebrows went up. Just slightly.

She stopped about six feet away.

Up close, the heat came off him. She could feel it on her bare arms, on her face. She'd been around heat like that every day of her life since she phased: Jacob, Seth, Sam, the whole pack, all of them burning. It had never once occurred to her until this exact second that there was nobody else in the world who did. No human. Nobody she'd ever met. And here he was, a stranger, an English guy in a field, running as hot as she did.

Up close, he smelled the same as he had from the end of the line. Earth. Pine. Wet stone. And under that, deep down, the other smell. The one that made her hackles want to lift.

She had no hackles. She was on two legs. They tried to lift anyway; she felt the skin crawl up the back of her neck.

"Morning," he said.

"Fix it."

He looked at her.

"Whatever it is," Leah said. "Whatever you did. Fix it. Take it off me. Undo it."

"Can't."

"Can't or won't?"

"Can't." He said it the way he'd said everything she'd heard him say all morning, flat and plain and in no hurry. "Don't know how. Don't know what it is yet. I didn't do it, if that helps."

"It doesn't help."

"Didn't think it would." He shifted the book under his arm. "Volume One's useless. Five lines. Two of them say *insufficient data*. I've sent for Volume Two."

"You sent for a *book*."

"I sent for a book."

"You—" She heard her voice crack, and hated it, and drove straight through it. "You set thirty people on *fire*. With your *fingers*. You called lightning out of a clear sky and blew a guy into nothing, and you tore through Bella's head like it was wet tissue, and you're standing here telling me you can't—that you've sent for—"

"Thirty-two," he said.

"What?"

"Thirty-two. In cloaks. And *people*'s a bit of a stretch."

She stared at him.

He stared back, with no apology anywhere on his face. He looked tired, mostly, the kind of tired that had nothing to do with sleep, and underneath that, if she was honest, he looked about as fed up as she felt.

"What are you?" she said.

"Tired."

"That's not an answer."

"Bloke from London," he said. "Works for the government. Had half an egg sandwich for breakfast. That's about all you'll get out of me before lunch."

"That noise you made."

"Indigestion."

"Bullshit."

"Yeah," he agreed.

She waited. He looked at her a moment longer, and then he looked away, out across the field toward the trees she'd come out of. His jaw worked once, slowly, like he was chewing something over.

"It was me," he said finally. "Something in me. You were snarling at it and it liked that. It answered. I didn't tell it to." He looked back at her. "That's honest. That's all I've got on it, and it's all I'm doing on it, today or any other day. You can stop asking."

"It made my tail go *down*."

"I noticed."

"*Everybody* noticed."

"Emmett definitely noticed," he said, and something happened at the corner of his mouth, in the place a smile would go if he'd let one.

She wanted to hit him. She wanted to hit him so badly her hand curled into a fist at her side all by itself. She thought about it. She thought about drawing back and putting everything she had into his stupid stubbled jaw, the way she'd wanted to hit Paul every single day for a year. She thought about it in detail.

Her arm wouldn't do it.

She felt it not do it. It was like the edge of the trees again. She told her arm to move and her arm hung there at her side with her fist on the end of it, heavy and stupid, and stayed where it was.

His eyes went down to her fist. Then back up to her face.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "I wondered about that."

"Shut up."

"All right."

"I'll find a way round it," she said. "I'll find a way round all of it. I'm not—I'm not *Emily*. I'm not some little doe-eyed—I'm not going to *follow* you around. I'm not going to make you *muffins*. I don't care what it does to me, I'm not going to—"

"Didn't ask you to."

"*Good.*"

"Don't like muffins."

"*Good.*"

They looked at each other.

A little way off she could hear Seth whining, very softly, from the edge of the trees. She could hear the scratch of the anorak girl's quill. From the Cullen side of things she could hear a low murmur of voices, and over the top of them, crisp and clear and delighted, the loud nomad with the ponytail, Garrett, saying, "Five on the girl," and his blonde telling him to shut his mouth.

"Leah," the man said.

She went stiff.

"Got it out of Bella's head," he said. "Sorry. Seemed a bit rude to keep calling you Lassie."

"You called me Lassie twice."

"Twice. Right." He took his hand out of his pocket and held it out to her, a big square hand, palm sideways, like two people meeting at a bus stop. "Harry," he said. "Seeing as we're doing this."

She looked at the hand.

She'd heard it already. She'd heard it from the end of the line; she'd stood in the trees and made herself think it all the way through; she'd braced for it the whole walk across the field. It didn't matter. It hit her anyway, right in the place under her breastbone where the rope came out, so hard and so fast that she flinched. Her whole body jerked back half an inch, as if he'd swung at her.

His hand stayed where it was. His eyes narrowed, just slightly. He'd seen it. Of course he'd seen it. He'd heard her heart jump; he had the ears for it, she could tell by now, she'd watched him turn his head at things across the field that no human could have heard.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing."

"Your heart just went like a—"

"*Nothing.*" She looked at his hand. She didn't take it. "My dad's name was Harry."

He didn't say anything for a moment.

"Was," he said.

"He died. Two years ago. Heart attack." She stopped there. She was not going to say the rest. She was not going to stand in the middle of a field full of bloodsuckers and wizards and tell a stranger with a rope through her chest that she'd been the one standing in front of him when it happened. That she'd been the last thing her father ever saw, and what she'd been at the time. She shut her mouth on it so hard her teeth clicked.

He looked at her. Then he let his hand drop, slowly, back to his side.

"Sorry," he said.

That was all. Just the one word, the way you'd say it if you'd stood on somebody's foot, and then he stopped talking. At the funeral people had said *I'm sure he was a great guy*, and *that must be so hard*, and *I know how you feel*, and every one of them had made her want to scream. He said *sorry* and shut up.

It shouldn't have helped. She didn't know why it helped.

"Don't," she said anyway.

"All right."

"Everybody's going to say it now. Your name. All the time." She heard her voice go thin and couldn't stop it. "My mom's going to have to say it."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Could go by Potter," he said. "Most people do."

Leah opened her mouth. She had no idea what she'd been going to say.

Something cracked beside them, loud and sharp, and she jumped about a foot in the air.

The little grey thing from before was standing in the snow beside them. It came up past his knee and to about the middle of her thigh, and it had an enormous wicker hamper held over its head in both arms and a book the size of a paving slab clamped under one armpit. The hamper was bigger than it was. It didn't seem to care. It set the hamper down in the snow with a grunt, dropped the book on top with a heavy *thump*, straightened up, turned round, and saw her.

Its ears went straight up.

Leah took a step back. She couldn't help it. The thing had the biggest eyes she'd ever seen on anything, huge and wet and pale and bulging, and they were fixed on her, and it was looking her over the way her grandmother used to look over the meat at the butcher's in Port Angeles: slowly, carefully, all over.

Then it bowed.

It went all the way down. It folded itself in half at the waist and kept going, until the tip of its long thin nose was touching the snow and its ears hung down either side of its head like a pair of wet dishcloths, and it stayed there.

"What—" said Leah. "What is it *doing*?"

"Kreacher," the man said. "Up."

"Kreacher is only greeting—"

"You're greeting nobody. *Up*."

The thing came up. Slowly. With enormous dignity and its nose in the air, and a little crust of snow on the end of its nose that it didn't bother to wipe off. It looked at the man with deep reproach.

"Kreacher," the man said to Leah, as if that explained anything. "House-elf. He's mine. Or I'm his, depends who you ask. He's about a hundred and forty and he doesn't like anybody, so don't take it personally."

"He just *bowed* at me."

"Yeah. Don't take that personally either."

Kreacher was looking at her again. He looked at her bare feet in the snow, and her cut-offs, and her tank top with the hole under one arm, and her cropped hair, and her face. He took his time about it. Then he turned to the man and said, in a low, gravelly, carrying voice that he obviously thought was a whisper, "Master's lady is too thin."

"Kreacher—"

"Too *thin*, Master. Look at her. Kreacher can see her collarbones. Kreacher's old Mistress would never have let a lady of the house go about with—"

"I'm not his *lady*," Leah said.

Kreacher turned and looked up at her with his enormous eyes.

"Kreacher," he said, "sees what Kreacher sees."

Then he turned his back on both of them, flipped open the lid of the hamper, and began unpacking it into the snow.

Except that nothing went *on* the snow. Leah noticed that after the first few seconds. He snapped his long fingers and a tartan blanket unrolled itself across the crust, and when she looked closer the snow under it wasn't wet; the blanket sat on top of it as dry as if it had been spread on a lawn in August. Then he started taking things out. A pie, round and tall and golden-brown, steaming so hard in the cold air it looked like it was on fire. A whole roast chicken on a blue-and-white plate. Half a ham. A loaf of bread as long as Leah's forearm, a wedge of yellow cheese, a jar of something brown and lumpy, a jar of pickled onions, a great steel flask, a stack of plates, knives and forks rolled up in a napkin, and a dish with a cloth over it that smelled of hot sugar and syrup and something toasted.

And two cups. Two plates. Two of everything.

The man looked at the second cup.

"Two cups," he said.

"Kreacher is not a *fool*," said Kreacher, without turning round.

Leah's stomach growled.

It was loud. It was so loud that, across the field, she heard Emmett snort. Her face went hot. She hadn't eaten since five o'clock that morning, when she'd stood in the Cullens' kitchen and wolfed down a dozen of Esme's eggs standing up with her back to the room, and she'd been in wolf form for six hours since, and the wolf burned through food like a forest fire. She'd been starving for hours. She'd just been too busy to notice.

The man was looking at her.

"Don't," she said.

"Didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"I was about to say there's a whole chicken," he said. "And I can't eat a whole chicken. Well. I can. But I'd rather not with you stood there watching me like a dog at a barbecue."

"Did you just call me a *dog*?"

"I said *like* a dog. It's a simile. They teach those in school." He dropped down, all at once, and sat cross-legged on the edge of the blanket with the great book laid down beside him, and picked up a plate, and Kreacher put a slab of pie on it before he'd finished picking it up. "Sit down, Leah. Eat something. You can go back to hating me after. I'll still be here."

That was the problem, she thought. That was exactly the problem.

She stood there for a long moment in the snow.

Then she sat down. On the far side of the blanket from him, as far away as it would let her, with her knees pulled up and her back very straight. Kreacher handed her a plate without a word. It had a chicken leg on it, and a slab of the pie, and a thick piece of buttered bread, and a pile of the ham. She looked at it.

"Eat," said Kreacher. "Lady is too thin."

She ate.

She ate the chicken leg in about four bites, and then the pie, and the pie was so good she nearly made a noise out loud, rich dark gravy and soft beef and little pieces of something that might have been kidney, and she didn't care; and then the ham and the bread, and Kreacher took the plate out of her hands while she was still chewing and gave it back to her full again. She didn't look up. She didn't look at the man. She could hear him eating, across the blanket. He ate the way the pack ate: steadily, without stopping, without talking, with the total concentration of somebody feeding a fire that never went out. Half the pie. The other chicken leg. Both wings. A heap of ham. A hunk of cheese the size of his fist. Kreacher kept filling his plate and he kept emptying it.

She looked up at last, chewing.

"You eat like Paul," she said.

He looked at her over a chicken bone. "Paul's the silver one?"

"Yeah."

"Laughing like a hyena earlier."

"That's Paul."

"Then I'll take that as an insult," he said, and went on eating.

She didn't laugh. She was very careful not to laugh. She ate another slice of bread so she wouldn't.

Kreacher poured the tea. It came out of the steel flask brown and strong and steaming, and he put milk in the man's without asking and then stood holding the jug over Leah's cup, looking at her, waiting. She said, "Uh. Yeah. Sure," and he put milk in, and three spoons of sugar without being told, and handed it to her with both hands. She held the cup. She didn't feel hot things much anymore, but she felt that; it came through the china into her palms, and the smell of it came up in the steam, and for a second it smelled so much like her grandmother's kitchen that she had to look away.

"The Bulgarian," Kreacher said, "had the book."

The man looked up. "Course he did."

"The book was not on the shelf. The lady at the desk said the book was with the Bulgarian, in the reading room, and the Bulgarian had been reading it for six weeks, and the Bulgarian would not give it back."

"What did you do to the Bulgarian?"

"Kreacher showed the Bulgarian the chit."

"And?"

Kreacher considered this. "And then Kreacher took the book."

"Is he all right?"

"The Bulgarian," said Kreacher, "is in the reading room." He paused. "He is upside down. But he is in the reading room."

The man closed his eyes briefly. Opened them again. "Fair enough," he said, and put down his plate, and wiped his hands on his jeans, and reached for the book.

Leah watched him pull it into his lap. It was a twin of the first one: same cracked brown leather, same brass caps on the corners, same faded gold on the spine. *BONDS OF SOUL AND SHAPE.* *Vol. II. Kraken-Ties to Zouwu.* He opened it at the back and ran his finger down the index, muttering under his breath, and stopped.

She found that she'd leaned forward. She made herself lean back.

He turned the pages. They were thick and creamy and crowded with tiny black handwriting. She could read it from where she sat; she'd been able to read the small print on a cereal box across a room since she phased. She didn't want to read it. She read it anyway. *Lamia-bonds.* *Life-debts (wizarding).* *Life-debts (goblin; contested).* *Mate-bonds (Veela).* *Merrow-ties.* He turned past all of them without slowing down, faster and faster, until he stopped quite suddenly about two-thirds of the way through and smoothed the page flat with the side of his hand.

She saw the heading from across the blanket.

*Bonds, reaching the beast.*

He read it. She watched his eyes go down the page. She watched them stop, and go back up, and come down again slower. Then he sat back a little, and didn't say anything, and didn't move.

"What," she said. "What does it say?"

He didn't answer for a second. Then he turned the book round on the blanket and pushed it across to her.

She hadn't expected that. She looked at it. Then she put down her tea, and pulled the book the last few inches toward her, and bent her head over it.

The writing was small and old-fashioned and slanted, with long tails on the letters, but she could read it.

*Bonds, reaching the beast.*

*Of all the bonds treated in this Compendium, these are the least understood and the most to be feared. They arise where a bond formed upon one party (by imprint, by claim, by the mating-instinct of the shifting kinds, or by such like) comes to rest upon another who carries within him a beast of his own, by whatever means it came there. In such cases the beast may take up the bond as its own. The parties will know that it has done so by these signs: that the bond, which before ran in one direction only, now runs in both; that each party may feel the other at a distance, more or less keenly according to the strength of the bond; and that the beast in the one will answer to the beast in the other, in voice or in posture, without the leave of either party and very frequently against the express wishes of both.*

Leah stopped reading. She read the last line again.

*Without the leave of either party and very frequently against the express wishes of both.*

She thought about her tail going down between her legs in front of two packs and two dozen bloodsuckers and the Cullens and *Emmett*. She thought about the sound that had come out of his chest, slow and deep and pleased. *There you are.*

She kept reading.

*The beast, having once taken hold, will not readily let go; no charm, potion, or working has yet been shown to loosen its grip. It is the testimony of all the recorded cases, which are few, that the bond is drawn to its fullest at the full of the moon, when the beast in the bound party lies nearest the surface. The parties are on no account to be separated at that time. In the two instances known to this author where a separation was attempted (Brașov, 1702; the Company's post at York Factory, upon Hudson's Bay, 1811), the consequences were—*

The rest of the page wasn't there.

It had been torn out, roughly, along a long ragged diagonal that ran from the middle of the left-hand margin to the bottom right-hand corner, so that the second half of the sentence was gone and everything after it too. Somebody had done it a long time ago. The torn edge had gone soft and brown with age.

In the margin beside it, in faded green ink, in small neat letters, somebody had written a single word.

*Don't.*

Leah stared at it.

She turned the page with fingers that didn't feel like hers. The back of it was the back of the torn half, nothing; but the page after that was whole. It started in the middle of a sentence about somebody in Prague, and she skipped it, and then her eyes snagged on a paragraph near the bottom, set a little apart from the rest, and stopped there.

*No dissolution of a bond reaching the beast has ever been recorded. This author has been unable to determine whether this is because none has been achieved, or because none of the parties, having once come to the fullness of the bond, has wished it.*

She read it three times.

Then she shoved the book away from her, hard, so that it slid back across the tartan and bumped against his knee.

"I'll wish it," she said.

He looked at her.

"I'll *wish* it," Leah said again. Her voice came out rough. "Whatever *fullness* is, whatever it means, I'll wish it. I'll wish it every day. Every single day for the rest of my life. They can put *that* in their stupid book."

He looked at her for a long moment across the blanket, with the book lying against his knee. Then he nodded, once.

"Noted," he said.

"Good."

"I'll write it in the margin if you want. Under *Don't*."

"Shut up."

He picked up his tea and drank some.

Leah looked away from him, out over the field. At the black star in the snow. At the Cullens standing in their huddle, all pretending not to watch. At the Registry people going back and forth with their clipboards. At the sky, low and flat and white.

"The full moon," she said, after a while. "What happens at the full moon?"

"Dunno."

She looked at him sharply.

"I don't," he said. "That's honest. I don't know what this does at the full moon. I don't know what it does on any day of the week. I found out the same minute you did. I've just done more reading."

"When is it?"

"Thursday."

"How do you know that off the top of your head?"

He drank his tea.

"I'm English," he said. "We talk about the weather."

She looked at him for a long time. He didn't look back. He drank his tea and looked out across the field with his face giving away absolutely nothing at all. And Leah, who had spent two years in a pack where nobody could keep a single thought to themselves, who had heard every lie Paul ever told and every one of Jared's daydreams and every filthy thing Sam ever thought about Emily, sat on a tartan blanket in the snow and understood that she was sitting next to the first person in two years whose head she couldn't get into, and that she was going to have to work him out the slow way. The way people used to. By watching.

She picked up her own tea again. It had gone lukewarm. She drank it anyway.

Kreacher took the cloth off the dish with the hot-sugar smell. Underneath was a tart, golden and glistening, cut into slices.

"Treacle," Kreacher said, with deep satisfaction, and put the biggest slice on the man's plate, and the second-biggest on hers.

Alice had lost the field at eleven minutes past ten.

She knew the exact minute because she had been watching it so hard. For weeks there had been nothing else to watch. Every road she looked down ended in the same clearing, the same snow, the same white sky; she'd run through Brazil and up through the jungle with that field in front of her eyes like the light at the end of a tunnel, and every hour she'd looked at it again, and every hour it had shifted a little. Aro deciding. Caius deciding. The black wedge moving forward across the snow, and the fire, and the end of everyone she loved, over and over, in a hundred variations. Each one a little different. All of them the same.

Then she'd found Nahuel, and the fire had begun to waver.

She'd watched it waver all the way north. On the plane. In the rented car. On foot for the last forty miles, flat out through the forest with Jasper on one side of her and Kachiri on the other and Huilen and Nahuel coming behind, the first time in her life she'd had to slow down for anyone with a heartbeat. She'd watched Aro's future wobble and split: the black wedge hesitating, Aro's hand going out to Nahuel's palm, Aro's face changing, the fire drawing back, coming back, drawing back again. It had been so close. It had been balanced on the edge of a knife.

And at eleven minutes past ten, it had gone out.

All of it. Every cloak, all at once, like a hand closing over a row of candles. Aro's future had simply stopped. So had Caius's, and Marcus's, and Jane's and Alec's and Demetri's and Felix's, and the thirty-odd others she'd been watching for weeks until she knew the shape of each of them the way she knew the shape of her own hands. In the space of one step. She'd been in the air, jumping a creek, when it happened, and she'd come down so badly on the far bank that she'd gone to her knees in the water.

Jasper had hauled her up. *Alice. Alice, what is it, what did you see—*

She hadn't known how to tell him. She still didn't.

Because her family hadn't gone out. Their futures were all still there, Carlisle's and Esme's and Edward's and Bella's and Rosalie's and Emmett's, going on into the afternoon and the evening and the next day. But they were *wrong*. They went on around a hole. There was a gap in the middle of every one of them, a place where something stood that she couldn't see, and the hole moved when they moved and turned when they turned, and none of them could look away from it.

She'd thought it was the wolves. For a few wild seconds she'd thought the wolves had done something. The wolves were always holes. But the wolves were fog, soft and shapeless, smudging everything around them. This had edges.

She'd run harder than she'd ever run in her life.

Two minutes later, the Romanians had gone flat. One of them out, like a lamp. The other one going on into the future, but grey, grey all the way down, every road he walked as flat and empty as a beach in winter.

She hadn't stopped to tell Jasper about that one. She hadn't been able to say anything at all.

Now she came out of the trees on the north side of the clearing and stopped so hard the snow sprayed up around her knees.

Jasper stopped beside her. She felt him take it in. She felt him go still in the particular way he went still, the soldier's way, and she knew without looking that he had already counted every living thing on the field and where it stood and which way it was facing.

She was looking at the ash.

She'd seen it already, in a way. She'd been seeing it for half an hour, the way you see the shape of a thing in the dark. It was different with her eyes. There were heaps of it, grey heaps, scattered across the trampled snow in the exact place the black wedge had stood in every vision she'd had for a month. Each one in its own black ring of wet, steaming earth. Each one with a little glint of gold in it. The air above the field still held a faint purple haze, and the smell came and hit her in the face, thick and sweet and burnt, and she knew that smell. Every one of them knew it.

And behind the ash, alive, every one of them—

Carlisle. Esme, with her hands knotted together in front of her. Rosalie in a crouch with Emmett's arm across her. Edward. Bella, standing rigid at Edward's side with her chin up. The russet wolf with the child on his back.

All of them. Everyone. Alive.

"Alice," Jasper said quietly.

She couldn't look away from them.

"*Alice.*" He touched her arm. "Look at the man."

She looked.

She saw the black star first: a scar burned into the frozen ground ten yards out from the ash, the snow gone from around it in a wide ring, glittering faintly at its heart. And a few yards beyond that, there was a tartan picnic blanket spread out on the snow.

There was a picnic on it.

A real one. A wicker hamper and plates and a steel flask and the remains of a roast chicken. A small, grey, wrinkled creature in a tea towel was kneeling at the edge of it, packing things neatly back into the hamper. Sitting cross-legged on the blanket, with a slice of something golden on a plate in his lap and an enormous leather-bound book lying shut beside him, was a dark-haired man in a scuffed leather jacket. And sitting on the far side of the blanket from him, as far away as the blanket allowed, with her knees drawn up and a teacup in one hand and a fork in the other and the most murderous expression Alice had ever seen on a human face, was Leah Clearwater.

Leah Clearwater was eating a tart.

Alice looked at the man. She looked at him properly, with the other sight, the way she'd been trying to look at the hole in her family's futures for half an hour.

There was nothing there.

It was the hole. She'd known it would be. Knowing and seeing were different things, though, and seeing it was worse. The wolves were fog. The child was fog. You looked at them and everything near them smeared and softened and ran together like wet paint. This man was simply absent. She looked at him and his future wasn't there, and neither was anything touching it. It was as if someone had taken a pair of scissors to a photograph and cut him neatly out, and behind the place where he should have been there was a perfectly shaped piece of nothing at all.

The edges of it were sharp enough to cut.

"I can't feel him," Jasper said, very low. "Anything. It's like trying to feel a wall." He was silent for a moment. "The girl, though. The wolf girl. Alice, I've never felt anyone like her. Not in a hundred and sixty years." He shook his head. "There's a hurricane in her. And right in the middle of the hurricane, there's something sitting perfectly still."

Behind them, at the edge of the trees, Alice heard Huilen's heart and Nahuel's heart come up, quick from the run, and stop. Kachiri came out of the trees on her other side, saw the ash, and said something short and savage in her own language that needed no translating.

Across the field, every head had turned.

"*Alice.*"

It was Bella. Bella was moving, crossing the snow so fast she was nothing but a blur, and Alice was moving too, and they hit each other halfway with a crack like two boulders meeting, and Bella's arms went round her so hard that if she'd been human it would have broken her in half. Alice held on. She held on with her face pressed into Bella's hair, which smelled of snow and Edward and Renesmee and burnt Volturi, and didn't let go.

"Where *were* you," Bella was saying into her shoulder. "Where were you, where were you, I thought—we all thought—I kept thinking, Alice, I thought about you all *morning*—"

"I know," Alice said. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry." She pulled back far enough to see Bella's face. "I went to get *him*."

She turned. Bella turned with her.

Nahuel had stopped at the very edge of the trees with Huilen beside him. He was standing very straight. His dark eyes were moving over the field, the ash, the scorch, the steaming black star, and Alice watched him register each thing and file it away and move on, the way he'd done with everything since she'd met him: like a man who had been ready for a long time to see something terrible, and was only mildly surprised by the form it had taken.

"His name is Nahuel," Alice said. "He's a hundred and fifty years old. He's like her, Bella. He's like Renesmee. Half and half. He's been alive for a hundred and fifty years and he's never hurt anybody he didn't mean to, and he stopped growing when he was seven, and I was going to—" Her voice cracked. "I was going to show Aro."

Bella looked at Nahuel. Her face did something Alice had never seen it do.

"Oh," she said.

Alice heard something then. A soft little *zip*.

She turned her head. They all did. A long ribbon of yellowed cloth printed with black numbers had come shooting out across the snow from the direction of the wolves, from the hand of a thin man in a dark coat standing among Sam's pack with a tray of glass vials, and it was now wavering in the air a foot in front of Nahuel's chest, quivering like a pointer.

Nahuel looked down at it.

"I'm so sorry," the thin man called from across the field. "It's very keen. Is that—" His voice went up. "Excuse me, is that a *heartbeat*? Is that—Ffion. *Ffion.* There's another one!"

"There's *what*?" said a new voice, from the picnic blanket.

The man in the leather jacket had stood up.

Alice hadn't seen him do it. She couldn't; she couldn't see him move before he moved. He was simply on his feet, plate still in hand, staring across the field at Nahuel, and the expression on his face was the expression of a man who has opened a cupboard and had six more things fall out on him.

"There's *more* of them?" he said.

"I am Nahuel," Nahuel said, in his careful, accented English. He did not seem particularly alarmed. Alice had never once seen him particularly alarmed. "My mother was human. My father is not. I have three sisters, by other mothers. Also half. And my aunt." He laid a hand on Huilen's shoulder. "Huilen. She is like all of you. Cold. She raised me."

The man in the leather jacket set his plate down on the hamper, very carefully.

"Three sisters," he said. "By other mothers."

"Yes."

"And your mum's—"

"My mother died when I was born. I tore my way out of her." Nahuel said it simply, the way he said everything. "The mothers of my sisters also."

"Right." The man's voice had gone very flat. "And your dad. Where's he?"

"Chile, when I saw him last. Perhaps Peru by now. He moves."

"And he's still—"

"Yes," said Nahuel. "He is still trying. He wishes to make a new race."

The man didn't say anything.

He didn't need to. Alice couldn't see him, not one second of anything he meant to do. But she could see everyone else in the clearing, and every one of their futures had just jerked, all at once, the way a room full of people jerks when somebody slams a door. Edward's. Carlisle's. The grey-haired American's. The Frenchwoman's—Alice didn't know who she was, a small dark woman in a long grey coat standing by the ash with a clipboard of her own, but Alice saw her future lurch and tilt sideways and come down hard on a single word.

"Potter," the Frenchwoman said, across the field, without looking up from her clipboard. "*Non.*"

"Didn't say anything."

"You stood *up*."

"I stood up to *stretch*."

"You stood up to go to Chile."

"Peru, possibly," the man said. "He moves."

"*Potter*—"

On the blanket behind him, Leah Clearwater had put down her teacup. Alice saw her do it. She saw Leah's hand come up and close on the back of the man's leather jacket, at the hem, a fistful of it, and hold on, the way you'd grab the collar of a dog that had seen a cat.

Alice was fairly sure Leah hadn't meant to. Leah was looking down at her own hand with an expression of pure horror.

The man looked down at it too.

For a second, nobody on the whole field moved at all.

Then Leah let go of his jacket like it had burned her, and snatched her hand back into her lap, and stared straight ahead across the snow with her jaw clenched so hard Alice could see the muscle jumping from forty yards away.

The man looked at her a moment longer. Then he blew out a long breath through his nose.

"Later," he said. "Box marked Later." He sat back down on the blanket, a little less far from Leah than he'd been before, and picked up his plate again. "Write it down, Sabine. *Chile, possibly Peru.* I want it on a form by tonight."

"It is not our jurisdiction."

"Make it our jurisdiction."

"I will do the form," the Frenchwoman said, in the voice of a person repeating something for the fortieth time that day, and went back to her clipboard.

The thin man with the tape measure had reached Nahuel by now. He was standing in front of him with the bobble-hatted girl panting at his elbow and her quill scribbling madly, saying, "A hundred and *fifty*? Did you say a hundred and *fifty*? Would you—would you mind terribly if I—" and Nahuel was looking down at the tape measure, which had wrapped itself round his wrist in a hopeful spiral, with polite and careful interest. Carlisle had arrived at his other side and was holding out his hand, saying something low and kind about how very glad they were to meet him; and Esme was coming; and Huilen had drawn herself up, small and fierce and watchful; and Kachiri had crossed the field in four long strides to Zafrina and Senna, and the three of them had their heads together, talking fast in their own tongue.

Alice let them all go. She'd done what she came to do. Nobody needed it now, and that was all right. That was more than all right.

She let go of Bella's hand, and walked over to the picnic blanket.

Leah saw her coming first. Alice watched her see it: the dark head coming round, the eyes narrowing, the whole lean body going tight. Leah had never liked any of them. She'd made that very clear in the Cullens' kitchen, over and over, every day for months; she put up with them for Jacob's sake and not one inch further. She looked at Alice now as if Alice were the last straw in a day made of nothing but last straws.

The man didn't look up from his tart until Alice was standing at the edge of the blanket.

Then he did.

His eyes were very green. That was all she could tell about them. She looked into them and tried, one more time, as hard as she knew how, to see what he would do in the next minute, the next second, the next breath. It was like looking into a mirror with the silver scraped off the back.

"I can't see you," Alice said.

"Good," said the man.

"I mean it. I really can't see you. The wolves are like fog. You're like—" she hunted for it "—like somebody cut you out of the picture with scissors."

He considered that. "Sounds about right."

"Everything you're close to, I can't see either. Not properly." She looked at Leah, and back at him. "I couldn't see her before. Now I can't see her *more*."

"Thanks," said Leah. "Really. Great."

"Everything goes around you," Alice said. "Everybody's future. It all goes around you, like water around a rock." She stopped. She hadn't meant to say the next part. It came out anyway, the way the visions sometimes came, without asking. "And Thursday."

The man stopped chewing.

"Thursday's gone," Alice said. "I looked. While I was running. Thursday night is—black. Completely black. And it's everybody near you, too. Forks, La Push, half the peninsula. It's as if someone turned off all the lights."

He swallowed. He looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked down at the tart on his plate.

"Yeah," he said. "It does that sometimes."

Leah had turned her head to stare at him.

"What happens on Thursday?" Alice said.

"Full moon."

She waited.

He took another bite of tart.

"He says that," said Leah, after a moment, flat and sour. "He says that and then he doesn't say anything else. I've tried. You can stand there all day if you want."

Alice looked at her. Leah looked back. And for one strange second, in the middle of the most extraordinary morning of Alice's very long life, it seemed to her that she and Leah Clearwater might have something in common.

She looked down at the two of them. The man with the tart and the book. The girl with the teacup, sitting stiff and furious a foot and a half from him, closer than she'd been a minute ago, and, Alice was almost certain, entirely unaware of it.

Two holes in the future, side by side, their edges almost touching. And nothing that Alice could see between them at all.

Sabine came back across the snow at a quarter to twelve, with her clipboard under her arm and a fresh cup of coffee in her hand that Harry hadn't seen her get.

He was still on the blanket. Kreacher had packed away everything except the flask and the last two slices of treacle tart, which he'd left on a plate exactly halfway between Harry and Leah with the air of a man leaving the last two chocolates in the box for a couple to fight over. Neither of them had touched them. Leah hadn't moved since the little seer walked away. She sat with her knees up and her arms round them and her eyes on the far trees, and she hadn't said a word in ten minutes, and the line between them sat under Harry's breastbone humming quietly, like a phone line with nobody talking on it.

Volume Two lay open across his knee at the torn page.

Sabine stopped at the edge of the blanket. She looked at Leah first, and inclined her head very slightly. "Mademoiselle."

Leah looked at her and said nothing.

Sabine looked at Harry.

"Registry is finished with the Cullens," she said. "And the Irish, and the Egyptians, and the Amazons, and the Denalis, and the nomads, all of them. And the new one, the boy from Chile, and his aunt. Pellow wants to go to Chile."

"Everybody wants to go to Chile."

"Pellow wants to go to Chile with a *tape measure*. You want to go to Chile with a lightning bolt. There is a difference." She sipped her coffee. "MACUSA are finished with the field. The smoke column has been attributed to a controlled burn by the Washington State Department of Natural Resources. The little man in Seattle has a new teacup. The wolves are not finished. The wolves want their elders." She glanced at Leah again. "Their council, the American says. They will give nothing to anybody except through their council."

"Good for them."

"It is bad for me. It is another day." She looked down at the book on his knee. "That is Volume Two."

"That's Volume Two."

"Is it useful?"

He turned it round on his knee and held it up for her. He watched her read. He watched her eyes go down the long slanting lines and stop at the torn edge, and then at the word in green ink in the margin, and he watched the corner of her mouth go tight.

"*Don't*," she read aloud.

"Helpful."

"*The parties are on no account to be separated.*" She looked up at him. Her face had gone very still. "Thursday."

"Thursday."

She looked at Leah. She looked back at him. She was quiet for a while, and Harry, who had worked for her for three years, waited, because he knew the look of her when she was turning a problem over in her hands and feeling for the side of it with a handle on.

"*Bon,*" she said at last. "You are posted."

"I'm what?"

"Posted. As of now. Confederation liaison to the Quileute tribal council, for the purpose of the registration of the Quileute shifters, who have been filed, as you yourself said, in the wrong section of the Registry since nineteen-thirty-something. You will meet the council. You will explain to them what the Registry is and what it is for. You will see the registration done, and done properly, and you will remain on this peninsula until it is finished." She drank her coffee. "Or until after Thursday. Whichever is later."

"Sabine."

"It needs a liaison. You said it yourself. Two packs. Wrong section."

"Sabine, I've got a *fly rod*."

"Then you will fish. There is a great deal of water here. I am told it is very beautiful." She lowered the cup. "I am not having you on another continent on Thursday night, Potter. With this." She tapped the torn page with one finger. "I am not having you in *London* with this. You will stay here, near her, and if anything happens it will happen *here*, where there are no Muggles closer than the town and everyone who might see it is already in the Registry. That is my decision. You may file a complaint."

"Who with?"

"With me," said Sabine. "I will read it on Friday."

He looked at her for a long moment. She looked back at him over the rim of her coffee cup.

"Fine," he said.

"Thank you."

"I'm writing *under protest* on the form."

"Write what you like on the form. Nobody reads them." She turned to go. Then she stopped, and looked back over her shoulder, past him, toward the black star. "You have a visitor," she said, more quietly, and walked away.

Harry turned his head.

The Romanian was standing at the edge of the black star.

The other one. The one who'd stayed behind. Stefan, Harry thought; the name had come out of Bella's head with everything else, though he hadn't stopped to look at it at the time. He was standing at the very rim of the circle of bare earth where the snow had burned away, with his papery hands hanging at his sides, looking down at the glassy black stuff at the centre of the star. He'd been there a while, Harry realised. He'd been quiet enough about it that Harry hadn't heard him come.

Harry put the book down on the blanket and got up.

"Where are you going?" said Leah.

It was the first thing she'd said in ten minutes. She said it sharply, and then looked as if she wished she hadn't said it at all.

"Not far," Harry said.

He walked over. He stopped on the far side of the black star from the Romanian, with the scorched earth between them, and put his hands in his jacket pockets, and waited.

The Romanian didn't look up for a long time.

"Was it quick?" he said at last.

His voice was very dry and very quiet. His English was more heavily accented than his friend's had been, every word handled carefully, like a man carrying something fragile across a room.

"Yeah," said Harry.

"Quicker than the others?"

"About the same."

The Romanian nodded slowly.

"He would have hated that," he said. "He always wanted things to take a long time. Vengeance. Supper. Arguments. Once we argued for eleven years about the proper way to take a castle on a hill." He crouched, slowly, the way an old man crouches, and touched one finger to the edge of the glassy black at the heart of the star. It was still warm; Harry could feel it from where he stood. The Romanian left his finger there. "Eleven years. He was right, in the end. I never told him."

Harry said nothing.

"He jumped," the Romanian said. "I saw. You did not move."

"I'd have let him walk."

"I know." He took his finger away from the glass. "He knew also. That is why he jumped. Vladimir always preferred a cliff to a road." He stood, slowly, and looked at Harry for the first time. His eyes were red and dull and very, very tired. "I have been reaching for his sleeve for fifteen hundred years. I find I do not know what to do with my hand."

Harry looked at him.

He thought about saying *sorry*. It was the only word he'd had for Leah, and it had been the right one, or near enough. It wasn't any use here. He'd killed the bloke. He'd do it again, too, if the bloke came at him again; the Romanian knew that; and *sorry* would have been a lie, and they'd both have known it.

"Put it in your pocket," he said instead.

The Romanian looked down at his own hand. He looked at it for a long time. Then, very slowly, he put it into the pocket of his long coat.

"Like this," he said.

"Like that."

The Romanian stood there with his hand in his pocket. Something moved across his face. Harry couldn't have said what it was. It might have been the very beginning of a feeling, a long way off, coming toward him across a great deal of grey.

"I suppose," Stefan said, "that I am meant to disperse."

"That's the idea."

"Where does one disperse to? After fifteen hundred years?"

"Anywhere you like," Harry said. "That's sort of the point of it."

The Romanian thought about that. Then he nodded to Harry, once, a short, formal, old-fashioned nod, the kind you'd give across a table at the end of a negotiation, and turned and walked away toward the trees with his hand in his pocket.

Behind Harry, Ruth Calloway's flat-footed tread came across the snow at speed. "Hey. *Hey*—Potter, he's not registered, we need his—"

"Let him go, Ruth."

"I need a name and a—"

"Stefan. Romanian. Old. He's dispersing." Harry watched the Romanian reach the trees, and go in among them, and not look back. "Put *see Potter*."

Calloway stopped beside him. She looked at the trees where the Romanian had gone. She looked at Harry. Then she let out a long, flat-footed, American sigh, and took out her notebook.

"You know how many *see Potters* I got on this page?"

"Four?"

"*Six.*" She wrote it down anyway. "Your guy from the council's here."

Harry turned round.

A man was walking across the snow toward them from the direction of the wolves. He was barefoot, in a pair of cut-off jeans and nothing else, in the middle of January, and he walked like a man who had never in his life felt cold and didn't intend to start. He was big. A couple of inches shorter than Harry and heavier through the chest and shoulders, with short black hair and a face that looked as if it had been carved out of the side of a hill by someone who'd been told to make it serious. Older than the others. Mid-twenties, maybe. He walked straight, with his hands loose at his sides, and his dark eyes stayed on Harry's face the whole way across.

Harry had seen that face before. In Bella's head. Looking at a woman with a scarred face as if she were the only fixed point in the universe.

Sam Uley stopped a few feet away.

"You're the liaison," he said.

"Apparently."

"The council's Billy Black, Old Quil Ateara, and Sue Clearwater." He said the last name without any change in his voice at all. Harry heard his heart do something, though. "You want to talk to the tribe about this Registry, you talk to them. Not to us. Not to the packs. Them."

"Fine by me."

"Billy's at his place in La Push. He's been sitting by the phone since sunup."

"I'll go round."

Sam looked at him. Then his eyes went past Harry's shoulder.

Harry didn't turn round. He didn't need to. He could feel her through the line, sitting where he'd left her on the blanket, knees drawn up, arms round them. He could feel her looking at Sam. And Harry, who had spent forty seconds in Bella Cullen's head and knew more than he'd ever wanted to about what Sam Uley had once been to Leah Clearwater, felt through that humming wire under his breastbone exactly how much she felt, looking at Sam now.

Nothing. A big man in a field. She looked at him the way she'd have looked at a tree.

And Harry watched Sam see it.

He watched it land. Sam was a decent Alpha, from what Harry had seen of him; he'd held his pack on the line all morning with nothing but his own weight, and he'd have had two years of looking at Leah's face, and he'd know it as well as anybody living. He looked at her now across the snow and saw nothing at all looking back at him, and something went through him that Harry couldn't put a name to and didn't try. It was close to pain. It was the look of a man who has carried a heavy thing for a long time, and had it suddenly lifted off him, and finds his arms don't know what to do.

Sam's eyes came back to Harry. They were harder.

"She's one of ours," Sam said, quietly. "Whatever you are."

"So I keep hearing."

"None of us can touch you. You know that? Not me. Not Jacob. Not anyone. Not ever. Not after this." His jaw worked. "That's the oldest law there is."

"Gathered."

"Doesn't mean we won't want to."

"Join the queue, mate," Harry said. "It's a long one. There's a bloke in Geneva near the front, hanging upside down."

Sam looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned and walked back toward his pack.

---

When Harry got back to the blanket, Carlisle was standing at the edge of it, with Esme a step behind him.

Leah hadn't moved. Kreacher had. The elf had planted himself between the doctor and the hamper with his arms folded over his tea towel and his ears flat, and he was looking up at Carlisle Cullen the way he'd once looked at Mundungus Fletcher.

"Mr. Potter," Carlisle said. "Forgive me. I couldn't help overhearing that you're to remain in the area for a time."

"Couldn't help it. Right." Harry nodded at the field in general. "Nobody in this clearing can help anything, apparently."

Carlisle accepted that with a small inclination of his head. "If that's the case, we'd be very glad to offer you somewhere to stay. Our house is large. There are several rooms nobody uses. Esme—"

"There's a guest room on the second floor," Esme said. Her voice was soft. She was looking at him with an expression he couldn't quite read, and that he suspected, from what he'd seen of her in Bella's head, was mostly worry about whether he'd eaten. "It has its own bathroom. And a view of the river. You'd be very welcome."

Harry opened his mouth.

"No," said Leah.

Everybody looked at her.

She hadn't got up. She was sitting exactly where she'd been, with her arms round her knees and her eyes on the trees, past all of them.

"He's not staying with the leeches," she said.

Esme's face did something small and hurt, and then smoothed itself out again. Carlisle's didn't do anything at all; he'd been called worse, Harry gathered, by better.

"Wasn't going to," Harry said.

Leah's head turned. "Where, then?"

"Got a tent."

Kreacher rose up at the edge of the blanket like a small furious ghost.

"Master," he said, "is *not* sleeping in a *tent*, in the *snow*, like a—like a—" He hunted visibly for the worst thing he knew. "—like a *Weasley*."

"The Weasleys haven't slept in a tent in years, Kreacher."

"Kreacher *remembers* the tent."

Leah had tuned Kreacher out. She was looking at Harry, and her jaw was working, and Harry could feel it going on in her through the line: a long, ugly, private fight, conducted entirely in silence. He knew what it was about. He'd watched her try to walk into the trees an hour ago, and stop dead at the edge of them as if she'd hit glass. He'd felt where the line ran out. If he pitched a tent in the woods behind the Cullens' house, she'd be sitting on the other side of the river all night in the snow, as close as it would let her get, hating every minute of it. If he went to a hotel in Forks, it'd be the car park. She knew it, too. He watched her know it.

"My mom has a couch," Leah said, through her teeth.

Nobody said anything.

"Your mum's on the council," Harry said.

"Yeah."

"So it's work."

"It's a *couch*." She got up. She did it all at once, the way she seemed to do everything, unfolding off the blanket in one movement, and stood there in the snow in her bare feet and her cut-offs, looking down at him. "It's lumpy. Seth spilled grape soda on it when he was nine. One of the springs comes up through the middle if you lie on it wrong. You'll hate it."

"Probably."

"*Good.*"

From across the field, quite distinctly, Emmett said, "Grape soda," with something close to reverence, and somebody hit him.

Leah ignored it. "I'm going to go phase," she said. "Seth's been sitting in the snow for an hour, and if he doesn't get to tell Jacob about the house-elf soon he's going to explode." She looked at Kreacher. "No offence."

Kreacher bowed. A small one, this time. "None taken, Mistress."

Leah closed her eyes. "*Don't* call me that."

"Kreacher calls the lady what Kreacher—"

"Kreacher," said Harry.

Kreacher shut his mouth. His ears stayed up. He looked entirely unrepentant.

Leah opened her eyes again. She looked at Harry for a long second, and he looked back, and neither of them said anything. Then she turned and walked off across the field toward the trees, toward the sandy wolf sitting at the edge of them, who saw her coming and leapt to his feet and began wagging his whole back end like a puppy.

She didn't look back.

Harry watched her go. He felt the line pay out behind her, slack and long. And at the treeline, where an hour ago it had pulled her up short like a dog on a chain, it didn't. It let her go. She went past the grey trunk where she'd changed before, a few yards into the shadow under the firs, and on, further than she'd been able to go all morning. He felt it stretch, and hold, and not pull.

She stopped anyway.

Just for a second. One hand on the trunk of a tree. He couldn't see her face from there. Then she went on into the dark under the branches, and a moment later he felt the heat of her change go up the line like a struck match, and heard a wolf's feet hit the snow, and Seth's delighted yelp.

Esme had put her hand on Carlisle's arm. Carlisle was looking at Harry with an expression of polite and boundless clinical interest.

"Mr. Potter," he said. "If I might ask—"

"No, Doc."

"I only meant to ask whether you'd like Esme to send something over for Sue. For the couch. Blankets. She'll have been up since before dawn waiting for news, and she won't have had time to—"

Harry stopped.

He looked at Carlisle. Carlisle looked back, mild and patient, a man who'd spent three hundred years waiting for people to come round.

"Yeah," Harry said. "All right. Cheers."

"Not at all," said Carlisle, and turned to go. Then he turned back. "And I would very much like to borrow Volume One," he said, "at some point. Purely for my own interest."

"Ask the Registry."

"I did. Mr. Pellow says it's on loan to you."

"Then you'll have to wait for me to finish it."

"I've waited longer," Carlisle said, and smiled, and went back across the field to his family with Esme on his arm.

---

The Registry left at half past twelve.

They went the way they'd come, in a ragged string of cracks along the north treeline, one after another like a box of bangers going off. The two in Confederation cloaks went first, with a sealed crate of ash and gold floating between them. Ffion went next, clutching her clipboard to her chest, its quill still twitching. Pellow went last of the Registry lot. He stopped at the edge of the trees, turned round, and waved at the little girl on the back of the russet wolf, and Renesmee, after looking at Jacob and Bella and Edward one after another, lifted one small hand and waved back. Then Pellow was gone, and the tape measure with him.

The Americans went after that. Calloway paused before she Disapparated and looked across the snow at Harry, and touched two fingers to the brim of her fedora.

"Statement," she said. "Tonight."

"Tonight, Ruth."

"Six *see Potters*, Potter."

"Seven," said Harry. "You forgot the field."

She looked at him for a long, flat moment. Then she was gone.

Sabine went last of all. She stood beside him for a while first, looking out at the black star and the ash rings and the vampires beginning, at last, to drift apart into their own little groups, the Irish to the south, the Egyptians to the east, Garrett and Kate walking off together toward the river with their heads close and Garrett's hands moving as he talked. She finished her coffee. She made the cup vanish.

"An owl," she said. "If it itches."

"If it itches."

"And on Friday morning, a report. A real one. With nouns."

"With nouns."

She looked at him. Then she reached up, quite unexpectedly, and patted his cheek, twice, the way a French grandmother would pat a boy who'd come home with a black eye.

"*Bonne chance*, Potter," she said, and was gone.

Harry stood in the snow with his hands in his pockets.

The clearing was very quiet. The purple haze had thinned to almost nothing. The black star had stopped steaming. Across the field, the Cullens had begun to gather themselves up: Bella with the little girl in her arms now, Edward's hand at the small of her back, Alice and Jasper and the Chilean boy and his aunt walking together toward the trees with Carlisle. Rosalie was carrying the kid's backpack. The russet wolf stood a few yards from Bella, watching the Cullens leave with his ears flat; then he turned his great head and looked straight across the field at Harry, for a long, hard moment, before he went after them.

Sam's pack had already gone. Harry had heard them go: a long rush of paws and branches heading south and west, toward the sea.

At the edge of the trees on the far side of the field, a slim grey wolf and a sandy one were waiting. The sandy one was bouncing. The grey one stood quite still with her head up, looking at him.

Kreacher had finished packing the hamper. He stood beside it with the two volumes of *Bonds of Soul and Shape* stacked on top, one on the other, which made a pile nearly as tall as he was, and his long fingers folded over his tea towel.

"Master will need clean clothes," he said. "For the lady's mother's house."

"I've got clean clothes."

"Master has *one* clean shirt, in the bag, at the airport, in Seattle, in a *locker*."

Harry looked down at him.

"Kreacher looked," said Kreacher.

"Course you did."

"Kreacher will bring the shirts. And the good boots. And the razor." Kreacher looked up at Harry's jaw with deep disapproval. "*Especially* the razor."

"Fine. Bring the razor."

Kreacher nodded, satisfied, and put one long hand flat on top of the stack of books. Then he paused.

"Master," he said.

"What?"

The elf's enormous eyes had gone past him, across the field, to the grey wolf at the edge of the trees. They stayed there a moment. Then they came back to Harry, and something was in them that Harry had seen there only a very few times in all the years he'd known him: once in the kitchen at Grimmauld Place, with a fake locket in his hands, and a few times since.

"Kreacher's old Mistress," Kreacher said, "would not have approved of the lady."

"I'd gathered."

"She would not have approved of *anything*." Kreacher sniffed. "Kreacher approves."

He vanished with a crack, and the hamper and both volumes went with him.

Harry stood in the empty field.

After a moment he crouched down, where the blanket had been, and found that Kreacher had left something behind on the snow. A pen. A plain black Muggle biro, the kind he kept in his jacket pocket for forms, which he was fairly sure had been in his jacket pocket five minutes ago.

He looked at it for a while. Then he picked it up, and put it back in his pocket, and thought about the torn page, and the word in faded green ink in the margin.

*Don't.*

He'd write *Noted* under it later. In black. She'd told him to shut up when he offered, but she hadn't actually said no.

He stood up and looked at the sky.

The cloud had closed over it again completely, flat and white and low, the way it had been all morning. There was nothing up there to see. He didn't need to see it. He knew exactly where it was. He'd always known, the last few days before; he could have pointed to it through a foot of concrete. Fat and lopsided and climbing, somewhere up behind the white, two nights short of full.

Down at the bottom of the dark, the wolf rolled over onto its side and stretched, long and slow, from the tip of its nose to the end of its tail, and settled again with its chin on its paws. It was looking up the line. Up the line, across the field, to the edge of the trees.

*Thursday,* Harry told it. *Don't get excited.*

The wolf thumped its tail on the stone. Twice.

Across the field, the grey wolf turned and went into the trees, and the line went with her. Harry put his hands in his pockets, and followed.
