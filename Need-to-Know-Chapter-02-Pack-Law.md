# Need to Know

## Chapter Two: Pack Law

---

## Part One: The White Couch

---

### I. Leah

*The Cullen boundary — Monday, November 20th, 2006, 6:58 p.m.*

*Ask him myself?* Jacob said. *ASK HIM MYSELF? Leah, I can't even find him! Nobody can find him! Seth's been trying to remember where that tent is for six hours and every time he gets close he starts thinking about tacos!*

*Burritos,* Seth said. *It was burritos the last time.*

*THAT'S NOT THE POINT.*

Leah ran.

The river was behind her. The big spruces were behind her, and the little green tent with moss on its guy lines, and the banana slug, and the man standing on the bench in the dark with his hand still up in the air at the height of her face. She'd felt him put it down. She'd felt him stand there a long time after, listening to her go. She'd felt him turn around and go inside.

She could feel him now. Twenty miles back and getting further, a fixed point at the end of the rope, like a lighthouse you could feel instead of see. He was in his kitchen. He'd put the kettle on. Faint, so faint she'd have thought she was imagining it if she hadn't been feeling it for six hours straight, there was music coming down the line: a big slow grinding drumbeat and a guitar going round and round over it like something climbing a spiral staircase, and under it the hiss of a kettle starting to sing.

She had no idea what the song was. She had a feeling she was going to find out.

*Leah,* Jacob said. *Leah, are you listening to me?*

*Kind of hard not to, Jake. You're yelling in my skull.*

*Because there's a WALL in your HEAD!*

*I noticed.*

*And you're not even—you're not even* freaked out *about it! Why aren't you freaked out about it? There's a gray wall in the middle of your brain and you're running along like you're on your way to the store!*

She thought about it. She actually thought about it, crossing the old logging road at the bottom of the Calawah drainage in one jump, the gravel spraying out from under her back feet. She looked at the wall in her own head the way you'd look at a new piece of furniture somebody had put in your living room while you were out.

It was right there. Smooth and gray and cold, like the side of a river stone. Behind it were things she knew she knew. She could feel the shape of them, the weight, the way you can feel a coin in a closed fist. A word that started with a *U*. A room with no windows. A city on a hill. A voice on a phone that had made the hair stand up on her arms. They were all there, every one, and she couldn't touch any of them, and neither could anybody else.

And wrapped around the outside of the wall, holding it shut, there was something she knew the smell of now. Old. Very old and very cold, cold like the stone he'd talked about in the kitchen, the black one in the little room. It had come down the rope from his end and put itself in her head, and it was sitting there like a dog on a doorstep, and it was *his*.

She found she didn't mind it there at all.

*It's his,* she told Jacob.

*What's his?*

*The wall. It's a thing he's got. It came down the—it came over from him. It's keeping his stuff safe.*

*In YOUR head.*

*Yeah.*

*Leah, that is the creepiest thing I have ever heard in my life, and I watched a baby chew her way out of her mother.*

*It's not creepy,* Seth said. Then, uncertainly: *Is it creepy? It's a little creepy.*

*It's fine,* Leah said. *It's his stuff. I've got his stuff. Big deal.*

She felt Jacob go quiet. The bad kind of quiet, where he was trying to decide whether to yell some more, and she could feel the whole pack-mind hum with it, Jacob back at the Cullen place in his wolf shape on the lawn, pacing a groove in Esme's grass. Seth at the Cullens' too, in the woods behind the garage, phased, with his nose pointed toward her.

*Where are you going?* Jacob said finally.

*Where are you?*

*Cullens'.*

*Then that's where I'm going.*

She felt his surprise. She felt Seth's. She'd never once, in all the months since the treaty line got redrawn and Jacob walked out of Sam's pack and she'd followed, gone to the Cullens' when she didn't have to. She ran their boundary. She ran their loops. She'd sat in the trees at the edge of their lawn for a whole afternoon two weeks ago and watched a witch light their living room up like the Fourth of July, and she hadn't gone a foot closer than the trees. Seth went in and out of that house like it was the corner store. Jacob slept on their couch. Leah stayed outside, where the air was clean.

*You're coming here,* Jacob said. *To the house.*

*You want to talk about it or not?*

*I want to talk about it.*

*Then I'm coming to the house.*

---

She came out of the trees at the bottom of the Cullen lawn at seven minutes past seven, by the clock she could see glowing on the stove through the big glass wall at the back of the house.

Every light was on. Every light was always on, in that house. Like they were scared of the dark, which was funny, considering. She stopped at the edge of the trees in the wet ferns with her sides heaving and looked at it: the long white face of it, the glass, the porch. She could see shapes moving behind the windows. A blond head. A dark one. Somebody small with a lot of bronze curls being carried on somebody's hip.

The stink came down the lawn at her like a wave. Bleach and burnt sugar and flowers left in a vase. She'd been smelling it every day for most of a year and it still made the back of her throat close up.

Jacob was on the lawn. Huge and russet and soaked, standing in the middle of a ring of churned-up grass where he'd been pacing, his ears flat, his tail stiff out behind him. He'd stopped when she came out of the trees. He was staring at her.

Seth came out from behind the garage at a run, all legs, and skidded to a stop in the wet grass about ten feet from her, and stared at her too.

*Lee,* he said. *Lee, are you okay? Are you—*

*I'm fine.*

*You're* sure*? He didn't—*

*He made me eggs, Seth.*

Seth's mouth fell open. Wolves couldn't really do that, but Seth managed it. *He made you EGGS?*

*Six. And bacon. And toast.* She trotted past him, toward the big cedar at the corner of the lawn, where nobody in the house would be able to see her. *His kitchen's nicer than Mom's. Don't tell her.*

She went behind the cedar and let go.

It went easier than it ever had. That was the first thing she noticed. Usually phasing back was like pulling a splinter, a hard sharp yank and then a second of feeling hollowed out, too small, too cold, stuck back in a body that couldn't run. This time she just let the wolf go and it went, the way a dog lies down when it's tired, and she was standing barefoot in the wet cedar needles in the dark with the rain coming down on her bare shoulders. And she didn't feel hollow at all. She could still feel the wolf. Right there, under her ribs, curled up and content with its chin on its paws. And past it, down the rope, the other one. Lying down behind its three locks in the dark twenty miles away, with its eyes open.

Both of them. Watching each other down the line.

She untied the leather cord from around her ankle and shook out her cutoffs and her tank top, which were soaked through and smelled like river, and pulled them on. They stuck to her. She didn't care.

She walked out from behind the cedar and up the lawn.

Jacob phased as she passed him. She heard the tear and the thud and the swearing, and she heard him yank his shorts up and come after her at a jog, barefoot, still buttoning them. Seth didn't phase. Seth stayed a wolf and trotted along on her other side with his nose about an inch from her elbow, like he thought she might fall over.

She went up the porch steps. Three of them. White-painted wood, wet with rain.

The front door was open. Edward was standing in it.

He was looking at her the way he'd looked at the witch two weeks ago, the day she lit the room up. Head a little on one side. Eyes very still. Like a man listening to a radio station that kept slipping in and out. She knew he was reading her. She'd always known when he was reading her; it felt like somebody standing too close behind you in a line. Most of the last year she'd thought the ugliest things she could think of, as loud as she could, any time he was within a mile, just to make him wince.

She didn't bother tonight. Let him look.

"Leah," Edward said.

"Edward."

"You're—" He stopped. Something moved in his face. "You want to come in."

"Yeah," said Leah. "I do."

He stepped back out of the doorway.

She walked into the Cullens' house for the first time in her life.

---

It was warm in there. All that glass and white and stone, a house full of people with no heartbeats, and somebody had a fire going in the big fireplace at the side of the long room, and the air was dry and still and smelled of woodsmoke, and of coffee from the kitchen, and of honey. Honey everywhere. And under all of it, so thick she could taste it, the stink.

She stood just inside the door and breathed it in.

It was awful. It was the same as it had always been. Bleach and sugar and dead flowers, seven of them and the little half one, the whole house soaked in it.

She waited for it to close her throat. She stood there and waited, and waited some more.

It smelled bad. Like a gas station bathroom. Like the inside of Paul's truck. Something she could stand in the middle of and think *ugh* and keep on standing there. For the first time since the spring, the smell of them didn't make her want to tear anything apart. The wolf under her ribs lifted its nose and took one sniff and put its head back down, bored.

Everybody was looking at her.

They were all there. Carlisle by the fireplace, with a medical bag open on the coffee table. Esme in the kitchen doorway with a dish towel. The blond one, Rosalie, sitting on the arm of a chair with her arms folded, and Emmett sprawled in it under her. The small dark one, Alice, on the stairs, with the scarred blond one standing behind her, one hand on her shoulder. Bella on the white couch, very still, the way they all went still when they were trying to look normal, with the kid in her lap. The kid had her thumb in her mouth and her eyes on Leah. Big brown eyes. Curious.

And at the other end of the white couch, lying on her stomach with a bag of ice on her back and her chin propped on a cushion and honey still crusted gold around her wrists, Layla.

Layla lifted her head off the cushion and looked at Leah. Her face was gray with tiredness and there was a bruise coming up along her jaw where she must have hit the log. Her eyes were very sharp.

"Hey," said Layla.

"Hey," said Leah. "How's your back?"

"Like I got thrown into a tree by a bullet."

"You did."

"I'm aware."

Leah looked around the room once more. Nobody said anything. The fire popped.

There was a bowl of apples on the coffee table, next to the medical bag. Big red ones, shiny, the kind nobody in this house ate. They kept them for Jacob, she knew. They kept a whole fridge for Jacob. She leaned over and took one, and bit it, and it was cold and sweet and crisp, and she realized she was starving again. She'd eaten six eggs four hours ago. She could have eaten six more.

She walked over to the other end of the white couch, where there was room, past Bella's feet. Bella pulled her feet in. Leah dropped onto the couch next to her, in her wet cutoffs, and the white cushion went *pff* under her and immediately started to get damp, and she put her bare muddy feet up on the glass coffee table, crossed at the ankle, and took another bite of the apple.

In the kitchen doorway, Esme's dish towel went very slightly tighter in her hands. She didn't say anything.

"What?" said Leah, through apple.

Jacob was standing in the middle of the room with his shorts half buttoned and rain running down his chest, staring at her feet on the coffee table like they'd grown teeth.

"*What?*" he said. "*What?* Leah—"

"Yeah?"

"You *imprinted*."

"Yeah."

"On—" He threw both hands out at his sides, at the window, at the dark, at the whole northwest of the peninsula. "On *him*!"

"Yep."

"On the *guy*! On the guy with the *gun*! The guy who put Layla in a *log*! The guy who held you down on a rock with your face in the moss and went through Seth's head like a—like a—" He groped for it.

"Like a burglar," said Seth helpfully, from outside. He'd stuck his whole head in through the open front door, which was the only part of him that would fit, and his ears were straight up.

"Like a *burglar*!" said Jacob. "He's got a *vampire* in his *closet*, Leah! In a *circle*! In a room full of *jars*! I saw it! In your head! There's a hand in one of those jars and it's *still moving*!"

"Two hands," said Leah. "There's two. They're from Finland."

"*WHY DO YOU KNOW THAT?*"

"He told me."

Jacob put both hands on top of his head and turned around in a full circle in the middle of the Cullens' living room, like a man looking for something to hit.

"How," he said, to the ceiling. "How. How could you imprint on *him*?"

Leah put her apple down.

She did it slowly. She set it on the glass coffee table next to her foot, very carefully, the way her mom set things down when she was about to say something. She took her feet off the table. She sat up.

Something in her chest had gone hot and bright and very, very clear.

"Okay," she said. "Okay, Jake. Let's do this."

Jacob turned around.

"Let's talk about imprints," said Leah. "Let's actually sit down and talk about them, since you want to. Let's do a little comparison." She held up one finger. "Yours is ten weeks old."

Jacob's face changed.

"Yours," Leah said, "is a literal baby. Yours is a literal baby vampire *hybrid*. You looked at her when she was about four minutes old, covered in her mom's blood, and you had your teeth out, Jake, you were going to *kill* her, and then she opened her eyes and that was it, you were done, you've been sleeping on this couch with her on your chest ever since like a golden retriever."

On Bella's lap, the kid took her thumb out of her mouth and looked at Jacob with great interest.

"And I've never said one word about it," said Leah. "Not one. Because I'm a supportive pack member. And because it's not your fault. And because you stood in this room, *right there*, about three feet from where you're standing now, and told Sam's whole pack that it's gravity. That nobody picks it. That it just happens to you, and that's the end of it, and they could all go to hell."

"Leah—"

"I'm not done." Two fingers. "Sam's is his ex's cousin. Who he messed up so bad she's got scars down one side of her face. Paul's is your sister, which—fine. Rachel's great. Rachel can't run a patrol loop. Jared's is a girl who wrote his name in her notebook for two years before he noticed she was alive. Quil's is *three*. Quil's imprint still needs help getting her shoes on." She took a breath. "Seth's is the best one up till now, and I'll give him that, because Layla's great and Layla stopped the rain."

From the other end of the couch, Layla said, into her cushion, "Thank you."

"You're welcome." Leah looked back at Jacob. "So let's grade them. Let's actually grade them. If we're grading these, Jake, if we are actually sitting here in this living room scoring every imprint anybody in either pack has ever had, mine's the best one. Mine's the best one *anybody's ever had*." She put up her fingers again, one at a time, and ticked them off. "A. He is not a baby."

Somebody on the stairs made a small noise. It might have been Alice.

"B. He's human. Mostly. He's got a heartbeat. He eats food. He makes eggs. He's got a fridge with beer in it. He is a grown man with a job and a bike he rebuilt himself, and he's not going to need me to teach him to *read*."

Emmett, in the armchair, put his fist against his mouth. His shoulders had started to shake.

"And C." Leah held up the third finger, right in Jacob's face. "He's also a *wolf*."

The room went quiet.

"Which," said Leah, "none of yours are. Not Sam's. Not Jared's. Not Paul's, not Quil's, not Seth's. Not yours, unless you know something about that kid nobody's told me." She lowered her hand. "Mine is a grown man who can do magic and has a wolf in him that's bigger than you. So take that, Jake. Take that and put it in your pipe."

Emmett lost it.

He made a sound like a tire going flat, a high whistling wheeze, and then he slid sideways out of the armchair onto the floor and lay there on the white rug with both hands over his face, shaking. Rosalie, on the arm of the chair, looked down at him with an expression of enormous disgust. Then the corner of her own mouth went up, very slightly, and she looked away out the window so nobody would see it.

On Bella's lap, the kid had started to giggle. She didn't know why. She was giggling because Emmett was giggling. Bella had her hand over her own mouth.

Jacob stood in the middle of the room with his mouth open.

"That's—" he said. "That's not—you can't *grade*—"

"And another thing," said Leah. "Do you think I *chose* this?"

Jacob shut his mouth.

"Is that what you think?" She leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "You think I was lying there on that rock with my face in the moss and him standing over me holding Seth down with his *brain*, and I thought, yeah, him, that's the one, let's do that? You think I picked the guy who'd just put Layla through the air? Is that what you think happened?"

"No," said Jacob. Quietly. "No. I don't."

"Because I seem to remember you telling everybody that's not how it works."

"I know."

"You said it's gravity."

"I *know*, Leah."

"So was that true for you, and not for me?"

Jacob didn't say anything. He stood there in the middle of the room with the rain drying on his chest and looked at her, and she watched it go across his face, all of it, one thing after another, the way it always went across Jacob's face because Jacob had never in his life been able to hide anything. Anger. Then something like embarrassment. Then something under both of those, older, that she'd seen on him the night of the newborns, when he'd been lying in the grass with his bones broken and she'd been standing over him knowing it was her fault.

Worry. For her.

"I'm sorry," Jacob said. "Okay? I'm sorry. I didn't mean it like that."

"Yeah, you did."

"Okay, I did. A little." He scrubbed his hands through his wet hair. "Leah, he *scares* me. You get that, right? I've fought newborns. I've fought—I fought *Sam*, sort of. I stood on a field full of bloodsuckers that wanted to rip my throat out and I was fine. I wasn't scared. I saw that guy in your head, standing on that rock in his suit with his hands in his pockets, and I wanted to *hide*." He looked at her. "And you went *after* him. Alone. Into his woods. Into his tent. And I couldn't even find you. Do you know what that was like?"

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Yeah," she said. "I know what that was like. I've been doing it to you since June."

Jacob made a sound that might have been a laugh. It came out cracked.

"I'm fine," said Leah. And then she heard it, how it sounded, how many times she'd said it to him in the last year in exactly that voice, the voice that meant *I'm not and back off*, and she stopped. She thought about it. She tried saying it again in her head, the way she'd tried to lie to Harry on the bench in the rain, to see if anything stuck in her teeth.

Nothing stuck.

"I'm actually fine, Jake," she said, and heard her own voice come out different. Lower. Surprised. "Like, really. For real. I don't know what to tell you."

Jacob stared at her.

From the open front door, Seth whined, very softly, and pushed his big sandy head another foot into the hall, until his shoulders hit the frame.

"I know," Leah told him, without looking around. "I know, Seth. Don't make it a whole thing."

She picked her apple back up off the coffee table and took a bite. And on the far side of the room, standing very still beside the fireplace with his eyes on her face, Edward Cullen made a small, quiet sound, like a man who'd just heard a song come on the radio that he hadn't heard in a very long time.

### II. Edward

*The Cullen house — 7:19 p.m.*

The radio was off.

That was the first thing he heard in her, and for a few seconds it was the only thing, because he couldn't make himself hear anything else.

He'd lived with Leah Clearwater's mind for the better part of a year. Never by choice; nobody, he suspected, had ever lived with Leah Clearwater's mind by choice, least of all Leah. It had been a loud mind, the loudest of the wolves after Jacob's, and it had always sounded the same: a hard bright surface of contempt, thrown up like a wall of noise, and under the wall, never stopping, a song. Two names. *Sam Emily Sam Emily Sam.* Over and over, like a jukebox someone had kicked so it couldn't switch records. Running under every thought she'd ever had in his hearing. Running under her hatred of him, under her love for her brother, under her running. He'd heard her try to drown it a thousand times. Every insult she'd ever thought at him, every image she'd pushed at him across the treaty line to make him flinch, had been shouted over the top of that song.

The song had stopped.

He stood by the fireplace and listened to the place where it had been. Quiet there. A clean, open quiet, like a room after a long party when the last guest has finally gone home and someone has opened the windows.

And in its place, something else.

A direction. That was the only word he had for it. Leah's mind had always been a thing turned in on itself, a fist. Now there was a line running out of the middle of it, taut, humming, pointed northeast through the wall of the house and twenty miles of rain toward something he couldn't see. Every thought she had leaned a little toward that line, the way grass leans in a current. She was sitting on Esme's couch eating an apple and arguing with Jacob, and the whole time, every second, some part of her was facing northeast.

And there was music coming down it.

Faint. Very faint, at the edge of what even he could hear. A slow, enormous, grinding rhythm and a guitar figure that climbed and climbed and never arrived, and strings coming in under it like weather. He knew it. It took him a second, because he'd never once expected to hear it in a Quileute girl's head, and then he had it: Led Zeppelin. "Kashmir." He'd owned the album in 1975. He'd played it on the piano for Emmett once, badly, as a joke.

She didn't know what it was. He could hear her not knowing. It was coming down the line from the other end, from whoever was there, and she was letting it, the way you'd let a radio play in another room.

And then there was the wall.

---

Jacob had stopped talking. Seth had gone quiet in the doorway. In the armchair, Emmett was sitting back up, wiping his eyes, and Rosalie was looking out the window with her mouth pressed into a line to stop it doing anything. Layla had put her head back down on the cushion. The fire popped.

"So," said Rosalie, to the window. "Since we're grading. Does the best imprint in history have a name?"

"Yeah," said Leah.

"Are you going to tell us what it is?"

Leah took a bite of apple and looked at Edward.

He felt her do it. Felt her turn the thought over and look at it and decide, with a small hot spark of something that he realized, after a moment, was *glee*. He had never once heard Leah Clearwater feel glee.

*You want it, mind-reader?* she thought at him. Clearly, deliberately, the first thing she'd thought directly at him all evening. *Go get it. See if he lets you.*

"It's behind the wall," said Seth from the doorway. He'd phased back at some point; Edward hadn't noticed. He was standing on the porch in a pair of shorts, dripping, with his hands braced on either side of the doorframe and his eyes very wide. "We tried. Me and Jake. When she phased on the way back. It's all just gray, Edward. Everything about him. Everything important. We can see the tent, and the kitchen, and the room with the jars, and the—" He swallowed. "The vampire. But who he is, what he's doing here, his name, all of it's behind this wall."

"Can you get past it?" said Jacob. He'd turned to Edward. "Can you? You read Bella's—no, you can't read Bella's. But you can read everybody else's. Can you get through it?"

Every head in the room turned toward him.

Edward looked at the wall.

---

It was remarkable. He'd say that later, to Carlisle, alone, and Carlisle would understand that he didn't use the word lightly.

He'd seen minds with blank places in them before. Every mind had them. Repressions, the alienists in his human years would have called them, and they'd have been right, more or less: things a person had decided, somewhere far down, not to look at. They were murky, mostly. Soft. A bruise in the thinking. You could feel the shape of what was under them if you were patient, the way you could feel a stone through a blanket.

This one was gray and smooth and very cold, and it sat in the middle of Leah's mind like a stone in a riverbed, and it had *edges*. That was what stopped him. Precise ones. Somebody had *fitted* it, the way a tailor fits a coat, and he could follow its outline through her memories the way you'd follow a seam in a tailored coat, and everywhere he followed it, it went exactly around certain things and not a hair farther. Here was a kitchen, bright and clear: a blue refrigerator, a cast-iron range, a man's hands cracking eggs one-handed into a pan. And here, in the same memory, in the middle of the same sentence the man was saying, a gray place exactly the size of one word. Here was a round room full of jars, every jar sharp and visible, a gray hand curling and uncurling in brine, and in the middle of the room a circle of silver with a body in it in a red jacket, all perfectly plain. And over the man's voice saying what the room was *for*, gray.

Whatever had done this had gone through her afternoon like an archivist with a razor, taking out certain lines and leaving the rest of the page.

He leaned on it. Gently. The way he'd lean on any closed door in a mind.

Something on the other side of it noticed him.

He felt it. A cold, heavy, patient attention, turning toward him from the far side of the gray. Very old. Older than him, older than Carlisle, older, he thought, than anything he'd met in Volterra. It noticed that he was there, the way a large animal asleep across a doorway might open one eye at a mouse, and then it went on lying there. It didn't bother to push.

He took his attention off the wall very quickly.

"Edward?" said Bella.

"The wall belongs to someone else," he said. His own voice sounded odd to him. "Something put it there. It's—guarding."

"I *told* you," said Leah. "It's his."

"It's his," Edward agreed slowly. "Or it belongs to something that belongs to him." He looked at her. "Did you know it would look at me?"

Leah shrugged one shoulder, eating apple. *I figured.*

"What do you mean, *look at you*?" said Jacob.

Edward didn't answer. He'd gone back to the edges.

Because the edges were where the work showed. That was true of any work, he'd always found, a piano piece or a lie or a forgery. And at the edges of this one he could see what it had chosen to take and what it had chosen to leave, and the choices made a shape.

It had taken things that belonged to an institution. He could feel that much. Each sealed place had the same flavor, like the same stamp pressed into wax on a hundred envelopes. Rooms, procedures, a word beginning with a hard consonant that sounded like a title, a city, a plan. Everything that belonged only to the man, it had left alone. That was lying all over her mind in plain sight, scattered through her afternoon like clothes on a bedroom floor. Scraps. The man swearing at a banana slug. The man saying *I'd rather see your face*. The man standing in a doorway saying *stop calling me the gun guy in your head, it's undignified*. A record player. A motorcycle. A cupboard under some stairs.

And there, near the edge of the wall, at the very beginning of everything, was a memory of rain.

He went into it carefully. A flat green bench of ground under enormous trees. A small army tent with a slug on it. The man in front of the tent, close enough to touch, with water running off his short dark hair, and his face wearing an expression of a kind Edward had seen before only on men who'd stepped onto a stair in the dark that wasn't there. *Ask me something*, he was saying. *Ask me my name.*

And in Leah's memory, there was a moment where the man had opened his mouth to answer, and something had come up behind his teeth, a word, a name, a whole life built around it, and Leah had felt it come, through whatever it was that tied them, the way you feel someone draw a breath beside you in the dark—

And that name was gray. A perfect gray oblong, the size of one word, sealed.

And then the man's mouth had shut, and opened again, and said something else. And that one was not gray at all.

Edward stood very still beside the fireplace.

"He has two names," he said.

The room waited.

"One of them belongs to—whatever made the wall. He tried to give her that one first, and he couldn't. It's sealed." He looked at Leah. She'd stopped chewing. She was watching him with her chin on her fist and something in her face he couldn't read and didn't need to, because he could hear it: a fierce, hot, satisfied attention, like someone watching a horse they'd bet on come around the last turn. "The other one belongs to him. And she's had it all afternoon, in plain sight. She just wasn't going to hand it to us."

"So what is it?" said Rosalie.

Edward told them.

"Harry Potter," he said.

---

For about a second and a half, nothing happened.

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked. In the doorway, Seth's mouth opened a little.

"Harry," said Seth. Quietly. Almost to himself. "His name's *Harry*?"

Leah didn't look round.

"Lee," Seth said. "That's Dad's—"

"I know what it is, Seth." Her voice was flat. Under it, in her mind, Edward heard a door very carefully shut: a man in a flannel shirt at a kitchen table, laughing, a fishing reel in pieces in front of him on a sheet of newspaper, there and gone. "Don't."

"I'm just saying—"

"I said *don't*."

Seth closed his mouth.

And then, from the far end of the couch, very softly, Layla Quinn said, "No."

Edward had been bracing for it without knowing he was bracing. He'd heard her mind go silent the instant he'd said the name, and in two weeks he'd learned that Layla's mind was never silent. It was the most crowded place he'd ever been. Words on top and the deep organ-note humming underneath, and hanging from it in their hundreds, the old Latin keys, swaying. All of it, the whole cathedral, had stopped dead when he said *Harry Potter*, the way a congregation stops when someone drops a hymnal in the middle of a prayer.

Now it came back, all at once.

It came back like a building coming down. He actually took a half step back from it, beside the fireplace, and saw Carlisle's eyes flick to him. Newspaper headlines, crowding, overlapping, black type on yellowed paper with the photographs *moving* in them, a skinny dark-haired boy with round glasses and a thin, lightning-shaped scar on his forehead who kept turning his face away from the camera. A thick type that said NEW YORK GHOST across the top of every page. A date: MAY 1998. A word that came with a cold drop in Layla's stomach every time it surfaced in her memory, a name like a flinch, *Voldemort*. A woman with cropped gray hair and a voice like a whip, standing in front of a blackboard covered in diagrams, saying, *Lucky. Write that down. That boy is the luckiest duelist in history.* And under all of it, over and over, the same image, sharp as a blade. A gray suit on a gray slab in the rain. Two fingers lifted like a man hailing a taxi. Her own spell, her best spell, sitting on his fingertips like a moth, and his eyebrows going up, and his voice saying, *Oh, that's clever.*

Layla sat up.

She did it too fast. The bag of ice slid off her back and hit the floor with a wet slap, and she made a sound through her teeth and went white, and Carlisle was beside her in an instant with a hand on her shoulder. She didn't notice him. She was staring at Edward.

"Say it again," she said.

"Harry Potter."

"*Potter.*" Her voice cracked. "Harry Potter. You're sure? You're absolutely sure? That's what's in her head?"

"Hey," said Leah. "I'm right here."

Layla turned and stared at her instead.

"Is that what he said?" Layla said. "Did he say *Harry Potter*? Those words?"

"Yeah," said Leah. "Why? Who's Harry Potter?"

"Who's—" Layla made a sound. It started out as a laugh and turned into something else halfway, a choking sound, and she put both her honey-crusted hands over her face. "Oh my God. Oh my *God*. Oh, *mierda*. Oh, Gran." She was shaking again. Edward could hear her heart from across the room, going like a hummingbird's. "Glasses? Did he have glasses? A scar? Here?" She touched her forehead, above her right eyebrow, without taking her hand from her face.

"No glasses," said Leah. "No scar. He's got a crooked nose."

"Of course he does," said Layla into her hands. "Of course he doesn't have a scar. Why would he have a scar. It's been eight years. He's—" She dragged her hands down her face. "Seth. Seth, I threw a Disjunction at Harry Potter."

"Okay," said Seth, uncertainly.

"I threw a *Stunner* at him. I threw *roots*. I had *honey on my hands*."

"Layla," said Carlisle gently, "perhaps you should lie back down—"

"I'm not lying down, I'm having a *crisis*." She took a breath. She took another one. Edward heard her reach for something inside herself, a cold clear voice, a working voice, and pull it up around her like a coat. When she spoke again her voice was steadier. "Okay. Okay. None of you know who that is."

"No," said Carlisle.

"Nobody in this room."

"Should we?" said Rosalie.

Layla looked around the room at all of them. At seven vampires and two wolves and one wolf in a doorway, every one of them looking back at her with exactly the same expression, which was polite, attentive, and totally blank. And Edward heard her realize, all at once, how far outside her own world she'd come.

"Right," she said. "Right. Okay. You wouldn't. You'd never have heard of him. It was all on our side of the—" She waved a hand. "Okay. Listen. All of you. You remember I told you there's a government? Lots of governments? One in every country, hidden inside the regular one?"

"We remember," said Carlisle.

"There was a war. In Britain. Our side of Britain. Twice, really, with a gap in the middle. There was this wizard. The worst one in—" She stopped. "The worst one anybody can remember. Ever. Tom Riddle. He called himself Lord Voldemort. People in Britain were so scared of him they wouldn't say his name out loud. Even after he was dead. They still mostly don't. He wanted to—he believed people like me should rule people like Charlie. That anybody without magic was basically livestock. And that any witch or wizard with a Muggle parent was a thief who'd stolen it. He had an army. He killed—" She stopped again. "A lot. He killed a lot. Whole families. For years. And in 1981 he went to a house in a village in the west of England to kill a baby."

The room had gone very quiet.

"The baby's parents got in the way. He killed them. And then he turned his wand on the baby, and he used the Killing Curse, which is the one curse there's no counter for. No shield stops it. Nobody had ever survived it. Not once, in all of history." Layla's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone reciting something she'd learned in school and never once in her life expected to need. "And it bounced. Off the baby. Back onto him. And it destroyed him. And the baby walked away with a scar on his forehead shaped like a lightning bolt. And for the next seventeen years every witch and wizard in the world knew his name. Harry Potter. The Boy Who Lived."

Jacob said, very quietly, "Oh."

"That's not the end," said Layla. "Riddle came back. Fourteen years later. Nobody knows how; there are a hundred theories. And there was another war. And at the end of it, in May of 1998, there was a battle at the school in Scotland. Hogwarts. Hundreds of people. Kids. And at dawn, at the end of it, in front of everybody, Harry Potter and Tom Riddle had a duel. Just the two of them. In the Great Hall." She swallowed. "And Harry Potter won it. He was seventeen. He won the most important duel in a hundred years with a Disarming Charm. A first-year spell. I learned it when I was eleven. And Riddle's own Killing Curse bounced. Again. And killed him. For good."

She was quiet for a second.

"I was fourteen," she said. "At Ilvermorny. I remember the morning the *Ghost* came. Everybody in the dining hall was standing on the benches. People were crying. My dueling instructor, Calloway, she made us study it, that fall. Frame by frame, from every account there was. And she said—" Her mouth twisted. "She said it was the luckiest duel in history. She said the Potter boy was brave and good-hearted and had a dead mother's protection and a wand that liked him, and none of that made him a duelist. She said Riddle beat himself. She said we should write down the word *lucky*."

She looked at Leah.

"He wasn't lucky," she said.

Leah looked back at her, and didn't say anything, and in her mind Edward heard one short, flat, satisfied word: *No.*

"Nobody's heard from him in years," Layla went on. "He just—went. In '99, I think. The *Ghost* ran a piece once, a few years ago. 'Where Is Harry Potter?' Four columns of nothing. Some people said he was in Africa. Somebody said he was dead. Somebody said he was a hermit on an island. He gave one interview after the war and then he never gave another one, and he never had his picture taken again, and that was it. He was just gone." She laughed, the cracked one. "And it turns out he's been on the Olympic Peninsula. In a *suit*. With a *gun*."

---

There was a silence.

Edward listened to it. He listened to all of them, one by one, the way he always did in a silence, and he heard each of them take it in, and he heard each of them, one after another, come down in exactly the wrong place.

Emmett: *So he's famous. Cool. Famous guys are usually soft.*

Rosalie: *A celebrity. A war hero. A boy who got lucky twice, by her own account, and has spent eight years being admired for it. Wonderful.*

Jasper, on the stairs, very calm, very cold: *One man. We are eight. Eleven, with the wolves. Twelve, with the witch. Twenty, if Sam's people come. He's powerful, granted. So was Maria's general in Monterrey, and there was an alley in Monterrey he didn't come out of.*

Carlisle, worried, gentle, hopeful: *A boy who fought a tyrant. Surely he can be reasoned with.*

Jacob: *Okay. Okay. He's a wizard war hero. Fine. We've got two packs. He's one guy.*

Esme only: *She's so happy. Look at her. Why isn't anyone looking at how happy she is?*

And Edward stood by the fire and heard all of it, and found that he was waiting, with a kind of curiosity, for Leah to hear it too.

She did. He could tell the exact second, because she put her apple down.

"Oh my God," Leah said. "You're not getting it."

Everybody looked at her.

"You're none of you getting it." She sat forward on the couch. "I can see it on your faces. Even yours." That was to Jasper, on the stairs. "*Especially* yours. You're sitting there counting. I can see you counting. You're thinking, okay, he's tough, but we've got numbers."

Jasper's face didn't change. Edward heard him think, very mildly, *Well. Yes.*

"Let me tell you something about numbers," said Leah. "This afternoon, on that ridge, he held me and Seth flat on a rock. Both of us. Together we weigh about as much as a truck. He held us down without looking at us. He was holding a vampire in the air behind him at the same time, like a coffee table, and he was fighting *her*." She jerked her chin at Layla. "And she's good. I saw her. She was throwing stuff at him so fast I couldn't even see it, and he was catching it with his hand and throwing it back, and *he never moved his feet*. Not once. I was lying there with my face in the moss and I watched his boots the whole time and they never moved."

"Leah—" Carlisle began.

"I'm not done." She held up a hand. "He's been here two months. *Two months.* And none of you smelled him. Not one of you. Not Jake, not Seth, not me, and we've got the best noses on the peninsula, and none of you, with your super-vampire everything. He's been in Forks. In the *town*. He's been walking around. Eating." She looked at Alice, on the stairs. "And you never saw him coming, did you."

Alice's face went pinched and unhappy. *No.*

"And you." Leah turned to Edward. "I bet you can't hear him either. I bet when he's standing in front of you, you're going to get nothing. I've been in there. I've felt it. He's got locks in his head, like doors on a bank. He keeps stuff behind them that's bigger than this house."

Edward didn't answer. He didn't need to. He could hear her know she was right.

"And in October," said Leah, and her voice went quieter, "he was up on a ridge a mile from here with a pair of binoculars, and he watched her." She pointed at the child in Bella's lap. "Riding on your back, Jake. Holding onto your fur with both hands. Laughing. He told me. He saw it. He's been watching all of you like that, the whole time, from a mile off, and none of you ever knew he was there."

Bella's arms went tight around her daughter. Edward heard it in her, a hot, white, newborn flare of something that wanted very badly to find the man on that ridge and take him apart. He reached over without thinking and laid his hand on her knee.

"He could've come in any night," Leah said. "Any night for two months. He could've taken any one of you. Walked in here and put you all flat on the floor like he did us and taken whoever he wanted and walked back out, and you'd have been lying there on the rug with your eyes going round and round like that leech in the jacket." She looked around the room. "He didn't. Because you're not what he wanted. That's the only reason. That's the *only* reason you're all sitting here."

She let that sit.

"So stop it," she said. "All of you. Stop sitting there making plans like he's some newborn that wandered over the border and we just need to get enough of us around him. I don't even know if there's a word for what he is. I was *in there*. I *felt* it." She sat back on the couch. She picked her apple back up. She took a bite. "And he's mine."

The fire popped.

"So," said Leah, through apple, looking straight at Jacob. "See? Better."

Emmett made the flat-tire noise again. Rosalie hit him on the back of the head without looking.

And Edward, standing by the fire, found that he had to look at the floor for a moment, because the thing that was happening to his face was a smile, and he wasn't at all certain anyone in the room would forgive him for it.

---

It was Jasper who said it. Of course it was. Edward heard it coming two seconds before it arrived, cold and reasonable, rising up in Jasper's mind out of a hundred years of thinking about the worst thing that could happen and then planning for it.

"Unless," Jasper said, from the stairs, "he's done something to her."

Every head turned.

Jasper came down the last two steps. He did it slowly, with his hands loose at his sides, the way he moved around the wolves. "I mean no offense, Leah."

"Bet you don't."

"I don't. I'm asking a question somebody in this room has to ask. Miss Quinn says there are love charms." He looked at Layla. "Potions. Spells that make a person want somebody. You said so, two weeks ago. You checked Seth for one."

"I did," said Layla slowly.

"This man went into Seth's mind like walking through an open door. He has some kind of control over people's thoughts. Edward says there's something living in her head now that isn't hers." Jasper's voice was perfectly calm. "Forgive me. But how do we know what happened on that ridge was an imprint? How do we know he didn't take one look at a wolf who was about to tear his throat out, and decide it'd be useful to have her on a leash?"

The room went still.

Edward heard Leah hear it. He heard something flare in her, hot, and then, a second later, go quiet again, and he heard why. She'd checked. She'd done it without meaning to, the instant Jasper said the word *leash*: gone looking down the line in her own chest for a hook, a pull, anything at the far end yanking her along. And she'd found the man at the other end of it standing in his kitchen with a cup of tea, listening to a song about Kashmir, and the big thing behind its locks at the bottom of him lying with its chin on its paws, and both of them, the man and the thing, facing her way down the line, the same way she was facing theirs.

"Check," said Leah.

Jasper blinked.

"Go on." She looked at Layla. "You did Seth. Do me. Do the thing. If there's a leash on me I want to know about it more than you do."

Layla looked at her for a long moment. Then she held out her hand, and Seth, without being asked, came in off the porch and took the pale mesquite wand out of his own back pocket and put it in her palm.

She got up off the couch. It took her a while. Carlisle had one hand under her elbow the whole way and she let him, which told Edward more about how much her back hurt than anything she'd said. She came around the coffee table and stood in front of Leah, a little bent, her face gray and set, and pointed the wand at the center of Leah's chest.

"Hold still," she said.

"I am still."

"Stiller."

She said the words. Edward knew them, now; he'd heard them in her memory of the bee yard. Old words, older than Latin, with a shape to them like a key turning. A breath of smoke came out of the wand tip and touched Leah's tank top and spread.

And the line appeared.

Everyone in the room saw it. Edward heard every one of them see it: a cord, thick as a wrist, coming out of the center of Leah's chest a little to the left. Glowing, in the bright room, like a coal held in a cupped hand. The same coal-red as Seth's had been, in Layla's memory of the yard.

And twisted around it, all the way along, a second strand. Very dark. Black and deep green, like the bottom of a lake, wound around the red one in a tight, even spiral, the way you'd twist two wires together to make them stronger. The two of them ran out of Leah's chest together and across the room and through the glass of the big back window, northeast, into the dark, and disappeared.

Layla went very still.

"There," she said quietly. "Look. That's him. The dark one. That's coming *in*. From his end." She traced the twist with the wand point, not touching. "Seth's only went one way. This goes both. It's—braided."

She moved the wand down, to where the cord went into Leah's chest. Edward watched her face. He watched her look for the hook. He heard her mind doing it, the cold careful voice going over every inch of the place where the line met the woman: *come on, where's the barb, where's the pull, where does it grab.*

The cord went into Leah's chest and kept going. Down. Through her, the way a root goes down through soil, into somewhere under her that Layla's spell lit up for an instant—something gray and lean and fast, curled up—and then couldn't follow. And around the place where it went down, there was no web. No gold threads of somebody's magic, woven in. Nothing hooked. Nothing set. The cord just went down into her and *tied*. Like a rope around a post.

Layla let the spell go. The line faded.

She stood there for a second, leaning a little on Carlisle's arm.

"There's no hook," she said. "None. Not on her end. And I can't see his end, it goes somewhere I can't follow, but I can see enough of it to know there's nothing on it either." She looked at Jasper. "It's the same thing Seth's got. Same red. Same—whatever it is. It's old. It's from here. Nobody put it on anybody." She glanced at the window, at the dark. "And then something from somewhere else grabbed hold of it from the other end and wound itself all the way around it. And tied it off. At her end." She shook her head slowly. "I've never seen anything like it. That's three times today."

Jasper looked at the place where the line had been for a long moment.

Then he inclined his head to Leah. Courteously. The way, Edward thought, a man in a gray uniform would once have inclined his head to an officer from the other side, under a white flag.

"My apologies, ma'am," he said.

"Whatever," said Leah. But Edward heard something in her ease, a quarter of an inch.

---

They made a plan anyway.

Of course they did. Edward had known they would; he'd heard it building in every mind in the room since Layla said the words *Boy Who Lived*. A family of vampires who had survived as long as this one had did not survive by trusting things they couldn't see. And there was a great deal here they couldn't see.

Carlisle wanted to meet him. Carlisle, being Carlisle, wanted to sit down with this man in a room and talk, and was already composing in his head the courteous, careful sentences he'd begin with, the way he'd composed sentences for the first meeting with Ephraim Black in 1936.

Jasper wanted contingencies. He said so. He said, in his quiet, reasonable voice, that whatever this man was and whatever Leah felt, he had a captive vampire in a room, had been watching their family for two months, and was operating on this peninsula for reasons nobody in the room knew. "I'm asking that we be ready," Jasper said. "Nothing more than that. Ready."

Rosalie said they could leave. Flatly, from the arm of the chair. They'd left places before. They'd left Forks once already, two years ago. They could pack the house in a night and be in Denali in two days, and let the wizards and the wolves sort it out.

"We are not leaving," said Esme, from the kitchen doorway, and Rosalie looked at her, and didn't argue.

Emmett said he'd like a rematch, on Layla's behalf, and Layla said from the couch, without lifting her head, "Please don't."

Alice said nothing. Alice was sitting on the stairs with her eyes half closed, trying to see the next day and getting nothing but a heat shimmer, and Edward could hear how much she hated it.

Jacob paced. And while he paced he talked, and Edward could hear the shape of the plan coming together in his head, built out of the same parts as Jasper's but louder.

"I have to call Sam," Jacob said. "Tonight. I have to. He said a net, right? Leah? He said he's got a net over the whole peninsula. That's the *rez*, too. That's Sam's land. That's Billy's house. Sam's got to know."

"Tell him whatever you want," said Leah. She was lying back on the couch now, her head on the armrest, her feet back up on the coffee table. Edward listened, as Jacob said *Sam*, for the old song. He listened hard. Nothing. Not a flicker. She heard Jacob say the name and her mind went on lying there, warm and loose and faced northeast, the way a sleeping dog's ear doesn't twitch at a noise it's stopped caring about.

"So we tell Sam," Jacob said. "We get both packs. First thing. And Layla—" He turned. "Layla, can you find the tent? If it's wards? You break wards, right? That's your whole job?"

"Was my whole job," said Layla, into the cushion.

"But you *could*. You could find it. Track it, or—"

"Maybe. Probably. Eventually." She lifted her head. "Jacob, they're *his* wards."

"So we find it. Both packs. And the Cullens, if they want. At dawn, when he's—I don't know. Asleep. Whatever. We surround it. We don't go in. We just—make sure he knows we're there. Make sure he knows he can't just go through people's heads and hold them down on rocks and walk off with—"

"Jake," said Leah.

"—and then *Carlisle* talks to him, fine, Carlisle can do the talking, but we're all *there*, so he knows—"

"Jake."

"*What*, Leah?"

She'd sat up on the couch, slowly, and she was looking at him, and Edward heard in her a feeling he'd heard in her only once before, on the field in June, at the very beginning of the fight, before she'd done the stupid thing. A kind of cold, clear, delighted patience. The patience of a hunter who can see the rabbit, and can see the rabbit hasn't seen her.

"You guys know how this works, right?" she said.

Jacob stopped pacing.

"Imprinting," said Leah. "You all know how it works. You've had it explained to you. Some of you—" she looked at Jacob, "—have done the explaining." She tipped her head. "So what was that one law, Jake? The one that stopped Sam?"

Jacob didn't say anything. Edward heard his mind go back. Ten weeks back. To this room, to this couch, to the night the child was born, with Bella dying upstairs and Rosalie standing over the bassinet with her teeth out and the whole of Sam's pack coming through the trees toward the house with their minds full of one thought, *kill it, kill the thing before it kills us*. And Jacob going out onto the porch, alone, to meet them, with the child's face burning in the middle of everything he was.

And Sam stopping at the bottom of the steps. All of them stopping. Because they'd felt what Jacob felt, through the pack bond that had once held them all, and every wolf in Sam's pack had known, the second they felt it, that there was one law older and stronger than any order Sam could give. One law no Alpha could override. The oldest law the wolves had.

Edward had heard it then, in twelve minds at once. He heard it now in one.

"Say it, Jake," said Leah.

"No wolf," said Jacob slowly, "can harm another wolf's imprint."

"Absolute," said Leah. "Right? That's the word Sam used. *Absolute*. It's the one law we've got that nobody gets to break. Not Sam. Not you. Not anybody. Sam had his whole pack coming through those trees to kill her—" She pointed at the child, who had fallen asleep against Bella's shoulder with her fist in Bella's hair. "—and he couldn't. He couldn't touch her. He *couldn't*. None of them could. Their own wolves wouldn't let them."

She leaned back again.

"So you go ahead and call Sam," she said. "Call the whole pack. Bring everybody. Go stand around his tent at dawn with your teeth out. And the second any one of you puts a paw on him—any one of you—you're breaking the oldest law there is. On *my* imprint." She smiled, very slightly, with a lot of teeth. "Go on. I'd love to see you try. I'd love to see your wolf let you."

Jacob stared at her.

"And you lot." She turned her head on the armrest and looked at the Cullens: Carlisle by the fire, Jasper at the foot of the stairs, Rosalie and Emmett in the chair. "Your treaty says you don't bite humans. Right? That's the deal. That's the whole deal. You bite a human and it's war." She shrugged. "He's human. Mostly. He's got a heartbeat. So he's covered too. Congratulations. He's the most protected guy on the whole peninsula." She closed her eyes. "Honestly? I watched him today. That law's for *you*."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

In the doorway, Seth said, very quietly, "She's right."

"I know she's right," said Jacob.

"She's usually right about that stuff."

"I *know*, Seth." Jacob sat down, heavily, on the floor in the middle of the room, with his back against the armchair Emmett was in, and put his face in his hands. "Oh, man. Oh, man, Sam is going to *lose his mind*."

"Probably," said Leah, with her eyes closed.

"He's going to come over here. He's going to want to *see* the guy."

"Let him."

"Leah, if Sam and that guy are in the same place—"

"Then Sam'll find out what I found out." She opened one eye. "You want to know something funny, Jake? When you said his name just now? Sam's?" She closed the eye again. "Nothing. I felt nothing. First time in two years."

And Edward, beside the fire, listening, heard that it was true. He heard it all the way down. He thought about a hundred and five years of hearing people tell themselves the truth and tell themselves lies, and how rarely, in all that time, he'd heard anyone say a true thing that cost them nothing to say.

---

She left at twenty to ten.

She got up off the couch, stretched until her spine cracked, and picked up the core of her apple and looked around for somewhere to put it. Esme came and took it out of her hand without a word. Leah looked at her for a second. Then she said, "Thanks," and it came out awkward, like a word in a language she'd learned from a book, and Esme smiled at her as if she'd been given flowers.

"I'm going home," said Leah, to the room. "I'm tired. I've had a day."

"Leah," said Carlisle. "Will he—would he be willing to come here? To talk with us? I think, given everything, it would be best if we—"

"I'll ask him."

"Tonight?"

"Tomorrow. Maybe." She shrugged. "If he feels like it."

"If *he* feels like it?" said Jacob from the floor.

"He's English," said Leah. "He's weird about visiting. He calls it popping round." She went to the door, where Seth moved aside to let her by. She stopped next to him. She put her hand flat on top of his head, the way you'd pat a dog, and pushed it down, hard, so that he staggered, and Seth laughed, startled, and pushed back. "Go look after your witch."

"I am."

"Take her home. Run her a bath. He said she needs about six aspirin."

"He *said*?"

"On the ridge. He said she'd be fine. Bruised. Hot bath, six aspirin, Murt-something if she's got it." She looked past him at Layla, on the couch. "He checked you on the way over. He said nothing's broken."

Layla had gone very still again.

"He *checked*," Layla said.

"That's what he said."

"While he was walking across the crater. On the *air*."

"I guess."

Layla laid her head back down on the cushion and looked at the ceiling for a long time.

Leah went down the porch steps and across the dark lawn to the big cedar, and Edward listened to her go. He heard her pull off her damp clothes and tie them to her ankle, and he heard her phase, an easy, unforced sound, and he heard her come out from behind the tree on four feet and stand there a second in the rain with her nose up.

Facing northeast.

The music in her head had changed. He heard it go as she ran, fainter and fainter, south and west toward the coast and La Push. Something softer now, acoustic, a guitar picking out a melody that sounded like a stream over stones.

He stood at the open door for a long time after she'd gone, listening to the forest and the rain, and to nothing at all where tomorrow a man was going to be standing in this house, with a silence in his head that had looked at Edward through a gray wall and not bothered to get up.

"Edward," said Bella quietly, behind him.

"Yes."

"Are you all right?"

He thought about it.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "I've never not been able to hear someone who wasn't you."

Bella came and stood beside him at the door. She didn't say anything. She took his hand. Her own was cold and hard and perfect, and holding it was like holding a stone that had been in the sun all day, and he held it while the rain came down on the lawn, and somewhere off to the northeast, twenty miles away, a kettle that he couldn't hear was whistling in a kitchen that he couldn't find.

## Part Two: Standing Authority

---

### III. Harry

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — 7:34 p.m.*

He'd finished his tea first. He'd made a point of it.

She'd gone into the vampires' house. He'd felt it happen from his kitchen, standing at the range with the mug in both hands and the second side of *Physical Graffiti* going round: felt her stop at the edge of a lawn somewhere east of town, and change, and walk up some steps, and then the smell came down the line at him like somebody had opened an oven full of burnt sugar. He'd put the mug down and gripped the edge of the range and waited for her to come back out.

She hadn't come back out. She'd sat down.

He'd felt her sit. Felt her put her feet up on something. Felt something hot and bright and furious go through her, and then felt her start talking, and keep talking, and through all of it, underneath, the line between them stayed easy. Loose. Like a dog's lead when the dog's lying down.

So he'd finished his tea. Then he'd put his boots on and his coat and gone to clean up her brother's girlfriend's mess.

---

The ridge was black and wet and stank of cordite and scorched moss and honey.

He stood on the granite slab in the dark for a while and let his eyes do what they'd been doing for a few years now, which was get better than they had any right to. The rain was coming straight down. The trees along the spine of the ridge were black shapes against a sky that wasn't much lighter. Below him, on the left, the river went on in the valley, loud and high.

It was a mess. He'd known it would be. He hadn't had time to look at it properly that afternoon, what with one thing and another.

The crater first. Six feet across, a foot and a half deep, a ragged bowl of black mud and smashed rock with a ring of spatter thrown out round it. The round had gone in about three inches and then delivered everything forward and up, which was what he'd built it to do, and the moss on the far side was scoured flat in a fan. Past the fan, a furrow. Twenty feet of it, torn through the ferns where she'd gone backward on her shield like a sledge. At the end of the furrow, the nurse log, with a dent in its moss the shape of a woman's back. Ferns crushed flat all round it. A long black hair caught on a sapling.

The boulder he'd put her Blasting Curse into was gravel. Twenty feet to the left of the slab, a spray of granite chips and a scorch.

Up in the hemlocks, twelve feet off the ground, the raw white stump of the branch her first Stunner had taken off. The branch itself was lying in the moss below.

Further along, past the slab, a patch of ground where the moss had turned into roots. Hundreds of them, thick and black and wet, standing up out of the rock like the fingers of something buried, closed round nothing. Her transfiguration. It hadn't worn off. Good transfigurations didn't.

And all over the slab, faint, he could smell blood. Wolf blood. The boy had bitten through his own leg getting to that bead.

Harry went to work.

---

The roots first, because they were the most obviously wrong thing on the ridge. He crouched next to them and put his hand flat on the rock among them and found the shape of what she'd done. Neat. Fast. She'd turned the moss inside out, more or less, and told it to grab. He told it to stop grabbing and turn back, and it went, root by root, down into the rock and flat and green again, a little patch of moss on granite like every other patch. He rubbed it with his thumb. It came up wet and cold and ordinary.

The branch. He lifted it out of the ferns without touching it and looked at the stump twelve feet up and at the clean edge of the break, and decided to leave it. Branches came down in storms. He laid it on its side in the moss with the broken end toward the slab and pressed it into the ground an inch, so it looked like it had been there since the weekend.

The furrow. He walked it end to end, and behind him the ferns stood back up. He let them come up bent and broken; ferns that had been flattened by a woman flying twenty feet backward on a shield ought to look like ferns flattened by something. Storm-damaged. Wind-thrown. He put the black hair in his pocket.

He spent longest on the slab.

Wolf blood on granite. Some of it had gone into the cracks. He found every drop, crouched in the rain with his eyes half shut, the way he'd once found every drop of a Healer's blood on the floor of a flat in Minsk; found it by smell and by something under smell, and lifted it, and it came up out of the rock in a fine red mist and hung in front of him and he folded it down to a bead the size of a lentil and put that in his pocket too. Rain would have done for most of it. Rain wouldn't have done for all of it. A wolf with a good enough nose, or a vampire, or an American Auror with a decent Revealing Charm, could have found the rest.

Then the residue.

That was the part that mattered. Everything else was set dressing, for rangers and hikers and the odd Muggle with a camera. Residue was what a witch or wizard would find. It hung over the whole ridge like the smell after fireworks, thick enough that he could have walked it blindfolded: her magic, gold and sharp, sprayed everywhere; his own, almost nothing, a faint grey smear here and there where he hadn't bothered to sink it; and over everything, very strong, the smell of a thunderstorm. The feather in her wand. It leaked into every spell she threw. He'd noticed it in the fight. He'd noticed it on the wand when he'd held it, the thing trying to hex him from inside its own handle.

He took a stone out of his coat pocket.

It was river stone, grey, the size of a hen's egg, carved all over in the same notation as the two hundred and eleven he'd buried across the peninsula in September. A sink. He'd made a dozen spares. He closed his hand round it and opened his mind and pulled, and the residue came. All of it, off the rock and the moss and the trees and out of the air, a long slow drain like bathwater going down a plughole, into the stone, until the ridge smelled of nothing but rain and burnt moss and gravel and a cut branch.

He opened his hand. The stone was hot. He dropped it in the other pocket.

Then he went to find a tree.

---

He'd tried making lightning once. Dartmoor, 2004, a hillside, a long weekend and a theory. He'd got a bolt out of it, eventually, and been deaf in his left ear for three days, and the National Grid had lost power to most of Okehampton for an hour, and Croaker had looked at him across the desk on the Tuesday morning and said, *Never again, Potter,* in the voice he used for things that weren't requests.

So this time he made a path for it.

The snag was forty yards along the ridge from the crater: an old dead hemlock, broken off at about eighty feet, grey and barkless near the top and peeling lower down. The tallest thing on the spine. If anything up here was going to get hit in a storm it'd be that. He stood at the bottom of it and looked up into the rain.

He transfigured a filament. Copper. Thinner than a hair, and long; from the broken top of the snag, down the trunk on the lee side, into the moss at the foot of it and then along the ground, three inches down, straight to the crater. Then down into the crater, and into the rock at the bottom of it, as deep as he could push it. He felt it go in, a thin hot line of metal in the stone, like a needle in a vein.

Then he sat down with his back against a cedar out of the worst of the rain and waited.

The cloud was low and heavy. He'd been watching the weather for a week; there'd been two cells come through since Thursday with lightning in them, out over the water, and this was the back end of the second. The air on the ridge had that feel to it. He could feel the charge up there, a vast slow pressure overhead like a held breath. He gave it a nudge. Not much. Just enough to make the top of the snag the most interesting thing within a mile.

He took a Mars bar out of his inside pocket and ate it.

Twenty minutes in, he felt Leah get angry. Really angry. A hot white jolt through the line that made him sit up against the cedar with the Mars bar halfway to his mouth. He held still. He didn't go down the line to see what it was about; he'd decided that on the bench, watching her run, that he wasn't going to go down the line uninvited. It lasted about a minute. Then it went quiet. Then, a few minutes after that, something came up the line at him that he'd felt from her only once before, standing in the rain by the tent saying *I'm freaking out*. Something hot and pleased and fierce.

He sat there in the rain and didn't know what it was, and found he was grinning anyway.

At eight-thirty-one the sky opened.

He saw it come. A white line, crooked, out of the cloud and straight down into the top of the snag. The flash lit the whole ridge, every tree, every rock, the river in the valley, the rain hanging in the air like a curtain of glass beads; and the sound arrived at the same moment, a single flat crack so loud it landed like a blow. The snag jumped. Bark went off it in a long spiral strip from top to bottom, peeling away like the skin off an orange, and the trunk split from the broken crown halfway down with a noise like a ship's mast going. Steam blew out of it. The filament went white and vanished, every inch of it, all at once, down the trunk and along the ground in a line of blue fire under the moss and into the crater.

The crater lit up from inside. It went off like a drum. Mud and gravel jumped out of it in a ring, and settled, and steamed.

Then it was dark again and his ears were ringing and the rain was hissing on hot wood.

Harry stood up. He walked over.

The snag was split and smoking, its bark lying in curled strips round its foot. Running from its base to the crater, through the moss and the soil, was a scorched trench two inches wide where the current had followed the filament, and where it had gone into the rock at the bottom of the crater the stone had cracked into a star. Down in the cracks, where the filament had been, there was a thin twisted thread of green-black glass, fused out of the soil and the sand in the granite. He knelt and looked at it. Fulgurite. He'd only ever seen it in a museum in Kensington.

He looked at the whole thing for a while: the snag, the trench, the crater with its star of cracks and its glass, the gravel of the boulder lying twenty yards off in the rain, the branch down in the ferns. A dead tree on a high ridge, a cold-season thunderstorm, a strike with enough current in it to throw rock around. Any ranger who walked up here this week would shake his head and take a photograph and go home.

And the strike was real. That was the bit he liked. The Americans had a network of sensors across the whole country that recorded every lightning strike to ground, time and place to the nearest few hundred yards. If anybody in a uniform ever wanted to check, there'd be a strike on the record: Monday, November 20th, 8:31 p.m., north-east slope of the Bogachiel.

A good lie, in his experience, wanted as much truth in it as you could pack in round the edges. You just needed the middle to hold.

---

He found the American kit on the way out, which he hadn't expected, and which he didn't like.

He'd done a sweep in September. A proper one, the whole peninsula, three days, looking for exactly this: any sign that MACUSA had sensors in the park. They hadn't. The nearest thing he'd found was an old Ministry of Something survey marker from the thirties on Mount Olympus, dead as a doornail. So when he walked the ridge one last time in a widening spiral with his senses open, looking for anything else of hers he might have missed, he wasn't looking for American equipment. He found it anyway. Three miles south, on the next ridge over, he felt it: a faint, steady hum at the edge of his attention, regular as a pulse, coming from a Douglas fir at the top of the slope.

He Apparated over and stood under the tree.

It was in the trunk. Behind the bark, about nine feet up, a disc of brass the size of a coaster, set flat into the wood and grown over with a skin of bark so thin you'd have needed to put your hand on the exact spot to feel the edge. He didn't need to put his hand on it. He could see it, the way he could see wards: a little bright knot of magic in the tree like a coin in a pocket. It had an eagle on it. It had a number engraved round the rim.

He'd seen the drawings of these in 2004, in a file on Level Nine that somebody had stolen from somebody else. MACUSA field monitors. Passive, very sensitive. They sat in a place for months and listened, and recorded, and once a week they whispered whatever they'd heard down a line back to whoever had planted them. Somebody in Seattle would be getting a weekly summary of everything magic that had happened in a ten-mile circle round this tree.

The bark over it was fresh. A fortnight old. Maybe a bit more.

He stood in the rain and thought about that.

A fortnight ago was the fourth of November. The night the witch stood in her grandmother's back garden and held a few million raindrops in the air at once for a boy she fancied, and then the next afternoon went up the hill and lit the vampires' living room up like a planetarium. He'd felt both of those from his kitchen. Everybody within thirty miles with any sensitivity at all would have felt both of those.

So the Americans had felt them too. And they'd come out and planted a listening post, quietly, on a ridge three miles from where Layla Quinn had just had a duel.

Croaker had said they were making noises. Croaker had said *nothing with your name on it.* Croaker had been right. It was her name it'd have on it, if it had one.

Harry reached up and put his hand flat on the bark over the disc.

He went in gently. The thing was built well, with a little tamper-ward round it that would have shrieked down the line to Seattle if anyone tried to pull it out of the tree, and it held a week's memory in it the way a shell holds the sound of the sea. He didn't touch the tamper-ward. He went in underneath it, the way you'd go in under a fence, and found the memory, and turned it over.

There. Eleven forty-two this morning. A spike, enormous, right off the top of its little scale. The disc had nearly fallen out of the tree. He could read the shape of it, if he squinted: a rush of hot gold, a lot of it, sharp and fast. And through all of it, over and over, the smell of a storm. The Thunderbird. Every spell she'd thrown had carried that smell up the valley like a flag.

He thought about it for about four seconds. Then he started to edit.

He left the spike exactly where it was, the same time, the same size. Gaps were what people looked at. What he changed was the shape. He took the gold out of it, thread by thread, the human part, the bit that said *a witch did this*, and every thread he took out he replaced with more of the storm. He thickened the feather-smell until it was all there was. Then he gave it a tail. A long faint smear across the disc's memory, coming in from the south and going out to the north-west over the next forty seconds, the way a big animal would register if it came in low over the ridge in a hurry and kept going.

He drew his hand back and looked at what he'd made.

As far as the little brass disc in the tree was concerned, at eleven forty-two this morning, something very large and very magical with a thunderstorm for a heartbeat had come up the Bogachiel valley from the south, panicked, let loose with everything it had over the ridge, and gone off north-west toward the coast.

Thunderbirds lived in Arizona. Mostly. They didn't come up as far as Washington. Mostly. Somebody in Seattle, in about four days' time, was going to read this week's summary off this disc and put their coffee down very slowly, and then they were going to ring a Magizoologist, and the Magizoologist was going to spend most of December walking up and down the Olympic coast in the rain with a pair of Omnioculars looking for a bird that wasn't there.

And there'd be a strike on the Muggle record at eight thirty-one tonight, in the same valley. Weather was weather. Big storm birds made weather.

"Have fun with that," Harry said to the tree.

He left the bark exactly as it had been. He Apparated home.

---

Gary was on the canvas by the door.

"Don't," Harry told him, and went in.

It was a quarter to ten. He hung his coat up and pulled his boots off and stood in the hall in his socks with the rain drumming on the roof somewhere far overhead, and felt for her.

She was moving. She'd come out of the vampires' house and changed and was running, west and south, toward the coast. Toward La Push. Toward home. He could feel the ground going by under her and the cold air coming into her chest and the wolf in her running loose and easy. And down at the bottom of him, behind the locks, the thing he kept there lifted its head off its paws and turned it, slowly, following her, the way a compass needle follows north when you walk round with it.

It was calmer than it had been. A lot calmer. Since he'd put his hand on her face out there on the bench, it had been lying quiet. Head up. Watching. But quiet.

He looked at the mirror hanging on its nail by the telephone.

The thing at the bottom of him stopped watching her and looked at him instead.

He felt it do it. He'd never felt it do that before, not in three years; turn its whole attention round, off whatever it was looking at, onto him, the way a dog lying by a fire will turn its head when you pick up the car keys. It knew what the mirror was. It had been there at twenty to six, at the bottom of him, when he'd stood here and said *No, sir. Nothing else.* It knew.

He reached for the mirror.

It growled.

He felt that in his chest like a second heartbeat, low and long, shaking. It came up through the locks. The locks had been holding against it for two months like three iron doors, and since one o'clock this afternoon they'd been more like three garden gates, and the growl came up through them as if they weren't there and filled the hall. He heard it. Actually heard it. In his own throat, under his breath, a sound he'd never made in his life coming out of him in a Ministry hallway in a tent in Washington State.

He took his hand back off the mirror.

"Yeah," he said, out loud. "I know."

It went on growling. It got up. He felt it get up off its belly and stand, down there in the dark, with its head low and its ears flat and every hair on its back standing up, and lean on the gates. It was trying to get between him and the mirror. Between the mirror and *her*. London meant nothing to it. It understood the mirror was a door, and that through the door was something old and patient and grey that collected things in jars, and that he was about to open that door and say her name into it.

"I'm not giving her to him," Harry said.

The growl didn't stop.

"I'm not. He's not having her. Nobody's having her. You were there this afternoon; you heard what I told her. They want her, they come through me." He stood in the hall in his socks and talked to his own chest like a madman. "But I'm not lying to him either. All right? I've known him eight years. He's never lied to me once, not about anything that mattered. He sent me to Volterra and told me exactly what I'd find. He told me exactly what the oath would do before I put my hand on that stone." He breathed out. "I lied to him tonight. To his face. In about four seconds. I'm not having that sit there."

The growl went lower. It didn't stop.

"You don't get a vote," said Harry.

It leaned on the gates harder.

"Fine. You get half a vote." He reached for the mirror again, and this time he didn't stop. "And you can sulk about it afterwards."

He took the mirror down off its nail. The growl went up into a snarl, down at the bottom of him, a long ugly sound he could feel in his back teeth, and then cut off, abruptly. He felt it turn its back on him. It went and lay down again behind the gates, facing away, nose toward the south-west and La Push, with its spine to him.

Sulking.

"Right," Harry said. "Good. Thank you."

He held the mirror up. "Croaker."

---

The glass went grey, then dark, then it was the little wood-panelled room again with the green-shaded lamp. The drifts of paper on the desk were in a slightly different arrangement. Croaker was in the same chair in the same robes. There was a cup of tea at his elbow now, with a saucer on top of it to keep it warm. He was writing.

He didn't look up.

"Potter," said Croaker. "It's a quarter to six."

"You're up, sir."

"I am always up." The pen scratched. "You said that four hours ago. You'll want a new opening."

"I lied to you," said Harry.

The pen stopped.

Croaker didn't look up for a moment. He sat with the pen resting on the paper, looking at whatever he'd been writing. Then he put the pen down, square with the edge of the page, and picked up the saucer off his tea, and set it to one side, and lifted the cup and drank, and set the cup down, and only then raised the tap-water eyes out of the mirror to Harry's.

"Yes," he said.

Harry stared at him. "You knew."

"You said *it's the rain, sir*." Croaker folded his hands on the desk. "In eight years you have never once blamed the weather for anything. Not in Lagos, when it rained for a month and you were sleeping in a drain. Not in Minsk, at thirty below. You don't blame the weather, Potter. It's one of the things I like about you." He waited. "Go on."

Harry opened his mouth.

At the bottom of him, with its back turned, the thing behind the gates went perfectly still.

"I'm compromised," Harry said.

Croaker didn't move.

"There's a local. A woman. She's—one of the shapeshifters. The wolves. She was on the ridge when I took the specimen. She and her brother flushed him, and then her brother's girlfriend turned up, who's a witch, an American, and there was a—" He stopped. "There was a fight. The witch. I'll put it in the report. That's not the point. The point is after. I looked at the woman. The wolf. I took my glasses off to get a proper look at her, because I couldn't read her, and I looked at her, and something happened."

"Something," said Croaker.

"A bond. A—" He hunted for the word, and found the one he'd been avoiding for nine hours. "The boy's got a word for it. Her brother. The shapeshifters have a word for it. They call it—"

"Imprinting," said Croaker.

Harry stopped dead.

The mirror was very quiet. The green lamp hummed.

"You know the word," Harry said slowly.

"I know a great many words, Potter. I've been in this Department since 1945." Croaker's face didn't change at all. "Go on. You imprinted. Or she did. Or, if you're going to tell me what I think you're going to tell me, both."

"Both," said Harry. "Both ways. Both of us. At once. It's not supposed to do that, sir, I went through the boy's head this afternoon, I saw every imprint that pack's ever had, and every one of them went one way. This one went both. It's tied off at both ends. I can feel her. Right now. I know where she is. I know what she's feeling. I know she's on four legs running south-west about forty miles an hour and she's tired and she's happy." He heard his own voice go rough on that and didn't stop. "And I can't lie to her. Physically. I tried. I opened my mouth to give her my legend and my name came out instead. And she can't lie to me. And—"

He stopped.

"And?" said Croaker.

Harry put his hand flat on the top of his chest, under his collarbone, where the oath lived. It was sitting there the way it always sat. Cold, heavy, quiet.

"And I told her," he said. "Everything. In my kitchen, at half past one this afternoon. What I am. What we are. What we do. Volterra. The strike. Why I want the specimen. All of it. And the oath didn't do anything." He took his hand off his chest. "It didn't even twitch, sir. It sat there and listened to me tell a Quileute girl in a pair of cut-off jeans every single thing I've ever sworn not to tell anybody, and it didn't so much as clear its throat."

For the first time in eight years, Harry saw Saul Croaker's face move.

The eyebrows. They went up perhaps an eighth of an inch, and stayed there, and the tap-water eyes went very slightly wider, and very slightly brighter. Like a man who's been told there's a new sort of beetle in his garden.

"It didn't fire," Croaker said.

"No, sir."

"Not at all."

"No, sir. And then she went home, and changed, and her pack came into her head, the way they do, they can all hear each other. And they started to see it. And the oath woke up. It went down the—it went down the line, sir. Into her head. Twenty miles. And it sealed everything of ours that was in there. Everything. Neat as you like. They could see my kitchen and they couldn't see a word of what I'd said in it." He swallowed. "It treated her head like it was mine."

Croaker sat back in his chair.

He sat there for a long time, looking at nothing, with his hands folded on his stomach. Harry waited. Down at the bottom of him, the thing behind the gates had turned its head round, a little, over its shoulder, and was listening.

"That stone," said Croaker at last, "was quarried before there was a Britain, Potter. Before there was a Ministry. Before there was anyone in these islands who'd have known what to call a Ministry. Merlin didn't make it. Merlin found it. It has sat in that room on Level Nine since before the Normans, and every Unspeakable since has put his hand on it, and in all that time it has never once been wrong about who is inside and who is outside." He paused. "Not once."

"Well," said Harry. "It is now."

"No," said Croaker. "I don't think it is."

Harry looked at him.

"I think," said Croaker mildly, "that it's looked at whatever ties you to this young woman, and come to a conclusion about it. And I think the conclusion is that she is inside. Which, as far as the oldest thing in this building is concerned, settles the matter." He picked up his teacup. "Interesting."

"Sir—"

"I said interesting, Potter. Actionable is a different word." He drank. "Finish. You've more."

He had more. He gave it to him. He did it the way he'd given him the specimen report four hours ago, in order, plainly, the way Croaker liked.

The child. A child in the coven's house, a girl, born about a week before Harry arrived, half vampire and half human, growing like a weed; the gold-and-red on the lattice that had sulked for two days. He'd known about her since September. He hadn't reported her. He'd been deciding.

Casimir's errand. Sent by the old man in Volterra to check whether the Cullen boy's wife had been changed, because Caius had been nagging. And Casimir, at four o'clock this morning, standing in the trees behind the white house for an hour listening to a heartbeat that went like a bird's.

The telephone call. Made at ten past two this afternoon, from the specimen room, under Imperius, without authorization, without planning, without a single other person in the Department knowing it was happening. Aro on the other end, laughing. *Nothing at all of interest, in all that rain?* And Casimir straining under the curse to say *heartbeat*, and Harry holding him on the rein with everything he had until he said *dogs* instead.

The witch. Layla Quinn. Her breach: the shapeshifters, the coven, and a Muggle police chief. Clause Seventy-Three. And the deal Harry had made with her lying in the ferns: her silence for a favour, any favour, whenever he rang.

The MACUSA monitor in the Douglas fir. The fortnight-old bark. The thunderbird.

When he was done, Croaker was quiet for a while.

"A child," he said at last.

"Yes, sir."

"Half and half."

"As near as I can tell. She's got a heartbeat. She eats. She's got venom in her, too, or something like it. The lattice can't make up its mind about her." Harry hesitated. "She's ten weeks old and she looks about three. I watched her ride round on a wolf's back last month, laughing her head off."

"And Aro doesn't know."

"Aro doesn't know."

"Because you stopped a vampire from telling him. On a telephone. At ten past two this afternoon. Without asking anyone."

"Yes, sir."

"If it had gone wrong—"

"It didn't."

"If it had gone wrong, Potter, Aro would now be in possession of the knowledge that someone is holding his friend in a place his tracker can't reach, and making him say things he doesn't mean. Which is to say, he'd know there was someone. And in the spring, when we go to Volterra, he'd be waiting."

"It didn't go wrong," said Harry. "Sir."

Croaker looked at him for a moment longer.

"It was the right call," he said. "Don't ever do it again."

"No, sir."

"And the witch." Something happened at the corner of Croaker's mouth that might, on another man's face, have been the beginning of a smile. "You've blackmailed an American curse-breaker."

"Yes, sir."

"Is she any good?"

Harry thought about the Disjunction sitting on his fingertips. "She's very good."

"Then good." Croaker set his cup down. "We need one. Ours are all in Egypt."

"Sir," said Harry. "There's one more thing."

Croaker waited.

Harry found that his hand had gone back to his chest. Lower than the oath, and to the left, where the line went in.

"You should send someone else," he said. "Out here. Grimshaw. Or Pell, Pell's good with the Americans. Somebody who isn't—" He stopped. "I'm compromised, sir. I said so. I meant it. I'm not reliable. Not any more. Not where she's concerned." He made himself say it all the way to the end, plainly, the way he'd have wanted it said to him. "If it ever comes down to the Department or her, it's her. Every time. Every single time. I wouldn't even have to think about it. And you can't have a man out here who's like that."

The mirror was silent.

Down at the bottom of him, the thing behind the gates had turned all the way round. It was facing him again. Its ears were up.

Croaker looked at him out of the mirror for a long time. His face was as still as it always was. Harry couldn't read it. He'd never once been able to read it, in eight years; he'd tried, early on, when he was young and stupid and still thought being good at Legilimency meant you ought to use it on everyone, and he'd gone into Croaker's head for exactly half a second and come out the other side with a nosebleed and a very clear memory of being handed his own mind back, folded, like a shirt.

"Are you finished?" said Croaker.

"Yes, sir."

"Good." Croaker picked up his pen. "You're staying."

Harry blinked.

"You're not going anywhere, Potter. You're right where you need to be." The pen went back to the paper. Scratched. "Do you know how many letters there are in that drawer?" He nodded, without looking, at the left side of the desk. "From people upstairs. Every year. Every year since you came downstairs, there's been someone in the Minister's office or the Wizengamot or the Auror Office who thinks Harry Potter would be very much better used somewhere he could be seen. Somewhere he could have his photograph taken. Shaking hands. Opening things." Scratch. "I keep them. I file them. I've never answered a single one."

"Sir, I don't—"

"Four hours ago, you lied to me. Very well, I'll grant you. It was a good lie. Short, no detail, an excuse I couldn't check." Croaker didn't look up. "And then you stood in your tent and thought about it for four hours, and you rang me at a quarter to six in the morning, to tell me you'd done it. And to tell me exactly how far you could be trusted, and where it stopped." Scratch. "There isn't another man or woman in this building who'd have made that call. I've been here sixty-one years. I know. They'd have sat on it. They'd have told themselves it didn't matter, or that they'd tell me when it was convenient, or that what I didn't know couldn't hurt me." He set the pen down again and looked up. "That's why. That, exactly. That integrity is exactly why I'll never get rid of you, Potter. And I shan't be sending Grimshaw anywhere. Grimshaw lies to me twice a week about his expenses."

Harry stood in his hallway in his socks with the mirror in his hand and found he didn't have anything to say.

"As for *the Department or her*," said Croaker. "Every man worth having has something he'd choose over us. The ones who say they haven't are lying, or worse, they're telling the truth, and those are the ones I lose sleep over." He tapped the desk once with a fingertip. "Now. Practicalities."

"Sir."

"The Forks coven. They're down as asset-probable. Upgrade them. Asset. You're to cultivate them. You're to keep that child out of Aro's hands; that's a standing order now, not a favour you did on a whim. If the Italians find her, they'll go to war over her, and they'll go to war in the wrong place and at the wrong time." Croaker glanced at a sheet of paper. "The packs. Both of them. I'd like a working relationship. A formal one, if they'll stomach it."

"They won't like that, sir."

"Then we'll find something they will like. If it takes making you liaison to La Push to keep you there," said Croaker, "so be it. Department liaison to the Quileute shapeshifters. We've never had one. We've never needed one." He made a small dry sound in his throat. "I'll have a title drawn up. Something with a lot of words in it. You'll hate it."

"I already hate it."

"Good. Given the amount of supernatural activity on that peninsula," said Croaker, "two packs, a coven with talents, a hybrid child, a curse-breaker with a big mouth and a fondness for theatricals, a field office in Seattle getting curious, and you, I imagine you'd have been staying a while longer anyway." He picked up his pen. "This just makes it tidy."

"Yes, sir."

"One more thing." Croaker held up two fingers, without lifting his eyes from the page. "You'll need to tell the coven about Casimir's errand. They need to know someone came. They need to know what was asked and what was answered. If you're going to keep that child safe you'll want her family frightened in the right direction."

"The oath won't let me."

"The oath will let you," said Croaker, "if I tell it to." He laid his left hand flat on the desk in front of him, palm down, the way Harry had laid his own on a black stone eight years ago. "Unspeakable Potter. I am releasing the Casimir material to you for use with the Forks coven and the local packs at your discretion. The errand. The telephone call. The child. What was asked and what was answered." His voice didn't change; it was the voice he used for reading out the canteen rota. "Nothing of ours. Nothing of why."

And under Harry's collarbone, the oath moved.

He felt it. A slow, cold, heavy turning, like a key going round in a very old lock. Something in his chest that had been closed went open, a crack, exactly the width of one afternoon, one telephone call, one child's heartbeat. And then he felt it go further. Down the line. Out of him and away south-west across the dark, thirty miles of it, into a running wolf—and in her head, the grey stone wall it had put there at half past six shifted. A section of it, exactly the shape of a phone call, went soft, and thinned, and was gone. And behind it, bright and clear in Leah's memory, an old man's voice laughing down a telephone line.

Thirty miles away, running, Leah stumbled.

He felt her stumble. He felt her stop on a beach somewhere, in the dark, with the surf going, and turn her head to look back up the line toward him. A question in it. *What did you just do?*

*My boss,* he thought down the line, before he could decide not to.

He felt her think about that. Then he felt her shake herself, nose to tail, the way a dog does coming out of water, and start running again.

"There," said Croaker. "You'll have felt that."

"Yes, sir."

"Good." Croaker picked up his teacup, found it empty, and looked at it with mild disappointment. "And Potter."

"Sir."

"The young woman."

At the bottom of Harry, the thing behind the gates went absolutely still.

"No one in this Department will lay a finger on her," said Croaker. "Or the child. Not to study. Not to measure. Not for a paper. Not for anything." He set the cup down on its saucer. "You have my word."

Harry looked at him.

He thought about eight years. About Volterra, and the oath, and every other thing Croaker had ever told him, flatly, in that same voice, without decoration. He couldn't remember a single one that had been wrong.

"I'll hold you to that, sir," he said.

"I'd expect nothing less." Croaker picked his pen back up. "Your report's due Friday. Write two. One for the file and one for me. Put the part about the oath in mine."

"Yes, sir."

"I'd very much like to understand it, one day."

"No," said Harry.

"Eventually," said Croaker, and the mirror went dark.

---

He hung it back on its nail.

He stood there in the hall for a while, the way he'd stood there four hours before, with his hand flat on the wall beside the mirror and his head down.

Then he went and sat on the bottom of the three wooden steps by the door, in his socks, with his elbows on his knees, and felt for the thing at the bottom of him.

It was still lying down behind the gates. It had turned back round, though. It was lying with its chin on its paws and its eyes on him, and he could feel it thinking about what it had just heard, in the slow, enormous way it thought about things. Turning it over. Sniffing it.

*Nobody touches her.*

It had understood that bit. He could tell. London and the stone and the Department and Grimshaw's expenses had gone straight past it. What it had caught was an old grey thing at the far end of a door saying *nobody touches her*, and that whatever else that old grey thing was, it hadn't been lying.

It let out a long breath. He felt that too, in his own chest: a long, slow, grudging sigh.

Then it put its head down on its paws, and turned it to the south-west, and went back to watching her.

"You're welcome," said Harry.

It didn't say anything. It never did. But the gates, all three of them, felt about a foot wider than they had that morning, and he had a feeling that if he went and checked them in the morning they'd be wider still.

He got up and went and washed his mug.

## Part Three: Sleepless

### IV. Leah

*La Push — 10:22 p.m.*

The kitchen light was on.

She saw it from the trees behind the house, a yellow square in the dark with rain slanting through it, and she stood in the ferns for a minute on four feet and looked at it before she phased. She knew what it meant. Her mom went to bed at ten. Her mom had gone to bed at ten every night of Leah's life except the night Dad died, and the night Leah and Seth didn't come home, and the morning after Seth met Layla. If the kitchen light was on at twenty past ten, her mom was sitting at the table under it, waiting.

She phased back. She untied her clothes from her ankle. They were still damp from the afternoon and now they were soaked from the run, and she pulled them on anyway and stood barefoot in the wet grass behind the woodshed, and checked the line.

He was in his kitchen. Washing something. A mug, she thought. She could feel the hot water on his hands, almost, the way you can almost feel a sound you're remembering. Something had happened down the line about twenty minutes ago, on the beach at the bottom of the reservation, something cold going through the wall in her head like a draft through a crack, and a piece of the gray had just gone. Thinned and gone. And behind it there'd been an old man's voice on a telephone, laughing. *Nothing at all of interest, in all that rain?*

*My boss,* he'd said, down the line, when she asked.

She'd stood on the sand with the surf going and thought about that. About a man who could hold two wolves flat on a rock without looking at them, and still said *my boss* like that. Like it meant something to him. Then she'd shaken herself off and kept running, because there wasn't anything else to do with it.

She went in the back door.

Her mom was at the kitchen table in her bathrobe, with a mug of tea in both hands and her reading glasses pushed up on top of her head. There was a plate in front of the chair across from her with a piece of fry bread on it and a sheet of paper towel over the top to keep it warm.

Sue looked up when the door opened. She looked at Leah standing in the doorway, wet through, barefoot, with mud to the knee. She looked at her face for a long moment.

"Sit down," Sue said. "Eat."

Leah sat. She took the paper towel off the fry bread. It was still warm. She hadn't known she was hungry again until she smelled it. She ate half of it in two bites.

Her mom watched her eat.

"So," said Sue.

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything."

"You said *so*."

"*So* isn't anything. *So* is a word people say." Sue drank her tea. "Seth called."

Of course Seth had called. Leah ate the rest of the fry bread.

"Jacob called Billy," said Sue. "Billy called me. Then Seth called again, from the witch's house, to tell me what Jacob had told Billy was wrong, and to tell me what really happened. And then Billy called back to say Seth was exaggerating. And then Emily called."

Leah stopped chewing.

"Sam told her," said Sue. "Jacob called Sam, a little after nine. She wanted to know if you were all right." She set the mug down on the table, very carefully, the way she set things down when she was trying not to say something. "I told her I'd let her know."

Leah swallowed. She waited for it. She sat at the kitchen table where she'd eaten breakfast every day of her life and said, in her head, *Emily*, and then *Sam*, deliberately, the way you'd press on a bruise to see if it still hurt.

She felt Emily. A little. She'd always feel Emily; Emily had been her best friend since before either of them could walk, and Emily had cried at Dad's funeral harder than anybody except Mom, and there was a picture on the fridge right now, held up with a magnet shaped like a salmon, of the two of them at eight years old on First Beach in matching red swimsuits. That was a bruise. That would probably always be a bruise.

She felt for Sam.

There wasn't anything there. A place where something had been. Like the gap where a tooth came out, that your tongue keeps going to. She went to it, and it was smooth.

"Tell her I'm fine," said Leah.

Her mom looked at her.

"I mean it," said Leah. "Tell her I'm fine. Tell her—" She stopped. She didn't know what else to tell her. "Tell her I said thanks for asking."

Sue's eyebrows went up a long way.

"Okay," Sue said slowly. "I'll tell her."

They sat for a while. The rain came down on the roof. In the next room the clock on the mantel ticked, the one that had been Dad's grandfather's, that ran four minutes fast and always had.

"Seth says he's English," said Sue.

"Yeah."

"Seth says he's a wizard. Like Layla."

"Yeah."

"Seth says he shot at Layla."

"At the *ground*, Mom. Next to her."

"Seth says he put her through the air twenty feet."

"She's fine. She's bruised. He said so."

"Seth says," said Sue, "he's got a vampire tied up in his house."

"In a circle. On the floor." Leah wiped her fingers on the paper towel. "It ate a lady from Olympia, Mom. A dental hygienist. She was taking a picture of the river. Her name was Dana."

Her mom didn't say anything to that for a moment.

"Well," Sue said. "Good." She picked up her tea again. "Seth says he's a lot older than you."

"Six years."

"Your father was seven years older than me."

"I know, Mom."

"Seth says," said Sue, and this time she didn't look at Leah when she said it, she looked at her tea, "his name is Harry."

The clock ticked.

Leah looked down at the table. There was a scar in the wood by her right hand, a long pale gouge, from the time Dad had been cleaning a fish here when she was six and the knife had slipped and Mom had yelled at him for twenty minutes about the table, and he'd stood there letting her, grinning, holding his bleeding thumb up in the air, until she ran out of things to yell and started laughing. Leah had sat right here and watched the whole thing. She put her finger in the gouge.

"Mom," she said.

"I'm not saying anything."

"You *are*."

"I'm saying his name is Harry. Seth says it is. Is it?"

"Yeah."

"Then I'm saying a true thing." Sue drank her tea. "I'm not saying anything about it."

"Good."

"Your father," said Sue, "would have hated him."

Leah laughed. She didn't mean to. It came up out of her chest the way the laugh had come up out of her on the ridge above the Cullen place, back at the beginning of October, the night Seth's head was full of Layla and Jacob was yelling *DUDE* and she'd nearly fallen over a root. It surprised her. She put her hand over her mouth.

"Yeah," she said behind her hand. "Yeah, he would."

"A white man. With a gun. And an accent." Sue shook her head. "In a suit, Seth says. Your father would have taken one look at him and gone out to the garage and started cleaning his shotgun where the man could see him through the window."

"He'd have done it on purpose."

"He'd have done it every time the man came to the door. For a year." Sue set her mug down. "For about a day."

Leah looked up.

"For about a day," her mother said again. "And then he'd have found out the man knew something about engines, or fishing, or that the man could hold his beer, or that the man would sit on the porch with him in the rain and not say anything for an hour. And that would have been that. Your father never could hate anybody longer than a day. It wore him out." She stood up and took Leah's plate. "He'd have taken him out on the boat. Bet you anything."

She carried the plate to the sink.

Leah sat at the table with her finger in the gouge in the wood and her eyes stinging, and down the line, thirty miles off, she felt the man in the kitchen in the tent put the clean mug upside down on a towel and stand there with his hands on the edge of the sink and his head down, the way you stand when you're tired, and she wondered if he could feel this. Her, at this table. Her dad's table. She thought maybe he could.

"Thursday," said her mom, at the sink, with her back to her.

"What?"

"Thursday. Bring him."

"What's Thursday?"

Sue turned around and looked at her over the top of her reading glasses, which had come down off her head at some point and were sitting on the end of her nose.

"Leah," she said. "It's Thanksgiving."

"Oh." Leah had forgotten. She'd forgotten completely. Last year she hadn't come, she'd been out on patrol on purpose, running the long loop by herself, because Emily and Sam had come for dessert. "Mom. He's English."

"So?"

"So they don't *have* Thanksgiving. They don't—it's a pilgrim thing, they were the ones the pilgrims left. They're probably mad about it."

"Then he can learn," said her mother. "Bring him. Two o'clock. Tell him to bring something."

"Like what?"

"I don't care. A pie." Sue turned back to the sink. "Englishmen can make pies. They make all kinds of pies. They put kidneys in them."

"Mom, I am not bringing a guy to Thanksgiving who puts *kidneys* in a—"

"Two o'clock," said Sue. "Go to bed."

---

She went to bed.

She took a shower first, standing under the hot water until it ran cold, with her forehead against the tile. She put on a T-shirt, an old one of Dad's, huge on her, washed so many times it was soft as a rag, and a pair of sleep shorts, and she got into bed in the little room at the back of the house that looked out over the yard to the trees, the room she'd slept in since she was born. She turned off the light.

She lay there.

The rain came down on the roof. The wind came in off the ocean and moved the cedars at the edge of the yard. In the next room, the clock ticked, four minutes fast. Down the hall, she heard her mom's door close, and the bed creak, and then nothing.

She lay on her back and looked at the ceiling, and felt the line.

It went out of the middle of her chest and through the wall and away northeast, across the reservation, across the river, across twenty-odd miles of dark forest, and into him. She could feel exactly where he was. She could have closed her eyes and pointed.

He'd gone to bed too. She'd felt him do it. Felt him turn off the record player, and the lights, and go down the long slate hallway past all the doors to the little room with the narrow bed made tight as a soldier's, and get into it.

He wasn't sleeping either.

She knew that the way she knew her own heartbeat. He was lying on his back in the dark, the same as her, looking at a ceiling. She could feel the narrow bed under him. She could feel him lying very still, with his hands folded on his chest, the way somebody lies who's been trained to go to sleep anywhere, in a drain, in the snow, and who's lying there now, wide awake, not understanding why it isn't working.

She knew why it wasn't working. It wasn't working for her, either.

It was too far.

That was all. It was the stupidest thing. She'd watched Sam pace the edge of the Makah reservation at three in the morning for a week, the first month after Emily, because Emily's parents wouldn't let him in the house. She'd watched Jacob sleep on the Cullens' couch every single night for ten weeks, curled up with that baby on his chest like a dog on a porch. She'd watched Quil drive to Claire's mom's house on the weekends just to sit in the car outside. She'd thought every one of them was pathetic.

She lay in her bed and the line went out of her chest and pulled, steadily, gently, like a tide going out. And the whole of her, every inch, from her scalp to her toes, wanted to get up and go out the window and run thirty miles in the dark to a little green tent with a slug on it, just to be in the same place.

She lay there.

Down at the bottom of her, under her ribs, her wolf wasn't lying there. Her wolf was up. She could feel it, pacing, a long slow circuit round and round the inside of her, nose down, tail low, the way a dog paces a room when you've put it in there and shut the door. It stopped every few rounds and put its nose to the northeast and breathed in, as if it could smell him from here. Then it paced again.

*Go to sleep,* she told it.

It looked at her.

*Go to sleep. It's one night. We'll see him tomorrow.*

It paced.

She rolled over onto her side. She put the pillow over her head. She lay there in the dark with the pillow over her head and the rain on the roof and the line in her chest, and she felt him, thirty miles away, roll over onto his side too, at almost exactly the same second, and lie there.

At midnight she was still awake. She heard the clock in the next room strike, twelve times, four minutes early.

At half past twelve she was still awake.

At ten to one, she felt him get up.

---

### V. Harry

*The tent — 12:44 a.m.*

He'd given it three hours.

He'd done everything right. He'd washed the mug and turned the record off and checked the circle—Casimir lying in the middle of it the way he'd left him, statue-still, the red eyes open and fixed on the ceiling—and checked the wards and checked the gates and brushed his teeth and gone to bed at ten past ten like a sensible person. He'd lain on his back on the narrow bed in the dark with his hands on his chest and breathed the way the old woman in the Rwenzori had taught him, four counts in, seven held, eight out, which had put him to sleep in a storm drain in Lagos with the water coming up round his knees, and in a hide on a mountainside in the Carpathians at thirty below, and on the floor of a Healer's waiting room in Mumbai with a hole in his side.

It didn't work.

He lay there. He breathed. He counted. He got to four hundred breaths and gave up counting.

The line was the problem. It ran out of his chest and through the wall of his room and away south-west, thirty miles of it, into a house on the reservation and a bed in a little room at the back of it, and the woman lying in that bed. He could feel her lying there. On her side now, with a pillow over her head. Awake. Breathing the way people breathe when they've been trying not to be awake for a long time and are starting to get angry about it.

And down at the bottom of him, the thing behind the gates was pacing.

It had started at about half past ten. It had lain quietly till then, chin on its paws, eyes south-west, the way it had after the mirror. Then he'd turned the light out, and some time in the dark it had got up, and now it was walking. Round and round, behind the gates. He could feel every step. Every few circuits it stopped and pushed its nose against the gates and breathed in, long and slow, toward the south-west, and then started walking again.

In September, when he'd first locked it down, it had leaned on the gates for a week, every night, so hard he'd woken up with his jaw aching from clenching it in his sleep. Tonight it walked, and stopped, and breathed at the gates, and walked again, and kept its weight off them the whole time.

It was waiting for him to open them.

He lay on his back in the dark and thought about that.

He'd been thinking about it since he'd stood in front of her on the bench, with her face against his hand and his own wolf hitting the gates so hard he'd had to say *not today* out loud, like a bloke talking to a dog. He'd meant it. He'd been afraid of what came out, if it came out within half a mile of her: two months of it penned down there, angry, and her standing right in front of it. He didn't know what it would do. He'd never let it out near anybody. He'd only ever let it out alone, in the dark, on a hillside, and run until it was tired, and come home.

He thought about the way it had lain down when he put his hand on her face. About the way it had sighed after the mirror. About the way it was walking now, round and round, patient, not pushing.

Some time this afternoon, between the ridge and the kitchen and her face under his hand, all the anger had gone out of it. What was left was this. Walking. Waiting.

At twenty to one he sat up.

He sat on the edge of the narrow bed with his feet on the cold floor and his elbows on his knees, in his boxers and an old grey T-shirt with a hole in the hem, and put his face in his hands.

"This is a terrible idea," he said, into his hands.

Down at the bottom of him, the pacing stopped.

"It's one in the morning. She's asleep." She wasn't asleep. He could feel her not being asleep from here. "It's thirty miles. It's *their* side. Uley's lot run that bit. If one of them smells you, there'll be trouble, and I'll have to sort it out, and I'm *knackered*."

The thing at the bottom of him didn't move. It was standing still behind the gates with its nose to them, looking up at him, the way it had looked up at him every morning for two months. Except it had never looked up at him like this. With its ears forward and its tail just moving, very slightly, side to side.

Harry took his face out of his hands.

"Fine," he said. "Fine. But you behave."

He stood up. He went out of his room and down the long dark hallway in his bare feet, past the library with the fire banked low, past the kitchen, past the workshop door, to the three wooden steps and the flap. He went up the steps and out into the night.

It had stopped raining. The cloud had broken up, and there was a little moon behind it somewhere, enough to turn the edges of the cloud silver and put a grey light down through the spruces onto the bench. Everything was dripping. The river roared in the gorge below.

Gary was on the canvas by the door.

"Don't wait up," Harry told him.

He walked out into the middle of the bench, onto the moss, and stood there barefoot in the cold, and closed his eyes, and went down.

---

The gates were exactly where he'd left them.

Three of them. His own work. He'd built them in the second week of September, in the workshop, over four nights, out of three layers of the hardest Occlumency he knew how to make and a binding he'd lifted from a Romanian dragon-keepers' manual and adapted, and braided them into his shields so that nothing could get past them without his say-so: not a smell, not a sound, not the wolf. Every morning for sixty-six days he'd walked down to them and checked them, and every morning they'd been shut tight, and the thing on the other side had lifted its head when he came and looked at him and put its head back down.

They didn't look much like gates now. Since one o'clock this afternoon, since the ridge, something had been happening to them. They'd gone thin. Soft at the hinges. The braid had come loose in a dozen places, like a rope that's been out in the weather too long. If he'd been checking them for a client, he'd have told the client to replace them.

The wolf was standing on the other side.

It had stopped pacing. It was standing perfectly still with its nose an inch from the first gate. Waiting.

Harry put his hand on the first gate. It came apart under his palm like wet paper.

The second one, he had to pull. Not much. It came.

The third one he stood in front of for a few seconds, in the dark, at the bottom of himself, with the wolf on the other side of it close enough to touch. He could see it, now. Down here, he always could. Black as the inside of a hole in the ground. Bigger than him. Its eyes were the same colour as his. They always had been. He'd never got used to that.

"You behave," he told it.

It looked at him.

He opened the third gate.

---

The change took him the way it always did, which was all at once and from the inside, like falling into deep water.

He'd done it by the book, the first time, years ago: the mandrake leaf, the dew, the chrysalis, the storm. Most people who did it by the book ended up with a body that was theirs in a different shape, and nothing else. A cat, an owl. A big black dog. He'd ended up with that. And with something else, besides. The something else had been waiting a long time for somebody to do it by the book, on that particular hill, in that particular storm, and it had come up out of the ground to meet him, and asked if it could share.

He still didn't know what to call it. He'd said yes anyway. He'd been twenty-three and on his own on a hill in a thunderstorm and he'd felt it, all of it, enormous and old and patient and so lonely he'd nearly been sick, and he hadn't been able to think of a single good reason to say no.

So now when he changed, there were two of them in it.

He felt himself go down onto four feet. Felt the moss come up under his paws, cold and wet and springy, and the air come into his chest, a lot of it, a whole new size of breath. Felt his weight shift forward and down and out, felt his spine stretch and his shoulders come up and his skull lengthen and his teeth change in his mouth. His clothes went with him, the way they always did; he'd never understood that bit and had stopped trying. One second there was a man standing barefoot on a bench of moss under the spruces in a T-shirt with a hole in it. The next there wasn't.

And the night came up round him like somebody turning on all the lights.

He'd forgotten. Two months, and he'd forgotten what it was like. The smells first, a whole world of them, every one sharp and separate and laid out in front of him like a map. Spruce, and moss, and wet earth, and the river: cold, mineral, fish in it. Elk, two days old, down by the water. A marten, an hour ago, up the big tree on the left. The slug. Gary had a smell. Gary smelled like a mushroom that had opinions.

And under all of it, faint and far, coming on the wind off the sea, so faint his own human nose would never have caught it in a thousand years: her.

The wolf lifted its head.

He let it. He didn't hold it back. He was in it, and it was in him, and for the first time in two months the two of them wanted exactly the same thing, at exactly the same time, as much as either of them had ever wanted anything.

He went.

---

He went down off the bench through the spruces in the moonlight and into the gorge and across the river, and the river was nothing. Cold. Fast. Up to his chest. He went through it in three bounds and up the other side, and then he was in the big trees, and running.

He'd never run like this. He'd always run alone, on hillsides, in the dark, with nowhere to go, just to wear the thing out. Running somewhere was different. Running somewhere was like falling downhill. The line pulled out of his chest straight south-west, taut as a wire, and he followed it, and the forest went by on either side in the dark like the walls of a tunnel.

He didn't make a sound. He'd noticed that, the first year, and it had unnerved him for a long time. He was big, and the ground ought to have shaken. It didn't. His paws went down onto moss and rock and deadfall and came up again and left nothing behind but the print, and no noise at all; his weight went down into the ground the way water goes into sand. He'd worked out, eventually, that he was doing it himself, some part of him at the bottom of the magic, taking the weight off each paw at the instant it landed and putting it back on as it left, all the time, without thinking. Like breathing. He went through four miles of old growth and a herd of elk bedded down in a clearing and didn't wake a single one. He felt them, as he passed. Felt them not wake.

He came out of the trees above the Sol Duc, and crossed it, and went west.

The country went lower and flatter as he got toward the coast. Second growth. Logging roads. The 110, a strip of black wet tarmac in the moonlight with nobody on it, and he crossed it in two strides. The smell of the sea got stronger. Salt, kelp, the cold mineral smell of the surf, and then the smell of people, of woodsmoke and cars and dogs and bait, and he was coming up on the edge of the reservation.

And there were wolves.

He smelled them before anything else. Their scent was all over the trees here, marking the boundary, sharp and musky and wild, the same smell he'd had in his nose on the ridge this afternoon from her and her brother, except different, the way two people's handwriting is different. Other wolves. A lot of them. He'd keyed them rust on the lattice; the western pack. He'd kept away from here for sixty-six days on purpose.

Two of them were out tonight. He could smell them, fresh. A mile north, on the edge of the bluffs, running a loop. One heavy and slow. One faster, rangy, with a temper on him you could smell from here. Coming round this way.

He thought about it for perhaps a second.

He could have gone round. He could have gone up the river and come in from the north and stayed downwind. He could have put a Disillusionment on himself, or a scent-sink, or any of a dozen other things; the magic was all still there, wolf or no wolf, it always was. He could have gone through the middle of the reservation like a ghost.

He didn't bother.

He didn't know why. Maybe it was the wolf. Maybe it was that he'd spent sixty-six days locked down tight, his scent sealed, his face blank, sneaking past every nose on this coast, and he was tired of it. Maybe it was that the line was pulling so hard he didn't want to waste a single second going round. He went straight in, across the boundary, under the cedars, into Quileute land, with his smell pouring off him like smoke off a bonfire, and let them smell it.

Behind him and to the north, about a minute later, he heard one of them stop. Heard it put its nose up. Heard it make a sound, low, in its chest, the sound a dog makes when it smells something in its yard it doesn't know.

He kept going.

---

The house was small and single-storey, with a deep porch at the front and a woodshed at the back, and a yard that ran down to a line of cedars. There was a dented old Ford Taurus in the drive. There were flower beds along the side of the house, dug over for the winter, black soil and mulch and a few last stalks of something tied to bamboo canes. There was a wind chime on the back porch made of fishing weights that moved a little in the breeze off the sea and didn't ring.

He stopped at the bottom of the yard, in the cedars, and stood there.

He knew which window. He'd have known with his eyes shut. A small window at the back corner of the house, dark, with a gingham curtain half drawn across it and the sash open about two inches at the bottom, the way people leave a window when they run hot and the room gets stuffy. The line went straight in through that gap.

He came up out of the cedars and across the wet grass to the flower bed below the window.

He ended up in the flower bed without meaning to. Directly under the window, as close as he could get, in the soft dug-over black earth between two bamboo canes. He felt it give under his paws. He felt his prints going down into it, deep and clean and perfect, the size of dinner plates. Somebody was going to find those in the morning. He couldn't bring himself to care.

He sat down.

Sitting, his head came up level with the gutter. He'd be looking down into her room like something out of a fairy tale with a grandmother in it. So he folded down onto his belly instead, in the soft black earth between the bamboo canes, a wolf the size of a small truck lying in a flower bed, and put his chin down on his forepaws, and looked up at the gap at the bottom of the sash.

He made no sound. He kept out of her head; he'd decided that on the bench and it held. He sat there, close enough now. Close enough that the line between them, which had been pulling all night like a tide going out, had stopped pulling. Close enough that it just hung there between them, slack and easy, from the middle of his chest to the gap in the window. Close enough that the thing he was sharing a body with had put its chin on its paws, the way it had every morning for two months, except that this time the thing it was watching was right there, and it went completely, perfectly still.

Behind the window, in the dark, he heard her sit up in bed.

---
### VI. Leah

*La Push — 1:16 a.m.*

The line went slack.

That was how she knew. She'd felt him get up off the narrow bed in the tent and go down the hallway and out, and felt something happen out there on the bench under the spruces that she didn't have a word for, a kind of opening, like a door at the bottom of a cellar swinging wide. And then for twenty-five minutes she'd lain in her bed with the pillow over her head and felt the far end of the line *move*. Coming toward her. Fast. Getting shorter and shorter, the way a fishing line gets shorter when you reel it in, until she could feel him crossing the river, and the highway, and the edge of the reservation.

And then it stopped pulling.

It had been pulling all night, steady, like a tide going out, and she'd stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing the sound of a refrigerator. Until it stopped. All at once the line just hung there, from the middle of her chest out through the wall of her room, slack and easy. Close.

Very close.

She took the pillow off her head and sat up.

The room was dark. Gray light came in round the edge of the gingham curtain, moonlight, the rain gone. The window was open two inches at the bottom, the way she always left it. She could smell the yard through the gap: wet grass, cedar, the sea, Mom's mulch in the flower beds.

And something else.

Pine. Turned earth. Cold stone, like the inside of a cave. And under it, strong now, pouring in through the two-inch gap in her window like smoke: wolf. Huge and wild and dark and old.

Her own wolf came up off the floor of her so fast she gasped.

She got out of bed. She crossed the room in two steps, barefoot on the cold boards, and pushed the curtain aside, and looked out.

The yard was gray and silver in the moonlight. The cedars at the bottom of it were black. The wind chime on the back porch moved and didn't ring. Everything was dripping. She looked down the yard to the trees and saw nothing, and looked left toward the woodshed and saw nothing, and then she looked down.

Directly below her window, in her mom's flower bed, between two bamboo canes, two green eyes were looking up at her.

---

He was lying down. That was the first thing her brain managed, and it was a stupid thing, but it was all she had. He was lying on his belly in the black dug-over earth with his chin on his forepaws, like a dog by a fire, and he'd had to lie down because if he'd sat up his head would have been higher than the window.

He was black. Black like the space between stars. Black like the bottom of a well. The moonlight didn't sit on him the way it sat on everything else in the yard. It went into his coat and stayed there. Where the wind moved his fur she could see something under the black, a dark gray, charcoal, like the inside of a burned log. His head was the size of the whole window. Bigger. His ears were up, toward her. And his eyes.

They were the same. That was the thing that got her. The wolf looked at her with his eyes. That green. Bottle-in-the-sun green, the green she'd seen on the ridge when he took his glasses off. Exactly the same, looking up at her out of a wolf's face in her mother's flower bed at quarter past one in the morning.

He lay there and looked up at her.

She put her hand on the sash and pushed it all the way up.

The cold came in. The smell came in, so strong now it made her eyes water, and her wolf hit the inside of her ribs like a dog throwing itself at a door, and she gripped the windowsill with both hands until her knuckles hurt.

"What are you *doing* here?" she whispered.

The wolf in the flower bed lifted its head off its paws.

"Couldn't sleep," it said.

Leah let go of the windowsill and nearly fell over backward.

It was his voice. Low, rough, English, dry as toast. Coming out of a wolf. Out of a wolf's *mouth*. She saw the jaw move. She saw the lips move round the words, black lips, enormous teeth behind them, and the words came out clear as anything, as clear as if he'd been standing in her room.

"You can *talk*?"

"Apparently."

"You can *talk*? Like that? Out loud?"

"I've never really had anyone to try it on." The big head tilted, very slightly. "Bit of a surprise to me too, the first time. I was on my own on a hill. Said *bloody hell* out loud and frightened myself half to death."

"We can't do that. None of us. When we're phased we can't talk at *all*. We can only—" She stopped. "That's so unfair."

"Perks," said the wolf.

She stared at him. She stood at her window in her dad's old T-shirt and her sleep shorts with the cold coming in round her bare legs, and stared down at a wolf the size of a pickup lying in her mom's flower bed, talking to her in an English accent, and something happened in her chest that she didn't have any warning for at all. It came up out of her like the laugh at the kitchen table, except bigger, except it was all of her.

"You're lying in my mom's flower bed," she said.

The wolf looked down at itself. It looked at the flower bed, and the two bamboo canes on either side of its head, and the black dug-over earth under its paws, which it had sunk into about four inches.

"Ah," it said.

"She's going to kill you."

"She'll have to get in line." The ears went back, a little. Embarrassed, she thought. A wolf the size of a truck, embarrassed. "I was going to sit at the bottom of the garden. Under the trees. Very respectful. And then I sort of. Ended up here."

"Yeah," said Leah. "I know how that goes."

The green eyes held hers.

"I didn't go in," he said. "Your head. I want you to know that. I haven't been in since the bench. Not once. I just—" The big head dipped. "I couldn't sleep, Leah. I lay there for three hours. It was too far."

"I know."

"I know you know."

"It was too far for me too." She swallowed. "I had a pillow over my head."

"I know. I felt it." The tail moved, once, in the flower bed, and knocked over a bamboo cane. They both looked at it. "Sorry."

Down at the bottom of her, her wolf had stopped pacing. It was standing perfectly still, with every hair up and its nose pointed straight out the window. And she could feel his, through the line. All day it had been down at the bottom of him, behind its locks. Now it was *right there*, in the flower bed, in the shape of him, looking up at her through his eyes. The locks were gone. She couldn't feel them at all.

*You let it out,* she thought.

She didn't say it. He heard it anyway, of course. She watched him hear it.

"Yeah," he said. "It asked very nicely."

She put one foot up on the windowsill.

"Leah—"

"Shut up."

She climbed out. It took some doing. The window was small and she was tall and she had to fold herself through it sideways, one leg and then her shoulders and then the other leg, and she came down barefoot into the flower bed beside him with a squelch, into the cold mud up to her ankles, about a foot from his face.

He'd gone completely still.

She stood there. Up close, in the moonlight, he was enormous. Lying down, his head was level with her chest. His breath came out of him in long slow clouds that smelled of pine and cold and something wild, and the heat coming off him was like standing next to a woodstove. She could see his ribs moving under the black coat. She could see her own reflection, tiny, in the green of his eye.

She put her hand out and laid it flat on the side of his face, the way he'd laid his on hers on the bench.

He closed his eyes.

"Okay," Leah said. Her voice shook. "Okay. Hold on. I'm not doing this in Dad's shirt."

She turned around. She went three steps to the back porch and pulled the T-shirt off over her head and folded it, quickly, and put it on the top step under the wind chime, where it would stay dry. Behind her, she heard the wolf in the flower bed turn its whole head away and look very hard at the woodshed. She heard it. A big rustle in the mulch, and then nothing.

She almost laughed again.

Then she went round the corner of the woodshed, out of sight of the house, and let go.

---

It was the fastest she'd ever phased in her life. She didn't decide to. Her wolf took one look at the chance and went through her like a door slamming open, and her sleep shorts went to rags, and she came down onto four feet in the wet grass behind the woodshed with the whole of the night coming up round her at once: the sea and the cedars and the mud and Mom's mulch and the woodsmoke from the chimney and the old fishing-weight wind chime and, over all of it, filling her head, him.

She came round the woodshed.

He'd stood up.

She stopped dead in the middle of the yard and looked at him.

She'd felt how big he was, on the ridge, through the line. Like standing at the bottom of a mountain in the dark. Feeling it was different from seeing it. He was standing at the edge of the flower bed in the moonlight with the black mud to his ankles and she had to put her head back to see his face. Jacob was the biggest wolf in their pack, the biggest one she'd ever run next to except maybe Sam, and Jacob's head would have come up about to this one's ear. A head taller than Jake at the shoulder, easy. Longer, too, by a couple of feet, nose to tail. Built heavy through the chest and shoulders and narrow in the hips, long in the leg. And the black. He stood in the moonlight and the moonlight just disappeared into him.

He was looking at her.

She knew what he was seeing. A gray wolf, soft dark smoke-gray, silver at the tips. Long-legged. Narrow. Built for speed. Fastest thing on the peninsula.

"Huh," he said. "I had you down as about your brother's size."

*Seth's still growing. I've got a head on him.*

"I had him flat on his belly at the time, to be fair."

*You did. I remember.*

She heard him hear it. She didn't have to push it at him, or think it at him hard, the way she thought at Edward to make him wince. She just thought it, and it was there between them. Like the pack. Like when Seth phased, and she could hear him. Except she couldn't hear Seth now, or Jacob, or anybody; the pack mind was dark and empty, nobody phased, nobody else in there. Just her. And him, on the other end of the line, right there in her head, the way he'd been in her head all day. Except louder now. Closer.

And under him. Under his voice, under the dry English running commentary that was what he sounded like inside, there was the other one. Bigger. Older. Darker. Looking at her out of the same green eyes.

Her wolf went to it.

She didn't tell it to. She'd think about that later, a lot. She didn't decide anything at all. One second she was standing in the middle of the yard on four feet looking up at him, trying to think of something to say, and the next her wolf had crossed the grass between them in two strides and put her nose against his.

His wolf didn't move. He stood very still, with his head lowered, and let her.

She breathed him in. All of him, up close, nose to nose, so close she could feel his breath on her face, and her wolf made a sound in her chest she'd never heard herself make. Low. Whining. And her tail was going. She could feel it going. Side to side, fast, the way Seth's went when Layla came into a room, the way she'd sworn hers never would, not for anybody, not ever.

*Oh my God,* thought Leah, very small, from somewhere inside her own head. *Oh my God, stop it.*

Her wolf did not stop it.

It pushed its head up under his jaw. Hard. It shoved the whole length of its face along the side of his throat, along the thick black ruff, breathing in, and his wolf made a sound back, deep, a rumble she felt all the way down her spine and into her paws, and then he put his head down over her neck. The whole weight of it. Across the back of her neck and shoulders, heavy and hot, holding her there. The way she'd seen Sam do with Emily's dogs, the way big dogs do with each other. She should have bristled. She'd have taken Paul's ear off for less.

Her tail went faster.

"Well," said his voice. In her head and in her ear at the same time, a little strangled. "I've no idea who's driving."

*Not me,* said Leah.

"Not me either."

His wolf took its head off her neck and stepped back and looked at her, and her wolf looked back. And then her wolf did something that she would never, ever, as long as she lived, admit to anybody she had done. It dropped its chest to the wet grass with its back end up in the air and its tail going, and its ears forward, and *bowed*. Like a puppy. Like the stupidest, happiest puppy in the world.

*I'm going to die,* thought Leah. *I'm going to die of this.*

His wolf looked at her for exactly one second.

Then it bowed back.

A head taller than Jacob at the shoulder, chest down in the flower bed, back end up, tail going, ears forward. A wolf the size of a truck in a play bow in her mom's yard at one in the morning. She heard him make a sound in her head that was a laugh and a groan at the same time, a sound like a man watching himself fall down the stairs in slow motion.

From somewhere to the north, up on the bluffs, a wolf howled.

It came down through the trees long and harsh and furious, and both of them froze, mid-bow. It was a challenge. Somebody up there had smelled something in his territory he didn't like, and was coming to find it, and wanted it to know.

"That'll be the one with the temper," said Harry.

*Paul.*

"He's been sniffing round the bottom of the garden for twenty minutes. Working himself up to it." The green eyes went north, toward the sound. Calm. Mildly interested at best, like a man glancing at a car alarm going off down the street. "D'you want me to go and have a word?"

*God, no.*

"I'd be very polite."

*No, you wouldn't.*

"No," he agreed. "I wouldn't."

*Let's just go.* She was already moving, her wolf already wheeling round, her tail still going. *Come on. I know a place.*

She ran.

---

She ran south, through the cedars at the bottom of the yard and down the dark streets of the village, past the dark houses, past Billy's, past the old tribal center, onto First Beach. The sand was hard and wet under her paws and the surf was coming in white in the moonlight on her right, and the big black sea stacks stood out in the water like the backs of something sleeping, and she ran along the edge of the waves where the sand was hardest and fastest.

He ran with her.

He ran beside her, a little behind, on her left, the side away from the sea. She kept waiting to hear him. Something that big, on wet sand, she should have been able to hear from a mile off. She couldn't. She could hear her own paws, slapping, and the surf, and the wind, and her own breath. And beside her, nothing. Like running next to a shadow. She looked over and he was right there, huge and black, keeping pace, and she couldn't hear him at all.

*How are you doing that?*

"Doing what?"

*Not making any noise.*

"Oh. That." He sounded faintly surprised, as if he'd forgotten. "Dunno. Always done it. I think I'm holding myself up a bit. Without meaning to."

*You're doing magic. Right now. While you run.*

"I'm always doing magic," said Harry. "I just mostly don't notice."

She put on speed.

She went as fast as she could go. She'd never in her life run with anybody who could keep up with her when she really went; Jacob couldn't, Sam couldn't, Seth fell behind every time and laughed about it. She stretched out flat along the edge of the surf with her ears back and the wind screaming and the wet sand flying out behind her in clumps, and she looked over.

He was right there.

He was barely breathing hard. He was running beside her with his ears forward and his mouth a little open and his tongue out, and his eyes, when they met hers, were laughing.

"Go on, then," he said.

She went faster.

She didn't know she had faster. She found it, somewhere, down at the bottom of herself, where her wolf was. They went off the end of First Beach and up over the rocks and through the trees on the point, and down onto Second Beach, which was long and wild and empty, and along it so fast that the sea stacks went past like telephone poles from a car, and he stayed with her. Every stride. And somewhere along Second Beach, in the moonlight, with the surf going and the wind in her face and him beside her, she realized she was making a sound she'd never made running. A kind of yelping. A high, wild, ridiculous sound, over and over, the sound a dog makes when it's let off the leash on a beach for the first time.

She was happy. That was what it was. It took her a while to recognize it. She was so happy it was coming out of her as noise.

She didn't stop it.

---

The cave was in the headland at the far south end of Third Beach, where the cliff came down to the sea.

She'd found it when she was fourteen. She'd come down here on her own one summer, to get away from something, she couldn't even remember what now, and climbed up the rocks above the tideline at low tide and found it: a hollow in the cliff face about thirty feet up, behind a screen of salal and stunted spruce, where the sandstone had been eaten out by a thousand years of wind into a room. Long and low and dry. A floor of soft gray sand. An opening that faced west, straight out over the sea, so that you could lie at the back of it and see nothing but water and sky and the sea stacks standing in the surf with trees growing on top of them, bent over sideways by the wind.

She'd brought Seth here once, when he was nine. She'd never brought anybody else. She'd come here the night Sam told her about Emily. She'd lain on the sand at the back of it, in human form, eighteen years old, and screamed into the rock until she couldn't anymore.

She went up the rocks in three jumps and through the salal and into it.

He came in behind her. He had to duck his head at the entrance. He stood just inside, in the dark, with the moonlight behind him, and she heard him breathe in, slow, smelling it. The sand. The salt. The old dry stone. And under all of it, faint, faint, old as anything, her. Fourteen-year-old her, and eighteen-year-old her, screaming.

She felt him find it, and understand it, and put it away somewhere careful, the way he'd put things away in the specimen room. He said nothing about it. He stood there for a moment. Then he came the rest of the way in and turned round, once, the way a dog turns round before it lies down, and lay down on the sand at the back of the cave facing the opening, facing the sea.

She stood in the middle of the cave and looked at him.

Then she went and lay down against him.

Her wolf did it. Her wolf went and lay down along the whole length of him, her back against his chest, her head on his forepaws, and he curled round her. All the way round, his big body making a wall at her back and his tail coming round over her paws and his head coming down over her neck again, heavy and hot, the way it had in the yard. And they lay there in the dark at the back of the cave with the moon on the water and the surf coming in below them, slow and huge, and she could feel his heart going against her spine.

It was going as fast as hers.

"Leah," he said. Very quietly.

*Yeah.*

"I think," he said, "I've lost the vote."

*Me too.*

She'd think about it later, in the morning, in words. There weren't any words for it in the cave. There was the dark and the sea and the heat of him all down her back, and his heart against her spine, and two wolves who'd been alone a very long time, longer than either of the people inside them, deciding between themselves, without asking anybody, that they weren't going to be alone any more.

They didn't ask.

And she found, when it came to it, that she didn't mind at all.

## Part Four: Morning

---

### VII. Leah

*The cave above Third Beach — Tuesday, November 21st, 7:52 a.m.*

She woke up because the sun was in her eyes.

That was so strange that for a while she just lay there with her eyes shut, feeling it. Sun. On her face. Thin and cold and pale, November sun, but sun, coming in low over the sea through the mouth of the cave and lying across her like a blanket. She couldn't remember the last time she'd woken up to sun. It rained here three hundred days a year. She'd stopped expecting it.

Then she felt the rest.

She was human. She'd phased back in her sleep; she must have, some time near dawn, the wolf letting go on its own the way it had let go so easily last night behind the Cullens' cedar. She was lying on her side on the soft gray sand at the back of the cave, naked as the day she was born, and her back was against something enormous and black and hot and breathing.

His fur was thick and soft against her skin. She could feel his ribs going up and down, slow, asleep. His big head was lying on the sand beside hers, so close that his breath stirred her hair. One huge black forepaw was lying across her waist, heavy as a log, holding her there.

She lay very still.

The sea was below them, going in and out. Gulls, somewhere. The sound of the wind in the salal at the mouth of the cave. And inside her, very quiet, curled up under her ribs, her wolf. Asleep. Completely, perfectly asleep, for the first time since she'd phased. She'd never felt it sleep before. It had always been up, pacing, restless, watching. Now it was lying with its nose tucked under its tail, out cold, and down the line from it, from the black body at her back, she could feel the other one. Asleep too. The two of them, curled up together at the two ends of the rope.

She thought about last night.

She thought about it for quite a long time, actually, lying there in the sun with her eyes shut and a wolf's paw across her waist, and her face got hotter and hotter, until she thought there was a fair chance she was going to set the sand on fire.

Behind her, the breathing changed.

She felt him wake up. Felt it through the line before she felt it in his ribs, a slow surfacing, the way you come up out of deep water. Felt him find where he was. Felt him find *her*. Felt him go perfectly, totally still.

Neither of them moved.

"Morning," he said, eventually. Out loud. In her ear. A wolf's voice, rough with sleep.

"Morning," said Leah, to the cave wall.

There was a long pause.

Then the paw across her waist was gone, and the heat at her back changed, and the sand shifted, and there was a sound she didn't have a name for. Something folding up. And she rolled over, and there was a man sitting on the sand at the back of the cave with his back against the rock and his knees up, in a pair of gray boxer shorts and an old gray T-shirt with a hole in the hem, running both hands back through his hair.

She looked at him.

"You get to keep your *clothes*?" she said.

He looked down at himself. He looked at her. His eyes went to her face very fast and stayed there, very carefully, about four inches above her collarbone.

"Perks," he said.

"That is *so* unfair."

"I know. I'm sorry. I've no idea how it works." He was already pulling the T-shirt off over his head. He held it out to her across the sand, without looking, his face turned toward the mouth of the cave. "Here."

She took it. She pulled it on. It still held the heat of him and it smelled like him, pine and stone and sleep, and it came down to the middle of her thighs, and the hole in the hem was right over her left hip. She sat back against the rock beside him, not touching, with her knees pulled up inside the T-shirt and her arms round them.

They sat there.

The sun came in. The sea went in and out below the cave, blue this morning, actually blue, with the sea stacks standing up out of it black and green and the surf white round their feet. A gull went past the mouth of the cave, close, and looked in at them with one yellow eye, and kept going.

"So," said Harry.

"So," said Leah.

"We're not going to make this a whole thing."

"No."

"Good." He nodded. He looked out at the sea. "Good. Me neither."

They sat there some more.

"I mean," said Harry, after a while, to the sea, in the voice of a reasonable man stating a reasonable position, "it was good sex. So what's the big deal?"

Leah turned her head and looked at him.

"Yeah," she said. "But as *wolves*."

"Well. Yes."

"As *wolves*, Harry."

"I was there, love."

"We've known each other for like *nineteen hours*."

"Eighteen and a half." He was still looking at the sea. "Not that I'm counting." There was a pause. Then, carefully, not looking at her, in a different voice: "You didn't like it?"

She stared at him.

She opened her mouth to say something, something sharp, something that would put him back in his place and keep her face from catching fire. And she felt it come up and get stuck behind her teeth, the way the lie had got stuck on the bench in the rain. Because it wasn't true. Whatever sharp thing she'd been going to say wasn't true. And she couldn't.

"No," said Leah, and heard her own voice come out strangled, "it was *fucking great*."

He turned his head.

"It was," she said. She couldn't stop now. "Okay? It was. It was great. Happy?" She glared at him. "But next time somebody else phases, they're going to get an *eyeful*."

He looked at her.

"The pack," said Leah. "It's—we see everything. Anything in your head when you're phased, everybody else who's phased sees it. Everything. Jake. Seth. My *brother*, Harry. If I phase while either of them is phased, and I think about last night for even one *second*, it's going to go right into their heads like a—like a slide show. In full color. And I'm going to think about it. You know I am. I'm thinking about it *right now*."

"I know," he said. "I can feel you thinking about it."

"*Stop* feeling me thinking about it."

He grinned.

It was the first time she'd seen it. She'd seen him smirk, on the ridge; she'd seen the corner of his mouth go up when he called her Lassie. This was different. It went all the way across his face, crooked, a little lopsided, his crooked nose wrinkling at the top, and it made him look about ten years younger, and it made her stomach do something she was going to have to have a long talk with it about later.

"I've got nothing to hide," said Harry.

She hit him in the arm. Hard. Hard enough that a human would have had a bruise for a week. He rocked over sideways on the sand, laughing, and didn't even try to get out of the way.

"Oh my *God*," she said. "Oh my God, I'm never phasing again."

"You'll have to eventually."

"I'll live on two legs. Forever. I'll get a car."

"You'll be on two legs till you can go five minutes without thinking about it." He sat back up, still grinning. "Which, at a guess, is about a year."

"I'm going to push you off this cliff."

"Go on, then."

She looked at him. He looked back. His hair was standing up on one side where he'd slept on it, and there was sand on his shoulder, and he was sitting in a cave in his underwear in November with the wind coming in off the Pacific and not even shivering, because he ran as hot as she did, and his eyes were the same green they'd been last night out of a wolf's face. And the line between them was lying there, easy, slack, a rope with nobody pulling on either end. Just tied.

"Are you hungry?" he said.

"I'm *starving*."

"Hang on, then."

And he was gone.

There was a sound like a page turning, and the place on the sand beside her was empty, with the shape of him pressed into it. She stared at it.

About forty seconds later, the sound came again, and he was sitting there again, in exactly the same place, in his boxers, holding a wicker basket on his knees and with a bundle of clothes under one arm and a tartan blanket over his shoulder.

"You can just *do* that?" said Leah. "In your *underwear*?"

"I can do it in anything. I've done it in a bath. Wouldn't recommend it." He put the basket down on the sand between them and opened it. The smell came out and nearly knocked her flat. Bacon. Toast. Coffee. "Bacon sandwiches. Bacon butties, if you're from where I'm from. Six. Four for you. Flask of coffee for you and a flask of tea for me, because I'm not a barbarian. Couple of apples. A Mars bar." He handed her the bundle of clothes. "And those. They're mine, they'll be too big, but they're dry."

It was a pair of gray sweatpants with a drawstring and a dark blue sweater, thick, knitted, with a big pale letter H on the front of it in a different-colored wool.

She held the sweater up and looked at the H.

"Did you get this made?" she said. "With your *initial* on it?"

"No." He'd gone a bit pink round the ears. "Somebody's mum knits me one every Christmas. Has done since I was eleven. I've got about fourteen. They all have an H on." He took a bacon sandwich out of the basket and bit it. "Shut up."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

She put the sweatpants on under the T-shirt, and the sweater on over it, and pulled the sleeves down over her hands, which they covered entirely, and rolled them back up. The H sat square in the middle of her chest. It was the softest, heaviest thing she'd ever worn.

She took a bacon sandwich out of the basket and bit it.

She closed her eyes.

"Oh my God," she said through it.

"I know," said Harry. "It's the bread. You lot don't do proper bread."

---

They ate on the rocks at the mouth of the cave, with the tartan blanket spread over the sandstone and their legs hanging over the edge, thirty feet above the sea, in the sun.

She'd never seen the beach look like this. Ever. She'd grown up on this coast and it had always been gray: gray sea, gray sky, gray sand, gray mist hanging in the tops of the trees on the sea stacks like smoke. This morning it was all color. The sea was deep blue going green where it came in over the reef, and the surf was white, and the sea stacks were black rock and dark green spruce, and the sand on Third Beach below them was gold where the sun hit it. The sky was so blue it hurt. She could see all the way to the horizon. She could see a fishing boat out there, tiny, red.

She ate four bacon sandwiches. She drank most of the coffee. He ate two and drank his tea out of the lid of the flask with his pinkie sticking out, on purpose, to make her laugh, and it worked.

They talked.

She'd been afraid of that part, a little, if she was honest, and she was finding she pretty much had to be. She'd thought it might be weird. Talking, after. With the thing in the cave behind them, and the thing in the rope between them that wouldn't let either of them say anything that wasn't true. She'd thought they'd sit here eating sandwiches in some kind of awful silence.

Then he asked her a question, and she answered it, and it turned out that when you couldn't lie to somebody, and they couldn't lie to you, there wasn't much point in being careful. You just said things. So they did.

He asked about the pack. How it worked, the thing in the head. She told him. About phasing for the first time in the yard, screaming, and Seth phasing two hours later trying to get to her. About being the only girl. About living in Sam's head for a year, which made him go quiet and look at the sea for a while. About Jacob, and Jacob walking out of Sam's pack the night before the baby was born, and her following him, the only time in her life she'd ever done anything just because it felt right.

"And the law," he said. "Last night. You used it on them."

She looked at him. "How'd you know about that?"

"I didn't go in," he said. "I promised. But I felt you get pleased about something about half eight. Really pleased. Like you'd won something. I've been wondering all night."

So she told him that too. *No wolf can harm another wolf's imprint.* Jacob saying it out loud in the middle of the Cullens' living room, with his face in his hands. *Honestly, that law's for you.*

He laughed so hard he nearly fell off the rock. He had to put his tea down.

"You told seven vampires," he said, when he could, "that a law about not hurting me was for *their* protection."

"Well, it is."

"It is," he agreed. "It absolutely is. I'd just never have thought to say it." He wiped his eyes. "God. I'd have paid money to see the blond one's face."

"Which blond one?"

"Either."

She told him about her dad.

She hadn't meant to. It came out sideways, the way things did with him. He'd asked about the sweater, what her mom would say about her coming home in it, and she'd said her mom had already said plenty, and then she was telling him about the kitchen table last night. About the gouge in the wood. The fish knife. Dad standing there grinning with his thumb in the air while Mom yelled.

"His name was Harry," she said.

She felt him go still beside her.

"I know," he said quietly. "I felt it. Last night. You at that table. I didn't know what it was. I felt you put your finger in something." He was looking at the sea. "Seth said it, didn't he? In the vampires' house. When he found out my name."

"Yeah."

"And you told him not to."

"Yeah."

He didn't say anything for a while. The surf came in.

"Do you want me to use the other one?" he said. "I've got a spare. I've been Evans for two months, I'm used to it. If it's—"

"No," said Leah. Fast. Before she knew she was going to say it. She thought about it, after, and it was still true. "No. Mom says he'd have hated you."

"Sounds like a sensible man."

"For about a day." She pulled her knees up inside the sweater. "She says after a day he'd have found out you knew about engines, or fishing, and that'd be it. He couldn't hate anybody longer than a day. It wore him out." She looked at him sideways. "She says he'd have taken you out on the boat."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I'd have liked that," he said.

"Do you know anything about fishing?"

"Not a thing," said Harry. "But I'd have gone."

She told him about Thanksgiving. He put his face in his hands.

"I'm *English*," he said through his fingers.

"That's what I said."

"We don't—that's the whole *point* of Thanksgiving, isn't it, it's about getting away from *us*. I'll be the only one at the table representing the people you all ran off from."

"That's what I said!"

"What did she say?"

"She said you can learn." Leah ate a bit of apple. "Two o'clock. Thursday. You have to bring something."

"What?"

"A pie."

He took his face out of his hands. "I can do a pie."

"No kidneys."

He looked at her with an expression of deep personal injury. "What've you lot got against *kidneys*?"

She told him about the cave. About fourteen, and eighteen. He listened without saying anything, with his eyes on the sea, and when she was done he said, "I know. I smelled it. When we came in." And then, after a while: "I'm glad it was here, though. Last night. I'm glad it was this place."

She found she was too.

And he told her about himself. Not all of it. She could feel the shape of how much there was, behind the wall in her own head and behind something in his, the cold stone weight of the oath sitting in him like a stone in a river. But he told her what he could. About a cupboard under some stairs. About a letter that came when he was eleven, and a school in a castle, and finding out his mother and father had been murdered and that the whole world knew his name before he did.

"Layla told them," said Leah. "Last night. About the war. The baby. The curse bouncing."

He made a face. "I'll bet she did."

"She said you won with a first-year spell."

"I did." He shrugged. "It's a good spell. People underestimate it." He drank some tea. "That's sort of the point."

"She said her teacher said you were lucky."

"I was," said Harry. "Very. For years." He set the tea down. "And then I spent eight years making sure I'd never need to be again."

She thought about him on the ridge. Two fingers raised like a man hailing a taxi.

"Your dad," she said. "What was he like?"

"Don't know. Not really. He died when I was one." He pulled his knees up. "People told me things. He was a show-off. Played Quidditch. That's a—it's a sport. On broomsticks. Don't laugh."

"I'm not laughing."

"You're laughing on the inside, I can feel it." He almost smiled. "He was a bit of a git at school, apparently. He grew out of it. He married my mum. And he could turn into an animal. Like me. Not like me. He did it by the book, with his two best mates, when they were kids. Illegally." He looked at the sea. "A stag. That's what he was. Big one. His mates called him Prongs."

"Prongs," said Leah.

"Because of the antlers."

"That's a terrible nickname."

"It's a fantastic nickname." He picked up a pebble off the rock and turned it over in his fingers. "My Patronus is a stag. That's a—it's a spell. A sort of guardian. You make it out of the happiest thing you've got, and it comes out as an animal, and it's meant to tell you something about yourself. Mine's always been a stag. Since I was thirteen." He threw the pebble out over the edge, and they watched it fall, and vanish in the white water at the foot of the cliff. "First time I made one, I thought it was him. Coming to save me. Turned out it was me. I've always sort of liked that it looked like him, though."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"And the wolf?" she said. "What's that? Where's that from?"

He didn't answer straight away. She felt him think about it. Felt him look at it, the way she'd looked at the wall in her own head, and decide what he could say about it that was true and that he understood well enough to say.

"There was a storm," he said finally. "On a hill. In Scotland. I was trying to learn to do what my dad did. By the book. And I did it. And something else turned up as well." He shrugged. "It asked if it could come along. I said yes. That's about as much as I know, Leah. I'd tell you more if I had it."

"Something turned up," she said. "And asked."

"Yeah."

"And you said yes."

"Yeah."

She thought about Billy Black at the bonfire every year with his big slow voice. Taha Aki, outside his body, finding a wolf in the forest, and asking.

"That's our story," she said slowly. "That's—that's Taha Aki. Our first one. Except backwards."

"I know," said Harry. "I noticed that. About twenty hours ago. In your brother's head." He looked at her. "Gave me a bit of a turn, to be honest."

---

Seth came down the trail from the parking lot at five past eleven.

She heard him before she saw him: big feet, crashing, trying to be quiet and not managing it, and breathing hard, and then, at the bottom of the trail where it came out of the trees onto Third Beach, stopping. She looked down from the rock. He was standing at the edge of the trees in jeans and a sweatshirt with his hands jammed in the front pocket, staring up at the cliff, at the screen of salal in front of the cave.

He saw her. He saw her sitting on the rock at the mouth of the cave in a sweater with an H on it, next to a man in his underwear with a flask of tea.

He looked at the sand very hard.

"Hi," he called up. "Hi. Lee. Um. I didn't phase."

"Okay."

"I swear to God. I haven't phased since last night. I'm not phasing. I'm never phasing again. I just want you to know that."

"Okay, Seth."

"I just knew you'd be here, because you always come here when—" He stopped. He looked at the sand some more. "I just knew."

"Morning, Balto," called Harry.

Seth winced. "Can you please not—"

"No."

Seth sighed. It went all the way through him, the whole six foot four of him, like a sail losing the wind. Then he came across the sand and started up the rocks, and stopped about halfway, where he could see them both properly, and stood there with one hand braced on the rock and his face carefully pointed at a spot just over Leah's left shoulder.

"Mom found the footprints," he said.

Leah closed her eyes.

"In the flower bed," said Seth. "Under your window. At like six. She went out to get the paper." He swallowed. "She said they're the size of dinner plates. She said she stood there for ten minutes with her coffee. And then she went and got the disposable camera out of the kitchen drawer and took a picture."

"She took a *picture*?"

"She said it was for the scrapbook." Seth's face was very red. "And Dad's shirt was on the back step. Folded. And your shorts were behind the woodshed. In, um. In pieces."

Beside her, Harry had put his tea down and was looking at the sea with an expression of great interest.

"And Paul," said Seth, "smelled a wolf. In our yard. At like one in the morning. A big one. A *strange* one. Not one of ours, not one of Jake's. He went crazy. He woke up Sam. Sam came over at seven and stood in the flower bed and looked at the prints for like five minutes and didn't say anything. And then he went to the Cullens'." Seth finally looked at Harry. Straight at him, for the first time. "He's there now. Sam. With Paul and Jared. And Jake's there, and Layla, and all of them. Everybody. They're waiting." He swallowed again. "Jake says bring him."

Leah looked at Harry.

Harry looked at the sea for a moment longer. Then he picked up his tea again and finished it, all of it, and screwed the lid back on the flask.

"Right," he said. "Well. That's the morning over."

He stood up. He brushed the sand off his boxers. He looked down at himself, and Leah watched it happen: the gray boxers and his bare legs ran together and went dark and smooth, the way ink spreads in water, and then he was standing on the rock at the mouth of the cave in the gray suit. The white shirt, open at the collar. The long black coat. The boots. The dark glasses settled on his nose out of nowhere.

Down on the rocks, Seth's mouth fell open.

"You're wearing the suit," said Leah.

"Meeting the in-laws."

"They're not your *in-laws*."

"Jacob's as good as married to their granddaughter. You're Jacob's—whatever you are. Second-in-command. Beta. That makes them my in-laws, near enough." He held out his hand to her. "Come on, Lassie. Let's go and be stared at."

She looked at the hand. She looked at the suit, and the glasses, and the coat moving a little in the wind off the sea. She looked at the man inside them, who an hour ago had been lying on his side on a rock laughing so hard he'd had to put his tea down.

She took his hand. He pulled her up.

"Balto," Harry said. "Come here."

"Why?"

"Because I'm taking us there, and it's quicker if you're holding on."

Seth looked at the hand Harry was holding out to him. He looked at Leah.

"Is it going to be like the garden hose?" he said. "Layla says it's like the garden hose."

"Layla does it loud," said Harry. "Come here."

Seth came up the last of the rocks and took his other hand.

And the cave, and the rocks, and the blue sea and the gold sand and the sea stacks with the trees on them, all of it folded up very small and went away, with a sound like a book being closed.

## Part Five: The Interview

---

### VIII. Edward

*The Cullen house — 11:14 a.m.*

He heard them arrive, and then he heard nothing at all.

He'd been listening for them since seven, since Sam Uley had walked up the drive in a pair of cut-offs with his face like a closed fist and two of his wolves padding behind him in the trees, and stood at the bottom of the porch steps and said, *Where is he?* Edward had been listening for three things. The bright loose heat of Leah's mind. The anxious, cheerful clatter of Seth's. And a third thing, which he'd been listening for and dreading in about equal measure, because he didn't know what it would sound like.

He heard all three of them appear on the lawn, at once, with a sound like a book being closed.

There was no approach. No engine on the drive, no footsteps in the trees, no minds getting louder as they came closer. One moment the lawn was empty except for the rain-flattened grass and the wet glitter of the November sun on it, and the next there were three people standing in the middle of it, about forty yards from the porch, and two of them were thinking very loudly.

Seth: *Oh my gosh. Oh my GOSH. That was SO much better than Layla's. That didn't feel like a hose at all, that felt like—like stepping off a curb, like I didn't even—Layla's going to be so mad—*

Leah—

Edward looked at the ceiling.

He did it quickly. He did it so quickly that Bella, beside him on the couch with Renesmee in her lap, turned and looked at him, and on the far side of the room Jasper's head came round. He fixed his eyes on a point in the plaster above the fireplace, very hard, and thought about the Goldberg Variations. Aria. First variation. Second. He'd learned them in 1958, in a cabin in Ontario, over one long winter, and he'd never in his life been so grateful to have something by heart.

It hadn't helped. Leah's mind had come onto the lawn the way the sun had come out that morning, all at once and everywhere, and what it was full of was a cave. A cave and the sea and the moon on the water and the dark and the heat of something enormous all along her back, and other things, a great many other things, very vivid, in no particular order, which she was thinking about at that moment with the whole of her attention, and which Edward Cullen had not been invited to hear and would have given a great deal to un-hear.

On the lawn, Leah stopped dead.

He felt her remember. He felt it hit her: *Edward. Edward reads minds. Edward is RIGHT THERE. I forgot. Oh my God, I forgot about Edward.*

*Don't you DARE,* she thought at him, across forty yards of lawn and a glass wall. *Don't you dare, leech. Get OUT.*

*I'm trying,* he thought back at her, uselessly, since she couldn't hear him. *Believe me, I'm trying.*

"Edward?" said Bella. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing." He was still looking at the ceiling. "Nothing is wrong. Everything is entirely fine."

On the lawn, Leah had turned to the man beside her and was saying something through her teeth, too low for anyone but a vampire to hear. "He reads minds. Edward. The bronze one. I *forgot*."

And the man beside her said, in a low, dry, English voice, "Ah." And then, after a moment, with an audible grin in it: "Well. Nothing to hide."

Leah hit him. Edward heard the thump of her fist on his arm. He heard Seth say *ow* in sympathy.

And then he made himself stop looking at the ceiling, and look out through the glass at the third person on the lawn, and listen.

---

There was nothing there.

He knew what nothing sounded like. He'd lived with Bella's nothing for two years and more: the soft, absolute, untroubled silence of a mind that simply didn't reach him, the way a sound doesn't reach you through a pane of glass. He'd hated it, once. Now it was the most restful thing in his life. Bella's silence was an absence. There was nobody standing at the window, because there was no window.

This was a window. Every shutter closed.

The man was standing on the lawn beside Leah in a gray suit and a long black coat and dark glasses, with his hands in his coat pockets and the sun on his shoulders, looking up at the house. He was solid and real and close enough that Edward could hear his heartbeat, slow and heavy, a deep double knock. Edward could smell him through the open front door: pine, stone, coffee, bacon, the sea. And where his mind should have been, where every human mind Edward had ever encountered had been for a hundred and five years, a stream, a voice, a radio playing in another room, there was a house.

That was the only way he could think of it. A big old house in the dark with every shutter shut and every door locked, very solid, very quiet. And somebody inside it. He could feel that. The way you can feel, walking past a dark house at night, that someone is sitting inside in the dark watching you go by.

He reached. Gently. The way he'd lean on any mind that was hard to hear.

The house didn't move.

He reached harder. He put his attention against it the way he'd put his shoulder to a door.

And he felt someone inside the house look up.

It was very quick. A glance, a turning of attention, the way a man reading by a fire looks up when he hears the gate. And then something came out of the house. He had no other words for it. Something came out of the closed shutters and across the lawn and in through the glass wall and into him, very light, very cool, quick as a pickpocket's hand; and went through him. All of him. In about a second. He felt it pass through every room he had, a hundred and five years of rooms, the way he'd passed through other people's for a century, flick, flick, flick, a hand through a filing cabinet, and then it was gone, back across the lawn and into the house, and the shutters were closed again.

Edward found he had taken a step back from the window.

He had never in his life been read. He'd heard minds for a hundred and five years. He'd heard Aro read people with a touch of his hand, and he'd let Aro read him, in Volterra, under protest. Nothing in all of it had prepared him for what it felt like from the other side. To stand still and feel someone else go through you, unhurried, and know there was nothing you could do.

On the lawn, the man in the gray suit took his dark glasses off and folded them and put them in his inside pocket, and looked straight at Edward through the glass.

"Mr. Cullen," he said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. "Door's locked. You can knock all you like."

Edward said nothing.

"Rude, isn't it," the man said. "Somebody told you that recently. *That's incredibly rude.* I liked that. She's got a good line in understatement, your witch." He started walking toward the house. "Shall we go in? I've had a long morning and your friend on the steps looks like he's going to burst something."

---

They came up the lawn together. Leah on his left, close, closer than she needed to be, with her shoulder almost touching his arm. Seth on his right, a pace behind, with his hands jammed in the front of his sweatshirt and his eyes going everywhere. Leah was wearing a pair of gray sweatpants with the drawstring pulled tight and the cuffs rolled four times, and a dark blue sweater with a large pale letter H knitted into the front of it, and her face was the color of a brick.

Edward heard Seth notice the H for the first time, halfway up the lawn, and go through about four stages of grief in a second and a half.

Sam was on the porch steps. He'd been on the porch steps for four hours, refusing to come inside, refusing to sit down, standing with his arms folded and his feet planted like a man waiting for a train he intended to stop with his body. At the edge of the trees on either side of the lawn, two wolves stood among the trunks: a dark silver one to the left, big and stiff-legged, with his hackles up from his ears to his tail. A brown one to the right, quieter, watchful.

Paul's mind, in the silver wolf, was a red roar. Jared's was a low steady wariness, like a man with his hand on a holstered gun.

Inside the house, behind Edward, the family had arranged themselves without anyone saying a word. He didn't need to look to know where they were. He could hear them.

Carlisle, at the foot of the stairs, composing courtesies.

Esme, in the kitchen doorway, with a fresh pot of coffee she'd made the instant she'd heard Seth's mind on the lawn. Because there was a guest coming, and you made coffee for guests.

Rosalie, on the arm of the big chair again, very still, her mind sharp and cold and full of one thought, which was the gun she'd seen in Leah's memory last night, and the thing it had done to the ground.

Emmett, in the chair under her, practically vibrating. *Here he comes, here he comes, here he comes.*

Alice, on the stairs, with her eyes closed. Looking. Trying to see the next ten minutes, and getting heat shimmer, and getting angrier.

Jasper, beside her, one hand on her shoulder. Calm. Utterly calm. The particular glassy calm Edward had heard in him only a handful of times, in the moments before something very bad happened.

Bella, on the couch beside him, with Renesmee in her lap. Silent to him, as always. But he could see her. Every line of her had gone taut as a drawn bow, her arms locked round their daughter, her eyes on the window.

And Renesmee, in her mother's lap, with her thumb in her mouth, watching the man in the long coat come up the lawn. Her mind was bright and curious and entirely unafraid, the way it always was, and full of one question. Jacob had sat by her crib last night and told her about him, because she'd put her hand on his face and asked, and Jacob had never once been able to say no to her. A man on a rock in a long coat. A wolf at the bottom of him.

*Is that the wolf man? Is he a wolf? Can I see?*

On the far end of the couch, where she'd been lying on her stomach since nine with a fresh cushion under her chin, Layla Quinn had sat up. Her back was better; she'd found something in her grandmother's stores last night, a jar of something yellow and pungent that Edward could still smell on her. Her face was gray with lack of sleep and her mind was doing the thing he'd heard it do on the night she'd first come here, when she'd stood two steps inside the front door and counted the exits. The organ-note underneath, tightening. The Latin keys, swinging.

And Jacob, in the middle of the room, standing, because he hadn't been able to sit down all morning either.

The man came up the porch steps.

Sam didn't move out of the way. He stood on the top step with his arms folded and looked at the man coming up toward him, and the man stopped on the step below him and looked back, and Edward stood at the window and heard Sam Uley's mind go very quiet and very careful, the way a man's mind goes when he's put his hand down in the dark and touched something with scales.

"You're Uley," said the man.

"You were on my land last night," said Sam.

"I was," the man agreed. "We'll get to that. I'd like a cup of coffee first, if it's all the same to you, and to sit down." He looked past Sam, through the open door, at the living room. "And I think everybody else in there would like to have a look at me, and I'd rather they did it all at once."

Sam didn't move.

Leah said, quietly, "Sam."

That was all. Just his name. Edward heard Sam hear it. He heard Sam brace for it, out of long habit, the way he'd braced every single time Leah said his name for two years; heard him brace for the hate in it, or the hurt, or the long thin wire of grief she'd never been able to keep out of her voice when she said it. Edward knew exactly what that wire had sounded like. He'd heard it in both their heads.

There was nothing in it. Leah said *Sam* the way she'd have said *Jared* or *Embry* or *Paul*. Tired, a little. Impatient. Like a woman telling a man he's standing in a doorway.

Sam heard that too. Edward felt it land in him like a stone dropped into a well. And Sam Uley stood on the top step for a moment longer, with his arms folded, looking at Leah's face. And then, slowly, he stepped aside.

---

The man walked into the house.

He stopped two paces inside the door, the way Layla had stopped, the first time. Edward watched him look round the room. He did it once, slowly, left to right, the way a man might look round a pub to see if there's anyone he knows. Layla had counted exits with her heart going like a bird's. This man's face stayed idle. His eyes went over each of them. Carlisle. Esme in the doorway. Rosalie and Emmett. Alice and Jasper on the stairs. Bella. Renesmee. Edward. Layla. Jacob. Each one for about a second.

And Edward heard nothing at all from him. Nothing. He stood at the window and listened with everything he had, and the house stayed shut.

But he heard Jasper.

He heard Jasper's mind go out across the room toward the man the way it went out toward everyone, automatically, feeling. And he heard what came back.

*Nothing,* thought Jasper.

It went through Jasper like a cold wind.

*He's not afraid. He's not afraid at all. Of any of us. There are nine of us in this room and two wolves on the porch and a witch on the couch who tried to kill him yesterday, and he's standing there and he isn't afraid. I can't find it. Not one thread of it. A little irritation. Hunger, I think; he's hungry. Curiosity. A kind of—fondness, when he looks at the Clearwater girl. A great deal of that. And under all of it—*

Jasper stopped.

*He's done counting,* he thought. *He did it on the lawn. He did it before he came up the steps. He knows exactly where every one of us is and what each of us can do, and he decided, before he walked through that door, what he'd do about each of us if he had to, and in what order. And he isn't even thinking about it any more. It's just there. Like knowing where the doors are.* A pause. *I knew men like that, under Maria. Two or three. In a hundred years. I made very sure, every time, to stand where they could see my hands.*

And then, very quietly, Jasper did what Jasper did.

Edward felt it go out from the stairs like a soft wave. Calm. Lethargy. The heavy, comfortable, sinking feeling Jasper had pushed into a thousand rooms; a hand on the shoulder, a warm bath. He aimed it at the man in the gray suit like a man rolling a ball across a floor.

The man turned his head and looked at Jasper.

"Don't," he said.

That was all. He sounded like a man telling a dog to get off a sofa. And Edward felt the wave stop. Felt it hit something about a foot in front of the man, an invisible wall, smooth and cold, and slide down it like water down glass, and go nowhere.

Jasper went very still on the stairs.

"I've had people in my head who were a lot better at that than you, mate," the man said. "One of them killed my mum and dad. I didn't like it from him and I'm not going to like it from you." He turned back to the room. "No offence."

*None taken,* thought Jasper, faintly, from somewhere very deep.

On the stairs, Alice made a small, sharp sound, and opened her eyes.

"I can't see you," she said.

The man looked at her.

"I can't see you," Alice said again. Her voice was tight. "At all. Not what you'll do in a minute, not what you'll do in an hour. You're—you're a blank. A shimmer. I've been trying since you got on the lawn."

"Yeah," said the man. "Sorry about that. It's partly what I am. Partly on purpose."

"On *purpose*?"

He took his right hand out of his coat pocket. There was a coin in it, a big old copper penny, worn smooth. He held it up between his finger and thumb so she could see.

"You see what people decide," he said. "Don't you. That's how it works."

Alice stared at him.

"I heard about you," said the man. "Before I came." Something moved behind his eyes and was gone. "So I try not to decide things. Not where you'd see. I let this do it." He turned the penny over. "Heads, I sit down. Tails, I stand."

He flipped it.

Alice gasped.

Edward heard why. The instant the coin left the man's thumb, spinning, the shimmer in her vision tore open, and she saw: him, sitting, in the armchair by the window, crossing his legs. Clear and sharp. A second later the coin came down on the back of his hand and he covered it, and she saw it again, the same picture, solid, certain. And then the shimmer closed over it like water.

He took his hand off the coin. Heads.

"There," he said. "You saw that, didn't you? For about a second."

Alice didn't answer. She didn't need to.

"That's all you'll get," said the man. "That second. It's about all anybody gets." He put the penny away. And then he walked across the Cullens' living room, between Jacob and the coffee table, and sat down in the big armchair by the window, and crossed his legs.

"That's Dad's chair," said Bella.

It was the first thing she'd said. Her voice came out higher than she meant it to, and Edward heard Rosalie hear it, and Emmett, and felt Bella's arms go even tighter round Renesmee.

The man looked up at her. He looked at her for a long moment, and Edward saw his eyebrows move. Up, a fraction. And Edward felt it again, very faint, the cool quick hand coming out of the closed house and reaching across the room toward his wife—

—and stopping.

He saw the man's face change. Interest. A sharp, bright, curious interest, the way a man's face changes when he puts his hand on a wall he thought was plaster and finds it's stone.

"Huh," the man said softly. "Runs in the family."

Bella went rigid. "What does?"

"Your dad's got one too. Smaller. Same make." The man's eyes were on her face, intent. "Most people's heads, there's a door, and you push it, and it opens. Your dad's got a door with a big quiet bloke sitting with his back to it. Didn't know he had it. Probably never will." He tilted his head. "Yours is a wall. A good one. I can't even find the door."

"Edward can't hear me," Bella said. "He's never been able to."

"I'm not surprised." The man was still looking at her. Edward could see the interest in his face, sharp as a blade. "Do you know you do that?"

"Do what?"

"Push it out. A bit. Round the edges. I felt it, just now, when I came in. You've got your arms round the little one and the wall's round both of you. Not much. An inch. But it's there." He looked at her for a moment more. "I'd bet you could do that to other people, if you practised. I'd bet you could put it round a whole room."

Bella stared at him.

So did Edward. He'd never once thought of it that way. Not once, in two years. He looked at his wife and his daughter in her arms, and for a fraction of a second he could almost feel it, a soft blank edge in the air round the two of them, where Renesmee's bright loud mind went quiet at the very rim.

"Relax," said the man. He'd looked away. "I'm not going to do anything about it. I'm nosy. It's a professional failing." He looked at Esme in the kitchen doorway. "Is that coffee?"

"Yes," said Esme. She'd been holding the pot the whole time. "Yes. Would you like some?"

"Love some. Milk, two sugars. Ta."

Esme went to get a cup. Her mind was a soft, astonished muddle, and very clearly, in the middle of it, the thought: *He said ta. Nobody's said ta to me since 1921.*

Leah came and sat on the arm of the armchair. She didn't ask. She sat down on the arm of Charlie's chair right beside him, with her hip against his shoulder, and folded her arms over the H on her chest and looked round the room at all of them with an expression that dared any one of them to say a single word.

Nobody did.

Seth came and sat on the floor by the couch, at Layla's feet, the way he'd sat the first night.

Sam came in last. He came in through the front door and stopped just inside it, and stood there, with his back to the wall and his arms folded, where he could see the whole room.

The man in the armchair looked at him.

"Right," he said. "Everybody here?" He glanced round. "Good. Let's do this, then. I'm Harry. Some of you already know that, because your mind-reader got it out of Lassie's head last night." He nodded at Edward without looking at him. "Before I came in, I had a look in his. So now I know what you all know, more or less. I know who everybody is. I know about the bear." That was to Emmett, who sat up very straight. "I know about the little one. And I know that last night, in this room, you all sat round and made a plan about me, and then she told you about your law." He jerked his head at Leah. "So nobody's going to do anything stupid. Which saves us all a lot of time."

Edward felt his own face go still.

The man had been in his head for one second. One. And he'd taken last night. All of it. Edward thought back over it, the whole evening, every word, every face, and couldn't think of anything in it he'd want this man to have, and couldn't think of anything in it the man didn't now have.

"So," said Harry. He accepted a cup of coffee from Esme with a nod of thanks and took a sip and closed his eyes briefly, the way people did when coffee was good. Then he opened them, and looked round the room again. "Who wants to go first?"

### IX. Leah

*The Cullen house — 11:31 a.m.*

Sam went first.

She'd known he would. She'd known it on the porch, when he'd stood on the top step with his arms folded and his jaw tight and his eyes going between her and Harry like a man watching a coin spin. She'd known it the way she knew everything about Sam, because she'd spent two years in his head and four years before that wanting to be. He'd been holding it in since seven o'clock this morning, standing in her mom's flower bed looking at footprints the size of dinner plates, and he couldn't hold it any longer.

"You crossed the boundary," Sam said. "Last night. Onto our land."

"Yep," said Harry.

"Without asking. Without anyone knowing."

"Paul knew." Harry took a sip of coffee. "Paul knew within about a minute. He's got a good nose on him, Paul. Bit of a temper."

Out on the lawn, through the open door, the silver wolf in the trees curled his lip.

"You walked straight through the middle of the reservation," said Sam. "Past people's houses. Past kids' bedrooms. To Sue Clearwater's house."

"To Leah's window," said Harry. "Let's be accurate."

"And you lay in her mother's flower bed."

"I did. I'll pay for the bamboo."

Sam's jaw went tighter. Leah watched it. She watched the muscle jump at the hinge of it, the way it had jumped a thousand times, and she waited for her chest to do the thing it had always done when Sam was angry, the twist, the old ache, the wire pulling tight.

Nothing pulled.

She looked at him. She actually looked at him, standing against the wall by the door with his arms folded, the way she hadn't been able to look at him in two years without it hurting too much to see straight. He looked tired. That was the first thing. He looked so tired. He was twenty-one years old and he looked about thirty-five, with lines at the corners of his eyes and a crease between his eyebrows that never went away now, even when he slept. He'd been carrying a pack since he was nineteen. Alone, the first year, before Jared and Paul. And he'd been carrying her, too. She could see it now, looking at him. She'd felt it every day in the pack mind, the guilt, that sticky brown sludge of it under every thought he had about her, and she'd hated it, she'd *hated* it, because it was guilt and not love and it was all he had left to give her.

It had been heavy for him too. She'd never once thought about that.

"There's a treaty," said Sam.

"Not with me there isn't."

"Then maybe there should be."

Harry looked at him over the rim of his cup. "Maybe," he said. "We'll talk about it. Not today."

"You don't get to decide that."

"I've just decided it," said Harry mildly. "So it looks like I do."

And that was when Paul came out of the trees.

---

She heard him before she saw him. The low ugly growl starting up on the lawn, and the heavy thud of paws on wet grass. She turned her head and looked out through the open front door and there he was: the big dark silver wolf, coming out from under the trees and across the lawn toward the porch. Stiff-legged. Head low. Hackles up from his ears to his tail. Lips peeled back off every one of his teeth.

He'd been listening. All of it. Standing in the trees for four hours, with his temper going round and round in him like a dog chasing its tail, and he'd heard the stranger in the suit tell his Alpha *I've just decided it*, and that was it. That was Paul. That had always been Paul. She'd seen him phase in the middle of an argument with Jacob in a grocery store parking lot once and take the side mirror off a Chevy.

"Paul," said Sam, sharp and low. The Alpha voice. Leah felt it go out of him like a pressure wave, and she didn't feel it pull at her, because she wasn't his any more, but she saw it hit Jared in the trees and saw Jared flinch.

Paul kept coming.

He came up the lawn and up the porch steps and stopped in the open doorway, filling it, huge and silver and snarling, and his eyes went past Sam and past Jacob and found the man in the armchair by the window, and found Leah sitting on the arm of it right beside him. And he came in. One step. Over the threshold. Into the Cullens' house, which no wolf in Sam's pack had ever set foot in, with his head down and his teeth out, toward the two of them.

Leah didn't even have time to stand up.

Under her, through the line, she felt Harry's wolf stand up.

She felt it the way you feel a big dog stand up beside you in the dark: the weight of it shifting, the air changing, the sense of something enormous getting to its feet very close to you. All at once. From lying down, chin on paws, eyes half closed, to standing, every muscle locked, every hair up, in the space of one heartbeat. It stood up because a wolf had walked into the room *toward her*.

And it came out of him.

Harry didn't move. He sat in the armchair with his coffee cup in one hand and his legs crossed, and his face didn't even change. But something came out of him, out of the place at the bottom of him where the wolf lived, out through the gates that weren't there any more, into the room. She had no word for it. It was like the pressure before a storm, that thick heavy feeling in the air before the sky splits, except it came all at once, in a second, and it came from the armchair.

The temperature dropped. She felt it on her skin. Every window in the long white room ticked, all at once, a sound like a lot of small knuckles rapping on glass. The fire in the fireplace went flat and blue and low. On the coffee table, the bowl of apples rattled.

And every wolf in the house went down.

She saw it happen. She felt it happen. Paul, in the doorway, stopped mid-step and his legs just folded, all four of them, and he went down onto his belly on the Cullens' white floor with his ears flat back against his skull and his tail clamped under him, and a sound came out of him that she'd never heard Paul make in her life. A whine. High and thin, like a puppy. Out on the lawn, at the edge of the trees, Jared went down too. On the floor by the couch, Seth gasped and grabbed at the edge of the coffee table and hung onto it with both hands, white-faced. In the middle of the room, Jacob staggered. He actually staggered, sideways, a step, like the floor had tipped, and caught himself on the back of the couch.

And Sam.

Sam was standing against the wall by the door with his arms folded, and she watched his knees go. Not all the way. Just a dip, a bend, the way a man's knees bend when somebody lays something very heavy across his shoulders without warning. His arms came unfolded. His back hit the wall. His face had gone gray, and she could see him fighting it, see every muscle in his neck stand out like rope, see his jaw clench so hard she thought she heard his teeth creak. And she knew what he was fighting, because she'd felt it herself, once, in the very first week, the night Sam had used the Alpha voice on her for the first time. She knew what that feeling was.

His wolf wanted to lie down. His wolf wanted to roll over and show its throat.

Sam Uley, Alpha of the Quileute, the biggest wolf on the peninsula for two years, stood against the Cullens' wall and fought his own wolf with everything he had to keep it from baring its belly to the man in the armchair.

She didn't feel any of it.

She sat on the arm of the chair, and the pressure went past her and round her and over her like water round a stone. She felt it the way she'd felt him on the bench last night, the big dark thing at the bottom of him, standing up. And where everybody else in the room felt it like something huge and hungry coming up out of the dark, she felt it like a wall going up at her back. Like the black body curled all the way round her in the cave. Something standing between her and the door.

Her own wolf, under her ribs, lifted its head, and looked at Paul flat on his belly on the floor, and looked at Sam fighting his knees against the wall, and thumped its tail. Once. Smug.

The Cullens felt it too. She didn't know how; they weren't wolves. But she heard Rosalie hiss through her teeth, and saw Emmett's hand close on the arm of the chair hard enough to crack the wood, and saw Bella's lips come back off her teeth, a newborn's reflex, and the blond one on the stairs, Jasper, had gone absolutely white, whiter than white, and had stepped down one stair and in front of Alice without seeming to know he'd done it.

It had lasted about two seconds.

"Easy," said Harry.

He said it to nobody. Quietly. To the coffee in his cup, maybe. And it stopped.

All of it. At once. The pressure went out of the room like somebody had opened a window. The temperature came back. The fire came back up yellow. The windows stopped ticking. On the floor in the doorway, Paul lay flat on his belly, shaking, panting, and didn't get up.

Harry took a sip of his coffee.

"Sorry," he said. He was looking at Paul. "He doesn't like it when people come at her."

Sam's voice came out of him like gravel. "Who doesn't?"

Harry looked across the room at him.

"Me," he said.

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Sam was still against the wall. He'd got his knees straight again. He was breathing hard, through his nose, and his face was still gray, and he was looking at Harry the way Leah had seen him look at exactly one other thing in his life: the newborn army, coming out of the trees in June, the second before the fight.

"Sam," said Leah.

He looked at her.

"You know the law," she said.

"I know the law, Leah."

"Then tell Paul."

Sam looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked down at Paul, flat on the floor in the doorway, still shaking.

"Paul," he said. The Alpha voice again, but quiet now. "Back. Outside. He's Leah's."

Paul got up.

It took him a couple of tries. His legs didn't want to work. He got them under him and stood up and backed out of the doorway, slowly, still low, never taking his eyes off the armchair, and went down the porch steps backward, and across the lawn backward, and into the trees, and Leah heard him go about fifty yards into the woods and lie down.

Sam watched him go. Then he looked back at Harry.

"Was that necessary?" he said.

"No," said Harry. "That's why I stopped it." He set his coffee cup down on the side table, on a coaster. "I don't do that on purpose, if it helps. That one's him." He touched his chest, low and to the left. "I had him locked up for two months, and as of yesterday afternoon I haven't. We're still working out the rules." He looked at Sam steadily. "Your boy came into a room at her with his teeth out. That's one of the rules. Probably the first one."

Sam didn't answer.

"You know about rules," said Harry. "You've got one. Absolute, she says. Can't be broken. I'd say he's got one too. And his is a lot older than yours." He paused. "I'd make sure Paul knows it."

"He knows it now," said Sam.

"Good."

They looked at each other across the room. Leah watched them. Sam with his back against the wall and his face still gray, and Harry in the armchair with his legs crossed and his coat open and the dark grip of the gun showing under his left arm. She felt Harry look at Sam. Through the line. She felt him look at the man she'd loved for four years, the man who'd broken her heart and then had to live inside her head while she bled from it.

She'd told him all of it. At the tent, in the kitchen, and again this morning on the rock, with the sea going in and out. He knew everything. He knew exactly who Sam was.

She waited to feel what he felt about it.

What she felt, through the line, was the wolf at the bottom of him, lying back down. Slowly. With its eyes on Sam. Disliking him. Disliking him thoroughly, the way a big old dog dislikes a fox that's been in the henhouse.

And Harry, on top of it, holding it down with one hand, the way you'd hold a dog's collar. Not saying a word. Because it wasn't his to say anything about. Because she hadn't asked him to.

She felt him decide that, very clearly, and she felt something in her own chest go loose and hot and grateful, and she didn't know what to do with it, so she put her hand on his shoulder. Just for a second.

He didn't look at her. But under her hand she felt his shoulder go easy.

---

They all went back to their places. It was weird; it was like something had cracked open in the room and then closed back up, and everybody had silently agreed to pretend it hadn't happened. Carlisle cleared his throat and came forward from the foot of the stairs. Esme came round with the coffee pot, and Harry held out his cup for a refill, and said *ta* again, and Esme smiled.

Sam stayed against the wall.

When Carlisle had started talking, polite and careful, about how grateful they were that Mr. Potter had agreed to come, and how they hoped they could all come to some understanding, Leah got up off the arm of the chair. She felt Harry feel her go, a little tug on the line, and then let go of it. She walked across the room to the front door.

Sam watched her come.

She stopped in front of him. Close enough that she could smell him, the old familiar smell, cedar and sweat and the sea and Emily's shampoo, the smell that for four years had made her heart do something stupid every single time.

It smelled like Sam. That was all.

"Can I talk to you?" Sam said. Low. "Outside. A second."

She looked at him. She thought about saying no. Then she thought she didn't need to.

"Yeah," she said. "Okay."

They went out onto the porch. The sun was still out. The lawn was steaming a little where the sun hit it, and out in the trees on the left she could see Paul, lying on his belly in the ferns with his head on his paws, staring at the house. He'd stopped snarling. He looked like he'd been hit by a truck.

Sam stood at the top of the steps with his hands in the pockets of his cut-offs and looked out at the lawn, not at her.

"I felt it," he said. "Yesterday."

She waited.

"About one. In the afternoon. I was at the house. Emily was making lunch." He was quiet for a second. "And something let go. In my chest. Like a—I don't know. Like a hook coming out. I didn't know what it was. I stood there in the kitchen and I thought I was having a heart attack. Like your dad." His jaw worked. "And then Jake called last night and I knew."

"Yeah," said Leah. "It let go."

"It's gone."

"Yeah, Sam. It's gone."

He nodded slowly. He still wasn't looking at her. She looked at the side of his face, at the crease between his eyebrows, and she realized he was trying not to cry. Sam Uley. Standing on a vampire's porch in the sun, with his Alpha face on, trying not to cry.

"Are you happy?" he said.

She thought about it.

She thought about it properly. She thought about the cave, and the sea, and the bacon sandwiches on the rock. She thought about a wolf the size of a truck lying in her mother's flower bed with its chin on its paws, saying *couldn't sleep*. She thought about a man in a gray suit sitting in a chair inside this house right now, holding his own wolf down by the collar for her sake, without being asked. She thought about her dad's table, and her mom saying *for about a day*.

She tried, just to see, the way she'd tried on the bench in the rain, to say something else. Anything else. *I don't know. It's complicated. Leave me alone.*

None of it would come. There wasn't anything else to say.

"Yeah, Sam," Leah said. "I'm happy."

Sam closed his eyes.

He stood there for a long moment with his eyes shut and the sun on his face. And she watched something go out of him. Out of his shoulders, out of his jaw, out of the crease between his eyebrows. It went the way her own wire had gone yesterday on the ridge: slowly, the whole length of it, unwinding. Two years of it.

"Good," said Sam. His voice cracked. "Good. That's—good."

He opened his eyes. He looked at her, for the first time since they'd come out. His eyes were wet.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For all of it. I never—I'm sorry, Leah."

"I know," she said. "I know you are. I've been in your head, Sam. I know exactly how sorry you are." She looked out at the lawn. "You can stop now."

He laughed. It came out of him cracked and wet, and he rubbed his face with one big hand.

"He's kind of terrifying," he said, behind his hand.

"Yeah."

"Like, Leah. Really, actually terrifying. I've never felt anything like that. My wolf wanted to—"

"I know what your wolf wanted to do." She almost smiled. "I was sitting right there."

"You didn't feel it."

"I felt it," said Leah. "It just wasn't pointed at me." She turned to go back in. At the door she stopped. "Mom's doing Thanksgiving. Thursday. Two o'clock."

"I know. She invited us for dessert. Like last year."

"Come for dessert," said Leah. "Bring Emily. Tell her I said."

Sam stared at her.

"He's coming," Leah said. "Harry. Mom invited him. He's bringing a pie." She went inside. Over her shoulder, she added: "No kidneys."

She left him standing on the porch with his mouth open, and went back across the room, and sat back down on the arm of Charlie's chair, and Harry, without looking up from Carlisle, without breaking off what he was saying, moved his hand over and put it on her knee.

### X. Edward

*The Cullen house — 11:52 a.m.*

Carlisle had a gift for it. Edward had watched him do it for ninety years: walk into a room full of frightened people, or angry people, or people who'd come to kill him, and talk, in that soft, unhurried, reasonable voice, until the room was a different room. He'd done it with the Quileute in 1936. He'd done it with the Denali sisters, and with a nomad coven in the Yukon in 1951 who'd come to his door with their eyes red and their minds full of murder, and who'd left a week later with their eyes going amber and their minds full of questions.

He did it now. He came forward from the foot of the stairs and stood by the fireplace, not too close to the armchair, and folded his hands, and said, "Mr. Potter. Thank you for coming."

"Harry's fine."

"Harry. Thank you." Carlisle inclined his head. "I'm Carlisle Cullen. This is my wife, Esme, and my—"

"I know who everybody is, Doctor."

"Of course. You said." Carlisle didn't lose his footing; Edward heard him file it, calmly, alongside everything else he'd learned this morning. *He was in Edward's head for a second, and in that second he took our names.* "Then perhaps I can simply ask. What is it you want here?"

Harry looked at him for a moment over his coffee.

"One of your lot," he said. "The other sort, mind."

"The other sort."

"Red eyes. The ones that eat people." He took a sip. "I came here two months ago to catch one. Alive. I put a net down over the whole peninsula, end to end, and sat in the woods and waited for one to walk into it. Yesterday one did. Lassie and Balto chased it straight into it, which was kind of them, and then I picked it up and took it home."

"Casimir," said Edward.

Harry's eyes flicked to him. "You got that off her last night."

"The name. A circle on a floor. A red jacket." Edward made himself hold the man's gaze. "Very little else."

"That's because there's very little else she's allowed to give you." Harry turned back to Carlisle. "Casimir Wolski. Born in Poland when your Queen Anne was on the throne, near enough. Turned in 1731. Been killing people ever since. He's in a circle in my back room right now, and he's going to stay there till I've finished asking him things, and then he's going to burn."

There was a short silence.

"You're going to question him," said Rosalie, from the arm of the chair.

"I already have. Some."

"And when you've finished?"

"I said. He burns." Harry looked at her. "Lassie's asked to light it. I've said she can."

Edward heard Carlisle's mind go still and unhappy. He'd known it would. Carlisle had been arguing with himself about questions like this for three and a half centuries, and the argument had never once been settled. *Every one of them was a person, once. Every one of them could choose differently, if someone showed them how. I did. Eleazar did. Kate did, Tanya, Irina—*

"Doctor," said Harry.

Carlisle looked at him.

"I can see you working up to it," said Harry. "So let me save you the bother." He set his cup down on its coaster. "He killed forty-one people this year. I counted. I was inside his head and I counted. The forty-first was a dental hygienist from Olympia called Dana Whitcomb. She was thirty-four. She went up the Hoh on her own on Sunday because her boyfriend cancelled on her, and at about six o'clock in the evening she stopped by the river to take a photograph of the light on the water." His voice didn't change at all. "He let her take it. He waited till she'd got the shot. And then he spent most of an hour on her, because he'd been in Vancouver a month eating junkies under a pier and he wanted something nice."

Nobody in the room moved.

"So, no," said Harry. "I'm not going to offer him a diet. He had three hundred years to work it out on his own. You did. Your family did. He looked at the same choice you looked at, three hundred times a year, and he chose Dana Whitcomb." He picked his coffee back up. "That's as far as I'll argue it. It's not a debate."

Carlisle was quiet for a long moment.

"No," he said at last, softly. "No. I don't suppose it is."

Edward heard what it cost him to say it. He heard, too, under the cost, something Carlisle would never have said aloud: a small, tired relief. That someone else, for once, had made the decision, and was going to carry it.

"This net," said Jasper.

He'd come down off the stairs. He was standing by the foot of them now, with his hands loose at his sides and his eyes on the armchair. Edward could hear him still holding the cold of the man's emotions in him like a stone in a pocket, turning it over, unable to put it down.

Bella's arms tightened.

"Where?" said Jacob. "Where does it go? This net."

"Over our land."

"Who sent you?" said Jasper.

He said it quietly. Edward had known it was coming; he'd heard it sitting in the front of Jasper's mind since the man walked in, the way a soldier's question sits there, patient, waiting for its turn. *A net over three thousand square miles. Two months of watching us. A circle in a back room. Nobody does that alone, for nothing. Somebody is paying for this. Somebody wants something. Who?*

"I'd like an answer."

"I know you would." Harry set his cup down. He uncrossed his legs and put both boots flat on the floor and sat forward a little in the chair, with his elbows on his knees. "Watch."

He opened his mouth.

Edward was watching. Every vampire in the room was watching, and Leah, and Layla, and both the wolves. And all of them saw it happen.

Harry opened his mouth to speak, and his throat closed.

There was no other way to describe it. Edward watched it close. The muscles of the man's neck stood out all at once like cords under the skin. His jaw locked open. His face went dark red, then darker, and a vein stood up at his temple, and a sound came out of him: a thin, strangled click, like a man trying to swallow something too big. His hand came up off his knee, halfway to his throat, and stopped, and clenched.

Leah was off the arm of the chair and on her feet before anyone else had moved.

"Harry—"

He held up his other hand. *Wait.* His eyes were watering. He sat there with his jaw locked open and his throat working, and Edward could see that he was pushing, actually pushing against whatever it was, the way a man pushes against a door somebody's holding from the other side.

Then he stopped pushing. He shut his mouth.

The cords in his neck went slack. The color went out of his face. He coughed, once, hard, into his fist, and sat back in the chair, breathing.

"There," he said. His voice was hoarse. "That's your answer."

Leah was standing beside the chair with both hands in fists. She looked as if she wanted to hit somebody and couldn't work out who.

"That's as far as I get," said Harry. "Every time. I try to tell you who sent me, or why, or what for, and that happens, and if I keep pushing it gets worse." He coughed again. "Trust me, I'd love to tell you. I physically can't."

"Something's stopping you," said Edward slowly.

"Something I agreed to. A long time ago." Harry rubbed his throat. "It's what made the wall in her head, if you want to know. Same thing. It treats her like she's me." He glanced up at Leah. "Sit down, love. I'm fine."

Leah didn't sit down. She stood there with her fists clenched for a moment longer, glaring at him, and Edward heard her mind: furious, frightened, and very, very clear. *You could've just SAID you couldn't. You didn't have to SHOW them.*

"Yeah, I did," Harry said to her quietly. "They'd never have believed me otherwise." He looked round the room. "Would you."

Nobody answered.

On the couch, Layla Quinn had gone very still. Edward could hear her mind racing. The cold, clear working voice had come up in her, and it was going through everything it knew, fast. *A binding. An oath. Something keyed to speech. A geas? No, a geas compels, it doesn't gag. An Unbreakable Vow kills you if you break it, it doesn't stop you trying. Something older. Something institutional, maybe. Something you swear to when you join—join what? Who swears people to that? The Aurors don't. Nobody I've ever worked with does. I've never seen anything like it. That's four.* She had no answer. Edward could hear her have no answer, the way he'd heard her have none on the ridge. She went round and round it and came back every time to the same blank wall, every glyph scratched off. *Whoever holds the other end of that,* she thought, at last, *I never, ever want to meet them.*

"So that's the bit I can't tell you," said Harry. He'd picked his coffee back up. His voice was still a little rough. "Now here's the bit I can."

He looked at Edward.

"As of about ten o'clock last night," he said, "I've been given leave to tell you about Casimir. What he was doing here. Who sent *him*." He paused. "You're going to want to sit down for this one, Mr. Cullen."

Edward didn't sit down.

"He was sent," said Harry, "from Volterra."

---

Edward had known. Somewhere, under everything, he'd known since last night, since he'd stood at the open door with the rain coming down and Bella's hand in his and thought, *if Aro ever touches any of us, he'll see this afternoon*. He'd known the way you know a sound you've been waiting for. And still, when the word came out of the man's mouth, it went through him like a blade.

Behind him on the couch, Bella stood up.

She did it in one movement, newborn-fast, Renesmee locked against her chest, and the coffee table jumped a quarter of an inch as her knee caught it. Across the room, Rosalie was on her feet too, and Esme had put her hand to her mouth in the kitchen doorway, and Alice on the stairs had gone white and blank, her eyes unfocused, searching, searching, finding nothing but shimmer.

"The old one sent him," said Harry. "Aro. In October. He had the tracker go and find Casimir in Vancouver. Casimir owed him a favour from a long time ago. And the favour was, come down here quietly, and see whether Carlisle's pet had been changed yet."

*Carlisle's pet.* Edward heard Bella hear it. He felt it in the air between them like heat.

"Because they told you last year," said Harry, to Bella now, "in Italy, that you'd be changed or you'd be dead. And they said they'd check. And the blond one, the one with the face like he's smelled something off, has been on at the old one about it ever since. So the old one thought he'd send somebody nobody'd notice. A nomad, passing through. Have a look, ring home, and then come to Italy in the spring so Aro could take his hand and see for himself."

"And did he?" said Bella. Her voice was perfectly level. Edward had never heard it so level. "Ring home?"

Harry looked at her.

"Yeah," he said. "He did. Yesterday afternoon. About ten past two."

The room went silent.

"Do you want to hear it?" said Harry.

He was looking at Edward again.

"I can show you," he said. "If you want. I can put it in your head. You'd know if I was lying. Your sort can tell the real thing." He tilted his head. "It's not pleasant. You'll hear his voice."

Edward heard himself say, "Yes."

"Then look at me."

Edward looked at him.

And one of the shutters in the closed house opened.

---

He was standing in a round room.

Stone floor. Shelves to the ceiling, jars on every shelf, things moving in the jars. In the middle of the floor, a circle of silver, four rings deep, and inside it a man lying on his back with his arms flung out and his long black hair spread on the stone, in a red rain jacket a size too small. A wooden chair to one side, and Leah on it, barefoot, in cut-offs, leaning forward with her elbows on her knees and her face hard as a fist.

He was crouched at the edge of the circle. He was holding a cheap gray cell phone out over the vampire's face. He could feel the vampire's mind under his hand like a horse under the reins, a big hot fast animal, three hundred years of it, straining and straining and held.

A ring. A strange double ring, foreign. Another.

"*Pronto.*"

Edward knew that voice.

He'd stood in front of that voice, in a wide, cool, wood-paneled reception room with fresh flowers on the desk, in a palace on a hill in Tuscany. Eight months ago. With Bella's hand in his, and Alice beside him, and the guard all round them in their dark gray cloaks. A pretty dark-haired human girl behind the desk, smiling at them with her whole face. *Hello, Jane. Hello, Alec.* Waiting to be one of them.

Gianna.

"Gianna," said the vampire on the floor, in his low lovely bored voice. "It is Casimir. Put me through to the master."

Hold music. Violins.

And then:

"*Casimir. My dear old friend. How very good to hear your voice.*"

Edward stood in the man's memory with Aro's voice in his ear, light and delighted and childlike, a kind old uncle on Christmas morning, and every hair on a body he wasn't wearing stood on end. He'd heard that voice in his own mind, in Volterra, with Aro's paper-thin hand on his. He'd heard it say *Bella* in exactly that tone of fond, acquisitive wonder. He'd heard it decide, in that tone, whether she lived.

"*You've been to see our Carlisle?*"

"I have."

"*And?*"

Edward felt the man lean on the reins. Felt the words go down into the vampire's mind one at a time, like coins into a slot.

"The girl is changed. She is one of us. Since the end of summer. Her eyes are already going gold, the way his are, the animal way. The family are as they always were. Dull. The same. Nothing to report."

"*Ah. Ah, that is a relief. Caius will be so* disappointed.*" A soft, silvery laugh. "*And nothing else, my friend? Nothing at all of interest, in all that rain?*"

And under the reins, the horse surged.

Edward felt it the way the man had felt it. The vampire's own mind, the real one, under the curse, coming up all at once out of the dark, three hundred years of it, scrabbling, *the heartbeat, the heartbeat, I stood in the trees for an hour, it went like a bird, tell him, tell him, TELL HIM*—

And Edward felt the man hold.

He felt it from the inside. That was the thing he'd carry out of the memory with him, the thing he'd lie awake with. He felt the man put everything he had on the reins. Everything. He felt the whole weight of a will he couldn't measure come down on that surging mind like a hand on a struggling animal's neck, and press, and press, and not let go. He felt the thing at the bottom of the man lift its head at the strain, and felt the man not even notice it. He felt how close it was. A hair. A breath. The vampire's mouth already shaping the first sound of *heart*—

"Dogs," said Casimir. Bored. "The dogs still run there. The Quileute dogs. They chased me out of the woods. Big, stupid things. Tiresome."

"*Ah, the wolves.*" Amused. "*How tiresome indeed. Carlisle does keep the oddest company.*"

Rain. Mexico. A tired, irritable laugh, so real it turned Edward's stomach.

"*Go, then. Go and be warm. And come to see me in the spring, Casimir, won't you? I would so like to see you. I would so like to* see.*"

Click.

And the shutter closed.

---

He was standing in his own living room.

He found he'd put one hand out against the back of the couch. He didn't remember doing it. He was holding on to it. He could feel the wood starting to give under his fingers, and he made them stop.

Everybody was looking at him.

"Edward," said Bella.

He turned to her. She was standing beside the couch with Renesmee held so tight against her that the child had stopped squirming and gone very still, looking up at her mother's face. Bella's eyes were huge.

"It's true," Edward said. His voice came out strange. "All of it. It was Aro. I'd know his voice anywhere. And Gianna—the girl at the desk, love, you remember her—she answered."

Bella's face did something terrible.

"They sent someone," said Edward. To all of them. He made himself say it clearly. "To look at us. To look at you. He stood in our trees, on the north side, at four o'clock on Monday morning. For an hour." He swallowed. "He heard her. Renesmee. Her heart. He didn't know what she was. He'd never heard anything like her. He stood there for an hour, listening to it, thinking about how much Aro would want to know."

Rosalie made a sound like tearing silk.

"And he tried to tell him," said Edward. "On the telephone. Yesterday afternoon. Aro asked him if there was anything else of interest, and he tried to tell him about her. He *tried*. He was this close." He held up his finger and thumb, a hair apart. "And—"

He looked at the man in the armchair.

Harry was sitting back in Charlie's chair with his coffee in one hand. He looked tired. That was all Edward could see in his face. Tired, the way a man looks after he's carried something heavy a long way.

"And he held him," Edward said quietly. "He held him down. With everything he had. And Casimir said *dogs*." He looked at Bella. "Aro doesn't know. He thinks you're changed and gold-eyed and dull and there's nothing else in this house. He has no idea she exists."

Bella stood very still.

Then she crossed the room.

She did it slowly, which for a newborn was an act of enormous will; Edward could see her doing it, holding every muscle back. She walked across the Cullens' living room with her daughter in her arms, past Jacob, past the coffee table, and stopped in front of the armchair by the window, so close that Leah, standing beside it, shifted her weight and looked at her.

Harry looked up at her.

"Why?" said Bella.

He looked at her for a moment. Edward watched him. He didn't hear anything, of course; the shutters were all closed again, every one. But he watched the man's face, and he saw him think about the answer, and decide to give the true one, or as much of it as he had.

"You're useful," said Harry.

Leah snorted.

Harry didn't look at her. Something happened at the corner of his mouth.

"Your family's useful to me," he said. "The people I work for marked you down as useful before I ever got here. If the Italians come over here for a look and see something they don't like and burn this place to the ground, I've lost something I was told to keep. So." He shrugged. "That's the professional answer."

"And the other answer?" said Bella.

He looked past her, at the child in her arms. Renesmee was looking back at him. She'd taken her thumb out of her mouth.

"I was up on a ridge in October," said Harry. "About a mile north of here. With a pair of glasses. And I saw her go by on the big ginger lad's back. Holding on to his fur with both hands." He paused. "She was laughing her head off."

In the middle of the room, Jacob made a small sound.

"I'm not having that old bastard put his hand on her," said Harry. "That's the other answer. Not on my watch. Not ever."

Bella stood in front of him for a long moment with her daughter in her arms. Edward couldn't hear her. He never could. But he could see her face, and he watched what happened to it, and he'd been married to her for three months and in love with her for two years and he'd never seen her look at anyone outside the family like that.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't," said Harry. "It was business. Mostly."

"Thank you anyway."

He looked up at her for a second more. Then he nodded, once, and looked away out of the window, and drank his coffee.

---

"He'll expect him in the spring," said Edward.

He'd sat down at last, on the arm of the couch. His hand was still not entirely steady. He put it flat on his knee.

"Yeah," said Harry. "He will."

"And when Casimir doesn't come?"

"Then he's a nomad who didn't come." Harry shrugged. "They go missing. Your lot. All the time. Aro's not going to send the tracker out after every one that forgets to ring. He's got the answer he wanted. He's got the blond one off his back. He'll think about it for a minute and then he'll forget." He set his cup down. "You've got bigger things to worry about."

"What do we need to worry about?" said Carlisle.

"Hands," said Harry.

They looked at him.

"That old man reads everything off a handshake," said Harry. "Everything you've ever thought. Everything you've ever seen. You know that; you've been there." That was to Edward. "So you need to think, very carefully, about every single person who's ever seen that child. Or ever will. And whether there's any chance, any chance at all, that one of them'll ever be in a room with him and shake his hand." He looked round at them. "Because if one of them does, it won't matter what Casimir said on the phone. He'll know. And he'll come."

Edward heard Carlisle's mind go somewhere. North. To Alaska. To a big house in the snow outside Denali, and five people who'd been the nearest thing to family the Cullens had ever had outside themselves. Tanya. Kate. Carmen and Eleazar. Irina.

Irina, who'd lost Laurent to the wolves ten months ago, and had never forgiven them. Irina, who'd walked out of the wedding reception in August the moment she caught sight of Seth. Irina, who'd said on the telephone at the end of October, to Esme, in a voice Edward had heard all the way across the house, *We'd like to come down at Christmas, Tanya and I, if you'll have us. It's been too long.*

He felt himself go cold.

"We have friends," said Carlisle slowly. "In Alaska. Old friends. They were talking about visiting. In December."

Harry looked at him for a long moment.

"Then I'd keep them in Alaska," he said.

"Irina," said Edward, very quietly. He hadn't meant to say it aloud.

Nobody asked him what he meant. Carlisle looked at him, and Esme looked at him, and Rosalie, and he saw each of them go to the same place he'd gone, and come back.

"And the spring?" said Edward. "What happens in the spring?"

Harry looked at him.

"I wouldn't worry about the spring," he said.

And Edward, watching him, saw his throat move. Just a little. The cords at the side of his neck tightened, very slightly, and then relaxed, as if something there had leaned on them for a moment, gently, the way you'd put a hand on someone's arm to stop them saying too much.

Harry coughed.

"That's all I've got on that," he said.

---

### XI. Layla

*The Cullen house — 12:20 p.m.*

She'd been trying to read him for an hour.

It was the only thing she knew how to do. She'd been doing it since she was nineteen, standing in front of doors, in front of sarcophagi, in front of altar stones in the Yucatán with the jungle dripping all round her and an old crew chief at her shoulder saying *take your time, Quinn, the dead aren't in a hurry*. You looked. You found the seams. You found where the maker's hand had slipped, the place where the glyph had been carved a hair too deep, the one stone in the wall that had been set by somebody tired. Everybody left something. Every curse in the world had a signature, if you were patient.

She'd been sitting on the Cullens' couch with her back on fire and Gran's thirty-year-old Murtlap salve stinking under her shirt, looking at Harry Potter for an hour, and she had nothing.

She'd tried the obvious first. British. Fine; he was Harry Potter; of course he was British. So was he British Ministry? Aurors? She'd worked with British Aurors, once, on a job in Belize, two of them, a man and a woman, polite and dogged and by the book, and they'd had wands in their hands from the moment they got off the boat, and they'd had to file a form in triplicate every time they cast anything stronger than a Lumos. They'd been very, very good. They'd been nothing like this. No Auror she'd ever heard of would be on American soil running a ward net over three thousand square miles of a national park. MACUSA would have had their badges and their wands and probably their kneecaps.

And Aurors didn't carry guns. Nobody carried guns. She kept coming back to that. And Aurors didn't have whatever it was that had just closed his throat like a fist, in front of all of them, while he sat there and let it.

So not an Auror. Hit-wizard, maybe. Freelance. Some private outfit, some rich old family with a grudge against vampires. But freelancers didn't answer to anybody. And he'd said *the people I work for* to Bella, twenty minutes ago, plain as day. So somebody had him. Somebody had him on a leash long enough to let him run around Washington State with a gun and a net, and strong enough to strangle him in a vampire's living room if he tried to say their name.

She couldn't think of anyone. That was what frightened her. She'd worked in the field in four countries for three years and she couldn't think of a single outfit, government or private, that would make a man like that swear something like that. Or that a man like that would agree to swear it to.

She looked at him sitting in Charlie Swan's armchair with his coffee and his crooked nose, and she thought: *I don't know who you are. I don't know what you are. I don't know who you work for or what they want. I don't know anything about you except your name and that you could have killed me yesterday as easily as breathing and didn't.*

And that he'd checked her for broken bones, walking across a crater on the air.

She was still sitting there trying to find a seam when the little girl climbed down off her mother's lap.

---

Bella had gone back to the couch. She'd sat down beside Edward with Renesmee in her arms and her face still doing the thing it had been doing since the phone call, a kind of stunned stillness. And Renesmee, who had been very quiet and very still in her mother's arms for twenty minutes, which for Renesmee was a lifetime, put both her hands on her mother's arm and pushed.

Bella looked down at her.

Renesmee pushed again. She looked at her mother. Then she looked across the room at the man in the armchair, and back at her mother, and Layla watched her put one small hand flat on Bella's cheek.

Whatever she showed her, it made Bella's face change.

"Edward," Bella said.

"I know." Edward was looking at their daughter too. "She wants to."

"Is it—"

Edward looked across at Harry. Layla watched him look; watched him try, by reflex, to hear something, and get the closed house, and give up. Then he looked at Leah, standing beside the armchair. And whatever he heard in Leah's head made him close his eyes for a second and open them again.

"It's all right," he said.

Bella held on a moment longer. Then she opened her arms.

Renesmee slid down off her lap onto the white rug. She stood there for a moment, small, in a little green corduroy dress and striped tights, with her bronze curls down her back, and looked round at everybody. Then she walked across the room.

She walked the way she did everything, which was too well for her age; ten weeks old and steady on her feet as a five-year-old. She walked past the coffee table and past Jacob, who put out one hand toward her, without seeming to know he'd done it, and then pulled it back. She walked right up to the armchair by the window and stopped in front of Harry's knees and looked up at him.

Harry looked down at her.

He'd gone very still. Layla saw it. He'd been still before, sitting in the chair drinking coffee, the stillness of a man at rest. This was different. He'd gone still the way you go still when a butterfly lands on your hand.

Renesmee held up her arms.

There was a moment. Harry looked at Bella across the room. Bella's face was rigid. She nodded, very slightly.

He put down his coffee. He leaned forward and picked Renesmee up, carefully, one hand under her arms and one under her knees, the way you'd lift something you were afraid you might break, and sat her on his knee.

Renesmee looked at him from about a foot away for a long moment with her big brown eyes. Then she lifted her hand and put it flat on his cheek.

"Ah," said Edward, very softly, from across the room. "He's letting her in."

Layla didn't understand what he meant at first. Then she watched Harry's face, and she did.

Something opened in it. That was the only way to say it. She'd been looking at that face for an hour, trying to find a seam, a slip, anything, and it had been like looking at the side of a cliff. And when the little girl put her hand on his cheek, something in it opened, the way a door opens a crack in a house you'd thought was empty, and for a moment Layla saw a light on inside.

He was letting the child see. Whatever he kept behind all those shutters that Edward couldn't get through, he was opening one of them for a ten-week-old girl with her hand on his face.

His eyes had gone unfocused. Then they focused again, on Renesmee, and he laughed. Very quietly. A short, surprised breath of a laugh.

"No," he said to her. "Not in the house, love. Your gran'd have my hide."

In the kitchen doorway, Esme made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something else.

"What did she ask?" said Jacob. His voice was rough.

"Whether I'm a wolf too." Harry didn't look away from the child. "And if she can see. She's seen yours." He tilted his head at Jacob. "She'd like to know if mine's bigger."

Jacob opened his mouth.

"It is," said Leah.

Jacob shut it.

Renesmee's hand was still on Harry's cheek. Layla watched the little girl's face change: a flicker, a thought, and she pressed her hand harder, the way she'd pressed it on Edward's face two weeks ago to tell him what the bear should have done. And Harry's eyebrows went up.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, I see." He glanced across the room. At Layla.

Layla felt her stomach drop.

"She's showing me your lights," said Harry. "The blue ones. From the other week." He looked back at Renesmee. "You liked those, didn't you?"

Renesmee nodded, solemn.

"And you'd like some more."

Another nod.

Harry looked at her for a moment. Then he looked up, across the room, at Bella and Edward on the couch.

"I can do something better than lights," he said. "If that's all right. Won't hurt her. Won't hurt anybody. It's a bit of silver. It runs about a bit and then it goes."

Edward looked at Bella. Bella looked at her daughter, sitting on a stranger's knee with her hand on his face and her whole small body leaning forward with eagerness.

"Okay," said Bella.

Harry held his right hand out, palm up, in front of the little girl. Open, the way you'd hold your hand out to show someone there's nothing in it.

"*Expecto Patronum,*" he said.

He said it softly. He didn't need to say it at all; Layla knew that much about him by now. He said it for Renesmee, she thought. So she'd hear the words. So it'd be a proper spell, with a name, the way spells were in stories.

Silver came out of his hand.

---

Layla knew what was going to happen. Everybody knew what was going to happen. Every witch and wizard in the world who'd ever read the *Ghost* knew what Harry Potter's Patronus was. It had been in every account of the war. It had been in the four-column piece about where he'd gone. It had been in the very first story anybody ever told about him that wasn't about the scar: a thirteen-year-old boy on the shore of a lake at night with a hundred Dementors coming down on him out of the dark, and a silver stag coming out of his wand and driving them back across the water like leaves. A stag. Antlers like a crown. People had painted it. There was a mural of it in a café in Albuquerque she used to go to after work.

So she watched the silver pour out of his open hand and gather in the air in front of the little girl, bright, swirling, like mist with the moon inside it. And she waited for the antlers.

It came down onto four feet on the white rug.

It was lean. That was the first thing. Long in the leg, narrow in the hip, deep in the chest. A narrow head. Tall ears. A long brush of a tail. It stood in front of the armchair on the Cullens' white rug, made of light, shining so brightly that every vampire in the room was lit up silver down one side, and it shook itself, nose to tail, the way a dog shakes itself coming out of water.

It was a wolf.

Layla knew which wolf the second she saw it, and so did everybody else in the room, because they'd all seen the real thing. It was long and lean and fine-boned and fast-looking, built like a greyhound, built like something made for running. It held its head a particular way, high and a little to one side.

It was Leah.

Nobody moved.

The silver wolf stood on the rug and looked round the room with its bright, blank, shining eyes. Then it trotted. A slow, easy circle, all the way round the long white room, past the fireplace and the stairs and the kitchen doorway, and every vampire it passed went still as it went by, and the light of it went over their faces. It went past Jacob and he turned to watch it go. It went past Seth on the floor and Seth's mouth fell open. It went past Layla on the couch, close enough that she felt it, a cool clean brush of air, like the breath off snow, and the hope in it, and the joy, so strong it made her eyes sting.

It came back round to the armchair. It stopped in front of Renesmee, on Harry's knee, and lowered its head.

Renesmee put out both hands and touched its face. The light went up her arms, silver, the way the blue had gone up her arm two weeks ago, and she laughed, a high, delighted shriek, and threw her arms round its neck, and it let her.

Then it lifted its head out of her arms and turned and went to Leah.

Leah was standing beside the armchair. She'd been standing there since the throat thing, with her fists clenched, and she hadn't moved since the silver came out of his hand. She was staring at it. Her face had gone completely blank.

The silver wolf came and sat down at her feet. It looked up at her. And then it put its shining nose against the back of her hand, the one that was still clenched in a fist at her side, and pushed, gently, until the fist came open.

Leah looked down at it.

Harry hadn't moved.

He was sitting in the armchair with the little girl on his knee and his right hand still held out in front of him, open, empty. And he was staring at the silver wolf sitting at Leah's feet.

Layla watched his face. She'd spent an hour looking for a seam in it. She found one now, and she wished she hadn't, because it wasn't hers to see.

He hadn't known. That was the first thing. He'd had no idea. He'd held out his hand to make something he'd been making for thirteen years, the thing he'd made at thirteen on a lakeshore, his father's shape, and something else had come out, and he was sitting there looking at it like a man who'd opened his front door and found the sea.

And under that, she saw him understand it. She watched it land. And it went across his face, all of it, one thing after another, so fast she could barely follow. Shock. Then something very like grief, sharp and sudden and real. And then, coming up under the grief, slowly, like light under a door, something else. Something so naked she had to look away.

She looked away.

"Your Patronus changed," she heard herself say.

Her voice sounded very loud in the silence.

"Yeah," said Harry. His voice was hoarse again, and this time there was nothing in his throat to make it so. "Yeah. It did."

"What does that mean?"

That was Leah. She was still looking down at the silver wolf at her feet, its nose in her open palm. "What does it mean? You said—this morning, you said it was a stag. You said it was always a stag."

Harry didn't answer. Layla looked back at him. He was looking at Leah, and he opened his mouth, and nothing came out of it. He'd simply run out of voice.

So Layla answered for him.

"It happens," she said. "Sometimes. Not often. A Patronus is—it comes out of the best thing in you. The happiest thing, the strongest. That's what it's made of. It takes the shape of whatever that is, and mostly that never changes your whole life." She swallowed. "But sometimes something happens to a person that's so big it moves everything else over. And the Patronus moves with it. Grief does it, sometimes." She thought of her own, a fox, which she hadn't tried to cast since Mexico, because she was afraid of what might come out. "Mostly it's love. It changes to match whoever—" She stopped. "Whatever's at the middle of you now."

Leah looked up from the silver wolf at her feet. She looked at Harry.

"It was your dad's," she said quietly.

"Thirteen years," said Harry.

That was all he said. He sat there in Charlie Swan's armchair with Renesmee on his knee and his hand open, and looked at Leah standing over the shining wolf at her feet, and said *thirteen years*, and Layla, who had read curses in four countries and walked into tombs and been thrown twenty feet into a log by a bullet that didn't touch her, found that she had to look at the ceiling for a while.

The silver wolf at Leah's feet looked up at her for a moment more. Then it faded. Slowly. From the tail up, like mist burning off in the sun, until there was nothing at Leah's feet but the white rug and the light from the window, and Leah's hand, still open.

Nobody said anything.

In the middle of the room, Jacob Black sat down on the floor.

He did it without seeming to mean to, the way he'd sat down last night. His knees just went, and he sat on the rug with his back against Emmett's chair, and looked at Leah, and then at Harry, and then at Leah again. And Layla heard him say, under his breath, so quietly that maybe only she and the vampires caught it:

"Oh. Oh, man. It's real."

---

Harry was the first one to move.

He did it carefully. He picked Renesmee up off his knee, one hand under her arms and one under her knees, and held her out toward the couch, and Bella came and took her. The little girl went without complaint. She was still glowing faintly, silver round the edges, and she was looking at Harry over her mother's shoulder with an expression of total satisfaction, like a child who's been given exactly the present she asked for.

Then he picked his coffee back up and drank the rest of it, which must have been stone cold.

"Well," he said. His voice was nearly normal. "That's new."

Leah was still standing beside the chair. She hadn't moved. She was looking at her own open hand.

Harry looked at her for a moment. Then he reached up without looking and took the hand, and held it, and looked across the room at Layla.

"Quinn," he said.

She jumped. "What?"

"Your Disjunction."

She stared at him.

"Your third braid's backwards," said Harry.

"My—"

"Third braid. The one that comes in under the first two and ties them off. You've got it spinning widdershins. Should be sunwise." He drew a little spiral in the air with one finger of his free hand. "That's how I turned it, yesterday. That's the seam. Everything else about it is lovely. That one braid, you've got the wrong way round, and it leaves a gap at the back about this wide." He held up his finger and thumb, a hair apart. "If you know where to look you can get a finger in and turn the whole thing round. Fix that, and nobody's turning it."

Layla sat on the couch with her mouth open.

"Nobody?" she said.

Harry thought about it.

"Well," he said. "I could. But it'd take me a lot longer."

She looked at him. She thought about the spell. Three years of work, at kitchen tables, in tents, in a rented room in Mérida with the fan going round and round overhead. She thought about the third braid. She'd always had a feeling about the third braid. Something about the way it sat. She'd never been able to work out what.

She thought about him on the ridge, with her spell on his fingertips, turning his hand like a man turning over a stone to see the underside. *Oh, that's clever.* A second and a half.

"Why are you telling me this?" she said.

He looked at her as if it were obvious.

"Because when I ring," said Harry Potter, "I'm going to need it working."

### XII. Jacob

*The Cullen house — 12:41 p.m.*

He didn't decide to ask. He just heard himself doing it.

"Can I talk to you?" Jacob said. "Outside?"

The room had come apart a bit after the silver wolf. That was the only way he could describe it. Nobody had said anything for a long time, and then everybody had started saying things at once, quietly, the way people talk at a funeral after the coffin's gone in. Carlisle had gone over to the armchair and was asking Harry something about the net, about the stones, about how deep he'd buried them, in his gentle doctor's voice, and Harry was answering, short and plain, with Leah's hand still in his. Esme was making more coffee. Layla was sitting on the couch staring at nothing, with her lips moving, drawing little spirals in the air with one finger. Seth was watching Layla. Emmett had got up and was walking in a slow circle round the spot on the rug where the silver wolf had stood, looking at it, as if it might have left footprints.

And Jacob had been sitting on the floor with his back against Emmett's chair, looking at Leah, for ten minutes. At her face. At the way she was standing next to the armchair with her hand in his, not looking at him, looking out the window, with her chin up and her jaw set the way it always was, and something in it he'd never seen there, not once, not in the whole year he'd known her as a wolf, or the sixteen before that he'd known her as Sue Clearwater's scary older daughter who used to throw sand at him on First Beach.

She looked like somebody who'd put down something heavy.

So he got up off the floor, and heard himself say it.

Harry looked up at him from the armchair. He looked at him for a second with those eyes, the green ones, and Jacob felt a cold little drop go through his stomach, the same as he'd felt on the lawn when the man first walked out of nowhere. And then Harry looked up at Leah.

Leah looked at Jacob. Then she let go of Harry's hand.

"Go on," she said. "He's not going to bite you."

"Which one of us are you talking to?" said Harry.

"Both," said Leah.

---

They went out onto the porch.

The sun was still out. It was crazy; it had been out all morning, which never happened in November, and the whole lawn was steaming gently where it hit, a thin white mist coming up off the grass and drifting toward the trees. Out at the edge of the woods on the left, Paul was still lying on his belly in the ferns with his head on his paws, staring at the house. He'd stopped staring at the house and started staring at the man who'd just come out of it. His ears went flat. He didn't get up.

Harry leaned on the porch rail and looked at him.

"Tell your mate no hard feelings," he said.

"He's one of Sam's."

"Tell him anyway." Harry turned round and leaned back on the rail, with his elbows on it, and looked at Jacob. "Go on, then."

Jacob stood there. He'd had it all worked out on the floor. He'd had a whole thing. He opened his mouth and none of it came out.

"Is it real?" he said instead. "For you?"

Harry looked at him for a moment.

"You saw the dog," he said.

"Wolf."

"You saw it."

"Yeah," said Jacob. "Yeah. I saw it."

He had. He'd watched it go round the room and come back and sit down at Leah's feet and push its nose into her fist until it opened, and he'd sat down on the floor because his legs wouldn't hold him up. He'd watched it on Harry's face too. The thing that happened there when the silver came out the wrong shape. He'd seen that before. He'd seen it on Sam's face, in Sam's memory, a hundred times. He'd seen it on his own, in the Cullens' hallway mirror, the morning after.

"If you hurt her—" Jacob said.

"You'll what?" said Harry.

He said it mildly. He didn't move off the rail. But Jacob felt it, very faint, from the place in him where his wolf lived: something on the other end of the porch lifting its head. And he felt his own wolf go very still and very quiet at the bottom of him, the way a dog goes quiet when a bigger dog walks into the yard.

Harry seemed to feel it too. Something in his face changed.

"Sorry," he said. "Habit. Go on."

Jacob swallowed. "I'll find a way," he said. "I don't know how. I'll find one."

Harry looked at him for a long moment.

"Good," he said. "Somebody should have one."

He said it like he meant it. Jacob stood there and couldn't work out what to do with that.

"Same goes the other way," said Harry. He tipped his head toward the open door, toward the long white room inside and the couch where Bella was sitting with Renesmee in her lap, still faintly silver round the edges, still looking out at the porch. "Anybody comes for that kid, I'll be there before you've finished phasing. Call it a standing arrangement. That's how it's going to be now."

"Why?" said Jacob. "For real. You said business."

"I said mostly."

"So what's the rest?"

Harry looked at him. Then he looked away, out over the lawn, at the steam coming up off it in the sun.

"Told you," he said. "I saw her laughing."

"That's it?"

"That's plenty," said Harry.

Jacob stood on the porch and thought about that. He thought about the ridge in October that he'd never known anybody was standing on, and Renesmee on his back with both fists in his fur, shrieking with laughter every time he jumped a log, and a man up there in the trees a mile off with a pair of binoculars, watching. Deciding something. And never once letting them know he'd decided it.

"Okay," said Jacob.

"Okay," said Harry.

They stood there.

"Last night," said Jacob. "I said—to Leah. I said *how could you*. About you."

"I know."

"She told you."

"She told me what she said back." Harry's mouth did something at the corner. "In some detail."

Jacob groaned. He put both hands over his face. "A, B, C."

"I liked C."

"Of *course* you liked C."

"I'm also a wolf," said Harry. "Apparently. It's in the rules now."

Jacob took his hands off his face. He looked at Harry, leaning on the rail in his gray suit with his long coat open and the sun on his hair, and he thought about the shape he'd seen at the bottom of the rope in Leah's head yesterday. The big dark thing behind its locks, lifting its head. The way it had felt, through the pack mind, like standing at the foot of a mountain.

"Is it bigger than me?" he said. "Really? Like she said?"

Harry considered him.

"Little bit," he said.

"How much is a little bit?"

Harry reached out and held his hand flat in the air, palm down, about a foot over the top of Jacob's head.

Jacob stared at it.

"That's a whole *head*," he said.

"Give or take."

"That's—at the *shoulder*?"

"At the shoulder."

"You're kidding me."

"Ask Lassie," said Harry. "She's measured."

Jacob opened his mouth. Then he thought about what that meant, exactly, and when, and he shut it again and looked very hard at the lawn, and felt his face go hot, and on the rail beside him Harry Potter started to laugh.

---

Emmett came out about two minutes later. He came out the way Emmett went everywhere, like a Labrador who'd heard the word *walk*, and stopped in the doorway with his eyes fixed on the left side of Harry's coat.

"Is that a gun?" he said.

Harry looked down at his coat, where it had fallen open. The dark grip of the pistol was showing under his arm in its leather holster.

"It is," he said.

"Like a *real* gun? Like a Muggle gun? Layla says wizards don't use guns."

"Layla's right. Most don't."

"That's the one that did the thing. The hole. On the ridge." Emmett had come out onto the porch. He was standing about four feet away, bouncing a little on his toes. "I saw it in Leah's head. Last night. When Seth told it. The ground just—" He made an explosion with his hands. "*Boom.*"

"That's the one."

"Can I hold it?"

"No," said Harry.

Emmett's face fell so far it was almost funny. "Everybody says that," he said. "Every single person. Layla said it about her stick."

"Wand."

"Whatever. Everybody says no." Emmett looked at the grip of the gun with longing. "Can I at least *see* it?"

Harry looked at him for a moment. Jacob watched him. He could see Harry thinking about it, weighing it, and then he could see him decide, with a kind of shrug, that there wasn't much harm in it.

He reached into his coat and took the pistol out.

He did it fast. Jacob didn't even see his hand move; one second the coat was hanging open and the next the gun was in his hand, held low, pointed at the porch boards, his finger lying straight along the side of it outside the trigger guard. It was big and blocky and old, dull blue-gray steel worn silver at the edges.

"Colt," said Harry. "1911A1. Made in 1943. US Army. Some lad carried it from Normandy to the Rhine and wrote letters home to a girl in Ohio every week, and when he died his family sold his house and everything in it, and I bought it in a box with his medals for eleven quid." He turned it a little in the sun so Emmett could see the side of it, the worn places where somebody's thumb had ridden the safety. "Seven in the magazine. Except it never runs out. And I can choose what comes out the end."

"Choose like what?"

"Like what I want."

"Does it work on us?"

"Want to find out?"

Emmett actually thought about it. Jacob watched him think about it. "Kind of?" he said.

"*Emmett,*" said Rosalie, from somewhere inside the house, in a voice like a whip.

"I'm just *asking*."

"Short answer," said Harry, "the round I used yesterday would take your arm off at the shoulder and keep going. Into the next county, probably. I built it for granite." He looked Emmett up and down. "You're about granite."

Emmett's face lit up like Christmas morning.

"*Dude,*" he said.

Harry put the gun away. He did it as fast as he'd taken it out. "Don't tell the blond one I said that."

"She heard you. She hears everything."

"Then tell her I said it nicely."

"Dude," said Emmett again, softly, to himself, and went back into the house looking dazed and happy, and Jacob heard him say to Rosalie in the hall, *Rose, he built a bullet for* granite, and Rosalie say something back that made Emmett laugh.

Harry looked at Jacob.

"Is he always like that?"

"Pretty much."

"Christ," said Harry, and sounded almost fond.

---

Sam came out next.

He came out slowly. He stood in the doorway for a second and looked at the two of them on the porch, Harry on the rail and Jacob beside him, and Jacob saw his face do something, a quick flicker of something old, and then go back to the Alpha face. He came out and stood at the top of the steps with his hands in his pockets and looked out at the lawn, at Paul in the ferns, at Jared at the other edge of the trees.

"We need to talk about the boundary," Sam said. To the lawn.

"We do," said Harry.

"You can't just come onto our land whenever you want."

"I know." Harry didn't move off the rail. "Next time, I'll ask."

Sam turned his head. "You'll ask."

"I'll knock." Harry shrugged. "Metaphorically. I'll let you know. Somehow. I'll send Lassie with a note."

"Leah's not your messenger," said Sam, and Jacob braced, because he'd heard Sam say Leah's name in that voice a hundred times in the pack mind, sharp, protective, guilty. But it came out different. It came out almost like a joke.

"No," Harry agreed. "She'd eat the note."

Sam almost smiled. Jacob saw it happen, the corner of his mouth going, and then Sam put it away. But it had been there.

"And this net," said Sam. "It stays?"

"It stays."

"Over the rez."

"Over everything." Harry looked at him. "It doesn't see you. It doesn't touch your people. It catches one thing, and that's things that eat people. If one of those ever walks onto your land, it'll go up by the ankle, and I'll know, and I'll come and get it. And I'll tell you. You can come and watch, if you want." He paused. "You've got kids on that reservation, Uley. I've seen them. Down at the beach. I'd have thought you'd want something like that over them."

Sam didn't say anything for a long moment.

"And you," he said. "You're staying?"

"I'm staying."

"For how long?"

"A while." Harry shifted on the rail. "As of last night, I've been made liaison."

Sam turned all the way round. "Liaison."

"To you lot. Both packs. It's a new job. I think it's got about eleven words in the title. I haven't seen it yet. I've been told I'll hate it."

"Liaison for *who*?"

Harry looked at him.

"Me," he said.

Sam looked at him for a very long time. Jacob looked at him too. He looked at that face, the crooked nose and the stubble and the eyes, and thought about the man sitting in the armchair with his jaw locked open and his throat working, unable to say who he worked for. And he thought, *that's the real answer, isn't it. Whoever he's liaising for, he'll never be able to tell us. So he's saying* me. *And it's the only true thing he can say.*

"We didn't ask for a liaison," said Sam.

"Nobody ever does," said Harry.

"And if we say no?"

"Then I'll be the liaison you don't talk to." Harry shrugged. "I'm still staying. I've been invited to Thanksgiving."

Sam closed his eyes.

"Two o'clock Thursday," said Harry. "Clearwaters'. I'm bringing a pie. I gather you're coming for dessert."

"Leah said," said Sam, with his eyes shut.

"Good. I'll save you a slice." Harry pushed himself off the rail. "What d'you like? I can do apple. I can do a treacle tart, but I don't think you lot have got golden syrup. Is pumpkin a pie? Is it really? Leah says it's a pie. I don't believe her."

Sam opened his eyes and looked at him.

And Jacob Black, standing on the Cullens' porch in the November sun, watched Sam Uley, Alpha of the Quileute, the man who'd been carrying the whole tribe on his back since he was nineteen years old, look at the most frightening thing either of them had ever met in their lives standing there asking him about pumpkin pie. And watched him lose the fight with his own face.

Sam laughed. Short. Surprised. Almost a bark. He put his hand over his mouth.

"Pumpkin," he said, through his hand. "Emily likes pumpkin."

"Pumpkin it is," said Harry.

---

Leah came out onto the porch about a minute later with her hands in the sleeves of the blue sweater and her hair sticking up on one side where she'd slept on it in a cave.

"We're going," she said.

"Are we?" said Harry.

"I'm starving."

"You had four bacon sandwiches three hours ago."

"So?"

Harry looked at her. Then he looked at Jacob, and at Sam, and Jacob saw something pass across his face that he recognized, because he'd felt it on his own face every single day for ten weeks: the look of a man who's just realized he's going to spend the rest of his life doing exactly what somebody else says, and doesn't mind at all.

"Right," said Harry. "That's me, then."

Carlisle had come to the door behind Leah. He stood in it with his hands folded.

"Will you come back?" he said. "I'd very much like to talk further. When you have time."

"Probably." Harry was buttoning his coat. "Somebody's got to keep an eye on the kid."

"And Mr.—Harry." Carlisle hesitated. Jacob could see him hesitating, the way he did when he was going to say something he'd thought about carefully. "Thank you. For what you did on that telephone. Whatever your reasons."

Harry looked at him for a moment.

"Keep your friends in Alaska, Doctor," he said. "That's thanks enough."

He went down the porch steps with Leah beside him. At the bottom he stopped and turned round and looked up at Jacob.

"Jacob," he said.

It was the first time he'd used the name. Jacob blinked.

"You did all right," said Harry. "Last night. With her. You were worried. That's all right. Somebody should've been." He looked at Leah beside him, and back at Jacob. "Just so you know. I'm not taking her anywhere. She's still yours. Your pack. Whatever you call it."

"Beta," said Leah. "I'm his beta."

"Your beta," said Harry. "She's still that. I'm just—" He thought about it. "Extra."

Jacob looked at him. Then he looked at Leah, standing at the bottom of the Cullens' porch steps in the sun in a sweater with somebody else's initial on it, and she looked back at him, and for the first time in the whole year he'd known her as a wolf, she smiled at him. A real one. Crooked. Like her dad's.

"See you, Jake," she said.

"See you, Lee," said Jacob.

Harry held out his hand. Leah took it. And they were gone, with a sound like a book closing, and the lawn was empty except for the steam coming up off the grass, and two dents in it, side by side, where they'd been standing.

Out in the ferns at the edge of the woods, Paul lifted his head off his paws for the first time in an hour, and looked at the empty place, and let out a long, slow breath through his nose.

"Yeah," said Jacob, to nobody. "Me too, man. Me too."

### XIII. Charlie

*Forks Diner — 2:47 p.m.*

He'd been up the Hoh since seven, and he was cold to the bone, and he wanted his pie.

The car had been found last night at closing time by a ranger doing the rounds of the lot at the visitor center: a blue Corolla with Thurston County plates and a dental practice parking permit hanging off the mirror, sitting by itself at the far end of the lot with the rain coming down on it. The ranger had run the plates out of habit. Nothing had come back. Then at six this morning a woman in Olympia had called the Thurston County Sheriff to say her sister hadn't come home from a hike and wasn't answering her phone, and by seven o'clock the Park Service had a search going, and Jefferson County had people up there, and because the Hoh road ran out of Forks and everybody in Forks knew the Hoh road, somebody had called Charlie.

He'd spent seven hours walking the south bank in the rain with a radio on his shoulder and a photograph of Dana Whitcomb in his pocket. Thirty-four. Brown hair. Big smile. She'd had a camera with her, her sister said. She liked to photograph water.

They hadn't found anything. Not a footprint, not a scrap of cloth, not a lens cap. The river was high and brown and loud. It had been raining for three days, and then this morning the sun had come out for the first time in a month and turned everything to steam, and somewhere in all that steam a woman was gone.

He'd seen a lot of searches. He knew what the faces of the Park Service guys looked like by noon. He knew what his own face looked like.

So he wanted his pie.

He came in out of the cold and stood just inside the door and stamped the mud off his boots and took off his hat. Doreen was already pouring his coffee. He went to his stool at the end of the counter. And then, out of habit, because it was what you did, because he'd done it every time he'd walked into a room for twenty-five years, he looked round.

And stopped.

The English photographer was in the back booth.

Evans. Harry Evans. Charlie had run his plates in his second week in town, which was a thing you did with strangers who rented brown Ford Explorers at Sea-Tac and paid cash for everything; it had come back clean as a whistle. He'd been in every day for nine weeks. Same booth. Back to the wall. View of the door and the parking lot and the kitchen pass. Charlie had noticed that the first day, because it was where Charlie would have sat. Friendly guy. Loud. Ate like a horse. Tipped too much. Talked to anybody who'd stand still about the light in the Hoh at seven in the morning.

Charlie had never been able to say what it was about him. Something about the way he came in. The way he sat. The way his eyes went round the room once, every time, just like Charlie's did, and then went quiet. Like a guy who'd already found everything in the room he needed to find.

He was wearing a suit.

A gray one. White shirt. A long black coat hanging on the hook behind him. Charlie had never once seen him in anything but flannel and a waxed jacket. He was sitting in the back booth in a suit, with two slices of blackberry pie in front of him, on two plates, and he was eating one of them.

And sitting across from him, with a cheeseburger in both hands and her mouth full, wearing a pair of men's gray sweatpants with the cuffs rolled up four times and a dark blue sweater with a big letter H knitted on the front, was Leah Clearwater.

Charlie stood there by his stool.

He'd known Leah Clearwater since she was born. He'd been at the hospital in Forks the night she came, because Harry Clearwater had been his best friend since the third grade, and somebody had to keep Harry from passing out in the waiting room. He'd been at her christening. He'd been at Harry's funeral, nine months ago, standing at the back with his hat in his hands, watching her stand at the front with her mother and her brother, nineteen years old, white-faced, not crying, staring at the coffin like she wanted to tear it open with her teeth.

He didn't think he'd seen her smile since.

She was smiling now. She was sitting in the back booth of the Forks Diner with a cheeseburger, laughing at something the Englishman had just said, with her mouth full, with ketchup on her chin. And as Charlie watched, the Englishman reached across the table with his fork, and took one of her fries, and she stabbed the back of his hand with her own fork, not very gently, and he laughed.

Charlie sat down on his stool.

"Hey, Chief," said Doreen. She set his coffee down. "Blackberry?"

"Yeah. Thanks, Doreen." He didn't look away from the booth. "When'd they come in?"

"Twenty minutes ago." Doreen glanced back over her shoulder. "Just *appeared*, I swear. I didn't hear the door. Turned round and there they were in the booth. He says, *Doreen, my love, I believe you owe me two slices.* And I did. I'd saved 'em." She lowered her voice. "Sue Clearwater's girl. Can you believe it? With English."

"No," said Charlie truthfully.

"He's in a *suit*."

"I noticed."

Doreen went to get his pie. Charlie sat with his hands round his coffee and did the math.

He'd been doing math like this for three months now. Ever since the day Jacob Black took his shirt off in Charlie's front yard. He'd got good at it. You took what you knew, and what you'd seen, and you put them side by side, and you looked at the shape they made. And then you decided whether you wanted to know what the shape was.

What he knew: Leah Clearwater was one of them. He'd never been told. He'd never asked. But he'd seen her with Jacob and Seth, and seen the way she moved now, since the spring, the way all the La Push boys moved, and seen her once, in September, walking out of the trees on the Cullens' road at dawn in a pair of cut-offs and a tank top in forty-degree weather, barefoot, with her shoulders steaming. He'd driven past and not slowed down.

What he knew: there'd been a witch at the Cullens' house two weeks ago. A real one. He'd seen her fill the room with lights. He'd watched his granddaughter, who was ten weeks old and talked with her hands, touch one of them and laugh.

What he knew: there was a woman missing up the Hoh, and they hadn't found a single trace of her, and the Englishman who'd been sitting in the back booth for nine weeks talking about the light in the Hoh had never been a photographer. Charlie had known that, somewhere, for weeks. Nobody who looked at a room like that was a photographer.

What he'd seen: that same Englishman, in a suit, eating pie with Harry Clearwater's daughter, who was smiling.

He looked at the shape it made.

Then the Englishman put down his fork, and said something to Leah, and got up out of the booth, and walked across the diner, and sat down on the stool next to Charlie's.

---

"Chief," he said.

"Mr. Evans," said Charlie.

"Potter, actually." The Englishman folded his hands on the counter. "Evans was for the moss."

Charlie looked at him.

Up close, he looked tired. That was the first thing. Charlie hadn't noticed it from across the room. Tired round the eyes, the way men look at the end of a long shift. He had a couple of days of stubble and a nose that had been broken once and set crooked, and his eyes were green, very green, and they were looking at Charlie steadily, with something in them that Charlie couldn't name. Patience, maybe.

"Potter," said Charlie.

"Harry."

"Harry Potter."

"That's me."

Charlie turned his coffee cup round on the counter. "You're not a photographer."

"I take a lot of photographs."

"Of moss."

"Some of them aren't bad," said Harry Potter. "I'll show you some time."

Doreen came and put Charlie's pie down in front of him. She looked at the two of them sitting side by side at the counter, and her eyebrows went up into her hair, and she went away again, slowly, wiping her hands on her apron, and took up a position by the coffee machine where she could pretend she wasn't listening.

Charlie picked up his fork. He didn't eat anything.

"You've been up the Hoh," said Harry.

Charlie looked at him. "How do you know that?"

"Glacial silt on your boots. That gray stuff. You only get it on the Hoh." The Englishman nodded down at Charlie's feet without looking. "And you smell of Park Service coffee. It's the worst coffee in America. I'd know it anywhere."

"There's a woman missing," said Charlie.

"Dana Whitcomb," said Harry.

Charlie went very still.

He sat on his stool with his fork in his hand and didn't move. He felt it go through him, all the way down, cold, the way it had gone through him on his front steps three months ago with Jacob standing in the yard and something enormous where Jacob had been.

"I didn't say her name," he said.

"No," said Harry.

They sat there.

The coffee machine hissed. In the back booth, Leah Clearwater had stopped eating. She was sitting very still with her hands round her glass of Coke, watching them.

Charlie looked at the man beside him for a long time. He looked at his face. He'd looked at a lot of faces across a lot of tables, in twenty-five years. Faces that were lying and faces that were scared and faces that were about to tell you something you didn't want to hear. He knew that last one best. He'd worn it himself, on other people's porches, at two in the morning, with his hat in his hands.

The man beside him was wearing it now.

"You won't find her alive, Chief," Harry said quietly. "I'm sorry."

Charlie put his fork down.

He'd known. He'd known by noon, walking the south bank with the river roaring and the steam coming up off everything. You always knew, by noon. You kept walking anyway. You kept walking because somebody's sister was sitting by a phone in Olympia, and because you might be wrong, and because walking was the only thing you could do.

"The one who did it," he said. His voice came out rough.

"Won't hurt anybody else," said Harry. "Ever."

Charlie looked at him.

"Is that a—" He stopped. He tried again. "Is that a need-to-know thing?"

"It is," said Harry. "Yeah."

Charlie nodded slowly. He looked down at his pie.

"Tomorrow," said Harry, "send your search team up the south bank. Two miles above the visitor center. There's a big Sitka spruce down across the river there, roots in the air, came down in the storm in October. Look on the near side of it, in the rocks below the root ball." He paused. "She'll be there. It'll look like she fell. Slipped on the bank taking a picture, went in, got caught in the roots. Nobody'll find anything they'll need to explain."

Charlie stared at the counter.

"How do you know that?" he said.

The Englishman didn't answer for a moment.

"Her family should be able to bury her," he said.

That was all. Charlie sat there and turned it over. He thought about the sister in Olympia. He thought about the photograph in his pocket, the big smile. He thought about how many searches he'd been on where they never found anything at all, and the families who came back to the trailhead every year on the anniversary and stood there looking at the trees.

He thought about asking again. He thought about it for a long time.

"Okay," said Charlie.

The man beside him let out a breath. Charlie felt it, more than heard it. Like something set down.

"Okay," said Harry.

---

They sat there for a while. Charlie ate some of his pie. It was good. It was always good. He didn't taste much of it.

"You and Leah Clearwater," he said, eventually.

"Me and Leah Clearwater," said Harry.

Charlie glanced back over his shoulder at the booth. Leah was watching them still, with her chin up and her eyes narrowed, the way she'd watched everything since the spring. Daring him.

"Sue know?" said Charlie.

"Sue took a picture," said Leah, from the booth.

Charlie looked at her. Then, despite himself, despite seven hours in the rain and a missing woman and everything, he felt his mouth go up at one corner.

"Course she did," he said.

He turned back to his pie. He took another bite. Then he put his fork down again.

"My daughter," he said. Quietly. To the counter.

He felt the man beside him go still.

"She's safe," said Harry. "Safer than she was yesterday."

Charlie didn't look at him. "And the—" He stopped. He'd never said it out loud, to anyone outside that house. *Granddaughter.* He couldn't make the word come out in the middle of the Forks Diner with Doreen pretending to polish the coffee machine. "The little one."

"Same," said Harry.

"Is that need-to-know too?"

The Englishman thought about it.

"That one," he said, "you can have."

Charlie sat with that for a while. He felt something in his chest, something that had been sitting there tight and cold for three months, since the day he'd held a baby who was somehow his daughter's daughter and watched her put a picture in his head with her hand, go loose. Just a little. Just enough.

"Okay," he said.

He picked his fork back up. He ate his pie.

Harry got up off the stool. He stood there for a second beside Charlie, with one hand on the counter.

"Your daughter gets it from you, by the way," he said.

Charlie looked up. "Gets what?"

Harry looked at him for a moment, with something at the corner of his mouth.

"Never mind," he said. "Need to know."

He went back to the booth. Charlie watched him go. He watched him slide back in across from Leah, and say something to her, low, and watched her say something back, sharp, and watched him laugh. He watched Leah reach across the table and take a forkful of his second slice of pie without asking, and eat it, and close her eyes.

He turned back round. He looked at the coffee machine.

He'd been looking at that coffee machine every day for three months. Every day, over his pie, staring at it, standing at the edge of something very deep and very carefully not looking down. He'd got used to it. He'd thought he'd be doing it for the rest of his life.

He looked at the coffee machine, and found he didn't need to, quite so much.

Doreen came and topped up his cup.

"Well?" she said, in an urgent whisper. "What'd he say? What's going on? Is he really English? Is he *dating* her? Charlie. *Charlie.*"

Charlie took a sip of coffee.

"Need to know, Doreen," he said.

### XIV. Croaker

*Level Nine, the Ministry of Magic, London — Tuesday, November 21st, 11:40 p.m.*

The file had come up from the archive in August.

Potter had come back from Volterra in July and sat in the chair across this desk and said, very quietly, *There's thousands of them, sir. And nobody knows.* Croaker had sat with Potter's report for three days. And on the fourth day he'd gone looking, the way he always went looking, through the card index in the sub-basement that nobody but he and two very old house-elves knew how to read, for anything, anything at all, in four hundred years of the Department's records, that might have been about the same thing and been missed.

He'd found one card. He'd sent for the file himself, on a grey form, in his own hand, and it had taken the elves three weeks to find it.

He took it out of the locked drawer now, on the right-hand side of the desk, and laid it on the blotter under the green lamp. It was thin. Brown card, gone soft at the corners, tied with string. The label on the front was typewritten, in the faded purple of an old ribbon:

OLYMPIC PENINSULA, WASHINGTON STATE, U.S.A.
QUILEUTE — SHAPESHIFTERS (CANINE)
1936
CLOSED

He untied the string.

There wasn't much in it. Eleven field reports, on onionskin paper, in a neat sloping hand that had been taught copperplate at a good school and never quite forgotten it. Unspeakable Edwin Thackery, Grade Four, sent to the Pacific Northwest of America in the spring of 1936 to look into rumours, passed along by a Canadian colleague, of a tribe on the coast whose young men turned into wolves.

He'd found them. The reports said so. Report three: *They are real, sir, and they are nothing like our werewolves. There is no moon in it, no curse, no sickness. They change at will. They are, as far as I can determine, in the very best of health.* Report six: *They hear one another's thoughts when changed. I have no theory to offer for this. I should very much like one.* Report eight, and here the hand got smaller and faster: *There is a family in the area the tribe call the Cold Ones. Pale. Very fast. The tribe say they drink blood, and that the wolves exist in answer to them, and that a treaty has been made this year between them. I have seen one. A doctor. I do not know what to make of it. I enclose a sketch.*

The sketch was still there. A pencil drawing of a man's face, blond, very handsome, with a look on it that Thackery had rendered, rather well, as faintly sad. Under it, in Thackery's hand: *Calls himself Cullen.*

And clipped to report eight, on a slip of Department memo paper, in a hand Croaker recognised, red ink, the hand of the man who'd sat in this chair before him and before the war: *Folklore. Agent has been too long in the field. Disregard.*

Croaker looked at that for a while.

Then he turned to the last sheet in the file. A letter, on cheap American paper, dated the twelfth of October 1936.

*Sir,*

*I shan't be coming back.*

*I find that I am needed here, and, more to the point, that I wish to be. I have married. Her people have a word for what happened to me, the first time I saw her, on the beach at the river mouth in June. I thought it a very fanciful word when I first heard it. I find, on reflection, that it is exactly the right one.*

*I have kept my oath, and shall keep it. She knows nothing of the Department, and never will. I am sorry for that, though I could not tell you why.*

*Please accept my resignation, and my apologies, and my thanks.*

*E. T.*

And under that, in the same red ink, the same hand: *Agent lost to local entanglement. File closed. Do not reopen.*

Croaker sat back in his chair.

He'd read that letter perhaps a dozen times since August. He'd read it the night before he sent Potter, sitting exactly here, under exactly this lamp. He'd read the word *entanglement*, and the word *disregard*, and the phrase *Calls himself Cullen*, and he'd thought about a man who'd found, seventy years ago, the thing that would have saved half a million lives if anyone had listened, and had been called a fantasist for it, and had written one last letter and gone off to be happy on a beach.

He'd thought about who to send.

He picked up his pen. He took a fresh sheet of grey paper off the pile and wrote, in his small upright hand:

*POTTER, H. J. Unspeakable, Grade One.*
*Seconded as Liaison, Quileute Shapeshifters (both packs) and Forks Coven.*
*Status: Embedded.*

He looked at it. He thought about the oath that hadn't fired. About a stone older than England looking at a bond between two people and deciding, without consulting anyone, that they counted as one. About a voice in a mirror at a quarter to six in the morning, saying, without any attempt at all to soften it, *If it ever comes down to the Department or her, it's her. Every time.*

Thackery had said *she knows nothing, and never will,* and *I am sorry for that, though I could not tell you why.*

Croaker had always thought he knew why.

He wrote one more line.

*Local entanglement: confirmed. Do not recall.*

He laid the sheet on top of Thackery's letter, square, and closed the brown card over both of them, and tied the string. He sat for a moment with his hand flat on the cover.

Then he got up.

It was a long way down to the room with the stone. He walked it slowly, as he always did, down the black corridor and through the door with no handle and down the spiral stair that nobody but he had used in eleven years, to the little room at the bottom. No windows. One chair. And in the middle of the floor, on a plinth of plain grey granite, the stone itself. Black, and very cold, and older than anything.

He stood in front of it for a while.

He was the only one left who knew how to ask it. Old Bode had known, and Bode was dead. It was a small thing, the asking. You put your hand on it and you thought a question, very clearly, and if the question was one it had an answer to, it gave you a number.

He put his hand on it.

*How many,* he thought, *are inside?*

The cold came up into his palm. It went up his arm. It sat in the top of his chest under his collarbone, where his own oath had sat for sixty-one years, and it gave him a number.

He knew the number. He'd known it this morning. It was the number of Unspeakables on the Department's books, past and present, living, who had put their hands on this stone and sworn. He kept the roll himself. He'd checked it on Friday.

It was one higher than it had been on Friday.

Saul Croaker stood in the little room at the bottom of the spiral stair with his hand on the oldest thing in the building, and for the first time in a very long while, alone, where nobody in the world could see it, he smiled.

"Right where he needs to be," he said.
