# Chapter One — Disperse

*The clearing, north of Forks, Washington — January 2007*

---

## I. Bella

The mist took its time.

It came off Alec in a low white sheet, ankle-deep, the way fog lifted off the Calawah on cold mornings when Bella was still human and still had to scrape the frost off the truck's windshield with a CD case. It didn't stir the snow. It didn't care about the wind, which came out of the north at their backs, hard and dry, with the smell of the whole forest in it. The mist rolled the other way, straight into the wind, across a hundred yards of white ground between the two lines, and it did not hurry, because it had never needed to.

Bella watched it come.

She didn't need to breathe. She wasn't breathing. She stood at the front of the line with her feet planted in four inches of crusted snow and her hands loose at her sides and every part of her she knew how to reach pushed outward, into the thing she still didn't have a good word for. Edward called it her shield. Eleazar called it a gift. To Bella, after six weeks of practice in this same clearing, it felt like a muscle. A big, strange one, attached to nothing she could see, that she'd learned to stretch the way she'd once learned to stretch her hamstrings before gym class. It ran out from her in a long elastic bubble, over Edward on her left and Jacob on her right, back over Carlisle and Esme, over Emmett and Rosalie, over Kate and Garrett and Tanya, over the Irish and the Egyptians and the Amazons and the two Romanians, who had insisted on standing at the very end where they could see everything. It ran out across the wolves on both flanks, though for them she only had to hold it over Sam and Jacob. Edward had explained it twice: the pack minds ran through their Alphas like water through a pipe. Shield the pipe, and you shielded the whole pack.

So she held the pipe. She held all of it.

The mist reached the edge of the shield at the place where it bulged out in front of Bella's boots, and stopped.

It curled. It lapped at the invisible line like water feeling along the side of a boat, and then it began to spread sideways along the curve, slow, searching. She felt it as pressure, the way she'd feel a hand testing the outside of a closed door. Cold. Patient. Looking for the place where she'd gotten tired.

*No*, she thought at it. *Not today.*

On the far side of the field, Alec tilted his head. He was so young. That was still the part that made her stomach turn over, even now, even with a stomach that didn't do anything anymore. He looked about fourteen, a slim brown-haired boy in a charcoal cloak, with a face somebody had painted on a church ceiling, and he stood at his sister's side with his hands folded in front of him and watched his mist fail to touch anything, and the only change in his face was a small crease between his eyebrows.

Jane didn't bother looking at the mist. Jane was looking at Bella. She had been looking at Bella, steadily, for eleven minutes, and twice in those eleven minutes Bella had felt something sharp and hot slap against the front of the shield and fall away. Jane's mouth had gotten a little thinner each time.

Behind the twins, the rest of the Volturi stood in their long gray rank, the cloaks shading darker toward the center like a bruise. Thirty-two of them. Bella had counted three times. The guard in pale and mid-gray at the edges. Felix, enormous, near the front, his hood down. Demetri beside him. Santiago, Corin, others whose names Edward had murmured to her in the dark the night before, as if knowing their names would help. Renata hovering a half step behind Aro's shoulder, so close she might have been his shadow. At the very back, ringed in by guards in dark gray, two women in cloaks the color of charcoal: the wives. Sulpicia and Athenodora, Carlisle said. Who never left the tower in Volterra. Who had come all this way to watch.

And in the center, in black, the three of them.

Caius, snow-white hair, his face a narrow hatchet of disgust.

Marcus, who looked like he'd been carved out of the same pale stone he'd been sitting on for three thousand years and hadn't noticed when somebody moved him.

And Aro.

Aro was talking.

"—you must see it from where we stand, Carlisle," he was saying. His voice was light and lovely and pitched to carry without any effort, so the forty-some witnesses he'd brought, clustered at the tree line behind his guard in their mismatched coats and furs and city clothes, could hear every word. "I don't doubt your sincerity. I have *never* doubted your sincerity. I have seen inside your son's head, I have held the child's hand, I believe every word of what you tell me about her today. Today." He spread his long white hands. "But what will she be tomorrow? In a year? In ten? We have no precedent. We have no history. We have nothing to tell us what she will grow into, or whether she can be taught our ways, or whether, one day, she will decide the secrecy that has kept every one of us alive for three thousand years is a thing she doesn't care to keep. The unknown, my old friend. The unknown is the danger. And I am afraid my duty to our kind is very clear when it comes to the unknown."

"Aro," Carlisle said. He stood alone in the open ground between the two lines, twenty yards out from Bella, where he'd been standing for nearly an hour. His blond hair moved in the wind. His voice was steady. "You've heard the witnesses. You've seen her. You know—"

"I know that you love her," Aro said gently. "Love makes poor witnesses of us all."

The mist kept spreading. Along the curve, out past Bella's right side. Toward Jacob.

Jacob hadn't moved in forty minutes. He stood beside her in the snow, all of him, the russet wolf as tall at the shoulder as a horse, his breath going out in two long gray plumes. His ears were flat. His lips were pulled just off his teeth. And on his back, sitting up straight in the shaggy fur between his shoulder blades with her small hands buried to the wrist in his ruff and a backpack strapped tight over her blue wool coat, was Renesmee.

Four months old. She looked three. Her bronze curls were tucked under a white knit hat Alice had picked out in the fall. Her cheeks were pink with cold, or with the blood that moved under them, fast and constant, a hummingbird's heartbeat Bella could hear over everything else in the field. She hadn't said a word since Irina.

Bella didn't let herself think about Irina. There was a dark scorch on the snow over by the Volturi line, and above it a thin column of purple-gray smoke still rising, and the smell of it was heavy and sweet and all over everything, like somebody had lit a whole box of incense at once. She didn't think about any of it. She held the shield.

The mist found Jacob's side of the curve and pressed. She pressed back.

"So we come to it," Aro said, softly, regretfully, and turned his head a quarter inch toward his brothers.

Caius smiled.

Bella saw it happen in the guard. She didn't know how else to describe it. A change in thirty-two bodies that stood perfectly still: a settling, a lowering. The way a cat's whole body changes a second before it jumps. Felix rolled his neck. Demetri's weight came forward onto the balls of his feet. Somewhere off on the left flank, a wolf—Paul, it sounded like—began to growl, a low sound that went on and on, and then all of them were doing it, sixteen huge animals rumbling in the snow like a line of idling trucks.

Edward's hand found hers. His fingers were cold and hard and certain.

*Now*, Bella thought. *It's going to be now.*

And then the air in the middle of the field cracked.

It was a flat, ugly, enormous sound, like a rifle shot and a whip at the same time, and it came from nowhere—from the empty white ground fifty yards out, halfway between Carlisle and the Volturi, where a second ago there had been nothing but snow and Alec's mist. The snow jumped. A ring of it blew outward and pattered down. Thirty-two Volturi and twenty-seven vampires on the Cullen side and every wolf in the clearing flinched at the same instant, and Bella felt the flinch go through her shield like a ripple through a sheet.

A man was standing in the mist.

He was looking down at a white styrofoam box in his left hand. In his right hand he held a white plastic fork, and on the fork was a bite of something dark purple, and as Bella watched he put the fork in his mouth and chewed.

Nobody moved.

He swallowed. He looked up.

He was tall. Six-two, maybe, and broad through the shoulders and chest under an old brown leather jacket gone soft and pale at the seams. Under that, a dark gray henley, and under that, Bella could hear it from fifty yards, a heart. Slow and heavy and steady. Fifty-something beats a minute. A human heart, or close to it. He wore jeans and scuffed brown work boots and no hat and no gloves, in a field where the temperature was eleven degrees, and his hair was black and uncombed and going in about four directions. He needed a shave. His eyes, when he raised them and looked first at the Volturi and then, slowly, across at the Cullen line, were a sharp, bright green that Bella could see from where she stood.

The snow around his boots was melting.

She watched it go. A dark ring opening outward in the white, an inch, two inches, the crust sagging into slush and then into wet brown grass, steam lifting off it in thin threads into the cold. The same thing the snow did around the wolves when they lay down for too long.

Alec's mist reached him. It rolled up against his boots, around his ankles, over the wet grass, and Bella waited for him to go blank the way she'd watched it happen in Edward's memories of Volterra: the face slackening, the senses going out one by one like lights in a house.

The man looked down at the mist. Then he lifted one boot and nudged at it, the way you'd nudge a puddle to see how deep it was.

The mist curled away from his boot. He put his foot down. Nothing happened to him.

"Huh," he said.

His voice carried. English, and rough, the consonants clipped short and the vowels flattened out, like somebody who'd grown up south of London and spent a while since then in places where he'd had to raise his voice to be heard over engines.

"Edward," Bella said, without moving her lips.

"I can't hear him." Edward's voice was barely sound. His hand had gone very still in hers. "Bella, I can't hear anything. There's nothing there."

The words went through her strangely. Two years ago, in a biology classroom that smelled like formaldehyde and wet coats, he'd said nearly the same thing about her.

Across the field, the man finished looking at the mist. He looked over at Alec. Then he pointed at him with the plastic fork.

"You," he said. "Pack that in."

Alec's eyes widened very slightly.

The mist kept coming. The man watched it a second longer, and then he sighed, and he didn't do anything at all that Bella could see—didn't move his hands, didn't say a word—but the mist stopped, shuddered, and went out where it stood, like somebody had switched it off at the wall. The pressure on the shield vanished so suddenly that Bella rocked half an inch forward on her heels.

Alec took one step backward. Jane's head snapped around to look at her brother.

"Ta," said the man.

He looked down at his box again. There were two bites left of whatever it was. He considered them. Then, with the whole field watching him in a silence so complete Bella could hear the snow ticking down off the branches a quarter mile back in the trees, he ate them both, one after the other, chewed, swallowed, and closed the lid.

"Marionberry," he said, to nobody in particular. "Diner in town. Bloke behind the counter swore blind it was the best pie in the county." He looked up at the sky like he was thinking about it. "He wasn't wrong. You lot have got no idea what you've got out here."

The box was gone. Bella didn't see it go. One second he was holding it and the next his hand was empty and he was wiping his mouth with the back of his wrist, and the fork was gone too.

"Right," he said. "Hiya. Don't let me interrupt. Actually, no, sorry, that's a lie. Let me interrupt."

He turned in a slow half circle so he could see both lines at once, the Volturi on his left and the Cullens and their witnesses and the wolves on his right. When his eyes went across the Cullen line, Bella felt them go across her. Just a look. She'd gotten used, these last months, to being the most dangerous thing in most rooms, and she felt the look land and move on, and she felt every one of the twenty-six vampires around her feel it too.

"Name's Potter," he said. "Unspeakable, Department of Mysteries, on secondment to the International Confederation of Wizards, which is a mouthful, I know, I didn't pick it. And before anyone asks"—he held up one hand, palm out, toward the Volturi side, where Caius's mouth had just opened—"yes. Wizards. Yes, real. No, I'm not doing a trick for you. I've done one already. Ask the kid."

Nobody laughed. Behind Bella, very quietly, so quietly only vampire ears would have caught it, Emmett said, "Okay."

"So here's the deal," said Potter. "You lot—" he turned his head toward the Volturi "—and you lot—" toward the Cullens "—and the dogs, no offence, lads—" toward the wolves on the left flank, toward the wolves on the right "—have got about a hundred of your kind stood in a field four miles from a town of three thousand people, eleven miles from a stretch of the 101 that gets log trucks every twenty minutes, and you're about to have a punch-up that'd show up on the evening news in Seattle. Which is a breach of the International Statute of Secrecy, 1689, as amended. Which is my problem." He shoved his hands in the pockets of the leather jacket. "So by order of the Confederation, this gathering is dissolved. Everybody disperses. That's the bit where you all go home."

The wind blew. Snow hissed over the crust. In the trees behind the Volturi, one of their witnesses—a woman in a long fur coat—took a step back toward the forest, then stopped, looking at Aro.

Aro was smiling.

It started at the corners of his mouth and spread, slowly, until it filled his whole thin papery face, and his eyes, cloudy red under their milky film, went wide and bright as a child's on Christmas morning.

"Oh," Aro breathed, and stepped forward out of the line, his black cloak dragging through the snow. Renata moved with him like she was tied to him. "Oh, how *wonderful*."

## II. Aro

In the winter of 1609, in a long cold gallery of the Emperor Rudolf's castle in Prague, Aro had taken the hand of an alchemist.

He remembered it perfectly. He remembered everything perfectly; it was one of the few features of his condition he had never once regretted. The gallery had smelled of sulfur and tallow and the Emperor's hounds. The alchemist had been a big red-bearded Saxon, drunk on the Emperor's Tokay, with a length of polished pear-wood tucked up his sleeve that Aro had at first taken for a conductor's baton. Aro had come to Prague because Rudolf collected marvels—clocks and lodestones, a two-headed calf in a jar, astronomers, a stuffed bird from Mauritius that no one in Europe would ever see alive again—and Aro collected the kind of men who collected marvels. He had offered his hand in greeting. The Saxon had taken it, beaming.

And Aro had heard nothing.

He had known silence, before that night. A drunkard's thoughts could be thin. A dying man's could be quiet. This had been something else. The Saxon's mind had been a room with a door in it, and the door had been shut, and when Aro pushed—gently, the way one pushes at a door one expects to swing—the door had pushed back.

The Saxon had let go of his hand. He'd looked at Aro for a long moment with his small bright eyes, and then he'd laughed, and said in German, "Not that one, I think, my lord." And there had been a crack like a pistol shot in the gallery, and the Saxon was gone, and the only proof he had ever stood there was a smell like struck flint and a ring of melted frost on the flagstones where his boots had been.

Aro had hunted for him for eighty years. He'd sent trackers into Bohemia, Saxony, the Low Countries, Scotland. He had found rumors, and the rumors had gone on finding more rumors: men and women with sticks of wood, hidden streets in cities, a kind of parliament that met somewhere no one could name. He had come close, more than once. And then in 1692, all at once, the rumors stopped. Every one. As if somebody, somewhere, had gone through the whole of Europe with a broom.

In three thousand years it was the only collection Aro had ever failed to begin.

And now one of them was standing in the snow in front of him, wiping pie off his mouth.

"Mr. Potter," Aro said. He let the delight into his voice; there was no sense hiding it, and delight made people careless in ways they never noticed. He kept walking until he stood perhaps fifteen yards from the young man, in the trampled snow where Alec's mist had died. Renata stayed at his shoulder. He felt her shield around him, the pleasant pressure of it, like a cloak under his cloak. "You have the advantage of us, I'm afraid. You seem to know who we are."

"Some of you." Potter didn't take his hands out of his jacket. He looked Aro up and down, slowly, with an expression Aro could not read at all, and Aro had been reading faces since before there was a Rome. "You're Aro. Blondie with the face like he's smelt something is Caius. Bloke at the back who looks like he'd rather be dead is Marcus—fair enough, mate, I've read your file, I don't blame you." He nodded past Aro's shoulder. "The little ones are Jane and Alec. Big lad's Felix. Tracker's Demetri. Your missus is somewhere back there in the dark gray, with Caius's. And you've got about thirty more in hoods I haven't bothered learning the names of, because I didn't think I'd need to."

Behind him, Aro heard Caius make a sound in his throat.

"A file," Aro said. "How flattering. And what does it say, this file?"

"It's thick," said Potter. He took one hand out of a pocket and held it up with the thumb and finger a good four inches apart. "Mostly tour groups."

"Tour groups."

"Volterra." Potter put the hand back. "Your Heidi brings them in off the piazza. Forty, fifty at a go. German schoolteachers. Americans with bum bags. A lot of Japanese pensioners, for some reason. They go in for the special tour of the castle and they never come out. Department's been keeping count since about 1960." He shrugged. "It's a big number."

"We must eat," Aro said mildly. "As must you, I think."

"I had pie." Potter's green eyes didn't move off his. "Nobody died."

Aro laughed. He couldn't help it. It was such a small, plain, *rude* answer, and it had been so long since anyone had been rude to him who wasn't about to die. He laughed, and behind him he felt the guard shift uneasily at the sound, because they weren't sure whether it was the kind of laugh that came before a gift or before a killing.

He didn't know himself, yet. That was the delicious part.

He held out his hand.

"Come," he said. "Let me see. I so rarely meet anyone new. Truly new." He let his fingers open in the cold air between them, his palm up, the skin pale and thin and powdery, faintly translucent in the gray light. "I mean you no harm, my young friend. You have my word. A moment, only, and then you will know that, and I will know whatever you choose to—"

"Not a chance."

Aro's hand stayed where it was. "I beg your pardon?"

"I don't know where it's been." Potter tilted his head. "Actually, no. I do. That's the problem." He looked at the hand, then back up at Aro's face. "You've got a lovely voice, Aro. I bet that works on loads of people. My hand stays in my pocket."

"Aro." Caius's voice came from behind him, cold and flat as the field. "This is absurd. You are bandying words with a human—"

"Human-ish," said Potter. "But cheers."

Aro lowered his hand, interested. He let his eyes move over the young man as he had moved them over the alchemist four hundred years ago: the jacket, which was old and cheap and had been mended twice at the left elbow by someone who couldn't sew. The boots, scuffed, wet at the toes. The hands that weren't visible. The heartbeat, very slow, very even. And the heat. Aro could feel it on his face from fifteen yards like the heat off a hearth. The snow around those boots had gone entirely now, down to brown grass and black soil, and the ground was steaming.

Human-ish. Yes.

He did not turn his head. He didn't need to. "Jane, dear," he said quietly.

He watched Potter's face as he said it. He had watched hundreds of faces at this moment. The great ones and the small ones went the same way, in the end, when Jane looked at them: a flicker of puzzlement, and then the eyes going wide, and then the whole body folding down toward the ground as though every nerve in it had been dipped in molten lead. Aro had seen a Hun warlord scream for his mother. He'd seen a woman who'd walked into the sea at Cádiz on a dare in February go down on her knees in the dust.

Potter's face did nothing.

His eyes moved, a little. They moved past Aro's shoulder and found Jane, and held there, and the corner of his mouth went up on one side.

"Is she doing something?" he asked. "Wednesday back there's doing a face at me."

Behind Aro, Jane hissed.

"Love," Potter called past him, raising his voice a notch, "whatever that is, pack it in. You'll give yourself wrinkles." He paused. "Well. You won't. But you know what I mean."

Aro reached back without looking. Jane's hand was in his before he'd finished extending his fingers. Her skin was stiff with fury. He read her in an instant, the whole short sour book of her—the stake in Volterra, the crowd, her brother screaming beside her, a thousand years of watching the strong go down on their knees—and then, at the very top, the last ten seconds. She had pushed. She had pushed everything she had at the young man, again and again, all the burning she had ever carried, and it had gone into him like a lit match dropped into the sea. It had simply gone *in*, and gone *out*, and nothing had come back.

Aro let go of her. He held out his hand again, behind him, and another hand came into it. Chelsea's. He read her the same way. Chelsea had reached for the threads that tied the young man to the world, the threads she could loosen and tie and cut as easily as Aro could read a page—and she had found them. Oh, she had found them, a dense dark tangle of them, people and places and loyalties so thick and old and knotted they were nearly rope. She'd taken hold of one to pull.

It had burned her, somewhere under the skin of her fingers, in a place where nothing had burned since the night she was made. She'd let go with a jerk and had been standing there since with her lips pressed together and her heart, if she'd had one, going like a drum.

Aro let go of her too.

"Demetri," he said. "What do you find?"

"Nothing, master." Demetri's voice was low and tight. "There's no tenor. I can see him in front of me, I can smell him, but if I closed my eyes he would not be there. I could not follow him across this field."

Two, Aro thought. Two in one day. The girl, Bella, with her lovely shield, standing over there in the snow with her chin up and her hand in Edward's, with that precious, unprecedented child riding the wolf at her side. And this.

He had been wrong to think he'd never begin the collection. He'd simply been early.

"Mr. Potter," he said, and he softened his voice, and let it drop, let it become the voice he used in the long gold rooms in Volterra in the evenings, when he was explaining to some brilliant frightened newborn exactly how much he could offer them. "I would like to make you an offer."

"Pass."

"You haven't heard it."

"Don't need to. I've seen the brochure." Potter jerked his chin toward the black ring of ash in the snow, the one with the purple smoke still curling up out of it. "Who was she?"

"A witness. Who bore false witness." Aro spread his hands. "We have laws. As do you, it seems. I'm sure you understand."

"Yeah. Funny you should say that." Potter took a breath, let it out slow, and it smoked in the air in front of him. "Here's my law. You get one more go at this, and then I stop asking. Disperse. All of you. Go back to your castle and your tour groups, and I'll deal with your tour groups some other day, after I've had a sit-down and a cup of tea. Don't disperse, and I'm authorised to make it permanent." He held Aro's eyes. "That's a direct quote, by the way. From a form. I signed it this morning, before the pie. I'm not just being dramatic."

There was a silence in the field.

Aro looked at him. He looked for a long time—a long time for Aro, which was nearly two full seconds—and he did the thing he had done in every room and every field and every candlelit hall for three thousand years, before every decision. He counted.

One man. One heart. One body, hot as a coal and soft as any other body, which could be pulled apart as easily as any other body. A man who could make Alec's mist go out, and walk through Jane, and burn Chelsea's fingers from the inside—but Aro had faced shields before, and the trick with a shield was always and only to stop trying to go through it and go *around* it. Felix did not need a gift to break a man's neck. Felix needed only to reach him.

Thirty-two of the Volturi. The finest guard that had ever been assembled in the history of their kind. They had never lost. Not once, not to the Romanians, not to the southern armies, not to the children. Not ever.

And here was a single young man in a mended jacket, telling Aro of Volterra to go home.

Aro began to laugh again.

This time he let it come all the way up. He tipped his head back and laughed, and clapped his hands together once, softly, and when he was done he shook his head and smiled at Potter with real fondness. He meant it. He had decided in that moment that the young man would live—short a piece or two, perhaps, but living, and in Volterra, which was what mattered. He could be taught. They were always taught, in the end. Eleazar had been taught. Jane had been taught.

"You," he said, "are a *treasure*. Do you know that? And we will speak, you and I, at length, I promise you. I promise you that with all my heart." He touched his own chest. "But you must forgive us. We are in the middle of something rather important, and I'm afraid it can't wait."

And he turned his back on him.

He did it slowly, so everyone could see. So the guard could see, and the witnesses at the tree line, and Carlisle, out there alone in the snow, and the girl, and the wolves. He turned his back on the wizard the way he'd turn his back on a servant who'd finished pouring the wine, and he looked across the field at Carlisle and smiled.

"Now," he said. "Carlisle. Where were we?"

Behind him Caius said, "Felix."

Aro heard the big man go. The snow crunched once under Felix's boots, a sound like a dropped bag of sugar, and then there was only the hiss of his cloak as he crossed the ground faster than any human eye could have followed.

And under that, very small, very dry, almost lost in the wind, Aro heard a different sound.

A snap. Like a twig breaking. Like a man snapping his fingers.

The heat came first.

It came in his chest, under the breastbone, in the place where for three thousand years there had been nothing at all but cold and quiet and the slow slide of venom. A point of heat. A spark. It might have been a memory, for a fraction of a second, a candle flame from some long-ago room—and then the point spread. It went up through his throat and out along his arms and down his legs in a single white bloom, and he looked down and his own hands were lit from inside like paper lanterns, the bones in them black shadows behind the glow.

He had time to turn his head.

He had time to see the field, all of it, in the perfect clarity his kind had always had and always would. Felix in mid-stride, ten yards from the wizard, a man-shaped column of white fire. Demetri, burning. Renata, beside him, burning, her shield doing nothing at all because there was nothing coming *at* them, it was all coming from inside. Jane, her small face finally, finally wide-eyed with fear. Alec reaching for her. Caius with his mouth open on an order he would never finish. The guard in their long gray line going up all together, all thirty-two of them in the same instant, like a row of matches struck by a single hand.

And Marcus.

Marcus, beside him, was turning his face up toward the low gray sky. His eyes were closed. The fire was coming up through his cheeks, through his thin old lips. And on those lips, for the first time since a morning Aro had spent a very long time never thinking about—a sunlit courtyard, a body on the stones, his own sister's name—

Marcus was smiling.

Aro had time to understand that.

He didn't have time for anything else.

## III. Leah

It made a sound like the pilot light on the world's biggest gas stove.

*Whumph.*

That was all. One soft enormous breath of noise, and a light so white Leah's eyes slammed shut on their own, and when she got them open again a heartbeat later, the far side of the clearing was a row of thirty-two white columns standing in the snow like birthday candles. She could see the shapes in them. Arms. Heads. A big one ten yards out from the stranger, frozen mid-stride, the flames streaming off its back the way water streams off a swimmer coming up out of a lake.

Then the columns were gone.

All of them at once, between one blink and the next, and in the place where each of them had stood a soft gray cloud hung in the air for a second at about head height, and then the wind out of the north caught it and pulled it apart and the clouds came down across the snow in long slow curtains, like a second snowfall in the wrong color.

The heat got to her a second later. A wall of it, across a hundred yards of open ground, dry and hard as the air from an opened oven, and the snow in front of the Volturi line flashed to slush in a fan forty yards wide and steamed. Then the smell. Every vampire Leah had ever torn apart and burned in a pile in the woods had smelled like this: sickly and sweet and thick, like somebody had dumped a crate of cheap perfume on a bonfire. Purple-gray smoke rolled up off thirty-two black scorch marks and leaned south toward the trees. Except this time there wasn't any pile to burn. There wasn't anything. The smoke rose off bare ground.

Something small and bright dropped out of the gray cloud where Aro had been and fell into the slush with a hiss. Then another. And another, all along the line, pattering down into the wet snow like coins out of a slot machine.

Gold. Little gold pendants on chains. The crest the guard all wore.

*What*, said Seth in her head. *What. What. What—*

*Holy—* That was Embry. *Holy—holy—*

*They're gone.* Quil, his thought high and stretched like a wire. *They're gone, they're all gone, the whole—Jake, they're—*

*Hold.*

Jacob's voice came down through the middle of all of it, quiet. It ran through the pack mind like a steel rod through wet concrete, and everything in Leah's head that had been spinning stopped spinning and lined up along it.

*Hold the line. Nobody moves. Nobody.*

Leah held.

She was on the right flank, the outside end, with Seth on her left and Embry beyond him and then Quil and then Jacob in the center beside Bella. Her paws were sunk past the first knuckle in the snow. Her hackles were up so high she could feel the wind going through the fur along her spine. Under her, her heart was going like she'd just run the whole way from La Push, and she hadn't moved a foot.

Thirty-two. In one second.

She'd been ready to die today. She'd gotten up at four this morning and stood in her mother's kitchen in the dark and drunk a glass of orange juice standing at the sink, and looked at the photo of her dad on the fridge, and at the Post-it under it in Charlie Swan's square cop handwriting that said *Sue—back Thurs. Don't let Seth eat all the pie*, and she'd thought, very clearly, *okay*. That was all. *Okay.* She'd gone out the back door without waking her mom. She'd phased in the yard.

Now the people she'd been ready to die fighting were smoke and ash and jewelry, and she hadn't so much as bared her teeth at them.

Across the field the Volturi's witnesses broke.

Leah had wondered, all morning, what those forty-odd vampires were for. Window dressing, Edward had said. A crowd for the show. Forty of them in their mismatched coats at the tree line behind the guard, the kind of vampires you'd find drifting through a bus station at night. They'd come to watch the Volturi do what the Volturi did. Now they'd watched something else.

They went without a sound. One second they were standing in a clump at the edge of the trees with the ash still drifting down over their shoulders, and the next the clump came apart in every direction at once, like a flock of starlings when a hawk drops on it. Forty vampires, running full out, crashing back into the timber, branches whipping, snow showering down off the firs in white sheets. Leah heard them for maybe four seconds. Then she didn't.

The stranger watched them go.

"That's the spirit," he said.

He hadn't moved. He was standing exactly where he'd been, hands in the pockets of that beat-up leather jacket, in the middle of his own private little patch of wet brown grass. The ash coming down was landing on his shoulders and in his black hair. He reached up with one hand and brushed some off his collar, the way you'd brush off dandruff, and looked at the gray smear on his fingers and wiped them on his jeans.

Behind Leah's line, on the vampire side, nobody was breathing. Not the Cullens. Not the Irish or the Egyptians or the two tall Amazon women or the Denali crew. Twenty-odd vampires who didn't need to breathe in the first place and had just stopped pretending they did. She could hear the wolves on Sam's side across the field, though, plenty of them, panting and whining. She could hear Bella's daughter's heart, fast and light. She could hear Bella making a small sound in her throat, over and over, that wasn't a word.

And then, at the far right end of the vampire line, close enough to Leah that she could have reached him in two jumps, somebody laughed.

It was one short bark. Garrett. The tall one with the ponytail, Kate's guy, the Revolutionary War vet who'd talked all week about freedom and tyranny in a way that had made Leah want to bite him and also, a little, made her want to listen. He was standing with one hand clamped around Kate's wrist, and his face was white even for a vampire, and he was grinning.

"Well, I'll be damned," Garrett said quietly. "Two hundred and thirty years, I've been waiting for somebody to tell those people no."

Nobody answered him.

Beside him, the two Romanians hadn't moved. They were small and papery and gray, the both of them, the color of old newsprint, and they'd stood at the far end of the line all morning like a pair of crows on a fence. Stefan's mouth was a little open. Vladimir's wasn't. Vladimir was staring across the field at the black ring where Aro had been, and his face—

Leah didn't like his face.

"Right," said the stranger. "That's them sorted."

He turned around.

It was a slow turn. He let it take a second. He swung around on his heel on the wet grass until he was facing the vampire line, facing Carlisle, alone out in the middle of the snow twenty yards closer to him than anybody else, and past Carlisle, the rest of them.

"Now," he said. "You lot."

"Mr. Potter," Carlisle said. Leah would give the doc this: his voice didn't shake. "My name is Carlisle Cullen. I want you to know that we have no quarrel with—"

"Yeah. Hang on, Doc." The stranger held up one finger without looking at him. His eyes were moving down the line. Left to right. Slow. "I'm counting."

His eyes went over Emmett. Over Rosalie. Over Edward and Bella. Over Jacob, and the kid on Jacob's back, and paused there, very briefly, a little crease showing up between his eyebrows. Then on. Quil. Embry. Seth.

Leah.

That was what she'd think about later, over and over, lying awake: how ordinary it was. He looked at her the way he'd looked at the others, flat and quick, a guy counting heads. Green eyes, too bright, with smudges under them like he hadn't slept. Stubble. A spot of gray ash on his cheekbone he'd missed.

And Leah Clearwater's whole life slid six inches to the left and locked into place with a click she felt in her teeth.

She knew what it was. That was the worst part. She knew *exactly* what it was. She'd had it in her head four times now, secondhand. She'd had it in Sam's head first, from the inside, the afternoon he'd looked up across Leah's own mother's kitchen at Leah's cousin Emily, and the memory of that morning had lived in the pack mind ever since like a smell nobody could air out of a room. She'd had it in Jared's head. In Quil's, when he'd looked down at a two-year-old in a diaper and the whole world had rearranged itself around her. In Jacob's, four months ago, in the Cullens' living room, when he'd come in to kill a baby and stopped.

She knew how they described it. She'd heard every one of them try. Gravity, Jacob had said. Like steel cables.

It was cables. That part they'd gotten right. Every one of them she'd ever had—to her mom, to Seth, to the ground under her paws and the pack in her head and the long cold miles of La Push beach she ran when she couldn't sleep—every one of them came loose at once, and swung, and went *taut* again in the same direction, and that direction was a guy in a leather jacket fifty yards away with ash in his hair.

The ground felt different under her feet. She was standing on him. She was standing on the earth because he was standing on it. If he walked away, the earth would go with him, and she'd fall.

*No.*

She heard herself think it, and she heard everyone else hear her think it.

*No. No, no, no. No. Not—NO.*

The pack mind went dead silent. The kind of silence you get in a room right after somebody drops a full tray of glasses.

Then Embry, very small: *Oh, no.*

*Leah*, said Quil.

*Leah?* Seth. Seth, her baby brother, whose voice still cracked when he got scared, even in his head. *Leah, was that—Leah, did you just—*

*No*, Leah said. *Shut up. Shut UP. I didn't. I did NOT—*

*You're kidding me*, said Jacob. He said it almost gently, the way he'd have said it if somebody told him his car had been stolen out of a hospital parking lot while he was inside having a baby. *You're kidding me. Him? Leah, are you serious, right now, HIM?*

She wanted to say something back. She wanted to bite somebody. She wanted to put her head down in the snow and howl until her throat bled.

What she did was see Vladimir move.

She saw it before anybody, before Jacob, before Edward, who heard everything and should have seen it first. She saw it because every single part of her was watching the stranger now, whether she wanted it to or not—watching the air around him, the space behind him, the ground he was standing on—and the second something came into that space with bad intentions, her body knew it the way a hand knows a hot stove.

Vladimir crouched. Just a little. His lip came up off one long tooth. He'd waited fifteen hundred years for this field—Leah had heard him say it, the second night, standing in the Cullens' kitchen in his dusty coat, telling Edward what he'd do to Aro with his own two hands—and some English kid with a pie had walked in out of nowhere and taken it from him in one second. And now the kid was turning around and telling *him* to go home.

Leah saw it all go across that ash-gray face in less time than it took to blink.

Then Vladimir was in the air.

And Leah was already moving.

She didn't decide to. She didn't decide anything. Her back legs bunched and fired, and she was out of the line and across the first ten yards of snow before Jacob's voice even started to form in her head, going flat out at an angle, low, the way she'd go for the legs of a leech in the woods, aiming for the place where Vladimir would come down—

Vladimir lit up.

In the air. Halfway there. One second he was a small gray shape coming down out of a long flat leap with his hands out in front of him like hooks, and the next he was a streak of white fire hanging in the gray sky, and the next he was a puff of gray dust drifting down onto the snow ten feet in front of the stranger's boots.

*Whumph.*

The heat hit Leah in the face. She dug in with all four paws, and her claws tore up two long troughs of snow and dirt, and she skidded and slewed half sideways and fetched up on her haunches in the open, twenty yards from him, breathing so hard her ribs hurt.

The stranger's right hand was out of his pocket. She hadn't seen it come out. As she watched, he put it back.

He hadn't turned his head. He was still looking at the vampire line. At Carlisle. He'd done it without looking.

"Anyone else?" he asked.

Down at the end of the line, Stefan made a sound.

Leah had heard a lot of sounds come out of a lot of vampires in two years. She'd heard them scream when you got your teeth into them, and hiss when you cornered them, and that weird high shriek they made when the fire took them. She'd never heard anything like this. It started low and went up and up and broke in the middle. Stefan went down on his knees in the snow. He put out one hand toward the patch of gray dust ten feet from the stranger's boots, like he could reach it from fifty yards away. Then he stayed like that, kneeling, hand out, not moving.

Nobody else moved either.

Leah saw it go down the line like a wave. Emmett, who'd shifted his weight forward a quarter inch when Vladimir jumped—stopped. Kate, whose hands had started to crackle with that pale blue light she could call up, so bright Leah could see it in daylight—stopped. Garrett's grip on Kate's wrist got tighter. The taller of the two Amazon women had pulled her lips back off her teeth, and she left them there, and didn't come one inch closer. Out in the open, Carlisle had raised one hand toward Vladimir, too late, and he lowered it again very slowly, like a man setting down a glass of something that might spill.

The stranger looked down at the dust in front of his boots. Then over at Leah.

His eyebrows went up.

Leah's heart stopped dead in her chest. Here it was. She was a wolf the size of a horse who'd just charged him out of nowhere, teeth out, flat out, and she knew it, and he'd just shown everybody exactly what happened to people who went at him. Her muscles locked. She waited for the heat.

"You were going for him," the stranger said. "Weren't you? Not me."

He'd seen her angle. In the half second it had taken. With Vladimir in the air and Leah coming in from the side, he'd seen where she was aiming.

Leah didn't move. She couldn't.

He looked at her a second longer. Then he jerked his chin back toward the line of wolves.

"Ta, then," he said. "Back in your box, Lassie."

*LEAH.*

Jacob's voice hit her like a car. This was the Alpha voice, the one that came up out of somewhere older than Jacob, older than any of them, and went down into the middle of her and grabbed. *Get back here. NOW.*

Her body turned around.

She felt it go. Her paws picked themselves up and set themselves down and carried her back across the snow toward the line, and every step of the way the cables in her chest pulled the other way, back toward the stranger, so hard it was like walking into a gale. She made a sound. She didn't mean to. A high thin whine through her nose that she'd have killed anyone else for making. She heard it come out of her and couldn't stop it.

*Leah*, Seth said. *Leah, it's okay, it's okay—*

*It is NOT okay.*

*I know. I know. I mean—Leah—*

*Just stop TALKING.*

She came back into the line between Seth and the trees and stood there, shaking. Seth crowded in against her shoulder, sandy fur against gray, and she let him, which told her more about how bad it was than anything else could have.

In her head, everybody was talking at once.

Quil was already in her memories of the last thirty seconds, looking at the stranger's face through her eyes, the way he'd looked at Claire's face through his own, and his thoughts were full of a sick, sympathetic understanding she didn't want from him. Embry was babbling, *Is it permanent, is it always, does it—Jake, is she gonna—what if he—*

And Jacob. Jacob was furious. She could feel it, a hot red wash under the steel rod of his voice. Furious at her, for breaking the line. Furious at the stranger. Furious at the universe, probably, and at Taha Aki and every wolf back to the first one for the joke of it. And under all of it, under everything, the thing Jacob always had going on under everything now, like a radio left on in another room: Renesmee. The weight of her on his back. Her small cold fingers in his fur. Her heart going fast and light against his spine.

*Of course*, Leah thought. And she didn't care anymore who heard it. *Of course. Sam gets Emily. Jared gets Kim. Quil gets a toddler, Jake gets a—whatever she is. And I get the Terminator.*

Nobody said anything to that.

Out in the open, the stranger had turned his attention away from her. He was looking along the line again, back the way he'd come. Past Seth. Past Embry and Quil. Back to the center, where the big russet wolf stood beside Bella Cullen.

To the little girl on Jacob's back, in her blue coat and her white knit hat, with both hands buried in his fur.

The crease came back between his eyebrows. Deeper.

"Right, then," he said, and his voice had changed. Lower. Flatter. "What the hell are *you*?"

## IV. Edward

For an hour the clearing had been the loudest place Edward had ever stood.

More than a hundred minds. He had never been inside so many at once, all of them turned up as far as they would go, all of them pointed at the same patch of snow. The Volturi witnesses at the tree line, chattering with nerves and appetite. The guard, a hard, disciplined drone under everything, thirty-two minds trained for centuries to want what Aro wanted. Jane's, a single long note of spite. Caius's, a hot red impatience that never once let up. Aro's, bright and quick and curious as a magpie's, darting from Carlisle's face to Bella's to Renesmee's and back, turning everything over, weighing everything, delighting in everything. The wolves' minds, two great roaring rivers of them, one on each flank, with their Alphas at the heads like dams. His family's. His friends'. His daughter's, small and bright and silent at the center of it, thinking in pictures.

Then the sound of a gas jet catching, and thirty-two of those minds stopped.

He'd had no warning. That was what he'd remember. Not a flicker of fear in any of them until the fire was already in them, because none of them had seen it coming either; and then, for the length of one heartbeat, there had been thirty-two voices screaming from the inside at once, an enormous crashing chord of it, and then they had all hit the same wall at the same time and gone out.

Aro's last thought had been about Marcus.

Edward had caught it, the shape of it, before the wall. He didn't know what it meant. He didn't think he wanted to.

Now there were holes in the noise. That was the only way he could describe it. Thirty-two holes all along the far side of the field, where for an hour there had been the drone of the guard and Caius's red buzz and Jane's long thin note, and now there was nothing but wind. The witnesses' minds had fled through the holes like water out of a broken dam, forty of them pouring away into the timber, shrinking, *run run run don't look back don't look don't*, fainter, fainter, gone.

And then Vladimir.

Edward had heard him decide. He had heard every syllable of it—*fifteen hundred years and this child takes it from me and then dares, DARES*—and he had opened his mouth to say the Romanian's name. That was as far as he'd gotten. The Romanian's mind had flared up hot and bright as a struck match, and gone out exactly the same way, and the only thing left of him was dust and Stefan's grief, which was so loud and so wordless it was like standing next to a bell.

Edward hadn't moved fast enough. He'd heard it, and he hadn't moved fast enough, and he knew that if the stranger had decided to burn the rest of them in the next second instead of standing there with his hands in his pockets, Edward wouldn't have moved fast enough for that either.

He couldn't hear the stranger at all.

He'd been trying from the first second. Bella was an absence, smooth and perfect, a place where his gift slid off without catching. Potter was something else. When he reached for Potter, he could feel the shape of something there, something solid and close, like a door in a dark hallway. He could almost feel a lit room behind it. He could hear a kind of low hum, like a refrigerator running in another part of a house.

And when he pushed at the door, the hum stopped, and something on the other side pushed back. Very lightly. The way a man puts a hand flat on a door to let you know he's standing behind it.

Edward stopped pushing.

Around him, the minds of his family and friends were coming back one by one, the way hearing comes back after an explosion.

Carlisle, out in the snow alone—Carlisle was grieving. Edward hadn't expected that. Under the shock and the fear for all of them, there was a picture in Carlisle's mind, clear as a painting: a long, gold, candlelit room in Volterra, three hundred years ago, and Aro in it, younger-seeming than he'd been today, laughing over a book they were both leaning across. *My friend. Oh, my old friend.* And then, right on top of it, cold and level, Carlisle the doctor: *Count. Count everyone. Is everyone alive.*

Esme: *Carlisle, come back. Come back, come back to us, please, come back.*

Emmett: *Don't. Don't do anything stupid. Don't you even—don't.* He was talking to himself. He had wanted to jump, too, when Vladimir did—at Vladimir, to stop him. He'd caught himself at the last instant, and the heat had washed across his face, and now he was holding himself still by brute force, every muscle locked, while Rosalie's hand dug into his arm.

Rosalie had no words at all. Rosalie's whole mind was one image: Renesmee's face.

Eleazar—Eleazar was reaching. He'd been doing it since the first crack of sound. It was what Eleazar's gift did, what it had done for Aro for centuries; he could look at a vampire and feel the shape of the gift inside, the way a blind man feels a face. He was pressing now at the stranger with all of it, and Edward could feel the confusion coming off him in waves. *There's no edge. I can't—it's like reaching for the edge of a lake and finding the sea. Where does it stop? It doesn't stop. Carmen, it doesn't—*

Garrett, beside Kate, was exultant, and terrified, and exultant again. *Two hundred and thirty years. Two hundred and thirty years and some English boy walks out of the air and—Lord God almighty—*

Zafrina was looking at Renesmee too. Zafrina had been with them six weeks, long enough to fall in love with the child the way they all had, and her mind had gone very quiet and very focused. She was gathering something. Edward felt it, the jungle she carried around inside her, green and dense and hot and full of noise, the illusion she could drop over a man's eyes like a hood. She was holding it ready.

And off on the right flank, the wolves.

Jacob's pack had gone to pieces. Edward could hear it from fifty yards, a jangling mess of minds all shouting over one another, and under all of them, so loud it hurt, Leah. He'd heard the imprint happen. He'd heard the cables go. He'd been in Jacob's head when Jacob imprinted, four months ago, at the foot of the stairs in his own house; he knew exactly what it sounded like. This was the same. It was the same thing, landing in the one wolf in either pack who had spent two years telling anyone who'd listen that she would rather be dead.

Leah was not thinking in words at all now. She was thinking in a single direction. Every thought she had pointed at the man in the leather jacket like a compass needle.

"Edward."

Bella's voice—he still couldn't hear her mind—a thread of sound under her breath, for him only.

"I know," he said. "I'm watching."

*Right, then,* the stranger had said. *What the hell are* you*?*

And now he was looking at Renesmee.

Edward felt it land on her, the way a hand lands on a shoulder. On Jacob's back, between his shoulder blades, Renesmee went very still. Her fingers tightened in Jacob's fur. Her mind, which had been a jumble of pictures since Irina—fire, and smoke, and Aro's soft papery fingers on her cheek, and the man in the snow eating pie, and fire again—went white, and narrowed down to one picture.

The man's green eyes. Looking at her.

"Don't," Edward said aloud. He let go of Bella's hand. He took one step sideways, toward Jacob, toward his daughter—

And Bella gasped.

He'd never heard her make that sound. Not as a vampire. As a human she'd gasped at everything, at cold water and loud noises and the sight of him in sunlight, but since she'd woken up in this body she'd never once been startled into it. She jerked. Her whole body jerked the way a person does when a needle goes in, and her hand came up and pressed flat against her own breastbone.

"Edward—" she said, and her voice was wrong. "Something's—it's going *through*—"

And then Edward heard the shield break.

He had never heard her shield. He had no sense for it at all; it was the one thing in the world he'd never been able to perceive, the place where his gift stopped. But he heard it now. He heard it in Renesmee's mind, because the instant it gave, something came into Renesmee's head that had not been there before. Something cold, and fast, and enormous.

That was the first thing he understood, and the worst. When Edward heard a mind, he was listening. He stood outside the window and he heard what was being said in the room. Whatever this was, it had come in through the window. It was in the room. And it had begun to go through the drawers.

Renesmee's memories began to move.

They went backward. Fast. Faster than Edward had ever seen anyone remember anything. The morning—this morning—her mother's face over her in the cottage, the zipper of the backpack, Bella's fingers shaking as they did up the buckle, *keep this on, keep it on no matter what, Jacob will take care of you*. Gone. Next. Last night, Edward himself, sitting on the floor of her room with his back against her crib, humming. Gone. Next. Christmas morning, the tree, Charlie on his knees on the carpet laughing with his whole face while she tore the paper off a box with a stuffed wolf in it—gone. Next. Hunting in the snow with Bella, a hillside, a woman with silver-blond hair far off at the edge of the trees, watching—gone. Rosalie's arms. Rosalie's long hair falling around them like a curtain. Zafrina, showing her a waterfall. Gone, gone, gone. The first time she'd put her hand on Bella's cheek. Jacob. Jacob. Jacob. The smell of his fur. The fire in the big glass room, the first night. Gone.

He skipped past them. That was what made Edward's vision go red at the edges. He flipped through her life the way a man flips through a filing cabinet looking for one folder, and every memory he touched, he touched hard, and pushed aside, and the push left a mark. Edward could feel the marks. Like thumbprints in clay.

And under all of it, under every memory as it was ripped past, Renesmee was screaming.

She hadn't made a sound out loud. She sat rigid on Jacob's back with her eyes wide open and her mouth shut and her small fingers knotted in his fur, and inside her head she was screaming for her mother. *Mama. Mama. Mama. Jacob. Mama. MAMA.*

Edward reached her in less than a second.

He took her off Jacob's back. Her fingers didn't want to let go of the fur; he had to peel them loose, one hand at a time, as gently as he'd ever done anything, and then he had her, small and warm and rigid against his chest, her heart going so fast it was a hum. He turned. He put his back between her and the stranger, and he put his hand over the back of her knit hat and pressed her face into his coat so she couldn't see.

"Doesn't matter where she's looking, mate."

The stranger's voice. Calm. Not even raised. Fifty yards away.

"I'm already in."

"Get *out* of her," Edward said. He heard his own voice come out of him and didn't recognize it. "Get out of her. Get out of her *head*—"

"Working on it."

The memories kept moving. Older now. Earlier. The first week. The first day. The bright white operating light in Carlisle's study, Rosalie's face over her—and Edward, looking down into his daughter's mind, saw his own face for the first time from her side, covered in blood, and saw Bella beneath him, broken, and felt his daughter's very first memory of him, which was the sound of his voice.

And then further back than that.

Dark. Warm. Close. A heartbeat that was not hers, slow and labored and going slower. Voices through a wall. *She likes your voice*, said a voice Edward knew was his own, though it was muffled and far away. *She loves you, Bella.* And Bella's voice, very weak, laughing: *Of course she does.*

He'd forgotten. Months ago, in the worst week of his existence, he'd knelt by the couch in his own house and heard his unborn daughter think for the first time, and what she'd been thinking was that she loved them. He had forgotten that she could remember it too.

The hand in her mind turned the memory over, held it up to the light, and put it down.

Edward felt Zafrina move.

Her jungle went out of her like a thrown net. He felt it go past him across the snow, green and huge and full of screaming birds and rushing water and shadows, and fall over the stranger like a sheet thrown over furniture.

The stranger blinked. He tilted his head slightly, as though he'd heard a song he half recognized.

"Ooh," he said. "That the Amazon? Nice. Not now, love." And the jungle came apart on him like wet paper. Edward felt it tear. He felt Zafrina's shock come back across the field—*nothing, it took nothing, he looked straight through it*—and then her stillness as she understood what she'd done, and that she was still alive, and that he'd simply decided not to bother.

The stranger didn't look at her. He hadn't stopped looking at the place where Renesmee was, behind Edward's back.

"Edward," Bella said. She was holding the shield. He could see it in her face, in the hard set of her jaw and the faraway look in her eyes—whatever was left of it, whatever she could patch over the hole, she was holding it over all of them, over the rest of them, with everything she had. "Edward, I can't—he's *in*, I can't close it, there's a hole, I can't—"

"I know. I know, love."

He couldn't do anything. That was the clear, cold fact of it. He was standing in the snow with his daughter in his arms and her mind being pulled apart drawer by drawer, and the man doing it had just burned thirty-three vampires to ash without taking his hands out of his pockets, and there was not one thing in the world Edward Cullen could do about it.

Jacob could do something.

Edward heard it the instant Renesmee's weight left Jacob's back. Jacob had been holding still for her—only for her. He'd have run, if Edward or Bella had given the word; he'd have taken her and run as far and as fast as four legs could go, and that was the only reason he hadn't moved. Now she was off his back and in Edward's arms, and there was nothing holding him.

Jacob's mind went white. A clean, enormous white, like a sheet of lightning, and there was only one thought anywhere in it.

*Kill him.*

The big russet wolf came off the snow in a single explosive push, straight at the stranger, across fifty yards of open ground.

"Jacob, *no*—"

Edward didn't know if he said it, or Bella did, or both of them. It didn't matter. Jacob was gone, already a dozen yards out, and in the stranger's direction, every mind on the Cullen line lit up with the same picture—a streak of white fire, a puff of red-brown dust, a pair of gold pendants falling into the snow—

And from the right, low and flat and fast, aimed exactly at the place where Jacob's front feet would land, came Leah.

Edward heard her mind for a quarter second before she hit. There was nothing in it at all. No Leah, no words, no fury, no Jacob, no Sam, no two years of bitterness. Nothing but one burning direction, and a russet shape crossing it.

She hit Jacob in the shoulder at a full sprint, and the sound was like two cars meeting at an intersection, and the two wolves went down together in an explosion of snow.

## V. Jacob

Jacob went over twice and came up with his mouth full of snow and gray fur.

He spat it out. He was already scrambling, all four legs going at once, claws ripping down through the crust into the frozen dirt underneath, and Leah was scrambling too, and for a second they were just two big animals thrashing in a hole in the snow, tangled up in each other, neither one with any footing.

Then she was up. She was up first, because she was smaller and faster and always had been, and she got herself square in front of him. Between him and the guy in the leather jacket. Her head was down. Her ears were flat. Her lips were peeled all the way back off her teeth, and she was snarling at him, at *Jacob*, at her Alpha, a sound so low he felt it in his chest more than he heard it.

*Move*, Jacob said.

She didn't move.

*Leah. MOVE.*

*I can't.* It came out of her in a howl. In his head it was a howl; out loud it was still that snarl, steady as an engine. *I can't, Jake, I CAN'T, you don't—you know, you KNOW—*

He knew.

That was the hell of it. He knew exactly. It was all over her mind, every bit of it open to him the way every bit of his was open to her, and he could feel it. The pull. All of her pointed at the man behind her, and Jacob standing in the way of it, and the absolute bottom-of-the-ocean certainty in every cell she had that if Jacob got past her, something would happen to the one person in the world who mattered, and she would rather die. She'd rather die than let it.

He knew that feeling better than he knew his own name. He'd had it for four months. He was having it right now.

Because behind him, in his head, under Leah, under everything, Renesmee was screaming.

His ears gave him nothing from her but her heart, going so fast it was a hum. He couldn't hear her mind either; only Edward could do that. He felt her instead, the same way he always could, every second of the day, the way you feel the sun on your back with your eyes closed. And right now the sun had gone dark and cold and something was wrong with it, something was in it, and every single part of him was trying to get to her and the only way to get to her was through the man who was doing it.

Two imprints. In one pack mind. Pulling in opposite directions as hard as they could pull.

The others couldn't take it.

He felt Seth go. Seth was right in the middle of both of them—his sister on one side, his Alpha on the other, and their two minds hauling on him like a rope in a tug-of-war—and Seth just broke. He sat down in the snow back on the line and put his head down between his front paws and started to whine, high and thin, like a puppy. Quil had taken two steps toward Jacob and stopped, and taken a step toward Leah and stopped, and now he was standing frozen halfway between, ears going back and forth. Embry was saying *Jake, Jake, Jake, Jake*, over and over, like he couldn't think of anything else to say.

And on the other side of the field, the whole other pack was moving.

He saw it out of the corner of his eye. Sam's pack. All eleven of them coming forward off the left flank in a wedge, black Sam at the point with Paul and Jared off his shoulders, the young ones fanned out behind. Jacob couldn't hear them. He hadn't been able to hear them since he'd walked away from Sam last fall. He could see Sam's face, though—his wolf face, his big black head swinging back and forth between Jacob and Leah and the guy in the jacket—and he knew that face. Jacob had seen it from the inside a hundred times. That was Sam Uley trying to figure out what the hell was happening and who he was supposed to be fighting.

And then Sam's eyes landed on Leah. Just for a second.

Jacob saw Sam's whole body change. Saw it slow down. Saw the black wolf's head come up and his ears go back and something go across his face that Jacob had no word for. Sam knew. Sam, of all people, knew exactly what he was looking at.

*Leah*, Jacob said. *I'm sorry.*

He meant it. He'd never meant anything more.

And then he put the Alpha into his voice, all of it, everything that had come down to him from Ephraim Black and Taha Aki and every wolf before them, and said: *Leah. Get down.*

Her legs went out from under her.

He felt the command go into her and take hold. He felt her fight it. She fought it harder than he'd ever felt anybody fight anything; for one long half second her body hung there in the snow, shaking, legs trembling, her whole mind one long howl of *NO*—and then the command won, the way it always won, and she went down on her belly in the snow like someone had cut her strings.

Jacob went past her.

He went past her so close his shoulder brushed her ear. He heard her scream in his head. He didn't stop.

Forty yards. Thirty. He was running flat out now, and the open snow was going by under him in long white streaks, and the man in the leather jacket was getting bigger in front of him—the man who still hadn't looked at him. Still hadn't taken his hands out of his pockets. Was still standing there on his patch of wet brown grass, staring past Jacob's shoulder at the place behind Jacob where Edward was standing with Renesmee in his arms.

Twenty yards. Ten.

Jacob gathered himself to jump. His back legs bunched. He could already feel his teeth closing—

"Sit," said the stranger.

He didn't look at Jacob. He didn't take his hands out of his pockets. He just said it, in the same tone Jacob's dad used to use on the neighbor's dog when it got into the trash.

And Jacob sat.

The sitting was done to him. Something closed around him from every direction at once, around his legs, his ribs, his neck, his tail, every inch of him, like being dropped into wet cement that set in the same instant it touched him. It caught him mid-stride with all his weight going forward and simply stopped him. No jolt. No crash. He didn't even feel the momentum go. It was as if somebody had taken the whole speed out of him and set it aside to deal with later. And then the something lowered him, gently, back onto his haunches in the snow, eight yards in front of the stranger, front legs straight, head up.

Sitting. Like a dog.

He couldn't move. He tried. He tried with everything he had, every muscle in his body all at once, and nothing happened. He could breathe. He could move his eyes. He could feel his own heart slamming against the inside of his ribs. And that was all.

"Good boy," the stranger said.

He still hadn't looked at him.

For a second the pack mind was nothing but noise. Then Leah came through all of it, so loud it hurt, so full of relief that it hit Jacob like a wave of warm water and nearly drowned him. *He didn't—he didn't burn you, he didn't—* And on top of that, a second wave, right behind it, hot and furious and ashamed of itself: *Why do I CARE—*

*Shut up*, Jacob thought at her. *Everybody just—shut up—*

But she was right. He was alive.

He was sitting eight yards from the man who'd lit up Vladimir in midair for jumping at him, and Jacob had done the same exact thing, and he was alive. He'd come at him flat out with his teeth bared. And the guy had made him sit.

Jacob didn't know what that meant. He was too scared to think about what it meant.

Out on the left, Sam's pack had stopped. Sam had stopped first, the instant Jacob sat down, and the others had piled up behind him. Now the stranger's eyes flicked toward them, just for a second—the first time his eyes had moved since he'd started on Renesmee.

"And you can stay where you are, Scooby-Doo," he said. "You and the Mystery Machine. I've got no beef with you lot. Don't give me one."

Sam stayed where he was.

Jacob sat in the snow and stared up at the man who was hurting Renesmee, and couldn't do one single thing about it, and the snow came down, and the ash came down, and he had never in his life hated anyone the way he hated this man right now.

Then the stranger blinked.

It was a long, slow blink, like a guy coming up out of a dive in a swimming pool. His shoulders came down half an inch. He took one hand out of his pocket and scrubbed it down over his face, slowly, forehead to chin, and when it came away his face had changed.

Behind Jacob, very small, Renesmee started to cry.

Jacob had never heard her cry. Not once. Not really. She'd fussed, when she was tiny, the first week. She'd whimpered once when she bumped her head on the coffee table. Renesmee *showed* you things. She put her hand on your face and showed you what was wrong. This was different. This was a kid's crying, the real kind, big and wet and hiccuping, the kind that comes up from the bottom of you and you can't stop. It was the most human sound he'd ever heard her make.

"*Jacob*," she sobbed. "Jacob, Jacob—"

He couldn't turn his head.

He tried. He'd never tried anything harder. His neck didn't move. He sat in the snow facing the stranger, with his back to her, and heard her cry his name, and couldn't turn his head to look at her, and something inside his chest that he hadn't known could break broke.

"She doesn't know," the stranger said.

His voice was different. Rougher. He was looking past Jacob, at Edward, at Renesmee, and the line between his eyebrows had gone so deep it looked like a cut.

"She doesn't know what she is," he said. "She's got no idea."

"Of course she doesn't *know*." Edward's voice. From behind Jacob. Raw and shaking. "She's four months old."

"Yeah." The stranger let out a breath. "Yeah, mate. Got that." He looked down at the snow for a second. Jacob saw his jaw work. "Four months. Christ."

Then he looked up again, past Renesmee.

At Bella.

Jacob couldn't see her. She was behind him somewhere, with Edward, with the kid. But he heard her, when the stranger looked at her. He heard her stop breathing, which she didn't need to do. He heard her feet shift in the snow.

"But you know," the stranger said quietly. "Don't you. You're the mum. You were there for all of it."

"Stay *out* of my head."

Bella's voice. Low, and so cold it didn't sound like Bella.

"No," said the stranger.

And Jacob, sitting frozen in the snow with the ash coming down on his fur, heard Bella Swan scream.

## VI. Bella

He didn't knock.

The first time—when he'd gone for Renesmee—it had come through the shield like a nail through her palm. One sharp, narrow point, driven straight through at the place where the bubble bulged out over Jacob's back, and then a hole, small and burning, that she'd felt every second since. She'd been holding the edges of it together the whole time with everything she had, the way you'd hold a cut closed with your fingers, and the whole time she'd known it wasn't enough. She could feel him on the other side of the hole. She could feel him going through her daughter.

This was different. This time he didn't bother with a point.

He just came.

She felt him gather, from fifty yards away. She didn't know how she felt it. Maybe it was the shield. Maybe it was the hole. It was like standing on a beach and feeling the water pull back around your ankles, all of it at once, before the wave—and she did the only thing she could think of. She pulled the shield in. All of it. Off the witnesses, off the wolves, off Carlisle out in the snow and Rosalie and Emmett and Edward and Renesmee, back, back, back, the whole huge elastic bubble of it collapsing in toward her like a parachute coming down, until it was wrapped around her own head as tight and thick as she could make it, layer on layer on layer.

*Mine*, she thought. *This one's mine. You don't get this one.*

He hit it.

There was no other word. He hit it like a truck hits a wall. Like the van in the school parking lot, two years ago, a lifetime ago, skidding across the ice toward her, except this time there was no Edward to put his hand out and stop it. The shield took the blow and bent. She felt it bend. She felt every layer of it bow inward toward the center of her, toward the soft place behind her eyes where she kept everything, and she *shoved*, she shoved back with all of it, every scrap of the newborn strength she still had in her four months after waking up, and for one long second it held.

Then he leaned on it.

He leaned the way a big man leans his shoulder into a stuck door, slow and steady and heavier than anything she'd ever felt, and the shield bent further, and further, and she heard herself make a noise, and then the shield stopped bending.

It tore.

She screamed. She heard herself do it, from somewhere far away. She'd screamed like that before, on Carlisle's operating table, with her spine snapping and the venom just starting to burn. She'd been sure then that she'd never feel anything as bad again for as long as she lived, and she'd been planning to live forever.

This was worse.

Worse because of where it was happening. Her body she'd given away, gladly, knowingly; her body she'd handed over for Renesmee and she'd do it again tomorrow. The inside was the one place in the world that had always, always been hers—the place Edward had never been able to reach, or Aro, or Jane, the place she'd been so proud of, secretly, ever since that first day in biology when he'd looked at her with those black furious eyes and couldn't hear a word. Her silence. Her own room. And somebody had just kicked the door in and was standing in the middle of it.

And he started to go through her things.

He went fast and he went rough. He started at the beginning and he went forward, and he went through her life the way a cop goes through a drug dealer's apartment—pulling out drawers and turning them upside down and kicking the contents across the floor and moving on.

Forks, in the rain. The airport in Phoenix. Her mother's face, waving. Charlie's cruiser at the curb. The truck—her old truck, the big red Chevy, its engine roaring. The cafeteria. The biology room, the smell of formaldehyde, the empty chair next to hers, and Edward sitting down in it with his black eyes and his hand clenched white on the edge of the table—

*Skip*, said a voice in her head.

It was his voice. English. Flat. Bored. Inside her skull like it had always been there.

The parking lot. The ice. The van. Edward's hand, leaving a dent in the side. *Skip.* Port Angeles. The men in the dark street. *Skip.* The meadow—

The meadow. The sun coming down through the clouds. Edward stepping out of the trees and into the light, and his skin going up like a field of diamonds—

There was a pause. A short, astonished pause.

*He—sparkles?* said the voice in her head. *Are you having a laugh?* And then, with real disgust: *Oh, for—SKIP.*

James. A ballet studio full of mirrors, and her own leg breaking, and the bite on her wrist, and the fire coming up her arm, and Edward's mouth on the wound pulling it back out. The voice didn't say anything about that. It just paused, and looked, and moved on.

Her birthday. A paper cut. Jasper's face. *Skip.*

The woods. Edward walking away from her. The hole in her chest she'd lived with for months. Jacob's garage. The motorcycles. The cliff, the black water. *Skip, skip, skip, Christ, come on—*

And then Italy.

He stopped.

She felt him stop. She felt the whole weight of him in her head come to a halt like a train hitting its brakes. Volterra. The clock tower, the fountain, the sun. Alice's yellow Porsche. The long stone hall, the high windows, the three of them on their thrones—and Aro rising, holding out his white hands. *Bella. Edward. How delightful.*

And then the door at the end of the hall opening, and Heidi coming through it, beautiful, in her short dress and her long cloak, with forty people trailing behind her. Tourists. Fanny packs and cameras and sunburned noses. An old man in a Hawaiian shirt. A woman holding a little boy's hand. Looking around at the stone walls and the high windows, and someone saying *how quaint*—

Bella had spent two years trying not to remember what she'd heard through that door after it closed.

He made her remember it. He held the memory up and he looked at it, all of it, every second, every scream. She felt something come off him then, for the first time. The first thing she'd felt from him at all besides that flat, bored, contemptuous impatience. It came up off him cold and hard and white-hot at the same time, and it was so big it filled her whole head.

She didn't have to wonder what it was. She'd felt it in Edward, when Edward talked about James. She'd felt it in Jacob.

Then it was gone, put away somewhere, and he was moving again. Faster.

The wedding. Alice's flowers, her father's arm. *Skip.* An island, white sand, a white room, the net over the bed—

*Not watching that*, said the voice, and the whole honeymoon went past her in a blur she could barely follow. Feathers. Bruises. *Skip.* A bathroom. A box of tampons, unopened, in her suitcase. Counting the days on her fingers. Standing in front of the mirror in the bathroom of the house on the island, with her shirt pulled up, staring at the small hard curve of her own stomach, and something moving inside it—

He stopped again.

Slower now. He went slower now. He'd found what he was looking for.

The phone, ringing. Carlisle's voice. Edward's face, frozen, horrified. The plane. Rosalie at the door. The couch in the big glass room. Her own body, day after day, going thinner, and thinner, her ribs cracking one at a time, the bruises spreading under her skin in big purple flowers where the baby kicked. The cup. The styrofoam cup with the lid and the straw, and what was in it, and how good it tasted, and Jacob's face across the room watching her drink it.

*Human*, the voice said, very quietly, almost to itself. *She drank human blood. From a cup. While she was carrying.*

Bella fought.

She didn't know how. She'd never done this. Nobody had ever been in here to fight. But she reached for the shield, the torn, ragged pieces of it, and she *pushed*. She gathered every shred of it she could find and she threw it straight at *him*, at the cold heavy thing standing in the middle of her mind turning over her memories—

And for half a second she was somewhere else.

A dark, narrow place. A cupboard, with a sloping ceiling and a bare light bulb and spiders in the corners, and a skinny boy in glasses curled up on a thin mattress, listening to footsteps on the stairs overhead. A flash of green light filling a small room, and a woman's voice screaming. A graveyard at night, with a boy lying on his back in the grass, a boy her own age, looking up at the sky with a surprised expression and no life in his eyes at all. A face, white as a vampire's, with slits for a nose and red eyes. A forest. Somebody walking through a forest in the dark, slowly, toward a clearing full of people in hooded robes, and the walker knew, Bella felt him know, that he was going there to die—

The door slammed.

*Don't*, he said.

It came down on her like a weight. One word. And then she was flat on her back in her own head, pushed out of his so fast and hard she felt it rattle in her teeth, and the thing in her mind was bigger and colder and angrier than it had been before, and the boredom was gone.

It finished. It finished fast and it finished hard. The birth. The snap of her own spine, so loud. Rosalie's face changing, her eyes going black, Jacob dragging her out. Edward's teeth. The blood, everywhere. The cry. A tiny face, covered in blood, with Bella's own brown eyes. *Renesmee*. The needle, the long silver syringe, going into her heart. Edward's mouth at her throat, her wrists, her ankles. The fire. Three days of fire. And waking, and the world in a thousand colors, and a thirst like a hand around her throat.

And at the end of it, as he went, one last thing. Him, reaching, looking for it, one quick deliberate tug: Jacob, at the foot of the stairs, in the big glass room, looking at Renesmee for the first time. Everything in Jacob's face changing at once.

He held that one a second. He turned it over.

Then he was gone.

Bella found herself on her knees in the snow.

She didn't remember going down. She was just there, hands sunk wrist-deep in the white, the cold coming up through her jeans, her head down, and she was making a sound, a long dry sobbing sound with no tears in it, because there weren't going to be any tears ever again. Her whole head felt scraped. Like someone had taken a wire brush to the inside of her skull.

Somebody was kneeling in front of her. Somebody had his hands on her face.

"Bella." Edward. "Bella. Bella, look at me."

She looked at him. Renesmee was against his chest, between them, held in one arm; she'd stopped crying and was hiccuping, her face red and wet, her small hand fisted in the front of Bella's sweater.

Edward's face was white. Whiter than white. His eyes were enormous.

"Bella," he whispered. "I heard you."

She stared at him.

"Just now," he said. "When he—when it broke. For a second. I heard you." His thumbs moved on her cheekbones. They were shaking. "You were saying *not her*. Over and over. *Not her, take me, not her, take me.*"

She'd been thinking it. She hadn't known she was thinking it. She'd been thinking it the whole time.

For two years she'd been the one mind in the world Edward couldn't read. And the first thing he'd ever heard from inside it was that.

"Can you—" Her voice cracked. She tried again. "Can you hear me now?"

He was quiet for a long moment. His face went still, the way it went when he was listening.

"Something," he said finally. "Very faint. No words. Like a radio in another room."

Bella reached for the shield.

It was there. It came when she called it, the way it always had, stretching out from her—slower than before, stiffer, like a muscle after a bad cramp—and she wrapped it around Edward and Renesmee and herself, and then out, and out, until it covered all of them again. But there was a place in it now, right in the center, right over herself, where it wouldn't close all the way. She could feel the place. It was sore, like a bruise you keep pressing to see if it still hurts. A seam. A scar. A spot where something had been torn and grown back wrong.

She didn't know if it would heal. She didn't know if vampires *could* heal, from something like this. She'd never heard of anything like this.

She lifted her head and looked across the snow at him.

He was standing exactly where he'd been. His hands were back in his pockets. He was looking at her, and his face had gone pale under the stubble and the ash, and there was a thin line of sweat at his hairline despite the cold. As she watched, he took one hand out and pressed the heel of it hard against his right temple, and held it there, and closed his eyes for a second.

So it cost him something. Good.

He opened his eyes.

"Right," he said. "Well. That answers that."

"You had no right."

She said it before she knew she was going to. It came out of her in a voice she'd never heard herself use, flat and low and shaking with something that was going to be rage when it was done being shock.

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Lady," he said, "you let a bloke with no pulse knock you up. You drank your way through a blood bank to keep the baby fed. Your husband cut her out of you with his *teeth*. You had a hybrid nobody on earth has ever registered, and you were going to put her on a wolf's back with a rucksack full of fake passports and send her off into the world, and not one of you lot thought to tell a single living soul." He shook his head slowly. "Don't you talk to me about right."

Bella stared at him.

The backpack. He'd seen the backpack. He'd seen everything. The passports, Jenks, the money, the instructions she'd written out for Jacob in her careful handwriting in the middle of the night. All of it. He had all of it.

"Now if you'll excuse me," he said, "I've got to make a phone call."

## VII. Harry

His head hurt.

It was a specific kind of hurt, and Harry knew it well: a hot nail behind the right eye, about an inch in, the kind you got from shoving too hard at something that had shoved back. He pressed the heel of his hand against it and held it there and breathed through his nose and waited for it to go down to a dull throb. It took about ten seconds. He let himself have them.

The mum's shield had been a proper one. He'd give her that.

He'd been through Occlumency walls before. Plenty of them. Death Eaters in holding cells, a Bulgarian smuggler with his whole head bricked up like a cellar, a nice old witch in Lisbon who'd turned out to be not so nice and not so old. Occlumency was a skill. You learnt it, and you built it, and if somebody better at it than you came along, they took it down a brick at a time. Hers was something else. Nobody had built it. It had grown on her, the way a pearl grows on a bit of grit, smooth and thick and all one piece, and when he'd gone at it he'd found one smooth skin with nothing to take apart. He'd had to tear it.

You couldn't tear something like that without leaving a mark. He'd known that going in. The kid had got the small end of it—he'd gone through the mum's shield to reach her, and the mum had taken most of the damage, holding the hole shut around the kid like a hand round a cut. The second time, there'd been nobody to take it for the mum. That one would scar. He'd felt it go.

He'd feel bad about the kid later. He had a list. She'd go on it.

Legilimency hadn't been his idea in the first place. He'd had seventeen years with a bit of Tom Riddle's soul lodged in his head like shrapnel, and when the shrapnel came out in a forest in Scotland, it had left the knack behind, the same way you come home from a long stretch abroad with the language still in your mouth. Snape had never managed to teach him a thing. Turned out what he'd needed was practice, and the Department had given him nine years of that, and these days he could go through most heads faster and harder than Tom ever had, because Tom had never once in his life had to work at anything and Harry had.

He took his hand off his temple. The nail had gone down to a throb. Good enough.

Right. What did he have.

He had a hybrid.

Human mother—bitten on the table the night of the birth, woke up three days later with red eyes. Vampire father. Conceived on an island off the coast of Brazil, born a month later in a doctor's study in a house in the woods outside Forks, Washington, and very nearly killed her mum on the way out. Female. Four months old, presenting as about three. Heartbeat, fast. Runs a bit warm. Eats food. Prefers blood; animal, mostly. Grows like a weed. Talks in full sentences. Has a gift—puts her hand on your face and shows you pictures, the reverse of what Aro used to do—and the gift went straight through her mum's shield like the shield wasn't there, which was interesting all on its own.

What he didn't have was everything else. How big she'd get. How fast. Whether she'd stop. Whether she had venom. Whether she could turn people. Whether she could have kids of her own one day, and what those would be. Whether there were more. The mum didn't know any of it. The father didn't know any of it. Nobody in that whole field knew any of it, and they'd been about to put her on a wolf's back with a rucksack full of cash and forged documents and send her out into the world on her own.

None of it was on any register. He'd read the Volturi file cover to cover on the Portkey over, all four hundred pages. Seven Cullens on the list: Carlisle, Esme, Edward, Rosalie, Emmett, Alice, Jasper. A note in the margin from 2006 about a human girl in Forks the Volturi had flagged for turning or killing. Nothing about a baby. Nothing about anything like her, anywhere, ever, in three hundred years of the Confederation keeping an eye on these people.

Two of the seven weren't here, either. The little dark-haired one and the blond one with the scars. He'd seen them leave in the mum's memories, a note left behind in a book, the mum half convinced they'd run out on her. He'd put a pin in that.

So. Unregistered. Unknown capability. Unknown risk. Sitting in a field four miles from a town of three thousand people with a heartbeat you could hear from fifty yards.

He looked across the snow at the family.

The mum was still on her knees. The father was kneeling in front of her with one hand on her face and the kid tucked against his chest in the other arm. The kid had stopped crying. She was hiccuping now, her face blotchy, and she'd twisted round in her dad's arm to look at Harry over her shoulder, with her mum's brown eyes. She'd gone past being scared of him. She was just looking.

He made himself look back at her until she looked away first. It didn't take long.

Then he turned his head and looked at the wolves.

The big russet one was still sitting where he'd put it, eight yards off. Jacob, the mum's head called him. Jacob Black. A kid, under all that fur. He was staring up at Harry with murder in his eyes, which was fair enough, and every muscle in that enormous body was straining against the hold so hard Harry could feel it like a tug on a fishing line.

He'd put him down gently on purpose. The Quileute were in a different file. A thin one, out of the North American office, with a lot of handwritten notes in it from the 1930s and a stamp on the front that said, more or less, *leave them be*. Nobody in the Confederation had ever quite worked out what the Quileute were. The general view was that they weren't hurting anybody, and that they killed vampires, and that anything that killed vampires in a place like this ought to be left to get on with it.

Plus, they smelled right. That was the bit that wasn't in any file. Under the wet-dog fug and the pine and the cold, every one of those wolves smelled like something Harry's own nose knew from the inside. He wasn't going to think about that right now.

He let go of the wolf.

Jacob came out of the sit like a dropped spring. Harry had half expected him to come straight on; he'd been ready for it, ready to set him down again. But the second the hold was off him, the big wolf spun on his back feet and went the other way, across the snow in three huge bounds to the mum and the father and the little girl, and came down in the snow next to them and put his enormous head down beside her, and she let go of her dad's coat and grabbed his fur in both fists and buried her face in it.

Harry looked away from that.

That was when he saw the grey one.

She'd been forty yards back when he'd gone into the mum's head. Down on her belly in the snow where the big lad had put her with whatever it was he'd said to her—some sort of order; Harry had seen her go down like she'd been shot. She was still down on her belly. She was eight yards back now.

There was a trench in the snow behind her.

Thirty-odd yards of it. A long, low, ragged furrow, ploughed up through the crust down to the black dirt, from the place where she'd dropped to the place where she was lying now. She'd been crawling. The whole time he'd been in the mum's head, she'd been crawling toward him on her belly through the snow, a foot at a time, like a soldier going under wire.

She was looking at him.

He'd seen that look. Twenty seconds ago. In the mum's memory of a lanky dark-haired kid at the foot of a staircase, in a big glass room full of vampires, looking down at a baby. Every part of that kid's face changing at once, rearranging itself around one fixed point, like iron filings when you put a magnet down under the paper.

The mum had a word for it. She'd picked it up from the wolves. *Imprinting.*

Harry closed his eyes.

"Oh," he said, "you have *got* to be kidding me."

When he opened his eyes she was still looking at him. Her ears had come forward. The tip of her tail moved, once, in the snow, and then went still like she'd caught herself doing it and was furious about it.

He knew her name too, now. It was in the mum's head, along with a lot of other things the mum didn't much like about her. Leah. Leah Clearwater, Sue Clearwater's girl. The only girl wolf anybody in that tribe had ever heard of. The mum had a memory of her standing in a kitchen doorway with her arms folded, saying something so sharp it had made the mum's face go hot.

"Leah, is it?" he said.

The grey wolf's ears went flat against her skull.

"Right," said Harry. He pointed at her. "No. Whatever this is—absolutely not. I've got a hybrid, a field full of ash, a vampire on his knees over there who's going to want a word with me in about a hundred years, and a form to fill in for every single one of 'em. I've got no room in my diary for whatever you're doing with your face. So pack it in."

The grey wolf looked at him.

Then, very slowly and very deliberately, without taking her eyes off his, she crawled forward one more foot.

"Son of a *bitch*," said Harry.

Somewhere off to the left, one of the wolves in the other pack made a noise that might have been a cough. Or might not.

He turned his back on her. He needed both hands for this, and he needed his head clear, and he couldn't do either with that look on him.

He took the other hand out of his pocket. He thought about Ron and Hermione at the kitchen table in Grimmauld Place, Ron with jam on his chin, both of them laughing at something he'd said, the morning after it was all over. He'd used the same memory for nine years. It had never let him down.

The stag came up out of the air in front of him.

It came all at once, the way it always did, bright silver and solid-looking and taller at the shoulder than he was, and it stood on top of the snow without making a mark in it. Its breath didn't smoke. Its antlers went up into the gray air like branches in frost. It turned its big head and looked at him with eyes made of light.

Behind him he heard twenty-odd vampires, who did not need to breathe, all breathe in at once.

He didn't look round. He talked to the stag.

"Potter to Confederation duty desk," he said. "Priority one. Pass it straight up to the Director, and copy in the Beings Office and the North American liaison. Location: Forks, Washington, United States, field four miles north of the town, Department's map reference in my morning filing." He took a breath. "Item one. The Volturi declined to disperse. Volturi leadership, guard, and two spouses neutralised. Thirty-two. One local vampire, Romanian, name of Vladimir, attempted assault on the attending officer and was neutralised. The Volturi's witnesses scattered, roughly forty, all directions. I've got scent on most of them if anybody wants it. Volterra's going to be very quiet for a bit. Somebody might want to have a think about who notices."

The stag stood there in the snow, listening.

"Item two." He looked back over his shoulder once, at the little girl with her face buried in the russet wolf's fur, and then back at the stag. "One unregistered hybrid. Vampire-human. Female. Born September last year. Four months old, presents as about three. Rapid growth, rate unknown, end point unknown. Heartbeat. Eats and drinks—food and blood both. Contact-based projective gift, goes through mental shielding. Venom unknown. Reproduction unknown. Mother's a human-turned vampire, father's a vampire, turned 1918. Not on any register. Nothing like her in any file we've got."

He paused. The nail behind his eye gave one last throb.

"She's not hostile," he said. "She's a kid. She's frightened. I've been through her head and her mum's and there's nothing in either of them to say she's ever hurt anybody." He stopped. Then: "Doesn't matter. I need a containment team ready. Full assessment protocol. Beings Office lead. No Hit Wizards—I don't want anybody turning up with their wand out. I want a Healer who knows children and somebody who knows what a vampire is, and I want them here before the family's had time to think about running. I'll hold the site till you arrive. Potter out."

The stag looked at him a second longer. Then it turned, and went up the slope of the field toward the trees in long silent bounds, and before it reached the tree line it simply wasn't there anymore.

Harry let out a breath.

When he turned round, the father was on his feet.

He'd put himself in front of all of them—the mum, still on her knees, the kid, the russet wolf curled round her like a wall of fur. Edward Cullen, turned 1918, a bit of a drama queen, if the mum's memories were anything to go by, though Harry supposed he'd earned it today. His gold eyes had gone nearly black.

"Containment," he said.

"Yep."

"You're not taking her."

"I'm not taking anyone anywhere." Harry put his hands back in his pockets. "Nobody's going in a cell. Nobody's going in a cage. A team's coming, they're going to look at her, they're going to ask your lot a lot of very boring questions, and then a lot of people a lot cleverer than me are going to sit in a room in a building you'll never see and work out what she is and what she needs. Till then, she doesn't leave my sight." He nodded at all of them. "Neither do you."

"And if we refuse?"

From somewhere behind Edward, the blonde one—Rosalie, the mum's head had her down as; the one who'd stood guard over the mum for the whole pregnancy like a dog over a bone—made a sound like a cat about to go for somebody's face. The big dark-haired one had both his arms round her from behind and wasn't letting go.

Harry looked at Edward for a long moment.

"Mate," he said. "You saw what I did to the Addams Family. And Vlad. That's the last thing in the world I want to do to you lot, and it would be a hell of a lot easier for me than it would be for you. So let's not."

Out in the snow, the doctor said, very quietly, "Edward."

Edward didn't move. His jaw worked. He looked down at his daughter. At his wife.

He didn't say anything else.

"Right." Harry turned to look along the rest of the line. The Irish lot, the Egyptians, the Amazon women, the Alaskans, the two couples at the back whose names he hadn't bothered to fish out. The Romanian on his knees. "Everyone else. Everyone not named Cullen and everyone not covered in fur. Disperse. That order still stands. You've got one minute and then I start asking how you all got into the country."

They went.

Not all at once, and not happily. The tall Amazon woman went last of hers, and she went to the kid first and put one long hand on top of the white knit hat and said something to her in a language Harry didn't know, and the kid touched her face back. Then she looked at Harry, one long flat look that told him exactly what she'd do to him if she ever got the chance, and went. The Irish went without a word. The Egyptians went with the old one hauling the young one along by the arm. Two of the Alaskans hung back, the blonde with the sparks in her hands and her ponytailed fella. The ponytail one stopped beside Harry on his way past. He didn't come too close.

"For what it's worth," he said quietly. "Thank you." He jerked his chin at the long black scorched line across the field where the Volturi had stood. "For that. I've wanted to see somebody do that my whole life. Both my lives."

Harry looked at him.

"Don't thank me," he said. "I've got paperwork."

The ponytail one almost smiled. Then he took the blonde one's hand and they were gone.

The Romanian went last. He got up off his knees very slowly, like an old man, and stood looking at the gray smudge in the snow ten feet from Harry's boots where his brother had come down. Then he lifted his head and looked at Harry. Harry had been looked at like that before, by people who'd later tried very hard to kill him. He looked back.

"Go home, Stefan," he said. "Sorry about your brother."

Stefan didn't answer. He turned and went into the trees.

And then it was just them. The Cullens. The two packs of wolves. The doctor out in the middle of the snow with his hands at his sides. The thirty-three black scorch marks and the little gold pendants glinting in the slush. Harry, with his hands in his pockets, and the smell of burnt incense hanging over everything, and the snow coming down.

And the grey wolf, eight yards behind him, who had not dispersed, and who was still on her belly in the snow, and who—he didn't have to turn round to know it—was still looking at him.

He stood there and waited for his team.

He'd been waiting about forty seconds when he heard it.

South. Coming up through the trees from the far side of the field, a long way off yet, a mile, maybe more. Running feet. Vampire feet, the light fast patter they made that most ears never caught. Two sets. Three. Four. Five.

And a heartbeat.

He tilted his head and listened, and he knew that heartbeat before he'd finished hearing it. He'd been hearing one just like it for ten minutes. Fast and light and too quick for anything human, like a bird's. Like the kid's.

Behind him, he heard Edward Cullen go very still, and then make a sound Harry had never heard a vampire make before. Something like a laugh. Something like a sob.

"Alice," Edward whispered.

Harry looked up at the sky. It was low and gray and full of snow, and it didn't have anything useful to say to him.

"Right," he said, to nobody. "We're going to need a bigger boat."
