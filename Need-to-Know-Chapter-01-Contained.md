# Need to Know

## Chapter One: Contained

*Forks and La Push, Washington — October and November 2006*

---

### I. Seth

*Monday, October 2, 2006*

The list was in his mom's handwriting, and the last line was underlined twice.

*Eggs (5 doz.)*
*Ground beef — the big tubes, 4*
*Bread, milk, butter*
*Potatoes (10 lb.)*
*SOMETHING GREEN, SETH. A VEGETABLE. I WILL KNOW.*

Seth Clearwater stood in the produce section of the Forks Thriftway with the list in one hand and a head of broccoli in the other and tried to decide whether broccoli counted, or whether his mom would say it was a tree and send him back.

It was a little after five. The store had that end-of-day feeling, half the checkout lanes dark, a kid in a green apron stacking cans of pumpkin into a pyramid near the front doors because it was October now and somebody at corporate had decided Forks needed a pumpkin pyramid. Rain ticked on the skylights. The fluorescent lights hummed. Somewhere in the back an old radio was playing a Mariners game that had ended an hour ago, the replay, the announcer's voice coming and going through the swinging doors to the stockroom every time the kid went back for more cans.

Seth put the broccoli in the cart. Then, after thinking about it, he put in a second one. If one tree was good, two trees was a forest. You couldn't argue with a forest.

He was tired, the good kind of tired, the kind you got from running all night and spending all day on a couch that was nicer than any couch you'd ever been allowed to sit on, watching a baby learn to laugh. Except Nessie wasn't really a baby anymore. She was three weeks old and she looked like she was going on one, all bronze curls and fat cheeks and those brown eyes that were exactly Bella's old eyes, the human ones. Yesterday she'd put her hand on Seth's face and shown him a picture of himself as a wolf, all big and sandy and goofy, with his tongue hanging out the side of his mouth, and she'd been *laughing* in the picture. Laughing at him. In his own head.

He'd laughed so hard he'd fallen off the couch. Rosalie had looked at him like he was something the cat dragged in. Then she'd looked at Nessie laughing at him, and her face had done the thing it only did for Nessie, and she hadn't said anything.

That was three weeks of his life now. The Cullen house, the couch, the baby, Jacob asleep sitting up with his head tipped back against the wall because he wouldn't leave the room, and patrols. A lot of patrols. Sam's pack ran the reservation and the treaty line and Jacob's pack ran the Cullens, which was three wolves covering a lot of forest, which meant Seth spent his nights running and his days on the couch and his mom spent her days calling his cell phone and asking if he remembered what his own bed looked like.

Hence the list. Hence the forty dollars folded in his back pocket. *You're coming home for dinner,* she'd said on the phone that morning, in the voice that was not a question. *You and your sister both. I'm making meatloaf. I'm making a lot of meatloaf.*

Seth pushed the cart out of produce and down the main aisle, scanning the signs. Bread was at the back. Eggs were at the back. Milk was at the back. Everything good was at the back, so you had to walk past everything else, which his mom said was a trick and Seth thought was probably a pretty good trick, since he'd already thrown in a box of Pop-Tarts that wasn't on the list.

He was passing aisle four, coffee and tea, when he heard a woman swear.

It was quiet, a low, tired, very sincere *son of a bitch*, the kind people say to themselves when they think nobody's around, and Seth turned his head because he was nineteen and the pack hadn't knocked the curiosity out of him yet, and Leah said it never would.

There was a woman halfway down the aisle standing on the bottom shelf.

She had one boot on the metal lip of the shelf and the other one braced against the side of a display of filters, and she was stretching up with her whole arm toward the very top, where there was one last can of something pushed all the way to the back, out of reach. The Thriftway's top shelves were dumb high. Seth's mom always complained about it. *Who are they built for, giants?*

Well. Yeah. Kind of.

"Hey," Seth said. "You need a hand?"

"I need a ladder and a better attitude," the woman said, still reaching. "But I'll take a hand."

He left the cart and walked down the aisle. She hopped off the shelf as he got there and stepped back to give him room, and he reached up over her head without having to stretch and closed his hand around the can. Dark roast. Some brand with a mountain on it that he'd never heard of, the expensive kind with the gold foil on top.

He brought it down. He turned around to hand it to her.

And she looked up at him.

---

Afterward, Seth tried to explain it to Leah, and he couldn't. Leah already knew. Everybody in the pack knew what it looked like from the inside, because they'd all been in Sam's head when he saw Emily, and Jared's, and Paul's, and Quil's, and Jacob's, three weeks ago, when he'd looked at a baby and the whole world had flipped over and landed in a different place. Seth had felt it secondhand five times. He'd thought he knew what it was.

He hadn't known anything.

It was like somebody cut the strings.

Every single thing that held Seth Clearwater to the earth. His mom. Leah. The pack. Jacob. Nessie laughing in his head. The Cullens' couch. The memory of his dad's hand on the back of his neck, which he didn't let himself think about very often because it still did something bad to his chest. All of it. All the strings he hadn't known were there until they went slack, because there was suddenly something else holding him, and the something else was a woman about five foot five with a can of coffee halfway between them, and he could feel the floor tilt under his feet as everything in him rearranged itself around her like iron filings around a magnet.

The strings didn't stay cut. That was the part nobody had ever explained right. They came back, every one of them, his mom and Leah and the pack and the baby. But they were tied to something new now. They were tied *through* her. She was in the middle of all of it and everything else was hanging from her, and that was just how it was going to be, forever, and he knew it the way he knew which way was down.

She had brown skin and a scatter of darker freckles over her nose and dark curly hair piled up on top of her head with a pencil stuck through it to hold it there. Her eyes were gray, plain gray all the way through, like the sky over First Beach in the morning, and there was a scar through her left eyebrow, a thin white line that split the brow into two pieces and kept going a little way up her forehead. She was wearing a canvas jacket that had been somebody else's first, men's cut, sleeves rolled, and hiking boots with the laces wrapped twice around the ankle, and a silver ring on her right hand with a dark blue stone in it.

She smelled like rain on a hot road. Like cedar. And under that, something Seth had never smelled in his life, sharp and clean and a little bit electric, like the air in the second right before lightning hits.

"Thanks," she said. She reached for the coffee.

Seth realized he was still holding it.

He let go. Their fingers touched on the foil.

She jerked her hand back like he'd burned her, and the can dropped, and Seth caught it before it hit the floor without thinking about it, his hand just *there*, and held it out again.

She didn't take it this time. She was staring at him.

"Jesus," she said. "Are you sick?"

"What? No."

"You're burning up."

"Oh." Seth looked at his own hand. "No, I—I run hot. It's a, it's a family thing. Genetic. We all run hot. It's fine, it's like a metabolism thing, I'm totally—" He stopped. He was talking too much. He was always talking too much, but this was a lot, even for him. "I'm Seth," he said.

There was a little line between her eyebrows now, right over her nose. She was looking at him with a very specific kind of attention. He'd seen Carlisle look at Nessie that way, when he was measuring her. Like she was listening to something Seth couldn't hear.

Then the line went away and she took the coffee.

"Layla," she said.

"Hi, Layla."

"Hi, Seth." One corner of her mouth went up. "You can stop smiling at me like that any time."

"Sorry." He didn't stop. He couldn't have stopped if somebody had paid him. "You're new."

"Is it that obvious?"

"It's Forks," Seth said. "Everybody knows everybody. I'd know you."

That came out wrong. That came out really, really wrong. He felt his whole face heat up, and with his face that was saying something, and he opened his mouth to fix it and Layla laughed.

It was a short laugh, surprised out of her, like she hadn't meant to. She put a hand over her mouth. It didn't help.

"Okay," she said through her fingers. "Okay, Seth. Yes. I'm new. I moved in in August. I've been working on the house, mostly, I haven't been into town a lot." She lowered her hand. "Do you work here?"

"No. I'm getting groceries for my mom."

She looked past him, up the aisle, at his cart. At the two heads of broccoli and the box of Pop-Tarts and the empty space where the rest of the list should be.

"Going well?" she said.

"I'm pacing myself."

The kid in the green apron came out of the back with another box of pumpkin and stopped at the head of the aisle and looked at the two of them, Seth standing there grinning like an idiot at a woman holding a can of coffee, and decided he didn't want to be involved, and went away again.

Layla tucked the coffee in the crook of her arm. "Well," she said. "Thank you, Seth. For the reach."

"Anytime." He meant it. He meant it so much it scared him a little bit. "Seriously. Anything on a high shelf. I'm good for it. I'm, like, a professional."

"A professional reacher."

"Certified."

She was laughing at him again, mostly with her eyes. She started to turn, and stopped, and turned back, and that line was between her eyebrows again.

"Can I ask you something weird?" she said.

"Sure."

"Are you—" She stopped. She seemed to change her mind about the question halfway through it. "Never mind. It's nothing. It's a weird question."

"I like weird questions."

"I bet you do." She looked at him a second longer, gray eyes going over his face, his shoulders, his hands. Seth stood there and let her look. He would have stood there for a year. "Nice to meet you, Seth," she said, and walked away up the aisle with her coffee, and turned the corner, and was gone.

Seth stood in aisle four for a long time.

Then he went and found his cart and pushed it in the direction she'd gone, which happened to be the opposite direction from the eggs.

---

He found her at the checkout. She was in the only open lane, unloading a basket onto the belt. Coffee. Eggs, one dozen, brown. A bag of potting soil. A stack of mason jars in a cardboard flat. Three bundles of dried rosemary and a bottle of olive oil and a box of plain white candles, the long kind for dinner tables, and a box of kitchen matches, and a packet of sewing needles.

Seth got in line behind her with his cart. His cart still had two broccolis and a box of Pop-Tarts in it.

Layla looked at the cart. Then at him.

"Hi," Seth said.

"Hi, Seth."

"I just remembered, I need to—I'm almost done."

"Uh huh."

The checker was Mrs. Stanley, Jessica's mom, who knew everybody. She rang up Layla's mason jars and looked over at Seth and said, "Seth Clearwater, does your mother know you're shopping?" and Seth said, "Yes ma'am, she sent me," and Mrs. Stanley said, "With a list?" and Seth said, "Yes ma'am," and held it up, and Mrs. Stanley looked at the list and then at his cart and then at Layla, and something happened behind her eyes that made Seth want to crawl under the gum display.

"Forty-two eighteen, honey," Mrs. Stanley said to Layla.

Layla paid in cash. She picked up two of her bags. There were three bags. Seth was already reaching for the third one.

"I got it," he said.

"I can carry a bag."

"I know. I'll carry it anyway."

She gave him a look. He gave her the bag back. She took it, and stood there holding all three, and then she sighed, very slightly, and held one out to him.

"Fine," she said. "You can carry the jars."

---

It was raining in the parking lot. It was always raining in the parking lot. Layla's truck was an old Ford Bronco, the boxy kind, dark green with a white top that had gone yellow, and a dent in the back bumper, and New Mexico plates. There was a sticker in the rear window from some place called the Santa Fe Dueling Society.

"Dueling?" Seth said.

"Fencing," she said, without missing a beat. "It's like fencing."

She opened the back. He put the jars in, careful, and the bag with the eggs. She put the soil in and closed the hatch and turned around and leaned on it, and the rain was coming down on both of them, and she didn't seem to care.

"Where's the house?" Seth asked.

"Out on the Calawah Way. Past the bridge."

He knew that road. There was only one house out there that anybody had moved into lately. "The old Hollis place? Ruth Hollis?"

Layla's face changed. Something went soft in it, and then careful. "You knew my grandmother?"

"My mom did. She used to buy eggs from her. Mrs. Hollis had all those chickens, and the big garden with the fence, and the—" Seth stopped. He remembered the funeral notice in the paper. April. His mom had cut it out. "I'm sorry. About your grandma."

"Thanks." Layla looked down at her boots. "She left me the house. I didn't know what else to do with it, so I'm living in it." She looked back up. "She used to say Forks was the only place left in America where nobody asked you any questions."

"That's not true at all," Seth said. "Mrs. Stanley's going to tell everybody about you by tomorrow."

"About me?"

"About me carrying your jars."

She laughed again. It went right through him, the same as the first time. He was starting to understand that it was always going to.

"Go get your eggs, Seth," she said, and opened the driver's door. "Your mom's waiting."

"Can I—" He didn't know how to finish it. He didn't know what he was asking for. He knew he would ask for anything.

Layla climbed up into the cab. She sat with the door open, rain dripping off the edge of the roof between them, and looked at him for a long second with that listening look on her face again.

"You can come fix my porch steps," she said. "The bottom one's rotten. I put my foot through it last week."

"I can do that," Seth said. "I'm good at that. I'm really good with—steps."

"Saturday," she said. "If you're not busy."

He was going to be busy. He was going to be on patrol Friday night until four in the morning and on the Cullens' couch until Jacob let him go, and his mom was going to want him home, and Leah was going to want to kill him.

"I'm not busy," Seth said.

She shut the door. The Bronco started on the second try with a sound like a garbage disposal eating a spoon, and she backed out, and gave him a little wave through the rain-streaked window, and drove off toward the highway.

Seth stood in the parking lot with the rain running down the back of his neck.

Then he went back inside and got the eggs.

---

He was forty minutes late for dinner. His mom took one look at his face when he came through the back door with the bags and set down the spatula she was holding.

She didn't say anything at first. She just looked. Sue Clearwater had been married to a man in this tribe for twenty years and buried him last spring. She had a daughter who'd had her heart torn out by the same thing that had just happened to her son. She'd seen this face before, on other people's kids. Seth saw her see it. He saw her decide what to feel about it, and how long it took, which was about three seconds, and what she decided.

"Oh, honey," she said.

"Mom—"

"Who is she?"

"Her name's Layla," Seth said. "She's Ruth Hollis's granddaughter. She—she lives in the house. She has a Bronco. Mom, she has *gray eyes*."

His mom crossed the kitchen and put her arms around him, which meant reaching up, now. She held on for a long time. He could feel her thinking. He could feel her deciding to be happy for him, the way you decide to put your foot down on ice you aren't sure about.

"Did you remember the something green?" she said into his shirt.

"I got two."

"Two what?"

"Broccolis."

"That's my boy."

---

Leah found out that night, on patrol, about two seconds after she phased in, because Seth couldn't think about anything else.

He felt her land in his head like a door kicked open. Felt her see it. Gray eyes, scar, *son of a bitch*, the coffee can, the parking lot, the rain, the laugh. All of it, played back from his memory in the pack mind before he could stop it, the way everything always played back. You didn't get to have secrets as a wolf. Leah of all people knew that better than anyone.

He felt her go very still, somewhere out by the eastern ridge, a mile off.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Oh, you have got to be kidding me.*

*Leah—*

*No. Don't. Just—give me a second.*

He gave her a second. He ran the long loop down toward the river and the Cullens' back lawn and didn't think at anything, as hard as he could, which wasn't very hard, because every time he didn't think at anything his head filled up with gray eyes again.

Leah's mind was a mess. It usually was. Seth had lived in it for half a year now and he'd gotten good at not looking at the worst of it. Sam was in there, always, a sore spot like a tooth that had been pulled and left the nerve behind. Emily was in there. The day Sam came back from the Makah rez with that look on his face, the same look Seth had been wearing all night. All of it was in there and it was all lighting up now, all at once.

And then it went quiet.

It took a while. He felt her push it down, one piece at a time, the way you'd put stuff back in a closet that had fallen out when you opened the door.

*Okay,* she thought finally.

*Okay?*

*She's not from the rez.*

*No.*

*She's not even from here.*

*New Mexico. I think. She has plates.*

A long pause. The rain came down through the canopy and Seth ran under it and listened to his sister breathe a mile away.

*Is she nice?* Leah thought.

Seth stumbled. It was a stupid thing to stumble over, a root he'd jumped a thousand times, and he went down on one shoulder in the ferns and rolled and came up running again, and he didn't care, because she'd *asked*.

*Yeah,* he thought. *Leah, she's—yeah. She's really nice. She's funny. She swore at a shelf.*

*Good,* Leah thought. Then, after a second, quieter, so quiet he almost missed it: *Good. That's good, Seth.*

Then she went back to patrolling, and didn't say anything else for the rest of the night. But Seth felt her the whole time, out on the ridge, running her loops. And she didn't once go back to the closet.


---

### II. Layla

*October into November, 2006*

He fixed the step on Saturday. He fixed the other two steps on Sunday, because once you were in there, he said, you might as well, and the rot had gotten into the stringer, and you couldn't just leave a stringer like that, Layla, it was a *stringer*.

She had no idea what a stringer was. She found out. She found out a lot of things over the next five weeks.

She found out that Seth Clearwater was nineteen, which he told her on the second Saturday, sitting on her new porch steps with a sandwich in each hand, like he was confessing to a crime. She found out that he ate like three people and slept like a dead one, and that if you left him alone on your couch for ten minutes while you went to find a screwdriver you'd come back and find him flat out with one arm hanging off the side and his mouth open. She found out that he never wore a coat. Never. Not in the rain, not when the first hard frost came down the valley in the third week of October and turned every blade of grass in the yard white. He showed up in a T-shirt and cutoffs and no shoes half the time, steaming slightly, like a horse.

She found out that he ran at something like a hundred and eight degrees. She checked. She waited until he was asleep on the couch on a Wednesday afternoon and pointed her wand at the back of his neck from across the room and murmured the diagnostic Gran had taught her when she was eight, the one for fevers, and watched the number come up in silver smoke and dissolve.

A hundred and eight point two. Steady. That was just him.

She found out that he was gone a lot at night, and that he came back tired and hungry and sometimes with pine needles in his hair, and that he lied about where he'd been in the most transparently terrible way she had ever encountered in a grown man. *Out with Jake. Just driving around. Bonfire on the beach.* Three different stories in one week, and none of them matched the mud on his feet.

She found out that he was humming.

That was the first thing she'd noticed, in the coffee aisle, before she'd noticed anything else about him. Before the size of him, or the grin. Layla had spent three years making a living by noticing magic. It was the whole job. You walked into a dead man's vault in the hills outside Chihuahua and you stood very still in the doorway and you *listened*, with something that wasn't your ears, for the hum of whatever he'd left behind to kill you. A good curse-breaker could tell a Gemino trap from a blood ward from forty feet. A great one could tell you who'd cast it, and in what year, and whether they'd been in a hurry.

Layla was very good. Ortega had said so, the day he hired her, and Pike had said so, the day she quit. Pike had also said a lot of other things that day, and Layla had said some things back, and neither of them had been at their best, because Danny Ortega had been in the ground for a week by then, but that one part had been true.

And Seth Clearwater hummed like a power line.

She'd known in the first second that it came from somewhere outside wizardry. Wizard magic had a shape to it, a grain, like wood. You could feel which way it had been cut. Seth's was old and wide and wild and it had no grain at all, it just *was*, the way a river just was, and it lived in him, under his breastbone, and it was enormous.

She'd thought werewolf. Of course she'd thought werewolf, with the heat and the appetite and the nights out. She'd been afraid of it for about a week. Then the full moon came up over the Calawah on the seventh of October and Seth spent the whole of it on her porch with a hammer and a box of three-inch screws, telling her about some guy named Embry who'd eaten a whole rotisserie chicken on a dare, bones and all, and the moon sat up there fat and white over the trees, and he never so much as twitched.

So that ruled out werewolf.

Which left everything else.

---

She found the journal at the end of October.

The house was full of Gran's things. Layla had been working through them since August, one room at a time, the way you'd clear a site. The front rooms were easy. Furniture and dishes and fifty years of *Reader's Digest*. The kitchen was easy. The back bedroom was Gran's workroom and was not easy, because Ruth Hollis had been a witch for eighty-five years and a careful one, and everything in that room had been put away inside something else, under a charm, behind a ward, and half the wards were older than Layla's mother.

Layla had taken them down one at a time. It had taken her six weeks. She'd enjoyed it more than anything she'd done since Chihuahua.

At the very bottom of a cedar chest under the workroom window, under three moth-eaten quilts and a Disillusionment so old it had gone brittle and cracked when she touched it, like the skin on cold milk, was a stack of notebooks. Composition books. The black-and-white marbled kind. Gran had started keeping them in 1931, when she was ten, and stopped in 2005, and there were seventy-four of them.

Layla read the first one sitting on the floor of the workroom with her back against the chest. She read the second one in bed. By the end of the week she had read eleven and her whole body was cold.

*June 1934,* Gran had written, in a ten-year-old's round letters. *Papa says there are COLD ONES living in the big house on the Hoh road now. He says leave them be, they eat deer and not people, he can tell by their eyes. He says the Quileute boys are running as wolves again because of it. I asked can I see a wolf and he said no.*

*August 1934. I SAW ONE. By the river. It was as big as a horse. It looked at me and went away.*

*1936. The cold ones are gone. Papa says they made a treaty with old Ephraim Black and went. The wolves stopped. I am sad about the wolves.*

That was it. Three entries, out of seventy-four books. Gran had written about the cold ones and the wolves the same way she wrote about the weather and the chickens and her father's bad back. Like it was just a thing about the place. Like it was something everybody on the Peninsula would know if they paid attention. And then in the 1940s she'd met Grandpa, who was a No-Maj from Port Angeles who repaired boat engines and never knew what she was, and she'd stopped writing about anything but him for twenty years.

Layla closed the eleventh book and sat in bed with the rain coming down on the roof and thought about it.

Cold ones. She'd looked that up. The Quileute had legends, the library in Port Angeles had a book. Cold ones with hard white skin, who drank blood. And the tribe's warriors, who could turn into wolves to fight them.

Seth was Quileute. Seth ran at a hundred and eight. Seth hummed like a power line and lied about where he went at night.

Layla thought about it for a long time. Then she got out of bed and went downstairs and sat at Gran's kitchen table in her socks and wrote down everything she knew on a legal pad in two columns, the way Danny had taught her to do with a site she didn't understand. *Observed. Inferred.* She didn't sleep. When the sun came up she looked at the two columns, and then she tore the page off the pad and burned it in the sink, because she was a Hollis and that's what you did with paper like that.

Then she waited for him to tell her.

---

He told her on the sixth of November.

It was a Monday. It was raining, which went without saying. He'd come over after dark, which was early now, the light gone by five, and they'd eaten chili on the couch with the TV on and not really watching it, and he'd been quiet. Seth was never quiet. Seth narrated his life the way some people breathed. But that night he sat with the bowl in his lap and stared at the TV without seeing it, and his leg was bouncing, and twice he opened his mouth and closed it again.

Layla put her bowl down on the coffee table.

"Okay," she said. "What?"

He jumped. "What?"

"You've been trying to say something since you came in. Your leg's doing the thing."

He looked down at his leg. He made it stop.

"I need to tell you something," he said. "And you're going to think I'm crazy. And I need you to let me finish before you call anybody."

"Who would I call?"

"I don't know. Somebody. The police."

"Seth, the chief of police is dating your mother."

"I *know*. That's why it's complicated." He put his own bowl down. He turned on the couch to face her and put his hands on his knees and took a breath so deep she watched his whole ribcage go up, and she thought, with a very sudden and total certainty: *Oh. Oh, he's going to do it. He's going to tell me.*

She'd been ready for a week. She'd been ready since the legal pad. And she still felt her heart go hard and fast up into her throat.

"I'm—" Seth said. "There's this thing, with my family, with the tribe, it's like a—it's genetic, it's like a gene, and some of us have it, and when it, when it turns on—"

"Seth."

"Please let me finish."

"You're a wolf," Layla said.

He stopped.

He stared at her. His mouth was still open on the next word. Outside the rain got harder for a second, rattling on the porch roof he'd re-shingled for her two weeks ago, and then eased off.

"What," Seth said.

"You turn into a wolf. A big one. Your tribe's had it for generations. It's what the legends are about, the spirit warriors, the cold ones. It comes when the cold ones come." Her voice was steady. She was proud of that. "That's where you go at night. You're not at bonfires. You're patrolling. There are cold ones here, aren't there? Now. That's why it turned on."

He kept staring.

"How," he said. "How do you—did somebody—did Jake—"

"Nobody told me anything. My grandmother wrote it down." Layla reached over the arm of the couch, where she'd left the book on the side table three days ago, waiting, and held it out to him. The composition book, marbled cover, *1934* on the label in faded pencil. "She saw one of you. When she was ten. Down by the river. She said it was as big as a horse."

Seth took the book like it might go off in his hands. He opened it to where she'd marked the page. He read it. She watched his eyes go back and forth, and go back to the top, and read it again.

"That would've been Ephraim's pack," he said finally. His voice was small. "My great-great-uncle was in it. Or—I think he was my great-great-uncle. My dad would know." He swallowed. "She just wrote it down? Like, in a diary?"

"She wrote everything down."

"And she just. Knew. And didn't tell anybody."

"No."

"*Why?*"

Layla looked at him. At his face, which was open and stunned and scared and something else under that, something that looked a lot like hope, and she thought about the Statute of Secrecy, which was three hundred and fourteen years old and had been drilled into her since before she could hold a wand. Which said, in very plain language, what happened to witches who did what she was about to do.

She thought about Danny Ortega in the vault in Chihuahua, and how fast it had been, and how there hadn't been time to say a single thing he might have wanted to say.

"Because she was keeping a secret of her own," Layla said, "and she knew what it was like."

She held out her hand, palm up, between them.

Her wand was where it always was, in the holster along the inside of her left forearm, under her sleeve. She didn't draw it. She didn't need to, for something this small. Gran had taught her this one before she could read, sitting on the back step, the two of them with their hands held out the same way, cupped, like they were catching rain.

She breathed out, and there was a light in her palm.

It was small. A ball of white and gold about the size of a plum. It sat just above her skin and turned slowly and threw soft moving shadows up the walls and across the ceiling and across Seth's face, and she watched it land on him. She watched his eyes go wide and wider and the color drain out of his face and then come back all at once, hard.

"Oh my God," Seth said.

"Yeah."

"Oh my *God*. Layla."

"Yeah."

"Is that—are you—"

"I'm a witch," Layla said. "So was Gran. So was her father. It's a whole—there's a whole world of us. We're everywhere. We just don't tell anybody." She closed her hand. The light went out between her fingers like she'd caught a firefly. "There's a law about it. A big one. International. I'm breaking it right now. I could go to prison."

Seth looked at her closed hand. Then at her.

Then he started laughing.

It came up out of him like it had been under pressure. He put both hands over his face and laughed into them, his shoulders shaking, and kept laughing, and Layla sat there with her hand closed and her heart going a mile a minute and did not know whether to be hurt or not.

"I'm sorry," he gasped. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, it's just—I've been *freaking out*. For *weeks*. I've been so scared to tell you, I made Jake practice with me, I was gonna phase in your *yard* so you'd believe me, I was gonna take my *clothes off* in your *yard*, Layla—"

That did it. She lost it. She fell back against the arm of the couch laughing so hard it hurt, and he fell over sideways laughing too, and they lay there on opposite ends of Gran's old couch with their feet tangled in the middle and laughed until neither of them could breathe.

When it stopped, it stopped slowly. The TV was still going. Somebody on it was winning a car.

Seth sat up. He was looking at her the way he'd been looking at her since aisle four. Only now there was nothing behind it, no wall, no lie he was holding up. It was just his face.

"There's another thing," he said. "About the wolf stuff. I have to tell you the other thing, too, or it's not fair."

So he told her about imprinting.

He told it badly, and honestly, and all the way through. The gravity. The strings. The coffee can. How it had happened to his Alpha, three weeks before it happened to him, with a *baby*, and how it was only ever whatever the other person needed. How it had happened to his sister's boyfriend years ago, with her cousin, and what it had done to Leah, and how he understood why she hated it. How it meant he would be whatever Layla wanted him to be. A friend. A brother. A guy who fixed her steps. Anything. For as long as she wanted him around. And if she told him to go, he'd go, and it would hurt, but he'd go.

Layla listened to all of it. When he was done she sat for a while.

"So you didn't choose me," she said.

"No. I mean—not at first. No."

"Something chose me for you."

"Yeah." He was looking at his hands. "I know how that sounds."

"It sounds like a curse, Seth."

He flinched. She watched him take it. He didn't argue. He nodded, slowly, like it was fair, and that was the thing that undid her. That he didn't argue.

She'd broken curses for a living. She'd spent three years with her hands inside other people's old magic, the hungry kind, the kind that took away somebody's choices and twisted them around until the person didn't even know they'd had any. She knew what that looked like. She knew how it felt in the air. She'd been listening to Seth for five weeks with that same part of herself, looking for the hook, the twist, the place where something had gotten into him and pulled.

She'd come up empty every time. He was a river. He was just wide and wild and old, and he'd made himself into a guy who fixed stringers and fell asleep on her couch and was too scared for three weeks to tell her the truth, because he thought she'd leave.

"If you could," she said. "If you could take it back. The imprint. If it was gone tomorrow. What would you do?"

Seth looked up.

"I'd come fix your steps," he said.

"They're fixed."

"I'd find something else."

Layla leaned across the couch and took his face in both her hands. It was like holding a stovetop. She didn't let go.

"Then come here," she said, "and stop talking," and kissed him.

---

Later, much later, with the TV off and the rain still going and Seth asleep, she sat up in the dark and slid her wand out of its holster and lay it across her knee and thought about what she'd done.

It was a lot. MACUSA didn't hang people for Statute breaches anymore. They hadn't done that since Rappaport's Law came off the books. But they still sent you to prison for it, and they still sent the Obliviators, and the Obliviators didn't care much about the difference between a No-Maj who'd seen a spell and a nineteen-year-old Quileute boy who turned into a wolf. They'd take the whole night out of his head. They'd take *her* out of his head, as much as they could, if they found out. And he'd wake up one morning and not know why he hurt.

Nobody was going to find out. That was simple. She just wouldn't let them.

But he went out at night. Every night, almost. Into the dark. To find cold ones that drank blood. He'd said there was a whole family of them in a big glass house in the woods east of town, the same family Gran had seen in 1934, the same faces, and they were on the wolves' side now, mostly, it was complicated. But there were other ones too. Ones with red eyes. They came through sometimes. Last year there'd been an army of them, he said. In June.

He said it like it was weather.

Layla got up off the bed without waking him, which wasn't hard, and padded across the room to the dresser, and took out the little sewing kit she'd bought at the Thriftway the day they met. She took out a needle. Then she went and got his cutoffs off the floor, and the length of black leather cord that was always knotted through the belt loop, the one he said he used to tie them to his leg when he—when he did the thing. When he phased.

She sat on the floor in the moonlight with the cord across her lap and her wand in her hand and went to work.

It took her an hour. It was the most careful hour of spellwork she'd done since Chihuahua. Danny had called it a *calling knot*, the Mexican salvage crews used them, a little thing you wove into a bracelet or a bootlace when you were going down into a site and you wanted a way to bring your partner to you fast, no matter what, no matter how deep. It took a strand of your hair and a drop of your blood and a lot of patience. She bound the hair into the cord in a tight three-strand plait about an inch long, and she pricked her thumb and let one drop fall on the plait, and watched it sink in and vanish, and then she spoke the long working over it in Spanish, the way Danny had taught her, and felt it close like a fist.

When it was done there was a bead in the middle of the plait, small and dark and smooth, like a seed. It hadn't been there before.

In the morning, she showed it to him.

"Don't bite it for fun," she said. "Don't bite it because you're bored. Don't bite it because you want me to bring you a sandwich."

"Would you bring me a sandwich?"

"*Seth.*"

"Sorry."

"You bite it if you need me." She tied the cord back through his belt loop herself, and pulled it tight, and held onto it a second longer than she needed to. "Anywhere. Any time. I'll feel it, and I'll come. Wherever you are. You just have to break the seed."

He looked down at the cord. At the bead. He touched it with one finger, very gently, the way Layla had seen him touch the little girl in the photographs on his phone, the one with the bronze curls.

"That's magic," he said. "You made that. With magic."

"Yeah."

"You made it for me."

"Don't make it weird."

He looked up at her, and he was grinning, the whole stupid aisle-four grin, from ear to ear.

"It's already weird," Seth said. "I'm a wolf."


---

### III. Edward

*Saturday, November 11, 2006*

He heard her mind a quarter of a mile down the drive.

The Bronco's engine was loud enough to drown out most of what a human might say inside it, and she wasn't saying anything anyway. Seth was doing the talking. Edward had been hearing Seth for an hour, ever since he'd left La Push, the boy's mind as bright and noisy and transparent as it always was, a radio somebody had left on in an empty room. *Okay okay okay, it's gonna be fine, it's gonna be totally fine, Carlisle's gonna love her, Esme's gonna love her, Rose is gonna be Rose but that's fine, that's normal, Edward's gonna read her and that's gonna be weird, I should've warned her more about Edward—*

Edward, sitting at the piano with his hands resting on the keys and not playing, had allowed himself a small smile at that.

Then the Bronco came around the last bend through the cedars and he heard *her*.

*Seven. No, eight. Eight of them, the boy said eight, he said one of them's new, the girl, Bella, the one who was human until September. And the baby. Who isn't a baby. Who's eight weeks old and walking.* A breath. *God. Okay. Exits. Front door. Glass wall on the river side, the whole back of the house is glass, great, that's great, that's very defensible, Layla. Garage on the left. Windows on the second floor if it comes to that.*

Edward's fingers went still on the keys.

He'd heard minds like it before. Soldiers' minds. Jasper's, in the first years, counting doors in every room he walked into. A mind that had been trained to be afraid in an orderly way.

But there was something under it.

*Protego's not going to do much against strength like that, he said they can throw a car. Stupefy, maybe, if it works on them, nobody knows if it works on them. Incarcerous. Something structural, something to slow them down, the floor, the floor's the play, Reducto the floor and Apparate out while they're falling—*

The words came in a cadence he didn't recognize. Latin's bones, with something else grown over them. And each one came with a picture in her mind, a shape, the memory of a movement, a flick or a slash or a long smooth curve of the wrist, and with the shape a sense of weight, the way a pianist feels the weight of a chord in his hands before he plays it.

*It's fine. It's going to be fine. They're Seth's friends. Seth trusts them. Seth sleeps on their couch.* A pause. *Seth trusts everybody.*

Edward stood up from the piano.

"Carlisle," he said, at a normal volume, which in this house was enough.

His father was upstairs in his study. He was at the head of the stairs before Edward had finished the name. "Is she here?"

"Coming up the drive. She's armed."

Jasper was in the room before the last word was out, from wherever he'd been in the back of the house. Alice was a step behind him with one hand on his arm. Emmett came in from the garage wiping grease off his hands on a rag. Rosalie didn't come in at all. She was already in the room, on the white couch by the glass with Renesmee on her lap, and she didn't look up from the picture book she was reading, but Edward heard her mind sharpen like a knife on a stone.

"Armed with what?" Jasper said.

"I don't—" Edward stopped. He didn't know. He listened. *Wand's on my left arm. Ring's charged. Gran's knife in my boot, for all the good it'll do.* "A wand," he said. "She thinks of it as a wand. And a ring. I don't know what the ring does. It's—charged, she's thinking."

There was a silence in the room.

"A *wand*," Emmett said.

From the kitchen, there was the sound of Jacob Black laughing with his mouth full.

He came out with a sandwich. Leah came out behind him with nothing, arms crossed, and leaned against the frame of the kitchen doorway with the face she always wore in this house, the one that said she was breathing through her mouth and wanted everyone to know it.

"Told you," Jacob said. "Didn't I tell you? I told you you'd want to sit down."

"You said she was *different*," Rosalie said, still not looking up.

"She's different."

"You didn't say *wand*."

"Seth asked me not to." Jacob took another bite. "He wanted to tell you himself. It's a big deal for him. Just—be cool, okay? All of you. Be cool." He looked at Edward. "Especially you. Don't do the thing where you answer stuff she hasn't said yet."

"I'll try," Edward said.

"Don't try. Do it."

Bella came down the stairs. She moved like what she was now, so fast that even Edward only saw the end of it, her bare feet touching the bottom step without a sound. She looked at him first. She always looked at him first. Then she looked at Renesmee on Rosalie's lap, and then at the front door.

"Is it okay?" she said. "Edward. Is she—"

"She's frightened," Edward said. "She's prepared to fight. She doesn't want to." He listened to the Bronco come to a stop on the gravel outside. Listened to the engine die. Listened to the woman inside it sit for a moment with her hands on the wheel, breathing. *Okay. You've walked into worse. You walked into the Mendoza vault with Danny and a busted Revealer. You can walk into a house.* "She's very brave," he said.

---

She was smaller than he'd expected.

Seth came in first, filling the doorway, grinning so wide it looked painful, and stood aside, and she came in behind him. Five foot five in hiking boots. A scar through one eyebrow. She stopped one step inside the door and her eyes went around the room in a single sweep, left to right, the way Edward had heard her plan it in the drive. Front door. Glass wall. Garage. Stairs. Eight vampires, two wolves, one child. She did it in less than a second. She didn't move her head. A human would never have noticed.

Edward noticed. So did Jasper. Edward felt the small flicker of respect go through his brother's mind, and with it, the much larger, colder flicker that came right behind it. *Trained. Combat-trained. Watch her hands.*

"Hi," Seth said, to the room. "Everybody, this is Layla. Layla, this is—everybody."

"Hello, Layla," Esme said, coming forward. She'd been in the kitchen with Jacob. She had flour on her hands, which she'd been careful to leave there, because it made her look human and soft and harmless, and Edward loved her for it. "I'm Esme. We're so glad you came."

*Okay. Okay. The mom. Seth said she cooks for them. She cooks for the wolves. She doesn't eat and she cooks.* "Thank you for having me," Layla said, and her voice came out steady. Edward heard her be surprised by that. "Seth's told me a lot about you. All of you."

"Not too much, I hope," Carlisle said, from the stairs.

*Golden eyes. He said they'd have gold eyes, the ones that eat animals. Gran said the same thing. Papa says leave them be, they eat deer and not people, he can tell by their eyes.* Layla looked up at Carlisle and something happened in her face. "Doctor Cullen," she said. "My grandmother saw you. In 1934."

Every mind in the room stopped.

Carlisle came down the rest of the stairs slowly. At a human pace. He was very good at it, after three and a half centuries. He stopped a few steps away from her, not too close.

"Ruth Hollis," Carlisle said. "Seth said her name. I didn't—" He paused. "There was a girl. On the river road. Dark hair in braids. She used to watch the house from the trees, when we lived on the Hoh. Her father would come and drag her home by the collar."

Layla's chin came up. Her eyes were bright. "She was ten," she said. "She wrote it down. That you ate deer and not people."

"Her father knew that?"

"He could tell by your eyes. That's what she wrote."

Carlisle looked at her for a long moment. Edward heard him going back, in the patient, orderly way his father's mind went back, down the long hallways of his memory to a forest seventy years ago and a little girl in braids peering at him through the ferns. He'd thought she was a curious child. He'd thought her father was a careful man.

"Her father," Carlisle said slowly, "was what you are?"

"Yes."

"And what are you, Layla?"

She looked at Seth. Seth nodded at her. His whole face said *go on, it's okay, I swear it's okay*.

She held out her hand, palm up, and there was a light in it.

---

It went on for two hours.

Edward would remember those two hours with the same perfect clarity he remembered everything, every word and every face, and for the rest of his existence he would find something new in them each time he looked.

He would remember Emmett's face when she lifted the piano.

She didn't touch it. She didn't strain. She drew a slim pale wand out of her left sleeve, about a foot long, and pointed it at the Steinway, and said a word, *Wingardium Leviosa*, and the piano rose a foot off the floor and hung there, perfectly level, as if it were sitting on glass. Emmett walked all the way around it. He crouched and looked under it. He put one finger on the underside of the keyboard and pushed up, gently, and it bobbed like a boat.

"I can do that," Emmett said. "But I have to *touch* it."

"I know," Layla said. She was smiling, a little. It was the first time. "Seth told me. He said you threw a truck once."

"Small truck."

"Still."

He would remember Rosalie. Rosalie, who had sat on the couch for the first twenty minutes with Renesmee on her lap and an expression like marble, listening to Layla explain the Statute of Secrecy—*about three hundred years old, worldwide, every country signed it, the whole point is that nobody without magic is ever supposed to find out we exist, and I'm breaking it right now just standing here*—and saying nothing at all. Until Renesmee squirmed down off her lap, and toddled across the floor on legs that were eight weeks old and looked a year and a half, and stopped in front of Layla, and held up both her hands.

Layla looked down at her.

*What do I—do I pick her up? Seth, what do I—*

"She wants to show you something," Edward said, before he could stop himself.

Layla's head snapped up. Her eyes found him. *How did he—*

Jacob said, "*Dude,*" through a mouthful of sandwich.

"Sorry," Edward said. "I'm sorry. Bad habit."

*He's the one. He's the one who hears. Seth said one of them hears thoughts.* She was staring at him. And then she did something Edward had never seen a human mind do in a hundred and five years. She *closed*.

Not completely. Not well. But something went up in her thoughts, a wall, a crude one, like something a child would build out of blocks, and painted on the front of it in huge red letters as tall as the wall itself was the word **NO**.

He could see over it. It wasn't high. But the effort of it was so plain, and so *practiced*, that it stopped him cold.

"You've done that before," he said.

"Badly," Layla said. Her jaw was tight. "We have a—there's a discipline for it. Occlumency. For when somebody's in your head who shouldn't be. I was never any good at it. I'm better at hitting things." She looked at him with her chin up. "We have people who can read minds, too. We call it Legilimency. It's a skill. You learn it. It takes years and most people can't do it at all." A pause. "Is yours a skill?"

"No," Edward said. "I've just always had it."

*Great,* her mind said, behind the wall. *That's great. That's terrifying.* And then, with real curiosity, peeking up over the top of the blocks: *Can you turn it off?*

"No," Edward said.

"I didn't ask that out loud."

"I know. I'm sorry."

Renesmee was still standing in front of her with her hands up. She'd gotten tired of waiting. She grabbed a handful of Layla's canvas jacket and pulled, insistently, and Layla, without seeming to decide to, crouched down to her level.

Renesmee put her hand flat against Layla's cheek.

Edward watched it land. He watched the picture go into Layla's mind and bloom there, bright as a struck match. It was Renesmee's favorite. Two weeks ago, the first frost, Jacob carrying her out onto the back lawn at dawn wrapped in a blanket, and every blade of grass white, and the river smoking in the cold, and Jacob's breath coming out in clouds and Renesmee reaching up to try to catch them.

Layla gasped.

She didn't pull away. She stayed very still with the child's hand on her face, and Edward watched her mind go *wide*. All of it. The wall went down, the plans, the exits, the careful catalogue of spells, all of it just dropped away like she'd forgotten it was there, and there was nothing in her head at all but frost and smoke and clouds of breath and a little girl's delight.

When Renesmee took her hand away, Layla's eyes were wet.

"That's—" she said. Her voice cracked. "That's Legilimency. Backwards. She *put* it in. She *showed* me." She looked up, past the child, at Bella and Edward. "Do you know how hard that is? Do you have any idea? There are maybe five people alive who can do that on purpose and she's—how old is she?"

"Eight weeks," Bella said.

"*Eight weeks*."

Renesmee, very pleased with herself, patted Layla's cheek twice more for good measure and turned around and looked at Rosalie.

Rosalie looked back. Edward heard her mind make a decision that it had not wanted to make.

"Can you do anything," Rosalie said, "that a child would like?"

Layla stood up. She looked around the room, at the white walls and the white couches and the glass, and Edward heard her thinking about it, a list going by faster than a human could have spoken it. Then she reached out with her wand and touched the sheet music that Edward had left on the piano bench, which was still floating, a foot off the floor, and said something under her breath.

The pages lifted off the bench one by one. They folded themselves as they rose, corners tucking in, creases sharpening, until there were twelve paper birds hanging in the air of the living room. Then they started to fly. They went up into the high ceiling in a loose flock and wheeled there like starlings, swooping and turning, their wings making a soft rustling sound, and their shadows ran across the floor and up the walls and across Renesmee's upturned face.

Renesmee shrieked with joy.

Edward would also remember that. The sound of it. And Rosalie's face, watching the birds over the child's head, and Rosalie's mind saying, very quietly, so quietly he almost didn't hear it, *All right. All right, witch. You can stay.*

And he would remember that it was a Chopin nocturne. The paper. Opus 48. He'd been working on an arrangement of it for Bella for three weeks and he hadn't made a copy.

He decided it didn't matter.

---

"So how many of you are there," Jasper said.

It was later. The birds had come down and been unfolded and returned to the piano with a flick of the wand, every crease smoothed out, and Edward had checked, and every note was still there. Renesmee had fallen asleep on Jacob's chest on the floor by the fire. Esme had made coffee, because Layla drank coffee, and the whole house smelled of it, rich and dark and almost pleasant. Layla sat on the end of the white couch with Seth beside her, his arm along the back behind her shoulders, and everyone else had arranged themselves around the room in a loose circle that they all knew, without saying, was a circle.

Jasper sat in it like a soldier sits at a briefing. He'd been very quiet. Edward had heard all of it in his mind anyway, the whole time, under everything. The calculations. *Wand. Range? Unknown. Shield, she mentioned a shield. Mind defense, poor. Mind attack, others can. How many? How organized? Government?*

Layla held her coffee in both hands. "Worldwide? I don't know. Nobody knows exactly. A lot. In the States, we have—tens of thousands. Maybe more. There's a government, MACUSA, the Magical Congress. There's a school, up in Massachusetts. There are communities in every big city. Hidden. You'd walk past them every day."

"And they don't know about us," Jasper said.

"No."

"You're certain."

"I'd never heard of you." She turned her cup in her hands. "I mean. We have vampires. Our world does. We've always had them. They're—" She hesitated. *Don't be rude, Layla.* "They're different. They're pale and they sleep in the day and they drink blood, sure, but they eat, too, they can eat regular food, and the sun doesn't make them—" a glance at Edward, at his hands, where the light from the fire was catching very faintly on his skin, "—sparkle. It burns them. They're slow. They're kind of sad, honestly. There's a guy in England who's famous for going to parties. We have laws about them. They're listed. Classified. They have rights, kind of." She looked around the circle. "You're not on any list I've ever seen. Gran never said a word. If my grandmother hadn't written that diary I'd have walked past this house for the rest of my life."

Carlisle, in the armchair by the fire, had his hands steepled in front of his mouth.

"My father," he said, "hunted witches."

Layla's eyes went to him.

"In London. In the 1640s. He was an Anglican pastor and he believed—passionately—that there were witches and werewolves and vampires walking among the faithful, doing the devil's work. He led mobs. He burned people." Carlisle's voice was calm. His mind was not. Edward could see the faces, the old faces, under it, women with their hands bound and their mouths open. "I never believed he found a single real one. I was sure they were all innocent. Poor women, odd women. Widows. Herbalists. I carried that for three hundred and sixty years." He lowered his hands. "Was I wrong?"

Layla was quiet for a moment.

"Some of them," she said. "Maybe. Some of us. It was bad, back then. That's why we hid. That's the whole reason for the Statute." She paused. "But mostly they burned the wrong people. We teach it in school. Witches could stop the fire, most of the time. A Flame-Freezing Charm. There was one woman who let them burn her forty-seven times because she liked how it tickled." A small, crooked smile. "It was mostly the No-Majs who died. Muggles, the British call them. The ones with no magic at all. Your father mostly burned his neighbors."

Carlisle closed his eyes.

Edward heard him put the old faces away, one at a time, very carefully, and he didn't know whether the thing his father felt was better or worse. He suspected Carlisle didn't either.

"What does she mean, *she liked how it tickled*," Emmett said, to Rose, in a whisper that everyone heard.

"Emmett."

"I'm just asking."

Leah spoke from the kitchen doorway. She hadn't said a word in two hours. She hadn't moved from the frame.

"What else," she said.

Layla turned her head.

"What else is real." Leah's arms were still crossed. Her face was flat. Her mind was not flat at all. It was a storm, turned down low, the way Leah's mind always was. Under the storm there was something Edward almost never heard in her, and it took him a moment to recognize it. *Seth. Seth's in this now. Seth's in this up to his neck. Whatever she's part of, whatever's out there, he's in it. I want to know what's out there.* "Vampires. Wolves. Witches. What else."

Layla thought about it. Edward watched her go through the list.

"Dragons," she said.

Emmett made a sound Edward had never heard come out of him.

"Giants. Trolls. Goblins, they run the banks. Unicorns, but they're mostly in Europe. Merpeople, but they don't like us much." She shrugged. "There's a thing in South America that looks like a cat and can kill you by breathing on you. There's a lot."

"*Dragons,*" Emmett said again, to Rosalie. "Rose. *Dragons.*"

"I heard her."

"Can I fight one?"

"No," said seven people at once.

Leah didn't laugh. She looked at Layla for a long moment, and then at her brother, sitting there with his arm around a witch and his face full of sun, and Edward heard her decide something. It was an unhappy decision. It was still a decision.

*Fine,* Leah's mind said. *Fine. Dragons. Whatever. You hurt him and I'll find out if you burn.*

She turned and went back into the kitchen. A moment later the back door opened and closed, and Edward heard her cross the lawn toward the trees, and heard her mind go loose and wide and then vanish from his range entirely as she reached the edge of the forest and phased.

---

The rest came quickly after that, the way such things do once the first hard part is over. Jacob asked if magic could hurt Renesmee, and Layla said *I would never*, and meant it so completely that Edward told Jacob so without being asked. Bella asked about the Statute, how much trouble Layla was in, really, and Layla made a face and said *enough* and changed the subject. Esme asked if she was hungry, and she was, and Esme made her a sandwich that would have fed two of her.

Jasper didn't ask anything else. But Edward heard him filing it. All of it. The school, the government, the shield, the mind discipline, the people who could read thoughts the way Edward did, but on purpose. Who had to *learn* it. Who could learn it.

*If the Volturi knew,* Jasper thought, and stopped, and let the thought go unfinished, because he knew Edward could finish it for him.

Edward could. He'd stood in that tower. He'd felt Aro's cold, papery hand close around his own and draw every single thought out of him like water out of a well. He knew exactly what Aro did with gifts he found interesting. He collected them. He kept them. He had a girl who could make you scream and a boy who could make you nothing and a man who could bind you to the guard so tightly you forgot you'd ever wanted to leave.

A whole world of people who could lift pianos with a word. Who could build walls in their own minds. Who could, if they practiced, read other people's.

Aro would want them. All of them.

He looked over at Alice.

She was sitting on the stairs, where she'd been the whole time, with her chin in her hands. She'd been quiet too. That was strange. Alice was never quiet with someone new. Edward had assumed it was the wolves. Seth was sitting right next to Layla. Alice had complained for months that she couldn't see anything around them, that the dogs blotted out the future like spilled ink.

Her mind was on something else now.

*There's a hole,* Alice was thinking. She was thinking it over and over, the way she did when something bothered her and she couldn't let it go. *There's a hole south of town. Since last week. I can't see the river. I can't see the Bogachiel. I try to look down the highway and it just—stops. Like a smudge on a window.*

Edward caught her eye across the room.

*The wolves never run down there,* Alice thought at him, and her small, sharp face was troubled. *Sam doesn't. Jake doesn't. Nobody does. It's just empty. And I can't see it.*

He frowned. *Since when?* he mouthed.

*The third,* Alice thought. *Maybe the fourth. I thought it would go away.*

On the couch, Layla laughed at something Seth said, and threw her head back, and the fire caught the scar through her eyebrow and turned it gold. Renesmee slept on Jacob's chest. Rosalie watched them both with a face like a mother's. Somewhere out in the trees, Leah Clearwater ran the long loop of the eastern ridge with her own thoughts for company, and Edward couldn't hear her, and was grateful.

And somewhere south of town, past the reach of every gift in the house, there was a hole in the future, and it had been there for a week.

### IV. Charlie

*Sunday, November 12, 2006*

The girl parked nose-out.

Charlie Swan noticed it from the kitchen window, where he was standing at Sue's counter pretending he knew how to carve a chicken. The Bronco came up the gravel at a quarter to six with its headlights cutting through the rain, and instead of pulling straight in next to his cruiser like everybody else in the world, it swung wide across the yard and backed in, so the front bumper was facing the road.

So you could leave fast.

He'd known guys who did that. Two of them. One was a state trooper he'd ridden with for a summer back in '84, who'd done two tours in Vietnam and never sat with his back to a door in his life. The other one was a meth cook out of Beaver that Charlie had arrested in 1997, who parked nose-out at the grocery store and the post office and his own mother's house.

"Charlie," Sue said behind him. "You're sawing."

He looked down. He was sawing. He'd turned one chicken breast into a sort of loose pile.

"It's rustic," he said.

"It's sad." She came and took the knife out of his hand and kissed him on the cheek, quick, like it was nothing, like she'd been doing it for years. It still went through him every time. "Go sit down. Go be nice."

"I'm always nice."

"Go be *nicer*."

---

Seth came in first, shaking water off himself like a dog, which Charlie had decided some time ago not to think about. He had a pie in one hand and a grin on his face that Charlie remembered seeing on Bella's face at seventeen, and on his own face in a mirror once, a long time ago, before Renée, back when he'd thought he knew things.

The girl came in behind him.

Charlie stood up from the table. He'd heard about her, the way you heard about everybody in Forks. Gloria Stanley had told the whole town by the first Tuesday in October that Seth Clearwater was carrying jars for Ruth Hollis's granddaughter, and Charlie had pulled the girl's plates the same afternoon, quietly, from his desk, because that was his job and because he'd learned from Bella that it was better to know things. Layla Ann Hollis. Twenty-three. New Mexico license, clean. Registered the Bronco in Clallam County in August, paid the fees in cash. No record. No warrants. Not so much as a parking ticket.

She was about what he'd pictured, and also not. Smaller. She had a scar through one eyebrow, an old one, the clean white kind you got from something sharp. She wore a canvas coat that was too big for her and boots that had seen real miles, scuffed white at the toes, and she shook his hand with a dry, hard grip and looked him right in the eye while she did it.

She had calluses. Charlie felt them. On the inside of her first two fingers and along the edge of her palm, below the thumb. Not the kind you got from a hammer. He didn't know what kind they were.

"Chief Swan," she said.

"Charlie's fine."

"Charlie." She let go. "Thanks for having me."

"It's Sue's house. I just carve the chicken."

"He destroys the chicken," Sue said from the stove.

The girl smiled at that, a real one, quick, and Charlie relaxed about one notch out of ten. Then she sat down, and the chair she picked was the one at the end of the table with its back to the wall and a clear view of both the front door and the kitchen door, and he un-relaxed again.

Leah came in from the back about ten minutes later, barefoot, in a T-shirt and cutoff shorts in the middle of November, with her hair wet and her face set like concrete. Charlie had stopped asking about the shoes. Jacob had told him, the morning he'd taken his shirt off in Charlie's yard and turned into a goddamn wolf the size of a horse, that there were some things it was better not to ask about. *Need to know, Charlie. You really, really don't need to know.* And Charlie, who had stood in his yard that morning shaking so badly he'd had to sit down on the porch step, had decided that Jacob Black was absolutely right.

He had a daughter who was alive, and who'd come back from her honeymoon pale and gold-eyed and cold as a stone, and was happy. He had a granddaughter, or a niece, or whatever they were calling her, who grew an inch a week. He had Sue. He'd built a whole life in the last two months on not needing to know. It was a pretty good life. He liked it.

Leah sat down across from the girl and looked at her.

The girl looked back.

"Leah," Sue said.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing a thing with your face."

"This is my face."

The girl, Layla, said, "It's fine, Mrs. Clearwater," without looking away from Leah. "We've met." And something passed between the two of them that Charlie couldn't read, something flat and wary and level, the way two strange dogs look at each other through a fence. Then Leah looked away first, down at her plate, and said, "Hi," and Layla said, "Hi," and that seemed to be that.

Charlie looked at Sue. Sue gave him the smallest shrug in the world.

---

Dinner was good. Sue's dinners were always good. The chicken was in a pile but it tasted fine, and there were mashed potatoes and green beans with the little crispy onions and rolls from the bakery in Port Angeles and a gravy boat full of Sue's gravy, which Charlie privately believed could be used to resolve international conflicts.

Seth talked. Seth always talked. He talked about the Cullens, *the Cullens*, like it was normal for a nineteen-year-old kid to spend half his life on the couch of a family of—of whatever they were, and Charlie let him, because he'd learned that if you let Seth talk long enough you mostly found out things you didn't want to know, and then you could decide not to have heard them. He talked about Layla's house. He'd fixed the porch, apparently. And the gutters. And something about a stringer.

The girl talked less. She answered when Sue asked her things. She'd grown up outside Santa Fe. She'd gone to boarding school back east, up in Massachusetts somewhere, a small place, Charlie wouldn't have heard of it. She'd worked in salvage after. *What kind of salvage?* Old sites, she said, digs, estates, recovery work. Down south, mostly. Mexico. She'd been in it about three years and then she'd gotten out.

"Why'd you get out?" Charlie asked.

He asked it the way he'd ask a guy at a traffic stop where he was headed tonight. Easy. Friendly. Not really interested.

She looked at him for a beat too long.

"My partner died," she said. "On a job."

Sue put her hand on the girl's arm. Seth went quiet. Leah, across the table, looked up from her plate.

"I'm sorry," Charlie said, and meant it.

"Thanks." She picked up her fork again. "It was a while ago."

It wasn't, he thought. It wasn't a while ago at all. He'd been a cop for twenty-five years and he knew what a while ago looked like on a person's face and what last spring looked like, and this was last spring.

He let it go. He reached for the gravy.

He'd remember later that it was his elbow. He reached across for the gravy boat with his right hand and his left elbow caught the edge of his water glass, and he grabbed for the glass, and the back of his hand clipped the gravy boat instead, and it went over the edge of the table. He saw it go. He saw it tip in that long slow way things tip when you know you're too late. Gravy coming out of the spout in a thick brown arc. Sue's good tablecloth. Sue's good tablecloth and the floor and the girl's lap, because the girl was sitting right there at the end of the table.

The girl's right hand moved.

It was a small movement. A flick, down under the edge of the table, out of sight. Something pale and thin was in her fingers, like a chopstick, like a pencil.

The gravy boat stopped.

It stopped in the air. Four inches below the edge of the table. Tipped at about forty degrees, the way it had been when it went over. And the gravy that had come out of the spout, the whole thick brown arc of it, stopped too. It hung there in the air like somebody had pressed pause on a VCR.

Then it went *backward*.

It flowed back up into the spout. The way water would go if you ran a tape in reverse. Every drop. The last fat drop hanging off the lip of the spout pulled up and in and vanished. And then the gravy boat righted itself, slowly, and rose, and set itself back down on the table exactly where it had been. Not a drop on the cloth.

Nobody said anything.

The rain came down on Sue's roof. The clock on the wall ticked. In the living room the TV was on with the sound off, some football game, and a guy in a Seahawks uniform caught a pass and fell over and got up again.

Charlie looked at the gravy boat. He looked at the girl. Her face had gone gray under the brown. Her hand was still under the table. She was staring at him with her mouth a little open, and he knew that look, too, he'd seen it on a lot of faces over a lot of years. It was the look of someone who'd just done something she couldn't take back in front of a cop.

He looked at Seth. Seth looked like he was trying to swallow his own head.

He looked at Leah. Leah was watching *him*. Just him. With a funny little light in her eyes, waiting.

He looked at Sue.

Sue looked back at him, steady. She didn't say anything. Her hand was still on the girl's arm.

Charlie thought about it for what was probably three seconds and felt like a lot longer.

"Sue," he said.

"Mm-hm."

"That a need-to-know thing?"

Sue considered it.

"I'd say so," she said.

"Okay." Charlie picked up the gravy boat. He poured gravy on his potatoes. It was hot. It smelled great. He set the boat down in the middle of the table, carefully, with both hands, far away from his elbows. "Pass the rolls," he said.

There was a beat.

Then Leah laughed.

It came out of her like a bark. Short, loud, surprised, and she clapped her hand over her mouth, but it didn't stop, it kept coming, her shoulders shaking. Charlie had known Leah Clearwater since she was in diapers. He'd watched her grow up. He'd watched her go hard and quiet and mean after the Uley boy did what he did, and then after Harry died, and he could not, sitting there, remember the last time he'd heard her laugh.

Sue was looking at her daughter with her mouth open.

Leah laughed until there were tears in her eyes. Then she wiped them with the back of her wrist, and took a breath, and picked up the basket of rolls, and passed it across the table to him.

"Here, Charlie," she said, and her voice was still wobbly with it. "Rolls."

"Thanks, Leah."

The girl, Layla, let out a breath that Charlie thought she'd been holding since she sat down. Her hand came up from under the table. Whatever had been in it was gone. She put both hands flat on the tablecloth, on either side of her plate, and looked at them.

"Thank you," she said quietly. She said it to him.

"For what?" Charlie said. "I didn't see anything."

---

Later, on the porch, while Seth and Sue did the dishes and Leah disappeared out the back toward the trees the way she always did, Charlie stood with the girl under the overhang and watched the rain come down on the Bronco.

She had her hands in her coat pockets. She was looking at him sideways, like she was trying to figure out what kind of man she was dealing with. He was used to that, too.

"You parked nose-out," he said.

She almost smiled. "Habit."

"You sat with your back to the wall."

"Habit."

"Huh." He nodded at the yard. "Funny. You're the second person this month."

She looked at him properly. "Second?"

"English fella. Says he's camping down on the Bogachiel, way back off the river somewhere. Says he's taking pictures of moss." Charlie shrugged. "For a book. Rain forest. I sat at the counter next to him at the diner a couple times. He sat with his back to the wall, too. Corner booth. Watched the door every time it opened. Tipped thirty percent." He paused. "Ate a whole pie, once."

"A whole pie."

"Marionberry. In one sitting. Polite about it."

"Huh," the girl said. Her face did something he couldn't quite read. "Lots of people like walls."

"Sure."

"It doesn't mean anything."

"Didn't say it did." He put his hands in his own pockets. They stood there. "You take care of that kid, okay?" he said finally. "Seth. He's a good kid."

"I know he is."

"His dad was my best friend."

The girl looked at him for a long moment in the light coming out through the screen door.

"I'll take care of him," she said.

"Good," Charlie said. "Good." And then, because he'd been a cop for twenty-five years and couldn't help himself, even now, even here, even after the gravy boat: "And Layla?"

"Yeah?"

"Whatever you were going to do with that chopstick. If somebody ever puts a hand on him." He looked at the Bronco, nose-out in the rain, ready to go. "I didn't see that either."

### V. Harry

*Wednesday, November 22, 2006 — the Bogachiel River valley*

The rain woke him at twenty to six, the way it had every morning for nineteen days.

Harry lay on his back in the dark and listened to it. It came down on the canvas over his head in a steady hiss, the kind of rain that had no beginning and no end and had been falling on this valley, as far as he could tell, since the Cretaceous. Under it was the river, forty yards off, the Bogachiel running high and brown over its gravel bar. Under the river was nothing. No traffic. No birds yet. No voices.

He did the check before he did anything else.

He did it every morning, the way a man with a bad knee tests it on the first stair. He put his attention down under his breastbone, into the place where the wolf lived, and looked.

It was there. It was quiet. It was lying with its head on its paws in the dark, the way he'd left it, the way he'd been leaving it for seventy-one days now, since a cold morning in September on the other side of the world when he'd decided that this job wanted a man and nothing else. It lifted its head a little when he looked. It looked back at him. It put its head down again.

*Good lad,* he thought, and meant it, and also felt the small familiar pinch of guilt that came with meaning it.

He got up.

---

The tent was an army surplus two-man, olive drab, sagging at the ridge pole, with a patch on one side where something had chewed it in a previous life. From the outside it looked like the sort of thing a man would buy for forty dollars in a pawn shop in Aberdeen and regret. From the inside it was about the size of a decent house in Islington, and it had taken him three winters to build.

The bedroom opened onto a short corridor with a flagstone floor that was charmed against cold, because there was no bloody point in being a wizard if your feet were cold. The corridor opened onto the main room. Kitchen at one end, a big black range he'd salvaged from a manor house in Norfolk that the owners hadn't been using, which was to say they'd been dead for forty years. A long scrubbed table. A leather sofa with the arms worn pale. A cabinet full of records along one wall, floor to ceiling, and on top of it a turntable he'd spent most of a winter enchanting so it would run without electricity and never skip, even if somebody blew up the room around it, which had been tested.

He put on *Led Zeppelin II*. Side two. He dropped the needle on "Ramble On" and turned it up until the acoustic guitar filled the room, and then he went and put the kettle on and stood at the range in his shorts and an old grey T-shirt and fried six eggs and most of a pound of bacon.

He ate it standing up. Then he ate the other half of yesterday's marionberry pie, also standing up, straight out of the tin, with a fork. He'd developed opinions about marionberry pie in the last nineteen days. The diner in Forks did one that was nearly worth the rain.

At a quarter past six the mirror on the wall by the door went cloudy.

Harry wiped his fork on his T-shirt, crossed the room, and looked into it.

The face that looked back at him wasn't his. It was a long, grey, lined face with thin white hair combed straight back from a high forehead, and a pair of very pale eyes that had been looking at things nobody else was allowed to look at for longer than Harry had been alive. Behind the face was a stone wall with no window in it. It was quarter past two in the afternoon in London. It was always no particular time at all in the place the old man was sitting.

"Morning, sir," Harry said.

"Is it." Croaker's voice was dry and thin and very precise, like a pen nib on good paper. "You have bacon on your shirt."

"I have bacon in my soul, sir."

Croaker let that pass with the expression of a man who had let a great many things pass in his life and had stopped counting. "Report."

"Nineteen days of rain. Lot of deer." Harry leaned his shoulder on the wall by the mirror. "The Waltons are all present and correct. Eight adults and the kid. The new one, the girl, she's settled down a lot since I got here. Hunts with the husband, mostly. Very neat about it. They bury what they don't drink."

"And the wolves?"

"Run their patrols like clockwork. Two packs, as far as I can tell, the big one on the reservation and a little one hanging around the Waltons. Three of them most nights in the little one, sometimes five. They don't come down this valley. Never have, not once. It's off their map." He shrugged. "I've learned their schedules. I can tell you which one's got a limp."

"Which one has a limp?"

"None of them. They heal too fast. It was a joke, sir."

"I see." Croaker didn't smile. Harry had known him seven years and had seen him smile twice, and one of those times had been at a funeral. "The child."

Harry was quiet for a moment.

"Grows," he said. "About two inches since I got here. I've got photographs. Long lens, from the ridge. She was walking when I arrived. She's running now. She talks, I think. I haven't been close enough to hear it." He paused. "They love her. All of them. The big wolf, the one who's always there, he'd walk into a fire for her. You can see it from three hundred yards."

"Interesting," said Croaker.

"I'm not touching the kid, sir."

"Nobody has asked you to."

"Somebody will."

The pale eyes in the mirror looked at him for a long moment.

"When somebody does," Croaker said, "you will remember that I did not." He moved something out of the mirror's frame, a sheet of parchment, and Harry heard it rustle. "The Cullens remain category preserve. That has not changed. The Board's view is that a coven of eight who refuse human blood, living in some kind of armistice with a native protector line, and who have, by your account, a working relationship with at least one member of the Volterra hierarchy—"

"Had. Had a working relationship. I'm guessing it's frosty now."

"—are worth a great deal more to the Department alive and unaware than dead or alarmed. You will not approach them. You will not alarm them. You will continue to wait for something else to come through the door." The parchment rustled again. "Halloran's group would like hard numbers by Candlemas."

"Halloran's group can come out here and stand in the rain with me."

"Halloran's group," said Croaker, "is planning an operation against an organisation which has been operating out of the same Tuscan hill town since before the Statute was signed, which numbers at minimum thirty individuals with physical capabilities beyond any Being in our current taxonomy, at least six of whom appear to possess some form of innate mental or perceptual gift, and which has, by our best reckoning, eaten fourteen witches and wizards over the last two hundred years without anyone in Rome noticing. Halloran's group would like to know, before they walk into that building, whether a Stunning Spell will so much as tickle one of these creatures. I think that is a reasonable thing for them to want."

Harry didn't say anything.

"They would like to know whether the Unforgivables work," Croaker went on. "All three. They would like to know how fast these things heal, and from what, and what holds them, and whether Legilimency can reach their minds, and whether the gifted ones can reach ours. They would like a specimen, Harry. They would like a live one. And since every report out of Volterra for eight months has said the same thing, which is that a number of these creatures pass through that little town in Washington every year on their way somewhere else, and the Volterra people are taking an unusual degree of interest in it, I have put you in that town to get one."

"I know why I'm here, sir."

"I know you do. I am reminding you because you have been standing in the rain for nineteen days and men who stand in the rain for nineteen days begin to have opinions about the people they are watching." The pale eyes didn't blink. "You have the Department's dispensation. It has not been rescinded. Use it."

"Yes, sir."

"Is there anything else?"

Harry thought about it. He thought about the wolf, lying in the dark under his ribs with its head on its paws, and about seventy-one days. He thought about a girl in a green Bronco with New Mexico plates who'd been going in and out of an old farmhouse on the Calawah road since before he got here, with the youngest wolf in tow, and who he'd catalogued on his second day as *local, civilian, girlfriend of Wolf 3*, and not thought about since.

"No, sir," he said.

"Then good hunting," said Croaker, and the mirror went grey, and then it was just a mirror with Harry in it, a big man in a T-shirt with bacon on it, needing a shave.

He stood there a while longer anyway.

It had started with a little girl. That was the part of the file Harry kept coming back to, on the nights when the rain was loud and he couldn't sleep. In March, on a festival day in a Tuscan hill town, a seven-year-old No-Maj girl had told her mother that she'd seen a man standing in the sun by the fountain in the square whose skin shone like the inside of a broken geode, all over, like it was made of light. The mother had told a priest. The priest had told someone who was quietly on the Italian Ministry's books. And an Obliviator had driven up from Siena that afternoon and taken it out of the girl's head and filed a three-line report that said *probable student prank, glamour charm, no further action*, and the report had gone into a drawer in Rome.

The Department read other people's drawers. That was most of what it did, if you were being honest about it. And somebody on the ninth level had a standing instruction that any report with the word *luccicava* in it, *glittered*, came across their desk, for reasons that had to do with a different case entirely and a goblin silversmith who'd gone missing in 1971. So it did. And the somebody pulled on it. And under the little girl was the square, and under the square was the clock tower, and under the clock tower was a building nobody local ever went into, and under that was a family of something that had been running Volterra since before the Medici, with tour groups that went in through the front door on Tuesdays and didn't come out.

Eight months of watching after that. Long lenses on rooftops. A No-Maj tour guide in the Department's pay, who didn't know it, and who'd counted the people who went in and the people who didn't come back out. And one name, coming up again and again in the little scraps of conversation the watchers could catch: a coven in a wet American town at the top left corner of the map, that the old ones in the tower were very interested in. One of whom, it turned out, was the man who'd stood in the sun.

A new kind of vampire. That was how the first memo had put it, eight months ago, with a question mark after it. The question mark had come off in June.

---

He showered. He shaved, most of the way. He got dressed in the uniform, which on this job meant jeans and boots and a flannel shirt and a waxed jacket and a camera bag, because Harry Evans was a nature photographer from Manchester working on a coffee-table book about temperate rainforests for a publisher in London that did, in fact, exist, and had, in fact, paid him an advance, through three intermediaries, with Department money.

He'd shot eleven rolls of film since he got here. Some of it was very good. The moss on the Hall of Mosses trail at dawn, with the light coming down through the spruce. A Roosevelt elk standing in the river up near the Hoh. He'd send it all back to the publisher when he left, and the book would come out in eighteen months, and it would sell about four hundred copies, mostly to dentists.

The Department taught you a lot of things in the first year. Most of them were about doors. Where they were, who was standing in them, how to get out of a room if the door was suddenly full of people you didn't like. But the first thing, the very first thing, the thing old Croaker had told him in an empty room on the ninth level seven years ago, the day Harry had walked in off the lift with a list of questions and a bad attitude, was about faces.

*Hiding your face,* Croaker had said, *is the single best way I know to make a man remember it.*

So Harry didn't hide his. He walked around Forks in it. He ate in the diner in it and bought film at the drugstore in it and stood in line at the post office in it and said *morning* and *cheers* and *bloody hell, does it ever stop* to anyone who caught his eye. He let them see a big friendly Englishman with a camera and a waxed jacket and a lot of opinions about pie, and he let them file him under that, and he let them forget him, because there was nothing there to remember.

It helped that the face wasn't one anybody would know. There'd been a scar once, in a place people looked for it. There had been glasses. There had been a skinny, sharp-elbowed boy in every newspaper in Britain for seven years, and the boy had been seventeen the last time anyone took his picture, and the man in the mirror was twenty-six and six foot two and built like he'd spent the years in between carrying heavy things up hills, which he had. The scar had gone the year after the war. It had faded the way a bruise fades, a little more every day, until one morning he'd looked for it in the mirror and it was gone and the skin was clean. He'd fixed his eyes himself, at twenty, with a spell he'd adapted from a Healer's text in the Department library, because he'd been tired of having his glasses knocked off in fights.

Nobody looked at Harry Evans and saw anyone else. Harry had tested that, too.

He put the camera bag over his shoulder. Then he went to the armory, because he went to the armory every morning, the way some men go to church.

---

It was the room he'd built first. Before the kitchen. Before the bedroom, even. There had been a winter, the first winter in the Department, when he'd slept on a cot in the armory because the rest of the tent was still canvas and empty air.

It was long and narrow and cold, with a stone floor and racks down both walls. Shotguns on the left. Pump-actions, mostly, and a pair of side-by-sides he was fond of, and an old Winchester lever gun that had come out of a barn in Montana and was the best-balanced thing he'd ever held. Rifles on the right. A couple of pistols in a glass case at the end. Every piece of steel in the room had runes cut into it, by hand, by him, with a goblin-made graver that had cost him more than he liked to think about and a great deal of patience, and some of those runes had taken a month apiece.

The Department didn't use guns. Wizards in general didn't use guns, because most wizards still thought of No-Maj weapons as something between a toy and an embarrassment. Which was a mistake. Harry had spent a lot of the last seven years walking around the world learning how much magic there was out there that wasn't the kind they taught at school, and he had come to the conclusion, somewhere around year three, in a hut in the Mountains of the Moon with an old woman from Uagadou who was trying to teach him to Transfigure a stone without touching it or looking at it or, as far as he could tell, being in the same country as it, that the problem with wizards was they'd stopped being curious.

He'd never stopped. He'd gone into the Department at nineteen with a list of questions about a stone arch in a room on the ninth level, and the list had gotten longer every year, and he'd gone where it took him. The Mountains of the Moon. A monastery in Bhutan that didn't have a name. An Icelandic ward-wife in a turf house on a fjord who'd made him rebuild the same boundary ward three hundred times until he could do it in his sleep, and then made him do it in his sleep. A goblin rune-master in the old quarter of Lyon who'd cheated him at cards for a month and then, one night, without being asked, shown him how to cut a rune so it would *remember* things.

He'd learned to cast without a wand. That had taken two years and nearly killed him twice. He'd learned to cast without words, and without moving, and then without needing to think about any of it, which was harder. He'd worked out a way to Apparate through a point instead of a space, which meant you went *in* through something about the size of a needle's eye instead of tearing a hole in the air the size of a man, which meant there was no crack. He called it the Needle. Croaker had made him write it up and then classified it.

And he'd worked out the Lariat.

He'd been working it out for most of a year before Croaker sent him here, and it had taken him the first nine days on the Peninsula to lay it. Forty-one anchor stones, set along every game trail and logging road and river crossing on the north and east side of Forks, out past the Sol Duc and up toward the lake, everywhere something might come down out of the mountains. Each one cut by hand. Each one tuned to the next. Together they made a net about forty miles long and invisible to anything that had ever been born, wizard or No-Maj or wolf.

The Lariat was looking for one very particular thing. Something that moved with a heartbeat of zero and a temperature of about fifty degrees and a body as hard as marble, and that had human blood in its veins.

Human blood, specifically. Deer blood would pass. Elk would pass. Mountain lion would pass.

The Waltons could walk through the Lariat all day and it would never so much as twitch. Their blood had been deer and elk for decades. The wolves were invisible to it. Harry was invisible to it. But a nomad with red eyes who'd eaten a hiker in Port Angeles last week, who came down out of the Olympics looking for a second helping, would put one foot across the line and the line would close around his ankle like a noose around a rabbit and haul him up into the air and *hold* him there, upside down and stiff as a board, until Harry came along to cut him down.

There was a ring on Harry's right hand, a plain steel band, that would tell him when that happened. He'd been wearing it nineteen days. It hadn't so much as gone cold.

He went past the racks to the glass case at the end of the room and opened it and took out the Colt.

It was a 1911. A real one, an army one, made in Hartford in 1918 and carried across the Atlantic by somebody's grandfather and lost in a pawn shop in Leeds about the time Harry was born. The bluing was worn silver at every edge. The grips were walnut. He'd re-cut them himself, with runes running down both sides in a narrow band so fine you'd have to hold them up to the light to see them. He'd been working on the gun for three years. He still wasn't finished. He suspected he never would be.

He checked it out of habit, and put it in the shoulder holster under the waxed jacket where nobody in Forks would ever see it.

On the way out he stopped at the last door in the corridor and opened it and looked in.

It was the coldest room in the tent. White walls, white tiled floor, a drain in the middle, very bright light that came from nowhere in particular. Shelves down one side, floor to ceiling, of jars and boxes and lead-lined cases, every one of them labelled in his own blocky capitals. A Dementor's shed skin in a box with a lead lid. A basilisk fang, three feet long, from a pipe under a school in Scotland. A jar of something black and slow that had come out of a lake in Albania and, on bad nights, still knocked on the glass. In the centre of the floor there was a circle about eight feet across, inlaid in iron and silver and something darker that he didn't have a name for in English, with runes all the way around the rim, and a steel table bolted to the floor beside it.

The circle was empty. It had been empty for nineteen days.

Harry looked at it for a moment. Then he closed the door, and went out through the flap into the rain.

---

The diner was half full at ten and nearly empty by three. Harry knew because he'd been in at both times, most days. Pattern of life. You set yours, so people knew it, so that when you broke it they'd notice and you'd know they'd noticed. You learned theirs, so you knew who'd be where. Chief Swan in the second stool from the end, Tuesdays and Thursdays, half past seven, two eggs over easy and wheat toast and coffee, black, and he'd say *morning* to you twice before he'd say anything else. The Newton boy from the outdoor shop, Saturdays, with a blonde girl who talked with her hands. Two old men in the back booth every single morning at ten past six arguing about the Seahawks.

Harry sat in the corner booth with his back to the wall at a quarter to four in the afternoon and ate a cheeseburger with grilled onions and watched the door.

"You want pie, hon?" Darlene said, coming by with the coffee pot. Darlene was sixty and from Spokane and had worked in this diner for twenty-two years and had told him so the first morning, along with most of her medical history.

"Is the Pope Catholic, Darlene?"

"Not in this town he isn't, hon. Marionberry?"

"You're a goddess."

"I'm a grandmother of six." She topped up his coffee. "You got anything good today? With the camera?"

"Moss," Harry said. "Lot of moss."

"Lord, honey, I could've told you that for free."

She went off to get the pie. Harry ate his cheeseburger and looked out the window at Forks.

It wasn't much of a town. One main road, which was the highway, and a handful of side streets running off it into the trees. Logging trucks went through, and the rain came down on them. There was a hardware store and a drugstore and a Thriftway and the outdoor shop and a couple of motels. The police station, a little brick building with a flag out front, hanging wet. It was the kind of town you drove through on the way to somewhere else, and it had a family of eight vampires living at the end of a dirt road on the edge of it, and a tribe of men who turned into horse-sized wolves living ten miles west, and a ten-week-old girl who could already run, and nobody here had any idea. Or they did, and had decided it was better not to.

Harry had been in a lot of towns like that. Godric's Hollow had been one.

A green Bronco went by on the highway, heading north. He clocked it out of habit. New Mexico plates, dented rear bumper, yellowed top. The Hollis girl. The youngest wolf wasn't with her. She was alone, and she was driving fast, the way she always drove, and she turned left at the light toward the Calawah road and was gone.

He hadn't learned much about her. There hadn't been much to learn. She'd moved into a dead woman's house in August and she shopped at the Thriftway on Mondays and the youngest wolf, the sandy one, the one who looked like a Labrador that had swallowed another Labrador, had been going in and out of her place since October grinning like a lunatic. Harry had seen that grin before. He'd seen it on the big one, the russet one, the Alpha, every time he looked at the child. He'd made a note. *Wolves: some kind of bonding behaviour. Sudden. Very strong. Look into it.*

He hadn't looked into it. It hadn't seemed important. It had seemed like something that belonged to them.

Darlene brought the pie. He ate it slowly, because it was the last thing he had to do today, and he wanted it to last.

He thought about Croaker. *Men who stand in the rain for nineteen days begin to have opinions about the people they are watching.* The old man was right, which was annoying, because he was usually right. Harry had opinions. He thought the Waltons were the strangest thing he'd seen in seven years in a job that was nothing but strange things. He thought the doctor, the father, the blond one, was a man who'd decided a very long time ago to be good when he had every reason not to be, and had stuck with it, every day, for longer than there had been a Statute of Secrecy. He thought the big wolf would die for that child. He thought they all would.

He thought that the people who'd sent him here would, one day, look at the photographs of that child and ask what she was, and what she could do, and whether there might be more of her.

And he thought he'd cross that bridge when he came to it. And that he'd have the dispensation in his pocket when he did. And that he'd use it, if it came to that, on whoever he needed to, because that was the job. You did the job. You didn't hesitate. You didn't enjoy it. You didn't pretend afterwards that you hadn't done it, or that it had been somebody else, or that the reasons were cleaner than they were. That was the whole of the code, as far as Harry had ever been able to work it out. The rest was paperwork.

He finished the pie.

He was reaching for his wallet when the ring on his right hand went cold.

It was like a drop of ice water on the skin. Sudden and total. He stopped with his hand halfway to his back pocket, and sat very still, and looked at the steel band.

Cold. And getting colder. And tugging, very slightly, toward the north and east. Calawah. The north bank, maybe three miles up from the bridge, where the old logging road came down off the ridge.

Something had put its foot across the line.

And it had been running when it did. He could feel that, too, faintly, the way you can feel through a fishing line whether the fish took the bait or slammed into it. Running flat out. And behind it, very close behind it, he could feel two more things running. Hot. Big. Heavy-footed. Invisible to the Lariat, because the Lariat had never been built to see them. But the stones felt their weight go over, the way a spider feels the wind in its web.

Two wolves. Chasing it. Right into his net.

Harry took a twenty out of his wallet and then a ten and put them under the coffee cup. He stood up.

"Darlene, love," he said, as she came by. "I've got to dash. Think I left the kettle on."

"In a *tent*?"

"Big kettle." He winked at her. He shrugged into the waxed jacket, the Colt sitting heavy and familiar under his left arm, and went out the back past the kitchen and the restrooms and through the door with the EMPLOYEES ONLY sign into the little gravel lot behind the diner, where the dumpster was, and the rain.

There was nobody back there. There never was. He'd checked on the first day.

He took the sunglasses out of his inside pocket. Aviators, gold rims, entirely useless at four o'clock on a November afternoon in a rainforest. He put them on. The jacket changed as he did it, waxed cotton going black and smooth and longer, the flannel shirt underneath going white and crisp, the jeans going to dark wool, the boots to black oxfords polished like mirrors. Harry Evans, photographer, folded himself away into some back room of his own head and shut the door.

The man who was left stood in the rain behind the Forks diner for one second with his eyes closed under the sunglasses and his hand open at his side and felt, very far down under his breastbone, the wolf lift its head.

It was interested. It hadn't been interested in anything in seventy-one days.

*Down,* he told it. *Not now.*

It put its head down. Slowly.

He went in through the eye of the needle, and the only sound he left behind him was a tick, like a pine needle landing on a stone.

### VI. Leah

*The same afternoon — north bank of the Calawah River*

Seth was thinking about stuffing.

He'd been thinking about stuffing for six miles. Specifically, he'd been thinking about whether Layla liked the kind with the sausage in it, or the kind with the oysters, which was what their dad used to make, which their mom still made every year even though nobody but Charlie ate it, because it was what Dad made. And whether, if Layla didn't like the oyster kind, it would be rude to tell Mom. And whether Layla had ever even had Thanksgiving with a real family, because she'd said something once about boarding school, and something else about her parents being *complicated*, and what did complicated mean, and should he ask, or was that—

*Seth.*

*What?*

*It's stuffing. It's bread in a bird. She'll eat it.*

*You don't know that.*

*I know she's been eating at our house every Sunday for two weeks and she's never once turned anything down. She ate Mom's tuna casserole, Seth. Nobody eats Mom's tuna casserole.*

*Charlie eats it.*

*Charlie would eat a shoe if Mom put cream of mushroom soup on it.*

Seth sent a ripple of laughter through the pack mind, and Leah felt it go through her like warm water, and was annoyed at herself for liking it.

They were running the long northern loop. It was a quarter past four and the light was already going, the sky over the canopy gone the color of wet cement, and the rain was coming down through the old-growth in that soft endless way it did in November, more like fog that had decided to fall. Leah was out on the left, high up the ridge, where the trees thinned out and the ground was all rock and salal. Seth was down by the river, a quarter-mile below her and a little behind. It was a good loop. It was a boring loop. They'd run it a hundred times since September, and in all those hundred times the only thing they'd ever found on it was elk.

Jacob was at the Cullens'. Of course he was. Jacob was always at the Cullens'. Quil had Claire this afternoon and Embry was sleeping off a double, so it was the two Clearwaters on the north side, the way it was most days now. Leah didn't mind. She'd told Jacob she didn't mind. She'd been telling herself she didn't mind for two months, and it was even mostly true.

It was easier with Seth than with anybody. Even when he was thinking about stuffing. Seth's head was a nice place, mostly. It was full of light and noise and food and the Cullens and now Layla, all of it jumbled up, but there was nothing in it with an edge. There was nothing in it that cut you if you walked through it in the dark.

Her own head was full of edges. She knew that. She knew what it was like for the others, running with her. She'd heard Paul say it once, when he thought she'd phased out, back in Sam's pack: *It's like running through a room full of broken glass.* He hadn't been wrong.

It was better lately, though. That was the strange thing. It had been better since the gravy boat.

She still didn't know why that had done it. Charlie Swan sitting there with his potatoes, and the gravy hanging in the air, and Layla's face gone gray, and Charlie just—*pass the rolls.* Like it was nothing. Like the world could crack right down the middle in front of your face and you could still just pick up the gravy boat and get on with your dinner. She'd laughed so hard she'd had to go outside afterward and phase, just to let it all out. And when she'd come back in, Mom had looked at her in a way she hadn't looked at her in a long time.

*She wants to come to the bonfire after,* Seth was thinking. *Layla. After dinner. Jake said it was okay. Is it okay with you?*

*Why would it matter if it's okay with me?*

*Because you're my sister.*

Leah ran for a while without answering. Rock and moss and the trunks of the big firs going by, gray and black and silver in the rain.

*It's okay,* she thought finally. *She can come.*

*Really?*

*Don't make it a thing.*

*I'm not making it a thing.* He was making it a thing. She could feel him making it a thing, a big warm happy thing, swelling up in the pack mind like a balloon. *Leah. Thanks.*

*Shut up and run.*

He shut up and ran.

She was coming down off the ridge toward the old logging road when she smelled it.

---

It hit her like a slap.

Sweet. Sickly. Rotten-sweet, like flowers left too long in a vase, like the inside of a perfume bottle someone had poured bleach into. Every wolf in two packs knew that smell. Leah had been smelling it in her sleep since the first night she phased. It was all over the Cullen house, all the time, the whole place soaked in it, and she'd gotten used to breathing through her mouth in there, but out here in the open it was different. Out here it came at you sideways out of the rain, out of nowhere, and every hair on her body stood up at once.

And under the sweet there was something else. Something red and thick and copper.

*Blood,* she thought. *Seth. Seth. Leech. Fresh. North of me.*

He was there in her head instantly, all the stuffing gone, the whole of him snapping into focus like a dog who's heard a whistle. *Where?*

*Logging road. The old one. Coming down off the ridge.* She was already moving. She'd turned without deciding to turn and she was going flat out, downhill, through the salal, the scent getting stronger with every stride. *It's not one of them. Not the Cullens. It's—*

She hit the trail. She hit the place where it had walked out of the trees onto the old road, and the smell came up off the gravel at her so thick she almost gagged, and she knew.

Red-eyed. Human-drinker. And it had fed recently. It stank of it. It stank of somebody's whole life, poured out and gone sour in it. She could smell the person it had eaten. She could smell a woman's shampoo, and coffee, and the inside of a car.

*Oh God,* Seth thought. He'd hit the scent too, down by the river, a quarter-mile off. He was coming up the slope toward her, fast. *Leah. Leah, that's a person. I can smell—*

*I know. Move.*

*Call Jake—*

*Jake's with the baby. We've got it. Move, Seth.*

She ran.

---

It heard them coming. Of course it did. It was a leech. It had better ears than she did. It was about three hundred yards ahead of her on the old road when she got the first sight of it through the trees, a man-shape going at a walk, hands in its pockets, and then it went still in a way nothing alive goes still, and turned its head.

Its eyes were red. Even at three hundred yards, in the rain, in the dusk. Red like brake lights.

It looked at her. Then it looked past her, at the place where Seth was coming up the slope through the ferns.

Then it ran.

Leah had chased a lot of leeches. More than she wanted to count. Last June, the newborns had come at them in a wave, all teeth and screaming, and she'd fought them in the clearing until her jaws ached. Victoria had run. Victoria had been the fastest thing Leah had ever seen, faster than Leah, and Leah was the fastest wolf in either pack, and she'd chased Victoria for months and never once gotten close.

This one was fast. Nearly Victoria-fast. It went off the old road and into the trees on the downhill side, heading west, toward the river, toward the Calawah, a blur of pale skin and dark clothes threading between the trunks so smoothly it looked like it was skating.

Leah went after it.

She stopped thinking. That was the good part. That was the only part of being a wolf she'd ever loved, the one thing about it that was clean. When she ran flat out, everything else went away. Sam went away. Emily went away. The face her mother had worn the night Dad died went away. There was just the ground under her and the scent in front of her and the long, hard, pounding rhythm of her own heart, and she let it take her, and she *flew*.

She gained on it. She felt herself gain on it. Two hundred yards. One-fifty. Seth was coming up on her left, cutting the angle, trying to get between it and the river. Smart. He was smarter in a fight than anybody gave him credit for. *I've got the river,* he thought. *Push it right. Push it to me.*

*Pushing.*

She went wide, crashing through a stand of vine maple to make as much noise as she could. The leech heard her and veered, away from her, toward Seth, and Seth was there, a long sandy streak coming down through the trees with his ears flat.

The leech saw him. It changed direction again, in the air, the way they could, like a bird. Straight down the hill now. Toward a gap in the trees by an old cedar stump the size of a car.

A hundred yards.

Fifty.

It looked back at her over its shoulder. She saw its face. Thin, pale, dark-haired, a long nose. It was *smiling*. It was having fun.

Thirty. She bunched her back legs under her for the leap.

The leech went through the gap by the cedar stump.

And something caught it by the ankle and yanked it into the air.

---

It happened so fast she nearly went right underneath it.

One second the leech was running, full stride, looking back at her with that smile on its face. The next second its right leg went up, straight up, like somebody had grabbed it from above, and the rest of it followed, the whole body whipping up and over in an arc, and then it was hanging in the air upside down. Feet up. Head down. About four feet off the ground. Swinging, slowly, back and forth, like a side of beef on a hook.

Leah hit the brakes so hard she tore up a trench in the moss with all four feet and went sideways into a sword fern.

Seth skidded in on the other side. He nearly ran into it. He had to throw himself into a roll to avoid it and came up with his hackles all the way up and his lips peeled back and a snarl in his chest so loud it shook the rain off the ferns.

They stood there. Both of them. On either side of a leech that was hanging upside down from nothing.

There was nothing holding it. Leah looked. She looked hard. There was no rope. No branch. There was nothing above it but rain and the gray sky and the bare top of the cedar stump. Its right ankle was up in the air, the leg straight as a post, and the foot was turned at a funny angle, like there was a loop around it, pulling. But there was no loop. There was nothing there at all.

And it wasn't moving.

That was the second thing. Leeches didn't stay still unless they wanted to. When you caught one, it fought. They always fought. They twisted and clawed and screamed and tried to take your face off, and you had to get your teeth in fast and hard and keep them there. This one was just *hanging*. Its arms were down past its head, hands open, fingers spread. Its other leg was bent at the knee and stuck that way. Its face was upside down and absolutely frozen, the smile still on it, half a second old.

But its eyes were moving.

They rolled. Back and forth, back and forth, red and wild, from Leah to Seth and back again. It could see them. It could see everything. It just couldn't move.

*What,* Seth thought. *What. Leah. What.*

*I don't know.*

*Is it—did it—*

*I don't* know, *Seth.*

She took a step toward it. Then another. Every instinct she had was screaming at her to go for the throat right now, right now, while it was hanging there like a piñata, and there was something else in her screaming even louder to *stop*. Because there was a smell in the air that hadn't been there a minute ago. Under the leech-stink. Something sharp and clean and strange, like the air on the beach after a lightning storm, like a battery when you touch your tongue to it. It was coming off the empty space around the leech's ankle.

She knew that smell. She'd smelled it at Sue's house. On Layla's hands. After the gravy boat.

*Seth,* she thought, very slowly. *Seth. Back up.*

*What? Why?*

*Back up. Now.*

There was a sound behind her.

It was tiny. It was barely a sound at all. A tick, like a pine needle hitting a rock.

Leah spun around so fast she nearly fell over her own feet.

---

There was a man standing in the clearing.

He was about ten yards off, on the other side of the cedar stump, standing in the rain with his hands at his sides like he'd been standing there all afternoon. He was tall. Six-two, maybe. Broad through the shoulders, built heavy, built like the guys in the pack were built. He wore a black suit. An actual suit, black, with a white shirt open at the collar and no tie, and a long dark coat over it that went to his knees. His shoes were black and so shiny she could see the ferns reflected in them. His hair was dark and cut short, and he hadn't shaved in maybe a day.

And he was wearing sunglasses. Aviators. With gold rims. At four-thirty in the afternoon, in November, in the rain, in the middle of a rainforest, almost in the dark.

The rain was falling on him. She could see it land on his shoulders, on his hair, on the lenses of the stupid sunglasses. It was falling on everything. But his shoes were standing in black mud and there wasn't a speck of mud on them. Not one.

Leah breathed in through her nose, hard, and tasted him.

He wasn't a leech. That was the first thing, and her whole body sagged with it for half a second. There was no sweet, no rot. There was a heartbeat, she could hear it, slow and steady, about sixty beats a minute, the heartbeat of somebody sitting in a chair reading a newspaper.

He smelled like earth. Like turned earth, and pine sap, and cold stone, like the inside of a cave. And under that there was something else that made every hair down her spine stand up in a way she didn't have a name for. It was something close to the opposite of the leech-feeling. Something that smelled like the forest at night. Like *her*. Like the pack.

And he was steaming.

It was faint, in the dusk. But it was there. The rain landed on his shoulders and went up off him again in thin wisps of vapor, the way it went up off Seth when he came in from patrol, the way it went up off Jacob and Embry and Quil and every one of them. The way it went up off *her*.

*Leah,* Seth thought, in a very small voice. *Leah, he's steaming. He's hot. He's hot like us.*

The man looked at the leech hanging upside down from nothing. Then he looked at Leah. Then he looked at Seth. His face didn't change at all.

He reached into the inside pocket of his coat. Leah's lips came back off her teeth and the growl came up out of her chest before she could stop it, and he paused, very politely, with his hand halfway in, and waited for her to finish. Then he took out a small black leather wallet and flipped it open with one hand and held it up at them at arm's length.

"Evening," he said. "This situation is contained. I'm going to have to ask you to vacate the premises, please. This is now the site of a national security operation."

His voice was English. Proper English. Like somebody on the BBC. Like somebody off one of the shows Mom watched on PBS on Sunday nights, with the big houses and the horses.

Leah stared at the badge.

It was a badge. It had a gold eagle on it and some writing around the edge, and a card next to it with a photo. The writing said U.S. FISH AND WILDLIFE SERVICE. Under it, SPECIAL AGENT.

*Fish and Wildlife?* Leah thought. *Fish and* Wildlife?

*It says FBI,* Seth thought. *Leah, it says FBI. It's got the—it's got the blue letters, it says FBI right on it—*

*It says Fish and Wildlife.*

*It says* FBI*—*

The man looked from one of them to the other. Then, very slowly, he turned the badge around and looked at it himself.

"Ah," he said. "Fish and Wildlife for the lady. FBI for the gentleman." He closed the wallet with a snap and put it back inside his coat. "That's a strange old thing, isn't it. It shows you whatever you'll give the least lip to. You've got a problem with authority, love, but a soft spot for nature. I've made a note."

There was a long moment of silence in the clearing.

The rain came down. The leech hung upside down from nothing, its eyes rolling.

*Did he,* Leah thought. *Did he just—*

"Answer you?" the man said. "Yes. Sorry. Rude of me, I know. Can't help it, though. The two of you think like you're shouting down a well." He tilted his head. "You *always* think like that? Out loud? At each other? That must make for an interesting Christmas."

The bottom dropped out of Leah's stomach.

She'd been in Sam's head all spring and summer. She'd been in Jacob's head and Seth's head and Quil's and Embry's and every other wolf on the reservation's head since the night she first phased. She'd had every single thing she'd ever thought or felt or remembered spread out on a table for the whole pack to pick through, Sam and Emily and the wedding she'd had to stand at, and the nights she'd cried herself to sleep, and the stupid, private, hopeless thing she sometimes still thought about at three in the morning, the thing about kids, about never being able to have them now, about being some kind of genetic dead end. All of it. For eight months. She'd finally gotten to where she could stand it. Barely.

And now some stranger in a suit was just standing in the rain *reading* it.

*Get out of my head,* she thought at him, with everything she had. *Get the hell out of my head.*

"Would if I could, love," the man said. He didn't look bothered. He looked like a guy being asked to turn his radio down. "Honestly. It's not like I'm enjoying the view in there. Lot of furniture. Lot of sharp corners." He turned his head, and the sunglasses swung toward Seth. "Yours is nicer. Very cheerful. Bit of a pie situation going on."

*It's stuffing,* Seth thought, before he could stop himself.

"Stuffing. Sorry. My mistake." The sunglasses swung back to Leah. "And she's right, you know. Bread in a bird. Your girl'll eat it."

*Who the hell* are *you?*

"Me?" He spread his hands. "Bloke with a badge. Thought I'd made that clear."

*That badge said Fish and Wildlife.*

"And if it'd said Department of the Interior you'd have bitten me." He sighed. "Look. Lassie. I'm going to be very straight with you, because I can see we're going to get along. You're going to want to walk away from this one."

Leah's whole body went stiff.

*Lassie,* she thought. *Did he just call me Lassie?*

"Collie in a mood," the man said. "You've got the face for it. Very noble. Very cross. Very much like you'd pull a small boy out of a well and then bite him for falling in." He considered her. "It was that or Old Yeller, and nobody wants to end up like Old Yeller."

*I am going to rip your throat out.*

"You can try, love. Wouldn't recommend it. I'm in a very good suit."

Behind her, Seth made a sound. It was half a whine and half a cough and it was the sound he made when he was trying very, very hard not to laugh.

The sunglasses swung over to him.

"And you," the man said. He tilted his head the other way. "You're a Balto."

The laugh in Seth's chest died instantly. His ears went straight up.

*Balto?* Seth thought. *Leah. Leah. He called me* Balto*.*

*Balto's a dog, Seth.*

*Balto was a* hero*. He ran the medicine to that town in Alaska. In the blizzard. He saved* everybody*. There was a* movie*, Leah—*

"There was," the man said gravely. "Animated. Kevin Bacon. My cousin had it on video. Watched it eleven times one summer through a cupboard door." He nodded at Seth. "Balto was a very good boy."

Seth's tail moved. Just once. Leah felt it go, and felt him feel it go, and felt him try to stop it, and fail.

*Seth,* she thought, *I swear to God.*

*I can't help it! He said I was a good boy!*

The man turned away from both of them like they'd stopped being interesting. He walked over to the leech, still hanging upside down by the cedar stump, its eyes rolling, and stopped in front of it and bent down a little at the waist so his face was level with its face. Upside-down face. Right side up face. The rain running down both of them.

"Afternoon, Sparkles," he said.

The leech's eyes rolled to him and stopped. They stayed on him. Leah watched something happen in them, in that frozen face, something that looked a lot like fear.

"Long way from home, aren't you," the man said. "Bit off the beaten track. You know, I've been stood out here in the rain for nineteen days waiting for you. Nineteen. You have no idea how much pie I've eaten." He straightened up. "Right. Let's have you down."

He didn't touch it. He didn't do anything that Leah could see. He just looked at the empty air above the leech's ankle.

And whatever was holding it let go.

It dropped straight down. Four feet, headfirst, toward the ground, stiff as a plank. And the man stepped forward and caught it.

He caught it one-handed, around the middle, as it fell. Leah heard the sound it made against his arm, a dull, heavy *thud*, like somebody catching a bag of cement. Leeches were heavy. Leeches were made of something that was a lot closer to rock than to meat, and she'd tried to drag one off Jacob in June and it had been like trying to drag a boulder. The man didn't even bend his knees. He caught it and swung it up and tucked it under his arm, horizontally, the whole stiff length of it, head forward and feet back. Like a surfboard. Like a guy walking down to the beach with a surfboard under his arm.

The leech's eyes were still rolling. Its face was still frozen in that half-a-smile. It stuck out a foot in front of him and a foot and a half behind.

"Lovely," the man said, and patted its hip. "Right. That's me, then. Lassie, Balto. Pleasure." He nodded to them both. "Off you go. Mind how you go. Tell your Alpha the national security operation sends its regards."

*He knows about Jake,* Leah thought. *How does he know about Jake?*

"Big lad. Russet. Hangs about the big glass house east of town with a toddler on his shoulders. Hard to miss." The man adjusted his grip on the leech. "I know about all of you, love. I've known since I got here. Nobody's in any trouble. I don't want any of you. I wanted *this*." He hefted the leech an inch. "And now I've got it. So."

He started to turn away.

And Seth, standing there in the rain with his ears up and his tail still trying to wag and the whole of his big dumb open heart spread out in the pack mind for Leah and, apparently, anybody else who wanted to look, thought the thing that Leah had been trying very, very hard not to think for the last two minutes.

*Leah. He's like Layla.*

*Seth—*

*He's a wizard. He has to be. The thing that grabbed the leech. The way he showed up out of nowhere. The badge. Layla said there were spells that could—Leah, he's a* wizard*, like she's a*—

The man stopped.

He stopped mid-turn, with the leech under his arm and one foot already lifted. And he stood there. In the rain. For one second, two, three.

Then he put the foot down. Slowly. And turned back around.

He didn't say anything. He just looked at them. For the first time since he'd showed up, he looked at them like they were actually there, and Leah felt it. It was like a light swinging around in the dark and landing on her, steady and bright and cold. The sunglasses went from Seth to her and back again and didn't move after that. The rain came down between them.

When he spoke, his voice had changed. It was quieter. All the joke had gone out of it.

"Now that," he said, "is very interesting."

Leah felt her hackles go up all over again.

"Neither of you should know that word." He was looking at Seth now. Only at Seth. "Not the way you just thought it. That word came from somebody, didn't it, Balto? Somebody said it to you and meant it." A pause. "Who's Layla?"

*Don't think about Layla,* Seth thought instantly, desperately, *don't think about Layla, don't think about—*

And of course, the second he thought it, he thought about Layla.

All of her. Leah felt it go through the pack mind like a flood through a broken dam. Layla in the coffee aisle. Layla in the Bronco. Layla on the couch with a ball of light in her hand. Layla at the Cullens' with paper birds flying around her head and the piano floating. Layla at Sue's table with her face gone gray and the gravy hanging in the air and Charlie Swan saying *pass the rolls*.

The man stood very still and watched all of it go by.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you have *got* to be joking me."

He looked off to the south and east, through the trees, toward the Calawah road. Toward the old Hollis place. Something moved across his face, too fast for Leah to read, and if she had to put a name to it she'd have said he looked *annoyed*, and the annoyance was pointed at himself.

"Ruth Hollis's granddaughter," he said. "Green Bronco. *Civilian.* I had her down as a bloody *civilian*." He breathed out through his nose. "Nineteen days. And I drove past her house. Twice." He looked back at Seth. "She's good. I'll give her that. Very, very good. That's some ward on that old house." And then, softer, not to anybody in particular: "And she's told the Chief of Police."

*She didn't tell him,* Seth thought frantically, *she didn't tell him anything, it was an accident, it was the gravy, Charlie didn't see anything, he* said *he didn't see anything—*

"Course he didn't," the man said. He took a step toward Seth. Just one. "Balto. Listen to me. I need you to tell me exactly what—"

Seth panicked.

Leah felt it happen. She felt it from the inside, the way she felt everything he felt, and it was so big and so sudden that it knocked the breath out of her. With Seth the fear was always for somebody else. This time it came as a picture. A man in a black suit, standing on Layla's porch, at night. Knocking on her door. Layla opening it.

And under the panic, before Leah could think *no* at him, before she could think anything at all, she felt him remember the cord.

He ducked his head. He got his teeth on the leather tied around his right foreleg, the black cord he used to tie his cutoffs on with, and found the little plaited bit in the middle with the dark bead in it, the one Layla had made for him on the floor of her bedroom. He bit down.

Leah heard it crack. A tiny sound, like a seed between your teeth.

The man's head snapped toward it. "What did you just—"

The air beside Seth went *crack*.

It was a real crack, loud as a gunshot, the sound of something tearing. The ferns flattened out around it in a ring. And Layla was there.

She came out of nothing already moving. Already on her feet, knees bent, weight forward, like she'd stepped out of a doorway in the middle of a fight. Her hair was coming down out of the pencil. She was in socks, Leah saw, wool socks, no boots. She had a sweater on and her sleeves shoved up past her elbows, and in her right hand, held out in front of her straight and level at the man in the suit, was the long pale wand.

Her gray eyes found him. Found the leech under his arm. Found Seth with his hackles up, and Leah in the ferns.

She didn't ask a single question.

Red light came off the end of the wand like a flare.


---

### VII. Layla

*The same moment*

The knot pulled her out of her own kitchen like a fishhook through the navel.

She'd been standing at Gran's counter with a mug of coffee in one hand and the seventeenth composition book in the other, reading about the winter of 1949, and then the seed broke. She felt it break. A sharp, small *snap* somewhere behind her breastbone, exactly where she'd tied it, and then the pull, hard and total, toward the northwest. She dropped the book. She dropped the mug. She heard it hit the floor and didn't hear it break, because her wand was already out of the holster and in her hand and she was already turning on her heel into the pull, letting it take her, the way Danny had taught her, *don't fight it, mija, let it reel you in*.

The world squeezed. Went black. Came back.

Rain. Cold. Ferns. Moss under her socked feet, soaking through instantly. Dusk light, gray and dim, coming down through enormous trees. And Seth. Seth, right beside her, in his wolf shape, which she'd seen exactly four times and still couldn't look at without her heart doing something stupid. Huge and sandy and wet, hackles up all along his spine, lips peeled back off teeth as long as her fingers. Leah on the other side of him, gray, near enough his size, crouched low in a sword fern with her ears flat.

And a man.

Ten yards away. Black suit, black coat, white shirt. Sunglasses. And under his left arm, held horizontally like a plank of wood, a body. A man's body. Pale, stiff, dressed in jeans and a hiking jacket, with its face frozen in a grin and its red eyes rolling in its head.

Red eyes.

*Cold one,* Layla thought. *Human-eater. Seth said red is human. He's holding a cold one under his arm like a—*

She didn't finish the thought. She'd stopped finishing thoughts at moments like this a long time ago, in a vault outside Chihuahua, three seconds too late.

She fired.

It was a Stunner. Nonverbal, fast, her best, the one she'd opened every bout with on the Western Circuit for five years. It came off the tip of the wand like a flare and crossed the ten yards between them in less time than it took to blink, straight at the center of his chest.

He lifted his right hand. Not fast. He lifted it the way you'd lift your hand to check the time, and he flicked his index finger sideways, just the first joint, like he was flicking a crumb off a tablecloth.

The Stunner turned left.

It *bent*. Ninety degrees, in midair, two feet in front of his chest, like a ball rolling off the edge of a table, and went sideways into the trunk of a hemlock thirty feet away and hit it with a sound like a slammed door. Bark exploded off the tree. A ring of it, smoking.

The man looked at the smoking tree. Then back at her.

"Well, that's just rude," he said.

---

There was a part of her, afterward, that would go back to that moment again and again, and try to understand how long it had taken her to know.

Not long. A second. Less.

He'd done it with no wand.

She could see his wand. Or the shape of it, at least, a slim ridge under his left cuff, along the inside of the forearm, exactly where she kept hers. He hadn't touched it. He hadn't reached for it. He'd turned a Stunner, *her* Stunner, ninety degrees in the air with the first joint of one finger, and the finger had come from the hand that wasn't even his wand hand, and he had not said a word.

She knew how hard that was. Everybody who'd ever dueled seriously knew how hard that was. Wandless magic was a party trick. You could summon your glasses off the nightstand, if you were good, or light a candle. You could not catch a duelist's spell in flight and *redirect* it. That was a thing in stories. In the long, half-legendary accounts her dueling master in Santa Fe used to read to them on Sunday nights, old Señora Vidal with her cigarettes and her terrible knees, about the great duels. The famous ones. The ones that had been fought at a level so far above anything she would ever see in her own life that they might as well have been fought between gods.

*Dumbledore and Grindelwald,* Señora Vidal had said, *in 1945. Three hours. The witnesses said it was like watching weather. Like watching two storms decide which one was going to be the sky.*

*Dumbledore and Riddle, in the British Ministry atrium, in 1996. The fountain came to life and fought. Fire became serpents. Water became a wall. Dumbledore did not once raise his voice.*

*Three people,* Señora Vidal had said. *In a hundred years. Three who could fight like that. Remember their names. You will never see their like. Pray you never do.*

All three of them were dead now. Dumbledore had fallen off a tower. Grindelwald had died in his own prison. Riddle had died in a school in Scotland in 1998, finished off by a seventeen-year-old kid who everybody knew had done it with a Disarming Charm and a wand-lore technicality and a hell of a lot of luck. She'd read the papers. Everybody had read the papers. There was nobody left alive who fought like that.

And a man in a black suit had just turned her Stunner sideways with one finger.

The thought took no time at all. It went through her in the space between one heartbeat and the next. And it left her cold all the way down to her socks.

*Okay,* she thought. *Okay. Then we don't play fair.*

---

She went straight into the chain.

It was the one she'd won the Western Junior with at seventeen. Four spells, linked, nonverbal, each one masking the next, each one forcing a response that opened a door for the one behind it. You didn't throw it at a man to see if it would work. You threw it to end things.

*Expelliarmus*, low and fast at his right hand, to make him think about his wand.

He didn't think about his wand. He tilted his hand a little so the spell went past his knuckles and into the ferns behind him, and he didn't even look at it. But by then the second spell was already in the air, *Incarcerous*, and the ropes came out of the end of her wand in a thick black coil, a dozen of them, writhing, lashing out across the clearing to wrap him from neck to knee.

He looked at the ropes.

They turned into birds.

Starlings. A whole flock of them, black and iridescent and screaming, exploding outward from the place where the ropes had been and scattering up into the rain in every direction, so many and so loud that for a second the air was nothing but wings.

And through the wings came the third spell. *Confringo*. The Blasting Curse. A ball of orange fire the size of a grapefruit, aimed at the ground at his feet, to take the earth out from under him and fill the air with dirt and smoke for the fourth—

He caught it.

He reached out with his empty right hand and *caught it*. Like a cricket ball. It stopped in the air an inch above his palm and hung there, spinning, roaring, throwing hard orange light up into his face and onto the lenses of the sunglasses, and he looked at it for a second with his head tilted. Like he was interested in it. Like it was a bug.

Then he closed his hand.

The fire went out with a sound like a cigarette dropped in a puddle.

"Lovely form," he said. "Thunderbird, I'd guess. You lot always open big."

The fourth spell was already gone. She'd thrown it while he was looking at the fire. *Reducto*, at the ground. The whole forest floor under his feet, moss and roots and dirt and stone, and she put everything she had into it, all of it, the way she'd planned it in the Cullens' driveway two weeks ago, *the floor's the play, take the floor out and Apparate while they're falling*. The ground under him went up in a great brown roar, a crater six feet deep, a shower of earth and rocks and splintered roots going up into the rain.

He didn't fall.

The ground went out from under him and he just stayed there. Standing. On nothing. Six feet above the bottom of the hole she'd made, his shiny black shoes resting on air, the dirt and rocks raining down around him and bouncing off something she couldn't see. He looked down at the crater between his feet. Then he took one step to his left, onto the rim of it, onto solid ground, like a man stepping off a curb.

"That," he said, "was naughty."

And that was when Seth and Leah jumped him.

---

She saw them go. She'd have screamed at them not to if she'd had the breath. Seth from her right and Leah from her left, low and fast, going for him from both sides at once, the way she'd seen in Seth's memories of the clearing in June. Two wolves the size of horses, coming in under her spells with their teeth bared.

He didn't look at them.

He kept his face turned toward Layla, and he didn't move his hands, and both wolves stopped in the air.

Stopped. Mid-leap. Seth six feet off the ground with his front legs stretched out and his jaws open, Leah coming in low with her head down, both of them frozen in the air for one long horrible second, the rain falling past them. And then they went *down*. Straight down, hard, like a giant hand had come out of the sky and pressed them flat. Into the moss. Seth landed on his side with a grunt that shook the ground. Leah landed on her belly with her legs splayed. And they stayed there. They fought, she could see them fight, every muscle in their huge bodies straining, their claws tearing up the moss. But they didn't move. Not an inch. They lay there pinned to the forest floor like butterflies on a board, snarling.

He hadn't looked at them.

He'd done it without looking at them, while he was looking at her.

"Sorry, Balto," he said. "Sorry, Lassie. Grown-ups talking."

Layla's hands were shaking. She didn't let them. She'd been scared before, scared in vaults with things moving in the dark, scared in the Chihuahua job with the floor coming apart under her and Danny's voice going quiet on the other side of the wall. She'd learned to make her hands do what she wanted, even scared. She made them.

She threw everything she had left.

She didn't chain it this time. There was no point. She just threw, as fast as she could make the wand move, every hard thing she knew. A Bludgeoning Hex. A Severing Charm, straight at his throat. She Transfigured a dozen river stones out of the bottom of the crater into iron spikes as long as her forearm and threw them at him with *Oppugno*, all at once, a swarm of them. She threw a Bombarda that would have blown the front off a house.

He stood there and let it come.

The Bludgeoning Hex he slapped down into the dirt with the back of his hand, like he was swatting a fly, and the ground jumped. The Severing Charm he sent back. He flicked it, two fingers, and it came straight back at her along the line it had come out on, and she threw herself sideways and felt it go past her ear and heard it shear clean through the sleeve of her sweater, and felt cold air on her arm. The spikes stopped in the air in front of him. All of them. Hung there in a cloud. And then, slowly, every single one of them turned around until their points were aimed at her.

She stopped breathing.

They hung there for a second. Two.

Then they fell. All of them, at once, like somebody had cut their strings, straight down into the moss at his feet in a little clattering heap.

The Bombarda he caught on the palm of his left hand. Just held it up, palm out, like a crossing guard. The spell hit and spread out across nothing, across a curved invisible surface about a foot in front of his hand, and the light of it went all the way up and all the way down in a long white sheet, and the sound was like a car crash, and the trees around them shook water off every branch. And he didn't move.

He didn't take a step back. He didn't brace. He didn't move his feet at all. When the light faded, he was standing in the same place he'd been standing when she arrived, on the same patch of moss, in the same posture, with his left hand up. Breathing through his nose. His shoes were still clean.

She had nothing left.

She stood there in her wet socks with her wand out in front of her and her chest heaving and her arm bare to the cold where the sleeve had been, and she understood it all at once, the way you understand that you've stepped off a cliff.

She was good. She was one of the best duelists of her generation on the West Coast. She'd broken curses in places that killed grown men. She had walked into the Cullens' house knowing exactly how she'd fight eight vampires if she had to, and she'd meant it. She had never, not once, in her whole life, felt like this.

He had been playing with her.

Every spell she'd thrown, he'd taken it and turned it and looked at it and given it back, and he'd done it with no wand and no words and without his breathing ever changing, and he'd been talking to her the whole time. He'd pinned two wolves the size of horses to the ground without looking at them and kept them there. He'd stood on air. He'd *caught fire*. And she knew, she knew down in her bones, the way she'd known in the coffee aisle that Seth was something more than a boy, that she had not seen anything. That everything he'd done in the last thirty seconds had been the smallest, most bored, most polite thing he could think of to do. That there was something under it, something enormous and patient and cold, like the bottom of a lake, and she'd never seen it, and she never wanted to.

Señora Vidal had been wrong. There was one left.

She didn't know his name. She didn't know who he was or where he'd come from or what he wanted. She didn't know who he worked for. She couldn't even begin to guess. The accent was English, which meant precisely nothing; the best liar she'd ever worked with had been a kid from Lubbock who could do Glasgow well enough to fool a Glaswegian. He had a No-Maj gun under his coat, she could see the shape of it under the left arm. No MACUSA Auror in history had ever carried a No-Maj gun. No British Auror either, she was almost certain. No ICW agent. No Gringotts curse-breaker. Nobody. He didn't fit anywhere. He didn't fit *anything*.

He was looking at her. She couldn't see his eyes behind the glasses.

"Are you done?" he said. Not unkindly. "Only, I've got a vampire to put away, and he's starting to drip."

Layla raised the wand again.

She didn't have anything to throw. She knew that. She raised it anyway, because she'd been taught that you didn't lower your wand until somebody made you, and because Seth was lying on the ground behind her pinned under nothing, whining, and she'd said she'd come. She'd made him a promise in her bedroom with her blood in a cord and she'd *come*.

The man sighed.

"Right," he said. "Fair enough."

And he reached under his coat and took out the gun.

---

It was old. That was the stupid thing she noticed. It was a big, heavy, square-shouldered pistol, the bluing on it worn down to bare silver at every edge, the grips dark wood. It looked like something out of a war movie. He drew it smoothly, the way you'd take your wallet out of your back pocket, and he didn't point it at her.

He pointed it at the ground.

At the moss. About a yard in front of her feet.

She saw his finger move. She had time to get half a Shield Charm up, *Protego*, a thin silver shimmer in the air in front of her, more instinct than spell.

The gun went off.

It was quiet, which was the strangest part. A flat, heavy *thump*, like a door slammed in another room. And the bullet hit the moss a yard in front of her toes.

And the world blew up.

The ground came apart. She saw it come apart, in that last slow-motion instant, saw the moss bulge and split and the light come up out of it, white and orange, from somewhere down under the surface, the bullet going *in* and then going *off*. It was like a bomb going off under the dirt. A great flat punch of force and noise and light, and her half-made shield took the very front of it and shattered like a dropped plate, and the rest of it took *her*.

She went backward through the air.

She'd been thrown before. You got thrown, dueling. Nobody had ever thrown her like this. She went up and back like she'd been kicked by a horse the size of a house, her feet leaving the ground, the clearing whirling away from her in a smear of green and gray, and she had time to think, very clearly, *oh, that's a lot of air*, and then she hit the salal twenty feet behind where she'd been standing and went through it and into the sword ferns behind it and came down flat on her back in the wet.

She lay there.

The sky was gray. Rain was falling on her face. There was a high, thin whine in both her ears, like a mosquito in a dark bedroom, and under it, very far away, a sound that might have been Seth howling. She couldn't feel her hands. Then she could. She was still holding the wand. Señora Vidal would've been proud. Her whole body felt like one big bruise, but it was a bruise, she knew how bruises felt, nothing was broken, nothing was bleeding. He'd blown her twenty feet through the air with a bullet the size of her thumb and he had not *hurt* her. He'd chosen not to.

She lay in the ferns and stared at the sky and thought about that, about what it meant that he'd chosen.

Footsteps came through the salal. Unhurried. A shape leaned over her, dark against the gray, the sunglasses beaded with rain. He'd put the gun away. He was holding out his empty right hand, palm up, not reaching for her, just holding it there between them where she could see it.

"Right," he said. "Now. Might we lead this conversation with words, instead of spells and teeth?"


---

### VIII. Leah

*The same moment*

She couldn't move.

She'd been trying for a full minute and she couldn't move a single muscle, and it was the worst thing that had ever happened to her body, and a lot of things had happened to her body.

It was like being under a car. Like being under a hundred cars. The pressure was everywhere at once, on every inch of her, even, steady, pressing her flat into the wet moss so hard she could feel every twig and pebble under her belly. She could breathe. She could blink. She could move her eyes. And that was it. Her legs were splayed out in four directions and her jaw was jammed against the ground and every time she pushed, every time she put everything she had into getting one paw underneath her, the pressure just *matched* it. Pushed back exactly as hard. Not harder. It didn't hurt her. It just wouldn't let her up.

Six feet away, Seth was pinned the same way on his side, panting, his eyes rolling toward the ferns where Layla had gone.

*Layla,* he was thinking, over and over. *Layla, Layla, Layla, get up, please get up, please be okay, Layla—*

*She's okay,* Leah thought at him. *She's okay, Seth. I can hear her breathing. She's okay.*

She could. She could hear Layla's heartbeat from here, fast and hard and steady, and the rough rasp of her breath, and under it the high thin whine that meant her ears were ringing. And she could hear *him*. Walking through the salal toward her in his stupid shiny shoes. Stopping. *Might we lead this conversation with words, instead of spells and teeth?*

*When you let me up,* Leah thought at the back of his head, as loud as she'd ever thought anything, *I am going to bite your face off.*

"Noted, Lassie," the man said, without turning around.

---

Layla didn't take his hand.

Leah heard her not take it. Heard her lie there a second longer, breathing, and then roll over onto her side and push herself up onto one elbow, and then all the way up to sitting, by herself, in the ferns. Her wand was still in her hand. Leah could see the tip of it over the top of the salal, pointed at the ground.

The man took his hand back without seeming bothered. He crouched down instead. On his heels, his elbows on his knees, his long coat pooling in the wet, so his face was level with hers. About six feet away. Not too close.

"How's the head?" he said.

"Who *are* you?"

"Bloke with a badge."

"I didn't see a badge."

"Want to?" He took out the little black wallet again and flipped it open and held it up for her.

Layla looked at it. Leah watched her face. She watched it go still and then go narrow, the way it had at Sue's table when Charlie asked about the salvage business, the way it went when she was working something out. She looked at the badge for a long time. Longer than a person would look at a badge.

"It's blank," Layla said.

"Is it?"

"It's a blank card. There's nothing on it." She leaned forward. Leah saw her eyes moving, back and forth, very small, like she was reading something written in the air in front of the leather. "There's a—it's a Confundus. Sort of. It's built *out* of a Confundus, it's like somebody took one apart and put it back together around a mirror. It reads you. It reads whoever's looking and it shows them—" She stopped. "That's beautiful work."

"Thanks."

"There's no maker's mark on it."

"Shame, that."

"There's always a maker's mark. Every enchanter leaves *something*. A habit. A twist in the—" Layla looked up at him. "There's nothing on this. It's clean. It's completely clean."

"Well, that's the trick, isn't it." He closed the wallet and put it away. "Clever girl. Most people just see the eagle."

Layla stared at him. Leah could hear her heart. It hadn't slowed down.

"Who do you work for?" Layla said.

"Same as everybody, love. My mum."

"You're English."

"Am I, though?" the man said.

And it came out in a voice Leah had never heard before. Flat, nasal, American, the *r* in *though* all wrong, like a guy on a cop show set in Boston. Then he tilted his head and said, "Could be from just about anywhere, darlin'," slow and lazy, in an accent like a cowboy in a beer commercial. And then he said, in the English one again, the BBC one, like nothing had happened at all, "Accents are cheap. Don't buy them."

Layla's mouth was a thin line.

"ICW," she said.

He didn't say anything. His face didn't move.

"British Ministry. Some kind of task force."

Nothing.

"Freelance. Private. Somebody's money."

He just watched her. Leah watched him watch her. There was nothing on his face. It was like looking at a wall with a man painted on it.

"MACUSA doesn't know you're here," Layla said finally. She sounded sure.

"MACUSA," the man said, "doesn't know a lot of things." He shifted a little on his heels. "For example. MACUSA doesn't know that Layla Ann Hollis. Ilvermorny, Thunderbird House, class of oh-one. Western Junior Dueling Champion, two thousand. Formerly of Ortega and Pike Recovery, Santa Fe, New Mexico, until about last April, when there was a bad job in Chihuahua and a man called Danny didn't come out of it." He said it the way you'd read a grocery list. "MACUSA doesn't know that this young lady, in the last five weeks, has disclosed the existence of the magical community of the United States to one Quileute shape-shifter. And then to his entire pack. And then to a coven of eight vampires and their extremely precocious daughter. And then to Charles Swan, Chief of Police of Forks, Washington, a No-Maj, a sworn officer of the law, by means of a gravy boat."

Every bit of color went out of Layla's face.

Leah felt Seth go rigid beside her. She felt him think it, *how does he know, how does he know about Danny, she never even told me his name, how does he—*

"Your Balto knows more about you than you think," the man said, without looking around. "And you think very loudly when you've just been blown up, love. Most people do. You should work on that."

"Get out of my head."

"I'm out. Promise." He held up both hands. "I only popped in for a second. Just long enough for the highlights."

Layla's knuckles had gone pale around the wand. Leah watched her think about it. Watched her think about lifting it again. Watched her not do it.

"They're not No-Majs," Layla said. Her voice was very even. "The wolves. The Cullens. They're magical. They're—they're Beings, they're Beasts, they're *something*, the Statute doesn't cover—"

"Arguable," the man said. "Very arguable. I'd love to watch you argue it in front of a MACUSA tribunal, I really would. I'd bring popcorn." He tilted his head. "Chief Swan, though."

"He didn't see anything."

"That's what he said. Smart man. I like him. He has his eggs over easy." The man's voice didn't change at all. "And I'd hate, I'd really hate, love, for some lad from your Obliviator office to show up at his house one night with a scrubbing brush and go to work on the inside of his head. Because a nice young woman couldn't stand to see a bit of gravy hit a tablecloth."

Layla went very still.

"Are you threatening him?" she said.

He seemed surprised she'd ask. "I'm threatening *you*, love. Do keep up."

*Bastard,* Leah thought. She thought it as hard as she could, right at him. *You absolute bastard.*

"Mm," the man said. "Fair."

There was a long, long silence in the clearing. The rain came down. Somewhere off to the west, the river was loud.

"And what's stopping me from reporting *you*?" Layla said. "A foreign wizard. Unregistered. On American soil. Carrying a No-Maj weapon. Doing—whatever you're doing to that—" she jerked her chin toward the cedar stump, where the leech was leaning upright against the wood like an ironing board somebody had stood in a corner, its eyes still rolling. "You've broken about forty laws in the last five minutes."

"Forty-three," the man said. "If you count the starlings. Which you should, they were very good starlings." He leaned forward a little, his elbows on his knees. "Report me to who, love? And say what? *A man with no name, no wand, no face I can describe and an accent I'm not sure about blew me up in the woods.* And then some very nice, very bored lady at MACUSA with a quill and a clipboard says to you, *Miss Hollis, what were you doing in the woods?* And you say—what?"

Layla didn't say anything.

"Right," he said. "So here's how this goes. I didn't see any gravy. You didn't see me. We both go home and have a lovely Thanksgiving with whoever we're having it with. And one day, maybe soon, maybe not, I'm going to need something from somebody local who knows which end of a wand goes where. And when I knock," he said, "you're going to answer the door."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll be very disappointed in you." He stood up. His knees didn't crack. "And so, I suspect, will a lot of people with clipboards."

Layla looked up at him from the ferns. Her jaw was tight. Her eyes were wet, and Leah thought it was rage.

"What are you *doing* here?" Layla said. "What do you want with that—with *him*?"

The man looked over at the leech against the cedar stump. Then back at Layla.

"What vampire?" he said.

"*That* vampire."

"Ah. That vampire." He nodded, like it had just occurred to him. "Wildlife management. Says so on the badge." He glanced over at Leah, pinned in the moss, and if he'd had eyebrows over the sunglasses Leah thought one of them would have gone up. "Ask Lassie."

*I swear to God,* Leah thought.

And the pressure went away.

---

It went all at once. One second she was under a hundred cars and the next second there was nothing, just air, and her body didn't know what to do with it. She'd been pushing so hard for so long that when it let go she shot up off the ground like a spring, all four legs at once, and nearly went over sideways.

Seth was already gone. He'd come up off the moss and was through the salal in two bounds, crashing down into the ferns beside Layla, and Leah heard her say *oof*, and then *Seth, Seth, I'm okay, I'm okay, stop it, you're getting me wet*, and laugh, a small, cracked, shaky laugh, while he pushed his huge head into her chest and whined like a puppy.

Leah stood there in the clearing, and shook, and turned around.

The man was standing by the crater with his back to her. He'd turned toward the leech. He was walking over to it, unhurried, his hands in his coat pockets, like he had nothing left in the world to worry about.

Leah's lips came back off her teeth.

She went for him. She didn't decide to. Her body did it. She went low and quiet, the way she'd go for a leech, one long stride and then another, the moss silent under her paws. She was going to take him from behind, at the knee, the way you'd take an elk. She didn't care what he could do with his hands. She didn't care about the starlings or the fire or the stupid gun. He'd held her down. He'd put her on the ground like a dog and held her there and read her head and called her *Lassie*, and she was done.

He heard her coming. Of course he did. Halfway there, he stopped walking.

He turned around.

He reached up, as he turned, with his right hand, and took off the sunglasses.

He folded them, one-handed, the way you'd fold them if you were about to put them in your pocket, and looked at her.

At her. Really at her. For the first time. Past the gray wolf charging him across the clearing, into her eyes, from five feet away.

His were green.

That was all she had time to think. *Green.* Very green, a dark clear green like light coming through a bottle, like the river in summer where it went deep under the bank. And then she was looking into them and he was looking into hers and the whole world fell over.

---

She'd seen it happen five times. Six, now, with Seth. She'd been in Sam's head when he saw Emily. She'd felt it secondhand from the inside, felt the floor tilt and the strings go slack and everything rearrange itself around some other girl's face, and it had been the worst thing she'd ever felt in her life, because it was Sam, because it was *her* Sam, because she'd felt him stop loving her in real time with his own heart, and he hadn't been able to help it any more than she'd been able to stop it.

She'd thought she knew what it was.

She hadn't known anything.

It was a *rope*.

That's what it felt like. It felt like a rope coming up out of the middle of her chest, out of the deep place where her wolf lived, the place that had cracked open the first night she phased, and going across the five feet of air between them, fast as a thrown line, and hitting him. Going *into* him. Into the middle of his chest. Deeper than his heart. Down into some dark place under his ribs, some place like hers, and when it got there it found something.

It found a wolf.

She felt it. She felt the rope go in and wrap around it and pull tight, and she felt the wolf on the other end of it lift its head. A big one. Bigger than her. Bigger than Jacob, maybe, bigger than anything she'd ever felt in the pack mind, lying in the dark in there with its head on its paws. It had been lying very still, for a long time. She could feel that it had been still for a long time, like a muscle that hadn't been used. And when the rope hit it, it opened its eyes.

And then the rope came *back*.

It went all the way through him, through the wolf, and turned, and came straight back across the five feet of air, and hit her. Into her own chest. Into her own wolf. And tied itself off. A knot, pulled tight. She felt it close.

Both ways.

It went both ways.

She'd never felt that. She'd never felt it in anybody's head. Sam had gone to Emily like a stone falling down a well, and Emily had been at the bottom, and she'd caught him, but she hadn't fallen too. Emily had chosen. Emily had had time to think about it. Jared's girl, Paul's girl, Quil's little Claire, Jacob's baby. The rope went one way. It always went one way. The wolf fell and the other person caught them, or didn't, and that was how it was.

This went both ways.

She felt him fall. At the exact same moment. She felt the bottom go out from under him the same as it went out from under her, the same lurch, the same *oh*, and they were standing in the clearing five feet apart in the rain with a rope tied tight between their two chests and both of them falling down it toward each other.

He stumbled.

It was the first time she'd seen him do anything that looked like a mistake. He went back a step, a real one, an off-balance one, his heel coming down hard in the moss, and his free hand came up and pressed flat against the middle of his chest, against his shirt, right over the place where the rope had gone in. The sunglasses fell out of his other hand. They landed in the moss and he didn't look at them.

He was staring at her. His face had gone white.

"What the *fuck*," he said.

Leah's legs went out from under her.

She went down on her haunches in the middle of the clearing like somebody had cut her hamstrings. She couldn't help it. She sat there in the moss with the rain coming down on her and the rope in her chest pulling, *pulling*, toward him, toward the white-faced man in the black suit with his hand on his heart, and she couldn't breathe.

*No,* she thought. *No. No no no no no. Not me. Not* me*. Not this. Not after—I can't—I don't—*

And in the back of her head, from the ferns, where he'd stopped nuzzling Layla with his head up and his ears straight up and every hair on his body standing on end, she felt Seth see it. Felt him see it from inside her. Felt him know it, instantly, the way he'd known it in aisle four.

*Leah,* Seth thought. *Leah. Leah, did you just—*

*Shut up.*

*Did you just* imprint*? On* him*?*

*Shut* up*, Seth!*

The man was still staring at her. His hand was still flat on his chest. And something was happening in his eyes, in the green. Something was coming up in them from very far down, the way something comes up out of deep water. They went bright. For half a second, less, they went bright the way a wolf's eyes go bright when you catch them in your headlights, a hard green shine that had no business being in a man's face.

And she felt it, through the rope. She felt the wolf in him stand up.

It stood up all at once, inside him, like it had been waiting a very long time to do it, and it turned toward her. Toward her wolf. It *wanted*. She could feel how much it wanted. It wanted to come out and come across the clearing and put its nose against hers and *see* her, all of her, the wolf she was. And she felt her own wolf, inside her, answer it. Stand up too. Want the same thing.

She felt him shove it down.

It was like watching a man throw his whole weight against a door. His jaw clenched. His hand on his chest closed into a fist. The green shine went out of his eyes like a light being switched off. And he stood there breathing hard through his nose for one second, two, three, staring at her.

"Right," he said. His voice was rough. "*Right*."

He turned around. He walked to the cedar stump, fast, three long strides, and grabbed the leech around the middle and swung it up under his arm, and he didn't look back at her. He didn't pick up the sunglasses.

*Wait—* Leah thought.

He was gone.

There was a tick. A pine needle on a stone. And he wasn't there anymore. The leech wasn't there anymore. There was nothing by the cedar stump but the rain and a dent in the bark where the leech had been leaning, and a pair of gold-rimmed sunglasses lying in the moss.

---

The rope pulled.

It pulled *hard*. It went out of her chest in a direction, south and east, through the trees, away, and it was getting longer. She could feel it getting longer, could feel the distance going out of it like line going off a reel, faster than anything could run, miles in a second. And then it stopped. Somewhere. A long way off. It stopped and went taut and hung there, humming, between her chest and somewhere out there in the dark.

She knew where he was.

She knew it the way she knew where her own feet were. She didn't need a scent. She didn't need a trail. She could have walked there with her eyes closed.

*Leah,* Seth was thinking. *Leah, wait, hold on, Leah, just wait, we need to tell Jake, we need to—*

She was already running.

She didn't remember standing up. She was just up, and moving, and the clearing was going away behind her. She went past Seth and Layla in the ferns so fast she felt the wind of herself go past them, heard Layla say *Leah?* in a startled voice, heard Seth start after her and stop.

*LEAH!*

*Stay with her,* she thought back at him. *Stay with Layla. Get her home. I'm fine.*

*You're* not *fine, you're—*

*Stay with her, Seth!*

She ran. Down off the north bank. Across the Calawah at the shallows, the river freezing and loud around her legs, up the other side. South. Through the dark. The rope pulled and she followed it, through the old-growth and the second-growth and the clearcuts and back into the old-growth, across the highway in one long leap with a logging truck's headlights going by under her, into the trees on the other side.

She didn't think. She couldn't think. Every time she tried to think, it was Sam. It was Sam's face the day he came back from the Makah reservation. It was the way he'd looked at her, like she was somebody he used to know. It was standing at the wedding in that stupid dress. It was eight months of everybody's pity in her head, and Emily's scars, and Emily's kindness, which was so much worse than the scars. It was every night she'd lain awake hating this thing, this stupid magic thing that reached into people's chests and took them away from you and tied them to somebody else, without asking, without caring, without anybody ever getting a say.

And it had just done it to her.

To *her*.

And it had done it to him, too. That was the part she couldn't get past. That was the part that kept her running. She'd felt him fall. She'd felt the floor go out from under him the same as it went out from under her. Whatever this was, whatever the hell had just happened in that clearing, it had taken them both. At once. The same.

She didn't know what that meant. She didn't know if that made it better or worse.

She knew she had to find out.

The land went down, toward a river. A bigger one. The Bogachiel. She smelled it before she saw it, wide and brown and loud with rain. The rope was pulling her down toward it, toward a long gravel bar on the near bank, and she could feel the end of it now, very close.

And the pack mind opened up in the back of her head, wide and loud and sudden, the way it did when somebody else phased in. Jacob. Of course it was Jacob. Somewhere back toward the Cullens' house. She felt him land in her head and see everything at once, all of it, Seth's panic and Layla in the ferns and the leech hanging from nothing and the man and the suit and the gun and the starlings, and the rope. Felt him see the rope.

*Leah,* Jacob thought. *Leah, what the—Leah, stop. Stop right—*

She felt it coming. She knew what he was going to do. She knew the way his mind went hard and flat, like a door slamming, the instant before he gave an order with the Alpha's voice in it. He hated doing it. He'd only done it to her once, and he'd apologized for a week.

She phased.

She was going full speed when she did it and she came out of it on two legs, running, human, naked in the rain, and the pack mind went away like a light switched off, and Jacob's voice went with it, half an order, gone.

She stumbled. She fell to her knees in the gravel at the top of the bar, panting, freezing water soaking through, and knelt there with her hair in her face and her hands on the stones.

There was nothing on the gravel bar. Nothing at all. Just stones and driftwood and the river going by, loud and brown in the last of the light. No tent. No man. Nothing.

But the rope went straight into it. Into the empty air, about forty yards in front of her. Into nothing. And it pulled.

### IX. Harry

*The same evening — the Bogachiel River valley*

He came out of the needle in the middle of the specimen room and dropped the vampire into the circle.

He didn't lower it. He let go of it at chest height and stepped back, and it fell, stiff as a plank, and the circle caught it. The runes around the rim lit up one after another, iron and silver and the dark third thing, a ring of cold blue fire racing round the floor. The vampire stopped a foot above the tiles and turned upright, slowly, like a statue being stood up by invisible hands, until it was hanging in the centre of the circle with its feet a few inches off the floor and its arms at its sides. The binding settled around it with a sound like a held breath. Its eyes rolled. Its face was still frozen in that half-grin.

"Don't go anywhere," Harry said.

Then he leaned both hands on the steel table, and put his head down, and breathed.

It didn't help.

He could feel her. That was the first thing, and the thing that kept being the first thing, every time he tried to think about anything else. There was a line running out of the middle of his chest, out through the canvas wall of the tent, north and west, through miles of dark wet forest, and at the other end of it was a woman. He could feel exactly where she was, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark. She was moving. She was moving very fast, toward him, in a straight line, and she was a wolf, and she was furious, and she was frightened, and she was coming down off the north bank of the Calawah into the river at the shallows right now. He felt the cold of the water. He felt it close around her legs.

He'd have said that was impossible, two hours ago. He'd spent seven years in a department whose whole job was the edges of the possible, and he'd have looked you in the eye and told you there was no magic on earth that did that, that you couldn't tie two people together across ten miles of forest so tightly that one of them felt the other one's feet get wet.

And it went both ways. He'd felt it go both ways. He'd felt her fall the same instant he fell.

*Wolves,* he'd written, on his second day here, in a notebook that was sitting in the drawer of this table, right now, under his hands. *Some kind of bonding behaviour. Sudden. Very strong. Look into it.*

He'd thought it belonged to them.

He made himself stand up straight. He made himself go through it, the way he'd been trained to go through things. One piece at a time. Plain.

Item. He had, a quarter of an hour ago, become bound by some kind of native magic he didn't understand to a shape-shifter of the Quileute protector line, a member of the smaller pack. The grey one. The fast one. The angry one.

Item. The bond went both ways. He didn't know what that meant yet. He suspected it wasn't usual. Everything he'd seen in the youngest wolf's head about it had gone one way, wolf to other, like a stone dropping.

Item. The shape-shifters shared a mind. He'd known that before today. He'd confirmed it in the clearing in about four seconds. Everything one of them knew, the rest of them knew, the moment they changed. That meant everything the grey one knew about him, everything she saw and heard and smelled, went straight into the heads of the Alpha and the youngest and the two others and, by extension, straight into the big glass house east of town, where there was a man who could read minds.

Item. She was coming here.

Item. The Department's answer to a situation like this was written down. He'd read it in his first month. It was one word long, and it was an incantation, and it was the one they'd taught him in second year, the one Lockhart had tried to use on him in a tunnel under a school and had it blow up in his own face.

*Obliviate.* Take the afternoon out of her head. Take the clearing, and the badge, and the rope, if you could. And then go, and pack the tent, and walk away from this valley and the mission and nineteen days of rain, and file a report that said *compromised*, and let Croaker send somebody else.

He thought about it. He made himself think about it all the way through, the whole thing, start to finish. Her standing on the gravel in the dark. His hand coming up. The word.

The wolf came up off the floor of him so fast it knocked the air out of his lungs.

It was bigger than a growl. It was the whole animal, everything it was, standing up inside his ribs with every hair on end and its lips peeled back and its head down, *snarling*, at *him*, at the thought, at the hand coming up in his imagination, and he felt it come all the way up into his throat and his jaw and his eyes, felt the heat go into his face, felt the floor of the specimen room tilt.

He gripped the edge of the steel table until the metal creaked.

"Okay," he said out loud. "*Okay*. Okay. Not doing it. Not doing it. Down."

It didn't go down.

It stood there in the dark inside him with its hackles up and stared at him, and it had been seventy-one days since it had stood up at all, and he could feel very clearly that it had no intention of lying down again any time soon. And he could feel, past it, through it, at the end of the line, the grey wolf crossing the highway. Going over a logging truck in one long jump with the headlights passing under her belly.

The wolf in him turned its head toward that. Toward her. And whined.

Harry closed his eyes.

"Well," he said, to the empty room, to the vampire hanging in its circle. "That's going to be a problem."

The vampire's eyes rolled.

"Don't you start."

---

He felt her phase.

He felt it happen through the line, a kind of lurch, a turning inside out, and then she was smaller, and colder, and on two legs, and on her knees. On the gravel. Forty yards from the tent. On the far side of the outer ward, where his Disillusionment and the No-Maj-repelling layer and the heavy grey blanket of the Concealment met at the top of the bar and made a place that, from the outside, was nothing but stones and driftwood and empty air.

He went out through the flap into the rain.

She was right where he'd felt her. On her knees at the top of the bar, at the edge of the ward, with her hands flat on the stones and her hair hanging wet over her face and her shoulders heaving. And she didn't have a stitch on.

Harry turned around.

He did it fast, without thinking, the way he'd have turned away from a woman in a changing room he'd walked into by accident, and he stood there with his back to her in the rain, facing his own tent, feeling like a complete idiot.

She was looking at nothing. He knew that. From where she was kneeling she couldn't see the tent and she couldn't see him. She was looking at stones and driftwood and empty air, and the rope from her chest was going straight into it.

"Your clothes are on your leg, love," he said. He pitched it to carry, through the ward. "The cord. Left back leg. I'll wait."

There was a silence.

Then he heard her stand up. Heard the stones shift. Heard her breathing go fast and ragged, the way people breathe when a voice comes out of nowhere.

"Where are you," she said. Her voice was hoarse. It was the first time he'd heard it out loud. It was lower than he'd expected. Rough at the edges. "Where the hell are you?"

"About forty yards in front of you. Facing the other way. Get dressed."

"I can't *see* you."

"I know. That's the point of it. Get dressed, and I'll fix it."

A pause. Then the sound of leather cord being untied. Fabric. A zip. He stood there in the rain and looked at the olive canvas of his tent and listened to her get dressed and felt every second of it go down the line between them like a current. The cold on her skin. The wet. The fury, still, and under the fury something that felt a lot like fear, and under that, something that didn't have a name yet. It was the same thing he could feel in his own chest. He didn't have a name for it either.

"Okay," she said.

He turned around.

She was standing at the edge of the ward in a black T-shirt and a pair of cut-off denim shorts, barefoot on the stones, with her arms wrapped around herself. He could see the steam coming off her, the same as off him. She had her arms around herself the way people do when they're holding something in. Her hair was black and wet and fell past her shoulders. She had a long face, high cheekbones, a hard mouth. Her eyes were dark brown and they were looking straight through him at nothing, and her jaw was set so tight he could see the muscle jump.

He went down the bar toward her. He stopped about ten feet inside the ward, where he could see her and she couldn't see him.

Then he reached out with something that wasn't his hand and opened the door.

He did it gently. He didn't take the ward down. He just made a gap in it, about the size of a woman, in the place where she was standing, and let her through without moving her. One second she was outside it and the next she was inside, and he watched it happen on her face. Watched her eyes focus. Watched her see him, ten feet away in the rain in his black suit. Watched her see the tent behind him.

Her eyes went over it. The sagging ridge pole. The faded olive canvas. The patch on the side.

"That's it?" she said. "That's your *lair*?"

"Don't judge a book, Lassie."

"Don't call me that."

"Fair enough." He put his hands in his coat pockets. "What should I call you?"

She stared at him.

"You don't know my name," she said slowly. "You were in my *head*. You were in my head for ten minutes. And you don't know my *name*."

"Wolves don't think their own names much," Harry said. "Funny, that. I noticed it in the clearing. Balto thinks yours about once every four seconds, but he only thinks *Leah*, and I didn't want to presume."

"It's Leah."

"Leah." He tried it. It went down the line between them, and he felt her feel it go. "Leah what?"

"Clearwater."

"Leah Clearwater." He nodded. "Right."

"What's yours?"

"Harry."

He heard himself say it. He hadn't decided to say it. He'd opened his mouth to say *Evans*, or *does it matter*, or any one of the twenty easy things he'd said to people asking that question over the last seven years, and *Harry* was what came out. Just that. His actual name. To a woman he'd known a quarter of an hour, on a mission, in a foreign country, who shared her head with a pack of wolves and, through them, with a vampire who could read minds.

He stood there and felt it land, and felt nothing in him object.

"Harry what?" she said.

"Just Harry. For now."

She looked at him for a long moment. Her arms were still wrapped round herself.

"What did you do to me?" she said.

"Nothing."

"Don't—"

"Nothing," he said again. "I swear it, Leah. Whatever happened back there, it wasn't me. I didn't do it, I didn't plan it, and I don't know what it is. I'd tell you if I did."

She heard that. He watched her hear it, and weigh it, and not know what to do with it.

"You felt it," she said. Her voice cracked on the last word. "Back there. You felt it too. Didn't you."

And Harry, who had been lying for a living for seven years, who had lied to Aurors and goblins and Dark wizards and heads of state and his own best friends, who had once lied his way into a goblin bank, opened his mouth to say *no*.

It didn't come out.

He felt it get stuck. Somewhere between his chest and his mouth, the word just *stopped*, like a key that won't go into a lock, and his jaw locked up around it. Physically locked. He stood there with his mouth half open and his teeth clenched and the word *no* jammed somewhere behind them, and he pushed, and it didn't go anywhere. The line in his chest had gone tight as a wire.

He shut his mouth. Breathed. Opened it again.

"Yes," he said.

That came out fine.

Leah was staring at him.

"What just happened," she said. "What was that?"

"I tried to lie to you." He heard his own voice come out very flat, very calm, a long way off. "Just now. I tried to say *no*. Couldn't."

"*Couldn't?*"

"Couldn't. Wouldn't come out. Jammed." He put his hand up, without meaning to, flat on his chest, over the place where the line went in. "Huh."

"That's—" She stopped. She looked like she was going to argue. Then something went across her face, fast, and she looked away from him, down at the stones, and he felt her try it. He felt it down the line, as clearly as if she'd said it out loud. Felt her pick something, some small easy lie, and go for it.

"I don't care what happens to you," Leah said.

Except she didn't. She got as far as *I don't* and then her mouth stopped moving, and he watched her jaw lock the same way his had, the muscle standing out, her lips pressed together hard. She stood there with her fists clenched and fought it for a good five seconds, and he felt her fight it, felt her throw everything she had at it the same way she'd thrown everything at the pressure in the clearing.

Then she breathed out through her nose like a bull.

"*Damn* it," she said. "I *do* care. Damn it. I don't even *know* you."

They stood there in the rain and looked at each other.

"Well," Harry said. "That's new."

---

It was a while before either of them said anything else.

The river went by below the bar, loud and brown. The rain came down on the canvas of the tent and on the two of them. Leah had uncrossed her arms. She'd put her hands in the back pockets of her shorts instead, and was standing with her weight on one foot, looking at him with her head a little on one side, the way a wolf looks at something it hasn't decided about yet.

And he could feel her looking. Down the line. He'd never had anything like it. He could feel her attention on him like a hand.

"You smell like a wolf," she said.

He'd known that was coming. He'd known it in the clearing, watching her breathe him in.

"Yes," he said.

"You're not Quileute."

"No."

"You're not—you're not from here. You're not from *anywhere* around here. You talk like the Queen."

"I really don't."

"So what are you?"

He thought about it. He thought about how to say it without saying a word that wasn't true, and found that the line in his chest was waiting to see what he'd do, and that he didn't mind it waiting.

"I'm a wolf," he said. "Some of the time. And a man the rest of it. How I got to be both is a long story, and a private one, and I'm going to keep it for now, Leah, if that's all the same to you." He held her eyes. "I won't lie to you about it. Clearly. I *can't* lie to you about it. I'm just not going to tell you yet."

"Yet."

"Yet."

She took that. She chewed on it. He could see her chewing on it, could feel her deciding whether to push, and deciding not to. She'd felt it, down the line, the same way he'd felt her try to lie. She'd felt that he meant *yet*.

"I felt it," she said. "In the clearing. Your—" She gestured at his chest, an angry little jab. "*That.* When the—when it happened. It went into you and there was a wolf in there. A big one."

"There is."

"It stood up."

"It did."

"Is it—" She stopped. "Is it standing up now?"

Harry looked inside.

It was. It hadn't sat down since the clearing. It was standing in the dark under his breastbone with its whole body turned toward her, toward the woman on the gravel bar ten feet away, and its ears were up, and it was very, very still. The way a dog goes still at a door when somebody it loves is on the other side of it.

"It wants to see yours," he said. It came out quieter than he meant it to. "Badly."

Leah's face did something complicated.

She put her own hand up, flat, on her own chest. Her eyes went away from him for a second, inward, the way his had.

"Mine wants to see yours," she said.

Neither of them moved.

"I've kept him on a short lead," Harry said, after a while. He didn't know why he was telling her. He knew exactly why he was telling her: because the line was open, and he didn't want to close it, and he was finding that when he didn't want to close it, things just came out. "The last couple of months. Since before I came here. The job wanted a man and nothing else, so that's what it got. Seventy-one days." He breathed out. "I'm pretty sure that's done for now."

"Done?"

"Done. He's up. He's not lying back down. Not tonight, anyway. Possibly not for a good long while." He looked at her. "That's your fault, by the way."

"*My* fault?"

"Yours. Entirely. You and your face."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. He'd swear to it later. It was the place where a smile would have been, on somebody else, and she squashed it so fast he almost missed it.

He felt it go down the line anyway. Felt it hit his chest like a pebble hitting a pond.

He decided he liked that very much.

---

"So," Leah said. "Who do you work for?"

"Can't tell you that."

"Can't?"

"Won't, then. Same thing, tonight." He nodded at her head. "Your head's a party line, love. Everything you know, your whole pack knows the second you phase. Your Alpha. Balto. The other two. And the bloke at the Waltons' with the hair reads minds for a hobby."

"Edward."

"Edward." He nodded. "So I'm not going to tell you who I work for, or why I'm here, or what I'm going to do next week, because it'd be up at the Waltons' by breakfast. That's the truth. And I'm sorry for it. Everything else, ask me."

She looked at him sideways. "Everything else."

"Anything that's mine to tell."

"How old are you?"

"Twenty-six."

"Where are you from?"

"England. Small town. Grew up in a little house in Surrey with my aunt and uncle and a cousin who had *Balto* on video. I wasn't happy there and they weren't happy with me." He shrugged. "That's the short version."

"Are you a wizard?"

"Yes."

"Like Layla?"

He thought about Layla Hollis lying in the ferns with her wand still in her hand, and her Stunner bending sideways into a tree, and her face when the spikes turned round.

"Same family," he said. "Different branch."

"You blew her up."

"I blew up the ground in front of her. She's fine. She's going to have a headache and a very bad night's sleep and I'd imagine she's at home right now telling Balto exactly what she thinks of me." He paused. "She's good, you know. She's very good. She's the best I've seen out here in a long while. Her Stunner's got a kick to it like a mule. And that house of her gran's, I drove past it twice and never felt a thing. I've a lot of respect for whoever built those wards."

Leah's eyebrows went up. "You're *complimenting* her?"

"She earned it."

"You threatened her. You threatened to wipe *Charlie's brain*."

"I threatened to let *somebody else* wipe Charlie's brain," Harry said. "Which they would. And I wouldn't lift a finger to stop them, if she crossed me. That's also true." He met her eyes. "I do the job, Leah. I'll do whatever the job needs, to whoever it needs doing to, and I won't lose sleep over it. I'm not going to pretend otherwise to you. I couldn't if I wanted to."

She looked at him for a long time.

"What's the job tonight?" she said.

He tipped his head back toward the tent.

"Tonight the job's hanging in a circle in the back room," he said. "And I've got a lot of questions for it. I've been waiting nineteen days to ask them."

"The leech."

"The leech."

"What are you going to do to him?"

"Talk to him. To start with. Then look at him. Then we'll see." He didn't soften it. He didn't want to. "Some of it won't be pretty."

She didn't flinch. He'd half expected her to. He felt her think about it, down the line, and he felt what she landed on, and what she landed on was the smell of that thing in the clearing. Somebody's shampoo. Coffee. The inside of a car.

"He ate someone," Leah said. "Yesterday. Maybe this morning. I could smell her on him."

"I know. I could smell her too."

That surprised her. He felt it surprise her.

He looked at her, standing barefoot on the stones in the rain, with her hands in her back pockets and her hair stuck to her neck. With her chin up. With the line running out of her chest and into his, humming like a plucked string.

"You can come in, if you like," he said. "Watch. Ask whatever you want, while I work. I'll answer." He nodded at the tent. "Or you can go home and tell your Alpha the bloke in the suit's a menace. I won't stop you. You can walk out of this ward any time you like and it'll let you."

"Why would you let me watch?"

He opened his mouth to say *because you'd only follow me in anyway*, which was true, or *because it's easier to keep an eye on you in there*, which was also true, and was the professional answer, and was the one he'd have given anybody else.

"Because I don't want you to leave," he said.

It came out on its own. Plain as that. He stood there in the rain and listened to himself say it, and didn't take it back, because he couldn't, and because he didn't want to.

Leah stared at him.

Then she took her hands out of her pockets and walked past him, up the gravel bar, and stopped at the flap of the tent, and looked back over her shoulder.

"Well?" she said. "Are you coming, or what?"

---

She stopped dead two steps inside.

He'd seen people do it before. Ron had done it, years ago, the first time Harry had let him into the half-built shell of the thing, standing in the middle of the empty canvas air with his mouth open saying *bloody hell, mate* about nine times in a row. Croaker had done it, once, which had been worth the three winters on its own.

Leah didn't say *bloody hell*. She didn't say anything. She stood in the middle of his main room, in her bare feet, on his flagstone floor, and turned slowly round on the spot. The kitchen. The range. The long scrubbed table. The leather sofa. The wall of records. The turntable. The corridor going away in both directions, with doors off it. The ceiling, which was a good fourteen feet up, and had beams in it.

She looked at all of it. Then she turned back to him, where he was standing just inside the flap with rain dripping off his coat onto the floor.

"It's *bigger*," she said.

"It is."

"On the inside."

"It is."

"It's a *tent*."

"It is, a bit."

She looked at the turntable.

"Is that a *record player*?"

"Tent rules," Harry said. He shrugged out of the long coat and hung it on the peg by the door. "Bloke who owns the tent picks the music. Guest keeps her cakehole shut."

"Try it," said Leah Clearwater, "and see what happens to your cakehole."

He laughed. He hadn't meant to. It came up out of him short and loud and real, the first real laugh he'd laughed in nineteen days, maybe longer. And he felt it go down the line, and he felt it land in her chest, and he felt, very clearly, that she was trying very hard not to like it.

And failing.

He went down the corridor toward the back of the tent, toward the cold white room and the thing waiting in it. She came after him. He heard her bare feet on the flagstones behind him, and felt her, all the way down, every step.

The wolf in him walked beside her the whole way. Its ears were up.


---

### X. Leah

*The same evening*

The room at the end of the corridor was white, and cold, and it smelled like a hospital.

That was the first thing. After the warm kitchen smell of the main room, bacon and coffee and old leather and something baked with berries in it, this was like walking into a fridge. White tile on the floor, white tile on the walls, a drain in the middle of the floor that she didn't want to think about. A steel table bolted down next to it. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, flat and bright, so nothing in the room had a shadow.

The second thing was the shelves.

They went down the whole left side of the room, floor to ceiling, and they were full. Jars and boxes and cases, hundreds of them, every one with a white label in black block capitals. Some of the jars had things floating in them. Some of the boxes were made of dull gray metal and had locks. One jar near the bottom, about the size of a gallon of milk, was full of something black and slow that moved when she looked at it, and pressed itself against the glass on her side.

Leah stopped looking at that one.

There was a tooth on the third shelf. It was lying on a rack by itself, and it was as long as her arm, curved and yellow-white and wicked, and the label under it said BASILISK — FANG — UPPER LEFT — 1993.

"What's that from?" she said. "A dinosaur?"

"Close," said Harry, behind her. "Snake."

"*Snake?*"

"Big one. Long story. I was twelve." He went past her into the middle of the room. "Don't touch it. There's still venom in the hollow. It'd kill you in about a minute, and I'd be very cross about it."

She stopped looking at the tooth, too.

The third thing was the leech.

It was hanging in the middle of the room in a circle of cold blue fire. Standing, really, a few inches off the floor, upright, its arms down at its sides and its feet together, like somebody had stood it up in a display case at a museum. There was a ring set into the white tile around it, iron and silver and some other dark metal, with markings cut all the way around the rim, and the blue light came up out of the markings in a thin wall that went all the way to the ceiling. She could see the leech through it like she was seeing it through water. Its face was still frozen in that half-grin from the clearing. Its eyes were still rolling.

They stopped rolling when they found her. They stayed on her. Red.

She felt her lip come up off her teeth.

"He can see you," Harry said. "He can hear you. He can't do anything else. He can't even close his eyes." He was taking off his suit jacket. He folded it and laid it over the end of the steel table, and started rolling up the sleeves of his white shirt. "Sit, if you like. There's a stool."

There was a stool. It was by the shelves. Leah didn't sit on it. She went and leaned against the wall by the door with her arms crossed, as far from the leech as the room would let her, and watched.

He'd done something to his clothes. Out on the gravel bar it had been the suit, the long coat, the stupid shiny shoes. Now, as she watched, the shoes went dull and scuffed and turned into old brown boots, and the trousers went soft and faded and turned into jeans, and the white shirt stayed white but went thinner, softer, an old T-shirt with a faded logo on the chest. She couldn't read it. Something with a lightning bolt in it. He didn't seem to notice he'd done it. He just looked down at himself when it was over, like a man checking he'd remembered his wallet, and then took a notebook out of the drawer of the steel table, and a cheap blue ballpoint pen, and clicked it twice.

"Right," he said. He sat down on the edge of the table. He looked up at the leech in its circle. "Shall we?"

He didn't wave his hand. He didn't say anything. But the blue light flickered around the leech's head, and its face *moved*. All at once. The frozen grin went out of it like a dropped mask and its jaw worked and its lips pulled back and it took a breath, a huge, whooping, ragged breath like somebody coming up from underwater, and it *screamed*.

It was a horrible sound. It went right through the tile and into Leah's teeth. High and raw and furious, like metal tearing, and it went on and on, and Harry sat on the edge of the table with his notebook on his knee and waited for it to stop.

It stopped.

The leech hung in the circle with its head free and the rest of it still locked, and stared at Harry, and breathed hard through its teeth.

"Do you have any idea," it said. Its voice was low and accented, some accent Leah didn't know, thick and old. "Do you have *any* idea who I am. Who I *belong* to."

"Haven't the foggiest," Harry said. "That's rather the point of the evening."

"You will *burn* for this. You and your dogs. They will come for you. They will take you apart one piece at a time and they will make you *watch*—"

"Name?"

The leech spat at him.

It didn't get far. Leah watched it hit the blue wall and sizzle and slide down it like something dropped on a hot stove.

Harry looked at the place where it had hit. He wrote something in the notebook. Then he looked up at the leech again, into its eyes, and went still.

---

She didn't know what she was seeing at first.

He didn't move. He didn't do anything. He just sat on the edge of the table with the notebook on his knee and looked at the leech, right into its red eyes, and the leech looked back. And then the leech's face started to *change*.

The fury went out of it first. Then something came into it, something Leah had seen on a lot of faces but never on a leech's. It was the look of somebody who's just felt a hand on the back of their neck in a dark room. The red eyes went wide. The lips went back. A sound came out of it, low, a kind of moan, and it got louder.

Harry's face was blank. Completely blank. But Leah felt it.

She felt it down the rope. She'd been feeling him since the clearing, the whole time, like having a second heartbeat in her chest. Now that second heartbeat went somewhere. Went *in*. She felt him go into the leech the way you'd go into cold water, all at once, and then she felt it hit him.

It was like a flood. That was the closest she could get. Like standing at the bottom of a dam when the dam breaks. She felt it all come at him, a wall of it, faces and places and voices and *blood*, years of blood, centuries of it, so much and so fast and so *clear* that it felt like being there. All of it, every second, at once, and he was standing in the middle of it trying to read it like a man trying to read a newspaper in a hurricane.

She felt him stagger. In there.

His face didn't change. His hand on the notebook didn't move. But a thin line of red came out of his left nostril and ran down over his lip.

"Harry," she said.

He didn't answer. She felt him find his feet, in there. Felt him brace. Felt him start to *pick*, the way you'd go through a room after a flood, ignoring most of it, looking for something in particular. A name. A face. A town.

The leech was screaming again.

Then it was over. Harry blinked. He sat back. The leech sagged in the circle, as much as the circle would let it, its head hanging, and made a low, gasping, sobbing sound.

Harry put his hand up to his face and looked at the blood on his fingers like he didn't know where it had come from.

"Huh," he said. He wiped it on his jeans. He picked up the pen. "Marek," he said, and wrote it down. "Marek Hlaváček. Born near Olomouc, in Moravia, seventeen ninety-one, or thereabouts. He's not sure of the year, his mum wasn't much for calendars. Bitten in the winter of eighteen-thirteen, by a woman in a red coat, behind a field hospital, after Leipzig. He's been walking ever since." He wrote. "He's a hundred and ninety-two years old, and he remembers *every single day* of it, Leah. Every day. Perfectly. Every face. Every meal." He looked up at her. "Every meal."

Leah felt something cold go down her back.

"You were in his head," she said. "You went in his head. Like you were in mine."

"Bit further in than I was in yours." He clicked the pen. "Yours I just listened to. His I went into."

"Does it hurt him?"

"Yes."

"Does it hurt *you*?"

He considered that. He touched his upper lip again. "Bit," he said. "The going in's fine. It's the volume. They don't forget anything. Ever. It's all still there, all of it, sharp as the day it happened, every second of two hundred years, all stacked on top of each other with nothing filed and nothing faded. It's like trying to drink out of a fire hose." He wrote something down. "That's useful. That's very useful. Somebody back home is going to be very pleased with that."

"Somebody who?"

He gave her a look.

"Right," Leah said. "Party line."

"Party line."

She was quiet for a moment. She watched him write. Neat block capitals, the same as on the jars. LEGILIMENCY: EFFECTIVE. HIGH RESISTANCE TO NAVIGATION (CAUSE: VOLUME). NO OCCLUMENCY OBSERVED. RECALL: TOTAL. NOTE: OPERATOR BLEED AFTER ~40 SEC.

"Operator bleed," Leah read out loud. "That's you. Bleeding."

"That's me."

"You're writing down your own nosebleed."

"I'm writing down everything." He didn't look up. "That's the job. You write down everything. Even the stupid bits. Especially the stupid bits."

---

He did more after that.

Some of it she didn't understand. He walked around the circle with his hands in his pockets, looking at the leech from every side. He did something that made a thin line of light go out of his fingertip and touch the leech's bare forearm, and where it touched, the skin made a sound like a blade on a whetstone and a hairline crack opened, very thin, maybe an inch long. Something clear came up out of it. Thick, slow, glistening. Venom. Leah smelled it and her whole body went tight.

Harry watched the crack. He looked at his watch. He watched the crack close up again, the edges sliding together like a zipper, in eleven seconds, and wrote that down too.

He did it again in a different place, harder, and the crack was deeper, and it took nineteen. He wrote that down.

He asked Leah how they killed them. The wolves. She told him. Teeth. Pull them apart. Burn the pieces, fast, before they could crawl back together. She'd never said it out loud before, in a voice. It sounded different in a voice. He nodded and wrote it down like a recipe.

"Fire," he said. "Just ordinary fire? Wood fire? Matches?"

"Whatever's around. Jake carries a lighter."

"Huh." He wrote. "Fire's the thing, then. Any fire. Just enough of it, fast enough."

"Is that useful?"

"Very."

She didn't ask for who. She was getting the hang of it.

---

"Right," Harry said, after a while. He stood at the edge of the circle and looked at the leech, and the leech looked back at him with its head hanging and its red eyes full of something Leah would have called hatred if it weren't so scared. "This next bit's going to be ugly."

Leah uncrossed her arms.

"You can step out," he said. "Kitchen's down the hall. There's bacon in the cold box. There's a lot of bacon. You can come back after."

"What are you going to do?"

He looked at her.

"There are three curses," he said. "Where I come from. Three spells. They're illegal pretty much everywhere on earth. Use one on a person and you go to prison for the rest of your life. One of them makes you do whatever I want. One of them hurts. Just hurts. More than anything else there is. And one of them kills you." He said it all the same way. Flat. "I'm allowed to use all three. Where I work, it comes with the badge. And the people I work for need to know if they work on him. On his kind."

"Why?"

"Can't tell you that."

"Is it a war?"

He was quiet for a second.

"It's going to be," he said.

Leah looked at the leech. At the half-grin it didn't have any more. At the red eyes. She thought about the smell in the clearing. Shampoo. Coffee. The inside of a car. Someone who'd been driving home from work in Port Angeles, maybe. Someone who'd stopped for gas.

"I'm staying," she said.

He nodded, like he'd known she would. Then he turned back to the circle and lifted his right hand, and pointed one finger at the leech, and said one word.

"*Crucio*."

He said it quietly. She'd thought it would be loud. She'd thought there'd be light, or noise, some big ugly show of it. There was nothing. One word, spoken at a normal volume, like a man saying *excuse me* in a crowded room.

The leech came apart.

The body stayed exactly where it was, locked in the circle, every limb still. But its face. And its voice. Its head snapped back so hard Leah heard the sound of it, stone on stone, and its mouth came open, and what came out of it was past screaming. It was a sound she'd only ever heard once before in her life, in the clearing in June, when Jacob's bones broke and she'd heard it from inside his head. Except this went on. And on. And its eyes were open as far as they would go, and it couldn't close them, and she watched something go across them that she would see in her sleep for a long time afterward.

Harry was looking at his watch.

"Three," he said.

He dropped his hand.

The sound stopped. It just stopped, like a radio being switched off. The leech hung in the circle with its head back and its mouth open, shaking. Leeches didn't shake. She'd never seen one shake. It was shaking all over, a fine hard tremor, the whole length of it, like a tuning fork.

Harry wrote in the notebook.

CRUCIATUS: EFFECTIVE. FULL RESPONSE. 3 SEC APPLICATION. POST-EFFECT TREMOR (NOTE: NOT PREVIOUSLY OBSERVED IN SPECIES?)

He underlined NOTE.

Leah realized she was holding her breath. She let it out. She didn't move from the wall.

She was looking at his face. She'd been looking at his face the whole time. And there had been nothing on it. Not pleasure. Not anger. Not anything. It was like the curse had come out of a different man, somewhere behind his face, and this one had just stood in front of him and held the watch.

But she could feel him. Down the rope. She'd felt him the whole three seconds.

He'd gone somewhere. When he said the word, he'd gone into a room inside himself, a cold, flat, closed room, like a house where somebody's covered all the furniture with sheets. He'd gone in there and shut the door, and stood there in the dark while it happened, and she couldn't feel anything in there at all. Nothing. Not even him. Just a closed door with somebody behind it, very still.

Now he was coming out. She felt him do it. Felt him open the door, and step out, and close it again behind him. Carefully. Like it was a door he'd closed a lot of times.

"Do you like doing that?" she said.

He turned his head and looked at her.

"No," he said.

She felt it. True.

"Would you do it again?"

"Yes."

True, too.

They looked at each other across the cold white room.

"Okay," Leah said.

She didn't know what she meant by it. She'd figure it out later. She knew that she'd been inside Sam's head when he tore a newborn's arm off in June and felt how much he'd liked it, how good it had felt to him to finally have something to rip, and she'd felt Paul's head do the same thing, and Jared's, and her own. She knew what it felt like to like it. And she'd just been inside this man's head while he did the worst thing she'd ever seen anybody do, and there'd been nothing in there at all but a closed door, and a man standing very still behind it, waiting for it to be over.

She didn't know if that was better or worse. Like the rope. She kept running into that.

"The other one," she said. "The one that makes him do what you want."

"That one I need," Harry said. "That one I'm going to use for something. In a minute." He looked at the leech, still shaking. "Want to give him a second first."

"You're giving him a *break*?"

"I'm giving him a break because I want his voice to sound normal," Harry said. "Nice doesn't come into it."

---

She asked him things while they waited.

She didn't plan to. She just started. Maybe it was the room. Maybe it was the rope, which had been humming in her chest since the clearing and which got louder when she didn't say anything, like it wanted her to. She'd ask, and he'd answer, and every answer would go down the rope and land in her chest true, and she'd want to ask another one.

"Who was the snake?" she said. "The tooth. When you were twelve."

"Basilisk. Lived under my school. Ate a few people. Well, tried to." He was sitting on the edge of the steel table again with his boots swinging. "I killed it with a sword. It bit me on the way down. I nearly died."

"What stopped you?"

"A bird cried on me."

She stared at him. He held her eyes and shrugged, and it was true. It was completely, ridiculously true, she could feel it.

"Your school had a *snake*."

"My school had a lot of things. My school had a three-headed dog."

"Did you—"

"I didn't kill the dog. The dog was fine. I liked the dog." He swung his boots. "My turn."

"Your turn?"

"It goes both ways, doesn't it? You said." He tilted his head at her. "The rope."

She hadn't said that out loud. She'd thought it. In the clearing. She opened her mouth to tell him to get out of her head, and closed it again, because he wasn't in her head. She knew what it felt like when he was in her head. He wasn't. He'd felt it, too. Down the rope. The same as she had.

"Fine," she said. "Ask."

"Who's Sam?"

The room went very cold.

She'd known it was coming. She'd known since the clearing, since he'd stood there in his suit and said *lot of furniture in there, lot of sharp corners*. Sam was the sharpest corner there was. Everybody who'd ever been in her head had cut themselves on Sam.

She opened her mouth to say *nobody*. She felt the word get stuck. Felt her jaw lock up around it.

She shut her mouth.

"Sam Uley," she said. "He was my boyfriend. Since I was fourteen. We were going to get married. He phased first, before anybody, and he disappeared for two weeks, and when he came back he couldn't tell me anything, and I thought he was cheating on me. And then my cousin came to visit from the Makah rez. Emily. My best friend. And he looked at her." She heard her own voice. Flat. Calm. Like she was reading it off a card. "And that was it. He imprinted. Like Seth did. Like you—like whatever *this* is. He looked at her, and he stopped loving me, and he couldn't help it, and he married her last year, and I was a bridesmaid. And then I phased, and I had to live in his head. In his head, while he was thinking about her. For months."

Harry didn't say anything.

"So that's Sam," Leah said.

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she looked over at him. He was sitting very still on the edge of the table. His boots had stopped swinging.

"He's an idiot," Harry said.

"He couldn't help it."

"I know. I saw it in Balto's head. I know he couldn't help it." He looked at her. "He's still an idiot. He's an idiot for the two weeks he left you not knowing. He's an idiot for every day after that he didn't find a way to make it hurt less. He's an idiot because he could've had *you*, and he had you, and it took magic to make him let go." He paused. "You can check that, by the way. Down the rope."

She felt it go down the rope and land in her chest. True. All of it.

She looked away from him. At the white tile. At the jars. At the black thing in the big jar near the floor, which had stopped pressing against the glass and was just sitting there.

"I think I'm broken," she said.

She didn't know where it came from. She'd never said it out loud. Not to Mom. Not to Seth, though Seth knew, because Seth had been in her head. Not to anybody.

"Since I phased," she said. "Stuff stopped. Women stuff. It just—stopped. I think I can't have kids. I think I'm some kind of genetic dead end. I think that's why I never imprinted, why I'm the only girl, why—" She stopped. "I think I'm broken."

She waited for him to say *I'm sorry*. Everybody always said *I'm sorry*. She'd heard it in everybody's head.

"Maybe you can't," Harry said. "Maybe you can. I don't know. I'd have to look into it, and I'd want to ask first." He looked at her. "But that's a thing your body does, Leah. You're a wolf who runs at a hundred and eight degrees and heals like a starfish and turns into the better part of a ton of teeth when she's cross, and your body's had to make a lot of room for that. It's made some trade-offs. That's what bodies do." He held her eyes. "Broken's when something can't do what it's for. You came through my ward in a dead run with half a pack mind screaming at you to stop, by feel, from ten miles out, because something was pulling. That's about the least broken thing I've ever seen."

She didn't say anything.

She couldn't. Her throat had gone tight. She stood against the cold white wall with her arms wrapped round herself again and looked at the floor and felt it, all of it, down the rope, true, every word, and she didn't know what to do with it.

"Your turn," she said finally. Her voice came out rough.

"Go on."

"Have you ever—" She stopped. Made herself start again. "Was there ever anybody. For you."

"Once," Harry said. "I thought. Girl at school. Red hair. Brave as anything. Mean right hook." He smiled a little, at something she couldn't see. "We had a go of it, after the war. Then I took this job, and it took me away eleven months out of twelve, and she didn't want to marry a man she'd have to identify in a morgue in Bulgaria one day off a photograph. She was right. She's married now. Plays professional sport. Has a baby. I sent a very nice toaster."

"Do you still—"

"No." He shook his head. "I'd tell you. Can't not tell you, apparently. No. I'm glad for her. It was a long time ago."

True. All of it.

Leah looked at him sitting there on the edge of his steel table in his old T-shirt with the lightning bolt on it, with dried blood on his lip and a notebook on his knee full of notes about how fast a leech's skin closed up and how long it screamed, and she thought: *What* are *you?*

He looked up at her. He'd felt it. She knew he'd felt it.

"Working on that," Harry said. "Same as you."

---

The leech had stopped shaking.

Harry stood up off the table. He went to the leech's jacket, which was hanging off it inside the circle, and reached through the blue wall like it was nothing. Leah felt the hair on her arms stand up. He went through the pockets. A wallet that wasn't the leech's; it had a woman's driver's license in it, with a photo of a woman about forty with short gray-brown hair and reading glasses on a chain. Harry looked at it for a long moment. He put it on the table. A set of car keys. A cheap folding phone, silver, scuffed.

He flipped the phone open. He pressed some buttons. Leah watched his face.

"One number," he said. "There's only one number in it. Never called anyone else. Never got called by anyone else." He looked up at the leech. "Who's on the other end of this, Marek?"

The leech looked back at him and said nothing.

Harry didn't ask again. He just looked into its eyes. The leech moaned. It was shorter this time. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Harry's nose didn't bleed. He'd gotten better at it, Leah thought. In an hour. He'd gotten better at going into a two-hundred-year-old flood in an hour.

When he came out, his face had changed.

It had gone very quiet, the way the woods go quiet when something big is moving through them.

"Volterra," he said.

The word didn't mean anything to Leah. Then it did. Edward. Edward had said it, in the Cullens' kitchen, last spring, telling the story to the pack so they'd understand. Italy. A city on a hill. A tower. The people in the tower.

"The ones in the cloaks," she said. "They came here. Last June. After the newborns. There was a little blonde girl with them. She looked at Jake and he—" She stopped. "Edward said they're like the leech police. The ones who make the rules."

"Jane," Harry said. "The little blonde girl's called Jane. She makes you feel like you're on fire. All over. Inside and out. By looking at you." He said it like he was reading it off a shopping list he'd memorized. "She's in his head. He's met her. He's stood in that tower and she's looked at him and he's felt it." He tapped the phone against his palm. "He works for them. Sort of. They use people like him. He's a nomad. He walks around. He goes where he likes. And every so often, somebody in Volterra calls him on this phone and tells him to go somewhere and look at something, and in return they let him hunt in towns he's not normally allowed to hunt in. Nice towns. Towns with lots of tourists."

"They sent him *here*?"

"They sent him to look at the Waltons. The Cullens." He looked at her. "Specifically, to look at the girl. Bella. To see whether she'd been changed. There's some kind of deal, apparently. She knew about them, when she was human. They don't let humans know about them. So they told the Cullens to change her or they'd kill her. And they want to know which."

Leah felt sick.

"She's changed," she said. "She's—she's changed, she's fine, she's one of them now, she's got gold eyes, she's—"

"I know. I've seen her." He turned the phone over in his hand. "*He* hasn't. He got in last night. Came down off the mountains from the north, stopped in Port Angeles on the way for a—" He stopped. He looked at the wallet on the table. "For dinner. He was coming down this afternoon to have his first look at the house. Then he smelled two wolves, and ran, and ran right into my snare." He tapped the phone. "He's supposed to call this number by the end of the month. Tell them what he saw."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then they send someone to find out why." Harry looked at the leech. "And they've got someone for that, too. He's in his head. Demetri. A tracker. He can find anyone. Anyone at all, anywhere on earth, once he's got the—the *taste* of them, the way their mind feels. He finds them and he brings them back. It's his whole job." A pause. "Marek's very scared of Demetri."

Leah looked at the leech. Its red eyes had gone to Harry's hand. To the phone.

"So he calls," she said slowly.

"So he calls."

"And he tells them—what?"

"That the Swan girl's changed. That she's got gold eyes and hunts deer with the rest of them. That it's boring here, and wet, and it stinks of dog, and he's moving on." Harry flipped the phone shut. "That there's nothing to see. Nothing at all."

Leah looked at him.

"Why?" she said. "Why do you care what they hear about the Cullens?"

"Can't tell you that."

"Is it because of Nessie?"

He went still.

She hadn't meant to say the name. It had just come out. Nessie. The baby. The thing that every wolf and every leech in Forks would die for, the one thing that the Volturi, from everything Edward had ever said about them, must never, ever, find out about. She'd said it to a stranger in a tent with a notebook full of notes about how to hurt leeches. Her heart was going so hard she could hear it.

Harry looked at her for a long moment.

"It's partly because of the kid," he said.

True.

"Are you going to hurt her?"

"No."

True.

"Is anybody you work for going to hurt her?"

"Not while I'm here," Harry said.

He said it quietly. He didn't look away from her when he said it. And it went down the rope and hit her chest and it was true, and then some. It was the closest thing to a vow she'd ever felt from anybody, and it had come out of him like it had been sitting there waiting, and she felt him feel it come out, and not take it back.

"Okay," Leah said. Her voice shook. "Okay."

---

He did the third curse standing right in front of the circle, close enough to touch the blue wall, with the phone open in his left hand.

"*Imperio*," he said.

She watched it go into the leech. It was different from the other one. There was nothing on the leech's face, no screaming, no shaking. Its face just went slack. Smooth. Easy. Like somebody had wiped it with a cloth. The red eyes went soft and far away. Its mouth closed. The tension went out of its neck and its head came up and settled straight on its shoulders, and it looked at Harry with an expression Leah had never seen on a leech before, a kind of mild, dreamy, interested patience. Like a dog waiting to be told what to do.

"How's that feel, Marek?" Harry said.

"Good," said the leech. Its voice was soft. "It feels good."

"Lovely. Blink twice for me."

The leech blinked twice.

"Say *God save the Queen*."

"God save the Queen," said the leech, in its thick old accent, without a flicker.

Harry wrote something in the notebook with the phone tucked under his chin. Leah saw it. IMPERIUS: TAKES. Then he stood with his eyes on the leech's eyes for a long moment, and she felt him, down the rope, *settle*. Like a man getting comfortable in the driver's seat of a car. She felt him reach out and take hold of something, somewhere, and get a grip on it.

"Right," he said. "Now. You're going to make a phone call. And you're going to sound bored, Marek. Very bored. You've been standing in the rain for two days, looking at a house full of vegetarians. Your feet are wet. Everything stinks of dog. You want to go somewhere warm. Got it?"

"Got it."

"Good lad."

Harry pressed the button, and held the phone up to the leech's ear, through the blue wall, and the leech's hand came up out of the binding to take it. Leah went tight all over, watching that hand move. Harry didn't even glance at it.

She could hear it ringing. Wolf ears. It rang three times, very far away, very faint.

Then someone picked up.

"Marek." A man's voice. Smooth and deep and amused, with an Italian accent so soft it was almost a purr. Leah's skin crawled. It sounded like the voice of a man smiling. "You're early. I wasn't expecting you for another week."

"I'm done," the leech said.

It came out exactly right. That was the worst part. Leah had heard its real voice, screaming and threatening and spitting, and this was it, exactly, the same thick accent, the same weight, but tired. Fed up. Bored right through. And Harry was standing a foot away from it, perfectly still, with his eyes on its eyes, and his lips were moving.

Silently. Shaping every word, half a second before the leech said it.

"Done?" said the voice on the phone.

"The girl's changed. The Swan girl. She's one of them. Gold eyes. Hunts deer with the rest of them. Very tidy about it. Buries the bones." The leech sighed, a long, put-upon sigh, the sigh of a man who'd been standing in the rain for two days. "Boring as a church, Demetri."

"You saw her yourself?"

"From the ridge. Two nights running."

"And the others?"

"Same as ever. Eight of them, in the big glass house. The wolves stink up the whole valley. I don't know how they stand it." A pause. "I'm not staying."

There was a silence on the other end of the phone. Leah held her breath. She watched Harry. Harry didn't blink.

"Aro will be pleased," Demetri said finally. "He was curious about the girl. He'll want to hear it from your hand, of course. You'll come home for the solstice."

The leech's face didn't change. But Leah, watching Harry, saw something flicker across his. Just for a second. *From your hand.*

"Spring," said the leech. "I'm going south. Mexico, for the winter. It's warm. I'll come in the spring."

The silence was longer this time.

"Don't make me come looking for you, Marek," Demetri said. He said it pleasantly. Like a man telling a friend to drive safe. Leah's skin was crawling so hard it hurt.

"Wouldn't dream of it," said the leech, and closed the phone.

---

Nobody said anything for a while.

Harry took the phone out of the leech's hand. He put it on the steel table next to the dead woman's wallet. He stood in front of the circle, looking at the leech, and Leah felt him, down the rope, let go of whatever it was he'd been holding onto. Felt him take his hands off the wheel. The leech's face went slack, and then confused, and then, slowly, horribly, it understood what it had just done. She watched it understand.

Harry turned the blue light up, and the leech's face froze again, mid-understanding, and its eyes went back to rolling.

He went and sat down on the edge of the table. He picked up the notebook. He wrote for a long time.

IMPERIUS: SUSTAINED. FINE CONTROL (VOICE, AFFECT) ACHIEVED. APPROX 4 MIN. RESISTANCE ROSE STEADILY THROUGH APPLICATION. NOTE: SUBJECT IS STRONG-WILLED, OR SPECIES IS. TEST FURTHER.

Then, under that, in smaller letters:

TRACKER (DEMETRI) — MIND-BASED? RANGE UNKNOWN. TEST WARDS.

A-: READS BY TOUCH. TOTAL RECALL OF SUBJECT. "FROM YOUR HAND."

He underlined the last line three times.

"What does that mean?" Leah said. "*From your hand.*"

"It means the man in charge over there, Aro, reads minds by touch." Harry didn't look up from the notebook. "Takes your hand, sees everything. Everything you've ever thought. All at once. Like what I did to Marek, but faster, and without the nosebleed." He tapped the pen against the page. "So if Marek ever stands in front of Aro, and Aro takes his hand, Aro sees this room. Sees the snare. Sees me. Sees *you*."

Leah's mouth went dry.

"So he can never go back."

"So he can never go back," Harry said. "Not in one piece. Not with his memory in him."

"And Demetri can find him. Anywhere."

"So it seems."

"So what do you do?"

Harry closed the notebook. He looked at the leech in the circle. He looked at it for a long time.

"I've got until spring," he said. "Spring's a deadline. Before spring, he needs to be somewhere Demetri can't find him and Aro can't touch him." He tilted his head at the leech. "There's a short list of places like that."

"What's on the list?"

"Ash," Harry said, "is on the list."

He said it like it was weather.

"And the third curse?" Leah said. "The one that kills?"

"Not tonight. That's the end of the experiment, that one. I'm not done with him yet." He stood up and stretched, and something in his back popped. "Tomorrow. Maybe the day after. I've got a lot more questions."

Leah looked at the leech.

She thought about the woman in the photo on the license. Short gray-brown hair. Reading glasses on a chain. Somebody's mom, maybe. Somebody who'd stopped for gas in Port Angeles yesterday on her way home from work and never gotten home.

She looked at the leech's frozen face, and its rolling eyes.

She found out she didn't feel sorry for it at all.

She was still thinking about that when the rope in her chest *jerked*.

Not hard. But it moved. Something had happened at the other end of it. She looked up at Harry and found he was already looking at her, and his face had changed again.

"Your Alpha's here," he said. "Edge of the ward. North side. He's brought friends."

"How do you—"

"The ward told me." He was already reaching for his jacket. "The big lad. Balto. Your Layla. And—" He paused. He tilted his head, like he was listening to something very far off. "Something cold. One of the Waltons. Moving very fast." He shrugged the jacket on. It went long and black and turned back into a suit as it settled on his shoulders. His boots went shiny. "The one with the hair, I'd guess."

"Edward," Leah said.

"Edward." Harry looked at her. "He's going to want to read my head, isn't he."

"Yeah."

"Mm." He took a pair of sunglasses out of his inside pocket. Gold rims. Aviators. A new pair; she'd seen the old ones lying in the moss in the clearing. He looked at them, and then at her, and then he put them back in his pocket without putting them on. "Right," he said. "Let's go and say hello."


---

### XI. Jacob

*The same evening — the Bogachiel River*

Jacob had been sitting on the Cullens' floor building a tower out of wooden blocks when Edward stood up from the piano bench so fast he knocked it over.

Nessie had been helping. Nessie's version of helping was to wait until the tower was about as tall as she was and then put her hand on Jacob's cheek and show him a picture of it falling down, very slowly, with a crash and a lot of blocks bouncing everywhere, and then laugh, and then knock it down. They'd been doing it for an hour. Jacob would have done it for a year.

The bench hit the floor. Nessie turned her head. Everybody in the house turned their head.

Edward was standing in the middle of the room with his eyes on the north wall, like he could see through it.

"Seth," he said.

Jacob was on his feet before he knew he'd stood up. "What about Seth?"

"I can barely—he's at the very edge, he's three, four miles out—" Edward's face had gone strange. Tight. Listening so hard it looked like it hurt. "He's frightened. He's—Layla's there. Something's happened. Something hit her. There's a man—a man in a—" He broke off. His eyes went wide. "*Starlings*?"

Jacob was already going out the back door.

He phased on the lawn in a single stride. His clothes went to shreds. He didn't care. He landed on four feet with the wet grass under his paws and the pack mind opening up in his head like a door blown off its hinges, and it all came in at once.

Seth. Seth's head, Seth's memory, everything, the last five minutes, playing back on top of each other in a big messy pile the way it always did when somebody was panicking. A leech hanging upside down from nothing. A man in a black suit and sunglasses. *Lassie. Balto.* A badge. Layla appearing out of nowhere. Red light. Starlings. Fire. A crater in the ground. Seth pinned flat to the moss, unable to move, *unable to move*, and Jacob felt it like it was his own body. A gun. An explosion. Layla flying backward through the air.

And Leah.

Leah's memory was in there too, all of it, layered on top of Seth's, from a different angle, and Jacob saw the man turn around. Saw him take off the sunglasses. Saw his eyes. Green.

And felt it happen.

He'd felt it five times now, in other people's heads. Six with Seth. He'd felt it once himself, ten weeks ago, on a bloody couch with Bella dying across the room, when he'd looked at a baby and the whole universe had turned inside out and put her at the middle. He knew exactly what it was.

He'd never felt it go both ways.

He felt the rope go out of Leah's chest and into the man. He felt it hit something in there. Something big. Something with fur on it, that lifted its head. And then he felt it come back.

*Leah,* he thought. Not to anyone. Just the name. *Oh my God. Leah.*

She was running. She was miles off, flat out, going south, across the highway, toward the Bogachiel, following the rope, and he could feel the rope too now, through her, pulling like a hooked fish. He felt her not think about anything. He felt her running away from Sam and running toward this, both at the same time, so hard and so fast it was tearing her in half.

*Leah,* he thought at her. *Leah, what the—Leah, stop. Stop right—*

He felt the order come up in him. He hated it. He'd hated it every time. It was like a weight in his chest that wasn't his, an old weight, Ephraim's weight, his great-grandfather's, all the Alphas going back and back, and when he leaned on it, it came out of his mouth with a voice he didn't recognize and nobody in the pack could say no to it.

He leaned on it.

*Leah, STOP—*

And she was gone.

Just gone. Out of his head, like a light going off. She'd phased. Mid-stride. Before he could get the whole order out.

Jacob stood on the Cullens' back lawn in the rain with his heart going like a drum.

*Seth,* he thought. *Where are you. Exactly.*

---

It took him four minutes to get to the clearing. He'd never run so fast in his life.

Seth was there in his wolf shape, standing over Layla, who was sitting up in the ferns with her back against a fallen log and her wand across her knees. She had a sleeve missing from her sweater and moss in her hair and a bruise coming up on her cheekbone. She looked like she'd been hit by a truck. She looked up when Jacob came crashing out of the trees, and her hand tightened on the wand, and then she saw what he was and let go of it.

"Jacob," she said. Her voice was hoarse. "Hi."

*She's okay,* Seth thought at him, before he could ask. *She's okay, Jake, I checked, she says she's okay, nothing's broken, she says she's just—*

*I know. I saw.* Jacob looked around the clearing.

It looked like a bomb had gone off. Which, he guessed, one had. There was a crater in the middle of it six feet deep and ten across, raw dirt and splintered roots and rocks. There was a smaller crater, about twenty feet from where Layla was sitting, just a shallow scoop, like somebody had punched the ground. There was a hemlock with a ring of bark blown off it, still smoking a little. There was a heap of iron spikes in the moss at the edge of the big crater, all piled up neatly, like somebody had dropped an armful of firewood.

There was a pair of sunglasses lying in the moss by a cedar stump. Gold rims.

And there was the smell. Leech, everywhere, thick and sweet. And something else. Something like the inside of a battery. And under that, something he couldn't place at all. Pine and earth and cold stone. It was all over the place where the man had stood.

It smelled a little like the pack.

He heard Edward coming before he saw him. Edward came out of the trees at a dead run and stopped at the edge of the clearing so fast that his feet slid in the mud, and he stood there with his eyes going over everything, the craters, the spikes, the sunglasses, Layla. Jacob felt him reading Seth. Felt him reading Layla. Watched his face go white, or whiter.

"He did all of this," Edward said. "Without a wand."

"He *had* a wand," Layla said. "He never used it." She was looking at the big crater. "That one's mine. The rest of it's—" She stopped. "He didn't even move his feet."

Edward was quiet.

*I don't know,* Jacob heard Edward think, so clearly that for a second Jacob thought he'd said it out loud. That never happened. Edward never let anybody hear him. *I don't know what he is. I've never heard of anything like this. In a hundred years I've never heard of anything like this.*

*Where's Leah,* Jacob thought at him. *Can you hear her? She phased. I can't hear her.*

Edward closed his eyes. He listened. Jacob watched him listen, his whole body going still, his head turning slowly, south and east.

"No," Edward said finally. "Nothing. She's out of my range, or—" He opened his eyes. "Or something's blocking it."

---

They followed her scent.

It wasn't hard. It went in a straight line. Leah hadn't gone around anything. She'd gone over it, or through it. Down off the north bank. Across the Calawah at the shallows. Over the highway in a jump that had left her prints in the mud on both shoulders twenty feet apart. South, through the clearcuts, through the old-growth on the far side, down into the Bogachiel valley, where none of them ever went, because there was nothing down there. No leeches, no people, nothing to guard. Just trees and the river.

Seth ran beside him with Layla on his back. She'd climbed up there without being asked, at the clearing, and gotten a fistful of the fur at his neck in each hand and said *go* in a voice that meant she'd been on the backs of bigger things than him. Edward ran on Jacob's other side, silent, his face set.

Jacob was trying not to think.

He'd been Leah's Alpha for three months. He'd been in her head every day of them. He knew what she was like in there, every room of it, the broken glass and the locked doors and the one room at the very back she never went into, where Sam was. He knew how much she hated imprinting. How much she'd hated it every single time, in every single head, Sam and Jared and Paul and Quil and him. He'd felt her flinch when he'd imprinted on Nessie. He'd felt her flinch when Seth did.

And he'd felt what had just happened to her in that clearing.

*It went both ways,* Seth thought. He'd been thinking it over and over the whole run, like a song stuck in his head. *Jake, it went both ways. I felt it. He felt it too. He said—he said a bad word, he stumbled, it went into him and came back—*

*I know. I saw.*

*There's a wolf in him. Jake. There's a wolf in him, I felt it, she felt it, it* stood up*—*

*I know, Seth.*

*He's not one of us. He's English. He's—how can he have a wolf in him if he's not one of us?*

Jacob didn't know. Beside him, running, Edward was thinking about it too. Jacob caught the edge of it, the way you'd catch a radio station going past. *A Child of the Moon? No. Not under a new moon. They can't change at will, they can't do anything at will, they're animals. Caius hunted them nearly to extinction. Carlisle's seen one. This isn't—this doesn't fit—*

The land went down toward the river. The Bogachiel came up out of the dark, wide and brown and loud. There was a long gravel bar on the near bank. Leah's scent went straight down onto it.

And stopped.

It didn't fade. It didn't go into the water. It went about twenty yards out onto the stones, past a big pile of driftwood, to a place where the gravel was scuffed and kicked up, like somebody had fallen to their knees there. There was a smell there of Leah, human, not wolf. Fresh. And then about ten feet past that, it just *ended*. Like she'd walked into a wall.

There was no wall. There was nothing. Just the gravel bar going on toward the trees at the far end, empty, gray stones and driftwood and the river going by.

Jacob stood at the place where her scent ended and stared at nothing.

Layla slid off Seth's back.

---

She walked up next to him. She was limping a little. She stood where Leah's scent stopped and held her wand out in front of her, low, and closed her eyes.

Jacob watched her face. He'd seen her do magic a few times now. He'd seen her lift a piano and make paper birds for Nessie and catch a gravy boat. She always looked kind of the same when she did it. Focused. A little bit pleased with herself.

Now she looked like somebody who's put her hand flat on a wall and felt the whole building hum.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, *no*."

Jacob looked at Seth. Seth looked at Jacob.

"What?" Edward said.

Layla opened her eyes. She didn't take them off the empty air in front of her.

"There's a ward here," she said. "It's—it's huge. It's like a cathedral. There's a—there's three of them, layered, maybe more. Disillusionment on the outside, that's the easy one, that's what makes it look empty. Then a Muggle-Repelling—a No-Maj Repelling Charm, sorry, the British word, Gran used it—so anybody without magic who walks toward it will suddenly remember they left the stove on and turn around. And then under those there's something else, something heavy, I don't—" She stopped. She moved the wand a little to the left, then a little to the right, like she was feeling along a surface in the dark. "I don't know what that one is. I've never felt it before. It's—it's like Fidelius, kind of. It's like somebody took a Fidelius apart and used the pieces to build something else."

"Like the badge," Jacob said. He'd seen the badge. In Leah's head.

Layla looked at him. "Yeah," she said. "Exactly like the badge."

"Can you get through it?"

She laughed. It wasn't a happy laugh. "Jacob. I've broken wards that killed people. I've broken a ward in a vault in Chihuahua that was put down by a bruja in seventeen sixty and had eaten eleven treasure hunters. I could take that one apart in my sleep." She shook her head slowly. "I could stand here for a month and not find a seam in this. It doesn't have any. It's—" She stopped. She swallowed. "It's like the rest of him."

Jacob looked at the empty air.

Then he walked into it.

He just walked. Straight forward, the way Leah's scent went, one big paw in front of the other on the stones. He'd thought about it and decided he didn't care what Layla said. Leah was in there. Leah was his Beta, and his friend, mostly, on good days, and she'd run off into the dark with a rope in her chest after a man with a gun, and he was going to go and get her.

He took three steps.

And then he was standing at the place where Leah's scent stopped, facing the other way. Back toward the trees. Toward Seth, and Layla, and Edward. Who were all looking at him.

He hadn't turned around. He knew he hadn't. He'd been walking forward. He'd been walking forward the whole time, and he'd felt himself walk forward, three steps, and now here he was, facing backward, and the gravel under his paws was the same gravel he'd started on.

*What,* Seth thought.

Jacob turned around again. He walked into it again. Harder this time. Faster.

He ended up facing the trees again.

He did it four more times. On the fifth time, he tried running. He ended up exactly where he'd started, facing backward, with his own momentum carrying him two strides toward Seth before he could stop, and his heart going so hard it hurt.

"Jacob," Layla said gently. "That's the heavy one. It just turns you around. You can't get in unless he lets you in." She lowered her wand. "I'm sorry."

---

Edward's phone rang.

It was in his pocket. Jacob heard it buzz before it rang, the way he could always hear phones. Edward took it out and looked at it and answered it without saying anything.

"It's the hole," Alice said. Jacob could hear her perfectly. Her voice was small and thin and fast. "Edward. Where you are. Right now. That's the hole. That's the hole in the future, the one I can't see through, the one that's been there since the third. You're standing right on the edge of it. I can see you, and then I can't, it's like you're standing at the edge of a—a *page*, and the rest of the page is torn out."

"I know," Edward said quietly.

"What is it?"

Edward looked at the empty air.

"A man," he said. "I think. A man in a tent."

There was a long silence on the other end.

"Is Leah in there?" Alice said.

"Yes."

"Is she all right?"

Edward looked at Jacob.

Jacob didn't know. That was the worst part. He'd always known. For three months, he'd always known where every one of them was and what they were thinking and whether they were all right, and now Leah was somewhere on the other side of ten feet of empty air with a man who could stop wolves in midair and stand on nothing and catch fire with his hand, and Jacob didn't know *anything*.

He could feel the Alpha weight sitting in his chest. Heavy and useless. He couldn't use it. She was human. She was out of his head. And even if she weren't, even if she phased right now, he didn't know if he'd use it. He didn't know if he *should*. He kept seeing it, from inside her, over and over. The rope going out. The rope coming back.

Both ways.

"I don't know," Edward said into the phone. "I can't hear her. I can't hear anything past this line. I can't hear *him*."

"You can't hear him?"

"I couldn't hear him in the clearing, either. Not through Seth. Not through Leah's memory of him. I can see what he did. I can see his face. I can hear his voice. But every time I try to reach for what he was *thinking*, there's—" Edward stopped. "Nothing. A wall. Smooth. Like glass."

"Like Bella."

"Different," Edward said slowly. "Bella's a shield. She's just *not there*, to me. This is—there's someone there. Behind it. I can feel the shape of him. He's just not letting me in."

Seth whined, very quietly, at Jacob's side.

*Jake,* he thought. *What do we do?*

Jacob sat down.

He sat down on the gravel right at the line, where Leah's scent stopped, right at the place where the world turned you around. He sat with his front paws together and his back straight and his ears forward, and he looked at the empty air in front of him, and he didn't move.

*We wait,* he thought.

*For how long?*

*As long as it takes.*

Seth looked at him for a moment. Then he came and sat down too, beside him, a little bit behind, the way he'd sit next to Jacob on the Cullens' porch. Layla sat down on a log of driftwood beside Seth with her wand across her knees and put her hand in the fur on his shoulder. Edward put his phone away and stood behind them with his hands in his pockets. He didn't sit. He never sat when he was worried. But he didn't go anywhere either.

The rain came down.

The river went by.

They waited for forty minutes.

Then the air in front of Jacob rippled, like heat over a highway in August. It went soft and strange and then it opened, the way a curtain opens, without a sound.

And Leah walked out of it.

She was barefoot. She was in a black T-shirt and cutoffs, and her hair was wet and stuck to her neck, and her face was something Jacob had never seen on it in his life. He couldn't name it. He'd been in her head for three months and he couldn't name it.

The man was a half step behind her and to the left. Black suit. White shirt open at the collar. No sunglasses. His hands were in his pockets. He looked at Jacob, sitting on the gravel at the edge of his ward, and at Seth beside him, and at Layla on her driftwood log, and at Edward standing behind them all with his face like stone. He looked like a man who's opened his front door to find the whole neighborhood standing on his lawn, and is mostly wondering whether he has enough chairs.

His eyes went to Jacob last. Green. Very green.

"Evening," he said. "You must be the Alpha. I've heard so much about you."


---

### XII. Edward

*The same evening — the edge of the ward*

Edward reached for him the instant he stepped out of the air.

He couldn't help it. He'd been standing at the edge of nothing for forty minutes with Jacob's fear and Seth's fear and Layla's fury roaring in his head, and Alice's voice in his ear saying *a page with the rest of it torn out*, and the man was *right there*, ten feet away, a heartbeat and a face and a pair of green eyes. Edward reached for him with everything he had.

He hit glass.

It was the same as in the clearing, through Seth's memory. Smooth and cold and seamless, with something behind it. He could feel the shape of the mind on the other side the way you can feel a person standing behind a frosted door. He could feel that it was large, and very orderly, and very still. And he couldn't get a single word through.

Then the glass changed.

He'd never felt anything like it. It was as if someone on the other side had noticed him pressing his hands against it, and had turned around, and smiled, and reached over and switched on a radio.

A wail. A long, high, howling wail, a man's voice going up and up over a pounding wall of drums and guitars, so loud and so sudden that Edward actually took a step back on the gravel. It filled his head. It filled every corner of it. A hammering, galloping riff, the drums like hooves, the voice screaming over the top of it like something coming down off a mountain to burn a village. He knew it. He'd known it since 1970. Emmett had played it in the Jeep for most of that decade.

*Led Zeppelin,* Edward thought, dazed. *He's playing me Led Zeppelin.*

The man was watching him. His face hadn't changed at all. One eyebrow went up, very slightly.

"Bit of a fan, are you?" he said.

"He's—" Edward stopped. Everybody was looking at him. Jacob, Seth, Layla. Leah. "He's singing at me."

Jacob's head turned. *What?*

"In his head. He's—" Edward winced as the song hit the chorus. "It's 'Immigrant Song.' On a loop. Very loud."

"*Classic*," the man said. "Can't go wrong. I find it clears the sinuses." He tilted his head. "I'd turn it down, mate, but then you'd only go poking about where you're not wanted, and I'd have to put on Abba, and nobody wants that."

The song went on. Edward stopped reaching. It got quieter, like a car going away down a road, until it was just a faint thudding somewhere at the back of his skull. A warning. *I'm here. I'm still playing. Try me.*

He stopped trying.

---

He looked at Leah instead.

He didn't mean to. Or he did, but not the way he usually did. He looked at her because she was the one who'd walked out first, because she'd stopped walking at the exact edge of the ward, the line where Jacob had been turned around six times. One bare foot on either side of it. She stood there with her arms crossed and her chin up and her face doing that thing Jacob couldn't name. And her mind was wide open.

It was always wide open. Leah had never once, in all the months he'd known her, tried to keep Edward out. She'd told him once, in the Cullens' kitchen, when he'd apologized for hearing something she'd been thinking about Sam: *Why would I bother? Every dog on the rez has already seen it. What's one more?* She'd said it like an insult. He'd understood, later, that it was the closest thing to trust she knew how to give.

So he heard it all.

It came at him in a rush, the way it always did with the wolves, everything at once, the last hour folded up on top of itself. A tent. A tent that was bigger on the inside, with a kitchen, and a wall of records, and a long corridor. A white room. Cold. Shelves of jars. A tooth as long as her arm. A circle on the floor full of blue fire, with a nomad standing in it, red-eyed, a man with a thin face and a long nose.

He saw the man in the suit go into the nomad's mind. Saw the blood come out of his nose.

He heard a word, spoken quietly. *Crucio.* He heard what came out of the nomad afterward, and his whole body went still.

He saw a phone. A silver phone, open. He heard a voice on the other end of it, soft and smooth and amused, and he knew that voice. He'd stood in an alley in Volterra eight months ago with that voice at his shoulder, purring at him to come along quietly, and he would know it anywhere, for the rest of time.

Demetri.

He heard the nomad speak in a dead, bored voice that wasn't its own. *The girl's changed. Gold eyes. Boring as a church.* He heard Demetri say *Aro will be pleased.* He heard *from your hand*, and *don't make me come looking for you*, and he saw the man in the suit write something in a notebook and underline it three times.

He saw Leah ask a question. *Is it because of Nessie?* He saw the man go still.

*It's partly because of the kid.*

*Are you going to hurt her?*

*No.*

*Is anybody you work for going to hurt her?*

*Not while I'm here.*

And he felt what Leah had felt when she heard that. Felt it land in her chest, through whatever this thing was between them. Felt how true it was.

And then there were other things. Underneath. Things she'd said in that white room that had nothing to do with the nomad. Sam's name. A word, *broken*, and what she'd meant by it, and what the man had said back, and what that had done to her. Edward saw the edge of it. He saw the shape of it, the way you see the shape of a person in a lit window from the street.

He looked away.

He did it on purpose. He pulled back out of her head, out of that part of it, the way you'd step back from a door you'd opened by mistake. He'd never done it with Leah before. She'd never had anything in there she hadn't already given away.

The man in the suit was watching him.

"Mind your manners in there, mate," he said quietly. "Some of that's hers."

"I know," Edward said. "I'm sorry. I've stopped."

The man looked at him for a moment longer. Then he nodded, once.

---

Jacob phased.

He did it right there on the gravel, without warning, and came up on two legs, naked as the day he was born, steaming in the rain. He didn't seem to care. He was too angry to care. Edward could hear it in him, a great hot roaring pile of it, Leah and the rope and the starlings and Seth pinned to the moss and *I don't know anything, I don't know where she is, I don't know what he is*. He took one step toward the man in the suit.

"Who the hell—"

"Hang on," the man said. "Hang on, hang on." He held up one hand. He looked at the big pile of driftwood beside the gravel bar, a bleached gray log about the length of a man.

The log turned into a pair of sweatpants.

Gray. Cotton. Drawstring waist. They fell off the top of the driftwood pile in a soft heap at Jacob's feet.

"There you go," the man said. "Can't have a summit with the Alpha in the altogether. It's undignified. For me, mainly."

Jacob stared at the sweatpants.

Then, with the expression of a man who has decided to pick his battles, he picked them up and put them on.

"*Thank* you," Jacob said, through his teeth, tying the drawstring.

"Not at all." The man put his hands back in his pockets. "Now. You were saying. Who the hell what?"

"Who the hell are you."

"Harry."

"Harry *what*?"

"Just Harry," said the man. "For now."

"What are you doing here?"

"Wildlife management."

Layla made a noise on her driftwood log, a short ugly laugh with no humor in it at all. Harry's eyes went to her. They stayed there a moment.

"Miss Hollis," he said. "How's the head?"

"Go to hell."

"Been. Overrated. The food's terrible." He looked back at Jacob. "I'm here for my own reasons, mate. They're nothing to do with you. They're nothing to do with the Waltons."

"The—?"

"The Cullens. Sorry. Private joke." He shrugged. "I came for one thing, and your Beta and Balto very kindly chased it into my arms this afternoon, and I've got it now, and I'm grateful. That's all it was. I wasn't planning on any of you knowing I was here. I've done a very good job of it for nineteen days. I'd have done a very good job of it for another nineteen." His eyes went to Leah. Just for a second. "Plans change."

Jacob looked at Leah too.

"Leah," he said. His voice was different. Quieter. "Leah, are you—"

"I'm fine, Jake."

"You ran off. You ran off after him. I tried to—" He stopped. Edward heard him stop, heard him decide not to say *I tried to order you*. "I couldn't hear you. I didn't know what was happening."

"I know." She uncrossed her arms. "I'm sorry. I had to."

"Because of the—"

"Because of the thing. Yeah." She looked at him straight. "You saw it, Jake. You were in my head when it happened. You saw."

"It went both ways," Jacob said.

"It went both ways."

The two of them looked at each other for a long moment across the gravel. Edward heard them both. He heard the whole of it, going back and forth between them without a word said out loud. Jacob's fear. Jacob's anger. Under the anger, Jacob's memory of a bloody couch and a baby in Rosalie's arms and the whole world turning over. *I know what it is. I know you can't help it. I know you hate it. I know.* And Leah's answer, which was a single picture, held up and steady like a card: the man in the suit standing on the gravel bar with his hand flat on his chest saying *yes*, because he couldn't say *no*.

Jacob looked at Harry.

"You felt it," he said. "The same as her."

"The same," Harry said.

"You can't lie to her."

"Not a word. Tried. Jammed." Harry's mouth went crooked. "It's a bit of a novelty for me, honestly. I'm still getting used to it."

"And she can't lie to you."

"Neither of us can." Harry looked at Leah. "We found that out about a minute in."

Jacob was quiet for a long time.

"You held her down," he said finally. "In the clearing. You held her down on the ground."

"I did. I held Balto down too." Harry didn't look away from him. "They were coming at me with their teeth out, and I didn't want to hurt them, and that was the fastest way to make sure I didn't. I'd do it again." A pause. "I'd do it differently now. I'd take my sunglasses off first."

Something in Leah's mind, standing on the line, went very soft and very bright for a second, like a struck match. She crushed it out instantly. Edward pretended he hadn't seen.

---

"You know about the Volturi," Edward said.

Everyone looked at him. Harry turned his head. The music at the back of Edward's skull got a little louder, a little warning thump, and then settled.

"I know a lot of things, mate."

"You know about *Demetri*." Edward came forward a step, past Jacob, onto the gravel. He couldn't help it. "You put a nomad under some kind of—some kind of *control*, and you made him lie to Demetri. To his face. About Bella. And Demetri believed it." He could hear his own voice going fast and strange. "Do you have any idea what you've done? Do you have any idea how long Aro has been *waiting*, for an excuse, for anything, and you just—"

"Took it off the table," Harry said. "For now. Yes."

"*Why?*"

"Can't tell you that."

"Is it something you're planning? Against them?" Edward heard his own thoughts going ahead of his mouth. Leah's memory. The white room. *Is it a war?* And the man's answer, in that flat voice. *It's going to be.* "Someone's going to war with the Volturi. Aren't they. Someone you work for."

Harry's face didn't change. Not by a hair. The music in Edward's head didn't change either.

"You got that out of Leah," Harry said. "I knew you would. I let her watch anyway." He tilted his head. "So you know I told her I couldn't tell her. And I can't tell you, either. Except that I'm not a friend of anybody in that tower, and I'm not going to be. You can take that to your dad. He'll know what to do with it."

Edward stared at him.

He thought of Carlisle. Of the painting in Carlisle's study, the gold and red of it, Aro and Caius and Marcus on their balcony looking down, and Carlisle among them, from the years when they were his friends. He thought of Carlisle's face if anyone ever told him that something was coming for them. Something that could do what this man had done in that clearing with no wand, and that had people. People who were going to war.

He didn't know what Carlisle would feel. He didn't know what *he* felt. It went through him too fast to name. Something that was nearly fear. And under it, very small, something that he was almost ashamed to recognize.

Hope.

"I'd like a word with him, sometime," Harry said. "Your dad. Not tonight. When it suits him. I'll knock." He looked past Edward, at all of them, Jacob and Seth and Layla. "And I'd like this kept in the family. Your coven. Your pack. The other pack, if you have to, I know you've got some sort of arrangement. Miss Hollis. Nobody else. Not your cousins up in Alaska. Not the Irish lot. Nobody."

Edward felt the bottom of his stomach drop.

*Denali,* he thought. *He knows about Denali. He knows about Siobhan's coven. How does he—*

"You don't get to tell us what to do," Jacob said.

"No," Harry agreed. "I just get to ask nicely." He looked at Jacob. "This is me asking nicely, mate. I've had a long day."

---

It ended because Leah ended it.

She'd been standing on the line the whole time. One foot in, one foot out. Listening. Edward had kept carefully out of her head after the white room, but he could see her face, and he could see the way her eyes kept going to Harry, and away, and back, like a compass needle that won't settle.

Now she uncrossed her arms and stepped forward. Off the line. Out of the ward. Onto the gravel on Jacob's side.

Edward heard Harry's heart change. Just once. A single hard thump, out of rhythm. Then it went back to normal.

"I'm going home," Leah said.

Jacob let out a breath. Seth's tail thumped on the stones.

"Mom's making pies tonight," Leah said. "For tomorrow. She's going to want to know where I've been, and I'm going to have to tell her, and that's going to take all night, so." She looked at Jacob. "Can we go?"

"Yeah," Jacob said. "Yeah, Leah. We can go."

She turned around. She looked at Harry, standing a step inside his ward in his black suit with his hands in his pockets and the rain coming down on his shoulders and steaming off again.

"Thanksgiving's at two," she said. "My mom's house. In La Push. Seth'll give you directions."

There was a silence on the gravel bar so total that Edward could hear the river stones knocking together on the bottom of the Bogachiel.

Harry looked at her.

"Am I invited?" he said.

"You're invited."

"Does your mum know I'm invited?"

"She will."

He seemed to think about that. Edward watched him think. It was the first time all night Edward had seen him look like he didn't know what to do.

"Will there be pie?" Harry said.

"There's always pie."

"Then I'll be there."

It came out of him just like that. Edward heard it land in Leah, without even reaching for it, the way he'd hear a stone land in a still pond. *True.*

*WOOHOO,* Seth thought, at a volume that made Edward wince. *Thanksgiving! He's coming to Thanksgiving! Leah! Balto's getting a new—*

*Seth,* Jacob thought, *I swear to God.*

Layla stood up off her driftwood log. She stood there with her wand in her hand and her missing sleeve and moss in her hair, and she looked at Leah like she'd grown a second head.

"You invited him to *Thanksgiving*?" she said. "Leah. He blew me up. He threatened me. He threatened to have *Charlie's brain wiped*!"

"Allegedly," said Harry.

"*Allegedly?*"

"Charlie'll be there," Leah said. "He likes Charlie. He said so. He knows how Charlie takes his eggs." She shrugged. "And if he does anything to Charlie, I'll know. Won't I." She looked at Harry. "I'll know before he does it."

"She will," Harry said. He said it to Layla. "I'm afraid that's just true, Miss Hollis. And I'd sooner chew my own arm off than give her a reason. So you can sleep easy, on that one at least." He paused. "Bring the coffee. The good stuff. With the mountain on it."

Layla opened her mouth. Shut it. Her mind was a single long white scream of outrage with no words in it at all.

Then she turned around and climbed back up onto Seth.

---

They went.

Jacob phased, and his new sweatpants went to shreds, and Edward heard Harry say *oh, for God's sake* quietly behind them. Seth turned toward the trees with Layla on his back. Leah walked up the gravel bar, barefoot, human, with her shoulders very straight. Edward fell in beside her. He didn't say anything. Neither did she.

At the top of the bar, where the stones met the trees, she stopped.

She didn't turn around. She just stopped, and stood there, and put her hand flat on her chest, on the black T-shirt, right in the middle. And Edward heard her, without meaning to, without reaching, because she was thinking it so hard it came off her like heat:

*It's pulling.*

He turned around himself.

Harry was still standing on the gravel bar just inside his ward, exactly where they'd left him. His hands were still in his pockets. He was watching Leah's back. And Edward couldn't read his mind, and couldn't hear anything past that wall of glass but a faint, distant thump of drums. But he could read a face. He'd been reading faces for a hundred and five years, long before he'd ever heard a thought.

The man looked like someone standing on a railway platform, watching a train pull out.

Then the green eyes moved, and found Edward's, and held them.

And the music stopped.

All at once, like a needle lifted off a record. The glass wall in Edward's head went clear, just for an instant, just in one small place, as if somebody had wiped a circle in a frosted window with the side of their hand. And through it, very deliberately, one thought came. Plain. Steady. Pitched straight at him and nobody else, like a sentence spoken across a quiet room.

*If anything ever comes for that kid, I'll be there before you are.*

Then the circle frosted over. The drums came back, faint and far away.

And the air rippled like heat over a highway, and the gravel bar was empty.


---

### XIII. Harry

*Late that night — the Bogachiel River valley*

He felt her the whole way home.

He stood on the gravel bar inside the ward after the air closed and didn't move for a long while, and he felt the line in his chest pay out, slowly, north and then west, at the pace of a woman walking and then the pace of a wolf running, as she went up out of the valley and across the highway and away. Twelve miles. Thirteen. He felt the Calawah go under her. He felt the long dark run through the trees toward the coast. He felt the moment she reached the edge of the reservation, because something in her went tight, like someone squaring up to a door.

Then she phased. And walked. And stopped. And he felt something go through her that he didn't have a word for, a great hard rush of it, like a wave coming over a sea wall, and he understood after a second that somebody had put their arms round her.

Her mother, he thought. In a kitchen that smelled of pie.

He went inside.

---

The specimen room was the way he'd left it. Marek hung in the circle in the cold white light with his face frozen and his eyes rolling. The phone was on the table. The notebook was on the table. And beside the notebook was a wallet that didn't belong to anyone in the room.

Harry picked it up and opened it again.

*Carol Ann Pruitt. 4417 Lauridsen Blvd, Port Angeles. DOB 03/14/1961.* A woman with short grey-brown hair and reading glasses on a chain, squinting at a camera in a DMV office. A library card. A Safeway card. A photo, tucked in behind the license, of two teenage boys on a beach, both of them making faces.

He looked at it for a long time.

He couldn't give it back. That was simple. There was no version of this where a nice Englishman in a waxed jacket walked into the Port Angeles police station with a dead woman's wallet and a story. But there was a car somewhere, Marek's memory had been very clear on that, a blue Honda pulled off a logging road above Lake Crescent with the keys still in it. And there was a stretch of Highway 101 along the south shore of the lake where the road ran high and the drop was steep and people went over, sometimes, in bad weather, in the dark.

He'd see to it the car turned up there. Soon. With the wallet in the glovebox. It would be a lie, and it would also be an end, and the boys in the photograph would get to stop looking. People deserved to know where to stop looking. He'd learned that a long time ago, at the bottom of a lot of stairs.

He put the wallet in an evidence bag and sealed it and wrote *PRUITT — RETURN* on the label in block capitals and put it in the drawer.

Then he looked up at Marek.

"Spring," he said. "Don't get comfortable."

The red eyes rolled.

---

The mirror went cloudy at eleven.

Seven in the morning in London. Croaker never slept, as far as Harry could tell, or if he did he did it standing up, like a horse, in a cupboard somewhere on the ninth level. The long grey face came up out of the cloud with the stone wall behind it, and the pale eyes went over Harry once, top to bottom, the way they always did.

"You've been busy," Croaker said.

"Bit."

"Report."

So Harry reported.

He did it properly. Standing, hands behind his back, the way they'd taught him in the first year, in a flat, plain voice, every fact in its place. The Lariat. The trigger, sixteen-twenty-six local. The specimen: adult male, apparent age thirty, actual age one hundred and ninety-two, Moravian, nomadic, human diet. The retrieval. The circle. Legilimency, effective, with a list of caveats as long as his arm. Physical resilience, healing times, response to cutting charms. Fire. The Cruciatus. The Imperius.

Croaker listened without moving. When Harry got to the phone, the pale eyes sharpened.

"Demetri," Croaker said.

"A tracker, sir. From what I could get, he finds people by the feel of their minds. Range unknown. I'll be testing whether my wards can blind him." Harry paused. "And the man at the top. Aro. He reads by touch. Total recall of the subject, one contact. Anyone who goes back into that tower and gets a hand on them, he has everything they've ever seen."

There was a long silence in the mirror.

"Halloran's group," said Croaker at last, very quietly, "will want to hear that."

"Yes, sir."

"That alone. That *alone*, Harry, is worth the nineteen days." Croaker looked at him. It was very nearly approval. On Croaker it looked like a man trying to remember where he'd left his keys. "And Volterra believes their man is on his way to Mexico."

"Until spring. Then they'll want him home, to shake hands. He won't be going."

"No. He won't." Croaker made a note on something below the frame. "Very well. Good work. Very good work." He looked up again. "Complications?"

"None worth the parchment, sir."

It came out easy. It came out the way it had come out a thousand times in seven years, in a thousand rooms, to a thousand people, smooth as a stone off a riverbed. No jam. No lock in the jaw. Nothing.

So that was how it was, then. Just her.

"The local protector line," Croaker said. "You weren't seen?"

"No, sir."

"The Cullens remain unaware of you?"

"Yes, sir."

Croaker looked at him through the mirror for a long moment. Harry looked back. He'd been looked at by Croaker for seven years. He knew how to stand in it. He thought about the gravy boat, and Miss Hollis in her socks with her wand still in her hand, and the Waltons' clever son standing on the gravel bar with his face cracked open listening to Led Zeppelin, and about a woman with her bare foot on either side of a line. He kept his face exactly where it was.

"You look tired, Harry," Croaker said.

"Long day, sir."

"Take tomorrow."

"Thought I might. It's a holiday here. Thanksgiving."

"I'm aware of what it is." The old man's mouth moved. It might have been the third smile in seven years. It might have been indigestion. "Eat something."

"Planning on it, sir."

The mirror went grey.

---

Harry stood in front of it for a while, looking at himself.

Three lies in about forty seconds. To his own Head of Department. About the single most important thing that had happened on this operation, or any operation he'd been on in a year. For a woman he'd known six hours. He laid it out for himself in his head, plainly, the way he'd lay out a case file. Unspeakable compromised by local entanglement. Mission security degraded. Operative concealing contact with category-preserve subjects. Operative concealing a Statute breach. Operative concealing *himself*, and the reason.

He'd seen men burned for less. He'd done some of the burning.

He waited to feel something about it. Fear. Guilt. The cold flat voice that lived in the room with the sheets over the furniture, telling him what to do next.

Nothing came. Just the line in his chest, humming.

He went and put the kettle on.

---

He made tea. He drank it standing at the range, the way he'd eaten breakfast seventeen hours ago, a lifetime ago, when the wolf was lying in the dark with its head on its paws and the worst thing in his day was going to be the rain.

Then he went to the turntable. *Led Zeppelin II* was still on it, the way he'd left it that morning, side two, the needle parked. He turned the record over. He put the needle down at the start of side one and stood there with his tea while it played through, the big swaggering riffs and the howling and the blues, three songs' worth, and he didn't really hear any of them.

Then the fourth one started.

It was quieter. An organ, slow and swelling, and an acoustic guitar coming in over it, and Plant's voice when it came in was soft, nearly gentle, nothing like the screaming that came before. Harry had always thought it was a strange song to put in the middle of all that noise. Like somebody setting down a cup of tea in the middle of a bar fight. He'd always skipped it, mostly.

He didn't skip it.

He stood in his kitchen in his socks with his tea going cold and listened to it all the way through. And somewhere around the middle of it, he felt the line in his chest go soft.

It never went slack. It was a line tied at both ends, and it would hold, he was very sure of that now, it would hold if he went to the far side of the earth. But it went *soft*. Easy. The way a rope goes soft when the boat at the other end of it has stopped pulling, and come to rest, and is lying quiet on the water.

She'd lain down. He felt it. Thirteen miles away, in a house in La Push that smelled of pie, on a couch, probably, with a blanket over her, with her mother's hand on her hair maybe, Leah Clearwater had stopped fighting it, just for tonight, and gone to sleep.

And as she went, he felt something else, very faint, from the far end of the line. He felt her wolf lie down too. Turn round once, the way wolves do, and lie down in the dark inside her with its head on its paws. Facing southeast.

Facing him.

Harry put his tea down.

He looked inside, the way he did every morning. Under his breastbone. In the dark.

His wolf was lying down, too. For the first time since the clearing. It had come down off its feet at some point while he wasn't watching, and turned round once, and settled. Its head was on its paws. Its ears were up. It was facing northwest, toward the coast, toward thirteen miles of wet black forest and a house with the porch light on.

It was awake, and waiting. It was very patient about it.

"Yeah," Harry said to it, quietly, in the empty kitchen. "Yeah, all right. Me too."

---

He went back to the specimen room before he went to bed, because there was one more thing to do and he'd never been able to sleep with a thing left undone.

He opened the notebook on the steel table. He turned back past the night's work, past all the block capitals about healing times and fire and Demetri and *from your hand*, past nineteen days of notes about deer and patrol schedules and the price of film. Back to the second day. He found it near the bottom of a page, in pencil, already a little smudged.

*Wolves: some kind of bonding behaviour. Sudden. Very strong. Look into it.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took out the cheap blue ballpoint and drew a single neat line through *Look into it*. And under it, in the same block capitals he used for everything else, specimens and curses and dead men's names, he wrote one word.

*LEAH.*

He looked at that for a while, too. It sat there on the page in blue ink, plain, and true, and he found he didn't want to write anything else under it. He couldn't think of anything that would be true enough.

So he closed the notebook, and turned off the cold white light on the man in the circle, and went to bed.

Tomorrow was Thanksgiving. He'd been invited.

There was going to be pie.
