# Chapter Three: Bad Moon Rising

She came down the lake road at a quarter to six with frost in her eyebrows and a night's sleep owed to her, and the first thing she did in room six was untie the cord from her ankle and sit on the orange bedspread with the little leather pouch in her lap.

She didn't open it. Soft old leather, dark with handling, the drawstring knotted in a way she couldn't have untied in the dark if she'd been paid to. About as heavy as a deck of cards. It didn't hum or glow or do anything a thing like that should have done.

*If you want me, say my name into it.*

She put it in the nightstand drawer, under the Gideon Bible, and showered, and dressed, and got as far as the door. Then she went back and took it out from under the Bible and shoved it in the front pocket of her jeans. Wes had a key to every door in the building, and room six's lock was a joke. That was the reason.

She told herself so all the way across the street.

Dot looked at her over the flat-top for three long seconds.

"You sleep?"

"No."

"Mm." Dot slapped eight strips of bacon on the grill. "Pans."

---

The pouch rode in her pocket all day Monday and all day Tuesday, pressing against her hip bone every time she leaned over the sink. On Tuesday night, at eleven, sitting cross-legged in the middle of the bed with the heater clanking, she gave up and worked the drawstring loose with her thumbnail.

The mirror fit in her palm. Its silver frame had gone black wherever fingers had held it, and the glass had a faint milky haze around the edge, like breath that never cleared. A tired woman with wet hair looked out of it at her, and behind her shoulder the moose in its lake.

She felt like a kid playing telephone with two tin cans.

"Harry," she said to the glass.

For long enough that she started to put it down, nothing. Then her own face went soft and cloudy, like breath on a window, and cleared, and it was his. Too close, lit from below by a hard white lamp, with a wall of paper behind his head — shelves and stacks of it, leaning. His hair stood up on one side like he'd been dragging his hand through it for hours, and there was a smear of black ink along his jaw.

"Clearwater." His eyes went over her face fast, the way you'd check a dog for burrs after it came out of the brush. "You all right?"

"Fine."

"Good." He let out a breath. "Sorry. Fine. Good. Hello."

"Where are you?"

"Geneva. Under it, technically. There's an office in the mountain." He tipped the glass, and she got a view past his shoulder of a vaulted stone ceiling and, at the edge of the lamplight, a tall pale woman in a gray suit with her hair scraped into a bun, holding a clipboard and looking straight into the mirror. "Solveig. She's been standing there nine hours. I think she sleeps upright, like a horse."

"Form eleven-B," said Solveig Brekke, in the voice of a woman reading a parking ticket. "Section four. You have left it blank."

"Section four's where I describe the residual magic at the scene in my own words," Harry said. "My own words are 'a lot.'"

"That is a quantity, Lord Potter. Section four requires words."

Leah laughed before she could stop it. Short, out of her nose.

He turned the glass back to his own face. He looked, for a second, very pleased about something. Then he got it off his face.

"What can I do for you?"

She'd had it ready since the rock. She'd run it in her head over the pans for two days. The glass looked at her and every word of it went.

"Third date," she said.

He waited. He didn't help her. She hated him a little for that and liked it more than she wanted to.

"My choice this time."

"Course."

"I want to see the lodge. The one with the green roof. That you maybe bought."

"Possibly bought."

"And the chef. And the chocolate he's guarding." She held his eyes through the glass. "Thursday. After close. I'll be on your dock at nine."

"I'll send the plane."

"I'll swim."

A pause. Behind him, faintly, a pen scratching.

"It's October," Harry said. "There's ice on the shore. It's two miles across the bay."

"I swam Admiralty Inlet in the dark with a container ship going by. I can swim a lake."

He looked at her for a long moment, and the corner of his mouth went up. "Nine o'clock. I'll leave the light on."

"Fine."

"She's coming back with a stapler. I've got to go."

"Harry." He looked back. She didn't know what she'd been going to say. "Section four. Put 'a fuck of a lot.' That's words."

He laughed, loud, surprised out of him, and somewhere off to the side Solveig said "*Lord Potter*" like a door slamming, and the glass went cloudy, and it was her own face again, grinning at herself in a motel room at the end of the world.

The moose watched her.

"Don't," she told it.

---

At a quarter past eight on Thursday Dot said, without looking up from the flat-top, "Pie in the walk-in. Bottom shelf, behind the gravy. It's not for you."

Leah stopped with her hand on the back door.

"Lodge cook came over Tuesday," Dot said. Scrape. "Skinny fella. Bought four for the freezer. Said his boss eats them in his sleep." Scrape. "Seems to me if somebody was going that way they might take him a spare so he doesn't have to come back over here and look at me like I'm going to hit him."

"Is he scared of you?"

"Everybody's scared of me. Bottom shelf." Scrape. "Box comes back."

---

She went a quarter mile up the shore past the last house to a shelf of rock with deep water off the end of it, and stripped, and bagged her clothes with the pie on top, and stood there naked in the cold looking at a bundle too big to tie to her ankle.

Then she phased and picked it up by the knotted handles in her teeth, as gently as Seth had once carried a baby rabbit across the whole rez to the vet in Forks, and walked off the rock into the lake.

It took twenty-four minutes. The cold closed on her chest like a fist, and the fire came up to meet it, and she swam with her head high to keep the bag dry. Halfway across, a loon surfaced off her right shoulder, looked at her with its red eye, said something rude, and went under without a ripple.

The light on the far shore pulled apart into pieces as she came on. A floodlamp at the end of a long dock. The yellow floatplane tied alongside. A boathouse. And up a slope of frosted lawn, the lodge, long and low, built of logs so big she couldn't imagine the trees, with a green steel roof and a wall of windows glowing gold.

There was a man sitting on an upturned bucket at the end of the dock in a gray sweater, elbows on his knees, the way her dad used to sit on the porch steps waiting for her to get home from a date. A white towel with a monogram on it was folded over one knee. He watched her swim the last fifty yards with his head tilted.

She came up into the shallows at the base of the dock with water pouring off her, set the bag down on the planks very carefully, backed up two steps, and shook.

He'd been sitting exactly where her shake would get him. She hadn't planned that. She'd absolutely planned that.

He sat on his bucket with his hair plastered to his forehead and lake water running off the end of his nose and looked at the towel, soaked through.

"Right," he said. "I'll go and get another towel, shall I."

She chuffed at him. She couldn't help it.

She took the bag around behind the boathouse and phased, and dressed in the dark, and came back up the lawn with her hair dripping down her back and the pie box under her arm. He was waiting with a second towel. She didn't need it. She took it anyway, and put it around her shoulders, and didn't use it.

---

The chef was waiting at the kitchen door.

His name was Philippe Arsenault, he was from Montreal by way of a restaurant in Gastown with a three-month waiting list, and he took the pie box out of her hands before anybody could say anything and held it against his white jacket like a man receiving a flag at a funeral.

"She sent this? For me?"

"For you," said Leah. "Not for him."

"I have been trying to make that crust for six days. Lard. Butter. Vodka in the water." He made a small helpless shape with his free hand. "I asked her. She said, 'Practice.'"

"She doesn't care," said Leah.

"No," Philippe agreed, almost dreamily. "She doesn't." He turned to Harry, and his face shut like a refrigerator. "Dinner in ten minutes. You will not come into my kitchen."

"I only came in once."

"You came in four times. You ate the gremolata out of the bowl with a spoon."

The main room went up two stories to a ceiling of peeled logs. There was a fireplace of round river stones at one end big enough to walk into standing up, and at the other the wall of windows, looking across black water to Turnagain strung along the far shore in a thin line of yellow. She could pick out the gas station, the Prospector's half-dead neon, and one bulb she was nearly sure was the one over the door of room six.

There was a lake trout as long as her leg mounted over the mantel. There was a bearskin in front of the hearth, a black bear, with its head still on and its mouth glued open in a snarl.

She looked at it.

"Came with the place," Harry said. "I apologize to it every morning."

"You *apologize* to it."

"Seemed only right." He looked down at the bear with her. "I rang Hargreaves Monday. He bought it. The lodge, the plane, nine hundred acres of hillside. And Philippe, on a contract Philippe says he never signed." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I've told him he can go. He says he's in the middle of something with the pie crust."

She'd been angry about the money in the bar. She'd done the math on the rock, about dishes and five and a half million years. Standing on a dead bear with a fire going and the town lights across the water, she found that mostly she was hungry.

Philippe fed them for an hour and a half. Squash soup in bowls the size of hubcaps. Bread with salt crystals in the butter. The risotto — *the* risotto, Harry said, with total conviction — yellow with saffron and mushrooms Philippe had picked behind the boathouse that morning. A side of Arctic char roasted on a plank. A rack of elk sliced pink, the bones sticking up out of it like masts.

He'd been told. It didn't matter. He watched her take a fourth helping of the elk, and she saw go across his face the look her grandmother used to get at family dinners — a cook watching her food get eaten by somebody who meant it — and he went back into the kitchen without a word and came out with another rack.

"He likes you," Harry said, through elk.

"He hates you."

"Thoroughly. It's very restful."

The chocolate came last, on a silver tray, like evidence in a trial. The flat gold box from Brussels, its ribbon retied very badly and chewed at one end.

"Four times," said Philippe, pointing at the ribbon. "I put it on the high shelf. I put the flour in front of it. Every morning, it is somewhere else." His voice dropped. "He *licked* the box."

Leah opened it. Four rows of six had become four rows of five.

"I only had the ones on the end," said Harry.

"Of every row," said Philippe, and went back to his kitchen.

The hazelnut ones were in the third row. She bit one in half, and the shell cracked and something soft and nutty and burnt-sweet came out of it, and she sat at the long table with the fire going and shut her eyes. When she opened them he was looking at her the way he had over the caribou.

She didn't say a word about it. She didn't need to.

---

Philippe went to bed at eleven and took Dot's pie up the stairs with him. They heard his lock turn.

Harry put another log on the fire, by hand, with a grunt, and lowered himself onto the bearskin with his back against the hearthstones and his long legs out in front of him. Leah sat a couple of feet off with her knees up and her back against the end of the couch. The fire was very hot on the side of her face. She didn't move away from it.

"Third date," she said. "How are you a wolf?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might dodge again. Then he talked to the fire, plainly, the way he'd told her about the cupboard under the stairs.

"My dad could turn into a stag," he said. "My godfather into a big black dog. Their mate Remus was a werewolf, the moon kind, and at school the two of them taught themselves to change so they could run with him at the full moon. A werewolf won't bite a stag or a dog." He picked at a tuft of bear fur. "Animagus, it's called. A wizard who can turn into one animal, always the same one. It's hard. Most people who try end up with whiskers that won't go away."

"So you're one."

"Since I was twenty. I wanted a stag. Same as my Patronus — the silver thing you saw on the mountain. Same as my dad." He smiled very slightly. "You don't get to pick. It's whatever you are underneath. I did the last bit in a field in Yorkshire in a thunderstorm, and came out a wolf. Black. Big for a wolf, about the size of a Great Dane."

"Black like your godfather."

He looked at her.

"You said he was a big black dog," she said.

"Yeah." His voice changed, very slightly. "I thought about that. A lot."

"So how'd you get *that* big?" She meant the thing on the branch. A paw bigger than her head.

"I stayed in."

She waited.

"There's a rule," he said. "A day's fine. A week if you have to. Any longer and the animal starts doing your thinking. You stop thinking in words, then you stop missing them, and then somebody comes looking for you one day and you don't know your own name." He tugged the bear's fur. "There's a wizard in Bohemia in the fourteen-hundreds who went in as a hawk for a long weekend and stayed nine years. They never got him back. There's a painting."

"How long did you stay?"

"Eleven months."

Leah stared at him.

"I was twenty-one. The war was four years over, and everybody I knew had gone back to their lives, and I couldn't buy a pint of milk without somebody crying on me in the queue." He was looking at the fire very hard. "There'd been a girl. She'd waited for the boy I was at sixteen, and the bloke who came back from the war was somebody else. Nobody's fault. So one night in March I went up to the Highlands to run for the weekend. Above Loch Ness, where there's nobody for thirty miles." His voice had gone very level. "First week, I didn't want to come back. By summer I'd stopped thinking about it. I remember bits. Red deer on the hill at dusk. Salmon in the river. Sleeping in the heather when it was out — the whole mountain goes purple. And I got bigger. A bit bigger every month, and nobody there to notice. The wolf ate what I fed it. I fed it everything."

Leah thought about the Fraser. The sockeye on the rocks. Nine hours on frozen stone without a dream in her head. Six days, she'd lasted, before she walked into Turnagain, and somewhere around the fourth one she'd stopped counting.

"Who found you?"

"Hermione. In February. Ron had spent ten months looking in pubs. Hermione went to the library, and then she went to Scotland with a tent and a great many sandwiches and sat on a rock on the last mountain anybody'd seen me on. And when I came over the ridge she didn't call my name or come at me." The corner of his mouth went up. "She opened a book and read it out loud. A history of our school."

"She read you a *history book?*"

"Eleven hundred pages. Three days, in the snow, in the most irritating voice she owns. There's a chapter on the plumbing." He laughed then, quietly. "I lay in the heather sixty yards off and listened. I didn't know what the words meant anymore, only that I'd heard that voice say them before. And somewhere in the plumbing I remembered I'd hated the plumbing the first time round. And then I remembered my name."

The fire cracked. Leah found she'd stopped breathing, and started again.

"Most of me came back," Harry said. "The wolf came too. That's the bit that's not meant to happen. I could still smell everything. I couldn't get cold. And I'd grown this." He pulled up his lip on the left side with one finger like a man at the dentist, and there was the canine, long and curved and white. "And the wolf stayed the size it got to. Hermione says I left the door open too long and something walked in and liked the furniture. Ron says it was the Highland sheep."

Leah sat with her arms around her knees, looking at him.

"I'm not going to stay in," she said, too sharp.

He turned his head.

"I know what you're doing," she said. "Telling me that. I slept in my fur for six days on the way up here. I'm not stupid."

"I never said you were." He held her eyes. "And I'd be the last man on earth with any right to tell you what to do. It's lovely in there, is all. And quiet. And quiet's very hard to come out of."

"I came *out.* I got a *job.*"

"You did." He tipped his head back against the stones. "You wash Dot's pans. That's the most human thing I've ever heard of anybody doing on purpose."

---

"The clothes," she said, later. "You said that was third date too."

"Comes with the Animagus. Whatever you're wearing goes with you and comes back when you do. Nobody knows where it waits in between. There's a man in London who's been trying to find out for thirty-one years. He's very bitter."

"Show me."

He got up and took two steps back onto the bare planks, in his gray sweater and dark trousers and gray wool socks with a hole in the left heel.

"Don't blink," he said.

She didn't. It didn't matter. One moment there was a man in his socks, and the next, filling the space between the hearth and the long table, stood the black thing from the tree, ears brushing a crossbeam so that he had to duck his head under it. No crack of bone, no rip of cloth, no burst of heat. Nothing on the floor. His tail swept once and knocked a cushion off the couch.

Then he was a man again, on the same spot. The hole was still in the heel of the sock.

"Ta-da," said Harry.

Leah picked up the cushion his tail had knocked off and threw it at his head as hard as she could. He caught it.

"Do you know what I lost?" she said. "The first month? My dad's Creedence shirt. The real one, from the 1970 tour, so old you could read a newspaper through it. I slept in it every night after the funeral. And Seth stuck his head in my door at two in the morning and said Sam was on the phone, and I phased in my own bed." Her voice went flat. "There were pieces of it in the curtains."

He'd stopped smiling. He stood there holding the cushion.

"And you get to keep your *socks.*"

"I'm sorry," he said. And then, carefully, "About the shirt."

"Yeah." She looked at the fire. "Me too."

---

Later, lying on her side on the bear with the box of chocolates between them, she asked him about vampires.

"You said yours have capes."

"Some. Most look like a bloke who doesn't get out much." He was turning a chocolate over in his fingers. "I've had to go after four. A family in Tirana that'd been working through the old people's homes for a hundred years, a few at a time, where nobody'd ask. A woman in Marseille who kept tourists in her cellar like a larder." He ate the chocolate. "I didn't enjoy any of it."

"Killing them?"

"Meeting them." He looked at the fire. "I've nothing against what anybody is. I had a werewolf for a teacher. I've a mate who's a quarter giant. But call me mad — I've never trusted anything that has to look at me and decide not to eat me. Every day. That's a great deal of deciding."

"Ours say they don't drink people. Some of them. They say they eat animals."

"Through the neck. Warm." His mouth twisted. "I ate half a caribou in front of you on Sunday and I'd do it again tomorrow. That's dinner. Blood's the bit that carries the dinner round, Leah. It's the post van. Living on it's like trying to eat the post van." He shook his head. "I've never understood it. I don't think anybody sane could."

"*Thank* you," said Leah.

It came out of her before she could stop it, and then the rest came after, fast and bitter. "Everybody at home acts like it's this noble thing. *Oh, they've got such self-control.* They drink deer. They drink *bears.* They live in this house that smells like bleach and burnt sugar and something dead, and they don't eat or sleep or breathe, and people *want* to be them. Like it's romantic. Like it's a big beautiful sacrifice." She shut her mouth. She'd said too much — no names, no places, but she could feel the edges of it behind her teeth, the glass wall and the white couch.

He didn't ask.

"*Want* to be them," he said, after a while. "That's the bit I'd struggle with. Somebody with a pulse."

"Yeah."

He shook his head slowly and left it there. And she lay on the bear thinking about Bella Swan on a white couch with her arms gone to sticks and something eating her from the inside, and thought: *I'm not the only one who thinks it's crazy.* It was a small thought. It was very satisfying.

---

"When I go home," she said, much later, with the fire down to coals and the town across the water mostly dark.

She'd meant to say *if.* She let it stand.

"When I phase near them again — my brother, Jacob — they'll see all of it. The mine. Kell. You. Magic. Everything you've been filling out forms about." She was looking at the ceiling. "There's no stopping it. That's how it works."

He didn't say anything. He'd turned from the fire to look at her.

"You stay on the step," she said. "By the door. So is there a lock? From my side? Something I can learn?"

"Occlumency." He said the word carefully, like he was setting down something heavy. "Yes."

"Could you teach me?"

"I could try." He rubbed his jaw. "I had one teacher for it, at school. In a cellar. He went through my head like a burglar and sneered at what he found. I learned nothing from him but how to hate a person properly. I learned the rest on my own, later, when I had to."

"Then teach me the later way."

"You'd have to let me knock," he said. "To practice. I push, you hold, we see what gives. And the first few times you'll want to tear my throat out."

"I want to tear your throat out most of the time anyway."

"Fair." Then, quieter: "I'll stop when you say. Every time."

"I know you will."

She hadn't known she knew it until she said it.

"I used to crack my teeth," she said, to the ceiling. "In Sam's pack. Holding stuff down. I cracked a molar once, I clenched so hard. The dentist in Forks asked if I chewed ice."

He was quiet. Then: "Holding's the hard way. It's like keeping a door shut with your back. Your legs give out in the end. What I'd teach you is a door that shuts on its own." He looked at the coals. "Weeks, if you're very good."

"I'm faster than you."

"At running."

"At everything," said Leah, and heard him huff a laugh, low, through his nose.

---

They swam back together under the stars, her with the empty pie box in the bag in her teeth — Philippe had washed it and folded it flat and tied it with its own string — and him a black head the size of a barrel three yards off her shoulder, moving through the water with no splash at all.

Halfway across, the loon came up again. It looked at her. It looked at him. It went straight back under without a word.

*Smart bird,* Leah thought, and remembered he could hear her, and felt her ears go hot under the wet fur.

"Very," said Harry, low in the water, with the growl threaded under it.

On the rock shelf, when she came back around the spruce dressed and wringing out her hair, he was standing at the water's edge in his gray sweater and his holey socks, bone dry.

"Saturday," he said. "First lesson. Wherever you like."

She thought about it.

"The Galena. After close. In the kitchen. Dot goes home at nine."

"The *kitchen?*"

"I think better at a sink."

He looked at her a second longer, and grinned, slow and lopsided, with the long tooth showing.

"See ya later, alligator."

"In a while," said Leah, "crocodile."

He walked off along the rocks in his socks and was gone between one spruce and the next. She went home along the shore and slept four hours and was at the sink at six, and put the folded box on the steel counter by the grill.

Dot looked at it, washed and flat and tied with its own string.

"Hm," said Dot, and something at the corner of her mouth did a thing Leah had never seen it do before, and stopped.

---

"Again," said Harry.

It was the third Saturday of lessons, a little after ten, and the Galena was dark except for the kitchen. Leah stood at the three-bay sink with her sleeves shoved up. He sat on the steel prep counter across from her with his boots swinging and a slab of cold saskatoon pie on a napkin in his lap, which nobody had given him.

"I *am* doing it again."

"You're doing it angry. Angry leaks. Do it bored."

She shut her eyes.

*Everybody's got a front room,* he'd said, the first night, from the same counter. *The bit of the house you let visitors into. You furnish it with something dull. Something you can say in words, over and over, that means nothing. And you shut the door to the rest of the house. People come knocking, they get the front room. They sit on your dull sofa. After a while they leave.*

*What's yours?*

*The staircases at my old school. All hundred and forty-two. Which ones move on a Friday.* He'd shrugged. *Nobody's ever got past the fourth floor.*

The moose had lasted four seconds. The circuit around the bloodsuckers' house had been worse, because it came with the smell, and the smell came with everything else. On the second Saturday, with her hands in the water and her mind wandering, she'd found it without looking.

*Plates. Cups. Sheet pans. Fryer baskets. Soup pots. Grease trap. Floors.*

Dot's order. The one she'd been taught the first morning and did twice a shift without thinking. Seven words worn smooth as creek stones.

She furnished the front room with them. And behind the door she put the rest. The mine. Kell's coat folding down empty. The tree, the plateau, the caribou, the lodge, the bear on the hearth. The silver stag. A history book read out loud in the snow. Him.

*Plates. Cups.*

"Knocking," said Harry.

She felt it. The first time she'd thrown a wet sponge at his head. A pressure at the front of her mind, light, the way you'd rest your palm on a door to feel if anybody was home.

*Sheet pans. Fryer baskets.*

"Pushing."

The pressure leaned. The old reflex came up in her from Sam's pack, the one that said *clamp down, clench, hold it under,* and she let it go past her like a truck on the highway, and kept her hands moving in the water.

*Soup pots. Grease trap. Floors. Plates.*

The pressure leaned harder. The door held without her leaning back on it.

Then it was gone.

She opened her eyes. He was sitting with the pie halfway to his mouth.

"Grease trap," he said. "Several times. You've strong feelings about the grease trap."

"I *hate* the grease trap."

"It came through." He put the pie down. "That's all. Nothing behind it." He looked at her across the kitchen with his head tilted. "First night I got your dad's boat. Fourth night I got the chocolate, and you bit your cheek bloody. Tonight I leaned on you about as hard as I'd lean on someone I meant to get answers out of, and I got a grease trap." His nostrils moved once, as though checking. "Two weeks. I know Aurors who took a year to manage that. Wizard police," he added, at her look. "Very stubborn people."

"I'm stubborn."

"I'd noticed." He picked the pie back up. "Well done, Clearwater."

She turned back to the sink so he wouldn't see her face, and scrubbed a soup pot that was already clean, and felt the back of her neck go hot.

The back door opened.

Dot stood in it with snow on the shoulders of her parka and her car keys in her hand. She looked at Leah at the sink. She looked at the man sitting on her steel prep counter eating her pie off a napkin.

"Left my cigarettes," Dot said.

She crossed the kitchen to the shelf by the walk-in, picked up a soft pack of Player's she was never going to smoke, and put it in her pocket. On her way back she stopped in front of the counter.

"You," she said. "Off."

He got off.

"That's a food surface," said Dot, and went out, and they listened to her truck start and pull away.

Harry stood in the middle of the kitchen holding his pie.

"I'm frightened of that woman," he said.

"Everybody is."

"Do you think she'd let me buy the café?"

"Do you want to die?"

"Not today," he said, and ate the rest standing up, well away from any food surface.

---

Canadian Thanksgiving came and went on the second Monday in October while he was in Kazakhstan. Leah ate turkey at Brynn's mother's kitchen table with Dot and Wes and two of the old men from the corner booth, and on Tuesday morning a brown envelope was waiting on the steel shelf over the dish rack. Inside were her wages and a slip torn off an order pad that said *$9* in Dot's square capitals.

Dot had given her until Halloween. On the first of November, Leah came in the back door at six and found a second hairnet hanging on her hook beside her apron, still in its packet. Dot didn't mention it. Leah understood it was tenure.

---

"Churchill," Harry said. "Manitoba. On Hudson Bay."

"That's two thousand miles."

"Seventeen hundred. Same continent." He was sitting on the end of the town dock beside her in the dark, the last Sunday in October, legs over the water, watching the skim ice tick along the pilings. "There's a thousand polar bears lying about on the shore up there, waiting for the bay to freeze so they can go out and eat seals. They've been waiting since July. They lie on the beach like a lot of very large dogs at a bus stop." He looked at her sideways. "I thought you might like to meet some bears that aren't scared of you."

"You'd have to take me. The magic way."

"Four seconds. I'd have to hold your arm. That's all." He held up both hands. "It's called Side-Along. You'll hate it. Most people are sick."

She looked at the lake for a long time.

"Same continent," she said.

She was sick.

It felt like being pulled through a garden hose by somebody with very strong lungs. One moment she was on the dock with his hand around her arm, and then everything squeezed — ribs, eyes, skull — and let go all at once, and she was on her knees on frozen gravel in a wind that hit like a wall, bringing up Dot's meatloaf onto a clump of something gray and stiff.

He'd let go of her the instant they landed. He crouched beside her, not touching. "Breathe through your nose."

"I'm going to *kill* you."

"Course you are. Nose first."

She breathed through her nose.

Salt.

Cold, flat, enormous salt, coming off a gray sea that ran out under a sky like pewter until she couldn't tell where one stopped. Kelp rotting on the rocks. Wet stone. A wind with nothing in it for a thousand miles but ice. It smelled like First Beach in January. Like her dad's boat at dawn. Like every winter she'd ever had.

She knelt there with her hand over her mouth and breathed it in. He crouched beside her in the wind and didn't say anything.

---

The bears were everywhere.

Once she'd phased behind a boulder with her clothes tied on, she saw them. Two dozen, three, strung along the shore and the low stony ridges behind it as far as she could see. They lay on the kelp beds with their chins on their paws. They slept curled in hollows scraped out of the gravel, backs to the wind. Two young males up on a ridge stood on their hind legs chest to chest, shoving at each other in slow motion like old men slow-dancing. Up close they were the color of old ivory, with black noses and small black eyes, and they smelled like seal fat and fish and wet dog.

They didn't run.

Every animal she'd met since she first phased had run from her or wanted to — deer, elk, the grizzly by the creek, Dale. These lifted their heads and looked at a horse-sized gray wolf trotting down their beach with mild interest, and put their heads back down.

One big male twenty yards off got up instead. He came three slow steps toward her with his long neck stretched out and his nose working, and every hair on her back stood up.

Then he looked past her, at what was standing behind her shoulder.

He stopped. He looked for a while. Then he sat down hard on the gravel like a dog told to sit, yawned enormously, and lay back down on his kelp.

*Did you do that?*

"No." Harry's voice came low and rumbling. "He's sensible. He's just never been second-biggest on this beach before." His tail swept once. "They lock up the ones that come into town, you know. Big building out by the old airstrip. A bear goes through the bins, they dart him and put him in a cell till the bay freezes. No food, so he doesn't learn that town means dinner. Then they fly him out in a net under a helicopter and let him go on the ice." His ears went sideways, pleased. "The locals call it polar bear jail."

Leah looked at the big male asleep on his kelp, waiting for the sea to freeze.

*That's the most Canadian thing I've ever heard.*

"Isn't it?" said Harry, delighted.

They ran the shore all afternoon. East toward a long ragged cape where the bears lay thickest, giving a wide berth to the tundra buggies crawling along their rutted track on monster-truck tires with faces pressed to every window. Out onto the first shelf of new ice along the shore, gray and rubbery, that bent under her paws and didn't break, and under his didn't bend at all. Through the willow after a flock of ptarmigan already gone white for winter, which burst up in front of them like thrown paper.

The sun went down red into the bay, and the sky went green before it was fully dark.

---

She phased back behind a boulder on a ridge above the beach and dressed in the wind with her teeth chattering out of habit. When she came out he was sitting on the rock in his coat, human, collar up, looking at the water. She sat beside him. Below them on the shore the big male had woken and was sitting upright on his kelp like an old man on a park bench, looking out at the bay too.

"It smells like home," Leah said.

She hadn't meant to. It came out.

He didn't say anything. He waited.

"First Beach. In winter, after a storm." She watched the water go from gray to black. "My dad used to take me down after the big ones to look for glass floats. The old Japanese fishing floats. Blue and green. They still wash up sometimes. He found one the size of a basketball when he was ten and it sat on our kitchen windowsill my whole life." She swallowed. "I haven't smelled salt water in six weeks."

"Do you miss it?"

She thought about lying. He'd know.

"Yeah."

He nodded and didn't say *you should go home.* He didn't say anything about it. He sat beside her on the cold rock with the wind in his hair and let her have it, and she was so grateful he'd left it alone that it made her angry, and she turned to tell him so.

He was already looking at her.

She didn't grab him. She thought she might, and then she didn't. She took her hand out of her hoodie pocket and put it flat on his chest, over the coat, over the sweater under it, and felt for his heart.

It was slow. She'd known it would be. Slow and heavy as a big animal's asleep in a den, so slow she counted the gaps. *One.* A long wait. *Two.*

She leaned in and kissed him, and under her palm it jumped.

His mouth was hot and tasted of salt wind and the chocolate he'd had in his pocket all afternoon and kept denying. He didn't move for a second. Then he did, and his heart under her hand went from a sleeping animal to something running, and she laughed against his mouth because she'd done that. Her. On a rock in Manitoba with her own two hands. He made a sound low in his chest that she felt through her palm before she heard it.

When she leaned back his heart was still going hard. She left her hand where it was a moment longer, to feel it.

Then she checked, the way she'd been checking since the mine. Reached inside herself for the tilt. For gravity sliding sideways under her, her mother and Seth and her whole life going light and drifting off.

The bay was just as cold. The rock under her was just as hard. Her mother was still her mother. Seth was still a goof. She was still mildly annoyed with him on principle.

Nobody had done this to her. She'd sat on a rock and decided, the way you decide to jump off the dock at the start of summer, knowing the water's going to be cold.

On the beach below, the big male had turned around on his kelp and was looking up the ridge at them.

"We've an audience," Harry said, a little roughly.

"Let him look."

"For the record," he said, after a while. "That was a date."

"That was a field trip."

"There were bears."

"Bears don't make it a date."

"You kissed me in front of a bear, Clearwater. I'm counting it."

---

The lake froze in the second week of November. It went white from the edges in, a little more each night, until one morning the whole bay was gray-white ice from the town dock to the green roof, and the floatplane was pulled up on shore, and the loons were gone. It was dark by four.

Her mother called the pay phone at the gas station every Sunday at six. On the second Sunday in November, Seth got on first.

"Mom set your place for Thanksgiving," he said, before hello. "It's the twenty-third. It's eleven days away and the plates are *out,* Lee."

"Seth."

"And Charlie's coming."

Leah stood very still with the cold steel against her ear.

"Charlie *Swan?*"

"She says it's 'cause he'd be alone. 'Cause Bella's — busy." Seth's voice was cracking with the effort of not laughing. "But she's at his house three nights a week, and she bought a new *sweater,* Lee. A *red* one."

"Oh my God."

"I *know.* You have to come home. I can't be the only one who has to see this, I'm *fifteen* — "

She leaned her forehead against the phone box and laughed, the ugly hiccuping way, and Seth laughed on the other end, and somewhere behind him her mother said *Seth Clearwater, give me that phone,* and Seth yelped.

She called Harry on the mirror that night. He was in a tent somewhere with the wind screaming around it and snow in his eyebrows. She told him about the red sweater, and he laughed until he had to put the mirror down on his sleeping bag.

Then she told him she was going home.

His face did something in the flashlight glow she couldn't read. Then it settled.

"Good," he said.

"You keep saying that."

"I keep meaning it." He opened his mouth. "Where — "

He stopped.

She'd seen it coming since the ridge above the bay. She'd been ready for it. And she'd found she couldn't make herself say the name of the beach or the town or the river. There were kids on the rez who'd phase one day. There was Seth. There was a book in Switzerland that Solveig Brekke kept, and nothing Leah had ever loved was going in it.

"No," Harry said. "Don't. I'd only have to write it down." He held her eyes through the glass. "Go home, Clearwater."

---

Dot didn't say anything when she told her. She dug in the drawer under the register for a while and came back with an index card in a ziplock bag and put it on the steel shelf over the dish rack.

*SASKATOON PIE,* it said across the top, in square capitals. Then a list. Then, at the bottom: *Don't give this to the cook.*

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'll know."

Brynn cried, and made her write her address on an order pad. Leah wrote *Leah, c/o Dot,* and Brynn looked at it and said, "That's not an address," and Leah said, "It's the one I've got," and Brynn hugged her so hard her ribs creaked. Wes, when she turned in the key on its plastic fob, looked at her over the fishing show for a long time and said, "Room's yours if you come back. Moose'll be there," which was the most words in a row she'd ever heard him say.

She left on a Tuesday, an hour before dawn.

He was waiting at the deadfall above the lake road, in the waxed jacket with snow on his shoulders, and he turned his back without being asked while she stripped and rolled her clothes into the grocery bag with the recipe card and the cash and the mirror in its pouch, and tied it to her ankle with three hard knots.

When she phased and he turned around, he was black and huge and his breath was smoking.

"Fifty miles," he said. "Then I'll turn round. If you'll have me."

She went past him down the slope at a lope, south, and heard nothing behind her, and when she looked over her shoulder he was three lengths back, keeping pace without a sound.

They ran. Down out of the spruce and across the plateau with the snow blowing over it in long white snakes. Past the tilted rock where they'd lain under the green sky. Across the frozen river where it narrowed between two black cliffs, the ice booming under her and silent under him. The sun came up red behind them and threw two shadows ahead of them across the snow, one gray and long, one black and longer.

On the rim of a long white valley running south, with the road a dark thread along the bottom of it, he stopped.

She ran ten strides before she noticed, and stopped, and turned.

He stood on the rim against the sunrise with his tail swinging slowly and his head tilted.

"See ya later, alligator," Harry said.

*In a while,* she thought, *crocodile.*

He chuffed, low, through his nose.

She turned and ran down into the valley and kept her eyes on the snow ahead, because she knew herself. When she did look, from the far side an hour later, the rim was empty.

---

South was faster. Five days, downhill mostly, and this time she knew the road.

On the fifth morning she sat on a rock above Deception Pass with the tide roaring through the gorge underneath and furnished the front room. Seven words. Behind them, the door. Behind the door, Turnagain and everything in it. She checked it twice, the way you check a stove before you leave the house, and then she went down to the beach below the old fort and into Admiralty Inlet, west this time, toward the lighthouse beam turning slow and white over the chop.

She was halfway across when she heard the first voice, faint and grainy, like a radio two rooms away.

*— okay but narwhals are REAL, I'm showing her the picture tomorrow, they're basically unicorn whales — *

Seth. Mid-thought. Planning to explain narwhals to a two-month-old.

*Plates,* Leah thought. *Cups. Sheet pans.*

The voice stopped dead.

*...Lee?*

Then louder, so loud it rang in her skull: *LEAH?*

And then everybody. Like somebody throwing every light switch in a house at once. Seth on the Cullens' back lawn in his fur, his joy hitting her like a big dog jumping up on her chest with both front paws. Embry, half asleep on the treaty line, jerking awake. Quil — *Quil?* — in a backyard on the rez with a three-year-old in a dinosaur costume riding on his back. And Jacob, on the riverbank behind the glass house, lifting his head out of the wet ferns and swinging it north.

*Hey, Lee,* Jacob thought. Careful. Like a guy talking to a deer he didn't want to spook.

*Hey, Jake.*

*Where you at?*

She let them look. Black water. The lighthouse beam passing over her. The lights of Port Townsend on the bluff.

*You're in the INLET?* Seth was practically vibrating. *That's like an hour! Mom's gonna — I gotta tell Mom — *

*Since when are you two in here?* she thought at Quil and Embry.

*Since Jake asked,* Embry thought.

*Since Sam said we could go,* Quil thought, which was the more honest answer, and under it she caught a flash of what it had cost — a long night in Sam's kitchen, Emily crying, Sam's face like a closed door — and then Quil put it away.

*Jake made me second while you were gone,* Seth thought. *I was bad at it.*

*He asked everybody how they were feeling,* Quil thought. *On patrol. Every night.*

*That's a NORMAL QUESTION — *

She'd forgotten how loud it was. She swam through all of them talking at once, and felt them doing what they'd always done, poking into the corners of the last two months. The café. Dot's grill. Brynn talking about Sydney. The orange bedspread. The caribou — *wait, you took that down by yourself?* — and the bears — *POLAR BEARS? You saw POLAR BEARS?* — and then she felt Seth come up against the front room and stop.

*Lee. Why's there a dishwasher in your head?*

*Plates. Cups. Sheet pans. Fryer baskets.*

*It's just — it's dishes. Over and over. And there's a door behind it.*

*Is it a guy?* Quil thought immediately. *It's a guy.*

*QUIL.*

All three of them came crowding up to it at once, like puppies at a gate. They pushed. They sniffed along the edges. The seven words went round and round, and the door held.

And she felt Jacob go quiet behind the rest of them, and look.

He could open it. She knew that. The voice was in him, the one that came up from somewhere older than any of them and bent your bones to fit, and she had no idea whether a door she'd built at a dish sink in northern B.C. would hold against that. She swam on with the black water pushing at her chest and waited to find out.

*Huh,* Jacob thought.

Then, to the others, flat and easy, with just enough weight under it to be felt: *Drop it. You heard her.*

They dropped it.

She came up out of the water at Point Wilson under the lighthouse and stood on the gravel with the beam going round above her head, and shook.

*Sam would've made you open it,* Jacob thought, low, aimed at her alone, the closest thing to private the pack had.

*Yeah.*

*Good for you, Lee,* he thought, and went back to lying in the ferns, and she started running west.

---

There was a police cruiser in her mother's driveway.

She came out of the woods behind her mother's house a little after two in a thin cold rain, and phased and dressed and stood there looking at it. Forks PD. Light bar on the roof. Rain beading on the windshield. Her heart did something ugly — *Mom, something's happened to Mom* — and then the wind turned and she smelled fish chowder.

She went up the back steps and opened the kitchen door.

Her mother was at the stove in a red sweater, stirring the big pot with the wooden spoon that had the chip out of the handle. Seth was at the table with wet hair, halfway out of his chair. And in her father's chair at the end of the table by the window, with a bowl of chowder in front of him and his uniform jacket over the chair back, sat Charlie Swan.

Everybody looked at her.

"Hi, Leah," said Charlie.

"...Chief."

He looked at her a second longer — wet hair, flannel, jeans cinched with a belt, no coat, no car, no bag — and she watched him decide something. Then he picked up his spoon.

Her mother put down hers. She crossed the kitchen and put her arms around Leah and held on and didn't say anything at all. Leah stood in the doorway with her mother's chin on her shoulder, breathing in lotion and chowder and coffee and the old wet-wool smell of the house, and shut her eyes. Over her mother's shoulder Seth was grinning so hard it looked painful, and mouthing: *RED. SWEATER.*

"There's chowder," Sue said into her hair. "Don't eat it all standing up."

"No promises."

"Sit. You're too thin." Her mother let go and wiped her eyes on her wrist and turned back to the stove, all business. "Charlie, move down, she'll want the window."

Charlie moved down without a word. Leah sat in her father's chair and ate three bowls. Charlie ate two. Then he wiped his mustache with a paper napkin.

"Heard you were up in Canada."

"Yeah."

"Long way."

"Yeah."

He looked at the rain on the window. She waited for him to ask how she'd gotten there, or back, or why her hair was wet when she'd walked in out of the woods with nothing. Seth had told her on the phone about the day in September when Jacob went into Charlie's backyard and came out of the trees as something the size of a horse. How Charlie had gone inside, and sat down, and never asked Jacob one question since. *Need to know,* he'd said. *I don't need to know.*

"Catch any fish?" said Charlie.

"Sockeye. In the Fraser. Below the gondola."

His eyebrows went up. "Hell's Gate? In September?" He looked impressed in spite of himself. "What'd you use?"

Leah looked at him.

"My hands," she said.

Charlie looked back at her for a long moment with his spoon halfway to his mustache. Then he nodded slowly, like a man filing something in a drawer he didn't plan to open.

"Huh," he said, and went back to his chowder, and at the stove her mother didn't turn around, but Leah saw her shoulders shake.

---

On her third day home she walked up the Cullens' drive behind Jacob, and halfway up the river breeze hit her full in the face — bleach, burnt sugar, something dead in the sun — and her teeth were bared before she knew it. Two months of spruce and lake ice and fryer grease had wiped it clean out of her memory.

Rosalie Hale was on the white couch with a magazine. She didn't look up.

"I'd hoped Canada would keep you," she said.

"It tried."

There were two stainless-steel bowls on the floor by the kitchen door, the kind you bought at a pet store. One said FIDO in black marker. The other was new. It said LASSIE.

Leah looked at it. She looked at Rosalie, who was turning a page.

She picked up the bowl, took it into the kitchen, filled it from Esme's coffeepot, came back, and sat down on the far arm of the couch and drank out of it, looking at Rosalie the whole time.

Emmett, at the long table with a deck of cards, laughed so hard he had to put his forehead down on the glass.

"I like her," he said, muffled. "Rose. I like her."

"You would."

The kid was on the sheepskin rug by the fireplace with a picture book.

For three days Leah had been getting her secondhand, through Seth and Jacob. The last time she'd laid eyes on her, the kid had been a bulge under a blanket, eating a girl alive from the inside. Now she was turning pages by herself, and she looked about two, maybe more. Bronze curls to her shoulders. Bella's old brown eyes, the human ones. A heartbeat Leah could hear across the room, fast and light as a sparrow's. Her smell was half the house and half a person, both at once, and the wolf in Leah's chest had no idea what to do with that and pressed itself flat.

The kid raised her head and studied Leah for a long time, with a face a lot older than two. Then she got to her feet and walked across the rug — walked, steady — and stopped in front of the couch and held up one hand.

"She wants to show you something," Jacob said from the floor. He sounded like a guy asking for a favor. "Just let her."

Leah looked at him. At his face, doing the thing she'd felt from inside his head for three days and would probably feel for the rest of her life: gravity, tipped all the way over toward a two-month-old in a pinafore.

She bent down. Small fingers, fever-hot, settled against her face.

The picture dropped into her head whole, no warning. The riverbank behind the house at night, seen through the glass from very low down — the height of somebody small being held up to a window. Rain. And on the far bank, sitting by himself in the dark facing north, a big sandy-colored wolf. Not moving. Nose up. The way a dog waits at the end of a driveway for a car that isn't coming.

And over the picture, like a voice over a song, a question with no words in it. *Why?*

Renesmee took her hand away.

"He sat there every night," she said aloud, in a clear little voice. "Seth. Was he waiting for you?"

Leah's throat closed up.

"Yeah," she said. "He was."

The kid thought about it, nodded as if that settled something, and went back to her book.

Edward was leaning on the porch rail when Leah went out. He didn't come closer. None of them ever did. He looked at her with his strange gold eyes, troubled, which wasn't new, and curious, which was.

"Seven words," he said. "Over and over. And a door behind them I can't get through." He tilted his head. "Who taught you to do that?"

"Dot."

"Who's Dot?"

"Your worst nightmare," said Leah, and went down the steps.

---

Then it was same old, same old.

Patrol in shifts around the glass house, five of them now, ringing it in the rain the way they'd ringed it in September — only guarding it, now, instead of waiting to tear it down, and nobody could explain to her exactly when that had flipped. Embry hungry. Quil thinking about whether a three-year-old could be a stegosaurus *and* a princess for Christmas. Seth happy. Jacob at the house, because the kid was at the house.

Carlisle measured the kid every morning against the frame of the kitchen door, and the pencil marks climbed it like a vine. Alice tried to put Leah in a sweater twice and gave up. Jasper stayed on the far side of any room she was in, which suited her fine. Emmett lost eighty-one dollars to Seth at poker and paid it in quarters, out of spite. Esme left a thermos of coffee on the porch rail every afternoon Leah was on shift, without a word, and every afternoon Leah drank it without a word, and by the second week it had turned into a treaty neither of them would ever admit to signing.

Bella's eyes went from red to a dark, muddy orange, like a fire burning down. She was always polite. She'd stand in the doorway with the kid on her hip and say *Hi, Leah* in her new wind-chime voice, and Leah would look at the hard white face that used to belong to a clumsy girl from Forks High and think, every time: *You had a heartbeat. You had a dad who eats chowder in my mom's kitchen. You picked this.*

Edward heard her think it once. He didn't say anything. He looked at her across the porch for a long moment, and she looked back, and then Bella said his name and he went inside.

Charlie came to dinner every Sunday and took the chair at the far end of the glass table and ate Esme's pot roast with the face of a man at a magic show who'd decided he'd rather not know how the trick was done. He watched the kid get bigger week by week. He watched five enormous Quileute kids eat everything in the kitchen. He watched his daughter not eat, and not blink, and not breathe unless she remembered to. He never asked once.

American Thanksgiving, he carved the Clearwaters' turkey, badly. Her mother wore the red sweater. Seth kicked Leah under the table every time Charlie said *Sue,* and Leah kicked back so hard he yelped and had to pretend it was the gravy.

In the first week of December she saw Sam down on First Beach at low tide. She'd gone looking for glass floats, the way she used to with her dad. He was walking the waterline alone with his hands in his pockets. He stopped when he saw her.

"Leah."

"Sam."

Big and grave and tired, with the rain running off his short hair. He looked at her the way he'd looked at her for eighteen months, guilt standing in his face like water in a ditch, and she waited for the thing that had always come up in her chest when she saw him, the fishhook.

It came. Small. An old bruise that only hurt when she pressed on it.

"You look good," Sam said. "Canada was good?"

"Canada was good."

He didn't seem to know what to do with that. He'd never once seen her happy, she realized, not since the day Emily walked into her parents' kitchen.

"I'm glad," he said, and meant it.

"Yeah," said Leah. "Me too."

She walked on down the beach. She didn't find any glass floats. She didn't look back to see if he was watching.

---

She called Harry on the mirror four times in four weeks, lying on her old bed late at night with the rain ticking on the glass.

The first time he was in a tent in Mongolia with a goat trying to get in. The second he was at the desk under the mountain, with Solveig behind him saying *Lord Potter* every forty seconds like a clock. The third he was in a bath. He said he'd warned her. She said she'd hang up. He said *don't* very quickly and propped the mirror on the side of the tub facing the ceiling, and they talked for an hour about Charlie and the red sweater and the LASSIE bowl and Emmett's eighty-one dollars in quarters, and he laughed so hard at the bowl that she heard him slide under the water.

He never asked where she was. She never said. Once, lying on her bed with the mirror propped on her chest, she opened her mouth to say *La Push* — and the seven words came up on their own, and she shut it again.

The fourth time was the second week of December. His face was gray with tiredness, and a crease she didn't remember from Turnagain sat between his eyebrows. There were stone walls behind him, and a leather folder open on the table that he closed when he saw her looking at it.

"New job," he said. "Can't tell you about it. Sorry."

"Where?"

"Somewhere wet."

"Everywhere's wet."

"Wetter than usual." He rubbed his eyes. "Might be a week or two before I can ring. I'll ring when it's done."

"Okay."

He looked at her through the glass for a while. "Home's all right, Clearwater?"

She thought about the kid's hand on her cheek. About Seth on the riverbank. About Sam on the beach and the bruise that didn't hurt unless she pushed it, and the seven words holding every day against four voices and a mind reader.

"Home's loud," she said. "But yeah."

"Good."

"You keep saying that."

"I keep meaning it," said Harry. "See ya later, alligator."

"In a while, crocodile," said Leah, and the glass went cloudy, and it was her own face again, in her own room, with the rain coming down.

---

Alice lost the future at nine minutes past three on the Saturday before Christmas, and Edward felt it go.

He was at the piano with Renesmee on the bench beside him in a green velvet dress, plinking out the melody of Bella's song one key at a time while he filled in the chords beneath her. Esme in the kitchen with a lasagna the size of a suitcase for the wolves. Emmett and Jasper at the long table over a chessboard. Rosalie on the couch with a magazine she wasn't reading. Bella by the fire with Jacob half asleep against the hearth, his mind going slow and drowsy, *Nessie, Nessie, Nessie,* like a cat purring. Seth on the lawn in his fur, in the rain, wondering about garlic bread. Farther off, Embry and Leah on the north line — Embry hungry, Leah thinking about plates and cups and sheet pans the way she had since November, loudly and on purpose.

Upstairs, Alice's mind was full of the rest of the day. Charlie at six. Pot roast tomorrow. A dress she meant to buy at four.

And then it wasn't.

Her visions blurred around the wolves and around Renesmee, like a thumb smudged across a photograph. This time the whole picture went out at once, as though someone had walked into the room where Alice kept the future and turned off the lights.

His hands went still on the piano. Renesmee tipped her face up to his.

Alice was at the top of the stairs. Then she was at the bottom, her small face white even for her.

"Everything after four," she said. "All of it. Not just the wolves. Us. The house. Charlie. There's nothing there, Edward. It's gone."

The room went still. Eight vampires, a wolf, and a child with a heartbeat like a sparrow's, all still.

Edward listened.

The river. Rain on the cedars. A logging truck downshifting on the highway three miles off. Seth's mind on the lawn, ears up — *what? is it Sam?*

And at the bottom of the drive, at the county road, a sound like a branch snapping in a hard frost.

Then a heartbeat.

At the head of the drive, a mile and a half off, where a second ago there had been nothing but rain. Forty-four beats a minute, deep and heavy, more like a large animal's at rest than a man's. Then leather soles on gravel, coming up the drive at a stroll.

Edward reached out for whatever mind went with it, by reflex, the way he'd reached for every mind for eighty-eight years.

Nothing.

Bella's silence he knew the way he knew his own hands, a soft dark void where thoughts should be. This was a surface. Smooth and hard and seamless, like the door of a bank vault, without a seam to get a fingernail into. He could feel the shape of someone behind it. The door was shut.

The only other door like it he'd ever met was in Leah Clearwater's head, and hers had grease traps in front of it.

"Who is it?" said Jacob. He was on his feet, the warm drift gone out of him. "Edward. Who is it?"

"I don't know." He heard his own voice come out strange. "I can't hear him."

Renesmee was off the piano bench and in Bella's arms; Edward hadn't seen either of them move. Jasper was at the glass wall. Carlisle stood at the top of the stairs with one hand on the rail.

"Seth," Edward said, not raising his voice. Seth would hear him. "Bring in Leah and Embry. Now. And stay where you are."

*On it,* Seth thought, and Edward felt him reach out into the strange shared space the wolves lived in, and felt, faintly, the other two answer from the north line and start to run.

The footsteps came on up the drive. Edward watched them come through Seth's eyes.

The rain. The long gravel drive curving through the cedars, gray and green and dripping. And around the last bend by the big cedar, walking up the middle of it, a man under a black umbrella.

A plain black city umbrella. Under it a long black overcoat over a dark suit, black gloves, and black shoes polished to a shine that had no business on a gravel road in the Olympic rain forest. A slim leather folder under one arm. The umbrella was tipped forward against the rain, so from Seth's height there was no face under it, only a jaw.

*He doesn't smell like anything,* Seth thought. Bewildered. Nose working. *Edward, he doesn't smell like ANYTHING. No person smell. No leech smell. There's just rain where he is.*

The man came up the last stretch of the drive and onto the edge of the lawn. Seth was lying not twenty feet from him, a sandy-colored wolf the size of a horse, with his lips peeled back off his teeth.

The man glanced at him. That was all. One look from under the umbrella, unhurried.

"Afternoon," the man said to the wolf, in a clipped English voice, and kept walking.

*Did he just,* Seth thought, in total confusion, *say good afternoon to me?*

---

Carlisle opened the door before the man could knock.

He stood in the doorway with his hands where anyone could see them, the way he'd stood in a hundred doorways in three hundred years. The rest of them had arranged themselves without a word. Emmett by the stairs. Jasper at the edge of the glass. Rosalie in front of Bella, Bella with Renesmee on her hip, Jacob beside them shaking very slightly down the arms.

The man on the porch closed his umbrella. He shook it twice, neatly, and leaned it against the doorframe.

He was younger than the clothes. Mid-twenties. Six foot two, broad through the shoulders under the black coat. Black hair standing up at the back as though it had never lain flat in its life. Green eyes. A faint line between the eyebrows. A heartbeat of forty-four, slow and steady as a clock in an empty house.

The green eyes went past Carlisle into the room. Over the glass wall, the white couch, the piano, the family, one at a time, the way a man might look at a menu in a restaurant he hadn't chosen. They rested on Renesmee for a long time. Edward felt Bella's arms tighten.

"Dr. Cullen," the man said. "Harry Potter. International Confederation of Wizards. Special Enforcement." He didn't offer his hand. "I'd like a word. Do I come in, or am I meant to wait to be asked? I can never remember which of us that rule's for."

"Wizards," said Emmett, from the stairs, in a tone of deep delight.

The man didn't look at him.

Carlisle's mind had gone very careful. Edward caught the memory surfacing in it: a marble hall in Volterra, three centuries gone, Aro's light, amused voice. *There is an old understanding with the wand-bearers, my friend. They keep to their side of the curtain and we keep to ours. I would not cross them lightly.*

"Please," Carlisle said. "Come in, Mr. Potter."

The man stepped over the threshold, didn't take off his coat, and stopped three steps into the room.

Edward reached for him again.

The vault door. Smooth. Shut.

"You can stop that, Mr. Cullen," the man said, without looking at him. "There's nobody in."

Edward went cold. Colder.

"I've read your files," Harry Potter said. "All of you. They're old and mostly dull, which I'm told is the point. Seven of you, until this year. Animal diet. Keep to yourselves. Move every ten years or so before the neighbors notice you've stopped ageing. Reviewed once every fifty years by somebody in Geneva who reads the summary, stamps it, and goes to lunch." His eyes moved. "Dr. Cullen. Your father's sermons are in our archive. He hanged a woman in Southwark in 1661 for witchcraft. She set bones for a living and kept a cat. She's in the archive too." Carlisle's face didn't change. Edward heard his mind flinch like a struck animal. "Major Whitlock," the man said, and his eyes didn't move to Jasper at all, "if you take one more step to my left I'll assume you're trying to get behind me, and I'll take it personally."

Jasper stopped.

"Mr. McCarty. Tennessee. A bear." Emmett opened his mouth. "Mr. Cullen, there's a gap in your file between 1927 and 1931 in which you ate murderers. Very public-spirited of you." He looked at Rosalie. "Miss Hale."

That was all. Just the name, and a small nod.

Edward heard Rosalie's mind, braced, rigid, waiting for Rochester — for Royce King, for the street, for what she'd done to all five of them afterward in her wedding dress. And he heard it not come. He watched the man's green eyes move off her without another word.

Rosalie didn't relax. She didn't thank him either, even in her head. But she stopped hissing.

"Can I get you something, Mr. Potter?" said Esme, from beside Carlisle, in the voice she used for frightened patients and lost children and Jacob. "Tea? Coffee? There's lasagna — "

"No, thank you, Mrs. Cullen." Polite. Flat. "I don't eat in houses where I'm on the menu."

"Hey," said Emmett. "We don't eat *people,* man."

The green eyes turned, finally, to Emmett.

"No," Harry Potter agreed. "You eat deer. And elk. And bears, when you can get one. Through the neck, while they're still warm. You don't cook them. You don't skin them. You drink them." He said it like a man reading out a shopping list. "Call me mad, Mr. McCarty, but I've never thought blood was a sane thing to live on. Blood's the bit that carries the food about. I've eaten a great many things in my life and I've never once looked at something with a pulse and thought *that'll do for breakfast.*"

Emmett looked at him for a long moment.

"Okay," he said. "When you say it like that."

"Emmett," said Rosalie.

"I'm just saying, when he *says* it like that — "

"What do you want?" said Bella.

The man turned his head and looked at her.

Edward would think about that look for a long time afterward. He could find nothing in it to name. A steady, distant attention, the way a man looks at a fence he's been told to inspect, judging what it would take to knock it down.

"Mrs. Cullen," he said. "You'll be the one who used to have a heartbeat."

---

"Last spring," Harry Potter said, "something in Seattle killed thirty-odd Muggles over four months. Your papers called it a serial killer. Your Italian friends turned up afterwards and tidied it away. Late, as usual." He tucked the folder more firmly under his arm. "The Americans don't like things happening in their country that they have to read about in the newspaper. So they put a watcher on this county. Quiet. Very good at her job. A woman in a car with a flask of tea, on the county road at the bottom of your drive, on and off, since June."

"That's impossible," said Edward. "I'd have heard her."

"You'd have heard her if she'd let you." The green eyes came back to him. "You didn't. I imagine that stings a bit." He went on before Edward could answer. "In August, a girl went into this house in a wedding dress. In September she came back from a honeymoon, and was carried into this house, and for about two weeks the doctor here bought a great deal of blood from three different hospitals and nobody saw her at a window. Then nobody saw her at all. Then, on the twentieth of September, the chief of police of this town walked into his own back garden with that young man — " a glance at Jacob — "and came out of it ten minutes later the color of paper, and didn't go to work the next day."

Jacob's jaw worked.

"I've a fair idea what he saw," the man said. "It's not in my file. It's not going to be." He didn't explain that, and he didn't look at Jacob again. "And since October, Chief Swan has had Sunday dinner in this house every week with a child who was born in September and looks three in December, and who learned to read before Halloween, and who is going to be a grown woman, by your doctor's own notes, before she's eight." He turned his head and looked at Renesmee again, a long, flat look. "Nobody's ever seen one of her. Not in your lot's records, and not in ours. Something new, in a town of three thousand Muggles, with a police chief for a grandfather who's been told just enough to know he shouldn't ask. That's a breach of the Statute of Secrecy. That's a big one." He paused. "I've been sent to assess it, and to take appropriate action if necessary. Geneva's phrasing."

The room had gone so quiet Edward could hear Renesmee's heart going like a bird's against Bella's collarbone.

"*Appropriate action,*" said Rosalie, very softly.

"You stay away from my father," said Bella.

"Your father," said Harry Potter, "is the breach, Mrs. Cullen."

Bella went still. In that stillness, Edward felt the whole room tilt toward her.

"You were human in August," the man said, in the same flat, even voice. "You had a pulse, and a dad, and seventy-odd years in front of you. And you looked at all that and decided you'd rather spend forever drinking the blood out of things. For a boy." He didn't look at Edward. "Then, having done it, your lot's custom — the one that's kept you all out of the newspapers for three thousand years — is that a new one goes away. Fakes a death. Moves to Alaska. Lets her family grieve and get on with it. You didn't. You kept your dad. You kept him for Sunday dinners. And you let a lad strip off in his back garden to do it." The green eyes held hers. "So forgive me, Mrs. Cullen, if your judgment isn't the thing I'm going to be relying on, where that child's concerned."

Edward didn't know he'd moved until he was in front of Bella. He didn't know he was snarling until he heard it — the sound coming up out of his chest, low and ragged, that he hadn't made in front of his family since the day Bella's blood had sung to him in a biology classroom.

Harry Potter didn't move. His heart didn't change. Forty-four, slow and steady.

"Sit down, Mr. Cullen," he said. "You won't get there."

And then he frowned.

It was a small frown. The first thing his face had done since he walked in. His eyes had gone past Edward's shoulder to Bella, and fixed there, and narrowed slightly, the way a man's narrow when he puts his weight on a door and finds it doesn't give.

"Huh," he said.

Behind Edward, Bella drew a breath she didn't need, and he felt her shield stretch — the thing she'd learned to push out from herself since she woke — spreading like a bubble of glass over Renesmee, over him, over Jacob and the rest, and one mind after another went silent in his head. Rosalie. Emmett. Jasper. Alice. Carlisle. Gone behind Bella's glass.

Harry Potter's eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch.

"And now I can't hear any of you," he said. "That's new."

Edward stared at him.

"You've been in our heads," he said. "The whole time."

"On the step. Surface only." The green eyes came back to him, unbothered. "You've been reading everybody you've met for eighty-eight years, Mr. Cullen, and I don't imagine you ever once asked. I didn't see why I should." He looked at Bella again. The frown had gone. Something else had replaced it, and it wasn't warmer. "That's a useful trick, Mrs. Cullen. I'll put it in the report." A pause. "It doesn't change what you did."

"I'd like to see the child," he said.

"No," said Bella.

"Over my dead body," said Jacob, and stepped out from beside Rosalie, between the man and Renesmee, and the shaking had gone all the way up into his shoulders now, his whole body humming like a power line.

Harry Potter turned his head and looked at him.

Then his head tilted.

Edward saw it happen and didn't understand it. A slow tilt, a good thirty degrees, the whole head going over to one side the way a dog's does when it hears a sound it can't place. His nostrils flared. Once. Twice. His eyes went from Jacob to the glass wall, to the lawn beyond it, where Seth was on his feet in the rain with his hackles up, and back to Jacob.

"Ah," said Harry Potter, quietly.

It was the first time Edward had heard anything in the voice that wasn't flat.

"You I'm not here about," the man said to Jacob. "You or the one on the lawn. You're not in my file. You won't be. Sit down, son, before you come apart on Dr. Cullen's rug."

"Why?" Jacob's voice was a growl. "Why aren't we in your file?"

"Somebody asked."

"*Who?*"

"None of your business."

"I'm not your *son* — "

"No," Harry Potter agreed. "You're about nineteen, I'd think. Sit down."

Jacob didn't sit down. He stood there shaking between the man and the child, and the man stood there and let him, unbothered, the slow heart going on and on like a clock.

And through the back of the house, through the kitchen, Edward heard Leah Clearwater come in.

He'd heard her coming through Seth for the last minute, running, and then the snap of her phasing behind the garage. The back door. Bare feet on Esme's tile. The dish order going in her head like a drumbeat, *plates, cups, sheet pans,* hard and furious, the way it always went when she walked into a room full of vampires.

Harry Potter stopped.

He'd been about to say something else to Jacob. He didn't say it. His head came up, sharp. His nostrils flared, and this time it was long and deep, a breath drawn all the way in, and his head tilted the rest of the way over, the full thirty degrees and past it, and every line of him went still.

Leah came around the corner of the kitchen doorway with her black hair dripping down her back and her flannel shirt buttoned wrong, and stopped dead.

And through the door in her head, through the dish order, so loud and so clear that Edward flinched, came one thought, in words:

*Oh, you have GOT to be fucking kidding me.*

Then the door slammed so hard he felt it, and the dish order came down in front of it like a portcullis — *PLATES. CUPS. SHEET PANS* — and behind it he couldn't hear anything at all.

The man in the black coat turned around.

"Shit, girl," said Harry Potter. "Didn't know you were gonna be here."

---

She'd smelled him at the back door, and thought she'd lost her mind.

She'd come in off the north line with Embry behind her and Seth yelling in her head — *some guy, he's got an umbrella, he doesn't smell like ANYTHING, he said good AFTERNOON to me* — and through Seth's eyes she'd seen a black coat, a black umbrella, a pair of stupid shiny shoes, and thought: *lawyer.* Somebody from the county about the property line. A Denali cousin dressed up for Christmas. She'd phased behind the garage and dressed in eight seconds and buttoned her shirt wrong and come in the back way, into Esme's kitchen, into lasagna and bleach and burnt sugar.

And under all of it, faint as a voice two rooms off: earth after rain. Pine. Cold wet stone. The struck match.

She'd stood with one bare foot on the tile and thought, *Two months, and now I'm smelling him in the bloodsuckers' kitchen.* Then she'd heard the voice from the living room. Flat and level, every joke drained out of it. The voice from the mine. *You're about nineteen, I'd think. Sit down.*

She'd walked around the corner.

Now he was standing in the middle of the Cullens' white living room in an undertaker's suit with his hair sticking up at the back and his head tilted so far over it was nearly on his shoulder, and every vampire in the house was staring at her.

The wolf in her chest, which had spent four weeks lying flat on its belly every time she walked into this house, rolled over onto its back with all four legs in the air.

*Traitor,* she thought, behind the dish order, where nobody could hear it.

"What are *you* doing here?" she said.

"Working." His eyes went around the room, the glass and the white couch and the eight of them, and came back to her with honest bewilderment. "What are *you* doing here? You hate these people. You told me this house smells like bleach and burnt sugar and something dead."

"Charming," said Rosalie.

"I'm on shift," said Leah.

"On *shift.*"

"We guard the kid."

Something moved across his face, quick, and was gone. He didn't look at the child. She saw him not look at the child, and felt her stomach go cold.

"*Somewhere wet,*" she said.

"It's very wet. I've been in it since five this morning." He glanced down at himself. "I wore the shoes. I regret the shoes."

"You look like an undertaker."

"I'm meant to look official."

"You look like you've come to bury somebody."

That time he did look. One glance at the child on Bella's hip, and back to Leah, and his face didn't change at all, and that was worse.

"Leah," said Jacob, in a strangled voice. "You *know* this guy?"

"Yeah."

"*How?*"

She opened her mouth.

"We've been on four dates," said Harry.

"Three."

"Four. Churchill counts."

"Churchill was a field trip."

"There were bears."

"Bears don't make it a *date* — "

"You kissed me in front of a bear, Clearwater."

Emmett made a sound like a tire going flat, sat down on the bottom stair next to Alice, and put his face in both hands. His shoulders started to shake.

"Oh, my *God,*" said Rosalie. She'd come out from in front of Bella and was looking from Leah to the man in black and back with a face full of pure, incandescent joy. "This is the best thing that has ever happened in this house."

"Shut up, Rosalie."

"I really don't think I will."

Out on the lawn there was a pop and a rip, and then the thump of bare feet, and Seth came through the glass door in a pair of cutoffs and nothing else, dripping, his eyes as big as saucers.

"*Leah has a BOYFRIEND?*"

"He's not my *boyfriend* — "

Harry had turned to look at Seth. Leah saw his nostrils move and saw him get it, all at once: her smell, younger, on her little brother. Cedar and rain and salt water and wet fur.

"You're the poker one," Harry said.

Seth's mouth fell open. "She told you about *poker?*"

"Wait." Emmett had taken his face out of his hands. "He knows about poker night?"

"I know you owe him eighty-one dollars," said Harry, without looking at him.

"I *paid* that!"

"In quarters," said Harry. "I heard."

"Leah." Jacob's voice cut through it. He hadn't moved from where he stood between the man and the kid. The shaking had mostly left his arms. It was still in his face. He was looking at her the way he'd looked at her the night she followed him out of Sam's clearing, trying to work out whose side she'd come in on. "You told him. About us. What we are."

The room went quiet again.

"I told him there were wolves," Leah said. "I never told him where. Or who. Not one name, Jake. Not the rez, not the town, not you, not Seth. He doesn't even know what *beach.*"

"She didn't," Harry said. He'd turned back to Jacob, and some of the flat had come back into his voice, though not all of it. "I'd have found you lot in a week if she'd let slip a postcode. She never did. I started to ask her where home was, once, and stopped." He looked at Leah. "I'd have had to write it down."

Jacob looked at her for a long moment. Something in his face eased, a little.

"That's what's behind the dishes," he said.

"Some of it."

---

It went back to business after that. It had to. He was here for the kid.

She stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded and watched him do it, and learned more about him in ten minutes than she had in two months.

He didn't sit. He turned down Esme's coffee a second time, politely. He told Carlisle he'd come back at ten the next morning and wanted to see the child then, properly, and talk to her, and to Carlisle about his notes. He told Bella she could hold the child the whole time if it made her feel better, and that it wouldn't change what he saw. He told all of them that nobody was to leave the county and nobody was to say a word to Chief Swan, and that if anyone ran, he'd find them, because finding people was the thing he was best at. He said it in the same level voice he'd used on Kell on the mountain, and none of it sounded like a threat. That was the part that put the hair up on her arms. It sounded like the weather.

When he'd finished, the kid lifted her head off Bella's shoulder.

She'd been quiet the whole time, quieter than Leah had ever seen her. Now she looked across the room at the man in the black coat with her big brown eyes, and held out one hand, the way she held it out to anybody she wanted to show something to.

He looked back at her for a long time. His face didn't do anything.

He didn't go to her. He didn't put out his own hand.

"Tomorrow," he said to her. Not unkindly. Not kindly either. "Ten o'clock."

Renesmee put her hand down.

Then he turned to Leah in the kitchen doorway, and the flat went out of his face like water out of a sink.

"Walk?" he said. "It's raining. I've an umbrella."

"I run hot."

"So do I. It's a very good umbrella."

She looked at Jacob. Jacob looked back. She didn't need to be in his head to hear it. *Find out what he's going to do.*

"I'll be back," she said. To Jacob. To all of them.

"Lee — "

"I got it, Jake."

She walked across the Cullens' living room barefoot in her wrong-buttoned shirt, past Rosalie and Emmett and Edward at the piano, and Harry picked up his umbrella from beside the door and held the door for her, and she went out onto the porch, and he came after her and shut it.

---

The rain was coming down steady and gray on the cedars, the way it did eleven months a year. He put up the umbrella and held it over her. She let him.

They didn't talk for the first hundred yards. Behind them the glass house glowed gold against the trees. She didn't need to look to know they were all at the glass. They could hear a heartbeat at a mile.

"They can hear every word," she said.

"I know. I'd say the same to their faces." He kept walking. "I mostly just did."

Up close, under the umbrella, he smelled the way he'd smelled on the dock in Turnagain. There was rain on his eyelashes and the line between his eyebrows she'd seen in the mirror.

"What happens to the kid?"

He didn't dodge it.

"Depends what she is."

"She's a *kid.*"

"She's a kid who was born in September and reads Dickens," he said. "Who'll look twenty by the time she's seven, and after that, nobody knows. Nobody's ever seen one. She might stop growing. She might need blood or she might not. She might be gentle as a lamb, or she might be the most dangerous thing on this continent the first time somebody makes her properly angry, and her grandfather comes to Sunday dinner." He was looking down the drive, not at her. "If I go back to Geneva and say she's safe, they'll mostly listen. If I say she isn't — " He stopped. "That's what tomorrow's for."

"And if you say she isn't? Then what do you do?"

Three steps of gravel.

"I've never once killed a child," he said. "I'd like to keep it that way."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got today."

The gravel crunched under his ridiculous shoes and made no sound under her bare feet. Off in the trees to the east she could hear Embry in his fur, pacing them out of sight, because Jacob had told him to.

"Jacob imprinted on her," Leah said.

Harry stopped walking.

He turned and looked down at her under the umbrella, and his face had gone still the way it had on the rock on the plateau, when she'd told him about Sam and Emily.

"Imprinted," he said. "The gravity thing."

"Yeah."

"On a *baby?*"

"It's not — he'll be whatever she needs. Brother. Friend. For years. That's how it goes." She swallowed. "But if anything happens to her, it'll kill him. I've been in his head. I know. And Seth likes them. And Jake's my Alpha, and he's never once used it on me. Not when I left. Not when I came back with a door in my head he couldn't open. He told everybody to drop it." She looked up at him. "So that's what you're walking into at ten o'clock."

He stood in the rain with the umbrella over both of them for a long time.

"Bollocks," he said finally, quietly, with feeling.

"Yeah."

"That makes it a good deal harder."

"Yeah."

He looked over her head at the glass house, at the gold light and the shapes in it, and she watched him think the way she'd watched him take Kell apart on the mountain, one piece at a time.

"I'll tell you first," he said. "Whatever I decide. Before them. Before Geneva. You'll hear it from me."

"That's it?"

"That's most of what I've got." He looked back down at her. "And I'll look at her properly tomorrow. Better than I looked at her in there. At what she *is,* and leave her mum out of it."

"You really don't like Bella."

"I really don't." He didn't pretend. "She had a heartbeat, Leah."

"I know," said Leah. "I've been saying that since August. Nobody listens to me."

"I listen to you," Harry said.

---

She looked up at him under the black umbrella, in the rain, with the glass house lit up behind his shoulder and all of them in it.

For a year and a half, everything she'd felt had belonged to everybody. Every thought. Every bad night. Every time she'd looked at Sam. And for four weeks she'd kept this behind seven words where nobody could get at it. All of it. Every bit of him.

She thought about what she'd let them see.

She put her hand flat on his chest, under the open front of the black coat, over his heart. She knew what she'd find. Forty-four beats a minute. Slow and heavy, a big animal asleep in its den, the same heart that hadn't changed its pace once in that living room — not for Edward's snarl, not for Rosalie's hiss, not for Jacob standing over him shaking.

*One.* A long wait. *Two.*

She went up on her toes and kissed him, there in the Cullens' driveway, in front of the whole glass wall.

And under her palm, his heart went off like a startled horse.

Eight vampires heard it. She knew they did. She felt the umbrella tip and the rain come down the back of her neck and didn't care. His hand came up and cupped the back of her head, careful, and he made the sound in his chest, the low rumble she'd felt on the rock above Hudson Bay, and her wolf got up off its back and stood there with its tail going.

From two hundred yards up the drive, through a wall of glass and the rain, she heard Seth yell, "*OH MY GOD,*" and Emmett start laughing again, and Rosalie say, perfectly clearly, "Well, *finally,* maybe now she'll stop being such a — " and Esme say, "*Rosalie.*"

She let go.

His heart was still going hard under her hand. She left her hand there a second longer, to feel it.

"Well," Harry said, a little hoarsely. "That's five."

"That doesn't count."

"It's on a drive. It's in the rain. There's an audience." He straightened the umbrella. "I've had worse fifth dates."

"Name one."

He thought about it. "No," he admitted.

"Where are you even staying?"

"Hargreaves found me a house. Somewhere called Forks. On a river." He frowned. "I think he bought it. He might have bought the river."

"You're unbelievable."

"So I'm told." He tucked her hand into the crook of his arm under the umbrella, like somebody's grandfather in an old movie, and turned them back up the drive. "Come on. I'll walk you back. Your Alpha's going to want a word, and your brother's going to want several, and the blonde one's going to want to throw something."

"She wants to throw something at everybody."

"Then I'll be in good company."

They walked back up toward the lit house. She could see them all now. Carlisle and Esme. Emmett, still laughing, with Seth bouncing beside him in his cutoffs. Rosalie with her arms folded and a face like Christmas had come early. Jacob, alone at the end of the glass, watching them come, his face giving away nothing.

And Bella, with the kid on her hip.

Renesmee had one small hand pressed flat against the glass. She was watching the man in the black coat come up the drive, steady, the way she'd watched him across the living room.

Harry saw her. Leah felt his arm go still under her hand.

He started to hum. Low, under his breath, badly off-key, the way he'd hummed on the rock on the plateau. It took her four bars to place it.

"Don't," she said.

"Your dad's song."

"I know whose song it is."

He stopped. They walked on up the drive in the rain toward the house where the kid stood at the window, and the song went on in Leah's head anyway, the way it had on the boat every time, her father's voice flat and happy and terrible over the engine. Creedence.

*Bad Moon Rising.*

Ten o'clock tomorrow.
