# Chapter Three: Bad Moon Rising

The deadfall behind the last house on the lake road was an old spruce that had gone over in some windstorm before Leah was born. Its root plate stood up out of the ground on edge, a wall of dirt and stones and dead roots taller than a man, and for a week she'd been using it as a changing room. Nobody came up here. The dog at the last house had learned her smell and quit barking at it.

She came down out of the trees at a walk with the sky going gray behind the eastern peaks. The frost lay so heavy on the moss that every step crunched. Her legs had the long, loose ache that came after forty miles, there was caribou sitting in her belly like a stone, and her head was quiet.

She stepped in behind the root plate and let go.

Fur in, bones folding, the cold hitting bare skin that didn't care about it. She crouched in the frozen dirt until her hands quit shaking, then reached down and started on the knots at her ankle.

Her fingers found the pouch before her eyes did.

She'd forgotten about it. Somewhere between the plateau and the cottonwoods and the long gray miles of river, she had actually forgotten, and there it was, tied off against her plastic bag with a knot she hadn't tied: soft dark leather, worn shiny at the seams, the drawstring pulled snug. It sat in her palm weighing about as much as a deck of cards.

She got dressed first. Shorts, tank top, the gray T-shirt. The running shoes were stiff with frost and she had to beat them against the root plate before she could get her feet in. Then she sat down on a rock with her back to the dirt wall and opened the pouch.

The mirror was smaller than she'd expected. A square of glass the size of a playing card, set in a thin frame of some dark wood that had been handled smooth. No clasp, no handle, nothing written on it anywhere. Her own face looked back at her out of the glass, gray in the dawn light, with a smear of dried brown at the corner of her mouth.

She licked her thumb and scrubbed it off. Caribou. Blood came back through the phase with you sometimes, if you'd gotten enough of it on you.

She held the mirror up again and waited for it to do something.

It didn't. A pale girl with a black snarl of hair and shadows under her eyes looked out at her, and behind the girl the sky went slowly from gray to the color of skim milk.

*If you want me, say my name into it.*

She looked at it a long time. Then she put it back in the pouch and pulled the drawstring tight and shoved the whole thing deep in the pocket of her shorts, and went down the hill to wash dishes.

---

"You look worse than Friday," Dot said.

"Thanks."

"Don't thank me. Put an apron on." Dot cracked two eggs one-handed onto the flat-top, then two more, and looked at Leah sideways over the top of her glasses while they spat. "Something in your hair."

Leah reached up. Her fingers came away with a pinch of stiff, hollow, grayish-brown hair, coarse as broom bristle, the kind that came off a caribou hide in tufts and stuck to everything it touched. She'd showered at the motel for five minutes, which apparently hadn't been enough.

Dot looked at the hair. Leah looked at the hair.

"Went for a walk," Leah said.

"Uh-huh," said Dot, and flipped the eggs. "Bet you did."

Leah dropped the tuft in the trash and tied on her apron and went to the sink.

Monday breakfast was the highway crew, then the old men in their feed caps at the corner table, arguing about whether the new goalie the Canucks had gotten from Florida was going to be worth a damn. Then a couple of moose hunters from Alberta. Then a woman from the forestry office who ate half a muffin.

Leah washed. Plates and mugs and the egg pan and the bacon sheet and the big steel bowl Dot made pancake batter in. The window over the sink held the lake, flat and gray, steaming in the cold the way it did on still mornings, long slow ribbons of mist lifting off the water and turning gold where the sun found them. Across the bay the lodge with the green roof sat in its clearing at the bottom of the hill. Smoke was going up from its chimney.

She didn't look at it more than twice.

---

At ten past ten she heard the outboard.

It came across the bay from the direction of the lodge, an aluminum boat with a little motor on the back, going much too fast for somebody who was driving like that, swinging wide, correcting, swinging wide the other way. Leah watched it through the window with a sheet pan dripping in her hands. A skinny figure sat hunched at the tiller in a red parka with the hood up. The bow was piled with something that caught the light. Gold.

The boat came at the dock nose-first, way too hot, and hit the pilings with a hollow *bonk* that Leah felt through the kitchen floor.

"What in the hell," Dot said, coming to look.

The driver climbed out onto the dock on his hands and knees. Then he got up, pushed back his hood, tied the boat off with a knot that would not have held a dog, and started unloading.

Boxes. Gold boxes, stacked three and four high, tied with brown ribbon. He got a stack of five into his arms, couldn't see over it, and came up the dock toward the back of the café, feeling for every plank with his foot.

Leah went and opened the kitchen door before he could fall through it.

He was somewhere in his thirties, thin as a fence rail, with dark circles under his eyes like bruises and two days of stubble. Under the red parka he wore a white chef's jacket with two rows of buttons. He peered around the side of the stack at her.

"Is one of you Leah?"

"Me."

"Oh, thank God." He came in sideways through the door and put the boxes down on the steel prep table with the care of a man setting down a bomb. "Theo Marchand. I've been cooking for your — " He looked at her. He looked at Dot, who had arrived at Leah's shoulder with her spatula. He seemed to think several things and reject all of them. "For Mr. Potter. Across the lake. I'm instructed to give you these."

Leah read the lid of the top box. Curly gold script on cream: PEETERS & FILS. Underneath, smaller: *Maître Chocolatier — Bruxelles — depuis 1931.*

"There are eleven more in the boat," Theo said.

Dot said, "Eleven more *what?*"

"Boxes. Of chocolate. Larger ones." He wiped his forehead with the back of his wrist. "He brought them back from Belgium on Saturday night. They've been in the walk-in at the lodge, and I've been told that if I take any home to Vancouver I'm to — " he closed his eyes — "'enjoy it in good health.' I've worked in kitchens for sixteen years. I know what that chocolate costs." He opened his eyes. "Please take it. I need it out of my life."

"You're going home?" Leah said.

"Noon. The plane's taking me to Smithers, and then I'm on the four o'clock to Vancouver, and then I'm going to sleep for a week." He said it like a man describing heaven. "I have a wife. I have a cat. I've cooked for a week for one person and he ate like a — " He stopped. "He ate a great deal."

"Yeah," Leah said. "He does that."

Theo looked at her with something that might have been sympathy. Then he unzipped his parka, dug in the inside pocket of his chef's jacket, and came out with an envelope. Heavy cream-colored paper, folded once, *Clearwater* written across the front in black ink.

"He said to tell you he knows," Theo said. "And that he's sorry. And that he isn't."

He went back out for the rest.

---

It took him three trips, and Leah went out and carried the last two stacks herself because watching him on the icy dock made her nervous. When it was all inside, the gold boxes covered the prep table and half the counter and one of the stools, and the kitchen smelled so strongly of cocoa and roasted nuts and vanilla that even the bacon grease gave up.

Dot stood and looked at it all with her arms folded.

"Huh," she said.

Theo accepted a coffee and drank half of it standing at the hatch. Dot made him a fried-egg sandwich without asking, which he ate in about five bites, and then he looked down at his empty hands and seemed for a moment as though he might cry.

"Did you know he bought the lodge?" he said. "The pilot told me. The Hendersons have been trying to sell it for three years. A man in London rang them on Thursday morning and asked what they wanted for it, and they told him a number, as a joke, and he said fine." He shook his head. "They're moving to Kelowna. They're very happy. Mrs. Henderson cried on the phone."

He thanked Dot for the sandwich, very formally, and walked out through the back door and down the dock to the boat, and drove it away across the bay at the same terrifying speed he'd come, swinging wide, correcting. Leah watched him all the way to the far shore. He didn't hit anything. At ten to twelve the yellow floatplane took off from the lodge dock, roared low over the town, and turned south.

---

She read the note on her break, sitting on an upturned milk crate behind the café with her back against the wall.

The handwriting was small and slanting and messy, the letters crowding each other as though they'd been in a hurry to get somewhere.

> *Clearwater —*
>
> *As promised. I'm aware. Distribute to the deserving, or eat the lot in one sitting; I'd respect either. Feel free to tell me off. I'll be in Geneva being told off by professionals.*
>
> *I asked, by the way. Hargreaves bought it. The lodge, I mean, though the chocolate's also bought, so that's two. He says he "saw an opportunity." He always sees an opportunity. I've begun to think it's medical.*
>
> *Luc can write his name now. The werewolf. He did it on a napkin and then he cried again.*
>
> *— H.*
>
> *P.S. The praline with the hazelnut. Trust me.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it along its crease and put it in her back pocket with the mirror, and sat there on the milk crate with the cold coming up off the lake, and didn't think about anything at all for a while.

---

Brynn came in at eleven for the lunch shift and stopped dead in the kitchen door with her ski jacket half off.

"Oh my God," she said. "Oh my *God.* What is — Dot, what *is* all this?"

"Ask Washington."

Brynn turned around and looked at Leah with her mouth open. Then she grabbed the top box off the nearest stack, read the label, set it down with exaggerated care, and grabbed Leah's wet arm with both hands.

"Is it from the English guy?"

Leah kept scrubbing. "What English guy?"

"*Leah.* The English guy. *Everybody* told Mom. He tried to buy the bar. He paid five hundred dollars for a bottle of Scotch. Dale Pruitt put his hand on you and you threw him into the jukebox." Brynn's eyes were huge. "Ray says he dresses like the Queen's gamekeeper. Ray won't say if he's cute. Is he cute?"

"He's fine."

"*Fine.*"

"He's a guy, Brynn. He's got a face. It's fine."

Brynn looked at her for a long moment. Then her mouth fell open again, slowly, wider than before.

"You *like* him."

"No."

"You went red. You went *red,* Leah, you never go red, you didn't even go red when Dale — "

"It's the dishwater," Leah said. "It's hot."

"*You* are hot. You're always hot. It's your whole *thing.*" Brynn spun around. "Dot. *Dot.* She went red."

"I've got eyes," Dot said from the grill.

Leah put her head down and washed the stockpot with great concentration.

---

What she did with it was give it away.

She thought about it all through the lunch rush, with the gold boxes stacked at her back and the smell of them following her around the kitchen, and by two o'clock she'd decided. She wasn't going to sit in room six for a month eating Belgian chocolate off the orange bedspread like some kept woman in a movie. He'd written *distribute to the deserving.* Fine. She'd distribute it.

Dot got the biggest box. Dot looked at it, and at Leah, and put it on the shelf over the coffee station without a word, and Leah saw her look at it twice more before close.

The old men got one to share. They opened it at the corner table and looked at the rows of glossy little shapes nested in their paper cups with deep suspicion, and then Walt Kowalchuk ate one, and stopped arguing about the goalie, and sat there with his eyes closed.

The highway crew got one.

Brynn got two. Brynn hugged her so hard her back cracked.

After close she carried a stack across the street. Wes took his without looking up from the fishing show, grunted, and then looked up, and looked at the label, and looked at her for the first time since she'd checked in, really looked, with his eyebrows up over his reading glasses. "Huh," he said. Everybody in this town said *huh.* She was starting to think it was a dialect.

Ray got one at the Prospector, and opened it on the bar, and ate three in a row with an expression of growing betrayal, as if the chocolate had personally let him down by being this good.

There was one more box than she had people. She stood on the frosty sidewalk under the one streetlight with it in her hands and looked at the RCMP detachment down the block, where the lights were still on and the white SUV sat out front.

She thought about it.

Constable Trudel opened the door herself, in uniform pants and a gray RCMP T-shirt and socks, with a can of Diet Coke in one hand. She looked at Leah, then at the gold box, then at Leah again.

"Somebody bought too much," Leah said, and held it out.

Trudel took it, slowly. She turned it over and read the label on the bottom. "Brussels." The gray eyes came up. "Your friend who drove you up from Washington?"

"Different friend."

"You've got a lot of friends for somebody who's been here a week."

"Apparently."

Trudel looked at her a while longer. Then something in her face eased, very slightly. "Well. Thanks." She tucked the box under her arm. "That fella Kell called, by the way. Friday afternoon. Took a charter out to Dease Lake and arranged for somebody to run his car down to Smithers. So that's that, I guess." She frowned faintly, as if there were a stone in her shoe she couldn't find. "Funny. I was sure I had more questions for you."

"Good night," Leah said.

"Night," said Trudel, and shut the door.

---

Back in room six she sat on the bed with her boots on and looked at the last box, the small one, the one she'd kept back without letting herself decide to.

She untied the ribbon.

Inside, nested in brown paper cups, were twelve chocolates in four rows. Dark ones, milk ones, one dusted in something gold. In the corner, a little dome of pale milk chocolate with half a toasted hazelnut pressed into the top like a jewel.

She ate it.

She sat very still on the orange bedspread with her mouth full, and a sound came out of her she had not given permission for.

The moose looked down at her from its lake at sunset.

"Don't," she told it, and ate another one.

---

Early Wednesday morning, at ten past one, her jeans said her name.

She was asleep for once, on her stomach on the orange bedspread with the window cracked and the heater clanking, and the voice came out of the jeans on the chair by the door, muffled and polite, the way someone talks when they've knocked on a door at the wrong hour and are already sorry about it.

"Leah Clearwater?"

She was off the bed and in a crouch beside it before she was properly awake, lip up, heat climbing her spine.

"Sorry. Christ. It's me. It's only me."

She knew the voice. She stayed in the crouch a second longer anyway, out of principle, then got up and went and dug in the back pocket of the jeans and came out with the pouch. The leather had heated up like a stone left in the sun. The mirror inside was hotter, and when she tipped it out into her palm her own face wasn't in it.

His was. Close, a little too close, as though he were holding his end at arm's length and squinting. Hair worse than ever. A white shirt with the collar open and the sleeves rolled to the elbow. Behind him was a window full of daylight, and through the window a gray lake and gray roofs, and a tall white plume of water going straight up out of the lake into the sky like a geyser that had forgotten to stop.

"Hello," Harry said.

"It's one in the morning."

"It's ten past ten here. I've been up since six. Solveig's been at me since seven." He tilted his head, studying her through the glass. "You were asleep. I've woken you. I did think about that. I thought, she said ask, so I'll ask whether I can ring, and then I realized the only way to ask was to ring, and I'm afraid I got stuck."

"So you rang."

"So I rang." He looked faintly pleased with himself for having got through the explanation. "Can I ring? In general. Going forward."

Leah sat down on the end of the bed with the mirror in her hand.

"Not at one in the morning."

"Noted."

"Not when I'm at work."

"Also noted."

"And if I don't answer, you don't keep saying my name."

"I'd never." He held up a hand, palm out. "Once, then I go away."

She looked at the white plume behind him. "What's that?"

"Fountain. In the lake. The Genevans are very proud of it. It goes up a hundred and forty meters, apparently, and nobody can tell me why." He glanced back over his shoulder at it. "I've been looking at it for three hours. It's the most interesting thing in the room."

From somewhere off to one side, out of the frame, a woman's voice said flatly, "Eight hundred and twelve pages."

Harry didn't turn his head. "Solveig says hello."

"I did not," said the voice.

"She's very fond of me."

"I am not."

Leah found she was almost smiling, and stopped. "She came to see me. Friday. In purple ski pants."

"I know. I picked those." He sounded briefly delighted, and then the delight went out of his face. "She told me some other things. About the car." He grimaced. "I forgot the car."

"You forgot a whole car."

"I'm not a car person. I've only ever had the use of one car, when I was twelve, and it flew, and it went feral, and the last anyone saw of it it was living wild in a forest in Scotland." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I know how that sounds."

"The cop asked me where I crossed the border."

"The Mountie won't ask again. Solveig fixed it. But that's on me, and I'm sorry, and I mean it this time. And the other thing." He paused. "Brynn. The stag."

Leah didn't say anything.

"Solveig wrote it up under my name. In red. She underlined it twice; I heard the quill go through the parchment from across the room." He was quiet a moment. "I can fix that, if you want. It's tiny. Brynn'd never know. I'd lift the dream out and leave the rest exactly where it is. Thirty seconds, at the café, when she brings me a coffee."

He waited.

It took Leah a moment to understand that he was waiting for her. He'd laid it out and stopped, and the stopping was the point. He was asking.

She thought about Brynn turning the thermos around in her hands. *It was really pretty. Isn't that dumb?*

"Leave it," she said.

"You're sure?"

"She liked it. She said it was pretty. It's a nice dream." Leah looked down at the carpet. "It's the only part of that night she gets to keep. Leave it."

"All right." No argument. Just that. "I'll leave it."

The heater clanked twice. Out on Main Street a truck went by, slow, its tires crunching on the frost.

"Theo got off, then," Harry said. "With the chocolate."

"He brought it over in your boat. He almost hit the café."

"He's a terrible driver. I offered him the plane both ways and he said he'd sooner swim." His head tilted. "What did you do with it?"

"Gave it away."

There was a pause. "All of it?"

"To Dot. And Brynn. And the old guys at the corner table, and the highway crew, and Wes, and Ray. And the cop."

He stared at her through the glass. Then he started to laugh, properly, his head going back, and from off to the side Leah heard Solveig say something sharp in what was probably Norwegian.

"The cop," he said. "You gave Belgian chocolate to the Mountie who was asking where you crossed the border."

"She said thank you."

"I bet she did." He wiped his eye with the heel of his hand. "And Doreen? Did she try the praline?"

"She said *huh.*"

"Is that good?"

"That's the best thing she's ever said about anything."

He looked so satisfied that Leah wanted to reach through the glass and flick him on the nose.

"You kept one box," he said.

She hadn't told him that.

"You smell like hazelnut," he said, by way of explanation. "Even through this. It's a very good mirror."

"*Through* it? You can smell through it?"

"Not really. A bit. It's mostly a guess." The grin came back, crooked. "But I've just watched you go red, so I'll take that as confirmation."

"It's hot in here."

"It's one in the morning in the Yukon."

"It's *B.C.*"

"Is it? I'm never sure where the line is." He shifted, and the window behind him slid out of frame, and she got a glimpse of a desk buried under paper, actual stacks of it, yellowish and curling at the edges, held down by a coffee cup and what looked like a small brass telescope. "Listen. Before you hang up on me. Which I can see you're about to do."

She was. She stopped.

"Sunday," he said. "If you're free. Third date. I promised you some answers."

Leah turned the mirror over in her fingers, so the dark wooden back faced the ceiling and his voice came up out of her palm.

"I pick," she said.

"Done."

She turned it back. He was watching her, waiting, with his head tilted.

"We're going fishing," she said.

There was a long silence from Geneva.

"I've never been fishing," Harry said.

"I know. You'd have said so by now. You'd have told me about the time you caught a shark with a spell or whatever."

"I've never caught anything with a spell. I wouldn't know where to start." He frowned. "What do I need?"

"A rod. A licence. Clothes for the cold."

"I don't feel the cold."

"Then whatever you want. And no magic."

His eyebrows went up. "None?"

"None. Not one thing. You don't get to make the fish jump in the boat."

"What if I fall in?"

"Then you get wet."

He considered this with every sign of real alarm, which she enjoyed more than she was going to admit.

"A licence," he said finally. "Where does one get a licence?"

"The Mercantile sells them. Or the government. Or whatever. Figure it out." She looked at him. "Don't send Hargreaves."

"I'd never send Hargreaves." He paused. "I might ring Hargreaves."

"*Potter.*"

"I'll sort it," he said quickly. "Sunday. Fishing. No magic. Licence." He nodded to himself, as though committing a list to memory. "What time?"

"Six. In the morning. They bite early."

"Six," he repeated, in the voice of a man who had just been told the date of his execution, and she laughed out loud before she could stop herself, alone in room six at one in the morning, and saw him see it.

He looked pleased, and said nothing about it.

"Wait," she said, as the glass started to cloud. It was fogging over from the edges in, the way a bathroom mirror cleared, only backward. "How do I — if I want to — "

"Say my name into it."

"Which one?"

"Whichever you like. Potter, Harry, oi, you. It's not fussy." The fog closed in. Through the last clear patch in the middle she saw the crooked grin. "Night, Clearwater."

Then it was just a mirror again, and her own face in it, flushed, with her hair flattened on one side from the pillow.

She put it back in its pouch and lay down on the bedspread and looked at the ceiling. Somewhere very far away, on the other side of the world, a man was sitting in a room full of paper looking at a fountain, and in four days he was going to try to cast a fishing rod without magic, in front of her, at six in the morning.

*Whichever you like.*

She lay there a long time with that.

She'd called him Potter. Just now, about Hargreaves, without thinking. It had come out of her the way her hand came off a hot stove, without her deciding.

*Harry.* She didn't say it. She thought it, carefully, alone in her own head, where nobody could hear. It sat there in the dark like a stone in her hand. Her dad on the porch with his coffee. Her dad singing Creedence on the boat with a voice like a goose being stepped on.

She turned on her side, facing the wall.

*Potter's fine,* she thought. *Potter works.*

She fell asleep before she'd decided whether she believed it.

---

By Saturday afternoon the whole town knew.

Leah heard it from Brynn, who'd heard it from her mother, who'd heard it from Marjorie Sutherland at the Mercantile. The English guy had come in at two o'clock and asked, very politely, where one bought a fishing licence. Then Marjorie had asked if he had a rod. A reel, line, lures, a net, a tackle box, a vest, a hat, a thermos, a cooler. He'd said no to all of it, increasingly cheerful, and bought every item, and two of the rods, and told Marjorie to keep the change, which had been a hundred and sixty dollars.

"She says he let her pick out his *hat,*" Brynn said, delighted, rolling silverware into napkins at the counter. "Like a little kid. She says he stood there and let her put it on him."

Leah had bought her own licence that morning, thirty-six dollars counted out in ones and fives, while Marjorie squinted at her Washington license and copied it into a book by hand. Category, it said on the form: NON-RESIDENT ALIEN.

She'd folded it into her wallet and decided not to think about it.

---

At four minutes to six on Sunday morning she was standing at the end of the dock behind the café in the dark, and she heard the boat coming across the bay without any motor.

There was no outboard noise, no paddle, nothing. Just the hiss of a hull through still water, coming closer. Then it slid out of the blackness under the dock light, the same aluminum boat Theo had nearly wrecked, gliding in on a dead-straight line with its motor tipped up out of the water. It stopped one inch from the pilings. It didn't touch them.

Harry stood up in the stern.

Marjorie had dressed him. That was the only possible explanation. He was wearing a red-and-black buffalo plaid wool jacket, very new, with the fold creases still in it. Under it, the cream cable-knit sweater. Brand-new dark green rubber boots up to his knees. A khaki fishing vest over all of it with what had to be thirty pockets, every one of them bulging. And on his head a canvas bucket hat in a shade of olive, with five brightly colored trout flies hooked into the band like a row of medals.

Leah stood on the dock and looked at him.

"Morning," he said. "Before you say anything, the date starts at six. It's five fifty-six." He looked at his watch. "Fifty-seven. I'm entitled."

"You look like a catalog."

"Marjorie said this is what people wear."

"Marjorie sold you everything in the store."

"She was very helpful." He reached up and touched the brim of the bucket hat. "She was particular about the hat."

"Oh my God."

"Get in," he said. "Before I start a magic-free life with no idea how to drive this."

She got in.

The boat was a sixteen-foot Lund with a twenty-five-horse Yamaha on the back. She had the motor down and the choke out and the fuel line squeezed before he'd finished sitting down on the middle bench, and she pulled the cord once, hard, the way her dad had taught her, and it caught on the first try and settled into a rough, burbling idle that she felt through the seat. Blue smoke, gasoline, cold water. Her hand on the tiller.

It went through her so fast she had to look away across the bay for a second.

"All right?" he said.

"Fine." She twisted the throttle. "Sit in the middle. Don't stand up. If you stand up and fall in, I'm leaving you."

"You keep threatening to leave me in places."

"Keep giving me reasons."

---

Walt Kowalchuk had told her, on Thursday, between arguments about the goalie, that the lakers came up onto the rocks in the fall off Sheep Point, a mile and a half up the north shore, where an old slide had spilled a long tongue of cobble down into the water and the bottom dropped off steep beside it. He'd told her the way her dad used to tell people where the fish were, which was to say he'd made it sound like a secret and then told her everything, including which spoon.

They got there with the sky going from black to iron gray. Mist was lifting off the water in long slow coils, so thick in places that the far shore vanished and they were sitting in a small round room of gray. She cut the engine and let the boat drift.

The silence came down like snow.

Somewhere off in the mist a loon called, the long wavering cry, and another answered from so far off it was barely a sound at all. They'd be leaving soon, Brynn said. All the loons went south before the lake froze.

"Right," Harry said quietly. "What do I do?"

She showed him.

Open the bail. Hold the line against the rod with your finger. Bring the rod back over your shoulder, slow, and then forward, and let go of the line at the top of the swing, and close the bail when the spoon hits the water. Reel. Slow. Not that slow. Feel it bump on the rocks and lift the tip a little, so it doesn't snag.

He nodded at each step with total concentration. Then he brought the rod back, and forward, and the spoon slapped the water two feet from the boat with a sound like a hand hitting a table, because he'd forgotten to open the bail.

He looked at it.

"Again," Leah said.

The second time he opened the bail and forgot to let go of the line, and the spoon went straight up in the air and came down in the boat at her feet.

The third time he let go of the line too early and the spoon went backward over his shoulder and hooked itself into the band of the bucket hat, right next to a yellow trout fly, and stayed there.

He sat very still with the rod in his hands and the spoon in his hat.

"Don't," he said.

Leah had both hands over her mouth.

"Clearwater. Don't."

She lost it. She doubled over on the stern seat and laughed until the boat rocked, until she was wheezing, until the loon off in the mist went quiet as if it wanted to hear what was so funny. He sat there through all of it with a fishing spoon hanging off his hat, waiting with enormous dignity for her to finish.

"Hold still," she said finally, getting up, steadying herself on his shoulder. "Don't move. You'll put it through your ear."

She worked the hook out of the canvas. It took a while. She had to lean in close to do it, standing over him in the rocking boat with her knee against his thigh, and he did hold still, perfectly, the whole time. Hot as a stove, even through the plaid. He smelled like wet earth and pine and the new canvas of the hat. She didn't look at his face.

"There," she said, and sat back down, and found that her own face was hot.

"Thank you," he said gravely, and took the hat off and put it on the bench beside him, and picked up the rod again.

His fourth cast went thirty yards and landed right on the edge of the drop-off.

She watched him reel it in. Slow. Lifting the tip when it bumped. Exactly the way she'd said, as though he'd taken her instructions apart and put them back together inside his head, and from then on every cast landed where he aimed it. He'd made every mistake there was to make in the first three and then quit making them.

"You learn fast," she said, grudgingly.

"Only by doing it wrong first." He cast again. "I was a Seeker at school. Sport. You fly about on a broom looking for a little gold ball and you catch it. Got rather good at watching things and copying what works." The spoon hit the water. "Don't ask me to explain the broom."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. I could see you winding up to it."

She had been. She cast her own line out, and the spoon went out in a long flat arc and dropped into the water without a splash, and she let it sink and started reeling.

---

The first fish hit hers twenty minutes later.

It was a heavy, sullen pull, a weight that came alive all at once and went straight down toward the rocks. Leah let it run. She kept her rod tip up and her thumb off the spool and felt the line hum, and she was ten again, and twelve, and sixteen, on the *Clearwater* off James Island with her father beside her saying *easy, Lee, easy, he's not going anywhere you don't let him.*

She brought it up alongside the boat in four minutes. A lake trout, long and thick, its back the gray-green of wet slate and its sides scattered with pale cream spots, its fins edged in white. Five pounds, maybe six. She netted it herself, one smooth scoop, and lifted it into the boat flapping, and took it by the gills and knocked it once on the head with the little wooden club from the tackle box, and it went still.

She knelt in the bottom of the boat with the fish across her knees, breathing hard. When she looked up he was watching her.

"Your dad," he said.

"Yeah."

"He'd have liked that."

She didn't say anything. She rinsed her hands over the side in the freezing water and picked up her rod.

They fished. The mist burned off as the sun came over the mountains, and the lake turned from gray to blue, and the hills around it lit up in gold and rust where the aspens had turned. She caught two more, smaller, both keepers. He caught nothing. He didn't seem to mind. He cast and reeled and cast and reeled with the same steady patience, and he asked her things in between.

What her dad fished for. Salmon, mostly — kings in the summer, silvers in the fall. Halibut off the banks if the weather held. What the boat had been called. The *Sue C.,* after her mom, until her mom made him change it because people kept making jokes, and then just the *Clearwater.* Whether she'd driven it. Since she was eleven. Whether she missed it.

She didn't answer that one. He didn't ask again.

She asked him things too, because it seemed fair.

"Did your aunt and uncle ever take you anywhere?"

"Once." He reeled steadily. "The zoo. My cousin's birthday. Mrs. Figg down the road usually had me when they went out, but she'd broken her leg tripping over one of her cats, so they had to bring me along."

"How was it?"

"I let a boa constrictor out on my cousin."

Leah stopped reeling.

"Not on purpose," Harry said. "I was ten. I didn't know what I was yet. The glass just — went. And the snake thanked me on its way out." He cast. "They didn't take me anywhere after that."

"The snake *thanked* you?"

"Third date's for the wolf thing," he said. "The snake's a fifth-date story at least."

She was about to say something rude when his rod bent double.

---

It was a big fish. She knew it the second the line started screaming off his reel, a long steady scream that went on and on, and the rod tip hauled down to the water so hard the boat swung around.

"*Keep your tip up,*" she said.

He kept his tip up.

"Don't reel when he's running. Let him take it. Let him get tired."

He let him take it. The line kept going. Fifty yards. Seventy. The fish was heading for deep water, out off the end of the point where the bottom fell away into blue-black, and Harry sat on the middle bench with the rod bent in a hoop and his face completely still, doing nothing, doing exactly what she said.

"Okay. He's stopped. Now reel. Pump it — lift the rod, then reel down on the way back. Lift. Reel. Like that."

He did it like that.

It took eleven minutes. Twice the fish turned and ran again, and twice Harry let it go without a word and started over. Once it went under the boat and Leah lunged across him and shoved his rod tip down into the water to keep the line off the motor, and they were tangled up together for a second, his arm and her arm and the rod, and she could feel his heart going under her shoulder, fast and hard, faster than his face let on.

"No magic," she said, through her teeth.

"Wouldn't dream of it."

The fish came up at last beside the boat. Leah saw it before he did, a long dark shape coming up out of the blue, and her breath stopped.

It was huge. Easily twice the size of hers. Twelve pounds, fourteen. A big, old, hook-jawed lake trout with a back as wide as her hand was long, its spots gone pale gold in the sunlight, its fins edged white. It hung there just under the surface beside the boat, fanning slowly, exhausted.

She got the net under it on the first try. It barely fit.

Harry sat on the bench and looked at it in the bottom of the boat, and his face did something she hadn't seen it do before. He looked like a kid. Like Seth the first time he caught a salmon at eight years old off the end of the jetty and ran a quarter mile down First Beach screaming to show their dad.

"That's — " he said, and stopped.

"Yeah."

"Did I — "

"You did it." She sat back on her heels in the bottom of the boat, grinning, and she didn't bother trying to stop. "No magic. That's a good fish, Potter. That's a really good fish."

He looked at her. Then at the fish.

"Do we eat it?" he asked.

She thought about her dad, standing in the stern of the *Clearwater* with a forty-pound king in his arms, his hands gentle, lowering it back over the side. *The big ones make the little ones, Lee. You eat the little ones.*

"No," she said. "We put him back."

He nodded, and asked her how.

She showed him. Wet hands, so you didn't take the slime off. One under the belly, one at the tail. Hold him in the water facing forward, upright, so the water went through his gills. Wait. Harry knelt at the side of the boat with his sleeves pushed up and his arms in the lake to the elbows, holding a fourteen-pound trout in freezing water, and waited, as long as it took. It took nearly two minutes. Then the fish gave a slow, heavy kick, and slid out of his hands, and went down into the blue and was gone.

He stayed there with his arms in the water a while longer, looking down.

"Bloody hell," he said quietly.

"Yeah."

He took his arms out of the lake and shook them off and looked at her across the boat, and there was water dripping off his elbows and a hook-jawed look of pure happiness on his face, and Leah's wolf stood up inside her and wagged its whole back end.

She pretended to look for the thermos.

---

The lodge was bigger up close than it looked from town. Two stories of logs gone silver with weather, the green steel roof, a long covered porch facing the water with a row of wooden chairs on it. A boathouse. A floatplane dock with no floatplane at it. Inside, a big main room with a stone fireplace you could have parked a car in, a moose head over the mantel that looked a good deal more professional than the moose in room six, and a long wall of windows full of lake and sky.

Leah gutted the fish on the cleaning table at the end of the dock while Harry watched. A cut from vent to gills, a thumb down the spine to clear the blood line, gills out. Three fish in under two minutes. The gulls came out of nowhere, the way gulls did, and she threw the guts out over the water and watched them fight.

"How old were you?" he asked.

"Six." She rinsed the knife. "He gave me a little knife with an orange handle. Mom about killed him."

He cooked.

He took his vest off and pushed up his sleeves and stood at the big six-burner range in the lodge kitchen with a cast-iron pan and a stick of butter and did everything himself. He filleted the trout, cleaner than she'd have guessed. He dredged the fillets in flour. He put a tray of potatoes in the oven with garlic and rosemary from the pantry. He cut lemons. When the butter foamed in the pan he laid the fish in skin side down and stood there with his hand flat on top of the fillet for ten seconds so it wouldn't curl, and then let it go and didn't touch it again.

He did it all without talking much, with the absent, steady concentration of somebody who'd done it a thousand times.

They ate at the end of the long table by the windows with the sun going down over the lake. It was the best fish she'd ever eaten in her life outside her father's smokehouse.

"You can *cook,*" she said, with her mouth full.

"Mm."

"Like, actually cook."

"I made breakfast for my aunt and uncle and cousin from when I was about eight," Harry said. "Bacon, eggs, fried bread, tomatoes. Every morning. If I burnt the bacon I didn't get any." He cut a potato in half. "So I stopped burning the bacon."

She looked at him across the table. He looked back, perfectly mild, and ate the potato.

She didn't say anything. She took another piece of fish.

---

She asked him on the porch afterward, in the cold, with the sky gone deep blue and a lopsided moon a little past half hanging over the mountains to the south. They sat in two of the wooden chairs with their boots up on the railing and a bottle of the Prospector's Scotch on the boards between them. The lake lay below them silver and black. Across the bay, the lights of Turnagain were coming on one by one along the shore.

"Third date," Leah said.

"It is."

"So tell me."

He didn't stall. He poured them each an inch and looked out at the water for a while, gathering it, and then he told her.

"My dad was an Animagus," he said. "It's a wizard who can turn into an animal. One animal, always the same one. You don't get to pick; it's whatever you are, underneath. His was a stag." He turned his glass in his fingers. "My godfather, Sirius, was a big black dog. And there was a third one, a friend of theirs, who was a rat. Which turned out to be accurate. He was the one who sold my mum and dad to Voldemort."

Leah said nothing.

"They did it at school. Illegally. They weren't supposed to; you're meant to register with the Ministry and everything. But another friend of theirs was a werewolf, and they figured out that a werewolf won't hurt an animal, only a person, so they taught themselves to do it so they could keep him company at the full moon." He was quiet a second. "Fifteen years old. It's very hard magic. Most fully trained adults can't manage it."

"So you did it too."

"After the war. When I was twenty." He drank. "Everybody I'd lost had done it. My dad. Sirius. I wanted something of theirs that nobody could take away. That was the idea."

"And it turned you into *that?*"

"It's meant to take a while. You keep a mandrake leaf in your mouth for a month, from full moon to full moon, and you can't take it out, not to eat, not to drink, not to talk. If you do, you start over. I started over four times. The last time I made it to day twenty-nine and sneezed." His mouth twisted. "Then you make a potion and wait for a thunderstorm. There were no thunderstorms in Britain for five months. Then there was one, in the middle of the night, in April, over a hill in Devon, and I was standing on top of the hill in it in my pajamas, and I drank the potion."

He stopped.

"You're supposed to change into the animal and back," he said. "It's meant to be uncomfortable. People describe it as a sort of stretching. I'd read every account there was. I was expecting a stag."

"And?"

"And I was on fire." He said it plainly. "From the inside out. As though somebody'd swapped my blood for petrol and dropped a match in. And then I was being poured into something four times too big for me and it kept getting bigger." He looked at her sideways. "You know."

She did know. That was the thing. She knew it better than she knew almost anything.

"I came round at the bottom of the hill," he said. "It was raining. I'd rolled about a hundred yards and knocked down a dry-stone wall that'd been standing since Queen Victoria. There were sheep everywhere, screaming. And I looked down at my feet and there were these paws the size of dinner plates, and black fur, and I could smell — " He shook his head. "Everything. Every sheep on that hill. The farmer asleep a mile off. Rain on the stone. A fox in a hedge. I lay there in the mud and smelled the whole world for the first time, and I didn't have the first idea what I'd done."

"A wolf," Leah said.

"A wolf." He refilled his glass. "Animagi are normal-sized. That's one of the rules. A normal animal, normal weight. McGonagall — my old teacher — is a tabby cat, and she's a perfectly ordinary tabby cat. They don't run hot afterward. They don't heal like I do or eat like I do, or smell you from a hundred feet away and know what you had for breakfast. It doesn't stay in you when you change back." He was quiet. "Mine did. All of it."

"Why?"

"Nobody knows."

She looked at him.

"I've had three Healers have a look," he said. "Very senior. One of them fainted. Two men from the Department of Mysteries wanted to keep me for study, which I declined in the strongest possible terms. The Ministry's Animagus Register has exactly one line about me." He held up a finger. "*Potter, H. J. — Wolf (?).* With a question mark. In the Head of Department's own handwriting."

Leah laughed before she could stop it. He looked pleased.

"So you don't know what you are," she said.

"I know what I am. I just don't know why." He shrugged. "Six years. I've stopped minding."

She thought about that. She thought about her own first time in the backyard, her body coming apart, and nobody to tell her why except old men with stories. Billy Black and Old Quil and her mother looking at her like a riddle.

"Does anybody hear you?" she asked. "When you're like that. In your head."

"No. There's only me."

"Ever?"

"Ever."

She looked out at the lake. She'd run eighteen hundred miles to get what he'd had all along without trying.

"Is that lonely?" she asked.

He thought about it, the way he'd thought about her other questions. Honestly. Taking his time.

"Sometimes," he said.

She heard her own answer in it, from the bar, a week ago. *Does it hurt?* *Sometimes.* She didn't know what to do with that, so she drank her Scotch.

---

"The clothes," she said, a while later. "You said third date for that too."

"I did." He set his glass down. "Come on, then."

They went down the porch steps and out to the end of the dock, where the moonlight lay on the planks like spilled milk. He stood in the middle of it in the buffalo plaid and the rubber boots and looked at her.

"Watch," he said, and he didn't do anything she could see.

There was no sound. The air around him seemed to fold in on itself, the way air does over a road in summer, and then it was full of black. The dock didn't creak. The water around the pilings didn't move. One second a man stood on the planks, and the next a wolf filled the dock from one side to the other, his shoulder level with the top of her head, his breath steaming in the moonlight, green eyes looking down at her with a distinctly smug expression.

She walked around him. All the way around, slowly. There was nothing on the planks. No torn plaid, no shredded rubber, no buttons. She crouched and looked under him, and he lifted one enormous forepaw politely so she could see.

"Where'd they go?" she said.

"Couldn't tell you," said the wolf. "Wherever it is, they come back folded."

He changed back. The air folded and he was standing there in the plaid jacket and the sweater and the green rubber boots, exactly as before, not a thread out of place. The boots still had the shine on them from the store.

She reached out and grabbed a fold of the jacket and rubbed it between her fingers. Dry. Hot from his body. Real.

"That's *so* unfair," she said.

"I know."

"I have to *strip.* Every time. In the woods. In the snow, probably, soon. I have to tie my underwear to my ankle like a — "

"I could do something about that," he said. "Your bundle. The cord. If you wanted. I'd have to work out how, it's different magic, but I'd imagine there's a way." He paused. "If you wanted."

She was still holding a fistful of his jacket. She looked at it, and at him.

"Ask me again sometime," she said.

"I will."

She didn't let go of the jacket.

He stood there in the moonlight with her hand on his chest and his arms at his sides, and waited, the same way he'd waited for the fish, the same way he'd waited in the mirror for her to decide about Brynn's dream. He stayed exactly where he was and left it with her.

She pulled.

He came down the few inches she needed, and she kissed him.

It was short, and not gentle. He tasted like Scotch and lemon and the cold. He was so hot under her hands that it was like kissing a woodstove, the same heat she carried, the same heat Sam had carried, and she felt a flash of Sam and it was gone, because Sam had never in his life stood still and let her decide anything. Harry's hand came up and rested at the side of her jaw, very light, and his thumb moved once along her cheekbone, and that was all.

Her wolf was going crazy. She ignored it.

She let go of the jacket and stepped back. Her heart was pounding so hard she could hear it.

He looked at her. He didn't say anything. His eyes had gone very dark.

"Don't make it weird," Leah said.

His mouth went up at one corner. "Wouldn't dream of it."

"I'm going home now."

"I could drive you."

"You can't drive."

"I could drive you *magically.*"

"Date's not over till I'm home." She was already backing up the dock toward the boathouse. "No magic."

She went behind the boathouse and stripped in the dark and rolled her clothes and tied them to her ankle with three hard knots. When she came back out onto the dock on four feet he was standing at the end of it with his hands in the pockets of the ridiculous plaid jacket, waiting.

She walked past him to the edge, and looked back once.

*Fourth date,* she thought, and then remembered he couldn't hear it, because she hadn't said he could listen.

He tilted his head anyway, as if he'd heard something.

She went off the end of the dock in a long flat dive and hit the black water of the bay with a shock that went right through her. It was the coldest thing she'd felt since the inlet. She came up snorting, pointed her nose at the lights of the town a mile across the water, and swam.

Behind her, all the way across, she could feel him standing on the end of the dock, watching her go.

---

Doreen Pelletier had done Thanksgiving at the Galena every year since the year she'd stopped having anybody to cook for at home, and she did it the same way every time. Closed to the public at two on the Monday. Four tables pushed together down the middle of the room under the good tablecloths. A twenty-four-pound bird, two kinds of stuffing, mashed potatoes with too much butter, turnip, green beans, her mother's cranberry relish, and pie. Anybody in Turnagain who didn't have somewhere better to be could come, and she didn't ask why they didn't.

This year there were thirteen at the table, and one of them had a title.

"You told him four o'clock?" she said.

"I told him four." Leah was setting out forks, very straight, not looking up. "He said he'd bring something."

"What?"

"I told him if he bought it I'd throw it in the lake. I said make something."

Dot grunted and went back to the gravy.

He came at four exactly. He'd quit the plaid, thank God, which Marjorie had been telling everybody about for a week. Waxed jacket, the cream sweater, the boots shined up like a soldier's, hair standing up every which way. He had a pie dish in each hand under clean dish towels, and he stood just inside the door looking at the pushed-together tables and the old men in their good shirts and Wes in an actual tie, and he seemed to Dot for a second like a man who'd walked into somebody's church by mistake.

Then Leah came out of the kitchen and he saw her and stopped looking like that.

Dot watched him cross the room to the hatch.

"Mrs. Pelletier," he said. "Thank you for having me. Where would you like these?"

"What are they?"

"Treacle tart."

"What the hell's a treacle tart?"

"Golden syrup, breadcrumbs, lemon, butter, pastry." He held one up a little, like evidence. "I made them last night. Leah said if I bought anything she'd throw it in the lake."

"She'd do it, too."

"I know. I've seen her throw a logger."

Dot looked at him over her glasses for a while. Six-two, maybe more, broad through the shoulders. Clean-shaven. Hands big and square and scrubbed, no rings. Nothing to look at on him that told her what he was, except that the dish towels he was holding were from the Henderson place, she'd know that blue check anywhere, and he smelled very faintly of woodsmoke and pine and something else she couldn't place. He stood in her doorway holding two pies like he'd been asked to hold them by a judge.

"Put them on the counter," she said. "By the saskatoon."

"Thank you."

"I'll taste it. If it's no good I'll say so."

"I'd expect nothing less," he said, and went and put them by the saskatoon, and then stood at the counter looking at her pie like a man looking at a rival's horse.

---

She sat him at the far end of the table, between Leah and Walt Kowalchuk, because Walt was deaf in one ear and wouldn't notice if he said anything odd. Brynn was across from them. Brynn spent the first ten minutes of the meal staring at him with her chin on her hand until her mother kicked her under the table.

He ate.

Dot had fed a lot of men in forty years. Loggers off a two-week shift, highway crews, hunters, fire crews in a bad summer who'd been on the line for thirty hours. She'd seen men eat. She watched this one go through a full plate in about six minutes, without hurrying, without making a mess of it, and then look at the platter with a sort of polite hope, and she nodded at it, and he took a second plateful of everything. Then a third. Then he passed the turkey down to Wes and asked Dot how she did the stuffing.

"Sage," she said. "Sausage. Day-old bread. The rest is none of your business."

"Fair enough."

"Where'd you learn to make pastry?"

"Hogw — school," he said. "There was a lot of it about. And my aunt's kitchen, before that, but she didn't know I was learning."

Leah, beside him, gave him a look. Dot didn't know what the look meant. He seemed to.

Constable Trudel was halfway down the table on the other side, in her civvies and her pager, eating like she'd been on shift since six, which she had. Dot saw her watching him, the way Monique Trudel watched everything, taking his height and his hands and the cut of his jacket and filing it all away.

"So what brings you up our way, Mr. Potter?" Trudel asked, during the green beans.

"Fishing," he said.

Leah choked on her potatoes.

Brynn whacked her on the back. Dot watched the Englishman not look at Leah, very carefully, while Leah coughed.

"Catch anything?" Trudel asked.

"One. A lake trout. About fourteen pounds, she reckons." He tipped his head toward Leah. "She caught three."

Walt Kowalchuk, who heard what he wanted to, put his fork down. "Fourteen pounds? Off Sheep Point?"

"That's the one."

"What'd you get him on?"

"A red-and-white spoon. Leah picked it."

"Where's he now? You get him mounted?"

"I put him back."

Walt stared at him. "You put back a fourteen-pound laker."

"She told me to." He nodded at Leah again. "Said the big ones make the little ones."

Walt Kowalchuk turned all the way around in his chair and looked at Leah for a long time. Then he turned back to his plate and said, to nobody, "Huh," and Dot knew the girl had just been admitted to something that nobody in town under the age of sixty had been admitted to in about twenty years.

Trudel was still looking at him. She opened her mouth again, as if there was something else, and frowned, and closed it. Dot saw her rub her temple with two fingers like she had a headache coming. Then she reached for the rolls.

---

Dot tasted the treacle tart with the coffee.

It was too sweet. It was very nearly as sweet as eating a spoonful of syrup out of the tin. The pastry was short and the lemon cut through the sugar just enough, and there was something about it — the browned edge, the chew of it — that made her think of her grandmother's butter tarts in Saint Boniface, and she hadn't thought of her grandmother's butter tarts in thirty years.

He was watching her. So was half the table.

"Too sweet," Dot said.

He nodded, like he'd expected it.

She ate the rest of the slice. Then she cut herself another one and ate that too, and didn't say anything else about it, and when she looked up he was grinning at his coffee cup.

The second dish was gone by five-thirty. So was the saskatoon. She'd made three.

---

After, when people had their coats on and were arguing in the doorway about who was driving the Ernies home, Dot went into the kitchen and found the two of them at the sink.

Leah was washing. He was drying. He'd taken his jacket off and rolled the sleeves of the sweater up, and he was standing at Leah's elbow with a dish towel, taking the plates off the rack as fast as she racked them and stacking them on the steel counter in straight, neat piles. He'd done the roasting pan already. Dot could see it upside down on the rack, scrubbed back to bare metal, which was more than Brynn had managed in two years.

Leah said something Dot didn't catch. He said something back, low. Leah flicked a handful of dishwater straight into his face.

He stood there dripping.

And Leah laughed. Head back, shoulders shaking, one wet hand pressed against her mouth, a big ugly honking laugh that came right up from her belly. In four weeks Dot had heard the girl laugh maybe twice, and both times it had come out of her short and startled, like she'd been punched. This went on and on. She had to hold onto the edge of the sink.

He wiped his face with the dish towel, unhurried, and folded it, and put it down. Then he reached past her into the sink, cupped his hand, and Leah shrieked.

Dot went back out to the dining room before either of them saw her.

---

She caught him alone a half hour later, out back on the step behind the kitchen, looking at the lake.

It was full dark and cold, minus eight maybe, the stars out hard over the water and a skin of new ice growing out from the shore. He was standing on the step in his shirtsleeves with his jacket over his arm, not shivering, his breath going up in a column. Dot came out behind him in her coat and stood on the top step and took out her cigarette and held it.

He glanced at it.

"Quit in '89," she said. "I like to hold it."

"Fair enough." He looked back at the lake. "Thank you for dinner. I mean it. I've not had one like that in a while."

"Where do you usually go?"

"Friends. My mate's mum. There's about fifteen of them now, it's very loud." He was quiet a second. "Hers is good. Yours is better. I'll deny saying that in a court of law."

Dot turned the cigarette over in her fingers. Down at the shore the new ice made a small sound in the cold, a high clean ping, like somebody had flicked a wineglass.

"Andrew Kell," she said.

He didn't move. He didn't say anything. He waited.

"He coming back?"

"No," said the Englishman.

Dot looked at the side of his face for a long time. He stood there on her back step with his breath going up white, looking at the lake, and let her look as long as she liked.

"Good," Dot said finally.

"Yes."

"Brynn's been sleeping better."

"I'm glad."

She put the cigarette back in her coat pocket. "That girl in there," she said. "Washington. She's not the staying kind. Not up here. She'll go home one of these days. Maybe not this month, maybe not till spring. But she'll go."

"I know," he said.

"You know where home is?"

"South. That's all she's said."

"And you?"

He turned his head and looked at her then, finally. Green eyes, very level.

"I'll go where I'm asked," he said.

Dot considered him a while longer, a man in his shirtsleeves at minus eight who'd scrubbed her roasting pan without being asked. Then she nodded once and went back inside and shut the door, and left him there.

Brynn found her in the walk-in five minutes later, standing between the milk and the eggs with her arms crossed.

"Dot? You okay?"

"Fine," said Dot. "Hand me that crate. He ate all my pie."

---

The snow came for good the week after Halloween. It came at night, without wind, and in the morning the town was buried to the tops of the truck tires and still more was falling. The bays froze first. Then the lake froze out from the shore, a little farther every night, until only a long black lane of open water was left down the middle where the wind kept it moving. The floatplanes quit. The loons had gone. Dot started opening an hour later because nobody came in the dark.

On the eleventh of November the Galena closed at half past ten so Dot could stand at the cenotaph outside the Legion with the old men in their berets and medals, and Brynn pinned a red felt poppy on Leah's flannel before she went, fussing with it until it sat straight.

"You wear it till the eleventh hour," Brynn said. "Then you can put it on the wreath if you want. Or keep it."

"Okay."

"Are you calling your mom?"

Leah had been calling her mom every Saturday for seven weeks. She'd never told Brynn that. She looked at her.

"You always go to the pay phone on Saturdays," Brynn said, "and you always come back looking like somebody punched you in the stomach, and then you're nice to me for like an hour." She patted the poppy. "Tell her hi."

---

The pay phone outside the gas station had a drift against it up to the coin slot. Leah kicked it clear and scraped the silver off a new card with her thumbnail and dialed the long string of numbers, and it rang twice, and somebody snatched it up.

"Clearwater residence!"

Her breath went out of her.

"Seth."

"*LEE.*" It blasted out of the earpiece so loud she had to hold it away from her head. "Lee! Mom's at the store, she said you'd call, she said you always call at this time, I've been sitting here for like an *hour* — "

"Hi."

"Hi! Hi. Are you okay? You sound okay. You sound good. Are you still in Canada? Is it snowing? Mom says it's snowing."

"It's snowing."

"*Sweet.* Okay, okay, so, so much has happened, did Mom tell you about Nessie?"

"Who the hell is Nessie?"

"Renesmee. The baby." Seth was laughing already. "Jake calls her Nessie. Bella found out, right after she woke up, and she totally lost it. She was screaming, *you nicknamed my daughter after the Loch Ness Monster?* And then she went for him. Like, *went* for him." A pause. "I kind of got in the way."

Leah's hand closed on the receiver. "You *what?*"

"It's fine! It's totally fine. She broke my shoulder. And my collarbone, a little. Carlisle set it. It was healed in like a day."

"She broke your — *Seth.* When?"

"Like, weeks ago. Mom said not to tell you till you came home, 'cause you'd freak out."

"I'm freaking out."

"I know. That's why." He said it so reasonably that she wanted to reach down the phone line and strangle him. "She said sorry, like, a million times. She felt so bad. She's actually really nice now. She's way nicer as a vampire, Lee, it's weird."

Leah put her forehead against the cold steel of the phone box and breathed through her nose until the heat in her backed off. Eighteen hundred miles. She'd been eighteen hundred miles away, eating pie, while a newborn bloodsucker put her little brother's shoulder through a wall.

"Lee? You there?"

"I'm here."

"Don't be mad at Mom."

"I'm not mad at Mom."

"You sound mad at Mom." Then, as if that settled it, his voice dropped, suddenly confiding. "She's huge, Lee. Nessie. She looks like she's one already. Maybe two. She talks. Like, sentences. And when she touches your face you see stuff. What she's thinking. Pictures. It's so weird."

"That's creepy."

"It's kind of cool. You'd like her. Well." He considered. "You'd hate her and then you'd like her. That's how you do everybody."

She didn't argue.

He kept going. Embry had been coming around a lot, and Seth thought he might come over to Jake, and Sam was being kind of decent about it. Rosalie hated Jake. Rosalie also hated Seth, but less.

"And Charlie knows," he said.

"Knows what?"

"About us. Jake phased in front of him. In his yard. So he'd quit asking where Bella was." Seth sounded torn between awe and guilt. "Charlie kind of fell over. Then he got up and he was okay. He says he only wants to know what he needs to know. Need-to-know basis. He says it like a cop."

"He is a cop."

"Yeah." A pause. A long one, for Seth. "Mom's been over there a lot. At Charlie's. Since. Cooking and stuff. And he comes here. He fixed the porch step." Another pause. "Lee, I think they're — "

There was a voice in the background, and the clatter of a door, and the rustle of grocery bags. Seth said, "It's Leah," in a voice that was suddenly fifteen again, and then there was a scuffle, and then her mother.

"Hi, baby."

"Hi, Mom."

"He talked your ear off."

"Yeah."

"He's been sitting by that phone since ten." Sue Clearwater breathed out, and Leah could hear her setting the bags down, the crinkle of plastic, the refrigerator door. "What did he tell you?"

Leah looked at the snow coming down on the gas pumps.

"Charlie fixed the porch step," she said.

The refrigerator door shut.

"Yes," her mother said. "He did."

Neither of them said anything for a while. Leah could hear Seth breathing somewhere close to the phone, which meant he was standing right there in the kitchen being nosy, which meant Mom was going to have to say it in front of him, which meant she would.

"Charlie's coming for Thanksgiving," Sue said. "Ours. The twenty-third."

The cold went into Leah's ear through the steel of the receiver.

"Mom."

"I know."

"It's *Dad's* — it's been a year and a half — "

"I know how long it's been, Leah."

The way she said it stopped Leah's mouth. She said it quietly, in a voice gone thin with tiredness, the voice from the hospital hallway.

"He was alone," her mother said. "All that time. He thought his little girl was dying and nobody would tell him why. He sat in that kitchen every night with a phone that didn't ring. And I was alone too, honey. You were gone. Seth's fifteen and he lives over there with them more than he lives here." A breath. "So I made him dinner. And he fixed the step. I don't know what it is yet. I'm not asking you to like it."

Leah's eyes were stinging. She hated it.

"Is it — " She couldn't finish. *Is it like Dad. Is he sitting in Dad's chair. Do you sing Creedence in the kitchen with him.*

"He likes my chowder," her mother said, and her voice cracked, very slightly, right down the middle. "That's what it is right now, Lee. He likes my chowder."

Leah stood in the snow with her forehead pressed to the phone box and her eyes shut.

"Come home for Thanksgiving," Sue said, gentler. "If you want. If you're ready. You don't have to. You know you don't have to."

"I know."

"I'd like you to."

"I know, Mom."

"Okay."

They said goodbye. Leah hung up. The phone told her she had six dollars and ten cents remaining on her card. She stood there with her hand still on the receiver while the snow piled up on her toque, and up the street at the Legion a bugle started playing, thin and wavering in the cold, the long slow notes of the Last Post. Every head on Main Street turned toward it. A man shoveling his walk stopped and took his hat off.

Leah stood with her hand on the phone and listened to the whole thing.

Then she walked down the side of the closed café, through the snow, to the dock.

---

The dock was a white shelf over white ice. Snow lay a foot deep on the planks, unbroken, and the lake beyond it was frozen hard as far out as the black lane down the middle, where steam curled up off the open water into the falling snow. Across the bay the lodge was a gray shape with smoke going up. She walked to the end and stood there with snow up to her shins and her hands in her hoodie pocket.

She took the mirror out.

She'd carried it every day for seven weeks. In her jeans at work, in her bundle when she ran, on the nightstand at the motel. He'd rung her on it twice since Thanksgiving, from Lima and from somewhere in Kazakhstan where it had been the middle of the night and he'd been lying in a ditch again, whispering, and she'd answered both times and talked to him for an hour. There'd been one more date since Thanksgiving, if you counted a forty-mile run up onto the plateau in the first snow, and he did. She'd beaten him to the top again. She had never once used the mirror to call him.

She looked at her face in the glass. Pale, wet-eyed, snow on her eyelashes.

*Whichever you like.*

"Harry," she said.

It came out of her mouth and sat in the cold air. Her dad's name. She'd said it to the mirror, to a man who wasn't her dad, and the world didn't end. The snow kept falling.

The glass clouded, and cleared, and he was there. Dim light behind him, a stone fireplace, the edge of the moose head.

He looked at her face, and his own went still.

"Where are you?" he said.

"Dock. Behind the café."

"Can I come?"

"Yeah."

The crack was muffled by the snow. It came from behind her, the planks under her boots jumped, and a ring of loose snow blew outward off the dock and pattered down on the ice. When she turned around he was standing there in the waxed jacket with no hat and snow already settling in his hair.

He said nothing about the name. He came and stood next to her at the end of the dock, close enough that she could feel the heat coming off him through the snow, and looked out at the frozen lake, and waited.

So she told him.

Seth. The Loch Ness Monster, which made him laugh, a short surprised bark he swallowed fast. Seth's shoulder, which didn't. His eyes went flat and green and very still when she got to that part, and he asked only whether Seth was all right, and when she said yes, he heals, he nodded once and said nothing else about it. The baby who looked two at two months and showed you pictures when she touched your face. Charlie Swan falling over in his yard. The porch step.

The chowder.

When she got to the chowder her voice went, and she stopped, and stood there with her jaw locked. He stood beside her in the snow with his hands in his pockets and let her get it back.

"She's moving on," Leah said finally. "And it's Charlie Swan. And I'm up here washing dishes. And Seth's practically living with the bloodsuckers, and Jacob — " She shook her head. "Everybody's lives are just going. Without me. And I'm the one who left. I left a note under the *salt shaker.*"

"You did," Harry said.

"I wanted it. I wanted to be gone. I wanted to be somewhere nobody was in my head, and I got it, and it's — " She stared at the black water. "It's been the best two months of my life. I'm not sorry."

"Good."

"But I'm not going to be the thing they talk about at Thanksgiving. Poor Leah. Remember Leah? She ran off. I'm not doing that." She breathed in, and the cold burned all the way down. "I know I can leave now. I know I can be by myself. I did it. So I can go back, and if it's bad, I can leave again. Whenever I want. That's mine now."

She looked at him.

"I'm going home," she said.

---

He didn't answer right away.

He stood at the end of the dock and looked out at the lake for a long moment, his face very quiet, snow gathering on his shoulders. She watched him think. She'd learned what that looked like on him, over seven weeks. His eyes went somewhere else and his breathing slowed, and everything about him went very still.

Then he reached inside his jacket and took out a mirror.

A different one. Round, in a brass case with a lid that snapped open, like an old pocket watch. He flicked it open with his thumb and looked into it and said, "Lefèvre."

The glass went bright. A man's face came up in it, thin and gray-bearded, with rimless glasses and a high white collar. He was in a dark-paneled room with a lamp on. He looked like a man who'd been interrupted in the middle of dinner and had expected to be.

"Potter. It is nine o'clock on a Saturday night."

"Evening, Gus. I'm taking some leave."

There was a pause.

"*Leave,*" said the man in the mirror.

"Holiday. Vacation. Whatever the form calls it. Solveig will know."

"Starting when?"

"Now."

"For how long?"

"No clue," Harry said.

The man in the mirror took his glasses off. He pinched the bridge of his nose. "You have the Novosibirsk matter. You have the inquest in Lisbon on the twentieth. You have the Belgian review board, who would like nothing better than to — "

"Solveig has Belgium. Give Novosibirsk to Okonkwo, he's been begging for it. Lisbon can have my statement in writing."

"Potter. You cannot simply *take off.* What on earth makes you think you can just — "

"Because I'm fucking Harry Potter, Gus," Harry said, and snapped the lid shut.

The brass case started buzzing in his hand almost at once, a furious little rattle like a trapped bee. He looked at it. He put it back inside his jacket. It kept buzzing against his chest. He tapped the pocket twice with two fingers, and it stopped.

He turned to Leah.

"Aight," he said. "Where we headed?"

She stared at him with her mouth open.

"Did you just say *aight?*"

"The highway lads say it. At the café." He considered. "I thought it might help me fit in."

"Never say that again."

"Right." He brushed the snow off his shoulder. "Where *are* we headed?"

"*We?*"

"We."

"Who said you were coming?"

"Nobody," he said. "I'm asking."

She looked at him standing there in the snow, a man who'd just hung up on his boss with the F-word, asking.

"No," she said.

He nodded slowly, as if he'd expected that too. "Why not?"

"Because it's my home, and it's a mess." It came out faster than she meant. "There's two packs of wolves who can barely stand each other and eight vampires and a baby who's half a vampire and growing like a weed, and an Alpha who's two months old and imprinted on her, and Sam's pack patrols every inch of that land and they'd smell you from a mile off and then there'd be a *fight* — "

"I'd be very good."

" — and because I just got my head back, okay? I just got it back. I'm not going home with something *attached.*"

"Fair," said Harry. "Can I make a case?"

"No."

"I'm going to make a case."

"*Harry — *"

It was out before she knew it. Twice in ten minutes. She shut her mouth. He didn't so much as blink at it, but something moved behind his eyes, and then he went on as if she hadn't said anything.

"Two months," he said. "Everybody back there's had two months to decide what happened to you. Poor Leah. She cracked. She ran off to Canada and she's up there crying into a sink."

"I don't cry into the sink."

"I know you don't. They don't know that. They've had two months of picturing it." He held up a hand, palm out. "Now picture this. You walk back up that beach on your own two feet, head up, taking your time. And you've got a bloke with you."

"A *bloke.*"

"A wizard. Who turns into a wolf a head taller than any of them. Who you're dating."

"We're not dating."

"Four dates, Clearwater. Five, if Thanksgiving counts, and Doreen says it does. You kissed me on a dock. There's a form. You filled it in." His mouth went up at one corner. "In ink."

She opened her mouth. Closed it.

"Sam Uley," Harry said. "Running his patrol line one night, nose down, and he catches something on the wind that's bigger than he is. Something he's never smelled before. He spends a week out there in the dark wondering what the hell's got into his woods." He tilted his head. "And then he finds out it's yours."

Something happened in Leah's chest. Something small and mean and enormously satisfying.

"And the ones in your head," Harry went on. "Jacob. Seth. Whoever else. Every time you phase, they get whatever you're thinking about. Front row." He paused. "You could think about me."

"Oh my God."

"In detail."

"Oh my *God.*"

"Think about the race." He leaned in a little, and his voice dropped, conspiratorial. "Jacob Black, Alpha, two whole months in the job. And he gets to stand there in his own head and watch you beat something twice his size up a mountain. In first person. From your eyes. Over and over, every time you feel like remembering it."

Leah made a sound. She didn't know what sound. It was half a laugh and half something else, and it came out of her nose, and she clapped her mitten over her face.

"Paul," she said, through the mitten. "Paul Lahote would lose his *mind.*"

"Is Paul in your brother's lot?"

"Sam's."

"Then Paul gets the live show." He said it with total serenity. "In person. Up close. I'll stand very near him. I'll be extremely polite."

She was laughing now. She couldn't help it. She stood at the end of the dock in a foot of snow with a poppy on her flannel and her face wet and laughed into her mitten, picturing Paul's face, Paul's stupid face, Paul getting a good long look at a wolf the size of a pickup truck standing beside Leah Clearwater on First Beach with its head tilted.

When she got it under control, Harry had stopped grinning. He was looking at her.

"And they all got picked," he said, quietly. "The ones who imprinted. Sam. Jacob. Whoever else. Something reached down and picked for them, and they've all had to make their peace with it." He was quiet a second. "You'd be walking in with somebody you picked."

Leah stopped laughing.

The snow came down between them. Out on the black lane of open water, a raven landed on the edge of the ice, looked at them, and said something sarcastic.

"Also," Harry added, "I've never seen anything sparkle."

"*No.*"

"I'm only saying — "

"You stay away from the Cullens." She pointed at him with her mitten. "That's the first thing. You don't go near that house. You don't go near Forks. You don't go *looking.*"

"For now," he said.

"I mean it."

"So do I." He sounded completely serious. "If I clap eyes on eight vampires in a glass house in my official capacity, I have to write it up. A whole new kind of creature nobody in the Confederation's ever heard of. Solveig would make me do the forms." He shuddered. "Nobody wants that. Least of all your vampires."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Second thing," she said. "You don't buy anything."

"Anything?"

"Not a house. Not a boat. Not a *beach.* Nothing on the rez, nothing near it, nothing for my mom, nothing for Seth. Nothing."

"Can I buy groceries?"

"You can buy groceries."

"Excellent. Go on."

"You don't come to my mom's house till I say so. And you stay out of my head."

"Always," he said. That one came without a pause.

She breathed out. The snow had stopped, she noticed. When had the snow stopped? The clouds were pulling apart over the lake, ragged and gray, and behind them the sky was going the pale clear green it went up here in the short afternoons, with the sun already sliding down toward the mountains though it wasn't even one o'clock.

"I'll need somewhere to sleep," Harry said. "Not your mum's couch. I'm not mad."

"Not my mom's couch."

"Somewhere with a view, if there is one."

She thought about it. And as she thought about it, something came up out of her memory, clear and sharp as a photograph. A long curve of gray sand between two headlands. Sea stacks standing out in the surf like broken teeth. A waterfall coming down off the cliff at the far end, straight into the ocean. Her father's hand around hers on the trail through the trees.

"I know a place," she said.

"Good." He turned up his collar. "When?"

"I owe Dot a couple days. I'm not walking out on her with no notice. Not like — " She stopped. *Not like the salt shaker.* "Tuesday. Tuesday night."

"Tuesday night," Harry agreed.

She turned and looked down the dock at the back of the café, at the window over the sink where she'd stood every day for seven weeks, at the snow on the roof and the smoke going up from the chimney of the house next door. Up the street, the bugle had stopped. People would be coming out of the Legion now, stamping their feet, heading for the Prospector.

"I have to tell Dot," she said.

"Do you want me to come?"

"No," Leah said. "God, no. She'd make you scrub the grease trap."

"I'd do it."

"I know you would," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it, and walked up the dock to go and find Dot before she could change her mind.

---

She found Dot at the Legion, by the coffee urn, still in her good black coat with her poppy on it. Leah told her standing up, in about four sentences.

Dot filled a styrofoam cup and handed it to her.

"Figured," she said. "Tuesday?"

"Tuesday night. I'll work Monday and Tuesday."

"You'll work Tuesday till close." Dot looked at her over her glasses. "You made it past Halloween, Washington."

"I told you I'm not most people."

"You did." Dot filled a second cup for herself. "Drink your coffee. It's terrible."

It was.

---

Tuesday's last shift went the way every shift went. Highway crew, old men, a hunter with a broken truck, the lunch rush, the dead hour. Leah washed. Dot cooked. Brynn talked, more than usual, very fast, about nothing at all, and twice went into the walk-in and came back out with red eyes and said it was the onions.

At close Brynn flipped the sign and turned around and burst into tears.

"I *knew* it," she said, into Leah's flannel, holding on with both arms. "I knew you'd go. You never tell anybody anything and then you just *go.*"

"I told you."

"You told me *Sunday.*"

"That's two days' notice."

"That's *nothing.*" Brynn pulled back and wiped her face with her sleeve and shoved a napkin at her. An address in Prince George, in purple gel pen, with a phone number and three hearts. "You write me. Okay? An actual letter. You don't have a phone or an email or anything, you're like a pioneer, so you *write me.*"

"Okay."

"And when I go to Sydney, you're coming."

Leah looked at her. Round red face, pencil through her bun, UNBC hoodie. Alive. Going to nursing school in January on her own money.

"Go see your Opera House," Leah said. "Send me a picture."

Brynn burst into tears again.

Dot gave her an envelope and an index card.

The envelope had her last two days' pay in it and a good deal more than that, in Canadian twenties, and Leah opened her mouth and Dot said, "Christmas bonus. Early. Say one word and I'll spit in your eggs." The index card was soft and greasy at the corners, written on both sides in blue ballpoint in a hand that sloped hard to the right. At the top it said SASKATOON — MOM'S.

Leah held it in both hands.

"Nobody gets that," Dot said. "Brynn hasn't got that. Ray's been asking me for that since 1991. You give it to anybody, I'll know." She turned back to her grill, which was off, and scraped at it with her spatula anyway. "There's one in a box by the door. For the road. Don't let him eat it all."

"I won't."

"You will," Dot said, to the grill. "Go on. Get out of my kitchen."

Leah stood there a moment longer looking at Dot's back. Then she went over and put her arms around her from behind, quick and hard, the way she used to hug her dad when he was standing at the stove, and felt Dot go stiff as a board and then, for about one second, not.

"Go on," Dot said, rough. "Go."

She went.

---

Wes was watching the fishing show when she dropped the key on the counter. He looked at the plastic fob with the 6 on it and then up at her over his reading glasses.

"Hang on," he said.

He went down the row to room six with his master key and came back two minutes later carrying the moose. Frame and all. Front legs bending the wrong way at the knee, lake at sunset, the whole thing.

He held it out.

"Nobody else ever looks at it," Wes said.

Leah took it. She didn't know what to say. She said, "Thanks, Wes."

"Mm," said Wes, and went back to his show.

---

At eleven that night she stood at the end of the dock behind the dark café in the snow, with everything she owned piled around her boots.

It wasn't much. A duffel from the Mercantile with her jeans and flannels and wool socks in it. The blue toque with the pompom, on her head. The coffee-can money and Dot's envelope and the photo of her and her dad on the boat, all in the same ziplock, in the inside pocket of her hoodie with the mirror. The last half box of Belgian chocolate. Dot's pie in its cardboard box, tied with string. And a painting of a moose.

Harry looked at the moose for a long time.

"It was a gift," Leah said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking," he said, "that it's one of the worst paintings I've ever seen, and I grew up in a house where every picture on the mantelpiece was of my cousin." He held out a hand. "I'll carry it. With honor."

She gave him the moose. She kept the pie.

The lake was frozen solid now out to the middle. The stars were out, hard and close, and low in the north a faint green was beginning to stir. She looked at the town one more time. The Prospector's half-dead neon. The motel's buzzing light. The window over the sink.

"So how does this work," she said. "You've never been there."

"I need to see where we're going," Harry said. "I can't take us somewhere I've never been, not cleanly. I'd need a picture of it. A proper one, in my head." He was quiet a moment. "Which means you'd have to show me."

"Show you how?"

He didn't say anything. He waited.

She understood.

The cold went down her back. A year and a half of Sam in her head. Paul and Jared and Quil and Embry, rummaging. Every bad night. Every hope. Emily's name in all of it.

*I stay on the step outside, by the door.*

She looked up at him in the starlight. He was holding a terrible painting of a moose against his chest with both arms, and he was waiting.

"One thing," she said. "One memory. I pick it. I hold it up and you look at that and nothing else."

"That's all I'd need."

"If you go anywhere else — "

"I won't."

"I'll *know.*"

"You will," he agreed. "I'd want you to."

She closed her eyes.

She found it the way she'd found it on Saturday, without trying. Ten years old. September. Her father's big rough hand around hers on the trail through the trees, the second-growth spruce dripping, the sound of the surf getting louder ahead of them. And then the trees opened, and there it was. A long gray curve of sand between two dark headlands. Driftwood piled white under the bluffs. Sea stacks standing out in the surf like broken teeth, black against the bright gray sky. And at the far end, past the last of the stacks, a waterfall pouring straight down a hundred feet of cliff into the sea, white as milk.

*Third Beach,* her dad said. *Your mom's favorite. Don't tell her I brought you without her.*

She held it there, behind her eyes. The smell of it. The sound.

"Okay," she said. "Go on."

She felt him.

It was the lightest thing. Like someone reading over her shoulder, so quietly she could barely tell they were there. Something looked at the beach. Looked at the headlands and the stacks and the waterfall, carefully, the way he'd looked at the drop-off off Sheep Point before he cast. It didn't move. It didn't touch the edges. It didn't so much as glance at her father's hand.

Then it was gone.

She opened her eyes. Her heart was going like she'd run ten miles.

"Got it," Harry said quietly. "Thank you."

She checked. She couldn't help it. She went all the way around the inside of her own head, the way she'd done in the pumpkin field, and everything was where she'd left it. Nobody in the next room.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it."

She looked at him a while.

"Okay," she said. Her voice came out rough. She cleared her throat. "How bad is this going to be?"

"Bad," he said cheerfully. "Hold onto my arm. Don't let go. You'll want to be sick after. Try not to be sick on the moose."

She picked up her duffel and the pie. He got the moose under one arm and the chocolate under the other and stuck out his elbow, and she took hold of it, and looked one last time at the window over the sink.

"See ya later, alligator," she said to Turnagain, under her breath.

And the world turned inside out.

---

She landed on her knees in wet sand.

Something heaved inside her and she let go of his arm and crawled two yards and was sick into the dark, violently, everything Dot had fed her at lunch. When it was over she knelt there on her hands with her hair hanging down and her stomach turning slow cartwheels, and she breathed in.

Rain.

It was raining. Soft and steady and cold, the way it rained at home ten months of the year. Salt. Kelp rotting on the wrack line. Wet cedar and spruce from the bluffs behind her, and woodsmoke from somewhere far off up the coast, and the big slow roar of the Pacific coming in over the sand in the dark, a sound she'd fallen asleep to every night of her life until September.

She stayed there on her hands and knees and let the rain run down the back of her neck.

"All right?" Harry said, from somewhere behind her.

"Did I get the moose?"

"You missed the moose."

"Good." She sat back on her heels and turned her face up into the rain and let it wash her off, and if some of what ran down her face was hot, nobody was going to know about it but her.

---

When she could stand she looked around.

Third Beach, at night, in November. The long curve of sand going off into the dark in both directions. The driftwood piled up white under the bluffs. The sea stacks out in the surf, blacker than the sky. At the south end, faint through the rain, the long white thread of the waterfall coming down off Taylor Point into the sea.

Harry was standing a little way up the beach at the edge of the driftwood with his nose lifted. He'd set the moose down carefully against a log.

"There's a wolf been through here," he said. "On the trail up there. Two days old, maybe three. Big lad. Bad temper. Smells like he bathes in that body spray they sell in the chemist's."

"Paul."

"Ah." He sounded pleased. "The live show."

"What else?"

He was quiet, his head turning slowly, the way she'd seen him read the wind on the plateau. "People, a long way off. Woodsmoke. Fish. A diesel engine. Nothing cold." He looked at her. "Nothing that smells like bleach and burnt sugar. Not for miles."

She let her breath out.

"Is this your lot's land?" he asked.

"National park. The rez starts the other side of that headland." She pointed north, at the dark bulk of Teahwhit Head. "My mom's house is about three miles that way."

"Do I need a permit?"

"Yes."

He considered this. "I'll get a permit."

"You can't *buy* a national park, Potter."

"I wasn't going to say — "

"You were going to say Hargreaves."

He didn't deny it. He picked the moose back up and set off down the beach toward the waterfall, and she followed him with the pie.

---

The cave was at the foot of Taylor Point, above the high-tide line, behind a tumble of boulders as big as cars. She'd have walked right past it. She'd walked past it with her dad, probably, at ten years old. A dark slot in the rock, maybe eight feet high and four wide, going back into the headland about as far as a bedroom. It smelled of wet stone and old salt and, very faintly, sea lion, years gone.

Harry stood in the mouth of it and looked around.

"Well," he said. "It's a start."

"It's a hole."

"For now." He set down the moose. "Mind if I get on? You'll want to stand back a bit."

She stood back a bit.

He didn't move his hands. He didn't say anything. He just stood in the mouth of the cave with the rain coming down on him, and started.

The first thing she felt was the air change. A ripple went out from him across the whole cove, out over the sand and the driftwood and the boulders and up the face of the bluff and out over the surf, shimmering for a second like heat over a road, gold, the same as the dome over the mine. Then it was gone. The rain kept falling through where it had been.

"Anyone who comes down that trail," he said, "and isn't you or me, is going to remember they left the oven on. Anybody with a nose — your Paul included — is going to smell rock and sea and nothing else." He turned his head. "And anything that comes within five miles smelling like burnt sugar, we'll know about."

He reached into his jacket and took out a small dark mirror, oval, smoky, in a tarnished silver frame. He hung it on a nub of rock just inside the mouth of the cave, where it swung a little and settled. Leah looked into it. Her own face wasn't there. Nothing was. Just gray fog, swirling slowly.

"Foe-Glass," he said. "Shows you your enemies. When they're far off, they're shadows. When they're close enough to worry about, you can see the whites of their eyes." He looked at the fog. "Nobody, at the moment. That's what we like."

"That's creepy."

"Very." He went inside.

The cave got bigger.

She watched it happen and her brain refused to hold it. The walls went back, slowly and smoothly, the way the far end of a hallway goes back when you walk down it, and the rough wet rock smoothed itself as it went into something pale and dry and gray, like river stone. The floor flattened under her boots into big slabs fitted close together. The ceiling went up, and up, and kept going until it was twenty feet over her head, and then the rock thinned and cleared into sky. Low gray rain clouds sliding over, lit faintly from underneath, a few drops falling out of them and vanishing before they reached her.

"Like the hall at school," he said, looking up at it with his hands in his pockets. "It'll show you whatever's outside. Stars, when the weather clears."

There was a hearth now, in the back wall, cut into the stone. Fire was going in it without any wood, blue at the bottom and gold on top, and it gave off heat and no smoke at all. The rooms went on past it. She walked through them the way she'd walked around him on the dock, slowly, touching things.

A kitchen. A long counter of the same gray stone, a sink with a tap, a stove. A pantry door that opened onto shelves already loaded — eggs, bacon, bread, potatoes, a ham, sacks of flour, a wheel of cheese, three dozen tins of things, two bottles of Scotch from somewhere a lot better than the Prospector — and the air inside the pantry was cold as the walk-in at the Galena.

"Groceries," Harry said, behind her. "You said I could buy groceries."

A bathroom. She stood in the doorway of that one for a long time. The tub was carved out of the floor itself, round and deep, big enough for four people, and along its edge ran a row of brass taps, a dozen of them, each one a different shape.

"That one does bubbles," Harry said. "That one does bubbles that smell like cedar. That one does — I'm not entirely sure. Something pink. I'd stay away from it."

She thought about Wes's plastic shower stall. *Hot water takes a minute.*

"Okay," she said faintly.

Two bedrooms. Two. Side by side, off a short passage, each with a heavy door of gray driftwood worn silver.

"That one's yours," he said, nodding at the left-hand door. "It opens for you. Nobody else. Not me. Lock it or don't."

She looked at him.

"You said nothing about sharing," Harry said. "I assumed that was a separate form."

She went in. A bed, big, with heavy wool blankets in red and black. A window in the stone wall that couldn't possibly have been a window, because they were a hundred feet inside a headland, and it showed her the sea anyway — the black surf, the stacks, the rain. A hook on the back of the door with nothing hanging on it. That was all. It was perfect, and she wanted to hit him for it.

When she came back out to the big room he'd hung the moose.

He'd put it over the hearth, centered, slightly crooked, and it looked down at the blue-gold fire and the stone floor and the impossible sky with its legs bending the wrong way at the knee, like a man in a moose costume who'd finally remembered where he parked.

Leah sat down on the floor in front of the fire and laughed until she had to lie down.

---

There was one more thing.

He'd set it on a low shelf by the hearth while she was in the bedroom. A record player, the old-fashioned kind, in a wooden case with a lid. And beside it, leaning against the stone, a stack of records in their cardboard sleeves.

She knew the covers. She knew them before she was close enough to read them. She'd grown up with them on the shelf in the living room under the window, next to the TV, with the corners worn soft from her father's thumbs. *Green River. Willy and the Poor Boys. Cosmo's Factory.*

She stood there and didn't touch them.

"I know," Harry said quietly. "I know what you said about buying things. I can take them back. They're from a shop in Vancouver, I went this afternoon, and the man was very excited and wanted to talk about guitars." He paused. "Can I give them to you?"

She looked at the records for a long time.

*You want to help somebody, you ask me first.*

He'd asked.

"Yeah," she said. It came out very small. "Yeah. You can."

He nodded. He didn't make a thing of it. He took the top one out of its sleeve, careful, by the edges, turned it over to the second side, and set it on the turntable and lowered the needle, and stood back.

The hiss. The crackle. Then the guitar.

That bouncing, cheerful, stupid little guitar line she'd heard a thousand times on the boat, in the truck, in the shower through the bathroom door. And then John Fogerty's voice, singing about the moon and the trouble coming, happy as anything about it, like a man who'd seen the end of the world coming and decided to whistle.

Leah stood in the middle of the cave with her arms wrapped around herself.

She didn't cry. She'd decided a long time ago she was done crying in front of people. She stood there and listened to the whole song with her eyes closed and her father in the room, as close as he'd been since the living-room floor, a bad back and a big laugh and a voice like a goose being stepped on.

When it ended she opened her eyes. Harry was sitting on the floor by the fire, not looking at her, giving her the room.

"He'd have hated you," she said.

"Would he?"

"He'd have hated your hair. And your boots. And he'd have made you go out on the boat with him at four in the morning, and you'd have been sick over the side, and he'd have laughed so hard he'd have fallen down." She wiped her face with her sleeve, which was wet for some reason. "And then he'd have liked you. Probably."

Harry looked at the fire.

"That's how you do everybody," he said. "Your brother told you."

She'd told him Seth said that. On the dock, in the snow. He'd kept it.

"Shut up," Leah said, and went and sat down next to him on the heated stone in front of the fire, close enough that their shoulders touched.

---

Later, near two in the morning, she went out to the mouth of the cave.

The rain had stopped. The clouds were breaking up and going off east over the hills, and the stars were coming out over the sea in ones and twos. Behind the headland, over the black line of the trees, the moon was coming up late, lopsided and yellow, a little less than half of it. The surf ran in white over the sand and drew back.

Three miles north, around the headland, her mother was asleep in the little house on the edge of the rez with a porch step that Charlie Swan had fixed. Seth was asleep, or he was out running the line in his fur with Jacob in his head. Sam was out there somewhere. Paul. Emily. All of them. They had no idea she was here.

Nobody did. She was standing at the edge of her own place, with her own door, and her head was still hers.

Behind her, the air moved, and the heat of him came up at her back. She didn't hear him. She never heard him. When she glanced down, an enormous black forepaw had settled on the stone beside her boot, and then he lay down across the mouth of the cave the way a dog lies down across a doorway, his chin on his paws, his green eyes on the sea.

"First watch," the wolf said, very low, the words clean under the rumble. "I've nothing on my paws till spring. Go to bed, Clearwater."

She looked down at him.

Far off to the north, from the hills above La Push, a wolf howled. One long note, rising, held. Then it broke off at the top, the way it always broke off, because whoever was doing it had never once in his life managed to hold the end.

Seth.

She stood very still and listened to her little brother howl at the moon three miles away, without the first idea she could hear him.

"Tomorrow," she said, to nobody, to the sea.

Then she went inside to her own room, and opened her own door, and shut it behind her.

She didn't lock it.
