# Chapter Three: Bad Moon Rising

The sun came up over the east end of the lake while she was still in the trees above the gravel pit, and it caught her out.

She'd meant to be back by five. She'd meant to be phased, dressed, and standing at the three-bay sink with her apron on and her hair tied back before Dot unlocked the kitchen door. Instead the light came sliding down the mountains on the far shore, gold on the snow, then gold on the spruce, then gold on the frost along the lake road, and Leah came out of the timber behind the deadfall at a dead run with caribou still in her fur and knew exactly what time it was without having to look at a clock.

Late.

She dropped behind the deadfall and let the fire go.

It always hurt a little, coming back. The bones folding down. The heat staying where it was. She crouched naked in the frost with her breath smoking and her hands shaking and worked the cord off her ankle, three hard knots, and her fingers found something that hadn't been there yesterday morning.

The pouch.

It was threaded through the cord exactly where he'd put it, the drawstring tied off in a neat, tight knot she didn't recognize. A sailor's knot, maybe. Something her dad would have known the name of. It sat in her palm, small and soft and dark, the leather worn smooth at the seams the way old things got when a lot of hands had held them.

She put her clothes on first. Jeans, belt, wool socks, the flannel, the boots. Then she sat down on the deadfall with her hair hanging wet in her face and pulled the drawstring open.

A mirror. About the size of a playing card. The glass was old, with a faint greenish cast to it, like looking through the bottom of a bottle, and it was set in a thin frame of dark silver that had gone black in the corners and the grooves. There was no engraving and no maker's mark. Just a small square of glass that showed her a face she didn't entirely recognize.

Thinner than she remembered. Browner. Her cheekbones were sharper than they'd been in La Push, and there was a smear of something dark along her jaw that she hoped was mud. Her eyes looked tired and very awake at the same time.

She looked at the glass for a long while, waiting for it to do something.

It didn't. It was a mirror.

*If you want me, say my name into it.*

She thought about saying it. The word sat right there behind her teeth. She thought about what it would mean that she'd said it four hours after he'd stepped off a rock into the dark, and she put the mirror back in the pouch and pulled the drawstring tight and shoved the whole thing into the front pocket of her jeans, and went down the lake road to work.

---

Dot was at the flat-top with her back to the door when Leah came in through the kitchen.

She didn't turn around. She cracked two eggs one-handed onto the grill, then two more, then picked up the coffee mug at her elbow and drank from it and set it down again.

"Ninety-four minutes," Dot said.

"I know."

"Comes out of your pay."

"Okay."

Dot flipped the eggs. The kitchen smelled like bacon and burnt toast and the bleach Dot used on the cutting boards, and over all of it, coming off Leah's own skin, the cold mineral smell of the river and the plateau and something with a lot of blood in it. Dot sniffed once. Her eyes stayed on the eggs.

"You get into the dump?"

"No."

"You smell like a slaughterhouse in a snowbank."

"I went for a walk."

"Uh huh." Dot plated the eggs and slid them into the window and hit the bell. Then she finally turned around and looked at Leah, up and down, the same once-over she'd given her the first morning. Wet hair. Flannel buttoned wrong by one hole. A long scratch on the back of one hand from a spruce branch that would be gone by lunch. "Your fancy friend's cook is out front."

Leah stopped with the apron halfway over her head.

"Been sitting at the counter since half past six," Dot said, "with a box on his lap like it's going to go off. Won't order. Won't take his coat off. Asked for you by name." She turned back to the grill. "Wash your hands first. Wash 'em twice."

---

He was the skinny one. She'd heard Dot describe him in the bar: *orders eight breakfasts to go and looks like he wants to cry.* He was maybe thirty-five, slight and narrow-shouldered, with a buzz cut going gray at the temples and very tidy hands, and he was wearing a white chef's jacket under an unzipped down parka that was far too big for him. He was sitting on the third stool from the end, upright, with both his feet on the rail and both his arms around a white cardboard box the size of a shoebox, tied with a gold ribbon. When Leah came through the swinging door he stood up so fast he nearly knocked the stool over.

"You're Leah," he said.

"Yeah."

"Oh, thank God." He held the box out at arm's length with both hands, the way you'd hand somebody a baby that had just been sick on you. "Here. Take it. Please. Take it."

She took it. It was heavier than it looked.

"Danny Vo," he said, and seemed to sag about four inches when the weight left his arms. "I'm the — I *was* the — I cook. Cooked. At the lodge." He pointed vaguely across the lake. "He said guard it with my life. Those were his exact words. *Guard it with your life, Danny.* And then he went out the door, and there was this — bang — and he was gone, and I've been sitting in that kitchen for three days looking at that box." He laughed, a high strained sound. "There's a bear that comes up on the deck at night. Did you know that? Big black one. I think it could smell them through the box. I slept on the kitchen floor with a cast-iron pan."

Leah looked at the box. Gold letters pressed into the lid in a curling old-fashioned script: PEETERS & FILLE — CHOCOLATIERS — BRUXELLES — DEPUIS 1951.

"You didn't eat any?"

Danny looked genuinely shocked. "He'd *know.*"

"Plane's waiting." Danny zipped up his parka. Out the front window, at the end of the café's own little dock, the yellow floatplane was idling with its prop ticking over, a man in a ball cap standing on the float with a coffee. "I'm going home. He let me go. I was supposed to stay till yesterday, and then he rang the lodge Saturday night and told me I could go whenever I liked and he'd sort it out with Hargreaves, and I said I've got a contract through Sunday, and he said, *Danny, mate, I'm releasing you from your contract,* and I said what about the money, and he said — " Danny stopped. He swallowed. "Hargreaves rang yesterday and said I'm being paid through Christmas. Through *Christmas.* I've got two kids and a basement suite in Burnaby. My wife cried on the phone."

He looked at Leah, as if she might explain it.

"He does that," Leah said.

"He ate eleven eggs at four in the morning on Friday," Danny said, a little wildly. "Standing up. Out of the pan. And then he made a béarnaise to go with a steak at five in the morning, by hand, in about four minutes, and it was better than mine. It was better than *mine.*" He shook his head. "I went to culinary school for two years."

"Danny," called the man on the float, faint through the glass. "Wind's coming up."

"Right. Okay." Danny took a step toward the door, then turned back. "There's a card. In the ribbon. He wrote it before he left. I didn't read it." He hesitated. "I mean, I read the first line. It fell out once. I put it back."

The bell jingled. He was gone across the frosted planks of the dock in his too-big parka, and a minute later the floatplane's engine climbed to a roar, and the yellow plane went skimming off across the lake throwing up two long white wings of spray, and lifted, and banked south over the mountains, and was gone.

Every person in the café was looking at the box.

Leah took it into the kitchen.

---

She didn't open it until the lunch rush was over and Dot had gone out back to yell at the propane man.

The card was tucked under the gold ribbon. Heavy cream stock, folded once. The handwriting inside was terrible. It slanted uphill and then downhill, the letters jammed together like they'd been written on somebody's knee, which they probably had.

> *Clearwater —*
>
> *Third generation. The hazelnut ones are the round ones with the little gold fleck on top. Eat those first. Eat them before anyone sees the box, or you'll have to share, and I'll know.*
>
> *Danny is a good man and has suffered enough. Be kind to him.*
>
> *I asked Hargreaves about the shop. He made the kettle noise again.*
>
> *— H.*
>
> *P.S. I owe you a race. You owe me a best of three. I've been thinking about it in the bath.*

Leah read it twice standing at the sink. Then a third time.

*I've been thinking about it in the bath.*

She folded the card in half and shoved it in her back pocket, and pulled the ribbon, and lifted the lid.

The smell came up out of the box and hit her so hard she had to put her hand on the counter.

It smelled dark and bitter and roasted and deep, like coffee, like earth, with vanilla and cream and toasted nuts and something orange and something like brandy all braided through it, so many smells that her nose kept sorting and sorting and finding more. There were three layers, each in its own tray of fluted gold paper. Little squares and domes and shells and tiny bars, some dark as tar, some pale as milk, some with crushed nuts on top or a stripe of white or a gloss on them like a new car.

The round ones with the gold fleck were in the top row. There were six.

She ate one.

She stood very still in the middle of the Galena's kitchen with her eyes closed.

When she opened them Brynn was standing in the swinging door, in her ski jacket, with her mouth open.

"Oh my *God,*" Brynn said. "Is that the box? Mike Lacasse said there was a box. He said the English guy's cook came in at six-thirty and wouldn't let go of a box, and he gave it to *you,* and then he ran away on the plane like he was escaping from prison — "

"He kind of was."

"*Leah.*" Brynn came across the kitchen like a heat-seeking missile and stopped at Leah's elbow and looked down into the box, and her whole face changed. "Oh. Oh, those are *real.* Those are like — those are the ones in the magazines. Those are Belgian." She looked at Leah. "Did he *send you chocolates?* Did the English guy send you *chocolates?* From *Belgium?*"

"He went to Belgium."

"*Why did he go to Belgium?*"

Leah thought about a nineteen-year-old in a ditch in the Ardennes who'd cried for an hour. "Work."

Brynn made a sound like a kettle herself.

Leah looked at the five round ones left in the top row. She thought about the card in her back pocket. *Eat them before anyone sees the box, or you'll have to share.*

She took out a square one with a stripe of white across it and held it out to Brynn.

Brynn ate it. Her eyes went wide, and then wider, and then they filled up with tears, and she put both hands over her mouth and stood there making small noises through her fingers.

"I know," Leah said.

"*Leah.*"

"I know."

"That's — " Brynn swallowed. She took her hands away. "That's the best thing I've ever had in my whole life. That's better than Dot's pie. Don't tell Dot I said that. Don't you *dare* tell Dot I said that." She stared at the box, then at Leah, and something sharp and delighted came into her face. "He likes you."

"He's a weirdo."

"He *likes* you. He went to *Belgium* and came back with a *box.*" Brynn grabbed her arm. "Is that why you were late? Ray's mom told my mom that Dot said you were an hour and a half late. Were you with him? Did you go out with him? Did he — "

"I went for a walk."

"You always say that."

"I always go for walks."

"Uh huh," said Brynn, in a dead-on imitation of Dot, and then she laughed at herself so hard she had to lean on the steel counter. When she'd finished she wiped her eyes and looked at Leah again, softer. "It's nice, you know. You look — I don't know. You look less like you're about to bite somebody."

Leah put the lid back on the box.

"I could still bite somebody," she said.

"Sure you could," said Brynn, and went out to the floor humming.

Leah put the box on the top shelf of the dry storage room, behind the industrial cans of tomatoes, where Dot never looked, and went back to the sink. She could smell it all afternoon through the door.

---

She held out ten days.

She worked every shift, open to close, and ran every night after close, up the valley and back. The first Tuesday she bought a second pair of jeans and a pair of real winter boots, felt-lined, from the clearance rack at the Mercantile with the last of the money from her coffee can. The second Wednesday the lake froze for the first time all the way out to the end of the café dock, a skin of black ice so thin and clear she could see the pilings going down into the dark, and by noon it had broken up again into floating plates that knocked against each other in the wind with a sound like wind chimes.

She ate one chocolate a night, sitting on the orange bedspread in room six, looking at the moose. She rationed them like they were the last food on earth.

She didn't take the mirror out of her pocket. She knew it was there every second of every day.

That night, running the ridge above the lake road at one in the morning, she caught herself lifting her nose into the wind every time she crested a rise. She stopped doing it. She caught herself doing it again twenty minutes later.

The next night after close she sat on the end of the dock in her hoodie and her new boots with her legs hanging over the black water, and the lake making small cold sounds under her as the ice plates knocked against the pilings, and she took the pouch out of her pocket.

She held the mirror in her palm and looked at her own face in it.

"This is stupid," she told her face.

Her face agreed.

She thought about the card in her back pocket, which she'd moved from her jeans to her other jeans and back again three times, and which was getting soft at the fold.

"Potter," she said.

Nothing happened.

She felt her face go hot. She looked around the dock as if somebody might have heard her. The café was dark. Main Street was empty. A long way off across the lake, the lodge with the green roof sat black against the black trees with no lights on.

"Harry Potter," she said, louder, and felt like a complete idiot.

The glass went dark.

Her own face went out of it like a light switching off, and for a second there was nothing in the mirror but a deep greenish black, like looking into a pond at night. Then light swam up through it, yellow and fuzzy, and resolved, and she was looking into a room.

A big room. Stone walls. A tall arched window with rain streaming down it and a city behind it, streetlights and dark water and the black shoulder of a mountain. Shelves. More paper than she had ever seen in one place in her life, stacked on every surface, in leaning towers, in boxes, in drifts on the floor. And in the middle of the frame, very close, a pair of green eyes.

"Clearwater," said Harry Potter.

He looked like he hadn't slept in a week. His hair was standing up in about five directions, and there was a smear of what she hoped was ink across one cheekbone, and he was wearing a rumpled white shirt with the sleeves shoved up past the elbows and no collar. His eyes had gone very bright.

"You said my name," he said.

"It's a mirror. That's how it works. You told me."

"I know how it works. I made the — I had it made." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I didn't think you'd use it. I'd a bet with myself. I'd said a fortnight."

"You lost."

"I lost," Harry agreed, and the corner of his mouth went up. The long canine showed for half a second, then hid again. "Best thing I've lost all week. Hang on — "

Something moved behind him. A tall, rawboned shape in a gray cardigan and a hard blond bun, carrying a stack of folders so tall it came up past her chin.

"Is that the mirror?" said Solveig Brekke, flat and Norwegian and very far away. "Is that *the* mirror? Lord Potter, you have nine hundred and forty forms to sign before the Belgian delegation arrives at eight o'clock, and it is ten past five in the morning, and you are *not* — "

"Two minutes, Solveig."

"You said that about the Kroll report. You said Thursday. You did not say which Thursday."

"Solveig." He turned his head a little, still holding the mirror up. "It's her."

There was a pause.

Solveig Brekke's pale face appeared over Harry's shoulder in the glass, looking down at Leah with the same expression of deep, weary suspicion she'd worn in the doorway of the Galena.

"Hello," Solveig said.

"Hi," said Leah.

"He has been impossible," Solveig said. "For ten days. Impossible. He keeps taking the mirror out of his pocket and looking at it and putting it back. He took it out during a hearing with the Bulgarian Minister." She straightened up out of the frame. Her voice came from farther off. "Two minutes. I'm timing it. I am an extremely accurate timer."

A door shut. Harry let out a long breath.

"She's my boss," he said. "On paper. In practice I think she's my parole officer." He rubbed his face with his free hand, which smeared the ink further. "Hello."

"Hi."

"How are you? How's your shoulder? How's Dot? How's the — did Danny get out all right? Did he give you the box?"

"Danny got out. He slept on the kitchen floor with a frying pan because of a bear."

Harry winced. "Oh, God. Poor sod."

"He said you made béarnaise at five in the morning and it was better than his."

"It was." He didn't look sorry about it. "Did you eat the round ones first?"

"I gave one to Brynn," Leah said. "A square one. She cried."

"That's the right response." He was smiling now, properly, at the glass. The window behind him was streaked with rain. "Where are you? It's dark."

"On the dock." She turned the mirror so it caught the lake, the black water, the ice plates, the faint shape of the mountains against the stars. Then she turned it back. "Where are you?"

"Geneva. Office. I'd show you but it's mostly paper." He tilted his head. Even in a hand mirror, she could see him doing it. Thirty degrees. "Why'd you call?"

She'd known he'd ask. She'd thought about it for three days. She still didn't have a good answer.

"You said third date," she said.

He went very still in the glass.

"You said third date," Leah said again, "about everything. How you're a wolf. The clothes thing. All of it. You kept saying it. Third date, third date." She made herself look straight at him. "So. Third date. You said you'd answer."

"I did say that."

"So answer."

Harry looked at her for a long moment out of the old green glass. Then he set down a quill she hadn't noticed he was holding, and sat back, and the tiredness went out of his shoulders, and he looked much more like the man on the dock.

"Saturday," he said. "Dot closes at three on a Saturday. I'll come and get you at half past."

"Come and get me how?"

"You'll see."

"I'm not going to Paris."

"I know." He sounded almost offended. "I'm not taking you to Paris. I'm taking you somewhere with chairs. I promised you somewhere with chairs once."

"Where?"

"Mine," Harry said. "Possibly. I still haven't asked Hargreaves."

Behind him, the door opened. "That," said Solveig's voice, "was two minutes."

"It was ninety seconds."

"It was a hundred and twenty-one seconds. I'm an extremely accurate timer."

Harry rolled his eyes at the glass, at Leah, a whole conversation in it. "Saturday," he said. "Half past three. See ya later, alligator."

"In a while," Leah said. "Crocodile."

The glass went dark, and then it was her own face again. Her own face, on the end of a dock in the cold, with its mouth doing something she hadn't given it permission to do.

She put the mirror back in the pouch, and the pouch back in her pocket, and sat there a long time with her hands in her hoodie and her boots hanging over the black water.

Out on the lake, the ice plates knocked together like wind chimes.

---

"Thanksgiving's Monday," Dot said on Saturday, at five to three, counting the till. "Doors at four. You're coming."

Leah was mopping under the counter stools. "Okay."

"Wasn't asking." Dot snapped a rubber band around a stack of fives. "Bring your fancy friend. He can carve. I've seen his hands, he's got the hands for it." She didn't look up. "And tell him if he brings a bottle that costs more than my truck I'll pour it in the gravy."

Leah stopped mopping.

"Small town," Dot said, before she could ask. "Danny Vo told Mike Lacasse who told Ray who told me the man ate eleven eggs standing up. Anybody eats like that, I want to see it." She put the cash in the bank bag and zipped it. "Go on. He's out there."

Leah looked out the front window.

He was standing on the sidewalk across the street, in front of the Mercantile, with his hands in the pockets of the olive waxed jacket, reading the hand-lettered sign taped to the inside of the window about the church rummage sale with every appearance of deep interest. His hair was doing its thing. His boots were shining. Mike Lacasse's wife was standing in the Mercantile doorway with a broom, not sweeping anything, staring at him.

Leah hung up her apron.

---

"Come and get me how," she said, when they got to the end of the dock.

The lake was gray under a gray sky, the ice gone out of it again in the afternoon, the far shore soft with low cloud. The lodge was a smudge of green roof and dark logs four miles off across the water. Nobody was on Main Street. Nobody was on the lake.

Harry held out his arm, bent at the elbow, like a guy in an old movie about to walk a girl into a dance.

"Hold on," he said. "Tight as you like. Don't let go."

"What happens if I let go?"

"Then you'd be in two places, and only one of them would be the lodge." He saw her face. "I'm joking. Mostly. Just hold on."

She looked at the arm. She looked at him.

"This is the four seconds thing. The lurch."

"Less than four seconds. Four miles." His head tilted. "Or I could row you over. There's a canoe. It'd take an hour and I'd show off the whole way."

She took his arm. Under the waxed cotton it was as hard as a fence rail, and hot right through the sleeve.

"Breathe in," Harry said, "and don't — "

The world went into a tube.

That was the only way she could think about it, later. Somebody took the whole world and squeezed it through a tube a lot narrower than the world was, and she was in it, and her ribs came in and her ears popped and her stomach stayed on the dock. There was pressure on her eyes from every side. There was no air. There was black, and a sense of moving very fast in a direction that didn't exist, and then her boots hit wood planking hard enough to jar her teeth, and there was air again, cold and wet and smelling of spruce and lake water and woodsmoke, and she was standing on a different dock, and her stomach arrived about a second after the rest of her.

She made it to the edge. Just.

Most of Dot's split pea soup went into the lake. Then the rest of it. She knelt on the planks of the lodge dock with her hands braced on the edge and heaved until there was nothing left, and then heaved a bit more for good measure, and somewhere above her and behind her she heard Harry Potter say, very gently, "Yeah. That's normal. First time, everybody does that."

"*Don't,*" Leah said into the lake, "*talk to me.*"

He didn't talk to her. He crouched down beside her, not too close, and after a minute a folded white handkerchief appeared at the edge of her vision, held out between two fingers. She took it without looking at him. It was linen, and soft, and had a small embroidered *P* in one corner, and it smelled like earth after rain.

She wiped her mouth. She sat back on her heels.

"I'm never doing that again," she said.

"Most people say that."

"I *mean* it."

"I know you do." He was looking out at the lake, politely, the way you'd look away from somebody getting changed. "I'll row you back. Or you can swim. I'd half expected you'd want to swim."

She looked at him sideways. He was squatting on his heels with his forearms on his knees and his hands hanging loose between them, and he wasn't laughing at her. She checked. He wasn't smirking, wasn't doing the canine thing, wasn't hiding it. He looked like a man waiting for a kettle.

"You could've warned me," she said.

"I said breathe in."

"You could've *warned* me warned me."

"Would it have helped?"

She thought about it. "No."

"Well, then." He stood up and held out a hand to her. She looked at it. Then she took it and let him pull her up, and he let go the second she was on her feet, and turned around, and gestured up the dock with a little flourish. "Welcome to the lodge. I think I own it. Mind the bear."

---

The lodge was bigger than it looked from across the lake. Two stories of peeled logs gone honey-gold with age, a deck that ran the whole length of the front, a stone chimney as wide as a truck going up the middle of it. The green metal roof had a crust of old snow along the north edge. There was a pile of split firewood under a tarp that would have kept the Clearwater house warm for three winters.

The sliding door off the deck had four long gouges in the aluminum frame, about the height of Leah's chest.

"Danny's bear," Harry said. "Came back Wednesday. I had a word with him."

"A word."

"Several. He's gone over the ridge to think about his choices."

Inside it was one enormous room, two stories high, with a staircase going up to a loft along the back wall. A stone fireplace you could have parked a car in, with a fire already going, a big one, birch and spruce cracking and popping. Leather couches that had seen a lot of wet dogs. Bearskin rug, which made her lip lift. A stuffed lake trout the length of her arm over the mantel, with a little brass plaque under it that said 1987. An antler chandelier hanging from the beams with about forty bulbs in it, all of them on. The whole far wall was windows, floor to ceiling, and through them, across four miles of gray water, she could see Turnagain strung along the shore under its smoke. The white church. The café. The Prospector, with its half a sign.

It smelled like old wood and woodsmoke and lake. And meat. A lot of meat, somewhere close, and butter, and garlic, and something green and sharp like tarragon.

Her stomach, which had just thrown up everything she'd eaten since breakfast, sat up and paid attention.

"You cooked," she said.

"I'm going to cook. I've done the prep." He shrugged off the waxed jacket and slung it over the back of a couch, and under it he was wearing a dark gray sweater with the sleeves already shoved up to the elbows, and his forearms were thick and brown and didn't have a single mark on them. "Hope you're hungry. I've no idea how hungry you get. I've gone on the size of the caribou."

The kitchen was through an arch off the great room — a long galley with a restaurant range, six burners, and a steel counter down the middle where somebody had laid out, on a big wooden board, six ribeye steaks. Thick ones. Each about the size of Leah's hand spread out, and as thick as her wrist, marbled white through the red. Potatoes. Beans. Garlic. A block of butter the size of a brick.

"Six," Leah said.

"Two for you, four for me." He considered her. "Three and three. Sit down. You'll want something in you."

She sat on a stool at the end of the counter. He put a glass of water in front of her, and a heel of bread, and then he went to work.

She'd seen people cook. Her mom. Dot. Emily, God help her, who'd fed the whole of Sam's pack out of that tiny kitchen for two years and never once burned anything. She knew what it looked like when somebody knew what they were doing.

He knew what he was doing. She watched for the wand. It never came out. He used a knife, a big one, and his hands, and he moved around that kitchen like he'd been cooking in it for years, which she knew he hadn't. Potatoes into a pan with fat from a jar that smelled like duck, and into the oven. The cast-iron pans onto the range, two of them, heating until the air over them wavered. Salt on the steaks from a height, a lot of it. Into the pans. The sound they made. Then he cracked egg yolks into a steel bowl and set it over a pot of simmering water and started whisking with one hand while he dropped butter into the bowl a lump at a time with the other, and the stuff in the bowl went thick and pale and glossy, and he didn't look at it once. He was looking at the steaks.

"Danny said you did that in four minutes," Leah said.

"Danny exaggerates." He tipped a pan and spooned foaming butter over the meat. "It was three and a half."

"Where'd you learn to cook?"

"My aunt's kitchen." He flipped a steak. "I did most of it, from about six. Breakfast, anyhow. Bacon, eggs, fried bread. If I burnt anything I didn't get any." He said it in the same voice he'd use to tell her the weather. "Later on I got interested. There's a lot of hanging about, in my job. Waiting for people to do something stupid. I started reading cookbooks."

She thought about a cupboard under the stairs. About a little kid standing on a stool at a stove, making breakfast for people who locked him in with the spiders.

She didn't say anything. He didn't seem to want her to.

He plated it on two big white plates. Three steaks each, cut into thick slices, red in the middle, black at the edges, the béarnaise in a pool. A heap of potatoes gone crisp and gold. The beans with garlic and lemon. He put hers down in front of her and his down across the counter, and sat on the stool opposite, and handed her a knife and fork.

"Go on," he said.

She went on.

She ate the first steak without looking up. She ate the second one slower. Halfway through the third she put her fork down and sat back on the stool and looked at him, and he was watching her eat with his chin on his hand, and he'd already finished all three of his and most of his potatoes and was picking at the beans.

"Well?" he said.

She didn't want to tell him. She'd had a whole speech planned on the drive over, or the tube over, about how she didn't care how much money he had and he wasn't going to impress her with Belgian chocolate or a lodge he maybe owned, and she'd been ready to be unimpressed by anything he put in front of her.

"It's really good," she said.

His face did something. The corner of his mouth went up, and the green eyes went bright, and he looked down at the beans, and for one second he looked about fifteen.

"Thanks," he said. "That's nice to hear."

"Don't get a big head."

"Bit late for that."

She ate the rest of the third steak. He got up and put a fourth one in the pan without asking, and she didn't argue.

---

They took the coffee out to the fire.

He sat on one end of the long leather couch with his boots up on a stool and she sat on the other end with her legs pulled up under her and her mug in both hands, and there was a whole cushion between them, and the fire put out enough heat that even she could feel it on her face. Outside the windows the light was going. The far shore was a dark line with the lights of Turnagain coming on along it one at a time, yellow, small.

"Third date," Leah said.

"Third date," he agreed. He didn't sound like he was dreading it. He sounded like a man at the dentist who'd decided to get it over with. "Go on. Ask."

"How are you a wolf?"

He was quiet for a while. He turned the mug around in his hands.

"I don't know," he said. "Not properly. I can tell you what happened. I can't tell you why."

"Okay."

"There was a war. I told you that. When I was seventeen." He looked at the fire. "At the end of it, the bloke who killed my parents — Voldemort — he'd done some things to himself to make sure he couldn't die. Stuck bits of himself in things and hid them. And one of the things he'd stuck a bit of himself in, without meaning to, was me. When I was one. When he tried to kill me and it went wrong." His thumb moved on the mug. "So for him to die, I had to. That was the long and short of it. I walked out into a forest one night and let him kill me."

Leah didn't move.

"And I died," Harry said. "Or near enough as makes no difference. There's a place you go. Looked like a railway station, for me. King's Cross, all white and empty. I was there a while. And then I came back." He shrugged, one shoulder. "And then I killed him. Next morning. In front of everybody. That bit's in the history books, and they get most of it wrong."

The fire popped. A log shifted and settled in a shower of sparks.

"And then," he said, "about four months later, I started running hot."

Leah's breath went out of her.

"I thought it was a fever," Harry said. "I'd a temperature of a hundred and six for three weeks and felt fine. My teeth went funny. Those two." He touched one of his canines with his tongue, through his lip. "I could smell what everybody'd had for breakfast. I could hear Ron's heart going from the other end of the house. I ate everything in it. Hermione dragged me to hospital and they did every test they had, and some they made up, and none of them knew what to tell me." He was still looking at the fire. "A year after that, I tried to become an Animagus. That's a wizard who can turn into an animal. My dad was one. He was a stag."

"The deer," Leah said. "The light deer."

"Stag. Yeah." The corner of his mouth moved. "I thought I'd be a stag. I'd got it all planned. Spent nine months on the potion, and you have to keep a leaf in your mouth for a month, which is horrible, and wait for a lightning storm, which in England's easy enough, and then you drink it and you see what you are." He took a drink of coffee. "What came out was a bit bigger than a stag."

Leah put her mug down on the floor.

"Hermione's got a theory," Harry said. "She's got forty pages of theory. I've read eleven. Her best guess is that something came back with me from that station. Or that I came back with more room in me than I went out with, and something moved in." He was quiet. "I don't know. Nobody does. It's not in any book. I've looked."

Leah was staring at him.

*A chief, a long time ago, who could leave his body and walk around as a spirit. He wandered for a long time, a spirit with nowhere to go, and the only thing that would take him in was a wolf. So he went into the wolf. And when he finally came back for his body, he brought the wolf with him.*

"Taha Aki," she said.

He turned his head and looked at her.

"That's his name. The chief. In the story." She heard her own voice, and it sounded strange to her. "He left his body. He went somewhere else. And when he came back he brought the wolf with him."

"Yeah," Harry said quietly. "When you told me that on the rock, I nearly fell off it."

"You didn't say anything."

"It was your story. You'd only just told it me." He looked back at the fire. "And I don't know that it's the same. Probably it isn't. Your lot have been doing it for a thousand years and it's in your blood and you've got the stories for it. I've got eleven pages of Hermione's handwriting and a lot of very confused doctors." He paused. "But I liked hearing it. That somebody'd been there before. Even if it's only a story."

"It's a true story."

"I know," Harry said. "That's what I liked about it."

She thought about Taha Aki going into the wolf because nothing else would have him.

She thought about a seventeen-year-old walking into a forest at night to die so everybody else wouldn't have to.

She thought about telling him that it was the bravest thing she'd ever heard. She didn't, because she thought he'd hate it, and because he was already sitting very still at the other end of the couch with his eyes on the fire, the way you sit when you've just told somebody something and you're waiting to see what they do with it.

"So you're a wizard who died," she said instead, "and came back a wolf."

"That's about the size of it."

"That's so stupid."

His head came around. She was trying not to grin, and failing. He looked at her for a second, and then he barked a laugh, loud, startled, the same one she'd gotten out of him over the pie.

"It is, isn't it," he said. "It's *so* stupid. Hermione cried for a week. Ron laughed for a month. I had to go to the Ministry and register as an Animagus and the woman behind the desk fainted. Actually fainted. Right off her chair." He was grinning now. "They've got me down as *Canis lupus, variant, unclassified.* There's a form."

"Of course there's a form."

"I've got a whole file. It's got its own box."

She laughed. She picked her coffee back up and drank it and felt it go down hot and looked at him over the rim.

"Clothes," she said.

"Ah." He put his mug down. "I wondered when you'd get to that."

"You said third date."

"I did." He stood up. "Easier to show you."

He walked out into the middle of the great room, onto the bearskin rug, in his gray sweater and his dark trousers and his polished boots, and turned around to face her, and stood with his hands loose at his sides.

"Don't scream," he said. "Danny screamed."

Leah had seen a lot of people phase. She'd done it herself a thousand times. She knew what it looked like from the outside: the tremor, the shaking, the heat coming off the skin in a wave, the body coming apart from the inside like something bursting a seam, and then a wolf standing in a cloud of torn cloth.

He didn't shake.

One moment he was standing on the rug, a man in a sweater. The next — there wasn't a *between* she could find, though she was looking for it, though she'd have sworn later she didn't blink — there was something folding, something turning over on itself, like a coat turned inside out in one motion, and then the room was full of wolf.

He was so *big.* Indoors, he was so much bigger. On the plateau there'd been sky around him. Here there was a ceiling, and the antler chandelier was swaying very gently a foot above his ears, and his shoulder came up level with the top of the mantelpiece, and his tail swept slowly back and forth across the rug and brushed the far couch on every pass. He stood with his head low and his ears forward, looking at her, and the green eyes caught the fire.

The floor didn't creak. She'd been listening for it. Something that size was standing in the middle of an old log building on old pine planks and the planks were silent.

There were no rags on the rug. No shreds of gray sweater. Nothing.

"Well?" said the wolf, in Harry's voice, the rumble under it.

"Turn back," Leah said.

He did. The same fold, the same turning-over, the same nothing in between, and he was standing on the bearskin rug in his gray sweater, sleeves pushed up past the elbows exactly where they'd been, a wrinkle in the left one exactly where it had been. His boots were still shining. He held his arms out from his sides a little, like a magician who'd pulled off the trick with the doves.

Leah stood up off the couch.

She walked across the rug to him and grabbed a handful of his sweater at the chest and pulled on it and looked at it. Wool. Real. Hot from him. Not a thread out of place. She let go and grabbed his sleeve and turned his arm over and looked at the cuff. She bent down and looked at his boots.

"*Are you kidding me,*" she said.

"No."

"Do you know how many pairs of shorts I've lost?"

"Er — "

"*Eleven.*" She straightened up and jabbed him in the chest with a finger, and her finger bent a little against him. "Eleven pairs. Two bras. Three T-shirts. My good jeans, the only jeans I had that fit, the first week, in the backyard, in front of my *brother.* I've walked home naked through the woods behind La Push four times. *Four times.* I've had to hide behind a dumpster at the Thriftway in Forks. Do you know what it's like to tie all your clothes to your ankle in a plastic bag every time you want to go for a *run?*"

"No," Harry said. He looked like he was trying very hard to keep his face straight. "I don't."

"And you just — " she flapped a hand at all of him — "*do* that. With your *boots* on."

"I'm sorry."

"No, you're not."

"No, I'm not," he agreed. "But I'm sorry about the dumpster."

She glared at him. He was close, she realized. She'd walked right up to him. She was standing on the rug a foot away from him with her hand still half raised from jabbing him, and she could feel the heat coming off him, and smell him, and he was looking down at her with his head tilted and his eyes bright and very green.

He said, carefully: "I could do it for you."

Leah went still.

"If you wanted," Harry said. "It's not hard. A bit of work, getting it right — it'd have to be keyed to you, so whatever you're wearing goes where you go and comes back with you. I'd have to put it on you. On *you.*" His hands stayed where they were, at his sides. "I'd not do it without you asking. No rush. I'm asking. That's all."

She looked at him.

*You want to help somebody, you ask me first.* She'd said that, on the plateau. She hadn't meant herself. She hadn't thought she needed to.

He was asking.

"Ask me again," she said, "on the fourth date."

His eyebrows went up. "There's a fourth?"

"Dot's doing Thanksgiving. Monday. Doors at four." She stepped back off the rug, because she'd been standing too close for too long, and she knew it, and he knew it. "She says bring you. She says you can carve, she's seen your hands."

"She's seen my hands?"

"She says you've got the hands for it."

He looked down at his hands as if he'd never thought about them before.

"And she says if you bring a bottle that costs more than her truck," Leah said, "she'll pour it in the gravy."

"What does her truck cost?"

"*Harry.*"

He grinned at her. It was the first time she'd said his name to his face, and they both heard it, and he didn't say anything about it, and she was grateful.

"What should I bring?" he said.

She thought about it. She thought about the brown hundred-dollar bills fanned out on Ray's bar.

"Nothing that costs money," she said. "Bring something you made."

He thought about that, very seriously.

"All right," he said.

---

She didn't let him Apparate her back. She didn't let him row her back, either. She went down to the end of the lodge dock in the dark and told him to turn around, and he turned around, and she stripped and rolled her clothes into the bag and tied the bag to her ankle with the cord, three hard knots, next to the little leather pouch with the mirror in it. Then she let the fire take her and went off the end of the dock into the black water.

It was cold enough to stop her heart, for about a second. Then it was just cold.

She heard nothing behind her. She knew better by now than to expect to.

Halfway across, with the lights of the town getting bigger and the ice plates knocking around her, she turned her head, and there he was in the water ten yards off to her left, a black head with two black ears and two green eyes, swimming along beside her as if he did this every night.

*Show-off,* she thought.

"I heard that," he said, and his voice came across the water low and clean.

*You weren't supposed to.*

"You were thinking it in words." A pause. "Was that one meant for me?"

She thought about it.

*Yeah,* she thought. *That one was meant for you.*

They swam the rest of the way in without saying anything at all.

Dot pushed every table in the Galena together down the middle of the room into one long one, and covered it with three white tablecloths that didn't match, and by ten past four on Monday there were fifteen people sitting at it and a turkey on the counter the size of a small dog.

Leah knew most of them now. Ray, in a clean Molson T-shirt. Wes, who'd taken his ponytail out and combed his beard for the occasion. Brynn and her mother, Lorraine, who had Brynn's round cheeks and a laugh like a car alarm. Mike Lacasse from the Mercantile and his wife, Bev, who'd stood in the doorway with the broom. The three old men from the corner table, who turned out to be named Arnie, Gus, and Walt, and who were still arguing about Bertuzzi. Two of the highway crew, who had nowhere else to be. A tiny ancient woman named Mrs. Okada who'd taught every person at the table to read, including Dot, and who sat at the end like a queen.

And Harry, in the cream sweater, with a pie dish wrapped in a dish towel on his lap.

He'd come in at four exactly, through the front door like everybody else, and stamped the snow off his boots on the mat and said "Evening" to the room in general. The room had gone quiet and looked at him. Then Dot had come out of the kitchen with a carving knife in one hand and a fork the length of her forearm in the other and held them out to him, handles first.

"You carve," Dot said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"What's in the dish?"

He unwrapped it. A tart, in a scalloped tin, golden brown, with a lattice of pastry across the top and something underneath the lattice that was deep amber and shining.

"Treacle tart," Harry said. "I made it. Last night." He looked at Leah, sitting halfway down the long table, and then back at Dot. "I was told nothing that costs money."

Dot looked at the tart for a long time.

"What's treacle?"

"Golden syrup, mostly. Breadcrumbs. Lemon. It's English. It's — " he paused — "it's my favorite."

Dot took the tart out of his hands, and smelled it, and grunted, and carried it off to the kitchen without another word. Harry stood there holding the carving knife and fork.

"That means she likes you," Brynn whispered, very loudly, across the table.

"Brilliant," said Harry, and went to carve the turkey.

---

He carved it with his hands, the same way he'd cooked at the lodge. Leah watched. Everybody watched. He stood at the counter with the knife and fork and took the bird apart in about ten minutes, legs, thighs, wings, the breast in thick even slices fanned out on the platter like a deck of cards, and he didn't make a mess and he didn't waste anything, and when he finished he cracked the carcass in half with his bare hands, which made Gus drop his fork, and put it in Dot's stockpot.

"Huh," said Dot, from the kitchen door.

They sat down. Dot sat at the head of the table, by the kitchen. She didn't take her apron off. She bowed her head, and everybody else bowed theirs, and Leah, after a second, did too.

"For what we're about to receive," Dot said, in her cigarette voice. "And for the ones that aren't here to receive it." A pause. "Amen."

"Amen," said the table.

Leah kept her head down a second longer than everybody else.

*The ones that aren't here.* Her dad at the head of their table at home, every Thanksgiving, American Thanksgiving, in November, carving with the electric knife he'd bought at Costco and been so proud of. Getting turkey on the ceiling one year. Singing Creedence over the mashed potatoes. She'd done Thanksgiving last year with her mom and Seth, the three of them at a table built for four, and nobody had said anything about the chair, and nobody had sat in it.

When she lifted her head, Harry was looking at her from across the table. He didn't say anything. He didn't do anything. He looked at her for a second, steady, and then he looked away and asked Mrs. Okada to pass the cranberries, and Leah let her breath out.

Then the food came, and the noise came, and it was Thanksgiving.

It was loud. Lorraine Halvorsen's laugh went off like an alarm every three minutes. Arnie wanted to know what Harry thought of the Canucks' chances this year and Harry admitted he'd never seen a hockey game, and Arnie looked at him like he'd confessed to a murder. Walt asked what sport he followed, then, and Harry thought about it and said, "One you've never heard of," and Brynn said, "Cricket?" and Harry said, "Worse," and Leah choked on her potatoes.

Ray watched him all through dinner like he was waiting for him to try and buy the café. He didn't. He ate four plates. Leah ate five. Somewhere around the third plate, Leah noticed the highway guys had stopped eating to watch the two of them.

"Where do you *put* it," Bev Lacasse said finally, staring.

"Hollow legs," Harry said. "Both of us."

Halfway through dessert — Dot's pumpkin pie, Dot's saskatoon pie, and Harry's tart, which Dot had put out on the counter between them with no comment at all — Brynn leaned across the table with her fork in her hand and said, "Can I tell you something weird?"

"Always," Harry said.

"I had this dream. The week you came to town. There was this deer, in the trees, made of *light.* Silver. It just looked at me and ran off." Brynn laughed. "Leah thinks it was the fever. But it was so *real.* I keep dreaming about it. It's a nice dream, kind of. Like — I don't know. Like something was looking out for me."

Harry's fork had stopped halfway to his mouth.

Leah watched him. She watched him put the fork down, very carefully, on the edge of his plate. She watched him look at Brynn, at her round face and her messy bun and her pencil, and something went across his face too fast for Leah to name.

"That sounds like a good dream," he said.

"Right?" said Brynn, pleased, and went back to her pie.

Dot ate a slice of the treacle tart standing up in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded, where she thought nobody was watching. Leah was watching. Dot ate the whole slice. Then she cut another, and ate that one too, and then she came out and stood at Harry's shoulder and looked down at him.

"Who taught you pastry?" Dot said.

"Taught myself."

"How old?"

"Twelve. Thirteen." He looked up at her. "My aunt's kitchen. She didn't like me in the way much, so I'd get up early and use it before anybody was awake."

Dot was quiet a long moment. Something in her face shifted, the way it had shifted the day she'd looked at Leah's squelching shoes and bare legs and said *you running from somebody.* She put one big blunt hand on his shoulder, briefly, and took it away.

"It's good," Dot said. "Your crust's too short. Use less butter. You're welcome back anytime."

She went back into the kitchen.

Harry sat there with his fork in his hand looking at the kitchen door. Leah watched him swallow.

"Huh," said Ray, from the far end of the table, in a different voice than he'd been using all night.

---

They walked down to the dock afterward, while Lorraine Halvorsen and Bev Lacasse argued over who was washing and Leah had been told, firmly, by Dot, that she was not.

The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared and gone hard and black and full of stars, and it was cold enough that the lake was steaming, little wisps of mist coming up off the open water between the ice plates and drifting along in the dark. The dock planks squeaked under their boots.

"She knew," Leah said, at the end of the dock. "Dot. About your aunt. She looked at you and she knew."

"She'd make a good Auror," Harry said. "Bit terrifying."

"You liked it."

He was quiet for a second. "Yeah," he said. "I liked it."

They stood there. The steam came up off the water around them. The stars were so thick over the lake it looked like somebody had spilled sugar on black cloth.

"Brynn's deer," Leah said.

"Solveig told me." He didn't look at her. "She underlined it twice in my file. It's a thing I do, apparently. The big things I get right and the small ones I leave lying about for other people to trip on." He breathed out. "It won't hurt her. It's a — a leftover. A bit of the memory that stuck to the wrong side. It'll fade. Or it won't, and she'll have a dream about a silver deer for the rest of her life." He paused. "I can't decide if that's worse."

"She said it felt like something was looking out for her."

"Yeah."

"It was."

He turned his head and looked at her.

"Ask me," Leah said.

He knew what she meant. She watched him know it.

"Clearwater," Harry said, quite formally. "Would you like me to fix it so your clothes come with you when you change?"

"Yes."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes."

"It goes on you. Whatever you're wearing when you go, comes with you, comes back with you. Boots, coat, whatever's in your pockets. It's keyed to you. Nobody else can take it off but me, and I won't unless you tell me to." He paused. "And I'll need to touch you. Your wrist. That's all."

She held out her arm.

He took her wrist in his hand. His palm was very hot and very dry and his fingers went all the way around her wrist with room to spare, and he held it the way you'd hold a bird. He didn't do anything she could see. His eyes went somewhere else for a second, the way they'd gone when he'd looked at Brynn on the mountain, and his thumb moved once along the inside of her wrist.

Something went through her. Cold, like water poured down her spine, from the crown of her head to the soles of her feet. Then it was gone, and there was nothing, just her standing on the dock with her heart going a little fast and his hand around her wrist.

"Done," he said.

"That's it?"

"That's it. Try it later, somewhere nobody'll see." He let go of her wrist. "If your boots come back on the wrong feet, tell me."

She looked down at her wrist. Nothing on it. Nothing she could see or feel. She thought about eleven pairs of shorts and a dumpster behind the Thriftway, and the plastic bag and the cord and the three hard knots, every single time, for a year and a half.

She looked up at him.

He was standing very close, in the steam coming off the lake, with the stars behind his head and his hair sticking up, looking down at her with his head tipped a little. He had a crumb of Dot's pie crust at the corner of his mouth.

Her wolf got up off its back and stood there with its ears forward.

*Oh, what the hell,* Leah thought.

She took a fistful of the cream sweater and pulled, and he came down, and she kissed him.

It was short. It was long enough. His mouth was hot, and tasted like golden syrup and coffee and the crumb of crust, and he went very still for a second, completely still, the way he'd gone still in the tree when she'd told him to get out of her head. Then one of his hands came up and rested against the side of her neck, under her jaw, just resting there, and he kissed her back, slow, like he had all night.

When she let go of his sweater, she stood there with her forehead against his chest and her eyes closed, and checked.

She did it the way she'd done it in the pumpkin field and on the plateau and in the middle of Main Street. She reached around inside her own head for somebody else. For a pull. For the ground tilting, gravity moving, every plan she'd ever made sliding off a table and smashing on the floor.

The ground stayed where it was. The lake was a lake. Seth was still Seth, and her mom was her mom, and Dot was in the kitchen yelling at Lorraine Halvorsen about the gravy boat. She was still Leah. She still wanted to go home and sleep on an orange bedspread under a bad moose. She still didn't trust him further than she could throw him.

She also wanted to kiss him again.

That was all. That was everything. She could work with that.

"You all right?" Harry said, above her head. His voice had gone rough.

"Checking something."

"What?"

"That I'm still me."

He was quiet. His hand was still on her neck. She felt his thumb move, once, along her jaw.

"And?" he said.

"Yeah," Leah said. "Still me."

"Good," said Harry. "I like you."

She lifted her head and looked at him. He looked back at her, steady, with the crumb still at the corner of his mouth, and didn't make a joke.

She reached up and brushed the crumb off with her thumb.

"Fourth date," he said, after a moment.

"Shut up."

"Just keeping count."

"Shut *up,*" Leah said, and kissed him again, mostly to make him.

The last Sunday in October, it snowed for real.

It came down all morning in big wet flakes that stuck to everything, and by the time Leah walked to the gas station after church let out there was six inches on the ground and the plows hadn't come and Main Street was a white tunnel between white trucks. The pay phone had a cap of snow on its plastic hood. Somebody had scratched a new name into the steel under CASSIE + LOSER. It said JORDAN IS SORRY.

She'd called every Sunday. She'd promised. Her mom picked up on the second ring now.

"Hi, baby."

"Hi, Mom."

They talked about nothing for a while. The snow. Dot. Brynn's nursing school applications. Her mom's blood pressure, which was fine, and Charlie's back, which wasn't. Leah had learned to listen for the place in the conversation where her mom's voice changed. It came about six minutes in.

"Seth's been running north again," Sue said.

Leah leaned her shoulder on the phone box.

"Every night this week. He thinks I don't know. He goes up the peninsula to the lighthouse at Port Townsend and he sits on the bluff and he listens. Jacob told me. He says Seth goes all the way to the end of the land and sits there with his ears up until it's light." Her mom's voice went careful. "Jacob says sometimes he thinks he hears you. Just for a second. Like a radio. And then it's gone."

Leah closed her eyes.

She'd been gone six weeks. Six weeks of a quiet head. Six weeks of the lake and the café and the plateau and the bad moose and a stupid enormous wolf who went to Belgium and came back with chocolate and a treacle tart, and it had been the best six weeks of her life, and she'd known it while it was happening, and she'd let herself have it.

And her little brother had been sitting on a bluff at the end of the world every night, listening for her in the dark.

"Lee?"

"I'm here."

"I'm not asking you to come home." Her mother's voice was very steady. "I want you to know that. I'm not asking."

"I know you're not."

"I just thought you'd want to know."

"Yeah." Leah opened her eyes. The snow was coming down past the plastic hood, slow, fat flakes, and across the street the Galena's windows were steamed over and gold. "Mom. I'm coming home."

Silence on the line.

"For good?" Sue said.

"I don't know. For a while. For Seth." She swallowed. "I don't know yet."

"Okay," said her mom, and her voice cracked down the middle and put itself back together. "Okay. That's okay. That's — when?"

"Soon. A few days."

"Do you need money for a bus? I can wire — "

"I'll run."

"Of course you will." A wet laugh. Then a pause. A long, considering, motherly pause, the kind Leah had been hearing at the end of these calls for four Sundays running. "Lee. Bring whoever."

"There's no whoever."

"Mm-hm."

Leah stood in the snow with the phone against her ear and the cold steel against her shoulder. She thought about a dock and a crumb of pie crust and *I like you,* said plain, without a joke on the end.

"His name's Harry," she said.

The line went completely silent. For a second Leah thought the card had run out.

"Of course it is," said Sue Clearwater, very quietly. And then, after a moment, in a voice Leah hadn't heard out of her since before the funeral: "Your father would have *howled.*"

He was waiting on the café dock with two coffees in paper cups and snow in his hair.

He'd been in town since Thursday. He came most weekends now, and some weeknights, when whatever he was doing in Geneva or Prague or the mountains of Bulgaria let him go for a few hours, and he turned up at the café at close in the waxed jacket and walked her home, or they ran, or they sat on the dock like this and didn't say much. Dot had started putting a plate of whatever was left over on the pass for him without being asked. Brynn had started calling him *the Duke,* to his face, and he answered to it.

He handed Leah a coffee. She didn't drink it.

"Your mum," Harry said. He'd seen it on her face from fifty feet. "Everyone all right?"

"Everyone's fine." She held the cup in both hands. "Seth's been going to the lighthouse every night. At the top of the peninsula. Listening for me."

He went still.

"I'm going home," Leah said. "A few days. Maybe a week. Maybe for good, I don't know. I've got to go see him."

Harry didn't say anything.

He stood there on the end of the dock with the snow coming down on him, and he looked at her for a long moment, and then he looked away, out at the lake, at the white shore and the gray water. She watched him do it. She watched him take a breath and let it out and stand there with his hands around his coffee and the snow settling in his hair and on his shoulders, very quiet, very still, for about as long as it would take to count to ten.

Then he put his coffee down on the post at the end of the dock. He reached into the inside of the waxed jacket, the left side, and took out a mirror.

A different one. Bigger than hers, and newer, in a plain black frame. He held it up in front of his face.

"Solveig Brekke," he said.

The glass went dark and then light, and Leah, standing at his shoulder, could see into it at an angle — a stone room, gray daylight through a tall window, and the tall rawboned shape of Solveig Brekke at a desk under a lamp, writing with a Bic, who looked up and saw who it was and put the pen down with a sharp little click.

"No," said Solveig.

"I haven't said anything."

"You are calling me on a Sunday. You never call me on a Sunday. Something has happened, or you have bought something, or you are about to do something extremely irregular." Her pale eyes narrowed. "Is that snow? Where are you?"

"Canada."

"Still?"

The wolf in Leah's chest sat up.

She hadn't meant to say it. She didn't know why she'd said it. It came out of her before she could stop it, and the second it was out she knew she'd lost.

"Everyone."

"You do what I say."

"Within reason."

She phased behind the deadfall by the lake road, the same place she always did, out of habit, and stood there with her heart going hard in her chest, dressed in her jeans and the flannel and her hoodie and the felt-lined boots, with Dot's pie box tied to the cord on her ankle, and let the fire take her.

The heat came up. The bones came apart. She braced for the burst and the rip, the sound of cloth tearing, the way she'd braced a thousand times —

Nothing tore.

She stood on four feet in the snow behind the deadfall, gray fur and long legs and her own heart pounding, and there were no rags around her. No shreds of flannel in the snow. No felt-lined boots split down the seams. The pie box was still tied to her ankle, string and all. She stood there for a full minute with her nose down, sniffing the ground around her for any scrap of her clothes, and there wasn't one.

Then she let the fire go again, and came back up on two legs, and she was standing in the snow in her jeans and her flannel and her hoodie and her boots, and her boots were on the right feet, and there was a chocolate wrapper in the front pocket of her jeans where she'd left it at lunch.

She patted herself all over like somebody who'd just walked out of a car wreck.

Behind her, very quietly, a black wolf the size of a truck sat down in the snow and watched her do it, with his head tilted.

"Well?" Harry said.

Leah turned around. She opened her mouth to say something — she didn't know what — and what came out was a laugh, the pumpkin-field laugh, wheezing and hiccuping and ridiculous, and she bent over with her hands on her knees in the snow behind the deadfall and laughed until her eyes ran.

"I'll take that as a yes," said the wolf, and his tail swept the snow off the deadfall in one long arc.

---

They ran.

They ran south for five days. He lost the best of three on the second morning, on a long flat stretch of river valley below the Stikine, by two lengths, and then lost it again on the third morning by four, and complained about it for a hundred miles, and she let him. On the plateau where she'd watched the caribou pour away over the land like smoke, they found the herd again, or one like it, and he looked at her, and she looked at the young bulls at the edge, and they ate well that night. On the long high pass where she'd broken trail through a foot of snow on her way north, there were three feet now, and he went in front without being asked and she ran in his wake, in a trench he'd plowed with his chest, and didn't say anything about it, and neither did he.

They crossed the border at the same aluminum obelisk where she'd crossed going north. There was a gray jay sitting on top of it. It might have been the same one.

She lifted her back feet over the line, one and then the other, and walked back into the United States. Harry stepped over after her in one stride and looked down at the obelisk and said, "Should I have a passport for this?" and Leah thought, *You've got two hundred billion pounds and you're worried about a passport?*, and he said, "Fair point," and the gray jay didn't seem to care either way.

And on the fifth night, a little before dawn, they came down out of the Cascades to the Skagit flats.

The pumpkin field was still there. The hand-painted sign at the corner of the road had been changed. It said THANKS FOR A GREAT SEASON — SEE YOU NEXT YEAR. The field was picked over, mud and dead vines and a few split pumpkins rotting orange in the frost, and the sky was going gray in the east over the black shoulder of the mountains, and out of the gray, huge and white and far away, Mount Baker was standing up off the edge of the world.

Leah stopped in the middle of the field.

She stood there in the frost between two rows of dead vines and listened inside her own head, the way she had six weeks ago.

Quiet. Nothing. Not even a whisper of anybody. Her own blood, her own breathing, the wind in the stubble.

She phased back, and stood there in her jeans and her flannel and her boots, in the exact middle of the pumpkin field, and Harry stood next to her on four feet and didn't ask.

"This is where it went," she said out loud. "The last of them. Seth. I was standing right about here, and he went out like a light, and I laughed so hard I rolled in the dirt."

Harry looked at the dirt. He looked at the split pumpkins, and the mountain going pink on one side, and her.

"Good spot for it," he said.

They came down through Whidbey in the last of the dark, and out onto the beach below the old fort bluffs at Admiralty Head with the tide running hard and the stars going out one at a time.

Across the inlet, four miles of black water chopping in the gray light, was the Point Wilson lighthouse at the tip of its spit, its beam going round, and above it the bluff, and behind the bluff the lights of Port Townsend scattered up the hill. And beyond Port Townsend, low and dark and hunched against the coming day, the peninsula. Her peninsula. The Olympics standing up white in the dawn behind everything.

Leah stood at the edge of the water with her paws in the foam.

*When we get partway,* she thought, *I'm going to hear them. If anybody's phased. The link went out somewhere around here on the way up. It'll come back somewhere around here on the way down.*

"Will it hurt?" Harry asked. He was standing beside her, looking across.

*No. It'll just be loud.*

She thought about it. Six weeks of him on the step outside her head, by the door, hearing the words she meant him to hear. Six weeks of him telling her every time something came through she might not have meant.

*No,* she thought. *Stay.*

"All right."

She went into the inlet.

The cold hit her like a car, the same as last time, and the tide took her and started dragging her north toward the Strait, and she fought it, angling into it, swimming big ugly strokes with her chin up out of the water. Ten yards to her left the black head with two black ears went along beside her, swimming against the same tide with no apparent effort, the green eyes on the lighthouse.

A mile out, she felt the first of it.

It was so faint she thought she'd imagined it. A brush. A sense, at the very back of her head, of a room on the other side of a wall with somebody in it. Somebody small and sad and very tired, sitting very still.

Two miles out it was a voice.

It was grainy, flattened, like somebody shouting from the bottom of a stairwell, and it was saying the same thing it had probably been saying every night for a week, out of habit, without any hope at all, the way you'd say a prayer you didn't believe anymore —

*— Leah? Leah, you there? It's me. It's Seth. You there? You —*

*— LEAH?*

*Yeah,* Leah thought. Her throat was closing up, which was stupid, because she was a wolf in the middle of Puget Sound and she didn't have that kind of throat. *Yeah, Seth. It's me. I'm here.*

*LEAH. LEAH OH MY GOD LEAH WHERE ARE YOU, YOU'RE SO LOUD, YOU'RE SO — wait — are you in the WATER? You're in the water! You're in the inlet! I'm on the bluff! I'm right here! I can see the lighthouse through your eyes — I can see it from — you're RIGHT THERE —*

The pinball machine stopped dead.

*I know.*

*It's really big.*

*I know.*

The mine and the gold dome and Kell going to dust. The bar, the brown hundred-dollar bills. The dock. "Sam."

Paul's legs paddled harder. He could not stand down. He could not stand, at all, on anything.

Embry and Quil and Jared went down an inch at the knees without meaning to. Even Seth, on the top step, rocked on his heels.

Embry said a word his mother would have washed his mouth out for.

"Put him down, Harry," Leah said.

"Right you are." Leah went down the back steps.

She walked right out into the middle of the yard, where everybody could see, and let the fire take her.

Gray wolf. Flannel and jeans and felt-lined boots gone with her, no tearing, no rags, nothing. She stood there in the yard on four feet for a count of three with her ears up, looking at Paul. Then she let the fire go, and stood up on two legs in the middle of the yard in her jeans and her flannel and her boots, with her hair tied back and her hands in her pockets and not one thread out of place.

Paul stared at her from the puddle.

"*How,*" he said, "*are you wearing clothes.*"

Quil lost it first. He bent over with his hands on his knees and wheezed. Then Embry, holding on to a tree. Then Seth, from the top step, a high cracking honk. Even Jared turned away and put his hand over his mouth, and his shoulders were going.

"Six weeks," Leah said to Paul. "Lot can happen in six weeks."

Sam didn't laugh.

He was looking at Harry, and Harry had come down off the steps and was standing in the yard a few feet away from him, and for a moment it was just the two of them. Sam was taller. Leah had forgotten that. In human form Sam had a couple of inches on him and a lot of width, and he stood there with his fists closed, and Harry stood there with his hands back in his pockets.

"I promised her I wouldn't punch you," Harry said.

Sam's face went blank.

"She asked me not to," Harry said. "On a mountain, in Canada. So I won't." He looked at Sam a moment longer. "I think about it, mind. Recreationally."

Sam turned his head and looked at Leah.

She looked back. She thought about everything she could say. A year and a half of it. All the nights she'd lain awake rehearsing it, every word, the speech that would finally make him feel a tenth of what she'd felt. It was all still in there somewhere.

She didn't want it anymore. She checked, and she didn't.

"Go home, Sam," she said. "Emily's waiting."

It came out level. Her hands were steady in her pockets. Sam heard it come out level, and she watched him hear it, and something in his face let go that she hadn't known was holding on.

He nodded, once. He didn't say anything else.

And that was when the trees on the other side of the yard parted, and a russet wolf the size of a pickup truck came out of them at a trot, and stopped, and looked around the yard — at Sam, at Paul in the mud, at Embry hanging onto a tree, at Leah in her clothes — and then at Harry, and phased back to a very large, very tired-looking young man pulling on a pair of shorts.

"Seth," said Jacob Black, "woke me up at five in the morning to tell me Leah had a boyfriend who was a wizard. I thought he was having a stroke." He looked at Harry. "So you're the guy."

"I'm the guy."

"He said you're big."

"I'm told so."

Jacob looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at Leah. Then back at Harry.

"Show me," Jacob said.

Harry turned his head and looked at Leah. He didn't say anything. He just raised his eyebrows.

"Go on," Leah said.

He walked out into the middle of the yard, away from the flowerbed, and stood there in his waxed jacket, and there was the fold, the turning-over, the nothing in between —

The yard was full of wolf.

Black as the inside of a well. His shoulder stood well above the roof of the police cruiser. His head, when he lifted it, was higher than the top of the back door. His tail swept the gravel, slow, side to side, and the gravel didn't move, and when he shifted his weight the ground under him didn't so much as quiver. He stood there in the drizzle with his ears forward and his green eyes on Jacob, and looked down.

Jacob Black, who'd been the biggest of all of them since the spring, tipped his head back, and back, and back.

"Oh," said Jacob.

From the mud puddle, Paul said a word.

Quil said, "*Dude.*"

From inside the kitchen, through the window, with his back very firmly to it, Charlie Swan said loudly, "*I am not turning around.*"

"You're the Alpha," Harry said to Jacob, and his voice came out of that great black chest low and clean, the rumble under it. Every head in the yard jerked. Embry let go of the tree. "I'm on your land. I'm here because she is. If you'd like me gone, I'll go."

Jacob looked up at him for a long time.

Then he looked at Leah, standing in the yard in her clothes with her hands in her pockets, and something in his face went soft and surprised, the way it had gone the day she'd followed him off Sam's land.

"You're with Leah," Jacob said.

"I am."

"Then you're good." Jacob rubbed his face with both hands. "God. Okay. You're good. Just — don't step on anybody."

"Brilliant," said Harry. His tail swung. His ears pricked all the way forward. He turned his enormous head and looked at Leah, and his eyes had gone very bright. "Now. Which way to the ones that sparkle?"

It was raining at the Cullen house. Of course it was. It was Forks.

The big white house sat back from the river in its clearing with its glass wall and its long lawn, and the clouds were down so low over the trees that the tops of the cedars disappeared into them. Jacob walked in front, in his shorts, as if he lived there, which Leah supposed he more or less did now. Seth bounced along beside him. Leah walked behind with Harry, who was in human form again, in his jacket, and kept stopping to look at things.

The smell hit her at the tree line. Bleach and burnt sugar and something dead in the sun. Her lip came up off her teeth, the old reflex.

Harry's nose wrinkled. "Oh, that's *grim,*" he murmured. "You weren't exaggerating."

"I never exaggerate."

They were on the lawn. All of them. Carlisle in a sweater, pale and handsome and curious. Esme beside him. The big one, Emmett, grinning. Jasper, wary, at the back. Alice, small and frowning, with two fingers pressed to her temple like a woman with a migraine. Rosalie, ice-blond and furious-looking, with a little girl on her hip — a little bronze-curled girl who looked old enough to walk and couldn't possibly be, with Bella's brown eyes, who turned and saw Jacob and lit up like a lamp. Jacob went straight to her. Leah watched him go, watched his whole face change, and felt something complicated go through her, and let it.

And Bella. Leah almost didn't know her. White as paper, beautiful, with eyes the red of a stoplight. She stood next to Edward Cullen on the wet grass with her hand in his.

Edward was looking at Harry. He was frowning.

"I can't hear him," Edward said, to Carlisle, quietly. "At all."

"You're the one who reads minds," said Harry.

"Ordinarily." Edward's eyes narrowed, golden, intent. "You're very quiet."

"Am I? Sorry. Let me fix that." Harry smiled at him. The long canine showed. "Here. Have a listen."

Leah saw it land.

She saw Edward Cullen's face go from frowning to blank to horrified in about a second and a half, as the first verse came pouring out of Harry Potter's head straight into his, at full volume, word for word, and then the chorus, and then the chorus again.

Edward actually took a step back.

Leah couldn't stop herself. She didn't try. She threw her own head wide open and joined in, the whole thing, every word, the way she'd done in the cottonwoods, harmony and all.

"*Stop,*" said Edward Cullen. He'd put a hand up to his temple, like Alice. "Both of you. Please. I'm *asking.*"

"What?" said Emmett. "What is it? What're they doing?"

"Britney Spears," said Edward, through his teeth.

Emmett made a sound like a bull elephant falling down a staircase. Bella, beside Edward, put both hands over her mouth, and her shoulders started to shake, and Leah, who had liked Bella Swan about as much as she liked the smell of this house, felt something in her chest shift over and make a little room.

Harry let it go, eventually. Leah let it go a while after that.

Then Harry looked up at the sky. He looked at it for a long time, the low gray lid of it pressing down on the cedars. He looked so disappointed that Seth laughed out loud.

"I was told," Harry said to Carlisle, "that you sparkle."

Carlisle's mouth twitched. "In direct sunlight. It's — not often an issue, here."

"I've had a request in for six weeks." Harry sniffed the air, carefully, turning his head one way and then the other. "Nobody for miles who shouldn't see. May I?"

"May you what?" said Carlisle, interested.

Harry looked up.

Above the Cullens' lawn, the clouds parted.

Not much. A hole, round, maybe forty feet across, opened up in the gray directly overhead as if somebody had pushed a finger through wet tissue, and through it came a column of sunlight — real sunlight, thin and gold and October-slanted, from somewhere a long way above Forks — and fell straight down onto the lawn in a bright, clean circle on the wet grass.

Everybody looked at it.

Emmett Cullen grinned like a little kid, and walked straight into the middle of it, and spread his arms.

He lit up like a disco ball. Like a chandelier in a ballroom. Like somebody had dipped him in glue and rolled him in diamonds. Every inch of exposed skin on him threw light, thousands of tiny brilliant points of it, rainbow-edged, scattering off his face and his arms and his neck across the lawn and the glass wall of the house and the wet trees, so bright Leah had to squint.

Harry looked at him.

Harry looked at him for about three seconds, with his face perfectly still.

Then he sat down on the lawn. Then he lay down on the lawn, flat on his back on the wet grass in his waxed jacket, and laughed until he couldn't breathe. It went on and on. It came out of him in great helpless whoops, with that low chuffing rumble under it, the wolf-laugh coming up through the man, and he lay there with his arms over his face and his boots kicking, and he couldn't stop.

"*Sparkle,*" he wheezed. "You — you *do.* You actually — *Leah* — "

"I *told* you," Leah said.

"You *did.*" He wiped his eyes. "You did tell me. I'm *so* sorry. I'm so sorry, Mr. Cullen. It's — it's very beautiful." He went off again.

Emmett was laughing too, in the middle of his sunbeam, throwing light all over everything. Rosalie looked at Harry flat on the lawn with an expression of total contempt.

"*Wonderful,*" said Rosalie. "Another dog."

"Wolf," said Harry, from the grass, with dignity. "Variant. Unclassified. There's a form."

And the sunbeam, very slowly, began to move. It slid off Emmett, across the lawn, a bright circle on the wet grass, and stopped on Edward Cullen.

Edward stood there in it, glittering from his hair to his collar, with his arms folded and his jaw set, looking down at Harry with an expression of profound and weary suffering.

The sunbeam followed him when he stepped out of it.

He stepped out of it again. It followed him again.

Bella Cullen, née Swan, newborn vampire, a month and change into forever, sat down on the lawn next to Harry Potter and laughed until she'd have cried, if she still could.

"Does he play poker?" Emmett asked Seth, still sparkling, nodding at the man on the grass.

"He's got, like," said Seth, "two hundred billion pounds."

Emmett thought about that.

"Deal him in," said Emmett.

That night Leah sat on the back steps.

Her dad's steps. The rain had stopped. The clouds had broken up over the ocean, and the stars were out over La Push, fewer than in Turnagain and fuzzier, the way stars were on the coast. She could hear the rollers coming in on First Beach a mile off, the long slow sound she'd fallen asleep to her whole life. In the house behind her, Seth was asleep on the couch with his mouth open and his feet hanging off the end, and her mom and Charlie were at the kitchen table, talking low, and she could hear Charlie laugh now and then.

She'd phased twice that afternoon. Run the loop around the rez with Seth and Jacob, the three of them, the way she'd run the loop around the glass house nine hours a day in September. They'd been in her head. All of them. Seth's goofy joy and Jacob's tired, careful happiness and every one of her own six weeks lying open for them to walk through.

It had been loud. It had been fine. She'd come out of it at the end and checked, standing behind the woodpile in her clothes, and found herself still in there, all of her, right where she'd left herself.

The step creaked beside her. That was the only way she knew he'd sat down. He'd let it creak, she realized. On purpose. So she'd know.

He sat with his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging loose, looking out at the dark yard, at the mud puddle by the woodpile. He smelled like earth after rain, and pine, and cold stone, and the struck match, and her mother's chowder.

"Solveig's sent eleven owls," he said. "I've not opened any of them."

"Are you going to?"

"Eventually." He thought about it. "Possibly Thursday."

Leah laughed, quietly, into the dark.

They sat. The ocean came in and went out. Somewhere down the road, somebody's dog barked at nothing and gave up.

After a while, without looking at her, very low, badly off-key, Harry started to hum.

She knew it by the third note. She'd have known it by the first. Her dad on the boat, on the porch, in the truck, in the shower, every single time.

She didn't tell him to stop. She leaned her shoulder against his, which was hot right through the waxed cotton, and closed her eyes, and let him murder Creedence on her father's steps.

"You sing like him," she said, when he was done.

"Terribly?"

"Terribly."

"Thank you," said Harry, who understood.

Out over the water the moon was coming up, almost full, fat and yellow over the black line of the sea stacks off First Beach.

Leah Clearwater sat on her father's porch with her head on a wizard's shoulder and watched it rise, and thought, for the first time in a year and a half, that she was home.
