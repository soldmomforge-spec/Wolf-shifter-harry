## PART ONE — GRAVITY

### I. Thriftway

*Forks, Washington. Three weeks after Renesmee.*

Esme's list was written on the back of a seed catalogue in handwriting so neat it could have been typed, and it was the longest grocery list Seth Clearwater had ever held in his life.

Forty pounds of ground beef. Three dozen eggs, crossed out, *six* dozen written in above it. Butter. Potatoes, *the big bags, two of them*. Bread, milk, cheese, a list of spices Seth couldn't pronounce. Bacon, underlined twice, because in three weeks Esme Cullen had worked out that the fastest way to keep three wolves in a house full of vampires without anybody's hackles going up was to feed them, and the fastest way to feed them was bacon.

At the bottom, smaller: *And something for you, sweetheart. Anything you like.*

He read that line twice in the Thriftway lot with the rain ticking on the roof of his mom's car and felt his mouth pull up at one corner. Nobody had told him the vampires would be nice. The legends left that part out. The legends left out a lot of things, like how Rosalie Hale hummed when she thought nobody was listening, and how Emmett cheated at Xbox, and how a baby who was three weeks old and looked six months could put her hand on your face and show you a picture of yourself laughing.

Seth folded the list into his back pocket and ran for the doors.

Inside, the store smelled like floor wax and bananas and the bakery's last batch of something with cinnamon in it. The fluorescent lights buzzed. Mrs. Kemper from the post office was arguing with the deli guy about sliced turkey. Seth grabbed a cart, then a second cart, and pushed them both ahead of him like a train, which got him a look from a woman with a toddler and a grin from the toddler.

He did meat first. The butcher, a big guy named Dale who'd gone to school with Seth's dad, watched him pile ground beef into the first cart until it sagged.

"Feeding an army, Clearwater?"

"Pretty much."

"Your mom know you're buying out my case?"

"She's got Charlie now," Seth said. "Charlie eats a lot."

Dale laughed, and Seth laughed with him, and all of it was true, as far as it went. It was a story with most of the pages torn out. He was getting good at those. Officially Bella Swan was sick, and officially the Cullens were keeping close to home while she got better, and officially Seth was helping out because Jacob was Bella's best friend and Seth was Jacob's best friend and that's what you did in a town this size. Charlie knew more than officially now. Charlie knew Jacob could turn into a horse-sized wolf in his backyard. Charlie had decided he didn't need to know anything else, and everyone was being very careful to let him keep deciding that.

Eggs. Butter. Milk. Seth worked his way down the list and back up the aisles, and the carts got heavier, and the wheels of the second one started making a noise like a bird with a grudge.

He turned into the baking aisle for the spices and stopped.

There was a woman at the far end, up on the toes of her muddy boots, reaching for something on the top shelf.

That was all. A woman in a rain jacket two sizes too big, the hood pushed back. Dark hair piled up in a knot on the back of her head with a stick shoved through it to hold it, a slim dark stick like a chopstick or a pencil, except it was neither. Her basket was on the floor at her feet. Seth could see what was in it from where he stood, because his eyes had been better than human since the spring and he'd stopped thinking about it: three candles, loose-leaf tea in a tin, a bag of cat food, a bundle of rosemary, a jar of honey.

She wobbled on her toes, swore under her breath, and turned her head to check whether anybody had heard her swear.

She had gray eyes.

And something happened to Seth Clearwater that he had heard about four times, from four different people, and had never once understood.

Sam had tried to describe it, back when Emily was new and Sam still looked at her like he'd been shot. Jared had tried. Quil, about Claire, had gone red and said she was just the most important thing, period, and anybody who made it weird could fight him. Jacob had tried three weeks ago, holding Renesmee on the Cullens' couch with his face gone stupid, and given up halfway through a sentence.

None of them had said it right. Seth wouldn't have been able to say it right either.

Every string that tied him to the planet came loose at once. The floor and the fluorescent lights and the sound of Mrs. Kemper arguing about turkey and the weight of the carts under his hands, all of it went slack, and for half a second he was floating, untethered, nothing holding him to anything. Then the strings came back, every one of them, and tied themselves to the woman with the stick in her hair.

His mom. Leah. Jacob. The pack. The reservation and the ocean and First Beach and the smell of his dad's old truck. All of it was still there. All of it was still his. It was just that every single thing he loved had been rearranged to orbit around one point, and the point was standing in the baking aisle of the Forks Thriftway, scowling at a canister of sea salt she couldn't reach.

Seth let go of the carts.

He didn't decide to. His hands opened, and then he was walking, and then he was standing next to her, and he reached past her and took the salt down off the shelf. He was six-foot-five. He didn't have to stretch.

She came down off her toes and looked at the salt. Then she looked up, a long way up, at him.

"Thanks," she said.

Seth held the salt.

"Um," she said. "Are you going to give me that, or is it yours now?"

"Sorry." He held it out. His hand was shaking. He could see it shaking and couldn't make it stop. "Sorry. Here. Hi."

"Hi." She took the canister. She had a little crease between her eyebrows, a skeptical crease, like she was deciding whether he was drunk. Up close she had freckles across her nose and a fading scrape on her chin and a smell like rain and woodsmoke and something green and sharp underneath, like crushed leaves. "You okay? You look like you got hit by a bus."

"I'm good," Seth said. "I'm great. I'm—Seth."

"Okay, Seth."

"Clearwater. Seth Clearwater. I live down in La Push. On the rez. It's like fifteen minutes. You're new."

The crease got deeper. "Is it that obvious?"

"It's Forks," Seth said. "Everybody's new or everybody's known everybody since kindergarten. I've never seen you. I'd remember."

He heard it come out of his mouth and wanted to put it back. She blinked at him. Then, slowly, one corner of her mouth went up.

"Layla," she said. "Calloway."

*Layla.* He'd never liked a word so much in his life.

"I just moved here," she said. "Like, three days ago. My great-aunt's house. Ida Calloway? She passed in August. Out on Calawah Way, the yellow one with the porch that's trying to fall off."

"I know that house." He did. Everybody did. The yellow house with the wraparound porch and the garden that had gone wild the last few years, because Ida Calloway had been eighty-nine and stubborn and wouldn't let anyone help her with it. Seth's mom used to drop off soup there. "I can fix porches."

The other corner of Layla's mouth went up. "Is that a line?"

"No! No. I mean." Seth could feel his ears getting hot, which was saying something, because the rest of him ran at a hundred and eight degrees all the time. "I can actually fix porches. I helped redo my mom's last summer. And Jacob's dad's ramp. For his wheelchair. I'm good with a hammer, I'm not, like—that wasn't a line."

"Okay."

"It could be a line. If you wanted it to be a line."

She laughed. It came out of her surprised, a short bright sound, and she put her hand over her mouth like she hadn't meant to let it out, and Seth decided right there that he would spend the rest of his life trying to make her do that again.

Behind him, the second cart, the one with the squeaky wheel and forty pounds of ground beef in it, chose that moment to roll gently down the very slight slope of the aisle and plow into the end-cap display of canned pumpkin.

The pyramid went over like a wave. Cans bounced. Cans rolled. One rolled all the way down the aisle and stopped against Layla's boot.

They both looked at it.

"I'll get that," Seth said.

"You've got a lot of meat in that cart, Seth Clearwater."

"It's for a friend."

"A friend who eats forty pounds of hamburger?"

"Three friends. Big appetites." He crouched and started stacking cans, fast, and she crouched with him and started stacking too, even though he said she didn't have to. Their hands bumped reaching for the same can. Hers was cold from outside. She jerked back.

"God, you're *boiling*," she said. "Are you sick?"

"I run hot."

"Running hot is ninety-nine. That's a fever." She looked at him, really looked, the way his mom looked at him when she was trying to decide if he was lying about where he'd been all night. Her gaze went over his face, his shoulders, the size of him. Her hand went up, absently, and touched the dark stick in her hair, the way some people touch a necklace when they're thinking.

"It's normal," Seth said. "For me. It's a family thing. Genetic. I'm fine. Promise."

"Huh," Layla said.

That was all. Just *huh*. But she kept looking a second longer than people usually looked, with her head tipped a little to the side, like she was reading something printed on him in very small letters.

Then the assistant manager showed up, a skinny guy named Brent who looked at the pumpkin cans like they'd personally insulted him, and Seth apologized four times, and Layla apologized twice even though it wasn't her fault, and by the time the display was back up and Brent had stalked off, they were both grinning like idiots.

"I should finish," Layla said. She held up the salt. "I'm on a mission."

"Three canisters of salt is a mission?"

"You'd be surprised what salt's good for."

"Can I—" He didn't know how to finish the sentence. *Can I walk next to you for the rest of my life* seemed like a lot for the baking aisle. "Can I help you carry stuff to your car? It's pouring. And you've got, like, a lot of candles."

She looked at him for a second. He watched her think about it: big stranger, empty parking lot, new town, rain. He watched her decide.

"Okay," she said. "Sure. But I'm getting cinnamon rolls first. The bakery smells too good."

He forgot about Esme's carts. He'd remember later and feel terrible. At the time, the only thing in the store was Layla Calloway walking ahead of him to the bakery case, and the stick in her hair, and the smell of crushed green leaves.

At the register she paid in cash, which nobody under sixty did anymore, and counted it out carefully, like the bills were a little unfamiliar to her. The receipt printer jammed when it got to her transaction. The checker, a girl named Kayleigh who Seth knew from the reservation's summer program, smacked it twice and it started again. Layla watched the printer with an expression Seth couldn't read, and something that might have been guilt.

Her car was a dark green Ford Bronco older than Seth was, with a dented tailgate and Massachusetts plates. He put her bags in the back. The rain came down hard and cold and ran down his neck and he didn't feel it.

"Massachusetts," he said.

"Most recently. Before that, all over. I went to a boarding school out there." She closed the tailgate. "In the mountains. Way out in the middle of nowhere."

"Like a fancy prep school?"

"Like a very, very old one." Something crossed her face, quick, and was gone. "You'd never have heard of it."

"So you're out on Calawah. The yellow house."

"The yellow house."

"Do you have a phone? Like, could I call you? About the porch. I really can fix it."

She looked at him for a long time with the rain dripping off the end of her nose.

"I don't have a cell," she said finally. "Phones and I don't get along. But Aunt Ida had a landline. It's one of those old ones with the dial." She dug in her jacket pocket, came up with a pen, and then looked around for paper and didn't find any. She took his hand instead and turned it over. Her fingers were cold. His palm was hot. She wrote seven digits across it in blue ink, and her handwriting was looping and old-fashioned, like something off a letter.

"Don't get that wet," she said.

"It's raining."

"Then memorize it fast." She let go of his hand. "Thanks, Seth Clearwater. For the salt."

"Anytime. Seriously. Anytime."

She climbed into the Bronco. It took three tries to start. She pulled out of the lot and turned left toward the highway, and Seth stood in the rain and watched her taillights until they went around the bend by the gas station, and then he watched the bend.

He had the number memorized by the time she was gone. He'd have it memorized when he was ninety.

He was halfway to his mom's car when he remembered the two carts full of Esme's groceries still sitting abandoned next to the bakery case.

### II. Pack

He didn't phase on the way back. He wanted to, badly; he wanted to run, wanted to feel the ground go by under four feet and the trees blur, wanted to howl. But the car was full of groceries and the number on his hand was smearing and he had to drive with his hand off the wheel so it wouldn't get worse.

Edward was waiting on the Cullens' front steps when he pulled in. Just standing there in the rain in a gray sweater, hands in his pockets, with a look on his face like he was trying very hard not to smile and losing.

"Congratulations," Edward said.

Seth killed the engine. "You heard."

"From two miles out. You were thinking about her the entire drive." Edward came down the steps and took four bags of groceries out of the back in one hand like they were empty. "You were also thinking about her handwriting. In considerable detail."

"Shut up."

"And the word *Layla*. Repeatedly. With different emphasis."

"Edward. Dude."

Edward's smile won. It changed his whole face. He looked about Seth's age when he did that, and he did it more now than he used to, since Bella and since Renesmee. "I'm happy for you, Seth. Truly. Go inside before Jacob comes out here and drags you."

Jacob didn't drag him. Jacob was on the couch in the big white living room with Renesmee asleep on his chest, and when Seth came through the door Jacob took one look at his face and said, very quietly, so as not to wake her, "No *way*."

"Yeah," Seth said.

"Who?"

"Her name's Layla."

"At the *store*?"

"In the baking aisle."

Jacob stared at him for a long moment. Then he started laughing, silently, his whole chest shaking, and Renesmee rose and fell on it and didn't wake up. "The baking aisle. Man. Sam's going to lose it. Quil's going to lose it."

"Where's Leah?"

Jacob's laugh faded. He jerked his chin at the back of the house, toward the river.

Seth found his sister sitting on a rock on the riverbank with her knees pulled up and her arms around them, in the rain, in a tank top. She'd heard it. Of course she had. She'd been phased, probably, running the perimeter, and Seth had driven back with his whole head full of gray eyes, and he hadn't been phased, but Edward had heard it, and Jacob had seen his face, and the house had ears everywhere. And Leah always knew. Leah had known when Seth broke his arm in fourth grade before anybody told her.

He sat down on the rock next to her. It was cold and wet. Neither of them cared.

"Don't," Leah said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say sorry. Don't."

He didn't. They sat there. The river went by, high and brown with October rain.

Imprinting had taken Sam from her. That was the plain fact of it. Sam had loved Leah, and Leah had loved Sam, and then one day Sam had looked at Emily, Leah's cousin, and every string that tied him to the planet had come loose and re-tied itself somewhere else. Seth had always known that. He'd always felt bad about it, in the vague way you feel bad about something that happened to somebody else. Now he knew what it actually was. He knew exactly what had happened to Sam in that one second. He knew nobody could have stopped it.

And that made it worse, somehow, sitting here next to her.

"What's she like?" Leah said, after a while.

"She swears when she thinks nobody's listening. She pays with cash. She bought three things of salt and wouldn't tell me why." He looked at his hand, at the smeared blue numbers. "She laughed at me. Like, twice."

"Smart girl."

"Leah."

"I'm *happy* for you, Seth." She said it flat and fast, staring at the river. Then she said it again, slower, and the second time it sounded closer to true. "I'm happy for you. Don't screw it up. And if she's a jerk to you I'm biting her."

"She's not a jerk."

"You've known her for twenty minutes."

"Forty," Seth said. "Like, forty-five, if you count the parking lot."

Leah made a noise that was almost a laugh. She leaned over and bumped her shoulder into his, hard enough to rock him, and that was all, and that was enough.

When they came back up to the house, Alice was standing in the kitchen doorway with her arms crossed and a frown on her small face that didn't belong there. Alice didn't frown much. Alice usually already knew how things turned out.

"What's her name?" Alice said.

"Layla," Seth said. "Calloway."

"Where does she live?"

"Out on Calawah. The old yellow house. Why?"

Alice's frown deepened. She tapped one finger against her lips. Jasper had come up behind her, silent the way he always was, and put a hand on her shoulder.

"I can't see her," Alice said.

"Well, yeah," Jacob said from the couch. "Seth's all over her now. You can't see us. Maybe it's catching."

"That's what I thought at first." Alice shook her head. "When you're in a future, Seth, it's a hole. A blank spot. It's like trying to look through a wall. This is different. I saw you go into the store. I saw you in the parking lot. Then there's this—" She held up her hand and rubbed her fingertips together, like she was feeling static. "Fuzz. Over everything. Like a television that's between channels. And it started three days ago. Right over Calawah Way."

Three days ago. When Layla said she'd moved in.

"Could be nothing," Edward said.

"I don't see nothing," Alice said. "I see *something*, and I can't see what it is, and I don't like it."

Esme came in from the kitchen, wiping her hands on a towel, and put both her cold arms around Seth from behind and hugged him. "Ignore them," she said. "Congratulations, sweetheart. You'll bring her for dinner?"

"I don't know if—she doesn't know anything. About any of it."

"Then when she does," Esme said, and squeezed him once, and let him go to start unpacking forty pounds of ground beef into a refrigerator that had never held food before this month.

Emmett, from the stairs, said, "The baking aisle, Seth? Really?" and Rosalie, from somewhere upstairs, said, "Leave him alone, Emmett," which was the closest thing to congratulations Seth had ever gotten from Rosalie, and he'd take it.

Bella was by the big glass wall, looking out at the rain. Her eyes were still red at the edges, burning down toward amber week by week. She caught Seth's eye in the reflection and smiled at him, small and real, and mouthed, *Good for you.*

On the couch, Renesmee woke up. She lifted her head off Jacob's chest and looked straight at Seth with Bella's brown eyes, and reached out one hand toward him, wanting.

He went over and crouched down and let her touch his face. The picture she showed him was of himself, coming through the front door five minutes ago, lit up like a lamp.

### III. Porch

*Week three.*

The porch was worse than he'd thought.

Seth lay on his back in the mud underneath it with a flashlight in his teeth and looked up at the joists. Two of them were rotten clear through. A third had been sistered at some point in the seventies with a two-by-four that was now mostly fungus. The whole thing was holding itself up out of habit.

He spat out the flashlight. "Layla?"

"Yeah?" Her boots appeared at the edge of the porch, upside down from where he was lying.

"How long have you been walking on this?"

"Three and a half weeks. Why?"

"Don't anymore."

"That bad?"

"I'm amazed it hasn't dropped you into the crawlspace."

The boots were quiet for a second. "Huh," Layla said, in a voice he was starting to recognize. It was the *huh* she used when something made sense in a way she wasn't going to explain.

He slid out from under the porch, filthy, and sat up in the long grass. It wasn't raining, for once. The sky over Calawah Way was the flat white of an old dinner plate, and the wild garden around the yellow house dripped from last night. Ida Calloway's garden. Layla had spent most of three weeks out in it in rubber boots and a man's flannel shirt, pulling things up, putting things in, talking to plants under her breath. Some of the things she'd put in, Seth didn't recognize. He'd grown up on the peninsula. He knew salal and Oregon grape and sword fern and devil's club. He did not know the plant by the back steps with the purple leaves that turned, very slowly, to follow her when she walked past.

He'd noticed that. He'd decided not to say anything about it yet.

He'd decided not to say anything about a lot of things.

Like the fact that her kettle boiled the second she set it on the stove, and he'd stood right next to it the first week and seen that the burner wasn't on. Like the fact that it was always the exact right temperature inside the yellow house, though the furnace was older than his mom and he'd heard Layla on the phone with the propane company telling them she didn't need a delivery yet, thanks. Like the fact that she'd never once been surprised when he showed up, even the times he'd run there through the woods instead of driving, even the time he'd come at six in the morning with doughnuts. She'd opened the door before he knocked, in socks, holding two mugs.

Like the cat.

The cat was sitting on the porch rail right now, watching him with yellow eyes. It was enormous and gray and had tufts on the tips of its ears like a lynx and a tail like a lion's, with a puff of fur on the end. Layla called it Moose. Moose did not like Seth. Moose did not like anyone, as far as Seth could tell, except Layla. When Seth came near, Moose's fur went up in a ridge down its spine and it made a sound like a kettle, and the first time it had done that, Layla had said, "Huh," and given Seth a long look over the top of her tea.

"So what do we do?" Layla sat down on the top step, carefully, testing it.

"I'll pull the bad joists and sister new ones in. Replace some of the decking. Couple days, maybe. I'll get Jake to help. Jake's good at this stuff. He rebuilt a whole Rabbit from scratch."

"A rabbit?"

"A car. Volkswagen."

"Oh." She nodded like that made sense. Then she said, "What's he like? Jake."

"He's my best friend. He's—" Seth thought about it. "Loud. Stubborn. He'd die for you if you were his. He'd die for you if you *weren't* his, probably, if he thought it was the right thing. He's kind of in charge of us."

"Us?"

"Me and Leah. And him. We're—" He stopped.

It was right there. He could tell her. He'd been carrying it around for three weeks like a stone in his mouth, and every day it got heavier. Jacob had said he could. *She's yours, man. It's your call. Sam'd say the same.* Sam had told Emily. Jared had told Kim. Paul had told Rachel, Jacob's sister, and Rachel had thrown a lamp at him and then kissed him, according to Paul.

"We look out for each other," Seth said. "Like a team."

"A team," Layla said. She looked at him for a while. The purple plant by the back steps rustled, though there was no wind. "You know you can tell me things, right? Whatever it is."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean you have a face like a billboard, Seth. Every time you start to say something, you swallow it." She pulled her knees up. "And I'm not exactly—I'm not exactly a normal person either. I'm not going to freak out."

"How do you know you're not going to freak out?"

"Because I'm really, really hard to freak out." She said it lightly. Her hand went up to the stick in her hair and rested there. "Try me. Sometime. When you want to."

He looked at her hand on the stick. He'd looked at that stick a lot in three weeks. She wore it every day. She never took it out. Once he'd seen her pull it free and point it absently at the woodstove while she was reading, and the stove had come alive with a soft *whump*, and when he'd looked over she'd jammed the stick back into her hair and said the stove was temperamental, and he'd said okay.

"Sometime," Seth said.

"Okay."

Moose made the kettle noise at him from the porch rail.

"Your cat hates me," Seth said.

"Moose hates everyone." Layla reached out, and Moose, astonishingly, came and butted its huge head against her hand. "Moose is very old and very particular and thinks you smell like a dog."

Seth's heart went very still in his chest.

"Like a dog," he said.

"A big one." She was scratching Moose under the chin, not looking at him. "He doesn't like dogs."

### IV. Five Weeks

He told her on a Saturday, five weeks and one day after the baking aisle.

The porch was done by then. It had taken him and Jacob four days, and Jacob had walked in and shaken Layla's hand and looked at her for a long second with his eyebrows up, and afterward, on the drive back, had said, "She's got a stick in her hair."

"Yeah."

"Like, a stick stick."

"Yeah."

"And her cat looked at me like it wanted to eat me."

"Moose hates everyone."

Jacob had been quiet for a mile. Then he'd said, "There's something with her, Seth."

"I know."

"Like something *something*."

"I *know*."

"Okay." Jacob had drummed his fingers on the wheel. "Okay. Your call, man. Whenever you want. I've got your back."

So: a Saturday. Early November now, and the rain had settled in for the winter, steady and patient and cold. Seth came up the new porch steps at seven in the evening with a bag of takeout from the Chinese place in Port Angeles, because Layla had said she missed Chinese food, and Forks didn't have any.

She opened the door before he knocked. Socks. Flannel. Hair up, stick in it.

"You drove to Port Angeles," she said.

"You said you missed it."

"That's an hour each way."

"Fifty minutes. I drive fast."

She looked at the bag, then at him. She didn't say anything. She just stepped back to let him in, and on his way past she put her hand flat on his chest for a second, over his heart, and then took it away.

They ate on the floor in front of the woodstove, because Ida Calloway's dining table was buried under Layla's books and jars and notebooks. Seth had looked at the books, early on. Most of them were normal: field guides to Pacific Northwest plants, a Sunset Western Garden Book, a fat paperback on mosses. Some of them weren't. Some of them were bound in leather that had gone soft with age and had titles in gold that he could have sworn moved when he wasn't looking straight at them. Layla kept those ones at the bottom of the stack, and the stack in the corner, and when he'd asked about a jar of something that glowed faintly green on the windowsill she'd said, "Fungus. Bioluminescent. It's a local thing," and that was true, there were fungi like that on the peninsula, but this one had been pulsing in time with her heartbeat.

He knew her heartbeat by now. He'd never be able to tell her that without explaining everything else.

Moose sat on the windowsill and watched him eat with naked hatred.

Seth put down his carton of lo mein.

"Layla."

She looked up. Something in his voice did it. She put her own carton down, and wiped her fingers on a napkin, and waited.

He had practiced. He had practiced this in his head running perimeter at three in the morning, and he'd practiced it out loud in the shower, and Leah had heard most of the practicing through the pack mind and told him every version was terrible. In the end, sitting on the floor in front of the woodstove with soy sauce on his fingers, he didn't use any of it.

"I turn into a wolf," Seth said.

Layla looked at him.

"Like—a real one. A big one. Me and Jake and my sister and some of the other guys on the rez. It's a tribe thing. A Quileute thing. It's in the legends, the old stories, the cold ones and the spirit warriors and all that. It's real. All of it. I turn into a wolf. That's why I run hot. That's why I'm—" he gestured at himself, at the size of him, "—this. That's why your cat hates me. I'm not making it up. I know it sounds insane."

Layla didn't say anything.

The fire popped. The rain ticked on the window. Moose's tail twitched.

Then Layla started to laugh.

This laugh was different from the bright surprised one in the baking aisle. It was helpless, and it went on, and she put both hands over her face and bent forward until her forehead was nearly on her knees, and her shoulders shook.

Seth felt his face go hot. "I'm serious."

"I know." She came up, wiping her eyes. "I know you are. Oh my God, Seth. I know. I'm sorry. I swear I'm—" She took a breath. "Okay. Questions. Is it the moon?"

He blinked. "What?"

"Is it the full moon? Do you change on the full moon? Does it hurt every time? Are you aware, when it happens, are you *you*, or is it—" She was leaning toward him now, intent, the laughter gone. "Do you take anything for it? Have you ever bitten anyone?"

"No! No. It's—whenever I want. Mostly. Sometimes if I get really mad it just happens. It doesn't hurt anymore. It's me. I'm me in there. We can hear each other's thoughts when we're all phased, that part's kind of annoying." He stared at her. "Why would I bite anyone?"

"Because that's how—" Layla stopped. She sat back on her heels. She looked at him, and the crease between her eyebrows came in. "Whenever you want."

"Yeah."

"You choose it. And you're *you*."

"Yeah."

"Lycanthropy doesn't work like that," she said, mostly to herself. "Werewolves don't get a choice. They don't get to stay themselves." She stared at him. "Huh."

"Layla. What's lycan—"

"Okay," Layla said. "Okay. My turn."

She reached up and pulled the stick out of her hair.

Her hair came down all at once, dark and heavy, around her shoulders. She held the stick in her right hand the way you'd hold a pen you were about to sign something with. Up close, in the firelight, he could see that it had been carved. There were tiny patterns spiraling up the handle, and the end was darker and smoother, worn by her hand.

She pointed it at the oil lamps on Ida Calloway's mantel, the ones that had never been lit in five weeks because the electric lights worked fine. She flicked her wrist.

All three lamps lit at once.

She pointed it at the window. The rain running down the glass stopped. Seth could see it, the drops hanging there on the outside of the pane, fat and silver and perfectly still, like somebody had hit pause. She flicked again and they fell all at once, a little rattling curtain.

She pointed it at Moose. Moose said something rude in cat and jumped down off the windowsill.

"Fine," Layla told the cat. "Be like that."

She turned back to Seth. She held the stick up between them.

"I'm a witch," she said. "Wand. Cauldron. Went to school for it. Seven years. A castle in the mountains in western Massachusetts called Ilvermorny, which you will never be able to find, because it's hidden, and which I am absolutely not supposed to be telling you about. Neither is any of this." She waved the wand at the room, the jars, the books. The glowing fungus on the windowsill pulsed. "There's a whole world, Seth. There are thousands of us. Hundreds of thousands. There are laws about not telling people. There's a government. I could get in really, really big trouble for this."

Seth stared at her.

"So," Layla said. "That's my thing."

"You're a witch."

"Yeah."

"Like a *witch* witch."

"Like a wand-and-cauldron, went-to-school-for-it witch. And your thing is, you turn into a wolf." She was smiling now, unsteady, her eyes wet. "I knew it. I knew it was something. You walked into that aisle and you were—you were *loud*. Magically loud. My wand was humming in my hair. I thought you were a wizard who didn't know he was a wizard, at first. Then I thought you were some kind of—I don't know what I thought. I've been reading every book I brought with me for five weeks trying to figure out what you are, and there's *nothing*. Do you know how annoying that is? For a Horned Serpent?"

"I don't know what that is."

"It's my house. At school. We're the ones who read." She laughed again, wet. "Show me."

"What?"

"Show me. The wolf. Please. I showed you mine."

He went outside. He went out the back, down the steps past the purple plant, which turned to watch him, and into the trees at the edge of the yard where she couldn't see, and he stripped down and folded his jeans and tied them to his ankle with the leather cord, because he'd been raised right, and he let go.

It went through him the way it always did now: a heat that started in his spine and rolled outward, and then the world got sharper and louder and smelled like a thousand things at once, and he was on four feet, and he was big.

*Seth?* That was Leah, faint and far off. She was running the north line. *Seth, are you—oh, for God's sake. You did it. You told her.*

*She's a witch,* Seth thought at her. *Leah. She's a witch. Like a real one. With a wand.*

A long silence in the pack mind.

*Sure she is,* Leah thought. *I'm getting Jacob.*

He walked out of the trees.

Layla was standing on the back porch with the oil lamps behind her, and she had stopped the rain again. Not everywhere. Just over the porch, and over the steps, and over the path down through the garden: the rain fell all around her and just stopped about six feet over her head, like it had hit a glass ceiling, and ran off down invisible walls. She was holding her wand up and out to the side without seeming to think about it.

She saw him and her mouth fell open.

He was sandy-colored and enormous. He knew what he looked like; he'd seen himself in the Cullens' big glass wall. He was the size of a horse. His head was level with the porch roof.

Layla came down the steps. She walked out under her little umbrella of nothing, through the dripping garden, right up to him, until she was standing between his front feet looking up. Her face was white. Her hand with the wand in it was shaking.

She put her other hand up and laid it flat on his muzzle.

"Oh," Layla said softly. "Oh, you're *beautiful*."

Seth's tail did something he had no control over at all.

Afterward he phased back behind the woodshed and came out in his jeans and nothing else, and she was waiting at the bottom of the steps with his shirt, which he'd left inside, and a look on her face that was half laughing and half something else, and he took the shirt and didn't put it on.

"So," she said.

"So," he said.

"Are you going to tell me the rest of it? Why you look at me like that?" She tipped her head. "Because you do. You've looked at me like that since the salt. Like I'm—I don't know. Like I'm the North Star."

He told her. Imprinting, the legends, the strings. What had happened in the baking aisle. That it didn't mean she had to do anything. That he'd be whatever she wanted, a friend, a guy who fixed her porch, a wolf who lived in the woods behind her house, anything. That she got to choose. That it was only ever her choice.

She listened to all of it standing in the dark garden under the stopped rain.

When he was done she said, "Huh."

Then she stepped forward, and put her cold hand on the back of his neck, and pulled him down, a long way down, and kissed him.

The rain came down on both of them. She'd dropped the charm. Neither of them noticed for a good while.

Later, on the floor in front of the woodstove with the cold lo mein, wrapped up together in a quilt that smelled like Ida Calloway's cedar chest, she said, "There's something I have to tell you. About the law."

"The witch law."

"The Statute of Secrecy. It's international. Every magical government in the world signed it. You don't tell non-magical people. Ever. If MACUSA finds out I told you—"

"Mac-what?"

"Magical Congress of the United States of America. The government. Our government. They'd send people. Obliviators. They'd wipe your memory. Yours, and anyone you told. And they'd probably snap my wand." She said it lightly but her fingers had tightened on the quilt. "Though, honestly? I'm not sure it counts. You're not exactly non-magical. You're—something. There's a whole category for beings and creatures and I don't know where you'd go. Maybe it's a gray area. I really, really hope it's a gray area."

Seth was quiet for a second.

"Layla," he said. "There's something else."

"More?"

"Some of my best friends are vampires."

She turned her head on his shoulder and looked up at him.

"Your friends are *what*?"

### V. The Cullens

*The next day.*

Layla Calloway had sat through four years of Defense Against the Dark Arts at Ilvermorny under Professor Abernathy Quaid, who had lost two fingers to a Wampus cat and had opinions about everything, and she had read the vampire chapter of *Beings, Beasts, and Spirits of the Americas* three times for her O.W.L.s, and she knew exactly what she was going to find when Seth drove her up the long gravel drive through the trees.

She was wrong about all of it.

The house was the first thing. She'd expected something old, dark, crumbling, ivy, a crypt for all she knew. It was glass. Three stories of white wood and glass in a clearing by the river, with the whole back wall one huge window, lit up and gorgeous in the gray afternoon like something out of a magazine. There were flower beds. There was a *porch swing*.

"They have a porch swing," Layla said.

"Esme likes it."

"The vampire likes the porch swing."

"Esme's really nice," Seth said, for the fourth time since they'd gotten in the car. His hand was on her knee. It was always hot; it was like having a hot water bottle on her leg. "They're all really nice. Mostly. Rosalie's kind of—just don't look at her too long. And Jasper's a little weird about people. And Bella's new, so she's going to hold her breath, don't take it personally, you just smell really good. To her. Not in a bad way. Well. In a—it's fine. Edward's going to read your mind, I should say that now. He can't help it. He's cool about it, mostly."

"Edward reads minds."

"Yeah."

"And Alice sees the future."

"Yeah. Kind of. Not us, though. Not wolves. And she said she can't see you right, either. She said you were fuzzy."

Layla thought about her wand, in her hair. About what a Seer would see, if she tried to look at a future full of witches. *Fuzzy* sounded about right. Divination had been a fight between the future and every spell anyone ever cast in it.

"And Jasper does feelings," she said.

"He can tell what you're feeling. And kind of change it. If he wants to."

"Great," Layla said. "That's great. No, that's fine. That's totally fine."

The front door opened before they reached it, and a woman came out onto the porch, and Layla's right hand went to her hair without her permission.

Esme Cullen was beautiful in a way that made Layla's skin crawl, and the crawl came from somewhere older than anything Esme had done: the part of Layla's brain that had been a prey animal a long time before it had been a witch. Esme had caramel-colored hair and a heart-shaped face and a smile so kind it hurt to look at, and skin like marble, actual marble, smooth and pale with the faintest blue shadow under it. Her eyes were gold, like honey held up to a lamp.

*Gold,* Layla thought. *Seth said gold. Animal blood. They hunt deer.* Not in any book she'd ever read.

"You must be Layla," Esme said, and her voice was like a bell. She didn't come down the stairs. She stayed where she was, hands clasped in front of her, still in a way living things weren't still. "Welcome. We're so happy to meet you. Seth has told us—well. Seth has told us a great deal."

"Hi," Layla said. "Thanks. You have a—nice porch swing."

---

Inside, there were too many of them.

They had arranged themselves, Layla realized later, on purpose. Spread out around the huge white living room so that nobody was standing between her and the door, and nobody was close. She appreciated that later. At the time, all she could see was the stillness. Seven people in a room, and none of them breathing, and none of them shifting their weight, and none of them blinking unless they remembered to.

Plus three wolves, in human shape. The one who must have been Leah, by the window: tall and lean and furious-looking, with short black hair and arms crossed. Jacob, who she'd met on the porch, on the couch. And Seth, beside her, hot as a stove.

And a baby.

She hadn't been ready for the baby. Seth had told her about the baby, *sort of*, in the car, in a rush, *she's half and she's two months old but she looks older and she's really smart and don't freak out*, and Layla had nodded along and thought she'd understood. She hadn't. The child was in the arms of a young woman by the big window, a woman with long brown hair and red eyes, red going amber at the edges like coals cooling, who was very visibly and very carefully not breathing. The child looked about eight months old. She had bronze curls and brown eyes and pink cheeks. She was looking at Layla with absolute attention.

"Everyone," Seth said, "this is Layla."

The bronze-haired young man by the piano made a noise.

It was a small noise. It was the kind of noise a person makes when they put their hand flat on a stove they think is cold. He had taken a step back, and one hand was up near his temple, and his gold eyes had gone very wide.

"Edward?" said the blond man in the center of the room. He was the oldest-looking of them, maybe thirty, handsome in a pale kind way, with a doctor's calm hands. Carlisle, Layla thought. The father. Seth had said: *Carlisle's like three hundred years old and he's a doctor at the hospital and he's the best person I know.*

"There's a government," Edward said.

Everyone looked at him.

"There's an entire—" He was staring at Layla. Staring *through* her face. "Schools. There are schools. A school in a mountain, and one in Scotland, and one in—France? There's a Congress. There are laws. There's a *bank* run by—" He stopped. "Goblins. She's thinking about goblins. There's a newspaper in New York with pictures that *move*."

"Edward," Carlisle said again, gently.

"I'm sorry." Edward lowered his hand. He looked shaken. Layla had the strange sense that he didn't look shaken very often. "I'm sorry, Layla. I'm not—I don't mean to pry. You're very loud. You're terribly nervous and you're thinking about everything at once. It's a great deal of everything."

"Yeah," Layla said. Her voice came out thin. "I bet it is."

She'd known, intellectually. Seth had told her in the car. But knowing there was a man who could read minds and *standing in a room* while he flipped through yours like a library book were two different things. She thought, very deliberately, *Get out of my head,* and Edward winced and said, "I'm sorry. I can't. I'll try not to comment."

Someone on the stairs laughed, a big booming laugh. That was Emmett, Layla guessed: huge, curly-haired, dimpled, the size of a refrigerator. "Okay, so she's a witch. Like for real? Like *Sabrina*?"

"Like *Sabrina*," Layla said, "if Sabrina went to a boarding school in a mountain and had to pass seven years of exams and could get her wand snapped in half for telling you about it."

Emmett grinned. "Do a trick."

"Emmett," said the blonde woman on the stairs beside him, without looking at him.

That was Rosalie. Seth had been right; it was hard not to look at her too long. She was the most beautiful person Layla had ever seen, and she was looking at Layla the way Moose looked at Seth.

"Can she hurt the baby?" Rosalie said.

It got very quiet.

"Rose," Esme said.

"It's a fair question." Rosalie's gold eyes didn't move off Layla's face. "She's got a weapon in her hair. We don't know what it does. We don't know what *she* does. Seth's—" a glance at Seth, not unkind, "—Seth would think she was fine if she walked in here with a flamethrower. I'm asking."

Layla looked at the baby. The baby looked back.

"Yes," Layla said.

The air in the room changed. Every one of the seven still bodies got, impossibly, stiller.

"Yes, I could," Layla said. "I could hurt anyone in here. That's what a wand is. It's a tool. You could hurt her too. You're all about a thousand times stronger than me. I'm not going to hurt anybody. And honestly? If I tried, I'd probably be dead before I finished the incantation. Seth told me how fast you are."

She felt something then, a gentle pressure, like a hand on the back of her mind. Calm spread through her chest, smooth and cool. She looked over and found a tall, lean man with honey-blond hair standing very near the corner, watching her with his head tilted. His face and hands were covered in scars, fine crescent-shaped scars, dozens of them, faintly silver against the marble.

*Bite marks,* Layla thought. *Those are bite marks.*

"Stop that," she said to him.

The calm went away. Jasper Hale's eyebrows went up a quarter inch.

"You felt that," he said. He had a soft voice, a Southern accent worn down by time.

"Yeah. It was a Cheering Charm with the cheer taken out. I know what it feels like when someone's in my head." She took a breath. "Please don't. I know you're trying to help. But I'd rather be scared on my own."

Jasper looked at her for a long second. Then he inclined his head, a little old-fashioned bow, and she had the sense that she'd passed something.

"*You,*" said a voice right next to her, and Layla jumped about a foot.

A tiny woman with spiky black hair had appeared at her elbow. Layla hadn't seen her cross the room. Nobody could have crossed the room that fast.

"You're the static," the tiny woman said. Alice. Had to be. "Five weeks I've been looking at the north end of Calawah Way, and it's been like trying to watch television through a snowstorm, and it's *you.*"

"Probably," Layla said. "Sorry. There's a lot of magic in that house. Aunt Ida had wards up. And I've added a few. And there's me, and the wand, and the cat, and some of the plants. Seers have trouble with magic. It's like—every spell you cast is a decision the universe doesn't know about yet. It messes with the signal."

Alice stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, she grinned. "That's the best explanation anybody's ever given me for anything."

"It's from a textbook."

"I want the textbook."

"It's in Latin."

"I *read* Latin."

Carlisle Cullen came forward then, slowly and deliberately, the way you'd approach a deer. He stopped a respectful six feet away and held out his hand.

"Carlisle," he said. "Thank you for coming. I know this can't be easy. Please—will you sit? I promise you we're all quite civilized. And I have to confess I've been alive for three and a half centuries and I have never once, in all that time, come across anything quite like you." He hesitated. "I may have questions."

Layla looked at the hand. She thought about Professor Quaid, two fingers short. She thought about the vampire chapter.

She shook it. It was cold and hard as a river stone.

"Doctor Cullen," she said, "I have a *lot* of questions too."

---

It took three hours.

Afterward, Layla would remember it in pieces. Carlisle sitting forward on the white couch with his elbows on his knees, asking about the Statute, about MACUSA, about the International Confederation of Wizards, about the history of it all, how far back, how organized, who enforced it. Esme bringing her tea in a beautiful cup, tea that none of the rest of them could drink, and sitting quietly by while Layla explained what Obliviators did and watching Layla's face like a mother. Emmett asking her to levitate him, and Layla pointing her wand and saying *Wingardium Leviosa* with her very best swish and flick, and Emmett rising exactly one inch off the floor and hanging there, while sweat broke out on her forehead.

"You weigh a *ton*," Layla gasped.

"Thank you," said Emmett, delighted, an inch in the air.

She'd put him down. She'd turned Rosalie's offered hairbrush into a tiny gray mouse, and back, and Rosalie had watched the whole thing without blinking and then said, coolly, "Again," and Layla had done it again, and something in Rosalie's face had changed by a degree.

She remembered Jacob, hunched forward on the couch, protective as a dog with a bone. "Your government. MACUSA. What would they do? If they found out about us. Any of us."

"I don't know." It was the truth. "There's nothing in the books about anything like you. Not the wolves, not the—" she looked around the room, "—not the golden-eyed kind. The vampires we know about are different. They're—I mean, they're *dangerous*, they drink blood, but they're slower. They sleep. They're closer to human. They don't—" she waved at Esme, at the marble skin, "—sparkle, Seth said?"

"We don't call it that," Edward said, pained.

"They'd want to know about you," Layla said. "That's what I'd guess. Somebody would want to know. Study you, maybe. Figure out what you are. And they'd want to Obliviate everybody who knew about *them*. That's the law. Every Muggle in this room."

"We aren't Muggles," Rosalie said.

"That's the gray area," Layla said. "That's what I'm hoping."

She remembered the baby.

Bella had kept her distance for most of it, by the window, not breathing. But somewhere in the second hour Renesmee had started to wriggle and reach, and Bella had looked at Edward, and Edward had looked at Layla's mind and nodded once, and Bella had come over and crouched down a few feet away. Up close, Bella's red-gold eyes were wide and careful, and Layla could see the effort it was costing her, the way her throat stayed locked.

"She wants to say hi," Bella said, without breathing, using the last of the air in her lungs. "She—shows people things. When she touches them. It's okay. It doesn't hurt."

Renesmee reached out one small hand and put it on Layla's cheek.

Layla saw herself. Saw herself from the outside, through the child's eyes, a few minutes ago, with her wand raised and Emmett floating an inch off the white carpet. In the picture, Layla's face was scrunched and fierce and pink, and there were little gold sparks coming off the end of her wand that Layla hadn't even known were there. The picture had a feeling attached to it, the way a smell has a memory attached, and the feeling was pure delight.

"Oh," Layla said. Her eyes stung. "Hi. Hi, sweetheart."

Renesmee smiled. She had perfect tiny teeth.

And she remembered, near the end, Carlisle asking about the wars. Whether the wizarding world had them. Whether the Statute had ever come close to breaking.

"Lots of times," Layla said. "The big one was Grindelwald. In the forties. That was mostly in Europe, but it got into America too. Then in Britain, more recently, there was—" She hesitated. Old habits. Even in America, nobody liked saying it. "There was a dark wizard. Voldemort. He had a war in the seventies and then he came back in the nineties and had another one. He was—he was really bad. He wanted to get rid of the Statute and rule over Muggles. Kill everybody who disagreed. He almost did it, too. He took over their whole Ministry in '97."

"What happened?" Esme asked.

"A kid killed him," Layla said. "A seventeen-year-old. In May of '98, at the end of a huge battle at their school. Hogwarts. In Scotland. It was in every paper in the world. Harry Potter."

"He was famous before that, too. He was the Boy Who Lived. Voldemort tried to kill him when he was a baby, and it bounced. Nobody knows why. Every kid in America had his picture up on their wall in '98. I was eleven. I had him on my trunk lid at Ilvermorny."

She could see the photograph as she said it. Everybody her age could. The *New York Ghost*, May 3rd, 1998, the front page: a skinny teenager in torn, filthy clothes standing in the broken courtyard of a castle with smoke behind him, his glasses cracked, looking straight at the camera with eyes so green they came through the black-and-white somehow. Unsmiling. Exhausted, and fierce, and alive.

By the piano, Edward Cullen went very still.

"Who is he now?" Edward said. "What does he do?"

Layla shrugged. "Nobody knows. That's the weird thing. He was everywhere for a couple of years after, and then he just—stopped being anywhere. No interviews. No pictures. People say all kinds of stuff. He went to Tibet. He's dead. He's teaching. My roommate swore he works for their Ministry, in some department in the basement that nobody's allowed to talk about." She smiled a little. "Wherever he is, it's a long way from Forks."

Edward didn't smile back. He was looking at the space just in front of Layla's face, where the photograph was, as if he were memorizing it.

---

It was full dark when they left. On the front porch, under the light, with the rain coming down beyond the eaves and Seth's hot hand in hers, Layla stopped and dug in the pocket of her jacket.

"Here," she said. "I made you something. Last night. After you went home."

It was a bead. Just a single wooden bead the size of a marble, pale and smooth and carved with a spiral of tiny marks, strung on a thin braided leather cord. She'd sat up until three in the morning on it, with Aunt Ida's old rowan-wood offcuts and her best carving knife and Moose watching her suspiciously from the bookshelf, and she'd done the charm work four times before she'd gotten it right.

"It's rowan," she said. "Rowan's protective. And it's linked to one I'm wearing." She pulled the collar of her sweater aside to show him the twin of it on a cord around her neck. "If you're ever in trouble—any kind of trouble, I don't care what—bite it. Hard. You'll feel it crack. It'll pull on mine, and I'll feel it, and I'll know where you are."

"And then what?"

"And then I come." She tied it around his left ankle herself, crouching on the porch, next to the cord he used for his clothes, and tugged the knot tight. "It's called Apparition. It's like teleporting. I go *crack* and I'm there. It's rude and it gives some people nausea and I'm really, really good at it."

"Will it work if I'm a wolf?"

"It'll work if you're a wolf. It'll work if you're a wolf and I'm in the shower. It'll work if you're on the moon." She stood up. "Promise me you'll use it."

"Layla—"

"Your best friends are vampires, Seth. Your hobby is chasing other vampires through the woods. Leah told me. Promise."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he bent down, a long way down, and kissed her on the porch under the light while Emmett, somewhere inside, wolf-whistled, and Rosalie hit him.

"I promise," Seth said.

## PART TWO — JURISDICTION

### VI. Specials Board

*Two weeks later.*

Charlie Swan noticed the motorcycle before he noticed the man.

It was parked out front of the diner in the space Charlie usually took for the cruiser, which was annoying, but it was also the nicest thing he'd seen on two wheels in a long time, which made it hard to stay annoyed. Black, old, British-looking, heavy chrome gone soft with polishing. A lot of the parts looked custom. The leather seat was cracked the way good leather cracks. There was no plate holder on the back that Charlie could see, which was a ticket right there, until he walked around to the front and saw the plate was mounted low, under the headlamp, a Washington plate, current tags.

He looked at the bike for a little longer than he needed to. Then he went inside and shook the rain off his jacket and saw the man.

He was sitting in the back booth. Charlie's booth, technically, though nobody had ever told him that. Big guy. Six-two at least, broad through the shoulders, mid-twenties maybe. Black hair that hadn't seen a comb this week. Worn brown leather jacket over a gray Henley and a flannel shirt open over that. Jeans. Boots with real wear on them. He had a burger in front of him, and the remains of another burger, and a plate of fries, and he was reading a paperback with the cover folded back so you couldn't see the title.

He was sitting with his back to the wall, facing the door.

That was the first thing Charlie's brain flagged, the way it flagged a car with a broken taillight. Most people sat wherever. Cops sat facing the door. So did soldiers, some of them, the ones who'd been somewhere bad. And people with reasons.

The man looked up when the bell over the door rang. He looked at Charlie, and Charlie felt himself get looked at: face, jacket, the shape of the holster under the jacket, hands, back to the face. A half second. Then the man nodded, polite, and went back to his book.

Green eyes. Bright green. The kind you noticed.

Charlie took the stool at the counter.

"Chief," said Marla, coming down the counter with the coffee pot before he'd asked. Marla had been working the diner since before Bella was born. "Usual?"

"Yeah. Thanks, Marla." He tipped his head about a quarter inch toward the back booth. "Who's that?"

Marla's face did something complicated and amused. "That," she said, lowering her voice, "is Harry. He's English. He's been in here every day for two weeks. He tips like he's in a movie and he's eaten every pie I've got. Twice."

"Every pie."

"The pumpkin, the apple, the cherry, the marionberry, the pecan, the chocolate cream." Marla ticked them off on her fingers. "He said the pecan was 'a religious experience.' He asked me if we ever did key lime. I said in the summer. He said that was a reason to live."

Charlie grunted. "What's he doing in Forks?"

"Ask him yourself," said Marla, and raised her voice. "Harry, hon. This is the chief. Chief Swan. He wants to know what you're doing in Forks."

Charlie closed his eyes briefly.

When he opened them, the man had put a bookmark in the paperback and was sliding out of the booth. He came down to the counter with his coffee cup in one hand and stuck the other one out.

"Chief Swan. Harry Evans." The accent was English, all right, rougher around the edges than the movie kind, flatter, like somebody from a small town. "Sorry. I've been meaning to stop by the station and say hello, actually. Seemed polite, being the new bloke. Kept getting distracted by Marla's pastry case."

Charlie shook. The hand was hard and dry and very hot, like he'd been holding a mug for an hour. Good grip. Didn't try to crush, didn't go limp.

"New bloke," Charlie said.

"Contractor. Fish and Wildlife Service." Harry Evans slid onto the stool next to him, uninvited but not pushy about it. "Spotted owl survey. Northern spotted owls, *Strix occidentalis caurina*, endangered, terribly shy, live in old-growth and won't come out for anybody. They've got me tramping the national forest for three months counting nests and listening to things hoot at three in the morning." He smiled. "Glamorous."

"Fish and Wildlife hires a lot of Brits?"

"Fish and Wildlife hires whoever'll go stand in a rainforest in November for contractor rates." He shrugged. "I did a lot of this kind of thing back home. Scotland, mostly. Highlands. Wet, cold, miles from a decent curry. This is like that, only the trees are bigger and the pie's better."

Charlie sipped his coffee and looked at him. The story was good. It was boring in all the right places. It would check out, too, probably; Charlie could make a call to the field office in Lacey, and somebody would say *oh, yeah, Evans, he's on the owl contract*, because a guy who sat facing the door in a diner was a guy who'd make sure his story checked out.

He looked like a bird guy the way Charlie looked like a ballet dancer.

"Where are you staying?" Charlie said.

"Camping. Got a spot out in the national forest. Permit and everything." He lifted his coffee cup in a little salute. "Don't worry, Chief. I'm keeping my fires small and my food in a bear canister."

"There are bears."

"So I hear." The green eyes stayed on Charlie's face. "I heard you lost a couple of hikers out on the Hoh, back in September. Before I got here. Bear, the papers said."

Charlie set his coffee down.

It had been a bad one. A couple from Portland, early thirties, on the Hoh River Trail out past the Five Mile Island camp. The rangers found what was left on the fourth day. Charlie had driven out with the coroner because it was technically a county matter until the feds took it. He'd seen it. He didn't like thinking about it. The park service had shot a black bear a week later, a big boar, and called it closed, and the boar's stomach had been empty, and nobody had said anything about that, because what were you going to say.

There had been some talk, last year, before the Cullens' kid and before Bella got sick, about a lot of hikers going missing. Animal attacks. A pack of something. Charlie had stopped asking about that kind of thing lately. Asking had started to seem like a bad idea, in a vague way he didn't look at too closely. Need to know.

"Yeah," Charlie said. "Bad business."

"Was it a bear?"

Charlie looked at him. Harry Evans looked back, steady and mild.

"That's what the park service said," Charlie said.

"Right." Harry Evans nodded, slowly, as if Charlie had answered a different question. "Right. Well. I'll keep an eye out. I'm in the woods most of the day anyway. Anything big and toothy, you'll be the first to know."

Marla came back with Charlie's eggs. Harry Evans looked at them, then at Marla, then at the pastry case behind her.

"Marla," he said. "Light of my life. Is there any of that pecan left?"

"Half a pie, hon."

"I'll take a slice. No. Two slices. It's raining. Fat's insulating." He stood up and dropped a twenty and a ten on the counter for a bill that couldn't have been more than eighteen dollars. "Chief. Pleasure. I'll stop by the station this week, make it official. Put a face to the name."

"You do that," Charlie said.

The guy went back to his booth. Two minutes later Marla brought him two slices of pecan pie on two plates, and he ate both, steadily, without hurrying, reading his paperback, and every time the bell over the door rang his eyes went up and checked who it was and went back down.

Charlie finished his eggs. On the way out he stopped by the motorcycle again and looked at it in the rain.

There was a small decal on the back of the fuel tank, he noticed now, so faded he'd missed it before. It was a cartoon of a big black dog. Shaggy. Grinning.

He went back to the cruiser and sat behind the wheel without starting it. Through the diner window he could see the back booth. Harry Evans had turned a page.

Charlie thought about calling Lacey. He thought about the Hoh River Trail, and the boar with the empty stomach, and Bella's eyes the last time he'd seen her, that strange color she said was contacts, and the wolf that had been Jacob Black, in his backyard, eight weeks ago.

He started the car and didn't call anybody.

### VII. Baby

The bike's name was Baby, and Harry Potter had rebuilt it from a pile of scrap in a shed in Ottery St. Catchpole over the course of two winters, and it was the last thing of Sirius's he had left that he could touch.

It could fly. It could do a number of things the Department didn't know about and Arthur Weasley had only suspected. On the logging roads east of Forks in the rain, at dusk, Harry kept it on the ground and kept it legal and listened to Led Zeppelin through a charmed earpiece that had no business picking up a radio signal this far into the hills, and he thought about pie.

He also thought about Chief Swan.

Swan was a good copper. He'd made Harry as wrong inside a minute. He'd looked at Harry's hands and seen what they'd done for a living, and he'd looked at the bike and wondered why there was no dust on the cylinders, and he'd heard the question about the Hoh hikers and understood exactly what was being asked. And then he'd shut down. Harry had watched it happen across his face the way you'd watch a man close a door in a long corridor and lock it and walk away.

*Need to know.* He'd caught that much off the surface of Swan's mind, without even trying. The phrase had been sitting right on top, worn smooth, like a stone a man carries in his pocket and turns over.

Harry respected that. He'd put Swan down in his notes as *aware of something, choosing not to know*, which was the safest place a Muggle could be, and the saddest.

He turned off the forest road onto a track that didn't exist on any map and rode up into the trees for another mile. The track ran out at a stand of western red cedar, eight hundred years old, the trunks wide as cottages and wet black in the rain. The air in the stand was very still and very quiet. It smelled of cedar and moss and cold stone. A Muggle walking in here would have felt the hair on the back of their neck go up and remembered an urgent errand somewhere else.

Harry killed the engine and walked Baby the last fifty yards between the trunks, and the air parted for him like a curtain.

The tent was pitched in a small clearing at the center of the stand. From the outside it was an old canvas wall tent, army surplus, khaki gone gray with weather, with a stovepipe chimney and a patched fly. A Muggle who got this far, which no Muggle would, would have seen a hunter's camp. A fairly sad one.

He pushed Baby in through the flap, which was not wide enough for a motorcycle and which made no complaint about it.

Inside, it was about the size of a rugby pitch.

The first room was a garage, because Harry had wanted a garage. Concrete floor, a workbench, a rolling toolchest that had been his since he was nineteen, a lift. There was a radio on the bench, a 1960s Bakelite set that had nothing plugged into it and played Bad Company the second he walked in, because he'd charmed it to know his footsteps. He parked Baby on her stand and patted her tank, on the faded decal of the big black dog, like he always did.

"Good girl," Harry said.

Beyond the garage was the kitchen, and beyond the kitchen was the long room he thought of as the den, with a woodstove at one end and bookshelves floor to ceiling down both sides and a big scarred table in the middle, and beyond that a hallway that went back farther than it should have and had a number of doors in it.

He put two pies from Marla's in the kitchen. Then he took off the leather jacket and the flannel and hung them on the hook, and stood in the den in his Henley and looked at the map.

The map covered most of the table. It was the Olympic Peninsula, hand-drawn, eight feet long, inked on a single sheet of something that wasn't paper and wasn't parchment and that he'd spent three months of evenings in 2004 working out how to make. The coastline ran down the left edge, Cape Flattery at the top, Grays Harbor at the bottom. The Strait of Juan de Fuca ran along the top. Hood Canal ran down the right side. In the middle, the mountains rose in ink that had been charmed to show actual elevation, so that you could put your hand flat on Mount Olympus and feel it push up into your palm.

Scattered across the whole thing were eleven small points of light.

They were silver, mostly. Soft silver, steady, like stars on a clear night. Cape Flattery. Lake Ozette. Rialto Beach. The Hoh. Queets. Lake Quinault. Staircase. Hurricane Ridge. Lake Crescent. The Elwha. Sekiu. Eleven anchors. Eleven stones, each one about the size of a man's fist, each one buried three feet down in a place he'd chosen with a great deal of care and a dowsing rod, each one carved by hand with a sequence of runes that he had invented over the course of about eighteen months between 2003 and 2005 and had never written down in full anywhere except in his own head.

Strung between the stones, faintly visible if you looked at the map from the side, was a lattice. Thin lines of silver light crossing and recrossing the whole peninsula, like a net. Like the threads of a spider's web, if the spider had been a very patient, very bloody-minded bloke from Surrey.

He'd named it the Snare, because he was not a poet.

The edge of it rang a bell. That was all the edge did. Anything cold and heartless that came in off the sea or over the mountains set the bell going, and then the lattice went to work on it, quietly, reading it, the way you'd study a game trail for a few days before you set a wire. It took the better part of an hour to read something new and lay a loop around it. Once the loop was laid, the first time the thing inside it moved faster than a human could run, the loop pulled tight. A rope snare on a rabbit run worked the same way. The rabbit did all the work.

He'd built it like that on purpose. A vampire walking was a vampire he could watch, and learn from, and make a decision about. A vampire running had already made a decision about something.

There were three small gold lights moving slowly across the map, northeast of Forks, near a bend in the Calawah River. There were five more, steady, clustered in a single spot nearby, and one more beside them, smaller, pulsing in a way that he'd spent a long night working out was a heartbeat. Gold meant keyed. Gold meant known and excluded. The Snare saw them, logged them, and let them be.

The Cullens. Seven of them, plus Bella Cullen, née Swan, the Chief's daughter, plus the child.

He looked at the child's light for a while. Then he went to the kitchen and cut himself a slice of pie, and ate it standing up, and went down the hallway to make his rounds.

---

The first door on the left was the armory.

It was a long, narrow room lined with pegboard and steel, and it smelled of gun oil and cold iron and the sharp clean smell of fresh runework. There were long guns on the right-hand wall and short ones on the left and cabinets underneath. Muggle-made, every one of them. Harry had learned a long time ago that wizards dismissed what they didn't understand, and that what wizards didn't understand was mostly guns, and that a man who could stand behind a shield charm and duel for twenty minutes against curses he'd seen coming would go down like a sack of flour to something moving at nine hundred metres per second that he hadn't. He'd also learned, slower, in places he didn't think about much, that a lot of things in the world didn't care about bullets at all, and that a bullet could be taught to care about a great many things.

The runes had taken years. Getting enchantment to bind to a casing that was about to be thrown out of a breech at speed was its own entire branch of magic that nobody had worked out before, because nobody who knew anything about enchantment had bothered to try. He'd spent eight months on that alone, in the basement of the Department, burning through Muggle ammunition by the crateload and blowing up a series of test ranges. Croaker had signed off on the expense reports without reading them. That was the arrangement.

He walked the length of the room, checking. A pump-action twelve-gauge with a short barrel and the whole receiver etched with a fine silver lattice that glowed faintly when he touched it. A long bolt-action rifle with a scope he'd charmed to see heat and lies. A pair of pistols in a case. A carbine. Shells in racks, sorted by the color of the sigils stamped into their brass: blue for frost, red for fire, white for the ones that burst into a full-body bind on contact, black for the ones he didn't use unless he had to.

On the end wall, on its own, in a glass case, a dagger made from a single curved white fang a foot long.

He stopped at that one, like he always did. Looked at it. Didn't open the case. Basilisk fang. He'd gone back down into the Chamber in the summer of 1999 with a crowbar and a bag and a lot of feelings he hadn't been ready for, and come out with eleven of them. He'd given four to the Department. He'd used two. The rest were here, and in Gringotts, and one was in Hermione's cellar, because she had asked very nicely and also slightly threatened him.

Basilisk venom destroyed anything, more or less. He had a line in a notebook about the Cullens' kind that said *basilisk?* with a question mark after it, and he did not intend to cross it off until he'd tested it.

He went on to the end of the hall.

---

The last door on the right was the specimen room, and it was cold.

He kept it at four degrees Celsius. It was long and white and lit from the ceiling, very clean, and it had the slightly sour chemical smell that every lab in the world had, magic or not. There were steel benches down the middle with instruments on them, some Muggle, some not. There were shelves floor to ceiling down both walls, with jars on them, and the jars had labels in his handwriting, and some of the things in the jars were moving.

An Inferius hand, wrist-severed, in a glass box with a silver lid, flexing its gray fingers slowly open and shut. *Inferius, adult male, raised c.1995, Lestrange vault, Gringotts. Recovered 2001.* A swatch of something black and silky and folded in on itself, in a box marked with seven seals and a warning in three languages: *Lethifold. Partial. DO NOT OPEN.* A frosted jar that wasn't frosted with cold. *Dementor (residue), Azkaban, 2003.* A row of acromantula eyes, each one as big as a fist. A tooth, long and yellow, in a vial. *Vampire (Sanguini type), Romania, 2002. Comparison.*

He'd been building this room for six years. Before that he'd been building himself.

He thought about it sometimes, late, in rooms like this one. The war ended when he was seventeen. Everyone had expected him to be an Auror. Kingsley had more or less handed him the badge. He'd done four months of it. Four months of chasing down Death Eaters who were already running, of people asking for his autograph at crime scenes, of a job that was mostly the same job he'd been doing since he was eleven: someone pointed him at a bad thing and he hit it until it stopped. He'd been good at it. He'd hated it in a way he couldn't describe to anyone, not Ron, not Hermione, not Ginny before she'd left for the Harpies and not after.

What he'd realized, in the end, in a pub in Hogsmeade at two in the morning, with Ron asleep on the table beside him, was that he'd spent seven years being *done to* by magic. Magic was the thing that had killed his parents and marked him and hunted him and lied to him and been explained to him, half-explained, by men with their own reasons. He'd been the best duelist of his generation and he'd never once understood how any of it actually *worked*.

So he'd quit. He'd gone to Hermione and said, *Teach me how to learn,* and she'd looked at him for a long time and then gone and got a stack of books as tall as he was.

Eight years since the war. Six of them in the Department. Before that, and for a long stretch after, there had been something that the Department still referred to, in the one file that described it, as *the self-directed period*: Egypt with Bill Weasley and the curse-breakers, breaking into tombs that didn't want to be broken into. A winter in the Carpathians with the dragon handlers. Five months in a village in the Amazon whose name he wasn't allowed to write down, with a woman older than Dumbledore who'd taught him to cast without a wand by the simple method of taking his wand off him and refusing to give it back. Three months in a monastery in Bhutan learning to put his mind behind a wall and keep it there. Libraries. A lot of libraries. Arithmancy until his eyes bled. Runes until he dreamed in them.

He'd gone in a lot of directions at once. Everyone had told him to specialize. He hadn't wanted to specialize. He'd wanted all of it. He'd wanted to understand every single kind of magic there was, use it, and then take it apart and see how it was built and put it back together better. That was the whole of the plan. It was not much of a plan. It had taken everything he had.

Saul Croaker had found him in 2000, in a library in Alexandria, at four in the morning, asleep on his face on top of a fourteenth-century treatise on ward geometry. Croaker had sat down across from him and waited until he woke up and then said, without preamble, *We'd like you to come and work for us, Mr. Potter. We have a basement full of doors nobody has ever opened. You're the first person I've met in thirty years who looks like he'd want to.*

The Department of Mysteries had two halves, and nobody outside it knew that, and Harry was one of maybe a dozen inside it who'd worked both. One half was the long slow half: the Time Room and the Love Room and the Brain Room and the Death Chamber with its Veil, people who'd spent thirty, forty years on a single question and might die without the answer and thought that was fine. Blue-sky lunatics. Brilliant. The best in the world at what they did. Their job, the way Croaker put it, was to make sure that Britain was never surprised by magic, and that everyone else was surprised by Britain.

The other half went out and got what the first half needed. Samples. Specimens. Information. Sometimes people. That half had a desk in Croaker's office and a drawer in the desk and a list of names, and it had, by long and quiet tradition dating back to the 1700s and confirmed in writing by the Wizengamot in a session that had never been minuted, a set of authorizations that nobody else in the British Ministry had.

All three of them.

Harry had used two of the three on duty. He had used the third once, in 2003, in a warehouse in Marseille, on a man who had been about to use it first on a child. He did not think about Marseille very often. When he did, he didn't lose sleep. He didn't lose sleep over very much anymore.

He stood in the middle of the specimen room, in the cold, and looked at the far end.

The far end of the room was empty. It had been empty for three weeks. The floor there was bare stone, and carved into the stone was a circle eight feet across, and the circle was cut with runes in three concentric rings. In the middle of the circle stood a cylinder of glass a foot thick and seven feet high, open at the top, its base sunk into the stone. The glass had threads of silver running through it, fine as hair.

He'd built the cell based on everything the Department knew about the creatures in Volterra. That had been not very much. Three hundred pages of surveillance notes from an Italian Ministry informant, a dozen photographs nobody could get to come out properly, and one extraordinary afternoon in March of this year, in the main square of Volterra, on the feast of San Marco.

He went back to the den and got the mirror out.

---

The mirror was a two-way, about the size of a paperback, in a plain steel frame. He propped it against the sugar bowl on the den table, said, "Croaker," and waited.

His own face went away, after a moment, and was replaced by another.

Saul Croaker was somewhere between seventy and a hundred and fifty years old, and nobody knew which. He was small and dry and stooped, with a fringe of white hair and very pale blue eyes behind half-moon spectacles that Harry had always privately suspected he wore because Dumbledore had. He was eating a sandwich. Behind him, through the mirror, Harry could see the black tiled walls of the Department's lower level and a single torch burning blue.

"Potter," Croaker said. "It's half past two in the morning."

"It's half six here. Did I wake you?"

"I don't sleep. You know I don't sleep." Croaker put the sandwich down. "Well?"

"Nothing yet."

"Three weeks, Potter."

"It's a big peninsula. They're not exactly thick on the ground." Harry leaned back in his chair. "The local coven's keyed out. The Snare's holding. Wolves are still running their patrols, same pattern. They've got good discipline. Better than I'd have guessed. And I met the local police chief today."

"Swan."

"Swan. Good copper. Knows something. Doesn't want to know more."

"Sensible man." Croaker took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes. "I had a meeting today. With the Minister's people. Shacklebolt wants a timeline."

"He can want what he likes."

"He wants a timeline because the Italians have stopped returning our owls." Croaker's voice didn't change, but something behind it did. "Our man in Volterra missed his last two check-ins. We've had nothing since the second of November. His sister in Turin says she hasn't heard from him either."

Harry sat forward.

"Benedetti," he said.

"Yes."

"Bloody hell, Saul."

"We don't know anything for certain." Croaker put his spectacles back on. "But you'll recall what he told us in March. What they do. How they feed. He'd been inside, Potter. Under a Disillusionment, in the tunnels under that hill, four times since the summer. He named the guard for us, one by one. He named what they can do. Every week or so, one of them goes out into the city and brings back a tour group. Thirty, forty people, Muggles, tourists, from all over the world, *led into the castle*, like lambs, and none of them come out. Benedetti said he'd counted forty-one British nationals in the Italian missing persons files since 1950 that he could place in Volterra in the week they vanished. He said there were three wizards on that list. And he was getting closer." He paused. "If they have him, they know about us. If they know about us—"

"Then they'll start asking themselves what else is out there," Harry said. "And the bloke at the top of that castle is supposed to collect things. Talents. People who can do things."

"Aro. Yes. Benedetti seemed to think that the moment Aro learns there are humans who can do things like *we* do, every witch and wizard in Europe would become—" Croaker spread his hands, "—a collector's item."

Harry looked at the map. At the gold lights on the Calawah River.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" he said. "That kid. The Cullen boy. Edward. He goes and stands at the edge of the Piazza dei Priori at noon on the feast of San Marco and tries to take his shirt off in the sun in front of a few thousand Muggles in red cloaks, because he thinks his girlfriend's dead, and that's the *only reason* we know any of this exists. Benedetti happened to be in the square. Centuries those things have been sitting in Volterra eating tourists, and they did it so well nobody in the wizarding world ever twigged. And they get caught because a seventeen-year-old—"

"A hundred and four," Croaker said.

"—because a hundred-and-four-year-old teenager had a strop."

"Love," said Croaker, "makes people stupid. We have an entire room about it." He picked up his sandwich again. "The strike committee meets on the first of December. They're going to want to know what kills them, Potter. We know fire does. That's all we know. We know nothing about how they react to curses. We know nothing about how they react to *us*. We are planning to walk into a building full of things that can move faster than a Stunner, and I need to be able to tell twelve frightened people in a room whether a Killing Curse will even work on them."

"I know."

"I need a specimen."

"I *know*, Saul."

"Not one of the Cullens," Croaker said. "The Cullens are more use to us alive and talking than dead and in jars. Carlisle Cullen has been watching the Volturi for three centuries. He lived with them. If he's half what Benedetti said, he knows the inside of that castle better than anyone alive. And the boy reads minds, and the small one sees the future. That's two of Aro's dearest wishes sitting in a house in Washington State. They're assets. They're to be handled as assets. Do you understand me?"

"I'm not an idiot."

"You are frequently an idiot. You're also the best I've got." Croaker chewed. "Your authorizations stand. All three."

"Yeah," Harry said. "I know."

"Anything else?"

Harry thought about the child's light on the map, pulsing like a heartbeat.

"No," he said.

Croaker looked at him through the mirror for a long moment with those pale eyes. He had been an Unspeakable for longer than Harry had been alive. He didn't need Legilimency.

"Report when you have something," Croaker said, and the mirror went dark.

Harry sat for a while in the den with Bad Company on the radio and the rain on the canvas overhead, which from inside sounded like rain on a slate roof. Then he got up and cut another slice of pie.

He ate it looking at the map. At the three moving gold lights by the Calawah River, and the cluster of keyed lights at the house by the water, and one tiny light in the middle of them, beating.

He thought about the hospital.

He'd gone in on his third day. He'd sat in the car park until a particular silver Mercedes pulled in for the night shift, and then he'd taken out his knife and put it through the meat of his left palm, clean, between the bones, and wrapped it in his scarf and walked into the emergency department and said, in his best idiot-tourist voice, that he'd slipped while whittling.

Dr. Carlisle Cullen had stitched him up himself. Eleven stitches. Fine hands, very cold through the gloves. A patient voice. Gold eyes, and a smile that was very kind and also, the whole time, watching. Cullen had noticed that Harry's heart rate never rose above fifty-eight. He'd noticed that Harry hadn't flinched at the needle. He'd said, mildly, *Clean edges. You'd almost think it was a scalpel,* and Harry had said, *Sharp knife,* and Cullen had smiled and said nothing else and written something on the chart.

And while he'd been stitching, Harry had sat very still on the paper-covered table with his right hand in his lap, and cast eleven silent, wandless diagnostic charms, and read Carlisle Cullen like a book.

No heartbeat. No respiration except for speech. Core temperature eighteen degrees Celsius, room temperature. Skin density roughly that of granite. And underneath the skin, something moving, slowly, everywhere, thicker than blood. Something that resonated, when he touched it with his magic, like a bell rung underwater.

That resonance had been the key. The Snare listened for it.

The cut on his hand had healed by the next morning, which was normal, for him. He'd taken the stitches out himself.

### VIII. First Bell

*Three days later.*

The bell went at twenty past three on a Monday afternoon, while Harry was halfway through a cheeseburger.

It was a real bell. Brass, about the size of a teacup, hanging on a hook over the map table. It rang once, clear and high, and every hair on his arms stood up.

He put the burger down.

On the map, down at the bottom left, where the Queets River came out of the mountains toward the sea, a new light had appeared on the outer edge of the lattice. It was red. It was moving north, fast, at a long, steady lope that ate a mile every couple of minutes.

Harry stood at the table and watched it with the burger going cold.

Around the red light, very faint, a thin circle of silver had begun to draw itself. Dotted. A few dashes of light at a time, the way a pencil sketch goes down before the ink. The lattice reading what had come in. Laying its wire.

The red light crossed the Clearwater. It crossed the upper Hoh. It didn't stop or slow or turn aside for anything, and the silver circle went along with it, filling in a dash at a time. Harry finished the burger, eventually, because he was hungry and because watching wasn't helped by an empty stomach, and he ate the chips too, standing up, with his eyes on the map.

At six minutes past four, the red light slowed down.

It had reached the Bogachiel, southeast of Forks. It came down out of the high ground toward the river trail and stopped, and stood still for nearly a minute. Then it began to move again at a walk. Harry put his hand flat on the map next to it and pushed down a little, and the ink obligingly zoomed, the way it was supposed to, until he was looking at a stretch of trail a mile long. The red light was moving through dense woods about two hundred yards off the trail, parallel to it. Paced. Patient.

There were two other lights on the trail ahead of it. Small and dim and moving at a walk. Gray. Ordinary.

Hikers. Muggles.

The red light was following them.

"Right," Harry said to nobody. "Right, you bastard."

He went down the hall. He walked fast and wasted nothing. In the bedroom, the suit was hanging on the back of the door, where it always hung on an op: black, plain, well-cut, the kind of suit that the Muggle government men wore in films, because Harry had decided at twenty-two that if he had to have a uniform he wanted one that would make people nervous for reasons they couldn't articulate. White shirt. Thin black tie. Long charcoal wool overcoat that came to his knees. Black leather gloves. Sunglasses, because it was Washington in November and nobody sane would be wearing sunglasses, and people tended to notice that and get more nervous still.

He dressed in ninety seconds. He put his wand, holly and phoenix feather, eleven inches, in the holster up his left sleeve, where he hardly ever needed it now, out of habit and sentiment. He put the credential wallet in his inside breast pocket. He went to the armory and took down the short twelve-gauge, looked at it, and put it back. He wanted the thing whole.

He was back at the map at a quarter past, pulling on the gloves. The red light was still pacing the gray ones at a steady walk. The silver circle around it was nearly solid now, three short gaps left.

To the north, two amber lights had been drifting along the wolves' usual patrol line for the last hour, the way they did most afternoons. As he watched, both of them stopped. Then they turned south.

Harry stood very still.

At twenty-eight minutes past four, the second bell rang. This one came from somewhere in the canvas over his head, a deeper note than the brass bell, like a cello string plucked once. On the map, the last gap in the silver circle closed. The loop was laid.

At thirty-one minutes past, the amber lights came over the ridge above the river, and the red light broke and ran.

"Oh, *good* dog," said Harry Potter.

He watched it run for two minutes and forty seconds, gloves on, sunglasses in his hand. He didn't Apparate. There was no telling where the thing would be by the time he landed, and two wolves the size of horses in a forest at a dead run were no place to drop in blind. And he'd built something for exactly this. He wanted to see if it worked.

The red light fled north and east, away from the river, away from the hikers, at a speed no living thing on the peninsula could match except the two amber lights coming behind it. It doubled back across a creek. It went up and over something. The amber lights lost it, found it again, closed. The silver loop went with it every step of the way, tight and bright, waiting.

And then the red light crossed one of the silver threads of the lattice at a dead run, and the whole map chimed like a struck glass.

Harry put his sunglasses on, turned on his heel, and the tent and the map and the empty plate went away with a crack.

### IX. Lassie

*Eighteen minutes earlier.*

Leah Clearwater smelled it first, because Leah always smelled it first.

It came down the wind off the south slope of the Bogachiel valley like a slap. Sweet and wrong. Burnt sugar and cold metal and the back of a freezer. The smell of a thing that had been dead a long time and hadn't had the decency to lie down about it.

She stopped dead in the ferns. Twenty yards behind her, Seth stopped too.

*You smell that?*

*Yeah,* Seth thought. Then, a heartbeat later, sharper: *That's not one of ours.*

It wasn't. The Cullens smelled bad. All vampires smelled bad. But the Cullens smelled like *Cullens*, each of them their own particular kind of bad, and Leah had them all filed by now the way she'd filed the smells of every kid in her kindergarten class. Esme was honeysuckle gone rotten. Emmett was something like cedar. Bella, now, was freesia and bleach.

This was new. This was an animal she'd never met.

*Jacob,* Leah thought, reaching down the pack line, and felt him answer from the Cullen house, eleven miles north, sleepy and lazy and full of Renesmee. *Jake. Leech. Bogachiel trail, south side. Not a Cullen.*

Jacob came awake in her head all at once. *How many?*

*One. I think.* She lifted her nose. The scent was layered: old trail, older trail, and then fresh, very fresh, minutes. And under it, faint and sweet and human, the smell of two people. Sunscreen and wet nylon and one of them had eaten a banana. *There's hikers. Two of them. On the trail. He's following them.*

*Go,* Jacob said. *I'm coming. Don't wait for me. Go.*

They went.

---

It was the kind of running Leah loved and would never in her life have admitted she loved.

The world went narrow and sharp and fast. The ferns came up at her face and parted; logs came up under her feet and went away; the big hemlocks and Sitka spruce went by like the pillars of some endless hall. Rain on her fur. Mud on her paws. Seth was on her right flank, twenty feet off, matching her stride for stride, and in the pack mind his thoughts were a bright quick blur, *left, there, smell that, he's close, he's close*, and her own were the same.

They came over a ridge and down through a stand of vine maple and she saw the trail below, a muddy brown ribbon through the trees, and on it two figures in bright rain jackets, one red, one blue, walking slowly, with walking poles, talking. Laughing about something. The woman in red was pointing at a mushroom.

And up the slope from them, on the near side of the trail, a hundred yards off, in the deep shadow under a fallen cedar, something stood up.

It had been crouched. It rose in one movement, the way no human body rises, all at once, like a puppet yanked upright. A man. He looked like a man. Tall, lean, long dark hair tied back, a ratty brown coat, bare feet, and a face so pale it was almost blue in the shadow. He had been watching the hikers. He turned his head now, slowly, and looked straight up the slope at Leah.

His eyes were red. Bright, wet, fresh red, the red of something that had fed recently and well.

Behind him, the hikers walked on, and laughed, and didn't know.

Leah snarled.

She didn't decide to. It came up out of her chest from somewhere older than her, and it was loud enough that the woman in the red jacket stopped on the trail and said, "Did you hear that?" and the man in blue said, "Elk, probably," and they kept walking.

The vampire looked at Leah. He looked at Seth. He did the arithmetic. Two wolves the size of horses, on the high ground, already running.

He broke and ran.

Away from the hikers, northeast, uphill, into the deep timber. Fast. Blurring. Gone between one heartbeat and the next.

*Go, go, go!* That was Seth.

They went after him.

---

He was fast. They were all fast, the leeches, and this one was fast even for a leech, and he knew how to run. He didn't run in straight lines. He went up the trunks of trees and along the branches and dropped down behind. He doubled back across creeks to break the scent. Twice Leah lost him for a second and had to cast left and right with her nose down, and twice Seth's thoughts came hot and urgent in her head, *there, there, he went over the rocks*, and they had him again.

But they were faster, by a hair. Leah was the fastest of any of them, faster than Sam, faster than Jacob, and she knew it and she poured it on now, low and flat, ears back, her paws barely touching the ground, closing, closing. She could hear him now. He barely had footsteps, but she could hear the slap of his coat. The hiss of his breath. He was breathing hard, which he didn't need to do, which meant he was scared.

Good.

*Thirty yards,* Leah thought. *I've got him. Seth, cut left, push him toward the creek, I've got him—*

Twenty yards. Fifteen. The vampire glanced back over his shoulder, and his red eyes were wide and his lips were pulled back from his teeth.

He hit something.

Leah didn't see what. There was nothing there to see. One second he was running, in a clear stretch of forest floor between two big Douglas firs, nothing around him but ferns and air. The next second there was a sound like a guitar string the size of a suspension cable snapping tight, a deep humming *THRUM* that she felt in her teeth and the roots of her fur, and the vampire's feet went out from under him.

*Up.*

He went up. Straight up. Like something had grabbed him by the ankle and yanked. He went up twenty feet in a fraction of a second, feet first, and his coat flapped down over his head, and he hung there, upside down, from nothing, from absolutely nothing, from the empty air under the low branch of a Douglas fir. Swinging gently. Turning a slow quarter-turn one way, then back the other.

And he didn't move.

He didn't fight. He didn't twist or snarl or claw at whatever had him. His arms had flopped down past his head and stopped there, rigid, fingers half-curled. His face, upside down, was locked in a snarl. Only his eyes moved. His red eyes were rolling, wild, side to side, side to side.

Leah hit the brakes so hard she plowed a furrow through the ferns with all four feet and nearly went nose-first into the base of the fir. Seth came skidding up beside her, ears flat.

They stood there in the rain, panting, and looked up.

*What,* Seth thought. *The. Hell.*

Leah didn't have anything. She didn't have a single thought in her head. The vampire swung. Upside down. Rain dripped off his bare feet, where one ankle was pinched in tight by something she couldn't see, a groove pressed into the marble skin like he was hanging from a wire. There was a smell in the air now, over the leech-stink, sharp and electric, like the air right after lightning.

*Is that—did a Cullen—*

*No.* No Cullen could do that. Nothing Leah knew of could do that. She'd been the girl who turned into a wolf since the spring, and she'd gotten used to a lot fast, and she had never, ever seen anything like—

*Jake,* she thought hard down the line. *Jake, something's got him. Something caught him. He's just hanging in the air—*

*What?* Jacob's thoughts were full of the blur of trees; he was running flat-out, still miles off. *What do you mean something caught him?*

*I mean he's hanging upside down in the middle of the air! Like a rabbit! Like somebody set a snare and he—*

There was a sound behind them like a gunshot.

Leah spun. Seth spun. Both of them came round with their hackles up and their teeth out, and there was a man standing in the ferns ten yards away who had not been there one second ago.

He was tall. Six-two, maybe, and wide through the shoulders. He was wearing a black suit and a long charcoal-gray overcoat that came down to his knees, and a white shirt and a thin black tie, and black leather gloves. He was wearing dark sunglasses. In the rain. Under the trees. In November. His hair was black and wet and sticking up in the back like he'd slept on it. He had his hands in his coat pockets.

He was looking up at the vampire.

"Afternoon," the man said.

English. He was English.

Leah's lips went back off her teeth all the way. She put herself between him and Seth without thinking. A low snarl ran out of her chest and kept running.

The man looked at her. Then he took one hand out of his pocket, slowly, and she tensed, and what was in it was a black leather wallet. He flipped it open with his thumb and held it up so she could see it.

There was a badge inside. Gold. An eagle, a shield, letters. *FEDERAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION.* Underneath, an ID card with his picture on it.

*FBI?* Leah thought. *The* FBI?

*That's not what it says,* Seth thought, beside her, confused. *It says Homeland Security. Leah, it says Department of Homeland Security, it's got the—*

*It says FBI.*

*It says—*

"Afternoon," the man said again. He had a flat, even voice. Bored. Like a man reading a parking ticket. "This situation is contained. I'll have to ask you to vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

He closed the wallet and put it back in his coat.

Then he looked up at the vampire, and made a small flicking motion with two fingers of his gloved right hand, like a man shooing a fly, and the vampire fell.

He dropped twenty feet straight down, headfirst. He hit the forest floor with a crack like a boulder dropped on a sidewalk, and the ground shook under Leah's feet, and he lay there in the ferns, flat on his back, stiff as a plank. Arms at his sides. Legs straight. Not moving. Eyes rolling. A smell of crushed fern and split earth.

The man in the suit walked over to him.

He didn't hurry. Leah's whole body was screaming at her to go, to do something, to bite, to run, to put herself between this guy and Seth and *the leech* and the whole world. She didn't move. She didn't know which direction to move in. She stood in the ferns with every hair on her body standing up and her heart going like a drum and watched a man in a black suit and sunglasses walk up to a vampire, a red-eyed vampire, the kind that ate hikers, and stand over him, and look down.

"Hello, sunshine," the man said to the vampire. "Bit of a day, eh?"

Then he bent down, and took hold of the vampire's coat at the shoulder and belt, and lifted him.

He lifted him the way you'd lift a surfboard. One-handed, then shifted to the crook of his arm. The vampire stayed rigid. He stayed perfectly straight, perfectly stiff, a six-foot plank of marble in a ratty coat, and the man in the suit tucked him under his right arm, horizontally, head forward, like he was about to walk down to the beach and paddle out, and settled him there, and turned around.

The vampire weighed as much as a car. Leah *knew* that. She'd fought them. She'd felt what it was like to hit one, like hitting a statue. You couldn't lift one like that. Emmett couldn't have lifted one like that without at least making a face.

The man in the suit wasn't making a face.

He was looking at the two of them over the top of his sunglasses. Now that he'd pushed them down his nose a little she could see his eyes. Green. Very green.

*Who the* hell *is this guy,* Leah thought.

"Name's not important," said the man in the suit.

Leah went cold all the way down to her paws.

*Did he just—*

"Yes, I did," said the man. "Sorry. You lot are very loud. It's like standing next to a jukebox." He shifted the vampire under his arm. "And it's not exactly polite to talk about people right in front of them, Lassie."

*Lassie.*

*Lassie?*

*Get out of my head.* She threw it at him like a rock. *Get out of my head, you—*

"Then stop shouting," the man said mildly. "I'm just listening. You're broadcasting on every channel." He looked at Seth. Seth had his ears straight up and his mouth a little open, and in the pack mind his thoughts were a tumbling pile, *he can hear us he can hear us like Edward he can hear us what is he*—

"Balto," the man said to him. "Close your mouth. You'll catch flies."

Seth closed his mouth.

*Balto?* Seth thought, offended. *Like the cartoon dog?*

"Like the real dog. Good dog. Saved a town from diphtheria. There's a statue of him in Central Park." The man turned his head and looked south, back the way they'd come, back toward the trail, as if he could see through two miles of forest. "Your hikers are fine, by the way. Couple from Tacoma, red jacket and blue. They're about a mile and a half back that way, arguing about whether that was an elk, and they'll be back at their car by dark, and they'll never know how close they came. That was good work, the pair of you. He'd have had them tonight, once they'd pitched their tent." He hefted the vampire under his arm. "Wouldn't you, Lucan?"

The vampire's eyes rolled toward him and stared, wild with hatred.

*How does he know his name,* Leah thought. *How does he know his* name—

"I asked," the man said.

He hadn't asked. He hadn't said a word to the leech except *hello, sunshine*.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Oh. He's in the leech's head, too.* She thought about Edward. Edward, who read minds and was polite about it and hated it and apologized. Edward, who'd go pale and look away when he heard something he wasn't supposed to. This man in the suit didn't look like he'd ever apologized for anything in his life. He looked like he had been reading people's minds since breakfast, and would be reading them through lunch, and saw no problem whatsoever with any of it.

"None," he agreed. "Now. As I said. Vacate the premises, please. Go home. Tell the big one—" he cocked his head, listening, "—*Clifford*, isn't it? Big, reddish, very loud, running this way about four miles out? Tell Clifford not to bother. It's sorted."

*Jacob's going to kill him,* Seth thought. *Jacob is going to* kill *him for calling him Clifford.*

*Shut up, Seth.*

*He's like Edward but* worse. *How is he doing that? How is he even carrying—that's a vampire, he's carrying a whole vampire like it's nothing, he's got to be—*

And Leah felt the thought form in her brother's head before he thought it. She felt it the way she'd feel a sneeze coming in her own nose. She knew exactly where his mind was going, because she'd been sitting next to him in Ida Calloway's living room two weeks ago, and listening to a witch talk about governments and wands and ministries and secret departments in the basement, and she knew Seth, and she knew—

*Seth, don't—*

*He's a wizard,* Seth thought. *He's gotta be. He's a wizard. Like Layla.*

The man in the suit stopped.

He'd been half-turned away, as though he were about to leave. He stopped with his weight on one foot. For a second, nothing moved in the whole wet forest except the rain. The vampire under his arm, rigid as wood. The ferns. Leah's own heart.

Then, slowly, he turned back.

He pulled his sunglasses off with his free hand and folded them and put them in his breast pocket. Without them, his face was younger than she'd thought. Mid-twenties. Hard, square jaw, a nose that had been broken at least once, dark stubble. And those eyes, which had been looking at them before with mild boredom, and which were now looking at Seth, and only Seth, with total, absolute, unblinking attention. Leah had seen that look before. On Sam. On Jacob. On a cougar, once, in the hills above Neah Bay, right before it decided whether or not to come down off the rock.

"Now that," the man said quietly. "That's something you shouldn't know."

He didn't say anything else. He didn't move. He didn't need to. Leah felt the air change. The electric smell from earlier came back, ten times stronger, sharp enough to make her eyes water. The hair along her spine stood straight up. The ferns around the man's boots had begun to stir, very slightly, all of them leaning the same direction. Away from him.

*Seth,* Leah thought. *Seth, back up. Back up slow. Back up* right now—

Seth didn't back up.

She felt what he was going to do a half-second before he did it. So did the man in the suit. She saw his eyes flick, fast, down to Seth's left foreleg, to the little leather cord knotted around it with a pale wooden bead strung on it. She saw him see it. She saw him understand it. She waited for him to do something, anything, to stop it.

He didn't. He just watched.

Seth ducked his big head and bit down on the bead.

There was a small sharp *crack*, like a knuckle.

The man in the suit said, "Ah."

And twelve feet to his left, out of nothing at all, with a noise like a whip, Layla Calloway appeared in the ferns.

She came out of the air already moving. Her hair was wild, half out of its knot. She was wearing a flannel shirt and sweatpants and a pair of rubber garden boots and nothing else against the cold, like she'd been out in Ida Calloway's garden thirty seconds ago and hadn't stopped for a coat, and she had her wand up, out, pointed, and her face was white and furious and terrified and she didn't hesitate for a single heartbeat.

"*Stupefy!*"

A bolt of red light came off the end of her wand. Leah had never seen anything move that fast that wasn't a vampire. It went straight at the man's chest.

He flicked it away.

There wasn't any other word for what he did. He lifted his free left hand, the gloved one, and made a small flick of the fingers, the back of the hand going outward, the way you'd brush a crumb off a tablecloth. He didn't look at the spell. He didn't look at Layla. He was still looking at Seth. The red bolt bent in midair like light through water, and went sailing off to the right, and slammed into the trunk of a Douglas fir thirty feet away with a bang. Bark exploded. A patch on the trunk the size of a dinner plate went black and smoking.

Layla didn't stop. Her wand was already moving again, a hard, complicated slash, down and across and up, and she snarled something Leah didn't catch, and the light that came off it this time was different. White-blue, with a hard, bright core like a welding torch and something spinning around the outside of it like a drill bit.

The man in the suit held up his hand, palm out.

The spell stopped.

It stopped dead, three feet from his chest, in midair. It hung there. Leah could hear it. It was making a noise, a high, angry whine, like a hornet in a jar, and it was spinning, and spitting little blue-white sparks off the outer shell into the wet air, which hissed where they landed. It was trying to move forward. It couldn't.

The man in the suit, finally, turned his head and looked at it.

He looked at it for a long time. Four seconds. Five. He tilted his head to one side, like a mechanic listening to an engine. He reached out with one gloved finger and, very gently, nudged the spinning outer shell, and it changed pitch, and he nodded to himself as though it had told him something.

"Huh," he said. "Stunner. But you've wrapped it in a piercing charm, haven't you? A shell. Nasty little spiral on it." He turned it a few degrees, in the air, with his finger, examining the other side. "Built to drill through a Shield Charm before the payload goes off. Very nice. Never seen that one. Is that yours?"

Layla stood frozen in the ferns with her wand still pointed at him. Her chest was heaving. Her mouth was open. She didn't answer.

"It's sloppy on the back end," the man went on. "You're losing a good third of your power through the tail. You want to close the spiral tighter on the backswing." He made a fist.

The spell went out. It crumpled in on itself, like a moth closed in a hand, with a sound like a struck match being blown out, and was gone. A little curl of blue smoke drifted up from between his fingers.

He opened his hand and looked at his empty glove.

Then he turned and looked at Layla, properly, for the first time.

"I could have you arrested and your wand snapped for attacking an Unspeakable, love," he said. "Just so you know."

### X. Unspeakable

There were a lot of things Layla Calloway was afraid of.

Spiders, mildly. Heights, more than mildly. Deep water. Her mother's silences. The MACUSA Department of Magical Law Enforcement, and the Obliviators, and losing her wand, and losing Seth, which she'd only been afraid of for nine days, since the porch, and which was already the biggest.

Professor Quaid, in fourth-year Defense, had taught a whole lecture once on what he'd called the Things You Don't Pick Fights With, and he'd listed them on the board in his cramped handwriting with his three-fingered hand. Wampus cats. Nundus. Horned serpents, the real ones. Dark wizards with nothing to lose. And at the bottom, underlined twice: *The British Department of Mysteries.*

"You'll never meet one," he'd said. "Unspeakables don't leave the basement. But if one of them ever turns up, I want every single one of you to keep your hands where he can see them, say *yes sir* and *no sir* and *thank you sir*, and let him do whatever it is he came to do. Nobody knows what they do down there. Nobody knows what they're allowed to do. Rumor has it they're cleared for things the rest of us go to prison for. All three of the things. Do you understand me? Never fight one. Never cross one. Never lie to one. You won't win."

Layla had laughed with the rest of the class. It had seemed like a story, the kind teachers told to scare you.

Now she was standing in the wet ferns in her gardening boots with her wand pointed at a man in a black suit who had just caught her best spell, the one she'd spent all of sixth year building, the one that had put her through to the dueling semifinals at Ilvermorny, *caught it in his hand* and turned it over like a pebble and critiqued it and then crushed it out of existence like it was nothing.

Spells could be blocked. Deflected, absorbed, reflected, if you were very good and had a strong shield and a strong arm. Nobody held one in the air in front of them and looked at it.

He'd done it without a wand. He hadn't even used his voice.

"Unspeakable," Layla said. Her voice came out a croak.

"That's right." He was still holding the vampire, she noticed. Under his right arm. Like a surfboard. She'd been so busy looking at his hand she hadn't seen. A grown vampire, stiff as a log, and he was holding it like it weighed about as much as a rolled-up rug. "Lower your wand, please."

She didn't.

He sighed. "Calloway, isn't it? Layla Calloway. Ilvermorny, class of 2005. Horned Serpent. Registered with MACUSA's Seattle office as of the twenty-eighth of September, change of residence, one Calawah Way, Forks, Washington. Inherited from one Ida Calloway, deceased, Squib." He watched her face. "Lower your wand, Miss Calloway. I'm not going to ask a third time."

How did he—

She knew how. Of course she knew how. She felt it then, the way she'd felt Jasper Hale in the Cullens' living room, except where Jasper had been a gentle hand on the outside of her mind, this was something else. Something very cold and very light that had slipped in through the side and was standing, now, in the middle of her thoughts, looking around, the way a man stands in the middle of a room in someone else's house. Unhurried. Opening drawers.

She had sat through a week on Legilimency in seventh year. She'd learned the theory. She'd never felt it done.

She lowered her wand.

"Thank you," he said, and the cold thing in her head went away, all at once, like a door closing.

Beside her, a long low growl rolled out of Seth's chest. Seth had moved. Without her noticing, he'd stepped forward until he was between her and the man in the suit, his whole sand-colored bulk filling the space, his head low, his lips back. On her other side, so close Layla could feel the heat coming off her, was the gray wolf. Leah. Leah, who had only ever looked at Layla with suspicion, standing shoulder to Layla's shoulder now, snarling.

"Oh, *stop* it," said the man in the suit.

He sounded tired, like a father in a car with three kids in the back seat who wouldn't stop kicking. He looked at the two wolves, and at Layla, and then at the vampire under his arm, and then up at the sky, which was going steel-colored with the dusk.

"Right," he said. "Let's see. Balto, you're imprinted on her. Lassie, you're his sister, and you don't like her very much, and you'd tear my throat out for her anyway, because she's his. That about right?"

Leah's snarl cut off. Then started again, louder.

"Thought so." He shifted the vampire to his other arm. "Well, Miss Calloway. You've told them. All of them. The wolves. The coven up by the river. That's twelve individuals, not counting yourself, informed of the existence of the magical world and in some detail its structures and governments. There was a chat about goblins, I noticed." His eyebrows went up a little. "And Hogwarts. And you told them who I was."

Layla went cold.

"I didn't—I didn't know who—"

And then she looked at him, properly, for the first time, without the panic, and she did.

Without the sunglasses, standing in the ferns in the dusk. Older. Much older. The face had filled out and hardened and the cheekbones had come up and the nose was crooked now in a way it hadn't been. No glasses. The hair was the same, black and sticking up in the back, and the eyes were the same. Those were exactly the same.

She'd had that face on the lid of her trunk for six years.

"Oh my God," Layla said. "You're Harry Potter."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Yeah," he said.

There didn't seem to be anything to say after that. Layla stood there in the wet. Her mouth was open. She shut it. Seth, in front of her, had stopped growling. His ears had come up. In his head, she knew without being able to hear it, he was thinking *the guy from the poster, the guy from the poster, she said, the guy from the—*

"And that name," said Harry Potter, "doesn't leave this forest. Not ever. I'll know." He said it flatly. A fact. "Now. There's protocol. You know the protocol."

Layla knew the protocol.

"Obliviate everyone," she said, numbly. "File a breach report with MACUSA. Wand review."

"Wand review. Probably a snapping, given the scale of it. You told a coven of vampires and the better part of a shifter pack in one afternoon. That's a record. There'll be people in the office who want your picture." He said it without any particular expression. "I'd have to Obliviate the lot. All three wolves here, and the rest up the road. All seven vampires. The half-and-half. The mind-reader would be fun. Never tried to Obliviate a mind-reader. Take me the rest of the night, probably."

"No," Layla said.

He raised an eyebrow.

"No," she said again, louder. Her hands were shaking. She held onto her wand hard. "You're not doing that. You're not going into their heads and taking it away. They didn't do anything. *I* told them. I'm the one who broke the Statute. You want to snap my wand, fine. Snap it. Arrest me. Take me back to Britain and put me in your basement. But you're not touching them."

Harry Potter looked at her.

"Love," he said, not unkindly, "you think there's a single thing in this forest that could stop me?"

The wolves went still.

And through the trees behind them, to the north, there was a crashing, and a snarl like a chainsaw starting, and something came out of the forest at a dead run, huge and russet and furious, ploughing through ferns, mud spraying, teeth bared. It skidded to a stop beside Seth so hard it carved a trench, and planted itself, and looked at Harry Potter with eyes like black coals, and the sound coming out of its chest made Layla's teeth hurt.

Jacob. Jacob had come.

"And there's Clifford," said Harry Potter.

Jacob's snarl rose half an octave.

"Yeah, yeah. I heard." Harry Potter didn't look at him. He looked past Jacob, into the trees behind, and his face changed slightly. "Ah. And you've brought a friend."

A shape came out from between the Douglas firs. It walked. It didn't need to run, now that it was here, and Layla understood that it had been running before, all the way from the house by the river, keeping up with Jacob. It stopped at the edge of the little clearing, about twenty yards back. Bronze hair, wet with rain. A gray sweater. Gold eyes.

Edward Cullen stood in the dusk and looked at Harry Potter with an expression that Layla had never seen on his face, or anyone's. Like a blind man who'd walked into a room he thought he knew and found the furniture moved.

"You," Edward said.

"Me," Harry Potter agreed.

"I can't—" Edward's hands had closed into fists at his sides. His eyes moved over Harry Potter's face, back and forth, fast, as if searching for a seam. "I can't hear you. At all. There's nothing there."

"No," said Harry Potter. "There isn't. Sorry, mate. Line's busy." He tilted his head. "You're getting static, aren't you? From me. Like a radio between stations. That's a wall. Took me three months in Bhutan. I'd be happy to tell you about it some time if you ask nicely. Not today."

"You're the man in her memory." Edward glanced at Layla, and back. "The boy in the newspaper. In the castle. With the cracked glasses."

"I was a boy in a lot of newspapers." Harry Potter set the vampire down, finally. He did it carefully, leaning it against the trunk of the nearest Douglas fir, upright, like a man propping a ladder against a wall. The vampire's eyes followed him. "And you're Edward Cullen. Born Edward Anthony Masen, Chicago, 1901. Turned 1918, Spanish flu. Recently married. Recently a father. And on the nineteenth of March this year, at a few minutes past noon, in the Piazza dei Priori in Volterra, Italy, in front of a crowd of several thousand people in red cloaks celebrating the feast of San Marco, you walked toward a patch of sunlight and started unbuttoning your shirt."

Edward went still. Completely, inhumanly still. The kind of still the vampires went when they forgot to pretend.

"How," Edward said, very quietly, "do you know that."

"We had a man in the square," said Harry Potter. "Good bloke. Italian. Name of Benedetti. Worked for us. He saw a boy step toward the sunlight and he saw two men in gray cloaks come out of an alley and pull him back into the dark, faster than anything he'd ever seen, and he saw the girl come running, and he followed you all down into the dark under the square. And that afternoon, he sent us a very long letter." He paused. "That's how we found out about your friends in the basement, Edward. The Volturi. We've been sharing a continent with them since before Rome, if Benedetti's right, and we never knew. Then you had a bad day."

Nobody moved. Even Jacob had stopped growling.

"That," said Harry Potter, "is why I'm here."

The rain came down. Somewhere off in the forest, a raven said something rude.

"What," said Edward, "do you want with the Volturi?"

Harry Potter looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the wolves, one by one. Then at Layla. Then, for a second, up at the sky.

"Not here," he said. "Not in the mud. That's a conversation for tomorrow. Your house. Noon, if that works. Bring your dad. Bring the whole lot, I don't mind, everyone's going to want to hear it anyway." He paused. "And relax. All of you. I said *protocol*. I didn't say I was going to follow it."

Layla stared at him. "What?"

"You heard." He looked at her, then at Edward. "Your coven's on a list, Edward. Mine. The Department has decided you're worth more alive and talking than dead and in jars. That's from the top. If I Obliviate you, I lose twelve sources on the one thing I came here to learn about, and I get to start from scratch with a bunch of people who can't remember why they're nervous of me. That's stupid. I'm a lot of things. I try not to be stupid." He turned to Layla. "Your breach goes in a drawer, Miss Calloway. My drawer. In my desk. Which I can open whenever I like."

Layla understood exactly what he meant by that. She understood that he meant her to.

"That's blackmail," she said.

"That's leverage," said Harry Potter. "Blackmail's when you want something you're not entitled to."

Edward had taken a step forward. "If you think you can threaten my family—"

"I'm not threatening your family. I haven't threatened anybody. Lucan's the only one I've threatened today, and he deserved it." He nodded at the stiff figure propped against the tree. "He's eaten fourteen people since August, your friend here. I had a look. Two of them were on the Hoh, back in September. Park service shot a bear for it." He shrugged. "You lot don't eat people. I've watched you for three weeks. You hunt deer and elk and the occasional cougar up near Hurricane Ridge, the big one does, and you've been very careful. That gets you on my good list. Lucan's on the other list."

"You've been *watching* us," Edward said.

"For three weeks. Yeah." Harry Potter didn't sound sorry. "You walked in here, didn't you? Straight through the line. Same as the wolves. You didn't get hung up by your ankle." He pointed upward at the Douglas fir, at the empty air where Lucan had been hanging. "You know why? Because you're keyed out. All seven of you. Eight, counting the Chief's daughter. Spent three nights up a tree outside your house getting the readings." He paused. "Your doctor dad stitched up my hand, too. Very good job. Tell him I said thanks."

Edward's face had gone, if possible, paler.

Jacob made a sound in his chest that was half growl and half something else, and Harry Potter glanced at him.

"The kid?" he said, as if answering. "She's got a heartbeat. The ward can see her, but it won't touch her. It only closes on things without one." He looked at Jacob for a second longer than was comfortable. "And I don't do kids, Clifford. You can stop thinking about that."

Layla didn't know what Jacob had been thinking. She could guess.

"I've got a specimen going off," Harry Potter said. He bent and picked up the vampire again, surfboard-style, under one arm. "Well. Not *going off*. They don't, apparently. But I've a lot of questions for him, and I'd like to ask them before he works out how to wriggle. And I need a statement from you, Miss Calloway. A proper one. How much you told them, and what, and when. Tonight."

"Tonight," Layla repeated.

"My place." He looked at Seth, who had gone rigid. "Yes, Balto, you can come. I'd heard you thinking it before you'd thought it. You'll need to change back first, and put some trousers on. I'm not having a horse in the house." He turned to Jacob. "Two guests. That's all I've got room for in my evening. You've got a little girl at home and a family who's going to want to know what just happened, so it isn't you. Lassie goes back with you and tells it. Sparkles—"

"*What* did you call me?"

"—goes home too. No vampires in my house. House rule." He looked down at the vampire under his arm. "Present company excepted. Ask Lucan here how he feels about it tomorrow."

Edward looked at Jacob. Jacob looked at Seth. Something passed between the three of them, through the pack mind and through Edward's head and whatever Edward heard in Jacob's thoughts, too fast and too private for Layla to follow. Then Jacob's big russet head went down a fraction, a nod that wasn't quite a nod, and he looked at Seth again, and Layla knew he'd said something in there like *go, then. Go. Stay next to her. Anything happens, anything, you bite that bead again or you howl, and I'm there.*

Seth turned around and went into the trees.

"He doesn't have a bead anymore," Layla said, absurdly. "He bit it. It's broken."

"I'll make him another," said Harry Potter. "Better one. That one's lovely work, by the way. Rowan and a twinned Protean with a homing tag tied off on the back end. Didn't know Ilvermorny taught Protean charms."

"They don't," said Layla. "I read a book."

Harry Potter looked at her, then, for a long second, with something on his face that might almost have been interest.

"Huh," he said.

Seth came out of the trees ninety seconds later in a pair of cut-off jeans and nothing else, barefoot, steaming in the cold, with his chest heaving and his fists clenched, and came and stood beside Layla, and took her hand. His was burning. Hers was ice.

"Okay," Seth said. His voice was hoarse. "Okay. We're coming."

"Course you are," said Harry Potter. He held out his free hand, the gloved one, palm up. "Side-along. Grab on, both of you. Don't let go, and don't be sick on the suit. It's new."

Layla looked at the hand. Then at Edward, standing at the edge of the clearing in the dusk with his hands still in fists. At Jacob, huge and russet and bristling. At Leah, who was looking at her with an expression Layla couldn't read.

Then she reached out and took Harry Potter's hand.

It was hot. Through the leather glove, it was as hot as Seth's.

She had about one second to notice that and wonder about it, and to see Seth's face go strange as his own hand touched the glove too, and then the forest folded up around them like a closing book.

### XI. Specimen

Seth Clearwater had never Apparated before, and he decided, on the far side of it, flat on his back on a concrete floor looking up at a rafter, that he never wanted to do it again in his life.

It was like being shoved through a garden hose. It was like being stuffed into a bottle and shaken. It was like phasing, almost, that same feeling of every part of you coming apart and being put back, except that phasing was his, and this had been done *to* him, by somebody else, without asking.

"Sorry," said a voice above him. "Should've said. First time's a bugger. You'll be fine in a minute."

Seth sat up.

Layla was on her knees beside him, one hand on the floor, white as a sheet and swallowing hard. She'd been Apparating for two years and still looked like she was going to throw up. He put his hand on her back without thinking, and she leaned into it.

Harry Potter was standing over them. He'd already put the vampire down. Lucan was lying flat on a steel table nearby, on his back, rigid, still in his ratty coat, his red eyes turned to stare at the ceiling. There was a radio playing somewhere, some old rock song with a lot of guitar.

Seth looked around.

They were in a garage. That was the first thing that made no sense. A big concrete garage, clean and bright, with a workbench and a tool chest and an old black motorcycle up on a stand and a calendar on the wall from a tire company in 1987. It smelled like oil and rubber and coffee. Beyond the garage, through an open doorway, he could see a kitchen. A big one, with a big old stove and a lot of copper pots hanging up. Beyond that, a long room full of books.

He had felt the tent around him as they arrived. Canvas walls. Close. He'd heard rain on canvas, for half a second. And this was a garage the size of his mom's whole house.

"It's bigger on the inside," Seth said.

"Everyone says that." Harry Potter had taken off his gloves and was pulling his tie loose. "It's an Undetectable Extension Charm, with some extra work. About ninety thousand square feet, all told. Tent's mostly standard. The extra work's mine."

Layla had gotten to her feet. She was turning in a slow circle, staring. "That's not possible. You can't put an Extension Charm on that much space. It collapses. There's a ceiling. Every textbook says—"

"Textbooks say a lot of things." Harry Potter went to the bench and turned the radio down. "Come on. Kitchen. You're both freezing, and Balto's starving. I can hear his stomach from here."

Seth was starving. He'd been running for an hour. He hadn't noticed until right that second.

Harry Potter looked at him, and Seth had a strange, too-familiar feeling, like being caught at the Cullens' with Edward listening in.

"Yeah, sorry," said Harry Potter. "I'll try to keep out. You're very easy to hear, though. Like a dog with its head out a car window."

"That's not a compliment."

"I didn't mean it as one." He picked up the vampire again, surfboard-style, and walked off down a hallway that went back farther than the tent possibly could have, and over his shoulder said, "Kitchen. Eat anything you like. Don't touch the pie on the left. Back in five."

---

They didn't eat anything.

They stood in the big kitchen with the copper pots and the old iron stove and looked at each other. Layla's hand found Seth's. It was still cold. He chafed it between both of his.

"Are you okay?" he said.

"No." She laughed, short and shaky. "No, I'm—Seth, that's *Harry Potter*. That's the actual—he's actually—I had his picture on my *trunk*. I used to say goodnight to it. I was *eleven*." She put her free hand over her face. "I threw a Stunner at Harry Potter."

"He didn't seem mad."

"He said he could have my wand snapped."

"Yeah, but he didn't seem *mad* about it."

"That's worse!" She took her hand down. Her eyes were huge. "Seth, do you know what he did back there? With my spell? He caught it. He caught a spell in his hand. Without a wand. That's—you can't *do* that. Nobody can do that. Spells go where they're sent and they hit what they hit, and if you're really good you can bend them a little with a shield, but you can't just *stop* one. In the *air*. And look at it."

"He seemed to think it was a good spell."

"It's a *great* spell! It took me six months!" She took a breath. "And he fixed it. He told me what was wrong with it. In like five seconds. And he was *right*. I've been losing power on the back end since sixth year and I could never work out why."

Seth didn't say anything. He was looking at the hallway where Harry Potter had gone.

"He was hot," he said.

Layla gave him a very strange look.

"No, I mean—his hand. When I grabbed his hand. Like through the glove." Seth looked down at his own hand. "Like me. Like *us*. Wolves run hot. Like a hundred and eight. Humans don't. Vampires sure don't. And he smelled weird, too. I noticed it out there and I thought it was just because of the leech. He doesn't smell like a person. He smells like—" he searched for it, "—like dirt. Like pine trees and dirt, and rocks. Cold rocks. And gun oil. Mostly gun oil."

"Wizards smell like people."

"He doesn't." Seth frowned. "He doesn't smell like a wolf, either. It's like—" He didn't know what it was like. He gave up. "I don't know. Weird."

The radio in the garage changed songs.

"Did you see the badge?" Layla said, after a second. "What did it say, to you?"

"Homeland Security. Leah saw FBI."

"I saw MACUSA. Department of Magical Law Enforcement. With the seal." She let out a long breath. "It's charmed. It shows whatever you'd believe."

"Is that legal?"

"Seth," said Layla, "I don't think that man has ever cared about what's legal in his whole life."

From down the hall, Harry Potter's voice called, "I *can* hear you, you know. It's a tent."

---

He came back for them five minutes later. He'd taken off the suit jacket and rolled up his sleeves. His forearms were thick, roped with muscle and dusted with dark hair, and he'd pulled on a pair of thin blue gloves, the kind doctors wore.

"Right," he said. "I want a statement from you, Miss Calloway, but first I'm going to need about twenty minutes in the other room. You can wait here and eat, or you can come and watch."

"Watch what?" said Seth.

Harry Potter looked at him.

"I'm going to find out what makes them tick, Balto," he said. "I've got one of the things that ate fourteen people since August lying on my table. I've got a committee in London that's going to walk into a castle full of them next year, and twelve terrified people who need to know what kills it. Fire does. Fine. What else does? What doesn't? What makes it talk? What makes it hurt?" He paused. "You fight them. You've been fighting them since the spring. I'd honestly like you in the room. You might notice something I don't."

Seth's stomach turned over. He looked at Layla.

Layla's jaw was set. "We'll come."

"Your call." Harry Potter turned and went down the hall, and they followed.

---

Seth would remember the hallway, after. The length of it. Doors on both sides, plain wooden doors, and Harry Potter not stopping at any of them except one, about halfway along, on the left, which was standing open. Seth looked in as they passed.

Guns.

The whole room was guns. Long ones on one wall and short ones on the other and cabinets underneath. Seth had grown up on the rez. He'd grown up around deer rifles and his dad's old shotgun and the guys who went out for elk every fall. He knew what a gun looked like. These looked like guns, mostly. But there were lines etched into the metal of every single one, lines like silver writing that ran up the barrels and over the stocks, and the lines were *glowing*. Faint, slow, the way the coals in his mom's stove glowed. Some of them were blue and some were red and one, a short ugly shotgun on its own rack, was white. There was a smell of oil and iron and something sharp and clean underneath, like the air after a lightning strike.

At the far end of the room, in a glass case on the wall all by itself, there was a knife made out of a tooth. A single curved white tooth as long as Seth's forearm.

"Is that—" Layla said.

"Yes," said Harry Potter, without turning around. "Keep walking."

---

The room at the end of the hall was cold. Really cold. Seth's breath came out white. Layla wrapped her arms around herself.

It was long and white and very bright, like a hospital, like the room at Forks Community where Seth had said goodbye to his dad. There were steel tables down the middle with instruments on them, some of which Seth recognized from the Cullens' house, from Carlisle's office, and some of which he didn't recognize at all. Brass things with dials. Glass things with liquid moving in them that seemed to be moving the wrong way. A silver basin full of something that looked like smoke turned into water.

And shelves. Shelves floor to ceiling down both walls, with jars on them.

Seth didn't look at the jars for very long. He saw a hand. He saw a hand, gray and dead, in a glass box, and he saw the fingers on it move. Slowly. Curling and uncurling. He saw a row of eyes as big as his fist, black and shiny like a spider's. He saw a box with seven wax seals on it, and the box shifted, a little, on its shelf, as if something inside it had rolled over in its sleep.

He looked at the floor after that.

The vampire was at the far end of the room.

There was a circle cut into the stone floor there, with writing all around it, and in the middle of the circle was a tube of thick glass, seven feet tall, with silver threads running through it. Lucan was inside the tube, standing up, though Seth didn't think he was doing it on his own. He was still rigid. His coat had been taken off and his shirt cut away, and he stood there in his torn pants with his white marble chest bare and his arms at his sides, and his red eyes were following Harry Potter across the room with a look of such pure murderous hate that Seth could feel the hair on his arms go up.

Harry Potter stopped in front of the glass and looked in.

"Right," he said. "Lucan."

He made a small motion with one hand, and something in the circle on the floor changed. The writing glowed brighter. Inside the tube, the vampire's head moved.

Just his head. He jerked it, side to side, and his jaw worked, and he opened his mouth and a noise came out of him like a hiss and a scream at once, rattling the glass.

"There you go," said Harry Potter. "That's your head and your mouth. Everything else stays where it is. Let's have a chat."

"*Witch,*" said Lucan. His voice was cracked, rasping, accented with something old and Eastern European. "*Filthy—witch—sorcerer—you think you—when they find out, they will—*"

"Who's they?"

"*The Volturi will—*"

"Oh, the Volturi. Yeah. We know about the Volturi, mate. Do they know about you?" Harry Potter tilted his head. "They don't, do they. Don't know you from Adam. You're a nobody. Lone hunter. Two hundred and some years old and you've never once been to Italy. They'd kill you as soon as look at you, if they ever bothered to look." He paused. "And you're scared of them. You're very scared of them. I can feel it from here."

Lucan spat. It hit the inside of the glass and ran down, clear and thick. Seth smelled it, sharp and sweet. Venom.

"Thank you," said Harry Potter. "Saves me a needle."

He took a glass slide off the bench and a thin silver rod, and reached into the top of the open tube, *reached in*, his arm right over the edge where the vampire could have bitten it off if he'd been able to move anything but his head, and scraped the venom off the glass with the rod and smeared it on the slide and stepped back. He did it all quickly and without any fuss, the way Seth's mom scraped burnt bits out of a frying pan. He slid the slide under a brass instrument on the bench that looked like a cross between a microscope and a telescope, and looked into it, and was quiet for a minute.

"Huh," he said.

"What?" said Layla.

"Moves on its own. Even out of the body. That's interesting." He made a note in a notebook, with a pencil, in small cramped handwriting. "Doesn't need a host to stay active. You'd think it'd go inert." He looked up at Lucan. "That why the burn takes three days? It's working the whole time. Rewriting the house while the owner's still in it."

Lucan said something in a language Seth didn't know. It didn't sound like a compliment.

Harry Potter wrote something else down.

Then he put the notebook down and stepped back and looked at the vampire for a long moment.

"Imperio," he said.

He didn't say it loud. He said it the way you'd say *bless you*, and he didn't use his wand, and Seth didn't see anything happen. No light. No sound.

Layla made a sound beside him like she'd been punched.

Inside the tube, Lucan's face changed. All the hate went out of it, the way water goes out of a sink. It went slack. Peaceful. His eyes unfocused. For a second, just a second, he looked almost like a person.

Then his jaw clenched. His eyes snapped back into focus. And something in his face *fought*. Seth could see it. The muscles in his neck stood out like cables. His teeth ground together with a sound like rocks.

"Fighting it," Harry Potter said, half to himself. "That's interesting, too. Hardly anyone fights it that fast." He watched. "You've got a strong head, Lucan. Must be the venom. Everything's turned up. Perfect memory, too, I bet. Every face you've ever seen."

Lucan's mouth opened. It was trembling.

"*Tell me your name,*" said Harry Potter. Not loud. Pleasant.

"*Lu—Lucan.*" It came out of the vampire like it was being dragged.

"Where were you born?"

"*Brașov. Seventeen—seventeen—*" The jaw fought. "*Seventeen ninety-one.*"

"Who made you?"

Silence. The vampire's eyes bulged. Seth could see the effort it was taking, and he didn't know which side it was coming from, the vampire fighting or the man.

"*A woman. In Vienna. I never knew her name.*"

"Have you ever been to Volterra?"

"*No.*"

"Have you met any of them? Aro, Caius, Marcus? Any of the guard? Heard anything about them that the rest of your lot don't know?"

"*No. No. Only the—rules. Only what everyone knows. Keep the secret. Or they come.*"

"How many people have you killed since you came to Washington?"

Silence. The vampire's mouth worked.

"*Fourteen,*" Lucan said.

Layla's hand tightened on Seth's arm.

"The two on the Hoh," said Harry Potter. "In September. The couple from Portland."

"*Yes.*"

"Was it a bear?"

The vampire made a horrible sound. It took Seth a second to understand it was laughter.

"Thanks," said Harry Potter. "That's all I wanted." He made a small gesture, and the vampire sagged, all at once, like something had been cut. His head lolled. Then he came back to himself, snarling, thrashing his head against the glass.

Harry Potter picked up the pencil and wrote again. Seth watched him write. His hand was steady.

"That was the Imperius Curse," Layla said. Her voice was strange, high and thin. "That's an Unforgivable. That's—you'd get life in Azkaban for that. Anybody would. There's no—"

"Got a licence," said Harry Potter, still writing. "Want to see it?"

"*You're allowed?*"

"Unspeakables are. Always have been. It's in a drawer somewhere at the Ministry, in Latin." He finished his note, underlined something twice, and looked up. "Your Professor Quaid had it right, Miss Calloway. All three of the things. I've got all three."

Layla went white. "How do you know about Professor—"

"He was in your head about twenty minutes ago. Big chap in your memories. Three fingers." He put the pencil down. "Good teacher. You should write to him."

He went to the bench and picked up something Seth didn't recognize, a thing like a brass tuning fork with a crystal set in the crook of it, and walked up to the glass and touched the fork to it, and it made a long high clear note, and the crystal glowed. He watched the glow. Then he touched it to another spot. Then another. He wrote down numbers.

Then he cast something. Silently. A bright line of something like light went off his fingertip and through the gap in the top of the tube and hit Lucan on the shoulder, and Lucan shrieked, and there was a sound like a hammer hitting a stone, and Seth saw a crack appear on the vampire's marble shoulder. Fine. Hairline. White against white. It sealed itself up again while he watched, slowly, like ice forming over a puddle.

"Cutting curse," said Harry Potter. "Barely scratches. Heals in—" he looked at his watch, "—forty seconds. All right." Another note. "Reductor?"

He cast again. The sound this time was louder, and the vampire's head snapped back, and the crack on his chest was wider. It took longer to heal. Harry Potter timed it.

He cast four more spells. Seth didn't know what any of them were. Some of them did nothing. One of them made the vampire's skin go a strange blue color and steam, and the vampire screamed and screamed. One of them made the glass hum like a struck bell. Harry Potter wrote down every result in his little notebook in his small cramped handwriting, and his face didn't change at all. Not once.

Seth wanted to leave. He wanted to leave so badly his legs were shaking. He didn't, because Layla wasn't leaving, and Layla was standing beside him with her chin up and her face white and her eyes not moving off Harry Potter, and he wasn't going to leave her in here.

And because, under the wanting to leave, there was a part of him that was the wolf. A part of him that had fought these things since the spring. That had gone up a mountain last June with Edward and torn into Riley while Victoria screamed. That had heard the hikers laughing on the trail an hour ago, the woman pointing at the mushroom, and seen this thing in the shadow under the cedar, watching them. That part of him was steady. It was watching the cracks appear on the vampire's skin and *learning*.

He hated that part of him a little, right now.

"Last one," Harry Potter said.

He put the notebook down. He looked at Lucan. Lucan looked back at him, snarling, spitting, his red eyes full of hate and, Seth thought now, something else. Fear. Real fear.

"I need to know if it bites," Harry Potter said. Seth didn't know if he was talking to the vampire or to them or to himself. "If it doesn't, that's a whole branch of the plan gone, and better to know now. Two seconds."

"Wait," Layla said. "Wait. What are you—"

"*Crucio.*"

The vampire screamed.

Seth had heard vampires scream before. He'd heard them scream on the mountain last June, when he'd torn into Riley, and when they'd burned. He had never heard anything like this. It was *high*. It went up and up until it was past hearing and he could feel it in his teeth and his eyes, and the glass of the tube rang with it like a bell, and every jar on every shelf in the long white room rattled. The gray hand in its box clenched into a fist. The vampire's head thrashed. The rest of him couldn't move, and somehow that was the worst part, that his body stood perfectly still and straight like a mannequin while his head went back and forth, back and forth, and the scream went on, and on—

It stopped.

Two seconds. Harry Potter had said two seconds. It had been two seconds. Seth would have sworn on his dad's grave it had been a full minute.

The silence afterward rang. The jars stopped rattling one by one. In the tube, Lucan's head had dropped forward onto his chest, and he was making a small sound, over and over, a dry clicking sound in his throat, like a man crying with no tears.

Harry Potter picked up the notebook and wrote something down.

"Bites," he said quietly. "Right."

Seth found his voice. It came out as a growl.

"You didn't have to do that."

Harry Potter looked up.

His face held nothing Seth could name. He looked at Seth with those green eyes for a long time, and Seth made himself not look away.

"Yeah," said Harry Potter. "I did."

"He was *trapped*. He couldn't do anything. You already knew he—you had him. He'd told you everything—"

"He'd told me what he'd done. I needed to know what works on him." Harry Potter put the notebook down. "There's a castle in Italy, Balto. Three old men in it, a couple of hundred years older than him. Couple of dozen guards. One of them, a little blonde girl called Jane, can make you feel like you're on fire just by looking at you. Another one can take away all your senses, all at once, so you're blind and deaf and numb in the dark. They've got one who can find anyone anywhere in the world, and one who can make you love them, and one who can see every thought you've ever had by holding your hand. And sometime next year, if my boss gets his way, I'm going to walk into that castle with eleven other people who've got families. When I do, I want to know which of the things I can do will work on what's inside. So I need to know if the Cruciatus Curse works on the ones who don't need to breathe. I need to know if Imperius holds. I need to know if a Reductor does more than chip them. I can't learn that from a book, because there isn't one. I can't learn it from your friends up the river, because they're on my good list and I'm not going to put them in a box." He nodded at the tube. "So I'm learning it from him. He's eaten fourteen people. Two of them were a couple from Portland on a hiking holiday, and he followed them for three days before he did it, because he liked watching them be happy." He paused. "Want to see?"

Seth didn't say anything.

Harry Potter went to the silver basin on the bench, the one full of smoke-that-was-water. He touched two fingers to his temple and drew them away, and a long silvery thread came with them, like a strand of cobweb, and he dropped it into the basin, and the surface swirled.

"Pensieve," he said. "Stick your face in, if you want. It's from his head. Two seconds of it. That's all you'll need."

Seth looked at the basin.

He didn't put his face in. He didn't need to. He could see, on the surface of the silver stuff, just faintly, the shape of a tent, at night, and two figures inside it, outlined by a flashlight, and the flashlight moving as one of them laughed.

He looked away.

"Nice doesn't come into it," said Harry Potter. "I'm trying to be useful." He stripped off his blue gloves and dropped them in a bin. "And for the record, Balto, I get no kick out of it. It's a job. Somebody's got to do it, and I'm better at it than whoever'd do it if I didn't."

He looked at Lucan, in the tube, head down, clicking.

"I'm going to keep him a while," he said. "There's a lot more to learn. You'll want to go before I do any more of it." He turned back to them, and for the first time since the forest, his face did something that was almost tired. "Come on. Kitchen. I'm starving, and I still need that statement."

---

The kitchen was bright and smelled like coffee and sugar. Someone, somehow, had put a plate of sandwiches on the table while they were gone, and two slices of pie, and three mugs of tea. Seth couldn't tell if the man had done it from the other room without looking or if the kitchen did it on its own. He didn't ask.

He ate four of the sandwiches anyway. He couldn't help it. The wolf part of him was hungry, and it didn't care what it had just seen.

Harry Potter ate six. And the pie on the left, the one they hadn't been allowed to touch, which turned out to be pecan. He ate it straight out of the dish with a fork, sitting across the table from them with his shirtsleeves rolled up and his tie hanging loose and a glass of something brown beside him that wasn't tea.

He ate a lot. Seth noticed that. He ate the way Seth ate, the way the pack ate, steadily, a lot of it, like a furnace being fed.

"Right," Harry Potter said, through a mouthful of pie. "Miss Calloway. Statement. From the start, please. From the Thriftway."

Layla told him.

She told it plainly, holding her mug with both hands, her eyes on the table. The baking aisle. The salt. The heat coming off Seth's hand, and the way her wand had hummed in her hair. Five weeks. The kettle. The porch. Moose. The night of the Chinese food. What she'd told Seth, and the next day, what she'd told the Cullens. Everything. She didn't skip anything. She didn't make anything sound better than it was.

Harry Potter listened, eating, his notebook shut beside his plate. Once or twice he asked a question. *Did you name the school in Scotland, or did the mind-reader pull it out of your head first?* And, *Did the blonde one, Rosalie, actually see the transfiguration or just the result?* And, *How long did you hold the big one up?*

"About ten seconds," Layla said.

"With a straight Levitation Charm."

"Yeah."

"On something that weighs about as much as a small car." Harry Potter looked at her for a second. "That's impressive, actually. You'll have pulled something. Your shoulder hurt the next day?"

Layla blinked. "Yeah."

"Thought so. Next time, cushion it. Use a Featherweight first, then lift." He pointed at her with his fork. "You're good, Calloway. You're very good. That spell you threw at me in the woods. A Stunner in a piercing shell. You built that yourself, at seventeen, out of nothing. People get jobs in my department for less."

Layla looked at him. Her mouth opened. Nothing came out.

"I'm not offering," said Harry Potter. "Just saying." He pushed the empty pie dish away. "Here's what's going to happen. Your breach goes in my drawer. As far as MACUSA's concerned, you're a Horned Serpent girl gardening in a rainy town. They won't hear from me. In exchange, you're going to help me. You know the Cullens. They trust you, sort of. The wolves trust you, because he—" a nod at Seth, "—would walk into the sea if you told him to. When I talk to them tomorrow, I want you in the room."

"And if I say no?"

He shrugged. "Then you say no. Drawer's still a drawer."

Layla looked at him for a long moment.

"You'd really do it," she said. "Wouldn't you. Open it. If you needed to."

"Yeah."

"Even though you like me."

"I don't know you." He finished his drink. "But yeah. Even then."

Seth thought that was maybe the most honest thing anybody had ever said to him in his entire life, and also the most frightening.

"Okay," Layla said quietly. "Okay. I'll be there."

"Good." Harry Potter stood up. He took something out of his trouser pocket and dropped it on the table in front of Seth. It was a bead. Dark wood, black almost, smooth, with tiny silver lines running around it in a spiral, strung on a black cord. "That's holly. Bit harder-wearing than rowan, and it'll survive you phasing. Same principle as hers. Bite it and she gets the call. I've tied mine into it too. You bite it, I know about it."

Seth looked at it. "Why would I want you to know about it?"

"Because I'm faster than she is." Harry Potter picked up his coat. "And because that was your sister out there today, and your boss, and your girl. I've got a ward on this whole peninsula that catches the slow ones. Next one through might not be slow."

Seth picked up the bead. It was heavier than it looked.

"Thanks," he said. It came out grudging.

"Don't mention it. Seriously. Don't." Harry Potter held out his hand, the bare hand this time, palm up. "Come on. I'll drop you home. Then I've got work."

Seth took the hand. It was as hot as his.

Up close, holding it, he smelled it again: dirt and pine and cold stone, under the gun oil and the pie. And something else. Something underneath, deep down, that he'd smelled before and couldn't place, that made the wolf in his chest lift its head and go very, very still.

Then the kitchen folded up, and they were standing in the rain in Ida Calloway's back garden by the purple plant, and Moose was yowling at them from the porch rail, and Harry Potter was gone.

### XII. Two Minutes

At ten past eleven that night, Harry stood in front of the glass tube in the specimen room in his shirtsleeves with a cup of coffee and looked at Lucan for a long time.

Lucan had stopped making the clicking sound around nine. He'd been quiet since. He hung in the circle in his torn trousers with his eyes on Harry, and his eyes didn't move, and he didn't blink. He didn't need to.

"Lucan," Harry said. "Got a question for you."

The vampire said nothing.

"Do you believe in God?"

The vampire's lips pulled back. "*Do you, sorcerer?*"

"Dunno," said Harry. "Haven't decided. I've been in a room with Death, sort of. Seen the door. Talked to a bloke on the other side of it, once, in a train station that wasn't a train station. I still don't know what's past it. Nobody does. We've got a whole department working on it." He put the coffee down. "The doctor up the river thinks you lot have souls. Did you know that? Carlisle Cullen. He's three hundred and sixty-odd and he still thinks you've got souls. He's had a long time to think about it."

"*Cullen,*" said Lucan, with immense contempt. "*Animal drinkers. Pets.*"

"Yeah. Well. He's on my good list." Harry looked at him. "There's a curse. There's three, really, the ones they call Unforgivable, but there's one in particular. It doesn't hurt. It's very quick. Green light. And whatever it hits stops living. Doesn't matter what. Doesn't matter how strong. It doesn't damage the body. Nobody's ever been able to explain how it works, not properly. Best guess is it cuts something. The tie between the body and whatever's running the body. The soul, if you want to call it that." He picked up the brass instrument from the bench, a thing like a ship's barometer with three needles in it, and set it on the floor at the edge of the circle. "I've been wondering for three weeks whether it'll work on you."

Lucan's eyes went to the instrument. Then back to Harry's face.

He understood. Harry watched him understand.

"*They will—*"

"No," said Harry. "They won't. Nobody's coming, Lucan. Nobody's ever coming. You've got no one."

He looked at the vampire for a while longer. Fourteen. The tent on the Hoh at night. The flashlight moving as one of them laughed.

"Avada Kedavra," Harry said.

The light went across the room like a train going through a tunnel, green and enormous and silent, and filled the glass tube top to bottom, and the vampire went rigid, more rigid than he already was, every line of him locking at once.

On the instrument on the floor, all three needles swung to zero.

Lucan's red eyes went dull. Flat. Like a doll's. His head, which had been held up on its own, dropped forward, slowly, until his chin was on his chest.

Harry looked at his watch.

He stood there with his hands in his pockets and watched the second hand go round. Ten seconds. Twenty. The specimen room was perfectly silent. The Inferius hand on the shelf had stopped flexing. The box with the seven seals had gone still. Everything in the room, Harry noticed, had gone still. Waiting.

Thirty seconds. Forty.

At fifty-five seconds, the first needle on the instrument twitched.

Harry didn't move.

Sixty. The second needle came up off the zero. Then the third. Slowly. Like the arms of a clock being pushed by a finger.

At one hundred and eighteen seconds, Lucan's head came up off his chest. His eyes cleared. Red, wet, and very, very frightened.

He'd been somewhere. Harry could see that much in his face, without even touching his mind. He'd been somewhere for two minutes, and he'd come back, and he didn't know where he'd been.

"*What—*" Lucan's voice was a croak. "*What did you—what was—*"

"Son of a bitch," said Harry Potter, softly.

He picked up the notebook off the bench and wrote it down. *AK: effective. Not terminal. Full suspension of animus, all vital indicators zero. Duration 1:58. Spontaneous recovery. Subject conscious, disoriented, frightened. Recollection of interval: unknown.* He underlined *not terminal* twice. Then he stood and looked at it.

Two minutes.

Two minutes in which a thing that could move faster than a Stunner stood as still as a stone and didn't know where it was.

He thought about the castle. Three old men and a couple of dozen guards. Fire killed them. Fire was slow. Fire needed them to be in pieces first, and getting them into pieces was the hard part, and every second it took was a second something faster than you could see was coming for your throat.

But two minutes. Two minutes was a window. Two minutes was a long time if you had a lighter.

He finished his coffee. He went back to the den and propped the mirror on the sugar bowl and said, "Croaker."

It was seven in the morning in London. Croaker was eating porridge.

"Potter."

"Got one."

Croaker put down his spoon.

Harry told him. All of it. Lucan, the Bogachiel trail, the wolves. The Imperius, which held for nineteen seconds before the vampire started pushing back, which was about the time it took a well-trained wizard. The Cruciatus, which bit, which bit badly. The cutting curse, the Reductor, the freezing charm that had made the skin steam. The venom, and how it kept moving on its own after it left the body. And the Killing Curse.

Croaker was quiet for a long time after that.

"Two minutes," he said finally.

"One fifty-eight."

"Every time?"

"Only done it once. Next time I'll try two at once. See if it stacks." Harry rubbed his eyes. "And I want to try basilisk venom. And Fiendfyre, small. I want to see how small I can make it and still have it take. I want to see what a vampire does to Gubraithian fire. I want to try a Dementor, if we can get one to the States, which we can't. I want a second specimen to compare. I want—" He stopped.

"You want a lot of things."

"I'm an Unspeakable. Wanting things is the job."

Croaker almost smiled. "And there's something else."

Harry looked at him through the mirror.

"The witch," he said. "In the town. American. Calloway. She told them. All of them. Wolves and coven, everything, chapter and verse, the Statute and the Ministries and Hogwarts and me. Well. Not me, technically. She told them about the Boy Who Lived, which is me in a hat. The mind-reader got my face out of her head before I ever met him."

"Ah."

"She's in my drawer. I'm using her. She's good, Saul. She's very good. Self-taught spellwork you'd want to see. And they all trust her, which is going to save me about a month of being looked at sideways." He paused. "I'm going up to see them tomorrow. All of them. I'm telling them about Volterra."

Croaker looked at him over his spectacles for a long moment.

"How much?"

"Enough," said Harry. "The doctor lived with them. Decades. He'll know things Benedetti never got near. He'll know the guard by name, who has what gift, how the castle's laid out, how they feed, who matters. If I want that, I have to give him a reason to give it. So I tell him what's coming. And I tell him Aro's going to want his son and his daughter on a shelf one day, which he already knows, and I tell him we're the only people on earth who might stop that." He shrugged. "That's the pitch."

"And the child?"

Harry's face didn't change.

"No," he said.

"Potter—"

"No, Saul. She's a kid. She's ten weeks old. She's got a heartbeat. I'm not putting her on a list. I'm not putting her in a report. I'm not having somebody in a room in London start wondering what's in her blood. If anyone from the Department ever comes near her, it'll be me, and I'll be the one standing in the door." He said it flatly. "I don't do kids. That's not a negotiation."

Croaker looked at him for a long time.

"I'll note your objection," he said, at last.

"Note it in ink."

"Goodnight, Potter."

The mirror went dark. Harry sat in the den with the rain on the canvas and the map on the table, eleven silver stars on the peninsula and a cluster of gold ones by the river, and one small gold light in the middle of them, beating.

He got up and turned the radio on, low. Led Zeppelin, "Ramble On." He went and cut himself the last slice of pecan pie and ate it standing at the map.

Down the hall, in the cold white room, the vampire had started to scream again, very softly, in a language nobody had spoken in Brașov for two hundred years. Harry let him. He'd stop when he got tired of it. They didn't get tired, as it turned out. That was in the notebook too.

### XIII. Noon

*The next day.*

Carlisle Cullen had been alive for something over three hundred and sixty years, and in all that time, he had never once been visited by anyone who brought pie.

The man arrived at eleven fifty-eight. They heard him coming from four miles off. Edward heard him first, or rather heard nothing, and that was how he knew. "There's a gap," he said, standing at the glass wall with his hands in his pockets. "Coming up the highway. Like a hole driving toward us." And then they all heard the engine, a deep, old-fashioned, rumbling growl that rose and fell through the trees, and Emmett said, "Is that a *motorcycle*?" and Rosalie, who knew engines the way Carlisle knew bones, tipped her head and listened and said, "Triumph. Late fifties. Mostly. Something's wrong with it." Then she listened longer and frowned. "Something's very wrong with it. It's running too smoothly."

The motorcycle came up the drive in the rain and stopped in front of the porch. The rider swung off, pulled off his helmet, unhooked a white bakery box from a bungee cord on the back, and walked up the steps.

He wore an old brown leather jacket, a gray flannel shirt, jeans, boots. He carried the bakery box flat on one palm, like a waiter.

Carlisle opened the door before he could knock.

"Doc," said the man.

"Mr. Evans," said Carlisle.

The man smiled very slightly. "Potter, these days. Sorry about that."

"How is your hand?"

The man held up his left hand and turned it over. The palm was clean. No scar. Not even a line. Eleven stitches, three weeks ago, and Carlisle's work was good, but it wasn't *that* good. The skin there was smooth as a child's.

"Healed up nice," said Harry Potter. "Good doctor."

Carlisle looked at the palm a moment longer than politeness required. Then he looked at the man's face. He thought about the chart he'd written, that night in the ER. *Pt. calm. HR 58. No distress. Clean lac, ? self-inflicted.* He thought about how the man's skin had felt, through two layers of latex. Hot. He'd noted that too, and then he'd been called away to a car accident on 101, and he'd let it go.

"Please," Carlisle said, and stepped back. "Come in."

"Ta." Harry Potter stepped over the threshold. Then he stopped, just inside the door, and his eyes went across the room, once.

Carlisle watched them go. He'd seen soldiers do that, in three centuries of wars. He'd seen police officers do it. Never quite like this. The man's gaze touched every person in the big white room, one after another, and he seemed to put each of them somewhere in his head and move on, and it took about a second and a half.

Esme by the kitchen doorway. Rosalie and Emmett by the stairs. Alice and Jasper, near the glass wall; Jasper standing a half-step in front of Alice, very still. Edward by the piano. Jacob on the couch, human, enormous, arms crossed, hostile. Leah by the window, the same. Seth on the far end of the couch, and the Calloway girl beside him, pale and composed, her wand in her hair, her hand in his.

And Bella, in the armchair farthest from the door, holding Renesmee in her lap.

Bella was not breathing. She had stopped breathing the moment the motorcycle turned into the drive. She was watching the man in the doorway the way Carlisle had seen her watch the deer at the edge of the meadow, in her first week, before she'd learned not to.

Harry Potter looked at her. Then he lifted his free hand, the one not holding the pie, and made a small gesture at himself, a downward sweep, as though brushing rain off his jacket.

Carlisle smelled the change before he understood it. One moment, there had been a human in the doorway. Blood, pine, gun oil, sugar from the bakery box, something like turned earth. The next moment, most of it was simply gone, as if the man had stepped behind a pane of glass. The smell that remained was faint. Leather. Rain. The pie.

In the armchair, Bella's shoulders came down an inch. Very carefully, she took a small breath. Then another.

"Sorry," said Harry Potter, to her. "Should've done that on the porch. You all right?"

Bella stared at him. "You—what did you do?"

"Damper. On the scent. Wouldn't want to be rude to the new girl." He looked at Renesmee, who was looking back at him with enormous brown eyes, and his face changed. It was a small change. Something in it eased. "Hello, you."

Renesmee held out her hand.

Every person in the room moved. Jacob was off the couch. Bella's arms tightened. Edward took half a step.

Harry Potter didn't move at all. He looked at the small outstretched hand, and then at Bella. He didn't come any closer.

"Up to you," he said to Bella. "I don't mind either way."

Bella looked at Edward. Edward, Carlisle saw, had nothing to give her. For the first time in their marriage, Edward couldn't tell his wife what a man in the room was thinking. Bella looked back at the stranger. At his empty hand, resting at his side. At the pie.

She stood up and carried Renesmee across the room, three steps, and stopped in front of him.

Renesmee reached up and put her palm flat on the man's cheek.

Carlisle watched his face. He couldn't see what his granddaughter showed the man. He could guess. She'd been at the glass wall all morning, ever since Edward had told them who was coming, and Carlisle had seen her watching the drive. She had heard about the man who caught the spell. She had seen it in Seth's face, when Seth told it, last night, in this room, with his hands still shaking.

Harry Potter stood very still under the small hand.

Then he laughed. Just once, a short surprised breath through his nose. "Yeah," he said softly. "I suppose that is what it looked like." He looked down at her. "You're a clever one, aren't you."

Renesmee took her hand back and looked pleased with herself.

Then something happened that only Carlisle, perhaps, and Edward, and Alice, who missed nothing, saw.

Harry Potter's eyes went from the child to her mother. And stayed there. And narrowed, very slightly, the way a man's eyes narrow when he walks into a wall in the dark that he didn't know was there.

"Huh," said Harry Potter.

"What?" said Bella.

"Nothing. Just tried something and hit a wall." He looked at her with open interest. "Was that you? Or her?"

Edward was across the room before anyone saw him move, standing at Bella's shoulder. "What did you try?"

"Just a little look. Nothing deep." Harry Potter didn't look at Edward. He was still looking at Bella. "That was you, wasn't it. Some kind of shield." He shook his head slowly. "I've been inside the minds of Death Eaters who trained under Voldemort. Couldn't get a toe through that. That's a hell of a thing, Mrs. Cullen."

Bella's chin came up. "Don't do it again."

"Fair." He held up his free hand. "Won't."

Edward said nothing. But Carlisle saw his son's face, and saw something cross it that he hadn't seen there before, which was a sort of grim, reluctant pride.

"Well," said Harry Potter. He held up the bakery box. "I brought pie. Apple and cherry, from the diner. I'm told none of you eat, so that's mostly for the dogs. And me." He looked around. "Where d'you want it?"

---

They sat in the big white living room. Esme brought plates for the wolves and the witch and the guest. She brought forks. She brought, unbidden, a knife and a pie server, and set them down at Harry Potter's elbow, and he looked up at her and said, "Thanks, Mrs. Cullen," and she looked at him in the way she looked at boys who were too thin, though he was not thin, and said, "Esme. Please."

He ate a slice of the cherry before he said anything else. Then he wiped his mouth with a paper napkin, put the plate down on the white coffee table, leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, and began.

He told it plainly. Carlisle would remember that, later. No speeches. No drama. He laid it out the way a surgeon lays out instruments.

The Department of Mysteries, what it was, what it did; as much as he could say, which was not very much. Benedetti, the man in the square in Volterra. The letter. The investigation that had followed, seven months of it, quiet. The forty-one British nationals who had walked into Volterra and not walked out since 1950. Three of them wizards. The tour groups. Heidi, who led them in.

"You know her," he said to Carlisle, flatly.

"I knew of her," Carlisle said. "She came after my time." His voice was steady. He did not feel steady. "I left Volterra in the eighteenth century. They were—different, then. Fewer. Aro was gathering, even then. He liked talent. He liked to collect."

"Still does." Harry Potter looked at Edward, and then at Alice. "He tried to collect you two in March, didn't he? Asked you to stay. Asked nicely."

Edward said nothing. Alice nodded once, slowly.

"He'll ask again," said Harry Potter. "Not nicely, next time. That's what he does. He waits. He's patient. He's got forever. One day he'll find a reason, and he'll turn up on your lawn with his guard behind him and a long face about the law, and when he leaves, he'll have your son and your daughter, and the rest of you will be ash." He let that sit for a second. "That's from twenty pages of Benedetti's report."

The room was very quiet.

"And now Benedetti's gone missing," Harry Potter said. "Nearly three weeks ago. Missed his check-in. We think they've got him. If they've got him, they know about us. The witches and wizards. And if Aro knows that there's a whole hidden world full of people who can do things—" he spread his hands, "—then every witch and wizard in Europe just became a collector's item. And he's going to start shopping."

"So you're going to attack them," said Jasper.

It was the first thing Jasper had said. He said it quietly, from beside Alice, with his scarred hands folded behind his back like a soldier at rest. He was looking at Harry Potter with something Carlisle couldn't quite name.

"That's the idea."

"You," said Jasper. "And how many?"

"Twelve."

"Against Aro, Caius, Marcus, Jane, Alec, Demetri, Felix, Chelsea, Renata, Heidi, Afton, Santiago, Corin, and however many others they've collected that we've never heard of." Jasper's voice did not rise. "With *wands*."

"With wands," Harry Potter agreed. "And guns. And a few things I made up. And whatever else I can find out between now and then." He looked at Jasper for a long moment. "You were in a war, weren't you? Before. Long before this lot. You've got the look."

Jasper's face did not move. "Several."

"Then you know." Harry Potter leaned back. "You don't walk into a war without knowing what's on the other side. You find out. You find out everything. You find out what they eat and how they sleep and who hates who and what kills them. Then you go in."

"And how," said Rosalie, cold and clear from the stairs, "are you finding out what kills them?"

Harry Potter looked up at her.

Carlisle saw Seth, on the couch, go still. He saw the Calloway girl's hand tighten on Seth's. He saw Leah, by the window, look at the floor.

"I caught one," said Harry Potter. "Yesterday. Lucan. A nomad, from Romania, originally. He's eaten fourteen people since August. Two of them on the Hoh. Your wolves chased him into my snare. I've been asking him questions since."

The temperature in the room seemed to drop by several degrees.

"Asking him," said Rosalie.

"Asking him."

"What *kind* of questions?"

Harry Potter looked at her steadily. He did not look away, and he did not look ashamed.

"All kinds," he said. "Cutting curses chip you. Reductors crack you. Heal in about a minute. Imperius works but you fight it, harder than most people, and you start fighting it at about nineteen seconds. The Cruciatus Curse works. Works very well." He paused. "And last night I hit him with the Killing Curse."

Nobody breathed. In this house, that was not unusual. But Carlisle heard the wolves stop breathing, and the witch, and the child.

"It put him down," said Harry Potter. "Everything stopped. Every reading I've got. Gone. Flat. For a hundred and eighteen seconds."

He let that land.

"And then he came back."

Edward said, very quietly, "And Lucan?"

"Still in a box," said Harry Potter. "Still screaming, last I checked. He'll be there a while. He's got a lot more to teach me."

The silence went on for a long time.

Carlisle had lived more than three hundred and sixty years. He had walked out of Volterra and into the world. He had watched the world burn and rebuild itself four or five times over. He had been a doctor for most of it. He knew what the man sitting on his couch was. He had known it, he thought, since the ER, since the clean-edged wound and the steady heart. A man who would cut his own hand open to get close enough to read you.

He also knew, the way he had known in the eighteenth century in a castle in Italy, what the alternative was. What Aro was. What Aro would do. To Alice. To Edward. To Bella, now, with her shield. To his granddaughter.

He thought about the bakery box on the coffee table. About the man stopping in the doorway to hide his scent from a newborn so as not to be rude. About him waiting, empty-handed, for a mother's permission before letting a child touch his face.

"You've told us a great deal," Carlisle said. "More than you needed to."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

Harry Potter looked at him for a while. Then he shrugged, a small movement.

"Because I'm going to need you," he said. "You lived with them. You know things nobody else alive does. I'm not going to get those things by lying to you, because you'd work it out, and I'm not going to get them out of your head, because I said I wouldn't, and because—" a glance at Bella, a dry twist of the mouth, "—apparently I can't get into half the heads in this house anyway. So I'm telling you. You know what I am now. You know what I've got in a box. You know what's in Italy. You know what's coming." He spread his hands. "I'd rather walk into that castle with you on my side than without. But it's your call, Doc."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then I finish my pie, and I go home, and I keep doing my job. And if the Volturi ever come up that drive—" he nodded at the glass wall, at the gray November light and the dripping trees and the long gravel road down to the highway, "—I'll still be standing at the end of it. You're on my list. That's not up for negotiation either."

"Why?" said Edward.

Harry Potter turned and looked at him. "Why what?"

"Why do you care?" Edward's hands had come out of his pockets. "You don't know us. You've watched us for three weeks from a tree. You've told us, plainly, that your government thinks we're more *useful* alive. That we're assets. You have a man in a glass box you've been torturing for a day. Why would you stand at the end of our drive?"

For the first time, Harry Potter didn't answer right away.

He looked at Renesmee, in Bella's lap. Renesmee looked back at him.

"Because I've got a godson," said Harry Potter. "He's eight. His hair changes color when he laughs. His mum and dad died in a war when he was a month old, in a castle, fighting things that wanted to put people like him in boxes." He shrugged again, smaller. "And because I don't do kids. I've said that twice now. Once to my boss and once to Clifford. You can ask Clifford."

On the couch, Jacob Black uncrossed his arms. Slowly. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

Carlisle looked around the room. At Esme, who was looking at him. At Edward, who was looking at Bella. At Jasper, whose face was very still, and who met Carlisle's eyes and, after a long moment, gave the smallest nod. At Rosalie, cold, furious, unreadable, who looked at Renesmee and then at the man on the couch and set her jaw and said nothing at all, which from Rosalie was something.

At Alice, who was frowning at nothing, the way she frowned when she was trying to look somewhere and couldn't.

"I can't see you," Alice said abruptly. "At all. Layla's static. You're—nothing. You're a hole in everything. Worse than the wolves."

"Sorry," said Harry Potter. "I make decisions late. Drives my boss mad."

"How late?"

"About as late as it's possible to make them."

Alice stared at him. Then something in her face changed, and she laughed. It came out of her sharp and startled. "You *do* it on purpose."

"Bit." He almost smiled. "Habit. Comes from a lot of people trying to predict where I'd be so they could kill me. Turns out if you don't know yourself, they can't either."

Carlisle let out a breath he did not need.

"Mr. Potter," he said.

"Harry. Or *oi, you*. I'm not fussy."

"Harry." Carlisle leaned forward. "Volterra was built into a hill. The Volturi live beneath it. You'll want to know about the tunnels. There are more of them than anyone outside the guard has ever seen. Aro's chambers are in the oldest part. Marcus rarely leaves the throne room. Caius keeps his wife in the tower, and so does Aro, and the wives have their own guard. And Chelsea—you'll want to know about Chelsea first. Before any of the others." He stopped. "What else do you need to know?"

Harry Potter looked at him for a long moment.

Then he reached into his jacket and took out a small battered notebook and a pencil, and opened the notebook on his knee to a fresh page.

"Everything, Doc," he said. "From the beginning."

Outside the great glass wall, the rain kept coming down over the river and the trees and the long gravel drive, gray and patient and endless, the way it had come down on this peninsula for ten thousand years. Somewhere eleven miles south, in a glass tube in a cold white room in a tent that was bigger on the inside, a vampire had finally gone quiet.

On the couch, Seth Clearwater reached over without looking and took the last slice of cherry pie.

"Oi," said Harry Potter, still writing.

"You had three."

"I had *two*."

"You had three," said Leah, from the window. It was the first thing she'd said all day.

Harry Potter looked up at her. He looked at her for a moment with an expression Carlisle couldn't read.

"Fair enough, Lassie," he said, and went back to his notebook.

Leah's eyes narrowed. But she didn't say anything else. And Carlisle, who had been watching people for a very long time, thought that the corner of her mouth moved. Just a little.

Then he began to talk about Volterra.

*End of Chapter One.*
