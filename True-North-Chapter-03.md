# True North

## Chapter Three: Do Not Reply to the Signature

---

### I.

Something was knocking on the window.

Leah came awake the way she always did now, all at once, with her heart already going and her body halfway off the mattress before her brain caught up. Gray light. Log walls. The smell of woodsmoke and lake water and, all through the quilt and the pillow and her own hair, pine and wet stone and creek-water over granite.

She was still wrapped in his coat. She'd slept in it.

*Tok. Tok. Tok.*

She turned her head.

There was an owl on the windowsill.

It was the biggest owl she had ever seen, the size of a cocker spaniel, dark brown with tawny blotches down the back of its neck and a pale breast crossed with heavy bars, and two tufts of feathers sticking up off its head like horns. Its feet were huge and feathered. Its eyes were the color of a traffic cone.

It was looking at her.

It lifted one foot and knocked on the glass with a hooked black talon. *Tok.* Then it put the foot down and kept looking at her, with an expression that said, very clearly, that it had come a long way and did not intend to repeat itself.

Leah sat up in bed and stared back.

A small cream envelope was tied to one leg with waxed string. Beside the owl, propped against the window frame, was a little box the size of a deck of cards.

"No," Leah said.

The owl knocked again. *Tok.*

"It's six in the morning."

*Tok. Tok.*

She got out of bed and unlatched the window and pushed it open. Cold air came in off the lake. The owl did not move one feather. It turned its head very slowly, all the way around until it was nearly upside down, and looked at her from that angle instead.

"Okay," Leah said. "That's creepy."

The owl clacked its beak at her.

Up close, it smelled like feathers and mice and cold sky, and very faintly, under that, like *him*. Pine. Stone. It had been in his hands—his paws—recently. She could smell it on the string.

She untied the envelope. The owl let her, then nudged the little box toward her with its beak, the way a cat pushes something off a table, and fluffed itself up to twice its size and settled on the sill like a hen on a nest.

It was clearly not leaving.

Leah stood there in her cut-offs and T-shirt and his coat, with an envelope in one hand and a box in the other, and a giant owl sitting on her windowsill like it paid rent.

The envelope said, in loopy black handwriting that slanted hard to the right and looked like it had been done with a quill: *Leah Clearwater. Cabin Four. Brewster's Cabins. Atlin. (By the lake. The one with the coat in it.)*

She turned it over. On the back, pressed into a blob of dark green wax, was a seal: a shield with something on it she couldn't make out. Some kind of animal. A dog, maybe. Or a—

She broke the seal with her thumbnail and took out the card.

Heavy paper, thick and creamy, wedding-invitation paper. The same loopy scrawl inside, blotted in places, like he'd been too impatient to wait for the ink.

She sat down on the edge of the bed and read it.

---

> *Leah,*
>
> *Thank you for last night.*
>
> *I've been sitting here trying to think of a better word for it and I keep coming back to "amazing," which is a crap word, because people use it about sandwiches. So: it was the best night I've had in a very long time. Possibly ever. And I've been to a dragon-riding stag do in Romania, so that's saying something.*
>
> *You beat me at pool. You fed me a hate crime. You laughed at me. I'd like all three again, in roughly that order.*
>
> *Here's the thing. I grew up, from the age of eleven anyway, in aristocratic Britain. The wizarding part of it, which is about three hundred years behind everybody else on manners and proud of it. Old families. Old houses. Portraits that shout at you. And some things stay with a person whether they like it or not, and one of them is how you court a lady. After the first evening, you send a note. You say thank you. And you send something with it. My godfather's mother would climb out of her portrait frame and scream the house down if I didn't, and she's been dead twenty years, so you can imagine how strongly she feels about it.*
>
> *So. In the box.*
>
> *It's a bracelet. I made it myself this afternoon, after they finally let me out of the debrief, so it's a bit rough. It's keyed to you and only you. Put it on your left wrist. When you change, it'll size itself and sit on your foreleg, and whatever you're wearing goes into it, folded up very small. When you change back, it all comes back out, on you. Clothes. Shoes. Coat. The lot. Exactly how you had it on. You won't have to tie anything to your ankle ever again, and you won't be stood naked in a clearing while some idiot stares at a spruce tree.*
>
> *It won't come off unless you take it off. It won't break. It doesn't track you. I know you're thinking it. It doesn't. You can stand on it and stamp and swear at it if you like; I'd expect nothing less.*
>
> *Saturday. Nine o'clock, your time. I'll be early. I'll bring breakfast.*
>
> *Mind the owl. His name's Biltong. He's a Cape eagle-owl and he's got no manners at all and he'll sit there until you give him something to eat or something to carry back. Bacon works. Fingers also work, as far as he's concerned, so mind those.*
>
> *Thank you, Leah. Really.*
>
> *Harry*
>
> *P.S. Keep the coat till Saturday. I'm aware you were going to anyway.*

---

And then, underneath, in a completely different handwriting, small and neat and printed in a dark violet ink that shimmered faintly when she tilted the card, like oil on water:

---

> *Sir Harry James Potter, Order of Merlin, First Class*
> *Head of the Ancient and Noble House of Potter*
> *Head of the Noble and Most Ancient House of Black*
> *Senior Hit Wizard, Enforcement Office, International Confederation of Wizards (Geneva)*
> *Triwizard Champion, 1995 (jointly, with C. Diggory)*
> *Special Award for Services to the School, Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry, 1993*
> *Member, the Order of the Phoenix*
> *Honorary Auror, Ministry of Magic, Great Britain*
> *Youngest House Seeker in a Century; Captain (ret.), Gryffindor House Quidditch Team*
> *Patron, Lycanthropy Research Unit, St Mungo's Hospital for Magical Maladies and Injuries*
> *Honorary Life Member, Cape Lycanthrope Welfare Society*
> *Chocolate Frog Card No. 497, "Famous Witches and Wizards" (2001 series)*
> *Absentee Governor, Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry*
> *Third Place, Muizenberg Longboard Classic, Novice Division, 2003*
>
> *This signature has been appended automatically by the ICW Correspondence Office. Please do not reply to the signature.*

---

Leah read it twice.

Then she read it a third time, out loud, slowly, to the owl.

"*Sir* Harry James Potter."

The owl looked at her.

"*Order of Merlin.*" She looked up. "What's an Order of Merlin?"

The owl blinked, one eye and then the other, which was somehow worse than blinking both.

"*Chocolate Frog Card.*" Leah lowered the card. "He's on a *card*. Like a *baseball card*. On a *frog*."

The owl ruffled its feathers.

"*Third place.*" She was starting to laugh now, she couldn't help it, it was coming up out of her chest the same way it had at the pool table. "*Novice division.* He put his *surfing* on here. Who puts their surfing on—"

*Please do not reply to the signature.*

She bent over on the bed with the card in both hands and laughed until her eyes stung.

She sat up and wiped her eyes, and read it again, and this time she stopped on the third line, and the laugh went out of her.

*Head of the Noble and Most Ancient House of Black.*

Black.

She'd put that in a box last night and sat on the lid. And here it was again, printed in shimmering violet ink, on a card on her lap, with *Head of* in front of it.

She thought of Billy Black on the porch of the little red house, in his wheelchair with the blanket over his knees. Jacob's dad. The head of the Black family, she supposed, if you wanted to say it like that, which nobody in La Push ever had.

*Common name,* Harry had said. *Everybody knows some Blacks.*

She put the card face down on the quilt.

Then she picked up the box.

It was plain dark wood, smooth, with a sliding lid. She slid it open. Inside, on a fold of black velvet, was the bracelet.

Nothing fancy. That was her first thought, and she was relieved, because if it had been gold or diamonds or something she'd have had to throw it in the lake on principle. It was a thin band of braided leather, soft and supple, three strands twisted together and finished at each end with a small knot. There was one bead, a flat disc of something black and glassy, polished smooth, with a tiny angular mark cut into it that might have been a letter in an alphabet she didn't know.

The three strands of leather were black, gray, and green.

Black, like him. Gray, like her fur. Green, like—

"Oh, you *jerk*," Leah said.

She knew exactly what he'd done, and she hated him for it, and her wolf was sitting up under her ribs with its ears forward, making a small, interested sound she pretended not to hear.

She held it on her palm. It held a faint heat of its own, and when she brought it close to her face, it smelled of leather and pine and something else, sharp and clean, like the air after a thunderstorm. The same smell as the bar of white-violet light he'd put through Vadas's chest.

Magic, she thought. That's what magic smells like.

She sat there for a long time holding it.

Then she put it on her left wrist.

The ends slid together on their own. She felt them do it, a little tug, like a cat's tongue, and when she looked, the two knots had become one knot, and there was no gap in the band anywhere. It fit her wrist exactly. It wasn't tight. It wasn't loose. It sat there against her skin like it had always been there.

She tugged at the band. It didn't move.

*It won't come off unless you take it off.*

She thought, *I want to take it off*, and the knot went loose under her fingers, and the bracelet slid off her wrist into her palm.

She put it back on. The knot closed.

"Huh," she said.

The owl was standing on the sill again, looking at her with its traffic-cone eyes. The transaction was clearly not complete.

"I don't have any bacon," Leah said.

The owl shifted its weight from one foot to the other and looked pointedly at her fingers.

"Don't even think about it."

---

The Gold Pan opened at seven. Leah was there at seven-oh-two with the owl.

She hadn't meant to bring it. It had launched itself off the windowsill with a sound like a bedsheet being shaken out and followed her down the road from telephone pole to telephone pole, and when she went into the café it landed on the roof of Ray's truck outside the window and sat there.

Ray looked at it through the window. Then he looked at Leah.

"There's an owl," he said.

"Yeah."

"On my truck."

"It followed me."

"It's the size of my dog."

"It wants bacon."

Ray looked at her for a long time. He looked at the owl. He looked at the coat she was wearing, which was very clearly a man's coat, and very clearly not one she'd owned on Tuesday.

"Josie's off today," he said finally. "Migraine's still got her. You want the usual?"

"Yeah. And a side of bacon. To go."

"For the owl."

"For the owl."

Ray sighed the sigh of a man who'd been running a café in a town of three hundred people for twenty years and had learned that some questions weren't worth the trouble.

Leah ate two Lumberjacks in her booth while the owl watched her through the window. Gord came in and looked at the owl, and then at Leah.

Gord leaned over from his stool. "Heard something funny this morning," he said, to nobody in particular. "That photographer fella. Vale. Packed up and left in the night. His truck's gone, Aldridge place is all locked up. Shutters on the windows and everything. Like nobody's been there in a year."

Leah kept eating.

"Didn't even settle up at the store," Gord said. "Owes me for a roll of plastic sheeting. Funny."

"Never did like him," Ray said, from the grill. "Tipped too much."

"That's what Marg says," said Gord.

"Marg's right about most things," said Ray.

Leah finished her coffee and paid and took the paper bag of bacon outside. The owl dropped off the truck roof and landed on the porch rail beside her, so heavily that the rail creaked, and looked at the bag.

She took out a strip of bacon and held it out.

The owl took it so fast she didn't see it move, swallowed with its head tipped back, and looked at her again.

She gave it another strip. And another. It ate eleven strips of bacon, one after the other, and then it fluffed itself up to twice its size and closed its eyes in satisfaction, and she swore the thing almost looked like it was smiling.

"You're his owl, all right," Leah said.

Then she went back inside, and borrowed a pen from Ray, and took a paper napkin out of the dispenser on the counter, and sat down in her booth and wrote on it for a long time, and crossed it out, and took another napkin, and wrote on that one.

When she was done, she folded it into a small square and took it outside and held it up, and the owl opened its eyes and stuck out one leg, like it had been doing this all its life, which it probably had.

She tied the napkin on with the string from the envelope.

The owl looked at her for one more second. Then it dropped off the rail, spread its wings, and was gone over the roofs of Atlin, low and fast and silent, heading south.

---

### II.

She went into the trees behind Marg's cabins a little after noon, because she couldn't stand it anymore.

She'd caught herself all morning turning the bracelet around her wrist with her right thumb, over and over, the way she used to turn the engagement ring Sam had given her, and she'd made herself stop.

She needed to know if it worked.

She stood in the spruce with the coat on over her cut-offs and T-shirt and thought about Seth, who'd be in third period, and Jacob, who'd probably be on that white couch with the baby, making airplane noises.

If he was phased, he'd see. All of it.

Then she thought, *Screw it*, and let the heat come up.

She knew every part of phasing by now, every crack and stretch and burn, the way you know the potholes on the road to your own house. This time there was something new. In the first half-second, as her skin went hot and tight, the bracelet closed on her wrist, gently, like a hand. And then there was a soft, strange pulling all over her body at once, a feeling like being undressed very fast by somebody with a lot of practice, and the cut-offs and the T-shirt and the long heavy coat were just *gone*, and there was nothing between her skin and the cold air at all for one blink of an instant—

—and then there was fur, and paws, and the world opened up.

She looked down.

Around her left foreleg, just above the paw, was the bracelet. It had grown. It sat snugly in her gray fur, braided black and gray and green, with the little black disc catching the light.

There was nothing on the ground but spruce needles.

*Leah?*

Jacob. Of course.

She felt him come into focus in her head, the big flat church-voice of him, and she felt him notice everything at once: the woods, the coat that wasn't there, the bracelet on her leg. And behind it, because there was no behind in here, everything else. The owl on the windowsill. The card.

She tried not to think about the card.

You couldn't try not to think about something in the pack mind. It was like trying not to think about an elephant. It came up whole, every word, in Harry's loopy scrawl: *Thank you for last night. It was the best night I've had in a very long time. Possibly ever.*

And then the violet ink underneath.

*Sir Harry James Potter.*

Jacob was very quiet.

*Order of Merlin,* he said finally. *First Class.*

*Don't.*

*Chocolate Frog Card.*

*Jake. Don't.*

*He put his surfing on there.*

*I know. I already laughed about it. It's done. It's been laughed about.*

*Third place,* Jacob said, and she felt something in him crack, just for a second, and a laugh came up out of him in her head, short and helpless, and was gone. Then he went quiet again, and she knew exactly what he was looking at, because she was looking at it too, in her memory, in his memory, in the place where their memories overlapped and couldn't be pulled apart.

*Head of the Noble and Most Ancient House of Black.*

*It's a common name,* Leah said. *He told me. Everybody knows some Blacks.*

*Yeah,* Jacob said. *You said.*

He didn't say anything else for a long moment. She stood in the trees on four paws with the cold air ruffling her fur and waited.

*Dad told me once,* Jacob said, slowly, *that our name came from a white guy. Way back. Like, eighteen-hundreds. Some trader who came up the coast and stayed and married into the tribe. Nobody remembers his first name or anything about him. Just that he was a white guy and his name was Black, and the kids kept it.*

Leah stood very still.

*There were a hundred white traders on the coast back then,* she said.

*Yeah.*

*Half of them stayed. Half of them married into somebody's tribe.*

*Yeah,* Jacob said. *I know.*

*It's a crazy thing to think, Jake.*

*I know it is,* Jacob said. *I'm not thinking it. You're not thinking it.*

They were both thinking it.

She felt him put it down, carefully, like setting a glass on a high shelf. She did the same.

*The bracelet,* Jacob said, in a different voice. *What's it do?*

*I'm about to find out.*

She let the heat go.

The phase back came on the way it always did, the fire draining out of her bones, the shrinking, the strange lurch of the world going dim and flat and human. Her paws became hands on the spruce needles. Her spine shortened. She was kneeling, bent over, breathing hard—

—and the pulling came again, the other way. That fast, strange, all-over sensation, like being *dressed* this time, by somebody with a lot of practice and very cold hands. Something heavy settled across her shoulders.

She looked down.

Cut-offs. T-shirt. And the coat, all the way down to her ankles, the silver clasp done up at the throat, exactly the way she'd had it.

Not a wrinkle. Not a tear. The spruce needles on the ground under her knees hadn't even gotten on the hem.

Leah knelt there in the trees behind cabin four, fully dressed, and stared at herself.

Then she stood up and turned in a slow circle, looking down at herself like she belonged to somebody else.

"Okay," she said out loud. Her voice came out strange. "Okay. Okay."

A year and a half of cords tied around her ankle and clothes stuffed in plastic bags. A year and a half of phasing back in the woods behind somebody's house and waiting, naked and furious, for Seth to bring her sweatpants. A year and a half of the boys in her head seeing it, every single time.

She wouldn't ever have to do that again.

She looked down at the bracelet on her wrist. Black and gray and green.

She thought about the card. *You won't be stood naked in a clearing while some idiot stares at a spruce tree.*

She thought about him making it straight after Geneva, after the debrief and the forms and the Canadians and the handshakes, when he should have been asleep.

Her throat did something it hadn't done in a long time.

"Jerk," she said, to nobody, and it came out thick.

### III.

Biltong came back through the open window of the flat above the surf shop in Muizenberg a little after seven in the evening, Cape Town time, and landed on the back of the sofa with a thud that made the springs groan.

Harry was asleep on the sofa with his footpaws hanging off the end and his tail draped over the side onto the floor and the Geneva debrief file open on his chest. He'd been awake since Tuesday, more or less. He woke because Biltong did nothing quietly, and lay looking at the ceiling while the owl stamped up and down the back of the sofa above his head.

"Ja, ja," Harry said. "I'm up. I'm up."

Biltong stuck out one leg and glared at him.

There was a folded square of paper tied to it. A paper napkin. Harry could smell it from where he lay. Coffee. Bacon. Maple syrup. Fryer oil. And rain on cedar, salt water, fur.

He sat up so fast the Geneva file went sliding off onto the floor.

He untied the napkin with claws that were, he noticed with some embarrassment, not quite steady. Biltong watched him do it with an expression of profound contempt.

Blue ballpoint. Tall, sharp, slanted letters, pressed down so hard they'd nearly gone through the paper. On the back, the faint backward ghost of a first draft, crossed out.

> *Potter.*
>
> *It's ugly. I'm wearing it.*
>
> *Saturday. Don't be late.*
>
> *L.*
>
> *P.S. SIR???*
>
> *P.P.S. Your owl ate eleven strips of bacon and tried to eat Ray's dog.*
>
> *P.P.P.S. "Chocolate Frog Card No. 497." I am never letting this go. Never.*

Harry read it twice.

Then he read the P.S. again.

Then, very slowly, he thought about the paper he'd written on.

He'd written the note in Geneva. In a borrowed office on the fourth floor of the Confederation, after the debrief, half dead on his paws, with the bracelet finished and sitting in its box beside him. On the only paper on the desk, which had been a pad of Confederation stationery. Every sheet of which carried a charm, courtesy of the ICW Correspondence Office, that appended the sender's full official signature block the moment the letter was sealed. A charm you were supposed to switch off for personal correspondence. With the little dial. In the bottom corner. That Hermione had shown him four times.

"Oh no," Harry said.

Biltong clacked his beak.

"Oh, *no.*"

He put his muzzle in his paws.

"There's chicken in the fridge," Harry said, into his paws.

Biltong went to get the chicken. He knew how to open the fridge. Harry had stopped trying to stop him about a year ago.

Harry picked up the mirror off the coffee table and said, "Hermione Granger."

The glass cleared on a steamy kitchen and a face framed by an enormous amount of bushy brown hair, with a wooden spoon in one hand.

"Harry! Ron said you closed the Vadas file. He said there was a reception. He said there were Fizzing Whizbees."

"There were Fizzing Whizbees. Listen. The signature charm."

Hermione put down the spoon.

"What about the signature charm?"

"On the Confederation stationery. How do you turn it off?"

There was a pause.

"There's a dial," Hermione said. "Bottom right-hand corner of every sheet. It's very small and it's very clearly labelled. *Official*, *Personal*. I showed you. Four times. I drew you a *diagram*, Harry."

"I know."

"You sent a personal letter on Confederation stationery."

"Possibly."

"With the whole list."

"...Possibly."

She'd updated the list herself last spring and added the surfing as a joke. He watched her remember that.

"The Muizenberg Longboard Classic," she said.

"Ja."

"Novice division."

"*Ja*, Hermione."

Hermione put her hand over her mouth.

"Don't," Harry said.

She took her hand away. She was biting her lip very hard. "Who," she said carefully, "was the personal letter to?"

"No one."

"Harry."

"It was a thank-you note."

"For what?"

"For an evening."

The face in the mirror went perfectly still.

"Harry James Potter," Hermione said, in the voice she used when she'd found the one sentence in a three-hundred-page document that changed everything, "did you go on a *date*?"

Somewhere behind her, in the kitchen, Ron's voice said, "He *what*?"

"I have to go," Harry said.

"Is this the *hiker*? Ron said Hennessey said there was a *hiker* in the Canadian report, with a *dog*—"

"My owl's eating the fridge."

"Don't you *dare* close this—"

He closed the mirror.

He sat there for a moment with it in his paw. He thought about Hermione, who could find anything in any library on earth inside a week. He thought about a tribe somewhere on the Pacific coast whose name the Black family records hadn't bothered to write down. He thought about rain on cedar and salt water, and a girl across a bar saying *some of it, Potter*, and meaning it.

He put the mirror down on the coffee table and left it there. That was hers to tell, if she ever wanted to.

He read the napkin again.

*It's ugly. I'm wearing it.*

His tail started moving. Slowly, side to side, sweeping a clean arc across the floorboards.

He didn't stop it.

In the kitchen, Biltong pulled the fridge door shut with his beak and came back with a whole roast chicken leg, and sat on the arm of the sofa eating it, and watched Harry read the napkin a fourth time, and a fifth, and a sixth, with the expression of an owl who had seen a great many things in his life and had never seen anything quite this pathetic.

### IV.

On Friday morning Ray asked her if she wanted a job.

"Josie's still out," he said. "Headache's gone, she says, but she's wiped. I told her stay in bed. That leaves me with nobody on dishes and the hunting season on." He looked at her. "Dish pit. Ten hours. Hundred cash at the end of it. And you eat."

"I eat a lot."

"I've noticed." Ray paused. "Within reason."

Leah thought about her money. Seventy-one dollars and change, and Marg wanted forty-five a night. She had one pair of cut-offs, one T-shirt, a man's coat, and no shoes.

She was going to a bar in South Africa tomorrow. In cut-offs.

"Okay," she said.

The dish pit was a steel sink the size of a bathtub in the back corner of the kitchen, under a window that fogged up by ten and stayed fogged. Leah liked it immediately. Nobody talked to her. Plates came in and plates went out, the radio over the grill played old country songs out of Whitehorse, and every hour or so Ray slid a plate across the steel counter at her without a word. A burger. A grilled cheese. Pancakes. She ate them standing up between racks.

At two in the afternoon Josie came down the back stairs.

She was in sweatpants and an enormous Yukon College hoodie with the sleeves pulled over her hands, pale and tired and about fifteen years old.

"Oh my God," Josie said. "Ray put you on *dishes*?"

"Ray's paying me."

Josie came over and climbed up on an overturned milk crate beside the sink and sat there with her knees pulled up inside the hoodie, watching Leah work. She smelled like sleep and drugstore vanilla and, very faintly, lavender and something minty. Leah's hands paused in the water.

"How's your head?" she said.

"Better." Josie rubbed her temple. "Worst migraine of my life. I remember the lunch rush, and going for a walk because I felt weird, and then Ray banging on my door, and that's it. I lost like five hours."

Leah sprayed a plate.

"Ray said I had a cold cloth on my head. I don't even remember putting it there." Josie tipped her head back against the wall. "Oh! Did you hear about the photographer? Mr. Vale? He just *left*. In the middle of the night. Gord says the Aldridge place is all shut up like a tomb."

"I heard."

"Weird, right?" Josie frowned a little, like she was reaching for something on a high shelf and couldn't quite get it. "He was kind of weird, actually. Now that I think about it. He always wanted to take my picture." She shivered, inside the hoodie. "I'm glad he's gone. Is that mean? He was super polite. But I'm glad."

Leah put the plate in the rack.

"It's not mean," she said.

She didn't trust her voice for anything else. She picked up the next plate and scrubbed it a lot harder than it needed.

Josie watched her for a while.

"So," Josie said. "Ray says you went to the inn on Wednesday. With a *giant*."

"Ray talks too much."

"Ray says he was like seven feet tall and had a beard and was wearing flannel and looked like he could pick up a moose."

"He doesn't have a beard."

"*Aha*," Josie said.

Leah closed her eyes.

"So there *is* a he! Who is he? Is he a hunter? Please don't tell me he's a hunter."

"He's not a hunter."

"Is he nice?"

Leah thought about it.

"He's annoying," she said.

"Is he cute?"

Leah scrubbed the plate.

"Oh my *God*," Josie said. "He's cute. You went quiet. That means he's cute."

"He's—" Leah stopped. She thought about the shoulders. The teeth. The ears going soft. The tail. "He's a lot."

Josie made a noise like a teakettle.

"Are you seeing him again?"

"Tomorrow."

"Where's he taking you?"

"A bar."

"Which bar? There's only the one."

"Not here."

"*Whitehorse*?" Josie sat up. "He's taking you to *Whitehorse*? That's like three hours—"

"Further than Whitehorse."

Josie stared at her. "What's further than Whitehorse?"

"Everything," Leah said.

Josie looked at Leah's bare feet on the wet concrete, and the cut-offs, and the T-shirt, which were the only clothes Leah had worn in the three days Josie had known her.

"Wait," Josie said. "Where are the Nikes?"

"I ruined them."

"In *two days*?"

"I run a lot."

"What are you wearing tomorrow?"

Leah sprayed a plate.

"*Leah.* What are you *wearing*?"

"This."

Josie climbed down off the milk crate with the expression of someone going into battle.

"Ray," she said, "I'm taking Leah upstairs for ten minutes."

"She's on dishes."

"Ray, she's going on a date with a *giant* to *somewhere further than Whitehorse* and she's going to wear *cut-offs*."

Ray looked up from the grill. He looked at Leah. He looked at Josie. He looked at the stack of dirty plates.

"Ten minutes," he said.

---

Josie's room over the café was small and cluttered and smelled of vanilla and laundry: a single bed with a pink quilt and too many stuffed animals, a dresser covered in nail polish, a corkboard over the bed full of photographs. Josie with a woman who had the same round face and the same gap in her teeth, wearing scrubs. Josie at a bonfire. Josie on a dock, laughing.

And in the middle of the corkboard, taped up with two strips of Scotch tape, a glossy brochure. *Yukon College. Practical Nurse Program. Begin Your Career in Health Care.*

Leah stood in the doorway and looked at it, and something hot and tight went through her chest.

She'd been right. She'd have bet money.

Josie was digging in the dresser. "I'm too short for anything you'd wear on the bottom, but tops stretch. Hang on. I bought it in Vancouver for a wedding I never went to, and it's going to look *amazing* on you—*here*."

She came out of the drawer holding something soft and dark and green.

It was a top. Long sleeves, a wrap front that tied at the side, some kind of stretchy jersey fabric, the green of the deep water off First Beach on a cloudy day. Dark green. Bottle green.

Green like—

"No," Leah said.

"You haven't even tried it on."

"It's green."

"Green's your *color*." Josie held it up against Leah's chest and stepped back and squinted. "Oh my God. Oh my *God*. Look in the mirror. Look."

Leah looked in the mirror over the dresser.

She looked at herself, with her chopped hair and her tired face and her eyes, in a dark green top held up by a twenty-two-year-old girl with a gap in her teeth who had no idea she'd been six inches from dying in a cellar two days ago.

She looked okay. She looked, actually, really good.

She thought about green eyes over a pool cue.

"I hate everything," Leah said.

"You're wearing it," Josie said.

---

She bought jeans at Duncan's on the way back to Marg's that evening with Ray's hundred dollars, and a three-pack of socks, and a hair elastic she'd never use. Gord rang her up and looked at the jeans and the socks and her bare feet and said, "No shoes?"

"How much are the boots?"

"Which ones?"

"The cheapest ones."

Gord looked at her for a while. Then he looked at the wall of boots behind the counter, and then back at her.

"Hundred and twenty," he said, apologetically.

She had seventy-nine dollars left, and forty-five of it was Marg's.

"Never mind," Leah said.

She walked back to Brewster's barefoot in the dusk, with her jeans and socks in a paper bag and Josie's green top folded on top of them, and the coat on, and the bracelet on her wrist.

Marg was on her porch with her cigarette and her mug and her blanket. She watched Leah come up the drive.

"Got your rent," Leah said, and held it out. Forty-five dollars. She'd have to find more by Sunday.

Marg took it. She looked at Leah's feet.

"What size are you?" she said.

"Eight."

Marg got up and went into the house, and the screen door banged behind her. When she came back she was carrying a pair of boots. Brown leather, pull-on, with elastic sides and thick rubber soles, scuffed at the toes, broken in soft. She dropped them on the porch at Leah's feet.

"My daughter's," she said. "She's in Edmonton with a husband and three kids and a minivan. She hasn't worn those since 1997. Women's nine."

"I'm an eight."

"Wear two pairs of socks." Marg sat back down and picked up her mug. "And don't argue with me. I'm sixty-four. I've earned not being argued with."

Leah looked at the boots. Then she looked at Marg.

Everybody in this town kept giving her shoes.

"Thanks," she said.

"Uh-huh." Marg drank her coffee. "Josie told me about tomorrow."

"Josie talks too much."

"Josie told me your fella's taking you somewhere further than Whitehorse." Marg took a drag on her cigarette and let the smoke out slow. "You want some advice from an old woman who married the wrong man twice and the right one once?"

"No."

"Uh-huh. Here it is anyway. Watch how he treats the waitress."

Leah thought about Harry at the bar on Wednesday. *Cheers, boet. Sorry about that. Lovely boots.* Wes's flat look and the second glass and the fifty Harry'd left on the bar on the way out, when he thought she wasn't looking.

"He tips too much," she said.

Marg looked at her for a long time.

"Huh," Marg said. "Well. Nobody's perfect."

---

### V.

He was early.

She'd known he would be. She was awake at five anyway, lying in the bed with her eyes open, listening to the lake. At seven she showered and put on the jeans and two pairs of socks and Marg's daughter's boots and Josie's green top, and stood in front of the little mirror over the sink and looked at herself, and took the top off, and put it back on, and took it off, and put her black T-shirt on instead, and stood there, and took the T-shirt off and put the green top back on and said, "*Fine*," out loud to nobody.

At eight-thirty she put the coat on over the top, because it was cold out and she didn't want to explain to Marg why she wasn't cold. At eight-thirty-five she took it off again and folded it over her arm.

At eight-forty she heard Marg's voice in the yard.

"You're the coat."

And a voice she knew. Low, rough, the vowels landing in the wrong places.

"Ja. I'm the coat."

Leah went to the window.

Marg was standing at the bottom of her porch steps in her bathrobe with her mug, looking up. Way up. At the flannel guy, standing in the gravel drive with his arms full of white Styrofoam takeaway boxes, four of them, stacked, and a dented tin tucked under one elbow.

Through the shimmer, the wolf stood on the gravel with the boxes stacked in his forepaws and his ears tipped politely toward Marg, like a big dog that had been told to sit and was trying very hard. Dark charcoal shirt, dark trousers, and a long brown coat, shabby at the cuffs.

"She split my woodpile," Marg said.

Harry's ears went up. "Did she?"

"Thursday afternoon. Asked if I needed anything doing. I said the woodpile. Figured it'd take her a week." Marg drank her coffee. "Took her forty minutes. Whole cord. Stacked it, too."

"Sounds about right."

"Uh-huh." Marg looked at him a while longer, at the flannel and the boots and the boxes. "You got a car?"

"No."

"You walk here?"

"Sort of."

Marg's eyes narrowed. Leah could see her thinking about the last person who'd said *sort of* to her on that porch.

"Uh-huh," Marg said. "You bring her back. You hear me? You bring her back in one piece, or I'll find you, and I've got a .30-06 in the hall closet my second husband left me, and nothing but time."

"Yes, ma'am," Harry said.

"Don't *ma'am* me."

"No, ma'am."

Leah opened the door.

Harry turned around. His ears went straight up. His tail, under the shimmer, stopped moving entirely.

He looked at her for a long moment, in the jeans and the boots and the green top, with the coat folded over her arm.

"Hello," he said.

"You're early."

"I said I'd be early." He didn't take his eyes off her. "You're wearing green."

"Josie lent it to me."

"Remind me to tip Josie."

"You'd better not. She'll know something's up."

"Something is up," Harry said. "That's—ja. You look—" He stopped. His ears flicked. "I've got four Lumberjacks."

"Two plates," Leah said.

"Two each. I'm not a monster."

Marg looked from one of them to the other. Then she shook her head slowly and went back inside, and the screen door banged.

---

They ate on the steps of cabin four in the cold, with the sun coming up over the mountains and turning the lake gold. Ray had packed the boxes so tight the pancakes had gone flat. Harry had dropped the glamour the second Marg's door shut, and he sat two steps below her with his legs stretched out on the gravel, holding a plastic fork awkwardly in his big padded paw, with syrup in the fur on his chin.

"Ray gave me a look," he said, around a mouthful of bacon.

"Ray gives everybody a look."

"This was a specific look. He asked who the four Lumberjacks were for and stared at me for about a minute without blinking." Harry's ears went sideways. "Then he put extra bacon in yours. I could smell it."

"Did you eat it?"

"I thought about it."

She held out the coat.

He looked at it. He looked at her.

"Collateral," Leah said. "You came back."

He put down his fork and wiped his paws on a napkin, carefully, and took the coat from her. He shrugged off the shabby brown one, folded it on the step, and put the gray one on, and did up the silver clasp at the throat.

His nose flared.

Leah saw it happen. She saw his whole muzzle go still and his nostrils widen and his ears go up and then soft, very slightly. The coat had been on her for three days. Wrapped around her in her sleep. She knew exactly what it smelled like now.

"Rule four," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your nose said it."

"My nose has its own opinions," Harry said. "I've had very little say in it since 2003." His tail swept the gravel. "You're wearing the bracelet."

"It's ugly."

"So you said." He was looking at her wrist. "Did you try it?"

"Thursday."

"Did it work?"

Leah looked down at her breakfast.

"Yeah," she said. "It worked."

He waited.

"Thank you," she said. It came out rough. She hated saying it. She said it anyway. "I—yeah. Thank you."

His ears came forward. "Ja," he said quietly. "Pleasure."

"Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird."

"Your tail's making it weird."

He looked over his shoulder at his tail, which was sweeping the gravel in long, slow, happy arcs. He looked back at her.

"It's a tail," he said. "I don't control it."

"You controlled it fine on Wednesday."

"That was different. That was art." He picked up his fork again. "Eat. We've got eleven minutes."

---

The Portkey was a tin of rusks.

A red and yellow canister, dented in one corner, with an old woman in a bonnet on the label and *OUMA RUSKS* in big letters. Very obviously empty.

"That's it?" Leah said.

"That's it."

"A cookie tin."

"Rusk tin. My neighbour's. Mrs. Pillay downstairs. She'll want it back, so try not to dent it any more than it is." He checked something that wasn't a watch, a little brass disc he took out of one of the pouches on his belt. "Licensed. Hennessey signed off on it last night. He made a face."

"What kind of face?"

"The face he makes when he's going to put something in a file." He put the disc away. "Two minutes. Here's how it goes. You put one hand on the tin. You hang on to me with the other. When it goes, it's going to feel like somebody's hooked you behind your belly button and yanked. Don't let go of the tin. Don't let go of me."

"And then?"

"And then we land," Harry said, "and you'll hate it."

"Great."

She looked at the tin. Then she looked at him. "You said I could land on you."

"I did say that."

"I'm going to hold you to it."

His ears went up. "I'd hope so."

They knelt on the gravel at the bottom of the steps, facing each other, with the tin between them. Leah put her right hand flat on the lid. It was cold. Harry put his paw beside hers. Then he held out his other paw, and after a second she took it.

It was big. Much bigger than her hand. The pads were thick and rough and hot, and his claws curled over the back of her knuckles very carefully, not touching, and his grip was firm and steady.

Her wolf rolled over under her ribs, all four legs in the air.

*Shut up,* she told it. *Shut up shut up shut up.*

"Ten seconds," Harry said.

She looked up at him. He was looking at her. Green eyes, a foot away.

"Five."

"If I hate it," she said, "you carry me home."

"Deal."

"Two."

"Harry—"

"One."

The world grabbed her behind the navel and *pulled*.

---

It was the worst thing that had ever happened to her body, and she'd been turning into a wolf for a year and a half.

There was no up. There was no down. There was a howling wind and a whirl of color, and her knees had left the gravel and her stomach had stayed behind somewhere in British Columbia, and the only things in the universe were the cold tin under her right hand and the big paw clamped around her left, and she held on to both of them so hard her knuckles cracked.

And then the ground came up and hit them.

Harry hit it first. She felt him twist in midair, felt him yank her toward him and roll, so that when they landed, it was him on the bottom, flat on his back on something soft and green, with a *whump* that knocked the wind out of him in a loud *oof*—and her on top of him.

All of her. Sprawled across his chest. Her face in the fur of his neck. Her hands full of his shirt.

The tin rolled away across the grass with a clatter.

For a second nobody moved.

Leah lay there with her ear against his chest, listening to his heart going very fast, and breathing in pine and stone and creek-water and wolf, and that other thing underneath, the thing that smelled like home, and the shoulders under her hands were exactly as wide as she'd thought they were, maybe wider, and her wolf had stopped purring and started doing something else entirely, something that made her face go hot.

"Told you," Harry said, somewhere above her head. His voice came out wheezy. "You can land on me."

Leah pushed herself up on her hands and looked down at him.

He was lying flat on his back on the grass with his ears splayed out sideways and his eyes half closed, and his muzzle was drawn back in a grin, and his tail was going, she could feel it, thumping against the ground under both of them.

"You're enjoying this," she said.

"Massively."

She rolled off him, fast, and sat up in the grass with her heart going like she'd run fifty kilometers.

"Did you hate it?" Harry said, from the ground.

"Yes."

"Do you want me to carry you home?"

She thought about it.

"Not yet," she said.

He laughed, still flat on his back, a low rumble she felt through the grass.

Then she looked up, and saw where she was, and stopped thinking about anything at all.

---

### VI.

They'd landed on a small square lawn behind a high clipped hedge, in a big garden: red gravel paths between beds of roses, enormous old oaks, fat gray squirrels sitting up on the path to watch her.

And over the top of the hedge, over the treetops, filling up half the sky, there was a mountain.

It went straight up, sheer gray rock cut with deep gullies, and it was flat on top, like somebody had sliced it off with a knife. The evening sun had turned the whole face gold and rose. Across the top, a thick white cloud was pouring over the edge of the cliff and running down the rock like a waterfall in slow motion, and vanishing halfway down into nothing.

Leah sat on the grass and stared at it.

"Table Mountain," Harry said. He'd sat up beside her. "The cloud's called the tablecloth. Happens when the wind's from the southeast. The Cape Doctor, they call it. Blows all the muck out of the city."

"It's so big," Leah said.

"Ja."

"It's right *there*."

"It's right there," Harry said. "Every day. You get used to it." He looked up at it. "Eight years and I'm not used to it."

The air smelled like nothing she knew. Salt, sharper than the Pacific's. Roses. Diesel and hot pavement and grilling meat and spices she couldn't name, and people, a whole city of them, behind the hedge, under the hum of traffic.

It was six in the evening. The light was gold and slanting. Her body thought it was nine in the morning. She'd eaten breakfast forty-five minutes ago on the other side of the planet.

"Where are we?" she said.

"Company's Garden. Middle of town. Oldest garden in the country. The Dutch planted it to grow vegetables for the ships." Harry stood up and held out a paw to her. "That hedge is the Portkey yard. The Muggles can't see it. They walk right past."

She looked at the paw. She took it and let him pull her up.

He didn't let go right away. She didn't make him.

Then he did, and tucked the rusk tin into his coat pocket, and pulled the glamour up over himself.

"Right," he said. "Ten minutes' walk. You want to see a bit of it?"

---

Long Street went straight downhill from the mountain toward the sea, and it was crowded.

Saturday night was starting. Old buildings lined both sides, painted cream and yellow and pale blue, with lacy black iron balconies across their fronts and people leaning on the railings with drinks, shouting down to friends. Music came out of every other door: drums, horns, reggae. The sidewalks were packed with backpackers and students and couples dressed up for dinner, and white minibus taxis kept pulling up to the curb with their doors open and young men leaning out shouting "*Cape Town! Cape Town! Wynberg!*" and hammering on the roof.

Leah walked down the middle of it in Marg's daughter's boots, and couldn't stop turning her head.

Every smell in the world was here. Curry. Beer. Perfume. Fried dough. The sea at the bottom of the hill. Garbage. Hundreds of people, every one of them a line of scent she could have followed. It came at her from every direction at once, and for a second she had to stop walking and let it hit her, like a wave.

"It's a lot," she said.

"Ja. First week I lived here, I couldn't go out at night. My nose kept trying to follow everything at once." He kept his voice low. "Pick one. Follow it. Let the rest go behind it. Like picking out one instrument in a band."

She picked one. Curry, from a little shop on the corner with a hand-painted sign. She followed it. The rest went quiet behind it, a little.

"Better?"

"Better."

They walked on.

A man in a bright orange reflective vest was standing at the curb outside a bar, waving a car into a parking spot with a flashlight. He was about fifty, short and wiry, with gray in his beard. He saw them coming and his whole face lit up.

"*Harry!*" he said. "Howzit, my bru!"

"Sipho," said the flannel guy. "Howzit."

"Long time! Where you been?"

"Canada. Snow. Bad pie."

Sipho laughed. Then he looked at Leah, and his eyebrows went up, and he looked back at Harry, and his grin went very wide.

"And who is this?" he said.

"Not telling you."

"*Hey!* Harry!" Sipho turned to Leah. "Sisi, I have known this guy six years. Six years! Every Saturday he walks down here. Alone. Every week alone. Six years! And now—"

"Sipho," Harry said.

"—now he comes with a beautiful lady and he won't even tell me her name—"

"*Sipho.*"

"Leah," Leah said.

Sipho put his hand on his heart. "Leah. Welcome to Cape Town, Leah. Don't let this one buy you the cheap wine."

"He's buying me beer."

"Even worse," Sipho said, and winked at her, and went back to waving his flashlight at cars.

Harry walked on very fast. Leah had to lengthen her stride to keep up.

"Every Saturday," she said. "*Alone*."

"Shut up."

"Six years, and he still thinks you're a guy in flannel."

"He's a good bloke. I tip him."

"How much?"

Harry's ears went sideways.

"How much, Harry?"

"Enough," Harry said, and turned left into an alley.

---

The alley was narrow and dark and smelled of cat. It ran between a backpackers' hostel with a surfing zebra painted on the side and a shop selling carved wooden masks. A rusted drainpipe. A dumpster. At the end, a blank wall of old red brick.

Harry walked up to the wall, counted three bricks up from the drainpipe and two across, and tapped one of them three times with a claw.

The brick shivered.

Then it slid back into the wall, and the bricks around it followed, rippling outward, folding back and away like a deck of cards being shuffled, until there was an arch in the wall tall enough even for Harry. On the other side there was a short passage lit by a lantern, and at the end of it a heavy wooden door, black with age, with iron hinges and a door knocker shaped like a dragon's head with a ring in its mouth.

Over the door, burned into a plank of dark wood in tall, curling letters: *DIE OU DRAAK.*

Leah stood in the archway and looked at it for a long time.

"That's real," she said.

"That's real."

"You knocked on a *wall*."

"I did knock on a wall." He dropped the glamour. The flannel guy vanished, and the wolf stood there in the lantern light in his long gray coat, ears up, looking at her. "Last chance. You can still say no. I'll take you home."

Leah looked at the dragon knocker. Then at him.

"Open the door, Potter," she said.

He opened the door.

---

The noise hit her first. Then the heat. Then the smell, which was smoke, mostly, smoke and beer and curry and old wood and something sharp and peppery that made her eyes water, and under it, a whole crowd of people who smelled faintly, every one of them, like the air after a thunderstorm.

Magic. The whole room smelled like magic.

It was a long, low room with thick whitewashed walls curved at the top like the inside of an old barrel, and a floor of worn red tiles. A long bar of almost-black wood ran down the right side, backed by shelves of bottles in every color, some of them glowing, some of them moving. A fire roared in a huge hearth at the far end. Every table was full: witches and wizards in robes, people in jeans, a man in a three-piece suit with a pointed hat on top of it, two very short people with long ears playing cards in the corner where the cards kept exploding.

And the ceiling.

The ceiling was black with soot from one end of the room to the other, and covered in writing. Names, thousands of them, scrawled with fingers and sticks and wands, overlapping, some faded almost to nothing and some fresh. Dates. Hearts with initials in them. Somebody had drawn a dragon. Somebody had written *NEVER AGAIN* in letters two feet high, and somebody else had written *SEE YOU NEXT WEEK* underneath it.

Leah stood just inside the door, with her head tipped all the way back, staring up.

And then she realized the room had gone quiet.

She lowered her eyes.

Everybody was looking at them.

Every head in the place had turned toward the door, and every one of them was looking at him. The card players in the corner had stopped, with exploding cards in their hands. A wizard at the bar had stopped with his glass halfway to his mouth. A young witch by the fire had grabbed her friend's arm.

Somebody whispered. It carried in the silence.

"*That's Potter.*"

And then somebody else, louder, from the back of the room, a voice that had had a couple of drinks: "*It's the Wolf Who Lived!*"

The room broke into noise. Laughing, a few cheers, a lot of whispering behind hands. A girl at the table by the fire, maybe sixteen, was half out of her chair with her mouth open.

Leah looked at Harry.

His ears had gone flat, partway, just enough that she could see it. His tail had gone completely still behind him. His muzzle didn't change at all.

"In some places," Leah said quietly.

"In some places," Harry said.

"You said not here."

"I said not *Atlin*."

A middle-aged witch in a purple cardigan got up from a table near the door and came straight at them, with her hands clasped in front of her and her face all lit up. "Mr. Potter. Oh, I'm so sorry, I'm sure you get this all the time. My son Thabo, he's eleven, he's just started at Uagadou, and he keeps your Chocolate Frog card by his bed—would you sign something for him? Anything. A napkin. He'd die."

Leah looked at the side of Harry's muzzle.

*Chocolate Frog Card No. 497.*

She bit down on the inside of her cheek so hard it hurt.

"Ja, course," Harry said. His voice was perfectly even. "What's he like, your Thabo? What's he into?"

"Oh—Quidditch, mostly. He wants to be a Keeper."

"Has he got a broom?"

"Not yet. Next year."

Harry took the napkin she was holding out, and a quill she produced from her cardigan pocket, and wrote on it against the wall, in that loopy black scrawl. He wrote for longer than a signature. Leah could see it over his elbow. *Thabo—Keepers win matches. Seekers just get the credit. Don't let anybody tell you different. —Harry Potter.*

The witch read it and put her hand over her mouth.

"Thank you," she said. "Oh, thank you. Thank you so much." She looked at Leah, suddenly, as if she'd just noticed her. Her eyes went over the green top and the jeans and the boots and Leah's face, very fast, the way women's eyes did. "And who's—"

"Enjoy your evening," Harry said, very politely, and walked past her into the room.

Leah followed him.

She felt the eyes on her the whole way down the bar, and heard the whispers, because her hearing was better than anybody in the room knew. *Who's that?* *Is that a witch?* *She's not wearing robes.* *Is she a Muggle?* *Can't be a Muggle, she'd never get in.* *Where's he been hiding her?* *Is she American?* *She looks American.*

At the end of the bar, an old wizard in a moth-eaten green robe and a beard down to his belt looked at Harry as he went by, and said, not quietly at all, "Shouldn't be allowed in here. Beast's a beast. Ought to be on a lead."

Leah stopped walking.

The heat came up. It came up all at once, from her belly, through her chest, into her arms, and the bracelet on her wrist pulled tight, very slightly, like a hand closing. She turned around.

The old wizard was looking at her now. Watery blue eyes. A nose like a potato.

"And you'd be what?" he said to her. "The beast's pet?"

A paw closed on her elbow, lightly, big and heavy through the sleeve of Josie's top.

"Leah," Harry said, low. "Not worth it."

"He called you a *beast*."

"He calls me that every time I come in. Since 2003. His name's Cornelis and he's been banned four times." Harry's voice was very quiet and very even. "Grimbald will sort him."

"*Out*," said a voice from behind the bar.

It was a goblin.

He was about three feet tall, standing on a box behind the bar, with a long thin face and long pointed ears and a white apron over a waistcoat of dark red velvet, and very small, very sharp teeth. He was looking at the old wizard with an expression that could have taken the paint off a wall.

"Cornelis," the goblin said. "Out."

"I've paid for this drink—"

"Then you will drink it outside. In the alley. With the cats. Who are better company." The goblin pointed one long finger at the door. "In my bar, he is a customer. You are a customer. She is a customer. Customers who insult other customers are not customers. *Out.*"

Cornelis looked at the goblin. He looked at Harry. He looked at Leah, whose lips, she realized, had come back off her teeth.

He picked up his drink and went, muttering. The door banged shut behind him.

The bar noise came back up, slowly.

"Grimbald," Harry said.

"Potter." The goblin looked up at him with his small black eyes. "It's been a year."

"Eleven months."

"A year. You owe me for a curtain."

"I paid you for the curtain."

"You paid me for *one* curtain." Grimbald looked at Leah. His eyes went over her once, fast, and stopped on the bracelet for a second, and came back up to her face. Something in his expression shifted. Goblin faces were as hard to read as wolf faces.

"Hm," Grimbald said.

"This is Leah," Harry said.

"I gathered that." Grimbald looked at Harry. "She is not a witch."

"No."

"She is not a Muggle."

"No."

"She is not like you."

"Not exactly."

"Hm," Grimbald said again. He looked at Leah for a long moment. "Welcome to Die Ou Draak," he said. "Do not set fire to the curtains. The curtains are new." He climbed down off his box. "Sit. I'll send Nomsa."

---

### VII.

She smelled them about ten seconds after they sat down.

They'd taken a table under a scorch mark the size of a car tire, and she'd made Harry give her the seat against the wall, and he'd moved without arguing. She was looking at the menu, chalked on a slate over the fire and rewriting itself every few seconds, when the air from the corner shifted, and something came across the room to her that stood every hair on her arms straight up.

Wet dog, on top. Fur and mud and rain. Under that, something sick: a dog that's run a fever for a week, hot and sour, sweat gone stale in sheets nobody's changed. An old wound that never healed right. Something rotting very slowly under the skin. Copper, faint and old. And under all of it a wildness gone sour and cornered, something in a trap, nothing like the forest-and-rain of the pack or the pine-and-stone of Harry.

Her chair scraped back an inch before she knew she'd moved.

"Leah?"

She was looking at the corner. At a table half in shadow, by the end of the hearth, where two people were sitting with drinks in front of them. A man and a woman. The man was maybe fifty, gaunt, with a long lined face and gray in his hair and three pale scars running down one cheek and into his collar. The woman was younger, maybe thirty, with close-cropped hair and dark circles under her eyes and her hands wrapped tight around her glass. Neither of them was talking.

There were empty tables all around them. In a full bar, on a Saturday night. Nobody was sitting within three tables of them on any side.

The bracelet on her wrist pulled tight.

"Leah." Harry's voice was very low. "Look at me."

She didn't look at him. She couldn't look away from the corner.

"*Leah.*"

She looked at him.

"What do you smell?" he said.

"Them." Her voice came out wrong. Low. Rough. Not quite hers. "Something's wrong with them. They smell *sick*. They smell like—" She didn't have the word. "Like something that should be put down."

Harry's ears went flat, all the way.

"Ja," he said quietly. "They're werewolves."

Leah stared at him.

"The real kind. The kind that bit me." He kept his voice so low that even she had to lean in. "Pieter and Lerato. Full moon's a week Saturday. It comes on a week out, for a lot of them. Fevers. Short tempers. Can't sleep. They smell different." His muzzle twitched. "To you, I'd guess, they smell like prey."

"They smell like *monsters*," Leah said.

The words came out of somewhere very old. Somewhere older than her. They came up out of the same place the wolf came from, the place that had been bred for three hundred years to smell a cold one and go for its throat, and she heard them come out of her mouth and didn't know whether she meant them.

Harry looked at her for a long moment. Something moved in his face.

"They're people," he said. "Pieter was a schoolteacher in Stellenbosch for twenty years. Got bitten on a camping trip in the Cederberg. Lost his job when they found out. Lost his wife. Lerato's a Healer, or she was. Got bitten on a ward in a wizarding hospital in Johannesburg, by a patient who'd hid what he was. They run a support group on Thursday nights in a church hall in Woodstock. Twenty-two members. They've got a shed with a lock on it for full moons, and a rota, and a kettle." His voice didn't change. "I pay for the kettle. And the lock. That's the Lycanthrope Welfare Society. On my signature."

Leah didn't say anything.

"Every one of them's like me," Harry said. "Bitten. Only the curse took them, and it didn't take me. Nobody knows why. I got lucky. They didn't." His ears came up, partway. "So."

Across the room, the woman in the corner—Lerato—had lifted her head. She was looking at their table. Her nostrils flared.

She was smelling Leah.

Their eyes met. Lerato's eyes were brown, and tired, and very sharp.

She said something to the man. The man looked over. Then, slowly, he got up, and picked up his glass, and came across the room to their table, and the people at the tables he passed shifted their chairs away from him as he went.

He stopped beside their table. Up close the sick smell was strong enough to make Leah's eyes water. She held very still.

"Potter," the man said. His accent was Afrikaans, heavy and slow.

"Pieter."

"You look well."

"So do you."

"Liar," Pieter said, without heat. "I look like I've been dug up." He looked at Leah. He looked at her for a long time. "Lerato says to ask what she is."

"Lerato can ask me herself," Leah said.

Pieter's eyebrows went up. Behind him, at the corner table, she heard Lerato laugh, short and surprised.

"Ja," Pieter said. "Fair." He looked back at Harry. "She smells like wolf, Potter. But clean. Like rain. No sickness in it at all." His voice had something in it Leah couldn't name. "Like you. Only more."

"She's with me," Harry said.

"I can see that." Pieter looked at Leah again. "So there are two of you now."

Leah didn't answer.

Pieter nodded slowly, as if she had. His eyes went to the bracelet on her wrist, then to Harry's face, and then he smiled. It was a tired smile, and there was something bitter at the bottom of it, and something else on top of the bitter that looked almost like he was glad.

"Lucky," he said. To Harry. Or to both of them. "Some people are lucky."

"I know," Harry said.

"Ja. You do." Pieter lifted his glass an inch, toward Leah. "Enjoy your Drakensberg Red, meisie. Second pint's the good one."

He went back to his table and sat down beside Lerato, and after a moment they both looked over at Leah and nodded. Small. The kind of nod you'd give across a room to somebody you'd recognized from somewhere, without being able to say where.

Leah sat there with the sick smell still in her nose.

Monster. Prey. Put down.

She thought about the legends by the fire, and the cold ones whose throats she'd been bred to rip out. That was how the stories always went: the monster comes out of the dark and the wolves go to meet it.

Nobody had ever told her the monster might go to a support group on Thursdays. Or need a kettle.

"Are you okay?" Harry said.

"No," Leah said.

"Fair."

"Give me a minute."

"Take ten. Take all night." His tail swept the floor. "Nomsa's coming. Brace yourself."

---

Nomsa was six feet tall and about sixty, with close-cropped silver hair and enormous gold hoops in her ears and an apron that said *KISS THE COOK*, except somebody had scorched out *KISS* and written *FEAR* above it. She came across the room like a ship under full sail, and the crowd parted for her faster than it had for Pieter.

She stopped at their table and put both hands on her hips and looked down at Harry.

"*Eleven months*," she said.

"Hello, Nomsa."

"Don't you 'hello Nomsa' me. Eleven months, no owl, nothing, and I have to hear from Grimbald that you've brought a *girl*." She looked at Leah, once, head to toe, with very dark, very sharp eyes. "Hm."

Leah looked back.

"You're too thin," Nomsa said.

"I eat a lot."

"Not here, you haven't. Not yet." Nomsa looked at Harry. "Two bunny chows, mutton, the big ones. Two Drakensberg Reds. And a jug of water, and she's going to want it."

"We haven't ordered," Harry said.

"You don't order. I tell you. You've been coming in here five years and you still think it's a democracy." She looked back at Leah. "He told you about the beer?"

"He said I'd burp fire."

"You'll burp fire," Nomsa said. "First pint, smoke. Second pint, flame. Third pint, you don't get a third pint. Not since 2002, when that *mampara* from Pretoria set his eyebrows alight and took me to court." She sniffed. "I won."

"She won," Harry said.

"I always win." Nomsa looked at Leah for one more second. Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "You keep him in line, sisi. Somebody has to."

She sailed off toward the kitchen.

Leah watched her go.

"She's terrifying," she said.

"I told you."

"I like her."

"Ja," Harry said. "Everybody does. That's the terrifying part."

---

The bunny chow was half a loaf of white bread hollowed out and filled with mutton curry so hot it made her eyes stream, with the scooped-out bread perched on top for dipping. It was the best thing she'd ever eaten. She ate all of hers and half of Harry's, and he pushed the plate across without a word when she looked at it.

The Drakensberg Red came in tall glass tankards with pewter lids. It was black as motor oil, with a thick tan head that smelled of roasted coffee and chocolate and something underneath that, sharp and red and dangerous, like a chili pepper that had been dared to do something stupid. When she tilted the tankard, the light from the candles didn't go through it at all.

"Cheers," Harry said, and held his up.

Leah looked at him across the table, in the coat she'd slept in for three nights, which smelled of her now. At the soot-black ceiling behind him, and the exploding cards, and Pieter and Lerato in the corner, and the girl by the fire who still hadn't stopped staring.

Three days ago she'd been standing at a pay phone in Atlin telling Jacob she'd call.

"Cheers," she said, and drank.

It was thick and bitter and dark and tasted of coffee and burnt sugar and smoke, and then, about three seconds after she swallowed, the dragon pepper hit.

It hit the back of her throat like a slap. It went down into her chest and spread out like a fire in dry grass. Her eyes went wide. Her ears went hot. She put the tankard down very fast and grabbed for the water jug.

"Don't," Harry said. "Makes it worse."

She put the water jug down.

The heat in her chest was building. It was climbing. It was coming up her throat with a mind of its own, a big round pressure coming up from her stomach toward her mouth, unstoppable, like a—

She opened her mouth to say *oh no*.

What came out was a burp, loud and deep and completely unladylike, and with it, a big gray puff of smoke, rolling out of her mouth and up toward the ceiling in a lazy curling cloud.

The table next to them burst into applause.

Leah sat there with her mouth open and smoke trickling out of both nostrils.

Harry had put his muzzle down on his folded forepaws on the table. His shoulders were shaking. His tail was thumping against the leg of the chair.

"Don't," Leah said. Smoke came out with the word. "*Don't.*"

"I'm not," Harry wheezed.

"You're *laughing*."

"I'm not laughing," Harry said, and lifted his head, and he was very obviously laughing, his whole muzzle drawn back and his eyes squeezed half shut, "I'm *proud* of you."

Leah picked up the tankard and drank the rest of it, all of it, in one long swallow, staring him dead in the eye.

His ears went straight up.

"Leah," he said. "Leah, that's a lot—"

"Second pint," Leah said, and put the empty tankard down with a bang. "*Grimbald!*"

The goblin looked over from behind the bar. He looked at the empty tankard. He looked at Leah. He looked at Harry. Something that might, on a goblin, have been approval went across his long face.

He poured another.

---

It took about four minutes into the second pint.

She was ready this time. She felt it building and sat very still and let it come, watching Harry, who'd leaned forward on his elbows to tell her something about the tablecloth cloud, his muzzle about eighteen inches from her face. She thought: *condition two*.

She didn't turn her head.

The burp came up out of her like a cannon. And with it, out of her open mouth, a jet of fire.

A real one. Orange and gold at the tip, blue at the root, a foot long, maybe more, a tongue of flame that rolled out of her across the table with a sound like a gas stove lighting, *whumph*, and lit up the whole corner of the room orange, and went straight at Harry's face.

He was leaning on his elbows with his chin on his paws, and his eyes went wide, and he jerked back, too late, and the flame caught him across the end of the muzzle and up over the top of his head and between the ears, and went out.

There was a smell of singed fur.

The whole bar went silent.

Harry sat very still. A thin curl of smoke was rising from between his ears. The whiskers on the left side of his muzzle had shriveled into tiny curly black crisps. The fur on the top of his head, between the ears, was standing up in a ragged burnt tuft, like a little black flame of its own.

He looked at her.

Leah looked back at him, with her hands folded on the table, and a thin trail of smoke coming out of one nostril.

"Condition two," she said. "That's your fault."

There was a long moment of absolute quiet.

Then the table next to them started applauding, and the table next to that, and the girl by the fire was up on her chair clapping over her head. Somebody shouted "*Again!*" In the corner by the hearth, Pieter was laughing, a dry, rusty sound like a hinge that hadn't been opened in years, and Lerato had her face in her hands, shoulders shaking.

Grimbald climbed down off his box, came around the bar, and very solemnly handed Leah a stick of charcoal.

"You sign the ceiling," he said. "Anywhere you like."

Leah took the charcoal.

She climbed up onto the table in Marg's daughter's boots, among the empty tankards, with the whole bar watching, and found them almost at once near the scorch mark, because she was looking for them. *H. Potter, 2001.* And under it, in the same loopy scrawl, *HP — 2004 — sorry about the curtain.*

She reached up between them and wrote, in tall, sharp, slanted letters, pressing so hard the charcoal crumbled: *LEAH.*

She thought about it.

Then she drew an arrow from her name to the one that said *sorry about the curtain*, and wrote under it, smaller: *got him.*

The bar roared.

Leah climbed down off the table. Harry was still sitting there, with his singed whiskers and his burnt tuft and the smoke still curling faintly up from between his ears. He was looking at her. He'd stopped laughing. His ears were all the way forward. His tail had gone completely still.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing at all."

Then the door to the alley opened, and a young wizard with a camera came in, a big old-fashioned box camera with a flash attachment the size of a dinner plate. He looked around the room. He saw Harry. He saw Leah, standing beside the table with a stick of charcoal in her hand and smoke still coming out of her nose.

He raised the camera.

The flash went off with a bang and a puff of purple smoke, and the whole room went white.

When Leah could see again, the young wizard was already backing out the door, grinning.

"*Southern Cross Sentinel!*" he called. "Thanks, Mr. Potter! Lovely evening!"

The door banged shut.

Harry's ears went flat.

"Who was that?" Leah said.

"Wizarding paper. Johannesburg." He was looking at the door. "That'll be on the front page tomorrow."

"Is that a problem?"

He didn't answer right away. He was looking at her now. At the green top and the bracelet and the charcoal in her hand.

"For me," he said, "no. I'm used to it. For you—" His ears went sideways. "Somebody's going to want to know who you are. And Hennessey reads the *Sentinel*. And so does a woman in Ottawa with very neat handwriting."

Leah thought about it. She thought about Jacob, and the pack, and the forms, and the tribe, and the council fire, and the secret that was older than anybody. She thought about the paper landing on doormats all over a country she'd never set foot in before today, with her face on it, burping fire at the Wolf Who Lived.

She thought about how she'd come two thousand kilometers to get away from people who knew her.

She thought about Pieter's tired, bitter, almost-glad smile. *So there are two of you now.*

She looked up at the ceiling. *LEAH.* And the arrow. *Got him.*

"Let them look," she said.

Harry's ears came up slowly.

"Nobody in La Push reads a wizard newspaper from Johannesburg," Leah said. "And if your boss has a problem, he can take it up with me." She sat down, and picked up his tankard, which still had half a pint of Drakensberg Red in it, and looked him in the eye. "Third pint's not allowed, right?"

"Not since 2002."

"This is your second pint," Leah said. "Technically."

"Leah—"

She drank it.

Across the room, Nomsa came out of the kitchen with a fire extinguisher, a big brass one shaped like a dragon, and set it down on the bar beside Grimbald, without a word, and folded her arms, and watched.

Harry looked at the fire extinguisher. Then at Leah. Then at the smoke already beginning to curl out of her nose.

His tail started moving. Slowly, side to side.

"Ja," he said quietly, mostly to himself. "Okay."

And he leaned back in his chair, well out of range this time, and settled in to watch.
