# Need to Know

## Chapter One: Snare

## Part One: The Store

---

### I. Seth

*Forks, Washington — Sunday, October 1st, 2006*

The list was on the back of an envelope from the electric company, in his mom's handwriting, and the first thing on it was *SHORTS (CHEAP) (LOTS)*, underlined twice.

Seth Clearwater stood in the clothing section of Newton's Olympic Outfitters with the envelope in one hand and a pair of khaki drawstring hiking shorts in the other, and tried to do math.

The shorts were on clearance. Nine ninety-nine, marked down to six. He went through, on a good week, maybe four pairs. On a bad week, the kind of week where Jacob called a double patrol because something had come down out of Canada smelling like a dead dog wrapped in a perfume counter, he went through eight or nine. You phased with them tied to your ankle if you remembered, and then you forgot, or a strap snapped, or you phased mad, and then you were standing in the woods at four in the morning with nothing on but a leather cord and a bad attitude, and you had to walk home the long way around so Mrs. Ateara didn't see you from her kitchen window.

He'd done that walk three times since Labor Day. Once in the rain.

Six dollars a pair. He had a hundred and forty dollars of his mom's money folded in his jeans pocket, and the list also said *propane (2)* and *batteries D* and *DO NOT buy candy with this, Seth, I mean it*.

He took twelve.

He took them off the hanger rack one at a time, because the hangers kept tangling with each other, and he stacked them over his forearm until he had a pile of khaki up to his elbow. Then he stood there looking at the pile and thought about it, and went back and took three more, because winter was coming and Paul said winter was worse. You went through more. You got colder in human form when you were tired, and when you were cold you phased easier, and when you phased easier you lost more shorts.

Leah said a lot of things about being a wolf in the winter. Leah had never done a winter either. That had never once stopped her.

Fifteen pairs. Ninety bucks. He'd figure out the propane.

The store smelled like rubber and canvas and that gun-oil smell from the counter at the back where they kept the fishing stuff, and under all of it, because Seth couldn't turn his nose off any more than he could turn his ears off, he could smell the woman at the register. Mrs. Newton. Hairspray, coffee, a cat at home, lotion that smelled like fake cucumbers. She'd been watching him since he came in. She had the look on her face that people in Forks got when one of the La Push boys came into town, especially since last spring, when all the La Push boys had gotten very big very fast and started cutting their hair short and walking around in the rain in T-shirts.

Steroids, was the theory in Forks. Seth had heard Mr. Newton say it once, outside the post office, to a guy from the logging company. *It's in the water down there or it's in a needle, and I know which one I'd bet on.*

Seth had kind of wanted to tell him it was in the blood. Literally. But that was against about six rules, so he'd smiled at Mr. Newton instead and said *Morning*, and Mr. Newton had looked at him like he'd pulled a knife.

He was six foot four now. He'd been five foot eleven at Christmas.

He went to the register. Mrs. Newton watched him put fifteen pairs of shorts down on the counter. She looked at the shorts. She looked at him.

"Swim team," Seth said.

"In October."

"Cold-water training." He smiled at her. He had a good smile; he knew that. His mom said he'd been smiling since the day he was born, that he'd come out yelling and then stopped yelling and smiled at the nurse, and the nurse had said *well, aren't you going to be trouble*. "Coach is really intense about it."

Mrs. Newton started ringing up shorts. She did it slowly, one at a time, picking up each pair, finding the tag, beeping it. "There's no swim team at the reservation school."

"It's kind of an informal team."

"Mm-hm." Beep.

"Do you guys have propane? The little green ones? For the camp stoves?"

"Back wall, by the fishing licenses."

"Thanks."

He left the shorts on the counter and went to look for propane, and that was when he heard the woman in the rain jackets swear.

She didn't swear loud. Somebody without his ears wouldn't have heard it. It was a little breath of a word, frustrated, in a low voice that had some kind of accent under it he couldn't place, something that made the vowels sit flat and wide like a road in the desert. *Mierda.* And then, in English, softer, "Who designs these things."

Seth looked.

There were four racks of rain gear in the corner by the window, two of jackets and two of pants, and a woman was standing in the middle of them holding a jacket up by the shoulders and looking at it the way Leah looked at Jacob when he tried to explain a patrol rotation. The jacket was the bright yellow one, the expensive one, the kind of yellow that people bought so the helicopter could find them. She had another one, a green one, over her arm. She was frowning at the tags.

He couldn't see her face. She had her back half to him, and her hair was in the way, a lot of dark hair pulled back in a knot that had mostly given up. She was wearing a canvas jacket that was soaked across the shoulders, totally soaked, the kind of soaked you got from walking around Forks in a jacket that was meant for somewhere else. There was red dust in the seams of her boots, fine as flour. Seth could smell it from fifteen feet away, dry and mineral and baked, a smell that didn't exist anywhere on the whole peninsula, and under the dust she smelled like rain and old paper and some kind of soap with sage in it, and something else.

Something else. He couldn't name it. It was faint, and it was like the air right before lightning, that metal taste you got on the beach when a storm was coming in off the water and every hair on your arms stood up.

He didn't think about it. He thought about it later, a lot, but right then he didn't. Right then he just thought she looked like somebody who was about to spend two hundred dollars on the wrong jacket.

"The yellow one's not worth it," he said.

She turned around.

Afterward he tried to tell it, in his head, so he could get it right. He tried a bunch of times. Jacob had told it to him once, the way it had been for him with Nessie, standing in the Cullens' living room with his teeth out, ready to kill a baby, and then the baby had looked at him. Jacob had said it was like gravity moved. Like the whole world had been tied down to the earth by a million steel cables, everything, his dad and the pack and Bella and the reservation and the ocean and his own name, and all the cables got cut at once, and there was one left, and it went from the middle of his chest to her.

Seth had thought that sounded kind of dramatic. He'd thought that about Quil too, when Quil had tried to explain Claire. He'd heard it in Sam's head, before he'd left Sam's pack, a hundred times, Sam's memory of the first time he'd seen Emily, and it had always looked to Seth like something that happened to other people. Like a car crash. Like winning the lottery. A thing you heard about.

For Seth it went another way.

It felt like he'd been floating his whole life, a little bit, a couple inches off the ground, and he hadn't known, because everybody else was floating too. And then she turned around and he landed.

She had a narrow face, brown, with a hard straight nose and dark eyebrows that were pulled down at him, and a little white scar through the left one like somebody had drawn a line through it with chalk. Her eyes were a brown so dark it was almost black, with something lighter around the middle, a ring of amber like a coin in the bottom of a well. She was maybe twenty-two. Maybe. She had a mouth that looked like it was used to saying no to people and enjoying it.

She was looking at him like he was weird. Which, okay. He was standing there with his mouth open.

The ground was right where it was supposed to be. The store was right where it was supposed to be. Mrs. Newton was beeping. Rain was coming down on the window behind the woman, steady, gray, the same rain that had been coming down since Thursday. Everything was in its place, and none of it was holding him up anymore. She was. Every single thing he was, all of it, everything he had, was hanging off a line that ran out of the middle of his chest and into her, and the line was the only thing in the world that had any weight.

*Oh,* Seth thought. *Oh, no. Oh, wow.*

"Sorry?" the woman said.

He'd said something. Before. What had he said?

"The jacket," Seth said. His voice came out normal, which was amazing. "The yellow one. It's not worth it. It's like two hundred bucks and it's mostly the brand. It's waterproof, but it doesn't breathe, so you'll be just as wet in like an hour, only from the inside."

She looked at the yellow jacket. She looked back at him. "From the inside."

"Sweat. Sorry. You'll sweat. Everybody does in that one. My mom bought one for my dad when I was a kid and he wore it twice and he said it was like wearing a garbage bag." He was talking too much. He knew he was talking too much. He didn't seem to be able to stop. "The green one's better. The green one you've got. It's got the zips under the arms, see? You open those up and the air goes through. It's like ninety bucks."

She held up the green jacket and looked at the armpit zips like they'd personally offended her. "That's the most you've ever said about armpits to a stranger, isn't it."

"Yeah," Seth said. "Probably."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth, the little catch that comes right before a smile, and then she got it back under control.

"I'm from New Mexico," she said. "Originally. Before that I was in the Yucatán for two years. Before that, Peru. I have never owned a rain jacket in my life. I came up here on Tuesday in a canvas coat I bought in Taos, and I have been wet since Tuesday." She held the green jacket a little higher. "You're telling me this is the one."

"That's the one."

"And the pants?"

"Get the pants. You'll want the pants. You'll think you don't need the pants and then you'll go for a walk."

"I wasn't planning on going for a lot of walks."

"Everybody goes for a walk here eventually," Seth said. "There's nothing else to do."

She looked at him for another second. She looked at his arms, at the T-shirt, at the way his T-shirt was dry except where he'd come in from the parking lot. She looked at his feet, which were in flip-flops, because all his sneakers had a hole in them right now from the toe trying to come through. Then she looked back at his face, and there was a little line between her eyebrows that hadn't been there before.

"You're not cold," she said.

She said it flat, the way you'd read a thermometer. Seth had the stupid, sudden feeling she'd just noticed something he hadn't meant to show her. Something more than the T-shirt.

"I run hot," he said.

"Mm." She folded the green jacket over her arm. She hung the yellow one back up. "I'm Layla."

"Seth."

She held out her hand.

He had a second where he thought about it. You thought about it, when you were one of the pack, about shaking hands with people. You were a hundred and eight degrees. You felt like you had a fever. Most people said something. *Whoa, are you sick?* Sam's rule had been, keep your hands in your pockets in town, which Seth had always thought was kind of sad, and when he'd gone with Jacob he'd started shaking hands again, because Jacob didn't care, and because if somebody said *are you sick* you could just say *yeah, kind of* and they backed off fast.

He shook her hand.

Her hand was cold from the rain, and dry, and hard across the palm, calluses at the base of her fingers like she held something a lot. A tool. A pen, a hammer, he didn't know. Her grip was strong.

And then the air went funny.

He felt it in his teeth. That lightning taste, the one he'd smelled on her, came up out of her palm into his and ran up his arm like when you hit your funny bone, a fizz, a buzz, and every hair on his arm stood straight up. The jolt had started on her side. Something in her hand had jumped when it touched his, the way a cat jumps when you come in a room it thought was empty, and then it settled.

She took her hand back slowly, the way you'd step back from a dog you weren't sure about, and she put it in her jacket pocket, and her face had gone completely smooth, completely blank, the way Leah's face went when Sam came into a room.

"You do run hot," Layla said.

"Yeah."

"Like, a lot."

"I'm kind of a big guy," Seth said. "It's a metabolism thing. My doctor says."

Which was a lie, technically, because his doctor was Dr. Cullen and Dr. Cullen had said a lot of things about Seth's metabolism and none of them were *it's a big-guy thing*. Dr. Cullen had said it was fascinating. Dr. Cullen had wanted to take his temperature every hour for a day, and Seth had let him, because Dr. Cullen was the nicest guy in the world and also because Seth had been bored.

Layla's eyes narrowed a tiny bit. She didn't say anything.

"Anyway." Seth pointed at the pants rack. "Medium. Probably. They run big. And get the boots, too, if you're going to stay. Not those." He nodded down at her boots with the red dust in the seams. "Those are going to rot. Like, actually rot. It's a whole thing here. Everything rots."

"Comforting."

"You get used to it."

"Do you."

"No," Seth admitted. "Not really. But you stop noticing."

That got the smile. Real one this time, quick and crooked, just on one side, there and gone. It did something to the line in his chest. It pulled it. It was like somebody had plucked a guitar string that ran right through him, and the note was still going, and he could feel it in his back teeth.

"Okay, Seth," Layla said. "Thanks for the armpit consult."

"Anytime."

She went to the pants rack. Seth stood there for a second, and then he remembered he was in a store and there was a lady at the register who'd been beeping fifteen pairs of shorts, and he'd been sent here for propane. Two. Little green ones.

He went and got the propane. He got the batteries. He did not get candy. He went back to the register, where Mrs. Newton had folded all fifteen pairs of shorts into a bag with the expression of somebody who was going to tell her husband about this at dinner, and he paid her, and he counted his change, and he did not look at the corner with the rain gear more than four or five times.

She was looking at the pants. She was holding a pair up against herself. She was frowning at them, and talking to herself under her breath, in Spanish and then English and then something that wasn't either of those, a couple of sharp words he didn't know that sounded old and made the air near the window go still for a second, like a breath held.

He didn't think about that either. Not then.

"Seth," said Mrs. Newton.

"Huh?"

"Your change." She was holding out a dollar and some coins. He'd dropped it. He didn't know when.

"Oh. Sorry. Thanks."

"Are you all right? You look—" Mrs. Newton hesitated. She had the look of someone deciding whether *on drugs* was something you could say to a customer. "Flushed."

"I'm good," Seth said. "I'm really good."

He was. That was the weird part. He'd thought, the few times he'd thought about it, at night, on patrol, with Leah's head going *Sam Emily Sam Emily Sam* in the background like a song she couldn't turn off, he'd thought that if it happened to him it would be scary. It looked scary. He'd watched it from inside Sam and Jared and Paul and Quil, and later Jake, and it had always looked like being hit by something. Like losing a fight.

It felt like the first warm day after a long winter. It felt like coming home and the lights were on.

He took the bag of shorts and the propane and walked to the door, and at the door he stopped, because he couldn't not.

She was at the window now, holding the pants up to the gray light, checking the seams. She'd turned a little. He could see the side of her face.

"Hey," Seth said.

She looked over.

"You're the one who moved into the Quinn place," he said. "Out on Calawah Way. The blue house with the bees."

Her face went smooth again. "How do you know that?"

"Forks," Seth said, and shrugged, which made the shorts rustle. "It's like four thousand people. Somebody new moves into a house that's been empty for a year, everybody knows by lunch. My mom's friend Mrs. Ateara knew the old lady who lived there. Maeve. She said she used to bring honey down to the tribal center every fall."

Layla's face changed again. Something underneath it moved, quick, and he couldn't read it, but he was pretty sure it hurt.

"My grandmother," she said.

"Oh." *Oh, Seth. Nice. Great.* "I'm sorry. I didn't— She was nice. Everybody said she was nice."

"She was a pain in the ass," Layla said. "But yeah. She was nice." She folded the pants over her arm with the jacket. "I'm cleaning out the house. Figuring out what to do with it. With the bees."

"You're staying?"

He heard how he said it. He heard how much was in it. So did she; he watched her hear it, watched her eyebrows go up a fraction.

"For a while," Layla said slowly. "Probably. I don't know yet."

"Cool," Seth said. "That's cool. If you need anything. Moving stuff. Heavy stuff. I'm—" He held up the bag of shorts, and the propane, like they proved something. "I'm pretty strong."

"Swim team?"

He felt his face go hot, which was saying something. She'd heard. Of course she'd heard. It was a small store.

"Informal team," he said.

The smile came back. Both sides of her mouth this time. It was the best thing he'd ever seen.

"I'll keep it in mind, Seth," Layla said.

He went out into the rain.

He walked across the parking lot to his mom's car, which was an old Ford Taurus with a dent in the back door where Leah had backed into a post at the clinic in August. He opened the door and put the shorts on the passenger seat and the propane in the footwell, and then he sat in the driver's seat with the door open and the rain coming in on his left leg, and he looked back at the store window.

He could see her through the glass. Just the shape of her, dark hair, green jacket, moving down the aisle toward the boots.

The line in his chest went straight through the windshield and the rain and the glass of the store window and into her. He could feel exactly where she was. If she walked to the back of the store, he'd know. If she went out the other door, he'd know. He could have closed his eyes and pointed.

He sat in the car for nine minutes. He knew it was nine because he watched the clock on the dashboard the whole time, and because every single one of those minutes he was thinking: *Leah is going to kill me.*

Then he thought: *No. Leah is going to be weird about it, and then she's going to be mean about it, and then she's going to go for a really long run by herself, and then she's going to come back and be mean about it some more.*

And then he thought, because it was there in the back of his head, where it had always been, since the night she'd phased the first time screaming in the yard and he'd phased two hours later trying to get to her: *And she's going to be sad. Because it's me and not her.*

Layla came out of the store at 2:14 with two bags and a box under her arm. The box had boots in it. Waterproof ones, the good kind. Seth watched her walk to an old white pickup with New Mexico plates and a cracked tail-light and put everything in the cab, and then she stopped, with the door open, in the rain, in her new green jacket with the tags still on, and turned around.

She looked straight across the parking lot at his car.

He was forty yards away. It was raining hard. He was sitting in a car with the door open and his leg getting wet, like a total weirdo. She shouldn't have been able to see him well enough to know he was looking at her.

She looked at him anyway, for a long second, with that line between her eyebrows. Then she got in her truck, and she drove away, and the line in his chest went with her down Forks Avenue and turned left on Calawah, and Seth sat in his mom's car with both hands on the steering wheel, feeling it go, feeling it stretch and not break, feeling it hum.

"Okay," he said out loud, to nobody. "Okay. Okay."

He started the car.

He was halfway back to La Push before he realized he'd never found out what the lightning smell was.

### II. Layla

*Calawah Way, Forks — the same evening*

The bees knew before she did.

Layla Quinn noticed it the way she noticed most things now, a beat late and sideways, out of the corner of the part of her mind that never stopped working. She'd pulled the truck into the gravel by the side of the house and killed the engine, and she was sitting there with the rain drumming on the roof, listening to it, when she realized the hives were humming.

There were six of them, along the back fence where the yard ran out and the forest started, white boxes on cinderblocks gone gray-green with moss. Her grandmother had kept bees on this lot for forty-one years. They should have been quiet. It was October, it was raining, it was cold enough that her breath fogged the windshield, and bees didn't fly in the rain. They clustered. They sat in their boxes in a ball around the queen and shivered to keep warm and waited for spring.

They were humming. All six boxes. A low, steady note she could feel in the steering wheel under her hands.

She sat and listened to it for a while.

Then she got out, and got the bags, and the boot box, and went around the back of the house instead of the front, through the wet grass to the fence. She put her free hand flat on the lid of the nearest hive. The wood was soaked and cold, and under it the colony was going like a struck bell.

"What," Layla said to it. "What's got you up?"

The bees didn't answer. Bees, in her experience, very rarely did. But she stood there in the rain with her palm on the box and let her mind go loose and quiet, the way you did on a job when you were standing in front of a door somebody had cursed four hundred years ago and you needed to feel the shape of the thing before you touched it, and she felt the ward.

Her grandmother's ward. It ran through the hives, all six of them, a thread of old magic strung from box to box and around the property line and back, and the bees were part of it. Bees were the ward. That was how Maeve Quinn had done things: hedge-witch work, old-country work, from the side of the family that had come over from County Clare in 1849 and never quite stopped looking over its shoulder. You grew a ward. You fed it. You kept bees, and the bees kept the boundary, and anything that crossed the boundary that the bees didn't like, the bees told you.

The ward was awake. Fully awake, all the way around, every thread of it humming. It had been asleep since she'd gotten here on Tuesday. She'd checked.

Nothing had crossed. She'd have felt it. The ward was humming because of her.

She took her hand off the hive and looked at it. At her palm. Her right palm, the hand she'd held out to a very tall, very sweet boy in a store full of camping gear, three and a half hours ago.

"Huh," Layla said.

She went inside.

---

The house was blue on the outside and brown on the inside, every room, the brown of old wood and old books and the honey that had soaked into the kitchen floor over forty years of jars. It smelled like beeswax and dust and woodsmoke and, faintly, still, like her grandmother's cigarettes, Pall Malls, unfiltered, which Maeve had smoked one of every night at nine o'clock on the back step for her entire adult life and which, the doctors said, had nothing to do with what killed her at eighty-three. The doctors had said it was her heart. Layla had read the healer's report from the St. Jerome's clinic in Seattle, the magical one, behind the dry cleaner's on Third. It said her heart too. It said she'd been tired.

Layla put the bags on the kitchen table, on top of the stack of mail she hadn't opened. She took off the canvas coat, which was so wet it had gone stiff, and hung it on the hook by the door, and it dripped on the floor. She stood in her grandmother's kitchen in a damp flannel shirt and looked at the new green jacket in the bag with the tags still on it, and thought about the boy.

*I run hot.*

She'd thought, for the first second, that he was sick. Fever. Then she'd thought, no, look at him, look at the color in his face, look at the way he stood. He was the healthiest-looking thing she'd ever seen in her life. Sick people didn't look like that. And she'd thought that, and then she'd noticed she was thinking about how healthy he looked, and she'd made herself stop.

He'd been about six foot four. Russet-brown skin, short black hair, a face that was all open, a face that had never in its life tried to hide what it was thinking. He'd been wearing a T-shirt in the rain, in October, in Washington, and flip-flops, and he'd been dry. Bone dry, except for his shoulders, where he'd come in from the parking lot. And he'd looked at her—

She didn't know how to finish that.

He'd looked at her the way the bees were humming. That was the closest she could get.

Layla pulled her wand out of her sleeve.

Thirteen inches. Mesquite. Thunderbird tail feather. The old man at the wand shop in Albuquerque had told her, when she was eleven, that a Thunderbird feather would give her trouble for the rest of her life, and he'd been right. It was a nervous wand, a wand that sensed things before she did and sometimes cast before she'd decided to, and twice in her career it had saved her life by firing a Shield Charm at something she hadn't seen yet. She'd learned to listen to it. She'd learned to keep it on a short leash.

In the store, when she'd shaken his hand, it had jumped in her sleeve.

She'd felt it. Against the inside of her forearm, a hard twitch, like a sleeping dog's leg when it dreams. Her wand had felt something come up out of that boy's hand and it had sat up and pointed its ears. And her own magic had answered him too. That was the part she'd been trying not to think about for three and a half hours. It had come up out of her own palm without her calling it, a little spark, a reflex, the way your hand jerks off a stove. She'd felt it go. She'd watched his eyes, to see if he'd felt it, and his eyes had gone a little wide, and the hair on his arm had stood up.

He'd felt something. He didn't know what. She was sure of that much. He'd looked confused, and happy, and confused about being happy, and he'd gone on talking about armpits.

She laid her right hand flat on the kitchen table, palm up, and touched the tip of the wand to the center of it, and said, very quietly, "*Revelio vestigium.*"

It was a working spell, a curse-breaker's spell, the kind Ilvermorny never taught, the kind you picked up on the job from an old hand who'd picked it up from an older one. It showed you what had touched a thing. What magic, what residue, what had been left behind. She'd used it on doorframes in Cuzco and on altar stones in Chichén Itzá and on the inside of a sarcophagus lid in a private collection in Phoenix that had turned out to be full of something that had eaten the previous owner's hands.

Her palm lit up.

Faintly. A wash of color, like breath on cold glass. Her own residue, she knew that, a familiar gold, it was all over everything she owned. Rain, gray, faint. A smear of something rubbery and industrial: the jacket tags.

And across the center of her palm, where he'd held it, a print.

It glowed dull red. The red of coals. It had the shape of his hand, the heel of it and the long fingers, and it was still there, three and a half hours later, which was the first thing that was wrong, because residue from a person, a plain person, a Muggle, faded in minutes. And the red was moving. Slowly. A dim pulse, in and out, in and out, like something breathing.

Layla stared at it.

She knew a lot of residues. It was her job to know them. She knew the cold blue smear a ghost left, and the greasy green of a dark object, and the sharp silver of goblin work, and the gray dust of an Inferius. She'd seen werewolf residue once, at a scene in Utah, and it had been a bruise color, purple-black, sick and sour, the color of a curse that had taken a person and folded them in half.

This one was clean. There was nothing sick in it at all. It was red and it pulsed and it was, she thought, the oldest thing she'd ever had on her skin, older than the altar stones, older than anything in the Yucatán. It had the same feeling those places had, the feeling of something that had been running underneath the ground since before anybody came to write it down. Except it was alive. It was a boy in a T-shirt who wanted her to buy the jacket with the armpit zips.

"Okay," Layla said out loud. "Okay. What are you."

The print pulsed. In, out.

She closed her hand.

---

Her grandmother's journals were in the second bedroom, which had been Maeve's sewing room and then Maeve's bee room and then, for the last ten years, Maeve's everything room. Layla had been avoiding it since Tuesday. She'd gotten as far as the doorway twice and both times stood there looking at the boxes and the shelves and the smoking jacket on the hook behind the door and turned around and gone and done something else, like scrubbing the oven, which hadn't needed it.

She went in now.

The journals were on the bottom shelf of the bookcase, in a long row. Forty-one years, forty-one books, black leather, the cheap kind you could buy at the stationer's in Port Angeles. Maeve had written in every one in green ink, in a hand so small and slanted you needed a magnifying glass, and she'd written about bees and weather and the price of feed and her arthritis and which of the neighbors was cheating on whom. Layla had read some of them as a kid, sitting on the floor of this room in the summers when her parents sent her up from Albuquerque, trying to find the good parts. There hadn't been many good parts. There had been a lot about bees.

She found the last one. 2005. It was only half full.

She sat on the floor with her back against the bookcase and her wand in her lap and her right hand still closed in a fist, and she paged through it. January: snow, hives fine. February: snow, bees fine, Mrs. Ateara came by, her husband's knees. March: early bloom on the alders, bad sign. April—

She stopped.

April 11. *The Quileute boys are running hot again.*

She read it twice. Then she read the rest of the entry.

*Saw the Uley boy at the Thriftway, Sam. Taller by a foot than Christmas. Coming off him like a stove. Same as Ephraim's lot, back when I was new here and Grandmother Black was still alive to tell me about it. I thought they'd stopped. I thought all that was done with. Something's woken them. I can guess what. The doctor's family has been back two years now and I'd bet my hives the boys are the answer to that question.*

*Not my business. Never was.*

*Layla, if you're reading this, and I know you will be, because I know you, and I know you'll be up here clearing out my things and nosing in my books: it's not your business either. Leave them be. All of them. The cold ones and the hot ones both. They keep to their side and they keep the woods clean and I've lived forty years next door to that and never had cause to lift a finger. Don't you go lifting fingers. You came up here to rest. I know you did. I know about Mexico. Your mother told me.*

*Rest.*

Layla sat very still on the floor of her grandmother's room.

The rain came down on the roof. Out back, the hives were still humming. She could hear them through the wall.

*I know about Mexico.*

She turned the page, but that was it for April. May was bees. June was bees and a long rant about the new pastor. July, her heart. *Dr. Cullen says it's my heart. Lovely man. Very cold hands.* And then August, and the entries got shorter, and further apart, and the green ink got shakier, and the last one was September 3rd, and all it said was *Good honey this year. Best in ten. Tell Layla.*

Layla closed the journal.

She sat there for a while with it in her lap. She thought about Mexico, which she was trying not to do, which she had come twelve hundred miles north to a town where it rained three hundred days a year specifically to not do. She thought about the cenote, the black water and the white stone and the carvings around the lip of it that she'd read wrong. Read wrong by one glyph. And Tomás, who'd trusted her reading, because she was the best reader on the crew, everybody said so, she'd been the best since she was nineteen. Tomás going down the rope ladder into the dark. The water coming up.

She didn't think about that part. She'd gotten very good at not thinking about that part.

She thought about the boy instead. It was easier. It was a lot easier, it turned out.

*The Quileute boys are running hot again. Same as Ephraim's lot.*

*The cold ones and the hot ones both.*

She'd driven in on Tuesday afternoon, through Port Angeles and around Lake Crescent and down 101 with the rain coming in sideways off the strait, and she'd stopped for gas at the station at the north end of town, because the truck had been running on fumes since Sappho. She'd been standing at the pump, soaked, miserable, watching the numbers roll, when a car had pulled in on the other side of the island. A big red convertible, an M3, with the top up. Two people had gotten out, a blond woman and a huge man with dark curly hair, and they hadn't bought gas. They'd gone into the little store and come out with nothing and stood by the car for a minute talking low, and the blond woman had looked across the island at Layla. Just once. A cool, flat, uninterested look.

Layla's wand arm had gone cold to the elbow.

It had been the same feeling she'd had in the cenote, right before. A curse-breaker's feeling. You got it after enough years. The skin on the back of your neck learned things your eyes didn't, and what the back of her neck had said, at the gas station on Tuesday, was *that woman is not alive*.

She'd stood there with the pump handle in her hand, very still, the way you stood still when you met a mountain lion on a trail. The blond woman had looked away. The two of them had gotten back in the car and pulled out, and Layla had stood there for a full minute after they'd gone, with gas overflowing out of the tank and running down the side of her truck and pooling around her boots, before she noticed.

She'd told herself she was tired. She'd told herself it was the drive, and Mexico, and her grandmother's empty house waiting at the end of it.

She'd been very good at telling herself things for about a year now.

*The doctor's family. The cold ones.*

*Dr. Cullen says it's my heart. Lovely man. Very cold hands.*

Layla opened her fist and looked at her palm. The red print was still there. Fainter now. Still breathing.

Here was what she knew. There was a family in this town, a doctor's family, who weren't alive, who'd been here before, in her great-grandmother's time, and come back. And there were boys on the reservation, at the mouth of the river, who'd started running hot when the family came back. Who'd grown a foot in a winter. Who carried something in them so old it made her wand sit up in her sleeve, and who looked at you—

Who looked at you like that.

Her grandmother had said leave them be.

Her grandmother had lived next door to it for forty years and never lifted a finger. Her grandmother had kept bees. Layla had come up here, if she was honest, and she was trying to be, here, on the floor, at nine-thirty at night, with the rain coming down, to find out whether she could be the kind of witch who kept bees. Whether there was a version of her that didn't need to know what was behind every door. That could leave a thing be.

She thought about Seth's face when he said *you're staying?* All of it right there on the outside. Every bit of it.

"Damn it, Gran," she said.

She got up off the floor. She put the journal back on the shelf, in its place at the end of the row. She went into the kitchen and took the green jacket out of the bag and cut the tags off it with the kitchen scissors and hung it on the hook by the door next to the dead canvas coat, and she took the boots out of the box and set them by the door too, side by side, and she stood back and looked at them.

Then she went out on the back step, and sat down on it in the rain, where her grandmother had sat every night at nine o'clock for forty years. She didn't have a cigarette. She'd quit at twenty. She sat there anyway with her elbows on her knees and listened to the bees.

They hummed until nearly midnight. Then, slowly, one box at a time, they went quiet, and the ward settled.

It dozed. When she put her mind out to it, she found it had kept one eye open.

It was watching the road to the south. The road toward the coast, toward La Push, toward the mouth of the river.

It was waiting for him to come back.

So, Layla realized, sitting on her grandmother's step in the dark with her hair soaked through, was she.

### III. Leah

*The Cullen boundary, east of Forks — that night*

Leah was three miles into the river leg when her brother phased in, and the first thing he thought was a woman's face.

It hit her the way a wave hits you when you've got your back to the ocean. One second she had the pack mind to herself: the cold mud under her pads, the river on her left going loud and brown with four days of rain, the stink of the Cullen place a mile off to the east, that sick-sweet bleach-and-flowers smell that never washed out of her nose no matter how many times she ran through the creek. Her own thoughts, going around in their usual groove. Jacob, a dim warm hum way at the back, asleep in human form on the Cullens' couch with the kid on his chest, which was where Jacob slept now, every night, like a dog on a porch.

The next second, she had a face.

Narrow. Brown. Dark eyebrows pulled down. A white nick through the left one. Eyes so dark they were almost black, with a ring of something gold in the middle, looking straight at her.

At Seth. Looking at Seth.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Oh, you have* got *to be kidding me.*

*Hi,* Seth thought. Sheepish. Thrilled. Terrified. All three, all at once, and loud, the way everything in Seth was loud. *So. Um. Something happened today.*

She stopped running.

She stood in the mud on the bank with the river going past and her sides heaving and her ears flat, and she watched it. She didn't have a choice about watching it. That was the pack. That was the whole sick joke of the pack; you didn't get to look away. It came pouring out of Seth's head into hers like a bucket emptied over her, the store, the shorts, the rain jackets, *the yellow one's not worth it*, and the woman turning around, and then—

Leah had been on the other end of this before. She knew exactly what it looked like from outside. She'd watched it in Sam's head all spring and summer, every single time Sam phased, Sam's memory of the first time he'd seen Emily standing on the porch of her parents' house in Makah. Sam couldn't keep it out of his head. None of them could. Jared and Kim. Paul and Rachel. Quil and that little girl. Jacob and the baby on the Cullens' white couch three weeks ago, ugly and squalling and bloody, the room going sideways. She'd seen it all, every one, from the inside. She knew the shape of it like she knew the shape of her own teeth.

She'd never had it come from Seth.

It was different from Seth. Of course it was. Everything was different from Seth. When Sam had imprinted it had been like watching a man get hit by a truck, and then grab on to the truck. When Jacob had done it, it had been like watching somebody fall out of a window. Seth—

Seth had landed. That was what it looked like, from inside his head. Like he'd been up in the air his whole life without knowing it and somebody had put him down gently on his feet. And then the woman had smiled, the crooked one-sided smile, and the whole inside of Seth's head had lit up like the Fourth of July.

Leah stood in the mud and watched her baby brother fall in love with a stranger in Newton's Olympic Outfitters, and felt something in her chest go tight and then crack straight down the middle, very quietly, the way ice goes on a pond in March.

*Leah?* Seth thought. *Lee? Say something.*

*Give me a second.*

*Are you mad?*

*I said give me a second, Seth.*

He gave her a second. He gave her about six of them, which for Seth was heroic. She could feel him on the other side of the reservation, standing in the trees behind their mom's house, where he'd phased, where he always phased, holding very still with his tail down. Waiting for her to be mad. Waiting for her to be mean. She could feel him bracing.

That was the worst part. Him bracing.

Because she'd taught him to. Every single day since last spring she'd taught him to brace for her, every time she'd snapped at him in the pack mind, every time she'd let *Sam Emily Sam Emily* run in the back of her head like a radio left on in a room nobody used, every time he'd tried to be nice to her and she'd bitten his head off for it. Seven months of that. He was eighteen in March. Six foot four. He'd been the one to come find her the first night, when she'd phased screaming in the yard and he'd phased two hours later trying to get to her and the two of them had ended up standing in the woods behind the house as wolves, staring at each other, at what they were, too scared to think. He'd been seventeen. He'd thought, *Lee, it's okay, it's okay, I'm here*, over and over, even though it wasn't okay and he'd had no idea what he was talking about.

And he was standing behind their mom's house now, with the biggest thing that had ever happened to him lit up inside his skull, bracing for her to ruin it.

*I'm not mad,* Leah thought.

He didn't believe her. She could feel him not believing her.

*I'm not,* she thought again, harder, and made herself mean it. It took a second. *Okay? Seth. I'm not mad at you. You didn't do anything. It just happened. It's not like you picked it.*

*It's kind of like I picked it,* Seth thought. *It doesn't feel like I didn't pick it.*

*That's what they all say.*

*Does it hurt?* He was being careful. Nobody else in the world would have known he was being careful, but she knew him. *You. I mean. Does it hurt you. That it's me.*

There it was. Right out in the open, in the middle of both their heads, where neither of them could pretend they hadn't heard it.

The thing with the pack mind was, you couldn't lie in it. You could try. You could think *I'm fine* real loud over the top of everything else, and Leah did that a lot, but everybody could still hear the everything else underneath. Seth could hear it now. She could feel him hearing it.

*Yeah,* Leah thought. *A little.*

*I'm sorry.*

*Don't be sorry. You don't get to be sorry for this one. Got it?*

*Okay.*

*I mean it, Seth.*

*Okay.*

She started running again, because if she didn't she was going to lie down in the mud and not get up. She ran the river leg the way it was meant to be run, along the bank and then up the slope where the trail went through the big cedars and across the creek where the Cullen line met the old treaty line, and she let the running take it. That was the one good thing about being this. When it got bad, you could run. You could run so fast that nothing caught you. She was the fastest of all of them, faster than Jacob, faster than Sam, faster than Paul by a mile, and on nights like this she thought maybe that was why. Maybe the wolf had known what she'd need.

Behind her, a long way behind, she felt Seth start running too, coming up from the reservation on the southern loop. She didn't tell him not to.

She kept the woman's face in her head while she ran. It was there because it was in Seth's head and Seth couldn't stop thinking about it, so it was in hers. Narrow brown face. The scar through the eyebrow. That mouth.

And something else.

Leah slowed down. Then she stopped again, on top of the ridge above the Cullen place, with the house down there below her in the dark with every window lit, glass and white walls and that stink coming up off it in waves.

*Seth. Go back.*

*What? Back where?*

*In your head. The store. Go back to when you shook her hand.*

He did. He didn't ask why. He went back, and she felt him go back, and the memory came up again, the cold dry hand with the calluses at the base of the fingers, and the buzz. Like a funny bone. Like lightning. Every hair on his arm standing straight up.

*What was that?* Leah asked.

*I don't know.* Seth sounded happy about not knowing, which was so like him she wanted to bite him. *I thought it was, like, the imprint. Like a spark. You know?*

*Does the imprint usually give you a static shock?*

*I don't know! I've never done it before!*

*Jake didn't get one. Sam didn't get one. Quil didn't.*

*Maybe it's different for everybody.*

*Maybe.* She didn't think so. *Go further back. Before you talked to her. When you first heard her.*

He went back. The rain jackets. The woman with her back to him, holding up the yellow one. *Mierda.* The red dust on her boots, and the smell, Leah could smell it through Seth's nose, rain and paper and sage soap and something under it. A metal smell. That sharp taste you got on First Beach before a storm came in off the water.

*That,* Leah thought. *What's that smell?*

*I don't know. It's just her.*

*People don't smell like a lightning storm, Seth.*

*She does.*

*Go forward. When she's at the window. At the end.*

He went forward, and there it was. The woman at the window with the rain pants held up to the light. Talking to herself under her breath. Spanish, English. And then two short sharp words that weren't either of those, and the air near the window—

Leah watched it through her brother's eyes. The rain on the glass behind the woman's head. The way it slowed down. The raindrops sliding down the window, slowing, all of them at once, like somebody had pressed a button, for about half a second. And then going on again.

The hair stood up along Leah's spine.

*Did you see that?*

*See what?*

*The rain, Seth. The rain on the window.*

She felt him look. She felt him really look, for the first time, at his own memory, at something other than the woman's face. She felt it land.

*Huh,* Seth thought slowly. *That's weird.*

*Yeah. That's weird.*

*Maybe it's just—*

*Rain doesn't stop, Seth.*

He was quiet. In the back of his head, behind the quiet, she could feel the line. The imprint line. It went out of Seth's chest and away north and east, to Forks, to a house on the edge of town, and it was humming like a plucked string. He couldn't stop feeling it. She was getting used to feeling him feel it.

*I don't care,* he thought finally. *Whatever it is. I don't care.*

*Yeah,* Leah thought. *I know you don't.*

That was the thing about it. That was the thing that made her want to tear trees out of the ground. They never cared. Sam hadn't cared that Emily was her cousin, that Emily had been her best friend since they were in diapers, that Emily was going to be her bridesmaid. Jacob hadn't cared that the baby was half bloodsucker and had nearly torn its own mother apart on the way out. Once it happened, there was only her. Whoever she was. Whatever she was.

And now Seth was standing in the dark at the bottom of the reservation with his whole self hanging off a line into a stranger who made the rain stop. And he didn't care.

*Seth.*

*Yeah?*

*Be careful.*

*Of her?* He was honestly surprised. She could feel how surprised. The idea that he'd need to be careful of the woman on the other end of the line had not occurred to him at all, and wouldn't have, not in a hundred years, because it couldn't. That was what imprinting did. *Lee, she's—*

*I know what she is to you. I'm saying I don't know what she is. Neither do you. So be careful.*

He was quiet for a while. They ran. He was coming up the southern loop toward the treaty line now, fast, his feet loud in the brush, and she was running the ridge above the Cullen place, and the two of them were closing toward each other in the dark like two hands.

Then, way at the back of everything, a warm dim hum got louder, and louder, and a third voice came on like somebody turning on a light.

*What's going on?* Jacob asked, groggy, half asleep. *Why is Seth so—*

He saw.

He saw all of it in about a second, the way you did, the whole day dumped into his head at once, the store and the woman and the line, and Leah felt his whole self go *oh* and then light up, the way only Jacob could light up, like a bonfire with gas thrown on it.

*SETH,* Jacob thought. *Dude!*

*I know!*

*DUDE.*

*I KNOW.*

*When? Today? At* Newton's*?*

*She needed a jacket!*

*You imprinted in Newton's! On a rain jacket lady!*

*Rain jacket lady? She's from New Mexico! She was in the Yucatán! Her name's Layla! She has bees!*

Leah ran the ridge with the two of them going off in her head like a pair of firecrackers and let them. She didn't try to think anything. There wasn't anything to think. Jacob was happy. Jacob was so happy for him it came off him in waves, it filled up the whole pack mind until there wasn't room for anything else, and in all that happy there was, way down at the bottom, very small, a little hook of worry, and it was for her. For Leah. Jacob was thinking about her, too, under all of it. Wondering if she was okay.

She didn't let him see she'd noticed.

*She's old, though,* Jacob was saying. *Right? She's like, what, twenty-five?*

*Twenty-two,* Seth said. *Maybe. I don't know. I didn't ask.*

*Dude. You're eighteen.*

*Since March!*

*She's going to think you're a baby.*

*I'm six-four!*

*She's going to think you're a really big baby.*

*Jake—*

*I'm kidding! I'm kidding. Seth. Seriously. Congratulations, man. That's awesome. That's so awesome.*

*Thanks.*

*Your mom is going to lose her mind.*

*Oh, no,* Seth thought, in a totally different voice. *Oh, no. Mom.*

And Leah, for the first time in what felt like months, laughed. A real one. It came out of her wolf as a snort and a bark and nearly made her trip over a root, and she felt both of them hear it in her head, and go quiet for a second, surprised.

*You have to tell her,* she thought. *You have to tell her in person. And I want to be there.*

*Lee.*

*I want to be there, Seth. I want to see her face.*

*You're the worst.*

*Yeah.* She came over the top of the ridge and down into the cedars and there he was, coming up the trail toward her, sandy and long-legged, all legs and ears, like he'd been put together by somebody who'd heard about wolves but never seen one. His tail was going. He couldn't make it stop. *I'm the worst.*

He came up and stopped in front of her and dropped his head and shoved the top of it into her shoulder, hard enough that she staggered, the way he'd been doing since they were little, since before the wolves, when he was six and she was nine and he used to head-butt her in the stomach every time she came home from school.

She let him.

*Find out about the rain,* she thought.

*Lee—*

*Find out about the rain, Seth. Find out what she is. For you. Okay?*

He lifted his head. He looked at her. He had their dad's eyes, even in this shape. Dark and steady and too kind for his own good.

*Okay,* he thought.

They ran the rest of the line together. Jacob dropped out after twenty minutes, phased back, went back to the couch and the kid. At four in the morning Leah peeled off to the north to run the long loop by herself, the way she did, and Seth didn't follow her, because he knew better.

She ran until the sky started going gray over the mountains. She ran until her legs burned and her lungs burned and her head was empty, finally, of everything except the ground. And when she stopped, on the rocks above the Sol Duc with the mist coming up off the river below her, she stood there and let herself feel it, for one minute, the way she let herself feel it about once a month, alone, where nobody could see.

The wanting.

Every one of them, now. Sam, Jared, Paul, Quil, Jacob. Now Seth. Every one of them had found the one thing that held them to the ground. And she stood on a rock at dawn with nobody holding her anywhere, a she-wolf nobody had ever heard of in a thousand years of stories, who hadn't bled since the night she'd phased, who Sue Clearwater had stopped asking about grandchildren around the Fourth of July, who couldn't even say for sure what she was for.

She let herself feel it for one minute.

Then she went home, and phased back in the trees behind the house, and pulled on her cutoffs and her tank top, and went in the back door, and found Seth asleep at the kitchen table with his head on his arms and their mom standing over him in her bathrobe with a cup of coffee, looking down at him with an expression Leah couldn't read.

"He told you," Leah said.

"He told me," Sue said.

"How'd you take it?"

Her mother took a long sip of coffee. Her eyes didn't leave Seth's sleeping face.

"I made him pancakes," she said. "And then I called Billy."

"And?"

"Billy says the Quinn girl," Sue said, "is Maeve Quinn's granddaughter."

"Yeah. So?"

Sue set her coffee down on the counter, very carefully, the way she set things down when she was trying not to say something. She looked at Leah over Seth's head.

"Maeve Quinn," her mother said, "kept bees on the Calawah for forty years. Your father used to say the honey was the only thing in Forks worth driving for. He also used to say that in forty years, nobody ever once saw a single one of those bees fly into the woods on the wrong side of the treaty line." She picked up her coffee again. "Nobody. Not once. In forty years. Your father used to say it like a joke."

Leah stared at her.

"He told me about the rain, too," Sue said, and drank her coffee, and that was all she'd say about it.

### IV. Seth

*Calawah Way — Saturday, October 7th*

The dresser was oak, about a hundred years old, and Layla said it weighed as much as a Buick.

"It's fine," Seth said.

"It is not fine. It took three men to get it up those stairs in 1971. My grandmother told me. She made them lunch after. There's a picture."

"I've got it."

"Seth. There's a guy in Beaver with a dolly. I called him. He's coming Tuesday."

Seth looked at the dresser. It was in the upstairs hallway, where it had been for thirty-five years, a big dark slab of a thing with brass handles and a mirror on top in a frame shaped like a church window. Layla wanted it downstairs, in the front room, where she said the light was better and where she was going to put all the jars. He didn't know what jars. He hadn't asked. He'd been there for two hours and in two hours he had carried four boxes of old *National Geographic*s out to her truck, fixed the gutter over the back door where it had pulled loose, and eaten six of her grandmother's honey cookies out of a tin that Layla said were probably from last Christmas and might kill him. They'd been great.

He took the mirror off the top first. It came away on two pegs. He leaned it against the wall, careful, and then he squatted down and got his arms around the bottom of the dresser and stood up.

It was heavy. He'd give it that. Motorcycle heavy, maybe.

He walked it down the stairs.

He went slow, because the stairs were narrow and turned at the bottom and he didn't want to take out the banister, and he didn't want to drop it, because it was her grandmother's and he could tell from the way she'd touched it, when she was taking the drawers out, that it mattered. It was easy, mostly. It was just big. At the bottom he turned it sideways to get it through the arch into the front room and set it down against the wall where she'd pointed, gentle, so the feet didn't scrape the floor.

Then he straightened up and turned around.

Layla was standing at the top of the stairs. She hadn't moved. She was holding one of the drawers in both arms, full of old sweaters, and she was looking down at him with that line between her eyebrows that he was starting to think about a lot.

"That's where you wanted it, right?" Seth said.

"You carried that down the stairs."

"Yeah."

"By yourself."

"I lift," Seth said.

"You lift." She came down the stairs, one at a time, with the drawer. She put it on the floor beside the dresser. She looked at the dresser and then at him and then at the dresser. "You carried a three-man oak dresser down a flight of stairs by yourself, and you're not breathing hard."

He was, a little. He made himself breathe harder. That seemed to make it worse; her eyes went narrower.

"Swim team," Layla said.

"Informal team," said Seth.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed. It came out of her like it surprised her, a short sharp sound, and she put her hand over her mouth like she was trying to put it back.

"Okay," she said. "Okay, Hercules. Do you want a sandwich?"

He wanted a sandwich more than he'd ever wanted anything in his life, except possibly her. "Yeah."

They ate on the back step, because it wasn't raining, for once, just gray and damp and still, with the mist sitting in the tops of the trees across the yard. She'd made him three sandwiches and herself one, without asking, just put three on his plate like it was normal. He'd noticed that. He'd noticed everything. He'd noticed that she kept the door to the second bedroom upstairs shut, every time, and that when he'd gone near it with a box she'd said *not that one* in a voice that was a little too quick. He'd noticed that there was a smell in that part of the house, the lightning smell, much stronger, like a storm had been sitting in there for days. He'd noticed that she kept her right sleeve buttoned at the wrist, always, even when she was hot from carrying boxes and had pushed the left one up to her elbow.

He'd noticed she didn't have a TV.

He didn't ask about any of it. He'd promised Leah he'd find out about the rain, and he was going to, but every time he got close to it he looked at Layla's face and thought, *not yet*. She looked tired. She had a way of going still sometimes in the middle of a sentence, like she'd walked into a wall in the dark, and then picking the sentence back up a beat later like it hadn't happened. He'd seen it four times today. He'd seen Leah do the same thing for two years.

*Not yet.* That was okay. He could wait. The imprint wanted her to have things. That was all it wanted. Jacob had tried to explain that part too, once, and Seth hadn't gotten it. He got it now.

"Can I ask you something?" Layla said.

"Sure."

"The bees." She nodded down the yard at the hives along the back fence. "When you walked in this morning. What did you do to them?"

Seth looked at the hives. "Nothing?"

"They came out."

"Yeah, I saw that. Is that bad?"

"It's forty-eight degrees, Seth. Bees don't come out under fifty-five. They cluster. They don't fly." She was watching him over her sandwich. "You walked through the gate and three of my grandmother's hives opened up and about a hundred bees flew out and landed on you."

He remembered. He'd been a little freaked out, honestly. He'd stood very still in the middle of the yard with bees on his arms and his shoulders and one on his ear, crawling around, and he'd thought, *okay, don't panic, they can't sting through, can they? Can they?* And none of them had stung him. They'd just walked around on him for a minute, their little feet tickling, and then gone back in the boxes.

"I figured they liked me," Seth said. "I run hot. Maybe they were cold."

Layla didn't say anything. She ate her sandwich and looked at the hives.

"My grandmother," she said after a while, "used to say they could tell. The bees. Who was a friend."

"Yeah?"

"She used to say a lot of things." Layla picked a crust off her sandwich and threw it into the grass. A crow came down out of a fir tree and took it, and looked at Seth with one eye, and left. "She told me once, when I was a kid, that bees had been in my family longer than the family name had. That we'd always kept them. Every generation. Back to Ireland, back to before Ireland. She said bees were good at keeping secrets, because nobody ever thinks to ask them."

Seth thought about that.

"That's kind of a weird thing to tell a kid," he said.

"She was kind of a weird lady."

"My grandpa used to tell us we were descended from wolves," Seth said.

He hadn't meant to say it. It just came out, because she'd said something true and it felt like he owed her one back. He felt his ears go hot.

Layla turned her head and looked at him. "Did he."

"It's a tribe thing. Like a legend. The Quileute, we're—there's this story that the first one of us was a wolf, and a guy, the Great Spirit, or whoever, turned him into a man." He shrugged. "My dad used to tell it. Billy Black tells it. Every year at the bonfire. It's kind of a whole production."

"Do you believe it?"

"It's a story," Seth said. Which was true. It was a story. It just also happened to be true. He looked at her and tried to figure out how much she could see on his face, and he had the uncomfortable feeling, again, the one he'd had in the store, that she could see a lot. "Do you believe in the bees?"

Layla looked back at him for a long time.

"Yes," she said.

The mist sat in the trees. The crow came back, looking for another crust, and Layla threw it one.

Neither of them said anything else about it. They finished their sandwiches. Then he helped her carry the rest of the drawers down, and put the mirror back on top of the dresser, and she stood back and looked at it in the front room, in the gray light from the window, and her eyes got a little bright for a second, and he looked at the floor so she wouldn't have to know he'd seen.

When he left, at five, she walked him out to the road.

"Thank you," she said. "Really. Seth. You didn't have to."

"I wanted to."

"I know." She said it oddly. Like she was saying something else. "I know you did."

He walked down the road toward the highway, where he'd left his mom's car, and the line in his chest stretched behind him, back up the road to the blue house, and hummed. He didn't look back. He'd decided not to look back. He'd made it about fifty yards when he heard the bees again behind him, a low warm hum coming up out of the yard, out of all six hives at once, following him down the road like a song.

He looked back.

She was still standing at the gate, with her arms folded. She was watching him go. She lifted one hand.

He lifted one back.

*Forks Diner — Saturday, October 21st*

"Not this pie."

He put his menu down and looked at her across the table like she'd said something about his mother. "Okay. That's—I'm going to let that go, because you're new. But that's wrong. That's just wrong. It was raining again. *To patrol the woods around a house full of vampires to make sure none of the other vampires that come through eat anybody, and also to make sure the guy I used to follow around doesn't change his mind about killing the baby my best friend would die for, who's six weeks old and already talking.*

*First Beach, La Push — Sunday, October 29th*

The tide was out, and the pools in the rocks at the south end of the beach were full of anemones, green and purple, closed up tight against the cold. Layla crouched over one with her hands in her jacket pockets and her hair coming loose in the wind. Seth crouched next to her. He wasn't looking at the anemones.

"Tell me the story," she said.

"Which one?"

"The wolf one. The one Billy Black tells at the bonfire. You said there was more."

"There's a lot more." He picked up a stone and turned it over in his fingers. It was black and smooth and wet. "It's kind of long."

"I've got time."

So he told her.

He told it the way Billy told it, mostly, because that was how he'd heard it every year since he was a baby, sitting on a log at the bonfire with his dad's hand on the back of his neck. About Kaheleha, the great chief, who sent his spirit out of his body to fight. About Taha Aki, who couldn't get back into his body when Utlapa stole it, and how his spirit found a wolf, and asked to share it, and the wolf said yes. About how Taha Aki came back to the tribe in the shape of a wolf, and became a man again in front of them, and they knew him.

About the Cold Ones.

He told her about the one who came first, the cold man who killed the people of the village, whose skin was hard as stone and who drank their blood. How the spirit warriors, Taha Aki's sons, fought him in the shape of wolves. How the cold man's mate came after, in revenge, beautiful and terrible, and killed the wolves, one by one, and how Taha Aki's third wife, who was no warrior and had no magic in her, took a knife and stabbed herself in the heart so the cold woman would turn to the smell of blood, and in that second, the last wolves tore her apart.

He told her how, after that, whenever the Cold Ones came near, the young men of the tribe would start to run hot, and grow, and change. How they were still guarding the beaches. How they always would.

When he was done, Layla didn't say anything for a long time.

The waves came in and went out. A gull walked along the edge of the water looking for something. Up the beach, the big black rocks of the sea stacks stood in the surf with trees growing on top of them, bent over sideways by a hundred years of wind.

"The third wife," Layla said finally.

"Yeah."

"She had no magic in her." Layla was looking at the anemone. "That's how Billy tells it? Those words?"

"That's how my dad told it."

"So the others did." Her voice was quiet. "The chief. The spirit warriors. They had it. That's how the story goes. It's the only reason her part's worth telling. Everybody else in the story has magic, and she doesn't, and she does the thing that saves them anyway."

Seth turned the stone over in his hand. He didn't say anything. His heart was going very hard.

"My grandmother had a story like that," Layla said. "Sort of. About her grandmother, in Clare, in the famine. She said the soldiers came to the house and there was nothing to eat and her grandmother went out into the field and stood there and the soldiers couldn't see her. They walked right past her. Fifteen of them. And her grandmother stood in the field with the bees all around her, the whole swarm, like a cloud. And the soldiers walked right past." She pulled her hands out of her pockets and looked at them. "My grandmother told me that when I was six. And I said, *That's not true*. And she said, *Which part?*"

"What'd you say?"

"I said the part where they couldn't see her." Layla smiled, crooked and unhappy. "And she said, *Oh, that's the true part. The part that isn't true is that there were only fifteen.*"

Seth laughed. He couldn't help it. She looked over at him, and her face changed, and she laughed too, the short surprised one.

Then they stopped laughing, and they were just crouched there by the tide pool looking at each other, very close, close enough that he could see a little fleck of gold in the iris of her left eye, close enough that he could hear her heart going just as hard as his.

"Seth," she said.

"Yeah."

"There's something I need to tell you. At some point."

"Yeah," Seth said. "Me too."

She looked at him. She didn't look surprised. She'd known. He'd known she'd known. Of course she had. They'd been sitting next to it for four weeks like two people sitting on either side of a fire with their hands out, not saying anything about the fire.

"Is it bad?" she asked. "Your thing?"

He thought about it. He thought about the wolf, and the woods, and the treaty, and Jacob and the baby and the Cullens and the vampires that came through from Canada and the one he and Leah had taken apart at the Hoh River in September with his teeth. He thought about phasing in front of her. About her face. The way people's faces went, even people who'd heard the stories all their lives. Charlie's face. Emily's face, the night Sam had lost his temper, the scars.

"It's kind of a lot," he said. "Is yours bad?"

"It's kind of a lot," Layla said.

The wind came up off the water and pulled her hair loose and blew it across her face, and she didn't move to fix it.

"Next week," she said. "Okay? Next Saturday. You come up to the house. And we'll—both of us. Okay?"

"Okay."

"Promise me."

"I promise."

She held out her hand, palm up, between them, over the tide pool.

He put his hand in it.

The buzz came again. The lightning feeling, up out of her palm, into his. He was ready for it this time. He felt it go up his arm and into his chest and find the line there, the imprint line, the thing that ran from him into her, and he felt it *touch* it. The spark and the line. Like two wires crossing. For a second the whole line lit up, from his chest to hers, gold and red and humming, and he saw her eyes go wide, and he knew she'd felt it too. Whatever it was. She'd felt it from the other end.

"Oh," Layla said softly.

The anemone in the tide pool between them, the green one, closed up tight against the cold, opened.

All at once, all its little arms, the way they opened when the tide came in. And next to it, the purple one opened. And the one next to that. All down the line of the rocks, every anemone in the pool, opening up like flowers in the gray light, like it was summer, like the tide had come in.

They both looked down at it.

Then they both looked up at each other.

"Next week," Seth said. His voice wasn't steady.

"Next week," said Layla.

She didn't let go of his hand for a long time.

### V. Layla

*Calawah Way — Saturday, November 4th*

She set the table twice.

The first time she did it the way her mother would have, cloth and candles and the good plates from the high cupboard, the blue-and-white ones Maeve had brought over from Ireland in a crate of straw in 1965. She stood back and looked at it and thought it looked like a proposal. She took it all off again. The second time she did it the way her grandmother would have: the brown plates, the jar of honey in the middle with the dipper stuck in it, two mugs, a loaf of soda bread on the board with the knife beside it. That looked like a kitchen. She left it.

Then she took her wand out of her sleeve and put it on the table, next to the bread, and looked at it there.

She hadn't put her wand down in front of another person since Mexico. She hadn't put it down anywhere she couldn't reach it in under a second in fourteen months. She slept with it under her pillow. She showered with it on the sink. When she'd first come up here she'd driven twelve hundred miles with it in her sleeve, and every time she'd stopped for gas she'd kept her right hand loose at her side.

It lay on the scrubbed pine table, thirteen inches of pale mesquite with the grain running through it like smoke, and it looked very small and very ordinary. Like a chopstick. Like nothing.

Her hand wanted to pick it up so badly it ached.

She left it there.

It was ten past six. Full dark. It had been dark since five; it got dark early up here, and the rain had come back in the afternoon, a light steady drizzle that hung in the porch light like smoke. She'd told him seven. She'd been ready since four.

The bees started at six-fifteen.

She heard them through the back wall. All six hives, at once, the way they did every time he came, a low warm rising hum. Except he wasn't on the road. She went to the front window and looked, and the road was empty, black and wet under the one streetlight. She went to the back window.

The yard was dark. The hives were white shapes along the fence. Beyond them, the forest, solid black.

He was coming out of the forest.

She saw him step out from between two big hemlocks at the edge of the yard, behind the third hive, where there was no trail, where there was nothing at all but four miles of second-growth timber and then the river and then more timber all the way to the Pacific. He was barefoot. He was wearing a pair of cutoff khaki shorts and nothing else, in November, in the rain, and he was steaming. She could see it from the window. The rain was landing on his shoulders and coming off him as vapor, a thin white haze all around him in the dark, like he was standing in front of a fire.

He stopped in the middle of the yard and looked up at the house. At the window. At her.

Layla opened the back door and went out on the step.

"You're early," she said.

"Sorry." His voice was rough. "I couldn't wait."

"You came through the woods."

"Yeah."

"From La Push."

"Yeah."

"That's fourteen miles, Seth."

"I know." He was standing very still. His hands were in fists at his sides and she could see them shaking, a fine fast tremor that went up his arms into his shoulders. He was breathing hard. "I wanted to do mine first. Before I lost my nerve. Is that okay? Can I go first?"

The bees were very loud. She could feel the ward, all the way around the property, every thread of it lit and singing.

"Okay," Layla said. "Go first."

He nodded. He looked down at the grass. He looked back up at her.

"Don't be scared," he said. "Okay? Whatever happens. I would never—I'd never hurt you. Never. I couldn't. It's like the one thing in the world I actually can't do. So don't be scared."

"I'm not scared," she said, which was a lie, and they both knew it.

He backed up. Three steps, four, out into the middle of the yard, away from her, away from the hives. He stopped. He closed his eyes.

And Seth Clearwater came apart.

That was what it looked like. For the first half second it looked like something had hit him, from inside, like a blast. His back bowed. His arms came up. And then there was a sound like a sheet being torn down the middle, and the air where he'd been standing *bloomed*, and there was a shape in it, enormous, coming down onto four feet in the wet grass with a thud she felt through the step, and the cutoffs were gone, there were rags of khaki falling through the air like confetti, and her grandmother's yard was full of wolf.

Layla didn't move.

It was huge. That was the first thing. It was the size of a horse, a big horse, a draft horse, its shoulders higher than her head. Long-legged and rangy and loose, all legs and ears, with a sandy coat that went gold at the tips in the porch light and darker down the spine. Its head was as long as her arm. Its eyes were brown, and they were on her, and they were Seth's.

She'd known. She had known for five weeks. She'd known since she'd sat on the floor of her grandmother's room with the journal in her lap and read *same as Ephraim's lot*, and she'd known since the beach, since the story, since *she had no magic in her*. She'd known in the way you know a door is cursed before you touch it, all the way down in the back of your neck. It didn't help. Knowing a thing and seeing it standing in your yard in the rain breathing at you were two completely different animals.

The wolf lowered its head. Slowly. All the way down, until its chin was almost on the grass, and its ears went back, and it looked up at her from under its eyebrows. She'd seen dogs do that. She'd seen dogs do that when they'd knocked over the trash and they knew it.

*Don't be scared.*

Then the bees came out.

All of them. All six hives at once, like somebody had thrown open six doors. She heard them before she saw them, the hum going up and up into a roar, and then they were pouring out across the dark yard, thousands of them, a river of bees in the rain, in November, in the cold. They went to him. They went to the wolf in the middle of the yard and settled on him, on his back and his shoulders and his head, his ears, crawling in the sandy fur until he was covered in them, until the whole wolf was furred in bees and humming, gold on gold, and he stood there very still, the way he'd stood the first time, with his eyes going wide and white at the edges and his tail clamped down between his legs.

Layla laughed.

She didn't mean to. It came up out of her chest and out of her mouth before she knew it was coming, the way things had been doing for five weeks around him. It was so absurd. It was so completely, perfectly absurd. A wolf the size of a Clydesdale standing in her grandmother's backyard in the rain, terrified of bees.

"They like you," she said. "Seth. It's okay. They like you. They've liked you since the first day."

The wolf rolled one eye at her.

She came down off the step.

She walked across the wet grass toward him. She didn't hurry. She kept her hands where he could see them, the way you did with a big animal, open, at her sides. Rain was soaking through her shirt. Her bare feet were freezing. The bees parted for her as she came, a little path opening in the air, and closed behind her.

She stopped in front of him. He was so big she had to tip her head back to look at his face. The heat came off him like an oven door.

"Can I?" she said.

He lowered his head a little more. She put her hand on his muzzle.

The fur was coarse on top and soft underneath, and hot, and wet from the rain, and under it she could feel the bones of his face, and the breath going in and out of him, fast and shaky. The buzz came up out of her palm the way it always did. She let it. She didn't hold it back this time. She let her magic go out of her hand into him and say whatever it wanted to say, and she felt something come back. Huge and old and warm and not human at all, something that had been running in this forest before there were names for it. And under that, all around it, holding it, Seth.

"Hi," Layla said.

The wolf made a sound in its chest. Low. A rumble that went all the way through her hand into her arm.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. That's—yeah. That's a lot."

The rumble again. It sounded a little like a laugh.

She stood there with her hand on his face in the rain and the bees humming all around them and thought about it, for one second, very clearly, the way you thought about a door. She thought about the Statute. Clause Seventy-Three. Rappaport's Law, repealed in '65 but still in the bones of every witch and wizard in America, still in the way your parents taught you never, ever, under any circumstance. She thought about the MACUSA enforcement offices in the Woolworth Building, and what they did, and how fast. She thought about Tomás.

She thought about how every single thing she'd been taught since she was eleven years old came down to one word, and the word was *don't*.

Then she thought, *He went first.*

Layla took her hand off his muzzle and stepped back.

"Okay," she said. "My turn."

The wolf's ears came up.

She held out her right hand, palm up, and called her wand.

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to; she'd been able to do this since she was fourteen, and she'd never once done it in front of anyone who didn't already know. Through the back door of the house, across the kitchen, off the table, past the honey jar and the bread. It came out the open door like an arrow and across the yard in the dark and smacked into her palm, and her fingers closed on it, and the whole world got sharper.

The wolf flinched back a full step. Bees went up off him in a cloud.

"It's okay," Layla said. "It's okay. Watch."

She looked up at the rain.

She'd thought about what she'd do. She'd thought about it for a week. She'd thought about doing something simple: a light, a feather floating, a cup of tea pouring itself. She'd thought about doing something big. Fire. A Patronus, maybe, her fox. In the end she'd decided she'd know when she got there.

She was there.

She lifted the wand and turned it in a slow circle over her head, and said the old words her grandmother had taught her on the back step when she was nine, the words for holding still. And the rain stopped where it was.

Every drop of it in the whole yard, from the back step to the hives to the edge of the forest, every single raindrop hanging in the air around them. Millions of them. Hanging. Each one catching the porch light. The whole yard turned to glitter, a curtain of bright suspended beads strung through the dark from the ground to the sky, and the two of them standing in the middle of it, in a little pocket of dry air, with the bees going slowly round and round them like they were the center of something.

It was very quiet. The rain made no noise at all.

The wolf stood absolutely still in the middle of it. Its eyes were enormous. It turned its head, slowly, and looked at the drops hanging an inch from its nose, and she saw it go cross-eyed looking, and she bit her lip so she wouldn't laugh again.

"I'm a witch," Layla said. "That's my thing. That's what I had to tell you. My grandmother was one. My mom is. Her mom was. Back to Clare and before. There's a lot of us. We have a whole—there's a government. There's schools. There's a law that says I'm not supposed to tell you any of this, ever, not if you're my husband, not if you're dying. They'd take me away for it. They'd take your memory away." She swallowed. "So. That's mine. That's my lot."

The wolf looked at her through the hanging rain.

And then, somewhere off to the south, very far away, something howled.

It was faint. Miles away. Down by the coast. A long, rising, ragged howl that went up and up and broke at the top, and Layla felt the hair on her arms stand up, because she'd never heard a wolf howl like that. It sounded like someone shouting a name.

The wolf in front of her jerked its head around toward the sound, and its ears went flat.

"Is that—?" Layla said.

The wolf looked back at her. It looked embarrassed. It looked, she thought, exactly like a teenager whose mother had just walked in.

Then it turned, very carefully, not to brush against any of the hanging drops, and walked out of the pocket of dry air, through the curtain of rain, which burst against its shoulders and ran off it in streams, and went behind the third hive. And there was the tearing sound again, quieter, and a pause, and some rustling, and a muttered word that sounded a lot like *crap*, and then Seth came back out from behind the hive in a pair of khaki shorts that were not the pair he'd come in wearing, buttoning them, with his face bright red.

"Spare pair," he said. "I tied them to my leg. Leah makes me."

"Was that Leah?" Layla asked. "Howling?"

"Yeah." He came back through the hanging rain. It burst on him, and steamed. He stopped in front of her, in the dry pocket, with drops beading on his bare shoulders and evaporating. "She was phased. On patrol. When I—when you did that. We can hear each other. When we're wolves. In our heads. All of us in the pack. Everything I see, she sees." He winced. "And Jacob. Jacob's phased now too. I can feel him. They both saw it. All of it."

Layla looked at him.

"So they know," she said. "About me."

"Yeah." He looked miserable. "I'm sorry. I didn't think. I didn't think you'd—I didn't know what you were going to do. I didn't know it'd be so—" He waved his hand at the rain. "Big."

"What did Leah say?"

Seth hesitated.

"Seth."

"She said," he said carefully, "*She stopped the fucking rain, Seth.* And then she said a lot of other stuff. And then she howled."

Layla laughed again. She couldn't help it. She laughed until she had to put her hand over her mouth, and the hanging rain all around them trembled, all those millions of drops shivering at once in the porch light, and she got it back under control before it fell.

"I'm glad," she said, when she could. "I'm glad she knows."

"You are?"

"I don't want to do this halfway. I've been doing everything halfway for a year." She looked up at him. "You said it was a lot. Your thing. Is there more?"

He took a breath.

"Yeah," he said. "There's more. There's a—when we see somebody. Some of us. Not all of us, not always. We see somebody, and it's like—it's called imprinting. And it's like gravity changes. Like everything that was holding you down gets cut and there's only one thing left. And it's them." He was looking at her face. He wasn't looking away. She'd never seen anyone so brave in her life, and she'd seen people walk into tombs. "That's what happened. In the store. When you turned around. That's what happened to me."

The rain hung. The bees went round.

"So you didn't choose this," Layla said quietly. "Me."

"It doesn't feel like that."

"How does it feel?"

"Like I'd have chosen it anyway," Seth said. "Like it just saved me some time."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she lifted her wand again.

"Hold still," she said.

"What are you—"

"I take things apart," Layla said. "For a living. Used to. Curses. Enchantments. Bonds. Somebody puts something on a thing and I find out how it works and what it's tied to, and then I take it off. I'm good at it. I was the best in the Southwest for three years." She turned the wand point toward the center of his bare chest. "If something's got hold of you, I want to see it. I want to see what it is. Okay? I'm not going to touch it. I just want to look."

He swallowed. He nodded.

She said the words.

It was the deepest revealing spell she knew, a working spell, the one she'd used on the black stone in the cenote. She'd sworn she'd never use it again. It went out of the wand tip like a breath of smoke and hit his chest and spread, and for a second nothing, and then—

There.

A line. A cord. It came out of the center of his chest, where his heart was, a little to the left, and it was thick as her wrist, and it was the red of coals, the same red as the print he'd left on her palm the first day. It glowed in the dark like something alive. And it went out of him, through the dry air between them, and into her.

Into her chest. Right into the center of her. She looked down and watched it go in.

She looked for the hook. That was what you looked for, with a bond, with a compulsion: a love charm, a binding, a geas. You looked for where it went into the other person and *pulled*. There was always a hook. Something that went in and grabbed and dragged the other one along whether they liked it or not.

She searched the whole length of it twice. It went into her and it stopped, the way a hand stops on your shoulder. It held. And around it, where it went in, something else was woven. Something gold. Gold threads, fine as hair, a whole web of them, wrapped around the red cord at her end and running up it into the dark. Her own magic. Her own color. Five weeks of it. Every time she'd touched his hand and let the spark go. On the beach. In the store. Tonight. She'd been weaving it without knowing. Wrapping herself around the line from her end, the way honeysuckle goes up a post.

It was hers. Half of it was hers. She'd done half of it all by herself.

"Oh," Layla said.

"What?" Seth said. "What do you see?"

She let the spell go. The cord faded. The gold faded. They were standing in a pocket of dry air in her grandmother's yard with the rain hanging all around them and the bees going round, and nothing between them at all but about a foot of air.

"Nothing bad," she said.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah."

She put her wand in her back pocket. Then she put her hand flat on the center of his bare chest, where the cord had come out, and felt his heart going under it like a drum, and felt it go faster.

"Seth," she said.

"Yeah?"

"I'm twenty-two."

"I know."

"You're eighteen."

"Since March."

"I'm a disgraced curse-breaker hiding out in my dead grandmother's house from a thing that happened in a hole in the ground in Mexico. I haven't slept a whole night in fourteen months. I'm not—" She stopped. She started again. "I'm kind of a lot."

"I turn into a wolf," Seth said. "And my best friend's girlfriend is a vampire, and he's sort of in love with her baby, who's also kind of a vampire, but in a nice way. And my sister hates everybody. And I go through like nine pairs of shorts a week." He put his hand over hers on his chest. It was so warm she felt it to her elbow. "I'm kind of a lot too."

"Your best friend's *what*?"

"Later," Seth said. "I'll tell you later. It's a whole thing."

She looked up at him.

He was so tall. He was so warm, and so completely open, standing there in her yard in a pair of spare shorts with his heart going under her hand and everything he was right there on his face, everything, no doors anywhere, nothing bricked up, nothing hidden behind a glyph she might read wrong.

"Okay," Layla said. "Later."

She went up on her toes. He came down.

When she kissed him, the rain fell.

All of it, all at once, every drop she'd been holding, millions of them, the spell going out of her like a breath because she'd forgotten it was there. It came down on both of them in a single soaking rush, cold as the sea, and she gasped against his mouth, and he laughed, and she felt him laugh, all the way through, and then he picked her up off the wet grass like she weighed nothing at all and kept kissing her, with the rain pouring down and steaming off his shoulders, and the bees roaring all around them like a crowd at a ballgame.

Way off to the south, down by the ocean, two wolves were howling now. Together. Long and high and rising.

The ragged edge had gone out of it.

It sounded, Layla thought, a lot like a song.

### VI. Edward

*The Cullen house — Sunday, November 5th*

He heard them at the bottom of the drive, three miles out, and the first thing he heard was Jacob thinking, *Okay, don't freak out, nobody freak out*, which in Edward's experience was the single most reliable predictor in Jacob Black's head that someone was about to freak out.

Edward was on the floor of the living room with his back against the couch and his daughter in his lap. Renesmee was eight weeks old and the size of a child of two, with bronze curls to her shoulders and her mother's eyes, and she was working her way, slowly and with immense concentration, through a picture book about a bear who couldn't sleep. She could read it perfectly well. She'd been reading since the third week. She liked to be read to anyway, and she liked to hold the book herself, and she liked to turn each page and then press her palm to Edward's cheek and show him what she thought the bear ought to have done instead, which was generally something involving Jacob.

*He should have asked Jake,* she showed him now. A picture: the bear in its cave, awake, miserable; and then Jacob, in his wolf shape, enormous and russet, curling up around the cave's mouth like a wall, and the bear asleep at once.

"Jake would have been very helpful," Edward agreed.

Across the room, Charlie Swan, sitting in the big armchair by the window with a cup of coffee he hadn't touched, made a noise in his throat, wordless. Charlie's mind, to Edward, had always been a blur, a muffled thing, like a radio heard through a wall. Bella's had been silent. Charlie's was only hard to tune. But Edward didn't need to tune it to know what the noise meant. It meant *I'm watching my eight-week-old granddaughter put pictures in her father's head with her hand, and I am not going to ask.*

Charlie came every Sunday now. He'd come every Sunday for five weeks. He sat in the same chair, and he held his coffee, and he watched Renesmee, and sometimes he held her, and sometimes she put her hand on his face and showed him things and he went very still and his eyes got wet and he didn't ask. He'd said it to Jacob on the front steps of his own house the day he'd found out, and he'd said it to Edward the next day, and he said it to himself, Edward was fairly sure, about every fifteen minutes. *Need to know.*

Bella was beside him on the couch, close enough that he could feel her stillness at his shoulder, newborn-still, coiled and perfect. Her eyes were hidden behind the brown contacts she wore for Charlie, and the contacts itched, and she hated them, and she'd worn them every Sunday without complaint.

"Jacob's coming," Edward said quietly. "With Seth. And Leah, but she's staying in the trees." He listened. "And someone else."

Bella turned her head. "Who?"

Edward listened harder.

The fourth mind was coming up the drive in a vehicle, an old truck, its engine laboring on the slope; Jacob and Seth were in the cab with it, and Leah was a gray shape and a bitter hum running parallel to the drive, through the trees, keeping pace. The fourth mind was a woman's. That was the first thing.

The second thing was that it was the loudest mind he had ever heard in his life.

The volume was ordinary. The density was the thing. A human mind, to Edward, sounded like a voice talking, a stream, fast or slow, clear or muddy, but one stream, going one direction. Vampires were faster and had more channels, but they still ran like water. This mind ran like water too, on top. Words, plain ones, anxious ones. *Okay. Okay. A house full of them. Gran said leave them be, Gran said, and you're walking right up to the front door with your boyfriend the wolf. Good job, Layla. Outstanding.* But under the words there was something else, a second layer, a hum. A structure. Like walking into a cathedral and hearing the voices of the people talking and then, under them, the organ, playing one low note so deep you felt it in your feet. And hanging off that structure, in neat rows, like keys on hooks, like tools on a pegboard, there were—

Words. Other words. Old ones, Latin and something older than Latin, with shapes attached to them. With *motions* attached to them. A flick, a turn, a stab. Hundreds of them. They were simply there, ready, the way a pianist's scales are there in the fingers, and every one of them had a feeling attached to it that Edward had no word for.

He'd been alive for a hundred and five years. He'd heard the minds of saints and murderers and of the three oldest creatures on the earth, in Volterra, and none of them had sounded like this.

"Edward?" Bella said. "What is it?"

*She's going to put her hand in her pocket,* he heard, from the woman in the truck. *Not her pocket. Her sleeve. She keeps it in her sleeve. If anything goes wrong, if anything moves too fast, the big one first, the big one's the strongest, Incendio, no—Confringo, no, fire, they're stone, they're supposed to be stone, it'll be fire—*

"She's planning," Edward said slowly, "which of us to set on fire first."

Every head in the room turned.

Emmett, who had been lying on the floor under the piano with a handheld game, said, "Me. I bet it's me."

"It's you," Edward said.

Emmett looked delighted.

"Who," said Rosalie from the stairs, very flatly, "is coming up our drive planning to set my husband on fire."

Edward listened a moment longer.

*Witch.* It was in Jacob's head, and Seth's, and Leah's, all three of them, as clear as a bell, the image of a dark-haired woman with her arm raised in the rain and the rain hanging motionless all around her, millions of drops, glittering. Seth's memory. Seth couldn't stop replaying it. Over and over, with his heart going like a bird.

"Seth's girlfriend," Edward said. "She's a witch."

There was a short silence.

"Like," said Emmett, sitting up and hitting his head on the piano, "a *witch* witch?"

Edward didn't answer. Up the drive, the truck had come around the last bend, and in Seth's head, in Jacob's, in the woman's, there was the house: the long white face of it, the glass, every light on. And in the woman's mind, under the plain anxious words, Edward heard the deep organ-note of the structure shift. Tighten. Like something enormous drawing a breath.

He heard her think, very clearly, *Okay. Here we go.*

---

They came in through the front door, Jacob first, with his hands up a little at his sides as if he was walking into a room full of startled horses. Then Seth, who was so plainly, so radiantly happy that it came off him like heat off asphalt. Then the woman.

Edward looked at her. He was aware of every other person in the room looking at her too.

She was small next to Seth, though she wasn't a small woman; five-seven, perhaps, lean, the build of someone who'd done a lot of climbing. Dark hair braided back. A brown face, a hard nose, a white scar through the left eyebrow. A green rain jacket, zipped to the chin. Boots. She stopped two steps inside the door, the way people stopped when they came into a house where they couldn't see the exits, and her eyes went around the room.

He followed her eyes through her head. It was disorienting; she saw things the way a soldier saw them, or a surveyor. The glass wall at the back. The staircase. The distance to each person. Rosalie on the stairs, *the blonde from the gas station, the one who looked at me*. Emmett under the piano, *the big one, the big one first*. Jasper by the kitchen door, and her eyes stuck on him and something in her went cold all the way down, a long scar's worth of cold, *that one's dangerous, that one's been in fights, look at his arms*. Alice beside him. Esme. Carlisle, coming down the stairs behind Rosalie, *the doctor, Dr. Cullen, very cold hands, lovely man*. Bella.

Renesmee.

The woman's eyes landed on the child in Edward's lap and stopped, and the whole of her mind went white.

He listened, astonished. It was recognition. As if she'd looked at Renesmee and seen something there that the rest of them couldn't, a color, a shape, and it was so far outside anything she'd ever seen that her mind simply stopped for half a second, the way an eye stops on a total eclipse.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, look at you. Look at what you are.*

Then she looked at Charlie in the armchair, and her face did something wry, and she thought, *And the police chief. Of course. Of course the police chief is here.*

"Hi," Layla Quinn said to the room. Her voice was steady. Her right hand was loose at her side, the fingers a little curled, and Edward could see, in her head, exactly how far her wand was from her palm: four inches up her sleeve, held by a leather strap. "Sorry to barge in. Seth said you'd want to meet me. Since I'm—" She paused. "Well. Seth said you'd heard."

"We've heard," said Carlisle. He'd stopped at the bottom of the stairs. His voice was very gentle; it was the voice he used in the ER with people who'd been in car accidents. "You're very welcome here, Miss Quinn. I knew your grandmother."

"I know," Layla said. "She wrote about you."

Carlisle's eyebrows rose.

"She said you had cold hands," said Layla. "And that you were lovely." A pause. "She also said to leave you be."

"That," Carlisle said, "sounds like Maeve."

Something in the room loosened, a quarter of an inch.

Then Emmett said, from under the piano, "Okay, but can you turn me into a bear."

Layla's head snapped around. Edward heard her think *the big one*, and heard the organ-note in her mind tighten, every one of those hanging Latin keys suddenly swinging, ready, and he said, quickly and clearly, "Fire would work."

Her eyes came back to him, wide.

"For what it's worth," Edward said. "Fire would work. On any of us. You were right. But you don't need to plan it against Emmett. Of everyone in this room, he's the least likely to hurt you. He wants to be a bear."

The woman stared at him.

"You read minds," she said.

"Yes."

"You're reading mine. Right now."

"Yes. I'm sorry. I can't help it. It's a little like being in a room where everyone is talking at once. I can't stop hearing."

Her jaw worked. He heard her think several things in very quick succession, some of them in Spanish, one of them about Seth and the beach that she very obviously would have preferred he hadn't heard, and he looked politely at the ceiling until it passed.

"Okay," Layla said, finally. "That's rude."

"I know."

"That's *incredibly* rude."

"I know. I've been told. Frequently." He shifted Renesmee on his knee. "If it helps, your mind is the most interesting thing I've heard in about a century."

Her eyebrows went up. Seth, behind her, made a small growl in his throat, a puppy's growl, and Edward found himself smiling.

"I'm married, Seth," he said. "And she isn't my type. She's planning to set my brother on fire."

"Not anymore," Layla said. She'd let go of something; he heard it. The keys on their hooks stopped swinging. "Probably not anymore."

From the armchair by the window, Charlie Swan stood up.

Everybody looked at him. He had his coffee in one hand. He set it down carefully on the side table, on a coaster, the way Esme liked. He looked at Layla, and at Seth, and at Jacob, and at Edward on the floor with his granddaughter. He looked at Bella last.

"I'm guessing," he said, "this is a need-to-know thing."

"Dad—" said Bella.

"No, it's fine. It's fine." Charlie put his hands up. "I'll just go get some air. On the porch. You all do your—whatever. And come get me when you're done."

"Charlie," said Layla.

He stopped.

She was looking at him with an odd expression, and Edward, listening, heard why. She was looking at the Chief of Police of Forks, Washington, a Muggle with a badge, standing in a house full of vampires, a man the Statute of Secrecy existed to keep in the dark, and she was thinking about the law. Clause Seventy-Three. She was thinking about men from an office in New York in long gray coats who came to towns where things had been seen, and what they did to the people who had seen them, and how sometimes those people never quite came back right. She was thinking about it very coldly and clearly, the way you think about a cliff edge.

And then she was thinking about her grandmother. On the back step, at nine o'clock, every night, for forty years, smoking a cigarette, alone, with her bees, keeping her secret.

*I'm not doing that,* Layla Quinn thought. *I'm not doing it halfway.*

"You can stay," she said to Charlie. "If you want. It's your choice. But it's going to be a lot. And I can't take it back."

Charlie looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at Renesmee, in Edward's lap, who was looking back at him with her bright brown eyes, his eyes, Bella's eyes, eyes he'd seen in the mirror every day of his life.

He sat back down in the armchair. He picked his coffee back up.

"Well," Charlie said. "I'm already here."

---

She showed them.

She didn't do it the way Edward would have expected. He'd half expected smoke and fire, something theatrical, the way magic looked in the films Emmett liked, and he heard Emmett half expecting it too. Instead she took her wand out of her sleeve, a pale stick of what Carlisle, leaning in, said under his breath was mesquite, and she held it out on her open palm for all of them to look at, the way you'd show a dog your hand.

"It's a focus," she said. "That's all. It's a stick with a piece of a magic animal inside. Mine's a Thunderbird feather. They're American birds. They're—you'd never see one. They live in Arizona. Up high. The wand helps me aim. Without it, magic comes out of you kind of like water out of a hose with your thumb over it. Everywhere. With it, it's a stream."

"Can I hold it?" said Emmett.

"No," said Layla.

Then she lit the room.

She didn't say anything. She flicked the wand at the ceiling and every one of the forty-odd lights in the long white room went out at once, and there was a half-second of dark, and in the dark, in the air above their heads, a light came on. Soft and blue-white, the size of a fist, hanging in the air without a fixture. Then another. Then another, dozens, hundreds, filling the air of the room from the floor to the high ceiling like a slow snow of stars, drifting, turning, each one shining.

In Edward's lap, Renesmee gasped and lifted both hands.

One of the lights came down to her. Slowly. It drifted down out of the air like a snowflake, turning, and stopped an inch from her palm, and hung there, and Renesmee looked at it, and very carefully, very gently, she put her other hand to Edward's cheek, and showed him.

The light. And the woman's face. And a question.

"She wants to know if it's hot," Edward said.

"It's not," said Layla. Her voice had gone soft. "You can touch it."

Renesmee touched it. It went up her arm like water, a ripple of blue light, all the way to her shoulder and over her curls, and she laughed, a sound Edward had never heard from her before. Edward felt Bella, beside him, go absolutely still.

And then Renesmee held out her hand to the witch.

Layla looked at it. She looked at Edward. He nodded.

She crouched down, there on the carpet, and put her hand in Renesmee's, and Edward felt his daughter reach. Felt her do the thing she did, the thing she'd done since she was born, pushing a picture out of her own head into another person's through her skin. He felt it go. He heard it land in Layla's mind.

He heard the organ-note in Layla's head ring like a struck bell.

What Renesmee showed her was the light. The blue light, going up her own arm. And then her mother's face, Bella's face, the first time Renesmee had seen it, the morning Bella had woken up changed. And Jacob, the wolf, curled around her crib. And Charlie in the armchair with his coffee. All of it bright and simple and enormous. *Mine,* Renesmee was saying. *These are mine. And this—* the light, the blue snow, the witch's face, *is this mine too?*

Layla's eyes filled. She blinked hard.

"Yeah," she said. Her voice cracked. "Yeah, baby. If you want."

The lights came back on one at a time, slowly, around the room, like a town at dusk, and the blue snow of stars faded into them as they came. When it was over the room looked exactly the same as before, and nobody said anything.

Then Charlie Swan, in his armchair, said, in a strangled voice, "Okay. Maybe I need to know that one."

---

It took most of the afternoon.

Carlisle asked questions. Edward had known he would; Carlisle had been asking questions for three hundred and sixty years and he'd never once, as far as Edward knew, run out. How many of them were there. Where did they live. Did they age, did they die, did they get sick. Was it inherited. Could it skip. Could a person be born with it whose parents had none. Could it be taught.

Layla answered what she could. Edward listened to her answer and listened to her think, and he noticed, after a while, that the two were the same. She'd decided to hide nothing, and she kept deciding it, every few minutes, a small hard deliberate choice he could hear her make over and over like a hammer on a nail. *Not halfway. Not halfway.*

There were millions of them, she said. All over the world. Every country had a government of its own, hidden inside the ordinary one, and a law, older than the United States, that said they had to stay hidden. The International Statute of Secrecy. 1689. She said it with the same faint weariness people used for the date of the Magna Carta.

"And they've managed it," Carlisle said. "For three hundred years."

"More or less."

"I was alive in 1689," Carlisle said. "I was in London. My father hunted witches. He burned people for it." He said it very quietly. Esme put her hand on his arm. "He was wrong about all of them. I always believed he was wrong about all of them. And in three hundred and sixty years I have never met one of you. Not one."

"That's the idea," said Layla. "That's the whole idea."

"And you know about us? Your people?"

She hesitated. Edward heard the hesitation. It went right through her, a long cold unsettled moment.

"We have vampires," she said slowly. "Our books do. They're real, or there's—something real. A guy in Britain wrote a book about living with them. They're pale. They drink blood. Sunlight hurts them. Garlic, maybe. They're people with a disease, mostly. Sad. Kind of pathetic. One of the British ones goes to parties." She looked around the room. At Emmett, at Jasper. At Bella, newborn, perfect, coiled on the couch. "You're nothing like what's in the books. Nothing. Our ones wouldn't last ten seconds in this room."

"So your people don't know," said Jasper. It was the first thing he'd said all afternoon. He said it from the kitchen doorway, where he'd stayed, with Alice beside him. "About us. About what we really are."

"I don't think so." Layla shook her head. "I've never heard of anything like you. And I've been in a lot of tombs."

Edward thought about Volterra.

He thought about Aro, who read every thought a person had ever had with one touch of his hand, and who in three thousand years had never once, so far as Edward knew, encountered a mind like this one. He thought about what Aro would do if he ever did. What Aro would give for a woman who could stop the rain. He thought about the tower in the plaza, the noon bells, Bella running across the cobblestones to him through the red-cloaked crowd, and how close, how unthinkably close, he'd come to standing out there in the sun.

He thought: if Aro ever touches any of us, he'll see this afternoon.

He put the thought away. It didn't go far.

"If anyone in your government found out," Rosalie said, from the stairs. It was her first sentence too, and it was sharp. "About this. That you'd told us. What would they do?"

"They'd come," Layla said flatly. "Obliviators. Memory people. They'd erase every one of you. Try to, anyway—I don't know if it'd work on you, I've got no idea. They'd definitely erase him." She nodded at Charlie, who looked as though he'd been expecting that. "And they'd lock me up. Probably for a long time."

"Then why tell us?" said Rosalie. "Why take the risk? What's the catch?"

Layla looked at Seth.

Seth, sitting on the floor at her feet with his back against her shins, where he'd been for an hour, looked up at her.

"There's no catch," Layla said. "I'm just done hiding."

Rosalie looked at her for a long moment. Edward heard her weigh it. He heard her come down, reluctantly, on the side of believing it, because what Rosalie heard in it was something she knew from the inside: a woman who'd decided she'd rather lose everything than lie about the one thing she wanted.

"Fine," Rosalie said, and went upstairs.

"That means she likes you," said Emmett.

"That's what that means?"

"That's the nicest she's been to anyone in about five years," said Emmett. "Seriously. Can you turn me into a bear?"

"*No*, Emmett."

"A small bear?"

---

Alice caught Edward in the kitchen, at five, while Esme was making Charlie a sandwich he'd forget to eat.

*I couldn't see her,* she thought at him. Her mind was quick and bright and unhappy. *For five weeks. Seth's been with her for five weeks and every time I tried to look at her there was just a blur. I thought it was because of Seth, because of the wolves, they always blur. But Edward, I tried to look at her alone. Just her. Without him. And I still can't. It's like trying to look at something through a heat shimmer.*

"The magic," he murmured, too low for anyone else.

*Maybe. Or maybe it's just that she's tied to him now.* Alice frowned. *But I don't like not seeing. I don't like it at all. If there's one of her, there are others. And if there are others, and I can't see any of them coming—*

"They don't know about us," Edward said. "She said so."

*She said she didn't think so,* Alice thought. *That's not the same thing.*

He didn't have an answer for that.

---

It was dark when they left.

Edward walked them out onto the porch. Leah was still in the trees at the edge of the lawn, a gray shape among the gray trunks; she'd been there all afternoon, nose down, ears back, refusing to come closer than the tree line, refusing to phase. Her mind was the same tight bitter hum it had been since the first time he'd heard it, but there was something new under it tonight that Edward hadn't heard before, and it took him a moment to recognize it. Pride. Ferocious, prickly, unwilling pride. In her brother. In the woman who'd stood in a vampire's house and lit up the air for a child.

*At least one of us,* Leah thought, not knowing he heard, or not caring, *gets the fairy tale.*

Edward looked away from the trees.

On the steps, Layla stopped. She took Seth's left hand and turned it over and looked at his wrist, and then she took something out of her jacket pocket. A strip of pale leather, a bootlace, maybe, braided with a dark cord. On it, threaded through the middle, a single bead. It was black and glossy and about the size of a hazelnut, and when Edward looked at it in Layla's mind he saw that she'd made it in the night, at her grandmother's kitchen table, after Seth had gone home. Carved it out of something. Obsidian. She'd brought the stone from New Mexico. She'd sat up until four in the morning working on it, with the old words, over and over.

"Hold still," she said.

She tied it around Seth's wrist. It was too small. It would have been too small for a human wrist, let alone his; it barely met. She held it closed with her thumb and touched the bead with her wand and murmured something, and the leather *stretched*. Edward watched it happen. It lengthened and settled, snug, around Seth's wrist, and the bead lay against the inside of it, over his pulse.

"It'll stretch," she said. "When you change. It'll go with you. It won't break. I made sure. I tested it on—never mind what I tested it on." She looked up at him. "If you ever need me, you bite it. The bead. Hard. Crack it between your teeth. Okay? Wherever I am, wherever you are, I'll feel it. And I'll come."

"Come how?" said Seth.

"I'll just come," Layla said. "You'll see. We can—I'll show you some time. We can sort of. Step. From one place to another. Fast."

"Like teleporting?"

"Kind of. It's called Apparition. It's horrible. It feels like being shoved through a garden hose." She closed his fingers over the bead. "Don't use it for nothing. It only works once. I'd have to make another one, and it takes all night. But if you need me—"

"I'll bite it," Seth said.

"Promise."

"I promise."

She went up on her toes, there on the Cullens' porch, in front of all of them, and kissed him. Briefly. Then she turned around and walked down the steps and across the lawn to her truck, and she didn't look back, and Edward heard her mind the whole way, the organ-note humming, the Latin keys swinging gently on their hooks, and over it, very clear, a single thought, said over and over, almost like a prayer:

*Not halfway. Not halfway. Not halfway.*

Seth went after her, of course. He'd have gone after her into the sea.

Edward stood on the porch and watched the truck's taillights go down the drive. In the trees, Leah turned and loped away north to start her patrol, a gray shadow among the trunks.

Behind him, in the house, his daughter was asking her mother, with her hand on Bella's cheek, if the witch would come back tomorrow.

Edward thought of a city on a hill in Italy, past the mountains, past the sea, and three ancient men in their tower who knew nothing about any of this. Nothing about the witch, or the child who'd asked her for the light, or the rain that had stopped in a yard in Forks. For now, he thought, that ignorance was the only thing keeping all of them safe.

He stood there a long time, listening to the forest, wondering how long *for now* could last.

### VII. Harry

*North Fork of the Calawah River, Olympic National Forest — Monday, November 20th, 2006*

The tent was a two-man ridge tent in faded army green, the kind you could buy at any surplus shop in Britain for twelve quid, and it had been pitched in a stand of old-growth Sitka spruce on a bench above the north fork of the Calawah for sixty-six days. Moss had started on the guy lines. A banana slug the length of Harry's hand had been living under the left-side flysheet since October. He'd named it Gary.

Gary was there this morning, on the canvas by the door, when Harry came out at half five to take a piss. Fat and yellow and spotted black, gleaming in the dark. Harry looked at him.

"Morning, Gary," he said.

Gary didn't say anything. Gary never did. That was the main thing Harry liked about him.

It was raining. It had been raining when he got here in September and it would be raining, as far as he could tell, when they buried him. The air smelled of spruce and wet earth and the river, which was loud and high and the colour of milky tea in the dark below the bench. He stood under the trees in his boxers and a T-shirt and let the rain land on him, and checked the wards.

He did it without moving. Eyes half shut, breathing slow. The tent's wards first, close in: the Muggle-repellers, the notice-me-nots, the unplottable layer that made every map of this valley come out a quarter-mile short. Then the alarms. Then the sinks that ate any trace of magic he did inside the boundary so nobody with a sensitive nose could follow it home. All of it present, all of it quiet, sitting around the bench like a dog asleep across a doorway.

Then, further out, the net.

It took him a second to find the edge of it every morning. It was big. He'd built it over three weeks in September and early October, walking the peninsula end to end: up the Hoh and the Bogachiel and the Sol Duc, along the coast from Kalaloch to Cape Flattery, over the Bailey Range on foot with a pack full of river stones he'd carved himself, each one the size of a fist. Two hundred and eleven of them. He'd buried every one at a spot he'd picked with a dowsing rod and a lot of swearing, and every one was a knot in a lattice that ran from the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the Quinault and from the Pacific to the eastern slope of the Olympics, a little over three thousand square miles. Every stone was tied to every other stone. The whole thing was tied to him.

He let it come up under his attention. It felt the way a spider's web must feel to the spider: a vast, faint, trembling map, every thread humming with the weight of the rain.

Nothing on it. No red.

Plenty of gold. There always was. Gold meant venom, the ward's best guess at the word; whatever the cold ones had in place of blood, whatever moved them, the lattice could taste it, and it could taste what that venom had been fed on. Animal blood came up gold. He counted. Seven, clustered, at the house up the long drive off the highway east of town. Two of the seven not in the big house but at the small one in the trees behind it, close together. An eighth beside them, smaller, a thread of pale gold with a hot red pulse in the middle of it like a coal, which was the child.

He stood there and looked at the child for a while, the way he did most mornings.

The DoM's file on the Forks coven said: *Seven adults. Designation ASSET-PROBABLE. Do not engage.* It did not mention a child, because nobody in London knew there was one, because the only person who knew there was one, outside Forks, was Harry, and he had not put it in a report yet. He'd been deciding whether to for nine weeks. It had been born, near as he could tell, about a week before he arrived. The lattice had picked it up the day he'd laid the first stone in the valley above the Cullen place: something that read as venom and blood at the same time, gold and red, which the ward wasn't designed to recognise and had sulked about for two days. It was bigger now. A lot bigger. Growing fast. He'd seen it once, from a ridge a mile off, through a pair of Omnioculars, riding on the back of a russet-coloured wolf the size of a horse, holding on to its ruff with both fists, laughing.

He didn't know what it was. That was rare enough these days that he'd started to enjoy it.

Then the wolves. He'd keyed them in rust, because he'd wanted to know where they were, and because rust was the colour that came up when he'd tested the lattice on the first one he'd seen. Three of them in the eastern pack, the one that ran with the Cullens. Seven or eight in the western one, around the reservation. Mostly they were in human shape this time of the morning and the lattice couldn't tell them from anybody else, but there were two rust dots moving on the eastern patrol loop now, one fast, one following.

And one rust dot, on most nights lately, parked outside a blue house on Calawah Way.

The blue house was a blank on the lattice. A smooth place, like a stone in a stream, where the threads of his net bent round it. Somebody's wards. Old ones, grown slowly over years, the kind of hedge-work he'd only seen in the West Country and on a farm in Galway once, tied into something that lived and moved. Bees, he'd guessed, the first time he'd driven past and seen the hives. Pretty bit of work. He'd left it alone.

He'd left her alone, too. He'd made that call in his first week, when he'd run the town's magical footprint and found exactly one: a witch, newly arrived, settling into her late grandmother's house. He'd done the file on her from Forks Public Library and two discreet owls to people in Santa Fe who owed him favours, and it said what he'd expected. Trained, good, tired. American. Recently retired from a job that had ended badly. The kind of witch who'd notice another wizard if he walked past her in the street, and who'd have no reason not to send a polite letter to the MACUSA field office in Seattle about it.

So he didn't walk past her in the street. He'd kept to the other side of town. When the wolf boy had started parking outside her house every night he'd noted it as *local romantic attachment, no action*, and thought, not for the first time, that this was the strangest posting he'd ever had.

He went back inside.

---

Inside, the tent was a house.

He'd built it in stages over four years, and it still wasn't finished, and he suspected it never would be. You came in through the flap and down three wooden steps into a long hallway with a slate floor and pegs along the wall for coats, and the hallway went back a lot further than a two-man ridge tent had any business going. Doors on both sides. A kitchen on the left with a big cast-iron range and a table that sat eight, though nobody but him had ever eaten at it. A bathroom with an actual bath. His room, which was small, which he liked. A library that had started as a bookcase and been extended twice. A workshop at the back, big enough for a lathe and two workbenches and a motorbike.

He went into the kitchen, put the kettle on the range, and put a record on the player.

The player was a Rega from 1979 that he'd bought in a junk shop in Camden for six quid and spent three weekends fixing. The trick, if you wanted Muggle machines to work inside a space like this, was the lining; he'd spent most of 2004 on it, a mesh of insulating charms in the tent's skin, finer than silk, that kept the magic in the walls from bleeding into the wiring. He'd never written it up. Nobody at the DoM had asked. They thought it was a hobby. It was, sort of. It also meant he had a working telephone in the hall and a fridge that kept beer cold, and that the record player did what record players were supposed to do, which this morning was *Physical Graffiti*, side one, loud.

He stood at the range in his boxers and fried six eggs and a pound of bacon and four slices of bread in the bacon fat and ate all of it standing up, out of the pan, with a fork, and then he made two more eggs because he was still hungry. He was always hungry. That had started a few years back, when the other thing had started, and it hadn't let up since. His body ran hot and burned through fuel like a furnace, and if he missed a meal it let him know.

The other thing was quiet this morning. It had been quiet for two months. It didn't like that.

He could feel it if he went looking: down under his breastbone, low and to the left, where it lived. A weight. A pressure. Something big lying down in the dark with its chin on its paws and its eyes open. He'd put it there himself in the second week of September, before he'd laid the first ward stone, because he'd known before he got on the plane that there were shapeshifters on this coast with noses that could pick out a vampire at three miles, and the last thing he needed on a quiet job was some kid from La Push catching a whiff of something wild and big and canine coming out of an Englishman at the petrol station. So he'd locked it down. Three layers of his own work, braided into the Occlumency shields, which he'd never done before and wouldn't recommend. He took the scent with it. Mostly. He came across now, to an ordinary nose, as soap and coffee and the outdoors. To a wolf's, he'd guessed, as something that had been in the woods a lot.

It was holding. It was holding fine. Every morning he checked it, and every morning the thing behind the locks lifted its head when he came near, and looked at him, and put its head back down.

He did not, in general, think about it more than he had to.

---

After breakfast he trained for an hour in the workshop, which was the only room big enough.

Wandless first. Always. He'd learned it in Uganda in 1999, the year after the war, on the Department's leave and the Department's money, from an old woman in the Rwenzori Mountains who'd taught at Uagadou for sixty years before she retired and who'd looked at him the first day, a skinny English boy with famous eyes and a holly wand, and laughed for so long she'd had to sit down. *They taught you to wave a stick,* she'd said, when she'd finished. *Like a man who walks with a crutch his whole life because nobody ever told him his legs work.* He'd been there eight months. He'd walked out with his wand in his pocket and he'd only taken it out twice since, both times for show.

He ran the drills. Fourteen knives up off the bench into the air, spinning, a slow loop round his head like a mobile. Then faster. Then a book open in front of his face, held up by nothing, a monograph on Bulgarian blood-wards he was halfway through, while the knives went round and he read three pages and the knives didn't touch each other or him. Then shields, twenty different kinds, up and down, in sequence, as fast as he could think them. Then the hard ones. Disillusionment so complete the rain coming in through the workshop's skylight went round him. Silent Apparition across the room and back, four times, so close to soundless that the knives, still circling, didn't so much as wobble in the displaced air.

He was faster than he'd been last year. He was a lot faster than he'd been the year before. He'd stopped comparing himself to anyone else round about 2003, when he'd noticed that there wasn't much point.

Then he went to the armoury and cleaned the guns, because it was Monday.

The armoury was a narrow room off the workshop, lined floor to ceiling in pegboard and steel racks. The guns had started as a joke. A bloke he'd worked with on a job in Belfast in 2002, a Muggle-born Auror called Fennick who'd done six years in the Paras before his letter came late, had laughed at him for not knowing how to strip a rifle. So Harry had learned. Then he'd started wondering what you could do with one. Then he'd started finding out. A Muggle firearm, it turned out, was a lovely little machine for putting energy exactly where you wanted it at a very great speed, and if you were clever about what you put in the chamber, and clever about what you did to the chamber itself, it would put almost anything anywhere. Nobody he'd met in the magical world took them seriously. Which was, as far as he was concerned, about ninety per cent of the point.

He did the shotgun first, a Remington 870 with a cut-down stock, whose shells, if he wanted them to, came out as rock salt soaked in a solution that took the stuffing out of most things that walked around after death. Then the lever-action, a Winchester '94 he'd picked up in Montana on a different job and couldn't bring himself to leave behind. Then a few others that didn't get much use.

Then the Colt.

It was a 1911A1, made in 1943, US Army issue, and it had come to him in a house clearance in Hertfordshire with a box of medals and a stack of letters from a woman in Ohio. Steel, blued, the bluing worn silver at the edges where somebody's thumb had ridden the safety for three years in Europe. Seven-round magazine. Single action. It weighed about as much as a bag of sugar and it was the most beautiful object he owned.

He'd spent eighteen months on it. Off and on.

He field-stripped it on the bench now, by hand, the old way, slide off the frame, barrel out, recoil spring and plug, and he looked at the runes. They were cut into the inside of the slide where nobody would see them, so fine you needed a loupe, twelve hundred of them, his own notation, and they did two things. The first was that the magazine never ran dry. The slide came back and the next round was there, made fresh out of nothing in the half-second it took, and it would go on doing that until the sun went out or he stopped asking. The second was that he could choose what the round was.

He thought of it as a menu. There was the ordinary: plain ball, 230 grains, copper over lead, the round the pistol had been built for. There was the round that went through things, and the round that set fire to things, and the one he'd built in Prague that dropped a body-bind on whatever it hit. There was a silver one, which he'd never needed and kept on principle.

And there was the one he'd built for this job.

He'd spent August on it, in the windowless office on Level Nine, with the photograph from Volterra pinned to the wall over his desk. *Stone,* the file said. *Skin as hard as granite. Bullets flatten on contact.* So he'd built something that didn't need to go in. It hit and it went off, a fraction of a second later, a shaped charge of raw force channelled forward through whatever it had touched. He'd tested it on a chunk of Cornish granite the size of a car in a quarry outside St Austell, and then he'd had to go round Obliviating three quarrymen who'd been having lunch on the other side of the hill. The granite had come apart like a dropped cake. He'd sat on what was left of it afterwards eating a sandwich of his own, looking at the crater, thinking that a Muggle he'd once met in a pub in Leeds would have recognised it straight off. The bloke had played some tabletop game about space marines, and he'd spent an hour drunkenly describing their guns to Harry. *Mass-reactive,* he'd said. *Goes in and goes bang.* Harry had thought it sounded like a lot of effort for a toy.

He'd never told anybody at the DoM that that was where he'd got the idea.

He put the Colt back together. Slide on, pin in. Racked it. Checked it. Holstered it in the shoulder rig hanging on the peg by the door.

Then he went to look at the specimen room, which was empty, and had been empty for sixty-six days.

---

It was at the very back, past the workshop, behind a door of black oak bound in iron that he'd taken off a church in Shropshire the parish had been about to tear down. It needed his hand and a word to open.

The room was round. The walls were shelves, ceiling to floor, and the shelves were jars, and the jars were eight years of his life.

He walked round it slowly, the way he did most mornings, checking seals. A chupacabra skull from Puerto Rico. Two Inferius hands from a lake in Finland, still twitching in their jar of brine. Kelpie mane. Nundu whisker. A pressed Lethifold between two panes of enchanted glass, black and flat and patient, that he did not look at for very long. A fang, eighteen inches long and yellow at the root, sealed under three charms and a curse, from a chamber under a school in Scotland. He'd gone back down there for it in the summer of 1999, alone, at night, and stood a long time in the dark in front of what was left of the snake before he took it. There was enough venom left in that fang to kill a town. He'd been saving it for something.

In the middle of the room, on the stone floor, was a circle.

It was ten feet across and cut an inch deep into the flagstones and filled with silver, and inside the silver ring were three more rings, and inside those a pattern that had taken him most of September to finish. Containment. The best he'd ever made. Built from everything London had on the cold ones and everything he'd been able to work out on his own, which was more, by now, than London had. Built for something that was stronger than a troll and faster than a bullet and didn't need to breathe.

It was empty. It had been empty since he'd finished it.

He stood at the edge and looked at it.

The DoM's orders were very simple, the way DoM orders always were. They'd come on a single sheet of grey paper, unsigned, in Croaker's spidery writing, in August, and they said: *Proceed to the Olympic Peninsula, Washington State, USA. Observe the Forks coven. Do not engage the Forks coven. Obtain one hostile specimen, alive, for study. Do not breach the Statute. You are not there.*

That last line was the important one. He wasn't there. If MACUSA caught a British Unspeakable on American soil running a three-thousand-square-mile ward net over a national park, Britain would never have heard of him. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement. He was a man on holiday with a camera and a lot of luck.

The orders didn't mention the other thing either, because they didn't need to. Every Unspeakable who went into the field carried what Level Nine called standing authority. It had been in the Department's charter since 1717 and it had never once been written down anywhere a member of the Wizengamot might read it. It meant that the three curses that would put anybody else in Azkaban for life were, for him, tools in a drawer. He'd used two of them on people. He'd used the third once, in 2003, in a basement in Minsk, on a man who'd been selling Muggle children to a hag. He didn't think about it much. He'd been trained not to, and the training had taken.

Then there was what the orders didn't say, because orders from Level Nine never said why. He knew why. He'd been in the room.

It had started with a photograph. In the spring, the Italian Ministry's Obliviators had been called out to Volterra, a hill town in Tuscany, after a festival. A cluster of Muggle tourists had all gone home talking about the same strange thing in the main square at noon. Nothing too serious, a mass Confundus at worst, the sort of job the Italians did a dozen times a year. Except one of the tourists had a camera. And one of the Obliviators, before he wiped the camera, had looked at the pictures. And instead of deleting them all he'd sent one, quietly, through a back channel he shouldn't have had, to a friend of a friend in London.

The photograph was pinned up in Harry's office on Level Nine. He had a copy in the map room here. A crowded square, red cloaks everywhere, a clock tower. In the deep shade of an archway at the base of the tower, a young man, bare-chested, with his arms out at his sides, half-turned toward the light. One forearm had crossed into a slant of noon sun. And it was shining. It was throwing light off itself like a cut diamond, a spray of colour, and his face, in the shade above it, was the face of somebody who'd come there to die.

London had sent somebody to Volterra after that. Then somebody else. Then Harry, in June, as a tourist, with a guidebook and a camera and a sunburn. He'd walked through the old palace on a guided tour. He'd stood in front of the reception desk, polished wood and fresh flowers, and smiled at the girl behind it. Dark-haired, beautiful, Italian, human. Her name was Gianna. She'd smiled back. He'd spent nine seconds in her head.

Nine seconds had been plenty.

He knew what was in that building. He knew who. The three on the thrones, and the one in the middle whose skin was like onionskin paper and who read your whole life off your hand. The little blonde girl who could make you feel like you were on fire. Her brother. The tracker. The big one. He knew about the tours, the woman who went out to fetch them. Gianna had walked a group of them in herself, once, forty Dutch tourists, smiling, chatting, and stood outside the big double doors while the screaming started, and then gone back to her desk, and touched up her lipstick, and waited to be made one of them. That was her whole life. Waiting, at her desk, while they ate.

They'd been doing it for three thousand years. Heidi went fishing, and the fish came, and nobody in the whole of the Italian magical world had ever known. Nobody in the world had known. Somewhere, in the twisting of three thousand years of secrecy on two sides that had never met, they'd simply missed each other. The wizards had thought vampires were sickly things in Transylvanian castles that went to parties and wrote sad memoirs. The vampires had never once, as far as Gianna's memory went, said the word *witch*.

Harry had come out of the palace into the sunshine and walked down the hill to a café and sat there for an hour with a coffee he didn't drink.

Then he'd gone home, and written it up, and the head of the Department had read it, and then the head of the Department had gone upstairs, to a room on Level Nine that didn't have a name, and come back down three hours later with a face like a stone, and said a word Harry had never heard used in the Department before, which was *strike*.

They'd killed something north of half a million people, by the DoM's best guess, since Rome. Maybe a lot more. Muggles, mostly. Witches and wizards too, without knowing it; there were names on old missing-persons lists in the Italian Ministry's archive that matched Gianna's memories of tourists. And they'd keep doing it. They'd keep doing it until the sun burned out.

So London was going to stop them.

Which meant London needed to know how. Which spells worked, and which didn't. Whether a Stunner did anything. Whether Fiendfyre was overkill or the only option. Whether the gifts, the little blonde girl, the tracker, the old one reading your hand, worked on a wizard's mind the same as a Muggle's, or better, or worse. Whether a vampire could be Imperiused. Whether the tracker could find you through a Fidelius. Whether you could burn a building with three thousand years of them in it and get out alive.

London needed a specimen. A hostile one, a red-eyed one, a human-eater. The Cullens were off limits: too useful, too interesting, too likely to be on the right side when it came to it, and too likely to be missed. London wanted one nobody would miss. So Harry had built a net over the peninsula keyed to what the venom tasted of, set to snare anything that had been feeding on people, and sat down to wait.

Sixty-six days. Not so much as a nibble.

It was, he reflected, standing at the edge of his beautiful empty circle in his boxers with a cup of tea going cold in his hand, a lot like fishing.

---

He went into town at eleven, because the coin said to.

He flipped it in the hall by the door: a 1971 penny, the first year of decimal, that he'd had in his pocket since he was eleven and Dudley had dropped it down the back of the sofa. Heads, town. Tails, the Hoh. It came up heads.

The coin was tradecraft. The file on the Forks coven said one of them could see the future, the small dark one who'd come to Volterra with the boy. Gianna had heard Aro talk about her afterward, like a man describing a jewel he wanted. *She sees what people decide.* Fine. So he made as few decisions as he could. He let the penny make them, and he let it make them at the last second, and if the girl up the hill was watching the future of a strange Englishman in a rented car, she'd see him flipping a coin and then, for a few seconds, nothing at all.

He drove in, in the Explorer. It was a 2004 Ford, mud-brown, rented at Sea-Tac in September under the name on his British passport, which was Harry James Evans. The passport was real. So was the Washington State fishing licence in the glovebox, and the receipts from a Motel 6 in Aberdeen, and the library card from Islington Central Library in his wallet, and the dog-eared paperback of Chandler on the passenger seat, and the battered Nikon F3 with a 135mm lens that he really did take pictures with, most days, because a man who says he's a photographer had better have pictures. He had about four hundred of moss. Some of them weren't bad.

Harry Evans was a freelance nature photographer from north London, thirty or so, on a six-month working visa, putting together a book on the temperate rainforests of the Pacific Northwest for a small publisher in Bristol who did coffee-table books. The publisher existed. If you rang them they'd tell you all about him. Harry Evans was friendly, a bit loud, ate too much, tipped well, and talked to anybody who'd stand still about the quality of light in the Hoh River valley at seven in the morning. He drove the speed limit. He paid cash. He did not, under any circumstances, go near the reservation.

People in Forks had stopped looking at him after about a fortnight. That was the job.

He didn't hide his face. There wasn't any point. Nobody here had any reason to know it. Nobody in America had, really; the Daily Prophet didn't come this far, and the photographs it had run of him in '98 were of a skinny, half-starved boy with glasses and a lightning-bolt scar who, as far as Harry was concerned, had died in a forest in Scotland and not come back. The glasses he'd sorted in 2001; a Healer in Mumbai, an afternoon, a new pair of eyes. The scar had faded to nothing within a year of the war, the way scars do when what made them is gone. He'd filled out. He'd grown four inches after he turned eighteen, which nobody had expected, least of all him. People who'd known him at school walked past him on Diagon Alley now and didn't look twice.

He'd made peace with it. He'd made peace with most things.

The diner was on Forks Avenue, on the main drag, a low building with a gravel car park and a neon sign in the window that said OPEN in a pink that had been fading since about 1985. He parked where he always parked, round the side, nose out. Went in. Sat where he always sat: a booth by the back wall with a view of the door and the car park and the kitchen pass, which he'd picked on his first visit for reasons that had nothing to do with the vinyl.

"Hey, English," said the waitress. Her name was Doreen. She was sixty and had a voice like a cement mixer. "Cheeseburger?"

"You're a mind-reader, Doreen."

"You're a creature of habit, hon." She was already writing. "Fries. Coke. And?"

"What's the pie today?"

"Blackberry."

"I love you. Marry me."

"Get in line." She went off. Harry watched her go and thought, as he did most days, that she'd have made a cracking intelligence officer.

The burger came. It was enormous and dripping and it was the best thing in Forks by a distance of about forty miles, and he ate it in seven bites, and ate the fries, and sat back and waited for the pie.

The door went.

Rain, wet wool, gun oil, coffee, plain white soap. He knew who it was before he looked up. He looked up anyway, because Harry Evans would have.

Chief Charlie Swan came in out of the rain and stood just inside the door, shaking his hat, and his eyes went round the room the way every cop's eyes Harry had ever met went round a room. Exits. Hands. Faces. They passed over Harry in the back booth and stopped for half a second, and then went on to the counter, where the stool at the end was empty and waiting and Doreen was already pouring his coffee.

Harry watched him walk to it.

He'd done the file on Swan too, of course. You did the file on the local law the first week. Police chief, twenty-odd years. Divorced, long ago. One daughter, nineteen, Isabella, married in August to the Cullen boy. The one in the photograph. The daughter had got ill on the honeymoon, very ill, and then she'd got better, and since then she'd been glimpsed in town exactly once, through the window of a silver Volvo, and she'd been wearing sunglasses on a cloudy day.

The lattice had seen her, too. She'd been the last of the gold to arrive. Bright, brand-new gold, like a coin fresh from the mint.

So Harry knew what had happened to Charlie Swan's daughter. And he'd watched Charlie Swan come in here every day for nine weeks and order his coffee and his pie and sit at the end of the counter and eat it very slowly and stare at the coffee machine. A man with a face like the edge of a cliff. Harry had seen that face on Muggles before. Lots of times. Usually just before he Obliviated them.

He'd brushed against Swan's mind once, in October. The lightest touch, a surface skim, as Swan walked past his booth. Standard. You checked the local law. And he'd hit something he hadn't expected: a closed door, with a big stolid bloke sitting with his back against it. Swan didn't know he had it. A natural block, the kind you found in about one Muggle in fifty thousand, heavy and quiet and thick as a vault wall. Gianna's memory had said the daughter's mind was silent even to the old one's touch, even to the Cullen boy. Harry had sat in the booth with his pie and thought: *runs in the family, then.* And he'd left it there. You could get past a door like that. You could get past anything if you leaned on it. But Swan would have felt it, in the way people feel a draught and don't know why, and he was a cop, and he'd have looked round, and Harry would have been the only stranger in the room.

So he didn't lean on it. He watched Swan eat his pie, and Swan watched the coffee machine, and both of them minded their business.

Doreen brought Harry's pie. He ate the first bite with his eyes shut.

Then the ring on his left hand got hot.

It was a plain band of dull grey iron on his middle finger, and it was tied to the lattice, and it didn't get hot unless the lattice wanted his attention. Heat meant contact. He kept his eyes shut and kept chewing and let the lattice come up under his mind, faint and trembling, the whole peninsula like a map in the dark—

There. Red.

Way out to the south-east, in the upper Bogachiel, in the folds of country between the river and the ridges above the Hoh. A single point. Bright red, the deep red of blood in a syringe, crossing the outer ring of stones and heading north. Fast.

Harry opened his eyes.

He took another bite of pie. It really was very good.

On the lattice the red dot moved north and west, toward Forks, toward the Calawah, toward the Cullen place, at something like sixty miles an hour through old-growth forest with no trail. It was on the eastern patrol loop. And the two rust dots, the wolves, the fast one and the one following, were already turning.

They'd smelled it. Of course they had. They were coming at it from the north, the fast one well in front, closing.

Harry ate another bite and watched them on the map behind his eyes. The red dot slowed. Stopped. It had smelled them too. It sat there in the middle of the forest for about three seconds, which he thought must be how long it took a three-hundred-year-old killer to work out that the two enormous dogs coming at him were not, in fact, dogs.

Then it ran.

East. Back the way it had come, back toward the Bogachiel, back toward the outer ring. Straight toward the inner snare line he'd laid along the ridge above the river in the first week of October.

Harry finished his pie.

He put a twenty on the table, which was too much, and got up, and took his coat off the hook.

"Leaving already?" Doreen called from the counter.

"Got a call," he said. "Light's coming good up the Bogachiel. Save me a slice for tomorrow, would you?"

"For you, English," she said, "I'll save two."

At the counter, Charlie Swan didn't look round.

The ring on his finger went from hot to burning.

Sixty-six days.

Harry sat in the car for a second with his hands on the steering wheel and the rain on the roof, and grinned.

"Gotcha," he said softly. "You son of a bitch."

Then he got out of the car. He stood in the turnout, in the rain, and looked down at himself: jeans, boots, flannel shirt, an old waxed jacket. Harry Evans, photographer. He thought about it for a second, and changed his mind.

The flannel ran together and went smooth and dark, a charcoal-grey suit, single-breasted, white shirt, no tie. The jeans went with it. The waxed jacket lengthened and blackened into a wool overcoat that came down below his knees. The boots stayed boots; he liked the boots. A pair of dark glasses settled on his nose out of thin air. He felt under the left side of the coat, and the weight of the shoulder rig was there, and the Colt in it, and in his inside breast pocket a slim leather wallet that hadn't been there a second ago.

He'd never been entirely sure why it worked. Some of it was magic and some of it was just the suit. Nobody argued with a suit. Not in Italy, not in Belfast, not in Kampala, not in Washington State. You turned up at the scene of something strange in a dark suit and dark glasses and a long coat, looking bored, and every single person present, wizard or Muggle, man or monster, thought exactly the same thing. *Government.* And they stepped back.

He checked the ring. Still burning. The red dot hadn't moved. And the two rust dots were almost on top of it.

"Right," said Harry, to the rain.

He turned on his heel, and the turnout was empty, and the only sign he'd ever been there was a soft sound, no louder than a page turning, that the rain covered almost at once.

### VIII. Leah

*SORRY.*

She ran.

It had smelled them.

Leah slowed. Seth came up beside her, panting. They stood in the dripping trees with their noses up and their ears forward and listened, and up ahead, through half a mile of old-growth, they heard it make up its mind.

It went east.

Back the way it had come. Away from the Cullens. Away from them. Fast, faster than before, flat out, the fern sound going the other way now, a long ripping trail of it heading for the ridge above the Bogachiel.

*It's running,* Seth thought. *It's running away.*

*Yeah,* Leah thought. *It's smart.*

*Do we let it go?*

Tall. Taller than most of them. Lean. Long black hair tied back in a tail that streamed out behind it. A red rain jacket, a woman's jacket, a size too small, the sleeves halfway up its pale forearms. Red like a stoplight. The Cullens' eyes went a muddy burgundy when they'd been too long without hunting; this was something else, red like something that had been drinking people all its life and had drunk one yesterday.

It went faster.

Fifteen yards.

The vampire went over a hump of rock in the middle of the ridge, a big flat granite slab with moss on it, the color of a gravestone. It hit the slab with one foot, pushing off.

He panicked.

And Layla Quinn came out of it already firing.

### IX. Layla

*The ridge above the Bogachiel — the same moment*

Ninety seconds earlier she'd been in her grandmother's kitchen with her sleeves rolled up to the elbow and honey to the wrists, pouring the last of the October extraction into jars.

She'd been thinking about nothing. That was the nice thing about honey. You poured it and it went where it wanted, slow and gold, and you couldn't hurry it, and after an hour of it your head went quiet in a way it hadn't been quiet in fourteen months. She'd been on jar thirty-one of forty. She'd had the radio on, low, a station out of Port Angeles playing country. She'd been humming.

Then something put a hook through her breastbone and pulled.

She'd felt it like a fist. The bead. She'd built it so she'd feel it, she'd spent until four in the morning on it, but she'd built it in a quiet kitchen with her heart going normally and a cup of tea at her elbow, and she hadn't known, she hadn't really known, what it would feel like when it went. It felt like falling down a flight of stairs. It felt like *Seth*, all of him, the whole of the gold line she'd woven around the red, lighting up at once from his end and screaming, and the scream was her own spell, her own voice, *HERE, HERE, HERE*, and under it, his. Terror. Pure, white, animal terror.

The jar had hit the floor. She didn't remember dropping it.

She remembered the wand coming into her hand, because it was already there, because it had come out of her sleeve on its own the second the hook went in, the Thunderbird feather inside it sitting up and screaming *danger* the way it had in the cenote, the way it had in Phoenix, the way it did right before something tried to kill her. And she remembered turning on the spot in the middle of the kitchen, sticky to the elbows, the radio still playing, with no idea where she was going. Just the hook. Just *there*. Wherever *there* was.

The garden hose took her.

Fourteen miles in a fraction of a second, squeezed flat and dragged, and she came out of the other end of it into rain and cold and the smell of wet rock, already turning, already low, wand already up and spitting red, because she'd been taught by a woman named Calloway who'd run the dueling program at Ilvermorny for thirty years and had said to her, the first week, *You'll never once in your life have time to look before you shoot. So learn to shoot while you're looking.*

So she shot while she looked.

She saw a ridge. Gray rock and stunted trees, mist hanging in the gaps. A big flat slab of granite. Two wolves on it, the gray one and the sandy one, crouched, with their teeth out and every hair on their backs standing up. Seth, the sandy one, with blood on his foreleg and the leather band hanging off it in pieces. And between them, on the edge of the slab, a man.

A tall man in a dark suit and a long black coat. With a body under his arm.

That was what she shot at.

She'd thrown a Stunner. It was the fastest thing she had, the thing her hand did without asking her, and it went out of her wand point as she was still landing, a hard bright bolt of red across the twenty feet of rain between them, straight at the center of his chest.

He didn't look round.

He was looking at Seth. He went on looking at Seth. He lifted his right hand, the one that wasn't holding the body, off the dead man's ribs, an inch, and flicked one finger, the way you'd flick a crumb off a tablecloth.

The Stunner bent.

She saw it bend. She saw it go from a straight line to a right angle in the air a foot from his chest, and go off to his left, and into the trees, where it hit a hemlock about twelve feet up and took off a branch the size of her leg. The branch came down through the mist in a shower of needles and water. It landed in the moss.

He hadn't looked.

Layla landed. Her boots hit wet rock and she slid a little and caught herself, and she was already moving left, out of line, the way Calloway had drilled into her, never stand where you shot from, and the next one was already coming out of her wand, and the one after it was already shaped in her head.

*Okay,* said a cold, clear voice in the back of her mind, the voice she used for work. *Okay. That's one.*

---

The second thing she threw was a Disarming Charm, which was a mistake, and she knew it was a mistake about halfway through casting it. She'd been looking for his wand. You always went for the wand. She'd thrown it at his right hand, because that was the hand he'd used, and she'd thrown it hard enough to take a wand out of a troll's fist.

He didn't have a wand.

She saw that at the same moment she saw him lift his hand into the path of the spell, palm out, fingers open, and catch it. Catch the light of it, the scarlet flash, in his bare palm, the way you'd catch a tennis ball. And close his fingers on it. And it went out.

He opened his hand again. There was nothing in it. A little steam came off his palm in the rain.

Then he turned his head and looked at her for the first time.

Dark glasses. No, he had them off; they were in his other hand, the one that was holding the body. Green eyes. They looked at her for about a second. She'd been looked at by a lot of things, a lot of very old things in very dark places, and she'd learned you could tell how much trouble you were in by how long something took to decide about you. He took about a second.

"Oh, for God's sake," he said. English. "Not *you* as well."

And the body under his arm went up into the air.

He just let go of it. It didn't fall. It rose, rigid, a tall man with long black hair in a woman's red rain jacket, and floated up and backward over his shoulder and hung there behind him, horizontal, at about the height of his hip, like a coffee table. Its eyes were moving. Its eyes were red. They rolled toward her, and she felt her stomach go over: *it's alive. That's one of the cold ones and it's alive and it can't move.*

Then she was throwing again, because that was what you did.

*Incarcerous.* Ropes, out of nothing, out of the wand point, four of them, thick as her thumb, snaking through the air at him to wrap his arms and legs. Calloway had called it a *thinking* spell, a spell that gave you time. You threw ropes and while they were busy with the ropes you got to think.

He didn't get busy with the ropes.

He looked at them coming, and she saw them turn round. In midair. All four of them, as one, like a school of fish. They turned round in the rain and came back at her, at the same speed she'd sent them, and two of them wrapped her ankles before she'd finished moving and the other two went for her wrists.

She cut them. *Diffindo*, a slash of her wand across her own body, close enough that she felt the hex go past her skin. The ropes fell off in pieces. She was already moving again. Right this time. Three steps right, across the wet granite.

*That's three,* said the cold voice. *Three spells. He hasn't moved his feet.*

He hadn't. He was standing exactly where he'd been standing when she arrived, on the edge of the slab, his left hand still in his coat pocket. He'd turned his body a little, toward her. That was all.

The wolves moved.

She saw them out of the corner of her eye, both at once, the gray one and the sandy one coming off the slab from either side of him, two huge shapes, launched, jaws open, the gray one going for his throat and Seth for his legs—

They stopped.

In the air. Halfway. As if they'd hit a wall. And then they went down, both of them, hard, slammed flat onto the wet granite and the moss beside it like somebody had put a hand on each of their backs and pushed. They hit the ground with a sound she felt through her boots. And they stayed there. Pressed down. Their legs scrabbling at the rock. Their heads twisting. The gray one snarling, a long ragged furious sound, and Seth whining, high, frantic, trying to get his head round to see her.

The man hadn't looked at them. Hadn't moved his hands. He was still looking at her.

*Four,* said the cold voice. Very calm. *He's holding two of those wolves down without looking at them, and they weigh, what? A ton between them? And he's still watching you.*

"Let them go," Layla said.

"Stop throwing things at me," said the man, "and I will."

She threw something at him.

*Confringo*. A blasting curse, a real one, the first one she'd thrown with real intent. She aimed it at the rock at his feet, to blow it out from under him. She wasn't ready yet to aim at him. The spell went out of her wand like a white-hot coal.

He stopped it six inches from the ground in front of his boots. She saw it stop, hanging there, a blazing ball of orange in the rain, hissing. He looked down at it. He looked at it the way you'd look at a bug that had landed on your hand.

Then he moved his finger, and it went sideways, and into a boulder twenty feet to his left, and the boulder came apart in a blast of gravel and flame and smoke that rained down over all of them, little bits of granite pattering on the rock, on the wolves, on her shoulders.

*Five,* said the voice. *And now you know.*

---

She knew.

It had taken about seven seconds. She'd been doing this for a living since she was nineteen, and before that she'd been fighting in a ring at Ilvermorny since she was twelve, and she'd been North American Junior champion at eighteen and placed third in the open at twenty against grown men and women who'd been dueling for thirty years. She knew what it looked like when someone was better than her. She'd met a few. Calloway, who could still put her on her back in four moves at sixty-one years old. A Brazilian hit-witch she'd worked a job with in Manaus. An old man in Cuzco, a curse-breaker's curse-breaker, whose hands had been so scarred they looked like tree bark.

She'd lost to all of them. She knew how that felt. It felt like climbing a mountain and looking up and seeing somebody standing on the next peak over, higher than you, but on the same range. Same rock. Same air. You could see how they'd got there. You could see the path.

She couldn't see any path to this one.

Every single thing she'd thrown at him, he'd taken apart without moving his feet. Without a wand. Without saying a word. Without, as far as she could tell, *thinking about it*: he was still looking at her the way you'd look at a dog that wouldn't stop barking, and his breathing hadn't changed, and he was holding a vampire in the air behind him and two wolves flat on the ground beside him, all at once, all the time, and none of that seemed to cost him anything at all.

Bending a spell in flight wasn't a thing. You couldn't do it. She'd been taught that you couldn't do it, and she'd taught first-years at a summer dueling camp that you couldn't do it, and she'd believed it the way you believe in gravity. A spell, once it left your wand, was gone. It went where you'd sent it. You could block it. You could dodge it. You could, if you were very, very good, and you'd met that exact spell a thousand times before, *deflect* it with a counter of your own, a wand-to-wand parry, if you saw it coming early enough.

You couldn't reach out and take hold of it with your hand and turn it round. To do that you'd have to read the thing as it came at you, at the speed of light, read its whole structure, every line of it, the way she'd read a curse on a doorframe over three days with a lamp and a magnifying glass, and you'd have to do it in the fraction of a second it took to cross twenty feet of air, and then you'd have to rewrite it. With your fingers.

She knew three names.

Everybody who'd ever been serious about dueling knew three names. Calloway had made them memorize the duels. The great ones, the ones people still argued about in the bars on the dueling circuit at three in the morning. The ones there were only secondhand accounts of, because nobody who'd been close enough to really see them had been in any state to write it down afterward.

Albus Dumbledore. Gellert Grindelwald. Tom Riddle, who'd called himself something else.

That was the list. It had been the list for sixty years. Calloway used to say there was nobody else in the twentieth century who belonged on it, and there wasn't anybody coming who would, and the closest anybody alive was going to get was a long way down. And Layla had believed her, because Calloway was right about most things. And because all three of those names were dead now, and that had seemed like a good thing. That the list was closed.

She stood on a ridge in Washington State in the rain with her wand up and her heart hammering and honey drying sticky on her wrists, looking at a man in a gray suit who hadn't moved his feet, and she thought, very clearly, in the cold work voice: *The list isn't closed.*

And then she thought: *And none of those three did it without a wand.*

She was frightened. She didn't let herself be anything else, but she was frightened all the way down, in a way she hadn't been since the water came up in the cenote. Because she was going to lose. She'd known that for about four seconds now. The only question was how badly, and what happened to Seth after.

So she did the only thing she had left, which was to throw everything at once.

---

She went fast. Faster than she'd gone since the open at twenty. She stopped thinking. She let the wand go, let the Thunderbird feather off its leash, let it pick for her, the way it wanted to, the way she'd spent ten years training it not to.

A Disjunction. The curse-breaker's spell, her spell, the one she'd built half of herself, for taking apart wards and bindings and old enchantments from the inside, fired straight at him, in case there was something *on* him, some charm or armor or borrowed power she could pull out from under him like a rug. Behind it, a second Stunner, hidden in its wake, the old trick, two spells on one line. Behind that a Bludgeoning Hex, low, for the knees. And while all three were in the air she slashed her wand down at the moss under his boots and transfigured it, the whole carpet of it, into roots. Hundreds of them, thick and black and wet, coming up out of the rock to grab his ankles and hold him and drag him down.

It was a good sequence. It would have taken Calloway.

He stepped out of the roots. That was all. He was standing two feet to the left of where he'd been, on bare rock, and she hadn't heard him go, hadn't heard the crack, had barely seen the air shiver. Silent Apparition, two feet, standing still. The roots closed on nothing.

The Bludgeoning Hex went past his knees and he didn't bother with it at all; it went off into the trees and hit something and she heard wood split. The Stunner he flicked away again without looking.

The Disjunction he caught.

He put two fingers up in front of him, like a man hailing a taxi, and the spell hit them and stopped. And she saw him look at it. *Really* look, for the first time since she'd arrived. His eyebrows went up a little. He turned his hand slightly, the way you'd turn a stone over to see the underside, and she watched the light of her own spell move over his fingertips, the pale fizzing blue of it, and she watched him *read* it.

She watched him understand it, in about a second and a half. A spell it had taken her three years to build.

"Oh, that's clever," he said. He sounded surprised. "That's actually—huh. Did you make that?"

And then he turned his fingers, just slightly, a quarter-turn, like turning a key, and gave it back to her.

It came back at her down the line she'd sent it, faster than it had gone out, and it hit her Shield Charm. The one she'd had up since she landed, the one she always had up, the one that sat around her like a skin, the best shield she knew how to make, layered, braided, built to take a Blasting Curse at close range.

Her own spell went into it and took it apart.

She felt it go. Her shield, unraveling, from the inside, every braid of it coming undone at once like a sweater with a pulled thread, exactly the way she'd built the Disjunction to unravel things. Gone. In half a second. She was standing on the ridge in the rain with nothing around her at all.

*Oh,* thought the cold voice. *Oh, that's how he wins.*

She was already raising another shield. Her mouth was already moving. And he took his left hand out of his coat.

There was a gun in it.

A pistol. Big, blocky, old-looking, dull gray-blue steel worn silver at the edges. She knew enough about Muggle things to know it was a gun and not much more. He'd taken it out from somewhere under the coat in one movement, and his right hand was still up, still holding two fingers out toward her like he was going to catch whatever she threw next, and he was pointing the gun at her.

Something in her almost laughed. It was reflex; it was the training, it was twelve years in a world where a Muggle weapon was a joke, a thing you stopped with a Shield Charm and then Obliviated the idiot holding it. *A gun.* She was fighting a man who had just taken her best curse apart with his fingers, and he'd pulled out a *gun*—

Her new shield came up. She put everything she had into it. A wall, thick as she could make it, shimmering in the rain between her and the barrel.

He moved the gun down six inches, and shot the ground.

He shot the moss about two feet in front of her boots. That was where the barrel was pointing when it went off. She saw the flash. She heard the bang, a flat hard crack, small, like a door slammed in another room.

The ground came up.

She never saw it hit. There was no time to see anything. There was the crack of the shot and then, a hundredth of a second after it, *under* it, a second sound that was the whole world turning into a fist. The moss in front of her went up in a column. Moss and dirt and rock and a fountain of black mud, straight up, and with it a wall of force so huge and so sudden it felt like being hit by the ground itself. Like the whole ridge had picked itself up and swung at her.

It hit her shield. Her shield held.

It held, and that was the problem, because the shield was attached to her, and the shield went backward, and she went with it.

She went twenty feet through the air. She knew it was twenty feet afterward because she saw the furrow. She went backward off the edge of the slab and through the mist and through a stand of sword ferns as tall as she was, and into a nurse log, a big fallen cedar furred over with moss and saplings, and hit it across the back with a sound she heard from inside her own body. The air went out of her lungs all at once. Her wand went out of her hand. She saw it go, spinning end over end, up into the rain.

Then she was on her back in the ferns and the moss with her ears ringing like church bells and the sky going around above her, gray, gray, gray, and she couldn't breathe.

---

She lay there for a while. It might have been five seconds. It might have been thirty.

When she could see again, the first thing she saw was the hole.

It was right where she'd been standing, or two feet in front of it, a crater six feet across in the moss and the rock, smoking. Black mud thrown out of it in a ring. Little stones still rolling down its sides. The smell of it came to her through the ferns, sharp and hot and strange: scorched earth, wet rock, and something else, an electric smell, like the air in the cenote before the water came.

One bullet.

He hadn't even aimed it at her.

She lifted her head. It hurt. Everything hurt. Her back felt like somebody had taken a baseball bat to it, and there was blood in her mouth where she'd bitten her cheek, and her ears were ringing so loud she couldn't hear the rain. She got up on one elbow in the ferns and looked across the twenty feet of torn-up ground, at him.

She looked at him. Properly. For the first time.

He was standing on the edge of the slab where he'd been standing the whole time. He hadn't moved his feet. A tall man, broad in the shoulders, black hair cut short and soaked flat. Thirty, maybe; younger, possibly. A face she'd have walked past in an airport. The dark glasses were back on. The gun hung loose in his left hand, pointing at the ground.

In his right hand he had her wand.

He'd caught it. Out of the air, while it was spinning. She hadn't seen him do it. He was holding it by the middle, lightly, the way you'd hold a pencil you'd picked up off somebody's desk, and turning it a little, looking at it.

Behind him, the vampire still floated at hip height, perfectly level, perfectly still. Its eyes had stopped rolling. They were fixed on him now. On the back of his head.

Beside him, the two wolves were still pressed flat to the granite. Seth had stopped whining. He'd gone very still, his head turned toward her, his eyes on her, his sides heaving. The gray one, Leah, was still snarling. A long, low, continuous sound, like an engine.

The man looked at the mesquite wand in his hand for another second. Then he looked across the crater at her.

"Right," he said.

He didn't raise his voice. The ringing in her ears was so loud she shouldn't have been able to hear him, and she heard every word as clearly as if he'd said them in her ear.

"Now that we've all got that out of our systems," he said, "d'you think we might let words lead this conversation for a bit? Instead of spells and teeth?"

---

He came to her across the crater.

He walked over it, on nothing, six feet of smoking air, the way you'd walk across a floor, and stepped off the other side into the ferns and crouched down on his heels about four feet from her. Out of reach. He'd done this before, she thought; he knew exactly how far away to be. He put the gun back under his coat. He kept the wand.

Behind him, the wolves were still down. The vampire still floated.

"You all right?" he said.

She stared at him.

"Nothing broken. I checked on the way over." He tipped his head. "Bruised to buggery, I should think. You'll want some Essence of Murtlap on that back tonight, if you've got any, and if you haven't you'll want a hot bath and about six aspirin. You'll be fine. You'll be sore for a week."

"Who," said Layla. Her voice came out in a croak. She coughed, and tasted blood, and tried again. "Who the hell are you."

"Nobody you need to worry about," he said. "Long as we get on."

"What *are* you?"

"English." He smiled very slightly. "I'd have thought that was obvious."

She looked at him. She tried to do it the way she'd look at a doorframe. Find the seams. Find the shape of the thing.

English accent, but that meant nothing; she'd worked with a Bulgarian hit-wizard who sounded like he'd grown up in Kent. A badge, Seth had seen a badge, she'd felt it go through him in the bead before she came; an American badge, a federal one. A suit. A Muggle gun, which no wizard she'd ever met would carry, not MACUSA, not the Confederation, not the Aurors of any country she'd heard of, not the freelancers or the mercenaries or the hit-wizards for hire in Rio and Marseilles. Wandless. Silent. Apparition without a sound. A vampire caught in some kind of ward she'd never seen. He'd known what Murtlap was. He'd known what a Disjunction was the moment he touched it.

Every seam she found went nowhere. It was like reading a curse with every glyph scratched off.

"You're not MACUSA," she said.

"Am I not?"

"MACUSA would've arrested me."

"Maybe I'm on my day off."

"You're not American."

"Well spotted." He turned her wand over in his fingers. "Layla, isn't it? Layla Quinn. Maeve's granddaughter. Out on Calawah Way. The blue house with the bees."

Her whole body went cold.

"Your boy over there's very loud," he said, and jerked his head back at Seth, pinned on the slab. "I don't blame him. He was frightened. Frightened people think about the thing they'd most like to protect, every single time, it's practically a law of nature. And he thought about you." He paused. "And about the rain. The bee yard. Lovely bit of work, that, by the way, holding the whole yard like that. Not easy. And he thought about the house up the hill, and the family in it, and a living room full of little blue lights. And a man in an armchair, with a cup of coffee, and a badge of his own." He looked at her over the dark glasses. "You told the police chief, Layla."

She didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say.

"You told a houseful of vampires, which, I'll be honest, I'm not even sure is illegal; I'm not sure anybody's ever thought to write it down. You told a pack of shapeshifters, which is the sort of thing that'll get you a very long letter. And you told a Muggle police chief, in his own town, in front of witnesses." He let that sit. "That's Clause Seventy-Three. That's a MACUSA felony, top of the list. That's the International Confederation sending a team, and every one of those people having a little bit of their head scooped out with a spoon, and you spending the next fifteen years in a cell somewhere deep under New York finding out what MACUSA does to witches who can't keep their mouths shut. That's what that is."

The rain came down through the ferns. Her back throbbed.

"I know," Layla said.

"I know you know. You thought about it. You decided to do it anyway." He looked at her for a moment, and something in his face that she couldn't read moved and settled. "Can't say I'm sorry you did, as it goes. I didn't know about the bee yard. I'd never have known. Now I do." He turned the wand over again. "So here's how this is going to go."

She waited.

"I'm not going to tell anybody," he said. "Not MACUSA. Not the Confederation. Not anybody. Your little show-and-tell stays between you and me and the walls of my head. The chief keeps his memories. The family up the hill keeps theirs. Balto over there keeps his, which I imagine is a relief for both of you, given what's in them."

Layla felt her face go hot even through the cold and the pain.

"And in return," he said, "one day, I'm going to ring you. Could be next week. Could be ten years from now. And I'm going to ask you to do something for me. And you're going to say yes."

"What kind of something?"

"The kind you'll be good at." He shrugged. "Curse-breaking, probably. I've got a feeling I'm going to need a curse-breaker who knows her way round this coast. Maybe something else. I don't know yet. I'll know when I ring."

"And if I say no?"

He looked at her through the dark glasses.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. He let the silence go on for exactly long enough for her to think it all the way through herself, every bit of it: the crater, the wolves pinned flat, the vampire hanging in the air, the Disjunction coming back down its own line and taking her shield apart like wet paper. What he knew. What he could do with it.

"I'm not going to say no," Layla said.

"No," he agreed. "I didn't think you were." He held her wand out to her. Handle first. "Here."

She stared at it.

"Go on. It's yours." He waggled it a little. "Thunderbird feather, isn't it? Mesquite? Lovely. Bit jumpy. It's been trying to hex me since I picked it up." He held it out a little further. "Don't point it at me again, there's a good girl. I'd hate to have to take it twice."

She took it.

It held the heat of his hand. The Thunderbird feather inside it was shivering like a struck wire, the way it did when it had been very frightened, and when her fingers closed on it, it went quiet against her palm the way a scared dog goes quiet when its owner comes home.

He stood up. He looked down at her, there in the ferns, for a second longer.

"You're good," he said. "For what it's worth. You're very good. That last sequence, with the roots, that would've had most people I know." He paused. "You just picked the wrong day."

Then he turned his back on her, and walked back across the crater on nothing, toward the slab where the wolves were pinned.

He didn't do anything that she saw. He didn't wave his hand, or say a word, or look at them. But Seth came up off the granite like a spring let go, all at once, scrambling, his claws skidding on the wet rock, and he didn't even look at the man in the suit. He went straight across the slab and over the edge of the crater, the long way round, tearing up moss, crashing through the ferns, and she heard him coming toward her, and she let her head go back on the moss and closed her eyes.

*He's okay,* she thought. *He's okay. He's okay.*

That was when she heard the man in the suit say, in a completely different voice, in the voice of a man who'd just put his foot through a floor he'd thought was solid:

"*What* the *fuck*."

---

### X. Leah

*The ridge — the same moment*

The weight came off her all at once.

One second she was flat on the granite with something pressing down on every inch of her, like being buried under wet sand, so heavy she couldn't lift her head more than an inch off the rock no matter how hard she fought, and she'd fought. She'd fought the whole time. She'd fought until her shoulders burned and her claws were split. And the next second it was gone, and the air was just air, and she came up off the rock so fast her legs nearly went out from under her.

Seth was already gone. She felt him go in the pack mind before she saw him, a sandy streak going over the lip of the crater and down the other side and into the ferns, nothing in his head at all but *Layla Layla Layla is she okay is she breathing Layla*. She didn't stop him. She didn't try.

She turned around to face the man in the suit.

He was walking back across the crater toward her. On nothing. On air, six feet over a smoking hole in the ground, with his hands in his coat pockets, like a guy crossing a parking lot. He'd put the glasses back on. He'd put the gun away. Behind him, still floating at the height of his hip, the vampire hung in the air like a plank on sawhorses.

He stepped off the air onto the edge of the slab, about ten feet from her.

She went low. Every hair on her body was standing up. The growl came back into her chest on its own, deep and long and vibrating, and she felt her lips come back off her teeth, all the way back, until the cold rain hit her gums.

He'd hurt Layla. He'd put her brother on his belly on a rock and held him there. He'd walked into her woods in a suit and taken her kill and called her Lassie and gone through Seth's head like a burglar going through a drawer. And she'd been held down the whole time, helpless, with her face in the moss, and she was not going to be held down again. Not by him. Not by anybody. Not ever.

*Come on,* she thought at him. Hard. Right at him. *Come on. Try it now. Try it now that I can move.*

He stopped. He looked at her.

He tilted his head a little. And then, slowly, he took the dark glasses off again.

She didn't know why he did it. Later, she'd ask him, and he'd tell her, because by then he couldn't not tell her, and what he'd say was: *I wanted a proper look at you. You were the only thing on that ridge I couldn't work out.* But right then she didn't know anything. She just saw him take the glasses off and fold them and put them in the inside pocket of his coat, without looking away from her, and she saw his eyes.

Green. That green. The bottle-in-the-sun green.

He looked at her. Past the size of her, past the teeth and the fur standing up along her spine, into her eyes, the way people only looked at each other, and she looked back at him because she was too angry to look anywhere else.

And the world let go of her.

---

She'd watched it happen to six people.

She'd watched it from the inside, every time. She knew what it looked like. She knew the words they used for it. Gravity. Cables. The earth moving. She'd heard Sam think it a thousand times, Sam's memory of Emily on the porch at Makah, running in the back of the pack mind every night since the spring like a song on a jukebox somebody had kicked so it wouldn't stop. She'd heard Jacob. She'd heard Seth, seven weeks ago, in Newton's, with the rain jackets. She'd thought she knew it.

She hadn't known anything.

It went the way they all said it went. Every cable holding her to the ground, cut. All of them, all at once, so fast she didn't even hear them snap. Her mom. Seth. The reservation. The sea. Her own name. The pack. Jacob. The bitter, grinding hate she'd been carrying around in her chest like a stone for two years. Gone. All of it. She was floating, for one long weightless second, in nothing, high above the ridge and the trees and the rain, with nothing holding her anywhere.

And then she felt one cable that didn't go.

It was the oldest one. She'd stopped noticing it was there. It had been there so long it had grown into her, like a wire fence grows into a tree. It ran out of the middle of her chest and away to the southwest, to the reservation, to a little house with a porch and a yard full of flowers that a woman with scars down one side of her face kept watered. It ran to Sam.

It had been there since she was sixteen. Since before the wolf. Since a boy had walked her home from a bonfire on First Beach and kissed her at the end of the dirt road behind her mom's house, and she'd gone inside and stood in the dark of her bedroom with her back against the door and known that she was going to marry him. Two years of being his. Then the night he disappeared, and came back changed, and wouldn't tell her why. Then Emily. Then the wolf, and finding out why, finding out everything, in one screaming night, with her father dying of a heart attack in the next room. And every single day since then, in the pack mind, Sam's memory of Emily on the porch, and under it, Sam's guilt about her, about Leah, a thick sticky brown sludge of it he couldn't hide, every single day since the spring, so that she couldn't stop loving him and couldn't stop hating him and couldn't, no matter how fast she ran, get the wire out of her chest.

It was the only cable left. For one second it held her, the way it always had. It held her up there in the empty sky, the last thing tying her to anything.

And then she felt it go slack.

It let go. It went loose, like a rope when the person on the other end finally puts it down, and it slid out of her chest, slow, the whole length of it, four years of it, unwinding out of the tree it had grown into. She felt every inch of it go. And it didn't hurt. That was the thing that made her want to scream. It didn't hurt at all. It was the first thing about Sam Uley in two years that hadn't.

And then she landed.

She landed on the ridge, in the rain, on four feet, on the wet gray granite, with every hair on her body standing up, and she was looking into a pair of green eyes, and there was a man standing ten feet away from her in a long black coat with the rain on his shoulders, and he was the only thing in the whole world that had any weight.

All of it. Everything she was. Hanging off one line, and the line went out of the middle of her chest across ten feet of rain into him.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Oh, no.*

*Oh, God. No. No, no, no. Not* him.

She knew his face. She'd been looking at it for five minutes. And she looked at it now and it was a different face, it was the only face, and she could see every single thing about it at once, and she wanted to tear his throat out and she wanted to put her head under his hand and she could not, could not, *could not* tell which. A straight hard face, unshaven, a couple of days of dark stubble along the jaw. Dark eyebrows, level. A nose that had been broken once and set a little crooked. A mouth that had been smirking at her this whole time, a wiseass mouth, and wasn't smirking now. It had come open a little. Like he'd been hit.

That was when she felt the line keep going.

---

It went into him.

It went into the center of his chest, a little to the left, the way Seth's had gone into Layla's, the way they all did. She'd watched the other end of it in six other heads. She knew where it was supposed to stop. It was supposed to stop there, in the other person's chest, and hold, like a hand on a shoulder.

It didn't stop.

It went into him and kept going. Down. Through him. Down and down, through the man in the coat like he was a doorway, into somewhere under him that was dark and huge and much, much deeper than a man had any right to be, the way the water at the bottom of the ocean is deeper than the ocean has any right to be. Down through locks. She felt them. Big ones. Three of them, one after another, heavy and cold and tight as iron, wrapped around something, holding something down. The line went through them like they weren't there.

And at the bottom, in the dark, something was lying down.

Something enormous. She felt the size of it before she felt anything else. She felt it the way you feel a mountain in the dark when you're standing at the bottom of it, by the way the air changes, the way the night gets heavier on one side. It was lying with its chin on its paws. It had been lying there a long time. It had been held down a long time, and it was patient, and it was very, very angry about it, the way an old dog is angry, quietly, with its eyes open.

And the line went down into the dark and touched it.

She felt it lift its head.

It looked up. Up the line. Up through the dark and the three iron locks and the man in the coat, up the whole length of the line and out of his chest and across ten feet of rain, straight into her. And she *knew* it. She knew what it was. She'd smelled it on him, faint, that ghost of a smell under the coffee and the pine, the place in the woods where a big animal had slept last night and gone. It was a wolf. It was a wolf the way she was a wolf. Bigger. Older. Darker. A wolf like the oldest stories, the stories from before Taha Aki, the stories the old men told at the bonfire when they'd had too much to drink and thought the kids had all gone home.

It looked at her. It took its time. It looked at all of her.

And then it took the line in its teeth, and *pulled*.

She felt it come back up. Out of the dark, up through the locks, which groaned, she felt them groan, out of his chest and across the rain and back into hers. Into her. Into the middle of her chest, and through it, and down. Down into the place in her where her own wolf lived, the gray one, the fast one, the one nobody had ever heard of in a thousand years of stories, who'd been running alone in the dark since the night she was born screaming in her mother's backyard. Down to her. And it tied itself there. Knotted itself round her wolf the way it was knotted round his.

Both ends. Both ways. Her to him. Him to her. Her wolf to his wolf and back again, a rope tied off at both ends and pulled so tight it hummed.

Her wolf lifted its head too.

It looked up the line, back the way it had come, back through her and across the rain and into him and down to the bottom of him where the big dark thing was still looking at her, and it looked at that big dark thing, and for the first time in its whole life, her wolf did something that it had never done for Sam, or for Jacob, or for anybody.

It wagged its tail.

---

The man in the coat took a step back.

He did it like he'd been shoved. One step, back, his boot scraping on the wet granite, and his hand came up out of his coat pocket and went flat against the middle of his chest, right where the line went in, and pressed there, hard, like a man who's just been shot and is trying to work out how bad.

His face had gone white. All of it. Under the stubble, under the rain, white as paper. And the green eyes were wide, and they were looking at her like she was the floor that had just gone out from under him.

"What the *fuck*," he said.

His voice cracked on it.

From the ferns at the bottom of the slope, Seth's head came up out of the pack mind like a firework. *LEE?*

He'd seen it. Of course he'd seen it. He was still phased, standing over Layla in the ferns with his nose pressed to her shoulder, and he'd felt it go through Leah, all of it, the cables, Sam's line going slack, the drop, the landing. The rope. The thing at the bottom of the man in the coat, lifting its head. All of it, in about a second. She felt it hit him like a car.

*Lee. Lee. Oh my God. Did you just—did he just—LEE, DID YOU JUST*—

*Shut up, Seth.*

*YOU IMPRINTED. You imprinted on* the guy*. On the* gun guy*! On the guy who—Lee, he shot Layla! He shot at Layla! With a* gun—

*I KNOW, SETH.*

The man in the coat was still staring at her. His hand was still flat on his chest. And she could feel his heart. She could *feel* it, through the line, through the rope that ran from her to him; that slow steady newspaper-reading heartbeat, the one that hadn't twitched with two wolves at his back. It was going like a train now. It was going as fast as hers.

He'd felt it. Every bit of it. She could tell. He'd felt the line go in and he'd felt it go down and he'd felt the thing at the bottom of him lift its head, and he'd felt the thing *pull*.

She watched him try to work it out. She could see him do it. His eyes went to her and away and back. They went to the line, the place in the air between them where there was nothing to see, and she knew, she *knew*, that he could almost see it. Whatever he was, he could almost see it. He looked down at his own chest under his hand, and back up at her.

"No," he said. Quietly. To himself, maybe. "No. Absolutely not. Not today. Not on a bloody *Monday*."

And underneath him, at the bottom of the rope, down in the dark, the big thing behind the three iron locks stood up.

She felt it stand. All four feet. She felt the locks strain.

So did he. She watched it go across his face. Something in his jaw went tight as a fist. His hand pressed harder against his chest, and his eyes shut, for half a second, and she felt him push down on it, through the rope, she felt him lean on the three locks with everything he had and force the big dark thing back down onto its belly.

It went. Slowly. Growling. It went down, but it didn't put its head down. It kept its head up. It kept its eyes on her.

He opened his eyes.

He looked at her for one second more. There was something in his face she didn't have a name for. There was fear in it, she thought; a lot of fear, the fear of a man who'd just found out the ground could move. And under the fear, something like the way Seth had looked in the parking lot outside Newton's, in his memory, with his hands on the steering wheel and the rain coming in on his leg, watching a pickup with New Mexico plates drive away.

Then he put out his left hand without looking, and the floating vampire slid through the air into it, and he tucked it back under his arm like a surfboard.

And he was gone.

There was hardly a sound. Layla's Apparition had split the air on the far side of the slab like a rifle shot. His made a small sound. The page of a book turning. A breath let out. The rain closed into the place where he'd been standing, and there was nothing on the gray granite but rain, and a scuff mark from his boot, and the smell of pine and coffee and blackberries, going thin in the wet air.

Leah stood on the ridge alone.

For one second.

One second was all she could stand. One second of standing there in the rain with the rope still in her chest, tied off at both ends, humming like a power line, going *out* of her, away from her, northwest, fast, stretching out across miles of forest she couldn't see, away and away and away from her, and not breaking. And every inch of her pulling after it.

*Lee,* Seth was saying. *Lee, wait. Don't. Don't go after him. Lee, he's dangerous, he's—you saw what he did, he could—*

*I know what he did.*

*Then don't—*

*I can't not,* Leah thought.

And it was true. It was the truest thing she'd ever thought in the pack mind, and Seth heard it, and she felt him hear it, and she felt him stop arguing. Because he knew. Of everybody in the world, he was the one who knew. He'd sat in his mom's car outside Newton's Olympic Outfitters for nine minutes with the door open and the rain on his leg.

*Okay,* Seth thought, very small. *Okay. Just—be careful. Please.*

*Look after Layla.*

*I am.*

She was already running.

---

She didn't need a trail. There wasn't one. He'd left nothing on the ground, no scent going anywhere, no tracks; he'd just stepped out of the world, the way Layla did. It didn't matter. She had the rope.

She ran by feel.

She ran with her eyes half closed, the way you'd walk down a hallway in your own house in the dark, one hand on the wall. The rope went out of her chest and away to the northwest, and she followed it, and it was the easiest thing she'd ever done. Off the ridge. Down the long wet slope through the hemlocks toward the Bogachiel. Across the river where it ran shallow and loud over gravel, the water so cold it burned. Up the other side. Northwest, always northwest, through country she knew and then country she didn't, the big old trees getting bigger, the moss getting deeper, the light going greener and dimmer under the canopy.

She didn't think. She couldn't. There was nothing in her head but the rope and the running and, under both of them, very far down, her wolf. Running too. Running with her for the first time in her life like they were the same animal, like there was no seam between them at all. Running the way she'd always wanted to run: toward something.

Behind her, a long way behind, she felt Seth in the pack mind. He'd stopped thinking at her. He'd phased back, she thought, to hold Layla, to talk to her, to make sure she was okay. He was a small bright worried hum, getting smaller.

And then, far away to the west, another voice came on, the way a light comes on in a dark house.

*What happened?* Jacob thought. *Seth howled. I heard it from the house. Where are you guys? Why is Leah—*

He saw.

He saw it all in about a second, the way you did. The ridge. The rope of light, the vampire. The man in the suit. Layla, firing. The crater. And then the green eyes, and the cables cut, and Sam's line going slack, and the rope going down into the dark and the big thing at the bottom of it lifting its head—

She felt Jacob go completely silent. The whole pack mind, silent. Like a room when somebody's dropped a glass.

Then: *Leah?*

She didn't answer. She was crossing the Calawah. The north fork. She knew it because she'd been here once, on a hunt last winter, and there was a big split boulder in the middle of the river that looked like a broken heart. She went past it. The water was up to her chest. She came out the other side and went up the bank, up and up, into a stand of Sitka spruce so big and so old that their trunks were like the walls of buildings, into dim green silence, and the rope was pulling very hard now, very close, and she could feel—

Something tried to turn her around.

It came up out of the ground at the edge of the spruce stand like a smell, like a sound too low to hear. *You don't want to go up there.* It was in her head, the way the man's voice had been in her head. *You've forgotten something. You left the stove on. You have somewhere else to be. Turn around. Go home. There's nothing up there. There's nothing up there at all.*

She almost did. Her feet almost did. For a second she stood at the edge of the old trees with the rain coming down and her head full of fog and thought, *what am I doing here?* and couldn't remember.

And then the rope pulled.

It pulled from the other end. Hard. Like somebody on the other side of a wall had grabbed hold of it and yanked. It went right through the fog in her head like a hot knife through wax, and the fog went to pieces, and she felt it, the thing that had been trying to turn her round, a whole huge woven net of it hanging in the air around the trees, she felt it *let go*. Open. Part. Like a curtain pulled back. Like a door opened from the inside by somebody who hadn't meant to open it at all.

She went through.

Up the last of the slope, through the biggest trees she'd ever seen, onto a flat bench of ground above the river, mossy and dim and dripping. And there in the middle of it, under the spruces, in the rain, with a banana slug the size of a hot dog stuck to the canvas by the door, there was a tent.

A little green tent. Army green, faded, the kind with a ridge pole. The kind two people could just about lie down in if they didn't mind being close. Moss growing on the guy lines.

Leah stopped in front of it with her chest heaving and her legs shaking and the river roaring in the gorge below.

The rope went straight in through the flap.

From somewhere inside the tiny green tent, a long way inside, much further inside than there was room for, she heard a man's voice say, very clearly, in an English accent, to nobody:

"Oh, you have *got* to be joking."

---

### XI. Seth

*The ridge — twenty minutes later*

Layla wouldn't stop shaking.

She said she was fine. She'd said it nine times. She was sitting on the nurse log she'd been thrown into, with Seth's flannel shirt around her shoulders, the one he'd had tied to his leg, which was soaked, which was useless, and his arm around her, which wasn't. He'd phased back the second he was sure she was breathing. He was sitting there in nothing but his last pair of cutoffs with the rain coming down on his bare back and steaming off it, holding her against his side as hard as he dared, and she was shaking so hard he could hear her teeth.

"It's adrenaline," she said. "It's just adrenaline. It happens. I'm fine."

"You got shot."

"He shot the *ground*, Seth."

"Layla, you flew like twenty feet."

"I know how far I flew, Seth. I was there." She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. Her wrists were sticky with something; when he'd first gotten to her he'd thought it was blood, and his heart had stopped, and then he'd smelled it. Honey. She'd been doing the honey when the bead cracked. She had honey to the elbows. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm—" She took her hands away from her face and looked at the crater, twenty feet off across the torn-up ferns, still smoking a little in the rain. "I'm trying to think."

He let her think. He held on.

In the back of his head, Jacob was coming. Jacob was a mile off and closing, running flat out from the Cullen place, and his whole mind was a roar. Most of it was Leah. *Leah, Leah, LEAH, answer me, where are you, what's happening, Leah*—and Leah wasn't answering. She hadn't answered since she'd started running. Seth could still feel her, faint, way off to the northwest, a gray streak moving through the trees with nothing in her head but the line, the rope, pulling, and he'd stopped trying to talk to her a while ago. You didn't talk to someone in the middle of that. He knew. He remembered.

Then, a few minutes ago, he'd felt her cross a river, and climb, and stop.

He'd seen it through her eyes. A stand of big trees. Bigger than anything he'd ever seen on the peninsula, even up the Hoh. A flat place above a river, all moss and green dark. A little green tent with moss on the ropes. A banana slug on the canvas by the door, huge, like a yellow hot dog. And from inside the little tent, from way too far inside it, the man's voice: *Oh, you have* got *to be joking.*

And then Leah had phased back, and she was gone. The pack mind went quiet where she'd been, the way it always did, like a door closing.

Seth had tried, right after, to remember where the tent was. Just to know. Just in case. He'd tried to picture the river she'd crossed, and the big trees, and which way she'd turned.

He couldn't. He could see the tent, clear as anything, the slug and the moss and the faded green canvas. But where it was kept sliding off, like a word on the tip of your tongue. He tried four times. Every time he got close, his head just went somewhere else, to Layla, to the crater, to what he was going to have for dinner.

He'd stopped trying after that. It was kind of creepy.

"He's not MACUSA," Layla said.

Seth looked at her. She was staring at the crater.

"I know who MACUSA sends," she said. "I've worked with them. On jobs. Enforcement, the Aurors, the hit-wizards. They're good. Some of them are really good. None of them are that." She shook her head. "And they don't carry guns. Nobody carries guns. Seth, nobody in my world would *touch* one of those. They think they're toys. They think they're—" She stopped. "He made it do *that*. With a toy."

"Maybe he's like a—some other government?"

"Maybe." She didn't sound like she believed it. "He had an American badge. You saw it. I felt you see it, when the bead went. And an English accent. And he knew what Murtlap is, and he knew what a Disjunction is, which is a thing I made, Seth, I *made* it, I've never shown it to anybody outside a crew, and he picked it up off his fingers like he was reading a menu." She laughed. It came out wrong, high and cracked. "He *complimented* it. He said it was clever."

"It was clever."

"He took it apart in a second and a half and used it to take my shield off." She put her face back in her hands. "And he wasn't even trying. You saw him. He wasn't even *trying*."

He'd seen. He'd been pinned to a rock with his face in the moss, unable to move, but he'd seen.

"I don't know who he is," Layla said into her hands. "I don't know what he is. I don't know who he works for, or if he works for anybody. I've been going over it and over it, and every time I think I've got hold of something it just—" She took her hands away. Made a little motion with her fingers, like smoke going away. "It's like trying to read a door where somebody's scratched off all the glyphs. On purpose. Carefully. A long time ago." She looked at Seth. "Do you understand? He doesn't leave anything. Nothing. I've seen a lot of people who were good at not leaving anything. Never anybody like that."

"He knows about you," Seth said quietly. "About all of us. Because of me. Because I thought about it."

"Seth—"

"He went in my head. I felt him. It was like fingers. And I couldn't stop thinking about you, and the Cullens, and Charlie, and I just—it all came out, and he took it." His throat was tight. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. It's my fault. All of it. He wouldn't know anything if I hadn't—"

"Hey." She turned and put her hand on his face. Her palm was sticky with honey. "Hey. Look at me."

He looked.

"You were scared," she said. "And you thought about the people you love. That's what people do. That's what everybody does. He even said so." Her mouth twisted. "And you bit the bead. You called me. That's what it was for. You did exactly what I asked you to do."

"And you got thrown into a log."

"And I got thrown into a log." She smiled, crooked, and her eyes were wet. "I've been thrown into worse. I've been thrown into a pit full of Mayan bone-snakes. A log's nothing."

"What's a bone-snake?"

"Later."

---

Jacob came out of the trees at the far end of the ridge at a dead run and didn't stop until he was standing over them, huge and russet and heaving, with his lips back and his nose going everywhere at once. The crater. The burned moss. The broken boulder. The scuff of a boot on the slab. Seth felt him take it all in through his nose, and then felt him find the scent, the man's scent, pine and coffee and stone and blackberries and, under it, so faint, the other thing.

Jacob's hackles went up from his ears to his tail.

Then he phased. Right there, in the rain, without even going behind a tree. He came out of it on his knees in the moss, dragging his own cutoffs up off his ankle and yanking them on, and he was already talking before he'd got them buttoned.

"Where is she."

"Northwest," Seth said. "Somewhere. I can't—"

"What do you mean you can't?"

"I mean I can't remember! I saw it, I saw the place through her eyes, a tent, but I can't remember where it is. It slides. I try to think about it and it slides. I think it's—" He looked at Layla.

"Wards," Layla said. Her voice was flat. "Concealment wards. Probably a whole stack of them. Unplottable, Muggle-repelling, notice-me-not, maybe a memory charm on the boundary. If they're good enough, they don't just hide a place. They hide it from the inside of your head." She pulled Seth's soaked shirt tighter around her. "His will be good enough."

Jacob stared at her. Then he turned and looked northwest, into the trees, into the rain, like he could see through twenty miles of forest if he just looked hard enough. His fists were shaking.

"She *imprinted* on him," he said. "On *that*. Seth, I saw it. In your head. She looked at him and—" He stopped. He pushed both hands back through his hair, hard. "And then there was this—there was this thing. In him. At the bottom. That rope went down into him and there was this *thing*, it looked up, it—"

"I know," Seth said. "I felt it."

"What *was* that?"

Seth didn't answer. He didn't know how.

Layla had gone very still under his arm. He felt her go still. He felt the shaking stop, all at once, like somebody had switched it off.

"What thing?" she said.

Seth looked down at her.

"Seth. What thing. What did you see?"

"When Lee—when it happened. The imprint. The line." He didn't know how to say it. "It went into him. Like it does. And then it kept going. Down. Really far. Through, like, these locks. Three of them. And at the bottom—"

"Show me," Layla said. "Can you show me? The way the little girl does?"

"I can't do that."

"Then tell me. Exactly."

"There was a wolf," Seth said.

The rain came down. Out across the ridge, the crater gave off one last thin thread of smoke and went out.

"In him," Seth said. "At the bottom. Behind the locks. Lying down. A big one, Layla. Bigger than Jake. Bigger than Sam. Black, I think, I couldn't see, it was dark, but I could feel how big it was. It was like standing next to a mountain. And it lifted its head and looked up the line at Lee and it—" He swallowed. "It pulled. It pulled the line back. Into her. It tied it. Both ends."

"Both ends," Layla repeated.

"Our imprints don't do that," Jacob said quietly. "Ever. They go one way. You hold on to them. They don't—it doesn't pull *back*."

Layla was quiet for a long moment.

"There are wizards," she said slowly, "who can turn into animals. Animagi. It's hard. It takes years. Most people who try get it wrong and get stuck halfway or worse. There's maybe a few dozen in America who are registered." She was looking at the slab, at the scuff mark where the man had stood. "But that's—that's a skill. A transformation. You *do* it, like a spell. What you're describing, he carries around inside him, behind three locks, like a—" She stopped.

"Like a what?" said Seth.

"Like something that's alive," Layla said. "Like something with a mind of its own. Animagi don't have that. They're just people in fur." She looked up at Jacob. "Your wolves. Are they separate? From you? Is there a—someone else? In there?"

Jacob looked at her for a long time.

"Sort of," he said finally. "Yeah. Sort of. It's us. But it's also kind of not us. It's old. It was here before. The stories say it was here before there were people. Taha Aki asked it to share, and it said yes, and it's been sharing ever since." He looked northwest again. "That's how ours works. I don't know how his does."

"Neither do I," said Layla. "I've never heard of anything like it."

"That's the second time today," said Seth, "that you've said that."

"I know," Layla said. "I'm counting."

---

They walked down off the ridge, in the end. Layla could have Apparated them; she said so. But she said she didn't want to, she said her head was still ringing and she'd probably splinch somebody, and Seth didn't know what that meant and didn't ask. So they walked. Jacob in front, as a wolf again, scouting. Seth behind, human, in his cutoffs, carrying Layla's wand for her because her hands were still shaking too much to hold it, and holding her hand with the other one.

She told Jacob, as they walked, about the deal.

Jacob didn't like it. Seth felt him not like it from fifty yards off, even through his ears instead of the pack mind. Jacob phased back again to argue about it, which took a while, because he had to stop and find a tree and then come back.

"He's blackmailing you," Jacob said.

"Yes."

"He's going to call you up one day and make you do something. Anything. And you can't say no."

"Yes."

"And you *agreed* to that?"

"Jacob," said Layla, "I was lying on my back in a fern with my ears bleeding and my wand in his hand. He had a vampire floating behind him like a pool float and the two of you pinned to the ground like butterflies. If he'd said *I'd like you to sign over your firstborn,* I'd have asked where to sign." She stopped walking. She turned around and looked up at him. Jacob was nearly as tall as Seth and a lot wider, and she looked up at him like he was a kid. "And he was right. That's the worst part. Everything he said was right. I told Charlie. I told all of you. I broke the biggest law we've got, and if anybody finds out, they'll come here and take it all back. Out of all of your heads. Charlie's. Bella's. Your little girl's." She held his eyes. "He's the only reason nobody's going to find out. Do you get that? He knows, and he's not telling. That makes him the most dangerous person in the world to us. And it also makes him the only thing standing between all of us and a team from New York."

Jacob opened his mouth. He shut it again.

"So yes," Layla said. "I agreed. And so would you."

She turned around and kept walking.

Jacob looked at Seth over her head. Seth shrugged at him. *She's right,* he wanted to say. *She's usually right. You get used to it.* But he couldn't, because they were both human, so he just shrugged again, and after a second, Jacob's shoulders came down.

"And Leah?" Jacob said quietly. To Seth. "What about Leah? She's out there. With him. In his—whatever. His tent. And we can't even remember where it is."

Seth thought about it. He thought about the rope. About the thing at the bottom of the man in the coat, lifting its head, looking up the line at his sister. About the way the man's face had gone white. *Not on a bloody Monday.* About the way his heart had been going, through the line, as fast as hers.

He thought about his sister's wolf, the gray one, the one who'd been running alone since the spring, wagging its tail.

"I think," Seth said slowly, "she's probably the safest person in the world right now."

Jacob looked at him.

"Think about it," Seth said. "He can't hurt her. If it's anything like ours, he *can't*. And it goes both ways. Jake, it goes both ways. He's stuck. He's as stuck as she is." He almost smiled. "I bet he's freaking out."

---

They came up the long drive to the Cullen house at a quarter past one, with the rain coming down harder.

Edward was already on the porch. Of course he was. He'd have heard them coming three miles out: Jacob's head, Seth's head, and Layla's, the one he'd said was like an organ, the one he'd said was the most interesting thing he'd heard in a century. He was standing at the top of the steps with his hands at his sides and his face completely still, and behind him, through the glass, Seth could see the others gathering in the long white room, one by one. Carlisle. Esme. Alice, her face pinched with the not-seeing. Bella, with Nessie on her hip.

Edward's eyes went to Seth. Then to Layla, with the honey drying on her arms and the shirt around her shoulders. Then back to Seth.

Seth didn't know what Edward could hear in his head right now. Probably everything. The ridge, the rope of light, the crater, the man in the suit. The rope going down. The wolf. Probably all of it, the way the pack would have, in about a second.

Edward's face didn't change. Edward's face never changed. But something in his eyes did. Something went very cold and very still in them, the way Layla's had gone still on the ridge, looking at the crater.

"Come inside," Edward said. "All of you."

Then he looked at Seth, and said, quietly, so only Seth could hear:

"Show me what it felt like. When he went into your head." He paused. "For a hundred and five years I've been the only one in this part of the world who could do that. I'd like to know what I'm dealing with."

Somewhere off to the northwest, twenty miles away, in a little green tent with moss on its guy lines that none of them could remember the way to, Seth's sister was standing in front of the man with the green eyes.

And for the first time since she'd phased, Seth realized, there was nothing he could do to help her, and he didn't think she needed it.

### XII. Harry

*The tent — 1:20 p.m.*

He'd put the specimen in the circle first. Habit. Whatever else was going on, you secured the asset.

He'd come into the hallway out of the turn with the thing still under his arm and his own heart going like he'd run up a mountain, and he'd walked it straight through the workshop and past the armoury and through the black oak door, and laid it down in the middle of the silver rings on the stone floor, on its back, rigid, still in the dead woman's red jacket. He'd stepped back over the outer ring and said the word, and felt the circle close, all four rings of it, like a lid coming down on a jar. The red eyes had followed him the whole time. He'd stood and looked down at it for a second and waited to feel something about it. Sixty-six days.

Nothing. He'd felt nothing about it at all.

He'd felt her.

That was the problem. He could feel her. He'd felt her the whole way through the turn, which shouldn't have been possible, because nothing came with you through Apparition but what you were holding. She'd come with him anyway. The line had come through the turn with him like he'd been holding it in his teeth. It was still there now. It ran out of the middle of his chest, a little left of centre, where his hand kept going, and went out through the wall of the specimen room and the wall of the tent and away southeast across the peninsula. And it was moving. The far end of it was moving. Getting closer. Fast.

And at the other end of it, the near end, down under his breastbone, under three locks he'd built himself and checked every morning for sixty-six days, the thing he kept down there was on its feet.

It had never stood up before. Not since September. Not once. It lifted its head when he came near, and looked at him, and put it down. That was the arrangement. That was how it had always worked.

It was standing. It was standing with its nose pointed southeast up the line, and it was not putting its head down, and when he leaned on the locks to make it, which he'd done twice now, it leaned back.

He went into the kitchen and stood at the range with both hands flat on the cold iron and breathed.

*Right,* he thought. *Right. Think.*

He tried to think about it the way he'd think about anything. A new effect. Unknown mechanism. Observe, describe, hypothesise. He'd been in the Department eight years. He'd stood in front of the Veil with a clipboard. He'd put his hand in the Time Room's bell jar on a dare from Croaker and come out the other side forty seconds younger and with a nosebleed. He'd been inside the head of a dead man in Lagos. He knew how to look at a thing he didn't understand.

So: he'd taken off his glasses to look at the grey wolf, because she was the one thing on that ridge he couldn't read. The boy, Balto, was an open book; the witch was a good witch, and frightened, and easy. The grey one had been something else. Her head was a closed fist, angry, all edges, and under the anger there'd been something he couldn't get hold of, like a smell on the wind, and he'd wanted a proper look.

He'd looked her in the eye.

And everything that held him up had been cut.

He'd felt it the way you feel a lift drop. Everything. His work. London. The war, which he carried round like a stone in his pocket and had for eight years. The Department. The name he'd been born with and the name he'd been using. All the things that told him who he was and where he stood, cut, all at once, and him just hanging there for one second in nothing.

And then he'd landed on her.

That was the only way to say it. He'd landed. Everything he was had come down on a line that ran out of his chest into hers, and the line had gone through her like she was a door, and down, into something under her: something grey and fast and lean and fierce, curled up alone in the dark like it had been curled up alone its whole life. And it had looked up.

And at the same time, the same second, something had come back the other way down the line, through his chest and his locks and down to the bottom of him, and touched the thing he kept down there. And the thing he kept down there had looked up the line at her and seen the grey thing under her, and it had taken the line in its teeth and pulled.

And tied it off. At her end. Round the grey thing. Both ends.

That was the part he couldn't stop going over. The boy's head had been full of it, earlier, when Harry had gone through it looking for the witch. *Imprinting.* He'd seen what the boy had seen, the boy's sister had seen, the whole pack's worth of seeing: gravity, cables, a man in a parking lot. He'd read it as data. A local phenomenon. Interesting. He'd made a mental note to write it up.

It went one way. That was the thing about it. Every memory of it in the boy's head had gone one way. The wolf held on. The other person was held.

This went both ways.

And he didn't know what it was. He didn't know what had just happened to him. And for the first time in about four years, that wasn't fun at all.

He took his hands off the range.

Then the wards moved.

He felt them before anything else. The outermost ring of them, the Muggle-repellers and the confusion layer, the stuff that sat in the air around the bench like a thick wet fog and turned people round and sent them home. Something had walked into it. Something big and fast and four-legged that he didn't need the lattice to know about, because the line in his chest was pointing straight at it.

The wards caught her. He felt them do it. They wrapped round her the way they'd been built to, heavy and soft, pushing her mind sideways. *Go home. There's nothing here. Turn round.* And he felt the line in his chest go slack, felt her stop, felt her start to turn.

And he pulled.

He didn't decide to. He'd think about that later, a lot. He didn't decide. Something in him took hold of the line and yanked it, hard, before he knew anything about it, the way your hand jerks out to catch a glass going off a table.

And his wards opened for her.

He felt them open. Every layer, one after the other, the confusion layer and the repellers and the notice-me-nots and the unplottable shell, which was his best work and which had taken him two days to lay and which nobody, *nobody*, was supposed to be able to walk through without his say-so. They parted for her like a curtain. Like a door. Like she lived here. Like she'd been coming in and out of this place for years and the wards knew her the way the front door knows your key.

He felt her come up through the trees and stop outside the tent. He felt her breathing. He felt her heart.

"Oh," said Harry Potter, out loud, to his empty kitchen, "you have *got* to be joking."

---

She was standing in the rain by the flap when he came out. A grey wolf the size of a horse, soaked to the skin, chest going like a bellows, every hair on her standing up, with Gary the slug about three feet from her left forepaw on the canvas.

She looked at him. He looked at her.

The line between them went tight as a wire.

He'd expected it to be less, out here. He'd thought maybe it was a proximity thing, something to do with the ridge, with the fight, with the adrenaline. He'd thought that if he got some distance, some walls, his own ground under him, it'd settle. It hadn't settled. It had got worse. Standing ten feet from her with nothing in between, it was like standing next to a fire. He could feel every inch of the space between them.

And under his ribs, the thing behind the locks pressed forward. Toward her. Toward the grey thing under her. Hard. Like a big dog at a door that's just heard its owner's car.

He leaned on the locks. They held. Barely.

"You followed me," he said.

Her lips came back off her teeth. In his head, without his even reaching for it, her voice came in, clear as a bell. He didn't have to try. He'd been in a lot of heads; you always had to try, a little. With her, he didn't. It was like she was standing in the room with him.

*No shit I followed you.*

"How did you get through my wards?"

*I don't know. You let me.*

"I didn't let you."

*You pulled. I felt you pull.* Her ears were flat. *Right through the—whatever it was. The fog. I was going to turn around, I'd forgotten what I was doing here, and you yanked on it, and it all just—went.*

He didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. She was right, and they both knew she was right, and she could probably feel through the line that he knew.

*What did you do to me?* she said.

"Nothing."

*Bullshit. What did you—*

"I didn't do anything to you, Leah." It came out of him before he could stop it, her name, the name he'd pulled out of her brother's head an hour ago and filed and not used. It came out like he'd been saying it for years. "I swear to God. I looked at you. That's all I did. I looked at you and it happened. I didn't do anything."

*It's called imprinting.*

"I know what it's called. I read it in your brother's head. Gravity and cables." He pressed the heel of his hand into his chest, where the line went in. "I thought he was being dramatic."

*He was,* she said. *He was also right.*

The rain came down on both of them.

"Can you change back?" he said. "I can hear you fine like that. But I'd—" He stopped. He found he didn't know how to finish it. "I'd rather see your face."

She stared at him for a long moment. Then she turned around and went behind the nearest spruce, a big one, eight feet through at the base. There was a sound like canvas tearing. Some rustling. A muttered string of words that would have made a docker blush.

He turned his back. It seemed like the thing to do. He stood there facing the tent, looking at Gary.

"Don't look at me like that," he said to Gary.

Gary didn't say anything.

"You can turn around," said a woman's voice behind him.

He turned around.

She was standing by the spruce in a pair of cutoff denim shorts and a black tank top, both soaked, barefoot in the moss. Tall. Five ten, maybe. Lean in a way that came from running, long arms, long legs. Dark copper skin, wet with rain. Black hair cut short, ragged, like she'd done it herself in a mirror with kitchen scissors. A narrow face, all angles, dark eyes, and a mouth that looked like it had been built for saying no to people. She was steaming, a little, the way the boy had steamed in the witch's memory, the rain coming off her shoulders as vapour.

She looked at him the way people looked at something they'd backed into in a car park.

And the line went *through* him.

He'd thought it was bad before. He'd thought standing ten feet from the wolf had been bad. Standing ten feet from her face was different. It was like somebody had picked up the line where it went into his chest and plucked it, a hard bright note, and the note went down through him and down through the locks and hit the thing at the bottom, and the thing at the bottom threw its weight against the locks so hard that he actually took a step. Forward. Toward her. Before he could stop himself.

He stopped himself. He stood very still.

She'd seen. She'd felt it. Her eyes had gone wide.

"What," she said. Low. "What was that."

"That," said Harry, "was the bit of me that's been locked in the cellar for two months. And would very much like to come up and say hello to the bit of you that just went behind that tree."

She looked at him.

"I've kept it on a short lead," he said. "Since September. Since before I got here. I knew there were wolves on this coast, and I knew they'd have noses, and I didn't want anybody smelling something they shouldn't on a bloke at the petrol station. So I locked it down. Three locks. My own work. I check them every morning." He breathed out. "I'm fairly sure that's done now. The lead. I don't think it's going back on."

"You're a wolf," Leah said.

"Sort of."

"What does *sort of* mean?"

"It means I'm not entirely sure what I am," he said. "It means it's complicated, and I've never told anybody about it, and I'd like to keep it that way." He paused. "And it means I'm a bit afraid of what happens if I let it out within half a mile of you right now, so I'd like to not do that today, if that's all right with you."

She stared at him.

He stared back.

He had not meant to say any of that. He'd meant to say *it's classified*. He'd *started* to say *it's classified*. He'd felt the words in his mouth, ready, two words, the easiest two words in the language, he'd said them ten thousand times. And what had come out instead was the truth. The whole of it. In order. Like somebody had reached into his mouth and turned the words round.

"Hang on," he said slowly.

Leah's eyes narrowed.

"Hang on," Harry said again. "Ask me something."

"What?"

"Ask me something. Anything. Ask me my name."

"What's your name?"

"Harry Evans," said Harry. Or tried to. He felt the words come up, the legend, the cover he'd worn for sixty-six days, the passport and the library card and the publisher in Bristol, *Harry Evans, freelance photographer, north London*. He felt them get to his teeth.

And stop.

They just stopped. Like his jaw had locked. He stood there in the rain with his mouth open and nothing coming out of it, and then, without any say-so from him at all, his mouth shut and opened again and said, quite clearly:

"Harry Potter."

The rain fell. In the gorge below them, the river roared.

"Bollocks," said Harry.

"Who's Harry Potter?"

"Me, apparently." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Christ. Christ almighty. Ask me something else."

"Where are you from?"

He tried for *Islington*, which was what it said on the library card. "A cupboard under the stairs in a house in Surrey," his mouth said instead. "Number four, Privet Drive. Little Whinging. I lived in it till I was eleven. The cupboard. Not the house. Well, both."

Leah's face did something complicated.

"Now you," he said. "Lie to me."

"What?"

"Lie. Say something that isn't true. Anything. Tell me you're fine."

She opened her mouth. "I'm—"

She stopped. He watched her try. He watched it get stuck in her the same way it had got stuck in him, right at the teeth. Her jaw worked. Her eyes went wide and furious.

"I'm *freaking out*," Leah Clearwater said, through her teeth, like it was being pulled out of her with pliers. "I'm freaking out, okay? I was in love with the same guy for four years and I couldn't get him out and an hour ago I looked at you and he just *went* and I don't even know who you are and I'm standing in the woods in my wet shorts and I don't know what's happening to me and I'm *freaking out*." She stopped. She clapped a hand over her own mouth. She stared at him over it.

"Yeah," said Harry. "Me too. Same here. The rest of it, anyway."

They stood there.

"We can't lie to each other," Leah said, from behind her hand.

"Doesn't look like it."

"Is that a—is that you? Did you do that? Some kind of wizard thing?"

"No. I don't think so. I think it's the—" He waved his hand at the space between them, at the line, at nothing. "I think it's that. The rope. Whatever it is. It's not letting either of us."

"That's not how it works," she said. "Imprinting. Sam lied to Emily all the time. About little stuff. Quil lies to Claire about where babies come from."

"Well, I don't think it's working how it works, love," said Harry. "I don't think either of us is what it's used to."

He took her inside, in the end, because she was shivering, and because he couldn't think of a single good reason not to, and because every time he tried to think of one, his mouth tried to tell her about it.

She stopped at the bottom of the three wooden steps and looked down the hallway. The slate floor. The pegs. The doors going away and away down both sides, much too far, into a distance that a two-man ridge tent didn't have.

"Holy shit," she said.

"Bigger on the inside. Yeah."

"How—"

"Expansion charms. Four of them, nested, with a stabiliser so they don't eat each other. Took me two years to get right. The first version collapsed and I lost a kitchen." He went into the kitchen, and after a second she followed him. "Tea? Coffee? Beer?"

"It's one-thirty in the afternoon."

"Is that a no?"

She looked at the fridge. It was an old Smeg, pale blue, and it hummed.

"Beer," said Leah.

He got two bottles out of the fridge and opened them with his thumb and gave her one. She drank about half of it in one go, standing by the range, and looked round the kitchen. The big table. The record player. The sleeve of *Physical Graffiti* propped against the wall.

"Okay," she said. "Who are you?"

He thought about it.

He thought about it properly, for perhaps five seconds, standing in his own kitchen with a beer in his hand, with the most wanted secret in his world sitting in his chest like a coal.

Every Unspeakable took the same oath. You took it your first day, in a little room on Level Nine with no windows and one chair, with your right hand flat on a stone that was black and very cold and older than the Ministry. You said the words. And the stone put something in you, something that went down your throat and sat at the top of your chest under your collarbone, and from then on, for the rest of your life, it was there. If you ever tried to tell anyone outside the Department what you did, or what the Department did, it closed your throat. Gently. Just enough. Just like a hand. And if you kept pushing, it closed harder. There were stories, in the Department, about people who'd kept pushing. They were told as jokes. Nobody laughed at them.

He'd felt it close once, in 2002, in a pub in Belfast, very drunk, starting to tell Fennick something he shouldn't. Just the beginning of it. Like a thumb on his windpipe. He'd stopped.

He'd been carrying it eight years. It was the reason his name was Unspeakable. It was the reason he'd never told anybody anything, ever, not Hermione, not Ron, not Ginny on the last night before she'd packed her things.

He waited for it.

"I'm an Unspeakable," he said. "In the Department of Mysteries. It's part of the British Ministry of Magic. We study things nobody else is allowed near. Time, death, the mind, space. Love, sort of, there's a room, it's locked. We take things apart to see how they work and then we work out what to do with them. Sometimes that's research and sometimes it's somebody's front door at four in the morning. I've been doing it eight years. I was sent here to catch a vampire, because there's a nest of them in Italy that's been eating tourists since the Romans, and in about a year or two we're going to burn it to the ground, and we need to know how."

He stopped.

He put his hand flat on the top of his chest, under his collarbone, where the oath lived.

Nothing.

No thumb. No hand. Nothing closing. It was there; he could feel it, the cold stone-weight of it, sitting where it always sat. It just hadn't done anything. It had sat there and listened to him tell a Quileute girl in cut-offs every single secret he'd sworn on a three-thousand-year-old rock to keep, and it hadn't so much as twitched.

"That should've choked me," he said.

Leah was staring at him.

"That should have—there's an oath. We take an oath. It's in here." He tapped his chest. "I've felt it go off. I've felt it go off for less than *half* of that. It should've had me on the floor." He stared at her. "It didn't do anything. It didn't even *notice*."

"Maybe it's broken."

"It's not broken. I can feel it. It's fine. It's sitting there like a—" He stopped.

He looked at her. At the line. He couldn't see it, but he could feel exactly where it went, from his chest into hers and down, and back.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, that's—"

"What?"

"It's keyed to *other people*," Harry said. "The oath. It's built to stop you telling anyone outside. Anyone who isn't you, basically, or the Department." He swallowed. "It doesn't think you're outside. It doesn't think you're anyone else. As far as the oath's concerned, I'm talking to myself."

Leah looked at him for a long moment.

"Is that bad?" she said.

He thought about it. He thought about it as an Unspeakable, which was the only way he knew how to think about anything. He thought about what London would do if they found out. A Quileute shapeshifter whose bond with an Unspeakable was strong enough to fool the Department's own oath. Strong enough to make the oldest binding in the building treat two people as one. He thought about the Department's interest. About the room on Level Nine with the brains in tanks. About a circle of silver on a stone floor.

"They'd want you," he said. "London. If they knew about this. They'd want to know how it works. They'd want to take it apart and see." He met her eyes. "They'd have you in a jar, Leah. Maybe a figurative one. Maybe."

"How do you know?"

"Because I'd want to," said Harry. "If you were anybody else's. I'd have you in my specimen room by teatime and I'd be taking notes."

She didn't say anything. She drank the rest of her beer.

"So they won't find out," he said. "I'm not going to tell them."

"Are you allowed to not tell them?"

"No," said Harry. "Not even slightly."

"So you'll be lying to your boss."

"Yeah."

"For me."

He opened his mouth to say *for operational reasons*. "For us," came out. Then, because apparently he couldn't stop, "And for me. Mostly for me, if I'm honest, and it turns out I am. I don't want anybody taking you apart. The thought of it makes me want to put my fist through that wall."

Leah put her empty bottle down on the range. Very carefully. The way her mother put things down, he thought, without knowing how he knew that, and then realised he knew it because it was in her head, a picture of a woman in a bathrobe with a coffee cup, coming down the line between them as easy as breathing.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. My turn." She folded her arms. She was still shivering a little. "Since we're doing this."

"You don't have to—"

"Sam Uley," she said. "Four years. Since I was sixteen. He was going to marry me. Then he turned into a wolf and he couldn't tell me why, and then he saw my cousin Emily on her porch and imprinted on her and that was that. And then I turned into a wolf, the first girl ever, nobody's ever heard of one, and my dad died the same night, of a heart attack, in the next room, because of me, maybe, because he saw me change, I don't know. And then I had to live in Sam's head. Every day. Every single day since the spring I had to hear him think about her. And feel bad about me. And I couldn't get him out of my chest. No matter how fast I ran." She stopped. Her voice hadn't shaken once. "And then I looked at you on that rock and he was gone. Just like that. For the first time in two years I can't feel him at all. And I don't know if I want to kill you for that or—" She stopped.

"Or?" said Harry.

She didn't finish it. He found he didn't need her to. It was right there in the line, the end of the sentence, bright and hot and furious, and it was the same as the end of his.

The record player was silent. The rain drummed on the canvas roof, a long way up.

"Right," Harry said, after a while. His voice wasn't entirely steady. He cleared his throat. "Well. That's—right."

"Yeah."

"I've got a vampire in my back room," he said.

Leah blinked.

"The one you chased. He's in a containment circle. I've been waiting two months for him. I've got about forty questions for him and I'd like to start asking them before anybody comes looking." He hesitated. "You're welcome to come. If you want. You flushed him. You've earned a seat."

"You're going to question him."

"Yeah."

"Like, *question* him. Like cops. Or like—"

"Not like cops," said Harry.

She looked at him for a long moment. He felt her look, through the line, felt her turn it over, felt her think about the smell of the woman's deodorant on the red jacket. He didn't try to hide what he was thinking. He couldn't have. He didn't want to.

"Are you going to kill him?" she said.

"Eventually. When I'm done."

"And until you're done?"

"Until I'm done," said Harry, "I'm going to do whatever I have to, to find out what I need to know. Some of it's not going to be pretty. Some of it you're not going to like." He paused. "I'm not going to dress it up. I couldn't if I tried, apparently."

Leah thought about it. He felt her think about it. He felt her think about the dead woman, and the forty-odd others she'd smelled on vampires since the spring on this coast, and the newborns in June, and Jacob's bones breaking in her head. He felt her think about him, standing on the ridge in his suit with his hands in his pockets while two wolves and a witch threw everything they had at him, looking bored.

"I want to watch," she said.

"Thought you might," said Harry.

He went to the door. Then he stopped, in the doorway, with his hand on the frame, because something had just occurred to him, and he turned round.

"One more thing," he said.

"What?"

"Stop calling me *the gun guy* in your head. It's undignified."

Leah stared at him. Then, for the first time since he'd seen her, a corner of her mouth went up. Barely. It was there and gone.

"Stop calling me Lassie," she said.

"No," said Harry, and went down the hall.

Behind him, after a second, he heard her bare feet on the slate, following.

### XIII. Leah

*The specimen room — 1:50 p.m.*

The hallway went on for a long time.

She followed him down it barefoot on the cold slate, past door after door. A bathroom with a claw-foot tub. A little room with a narrow bed, made, tight, like a soldier's. A library, floor-to-ceiling books, a ladder on a rail, a fire going in a grate under a mantel with nothing on it but a photograph she didn't get a look at. He didn't stop to explain any of it and she didn't ask. She was too busy being in his head.

That was the thing. She hadn't expected that. She'd expected the rope, the pull, the way her whole body was pointed at him like a compass needle. She'd heard about that part her whole life. She hadn't expected to be able to *feel* him. Something underneath his thoughts. His mood. The shape of him. Walking down this hallway, he felt like a man walking into a job he'd been waiting for: focused, sharp, clean, a little hungry. And under that, way down, the thing at the bottom, behind its locks. Pacing. Walking up and down. Every time she got a step closer to him in the hallway, it stopped and turned its head.

Her own wolf was doing the same thing. She could feel it, curled up under her ribs, its head up, its ears forward. Watching his.

The hallway opened into a workshop. A big one, bigger than her mom's whole house, with a skylight full of rain and two long benches covered in tools she didn't recognize and a lathe and, in the middle of the floor, a motorcycle.

It was huge. Old, black and chrome, with a sidecar, the kind of thing you saw in old war movies. It gleamed like somebody had polished it that morning.

"That yours?" she said.

"My godfather's." He didn't slow down. "He lent it to a friend of his, and the friend crashed it in 1997 with me in the sidecar, and gave me what was left. Took me two years to rebuild." A pause. "It flies."

"Of course it does," Leah said.

"Don't touch anything," he said, without looking back.

"Wasn't going to."

"You were going to touch the lever-action."

She had been. It was hanging right by the door, long and brown and old-fashioned, like something out of a Western. She'd been going to run her hand down the stock.

"Get out of my head," she said.

"Can't," said Harry. "You get out of mine."

She tried. She did actually try, for about a second, the way you'd try to stop hearing a song. It didn't work. He was right there in the middle of her, like a stone in her shoe. Like a heartbeat.

"Yeah," he said. "That's what I thought."

At the end of the workshop there was a door. Old black wood, a church door, with iron bands across it and a big iron ring for a handle. He put his palm flat on it and said a word she didn't catch, a short soft word that made her ears ring, and something inside the door went *clunk*, heavy, like a vault.

He looked back at her, with his hand on the iron ring.

"Last chance," he said. "You can wait in the kitchen. There's more beer."

She looked at him.

He opened the door.

---

It was round. That was the first thing. A round room with a stone floor and a high dark ceiling, and every wall, all the way around, from the floor to as high as she could see, was shelves. And on every shelf there were jars.

Hundreds of them. Big and small, glass, sealed with wax and wire and something that shimmered. Some of them held liquid, brown, green, clear, and things floating in it. Some of them held things that weren't floating. That were moving. She saw a hand, gray and swollen, its fingers curling and uncurling slowly against the glass. She saw a skull with too many teeth. She saw a coil of black hair that looked wet even though it was dry. She saw something flat and black pressed between two sheets of glass on a high shelf, like a dead leaf the size of a bedsheet, and when she looked at it, it was looking at her, though it didn't have anything to look with, and she took a step back without meaning to.

"Don't look at that one," Harry said. "Not for long. It's asleep. Mostly."

"What is it?"

"Lethifold. A sort of living shroud. It comes into your room at night and smothers you in your bed and digests you on the spot, and leaves nothing. No body, no trace. Your family thinks you ran off." He was walking round the edge of the room. Checking seals, she realized. Touching each jar as he passed it. "Got that one in Papua New Guinea. It had eaten four people in a village outside Lae. The fifth was a little boy. He'd heard about Patronuses from his gran and he thought he'd try it, with a stick, standing on his bed. Didn't work. But he was making enough noise that I heard him from the road."

"You saved him?"

"I was in the area." He moved on to the next shelf.

She could feel through the rope that there was more to it. Something like *I'd been hunting it for three weeks*. And something like *he was eight*. But she didn't push.

On a shelf at about head height, under three separate seals, there was a tooth.

It was a fang, really. Long as her forearm. Curved, yellow-white at the tip and darker yellow at the root, the root of it still ragged, like it had been pulled out of something by force. It lay on a velvet cushion behind the glass. Even through the jar it had a smell, faint, bitter and wrong, a smell that made the back of her tongue go numb.

"What's that?"

"A tooth." He'd stopped. He was looking at it too.

"From *what*?"

"A snake," said Harry. "A big one. Lived under my school. Ate people. Turned them to stone, if they looked at it, or killed them, if they looked right in its eyes." He was quiet for a second. "I killed it when I was twelve."

Leah looked at him.

"With a sword," he said. "It bit me. Right here." He touched the inside of his right forearm, below the elbow. There was nothing there, she saw. No scar. Smooth skin. "I should've died. A bird cried on it. Long story." He looked back at the fang. "Its venom's the worst thing I know of. Kills most things. Destroys some things that can't be killed. Eats through just about anything. I went back down for that tooth when I was nineteen, alone, at night, and I've been saving it for something special for seven years."

He looked away from the shelf, toward the middle of the room.

"I think," he said, "it might be his birthday."

---

The vampire was lying in the middle of the floor.

There was a circle cut into the stone there, ten feet across, filled in with silver that shone, and inside that another circle, and another, and another, and inside those a pattern so complicated it hurt her eyes to look at, lines and curves and symbols all wound into each other like the inside of a watch. The vampire lay in the middle of the pattern on its back. Exactly the way it had hung on the ridge: rigid, arms out, fingers spread, its long black hair fanned out on the stone under its head. Still in the red jacket. The dead woman's jacket.

Its eyes were open, and they were on her.

Leah's lips came back off her teeth on their own.

"Easy," Harry said quietly. "He can't get out. He can't even twitch. That circle's built to hold something three times his size." He pulled a chair out from against the wall. A plain wooden kitchen chair. He set it down about six feet back from the outermost silver ring. "Sit there. Don't cross the silver. It's not dangerous for you, but if you cross it with your feet it'll think you're trying to get *in*, and it'll get excited, and I'll have to calm it down."

She sat. The chair was cold. She leaned forward with her elbows on her bare knees and looked at the vampire, and the vampire looked back at her, and the stink of it filled up the room, the burnt sugar and bleach and flowers, and under it the copper and salt of the dead woman, and something in her chest went hard and still and cold.

Harry stood at the edge of the outer ring with his hands in his pockets. He'd taken his long coat off and hung it on the back of the door. He was in the suit, the gray one, with the white shirt open at the neck and the gun in its holster under his left arm. He looked down at the vampire.

"Afternoon," he said. "Sorry about the wait. I had a guest."

The vampire's eyes moved to him.

"I'm going to let you talk now," Harry said. "Just from the neck up. If you scream, I'll put it back. If you try anything clever, I'll put it back. If you bore me, I'll put it back. Nod if you understand." A pause. "Sorry. Habit. You can't nod. Blink twice."

The vampire's eyes went still. Then, slowly, they closed, and opened. Closed. Opened.

"Lovely," said Harry.

He didn't do anything that Leah could see. But something in the room changed. A pressure she hadn't noticed till it shifted, like the moment your ears pop on the road up to Hurricane Ridge. And the vampire's face moved.

It was terrible to watch. It had been a statue for an hour and a half, and now the face came alive all at once on top of a body that was still a statue. The jaw worked. The lips peeled back. The muscles of its neck stood out like cables. Its tongue came out and wet its lips, dark and quick as a lizard's. And then it breathed in, a long hiss, and turned its head an inch on the stone to look at Harry full on.

"You," it said.

Its voice was low and smooth and accented, some kind of European she didn't know. Pretty. It made her skin crawl.

"Me," Harry agreed.

"What are you?"

"Busy. Let's start with you. What's your name?"

The vampire smiled. It had very white teeth.

"Do you know," it said softly, "whose friend I am?"

"Yes," said Harry.

That was all. He said it flat, bored, like a man answering a waiter. And Leah watched the vampire's face, and she saw the smile flicker.

"Then you know what will happen to you," it said, "when he hears what you have done."

"He's not going to hear."

"He will hear. He hears everything. He will send—"

"Name," said Harry. "Last time I ask."

The vampire looked at him for a long moment. Then it laughed. It was a soft, lovely, musical sound, and it bounced off the jars, and somewhere on a high shelf something in a jar shifted in its liquid as if it didn't like it.

"No," said the vampire.

"All right," said Harry. "Have it your way."

And he went in.

---

She felt him go.

She hadn't expected that either. She felt it through the rope like feeling a door open in another part of the house: a draft, a change in the pressure, and then he wasn't all the way *here* anymore. Most of him was. His body stood at the edge of the silver with his hands in his pockets and his eyes open, fixed on the vampire's eyes, not blinking. But part of him, the part she could feel at the other end of the rope, had gone somewhere else.

Into the vampire.

She'd felt it from the inside, on the ridge, when he went through Seth's head. A hand in a filing cabinet. Fast and sure. That was what she'd expected. This was different. This was like—

It was like watching a man dive into a river in flood.

She felt him hit it. She felt the cold of it come back up the rope at her, a deep black flat freezing cold, the cold of the bottom of a lake in winter. And fast. So fast. The vampire's mind went past him like a river at full spate, like a freight train, three hundred years of it, faces and rooms and streets and blood and blood and blood, all of it going past at once, a hundred miles an hour, so fast she couldn't make any of it out, just a smear, a roar.

And Harry stood in the middle of it, and let it go past him, and reached in, and took things out of it.

She felt him do it. One. Another. Another. Quick and exact, like a man picking fish out of a fast stream with his bare hands. She didn't know what he was taking. She felt each one come back up the rope at her, faint, a flash, gone. A city in the snow, with domes. A woman screaming in a language she didn't know. A room with a red marble floor and three chairs at the end of it, and in the middle chair, an old man with skin like onionskin paper, holding out his hand.

On the floor of the circle, the vampire had stopped smiling.

Its mouth was open. Its eyes were wide, stretched wide, the red rolling, and a long, high, thin sound was coming out of it, like air escaping. Its jaw was working, working, like it was trying to get words out and couldn't. It had felt him come in. It knew he was in there. And it couldn't do anything about it at all.

Harry blinked.

Just once. And he was back. All the way, she felt him come back up the rope, the cold going out of it like water draining from a sink.

He took his right hand out of his pocket and rubbed his eyes with his thumb and finger.

"Casimir," he said. "Casimir Wolski. Born outside Lublin, 1709. Turned in Kraków, 1731, by a woman he met in a church. Nomad since 1760. Never stays anywhere more than a year. Likes cities. Likes opera. Likes them young, if he can get them, and if he can't, he isn't fussy." He lowered his hand. "He's been to Volterra four times. He owes the old man a favor from the eighteen-eighties that I didn't quite catch, something to do with a woman in Vienna. And in October, the tracker came and found him in Vancouver, where he'd been eating junkies down by the docks, and told him the old man wanted a favor back."

The vampire, Casimir, stared up at him from the floor with its mouth still open.

"Fast," Harry said to Leah, without looking away from it. "Their heads. Faster than ours. Much. Like reading a book with somebody else turning the pages at about a hundred miles an hour. Got most of it, though."

"What was the favor?" said Leah. Her voice came out hoarse.

"To come here," said Harry, "quietly. And find out whether Carlisle's pet human had been changed yet. That's how the old man put it. *Carlisle's pet.*" His voice was very flat. "Because they made her a promise, last year, in Italy. They said she'd be changed or she'd be dead, and they said they'd check. And one of the old men, the blond one, the one with the face like a bad smell, has been nagging the other one about it ever since. So the old man thought he'd send someone nobody would think twice about. A nomad passing through. Have a look. Then ring Volterra and tell them. Then come back to Italy in the spring so the old man could take his hand and *see*."

"Bella," Leah said.

"Bella," said Harry.

"And he was going to tell them she's changed? That's it?"

Harry didn't answer right away.

She felt him go still, through the rope. Felt him look at something he'd taken out of the vampire's head, and turn it over, and not like it.

"No," he said. "That's not it."

He crouched down at the edge of the outer silver ring, on his heels, with his forearms on his knees, and looked at the vampire on the floor from about eight feet away. When he spoke again it was to the vampire.

"Tell her what you smelled," he said. "Last night. At about four in the morning. When you came down off the ridge on the north side and stood in the trees behind the big white house."

Casimir's lips peeled back.

"Tell her," said Harry, "or I'll go back in and get it myself, and this time I won't be gentle on the way out."

"A heartbeat," Casimir said.

Leah's whole body went cold.

"A heartbeat," the vampire said again. Softly. It was smiling again now. It had found something to smile about. "In the house of the cold ones. Fast. Like a bird's. And a smell. Blood and not-blood. Warm and cold, at once." Its red eyes slid to Leah. "Do you know what it is, dog? I do not. I have lived three hundred years and I have never smelled such a thing. I stood in those trees for an hour, breathing it." Its smile widened. "Aro will want to know. Aro will be *very* interested to know."

Leah was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. She was standing at the edge of the silver with her fists shaking and her whole body shaking and the wolf in her chest on its feet and screaming, and she could feel the change coming up her spine like a wave, *now, now, now*—

"Leah."

She looked at Harry.

He hadn't moved. He was still crouched at the edge of the circle, on his heels, his eyes on the vampire.

"He's not going to tell anybody," Harry said. "I promise you. Sit down."

She sat down.

She didn't want to. Her legs did it anyway. She sat back down on the cold wooden chair with her fists on her knees and the shaking running up and down her arms, and she breathed, and the wave went back down her spine. Slowly. It went.

She looked at him. She felt him. Through the rope, she could feel what he was thinking about the vampire on the floor, and it was something much colder than anger, and much quieter, and it frightened her, a little, and she found she didn't mind being frightened by it at all.

"That's the kid," Harry said to her quietly. "Isn't it. The little one. With the curls. Rides around on the big ginger wolf's back."

"Jacob's," said Leah.

"I've seen her on my map for two months. Never been able to work out what she is." He looked at the vampire. "I don't need to now. I just need to make sure *he* never gets to ask."

---

He did it in order. She'd give him that. He did everything in order, like he had a list in his head and was going down it, and she realized after a while that he did have a list. Forty questions. He'd said so in the kitchen.

He started with spells.

He did them from where he stood, at the edge of the circle, with no wand and no words, the way he'd done everything on the ridge. She only knew what they were because he told her. He said the name of each one out loud before he did it, flat and clear, the way her mom read the ingredients off a recipe card, and she understood after the second one that he was saying them for a record. For himself. For later.

"Stunner," he said, and a bolt of red light the size of a fist went out from somewhere around his right hand and hit the vampire in the chest.

It flattened. That was the only word for it. It hit the vampire's chest and spread out across it, like a water balloon hitting a wall, a splash of red light that ran out across the red jacket and the stone and was gone. The vampire didn't so much as blink.

"Stunner, no effect," Harry said. "Dispersed on contact. Surface. Like the shell's eating it." He tipped his head. "Double strength." Another one. The same. "No effect. Right. Tell Kingsley his lot can stop practising those."

"Who's Kingsley?" said Leah.

"Bloke I know," said Harry. "Body-Bind."

Nothing. A shimmer of light, gone.

"Body-Bind, no effect. Disarming, pointless, he hasn't got anything. Severing Charm." A flash. A hiss, like a knife across glass. A thin white scratch appeared across the vampire's cheek, very faint, and then, while Leah watched, it closed. Smoothed over. Was gone. "Severing, surface damage only, healed in—" a pause, "—four seconds. Christ. Right." He went on. Fire, a little lick of it, blue, that he let touch the vampire's hand for exactly one second before he put it out; the skin under it went dark, like a stone in a campfire, and the vampire hissed through its teeth, the first sound it had made since it had stopped laughing. "Fire. Works. As advertised." He wrote nothing down. She could feel him filing every bit of it somewhere neat and cold behind his eyes.

Then he stopped. And stood there. And she felt him decide something.

"Right," he said quietly. "Now the ones London's actually paying for."

He didn't explain. He didn't have to. She felt it through the rope before he did it, felt him reach for something that was different from all the others, something heavy and dark and cold that he kept in a separate place, the way her dad had kept his hunting rifle in a separate locked cabinet away from the other guns in the garage.

He looked at the vampire on the floor.

"*Crucio,*" he said.

It was the first time she'd heard him actually say a spell. The word came out of him quiet and level.

The vampire screamed.

It screamed with its whole face, the only part of it that could move. Its mouth went wide. Its eyes went wide. The sound that came out of it was high and huge and terrible, bouncing round and round the room off the hundreds of jars until it seemed to come from everywhere at once, and on the high shelf the Lethifold rippled behind its glass like something waking. And its body, the body that couldn't move, did. Just a little. Just a tremor, a fine fast shudder running through it from end to end like a struck bell, against the hold of the circle, which was holding it so tight that a little shudder was all it could manage.

Leah gripped the edges of the chair.

Three seconds. She counted them. Then Harry let go.

The scream stopped. It cut off, like a tape. The vampire lay on the stone with its mouth open, its chest still, its eyes staring at the ceiling. A long hissing sound came out of it.

"Cruciatus," Harry said, in the same flat recipe-card voice as before. "Effective. Fully. Response indistinguishable from human or more pronounced." He was quiet for a second. "So they feel pain. Proper pain. That's—" He stopped. "That's useful."

"Isn't that—" Leah heard herself say. Her voice came out strange. "Isn't there a law? Seth said Layla told him. There's curses you're not allowed to—"

"Three of them," said Harry. He didn't look round. "Life in prison for using any of them on a person. For everybody else." He watched the vampire on the floor. "Where I work, we're licensed. All of us. Always have been. Nobody upstairs likes to talk about it."

Leah sat very still on her chair.

She'd killed vampires. More than she could count. She'd torn them apart with her teeth and listened to them scream while she did it, and watched their pieces burn, and been glad. She'd stood on the field in June with the newborns coming out of the trees, and she'd gone for one of them alone just to hear it die. She wasn't squeamish. She'd never been squeamish. She'd thought, on the ridge, that she'd tear this one apart herself, and she'd wanted to.

She looked at Harry's face.

He was looking down at the vampire with a face as blank as a man reading a gauge. She looked at it, and then she looked down the rope at what was behind it, because she could, because he couldn't stop her, and found something small and tired and very far back. Like a light left on in a room down a long hall. He didn't feel guilty; she could tell that. Whatever it was had nothing to do with the thing on the floor. It was about himself. Something like *I used to mind this more*.

"Does that bother you?" she said.

He didn't look at her.

"No," he said. And then, because he couldn't stop: "Bothers me sometimes that it doesn't. Not today, though." He nodded down at the vampire. "I saw the woman. In his head. Her name was Dana. Dana Whitcomb. Thirty-four. She was a dental hygienist from Olympia, she was up the Hoh on her own because her boyfriend had cancelled on her, and he found her at about six o'clock yesterday evening by the river. She'd stopped to take a photo of the light on the water. He let her take it." Harry's voice didn't change at all. "And then he made it last. Because he was bored. Because he'd been in Vancouver a month eating junkies and he wanted something *nice*." He was quiet for a second. "And she was the forty-first this year. I counted. While I was in there. So, no, Leah. It doesn't bother me today."

Leah didn't say anything.

She thought about the deodorant smell on the jacket. She thought about the woman stopping to take a picture of the light.

"Okay," she said.

---

He did the next one without saying the word out loud. She felt it anyway, through the rope, felt the shape of it, the cold heavy thing coming out of its locked cabinet a second time.

This time the vampire didn't scream.

Its face went slack. All the fury went out of it, all at once, like a light switched off. Its jaw loosened. Its eyes, the red eyes, went soft and wide and blank and very, very calm, like a cow's in a field, and it lay there looking up at the ceiling of the specimen room with an expression of complete, empty peace.

And Harry staggered.

Only a little. Half a step. She felt it through the rope, a huge jolt, like a man who's grabbed the reins of a horse and the horse has bolted. She felt him dig in. She felt him *hold*. The vampire's face twitched, once, twice, something coming up from underneath the blankness, fighting, and she felt Harry lean on it, the way he'd leaned on the locks in his own chest, and the something went back down.

"Imperius," he said. His voice was rough. "Holds. But Christ, it fights." He let his breath out slowly. "It's like trying to put a bridle on a racehorse at full gallop. That's the speed again. Their minds go too fast. You have to hang on." He pressed his hand flat against his chest a second, then dropped it. "Holds, though. Holds. That's the main thing."

He crouched down at the edge of the circle again.

"Casimir," he said. "Where's your phone?"

"In my pocket," said the vampire, dreamily. "The left one."

Harry held out his hand. Something came up out of the left-hand pocket of the red jacket, a little gray cell phone, a cheap one, the kind you got at the gas station, and floated across the silver rings into his palm. He flipped it open. He looked at it for a moment.

"And the number?"

"The only number in it."

"Good lad." He looked at Leah. "I've got a lining in the walls of this place that lets Muggle stuff work. Took me a year. Never thought I'd use it for this." He held the little phone out over the circle. "Here's what's going to happen. He's going to ring Volterra. He's going to tell them that Carlisle's pet has been changed. Golden eyes coming in. That the family's the same as ever, that there's nothing to report. Nothing. And he's going to tell them he's off south for the winter and he'll be in Italy in the spring like a good boy."

"And they'll believe him?"

"They'd better."

"And in the spring? When he doesn't show up?"

"Then he's a nomad who didn't show up." Harry shrugged. "That happens. They can't keep track of everybody." He paused. "And by the spring, if London has its way, there won't be anybody in Volterra left to notice."

He looked down at the phone in his hand. Then he looked at the vampire.

"Casimir," he said. "You're going to say exactly what I tell you, when I tell you. You're going to sound bored, and a bit tired, and a bit annoyed about the rain. You're not going to say anything else. Do you understand?"

"Yes," said Casimir. He sounded happy about it.

Harry pressed the green button.

He held the phone a few inches over the vampire's face. Leah could hear it from her chair. The long faraway hiss of the line. A click. A ring, a strange double ring, foreign. Another. Another.

Then a woman's voice. Young, warm, pleasant, Italian.

"*Pronto.*"

Leah felt Harry go still through the rope. Felt him know that voice.

"Gianna," said Casimir from the floor, in his low lovely accented voice, bored, a little tired. "It is Casimir. Put me through to the master."

"*Ah, signor Wolski! Un momento.*"

Music. Hold music. Violins. Leah stared at the phone, at the vampire on the floor smiling vacantly up at it, at Harry crouched over it with his face like stone, and she thought, *this is insane, this is completely insane*, and she held her breath.

The music stopped.

"*Casimir.*"

A man's voice. Soft. Delighted. Light, almost breathless, almost childlike. A voice like a kindly old uncle on Christmas morning, opening a present. And something under it so old and so cold that the hair stood up on Leah's arms, all the way to her shoulders.

"*My dear old friend. How very good to hear your voice. You've been to see our Carlisle?*"

"I have," said Casimir.

"*And?*"

She felt Harry lean on the vampire's mind through the rope. Felt the words go down, one at a time, like coins into a slot.

"The girl is changed," Casimir said. "She is one of us. Since the end of summer. Her eyes are already going gold, the way his are, the animal way. The family are as they always were. Dull. The same. Nothing to report."

"*Ah. Ah, that is a relief. Caius will be so* disappointed.*" A soft, silvery laugh. "*And nothing else, my friend? Nothing at all of interest, in all that rain?*"

Leah stopped breathing.

On the floor, under the blank calm, something in the vampire's face *moved*. A flicker. A twitch at the corner of the mouth. Something coming up. She felt it through the rope as clearly as if it was happening in her own skull: the racehorse at a gallop, the reins sliding, Casimir's own mind, the real one, under the Imperius, three hundred years of it, *surging*, scrabbling, *the heartbeat, the heartbeat, tell him about the heartbeat—*

She felt Harry *hold*.

His face didn't change. His hand didn't move. But she felt him do it, through the rope, she felt it like a weight coming down: he put everything he had on the reins. All of it. She felt the thing at the bottom of him lift its head at the strain and she felt him not even notice it. And the flicker in Casimir's face went out.

"Dogs," Casimir said. Bored. "The dogs still run there. The Quileute dogs. They chased me out of the woods. Big, stupid things. Tiresome."

"*Ah, the wolves.*" The old voice was amused. "*How tiresome indeed. Carlisle does keep the oddest company.*" A pause. "*You sound tired, my friend.*"

Leah's nails dug into the wooden seat of the chair.

"Rain," said Casimir. He laughed, a short, tired, irritable laugh, and it sounded so real that Leah's stomach turned over. "Three months of rain. It is in my hair. It is in my clothes. You would sound tired too, Aro. I go south. Mexico. Somewhere with sun I can hide from, at least."

The old man laughed again. It went on for a while.

"*Go, then. Go and be warm. And come to see me in the spring, Casimir, won't you? I would so like to see you. I would so like to* see.*"

"In the spring," said Casimir.

"*In the spring.*" The voice was smiling. "*Thank you, old friend. You have done me a kindness. I shan't forget it.*"

Click.

Harry flipped the phone shut.

He stayed crouched there for a long moment, with the little gray phone in his hand, looking at nothing. Then he let his breath out, slowly, all of it, and Leah felt the weight come off the reins, felt the Imperius ease, felt the racehorse slow to a walk. On the floor, the vampire's face stayed blank and peaceful.

Leah realized she was shaking again. She made herself stop.

"That was him," she said. "That was the old one. Aro."

"That was him."

"He sounds—" She didn't know how to finish it. "He sounds *nice*."

"Yeah," said Harry. "They usually do." He stood up. He put the phone in his pocket. "I've been in the head of a girl who's sat outside his door while he ate forty Dutch tourists. Nice is what he does instead of a face."

He looked down at the vampire one more time.

"Casimir," he said. "If the tracker wanted to find you. Right now. Could he?"

"Demetri tasted me in Volterra," Casimir said dreamily. "In 1888. He could find me anywhere in the world."

"Here?"

The vampire's blank face creased, very slightly. A frown. Puzzled, like a child given a sum it couldn't do.

"I don't know," it said. "I can't feel anything. Here. I can't feel the world. It's like being in a box."

Harry looked at the walls of his tent, at the shelves and the jars and the black oak door. Leah felt something go through him that felt, through the rope, a lot like satisfaction.

"Good," he said quietly. "That's good. That's a data point."

---

He did the tooth last.

He took it down off its shelf under its three seals, and broke them one by one with his hand flat on the glass, and lifted the long yellow fang out of its jar with a pair of heavy leather gauntlets that came up to his elbows. The bitter smell filled the room. Leah's tongue went numb. On the high shelf the Lethifold went flat and still behind its glass, like something playing dead.

He knelt at the very edge of the silver. He did something to the circle, a word under his breath, and a gap opened in the rings, a little door about a foot wide, just big enough for a hand.

"Hold his arm still," he said to nobody, and the vampire's right arm, flung out on the stone, went somehow stiller.

He reached through the gap with the fang and touched the very tip of it to the end of the vampire's little finger.

It went through like a hot wire through butter. No sound. No resistance. The tip of the fang went in at the first joint and out the other side, and the end of the vampire's finger came away and rolled a half inch on the stone.

The vampire, under the Imperius, under the blank calm, screamed.

It was worse than the Cruciatus. Leah would think about it later and decide that it was worse because it came up from underneath the calm, from somewhere the Imperius couldn't hold, a sound from the bottom of three hundred years. Its face didn't change. Its face stayed blank and peaceful while the scream came out of it.

And the stump of its finger went black.

Black like rot. Like a bruise spreading. She watched it creep up from the cut, slow and steady, a dark stain under the hard pale skin, a quarter inch, half an inch, toward the knuckle—

Harry put his gauntleted hand flat on the vampire's wrist, and said something sharp and short in a language she didn't know, and the black stopped. Just stopped. Like a line drawn across the finger.

The scream stopped too.

Harry knelt there for a second. Then he reached through the gap with a pair of long silver tongs and picked up the end of the finger and dropped it into an empty jar, and sealed it.

The finger moved in the jar.

It crawled. Like a grub. Slowly, an eighth of an inch at a time, it pulled itself across the bottom of the jar toward the side nearest the vampire's hand, toward the rest of itself, trying to get back. Leah had seen that before; she'd seen it on the field in June, a hand that kept crawling across the grass after its owner was burning. They always tried to go back.

Except this one didn't go far. The cut end, where the fang had touched, was black, and as Leah watched it pressed itself against the glass, and stopped. And stayed stopped. It didn't twitch again.

And on the vampire's hand, the stump stayed exactly as it was. The scratch on its cheek had healed in four seconds. This stayed: a black, dead end on a little white finger.

Harry looked at it for a long time.

"Basilisk venom," he said, finally. Very quietly. "Doesn't heal. Doesn't reattach. Spreads if you don't stop it." He let out a long breath. "That's the first bit of real good news London's had in six months."

He sat back on his heels, and pulled off the gauntlets, and Leah felt him put the whole of it away, neat and cold and filed, behind his eyes. Every bit of it. She felt the thing at the bottom of him settle, too. It had been watching all of it. Calm. Patient. Like something big and old lying in the long grass, watching a herd.

She didn't know what she felt about any of it. She sat on the cold wooden chair with her hands between her knees and tried to find it.

"You protected the kid," she said.

He looked over at her.

"On the phone," Leah said. "You could've—you didn't have to. You could've let him say it. Let them come. What do you care?"

"Protecting an asset," said Harry. "The family's useful. London marked them useful. If the Italians come over here for a look and see something they don't like and burn the place down, I've lost—"

He stopped. She watched him try to stop. She felt him, through the rope, try to stop the rest of the sentence from coming, and fail.

"And the kid," he said. "A bit." He looked away from her, at the wall of jars. "I saw her through the Omnioculars, in October. On the big ginger one's back. Holding on to his fur with both hands. She was laughing." His jaw worked. "I'm not having that old bastard put his hand on her. Not on my watch. Not ever."

Leah looked at him for a long moment.

"His name's Jacob," she said. "The big ginger one."

"Is it."

"He's going to hate you."

"Most people do, eventually," said Harry. "I'm told it's part of my charm."

He stood up. He looked down at the vampire, still lying blank and calm in the middle of the circle with its ruined finger, and he did something with his hand, a little closing motion, and she felt the circle tighten again, all four rings, like a lid screwing down. The vampire's face went still. Statue-still. The face of something in a jar.

"What happens to him now?" she said.

"Now he waits," said Harry. "I've got another thirty-one questions. Some of them want doing with a clear head. And London'll want things measured that I haven't got the kit for out here." He paused. "A few weeks. Maybe a month."

"And then?"

"And then he burns."

Leah looked at the vampire. At the red jacket. At the place on the cuff where the dead woman's blood had dried.

"I want to do it," she said. "When it's time. I want to be the one."

Harry looked at her.

She felt him think about it, through the rope. Felt him think about the deal, about London, about procedure. Felt all of that go past him and away like leaves on a river.

"Yeah," he said. "All right. You can do it."

He held the door open for her, and she got up off the chair and walked past him, out of the round room full of jars, and he closed the black oak door behind them and put his palm flat on it until it went *clunk*.

In the workshop, under the skylight, with the rain drumming and the big black motorcycle gleaming between them, they both stopped.

They looked at each other.

And at the bottom of each of them, at the two ends of the rope, the two wolves lifted their heads.

### XIV. Harry

*The tent — 5:40 p.m.*

The mirror lived in the hall, on a nail by the telephone, in a plain black frame about the size of a paperback. It looked like something you'd buy in a pound shop to check your teeth in. The back of it said *PROPERTY OF H.M. MINISTRY OF MAGIC, LEVEL 9 — DO NOT REMOVE* in letters so small you needed the loupe, and under that, scratched into the wood by somebody who'd been issued it before him, *abandon hope*.

He took it down at twenty to six, which was twenty to two in the morning in London, because Croaker didn't sleep, and as far as anybody on Level Nine could tell never had.

Leah was in the kitchen. He could feel her there through the line, the way you could feel the sun on one side of your face with your eyes shut. She'd eaten. He'd made her a fry-up, the second of his day, and she'd sat at the big table that nobody else had ever eaten at and put away six eggs and most of a loaf without looking up, and he'd stood at the range and watched her do it and felt something in his chest he didn't have a word for. She was at the table still. She had the sleeve of *Physical Graffiti* in her hands and was reading the back of it with a frown.

He'd told her she didn't have to listen. She'd said she wasn't going to. They'd both known that was rubbish.

He held the mirror up and said, "Croaker."

His own face went away. The glass went grey, and then dark, and then it was a room. A small room, wood-panelled, lit by one green-shaded lamp, and in the middle of it, behind a desk piled with paper in drifts like snow, an old man in grey robes. Bald as an egg. A long face, deeply lined, with a mouth like a crack in a wall and pale eyes the colour of tap water. He was writing. He didn't look up.

"Potter," said Saul Croaker.

"Sir."

"It's two in the morning."

"You're up, sir."

"I'm always up. That isn't the point." He finished a line. He put his pen down. He looked up out of the mirror at Harry with the tap-water eyes. "Well?"

"Specimen acquired," Harry said.

Croaker was quiet for a moment. Nothing moved in his face. Nothing ever did. But Harry had worked under him for eight years, and he saw the old man's hand, lying flat on the desk beside the pen, go very slightly still.

"Condition?"

"Intact. Minus a fingertip." Harry leaned against the wall. "Male. Three hundred years old, give or take. Polish. Fed on humans; forty-one this year that I counted. Snared him at eleven-forty this morning on the ridge above the Bogachiel. He's in the circle. He's holding."

"And?"

"And I've done the first pass." He gave it to him. All of it, in order, the way he'd said it out loud in the specimen room for the record. Stunners: no effect. Body-Bind: no effect. Severing: surface only, four-second heal. Fire: works. Cruciatus: works, fully. Imperius: holds, but fights like a bastard, and you need to be strong to keep it on, and he'd recommend nobody below Grade Three try it on one in the field. Legilimency: possible. Fast. Recommend the same. And the tooth.

He told Croaker about the tooth.

Croaker listened to all of it without a word. When Harry got to the basilisk venom, to the finger in the jar that had crawled half an inch and stopped, to the stump that didn't heal, the old man closed his eyes. Just for a second. Then he opened them again.

"Doesn't heal," he said.

"Doesn't heal. Doesn't reattach. Spreads, if you don't stop it. I stopped it at the knuckle."

"How much of that tooth have you got left?"

"Plenty for me. Not enough for a war." Harry paused. "There's a lot more where it came from. If we want it. There's forty feet of snake in a cave under Hogwarts that nobody's been near since 1999. Somebody'd have to talk to McGonagall."

"I'll talk to McGonagall," said Croaker. Something in his voice had changed, very slightly. Harry knew what it was. The old man had been carrying the Volterra file around for seven months, the way Harry had been carrying the war around for eight years, and for the first time he'd just been handed something to put on the other side of the scale.

"Anything else?" said Croaker.

Harry looked down the hallway toward the kitchen. He could see the edge of the doorway. He could feel her on the other side of it, at the table, completely still, the album sleeve forgotten in her hands. Listening.

He thought about the ridge. The witch and her bee yard and her Disjunction, and a deal he'd made on his own account that London didn't know about and wasn't going to. He thought about a heartbeat in a white house in the woods, fast as a bird's, and a little girl on a wolf's back, laughing. He thought about a phone call to Volterra that the Department would have wanted to approve, and plan, and argue about for three weeks in a room with no windows, and that he'd made off his own bat in about four minutes because the alternative had been letting a three-hundred-year-old killer tell Aro about the child.

He thought about a rope that went down to the bottom of him and back, tied off at both ends. And an oath that didn't think she was anybody else.

He thought about the room on Level Nine with the brains in the tanks.

"No, sir," said Harry. "Nothing else."

Croaker looked at him out of the mirror for a long moment.

Harry had been looked at by a lot of people. Dumbledore, who could make you feel like he'd read your diary. Voldemort, through the inside of your own skull. The old man in the middle chair in Volterra, through Gianna's memories, smiling. Croaker's look was just very, very patient. Like a man waiting for a kettle he knew would boil eventually.

"You sound odd, Potter," Croaker said.

"It's the rain, sir," said Harry. "Three months of it. It gets in your head."

"Hm." Croaker picked his pen back up. "Full report by Friday."

"Yes, sir."

"Including the tooth."

"Yes, sir."

"And Potter." The old man had gone back to his writing. He didn't look up. "The Americans have been making noises. Their Seattle field office. Somebody there has noticed unusual magical activity on the Olympic Peninsula over the last two months, and has been asking questions about it in places where questions get overheard. Nothing specific yet. Nothing with your name on it. But they're looking." The pen scratched. "You are not there. Remember that."

"I'm not there, sir."

"Good," said Croaker, and the mirror went dark.

Harry stood in the hall for a while with the mirror in his hand, looking at his own face in it.

Then he hung it back on its nail, and went into the kitchen.

---

She was sitting at the table where he'd left her, with the album sleeve in her hands. She didn't look up when he came in.

"You lied to him," she said.

"Yeah."

"He's your boss."

"He's my boss."

"And you're going to keep doing it."

He pulled out the chair across from her and sat down in it. He put his elbows on the table.

"Looks like it," he said.

She put the album sleeve down. She looked at him across the table. He'd thought, on the ridge, that her face was built for saying no to people. It was. He could see now that it was built for a lot of other things too, and that most of them hadn't been used in a long time.

"What did he mean," she said, "about the Americans?"

"He meant the witch's fight on the ridge this afternoon was the loudest thing anybody's done on this peninsula since I got here, and I didn't get a chance to clean it up." He rubbed his face. "My own stuff's fine. I've got sinks on everything. Hers was out in the open. A Blasting Curse and a Disjunction and an Apparition from fourteen miles off, all in one spot, all in about thirty seconds. If somebody in Seattle's got a sensor anywhere near here, that'll have lit it up like Bonfire Night."

"So they'll come."

"They might come."

"And find her. Layla. And find out what she told us."

"They might." He met her eyes. "Which is why I'm going to go and clean up that ridge tonight. After you've gone. And I'm going to make it look like a lightning strike, because it's been a stormy week, and I'm going to go and have a look at what MACUSA's got in Seattle. Quietly." He paused. "And if anybody from New York ever does come looking for Layla Quinn, they'll find a curse-breaker living quietly with her bees who's never told anybody anything, and a police chief with a perfectly normal memory, and a houseful of very boring doctors. And they'll go home."

"Because of the deal."

"Because of the deal."

"Because you need her. For whatever."

"Because I need her," Harry agreed. "And because it's your brother's girl, and you'd never forgive me." He didn't bother trying to stop that one. He was starting to get used to it. "And because I liked her Disjunction. That's a lovely bit of work. I'd hate to see her in a cell for the next fifteen years when she could be making me more of those."

Leah looked at him across the table for a long time.

"You're a really weird guy," she said.

"So I've been told."

"Like, really weird."

"I'm aware, Lassie."

Her mouth went up at the corner again. Further, this time. Almost all the way.

---

It was full dark when she went. Half past six, the rain easing off to a drizzle, the spruces dripping, the river loud in the gorge. He walked her out. She stood in front of the tent on the bench in her damp cut-offs and her tank top, barefoot in the moss, steaming a little in the cold, and looked off into the black between the trees, southeast, toward home.

Gary was on the canvas by the flap. Harry didn't introduce them. It seemed like a lot, for one day.

"I have to change," she said. "To run home. It's twenty miles."

"I know."

"And when I change—"

"Your brother hears everything. And Jacob. And they see it all. Me. My name. This place. The circle. London." He'd been thinking about it since the kitchen, since the oath had sat in his chest and not done anything. He'd been thinking about it the way he'd think about a bomb. "Everything you know, they'll know. In about a second."

"Yeah."

"And then the Cullen boy reads it out of their heads."

"Yeah."

"Right," said Harry. "Well. We'd better find out, then."

She looked at him. "Find out what?"

"What happens." He shoved his hands into his pockets. "I don't know. I've got no idea, Leah. I've been doing this eight years, and I've got no idea at all, and I'm not going to pretend I have. Maybe it's fine. Maybe the oath doesn't care what's in your head, because it doesn't think it's your head. Maybe I'm about to be on the front page of every magical newspaper on the West Coast by Thursday. Maybe I'll have to go and Obliviate a whole pack of shapeshifters, which I imagine is a nightmare." He breathed out. "Only one way to know."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded.

She turned to go behind the spruce.

"Leah."

She stopped.

He hadn't meant to say anything. It just came out, the way everything had been coming out since one o'clock. He found he didn't mind. He found, standing in the dripping dark with the river roaring under them, that it was the best thing that had happened to him in about eight years, being unable to say anything to somebody but the truth. Like putting down a pack he'd been carrying uphill so long he'd forgotten it was on his back.

"Before you go," he said. "The—bit of me. In the cellar." He made a vague motion at his chest. "It'd very much like to see you. The other you. Properly. If you don't mind. Just for a second."

She went very still.

Then she went behind the spruce.

He turned round. He looked at Gary. He heard the tearing sound, the canvas-ripping sound, a lot louder than it had been at one o'clock. He heard something heavy come down onto four feet in the wet moss, and the moss give under it. He heard it breathe.

He turned back round.

She was standing in front of the spruce in the dark. A grey wolf. In the afternoon, on the ridge, through the dark glasses, with a vampire in the air and a witch throwing curses at him, he'd seen her as a problem: a big angry animal with a lot of teeth, one variable among several. He looked at her now with nothing else in the way.

She was tall at the shoulder, level with the top of his head, about her brother's size. Long-legged, deep-chested, built narrow and fine for speed, the way a greyhound was built, or a cheetah. Her coat was a soft dark smoke-grey, silver-tipped where the rain had caught it, darker down the spine. Her ears were up. Her eyes were on him, dark and steady, and they were Leah's.

And under his breastbone, the thing behind the locks went mad.

He felt it hit the first lock like a battering ram. He felt the lock hold, groaning. He felt it hit the second. He felt it throw itself up the line, toward her, toward the grey thing at the other end that was standing in front of him now in the dark with its ears up, *right there*, not ten feet away, close enough to smell, close enough to touch, and it wanted out. It wanted out so badly. It wanted to come up through him and out of him and stand on four feet in the wet moss in front of her and put its nose to hers and *know* her, the way he couldn't, the way only it could. It had been in the dark for two months. Behind locks. Down a hole. And now there was this, now there was *her*, and it was not going to wait.

He stood very still and held on.

It was the hardest thing he'd done all day. Harder than the Imperius. Harder than holding a three-hundred-year-old mind on a rein while Aro laughed down a phone line. He held the locks with everything he had, and he felt the thing at the bottom of him hit them again, and again, and he felt the grey wolf in front of him feel it too, through the line, and her ears went forward.

"Not today," he said. His voice was rough. "Not today. Sorry. Not today."

He didn't know if he was talking to her or to it.

The grey wolf took a step toward him.

He let her. He took his hands out of his pockets. She came across the moss, slowly, with her head low, the way you'd come up to a horse you didn't know, and she stopped in front of him and looked at him from about a foot away, her breath hot and fogging on his face, her eyes on his.

He put his hand up and laid it flat on the side of her face.

The fur was coarse and wet and hot under his palm. Hotter than his own skin, which was saying something. He felt the bones of her jaw. He felt her breathing.

And the thing at the bottom of him stopped hitting the locks.

It stopped. All at once. It stood at the bottom of him, behind the three locks, panting, and looked up the line at the grey thing at the other end, and he felt it see that she was here. That his hand was on her. That she wasn't going anywhere, not really, not the way it had been afraid of; the line wasn't going to break. It could feel that. It could feel that she was tied to it at both ends.

It lay down.

It lay down behind its locks with its chin on its paws, the way it always had. Except it kept its head up. It kept its eyes open. It kept them on her.

"Yeah," said Harry quietly. To it. To her. "Yeah. Me too."

The grey wolf pushed her face into his hand. Just slightly. Just once.

Then she stepped back, and turned, and she was gone into the trees, south-east, toward the river, so fast that the ferns she went through were still swinging after she'd passed and he could hear her going for a long way through the dark, the soft heavy thud of her running fading down the slope.

He stood there and listened. And because he couldn't not, he went with her.

It was easy. Easier than anything. He didn't have to reach. She was just *there*, at the other end of the line, running, her mind wide open to him like a door left on the latch. He felt the cold of the river as she went through it. He felt the ground under her feet. He felt her wolf, running, loose and fast and glad, and her, inside it, holding her breath.

And then he felt the pack.

It came on in her head the way a stadium comes on when the floodlights hit. Loud. All at once. Two other minds, one sandy and one russet, slamming into hers from miles away, both of them shouting.

*LEE.*

*Leah. Leah, are you okay, where have you* been*, what happened, what did he*—

*Oh my God, Lee, are you okay, are you hurt, did he*—

He felt them hit her. He felt them pour into her head like water into a hole. He felt them see. The tent, the slate hall. The kitchen, the fry-up. The specimen room, the jars, the Lethifold, the fang. The circle with the vampire on its back in the middle. The Stunners flattening. Fire. Him, at the edge of the circle, crouched on his heels, saying *Casimir. Where's your phone?*

And then he felt the oath wake up.

---

It woke up under his collarbone like a cold hand closing.

He hadn't felt it all afternoon. It had sat there in his chest while he told her every secret he'd ever been trusted with, and it had done nothing, because it had looked down the line at her and seen him. And now, for the first time since he'd taken it on a black stone in a little room on Level Nine eight years ago, he felt it do something he'd never known it could do.

It reached down the line.

He felt it go. Cold and heavy and very old, a thing made out of the oldest, coldest magic in the Department, going out of him and down the rope into her, twenty miles off in the dark, running. Into her head. Into the middle of the pack, where the two other wolves were pouring in like floodwater, seeing everything.

And it closed.

It closed on the things in her head that were his. The Department. His name. *Unspeakable*. The room on Level Nine. London. Volterra, and the word *strike*, and the old man laughing down the phone. The child. Every single thing he'd told her in the kitchen, every single thing she'd seen in the specimen room that belonged to the Department and not to her: it closed over all of it, the way it would have closed over his own throat. Gently. Completely. Like a hand.

He felt the two other wolves hit it.

He felt them come pouring into her head and run straight into a wall. A blank place. Grey. Smooth. Like fog, like a stone in a stream, like the bend in his own lattice around the witch's bees. Everywhere they went, half her afternoon was there, bright and clear: the tent, the jars, the vampire, him, the fry-up, her hand in his on the bench in the dark. And the other half wasn't. The other half was just grey.

*Lee,* said the russet one. Jacob. Very slowly. *What is that?*

*What's what?*

*That. That, in your head. There's a—it's like a wall. I can't see through it. What's behind it? What did he—*

*Why can't we see?* said the sandy one, Seth, scared. *Lee? Did he do something to you? Did he put something in your head?*

And Harry felt Leah, running through the dark twenty miles away with the pack shouting in her skull, go absolutely still in the middle of her own mind.

He felt her look at it.

He felt her look at the wall. At the grey. At the place where his secrets were, inside her, wrapped up and sealed in something that wasn't her and wasn't the pack and wasn't anything she'd ever felt before. Something cold. Something old. Something the oldest binding in the Department of Mysteries had looked at, and recognised, and filed under *his*.

He felt her understand it. Right then, running. He felt it land in her.

*Huh,* Leah thought. To herself. To him.

Then, to the pack: *I don't know, Jake. Ask him yourself.*

---

He came back to himself standing on the bench in the dark, in the drizzle, with his hand still up in the air at the height of a wolf's face.

He put it down.

He stood there for a long time. The river roared. The spruces dripped. Somewhere a long way off to the southeast, a wolf howled, high and long and rising, and then another one answered it, and then a third, and the sound came up the valley through the rain and went past him into the dark.

He'd come to this peninsula to set a snare. Sixty-six days, sitting by the line, waiting for something to put its foot in the loop.

He was starting to suspect it had gone the other way.

"Don't," Harry said to Gary.

Gary, on the canvas by the door, gleaming yellow in the dark, didn't say anything at all.

Harry went inside. He put the kettle on. He put a record on, side two this time, loud. He stood at the range with his hands flat on the iron and listened to the music and to the rain, and down at the bottom of him, behind three locks that he was no longer entirely sure he'd built to hold anything, the thing in the dark lay with its chin on its paws and its eyes open, facing southeast.

Waiting for her to come back.
