# Need to Know

## Chapter One — Snare

---

### I. Aisle Seven

*Forks, Washington — Sunday, October 1, 2006*

The list was in his mother's handwriting, and his mother's handwriting had opinions.

*Potatoes (RUSSET, not the red ones). 2 chickens. Milk x2. Eggs. Butter — REAL butter. Coffee — the Folgers Charlie likes, NOT the cheap one, Seth, I can tell.*

Seth Clearwater stood under the humming fluorescent tubes of the Forks Thriftway at a quarter past four on a Sunday afternoon with a plastic basket in each hand and rain still dripping off the ends of his hair, and he read the list a second time because the underlines made him feel watched. He was nineteen years old. He could lift the back end of a Subaru off the ground if he set his feet right, and he'd done it once, on a dare, in the parking lot behind the Quileute tribal office. He had run down and torn apart things that were older than the United States. He was still a little afraid of his mother's capital letters.

Three weeks.

That was how long it had been since the baby. Three weeks since the night the Cullen house had smelled like blood and bleach and the end of the world, since Bella's heart had stopped and Jacob had come out onto the porch looking like somebody had cut the strings out of him, and then — a few hours later — gone back inside and come out again looking like somebody had handed him the sun. Three weeks of the pack splitting and the pack holding, of Sam's wolves and Jacob's wolves pacing the same borders on different sides of an argument that had gone away overnight because nobody could kill a thing that Jacob had imprinted on. Three weeks of Seth spending more time on the Cullens' front steps than in his own bed. Of Bella with red eyes and a careful way of holding her hands. Of Renesmee, who was three weeks old and already looked like she'd skipped most of a year, who put her palm on your cheek and *showed* you things, pictures pressed straight into your head like a slide projector made of feelings.

The world had gone strange so many times in the last two years that Seth had stopped bracing for it. Strange was weather. You put on a jacket and went out in it.

He found the russets. He found two chickens, plump in their plastic, and set them in the basket like they were eggs, and then he found the eggs. He went down the dairy aisle and back up it, because he'd walked right past the butter while he was thinking about whether Edward would let him take the Volvo out to the coast road some night, just once, just to see what it did over ninety.

Aisle seven was coffee, baking, honey, syrup, the cereal nobody bought. It was nearly empty. A kid in a Spartans hoodie was restocking the oatmeal at the far end with the slack-jawed patience of someone being paid minimum wage on a Sunday. There was a woman halfway down on the left, up on the toes of a pair of mud-crusted hiking boots, reaching for the top shelf.

Seth registered her the way he registered everything now: all at once and in too much detail. Moss-green rain jacket, dark at the shoulders. Hair — dark, a lot of it, curling hard in the wet — piled up on the back of her head and skewered in place with what looked like a pencil made of carved dark wood. A shopping cart beside her with a load in it that didn't make any sense together: four big canisters of kosher salt, two mesh bags of oranges, a bundle of fresh rosemary, a box of fat beeswax candles, a family-size jar of instant coffee and then another one, and then — he counted — four more.

She smelled like rain. Like oranges. Like something else, too, something sharp at the back of it that his nose didn't have a word for, the way the air smelled down on First Beach right after lightning hit the water.

Her fingertips found the jar she was reaching for — honey, the big squeeze bear kind's older cousin, glass, a pound at least — and nudged it, and it tipped off the lip of the shelf.

Seth moved.

He didn't decide to. That was the thing about being what he was; the body went first and the brain caught up later and filed a report. One second he was standing at the end of the aisle with a basket in each hand and the next he had covered fifteen feet of linoleum and the jar of honey was sitting in his palm six inches off the floor, unbroken, the gold inside it still sloshing.

Both his baskets were still in his other hand. One of the chickens had shifted. That was all.

"Whoa," the woman said.

Seth straightened up and held out the honey and looked at her face for the first time, and the floor went out from under him.

He'd heard it described. Everybody in the pack had heard it described, in that unasked-for, unavoidable way they heard everything — through Sam's head first, and then Jared's, and then Quil's and Paul's, and then, three weeks ago, Jacob's, which had been the loudest of all of them because Jacob had been trying so hard to hate the thing he was looking at when it happened. Seth had felt the shape of it a dozen times secondhand. He had thought he knew.

He had known about it the way you know about the ocean from a postcard.

Every line that held him to the earth went slack at once. His mother. Leah. The pack. The rez, the beach, the smell of cedar shakes in the rain, the Cullens' porch, the drum of his own feet on the forest floor at night, the face of his father in the last photograph, laughing at something off-camera. All of it unhooked. For one sick half-second he was weightless, falling in every direction, and then all of those lines snapped taut again and every one of them ran through her.

Gray eyes. Dark lashes. A thin pale scar that ran up through her left eyebrow and split it in two. Freckles across the bridge of her nose that the Washington autumn hadn't gotten around to fading yet. A wide mouth that was, at the moment, a little open, because a total stranger had just teleported down a grocery aisle to catch her honey.

"Nice catch," she said.

"I — yeah," Seth said. "Thanks. I mean. You're welcome. It's honey."

One of her eyebrows went up. The scarred one. It came up in two pieces.

"It is honey," she agreed.

He was still holding it out. She took it from him, and her fingers brushed his, and she flinched, a small reflexive jerk, the kind somebody makes after touching a stove.

"Jesus," she said. "You're burning up. Are you sick?"

"I run hot."

He'd said it a hundred times. To teachers. To the lady at the clinic in Port Angeles. To Bella, once, back when Bella had still been warm herself and had been startled by it. It was the line. It always worked.

This time, she looked at him for a second longer than people usually did. Something moved behind the gray eyes, quick and careful, the expression of someone adding a number to a column. Then it was gone, and she smiled, and Seth forgot what the column was.

"Lucky you," she said. "I've been cold since I crossed the state line."

"It's the rain. It gets in."

"It really does." She put the honey in her cart beside the oranges. "I'm Layla."

"Seth."

"Seth." She said it like she was trying it out. "Do you work here, Seth? Is this, like, a service they provide? Because if so, I'm going to have to start writing reviews."

"No. I'm — no, I'm just shopping." He lifted his baskets a little, as evidence. One of the chickens slid. "For my mom. She's making dinner. Her boyfriend's coming. He's the police chief. That's not — I don't know why I said that."

"Because it's true?"

"Probably that."

She laughed. It was a short laugh, surprised out of her, and it went through him like a hand on the back of his neck.

"Well, Seth-who-is-just-shopping," she said, "you're a lifesaver. I've been up and down this store three times and I can't reach anything. Everything in this town is built for lumberjacks."

"You're new."

"Thursday." She pulled a face. "I bought a house. Over the phone, like an idiot. The old Hadley place, out on the Calawah road? With the porch that's actively trying to kill me?"

Seth knew it. Everybody knew it. A gray two-story farmhouse at the end of a gravel drive, half a mile past the last mailbox, with a sagging wraparound porch and a barn that had been leaning a little further east every winter since he was a kid. Old Mr. Hadley had died in it two years ago and his kids in Seattle had been trying to sell it ever since.

"I can fix porches," Seth said.

Layla tilted her head. "You can fix porches."

"I'm really good at porches." He heard himself and closed his eyes for a second. "I mean, I do carpentry. Stuff. Roofs. My dad taught me before he — I've done a lot of porches. On the rez. La Push. I'm from La Push."

"Okay," she said slowly, and there was a smile tucked into the corner of her mouth now that she didn't seem to be trying very hard to hide. "Seth from La Push who's good at porches. I might actually take you up on that, because the guy from the hardware store took one look at it and told me to burn it down and start over."

"Don't burn it down. The joists are fine, probably. It's just the decking. And the posts on the east side. And maybe the—" He stopped. "Sorry. I'm — sorry."

"For what?"

He didn't have an answer for that. He had a lot of answers for a lot of questions and none of them were for that one.

She reached up and tugged the pencil out of her hair. Up close, the pencil turned out to be carved, the wood dark and close-grained and worked into a long smooth taper with a band of some pale inlay near the thick end. All that hair came down around her shoulders in a wet black cascade, and she raked it back with her fingers and twisted it up again and stabbed the stick back through it in two quick motions, the way somebody does a thing they've done ten thousand times without looking.

For a fraction of a second, as it passed close to his face, Seth could have sworn the air around it hummed. He could have sworn the little hairs on his forearm stood up.

Then she was writing on the back of her own grocery receipt with an actual pen — a cheap blue Bic she pulled out of her jacket pocket — and tearing the bottom strip off, and holding it out.

"Here," she said. "Landline. They hooked it up yesterday. If you're serious about the porch."

He took it. A phone number. *Layla*, underlined once. Not a capital letter in sight.

"I'm serious about the porch," he said.

"Good." She turned her cart. One of the wheels was bad, the way one of the wheels was always bad, and it shuddered and dragged left. "Nice to meet you, Seth."

"Yeah," he said. "You too. Layla. Yeah."

She went around the end of the aisle. He listened to the bad wheel all the way to the registers. He listened to the beep of the scanner going over salt, salt, salt, salt, and the teenage cashier asking, "Having a party?" and Layla saying, "Something like that," in a voice that had a smile in it. He listened to the automatic doors sigh open, and the hiss of the rain outside, and her boots on the wet asphalt, and the dry cough of an old engine catching on the third try.

He stood in aisle seven for a long time.

The kid in the Spartans hoodie had stopped stacking oatmeal and was staring at him.

"Dude," the kid said. "You okay?"

"Yeah," Seth said. "I'm great. I'm really great."

He drove home with the receipt in his shirt pocket over his heart, because he was afraid that if he put it anywhere else it would blow out the window. He forgot the coffee. He walked into his mother's kitchen with two baskets' worth of groceries in four paper bags and Sue Clearwater went through them in thirty seconds, came up with a face like a thunderhead, and said, "Seth. *Seth.* Where's the Folgers?"

He looked at her. He was smiling. He couldn't stop.

His mother set down the butter. Her face changed — slowly, the thunderhead clearing and something else coming in under it. She had been married to a man who'd carried the old stories in his blood. She had raised two children who'd turned out to carry them too. She had learned, in the last two years, to read the signs.

"Oh, honey," she said quietly. "Oh, *no.* Who is she?"

---

That night it was his turn on the north loop, from the Calawah down along the treaty line to the river, and the minute he let the heat come up through his spine and the world tore and rearranged itself into four feet and fur and a thousand new smells, Leah was there inside his head, ten miles off, running the south end.

She didn't say anything at first. She didn't have to. It was all right there, laid out in his head like it was printed on the inside of his skull — aisle seven, the honey, the gray eyes, the scar through the eyebrow, the weightless half-second and the lines all snapping back. In a pack mind everything went to everyone at once, whether you wanted it to or not.

Seth felt his sister see it. He felt her stop running.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Oh, you have* got *to be kidding me.*

*Hi, Leah.*

*Don't "hi, Leah" me. Seth. She's buying* salt. *You imprinted on a woman buying six jars of instant coffee and enough salt to cure a whale.*

*Four. Four canisters of salt. There was six coffee.*

*Oh, well, that's* fine *then.*

Then there was another mind in the link, sliding in hot and sudden like a door banging open in a storm — Jacob, phasing somewhere near the Cullen place, his attention swinging over to them both like a searchlight.

*What's going — Seth?* A pause. Then, with feeling: *Oh, man. Seth. Did you just —*

*Yeah,* Seth thought. He was running again, flat out, for no reason at all, the trees blurring, the wet ground throwing up the smell of fern and cedar and the deep cold stone underneath. He could still smell her. He could smell oranges and rain and that sharp clean lightning-strike thing that he still didn't have a word for. *Yeah, I did.*

*She's older than you,* Leah thought.

*She's like twenty-three.*

*Like I said.*

*Four years isn't —*

*She has a* scar *through her* eyebrow, *Seth. Who gets a scar like that? What does she do?*

He didn't know. He realized he didn't know anything — not her last name, not what she did, not why someone would buy a house over the phone on the Olympic Peninsula and drive across the country in an engine that needed three tries to catch. He didn't know why she was cold. He didn't know why her hair stick hummed.

He didn't care. He knew where she lived and he knew her porch needed new decking and he knew her laugh, and it was enough to be getting on with.

*Leave him alone, Leah,* Jacob thought, and under the words there was something tired and fond and knowing that Seth had never heard in Jacob's head before three weeks ago. *You remember what it was like for me? He's not gonna hear anything you say for about a month.*

*Great,* Leah thought. *Fantastic. I live in a romance novel.*

But she started running again, and as she did, Seth caught one thought from her that she didn't mean to let through, quick and small and buried under all the sarcasm, the way the best things in Leah always were:

*At least she's not a vampire.*

### II. The Porch

*The Calawah Road — Sunday, November 5, 2006*

The porch was finished.

Seth stood on it in the dark with the rain coming down soft and steady past the eaves and ran his palm along the new rail, and the cedar was smooth under his hand because he'd sanded it himself, three passes, eighty grit and then one-twenty and then two-twenty, because Layla had said once in passing that she liked to sit out with her coffee in the mornings and he didn't want her getting splinters. The east posts were new. The decking was new. The joists had been fine, mostly, like he'd said, except for the two at the corner that a family of carpenter ants had been eating since about the Clinton administration. He'd sistered those. You could jump up and down on the whole thing now and it wouldn't so much as squeak. He'd checked. She'd laughed at him for checking.

Five weeks.

He'd called the number on the receipt the next morning at eight, and then hung up before it rang, and then called again at eight-oh-four, and she'd picked up on the first ring and said, "Porch guy," before he'd said a word, like she'd been sitting next to the phone. He'd shown up that Saturday with a truckload of lumber he'd talked Jacob's friend in Port Angeles into selling him at cost, and she'd come out onto the rotten old boards in two sweaters and a pair of thick wool socks and handed him a mug of coffee that was so strong it was basically a solid, and she'd watched him tear the old decking off for about an hour before she said, "Okay, you weren't lying, you really are good at porches."

He'd come back on Sunday. And the next Saturday. And the Wednesday after that, because she'd mentioned the gutter on the barn. And then he'd stopped needing excuses, because somewhere in there she'd started calling him on Tuesday nights to ask if he'd eaten, and he always said no even when he had, because she made chili in a pot big enough to bathe in and watched him eat four bowls of it with her chin in her hand and an expression on her face like a scientist watching an experiment go better than expected.

"Where do you *put* it?" she'd asked, the first time.

"Fast metabolism."

"You're like a furnace that eats sandwiches."

"Thank you?"

"Don't thank me. I'm jealous. I've been cold since August."

They'd walked First Beach on the one clear Sunday in October, all the way out to where the sea stacks stood up black against a sky so blue it hurt, and she'd told him about her work in the careful, edited way of somebody who'd practiced the edit. Consulting, she called it. Restoration. "Old buildings. Tombs, sometimes. Peru, mostly, and Mexico. Egypt once, for a year. I go in where it's not safe and I make it safe, and then other people come in after me and take pictures and write papers about it and get all the credit." She'd said it lightly, but she'd touched the scar through her eyebrow while she said it, the way you touch a sore tooth with your tongue. "I'm on sabbatical. Indefinitely. I wanted somewhere quiet where it rained a lot and nobody knew me."

"You picked the right town."

"I know," she'd said, and leaned into his side against the wind off the water, a cold, small weight against all that heat. "I'm a genius."

He'd told her things too. Half-things. True things with the corners sanded off. That his dad had died two years ago. That his sister was angry at the whole world and had earned it. That he had a friend whose wife had been really sick and had a baby and gotten better, kind of, in a way that was hard to explain. That he worked nights sometimes, for the tribe, sort of a security thing. That he couldn't always say where he was going.

She'd never pushed. That was the thing about Layla that he hadn't expected. She noticed everything — he'd watched her notice things, watched that quick, careful look come into her eyes like she was adding to a column — and then she'd look away and say nothing at all.

He'd noticed things, too.

Her kettle boiled faster than any kettle had a right to. He'd swear on his father's grave that one night he'd watched it go from cold to screaming in the time it took her to cross the kitchen and look at it. The furnace in the basement had been dead since before she'd bought the place — he'd gone down to check it the second week and found the pilot rusted solid and the blower motor seized — and the house was still always cozy, every room of it, like the heat came out of the walls. The barn had been leaning east for fifteen years. Three weeks ago it had stopped leaning. He'd stood in the yard staring at it and she'd come up beside him with her coffee and said, "Huh. Must've settled," in a voice so bland it could have been a weather report.

There were lines of salt under every windowsill in the house. Under the thresholds. Bundles of rosemary tied with red thread hung over every door. Orange peels drying on the kitchen windowsill, arranged in some kind of pattern that wasn't quite a circle. And that smell, always — under the coffee and the oranges and the chili, faint and clean and sharp — like the air on First Beach right after lightning hit the water.

And once, three weeks ago, he'd fallen asleep on her couch after dinner with the game on, and he'd half-woken in the dark to the sound of her voice, low and very close, saying words he didn't know in a rhythm that went up the back of his neck like cold fingers. The air had smelled like a thunderstorm. Something had passed over him, slowly, from his head to his feet, like a hand that wasn't touching him. He'd been so tired and so comfortable that he'd decided he was dreaming, and gone back under.

In the morning she'd been at the stove making eggs and humming and he'd decided he'd definitely dreamed it.

He was less sure now.

"You're doing it again," Layla said from the door behind him.

He turned. She was leaning on the frame in her two sweaters and her wool socks, a dish towel over one shoulder, her hair up with the stick through it.

"Doing what?"

"Standing on my porch in the rain looking like you're about to propose or confess to a murder." She came out and stood next to him at the rail. "Which one is it? Because I've only got one ring and it's my grandmother's and you can't have it."

Seth laughed. It came out wrong.

She heard it come out wrong. He watched her hear it. The joking went out of her face, slowly, and something else came in behind it, quiet and level and steady.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. What is it?"

Jacob had told him a week ago. *She has to know, man. Before anything happens. That's the rule. It's not fair otherwise.* And Jacob had looked at him with that new face of his, the one that had come in the night Renesmee was born, older around the eyes, and said, *And it's gonna be fine. You know it's gonna be fine. It's what she needs. That's how it works.*

Seth had believed him. Seth was still mostly believing him. He was just also about to throw up.

"I need to show you something," he said. "Outside. In the yard. I need you to stay up here on the porch, okay? Right here. And whatever happens — whatever you see — I'm okay. I promise. It's still me."

Layla looked at him for a long moment.

"Seth," she said carefully, "if you're about to show me a body—"

"It's not a body."

"Because I'll be honest, I've seen bodies, and I'm not great about them, but I *will* help you move one if—"

"*Layla.*"

She closed her mouth. Something flickered at the corner of it anyway.

"Okay," she said. "Porch. Right here. Got it."

He went down the steps into the rain. The yard was wet grass and old apple trees that hadn't been pruned in a decade, and the barn standing up straight at the back of it with the porch light just reaching its doors. He went around behind it where she couldn't see. He stripped off his shirt and his jeans and his shoes, fast, shivering a little — not from cold — and pulled on the pair of cutoffs he'd stashed in the hayloft, and knotted the old leather cord he kept around his ankle so it would hold the cutoffs through the change. He stood there for a second in the dark and the rain with his heart going like a hammer.

*Okay,* he thought. *Okay. Okay. Okay.*

He let the heat come up his spine.

It was always the same, and it was always new. The pressure behind his eyes, then the sound — the deep internal *tear* like heavy canvas ripping — and the world went sideways and the ground came up and he was taller, longer, heavier, his weight spread out over four wide feet, and the rain was suddenly a thousand separate sounds and the night was suddenly full of smells, apples and wet bark and old hay and diesel from the road and, from the porch, oranges and coffee and chili and Layla, Layla, Layla.

He walked out from behind the barn into the light.

He had seen himself in the river. He knew what he looked like. A wolf the size of a horse, sandy-coated, rangy, a little gangly still in the legs the way he'd been gangly as a kid. Ears that stood up too tall. A tail that was absolutely going to wag in a minute if he didn't think very hard about it.

He stopped at the bottom of the porch steps and looked up at her.

Layla had gone completely still.

She was standing exactly where he'd left her, one hand on the rail. The other hand had come up to her hair, and her fingers were resting on the end of the carved stick, not pulling it, just touching it, the way a cop's hand rests on a holster. Her face had gone very pale. Her eyes were very wide. He could hear her heart, and it was going fast, in the quick, even rhythm of someone running numbers in her head.

Seth sat down. Slowly. In the wet grass. He tried to look small, which was not a thing a wolf his size could do, and he tilted his head, and he whined, very softly, through his nose.

Layla made a sound.

He thought for a second she was crying. Then he realized she had put her other hand over her mouth and she was *laughing* — shaky, unsteady, almost hysterical, her shoulders shaking with it.

"Oh, thank God," she said through her fingers. "Oh my God. Oh, thank *God.* I thought you were *cursed.*"

Seth's ears went up.

She came down the steps. She'd said she'd stay on the porch, and she didn't, she came straight down into the rain in her wool socks and stopped about ten feet away from him with water already darkening her sweaters, and she looked at him — really looked, up and down, the way she'd looked at the porch the first day, the way he'd seen her look at the barn.

"I thought someone had done something to you," she said. "I scanned you. Three weeks ago, while you were asleep on my couch. I'm sorry. That was a massive violation of — that's a thing you're absolutely not supposed to do without consent, I know that, I just — you run at a hundred and eight degrees, Seth. I checked. You heal overnight. You cut your hand open on a nail on the porch and I watched it bleed and the next morning there was nothing there. Nothing. And when I scanned you there's something sitting in you, deep, all the way down, something *old* and *huge*, and I thought — I thought somebody had put it there. I thought somebody had cursed you and you didn't even know. I had a whole plan. I bought supplies. I was going to start trying to break it on Thursday." She laughed again, a little wildly. "Thursday! God. I bought so much salt."

The salt. The salt, and the oranges, and the rosemary, and the candles. Seth stared at her.

"Is this—" She stopped. Swallowed. Her voice went careful. "Does it hurt? Is this something that was done to you? Do you want it gone?"

He shook his head. Big, slow, unmistakable. *No. No. No.*

"Okay," Layla said. "Okay." She took a deep breath and let it out. Then her hand moved, and she pulled the stick out of her hair, and all that black hair came down around her shoulders in the rain.

"My turn," she said.

She lifted it.

He'd been right. It hummed. It hummed so loud now that his whole wolf body heard it, felt it, a vibration in the ground and the air and the fur along his spine, and he smelled lightning, sharp and clean and enormous, the way the air smelled right before a strike.

The rain stopped.

All of it. Everywhere. Across the whole yard, in the light from the porch and the dark past it, every falling drop just — *stopped,* hung in the air like somebody had pressed pause on the sky. Millions of them. A whole glittering curtain of water standing still between the earth and the clouds, each drop catching the porch light and holding it, so that the yard was full of tiny suspended stars.

Seth's jaw fell open. He couldn't help it. His tongue fell out a little.

Layla turned her wrist.

The drops began to move. They streamed *sideways,* toward each other in long glittering threads, pouring together in the air in front of him, and as they poured they took a shape — four legs, a long body, a tail, ears that stood up too tall — a wolf made of rainwater, life-sized, shining, every drop of it catching the light. It shook itself. It actually shook itself, like a dog coming out of a lake, and then it bounded once around him in a wide circle through the wet grass, and leapt, and as it leapt it came apart into a hundred thousand drops that fell back down to the earth with a soft sound like applause.

The rain started again. Ordinary rain. Coming down soft and steady out of the dark.

Layla was looking at him with water running down her face and that scarred eyebrow up in two pieces.

"I'm a witch," she said. "Hi."

---

He phased back behind the barn with his hands shaking so hard he could barely get the cutoffs untied, and he walked back out bare-chested in the rain, steaming in the cold like a racehorse, and she was sitting on the top step of the porch he'd built her with her wand across her knees, waiting.

He sat down next to her, close, his shoulder an inch from hers.

For a long time neither of them said anything.

"So," Layla said finally. "That's what you do at night. For the tribe. *Security.*"

"Yeah."

"There's more of you."

"Yeah. A bunch. It's — it's in the old stories. The Quileute. We come from wolves. Spirit warriors. It's in the blood, it's been in the blood forever, and it only wakes up when—" He hesitated. "When there's a reason. When there's something around we need to protect people from."

"What kind of something?"

He looked at his hands. "That's kind of a second conversation."

"Seth."

"It's a *really* big second conversation."

She let it go. He could feel her let it go, and he could feel her put it in the column anyway. "Okay," she said. "Okay. Second conversation."

"What about you? Are there — is it just you, or—"

"Oh, there's a lot of us." She turned the wand in her fingers. Up close, in the porch light, he could see the work in it: the dark wood carved along its length in a pattern of long, overlapping feathers, the pale band near the handle inlaid in what looked like bone or shell. "Witches. Wizards. Whole world of us. Schools, governments, newspapers, banks, prisons. Hidden. Right under everyone's noses. There's a law about it. International. Has been for three hundred years. We don't show No-Majs — that's what we call people who can't do magic — we don't show them anything. Ever. That's the first rule. That's the only rule, really. Everything else is footnotes."

"And you just showed me."

"I just showed you."

"Are you gonna get in trouble?"

She was quiet for a second. "If anyone ever found out? Yeah. I'd lose my wand, probably. Maybe more than that. There are people whose whole job is to come and fix it when somebody slips. They'd wipe your memory. They'd take the whole last month out of your head like it never happened, and you'd wake up one morning and wonder why you'd built a porch for a stranger."

"Then why'd you do it?"

Layla turned her head and looked at him. The rain was in her eyelashes.

"Because you just turned into a horse-sized wolf in my backyard," she said, "for me. Because you were scared to death and you did it anyway. Seemed rude not to."

Seth breathed out. It came out ragged.

"There's something else," he said. "About the wolf thing. Something you should know. Something that's — about you."

And he told her. Clumsy, too fast, getting it backwards in places and having to go back and fix it, the way he'd fixed the porch. Imprinting. What it was, as far as anybody knew. How it happened — the first look, the floor dropping out, every line going slack and snapping back taut. How it wasn't a choice. How it meant he would be whatever she needed. A friend, a brother, a guy who fixed porches. Whatever. Forever. How Sam had done it, and Jared, and Quil, and Jacob. How it had happened in aisle seven over a jar of honey.

She listened to all of it without interrupting. When he was done she was quiet for so long that he started counting raindrops off the eave.

"So you didn't choose me," she said at last. Her voice was level. She was checking the math.

"I chose to fix your porch," Seth said. "I chose to come back. I chose to eat your chili even when I'd already had dinner. I chose — everything. All of it. Every day. The first part just happened. Everything after was me." He swallowed. "I'd have chosen it anyway. I think. I'm pretty sure. If I'd just been a guy in a grocery store. I'd have called on the first ring."

"You hung up the first time."

"*You* knew that?"

"Caller ID," Layla said. "Some magic is just Qwest."

He laughed, and it came out right this time, and she was laughing too, and then she reached over and put her cold hand flat against his hot chest, right over his heart, like she was checking for something.

"Okay," she said quietly. "Okay."

And then she kissed him.

Her mouth was cold. He'd known it would be. He hadn't known about the rest — that she'd tasted like coffee and chili powder and that sharp lightning smell, that she'd make a small surprised sound against his lips like she hadn't quite planned on doing it, that her fingers would curl against his chest, that the whole world would go quiet except for the rain. He kissed her back carefully, the way you'd hold something you were afraid of breaking, and she laughed against his mouth and said, "I'm not made of glass, porch guy," and pulled him down by the back of the neck.

The porch didn't squeak at all. He'd checked.

---

It was after midnight when he phased again, out past the end of the Calawah road, taking the late loop that ran north to the river. He was still grinning. A wolf could grin. It looked stupid, but it could.

Jacob was in his head before his front feet hit the ground. Leah a heartbeat after, running the south line by the coast.

And Seth realized, a half-second too late, what he'd just done.

He felt them both see it. All of it, all at once, the way the pack mind always did it — the porch, the barn, the yard, the wolf sitting in the wet grass, and then the rain *stopping,* the whole glittering curtain of it hanging in the air, and the water-wolf leaping, and the wand, and *I'm a witch, hi.* And the conversation on the steps. And the kiss.

There was a long, ringing silence in the link.

*She's a WHAT?* Jacob thought.

*Oh, of course she is,* Leah thought. *Of course. Of* course *my baby brother's imprint is a witch with a magic stick. Why wouldn't she be. Why would anything in my life ever be normal for one single—*

*Leah—*

*Did she just — did she make a wolf out of* rain?

*Yeah,* Seth thought helplessly. *Yeah, she did.*

*That's actually kind of awesome,* Jacob thought, a little dazed. And then, sharper, his alpha voice coming up under the words like stone under water: *Wait. Wait, wait. Seth. She said she could get in trouble. She said there's people whose whole job is to — is she gonna get in trouble for this? Is she gonna get* you *in trouble?*

*She said only if somebody finds out.*

A pause.

Seth felt Jacob's attention swing. Felt it turn, the way a compass needle turns, toward the big white house on the river eight miles east, where Jacob had been sitting on the back steps until twenty minutes ago and where he'd be sitting again by morning, the way he was every morning, with a mind that was about as private as a billboard.

Seth felt Leah follow the thought to the end of it a second before he did.

*Oh,* Leah thought. And then, with enormous, terrible satisfaction: *Oh, Seth. You are* so *screwed.*

*Edward,* Seth thought.

### III. Thirteen

*The Cullen house — Tuesday, November 7, 2006*

Forty-four hours.

That was how long it had taken Layla Mercer to go from one person to—

She did the arithmetic again in the passenger seat of Seth's truck as it bumped up a gravel drive she'd never have found on her own, a drive that turned off the highway between two trees that looked like every other pair of trees on the peninsula and wound back through the ferns and the moss-hung maples for three miles of absolute nothing. She'd done the arithmetic a lot in the last forty-four hours. She kept hoping it would come out different.

Seth. That was one. Seth, she'd decided, didn't count. Seth had turned into a wolf in her backyard and she'd have had to be made of stone not to show him something back.

Leah and Jacob. Three. Through a *pack mind,* which, it turned out, was a thing, which Seth had explained to her on Monday morning standing in her kitchen with the expression of a golden retriever that knew it had eaten the couch. *We kind of hear each other. When we're wolves. Everything. All the time. I forgot. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.*

And then the second conversation.

*So, the reason the wolves wake up. The thing we protect people from. Okay. So. Vampires.*

She had made him say it twice.

And then he'd told her about the Cullens. And the treaty. And Bella, who had been human until eight weeks ago. And the mind reader. And the girl who saw the future. And the baby.

Eight vampires — and whatever the baby was — who now knew, through Jacob Black's head, that there was a witch living on the Calawah road.

Plus Seth, Leah, and Jacob.

Twelve.

She knew what was in the books. She'd taken Defense against the Dark Arts at Ilvermorny from a wizened old Wampus named Professor Atcitty who'd hunted vampires in the Carpathians in the fifties and who'd had exactly one vampire lecture, which he'd delivered every year in a dead flat voice while standing next to a jar of something nobody had ever been brave enough to ask about. Vampires were classed as Beings. Pale. Long-lived. Hungry. They burned in direct sunlight, unless they were very old or very careful. They didn't like garlic. They could be reasoned with, most of them, and *paid,* some of them, and the ones in the Americas mostly kept to the cities and the night and to a set of treaties with MACUSA that were older than the Woolworth Building.

Seth said the Cullens went out in the daytime when it was cloudy and stayed in when it was sunny because in the sun they *sparkled.* Seth said their eyes were gold because they ate deer. Seth said Emmett Cullen had once picked up a full-grown grizzly and thrown it. Seth said the only way to kill one was to tear it into pieces and burn the pieces, and that he had done this, personally, to several of them, and he said it in the same voice he used to tell her about the joists.

None of that was in any book she had ever read.

"You okay?" Seth said beside her.

"Fine."

"You've got your hand up your sleeve."

She did. She had the handle of her wand between two fingers inside the cuff of her jacket where the old dragonhide holster sat along her forearm. She'd worn it every working day for four years, in every tomb from Cusco to Saqqara, and she'd put it on this morning without thinking about it and then stood in front of her bathroom mirror for a full minute looking at it on her arm.

"Habit," she said.

"They're nice," Seth said. "Really. Esme's gonna try to feed you. Emmett's gonna try to arm wrestle you. Rosalie's gonna be kind of — Rosalie. But they're nice."

"You said they've killed people."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Some of them," he said. "A long time ago. Not anymore. Not for a long time." He glanced at her. "You said you had a gun once. In Peru."

"That was for snakes."

"Uh-huh."

The trees opened up.

The house stood in a wide clearing on the bank of a river, three stories of white siding and glass, the whole back of it — she'd see later — one enormous wall of windows looking out over the water. It was beautiful. It looked like the house of a successful architect in a magazine. Every light in it was on.

There were people on the front porch. Several of them. Standing very, very still.

Layla's curse-breaker brain did what it had been trained to do: it stopped seeing a house and started seeing a site. Entrances. Exits. Sightlines. The river behind, too fast and cold to use. The tree line forty yards off on three sides. The truck, keys in the ignition. She counted figures. She counted heartbeats.

She could hear Seth's heart beside her, slow and strong and impossibly hot, the way it always was.

From the porch, she couldn't hear anything at all.

---

The one who came down the steps to meet them was blond, and handsome in a way that made her want to check him for glamours, and he moved — she would think about this later, lying awake — the way water moves. No wasted motion at all. He stopped a polite distance from the truck and waited while she got out, and he did not offer his hand.

"Ms. Mercer," he said. "I'm Carlisle Cullen. Thank you for coming. I know this can't be easy."

His voice was kind. His eyes were gold. The color of the honey she'd dropped in aisle seven. The color of old coins.

And he smelled — she breathed in without meaning to — *sweet.* Overwhelmingly, unnaturally sweet, like a florist's shop with the windows shut, and under the sweetness something cold and dry and mineral, like the inside of a tomb that had been sealed for three thousand years. She knew that smell. She'd spent her whole career walking into it.

She let her eyes go soft.

It was the first thing they taught you, on the Gringotts course in Lima, before they taught you a single counter-curse. *See the work.* Every enchantment had a structure — threads, knots, seams, a grammar of intent laid down by whoever had cast it, and a good curse-breaker learned to see that grammar the way an architect sees load-bearing walls. You looked a little to the side of a thing and let your focus slide and the magic in it showed up like frost on glass.

She looked at Carlisle Cullen that way, and there was nothing.

No threads. No knots. No grammar. None of the crackling living lattice she could see on any witch or wizard she'd ever met, or on a cursed door, or on a ward. There was something in him — she could *feel* it, the way she'd felt the old, huge thing sitting deep in Seth — but it had no structure she knew how to read. It was still. Set. Finished. Like a curse that had done its work centuries ago and then hardened in place, the way lava hardens into stone. Like something that had been cast, once, a long time ago, and had never stopped being cast.

She blinked her eyes back into focus. Carlisle was watching her with an expression of mild, patient curiosity.

"Well?" he said gently. "What do you see?"

She almost lied. Then she didn't.

"Nothing I've got a word for," Layla said.

Something moved in his face. It might have been delight.

"How wonderful," he said. "Neither do we. Please. Come in."

---

There were seven of them in the great white living room, and one more thing that wasn't any of them, and they all watched her come in the way a room full of cats watches a door.

Carlisle introduced them. She filed them the way she filed everything.

Esme: soft-faced, caramel-haired, the only one who smiled at her right away. The rest of them fanned out in her mind like a team in a briefing. Emmett, huge and dark-haired and grinning, sitting on the arm of a sofa that by rights should have collapsed under him. Rosalie, blonde and devastating and not smiling at all, standing by the stairs with a child on her hip. Jasper, honey-haired and lean, with a face that was courteous and very, very still, and under the sleeves of his shirt, at the wrists, something pale and crescent-shaped and repeated — scars, dozens of them, bite marks, white on white — that made every hair on Layla's arms stand up. Alice, small and dark and sharp, perched on the back of a chair like a bird, staring at Layla with a fierce little frown.

Bella. Brown-haired, pale, beautiful in a way that still looked new on her, like a dress she hadn't gotten used to. Standing very still by the windows. Her eyes were *red* — a deep, saturated, unmistakable crimson — and she wasn't breathing. Layla could see her not breathing. Bella saw her see it and said, quietly, "I'm sorry. It's — I'm new. It's easier if I don't. It's not you."

"It's okay," Layla said, and found that she meant it, mostly.

And Edward. Bronze-haired, tall, standing beside Bella with a hand at the small of her back. He was looking at Layla with an expression she couldn't read at all — a sort of intense, frustrated focus, like a man trying to hear a radio in the next room.

"You're the one who reads minds," Layla said.

"I'm the one who *usually* reads minds," Edward said. His voice was dry and precise. "I can't hear you."

"Good."

Emmett barked a laugh.

"It's different from Bella," Edward said, mostly to himself. He tilted his head, frowning. "Bella's is — silence. Nothing. A wall with no door. Yours is different. It's like standing outside a room. I can hear that there's someone in it. I can't hear anything they're saying."

"It's called Occlumency," Layla said. "Every curse-breaker learns it. Some of the old tombs have things in them that go for your head before they go for anything else. You learn to close the doors or you come out drooling." She paused. "Or you don't come out."

"Fascinating," Carlisle said, and he sounded like he meant that, too.

"I keep seeing you," Alice said abruptly.

Everyone looked at her.

"And then I don't." The little vampire's frown deepened. "It's like a radio station. Like when you're driving out of range. You fade in and fade out, fade in and fade out. Everything around you is fuzzy. I can't see the wolves at all, I never can, but you're something else. You're *flickering.*" She sounded personally offended about it. "I don't like it."

"Sorry," Layla said, and then, because she couldn't help it: "What do you see? When I fade in?"

Alice studied her.

"You drink too much coffee," she said.

"That's just true now."

"It's true in the future too."

Emmett laughed again. Something in the room eased a quarter-turn.

"So what're you here for?" Rosalie said.

The ease went right back out. The blonde vampire was looking at Layla over the child's head with an expression like a shut door, and her voice was perfectly polite, and Layla felt every word of it.

"Rose," Esme said quietly.

"It's a fair question." Rosalie didn't take her eyes off Layla. "She's here, in our house, with something up her sleeve." Layla went still. "Yes, I can see it. I can hear the leather creak when you move your arm. So. What are you here for?"

Layla looked at her. Then she drew her hand out of her sleeve — slowly, very slowly — and let them all see the wand. Then she put it back.

"My boyfriend spends half his life in your living room," she said. "I needed to see whether you were going to eat him."

Silence.

Then Emmett fell off the arm of the sofa laughing.

The child laughed too.

Layla hadn't really looked at her yet. She'd been aware of her the way you're aware of the most dangerous thing in a room — by not looking at it. Now she looked.

She was small, maybe a year and a half by the look of her, though Seth had said eight weeks, and Layla had stopped believing in numbers somewhere around the time she'd started counting vampires. She had a head of bronze ringlets, the same impossible color as Edward's hair, and a round, rosy, *living* face, with color in her cheeks, and Bella's eyes — Bella's eyes from before, Layla guessed, deep chocolate brown. And Layla could hear her heart. It was going like a bird's, fast and light and strong, and it was the only heart in the room besides Seth's and her own.

The little girl was looking straight at Layla's face. She hadn't so much as glanced at the wand.

She held out both arms.

"Nessie," Bella said softly, warning.

The child looked at her mother. Something passed between them — Layla saw the small hand flick out and touch Bella's wrist, saw Bella's face change, soften — and then Bella looked up at Layla and said, carefully, "She'd like to show you something. It's all right. It doesn't hurt. If you'll let her."

Seth, at her elbow, said very quietly, "It's okay. It's really cool."

Layla looked at Rosalie.

Rosalie looked back at her for a long, measuring moment. Then she crossed the room — and Layla had to force herself not to step back, because none of them moved the way people moved, they moved like film with frames cut out — and stopped in front of her, and held the child out just far enough, and not one inch farther.

Renesmee reached up and laid her palm flat on Layla's cheek.

The room went away.

She saw a wolf. Huge, russet-red, lying in the sun on the riverbank with its chin on its paws, watching her — no, watching *Renesmee* — with an expression of such bottomless devotion that it made Layla's chest hurt. She saw another wolf, sandy and gangly, its tongue lolling, *laughing* the way only dogs can laugh, leaping in circles in the snow. *Seth.* She saw Bella's face, very close, and Edward's, and felt how much they were loved. And then a question, as clear as if the child had spoken it aloud: an image of something shimmering in the air — glittering, colored, nameless — and a feeling attached to it, bright and hopeful and shy.

*Magic? Show me?*

The room came back.

The child took her hand away. She was looking at Layla expectantly.

Layla laughed — she couldn't help it — and drew her wand.

Rosalie went rigid. Every vampire in the room changed position without seeming to move. Layla ignored all of them and pointed the wand at the empty air above the child's head, and twisted her wrist, and whispered.

Paper birds came out of nothing.

They folded themselves out of the air — first one, then six, then twenty — cranes, small and white and perfect, creasing and tucking and snapping into shape like invisible fingers were making them, and then they began to fly. They swooped around Renesmee's head in a slow spiral, then around Rosalie's, then out across the great white room in a flock, wheeling over the piano and past the glass wall and over Emmett, who ducked, grinning, and back again.

Renesmee shrieked with delight. She reached for them. One of them landed in her open palm and sat there, its paper wings trembling.

Esme put her hand over her mouth.

Even Rosalie's face changed — just for a second, a hairline crack in the shut door, something unguarded showing through.

Edward said, very quietly, "She's never seen anything like that before."

"Neither have I," said Jasper, from the corner, the first thing he'd said.

---

Later, when Renesmee had gone up with Rosalie and the paper birds had followed them up the stairs in a fluttering line like ducklings, Carlisle sat down across from her in a white armchair and folded his hands and said, "May I ask you something, Ms. Mercer?"

"Layla."

"Layla. Seth said you could be punished. For telling him. For telling us."

"Yes."

"Badly?"

"Badly enough." She turned her coffee cup in her hands. Esme had, in fact, tried to feed her. The coffee was very good. "There's a law. The Statute of Secrecy. International. Every magical government on earth signed it in sixteen eighty-nine. We stay hidden. That's it. That's the law. I've broken it about four different ways since Sunday night." She looked up. "If anyone finds out, there are people who come. They'd take my wand, maybe. They'd wipe every one of you. They'd try, anyway. I don't know if it'd work on you. I don't know if anything I know works on you."

"We have a law very like it," Carlisle said.

She looked at him.

"Our existence must remain secret," he said quietly. "That's the only law. Everything else is—"

"Footnotes," Layla said.

He smiled a little. "Yes. And there are those who enforce it. A family. Very old, very powerful. They live in Italy." Something passed behind his gold eyes, quick and closed. "They are not gentle with those who break it."

"Then we're in the same boat."

"So it seems." He was quiet a moment. "Your secret will be kept in this house. You have my word. And I suspect you'll find that a family of vampires is very, very good at keeping secrets."

"I'm trusting a house full of vampires with my wand," Layla said.

"And we," said Bella, from the window, "are trusting a witch with our daughter."

Layla looked at her. The newborn vampire held her gaze steadily with those terrible red eyes, still not breathing, and Layla understood suddenly that this — this, right here — was the whole negotiation, and that it had already happened, upstairs, with a paper bird sitting in a little girl's hand.

"Okay," Layla said. "Okay. Deal."

She asked one more question before she left.

"Are there more of you? Others? Like you?"

"Many," Carlisle said. "Most of them don't eat as we do." He touched the corner of his own eye. "Gold, for us. Red, for those who feed on humans. If you ever see red eyes in these woods that aren't Bella's—"

"Run?"

"Call Seth," said Edward.

---

She made the bracelet that night at her kitchen table.

Seth went out into the yard and phased, and she went out after him with the kitchen scissors and a flashlight, and clipped a long sandy hank from the thickest part of his ruff while he sat there in the wet grass making a low, deeply aggrieved noise in his chest.

"Don't be a baby," she told him. "It'll grow back by morning. You know it will."

He huffed at her. It blew her hair back.

She spun the fur into a thread at the table under the yellow kitchen light. She'd learned to spin from her grandmother, the one with the ring, a hedge-witch in the hills outside Asheville who'd never trusted anything she couldn't make with her own hands. Sandy fur and fine copper wire and one long strand of her own black hair, braided together tight and even. Seth sat across from her in a T-shirt and watched her do it with his chin on his fists.

When the cord was long enough she threaded a single bead onto it. Black. Glossy. Volcanic glass, chipped and worn smooth.

"Obsidian," she said. "From the first tomb I ever cleared. Outside Cusco. I took it out of the wall where the trigger for the first curse was set. I've carried it ever since." She tied it off. "It's been in a lot of bad places with me."

She laid the bracelet on the table between them and put her wand-tip on the bead, and closed her eyes, and worked.

It took the better part of an hour. It was a variation on the Protean Charm — she'd learned it from a Gringotts team leader in Cairo who'd used it to keep track of his crew in the deep chambers — linked to her grandmother's ring on her right hand, with a homing thread woven through the link that she'd built herself, over a long cold winter in Lima, out of three different texts and a great deal of swearing. Seth watched the air above the table shimmer and fold. He watched the bead go dark, and then darker, and then, for a moment, glow from inside like a coal.

When she opened her eyes he was staring at her like she'd hung the moon.

"Okay," she said, a little hoarse. "Give me your wrist."

She tied it on his left wrist, snug, and knotted it twice.

"It's partly made out of you," she said, "so it'll go with you when you change. It'll stretch. If you're ever in trouble — real trouble, something you can't handle, something big — you bite down on the bead. Hard. Hard enough to crack it. I'll feel it here." She held up her right hand with the ring on it. "And I'll come. Wherever you are."

Seth turned his wrist over. The obsidian bead caught the light.

"Like the Bat-Signal," he said.

"Exactly like the Bat-Signal."

"What if you're far away? Like — really far?"

"I can go a long way," Layla said.

"Can you take me sometime? When you — do the thing? The disappearing thing?"

"You'll throw up."

"Worth it," Seth said, and grinned at her, and she laughed and put her face in her hands and thought, *Oh, I'm in so much trouble.*

*La Push — Sunday, November 12, 2006*

The Clearwater kitchen was small and hot and crowded and smelled like roast chicken, and Layla was trying very hard to be normal in it.

Sue Clearwater had hugged her at the door. Just hugged her, without warning, a short, strong, practical hug from a small woman with a gray streak in her black hair and Seth's eyes, and then held her at arm's length and looked at her for a long moment the way women look at things they've already decided about. "You'll do," she'd said, and that had apparently been that.

Leah was mashing potatoes at the counter with a violence that suggested the potatoes had wronged her personally. She'd said "Hey" to Layla when she'd come in. That was all. But she'd moved down the counter to make room, and she'd handed Layla a peeler without being asked, and Layla had decided to count that.

And in the living room, in a recliner that had clearly been rearranged into the best viewing spot, a man in a flannel shirt with a mustache and a can of Rainier was watching football with the deep, quiet attention of somebody who did not have a lot of hobbies and took the ones he did have seriously.

"That's Charlie," Seth murmured.

"The police chief."

"Bella's dad."

Layla looked at him. Looked back at the man in the recliner. Something in her stomach turned over, slowly.

"Does he—"

"He knows about the wolves. Jacob showed him. Like, *showed* him. Took his shirt off in Charlie's front yard and phased." Seth winced at the memory. "He knows Bella's — different. He doesn't know what. He doesn't want to. He calls it need-to-know."

"Need-to-know," Layla repeated.

"He doesn't need to know," Leah said from the potatoes, without turning around, "and so he doesn't. It's kind of his whole thing."

It was going fine. It was going *fine.* The chicken was good. Charlie had nodded at her and said, "Nice to meet you," and gone back to the game, and when the Seahawks fumbled on the twenty he'd said, "Oh, come *on,*" in a voice of such deep and personal betrayal that Layla had liked him immediately.

And then Sue opened the oven and pulled out the cast-iron skillet of cornbread with a dish towel that was folded one time too few.

Layla heard it before Sue did. The tiny sizzle. Then Sue made a sound — sharp, cut off — and the skillet went down onto the stovetop with a clang, and Sue was holding her right hand against her chest with her face gone gray and tight, and the smell of burned skin was in the kitchen, and her palm, when Layla saw it, was an angry blistering red from the base of her fingers to her wrist.

Layla didn't think.

That was the problem with eleven years of training. The body went first. The wand was out of her sleeve and in her hand before she'd decided anything, and she had Sue's wrist in her other hand, and she was murmuring the old healing charm under her breath, the one every curse-breaker learned in their first month because you could not get to a hospital from the bottom of a tomb, and the air in the hot little kitchen sharpened and went clean, like a window opening onto a thunderstorm.

The red went out of Sue's palm like water draining from sand. The blisters flattened, shrank, sank back into the skin and were gone. The skin underneath was smooth and brown and unbroken, a little pink at the edges, and then not even that.

Sue stared at her hand.

Layla stared at Sue's hand.

Very slowly, she became aware that the television in the next room had gone quiet. Someone had muted it.

She turned around.

Charlie Swan was standing in the kitchen doorway with his beer.

He looked at Sue's hand. He looked at Layla's wand. He looked at Layla. He looked at Seth, who had gone very still and very wide-eyed by the refrigerator, and at Leah, who had stopped mashing.

The silence went on for a very long time.

"So," Seth said.

Charlie held up one hand. Seth stopped.

Charlie looked at the wand again. He had a cop's eyes, Layla realized — slow and patient and missing nothing, the kind of eyes that sat in a parked car at the end of a block for six hours and remembered every license plate.

"Is this," Charlie said slowly, "one of those things?"

Seth swallowed. "...Yeah."

Charlie nodded. He took a sip of his beer. He looked at Layla.

"Is it a need-to-know thing?"

Layla opened her mouth. Closed it. Thought about the Statute of Secrecy, and the Aurors and Obliviators in the Woolworth Building, and three hundred years of law.

"Honestly, Chief Swan?" she said. "Probably not."

"Then I don't," Charlie said.

He looked at Sue. His face changed — just a little, around the eyes.

"You okay?"

"I'm fine," Sue said. She was still looking at her hand. "I'm fine, Charlie."

"Good." He nodded once more. He turned around and went back to his recliner. The television came back on. A crowd roared. And then, from the living room, without turning around, not loud:

"Nice work, by the way."

Layla stood in the middle of the Clearwater kitchen with her wand in her hand and her mouth open.

Leah snorted. She went back to mashing.

"Welcome to the family," Leah said. "That's Charlie."

Later, much later, lying awake in the dark in the old Hadley farmhouse with Seth asleep beside her like a banked fire, Layla did the arithmetic one more time.

Leah. Jacob. Carlisle, Esme, Edward, Bella, Alice, Jasper, Rosalie, Emmett. Renesmee. Sue.

Charlie.

Thirteen. Fourteen with Seth, and she'd decided a long time ago that Seth didn't count.

She lay there and waited to feel the fear she knew she ought to feel. The fear she'd been raised with, the fear that every witch and wizard in America had drilled into them from the cradle — Rappaport's Law, and the Scourers, and the Obliviators coming in the night. She thought about the people whose whole job it was to come and fix things.

And she found that, mostly, she didn't care.

The Statute of Secrecy had been written for a world full of No-Majs with pitchforks. Nobody who'd written it had ever met a man who could pull a vampire's head off and then come home and sand a porch rail three times so she wouldn't get splinters. Nobody from the Woolworth Building had ever been to Forks, Washington. Nobody from the Woolworth Building had any reason to come.

It was the end of the earth out here. It was rain and trees and the gray Pacific.

Nobody would ever know.

### IV. Pattern of Life

*Forest Service Road 29, above the Calawah River — Tuesday, November 28, 2006*

From the outside, the tent was a tent.

Olive-drab canvas gone gray-green with age and weather, the kind of wall tent the British Army had been issuing since before the Somme, pitched square and tight on a level shelf of ground at the dead end of a Forest Service spur road eleven miles northeast of Forks. Guy ropes pegged into the duff. A stovepipe through a tin collar in the roof. A camp chair out front with a folded copy of the *Peninsula Daily News* on the seat going soft in the rain. Anyone who walked up the spur — and in nineteen days nobody had — would have looked at it and thought *hunter* or *hermit* or *one of those guys who lives in his car,* and then found themselves thinking about something else entirely, and then found themselves walking back down the road with a vague sense that they'd meant to do something up there and couldn't remember what.

Harry Potter woke up inside it at five in the morning, the way he always did, without an alarm.

He lay still for a moment in the dark and listened. Rain on canvas, light and steady. The creek forty yards down the slope. A varied thrush somewhere off in the hemlocks, giving its one long note. The faint, almost subsonic hum of the wards, which was less a sound than a pressure behind the ears, and which was exactly where it had been when he'd gone to sleep.

Nothing on the line.

He got up.

The bedroom was small and plain: a bed, a trunk, a chair, a lamp. He pulled on running clothes and went out through the corridor — flagstone floor, rough plaster walls, a run of iron hooks with a black wool overcoat on one of them — and through the kitchen, where he put the kettle on the cast-iron range without stopping, and out through the canvas flap into the cold.

He ran the spur road down to the river and the trail up the far bank to the ridge and back. Six miles. He ran it in the dark without a light, and he didn't slip, and when he came back up the last of the hill to the tent the kettle had just started to whistle, because he'd timed it that way on the second day and had been timing it that way since.

He made tea. Strong, a splash of milk, no sugar. He put a record on.

The record player sat on a sideboard in the kitchen next to two wooden crates of vinyl that had come across the Atlantic shrunk down to the size of a box of matches in his coat pocket. He'd picked the albums for the trip with more care than he'd picked the field kit. Zeppelin, all of it, up to *Presence.* Bad Company. *Who's Next.* *Machine Head.* *Sheer Heart Attack.* Two AC/DC records that he'd allowed on the grounds that Bon Scott had been born in Scotland and therefore counted. This morning he put on *Led Zeppelin II,* low, and stood at the window with his tea while the first track came in on that long, building riff.

The window looked out on a meadow full of morning sun and wildflowers that did not exist. He'd charmed it on the first night because the actual view was rain and the actual view could get stuffed.

Then he went to look at the map.

---

The map took up the whole of a long oak table in the room he called the study, and it was carved out of a single slab of old-growth Sitka spruce he'd felled himself on the second day, from a windthrow up near the Sol Duc where nobody would miss it. It showed the western half of the Olympic Peninsula in relief: the mountains pushing up through the wood in the east, the long river valleys running down to the sea in the west — Hoh, Bogachiel, Calawah, Sol Duc, Quillayute — the coast with its ragged edge of sea stacks, the town of Forks sitting in its clearing like a coin dropped on a forest floor. The reservation at La Push, marked in a fine line he'd drawn from a Bureau of Indian Affairs survey map and then corrected against what he'd measured himself. A small square in a clearing by the river, east of town, that he'd never been within four miles of.

And over all of it, the net.

It glowed faintly against the grain of the wood, a web of silver threads finer than hair. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They ran from the coast to the foothills and from the Hoh to the Sol Duc in long overlapping lines that crossed and recrossed at nodes the size of pinheads — twelve hundred and forty-one of those, each one marking a stone he'd set by hand in the forest, a river cobble with a rune cut into its underside, buried a hand's depth in the dirt and keyed into the whole. Eight days to lay. He'd done it on foot, mostly, Apparating from node to node with a pack full of stones, cutting runes by firelight until his fingers bled and then healing them and cutting more.

The net was a snare.

He'd started calling it the springe, after the old English word for a poacher's noose, because the first working version had been built on the same principle as the snares he'd read about in a book once, years ago, when he'd been teaching himself everything he could find about traps. A bent sapling under tension. A noose. A trigger. Something walks through the noose and touches the trigger and the sapling lets go.

The magic was less simple. The magic had taken him five months in the Department and six weeks of field adjustments, and three of the field adjustments had been about the trigger.

Because the trigger had to be selective. The net couldn't go off for an elk, or a black bear, or a hiker, or a logging truck. It couldn't go off for the Quileute — whatever they were, and he had a working theory, and the working theory was large and four-legged — and it couldn't go off for the eight vampires in the square by the river east of town, because those eight vampires were marked in the file in Croaker's own handwriting: VALUABLE ALIVE. NO CONTACT WITHOUT AUTHORISATION. And he hadn't had a single trace sample from any of them, because getting a trace sample would have meant getting close, and he had no idea how close was too close.

So he'd keyed the trigger to diet.

That had been the second adjustment. It had come to him on day four, sitting on a log above the Hoh at two in the morning, thinking about the file — about the small dark girl's eyes, in the one blurred photograph from Volterra, gold as an owl's, against the deep crimson eyes of the hooded figures who'd pulled the boy back into the shadows. *Different diets,* the Volterra watcher had written in the margin. *Animal blood vs. human? Speculative.* Whatever they were, these things were made of what they ate. The venom carried it. You could, if you were patient and good at your job and willing to spend three nights in a row doing arithmancy on the back of a Thriftway receipt, build a ward that tasted the air for it.

The springe ignored cold, and speed, and skin like marble. It asked one question of whatever crossed it: whether the venom in it had been made out of people.

He'd taken to calling that part of the stack the vegetarian clause.

He bent over the map and ran his eye along the lines. All silver. All quiet. The twelve hundred and forty-one nodes winking faintly, steady as stars. At the northeast corner of the table, on a little brass post like the ones on a fishing rod, a silver bell the size of his thumbnail hung motionless on a hair-thin wire. It had been hanging motionless for twelve days.

Two hikers had gone missing at Lake Crescent on Sunday.

Lake Crescent was twenty-five miles northeast, outside the net, on the far side of the Sol Duc valley. He'd heard about it on the radio on Monday morning, two students from Olympia, a boy and a girl, last seen at the Storm King trailhead. The Park Service was searching. The Park Service was going to find a pair of trekking poles and a dropped water bottle and a great deal of nothing.

Harry stood over the map and looked at the empty country between Lake Crescent and the edge of his net and drank his tea.

*Come on, then,* he thought. *You're hungry. You've had a taste. Come down to the coast where it's quiet.*

The bell hung still.

He went to check on the rest of the house.

---

The tent was a tent the way the Tardis was a police box.

He'd built most of it himself, over four years, in the evenings and on the weekends and on the long nights when he didn't sleep; the Department's standard field tent was perfectly adequate and he'd taken it apart in the first month to see how it worked and then built his own. Eleven rooms off a central corridor. The kitchen, which had a proper range and a proper kettle and a refrigerator charmed cold that ran on nothing at all. The study, with the map. A library — four walls of shelves, floor to ceiling, and on the bottom shelf of the far wall thirty-three black notebooks, numbered on the spine in white paint, one for every three months since September of 1998. Most of what he knew was in those notebooks. Most of what he was, too, probably, if he was honest about it, which he tried not to be before breakfast.

A training room with a sprung floor and padded walls that had been scorched in so many places it looked like a leopard. A workshop with a rune bench, a forge, and a lathe. A garage, which held a 1959 Triumph Bonneville that had once belonged to Sirius Black and which had come apart in midair over Little Whinging in the summer of 1997, and which Harry had spent two and a half years rebuilding, piece by piece, out of the wreck Arthur Weasley had saved in a shed. It ran. It flew. It leaked oil on the flagstones and he'd never been able to find from where.

The armory.

He stopped in the door of that one and looked, the way he always did, the way he imagined some people stopped in the doors of churches.

It was a long narrow room lined in oak, and racks ran down both walls. Wands, a few — spares, mostly, and one Bulgarian blackthorn he'd taken off a man in Sofia who'd no longer needed it. Knives. A goblin-made machete that held an edge on anything. And guns. Muggle guns, a dozen and a half of them, cleaned and oiled and racked, every one engraved along the receiver or the slide or the barrel in fine dense lines of runework that he'd cut himself under a jeweler's loupe.

He'd learned to shoot from a sergeant at Hereford who'd been told Harry was a liaison officer from a department he'd never heard of and had never once asked a question about why Harry's targets kept bursting into flame. He'd learned the rest — the part where you took the oldest, cleverest, most dangerous trick Muggles had ever invented and made it worse — in the workshop, alone, over eleven months, with a notebook and a great deal of swearing and two sets of eyebrows that had needed regrowing.

He'd been born into a world that thought a wand was the most dangerous thing a person could carry. He'd lived long enough to disagree.

A Remington 870 with a shortened barrel and a receiver engraved with a stack of hexes that he thought of, privately, as the Cerberus. A lever-action Marlin in .45-70, which he'd bought because it was beautiful and kept because it was terrifying. A pair of stubby Heckler & Koch submachine guns he'd never yet needed and would have been very surprised to. A Barrett, broken down in a case, with a rune-stack along the barrel that he had tested exactly once, on a hillside in the Cairngorms, and had then stood looking at the hillside for a long quiet while.

And on a velvet-lined shelf at the end, by itself, a Colt M1911 in .45 ACP with a worn blued finish and walnut grips, and a slide engraved so densely with runes that from three feet away it looked like it had been wrapped in black lace.

He took it down. He checked it, the way the sergeant had taught him, the way he'd done ten thousand times — magazine out, slide back, chamber empty, look, feel — and then he took a magazine from the drawer beneath the shelf and looked at the top round. Fat, short, copper-jacketed, and if you looked very closely at the base of it, ringed with a line of characters so fine they looked like a scratch.

Seven rounds in the magazine. One more in the chamber. Eight of the most expensive bullets on Earth, by labor; each one had taken him most of an evening.

He loaded the pistol and put it in the shoulder rig on the hook by the door, under the overcoat, where it would be when he needed it.

Then he went to look at the specimen room, because it was on the way to the shower.

---

The specimen room was cold.

That was deliberate. It was the largest room in the tent and the plainest: white tile walls, a white tile floor, bright light from no visible source. Two long steel tables, runed along their edges. A sink. Instruments laid out on a steel tray under a cloth. And in the middle of the floor, set into the tile in silver inlay, a circle nine feet across, a containment ring with three concentric bands of runework that had cost him more sleep than anything else in the tent.

Empty, at the moment. Waiting.

Along the far wall, in glass-fronted cases, lived the room's permanent residents. A coil of something dark and flat and faintly rippling in a lead-lined jar — two square feet of Lethifold, taken off a beach in Papua New Guinea in 2003, still alive in the way a starfish arm stays alive, still trying very patiently to get out. A Nundu's canine tooth the length of his hand. The skull of something from the Sonoran desert that a local had called a chupacabra and that had, in fact, been nothing of the kind. And, in a sealed jar of cloudy liquid on the end of the bottom shelf, a human hand, gray and wrinkled and waterlogged, which he'd taken from the black lake in a cave on the Cornish coast in the autumn of 1999, and which still, once an hour, on the hour, slowly flexed its fingers.

He'd gone back to the cave a year after the war because he'd wanted to understand what had nearly drowned him. He'd been going back to places like that ever since. Croaker called it his *thoroughness.* Hermione, the last time he'd seen her, at Ron's birthday, two years ago, had called it something else, and they hadn't spoken much since.

He looked at the empty circle in the floor.

Then he went and had a shower, and shaved, and put on the suit.

---

He drove into town in the Chevelle.

He'd bought it on his second day from a man in Port Angeles with a gravel lot and a cardboard sign — a 1970 Chevelle SS 454, black over black, with a hood like the deck of an aircraft carrier and an engine note that rattled the windows of the houses he drove past. He'd paid cash. He'd told the man with the gravel lot that he was a writer, over from England, doing a long piece for a magazine about the rainforest, and that he'd always wanted to drive an American car, and the man had looked at the cash and at Harry's accent and had said, *Well, buddy, you came to the right place.*

It was a terrible car for a spy. That was most of why he'd bought it. Nobody who was trying not to be noticed would drive a car you could hear from a mile off. Nobody who was trying to hide would walk into the same diner at the same time every morning and sit in the same booth and order the same thing, and learn the name of every waitress and the name of every waitress's kid, and tip twenty-five percent.

Small towns noticed hiding. So you stood in the middle of the room in a bright shirt and let them get bored of you.

The diner was on the highway at the south end of town, a long low building with a gravel lot and a sign that hadn't been repainted since about 1985. He parked the Chevelle where he always parked it, nose out, with a clear line to the road. He went in. The bell over the door jangled. The smell hit him — coffee, bacon fat, maple syrup, the yeast of the morning's bread — and the noise: a dozen loggers and fishermen and county road workers at the counter and the booths, a radio in the kitchen playing country music, the hiss of the grill.

"Morning, Harry," said Dot from behind the counter.

"Morning, Dot."

Dot was sixty-one and had worked at the diner for thirty-four years, and she had a son in the Coast Guard at Port Angeles and a granddaughter who wanted to be a marine biologist and a husband named Earl who was, depending on the day, either the love of her life or a dead man walking. She knew everything that happened in Forks, usually before it happened, and she had decided on Harry's third morning that he was too thin and was going to be fed whether he liked it or not.

He slid into the booth at the back, the one by the kitchen door, with his back to the corner. From there he could see the front door, the side door, the kitchen pass, the window onto the lot, and the reflection of the whole room in the glass of the pie case.

The pie case.

He looked at it with an emotion that he would not have admitted to in front of anybody in the Department.

He had been in America for nineteen days, and five of them had been during the week of a holiday that seemed to consist entirely of pie. Nobody had warned him. Nobody in the Department had thought to put it in the briefing. Pumpkin, and pecan, and apple, and something called a sweet potato pie that he still hadn't got to the bottom of, and a marionberry pie, which had turned out to be a kind of blackberry from Oregon and which had made him briefly reconsider his whole position on the colonies. The holiday was over. The pie remained. Dot said they'd keep making it through Christmas because people got sulky if they didn't.

"The usual?" Dot said, appearing at his elbow with the coffee pot.

"Please. And—"

"And a slice of the apple, heated up, with the cheddar. At seven in the morning. I know, hon. I've got eyes." She poured. "You're gonna die."

"Everybody's gonna die, Dot."

"Not of pie at seven in the morning, they're not. That's a special kind of stupid." She looked at him over her glasses. "You look tired."

"Long night. Writing."

"Uh-huh. You find any of that rainforest yet?"

"Bits of it. It's mostly wet."

She snorted and went away. He drank his coffee and watched the room.

He'd been watching it for nineteen days. He could have drawn it from memory. The two Hendricks brothers at the end of the counter, who ran a gyppo logging outfit up the Bogachiel and hadn't spoken a civil word to each other since 1996. The table of retired men by the window who argued about the Mariners every morning with a passion Harry had only previously seen in the stands at a Chudley Cannons home match. The girl who worked the register on weekdays, who was seventeen and was going to Western Washington next fall and who had, on his second morning, flirted with him so badly and so bravely that he had wanted to give her a medal. He'd been polite. He was always polite. He tipped twenty-five percent.

That was the job. Pattern of life. You learned the town, and the town learned you, and you made yourself into a part of the furniture so ordinary and so slightly ridiculous that nobody ever thought to wonder what you were doing out on the Forest Service roads at two in the morning. *Oh, that's just the English guy. The writer. Drives that loud car. Eats pie for breakfast.*

At seven twenty-eight, the bell over the door jangled, and Charlie Swan came in out of the rain.

---

The chief of police took off his hat, shook it once, said, "Morning, Dot," and sat down at the counter on the third stool from the end, which was his. Dot was already pouring.

Harry watched him in the pie case.

Charlie Swan was in the file. Not much of him — a paragraph, a photograph, a date of birth. *Father of Isabella Swan (see Volterra, March 2006). Divorced. Chief of Police, Forks, since 1990s. No known magical or other unusual affiliations. Assess.* And Harry had assessed, over nineteen mornings, in the way you assessed anyone. Forty-something. Fished. Watched sports. Didn't say much. Didn't miss much either. Drank his coffee black and ate his eggs over medium and read the *Forks Forum* from back to front, starting with the police blotter, like a man checking his own homework.

His daughter had married Edward Cullen on the thirteenth of August. Ten days later she had been reported seriously ill — some rare thing, a specialist clinic, the town had been full of it. Then, sometime in September, the stories had stopped. Charlie had stopped looking like a man waiting for a phone call. And had started, instead, looking like a man who'd been told something he'd decided not to think about.

Harry had seen that look before. He'd seen it on Arthur Weasley, the summer the Ministry fell.

Charlie turned his head and found him in the booth and nodded.

Harry nodded back.

It had been going on like that for a week and a half. Then, last Thursday — the holiday, when the diner had been half empty and Charlie had come in alone and sat for a long time over his coffee with nowhere to be until his dinner at three — Harry had come up to the counter to pay and asked, in the most offhand voice he owned, whether anybody fished the Bogachiel this time of year. And Charlie had looked up from the paper like a man hearing his own name in a foreign country, and they'd talked for forty minutes about steelhead.

He came over now. That was new.

"Mind if I—?" Charlie said, coffee in hand.

"Please."

Charlie sat down across from him in the booth. He sat — Harry noticed — on the side that put his back to the door, and he didn't seem to like it much, and he did it anyway.

"Heard a rumor you went out to the Bogachiel on Saturday," Charlie said.

"I did. Your gravel bar, below the hatchery bridge."

"And?"

"Nothing. Not a sniff. Lost two of your recommended flies on a log." Harry held up a hand. "I'm not blaming you. I'm a rubbish fisherman."

"You're fishing too early. It's still the fall run, the good fish don't come in till December. You want to give it two weeks." Charlie drank his coffee. His eyes went to the window. "And you might want to stay off the trails up north for a while. Lake Crescent, Sol Duc, all through there."

"The two kids from Olympia."

"Yeah." Charlie's voice didn't change much. His face did, a little, around the mouth. "Park Service is out with dogs. Clallam County's helping. They found the girl's pack this morning, off the Barnes Creek trail, about two miles up."

"Just the pack?"

"Just the pack." Charlie looked at his coffee. "Probably a cougar. We get cougars."

"Do you get many cougars that take two people at once?"

Charlie looked up at him.

It lasted a second. Maybe less. A flat, assessing, cop's look, that went over Harry's face and his suit and his hands on the table and the coffee and the pie, and came back to his eyes, and stayed there.

"No," Charlie said. "We don't."

Harry nodded slowly. He took a bite of the apple pie. It was very good. He'd have liked to be enjoying it more.

"Sorry," he said. "Writer. I ask too many questions. Occupational hazard."

"Uh-huh." Charlie kept looking at him for another second. Then he looked away, out the window at the rain on the lot, and something in his shoulders let go. "You and everybody else in this town."

They talked about the steelhead for another ten minutes. Charlie told him about the year he'd hooked a twenty-pound buck on the Calawah and lost it at the net and had dreams about it for a month. Harry told him about fishing with a friend's father in a lake in Devon when he was fourteen, and catching nothing, and falling in. It was true. Most of what he said in this town was true. It was easier that way, and there was less to remember.

At seven fifty-one Charlie's radio crackled on his belt, and he stood up and put his hat on and dropped three dollars on the table.

"Two weeks," he said. "Then go back to the gravel bar. Use something orange."

"Orange. Got it."

"And stay off the trails up north."

"Will do, Chief."

Charlie went out. The bell jangled. Through the window Harry watched him cross the lot to the cruiser in the rain and get in, and sit for a second with his hands on the wheel, looking at nothing, before he started the engine.

Dot came by with the coffee pot.

"He likes you," she said, pouring.

"Does he?"

"He doesn't sit with people, hon. He sits with Billy Black, and he used to sit with Harry Clearwater, God rest him. That's the list." She looked at him over her glasses. "And he sat with you."

Harry looked at the empty seat across the booth.

"Well," he said. "I'm very likeable."

"You're a pain in my behind is what you are," Dot said. "Eat your pie."

---

He spent the middle of the day being a writer.

He drove out to Rialto Beach and took photographs of the sea stacks in the rain with a Nikon that didn't have any film in it. He went to the library and checked out a history of the Olympic timber industry and a field guide to the birds of the Pacific Northwest and sat at a table in the window for an hour and a half and read both of them, actually read them, because the librarian was a busy, observant woman who'd once asked him a question about an earlier chapter. He bought a sandwich at the Thriftway. He walked around the town once, in the rain, slowly, past the high school, past the police station, past the little white house on the corner where the chief of police lived alone with a cruiser in the driveway and a light on in the kitchen.

He did not drive east, toward the river.

He'd been wanting to, for nineteen days. Every day the want got a little bit worse. It sat in his chest like an itch he couldn't reach. Eight of them, in a white house on the river, four miles from where he'd stood at the edge of his own limit and looked through the trees. Eight of them who drank animal blood and went to high school — had gone, until this summer, the doctor's children, and the doctor himself working twelve-hour shifts at the county hospital with his hands in people's open wounds and never, by all accounts, once losing his composure. Who'd lived in this town on and off for years. Whom the town liked, mostly, and gossiped about, endlessly.

And one of them could read minds. He was nearly sure of it. The Volterra watcher had seen the boy react, in the square, to the cloaked figures' approach, a full second before they came into view — *as though warned,* the report said. *Precognitive? Telepathic? Speculative.* There'd been a girl, too, small and dark, who'd driven a stolen yellow Porsche through the gate of Volterra like she knew exactly where the barricade would open.

Something in that house could hear him coming. Or see it. And the file said VALUABLE ALIVE, in Croaker's own hand.

So he stayed four miles off. He watched the square on the map instead, and their cars, through a scrying glass keyed to the steel of their door handles, which he'd charmed one at a time in the hospital car park on his fourth day without getting within two hundred yards of the doctor. He learned their routines from a distance. He let them alone.

He was good at waiting. He'd been doing it for a long time.

---

He was back at the tent by two. He changed out of the suit, put on a jumper and an old pair of jeans, made a pot of tea, and sat down at the rune bench in the workshop with a magazine of empty .45 cases and the loupe and the burin and the notebook, and started cutting rounds.

It was slow work. It was supposed to be. The runes went in a spiral around the base of the case, so small that each one had to be cut in the space between two heartbeats, and the stack that went into them had nineteen separate enchantments layered one on another in a particular order, and if you got the order wrong it wouldn't work, and if you got it very wrong you'd find out in the most immediate way possible. He'd got the order wrong a few times, early on. He'd been younger then.

He'd been younger for a lot of things.

He'd been seventeen in the spring of 1998, standing in the Great Hall with the elder wand in his hand and Tom Riddle's body on the floor and the sun coming up through the windows, and every single person in the room looking at him. He'd been eighteen when Kingsley Shacklebolt had offered him a place in the Auror Office, no exams, no training, just *walk in,* and he'd said he'd think about it. He'd been eighteen and a month when an old man in a moth-eaten tweed jacket had knocked on the door of number twelve, Grimmauld Place, with a bottle of Ogden's Old Firewhisky under his arm, and sat down at the kitchen table, and poured two glasses, and said:

*My name is Saul Croaker. I work in the Department of Mysteries. You've died twice, Mr. Potter, and you keep failing to stay dead. My department would very much like to know how.* He'd pushed a glass across the table. *So, I suspect, would you.*

Harry had looked at the glass for a long time.

He'd started the next Monday.

He'd been bad at everything. That was the part nobody would have believed, if anybody had ever been told. He'd been the Boy Who Lived, and the Chosen One, and the man who'd killed Lord Voldemort, and when he'd walked through the door of Level Nine for the first time he had been, measured honestly against the people in that department, an under-educated, half-trained, lucky teenager with a gift for Defense and a fondness for running headfirst at things. He hadn't taken Arithmancy. He hadn't taken Ancient Runes. He'd scraped through Potions with a cheat sheet in the form of a dead man's textbook. He'd spent his seventh year living in a tent and his sixth year chasing a Horcrux and his fifth year getting tortured with a blood quill and his fourth year—

He'd had a lot to catch up on.

So he'd caught up.

He'd done Arithmancy out of Hermione's old textbooks, with her handwriting all down the margins. He'd done Runes at a kitchen table in Bergen with a seventy-year-old ward-witch who rapped his knuckles with a ruler and called him *gutten.* He'd spent a year in Egypt, on loan, learning to see curse-work from Bill Weasley in the cool dark under the Valley of the Kings. He'd gone back to Hogwarts on Saturdays for three straight years and let Filius Flitwick — who'd been the European dueling champion in his day, and who could still, at an age he refused to disclose, make a grown man cry — throw him around the Charms classroom until he could stand his ground, and then until he could throw back.

And the wand.

It had started with a night in Little Whinging when he'd been fifteen, crouched in an alley with two Dementors coming, and his wand had been lying on the pavement six feet away in the dark, and he'd said *Lumos* in a panic, and the wand had lit. He'd never thought about it much, at the time. He'd thought about it a great deal later. It had taken him fourteen months, after he joined the Department, to make a feather rise off a desk without a wand in his hand. It had taken four years before he stopped carrying one into the field. Somewhere in there he'd stopped needing words, too, and then gestures, until the only thing between what he wanted and what happened was the wanting, and he'd sat up very late one night in the library of Grimmauld Place and written in the nineteenth black notebook *I don't think there's a ceiling,* and then looked at what he'd written for a long time, and then gone to bed.

He'd built things. The springe. The rounds. A way of taking hold of a spell in flight and turning it — catching its momentum the way a judo player catches a punch, and *bending* it — that he'd worked out over the course of a very long winter and still didn't have a proper name for. Croaker called it *the Potter Deflection* in reports, which Harry hated. Harry called it *turning,* which was what it was.

And he'd done things. The Department was a laboratory and it was a library and it was a vault, and it was also, when it needed to be, a knife. He'd been to Sofia, and Kinshasa, and the high desert south of Tucson, and a dozen other places that weren't in any report anyone outside Level Nine would ever read. He'd done work there that he was proud of, and work that he was not, and he wrote all of it down in the black notebooks in the same plain hand because that was what the notebooks were for.

He was cleared for all three of the Unforgivables. Every Unspeakable was. It was in Article Nine of the Department's charter, which existed in exactly one copy, which lived in a room he'd been in twice. He'd used the Imperius more often than he liked to think about. He'd used the Cruciatus Curse twice, both times on people who'd have done much worse to him given the chance, and he'd written both of them down. The other one, once.

He cut a rune. He breathed. He cut another.

Eight years.

On the shelf above the bench, the radio was on low — a classic rock station out of Seattle that came and went with the weather — and somewhere in the static Paul Rodgers was singing about feeling like making love. Harry hummed along under his breath. He'd finished three rounds and started a fourth. Outside the canvas walls the rain had picked up and the light was going, the way it went here in November, early and fast and gray.

At eleven minutes to four, in the study at the far end of the tent, the little silver bell on the brass post at the corner of the map began to ring.

Harry put down the burin.

He sat still for a moment, listening to it. It was a high, sweet, urgent sound, like a fishing bell on a rod, which it was meant to be. It went on ringing.

He stood up and walked to the study, not quickly, and looked down at the map. Out at the western edge of the net, about six miles south and west of the tent, in the hills between the Calawah and the Bogachiel, a single silver thread had gone bright, burning red. As he watched, the red pulsed once, twice, and settled — a steady glowing point, like a coal.

Something had walked into the noose.

Something whose venom had been made out of people.

Harry looked at it for one more second. Then he smiled, slowly. It was not a smile anybody in the diner would have recognized.

"Gotcha," he said.

He went to the door and took the shoulder rig off its hook and put it on. He took off the jumper and put on a white shirt and the charcoal suit jacket and the black wool overcoat, because he was a professional and there were standards, and he tucked a black leather wallet into the inside pocket of the coat. He pulled on a pair of black leather gloves. He reached up and switched off the radio.

Then he turned on his heel, and the tent folded away around him with a sound like a gunshot.

### V. Lassie

*The hills between the Calawah and the Bogachiel — eleven minutes earlier*

Leah smelled it first.

She always did. She was faster than any of them, faster than Sam, faster than Jacob on his best day, and her nose was better, too, and she'd never once heard any of them say so out loud, which was fine, because she heard them think it, which was worse. She was running the high line along the ridge above the Calawah at three-thirty in the afternoon with the light already going gray and soupy under the trees and Seth half a mile off to her left, down in the bottoms, singing to himself. Actually singing. Some song Layla played in her truck. Leah had heard it eleven times in the last two weeks through her brother's skull, and she was going to find the CD and break it in half.

*Can you not,* she thought at him.

*Can I not what?*

*Sing. In my head. Can you not.*

*It's a good song.*

*It's a* terrible *song, Seth. It's a song for people who cry at commercials.*

*You cried at a commercial last week. The one with the dog and the—*

*I will* end *you.*

And then the wind shifted, swinging around out of the northeast, down off the high country, and she stopped so hard her front feet plowed two furrows in the moss.

Sweet.

Sweet and *wrong* — the thick, cloying, overripe stink that went up the back of her nose and burned there like somebody had poured bleach into a bowl of rotting roses. It hit the back of her throat and made her lips pull back off her teeth before she'd even thought about it. Every hair on her spine came up at once.

Seth stopped singing.

*Leah—*

*I've got it.* She had her nose up, working the wind. *Northeast. Coming down off the ridge toward the Bogachiel. One of them. One. It's fresh, Seth, it's* right there—

*Is it — could it be one of the Cullens? Emmett hunts up here sometimes, he—*

*It's not Emmett.*

She knew all their smells by now. She'd had to. Seven of them, eight now with Bella, all with that same sickly-sweet base note, but every one different on top — Emmett like cut cedar and motor oil, Rosalie like expensive soap, Edward like a library, Alice like rain on hot pavement. She'd spent the last three months with her nose full of them. She'd have known any of them in her sleep.

This one smelled like the rest underneath. On top it smelled like old leather and woodsmoke and cheap cologne, and under that, threaded all through it so thick she could almost taste it—

Blood. Human blood. Not old, either. Days old, maybe. Not more.

Her lips came all the way back.

*Seth,* she thought. *Red eyes.*

She felt him feel it. Felt the sunny, singing, golden-retriever part of her brother go away somewhere and something else come up in its place, low and flat and cold, the thing that had been sitting under Seth since the first time he'd phased, and that she'd seen come out in the clearing that summer when the newborns came, and that she'd never in her life wanted to see come out of her baby brother again.

*Where?* Seth said.

*I'm on it. Swing wide, come up on the river side, cut it off from the Bogachiel. Don't let it get to the water.*

*Should I call Jake?*

Jacob was phased-out. He'd been phased-out since noon, sitting on the Cullens' couch with the baby asleep on his chest, and if they phased-in a howl right now he'd have to put her down and get outside and get his clothes off and get here, and by the time he did—

*No time,* she thought. *We've got it.*

She ran.

---

The hills between the Calawah and the Bogachiel were old second-growth, hemlock and Douglas fir and big-leaf maple hung so thick with moss that the trunks looked like they were wearing fur coats, and the ground underneath was sword fern and salal and fallen logs the size of school buses, slick and rotting and soft. In the gray light, in the rain, it was a hard place to run. Leah ran it like a highway. She went over the logs instead of around them. She went through the ferns low and flat, belly almost on the ground, her feet hitting rock and root and soft moss in a rhythm her body knew better than her brain did. The smell got stronger. Thicker. Sweeter. She could feel Seth to her right in her head, a quarter mile off and closing, running the river bottoms, a sandy streak through the gray.

There.

A flicker of movement up ahead, through the trees, a hundred yards off. Pale. A man's shape, tall and lean, in a long dark coat, walking — just *walking,* unhurried, down the slope through the ferns like he was taking a Sunday stroll, his head tipped back a little like he was smelling the rain.

Then he heard her.

She saw him hear her. Saw his head come around, fast, inhumanly fast, the way their heads always came around, no wind-up and no follow-through. Saw his face. Long and narrow and white as paper. Dark hair, slicked back. A thin mouth.

Red eyes. Bright, deep, fresh crimson. Two kids from Olympia's worth of red.

He looked at her — a gray wolf the size of a horse, coming at him out of the ferns at forty miles an hour with her teeth bared — and his eyes went wide.

Then he ran.

They always ran, the smart ones. Leah had stopped being insulted by it a long time ago. The dumb ones stood and fought because they'd never seen a wolf before and thought they were the scariest thing in the woods. The smart ones took one look and turned around and went, because a vampire on open ground could outrun anything on Earth except a Quileute.

Except her.

He went down the slope like a dropped stone, his coat flaring out behind him, so fast he blurred. Straight downhill. Toward the Bogachiel. Toward the water. If he hit the river he'd go under and she'd lose the scent and he'd come out a mile downstream and be in Kalaloch by dark and in Seattle by morning and some other kids from some other town would go out to some other trailhead next week and—

*Seth!*

*I see him! I see him, I'm coming!*

Her brother came up out of the river bottoms on the left like a sandy-colored freight train, angling hard across the slope to cut the vampire off from the water, and the vampire saw him and jinked right, uphill, back toward her, and she lunged for him and missed by a foot, her teeth snapping shut on nothing but the cold wind of his passing. He went sideways along the slope, faster than anything had a right to go, and they went after him, the two of them, Leah high and Seth low, driving him between them like a pair of sheepdogs driving a wolf — which was funny, a little, if she'd had time to think about it. She didn't.

He was fast. He was so fast. He went up the far side of a ravine in three jumps that were really one jump and she went up after him in two that were really four, her claws ripping the moss off the rock in long green strips, and she came over the lip of the ravine and he was there, twenty yards ahead, running flat out through an open stand of big old hemlocks where the ground was clear and soft and brown with needles—

And then his left foot came down, and the forest grabbed him.

---

She would try, later, to explain it to Jacob, and to the Cullens, and to Layla, and she would never quite get it right.

There was a sound. A high, sharp, snapping *thrum,* like a cable under enormous tension being cut, or a giant plucking one string on a guitar the size of a house. The air in front of her *jumped.* She saw it — a ripple, like heat shimmer over a highway, running up from the ground at the vampire's feet, fast as a whip-crack. Something she couldn't see snapped tight around his left ankle. And he went up.

Straight up. Feet first. So fast she lost him for a second.

He came to a stop with a jerk that she *heard* — a sound like a boulder hitting the end of a chain — twenty feet in the air, hanging upside down by one ankle from the lowest branch of the biggest hemlock in the stand. His coat had fallen down over his head and shoulders. His arms hung straight down toward the ground. And he didn't move.

He didn't *move.*

A vampire didn't hold still unless it wanted to. That was the creepiest thing about them, the first thing you learned, the thing that made your skin crawl — when they stopped moving they stopped completely, like a statue, like a dead thing. This was different. This was *stuck.* She could see it now as she skidded to a stop underneath him in a spray of wet needles, her chest heaving, Seth coming up on her left. She could see the cords standing out in his white neck where the coat had fallen away. She could see the fingers of his hanging hands trembling — just barely, just a fraction of a millimeter, the way a man's hands shake when he's trying with all his strength to lift something that won't lift. And she could see his eyes, upside down, crimson, rolling wildly in a face that was otherwise as frozen and white as a plaster cast.

He couldn't move. Something was holding every single part of him in place, from the inside.

He was swinging, very slightly. Back and forth. Back and forth.

*What,* Seth thought, *the* hell.

*I don't know.*

*Did — did you do that?*

*Do I look like I did that?* She circled the tree, fast, her nose working. The sweet stink of the vampire was all over everything. And under it — faint, sharp, clean, like the air after lightning hit the beach—

She stopped.

*Seth. Smell that.*

He smelled it. She felt him smell it, and she felt him *know* it, all at once, with a jolt that went through both their heads like a dropped wire.

*That's — Leah, that's what Layla's house smells like.*

There was a sound behind them like a gunshot.

---

They both spun around, so fast and so hard that Seth stumbled and caught himself on a root, and Leah's lips went back so far her gums showed and the growl came up out of her chest all on its own, deep enough to shake the needles on the ground.

There was a man standing in the clearing.

He hadn't been there. There was no sound of him coming, no smell, no movement in the trees. He had simply *not* been there, and now he was, standing twenty feet away in the open between two big hemlocks with his hands at his sides and the rain falling on him.

He was tall. Six-two, maybe, she guessed, and broad through the shoulders, with the kind of build that came from work more than from a gym. Black hair, short at the sides and messy on top, already going wet and flat in the rain. A couple of days of dark stubble. Somewhere in his twenties, she'd have said, late twenties. His face was a plain, square, ordinary face, the kind you'd pass on the street without looking twice.

His eyes were very green.

And he was dressed — she would think about this later, a lot — like a cop from a TV show. Like a fed. A charcoal suit, a white shirt open at the collar, no tie. A long black wool overcoat over the suit, open, falling to just below his knees. Black leather gloves. Black boots, heavy-soled and mud-spattered and laced up tight over the trouser cuffs, which was the only thing about him that looked like it belonged in the woods.

He smelled like rain and pine and wet stone. Coffee. Gun oil. Apples, faintly, and something like sharp cheese. And over all of it, *through* all of it, so strong it made her eyes water — that smell. Ozone. Lightning on the beach. Layla's house times a thousand.

His heart was beating. Slow and steady and even. Sixty beats a minute. Maybe less.

Two wolves the size of horses were standing in front of him with their teeth out, and his heart was going sixty beats a minute.

He looked at them. At her, and then at Seth, and then back at her. His face didn't change much. One eyebrow went up, maybe a quarter-inch.

Then he reached into his coat — slowly, with two fingers, the way you'd do it in front of a cop — and took out a black leather wallet and flipped it open and held it up at them, at arm's length, the way people did on TV.

There was a gold badge on one side. An ID card on the other with his photograph on it. Leah could read it from twenty feet. She could read a license plate from a quarter mile.

U.S. DEPARTMENT OF HOMELAND SECURITY, it said. SPECIAL AGENT. And a name she'd never be able to remember afterward, no matter how hard she tried, as though her eyes had slid off it.

"Evening, all," the man said.

He was English. Really, actually, honestly English, the voice clipped and dry and a little rough at the edges, like a guy in a movie about a heist.

"Right, then," he said. "This situation is contained. I'm going to have to ask the pair of you to vacate the premises, please, as this is now the site of a national security operation." He snapped the wallet shut and put it away. "Thanks ever so much for your help. Mind how you go."

Leah stared at him.

Seth stared at him.

Twenty feet above all of their heads, the vampire went on swinging gently from its branch. Back and forth. Back and forth.

*Is he,* Leah thought, slowly, very clearly, *talking to us? We're wolves. Is this guy seriously standing in the middle of the woods showing his badge to a couple of* wolves—

"I am, yes, Lassie," the man said. "Do keep up."

---

The world went very quiet.

Leah's growl stopped. It just stopped, cut off in her chest like somebody had pulled a plug. She felt her ears go flat back against her skull without asking her permission.

Beside her, Seth had gone completely, utterly rigid.

*He—* Seth thought. *Leah, he — did he just — he heard you. He* heard *you. Like Edward. He can hear us. He's like Edward.*

The man in the overcoat turned his head and looked at Seth. Right in the eyes. And Leah felt it — felt it in the link, in the place in the back of her mind where she and Seth and Jacob and the rest of them all ran together — like a draft. Like a cold breath of air from a door somebody had opened in a house she'd thought she lived in alone. Something touched the edge of the pack mind. Something that wasn't wolf at all. It listened, very lightly, without pushing, the way you'd lay your fingertips on a window to feel the bass of a party next door.

"Not quite," the man said to Seth, mildly. "He doesn't have to look at you, I'm guessing. I do. Who's Edward?"

Neither of them answered. Neither of them *thought* an answer, very loudly.

"Hm," the man said. "The tall one with the Volvo, I expect. Thank you, that's very helpful." He looked back at Leah. "You can stop doing that, by the way. I'm not going to steal your PIN number. Don't mind me. Occupational hazard."

*Occupational hazard,* Leah thought, a little hysterically. *He's reading my mind and it's an* occupational hazard—

"Isn't it just," the man agreed.

He turned his back on them.

He just turned around, with two wolves the size of horses standing twenty feet behind him who could have taken his head off his shoulders before he finished a breath, and looked up at the vampire hanging in the hemlock, the way you'd look up at a light fixture you were thinking about changing. He tipped his head to one side. He studied it for a long second.

Then he lifted one gloved hand and crooked a finger, and whatever was holding the vampire's ankle let go.

The vampire dropped.

Twenty feet, head-first, rigid as a board, straight down toward the forest floor like a falling statue—

And the man stepped forward and caught it.

He caught it under one arm. Around the middle. The whole stiff length of it — six feet of stone-hard, stone-heavy vampire coming down from twenty feet up — and he took the impact with a little grunt and a bend of the knees, the way you'd catch a heavy sack somebody had tossed to you off the back of a truck, and then he straightened up. The vampire's body stayed exactly as stiff as it had been in the air. Its arms stayed straight down at its sides. Its legs stayed straight. Its head stuck out in front of the man's chest and its feet stuck out behind his back, and he shifted it a little on his hip, hitching it up to get a better grip, and tucked it under his arm like a surfboard.

Like a surfboard. Like he was about to walk down to the beach.

The vampire's eyes rolled madly in its frozen face. It was trying to scream. Leah could see the muscles of its throat working, straining. No sound came out.

"There we are," the man said, a little breathless. He patted the vampire on its rigid hip. "Bit heavier than you look, aren't you? It's all that dense tissue. Never mind."

He turned back around to face them with the vampire tucked under his arm, and raised his eyebrows.

"Was there something else?"

*Who* are *you,* Leah thought. She couldn't help it. *What* are *you? You smell like* — *you can hear us — you* picked up a vampire—

"Oh, I'm nobody," the man said. "Civil servant. Very boring. Pension." He nodded at the vampire under his arm. "This one, on the other hand. This one's a bad lad. Aren't you, sunshine? You've been very busy up at Lake Crescent." The vampire's eyes rolled toward him and then away. The man's voice didn't change at all. "Two students from Olympia, on Sunday. Twenty and twenty-one. Her name was Kaitlyn. She was studying to be a nurse."

The rain came down. Nobody moved.

"So," the man said pleasantly, "he's coming with me. And you two are going to trot along home. Off you pop, Lassie." He looked at Seth. "You too, Balto."

Seth's ears went up.

*Balto,* Seth thought. A little wounded. *Balto was a* hero, *actually. He ran the serum to Nome. In the blizzard. There's a statue of him in Central Park.*

"He did," the man agreed gravely. "Very good dog."

*Seth,* Leah thought, *shut up. Shut up, shut up, stop thinking at him, he can—*

But Seth wasn't listening to her. She could feel him — feel his mind going fast, faster than she'd ever felt it go, racing and slipping and catching, putting things together. The smell. The ozone smell. The lightning smell, Layla's smell. The sound like a gunshot when the man showed up out of nowhere. The vampire hanging in the tree, stuck like a fly in amber. *Layla had said something.* At dinner. Weeks ago. About how she got around. About the thing she did, the disappearing thing, how it went *crack* and you were somewhere else — *it's called Apparating, it's how we all get around, the No-Majs just think it's a car backfiring*—

The thought came up through her brother's mind bright and sudden and clear as a bell, and Leah felt it go out of him, out into the open air of the link where the stranger was listening, and she couldn't do a single thing to stop it.

*Oh my God. He's a wizard. He's a* wizard, *like Layla, he's got to be, that's what that sound was, he* Apparated—

The man in the overcoat went still.

---

He'd been still before, in the way people were still. Easy. Loose. Weight on one foot. The vampire tucked under his arm and his free hand at his side and his face pleasant and bored and a little amused. Now he went still in a different way. Leah felt it in her fur before she saw it. Every bit of loose, easy amusement just *went out of him,* the way the light goes out of a room when somebody flips a switch, and what was left behind was very quiet, and very focused, and very cold.

He didn't move for a long moment. The rain fell on his shoulders. The vampire's eyes rolled wildly in its frozen face.

Then, slowly, deliberately, the man bent his knees and set the vampire down.

He did it carefully. He leaned it upright against the trunk of the hemlock, stiff as a board, like a man propping a ladder against a wall. He straightened. He tugged once at the cuffs of his gloves.

Then he turned around, and looked at them.

It was the same face. The same plain, square face. But he was *looking* at them now — really looking, at both of them, one and then the other, those green eyes going from Seth to Leah to Seth again and stopping there, and there was no pleasant boredom in it at all. It was the way Leah had seen Carlisle look at an X-ray. It was the way she'd seen Jasper look at a clearing before a fight. It went into her like a nail.

She felt that draft in the pack mind again. Stronger. Much stronger. The door in the back of her head swinging wide open and a cold wind blowing through.

"Now *that*," the man said quietly, "is interesting."

He took a step toward them. Just one.

"Say that again, Balto," he said. "That word. Say it slowly."

Seth didn't. Seth couldn't. Leah could feel him trying not to think it, the way you try not to think about a white bear, and failing, and the word going round and round in his head, *wizard, wizard, Layla, Apparate, wizard*—

"Ah," the man said. "And there's a name. *Layla.*" He tipped his head. "Layla. The house out on the Calawah road with all the very nice wards on it. The ones I've been politely not looking at for going on three weeks."

*Oh God,* Leah thought. *Oh no.*

"You two shouldn't know that word," the man said. His voice was very soft. "Not the way you're thinking it. You shouldn't know it at all."

He looked at Seth. He looked, Leah realized, at what was *in* Seth. She could feel him turning things over in there, light and fast and precise as a surgeon, like flipping through files in a drawer.

"Good God," the man said, almost to himself. "How *many* of you—"

Seth moved.

He didn't think about it. Leah would know, afterward, that he hadn't thought about it, because she'd been inside his head the whole time and there'd been nothing in it but a sudden blank white bolt of pure instinct, *trouble, real trouble, something big* — and her brother ducked his head and brought his left foreleg up to his mouth and bit down on the cord around his wrist. Hard.

She heard the obsidian bead crack between his teeth like a knuckle.

The man in the overcoat watched him do it. His eyebrows went up.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, *well done.*"

And the air on the far side of the clearing split open with a crack like a rifle shot.

### VI. The Fourth

*The same clearing — the same second*

The ring burned.

Layla had been at her kitchen table with a jeweler's loupe in one eye and a dragonhide glove on her left hand, bent over a silver reliquary the size of a cigar box that a private client in Guadalajara had paid a great deal of money to have made safe. Colonial, seventeenth century, Spanish silver over a cedar core, and somebody three hundred and fifty years ago had laid a hex inside the hinge that would quietly liquefy the bones of whoever opened the lid. Clever work. Old work. She'd been picking it apart thread by thread for two days and she'd been just about to lift the last knot—

And the ring on her right hand went white-hot against her skin.

She didn't remember standing up. She didn't remember the chair going over. She remembered the wand in her hand and the homing thread under her ribs, pulling like a fishhook, *south and west, six miles, hills, trees, Seth, Seth, Seth*—

She turned on the spot and went.

Apparition along a homing thread was ugly work. The thread chose your landing for you, dropped you at the end of itself wherever that end happened to be, and she came out of the black squeezing nowhere into a stand of old hemlocks in the rain with her knees bent and her weight low and her wand up, because that was how you came out of the black when you didn't know what was waiting on the other side. Eleven years. The body went first.

She saw it all in the first half-second, the way she'd been trained to see a room.

Seth, sandy and huge, his hackles up in a stiff ridge from his skull to his tail, a cracked black bead hanging from the cord on his foreleg. Leah beside him, gray and smaller and lower, her ears pinned flat and her lips peeled back from teeth as long as Layla's fingers. A body propped upright against the trunk of a hemlock like a plank leaned against a wall — a man, white-faced and dark-haired in a long coat, rigid, his arms stiff at his sides — and his *eyes* were open, and moving, rolling wildly, and they were red. Bright, deep, wet crimson.

*If you ever see red eyes in these woods—*

And a man in a black overcoat, standing between her and Seth.

Her wand decided to fire before she did.

It did that, sometimes. Shikoba Wolfe had carved it in 1931 around a tail feather from a Thunderbird out of the Arizona high country, and every Wolfe wand had a reputation — powerful, difficult, temperamental — and hers had a habit she'd never told anyone about, which was that when it sensed real danger close by it didn't always wait to be asked. It bucked in her hand. A Stunning Spell came off the tip, red and hard and fast as a bullet, before she'd even finished choosing a target.

The man in the overcoat lifted one gloved hand, almost lazily, and flicked his index finger.

Like he was flicking a crumb off a tablecloth.

The Stunner *bent.* It went from a straight line to a right angle in the space of a foot, without slowing down, and shot off sideways into the trees and hit the trunk of a Douglas fir thirty yards off with a sound like a car door slamming, and a chunk of bark the size of a dinner plate went spinning away into the ferns.

The man looked at her.

"Well," he said. "Hello to you too."

He was British.

That was the first thing her brain caught on, in the middle of all the other things — *British, he's British* — and it caught on it the way a sleeve catches on a nail, and she had no time to think about why. She was already moving.

---

She had been the second-ranked duelist in North America for three years running. She had been runner-up at the North American Open twice, both times to a seven-foot Hit Wizard from Calgary named Bertrand Fiske who'd been dueling since before she was born, and the second time she'd taken him to the third bell. She had a chain — every serious duelist had a chain, a sequence of spells built and practiced and drilled together until they flowed into one another like a single long movement — and hers was nine spells long and she had never once, in competition or out of it, seen anyone get past the sixth.

She opened with it now. Silent. As fast as she'd ever thrown it.

*Expelliarmus.* Scarlet. At his chest. She was looking for his wand, she realized as she cast — looking at his hands, his sleeves, his coat, waiting to see where the wand would leap from — and there was nothing to see. His hands were empty. Both of them. Black leather gloves, and nothing in them.

He turned the spell aside with the back of two fingers. It went off into the ferns.

*Incarcerous.* Ropes, thick as her wrist, lashing out of the air to bind him. He made a small motion like brushing hair off his forehead and the ropes swung wide around him like they'd hit the curve of a glass bowl and wrapped themselves twice around a hemlock trunk instead.

*Reducto,* at the ground between his feet, to break his footing.

The curse hit the needles and — nothing. It *went out.* She saw it go out. The blue-white blast of it sank into the forest floor like a match dropped in a bucket of water and was gone, and there was a faint hiss, and a wisp of steam.

*Petrificus Totalus. Diffindo. Confringo. Glacius.*

She threw them in a long hard spiral, the way Fiske had taught her by beating her with it, varying the angle and the height and the timing so no two came in on the same line. Body-Bind high. Severing Charm low, at his knees. Blasting Curse wide, to catch him if he moved left. Freezing Charm wide the other way to catch him if he moved right.

He stayed exactly where he was in the rain, feet planted, weight on his back foot, overcoat hanging open, and he *turned* them. One after another. A finger. Two fingers. A flat palm, rotated, like a man waving away smoke. The Body-Bind went up into the branches overhead and a whole limb of hemlock went rigid and stopped swaying. The Severing Charm went down into the forest floor and opened a long, neat, foot-deep slit in the dirt beside his boot. The Blasting Curse he caught with an open hand — *caught it* — and she watched it slow in the air in front of his palm like a fly in syrup, and then he gave it a little push, gently, the way you'd push a child on a swing, and it went off into the woods and blew a fallen log into a shower of rotten wood fifty yards off. The Freezing Charm he just *looked at,* and it fell apart in midair into a puff of white frost that drifted down onto the needles.

"Nice chain," he said. "Very tidy. Ilvermorny? You've got that Thunderbird wrist thing."

She threw the seventh spell.

The seventh was her own. A Bludgeoning Hex she'd rebuilt from the ground up during that long cold winter in Lima, spun around a core of Conjured iron so that it hit like a sledgehammer swung by a troll and couldn't be shielded with any standard charm — a Protego would stop the magic and the iron would go straight through. She had broken Fiske's arm with it the second time they'd dueled. She'd felt terrible about it for a week.

He saw it coming. She saw him see it. His eyes went to it with a little flicker of interest, the first real interest he'd shown, the look of a man spotting an unusual bird.

And he sent it back.

This time he reached out with his left hand, palm up, as though he were going to catch a tossed set of keys, and the spell came into the space just in front of his fingers and *stopped,* and hung there for a fraction of a heartbeat, spinning — she could see the dark iron core of it whirling inside the magic like a stone inside a fist — and then he closed his hand into a loose fist and flicked his wrist and it came straight back at her, on exactly the same line, at exactly the same speed. Faster.

She threw herself sideways.

It went past her ear so close she felt the wind of it lift her hair, and it hit the ground where she'd been standing, and the ground *jumped.* Dirt and needles and a slab of rock the size of a suitcase leapt into the air. She hit the ground on her shoulder and rolled and came up on one knee in the ferns with her wand up and her heart slamming against her ribs, and behind her a hole two feet deep was steaming in the rain.

"That one was lovely," the man said. "I mean it. I'd like to see the notes on that one."

---

Out of the corner of her eye she saw the wolves go.

They went at the same time, from both sides, the way she'd seen them go at deer in the hills behind her house — Leah low and from the left, fast, so fast she was a gray smear; Seth high and from the right, launching himself off his back legs in a long flat leap that would have taken a bull elk off its feet. They went for him without a sound. Layla screamed Seth's name. She didn't know if it was a warning or a cheer.

The man kept his eyes on Layla.

His head stayed where it was. His feet stayed where they were. He lifted his right hand a little, out to his side, fingers spread, palm down, like a man resting his hand on the head of a large dog.

Seth stopped in midair.

Leah stopped on the ground, mid-stride, so suddenly that her own momentum flipped her halfway over.

And then they both went *down* — slowly, with terrible steady pressure, like a giant hand pressing down from above — Seth sinking out of the air to the forest floor, Leah being flattened against the needles beside him, both of them spread-eagled on their bellies with their legs splayed out and their chins in the dirt. Layla saw Seth's claws dig in, saw the huge muscles in his shoulders bunch and strain until they shook, saw Leah's back arch as she threw every ounce of her strength upward. Neither of them moved an inch. The man's hand stayed exactly where it was, resting in the air, palm down.

His eyes were still on Layla.

He was holding two shape-shifting wolves the size of horses flat to the ground with one hand, without looking at them, and his breathing hadn't changed.

Seth whined, high and frantic, through his nose.

"Sorry, Balto," the man said, still looking at Layla. "Stay. Good boy."

---

Something happened to Layla then.

She would never be able to describe it properly afterward, not to Seth, not to herself. It was something like the moment on a mountain when you look down and realize, all at once, how high you are. It was something like opening a door in a tomb and feeling the air on your face and knowing, before you've seen anything, that the room on the other side is very, very large.

She let her eyes go soft.

*See the work.* It was the first thing they taught you and the last thing you forgot. She looked at him a little to one side, the way she'd look at a cursed lintel or a warded door, and let her focus slide, and waited for the magic on him to show up like frost on glass. She was looking for the shield. There had to be a shield. Nobody stood in the open and took a nine-spell chain without one. There'd be a structure, a lattice, some charm or ward or rune-cut talisman holding all this together, and if there was a structure there was a seam, and if there was a seam she could find it and pick it, the way she'd picked the hex in the reliquary hinge an hour ago. That was what she did. That was the only thing she'd ever been better at than dueling.

There was no shield.

No lattice. No seams. Nothing laid down around him, nothing cast, nothing waiting. Inside him, where she'd have expected to see the bright crackle of an ordinary wizard's magic, there was something so dense, so packed, so layered and folded in on itself that her eyes couldn't resolve it at all. It was like looking at a cliff face and trying to count the bricks. It was like looking at the sun through a pane of smoked glass.

He'd been taking each spell as it came, individually, by hand, and doing whatever he liked with it. No shield at all.

The way you'd swat flies.

Ruth Abernathy had been ninety-one the year she'd trained Layla. A tiny, fierce, papery old witch from Salem with a voice like a crow and a wand like a riding crop, who'd been a junior clerk at the International Confederation of Wizards in the summer of 1945 and had been in the crowd, that day, in the hills outside the little town where it happened. She had seen Albus Dumbledore and Gellert Grindelwald duel. She had been one of the people who'd written about it afterward. She'd only ever talked about it to Layla once, late at night, after a bottle of elf-made wine, sitting on the porch of her house on the North Shore.

*You'll meet good duelists,* Ruth had said. *You'll meet great ones. You'll be one, I expect, if you don't get yourself killed being stupid first. But one day, if you're very unlucky, you'll meet one of the other kind.* She'd turned her glass in her ancient spotted hands. *You'll know them when you see them, girl. Your best work will start to look like a question they're answering.*

Layla had asked her how many of the other kind there were.

*In my lifetime?* Ruth had thought about it a long time. *Three. Those two. And the British one. Riddle.* She'd said the name the way you'd step over something on the sidewalk. *The one who duelled Dumbledore in the British Ministry's atrium in nineteen ninety-six and made the fountain into a bloody battlefield. Three in a century, and you'll notice they all spent most of their time trying to kill each other. That's the only thing that keeps the rest of us alive.*

Ruth had died in 2003. By then all three of them were dead, too. Dumbledore on a tower in Scotland. Grindelwald in his cell in Nurmengard. Riddle in the Great Hall at Hogwarts, at dawn, at the end of the British war, at the hands of some boy whose name the American papers had printed in a font size usually reserved for moon landings.

All three. Dead. It had felt, at the time, like the world getting a little bit safer.

Layla knelt in the ferns in the rain with her wand up and her heart trying to break out through her ribs and looked at the plain, square-faced man in the black overcoat, standing perfectly still in a clearing in Washington State with two wolves pinned beneath one hand and her nine-spell chain lying in pieces in the dirt around his boots, and understood with a cold and total clarity that she was looking at a fourth.

He hadn't even drawn a wand. He didn't *have* a wand. He'd been playing with her.

Her hands were shaking. She'd never once, in eleven years, in any tomb on Earth, felt her hands shake.

She thought: *He's going to kill Seth.*

And then she stopped thinking at all.

---

She knew a curse.

She'd never cast it at anything living. She'd taken it out of the third chamber of a Wari burial complex in the high desert south of Ayacucho, where it had been sitting in the floor for twelve hundred years, waiting, and had already killed two Gringotts teams before hers went in. It wasn't a curse anyone had a name for. She called it the Stone Hunger. It went into the ground under a target and turned the earth to grinding teeth, and it didn't stop until there was nothing on top of the ground to grind. She'd broken it, and she'd kept the bones of it, and she'd told herself she'd kept it for study, and she'd known that was a lie the day she did it.

She came up off her knee and lifted her wand high, and began the long downward cut that opened it, and felt the old cold weight of it gather at the tip like water behind a dam.

The man's eyes changed.

She saw them change. The interest went out of them and something else came into them, flat and fast and absolutely decided.

With his right hand he kept the two wolves pinned to the ground. With his left he reached into his overcoat, under his arm, and came out with a gun.

A pistol. Big, blocky, blued steel gone dull and worn at the edges, with wooden grips. His hand came out from under the coat with it already pointed, without any hurry at all, the way a man takes his keys out of his pocket. Layla saw the slide. It was covered — covered *solid* — in fine black etching, so dense it looked like lace.

He pointed it at the ground. At the forest floor, a yard in front of her feet. And he fired.

---

The sound was enormous. A flat, slamming *crack* that hit her in the chest like a fist and went rolling away through the hills.

And then the ground came apart.

It came apart *upward.* The little copper-jacketed bullet hit the needles a yard in front of her boots and went into the soft dirt and for one quarter of one heartbeat nothing happened at all — and then the earth *erupted.* A column of dirt and stone and shredded fern and splintered root went up into the air in front of her like somebody had set off a mortar shell under the forest floor, and a wall of force came out of the ground with it, a flat, hard, giant's-palm shove of pressure that hit her from the shins to the forehead all at once.

It picked her up. It threw her.

She went backward through the air. She saw the sky, gray, rain falling up at her. She saw the tops of the hemlocks wheeling. She saw her wand fly out of her hand and go spinning end over end away into the gray.

She hit something. Soft, then hard. A bank of sword ferns, then the rotting log behind it. The air went out of her lungs in one single solid whoosh and didn't come back, and something in her left side went *crack,* small and sharp and bright, and her vision went white around the edges.

She lay there on her back in the ferns, thirty feet from where she'd been standing, and tried to breathe, and couldn't.

Her ears were ringing. A high, thin, endless whine, like a mosquito the size of a house. Dirt was still coming down — pattering on the ferns around her, on her face, in her hair. Little bits of root. Pine needles, drifting, turning in the air.

She dragged in a breath. It hurt like a knife. She dragged in another.

Somewhere far away, through the ringing, she heard a wolf howling. Seth. Frantic, desperate, furious, on and on.

She turned her head.

Through the drifting dirt and the rain she could see the place where she'd been standing. There was a crater there now. A smoking, ragged-edged hole in the forest floor four feet across and as deep as her knee, with the roots of the hemlocks around it torn and splintered and showing white.

From one bullet.

The man in the overcoat was standing exactly where he'd been standing. He hadn't moved his feet. His right hand was still out to his side, palm down, and Seth and Leah were still flat on the ground beneath it, and Seth was still howling. In his left hand the big pistol hung loose at his side, the muzzle pointed at the dirt, a thin curl of smoke going up from it into the rain.

As she watched, something came spinning down out of the gray sky toward him — her wand, end over end, falling — and without looking up he lifted his left hand an inch, gun and all, and the wand stopped falling. It hung in the air beside him. It turned, gently, slowly, and drifted over and settled into the curl of his fingers alongside the pistol's grip.

He looked down at it. He turned it over in his gloved hand.

"Wolfe," he said, thoughtfully. "Thunderbird. Lovely."

Then he looked up at her across the clearing, through the settling dirt and the rain, and raised his eyebrows.

"Right," he said. "Might we conduct the rest of this conversation with words, do you think? Instead of spells and teeth?"


---

### VII. Terms

Nobody answered him.

Seth was still howling. Leah had stopped fighting and lay flat with her ears back and her eyes fixed on the man's face. The rain came down. The last of the dirt pattered onto the ferns. And Layla lay on her back in the wreckage of a sword-fern bank with her wand in a stranger's hand and every breath going into her side like a knife, and stared at him.

The man in the overcoat waited a polite moment. Then he sighed, very slightly, and reached under his arm and slid the pistol back into its holster under the coat, and buttoned the coat one button over it.

"Go on, then, Balto," he said, and lifted his right hand.

Seth came up off the ground like a spring let go. He didn't even look at the man. He covered the thirty feet between them in two bounds and then he was standing over her, huge and sandy and soaking, shoving his great hot nose into her neck, her hair, her hands, her side — she gasped when he found the rib — whining high and frantic down in his chest. She got a hand up into the thick fur of his ruff and held on.

"I'm okay," she said. It came out a wheeze. "I'm okay, I'm okay, I'm—"

"She's fine," the man said from across the clearing. "Cracked rib, I'd think. Bruised from here to Christmas. I aimed."

Seth swung his head around and showed him every tooth he had.

"Yes, all right," the man said. "Fair." He looked down at Leah, still pinned flat beside him. "And you, Lassie. If I let you up, are you going to try and bite me?"

Leah's lips curled back. Whatever she thought, Layla couldn't hear it. The man could. His mouth twitched.

"That's a yes, then," he said. "Honest, at least." And he lifted his hand anyway.

Leah came up in one fluid surge and went sideways, fast, putting herself between him and her brother and Layla, and stood there with her head low and her hackles up in a stiff gray ridge, growling a long, low, continuous growl that Layla could feel in the ground under her back.

The man looked at her for a moment. Then he walked across the clearing toward them, not fast, his boots crunching on the needles, and stopped about ten feet away from Leah's teeth. And then he did something that Layla would think about for a long time afterward.

He crouched down. Right there in the open. Elbows on his knees, her wand loose in one gloved hand, down at eye level with a growling wolf the size of a horse, the way you'd crouch to talk to a child.

He looked past Leah at Layla.

"Breathe out," he said. "All the way. Then in, slowly."

She glared at him.

"Or don't," he said. "Lie there and wheeze. Your call, love."

She breathed out. All the way.

Something touched her side. Nothing she could see — a pressure, cold and then hot, sliding in under her ribs from the outside like a hand reaching through her jacket and her skin and taking hold of the bone. She felt the broken edges grate. She felt them *move* — line up, butt together, click — and then a deep, spreading, itching heat, and when she breathed in again the knife was gone. The breath went all the way down. Her eyes watered.

The man watched her do it.

"Better?" he said.

"You broke it," Layla said hoarsely.

"Your log broke it. I just introduced you."

Seth made a noise Layla had never heard a wolf make, somewhere between a howl and a growl. Then he turned, abruptly, and went crashing off into the trees behind her. She heard the sound she'd learned to know in the last three weeks, the deep internal *tear,* like heavy canvas ripping. She heard him swearing under his breath, in English, fumbling with something. The man turned his head politely away from the trees and studied the crater with interest until Seth came stumbling back out barefoot and bare-chested in his cutoffs with the rain sheeting off his shoulders and steam coming off him in clouds. He dropped to his knees in the ferns beside her and got his hands on her — her face, her shoulders, her side, burning hot — and his hands were shaking.

"Are you okay? Layla. Are you okay? Are you—"

"I'm okay." She caught one of his hands and held it against her cheek. "I'm okay. He fixed it."

Seth turned his head and looked at the man crouched ten feet away with something on his face she'd never once seen there. Something that made him look much older, and not at all like a golden retriever.

"You *shot* at her," he said.

"I shot at the ground," the man said mildly. "If I'd shot at her, Balto, we wouldn't be having a conversation."

---

Layla sat up.

It took a while. Seth helped. When she was sitting with her back against the rotten log and her legs out in front of her in the ferns and her ribs not screaming, she looked at the man properly for the first time.

He was younger than she'd thought. Twenty-five, twenty-six. He had a square, ordinary face with dark stubble along the jaw and dark hair plastered flat by the rain, and he was big through the shoulders under the overcoat, and he was very calm. There was nothing on him anywhere — no talismans, no rings, no charms she could see with her eyes soft or hard. No wand. Just the gun under the coat and her own wand loose in his fingers.

His eyes were green. Very green.

"Who are you?" she said.

"Bloke with a badge."

"Show me."

He raised his eyebrows. Then he reached into his coat with two fingers and took out a black leather wallet and flipped it open and held it out to her across the space between them.

She looked at it, and her stomach dropped through the floor.

MAGICAL CONGRESS OF THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA, it said, in silver letters on black, over a seal she'd seen a thousand times in her life — on her wand permit, on her curse-breaking license, over the doors of every MACUSA field office from Boston to Santa Fe. AUROR OFFICE. And a photograph of his face, and a name that slid off her eyes like water off glass.

For one awful lurching second she believed it.

Then she let her eyes go soft, the way she'd been trained, and looked at the badge a little to one side, and saw the work.

It was beautiful work. Even through the fear she could see that. Woven into the leather and the metal so fine and so close she'd never have caught it if she hadn't been looking for it — a Confundus Charm, layered and keyed and folded over on itself, clever and fine, the kind that reached into the looker's head and found the shape of the authority they were most afraid of, and then wore it.

"That's a Confunded badge," she said.

"It's a very good badge."

"It said Homeland Security," Seth said slowly. "To us. It said Department of Homeland Security."

"It says whatever you need it to say, Balto." The man snapped the wallet shut and tucked it away.

"You're not American."

"Aren't I?"

"You're British."

"Am I?"

"You *sound* British. You sound like the BBC. You fight like—" She stopped. She'd been about to say *like nothing I've ever seen,* and she bit it off. "No wand. Nonverbal. Wandless. You didn't use a shield. You don't *carry* a shield, I looked, there's nothing on you at all. You turned a nine-spell chain with your fingers. And you're here, on the Olympic Peninsula, in a suit, catching vampires in snares." She looked at the rigid figure still propped against the hemlock, its red eyes rolling. "You're—"

She knew the word. Everyone in her line of work knew the word. You heard it in the Gringotts canteens in Cairo and Lima, late at night, said low, the way you'd tell a ghost story: about the teams that went down into tombs and found that someone had been there first, and that the thing they'd come for was gone, and so was the curse that had guarded it for three thousand years, and nobody ever filed a report. About the British. About the department under Whitehall with no name on its door, whose people didn't answer to their own Ministry's law, whose people never said what they did because they weren't allowed to.

"You're an Unspeakable," Layla said.

The man looked at her for a long moment with no expression on his face at all.

"Never heard of them," he said. "Sounds made up. What is it, a band?"

---

She looked at him. Black hair. Green eyes. British. Twenty-five, twenty-six.

The thought came up out of the bottom of her mind, slow and strange, like a bubble rising through mud. *Some boy whose name the American papers had printed in a font size usually reserved for moon landings.* She'd been sixteen. She'd seen the photographs. Everyone had. The skinny kid with the round glasses and the wild hair and the—

She looked at his forehead.

Rain ran down it. There was nothing there. Smooth skin under wet dark hair, without a mark on it. The photographs had all shown the scar. Every single one.

She let the thought go. It sank back down into the mud.

"Let's talk about you instead," the man said. "Layla Mercer."

Every hair on her body stood up.

"Your name's on your mailbox, love," he said, with something that might have been patience. "And on the deed at the Clallam County assessor's office, which is public record, and on the registration of a 1987 Ford Ranger with North Carolina plates that needs a new starter motor. I clocked your wards on my third day. American style, all salt and citrus and rosemary. Very pretty. Very thorough." He turned her wand over in his fingers. "Witches are allowed to live where they like. I didn't give you a second thought."

He looked at Seth. Then at Leah, still standing between them, still growling. Then back at Layla.

"What witches are *not* allowed to do," he said, "is tell two wolves."

She said nothing.

"And their alpha. The big red one Balto's very fond of." He tipped his head toward Seth. "And a doctor and his wife and their five grown-up children and a brand-new daughter-in-law, who live in the white house on the river, and who are, by the sound of it, very nice for vampires. And somebody called Nessie, who I assume is either a dog or a monster in a Scottish lake—"

Seth went rigid beside her. All of him, all at once. Leah's growl jumped an octave and went jagged.

The man's eyes flicked between them. Something moved at the back of them, quick and quiet, the look of a man setting a file aside in a drawer he intended to come back to.

"Touchy subject," he said. "Noted." He went on, in exactly the same voice. "And Balto's mum. Sue. Who you healed. And—" His mouth twitched. "Oh, this is my favorite. The chief of police. Charlie Swan."

Seth made a sound.

"How do you know about Charlie?" he said. "How do you — *how do you know about Charlie?*"

"You're thinking about him very loudly, Balto. You're worried about him. It's sweet." The man looked back at Layla. "I had breakfast with him this morning, as it happens. He told me to fish the Bogachiel with something orange. Nice man. Very good at not asking questions." He tilted his head. "*Need-to-know.* I like that. I might steal it."

Layla's mouth had gone completely dry.

"That's—" He counted on his gloved fingers, silently, his lips moving. "Fourteen. Fourteen, in five weeks. You could run a parade."

"They're not *No-Majs,*" Layla said. Her voice came out higher than she meant it to. "None of them. They're — Seth's a *shape-shifter,* the Cullens are *vampires,* they were already — the Statute was written for No-Majs, it was never meant for—"

"Statute doesn't have a clause for *but they're very weird,*" the man said. "I've read it. All of it. Twice. Including the appendices, which nobody does." He set his elbows a little more comfortably on his knees. "And you know that. You know it better than I do. It's your country's law. You grew up under Rappaport's ghost. You know exactly what MACUSA does when a witch blows the Statute fourteen ways in five weeks in a town of three thousand No-Majs, because they made you learn about it in school with pictures."

She knew.

"Obliviators," he said. "For all of them. The wolves — well, nobody's ever tried that, have they? Shape-shifters of this kind aren't in any book I've read, and I've read most of them. Maybe it works. Maybe it doesn't. Maybe it works too well. The vampires—" He shrugged. "Nobody knows. I suspect MACUSA would try it with a hammer. Sue. The chief." His voice didn't change at all. "The Americans don't do delicate, love. You know they don't. Charlie Swan would lose a good deal more than the last five weeks. He might lose his daughter's wedding. He might lose her whole last year. He might lose quite a lot."

Seth was breathing hard through his nose beside her.

"And you," the man said, "lose your wand. And your license. And a few years of your life, I'd think, somewhere unpleasant. And the very interesting family by the river gets a visit from a lot of people in a lot of grey robes with a lot of clipboards." He paused. "I can't see that going well for anyone. Can you?"

The rain came down.

Layla's mind was going very fast. Faster than it had during the duel. *Think. Think.* She looked for the seam. There was always a seam.

She found it.

"You won't tell MACUSA," she said.

The man's eyebrows went up.

"You *can't.*" Her voice steadied. "You're British. You're operating on American soil, armed, without — I'd bet my wand without any kind of clearance from anybody in the Woolworth Building. You just set off a — whatever that was — in a national forest. You just shot at an American citizen. You call MACUSA and tell them about me, you have to explain what *you* were doing standing in a clearing in Washington State with a vampire in a snare. You've got as much to lose as I have."

The man looked at her for a long moment. And then — slowly — he smiled.

It was the first time she'd seen him do it. It changed his whole face. It made him look about twenty, and very pleased with himself, and somehow much more frightening.

"Oh, very good," he said. "Very good. You're right. I'd never *call.*"

He reached into his coat. Seth tensed. The man came out with something small that caught the gray light — a vial. A little stoppered glass vial, no longer than his finger, empty. He held it up between two fingers and tapped it with a fingernail. It rang, faintly.

"I'd post it," he said.

Layla stared at the vial.

"Memory," the man said. "Today's. Yours, Balto's, Lassie's — I don't need anyone's permission to take them, as I think you've worked out. I'd lift out the bits with my face in, obviously. Just the good bits. The witch, the wolves, the vampires. The healing in the kitchen. The chief in the doorway with his beer." He rolled the empty vial between his fingers. "Into an envelope. No return address. Care of the Director of Magical Security, Woolworth Building, New York. MACUSA's post room's very efficient. Never asks who sent what. Never has."

Nobody said anything.

The vial went back into his coat.

"That's blackmail," Layla said finally. Her voice sounded strange in her own ears.

The man considered this.

"That's a Tuesday," he said.

---

"What do you want?" Seth said.

He'd moved. Layla hadn't seen him move. He'd shifted around in the ferns so that his shoulder was in front of her, between her and the man, and he was still on his knees but there was something in the set of him that made her think, suddenly, of Leah. Of the way Leah stood.

"From her. From us." His voice was rough. "What do you *want?*"

The man looked at him. He looked, Layla thought, almost approving.

"Simple things, Balto," he said. "I'm going to be about for a while. When I knock on the door, I'd like somebody to answer it. When I ask a question, I'd like an honest answer. When I need a hand, I'd like somebody to lend me one." He looked at Layla. "And nobody mentions me. To anybody. Not your old mates at Gringotts. Not your mum. Not MACUSA. Not the nice lady at the Forks post office. Not the chief of police, as he doesn't need to know, does he?" A small pause. "In return, the Statute of Secrecy and I have never been introduced. Your fourteen keep their memories. The chief keeps his. You keep your wand."

He held it up. Her wand. Wolfe's carved feathers, the pale inlay at the handle. He laid it flat across his open palm.

"Deal?"

Layla looked at the wand.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll be terribly disappointed," the man said, "and you'll get to find out how long it takes MACUSA to get from New York to Forks."

There was no threat in his voice at all. That was the worst part. He said it the way you'd say *it looks like rain.*

"What's the vampire for?" Seth said.

The man glanced back at the rigid figure propped against the hemlock.

"Ask me no questions, Balto," he said.

Seth stared at him.

"...And I'll tell you no lies," the man finished. "Isn't that how it goes? Your lot have it too, I think." He looked back at Seth, and his face was perfectly plain. "He's going somewhere he can't hurt anybody. That's all you need."

"Are you gonna kill him?"

"Eventually," the man said.

---

The ferns at the edge of the clearing exploded.

Layla had heard him coming — they all had, she realized afterward, the drumming of huge feet on the forest floor, growing louder for the last thirty seconds under the sound of the rain — but she'd been so far inside the conversation that it hadn't reached her. It reached her now. A wolf came out of the trees at the north end of the clearing at a dead run, bigger than Seth, bigger than any wolf she'd seen, deep russet-red and black along the spine, and it was coming straight at the crouching man in the overcoat with its head low and its teeth bared and a sound coming out of it like a chainsaw.

*Jacob.* The shift change. He'd have phased to start his patrol, at four, and walked straight into the whole thing through the pack mind, all of it, the snare and the badge and the duel and the gun—

The man stayed in his crouch and turned his head. He looked at the oncoming wolf. And Layla saw Leah, still standing between them, swing her gray head around toward the russet wolf, and saw the russet wolf's stride falter, a hitch in the rhythm, as though something had been shouted at him, very loudly, by someone he trusted.

Jacob slewed to a halt fifteen feet from the crouching man in a spray of needles and wet dirt, his chest heaving, his lips peeled all the way back off his teeth, every hair on his body standing straight up.

He didn't attack.

The man looked at him.

"And this," he said, "must be Scooby."

Jacob's growl went up into a register Layla felt in her teeth.

"Calm down, mate," the man said. "Everybody's alive. Even him." He nodded at the propped vampire. Then his eyes narrowed very slightly. He tilted his head. "You're thinking that Lassie told you not to. That I'd put you on your back like I put the two of them. She's right. I would." He shrugged. "So shall we skip it? Saves us both a lot of bother. You can go and get your trousers on, and we'll have a chat, alpha to — whatever I am."

For a long, long moment the russet wolf didn't move.

Then he turned — stiffly, every line of his body screaming his opinion — and stalked off into the trees. The tearing sound. A few seconds of swearing, much more inventive than Seth's.

Jacob Black came back out of the woods in a pair of cutoffs with the rain running down his chest and his black hair plastered to his skull, and he stopped ten feet from the man and stood there with his fists clenched at his sides and his whole body shaking — just slightly, just at the edges, like a pot about to boil over.

The man stood up.

He did it slowly, unfolding from his crouch, until he was standing at his full height — a couple of inches shorter than Jacob, Layla saw, and broader. The two of them looked at each other across ten feet of wet forest floor.

"Who the hell are you?" Jacob said.

"We've done that bit."

"I don't care what bits you've done. *Who the hell are you?*"

"Civil servant," the man said. "Very boring. Pension."

"This is our land."

"Is it?" The man looked around the clearing with polite interest. "I've got a map that says it's Olympic National Forest. Fifteen miles outside the reservation boundary, as surveyed by the Bureau of Indian Affairs in 1984, which makes it federal land, and I've got a badge that says I'm federal. Want to see it?"

"I don't care what your badge says."

"Nobody ever does. It's very hurtful."

"You hurt my pack."

"I sat on your pack," the man said. "Gently. Your pack tried to eat me. I'd call it even."

Jacob's jaw worked. Layla could see the shudder running through him, the heat coming off him in visible waves where the rain hit his skin.

"Let me tell you how it is, Scooby," the man said, and his voice changed, going flatter without getting any louder. "Anything that comes onto this peninsula with red eyes is mine now. My lines are all over these hills. You can't see them and you can't smell them, and they won't touch you, and they won't touch your friends by the river either, because I've gone to a great deal of trouble to make sure they won't. They'll only take the ones that eat people." He nodded at the vampire against the tree. "That one killed two kids at Lake Crescent on Sunday. Twenty and twenty-one. He'd have been in the Bogachiel in another forty seconds, and out the other side, and in Seattle by breakfast, and doing it again by Friday. Your two were very quick, and very brave, and they'd have lost him at the water." He paused. "You're welcome, by the way."

Jacob said nothing. His fists were still clenched. But something in his face had shifted, very slightly — the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

The man studied him.

Then his mouth twitched.

"You're trying very hard not to think about something, mate," he said.

Jacob went still.

"It's quite something to watch. Like watching a Labrador not look at a sausage." The man's voice was mild. "It's all right. I'm not going to go looking." He paused, just long enough. "Today."

Jacob's face went a deep, dark red. Layla saw Seth flinch beside her. Saw Leah's ears go flat.

"Tell the doctor I'll be calling round," the man said. "Soon."

"Over my dead body," Jacob said.

"Let's hope not. I'd have to write a report."

He turned away from Jacob, unhurried, as though Jacob had stopped being interesting, and looked down at Layla where she sat against the log in the ferns. He held up her wand.

Then he tossed it to her.

It came end over end through the air in a lazy arc, and she caught it — her hand closed around the carved handle on reflex — and the old familiar hum of it went up her arm like a heartbeat coming back.

She could have cursed him.

She sat there with her wand in her hand and looked at him, and she thought about it, honestly, for one long second. She was sitting down. He was ten feet away and had his back half-turned to her. She had a wand and he didn't.

He was looking at her. He was waiting, she realized. Patient. Curious. To see what she'd do.

She lowered the wand to her lap.

The man nodded, once, as though she'd answered a question.

"Sensible," he said.

He walked back across the clearing to the hemlock where the vampire was propped. He bent his knees, got an arm around its rigid middle, and lifted — the same little grunt, the same hitch of the hip — until it was tucked under his arm again, stiff as a plank, head forward, feet back. Like a surfboard. The vampire's eyes rolled wildly toward the sky.

He turned around with it, and looked at the four of them — Leah standing gray and bristling in the ferns, Jacob shaking at the edge of the clearing, Seth on his knees, Layla against her log.

"Lassie," he said, with a small nod. "Balto. Scooby." His eyes came to rest on Layla. "Sabrina."

He turned on his heel.

The sound cracked off the hills like a rifle shot, and he was gone, and the vampire was gone with him, and there was nothing left in the clearing at all but the rain falling on the place where they'd been, and a smoking crater, and a ripped-up fern bank, and a long, neat, foot-deep slit in the forest floor beside where his boot had been.

---

For a long time nobody said anything at all.

Layla sat with her wand in her lap and her back against the rotten log and found that she was shaking, all of her, her teeth chattering. Seth put his arms around her and pulled her in against his chest, against all that heat, and held on, and she let him, and she put her face in his neck and stayed there.

Leah went into the trees. The tearing sound. A minute later she came back out barefoot in a pair of cutoffs and a black tank top, her short hair dripping, her arms wrapped around herself. She walked over to the crater and stood at the edge of it and looked down into it for a long time.

"So," she said finally, without turning around. "Your girlfriend's world has a CIA."

Layla laughed. It came out wrong — high and cracked, almost a sob.

"Worse," she said into Seth's neck.

Leah looked back at her over her shoulder.

"The CIA has to file paperwork," Layla said. She lifted her head. "The CIA answers to *somebody.* Congress. The President. Somebody. The Department of Mysteries doesn't answer to anybody. Not even their own Ministry. They do research — that's what everybody says — they study things. Time. Death. The mind. Things nobody else is allowed to touch. They've got a whole floor under London full of rooms nobody's ever been inside and come out to talk about." She swallowed. "And sometimes they leave the building. And when they do, things happen, and nobody ever finds out why, and nobody ever files a report. The goblins at Gringotts have a name for them. They call them the quiet ones." She looked at the crater. "That's what everybody says. I always thought it was just stories."

"He didn't seem that quiet," Jacob said.

He was still standing at the edge of the clearing. He'd stopped shaking. He looked, Layla thought, more frightened now than he had when he'd been angry.

"He heard everything," Jacob said. "Everything we were thinking. Like Edward." He looked at Seth. "He heard about Nessie."

"Just the name," Seth said quickly. "Just the name, Jake, I swear, I was trying so hard not to—"

"I know." Jacob rubbed his face with both hands. "I know. I was trying too."

"He didn't go looking," Leah said.

"*Today,*" Jacob said.

They all looked at the crater.

"We need to tell Carlisle," Jacob said. "Now. Tonight. All of it."

Layla nodded. She got her feet under her, slowly, with Seth's arm around her waist, and stood up. Her ribs didn't hurt at all. That was almost the worst part. She looked down at the wand in her hand, and then at the long, neat slit in the dirt where her own Severing Charm had gone into the ground beside the man's boot, as deep and straight as if it had been cut with a ruler.

"He didn't even have a wand," she said.

Nobody answered.

The rain went on falling into the crater, filling the bottom of it slowly with brown water, smoothing the torn edges, as if the forest meant to have the whole thing tidied away by morning.

### VIII. Specimen One

*The tent, Forest Service Road 29 — 4:31 p.m.*

He came out of the Apparition in the corridor of the tent with the vampire under his arm and his ears still ringing faintly from the .45, and stood for a moment on the flagstones while the walls stopped swaying.

Side-Along with a passenger who weighed the better part of three hundred pounds and was, technically, a statue, was not a thing he'd recommend. He'd felt the vampire's mass drag at the squeeze of the Apparition like a sea anchor. He'd have to write that down.

He'd have to write a lot of things down.

He walked the specimen down the corridor, past the kitchen and the study and the armory, and shouldered open the door of the cold white room at the end, and carried it to the middle of the floor and stood it upright in the center of the silver circle like a man standing a mannequin in a shop window. He stepped back out over the inlay. He let the paralysis go, a little — from the neck up, nothing else — and closed the circle behind him with a thought.

The runes in the floor lit, one band after another, in a ripple of cold white light. The air above the circle went very slightly thick, like the air over a road in summer.

The vampire's head snapped around.

It came around so fast it blurred, and the red eyes found him and fixed on him, and the thin white mouth opened, and the first sound out of it was a scream, wordless, high and raw and splitting, the sound of a thing that had been frozen and silent and upside down for three-quarters of an hour and had just been given its voice back. It went on for a long time. It made the instruments on the steel tray rattle.

Harry waited for it to stop.

When it had stopped, he took off his gloves, one finger at a time, and laid them on the steel table, and pulled up a stool from under the bench and sat on it, just outside the circle, with his elbows on his knees.

"Hello," he said. "I'm Harry. What do they call you?"

The vampire stared at him. Its head was the only part of it that could move. It moved it — jerking it left, right, up, testing — and then it went still and looked at him with a terrible, intent focus, the way a cat looks at a bird on the other side of a window. Its nostrils flared. It breathed in. Deeply.

Harry watched its pupils go wide.

"Yes," he said. "Sorry about that. I do smell nice, I'm told. You can't have any."

"Let me *go.*"

The voice was a surprise. Low and educated and faintly Southern, with a creak at the back of it like an old door. Harry filed it.

"No," he said.

"Do you know what I am?" the vampire said. Its voice dropped. Went silky. "Do you have the faintest idea what I am? What I can do to you? When I get out of this, little man, I'm going to—"

"Rourke," Harry said. "Elias Rourke. Born 1844, in Charleston. Turned in 1871, outside Savannah, by a woman with yellow hair whose name you never learned. You've been drifting up and down the West Coast since the fifties. You like hikers because nobody misses them for two days. You liked Kaitlyn because she screamed." He paused. "You'd forgotten about the woman with yellow hair, I think. You haven't thought about her in years. She's buried quite deep."

The vampire's mouth closed.

For the first time, something came into the crimson eyes that wasn't hunger.

"Get out," Rourke said, very quietly, "of my *head.*"

"In a bit," Harry said, and stood up, and went to get the instruments.

---

He'd done this before, with things like it. You built a protocol and you followed the protocol, because the protocol kept you honest, and because the protocol was the same whether the thing in the circle was a Lethifold or a Nundu or a man — and because you could look at a protocol later, at three in the morning, and know that you'd done what you'd done for a reason.

He set a quill on the steel table beside the stool. A long gray feather, cut and charmed, with a pad of foolscap under it. He touched it with one finger and it stood up on its nib.

"Specimen V-one," he said. "Twenty-eighth of November, two thousand and six. Sixteen thirty-eight local. Adult male, apparent age early thirties, actual age one hundred sixty-two. Diet human, confirmed by springe trigger and by direct Legilimency. Taken on the springe at fifteen forty-nine at node four-one-one, without incident." He considered. "Mostly without incident. Note: local complications. See separate report."

The quill scratched.

"Observation," Harry said, and looked at the vampire in the circle. He looked at it the way he'd been taught to look, the way Bill Weasley had taught him to look in the dark under the Valley of the Kings: a little to one side, with his eyes gone soft, letting the magic show itself.

He'd done this from the hemlock, for a second or two, before the wolves had started thinking interesting things. He hadn't had time to do it properly.

He did it properly now.

There was something in it. That was the first thing. Something outside biology, and outside wizarding magic too — he'd have known wizarding magic, he'd have seen the lattice of it — and very much there. It ran all through the body, in every part of it, settled and hard and finished, the way the color runs all through a piece of stained glass. A transformation that had happened once and then set. Like a curse cast a hundred and thirty-five years ago that had done its work and then never stopped doing it.

"Observation," he said again. "Magical signature present. Pervasive, uniform, static. Non-lattice. No identifiable casting structure. Reads as a completed transformation — single event, permanently fixed." He paused. "Compare Inferius animation, but self-sustaining and with full retention of the original mind. Compare the Lethifold. Compare nothing, really. Note: this is new."

The quill scratched. Rourke watched him with flat red eyes.

"You're a wizard," the vampire said slowly.

"Mm."

"I've met wizards." A thin smile. "In Charleston, before the war. A few since. Soft. Arrogant. Easy to frighten. *Delicious.* They burn like paper when you get hold of them. And not one of them could do what you just did to me in that tree."

"I'm told I'm a bit unusual," Harry said. "Hold still."

---

He worked for an hour and forty minutes.

Skin, first. It had the look of skin — pale, smooth, faintly translucent at the temples, the veins showing faint and blue and empty underneath — and none of the properties. He set the tip of a goblin-silver scalpel against the back of the vampire's left hand and leaned his weight on it and the blade skated off like it had been pressed against a polished stone countertop. He tried a Severing Charm, a light one, and it left a scratch, a white line like a diamond drawn across glass, that closed up as he watched, smoothing itself back over in eleven seconds.

"Dermal resistance consistent with crystalline structure," Harry said to the quill. "Severing Charm, standard intensity, superficial effect only. Self-repair, eleven seconds."

He went to the third drawer of the bench and took out the spell he'd been working on since September. He'd built it out of a dozen others and the notes of a ward-witch in Bergen and a long evening in the Natural History Museum's mineral gallery watching a Dutch gem-cutter split a raw diamond with a single blow along a line he'd found with a loupe. Crystal had planes. Crystal had grain. Find the grain, and a tap in the right place would do what no blade could.

He let his eyes go soft again. He looked at the vampire's left hand. He looked at the smallest finger. He found the grain.

He tapped.

The tip of the little finger came off at the first knuckle with a sound like a china cup dropped on a tile floor. It fell to the floor of the circle. There was no blood. There was nothing at all inside it but a smooth, faintly glistening, faintly translucent white, like the inside of a seashell, and the cut faces on the finger and on the fingertip were perfectly flat and gleaming, like polished glass.

Rourke made a sound worse than a scream — a long, shuddering hiss of outrage through clenched teeth.

The fingertip on the floor *moved.*

Harry watched it. It rocked, slightly, on the white tiles. And then it began, very slowly, to *crawl* — inching across the floor of the circle toward the vampire's feet, toward the hand it had come from, dragging itself along like a caterpillar, a quarter-inch at a time.

He flicked his fingers and a bell jar came down off the shelf and settled over it.

The fingertip bumped against the inside of the glass. Bumped again. Kept bumping, patiently, in the direction of the hand.

"Fascinating," Harry said. He crouched by the jar and watched it for a full minute, timing it. Then he stood. "Note: Cleaving along crystalline planes — the Bergen method, modified, see notebook thirty-one — effective. Detached tissue remains animate. Directional. Attempts reunion with the main body. Rate approximately one quarter-inch per second on tile." He considered the bell jar. "That's going to want burning, eventually, I'd think. Everything will. Fire's in the folklore. I want to know how hot."

"I will *kill* you," Rourke said. The silk had gone out of his voice entirely. "Do you understand me? When I get out of here I will find you and I will take you apart the way you—"

"You won't, though," Harry said, not unkindly. "Will you."

He let the fingertip go back. It took a minute and a half. He watched it seat itself on the stump with a faint grinding click, and he watched the seam where it had joined go faint, and then fainter, and then disappear.

He wrote that down too.

---

Venom next. He wanted a sample.

He could have taken one by force. He'd have had to get the jaws open, which by the evidence so far would have taken either a crowbar or a spell he didn't want to waste, and then he'd have had to get a vial into a mouth whose bite, if the folklore was right, could put a man in the ground or worse. He'd been looking forward to this part. It was a question he'd wanted the answer to for nine months.

He looked into the vampire's eyes and said, quietly, "*Imperio.*"

He didn't need the word. He used it anyway, for the record, for the quill. The curse went out of him like a breath, without a wand, without a gesture, and he felt it reach the vampire's mind.

And go *in.*

He'd put the Imperius on a lot of people. He knew what a human mind felt like under it — the way it went soft and pliant, like wax near a flame, the way the will under it went limp and floated. This was something else. The vampire's mind was *fast.* Faster than any mind he'd ever been inside. It ran a dozen tracks at once, two dozen, thoughts layered over thoughts over thoughts like a crowd of people all talking in a stadium, and the curse went into it and spread out over it and caught it, mostly — the way a net catches a shoal of fish, mostly — and he felt the whole thing go quiet under him, and still, and calm.

Except for one track. Way off at the edge. Running on, small and cold and steady, under the net. Watching him.

*Interesting.*

"Open your mouth," Harry said. "Wide."

Rourke opened his mouth. Wide.

Harry held the vial up to the white lips and said, "Spit," and Rourke spat, a long thin stream of clear, faintly viscous liquid that smelled of nothing at all and ran down the inside of the glass like syrup. Harry stoppered it and held it up to the light. It was clear as water. It caught the light in a faint, oily, rainbow sheen.

"Venom sample secured," he said. "Approximately four milliliters. Under Imperius." He set the vial carefully in a rack on the table. "Note: Imperius effective. Note: Imperius *mostly* effective. The specimen's mind runs multiple concurrent cognitive tracks. Curse captures the majority. One track — possibly more — remains outside the curse's hold, observing. Resembles a strong-willed human resisting at the early stage. Appears structural — built in — with no sign of effort." He thought about it. "Hold time to follow."

He held it. He sat on the stool and asked the vampire questions, and the vampire answered them, in its creaking Southern voice, flat and calm. How fast it could run. How long it could go without feeding. What the thirst felt like. Whether it slept. Whether it dreamed. How many it had killed — Rourke thought about that one for a long time and then said, *I don't know. I stopped counting in Reconstruction* — and whether it had ever heard of a family in Italy.

Rourke's eyes, under the curse, went flat and dull and frightened all at once.

"Everyone's heard of them," he said.

"Tell me."

"You don't — talk about them. You don't break their rules. You don't make a mess so big the humans notice. You don't make—" He stopped. "If you do, they come. And you don't see them coming. And then you don't see anything again."

"Have you ever seen them?"

"Once. In Chicago. In 1931. Three of them came for a nest that had gotten—" A long pause. "Loud. I watched from a rooftop. I left the city that night. I never went back."

"What did you see?"

"A girl," Rourke said. "Little. Blonde. She looked at the leader of the nest. She just *looked* at him. And he fell down and screamed until they burned him."

Harry wrote that down himself, in his own hand, in the margin of the foolscap.

At forty-one minutes, the small cold track at the edge of the vampire's mind started to move.

He felt it before he saw it. It started pushing at the net from underneath, lightly, testing, the way a fish noses along the bottom edge of a net looking for the gap. At forty-four minutes it found one. At forty-six, Rourke's eyes cleared, and focused, and fixed on Harry's face with a slow, horrible intelligence.

"I can feel you," the vampire said. "Holding on. Like a man holding a rope with a horse on the other end of it."

"Note," Harry said to the quill. "Imperius begins to degrade at forty-one minutes. Specimen aware of the curse and actively resisting by forty-six. Recast likely required at intervals of forty minutes or less for sustained compliance." He let the curse go. He felt Rourke's mind spring back up, all two dozen tracks of it, roaring. "Duration's shorter than I'd like. Still. Useful."

He looked at the vampire for a long moment.

"Next one's going to hurt," he said. "I'm sorry about that. I need to know."

Rourke's red eyes went wide.

Harry thought about Kaitlyn, who had been twenty-one, and studying to be a nurse, and who'd had trekking poles with pink tape around the handles, which he'd seen in the vampire's memory while it hung from the hemlock. He thought about the boy with her, whose name Rourke hadn't bothered to learn.

"*Crucio,*" he said.

---

The sound the vampire made was like metal tearing.

It went on for two seconds. Harry counted them. Then he let go, and the sound stopped, and in the silence afterward the instruments on the tray went on rattling for a moment from the vibration of it, and then were still.

Rourke's head hung forward. The rest of him stood rigid in the circle, the way it had been standing since the hemlock — paralysis still holding everything below the neck. But his face. His face was moving, twitching, the muscles of it jumping and fluttering under that hard white skin, and his mouth was open, and if he'd been human he'd have been gasping.

He didn't need to breathe. The sound he made was low and continuous, like a wounded animal's.

"Note," Harry said. His voice was level. "Cruciatus Curse, standard intensity, two seconds. Effective. Strongly effective. Pain response present and severe. Vocalization consistent with acute agony." He watched the twitching. "Residual neuromuscular — residual *something* — disturbance continuing post-cessation. Duration to follow."

It took four minutes for the twitching to stop.

He gave it one more, at the higher intensity, for three seconds, because he needed the comparison and because the Department would ask. Then he stopped. He'd got what he needed. The twitching took nine minutes to stop that time, and when it was done Rourke lifted his head and looked at Harry across the circle and didn't say anything at all.

"Note," Harry said quietly. "Cruciatus effective against this species. Response scales with intensity. Post-curse disruption scales with intensity and with duration. Possible tactical application: brief, high-intensity exposures for incapacitation, rather than—" He stopped. He thought about the girl in Chicago. The little blonde girl who looked at people. "Note: there are reports of a member of the Italian group with a capacity resembling the Cruciatus Curse, delivered by sight alone. Reliable source. Cross-reference."

The quill scratched.

Harry got up and went to the sink and ran the cold tap and splashed water on his face. He stood there with his hands on the edge of the sink and his head down for a little while.

Then he dried his face, and went back to the stool, and sat down.

---

"One more," he said.

Rourke looked at him.

"Last one," Harry said. "Then we're done. I'm sorry." He was, a bit. It didn't change anything. "You understand that I can't let you go."

"You were always going to kill me," Rourke said. His voice was hoarse. It sounded like it had been dragged over gravel. "From the start."

"Yes."

"Then *do* it."

Harry looked at him for a long moment.

This was the question. The one at the top of the list in Croaker's handwriting, the one the whole thing had been built around. The venom, the skin, the Imperius — all of that came second. If you were going to walk into a city full of these things, under a medieval hill town in Tuscany, with the Department's best behind you, you needed to know one thing above every other thing. You needed to know whether the oldest, simplest, surest weapon wizardkind had ever made would put one of them down.

He'd used it once before. On a man. In Sofia. He'd written that down too.

He stood up.

He kept his hands at his sides. He looked at the specimen in the circle, at the red eyes looking back at him, and he reached down into the place where the curse lived — the cold, still, final place at the bottom of himself that he'd learned to find in the Department, in a stone room on Level Nine, under supervision — and he meant it.

"*Avada Kedavra,*" he said.

The light that came out of him was green. The green of a lightning bolt. It filled the white room for a quarter of a second, threw every shadow on the walls sharp and black, and hit the vampire in the chest with a sound like a great rushing wind.

Rourke went down.

He went down all at once, the way the dead go down, the paralysis breaking with him, straight back onto the tiles of the circle, his head bouncing once with a sound like a dropped stone. His arms flopped out to his sides. His red eyes stared at the ceiling. Wide open. Empty.

Silence.

Harry stood very still and looked at him.

Then he looked at his watch, and began to count.

"Seventeen fifty-nine and twelve seconds," he said, and his voice was steady. "Avada Kedavra. Single cast. Full intent. Direct hit, center mass. Specimen down. No movement." He waited. "No response to stimulus." He flicked a finger and a pebble from a bowl on the shelf went skittering across the floor of the circle and bounced off the vampire's cheek. Nothing. "Specimen appears—"

He stopped.

The trouble was, there was nothing to check. A human, you'd check for a heartbeat, breath, body heat. This thing had none of those to begin with. Its skin had been cold and its chest had been still and its heart had been silent when it was hanging from the hemlock. It had been, by every measure he had, dead already.

He let his eyes go soft. He looked at the thing on the floor the way he'd been looking at it all afternoon, a little to one side, waiting to see the magic in it go out.

It didn't go out.

He stood there and looked at it, and the settled, pervasive, finished thing that ran all through that body like color through stained glass was *still there.* Dimmer. Quieter. Pulled down into itself, like a fire banked down to coals. But there.

"Oh," Harry said quietly. "Oh, *bollocks.*"

He watched the second hand of his watch go around.

One minute.

Two.

Three.

At three minutes and eleven seconds, the vampire's eyes blinked.

---

It came back slowly, and then all at once. A twitch in the fingers. A flicker in the eyelids. And then Rourke was moving — rolling over, up onto his hands and knees on the tiles, his head hanging, a long low ragged sound coming out of him like an engine turning over in the cold. He lifted his head. The red eyes found Harry. There was nothing in them at all for a moment, nothing but a blank, animal confusion.

Then there was something.

He came off the floor in a single explosion of motion, faster than anything Harry had ever seen move, straight at him, straight at the edge of the circle—

And hit it.

The runes in the floor flared white. The air above the inlay went hard and loud, like a sheet of tempered glass taking a hammer blow, and the vampire bounced off it — *bounced,* went flying backward across the circle and hit the far side of it and bounced again — and fell to the tiles in the middle of the ring. He was up again in a heartbeat. He threw himself at the edge again. And again. Each time the runes flared and the air rang and he came off it like a bird off a window.

Harry stood just outside the circle, two feet from the edge, and watched him do it. His heart was going rather fast. He noted that, too.

"Note," he said. His voice was a little rough. He cleared his throat. "Note. Avada Kedavra *ineffective* as a terminal measure against this species. Repeat. *Ineffective.* Specimen incapacitated for three minutes eleven seconds, then full recovery. No apparent lasting impairment." He watched Rourke hit the barrier again. "Hypothesis: the curse acts on the life in a thing. There's very little conventional life in this thing to act on. The animating transformation is—" He searched for the word. "Elsewhere. Underneath. The curse knocks it down. It doesn't put it out."

He thought about Volterra. He thought about the Department's strike plan, which he'd seen exactly once in draft, in a sealed room, and which assumed in eleven separate places that the Killing Curse would do what the Killing Curse had always done.

*Three minutes and eleven seconds.*

"Note for the Director," he said. "Urgent. This changes the arithmetic."

Rourke had stopped throwing himself at the barrier. He was standing in the middle of the circle with his chest heaving out of habit, his head lowered, staring at Harry with eyes that had gone from confusion to something else entirely — the slow, dawning, almost incredulous look of a man who has just realized he is not going to die after all.

He started to laugh.

It was a horrible sound. Creaking and hoarse and wet, like a hinge in a coffin.

"You *can't,*" Rourke said. "Can you? You can't. All your little tricks, all your — you tried, and you *can't.*"

Harry looked at him for a moment.

"Not like that," he agreed.

He lifted his right hand, palm up, at the level of his waist, and opened his fingers.

A flame stood up in his palm.

It was small. No bigger than a candle's. It burned without a sound, perfectly still, and it was the wrong color — a deep, rich, molten orange shot through at the heart with something darker, something that moved and coiled and seemed, if you looked at it too long, to have a shape. The air in the cold white room went suddenly, sharply hot. The quill on the table stopped writing and leaned away from it.

Rourke stopped laughing.

Every part of him had gone absolutely still, the way they all went still — the dead stillness, the statue stillness — and his crimson eyes were fixed on the little flame in Harry's palm with an expression Harry had never seen on any face before. It was older than the man. It was older than the vampire. It was the oldest fear there was.

"You know what this is," Harry said quietly. "Don't you. You don't know what it's called. But you know."

Rourke said nothing at all.

"It's called Fiendfyre," Harry said. "It's not for indoors. It's not for anything, really. It eats whatever it touches and it doesn't stop until somebody makes it." He looked at the flame. It looked back at him. "I'd been planning to save it for the dissection. Measuring burn rates. But I think we can skip ahead."

He closed his hand.

The flame went out. The heat went out of the room like a door slamming. The quill straightened back up on its nib.

"Not today," Harry said. "I've got more questions. You've got more answers. And I want to know how long it takes for that fingertip to grow back if I keep it in a jar." He picked up the bell jar with the twitching fingertip still bumping patiently against the glass — he'd cleaved it off again while Rourke was down, for exactly that purpose — and set it on the shelf beside the Inferius hand. "Sit down. Get comfortable. You're going to be here a while."

He looked into the vampire's eyes.

"*Imperio,*" he said. "Sit."

Rourke sat.

---

The mirror lived in a drawer in the study, wrapped in black silk.

It was a small square of silvered glass in a plain wooden frame, about the size of a paperback, and it had been made by the Department to a pattern a good deal older than the Department, and there were only two of it in the world. Harry sat down at the end of the long oak table with the map glowing silver under his elbows and unwrapped it and held it up in front of his face and said, "Croaker."

He waited.

The glass clouded. Cleared. And there was a face in it, an old man's face, long and gray and creased like a paper bag that had been folded and unfolded too many times, under a fringe of white hair. Behind him a lamp, and the edge of a bookshelf, and a window with the black of an English night in it. Half past two in the morning in London.

Saul Croaker looked at him over the top of a pair of half-moon reading spectacles. He did not look as though he'd been asleep. Harry had never once seen him look as though he'd been asleep.

"Potter," Croaker said. "You've caught something."

"I've caught something."

"Tell me."

He told him. Briefly. In order. The springe going off at node four-one-one, and the specimen, and the protocol. The skin. The cleaving. The venom. The Imperius, and the forty-one minutes, and the small cold track at the edge of the mind that never fully went under. The Cruciatus. Chicago, in 1931, and the little blonde girl who looked at people.

Croaker listened to all of it without interrupting. When Harry told him about the girl, the old man's eyes narrowed very slightly behind the spectacles, and he made a small note on something below the edge of the mirror.

Then Harry told him about the Killing Curse.

Croaker went very still.

"Three minutes," he said.

"And eleven seconds. Then up, and straight at me. Full strength. No impairment I could find."

"You're certain."

"I'm certain. I'll run it again tomorrow to be sure. Different intensity. Different angle. But I'm certain."

Croaker took off his spectacles. He folded them, slowly, and set them down somewhere below the frame of the mirror, and rubbed the bridge of his nose between his finger and thumb, and for a moment he looked every one of his years.

"Eleven places," he said.

"I know."

"The draft assumes it in eleven separate places, Potter. *Eleven.* The entire close-quarters phase is built on it."

"I know. I've read it."

"Then you know what this does to it."

"It means you'll need fire," Harry said. "A great deal of fire. Controlled fire, which is a contradiction in terms, in a medieval town full of Muggle tourists, underground. And you'll need every one of those things *in pieces* first, because fire won't touch them fast enough on its own, and they're stronger than anything we've got and faster than anything we've got and at least one of them can put a man on the floor screaming by looking at him." He shrugged. "That's what it does to it."

Croaker was quiet for a long time.

"Well," he said finally. "That's going to make the Italians a good deal more expensive."

"Yes."

"Put it all in writing. Everything. I want it on my desk in the morning. Our morning." He put his spectacles back on. "Was there anything else?"

Harry looked at the map under his elbows. At the little square by the river east of town that he'd never been within four miles of.

"There's a local complication," he said.

Croaker waited.

"There's a witch," Harry said. "American. Ilvermorny, Gringotts curse-breaker, good duelist, very good. Settled here in October. She's—" He thought about how to say it. "She's breached the Statute. Thoroughly. The wolves — the Quileute — they're shape-shifters, by the way, big ones, I'll send you that separately. She's told them. She's told the Cullens. She's told the chief of police, more or less, though he's done a very good job of not hearing it. Fourteen, all told."

Croaker's face did not change at all.

"And they've told you," he said.

"I had a look."

"Did you make contact?"

"I made contact."

"*Potter.*"

"It was unavoidable. The specimen ran through two of the wolves on the way into the net. They'd have been on top of it in another minute. One of them recognized Apparition from what the witch had told him. He called her in — she'd given him a charm — and she came in throwing curses, and the wolves joined in." He paused. "Nobody's hurt. The witch has a cracked rib. Had."

"Did they see your face?"

"They saw my face."

"Your name?"

"No."

"The Department?"

"She guessed. I didn't confirm."

Croaker took a long breath in through his nose.

"Obliviate them," he said. "All of them. Tonight. The witch, the wolves, the policeman. Leave the Cullens for the moment, I don't want you within range of the boy's mind until we know what that range is. But the rest — clean it up. I'll have an Obliviator on the next portkey to Seattle if you want help."

"No," Harry said.

Croaker looked at him.

"I haven't tested Obliviation on the wolves," Harry said. "Nobody has. They're a closed pack — they share each other's minds, I've seen it. If I take the memory out of two of them, I've no idea whether it stays out, or whether the rest of the pack's got a copy. I haven't tested it on vampires either. And if I wipe the witch and the chief and leave the Cullens knowing, I've just made eight vampires who know something happened and can't work out what, and one of them reads minds." He leaned back in the chair. "And besides that. They're more use to me knowing."

"Explain."

"The witch is a first-rate curse-breaker who knows the area and owes me. The wolves patrol every inch of this peninsula every night and they *hate* the red-eyed ones. They're the best early warning system I could ask for, and I don't have to feed them. And the Cullens—" He looked at the square by the river on the map. "The Cullens are marked valuable alive, in your handwriting. One of them got the Italians' attention badly enough to draw them halfway out of their hole last March. They *know* the Italians. They know how they think. I'd like to have a long chat with the doctor, Croaker. And the best way I know to get a long chat with somebody is to have them owe you, and know it."

"Leverage," Croaker said.

"Leverage."

"Over a witch who's breached the Statute fourteen times, and a coven of an unknown species, and a pack of shape-shifters you haven't classified, on foreign soil, without MACUSA's knowledge." The old man's voice was perfectly dry. "In a town of three thousand Muggles."

"Yes."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"Then it's my head," Harry said.

Croaker looked at him through the mirror for a long time.

"Yes," he said at last. "It is." He reached for something below the frame. "Your call, then. Your mess. I want a report on the wolves by Friday. And Potter."

"Mm?"

"The boy who reads minds. Find out the range before you go anywhere near that house."

Harry looked down at the map. At the square by the river.

"Working on it," he said.

The mirror clouded, and cleared, and showed him his own face.

He sat there for a minute, looking at it. A square, ordinary face. Tired around the eyes. Two days of stubble. A smear of dirt on one cheekbone from the clearing that he hadn't noticed until now.

Then he wrapped the mirror back up in its black silk and put it in the drawer, and checked his watch.

Six fifty-two.

The diner closed at eight.

He sat for another moment with his hands flat on the cool carved wood of the map, looking at that little square east of town. He thought about a man in a recliner with a can of beer, muting the television. He thought about a girl with a scar through her eyebrow, lowering her wand to her lap when she could have used it. He thought about a russet wolf trying very, very hard not to think about something, and two flat, panicked flashes of a name in the minds of two other wolves, and a doctor whom an entire town gossiped about with affection.

*Nessie.*

He thought about Croaker saying *find out the range.*

There was a quick way to do that, and a slow way. The slow way involved weeks of careful approach, and the scrying glass, and a great deal of patience.

The quick way involved walking up to the front door.

Harry stood up. He went and got his overcoat.


---

### IX. The File

*The Cullen house — 8:14 p.m.*

Edward had watched the man in the overcoat catch a falling vampire eleven times.

He'd watched it in Seth's memory, and in Leah's, and in Jacob's secondhand version of Leah's, and in all three of them over and over again in the two and a half hours since they'd come through the front door soaking wet and smelling of rain and wet dog and cordite. He'd watched the badge. He'd watched the duel from two angles, and the gun, and the crater. He'd watched the man crouch down in front of Leah's teeth like a farmer looking at a fence post. He'd listened to the voice — dry, English, unhurried — until he could have imitated it.

And none of it helped, because the one thing he wanted to see wasn't there. Memories were pictures and sounds. They never showed you what the other person was thinking.

He stood by the glass wall at the back of the living room with the river running black and loud below him in the dark, and Bella close at his side, and listened to his family think.

They were all there. Carlisle in the white armchair, fingers steepled, calm on the outside and on the inside running through two hundred years of everything he'd ever heard about witches, which was mostly nonsense and partly Salem. Esme on the arm of his chair, worrying about the girl. Rosalie by the foot of the stairs, where she could hear the slow, sleeping heartbeat in the nursery above, thinking one word over and over like a drumbeat: *Nessie. Nessie. Nessie.* Emmett on the sofa, replaying the duel with something perilously close to admiration and planning, in some detail, how he'd have gone at the man from above. Jasper beside the fireplace, very still, thinking in maps and angles and lines of fire, and arriving, every time, at the same unhappy conclusion. Alice cross-legged on the floor in the middle of the room with her eyes shut, searching, searching, searching.

Jacob, pacing. Back and forth across the rug, back and forth, a furnace in cutoffs and an old T-shirt, his thoughts a red roar. *He knows her name. He knows her name. He knows her name.*

Leah at the front window, arms folded, staring out at the dark drive, thinking about the cold draft she'd felt in the back of her own mind and how much she would like to tear the throat out of the man who'd opened the door it came through.

Seth on the couch with Layla, a blanket over both of them, his arm around her, his whole mind wrapped around her like the blanket. Thinking about how she'd shaken. Thinking that he had been the one to call her. That he'd *called her in.* That it was his fault.

And Layla — a closed room. Somebody inside it, moving around. He couldn't hear a word.

"Again," Jasper said quietly. "From the moment you arrived."

Layla lifted her head off Seth's shoulder. She had her coffee in both hands. Esme had refilled it three times.

"I've told you," she said.

"I know. Tell me again. Please."

She told him again. The nine-spell chain. The turn. The caught Blasting Curse. The ninth spell, the Bludgeoning Hex with the iron in it, caught in his open hand and sent back down its own line. The wolves pinned without a glance. The way she'd looked for his shield and found there wasn't one.

"He never moved his feet," Jasper said when she'd finished.

"No."

"Not once. Not when you fired. Not when the wolves came. Not when he drew." Jasper looked at the fire. Edward heard the thought under the words, the soldier's thought, clear and cold: *He chose that ground before any of them arrived. He chose it the way you'd choose a hill.* "He controlled the engagement from the first second to the last. And he did it holding back."

"You don't know he was holding back," Rosalie said.

"Yes," Layla said. "We do."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"I'd like to see him try it on me," Emmett said.

"No, you wouldn't," said Layla and Jasper, at the same time.

Emmett looked mildly offended.

"I can't see him," Alice said.

She'd opened her eyes. She was staring at nothing, and her small face was set and furious, and inside her head Edward could see what she was seeing — or rather what she wasn't. He'd watched her search for the man for two hours. She'd tried every way she knew. *The man in the overcoat. The man with the badge. The Englishman. The man who took the vampire.* She'd tried the future of the clearing, the future of the Forest Service roads, the future of the diner where Charlie ate breakfast, the future of this house. Every time she reached for him, there was a hole.

"With the wolves," Alice said, "it's like looking at a picture with bits cut out of it. I can see the edges. I can see where they *stop.* I can see everything around them." She shook her head. "With Layla it's flickering, like I said, in and out. With him there's nothing. No edges. No hole. No *him.* It's like he isn't in the future at all."

"He's in the future," Leah said from the window, without turning around. "Trust me."

"He had breakfast with my dad," Bella said.

Her voice was very quiet. Edward put his hand on the small of her back. Her mind, as always, was silence — the wall without a door — but he didn't need to hear it. He could see it in the line of her shoulders.

"This morning," Bella said. "At the diner. He sat across the table from my dad and talked about *fishing.* And then he went out into the woods and — and did that. And then he stood there and told Layla that if she didn't do what he said, he'd send her memories to the people who'd come and wipe my father's head." Her hands had closed into fists. "He'd lose the wedding. That's what he said. My dad would lose my *wedding.*"

"He said *might,*" Layla said quietly.

Bella looked at her.

"He said Charlie *might* lose it. He said it like—" Layla stopped and thought about it, and Edward watched her not let him hear her think about it. "Like he was reading the weather report. Like it had nothing to do with him."

"It has everything to do with him," Rosalie said.

"Yes," said Carlisle.

They all looked at him.

He hadn't moved from the armchair. His fingers were still steepled. His face was calm. Inside, he was thinking about Volterra — about the long marble halls and the three thrones, and about Aro's hand on his for the first time, nearly three hundred years ago, and the soft dry curious voice saying *how very interesting* — and about a man who'd stood in a clearing and called the hunting of their kind *a national security operation.*

"He said he would call on me," Carlisle said. "Soon."

"Over my dead body," said Jacob.

"Then we decide where," said Jasper. "And when. On our ground—"

"We decide *whether,*" Edward said.

Carlisle looked up at him.

"He can hear minds," Edward said. "He reads them. Through the eyes, it looks like, from the memories — he has to look at you. He went through Seth like a man going through a filing cabinet." Seth flinched. "I'm sorry, Seth. I don't mean it as — I know you couldn't help it. None of us could, if he looked at us. Except Layla. And possibly Bella." He looked at his wife. "If he comes here, he'll look at all of us. And there is a child asleep upstairs whom he doesn't know about yet except as a *name.*"

Above them, the slow small heartbeat went on.

"So we don't let him in," Rosalie said.

"Can we keep him out?" Esme said quietly.

No one answered her.

And then Edward heard the music.

---

It came out of nowhere.

That was what he'd remember. Later, he'd go over it again and again, and it would always come back to that. He'd been listening, the whole time, the way he always listened — to the house and the forest around it, to the deer bedded down in the alders a quarter mile upstream, to an owl, to a raccoon in the woodpile, to the distant hum of a truck on the highway four miles off and the bored, tired thoughts of its driver. He'd been listening for the man, specifically, for two and a half hours, the way you'd listen for a particular car turning into the drive.

There had been nothing. No footsteps on the gravel. No heartbeat on the porch. No thoughts in the dark.

And then, from just on the other side of the front door, from a mind standing six feet away on the porch boards — *music.*

Loud. Enormously loud. A pounding, galloping, driving riff, guitar and drums hammering, and over it a man's voice howling, high and wild and ragged, a war cry, a wail, the long rising banshee scream at the top of the song that Edward had heard on the radio in 1970 and been mildly impressed by. It came into his head all at once, at full volume, like somebody had kicked in the door of a concert hall.

Every vampire in the room was on their feet before the first bar ended.

Jasper was in front of Alice. Emmett was in front of Rosalie, who was at the bottom of the stairs, who had one foot on the first step. Esme was behind Carlisle. Edward had Bella behind him, and she let him, for once. Jacob had stopped pacing and was standing in the middle of the rug, shaking. Seth was on his feet with Layla behind him. Layla's wand was in her hand.

Leah turned around from the window. Her nostrils flared.

"That's him," she said. "That's him. He's on the *porch.*"

"I didn't see," Alice said. Her voice was very small. "I didn't see *anything*—"

Somebody knocked on the front door.

Three knocks, quiet and polite, the way the man from the gas company knocked.

Edward reached for the mind on the other side of the door, hard, the way he'd reach for a voice in a crowded room — and the music got *louder.* The voice at the top of the song went up and up, a wail like a storm coming in off the North Sea, and under it, behind it, around it, nothing came through but the song, filling everything, the way water fills a glass.

"Edward?" Carlisle said.

"I can hear him," Edward said slowly. "I can hear him, Carlisle. He's — he's *singing.* In his head. He's playing a song." He reached again. The guitar slammed down on the beat. "It's all there is. There's nothing behind it. He's put it there. He's put it there *on purpose.*"

"What song?" Emmett said.

Edward looked at him.

"Led Zeppelin," he said.

There was a short, strange silence.

"Huh," Emmett said. "Good song."

The knock came again. Three more. Just as polite.

Carlisle looked at the door for a long moment. Then he looked at his family — each of them, one by one — and Edward heard him make the decision, and heard the reason under it. *He came to the front door. He knocked. He could have come in any way he liked.*

Carlisle crossed the room and opened the door.

---

The man in the overcoat was standing on the porch in the rain.

He looked exactly as he had in the wolves' memories. Tall, broad through the shoulders, the black overcoat buttoned once over the suit, rain on the shoulders of it and in his dark hair. No gloves now. He was holding a white cardboard pie box by the string tied around it, the way you'd carry a pie to a neighbor's house.

He looked at Carlisle. Then he looked past him into the room — at all of them, one after another, quickly and thoroughly, a long sweep from left to right like a searchlight — and Edward watched his eyes stop on each face for exactly as long as it took to file it.

Then he looked back at Carlisle.

"Evening," he said. "Doctor Cullen, I presume." He held up the box a few inches. "I brought pie. Seemed rude not to. It's apple. Dot says it's the best in the county, and I've yet to catch Dot being wrong about anything."

In his head the song went on pounding.

Carlisle looked at the pie box. Edward heard his father's mind do something it almost never did, which was go briefly and entirely blank.

"Thank you," Carlisle said.

"Not at all." The man glanced up at the doorframe, then down at the threshold, then at Carlisle again, his eyebrows slightly raised. "I'm told I need an invitation. Or is that your lot? I can never remember which way round it goes."

"That's a story," Carlisle said.

"Most things are." The man waited.

Carlisle looked at him for one more long moment. Edward saw him weigh it — the man on the porch, the family behind him, the small heartbeat upstairs. Saw him decide that a closed door had never yet stopped anyone who really meant to come in.

He stepped back.

"Please," he said. "Come in."

"Ta," the man said, and wiped his boots on the mat, very carefully, both of them, and stepped inside.

---

He stopped two paces in from the door and stood on the white floor with rain dripping off the hem of his coat, and looked around the room as if he'd come to see about buying the house.

The glass wall. The river. The piano. The staircase. He looked at the staircase for a fraction of a second longer than at anything else, and Edward's whole body went cold, and Rosalie, at the foot of it, didn't move a muscle.

Then the man looked at Esme.

"Mrs. Cullen," he said, and held out the pie box.

Esme looked at Carlisle. Carlisle gave her the smallest nod. She crossed the room and took the box from the man's hand, carefully, and he let go of it, carefully, and they stood there for a second like two people passing something over a gap.

"Thank you," Esme said.

"Best served hot with a bit of cheddar," the man said. "Odd, I know. Grows on you."

His eyes came to Layla, behind Seth, with her wand in her hand.

"Sabrina," he said. "How's the rib?"

Layla's jaw tightened. "Fine."

"Good." He looked at Seth. "Balto." At Jacob. "Scooby." At Leah, by the window. "Lassie."

"Call me that in here," Leah said, "and I'll take your hand off at the wrist."

"Noted, Lassie," the man said.

Then he turned his head and looked straight at Edward.

"And you'll be Edward," he said. "The Volvo." He tilted his head slightly, and the song in his head swelled, as if someone had turned a dial. "You can stop that, by the way. You'll only get the one song. It's a very good song, but it does go on a bit."

Edward said nothing for a moment.

"How are you doing that?" he said.

"Practice."

"How far out could you hear me coming?"

The question was so direct and so unexpected that Edward answered it before he'd decided to. "I couldn't. Not at all. Not until you were on the porch."

The man nodded slowly, as though Edward had just told him the price of something.

"Good," he said. "That's what I wanted to know."

And Edward understood, with a sour little turn in his stomach, that he'd just been tested, and that he'd just told the man the result.

---

A slow, heavy wave of calm rolled out across the room.

Edward felt Jasper do it. Felt him reach out and lay it over everybody in the room like a blanket — the old trick, the one he'd used in a thousand tense rooms over a hundred and fifty years, a soothing, settling pressure. He felt Jacob's shoulders drop half an inch. Felt Seth breathe out. Felt Esme's worry soften.

The man in the overcoat frowned.

He turned his head, slowly, and looked around the room. His eyes went from face to face, unhurried, until they stopped on Jasper, beside the fireplace.

"That'll be you," the man said. "Somebody's leaning on my mood. Stop that, please."

Jasper went very still.

He was thinking, very fast, in a way Edward had heard maybe four times in the last fifty years. *He felt it. He felt it come in and he knew it wasn't his own and he traced it back to me. In two seconds. In a room with a dozen people in it.*

The calm went away. The room went tight again.

"Thank you," the man said.

---

"What do you want, Mr.—" Carlisle said.

"Evans," the man said. "Everybody in town calls me Evans. You might as well."

"Is that your name?"

"It's what everybody in town calls me," Evans said.

Carlisle let that go. Edward watched him file it.

"What do you want, Mr. Evans?"

The man looked around the room again. Then he unbuttoned his overcoat — slowly, with one hand — and Edward saw every vampire and every wolf in the room track the motion of it, and saw the shoulder holster underneath, and the dark worn grip of the big pistol. The man didn't touch it. He just stood there with the coat open, his hands loose at his sides, and Edward realized he'd done it on purpose. So they'd see. So they'd know where it was.

"Not much," he said. "Not from you. I'll be on this peninsula for a while, Doctor. I'm not here for you, or yours. I'm here for the ones with red eyes. Anything that comes onto this land eating people belongs to me now, and I'd be grateful if you'd leave them to me. My lines won't touch you, or your family, or your friends with four legs. I've gone to a good deal of trouble to make sure of that. You're welcome."

"And?" Carlisle said.

"And I'd like to talk to you," the man said. "Now and then. You've been around a long time. Longer than most, I think. I've got questions. I won't pry where I'm not wanted." A pause. "Much."

"And if we decline?"

The man turned his head and looked at Layla.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Layla looked back at him, and Edward saw her hand tighten on her wand, and he saw her go pale, and he saw Seth's arm go tighter around her.

Carlisle looked at the man for a long, long moment, and Edward heard something in his father's mind that he'd only heard a handful of times in nearly ninety years. It was anger. Cold, quiet, deep, and very old.

"You are threatening a young woman," Carlisle said, "whose only crime was to show kindness to people she loved."

"I'm describing the weather, Doctor," the man said. "You can dislike the forecast all you want."

"What did you do with the one you took?" Rosalie said from the stairs. "The vampire."

"Ask me no questions," the man said.

"He's thinking about the song," Edward said flatly.

"It's a very good song."

"Why should we trust you?" Rosalie said.

The man looked at her for a moment. Blonde and furious and beautiful at the foot of the stairs, with every line of her body set like a wall.

"Trust's for people who've known each other more than an hour," he said. "Try *useful* for now. We'll see how we get on."

---

Then he looked at Bella.

He'd glanced at her before, in the sweep of the room. Now he looked properly. And Edward, who could hear nothing from the man but a song, watched his face and saw something change in it — a narrowing around the eyes, a slight sharpening of focus. The same look Seth and Leah had both seen in the clearing, in the instant before everything changed. The look of a man reaching for something.

Bella looked back at him with her red eyes. She didn't flinch. She didn't move at all.

The man's eyebrows drew together. Very slightly. He tilted his head.

"Huh," he said.

He kept looking. A second. Two. Edward felt Bella go tense under his hand, and realized that she could feel *something,* a faint pressure, like a hand laid flat against the outside of a wall.

The man blinked and looked away.

"Now *that's* interesting," he said, half to himself. And then, to Bella, with something in his voice that might almost have been respect: "You're a closed book, Mrs. Cullen."

"So I've been told," said Bella.

"I'll bet you have." He looked between her and Edward. "You'll be the one from Volterra."

Nobody in the room breathed. Not that most of them needed to.

"And you're the one who had breakfast with my father this morning," Bella said. Her voice was very steady.

"Charlie's a good man," the man said. "I like him. He told me to fish with something orange."

"If you touch his mind," Bella said, "if you touch him, or let anybody else touch him—"

"Then you'd come for me, Mrs. Cullen," the man said, "and I'd have to do something about it, and I don't think either of us wants that." He held her gaze. "So let's all be very sensible, shall we, and none of that has to happen."

The silence afterward was very long.

And into it, from the top of the stairs, came the sound of small bare feet.

---

Edward heard her wake. He'd been hearing it coming for a minute — the slow sleeping heartbeat quickening, the drowsy half-dreaming thoughts turning over, surfacing. Voices. A strange voice, downstairs. Curious. She'd always been curious. Too curious, Rosalie said. *Who's that? Who's talking? Is it Seth? It sounds funny. It sounds like the man on the TV that Grandpa Charlie watches—*

He was moving before he'd decided to. So was Rosalie. So was Jacob. But she'd already come around the landing and started down the stairs, one small hand trailing on the banister, in a pair of pajamas Alice had bought her with ducks on them and her bronze curls flattened on one side from the pillow, rubbing one eye with the back of her fist.

Renesmee stopped on the bottom step, beside Rosalie's hip, and looked across the white room at the man in the overcoat.

The man looked back at her.

And the music stopped.

It stopped dead, mid-bar, mid-howl, like a needle ripped off a record. One instant the song was filling Edward's head, the guitar and the drums and the wailing voice, and the next instant there was — *nothing.* Absolute silence. Edward had lived for a hundred and five years with every mind in range pouring into his head, every whisper, every half-formed thought, the whole endless babble of the world, and he had never heard anything like the silence that came out of the man on the white floor of his living room in that moment. It was the silence of a door slammed shut on a room with something in it.

It lasted two seconds.

Edward counted them. He'd count them again later, lying awake in the dark beside Bella. Two full seconds in which the man in the overcoat stood completely still and looked at Renesmee, and thought something, and made very, very sure that nobody in the world could hear what it was.

Then the music came back.

A different song, this time. Quieter. Slower. Something with a piano in it.

Renesmee came down off the bottom step.

"Nessie—" Bella said.

Rosalie's hand closed on air where the child's shoulder had been. Jacob took one step forward and stopped, every muscle in his body locked, because she'd already crossed half the room, small and quick and utterly unafraid, her bare feet whispering on the white floor, and she stopped in front of the man in the overcoat and tipped her head all the way back to look up at him, and he was so very tall from down there.

Then she held up her hand.

The man looked down at her hand. Then he looked up, across the room, at Bella.

Edward would remember that. Of everything — the song and the silence and the gun under the coat — he would remember, afterward, that the man had looked at Bella first. That he'd waited.

Bella's face was white as paper. Her hands were fists. Edward could feel her shaking under his palm.

But Renesmee was already reaching, and Renesmee had never in her short life been told *no* by anyone in this house about who she was allowed to touch, and Bella knew it, and Edward watched his wife understand that the only thing she could do now that would not make this worse was to let it happen.

Bella gave one short, stiff nod.

The man went down on one knee on the white floor, slowly, the overcoat pooling around him, until his face was level with the child's. The way he'd crouched in front of Leah's teeth in the clearing.

Renesmee put her palm flat against his cheek.

Edward saw what she showed him. He always could. Her thoughts were never closed to him the way they were to everyone else; they came through clear and bright, and he saw the pictures pass from her small hand into the man's head the way he'd watched them go into a dozen heads before. Paper birds. Dozens of them, white and folded, swooping in a spiral around this room, around her own head, one of them landing trembling in her palm. Layla's face, laughing. A sandy wolf leaping in the snow. A russet wolf asleep in the sun, his chin on his paws.

And then the question, the same question she'd asked Layla three weeks ago, bright and hopeful and shy.

*Are you magic too? Show me?*

The man held very still under her hand for a moment.

Then he lifted his own hand, palm up, between them.

Light came out of it.

Silver light, soft and bright, pouring up out of his open palm like smoke, gathering, taking a shape. Four slender legs. A long neck. A head lifting, crowned — a stag, Edward saw. A stag made out of silver light, no bigger than a cat, perfectly formed down to the curve of each tine of its antlers, standing on the man's open palm. It lowered its head. It looked at Renesmee with eyes like stars.

Then it stepped down off his hand into the air and walked a slow circle around her, through the air at the height of her shoulders, its small silver hooves leaving faint shining prints that hung for a moment and faded. It went once around her and nuzzled her curls — she shrieked with laughter — and then it went up, up, toward the high ceiling, and came apart into silver mist, and was gone.

Renesmee was laughing so hard she had to hold on to the man's coat.

Across the room, Edward heard Layla's mind — closed, always closed — let one single thought slip out through a crack in the door, so sharp and so shocked that it got through.

*Corporeal Patronus. Wandless. Nonverbal.* And then: *He made it* small. *He made it* small *for her. Do you know how hard that—*

The door slammed shut again.

The man looked at Renesmee for a moment longer. Then, very gently, he took her small hand from his cheek and held it for a second in his own, and let it go. He stood up.

He looked at the child. He looked at Bella, at her brown hair, the shape of her face. He looked at Edward, at the color of his hair, at the color of the curls on the child's head.

"Yours?" he said.

"Mine," Bella said.

"Ours," Edward said.

The man nodded slowly.

"She's got a heartbeat," he said.

"Yes," said Carlisle.

"Runs a bit hot. Fast. Faster than it ought to. Heavier than she looks, I'd think. Eats—" He stopped himself. He looked at the child again, at the bright brown eyes looking up at him, and his face was perfectly plain, and in his head the slow song with the piano went on and on.

"Nessie," he said quietly. "Not a dog, then. Not a monster in a lake."

He turned his head and looked at Jacob, who was standing in the middle of the room shaking so hard the floorboards hummed.

"That's what you weren't thinking about," the man said.

Jacob said nothing at all.

Renesmee had let go of the man's coat. She'd gone back across the room on her quick bare feet, and Rosalie had swept her up onto her hip and was holding her very tight, too tight, and the child was squirming and looking back over Rosalie's shoulder at the man in the overcoat with her face still bright with delight, one small hand reaching after the place in the air where the silver stag had gone.

The man watched her go.

Then he let out a long, slow breath — the first time, Edward realized, that he'd seen him breathe like a man who'd been holding something in.

"Well," he said, very softly, to no one in particular.

He looked at the child on Rosalie's hip for one more long second.

"Nobody put *you* in the file," he said.
