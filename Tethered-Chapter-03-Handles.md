# Tethered — Chapter Three: Handles

The pie was apple, and nobody was eating it.

Esme had cut it into eight even pieces while Emmett was still shouting, laid the server down on the marble beside the dish, and then stood with both hands flat on the counter as if she'd lost track of what came after cutting. Then she'd gone through to the laundry room, and a second later Layla had heard the washing machine start, which was a thing Esme did when she needed her hands busy and there was nothing left in the house to clean. That had been ten minutes ago. The slices sat in their ring. They'd stopped steaming.

Layla took a plate out of the cupboard over the dishwasher. Somebody in this house had to eat the stupid pie, and she was the only one left in it who could.

Jacob was gone. He'd hung up on Sam in the middle of a sentence—*No, you listen, he's not some*—and gone out the back door dragging his shirt over his head, and the door had swung shut on its own weight and then bounced back open an inch. Seth had gone a minute after him. He'd stopped in the kitchen doorway first with one hand on the frame and his face doing the thing it did when he was trying to be fifteen kinds of okay at once, and he'd asked her if the guy was really gone, like actually gone, and she'd said, "As gone as he wants to be. Which—yeah. He's gone." And Seth had laughed, short and shaky, and looked out through the glass at the tree line where his sister had been standing, and said, "Leah," like that was a whole sentence, and gone after her.

The back door was still open its inch. Rain was coming in across the floorboards in a thin dark wedge.

Layla shut it.

Then she put a slice on the plate and stood at the island and ate it with her fingers. It was very good. She could barely taste it.

The crack was still in her ears. She'd been standing right here when it came out of the trees, and she'd known it before her brain had finished hearing it, the way you know your own name in a crowded room. A branch breaking, Leah would think, if Leah thought about it at all. Emmett had turned toward it like a dog hearing a gunshot. Layla had just closed her eyes.

Apparition. Clean, too. No stagger in the sound, no double report. He'd been standing at the edge of the lawn and then he'd been nowhere, and *nowhere* could be Port Angeles or Seattle or a field outside Johannesburg, and there wasn't a single thing in this house that could follow him.

In the living room, Emmett had stopped shouting and started walking, which was worse.

He went from the glass wall to the foot of the stairs and back. Four strides each way. He was keeping it to human speed, barely, and every time he turned, the sole of his shoe squeaked on Esme's floor.

"He said Charlie's name," he said, "in this house, like he was reading it off a grocery list. And Sue's. *Sue's*, Rose."

"I heard." Rosalie was perched on the arm of the white couch with rain still beaded in her hair. She'd come in from the garage a few minutes before the crack and gone straight to Emmett and put her hand flat on his back, and he'd been moving ever since, so she'd stopped trying to hold him still and just watched him go. "I heard you the first nine times."

"And we let him walk out."

"Yes," Carlisle said. "We did."

He was standing at the glass with his hands folded behind him, looking at the place on the lawn where the rain had come down through nothing. He hadn't raised his voice once, all afternoon. Layla was starting to think that was how you could tell Carlisle was frightened.

"Because he'd have—what?" said Emmett. "There's six of us. There's six of us and two wolves and Layla."

"There were six of us and two wolves and Layla when he walked in," said Edward.

Edward was on the piano bench. He'd turned around on it so his back was to the keys, and he was sitting very straight with his hands on his knees, and he hadn't moved in a while. Bella was beside him. She'd been a vampire for a year and a bit and she still didn't do *still* quite right; she did it too hard, like she was holding something in place by force, and there was a dent in the edge of the bench under her right hand where the wood had given. Every few seconds her eyes went to the ceiling. Renesmee was asleep up there. Layla had heard Bella go up twice in the last hour to check, so fast the stairs didn't creak.

"He was in this room with us," Edward said. "I didn't hear him once. Not a word. Not a feeling. I've been doing this for ninety years, and I have never had someone stand six feet away from me and be—" He stopped. "Bella's quiet is soft. It's like snow. You can tell there's something under it. His has edges. It's like a wall someone built on purpose, and he knows exactly where every brick is." He looked down at his hands. "And then when he was out there with Leah, she went quiet too."

Layla stopped chewing.

"He covered her?"

"The whole time. I got nothing from her. Nothing from either of them. Just this—" Edward frowned. "Hum. Like bees in a wall."

"Muffliato," said Layla.

Five faces turned toward the kitchen. Well. Four. Bella's was still on the ceiling.

"Muffli-what," said Emmett.

"It's a charm. Fills people's ears with buzzing so they can't hear you talk. It's old, it's British, people use it in libraries—" She put the plate down. "That part's easy. But he covered her head too, whatever he does to Edward, he did it to her, at the same time, from fifty feet, without a wand, while he was talking." She heard her own voice getting thinner and made it stop. "Nobody learns that in a library."

"And after?" Carlisle asked. "When he'd gone?"

Edward was quiet a moment. "After, she came back," he said. "And then she walked away and took it with her."

He looked like he was going to say something else. He didn't.

Layla picked up the plate again for something to hold.

"Okay," Emmett said. He'd stopped at the foot of the stairs. "Okay. So somebody explain it to me. Because he stood right there"—he pointed at the middle of the room, and Layla's eyes went to it whether she wanted them to or not—"and he said the Department of Mysteries, like that's a real thing, like that's a thing a grown man says out loud. That's a Scooby-Doo thing. That's the name of a haunted amusement park. What *is* it?"

Nobody answered.

Layla realized they were all looking at her again.

Three hundred and some years of Carlisle. A century of Edward, nearly. Rosalie and Emmett had been alive through two world wars. And they were all looking at her, in her socks, in their kitchen, holding a piece of pie, because she was the only person in the house who'd ever held a wand.

She put the plate down for real this time and came around the island and into the living room, and stood by the end of the couch, because she didn't feel like sitting.

"It's real," she said.

"Great," said Emmett. "What is it?"

"Okay. You know the CIA."

"Yeah."

"And DARPA."

"The—" Bella's eyes came down off the ceiling at last. "The military research people. The ones who made the internet."

"And the NSA."

"Layla," said Rosalie.

"Put them in a blender," Layla said. "That's the Department of Mysteries. The spies and the mad scientists and the people listening to your phone calls, except they're all the same people, and they've got magic, and nobody knows who any of them are. It's part of the British Ministry of Magic. The bottom floor, under everything, nine levels down under London. You take an elevator. The people who work there are called Unspeakables."

"Why," said Bella.

"Because they can't speak. About the work. It's a binding. That's the story, anyway—they take an oath and it's magical, and if they try to tell you what they do, their mouth just won't." Layla spread her hands. "They can say where they work. They can't say one thing about it."

"He talked a hell of a lot," said Emmett.

"Did he tell you one single thing he does?"

Emmett opened his mouth. Closed it.

"He told you about *you*," Layla said. "Every single thing about you. About himself he told you his name and who signs his checks. That's what an Unspeakable sounds like."

Carlisle had turned from the glass. "What do they do?"

"Nobody knows."

"Layla."

"I mean it. That's the point. Nobody knows." She pushed her hair back off her face with both hands. "There are stories. Everybody I grew up around knew the stories. It's like—you guys have Area 51. Roswell, little gray men in jars. That's what the Department of Mysteries is, for us, except it's real, and the British don't even pretend it isn't. They just won't say what's in it."

"So what are the stories," said Rosalie.

"Time." Layla held up a finger. "They study time. They've got these little hourglasses on chains, Time-Turners, and you turn them and you go backwards—"

"Shut up," said Emmett.

"Hours. A few hours. Supposedly. There were a bunch of them, and then something happened to them in the nineties and nobody's seen one since." Second finger. "Death. There's supposed to be a room with an arch in it. Just a stone arch, with a curtain hanging in it, and the curtain moves when there's no wind. If you go through, you don't come out. People say you can hear voices behind it." Third. "Thought. Brains. I mean literally. There's supposed to be a tank of brains, floating, and they—I don't know. Study them. Read them." Fourth. "Space. Planets. Nobody knows what for." She looked at her thumb. "And love."

Emmett snorted.

"Don't," Layla said. "Where I come from, love's a force. It does things. Things you can measure. Mothers have stopped curses by standing in front of their kids. There's supposed to be a door down there that nobody can open, not even them, and behind it is whatever they know about love, and every story I ever heard says it's the most dangerous room in the building."

Nobody laughed.

Esme had come to the kitchen doorway. Layla hadn't heard her move. She stood there with her arms wrapped around herself, and she was looking at Carlisle, and Carlisle was looking at her.

"And prophecies," Layla said, more quietly. "They keep them. Real ones. Somebody says something true about the future, and it gets written down—or recorded, or whatever they do—and they put it in a glass ball, and they keep the glass balls on shelves. Thousands of them. Rows and rows." She swallowed. "That's the stuff you hear. That's the stuff that *leaks*. Everything else, nobody knows. And the people who work there are supposed to be—"

She stopped.

"Supposed to be what?" said Edward.

"The best," Layla said. "At everything. That's the rumor. You get in because you're the best Charms person anybody's seen in ten years, and also you can brew a potion that takes six months and kills you if you breathe on it wrong, and also you can break a curse on a tomb in Egypt, and also you can duel, and also you can do maths that would make your eyes bleed. All of it. Every one of them. You become an Unspeakable when there's nothing left anybody can teach you." She looked at the middle of the room. "And then they teach you the rest."

"So he's a spy," said Emmett slowly.

"He's the spy, and the guy who builds the spy's gun, and the guy who listens to the phone call where you talk about the spy. And the guy who invents the phone." Layla shrugged, helplessly. "He's all of them."

"Is he police?" said Bella. "Can he arrest us? Is that what this is?"

"No. Okay. No." Layla made herself slow down. "Aurors are police. Aurors are the British magic cops. They arrest you. You get a trial. A real one, with a—" She waved a hand. "Whatever. A courtroom. A judge. Aurors have rules."

"And Unspeakables don't," said Carlisle.

"Unspeakables don't arrest people."

"What do they do with people?" Rosalie asked.

Layla didn't answer right away.

The rain picked up, a gust of it, flung against the glass like gravel. Somewhere down the hill the river had gotten louder. She thought about the way he'd stood in this room and looked at each of them in turn, without hurrying, like he was reading the spines on a shelf.

"There are three curses," she said. "In my world. Out of every spell there is, there are three that are so bad they've got their own name. The Unforgivable Curses. You use one on a person—any person—and that's it. That's your life. Prison, forever. No parole, no appeal, no nothing. It doesn't matter why you did it."

"What are they?" said Carlisle.

"The first one's the Imperius Curse." She looked at Emmett. "It makes you do what somebody tells you. Anything. You feel great. You feel like you're floating. He'd tell you to walk into the river and hold yourself down on the bottom until the fish ate you and you'd go, and you'd thank him on the way in."

Emmett's jaw shifted.

"There are people who can fight it," Layla said. "It's supposed to be really rare. It's supposed to take a crazy kind of willpower. Most people can't." She didn't look at Edward. She didn't have to; she could feel him listening. "Second one's the Cruciatus Curse."

"Which does?" said Rosalie.

"Pain." Layla's voice came out flat. "That's it. That's all it does. It's pain, and it doesn't stop until they stop it. There's no wound. There's nothing to heal. It just—it's every nerve you've got, all at once, and it goes on as long as they want. People go insane from it. Like, permanently. There were two people in England, back in the first war, Aurors, they got it for so long they didn't know their own kid when he came to visit them. They're still in a hospital."

Esme made a small sound in the doorway.

"And the third," said Carlisle.

"The Killing Curse." Layla had to stop and wet her lips. "Green light. You're dead. That's it. There's no mark, no wound, no cause anybody can find. Your heart just stops. Nothing blocks it. There's no shield, there's no counter-curse, there's no potion. You can dodge it, or you can put something solid in the way, and that's the list. If it hits you, you die."

The room was very quiet.

"Does it work on us?" said Emmett.

He didn't say it like a joke.

"I don't know," Layla said. "Nobody knows. Nobody knew there was an *us* to try it on. It kills things. I've never heard of it caring what."

"You said a person who uses one goes to prison," said Edward. "For life."

"Yes."

"And Unspeakables."

"The rumor is," Layla said, "they're allowed."

Edward closed his eyes.

"Legally. They're the only ones. Even Aurors aren't, except there was this one time in the first war when—it doesn't matter. Aurors aren't. Unspeakables are supposed to have some kind of exemption, written into something, I don't know what, nobody's ever seen it. Some people say it's made up. Most people believe it." She looked around at all of them. "And I don't think it matters whether it's real, because who's going to arrest him?"

---

Nobody said anything for a while.

Emmett went and sat down on the couch next to Rosalie. It took him a long time to do it, by his standards, and when he sat, the frame creaked under him, and Rosalie put her hand on the back of his neck and left it there.

"Okay," Emmett said, to nobody. "Okay."

"What can they do to my dad?"

Bella hadn't moved from the bench. Her voice was very level. Edward's hand had found hers on the wood, and Layla could see the shape of his fingers, holding on.

Layla had been dreading that one.

"Not him," she said. "Not—the Unspeakables, I mean. Not him, probably. That's not his department." She heard how that sounded and tried again. "Okay. You know about the Statute. The—Secrecy. I told you guys."

"No one's allowed to know," said Bella.

"No one's allowed to know. Anywhere in the world. Every magic government on the planet signed it in sixteen-ninety-something—"

"Sixteen ninety-two," said Carlisle quietly.

Layla stared at him.

"You said it once," he said. "In passing. I remember dates."

Layla filed that away to be unsettled about some other time.

"Right. Sixteen ninety-two. And if a No-Maj—"

"A what," said Emmett.

"No-Maj. No magic. Regular people. Charlie. You guys were No-Majs, before." She stopped, unsure. "Kind of. I don't know what you are now. I don't think there's a word. The British say Muggle. We say No-Maj. Same thing." She went on before anyone else could interrupt. "If a No-Maj finds out, the government sends people. Obliviators. They take it out."

"Take it out," Bella repeated.

"The memory. They wipe it. Like it never happened."

"So that's—" Bella's hand had tightened on the bench. The dent got deeper. "So they'd make him forget the wolves. Jacob. Me. What I am."

"Bella," Layla said. "MACUSA isn't the British. You have to understand. MACUSA is—the British are strict. We're crazy. The American government, the magic one, the Magical Congress, they're in New York, and they are *so* much worse about this than anybody. Until 1965 we weren't allowed to marry No-Majs. We weren't allowed to be *friends* with them. You got caught having coffee with a No-Maj and you lost your wand."

"Nineteen sixty-five," said Carlisle.

"Yeah."

"That's very recent."

"Yeah. In the twenties they executed people. For breaking the Statute. For less than what's in this house." Layla made herself look at Bella. "And they do it messy. They take everything near it. Everything that smells wrong. If something weird happened to you on a Tuesday, they take the whole Tuesday. If something weird's been happening for two years, they take two years."

Bella didn't say anything.

"In 1926," Layla said, "they Obliviated New York. The whole city. All of it, at once. They put it in the rain."

Every head in the room turned toward the glass.

The rain came down on the lawn, gray and steady, the way it had come down all day, all month, all of every year in this town. It ran down the window in long uneven threads. Out past the deck, the river was a hiss under it.

"They probably wouldn't do that here," Layla said. "They could."

"Two years," said Bella.

"Bella—"

"Two years ago my dad didn't know Sue. Not really. Not like now." Bella's voice was getting quieter, which on a vampire was its own kind of loud. "Two years ago he didn't know I was—he didn't know there was anything. Two years ago he didn't have Renesmee." She looked at the ceiling again. "She's his granddaughter. She's the weirdest thing in his whole life. If they take everything that smells wrong—"

"They'd take her first," said Edward.

Bella turned and looked at him.

He was looking at Layla. Layla didn't know what he was hearing in her head and she didn't want to.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm—that's what they'd do. If they knew. They don't know." She held up both hands. "*They don't know.* There's nobody from MACUSA in Washington. I'd know. I'd—I'm pretty sure I'd know."

"He knows," said Rosalie.

"Yeah," said Layla. "He knows."

"And all he has to do," Edward said slowly, "is tell them."

Nobody answered him.

Layla thought about what Harry had said in this room this afternoon about Charlie. She'd thought it was a threat then. She'd thought it was the kind of threat where he'd go and do something with his hands. It hadn't occurred to her until right now that he didn't have to go anywhere at all. He didn't have to lift a finger. He could sit in a tent somewhere and write a letter.

"What about you?" said Seth.

She hadn't heard him come in.

He was standing inside the back door, which he'd shut behind him without a sound, and he was wet to the skin and barefoot and wearing nothing but the cutoffs he'd had tied to his ankle. Rain was running off his hair into his eyes. He didn't wipe it. He was looking at her and nothing else, and his face was very young.

"What about me?" Layla said.

"You said they execute people. For breaking the rule." He came a step closer. His feet left wet prints on the floor. "You told us. All of us. You told me. You do magic in front of us all the time."

"Seth."

"Are you in trouble?"

She thought about lying. She'd gotten pretty good at lying, the last year or so. She'd lied to her whole world for him, and to his whole world for herself, and most days it just felt like living in two houses at once. But Edward was sitting right there, and it was Seth, and she was too tired.

"Yeah," she said. "Probably. If anybody wanted me to be."

"Does *he* want you to be?"

"I don't know what he wants."

Seth's hands had closed into fists at his sides. He wasn't shaking; Seth almost never shook. But the heat was coming off him so hard she could feel it from six feet away, and she watched him make his hands open again, one finger at a time.

"Okay," he said. "Okay."

"Hey." She crossed the floor to him and took one of the hands. It was so hot. It was always so hot. "Hey. We don't know anything yet."

"We know where he's sleeping," Seth said.

---

He told them standing in the middle of the living room with a towel Esme had brought him around his shoulders, dripping on the rug, and he told it the way Seth told everything, all at once and slightly out of order, and twice he had to stop and start a sentence over.

"Jake said tell you," he said to Carlisle. "Not to go. He said tell you so you'd *know*. Nobody goes. He was really clear about that. He looked at me." He tapped his temple. "In here. He *looked* at me."

"We won't go," said Carlisle.

Emmett said nothing.

"He's got a tent," Seth said. "Down at Bogachiel. Past the state park, like a mile. On some guy's lot, by the river—Pruett, the guy's name is Dale Pruett. A green tent under the hemlocks. He said—he told Leah—you'd smell it."

"Told Leah," Rosalie said.

"Yeah." Seth's eyes went to the floor. "That's what—that's what they were talking about. Out there."

Edward said nothing. Layla glanced at him and found him looking at his hands.

"He told her where he's camping," Carlisle said carefully. "Why?"

Seth was quiet for a second. When he talked again, his voice had gone careful too, and lower, and she realized he was trying to figure out how much of his sister's head he was allowed to give away.

"He wants to teach her something," he said. "This—thing. Occ—Occlu-something."

"Occlumency," said Layla.

Her mouth had gone dry.

"Yeah. That. He said it's how you close your mind. He said it's what he does to Edward. He said he doesn't know if it'll work on a wolf, because nobody's ever tried, but if it did, she'd have—" Seth stopped. Started again. "She'd have a door. That's what he said. A door in her head, that she could shut. On us. On the pack."

The room went very still.

"On you," said Esme softly.

"On me," said Seth. "On Jake. On Sam, if she ever—" He shook his head. "On everybody."

"And she said?" said Carlisle.

"She told him to go to hell." A ghost of a smile went across Seth's face and was gone. "And he said it was on the list."

"But she's going," said Edward.

He said it very quietly and flatly, the way he said things he'd heard. Layla looked at him, and Seth looked at him, and for a second Seth's face crumpled, just a little, before he got it back.

"She didn't say," Seth said. "She didn't have to. We could all hear her not saying."

Nobody had anything to add to that.

Layla's mind was going a hundred miles an hour. *Occlumency.* She knew what it was the way she knew what a heart transplant was. She'd read about it. She'd never met anyone who could do it, not really, not past the kind of thing a person did in a bad mood when they didn't want to talk. It was supposed to be so hard that people spent their whole lives at it and never got anywhere. It was one of those subjects they mentioned in the back of textbooks, in the chapter about things you'd never need. Unspeakables did it. Aurors did it, the good ones. Dark wizards did it, the really bad ones. And now he was going to teach it to a werewolf—a shape-shifter—a *Quileute girl* in a tent by the Bogachiel River, on some guy's lot, for free.

"Did he say anything else?" she asked.

Seth hesitated.

"Seth."

"It's dumb."

"Tell me anyway."

He pulled the towel tighter around himself. His eyes had gone wet, which on Seth meant nothing in particular; Seth cried at commercials. But this was different. She watched him swallow twice.

"He knew about Dad," he said. "He'd been in her head. He knew what it does to her, him having Dad's name. And right at the end he stopped and turned around and he told her she didn't have to call him Harry. If it—stuck. He said, *Potter does fine*." Seth wiped his face finally, with the corner of the towel. "That's all. It's dumb. It was just—nice. It was a nice thing to do. I didn't think he'd do a nice thing."

Layla sat down.

The couch was behind her, and her knees stopped, and then she was sitting on it, on the edge, with both hands flat on her thighs.

"Layla?" said Seth.

"Potter," she said.

"Yeah."

"He said *Potter*."

"Yeah, he—Layla? What's wrong?"

She laughed. It came out wrong, high and short, and she put a hand over her mouth to stop it.

"Oh my God," she said through her fingers. "Oh my *God*."

"What," said Emmett. "*What*. Is that supposed to mean something?"

Layla took her hand away from her mouth.

"He said Harry," she said. "On the ridge. He said *Harry* and I thought—I didn't even think. There's a million Harrys. He sounds English, or South African, or both, I couldn't tell, and there's a million Harrys in all of those places, it's like being called Mike. And then I saw him with no wand, and I thought, okay, he's good, he's really good, I didn't let myself think the rest of it, because why would *he* be in *Forks*—" She was talking too fast. She made herself breathe, which was a stupid habit in a room where nobody else had to. "He's supposed to have a scar."

"A scar," said Carlisle.

"Here." She touched her own forehead, above the right eye. "Lightning bolt. Every picture. Every single picture, every single story, he's got this scar, and he didn't—"

"He doesn't have a scar," said Bella. "He doesn't have any marks at all. I looked."

"I know. I *know*. I looked too. I thought—" Layla pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "So either he got rid of it, or it's a different Potter, and I don't think there's a different Potter. I think there's one."

"*Layla*," said Rosalie, sharply. "Who is he."

Layla dropped her hands.

"He's Harry Potter," she said. "He's—okay. Okay." She looked around at them, at their blank, beautiful, waiting faces, and tried to think how to make them understand something that every witch and wizard in the world had known since before she was born. "There was a wizard. In England. A really bad one. The worst one anybody's had in a hundred years. He was killing people all over England and nobody could stop him, and in 1981 he went to a house to kill a family, and he killed the dad, and he killed the mom, and then he pointed his wand at the baby and used the Killing Curse."

"The one that nothing blocks," said Edward.

"The one that nothing blocks. And it bounced. It didn't kill the baby. It hit the wizard instead, and the wizard just—went. Gone. Disappeared. And the baby had a scar shaped like a lightning bolt on his forehead and that was all. He was one. He was a year old."

"Harry Potter," said Esme.

"The Boy Who Lived." Layla's mouth twisted. "That's what they call him. That's on, like, *cards*. Kids have his face on stuff."

"And the wizard?" said Carlisle.

"Came back. When Harry was fourteen. There was another war. Two, three years of it, and it was bad, really bad, they were disappearing people off the streets in London. And it ended in 1998 at a school in Scotland, and Harry Potter killed him." She looked at the rug. "He was seventeen."

Emmett let out a long breath through his nose.

"So he's a war hero," Bella said.

"He's *the* war hero. He's—" Layla tried to find the comparison. "Imagine if Hitler shot a baby in the head and the bullet bounced back and Hitler vanished. And then thirteen years later Hitler came back, and the kid finished him, at seventeen, in front of everybody. That's—that's the scale. That's who he is to everybody I know. Everybody in the world who has a wand knows that name."

"And now he works for the Department of Mysteries," said Carlisle.

"And now he works for the Department of Mysteries." Layla gave a short, unhappy laugh. "Everybody thought he'd be an Auror. He was supposed to be the best Auror they ever had. Then nobody heard anything. There were rumors. People said he'd left. People said he was in a hospital. Somebody said he was living on an island with dragons." She shook her head. "Nobody ever said Unspeakable. And it makes so much sense. God, it makes so much *sense*."

"Why?" said Edward.

"Because when he was fifteen, he broke into it." Layla looked up at him. "The Department of Mysteries. Him and some school friends. In the middle of the night. There was a fight in there, a real one, with people who worked for the bad wizard. Adults. The whole building got torn up. And somebody died—I don't know who, nobody ever said who. And now he's grown up, and he works in the place where it happened." She swallowed. "The Killing Curse can't kill him, and he walks around the Department of Mysteries for a living, and he can go into people's heads, and nobody can go into his. And he's in Forks. And he's camping."

"Twice," said Seth suddenly.

Everybody looked at him.

"You said it didn't kill him when he was a baby. The curse. You said *twice*, before. When you were talking to Edward. You said the Killing Curse doesn't care what it kills and then you made this face." Seth pulled the towel tighter. "Like you were thinking it."

Layla had to think about it. She didn't remember making a face.

"There's a story," she said. "The night it ended. At the school. People say the bad wizard hit him with it again. In the woods, in front of a bunch of people, and he went down, and they all saw him go down. And then he got up." She shrugged. "That's the story. I don't know if it's true. The people who were there say it is."

"So the Killing Curse," said Emmett, "the thing nothing blocks, the thing that might or might not work on us. That guy's taken it in the face twice."

"Maybe."

"And he's the one who's allowed to use it."

"Maybe."

Emmett put his face in his hands and laughed. It went on for a while. Rosalie kept her hand on the back of his neck the whole time.

---

It was Emmett, of course, who said it, when the laugh was done.

"Bogachiel's twenty minutes," he said. "Less, if I run."

"No," said Carlisle.

"I'm just saying. It's a tent."

"It's a wizard's tent," said Layla.

Emmett looked up. "Seth said tent."

"Emmett. *Listen to me.*" She leaned forward on the couch. "A wizard's tent is bigger on the inside. A lot bigger. It could be a one-bedroom apartment in there. It could be a house. It could be a *maze*. And he's been sitting in it for however long, with nothing to do at night but put things on it, and you have no idea what's on it. You'd walk up to that tent and you'd lift the flap and you'd come out in a jar. Or you wouldn't come out. Or you'd come out and you'd be standing in your own driveway with no idea how you got there and you'd have forgotten Rosalie's middle name."

"Lillian," said Emmett automatically.

"For now," said Layla.

Rosalie's hand tightened on his neck.

"No one goes to Bogachiel," Carlisle said. He said it in the same quiet voice he'd used all day, but there was something under it now, and Emmett heard it, and stopped. "Jacob asked us not to. I'm asking you not to. He told Leah where he'd be because he wanted her to come. And he told her knowing she'd phase within the hour, and the whole pack would have it, and the pack would bring it here. He wanted us to know he wasn't hiding." He looked at the lawn again. "I believe him about that much."

"That's what worries me," said Edward.

"Yes," Carlisle said. "Me too."

Emmett didn't argue. He sat there with Rosalie's hand on his neck and his big hands hanging between his knees and he didn't say yes either. Layla saw Edward glance at him, once, a quick sideways flick. Then Edward looked away and let it go, and Layla thought about that later.

"Can you do it?" Bella asked her.

"Do what?"

"What he does. Close your head. Or—get into his."

Layla almost laughed again. "No."

"Is it hard?"

"It's—" She tried to think of how. "Okay. What Edward does is listening at a door. You can hear what's being said on the other side. Legilimency, which is what he did to Seth on the ridge, and what he did to all of you in here, I think—that's taking the door off its hinges and walking in. You can go through drawers. You can find stuff people forgot they had. You can find out what they had for breakfast when they were six." She shook her head. "Hardly anybody can do it. It's supposed to be one of the hardest things there is. And Occlumency's the other way, it's keeping people out, and it's harder. You have to be able to make your mind—quiet. All the way quiet. On purpose. Most people can't even do it for a second."

"Leah," said Seth softly, "can't do it for a second."

Nobody said anything.

"It's a discipline," Layla said, quoting without knowing she was quoting, because Seth had already told her everything else. "It's years. It's every day. He's going to make her want to scream."

"She already wants to scream," said Seth. "She's wanted to scream for two years."

He said it simply, not angry. Just true. And Layla, looking at his face, understood something about Leah Clearwater that she'd never let herself understand before, and about what Harry Potter had walked into this house and found lying there in the open, and picked up.

He'd known exactly what to offer her. He'd been in her head. He'd known what she wanted most in the world, and he'd walked out to the edge of the lawn in the rain and held it up where she could see it.

*Everybody in that house has a string on them*, Seth had said he'd said. *You haven't got anybody.*

Layla looked at Seth, standing on Esme's rug in his wet cutoffs with a towel around his shoulders, looking back at her. And she thought about the string between them, the one she'd felt pull tight on the ridge yesterday when she'd watched Harry Potter look at him, before she'd even known her wand was in her hand—and she thought about how a man who'd been inside Seth's head would know exactly where the other end of that string was tied.

It was tied to her.

"I'm going to ward the house," she said.

---

She did it in the rain, at eleven at night, with Seth following her around the outside of the Cullens' house holding an umbrella over her that she didn't need, because he wouldn't go inside and he wouldn't phase and he wouldn't let her do it alone.

It took her nearly two hours. She wasn't good at wards. She'd learned three or four out of books, and none of them were the kind of thing that stopped a person. They were the kind of thing that told you a person was coming. A Caterwauling Charm along the line of the drive, which would scream if something came up it. An Intruder Charm on the doors and windows, keyed to scent, because she didn't know how else to key it in a house where nobody's heart beat. A ring of something older around the whole property, low, at ankle height, that would tug at her sleep like a fishhook if anything crossed it that wasn't on the list.

She put Seth on the list. She put Jacob and Leah on the list. She put the Cullens on, one at a time, Carlisle and Esme and Edward and Bella and Rosalie and Emmett and Renesmee, saying their names under her breath over the wet grass while Seth held the umbrella and didn't say anything at all.

When she was done, she stood at the end of the drive and looked back up at the house. Every light was on. Through the glass wall she could see Esme in the kitchen, wiping down a counter that was already clean. Carlisle had gone up to his study. Edward was at the piano, not playing.

"Is it going to work?" Seth said.

"It's going to tell me he's here."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know," said Layla.

The rain ran off the umbrella in a ring around them. Seth was standing very close. He always stood very close; he ran so hot that she'd stopped needing a coat in October. She could feel him not asking the next thing.

"I won't let them take you," he said finally. "MACUSA. Or him. Or anybody."

"Seth."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it." She turned and looked up at him. He was so tall now, and he still didn't know what to do with his elbows. "That's the problem. You can't do anything about this one. If MACUSA comes for my wand, you can't bite them. If you bite them, they'll just—" She stopped. "They'll just take you too. They'll put you in a cage with a label on it. And then I'll do something stupid, and then they'll take me twice."

He didn't say anything.

"He knows that," Layla said. "That's what I can't stop thinking about. He knows that if he wanted to make me do something, he wouldn't have to touch me. He'd just have to look at you."

"I'd like to see him try."

"No," she said, "you really wouldn't."

He put his free arm around her. It was like standing next to a woodstove. She let herself lean on him, just for a second, in the rain, at the bottom of the Cullens' drive, with her wards humming low and thin all around them in a ring she'd made herself and didn't believe in.

"He told Leah she was the most dangerous wolf in La Push," said Seth.

"What?"

"He said it. Out there. Because nobody could pull her." Seth's voice had gone strange. "Because she hasn't got anybody."

"Seth—"

"I'm just saying. I think he meant it. I think he meant all of it." He was quiet for a while. "I think he meant us too. I think we're the opposite."

Layla didn't answer.

They went back up to the house together. Esme had made up the couch with blankets, and a pillow, and a glass of water on the side table, and Layla lay down in her clothes with her wand under the pillow and her shoes on the floor where she could find them, and Seth stretched out on the rug beside the couch with one arm up over his head, the way he slept everywhere, like a dog in front of a fire.

Edward was still at the piano. Bella had gone up to sit with Renesmee. The house was quiet and very bright.

Layla lay there and listened to her wards.

They hummed and hummed. At some point—she couldn't have said when—the hum turned into the rain, and the rain turned into the river, and she slept.

---

Harry came out of the squeeze on the riverbank at Bogachiel with his boots in two inches of gravel and the Bogachiel going past him, high and brown and loud with rain.

He stood there for a moment and let his ears catch up.

Nothing had followed him. Nothing could have, but he waited anyway, the full count, the way he'd been taught to wait by people who'd buried colleagues who didn't. The river. Rain on the hemlocks. A truck on the 101, a long way off, its tires hissing. Somewhere up the bank behind the tree line, Dale Pruett's dog had heard the crack and was thinking about barking, and decided against it.

The tent was where he'd left it, forty yards back from the water under the biggest of the hemlocks, a squat green A-frame with its guy ropes pegged into the duff. From the road it looked like a fisherman's tent that had been there too long. From the river it didn't look like anything. He'd made sure of that the first night.

He walked up to it. The wards opened for him and closed again behind, and he felt them settle, the way a dog turns twice and lies down.

Inside, the tent was a kitchen, a table, a cot, a cupboard, a cold box, a wood stove with a pipe that went up through a canvas roof eleven feet over his head, and a second room through a flap at the back that he hadn't bothered to furnish. It smelled of canvas and wood smoke and nothing else, because he'd taken the smell of everything else out. He lit the stove with a look and filled the kettle at the tap and put it on, and then he took his jacket off and hung it on the back of the chair and stood in the middle of the floor in his shirtsleeves with his hands on his hips, and was hungry.

He was nearly always hungry. He'd stopped minding.

He cooked four steaks in a cast-iron pan the size of a hubcap, and six eggs in the fat after, and half a loaf of bread toasted on the stovetop, and he ate all of it standing at the counter, and then he made two more eggs because the first six hadn't touched the sides. The kettle boiled. He made tea, strong, with too much sugar in it, the way Mrs. Weasley had made it for him the summer he was fifteen, when he'd come down to the kitchen at Grimmauld Place at four in the morning and she'd been there, and she hadn't asked.

Then he sat down at the table and took the phial out of his inside pocket and stood it upright on the wood in front of him.

The venom had settled overnight into two layers, which it hadn't done the first night. The bottom inch was clear as gin. The top quarter-inch had gone faintly milky, as if something in it had decided to rest. He'd taken it off the ridge with a cleaning charm turned inside out, a few drops' worth, while the thing they'd come out of had been too busy to object, and he'd sat up with it most of the night before, and he'd learned more from it in six hours than he'd learned from most things in six months.

He pulled the lamp closer and cast over it again. Three charms, then a fourth, then something old and Arithmantic that he'd had from a woman in the Department who'd made him swear on his mother not to write it down. The phial lit up, faintly, from the inside. Lines came off it in the air above the table, gold and pale green, branching, recursive. He looked at them for a long time.

Same as last night. The structure held. It was still doing the thing.

He got out a pad of paper and a pencil, because he didn't trust quills in the damp, and wrote VENOM at the top of a page and underlined it twice.

Under that he wrote: *Self-sustaining. No caster. No decay.*

He looked at that, then added: *Potion-ish. Behaves like a transfiguration that brews itself.*

Under that: *Ask Doc what he's tried.* Then he crossed out *Doc* and wrote *C. Cullen*, because the paper might end up somewhere it shouldn't, and then sat and looked at that and crossed out *C. Cullen* too and wrote *the physician*, and then he gave up on that and turned the page.

On the next page he wrote MINDS.

*Open. No natural barrier at all. None.* He thought about the ridge, the mind under his arm, what it had been like to go into it—a room with forty radios playing, every one on a different station, and every light on, and every door off its hinges. *Parallel tracks. Six, eight at once. Surface very loud. Depth very quiet.* He tapped the pencil on the page. *Human memory present under depth. Compressed. Faded. Intact.*

He underlined *intact*.

*The physician will want that.*

*So will R.*

*So will E.C., when he works out what it means.*

He turned the page again. GIFTS. He wrote for a while. Then he turned to a fresh sheet and wrote nothing on it at all for a long minute, and then he wrote:

*The child.*

And stopped.

He sat back in the chair and drank his tea.

He hadn't gone into her. He'd been near enough to feel her, the way you'd feel a stove through a wall, a small, steady, hot, strange little mind, thinking in pictures, and he'd left her alone, because he hadn't needed to go in and because she was a child. He knew what she was anyway. He'd had it out of Clifford's head, and Balto's, and Clearwater's, and—at one remove, through all three of them—out of every mind that had stood in a field in the snow and watched an old man in a dark cloak decide whether to kill her.

Half and half. Grows like a weed. Stops at seven. Sends pictures through her hands.

He'd known about her since yesterday on the ridge. Since then he'd thought of eleven different people in London who'd want her in a room.

He put the pencil down.

Then he pulled a fresh pad toward him, a different one, thinner, with the Ministry's crest embossed faintly into the corner of each sheet, and uncapped a pen, and wrote the report.

He wrote it twice.

The first one took him an hour and twenty minutes and ran to eleven pages, and it was true. He put everything into it. The ridge. The wolves—the pack-mind, the phasing, the treaty, the two packs and the line between them, *imprinting*, which he spelled out in full and then described in two clinical paragraphs that made his own jaw ache to write. The coven. The physician, the mind reader, the shield, the strength of the newest one, the hybrid child. The Volturi—everything he had on them, which was more than he'd have liked: the Italian girl who did pain with her eyes, the boy who made you feel nothing at all, the old man who could read a life off the back of your hand. The witch. Her breach. Her wards, which he hadn't seen yet but could guess at. The No-Maj police chief. The venom. The minds. The memories. *Recommend immediate assignment of a full team. Recommend liaison with MACUSA at Department level. Recommend—*

He read it back through once. Then he folded it in three and put it in an envelope, and sealed the envelope with his thumb, and the seal took his thumbprint and went dark, and he put it in the inside pocket of his jacket where the venom had been.

The second one took him four minutes.

*Survey of Olympic Peninsula, Washington, U.S.A. Reports of unusual non-wizard activity investigated. Local population of non-wizard magical persons small, stable, contained, culturally isolated, aware of the need for discretion. One witch, American, in proximity; no action necessary at this time. No threat to Statute observed. No further assignment recommended. Will continue light observation at own discretion.*

He signed it. He read it back. It was a very boring piece of paper.

He folded it once and left it on the table, where he'd see it in the morning.

Then he took out a third sheet, plain this time, no crest, and wrote a letter.

It was short. He'd written a lot of letters like it, over the years, to a lot of different offices. He addressed it to the Department of Magical Law Enforcement, Magical Congress of the United States of America, the Woolworth Building, New York. He wrote that he was writing as a concerned party on behalf of the International Statute of Secrecy, that he wished to bring to the Congress's attention a non-magical person resident in Clallam County, Washington, holding a position of civil authority—Chief of Police, Forks, Washington—who was in sustained possession of knowledge of magic and magical beings, and who had been for some time. He gave the name. He gave the address, which he'd had out of four different heads. He did not sign it. He folded it, and put it in an envelope, and wrote the address on the front, and didn't seal it.

Then he sat and looked at it for a while.

He'd met Charlie Swan's mind the way you meet a man's handwriting on an envelope—at second hand, through other people. Through Clifford, a great deal. Through Balto, a little. Through Lassie, in two or three flinches, a kitchen in La Push with a casserole dish going cold on the table and a man in a police uniform standing in the doorway with his hat in his hands, too careful, the week after the funeral. A decent copper. Slow to talk. Fished. Had never once, as far as anybody's memory went, told his daughter he loved her in so many words. Had a granddaughter he wasn't allowed to ask questions about. Had made himself stop asking.

He was the one nobody in that house would let go of. Every single one of them. Swan because he was her father. Romeo because of Swan. Doc and Esme because of Swan and Romeo both. Clifford because the man was his dad's best friend and had been at every birthday Clifford could remember. Balto and Lassie because of their mum. The little one because he was her grandfather and she loved him with the whole of whatever she had.

The witch, because she'd broken the law to be part of all of it, and if Charlie Swan went, so did she.

He'd never have to touch him. That was the thing. He didn't have to go near the man. He didn't have to cast a single spell. All he had to do was stop not sending a letter.

He put the envelope on top of the boring report, and the boring report on top of the pad, and he sat back and drank the rest of his tea, which had gone cold.

---

At ten past three the outer ward woke him.

He was out of the cot and on his feet before he was all the way awake, which was the same as it had been since he was seventeen, and he stood in the dark with one hand on the tent pole and listened.

Upriver. The far side of the state park, along the bank, coming down. Fast, then slowing. Very quiet. It didn't breathe.

He let it come.

He went to the flap and opened it a hand's width and looked out into the rain. There was nothing to see at first; it was black under the hemlocks, black on the water, and the rain was coming down so hard now that it hung in the air like smoke. Then something moved on the bank a hundred yards up, near the big snag where the river bent, and stopped.

It was big. Even at that distance, even in the dark, he could see it was big—broad through the shoulders, a head taller than a tall man, standing at the edge of the gravel with the rain sheeting off it. It stood there for a long time, not moving at all, the way only one kind of thing in this world could stand. Looking down the bank at the tent.

Harry stood in the flap and looked back.

Then the second ward caught it.

He hadn't put anything ugly on the outer ring. Nothing that hurt, nothing that held. Just the old Muggle-Repelling Charm, laid wide and thin and low, the one every wizard in the world put around a campsite—the one that made a hiker on the trail suddenly remember he'd left the oven on, or the cat in the car, or that he had to be somewhere else, somewhere urgent, right away. He'd set it that wide on purpose. He'd wanted to know.

The big shape on the bank flinched.

It stood very still for another second. Then it turned its head, slowly, back the way it had come, and stood like that, like a man who'd just heard his name called from a long way off. It turned around. It went back up the bank, not fast at all, at a walk, and it stopped once at the snag and looked back over its shoulder at the river, puzzled, and then it went on, and the dark under the trees closed behind it, and it was gone.

Harry let the flap fall.

"Huh," he said, to the inside of the tent.

He went back to the table and lit the lamp and found the pad, and under MINDS he wrote:

*Muggle-Repelling — effective. Full effect at 100 yds on one adult male. Turned without resistance. Puzzled after.*

And then, under it:

*They count as No-Maj for the purposes of the charm.*

And he sat and looked at that for a while, and thought about everything it meant, and wrote two more lines, and then he put the pencil down and went back to bed.

---

He was up again at half past five. He made tea and drank it. He made breakfast and ate it, and it was a large breakfast, and then he cleaned the pan with a flick of his fingers and put it away.

Then he took the unsealed letter off the table and slipped it into the inside pocket of his jacket, behind the sealed one. He folded the boring report once more and put it in the outside pocket, where it would be easy to get at.

He took a fourth envelope out of the cupboard—heavy, cream, already sealed and already addressed—and turned it over in his fingers. A copy of the letter he'd written last night, word for word, done in duplicate while he'd been writing the first. It had a charm on it that he'd learned from the same woman in the Department who'd taught him the Arithmancy. If his thumb didn't touch the seal once in every twenty-four hours, it would take itself to New York.

He touched it now. The seal ticked under his thumb, faintly, like a watch, and went still.

He put it back in the cupboard and shut the door on it, and put a ward on the cupboard, and a second ward on the first.

Then he stood in the middle of the tent and thought about the order he'd do things in.

The witch first. She was the hinge. If she went, the rest went with her—the boy would follow her, and the boy's alpha would follow the boy, and the Cullens would follow the wolves, because that was what they'd done a year ago in a field in the snow and they'd do it again. So the witch first, and fast, before she'd had her coffee. Then the big one, because he'd want to get his temper out of the way, and it was better for everyone if he did it early. Then the chit. Then the doctor, because the doctor was the one who'd actually decide. Then whatever was left.

The Clearwater girl wouldn't be there. He'd have bet the tent on it.

He put his jacket on. He checked the pockets—the letter, the sealed report, the boring one, the phial.

At twenty to seven, in the gray light, with the rain still coming down and not one drop of it touching him, Harry turned on his heel under the hemlocks and was gone.

---

At twenty to seven in the morning, Edward was sitting at the piano with his hands on the closed lid, listening to the house.

He'd been listening to it all night. He'd tried not to. He'd tried reading, and then he'd tried playing, very softly, the way he hadn't needed to play since Bella had slept in his old room with her heart going, and then he'd stopped, because every few bars he'd lift his hands off the keys to listen better, and you couldn't play like that.

So he listened.

Esme, in the kitchen, rolling out dough. Cinnamon rolls, four dozen, for the wolves. She'd been thinking in recipes since five, the way she did when she needed the inside of her head to be orderly: *butter, sugar, the brown not the white, a quarter of an inch, roll it tight*. Underneath, very quietly, she was thinking about a door at the edge of the woods that she'd watched a girl stand at for a long time in the rain.

Carlisle, upstairs in the study, turning the pages of one of his own notebooks from the eighteen-thirties without seeing them, and thinking, over and over, in a loop he couldn't get out of: *sixteen ninety-two. Sixteen ninety-two. There was a world, and I walked through it, and I never saw.*

Rosalie and Emmett, in the garage. Rosalie had the hood up on the Volvo and was doing something to it that didn't need doing. Emmett was sitting on an upturned crate by the workbench with his elbows on his knees, and he was thinking about an oven.

Edward had heard him go, at three. He'd heard the whole thing assemble itself in Emmett's head over an hour—*just to look, I'm only gonna look, twenty minutes, I'll be back before Rose even knows*—and he'd sat at this piano and let him go. He'd told himself it was because you couldn't stop Emmett once Emmett had decided. That was true. It was also true that Edward had wanted to know what was down there at least as much as Emmett did.

He'd heard him come back at four. Slowly, at a walk, up the drive, through Layla's wards, which had tugged at her sleep without waking her. And the whole way back, Emmett had been thinking, in a puzzled, circling, slightly stupid loop, about whether he'd left the oven on. Which was absurd. Emmett hadn't been within ten feet of an oven since 1935. He'd come in and gone straight to the garage and hadn't said a word to Rosalie, and Rosalie hadn't asked, and she knew.

Upstairs, past Carlisle, in what had been his own room, Renesmee was asleep. Her dreams were all pictures, as they always were—bright, sharp, unconnected: a heron on the river; Jacob's hand; the inside of a glass ball with something moving in it like smoke. That last one had come and gone half a dozen times since midnight. He didn't like it.

Jacob was asleep across her doorway, on the floor, like a draft excluder. He'd come in at ten past four smelling of rain and pine and wet earth, and gone up the stairs three at a time and lain down there without a word to anybody, and he'd been dreaming ever since about running—a long line through the trees, north, and a gray shape ahead of him in the dark that he couldn't catch up to and didn't try very hard to.

Leah was out of range. She'd been out of range since a little after midnight.

On the couch, Layla was dreaming about her wards, which was a thing he'd never heard a person do before. In her dream they were made of string, and somebody was walking along them very carefully, plucking each one like a guitar, and she couldn't see who. Seth was asleep on the rug beside her with one arm flung up over his head, dreaming about nothing in particular, the way only Seth seemed able to.

And beside Edward, on the bench, there was Bella.

Bella was a silence. He'd learned the shape of it a long time ago. It sat next to him the way the sea sits at the end of a road—you couldn't see past it, but you knew there was a great deal out there, moving. When she leaned her shoulder into his, as she was doing now, the silence leaned too. He'd stopped, a long time ago, wishing he could hear past it. Mostly.

That was the house. He'd been listening to it for nine hours, and it had all been exactly where he'd left it.

At twenty to seven, Esme's mind went white with surprise—the clean, total surprise of a person who'd turned around from the counter with a floury rolling pin in her hand and found something where nothing had been. *Oh—*

Edward was in the kitchen doorway before the thought had finished.

There was a man sitting at the kitchen island.

He was sitting on one of the high stools that nobody ever used, with his elbows on the marble and the pie dish from last night in front of him, and he had a fork in his hand, and he was eating the pie. Straight out of the dish. Five of the eight slices were gone, counting the one Layla had eaten. He was wearing the same dark jacket as yesterday, and the same boots, and there was no rain on him, and no mud on the boots, and he hadn't come through a door that Edward had heard, and he hadn't come up the drive.

He hadn't come into Edward's head at all. There'd been no smell of him, either, until Edward breathed in, standing there—and then there was, all at once, rain on stone and pine pitch and cold river rock, as if somebody had lifted a cover off it.

There were two silences in the house now.

Edward stood in the doorway and felt the new one the way you'd feel a missing tooth with your tongue. Bella's was soft. This one had edges. It sat at the kitchen island eating Esme's pie, six feet away, and it was so complete, so finished, that it had a shape—Edward could feel the outside of it, like a hand on the outside of a wall in the dark, and there was no seam in it anywhere, no crack, nothing to push against.

He pushed anyway. He couldn't help it.

"Morning, Romeo," said Harry Potter, without looking up. "You can stop knocking. You'll give yourself a headache."

Bella was beside Edward in the doorway. He hadn't felt her come.

Harry finished the slice he was on. He chewed, and swallowed, and cut another piece out of the dish with the side of the fork, and then he looked up at Esme, who was still standing at the counter with the rolling pin in her hand and flour to her wrists.

"This is very good," he said. "Is there any milk?"

Edward heard what happened in Esme's head then, and he would think about it later, for a long time. *He's hungry*, she thought, in the oldest part of herself, the part that had kept going through ninety years and a death and a fall from a cliff, the part that had fed every stray thing that had ever come to her door. And she put the rolling pin down, and she went to the refrigerator, and she took out the gallon of milk they kept for the wolves, and poured a tall glass of it, and set it on the island at his elbow.

And then she thought, very clearly: *What am I doing?*

"Thanks," said Harry, and drank half of it.

---

They came from everywhere at once.

Carlisle from the stairs, in his shirtsleeves, a notebook still in his hand. Rosalie and Emmett from the garage, Rosalie first, with a socket wrench she'd forgotten she was holding. In the living room, Seth woke up all at once, the way the wolves did—asleep and then on his feet in one motion, hot and wild-eyed, his head turning toward the kitchen—and on the couch, Layla sat bolt upright and put her hand under the pillow.

Edward heard her mind go off like a dropped tray.

*Wand. Where's my—it was right here, it was right here, I slept on it—*

"Here," said Harry.

He'd slid off the stool. He came through the kitchen doorway past Edward and Bella—close enough to touch; he didn't hurry and he didn't go around them—with the pie dish in his left hand and a wand in his right, holding it by the tip, handle out. He walked across the living room to the couch, and he held it out to Layla, and he waited.

Layla stared at it.

"You'll want it," Harry said.

She took it. Edward watched her fingers close on it, and heard what was going on in her head—a sort of horrified arithmetic, *under my pillow, under my head, I didn't feel it go, I didn't feel anything*—and watched her not point it at him. It took her a lot not to point it at him.

"How," she said.

"You keyed the ring to names," Harry said. "Lovely bit of work. Old. Very sensitive. I took it apart at the river side, walked in, and put it back together behind me. It's all still there. Every name. Check it."

Layla's eyes went unfocused for a second. Edward heard her reach out, the way he'd heard her reach out last night along her lines in the rain, and find them—all of them—intact, humming, exactly as she'd left them.

"Your Caterwauling Charm wants lifting a foot or two off the ground," Harry went on, "or it'll go off for every raccoon on the peninsula. The Intruder Charm on the doors you keyed to scent, which in a house where nobody smells like a person was brave. It'll do for wolves. It won't do for anything else." He sat down in the armchair at the end of the couch, the one by the glass, and set the pie dish on his knee. "And your back door doesn't lock."

"It's never locked," said Esme faintly, from the doorway. "We've never needed it to."

"No," Harry agreed. "I suppose you wouldn't have."

He ate a bite of pie.

Everyone was in the room now. Edward could feel them all around him, ranged in a rough half-circle that none of them had chosen on purpose—Carlisle by the stairs, Rosalie and Emmett by the garage door, Esme in the kitchen doorway, Bella beside him, Seth on his feet by the couch. Every mind in the room was pointed at the armchair by the glass, like compass needles. And in the middle of all of them, there was the silence, sitting with its legs crossed and a pie dish on its knee, looking at Layla.

Upstairs, Jacob had woken up. Edward heard him come awake on the floor outside Renesmee's door—*that smell, that's him, that's HIM*—and then heard him stay exactly where he was, on the floor, across the doorway, every muscle locked, because the most important thing in the house was behind that door and he was not going to leave it.

Good, Edward thought. Stay there.

"Layla," said Harry. "Let's count."

Layla said nothing. She was holding her wand in her lap with both hands.

"You've told a pack of shape-shifters that witches are real," Harry said. He didn't hold up fingers. He just said it, in the same even voice, the accent that wasn't quite English sliding the vowels flat. "You've told a family of vampires. You've cast in front of all of them—on the ridge, in this house, and on their lawn for two hours last night in the rain. You've told Balto everything you know. I'd guess you've told him a few things you only think you know."

"Leave her alone," said Seth.

"I'm talking to her, Balto."

"Stop calling me that."

Harry looked at him. It was a short look. Seth's mind—hot, frightened, furious, a bright red flare of it—went on burning, but Seth didn't say anything else.

"Under MACUSA's rules," Harry went on, to Layla, "which I didn't write, and wouldn't have, that's your wand, for a start. Snapped, or taken, or whatever they do with them now in New York. That's a hearing. That's probably a while somewhere I've never been and wouldn't like to go." He paused. "You'll have told them all that last night."

"Yes," Layla said.

"Good. Saves time."

Layla's mind was very loud. Edward tried not to listen and couldn't help it. She was thinking about the Woolworth Building, which she'd seen once, from the outside, as a child, holding someone's hand. She was thinking about a girl she'd heard of who'd been sent away for less. She was thinking about Seth. Mostly she was thinking about Seth.

"I'm not going to send anyone after you," Harry said.

Layla looked up.

"Why not?" said Rosalie, from the garage door. It came out like a slap.

"Because she's more use to me here with a wand," Harry said, "than in New York without one."

Nobody spoke.

"So, Layla. From this morning, you work for me." He said it the way someone else might have said *it's raining*. "There won't be any paper. You don't write to anybody in New York. Nobody in your family, nobody you were at school with, nobody you used to know who's got a job in that building now. If anyone writes to you asking whether you've met a British wizard in Forks, you write back that you've never heard of one, and you send me the letter they sent you. And when I need something done on this side of the water that wants a local wand and a local face, it's yours."

"And if I say no," Layla said.

She said it steadily. Edward heard how much it cost her.

Harry didn't answer.

He didn't have to. He looked away from her, very slowly, and let his eyes come to rest on Seth, standing beside the couch with his hands in fists and the heat coming off him in waves. He looked at Seth for perhaps two seconds. Then he looked back at Layla.

That was all.

In Layla's head, something sat down.

"Fine," she said.

"Good," said Harry. "I'll help you with the wards. You'll learn something." He went back to the pie.

Seth's mind had gone very quiet. Edward glanced at him and saw his face—young, and white around the mouth, and stunned—and understood that Seth had just watched himself be used, in front of everyone, as the thing you hold to a girl's head, and that he'd felt it happen and had done nothing, because there'd been nothing to do.

---

"Emmett," said Harry, without looking up from the dish. "How was your walk?"

Emmett went still by the garage door.

It was a very particular stillness, and everyone in the room knew it, even the ones who couldn't hear inside his head. Rosalie turned and looked at him. The socket wrench in her hand made a small sound, metal under strain.

"What walk," she said.

"Ten past three," said Harry. "Upriver, along the bank, about a hundred yards short of my tent. By the big snag where the river bends. You stood there for a good while. You stood very well. I'd not have seen you if I hadn't been looking." He ate another bite. "Then you remembered you'd left the oven on."

The silence that followed was very long.

"I don't—" Emmett's voice came out rough. "We don't use the oven."

"No," Harry agreed. "But you remembered it anyway."

Esme made a sound in the kitchen doorway that was almost, unbelievably, a laugh, and smothered it.

Carlisle's mind, by the stairs, had gone very sad and very tired. *Emmett*, it said, nothing else, just the name, the way you'd say it to a child who'd done the exact thing you'd asked him not to. Rosalie's mind had gone a white so hot it hurt to be near. Emmett's own mind was a mess—embarrassment, rage, a kind of hurt bewilderment—and, still, circling around underneath like a fish in a tank, the oven.

"Don't be hard on him," Harry said, to Rosalie. "He told me something I wanted to know."

"What," Rosalie said, between her teeth.

"That the Muggle-Repelling Charm works on vampires." Harry set his fork down in the dish. "It's the oldest bit of camping magic there is. Every witch and wizard in the world puts one around a tent. It doesn't hurt anyone, it doesn't hold anyone. It makes a No-Maj walking up the trail suddenly remember he's left the oven on, or the dog in the car, or that he's got to be somewhere else, right now, urgently. It doesn't touch wizards. It doesn't touch house-elves, or goblins, or anything else with a bit of magic in it." He looked at Emmett. "It turned you round at a hundred yards like you were a man walking his dog."

Emmett's jaw worked.

"So as far as my charms are concerned," Harry said, "you count as No-Majs. Your bodies are magic, and your venom's magic, and your gifts are magic—I'll come back to that—but your heads are No-Maj heads. Ordinary. Open." He picked the fork back up. "That's interesting. That's very interesting. I sat up for a while thinking about it."

"You son of a bitch," said Emmett.

"Next time," Harry said, "I'll let you all the way in. You can see what it's like on the inside."

Emmett went for him.

Edward felt it coming a quarter-second before it happened—the decision snapping shut in Emmett's head like a trap—and he didn't move to stop it. Nobody did. Emmett crossed the room in less time than it took to see him do it, low and fast and enormous, both arms out, the way he went into a grizzly, the way he'd gone into the newborns in the clearing the summer Victoria came, the way he'd been going into things since 1935, and the floor cracked under his back foot where he pushed off—

And he stopped.

In the air. A yard short of the armchair. Horizontal, both arms out, his feet a foot off the floor and his body tilted forward into the lunge, and he hung there, absolutely still, like a photograph of a man jumping.

Harry hadn't moved. He hadn't lifted a hand. He was sitting in the armchair with his legs crossed and the pie dish on his knee and the fork in his fingers, and he looked at Emmett hanging there in front of him with no particular expression at all.

"Rosalie," he said, without turning his head. "Don't."

Rosalie stopped. She'd been in the middle of the room. Edward had no idea how she'd got there.

Emmett's mind was screaming—a total, blank, animal outrage at being *stopped*, at not being able to move a finger. There was no pain in it at all. He couldn't feel anything holding him. That was the horror of it. He strained against nothing. Nothing gave.

Harry looked at him for another second. Then he moved him—lifted him, turned him gently in the air, the way you'd turn a sleeping child to carry it—and set him down on the couch beside Layla, sitting, his hands on his knees, his feet on the floor. And let go.

Emmett sat there.

"Not a mark on him," Harry said to Rosalie. "Check if you like."

Rosalie checked. She was beside Emmett in an instant, her hands on his face, his shoulders, his arms, and Emmett let her, staring straight ahead at nothing. Edward listened to the inside of his brother's head and heard only one thought, very small, at the bottom of everything, under the rage: *I couldn't move. Rose, I couldn't move.*

On the other side of Emmett, Layla had both hands wrapped around her wand and was looking at Harry with an expression Edward didn't have a word for. In her head, very clearly, she was thinking: *No wand. No word. No gesture. He didn't even look at him properly.* And then, after it: *A two-hundred-pound man going at a dead run, and he just—caught him. Like a ball.*

Emmett weighed a great deal more than two hundred pounds. Edward didn't say so.

"Right," said Harry, and set the pie dish on the side table. "Now that's done."

---

He took a folded sheet of paper out of the outside pocket of his jacket and unfolded it on his knee.

"I'm writing a report," he said. "Two, actually. I've done both. This is one of them." He looked down at the paper. "*Survey of Olympic Peninsula, Washington, U.S.A. Reports of unusual non-wizard activity investigated. Local population of non-wizard magical persons small, stable, contained, culturally isolated, aware of the need for discretion. One witch, American, in proximity; no action necessary at this time. No threat to Statute observed. No further assignment recommended. Will continue light observation at own discretion.*" He looked up. "That's it. That's all of it. That's the most boring piece of paper I've ever written, and I've written some proper rubbish."

He folded it again and put it back in the pocket.

Then he touched the left side of his jacket, over the inside pocket, lightly, with two fingers.

"The other one's in here," he said. "It's eleven pages."

"What's in it," said Carlisle.

"You."

Carlisle didn't say anything.

"All of you," Harry said. "Everything. Everything I got out of Clifford's head and Balto's and Clearwater's and everything I worked out in this room yesterday. The treaty and the two packs. The phasing. The thing they do with their—" He stopped. "I'll get to that. The physician. The mind reader. The shield. The newborn. The child." He let that sit. "The three old men in Italy and the girl with the eyes. The venom. The minds. What I just learned from Emmett. And at the bottom, my recommendation, which is a full team, and liaison with New York at Department level, and somebody senior coming over from London to have a look at the little one in person."

Upstairs, behind the door, Renesmee turned over in her sleep.

"I've got both of them," Harry said. "I'm going to send one. You decide which."

Edward could hear every mind in the room going in different directions, all at once, like birds coming off a wire. He made himself sort them. Carlisle: *what does he want, what does he want, what does he want.* Esme: *please, please, please.* Rosalie, crouched beside Emmett: *I'll kill him. I'll find a way. I'll find a way and I'll kill him.* Emmett: *I couldn't move.* Seth: *Layla. Layla. Layla.* Layla: *he means it, he'll do it, he'll do it and he won't even feel bad.* Bella: silence. And under all of it, upstairs, Jacob, who could hear every word, lying across a doorway with his hands flat on the floor and his whole body shaking with the effort of not coming down the stairs.

"And so you understand what's riding on it," Harry said.

He reached into the inside of his jacket again and took out an envelope.

He held it up so they could read the front. It wasn't sealed. The address was written in a plain, square, upright hand: *Department of Magical Law Enforcement, Magical Congress of the United States of America, The Woolworth Building, New York.*

"That's a letter to New York," he said. "It's about Charlie Swan."

Bella stood up.

Edward felt her go. He'd been holding her hand, and then he wasn't; she was on her feet, and the piano bench under him lurched sideways a foot with the force of her leaving it, and every one of her muscles was locked in a way he'd only seen once before, in a field in the snow—and her silence, the soft wide silence he'd lived beside for years, had gone hard as a wall.

"Sit down, Swan," said Harry.

She didn't.

"If you touch my father—"

"I won't touch him," said Harry. "I'm not going anywhere near him. I don't have to." He held the envelope a little higher. "That's the point. That's the whole point of it. I don't have to cast a single spell. I don't have to go near his house. I write two lines—I've written them—and I put a stamp on it, and I find an owl, and this town's full of owls. It's a long way to New York for an owl. Four days, maybe five. And then there's two people from the Woolworth Building standing on Charlie's front step in long coats, and they knock on the door, and he opens it, and they're very polite."

Bella was shaking. Edward could see it. He was on his feet beside her now, his hand on her arm, and she let him keep it there, barely.

"They'll take more than they need," Harry said. "That's how they work here. Layla will have told you."

"She told us," said Edward.

"Then you know. They'll take everything near it. Everything that smells wrong. The wolves. Clifford, phasing in his front yard. You—what you are, what you were, all of it. Sue, probably, because Sue came with the wolves. That's two years of a man's life, out, all at once, in a quarter of an hour, on his own front step." He paused. "And they'll take the little one first. Because she's the strangest thing in his whole life."

"*Stop*," said Bella.

"He'll still have a granddaughter," said Harry. "He just won't know who she is."

The sound Bella made was not one Edward had ever heard her make. It came out of her low and torn, like something big tearing in half, and she went forward—

He held her.

It took everything he had. She was the youngest of them and the strongest of them and she was the one he'd have bet on in a fight with anyone in this room, and he got both arms around her from behind and planted his feet and held, and the floor groaned, and he felt her nearly break his hold and then not, and then not again.

And the whole time, Harry sat in the armchair and watched her with the envelope in his hand, and didn't move, and didn't get up, and didn't do to her what he'd done to Emmett.

He could have. Edward knew that, suddenly, with total certainty. He'd let Edward do it.

"I haven't sealed it," Harry said, when she'd stopped. "It's not going anywhere. It doesn't need to." He tucked it back into his jacket. "There's a copy, too. Somewhere that isn't here, and isn't on me. If I don't check on it once every day, it goes by itself. So nobody in this room needs to spend any time thinking about what happens if I don't walk out of this house." He looked at Rosalie as he said it, and then at Emmett, and then, last and longest, at Bella. "It just goes."

In Rosalie's head, a whole careful plan—only half-formed, only a few seconds old, all about how fast and how low and from which side—folded itself up and went away.

"He's a person," Bella said. Her voice was shredded. "My dad. He didn't do anything. He's never done anything to anyone. He's just—he's just a person."

"Yes," said Harry.

"Then *why*—"

"Because it's easiest," Harry said. "I'd do it to all of you if I had to. Him included. He's just the easiest."

Edward felt Bella go slack in his arms. He didn't let go.

"So let's not," Harry said, more quietly, "have me have to."

---

Carlisle came down off the last stair.

He walked across the room—at a human pace, deliberately, his notebook still in his hand—and stood in front of the armchair. He didn't sit. He looked down at Harry, and Harry looked up at him, and Edward listened to his father's mind and heard it doing what it had always done in a crisis, for as long as Edward had known him: going still. Going cool. Laying everything out in rows.

"What do you want?" Carlisle said.

"Your notes."

Carlisle blinked.

"All of them," said Harry. "Everything you've ever written down. You've been keeping them since the sixteen-sixties, I'd guess—on what you are, and what the rest of them are, and the ones you've met, and what venom does, and what it doesn't do, and what you've tried, and what you've watched happen. Three and a half centuries of it. In a cupboard somewhere upstairs, or a lot of cupboards." He nodded at the notebook in Carlisle's hand. "That's one of them."

Carlisle looked down at the notebook as if he'd forgotten he was holding it.

"Eighteen thirty-six," he said, after a moment.

"Then that's a start."

"And in return?"

"I'll tell you what your venom is," said Harry.

Edward heard Carlisle's mind go white.

It was a kind of white he'd heard there only a handful of times in ninety years. A white that was all want. Three hundred and forty-some years of questions—*what am I, what are we, what is it that went into me in that cellar in London and didn't kill me, why does it take some and not others, why does it hurt, why does it stop, why do we stop*—and every one of them standing up at once in Carlisle Cullen's head like people in a church when the bride comes in.

He didn't let any of it reach his face. Edward was proud of him for that.

"You know," Carlisle said.

"I've got a good idea. I'd like to see your notes before I tell you I'm sure." Harry tilted his head. "Do you want the short version now?"

"Yes," said Carlisle, too fast.

"It's a transfiguration," said Harry.

Layla, on the couch, inhaled sharply.

"The best I've ever seen," Harry went on, "and I've seen some. Nobody cast it. It's in the venom—the venom carries it, the way a letter carries the words. It goes into a body and it turns that body into something else, all the way through, and then it keeps it turned. Forever. With no upkeep." He looked at Layla. "Tell them what's strange about that."

Layla's mouth opened. Closed. "Transfiguration wears off," she said. "Everything wears off. You turn a teacup into a rat and in an hour or a week or a year it's a teacup again, unless somebody keeps it up. That's—that's the first thing they teach you. Nothing holds forever on its own."

"This holds," said Harry. "On its own. Three hundred and forty years and counting." He glanced at Carlisle. "That's why your skin's like that. That's why you don't age. That's why a crack in you closes up like water—the shape keeps putting itself back. That's why it takes teeth like yours, or something as big and odd as Clifford, to tear you. That's why fire works. Fire's the only thing I know that unmakes a transfiguration from the outside without having to understand it first." He shrugged. "Venom's a spell that casts itself. Somebody started it, a very long time ago. I'd love to know who."

Carlisle said nothing. He didn't have to. Edward could hear him, and what he was hearing was a man who'd been looking for the edge of a map for three centuries and had just been handed it, folded, by a stranger, over breakfast.

"And your heads," Harry said, "are open."

Edward felt it land in the room.

"Wide open. I've been in some heads, in my time. I went into a few, in the war, with walls you'd break your teeth on. Yours have nothing. Not one brick. I went into the one on the ridge and it was like walking into a room with forty radios going and every light on and every door off its hinges. Six, eight trains of thought at once. All of it loud. Right up on the surface." He looked at Edward then, directly, for the first time since the kitchen. "Yours too, Romeo, I'd imagine."

"You'd be welcome to try," Edward said.

It was out before he'd thought about it. Bella, still in his arms, went rigid.

"No," said Harry, mildly. "I wouldn't."

He let that sit just long enough for Edward to wonder which part of it he'd meant.

"You're a Legilimens," Harry said. "Did Layla tell you that? A natural one. You were born with it, or it came with the venom, or both—I don't know yet. But you're untrained, so you can't turn it off, and you can't aim it, and you can't go past the top. You hear what people are thinking right now, this second, in words, or near enough. What they're keeping is out of your reach. You can't go down." He paused. "You're listening at the door with a glass. You've been doing it for ninety years, and you're very good at it, and you've never once been inside the room."

Edward said nothing.

He wanted to say something. He wanted very badly to say something. He found that there was nothing in his head to say it with, because the man in the armchair had just described the inside of his head more accurately in four sentences than Edward had managed to in ninety years, and every part of him, even the part that hated it, was listening.

"I could teach you to turn it down," Harry said. "If you wanted. Never all the way off. You'd be able to go into a room full of people and only hear the ones you wanted to." He looked at Edward a moment longer. "I'd imagine some days that'd be worth something."

It would be worth everything. Edward didn't say that either.

"There's more," said Harry. "Under the radios. A long way down. Quiet."

"What," said Esme.

She'd come in from the kitchen doorway at last. She was standing by the end of the couch with flour still on her hands, and she'd asked it very softly.

Harry looked at her. He was quiet for a moment.

"Everything," he said. "Your lives. Before. They're still in there. All of it. It's been squashed down very small, and it's faded—like a photograph somebody left in a window for a hundred years—but it's there. The venom didn't burn it out. It just put a great deal on top of it." He looked down at his hands. "The one on the ridge had a mother. He didn't know he still had her. She was right at the bottom, under all of it, with her face turned away. I found her in about four seconds. He'd been carrying her around for two hundred years and he had no idea."

The room went very quiet.

Edward heard Esme's mind fold in on itself like a hand closing around something small. A room in Ashland, Wisconsin, in 1921. A window. A baby who had lived for a few days. She'd held on to him so hard, for so long, with so much of herself, that he'd been one of the few things the venom hadn't faded. She'd never lost his face. She had always believed it was because she'd refused to.

He heard Rosalie, beside Emmett. *I don't want mine*, she was thinking. *Keep it down there. Keep it. I've got enough of mine. I've got too much.* And he heard her stop herself thinking past that, the way she always did, at the same place, a street in Rochester at night.

He heard Carlisle. *My father's face. I can't—I haven't been able to see it in a hundred years. The voice. I can still hear the voice.*

And he heard nothing at all from himself.

He tried. He stood there with Bella in his arms and he reached back, the way he'd reached a thousand times, for his mother, and he got what he always got—a fact. Several facts. Elizabeth Masen. Chicago, 1918, the influenza, a hospital ward. Bronze hair, which he'd got from her. Green eyes, which he'd got from her too, once, before the venom had taken them and given him gold. He knew she'd had green eyes the way he knew the date of the Armistice: because someone had told him. Because Carlisle had told him. Carlisle had seen her face, at the end, and remembered it. Edward had been there, in the bed beside hers, and he'd lost it.

He looked at the man in the armchair, who had green eyes.

"I could find them," Harry said.

He said it to the room. He was looking at Carlisle. But Edward was very sure, in the way he'd once been sure of things before he'd met a man he couldn't hear, that it was meant for him.

"It's easy enough. Like finding a book in a library after somebody's taken all the labels off the shelves. It takes a bit of time. It doesn't hurt." Harry's eyes moved, finally, and came to rest on Edward. "If anybody wanted me to."

Edward had nothing in his head at all.

"Think about it," said Harry. "It's a thing I can do. It's got nothing to do with the rest of this."

He turned back to Carlisle.

"And your gifts," he said, "are the only part of you that runs on what I run on."

Carlisle's eyes sharpened.

"Your bodies are a spell somebody else cast. Your heads are No-Maj heads with too many radios in them. But the gifts—Romeo's ears, and Swan's wall, and the little one's hands—that's real. That's magic. Same kind as mine. I can feel it when it's being used, the way you'd hear a kettle." He looked at Bella, still in Edward's arms, no longer fighting. "Swan's the one I can't stop thinking about."

Bella said nothing. Her silence was still hard. It didn't soften.

"An Occlumens closes one head," Harry said. "Their own. That's all. That's what it's for. That's all it's ever been for. And it takes years and it's miserable and most people can't do it. She's got one that goes round other people." He sounded, very nearly, impressed; it was the first time Edward had heard anything at all in his voice that wasn't flat. "Round a field. Round everyone on her side of a field, and she was eighteen. I've never seen that. Nobody I know has ever seen that. I'd like an hour a week of it."

"How do you know about the field?" said Bella. Her voice was raw.

"Clifford was there," said Harry. "So was Balto. So was Clearwater. They were all standing in it." He looked at her a moment longer. "And so was the girl with the eyes."

Bella went very still.

"Your Italian girl," said Harry, to Carlisle, "who does pain. She does it with her eyes. Looks at you and you're on the ground. No wound, nothing to heal, just every nerve you've got, all at once, for as long as she likes." He let a beat pass. "That's the Cruciatus Curse. That's an Unforgivable. She's casting one without a wand, with a look, every time she wants, and if she were a witch she'd be in a cell for the rest of her very long life. And your government keeps her on staff."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"And the old man," said Harry, "who reads you through his hands. All the way down. Everything you've ever thought." He shook his head slightly. "That's Legilimency, done properly, by somebody who's been doing it for three thousand years. He doesn't need a glass. He's been in every room you've got." He glanced at Edward. "He's the one I'd watch."

"We know," said Edward.

"I'm sure you do."

Layla's mind, on the couch, was a sort of steady, horrified hum. Emmett had stopped thinking about the oven. Rosalie had gone very quiet.

"What about the sparkling?" said Emmett.

It was the first thing he'd said since the couch.

Harry looked at him. "Light," he said. "Refraction. There's nothing in it. I checked." He paused. "Bit of a let-down, honestly."

Something happened at the corner of Emmett's mouth that wasn't a smile and wasn't anything else.

"And the—" Emmett stopped, started again. "The green one. Layla said. The one nothing blocks. Does it work on us?"

The room held its breath, even the people in it who didn't breathe.

"I don't know," said Harry.

"You don't *know*."

"Nobody's tried. Not that I know of. I'd quite like it to stay that way." He looked at Emmett for a long moment. "It kills things. It's never yet been fussy about what. But you're a spell that's been holding itself together for three hundred years, and it's a spell that pulls things apart. I genuinely couldn't tell you which one wins."

Edward couldn't hear whether it was true.

He found that he believed it anyway, and that was the worst part.

---

Renesmee came down the stairs at seven minutes past seven.

Edward heard her wake—quick, bright, all at once, the way she always did—and heard her hear the voices downstairs, and heard her think, in a picture, *the man*. And then he heard her get out of bed and cross the room and open the door, and find Jacob lying across it, and step over him.

Jacob came after her. Of course he did. He was down the stairs a step behind her, shirtless, barefoot, his whole body strung so tight it hummed, and he got one hand on her shoulder at the bottom of the stairs, and she let him leave it there, and kept walking.

She walked straight across the living room toward the armchair by the glass.

"Renesmee," said Bella.

She didn't stop. She was wearing a nightgown printed all over with small blue birds, and her bronze curls were flat on one side from the pillow, and she looked up at the man in the armchair with her mother's brown eyes, and in her head Edward could see what she was seeing: someone very big, in a dark coat, with green eyes and a pie dish on the table beside him and crumbs on his knee. And around him, as if he were sitting in the middle of a pool of still water, a silence. She could feel it. It was the first time Edward had ever known that she could feel what he felt—the shape of a mind he couldn't hear—and it shook him.

Jacob's hand was locked on her shoulder.

"It's fine," Harry said to him. "She can come."

"Like hell," said Jacob.

"She can come, Clifford."

Renesmee looked up at Jacob. She put her hand on his wrist. Edward saw the picture she sent him, very fast: *Jacob, please*—and under it, the thing she'd been dreaming about all night, the glass ball with smoke in it, and a tank with something floating in it, and a door that nobody could open.

She'd heard. Edward felt the bottom fall out of his stomach. She'd been lying awake upstairs while Layla talked, last night—half-awake, dozing, listening with ears that could hear a moth land on a curtain—and she'd heard all of it. The prophecies. The brains. The room you didn't come back from.

Jacob's hand loosened.

Renesmee crossed the last few feet of rug and stood in front of the armchair, very straight, the way she stood in front of strangers. Harry looked down at her. He didn't lean forward or smile or do any of the things adults did with children. He just looked at her, the same way he'd looked at the rest of them.

"Hello," he said.

She held up her hand. Palm out. A question.

Edward heard Bella's breath stop. She still breathed, out of habit, all the time; in terror she forgot to. He felt Jacob coil.

Harry looked at the small hand for a moment. Then he held out his own, palm up, and let her put hers in it.

Edward saw it happen, from the inside, from her side. He saw her reach for him with her gift, the way she reached for everyone—and he saw her find the silence, and press against it, and he saw the silence *open*. Not much. A hand's width. Like a door somebody had unlocked from the inside, and opened just far enough to let a small hand through, and stood behind, holding.

And then she showed him the tank.

The tank from Layla's story, as a child would have built it out of one sentence in the dark. Green water, and something floating in it. And then the same tank, but bigger. With her in it. Her hands flat against the glass. A row of people in long robes outside it, looking in, writing things down. And then one more picture, very small, a question with no words at all, just her face and his face and a feeling like a held breath:

*Are you going to put me in there?*

"No," said Harry.

He said it at once, to her, quietly, in a voice Edward hadn't heard him use. He didn't say anything else for a moment. He didn't tell her it would be all right, or not to worry, or that she was being silly. He just held her hand in his and looked at her and let her look back.

"Nobody is," he said. "That's what I'm here about."

Edward heard her believe him.

It went through her like light through a window. He felt it, through her, from the inside—the tank went away, all at once, the way a bad dream goes when you sit up in bed, and she let out a long breath, and her shoulders came down. And then, because she was Renesmee, she sent him one more picture, quick and bright as a bird: Harry himself, eating Esme's pie straight out of the dish with a fork, with a crumb on his chin.

There was a crumb on his chin. Harry put his free hand up and found it and brushed it away.

"Fair," he said.

Renesmee smiled. She took her hand back. She looked at it, for a second, and then at him, curiously, the way she looked at things she'd never seen before and wanted to understand, and then she turned around and went back to Jacob and put her arms around his waist.

Harry sat back in the armchair.

"Romeo reads," he said, to nobody in particular. "Swan blocks. The little one sends." He looked at Carlisle. "That's my whole department. In one family. Living in a house in the woods in Washington State, and not one of you can do any of it on purpose."

Carlisle said nothing. In his head, quietly, very far down, he was thinking about his father, who had spent his life hunting witches through the streets of London, and who had never once, in all those years, found a real one.

---

"Clifford," said Harry.

Jacob's head came up. He had both arms around Renesmee, and she had her face pressed into his stomach, and over her head his eyes were black.

"Call me that one more time," Jacob said.

"Imprinting," said Harry.

Jacob stopped.

"I went through it in Balto's head, and Clearwater's, and yours. All three of you. And through you, every one of the rest of them. Uley and Emily Young. Lahote and your sister." Harry paused. "Ateara and the little girl. Cameron and the girl he'd never looked at twice till he phased. You and her." He nodded at Renesmee. "Tying your soul to one person. For life. Without being asked. Without being able to say no." He shook his head slowly. "That's the bravest thing I've ever heard of in my life, and the most monumentally stupid, and I was in Gryffindor."

Layla laughed.

It came out of her before she could stop it—one short, startled bark—and she clapped her hand over her mouth. In her head Edward heard her explain it to herself, involuntarily, in a flood: *Hogwarts house. The brave one. The reckless ones. The ones who run into burning buildings because someone's in there and then don't know how to get out.* Edward got the joke, through her, about a second late.

Nobody else laughed.

"You've all handed the world a leash," Harry said to Jacob. "Every one of you who's done it. Anyone who wants Sam Uley on his knees needs Emily Young in a room and five minutes. That's the whole plan. One woman and a door they can shut. Anybody could think of it. A child could think of it." He looked at Jacob steadily. "Anybody who wants you needs her." He tipped his head at Renesmee. "And I'd imagine you'd walk into the room yourself, wouldn't you, and say thank you for the matches."

Jacob was shaking. Edward could feel it from across the room, the fine high vibration of a body that wanted, more than anything in the world, to come apart into something else.

"Not in the house," Edward said sharply.

"I know," said Jacob, through his teeth. "I *know*."

"And you're no better," Harry said, turning his head toward the rest of them. "You mate once and you're done, all of you. Doc's got Esme. Romeo's got Swan. Emmett's got Rosalie. You've each got one person in the whole world who'd make you do anything, and you've all very kindly gone and put them in the same house." He looked around the room, at all of them, one at a time. "Every single person in here is somebody else's hostage. I could walk out the door with any one of you, and the rest would follow me to the end of the earth and do whatever I said when they got there. You've all got glaring weaknesses. The wolves' are just the most glaring."

The room was quiet.

"Except Leah," said Harry.

Jacob's head jerked.

"She's the only one of the lot of you I couldn't do it with. There's nothing to pick up. There's nobody on the other end. If I wanted Leah Clearwater, I'd have to go through Leah Clearwater." He looked at Jacob. "You know what that's like. You've tried it."

"Leave her out of this," said Jacob.

"She is out of it," said Harry. "That's what I'm telling you."

Jacob didn't answer. Edward listened to his head and heard it twist around itself, over and over, in a knot he couldn't untie: *she's going to go, she's going to go to him tonight, I heard her, I heard her not say it, and I could stop her, I could stop her with one word, and I'm not going to, and he knows I'm not going to—*

"Sam wants to meet you," Jacob said instead, roughly. "He said—at the treaty line."

"I'll go to him."

"He said the line."

"I'll find him," said Harry. "Tell him I'll find him."

---

He stood up.

Everyone in the room moved at once, a sort of ripple, half a step back. He didn't seem to notice. He brushed the last crumbs off the knee of his jeans and looked around the room at all of them, and he summed it up for them without any change in his voice, the way you'd read back a shopping list to be sure you'd got it all.

"Doc," he said. "The notes. All of them. I'll come for them in a week, or you can send them with Layla. I'll give them back."

Carlisle said nothing. He was still holding the 1836 notebook in his hand, and he looked down at it, and Edward heard him decide.

"Swan. An hour a week. Your shield. Romeo can come and sit in, if it'd make him feel better, and it will."

Bella said nothing. Her silence was still hard.

"Romeo." Harry looked at him. "Stop knocking. And think about what I said."

Edward said nothing.

"Layla, you're mine. You know what that means. I'll do your wards on Saturday." He paused. "Balto. Look after her. You'd do that anyway."

Seth, white around the mouth, nodded once.

"Clifford. When I'm on your land, nobody bites. And tell Uley."

Jacob said nothing.

"Emmett, Rosalie—" Harry considered them. "Nothing. Just don't."

"Don't what," said Emmett.

"Whatever you were thinking about doing. Romeo'll tell you what it was." He turned his head toward Esme, last. "Thank you for the pie."

Esme looked at him for a long moment. Edward heard her think about a great many things, very quickly, and at the end of all of them she said, in a small voice:

"You're welcome."

"This is blackmail," said Carlisle.

He said it quietly. He'd come to stand nearer to Esme. He wasn't angry; he said it the way he'd have said a diagnosis.

"Yes," said Harry.

Carlisle seemed to wait for something else. Nothing else came.

"And if we refuse," Carlisle said.

"Then I send the long one," said Harry, "and the letter, and I go home. And in a month there's a team in this town, and it isn't me, and they don't eat pie. And none of you ever sees any of them, because they're very good. And one day the little one goes out to play in the garden, and doesn't come back in."

The room was absolutely silent.

"And what do we get?" said Rosalie. Her voice was flat and furious. "If we don't. If we do everything you want. What do we *get*?"

Harry looked at her.

"Nobody else," he said.

He let it sit.

"Not New York. Not London. Not my department, or anybody's. Not the International Confederation. And not, if I can manage it, the three old men in Italy."

"You can't promise the Volturi," said Carlisle.

"No. I can promise they'll never hear about any of this from my side. That's the most anyone can promise you about anything." Harry looked around the room once more. "I'm what's standing between that child and every room in London with a glass in the wall. Somebody's always going to be sitting where I'm sitting, deciding what goes in the report. Today it's me. You'll want to keep it that way."

"Swear you won't send it," said Bella.

It was the first thing she'd said in a long time. Her voice had come back a little. It was low and very steady.

"Swear you won't send the letter. About my dad."

Harry looked at her.

"I won't send it," he said, "while I'm getting what I came for."

"That's not a promise."

"It's the one you're getting, Swan."

He went to the door.

He didn't hurry. He walked across the white rug, past Seth and Layla and Emmett on the couch, past Carlisle and Esme, past Jacob with Renesmee's arms still around his waist. When he passed the child, he looked down at her, and she looked up at him, and neither of them said anything. Then he went into the front hall, and Edward heard the front door open—the actual front door, which nobody ever used—and heard the rain, loud, and heard it stop being loud as the door swung shut.

And then the second silence was gone out of the house, and there was only one.

Bella turned around in his arms and put her face against his neck and stood like that, not moving at all, for a very long time.

Behind them, Jacob gently unwound Renesmee's arms from around his waist, and knelt, and kissed the top of her head, and put her hand into Rosalie's.

Then he went after him.

---

The rain hit Jacob in the face the second he came off the porch, and he was glad of it.

He'd been inside that house too long. The whole place stank of them—sweet and sharp and cold, like bleach poured over flowers—and last night it had stunk of fear too, and this morning it had stunk of *him*, rain on stone and pine and something underneath that his wolf kept trying to put a name to and couldn't. Out here it was just rain. Rain and wet cedar and the river. He took a breath of it all the way down to the bottom of his lungs.

Potter was halfway down the drive.

He was walking down the middle of the gravel, unhurried, with his hands in his jacket pockets, like a guy on his way to get the mail, and the rain was coming down on him in sheets and bending away from him a few inches above his head, the way it would bend around a pane of glass. It ran down the outside of nothing and dripped off the edges of nothing onto the gravel in a ring around his boots. He was bone dry.

Jacob caught up to him in about six strides.

"Hey."

Potter stopped. He turned around.

Up close he was six-two, maybe. Shorter than Jacob, which most people were, but broad enough through the shoulders that it didn't look like much of a difference. Black hair, wet-looking but dry. Green eyes, a green you didn't see a lot of. No scar—Layla had gone on and on about the scar last night, and Jacob had looked, and there wasn't one. There wasn't a mark on him anywhere.

"Clifford," he said.

"Don't."

"All right." He waited.

Jacob had a lot of things to say. He'd lain on the floor outside Nessie's door for an hour and listened to all of it through the floorboards and thought of every single one of them. Now that he was out here, standing in the rain in the middle of the drive with this guy looking at him like he had all day, they'd all gone somewhere.

"You don't talk about her," he said finally. "Nessie. Not like that. Not in front of her."

"She wasn't in front of me when I said most of it."

"She heard you anyway. She hears everything."

"I know," said Potter. "So I told her the truth."

"You told her nobody was going to put her in a tank."

"Yes."

"Is it the truth?"

Potter looked at him for a while.

"It's true while I'm here," he said. "That's the best anybody's got."

Jacob's hands were shaking. He shoved them in his pockets, which on a pair of cutoffs didn't do much.

"And the other thing," he said. "What you said. About imprinting."

"That it's brave," said Potter.

"That it's *stupid*."

"I said both."

"Yeah, well." Jacob felt the heat climbing up his back, up the nape of his neck, into the roots of his hair. He made himself breathe through it. "You don't get it. Nobody chooses it. It just happens. You look at somebody and the whole world moves over, and that's it, that's you, for the rest of your life. Nobody's *stupid*. You don't get a vote."

"I know," said Potter.

"Then why—"

"You don't get a vote," Potter said, "and you'd vote for it anyway. Every one of you. If somebody came to you tonight and said they could take it out of you, clean, no pain, you'd be free—you'd break their neck." He tilted his head slightly. "That's the brave part. The stupid part is that everybody else in the world can see the handle, and you can't."

Jacob didn't say anything.

He thought about Sam. About Sam's face, the first year, when he couldn't be in a room with Leah and couldn't be out of a room with Emily. About Quil, carrying Claire around First Beach on his shoulders while she pulled his hair, so happy it was hard to look at. About Paul, who'd gone from the worst-tempered guy Jacob knew to somebody who did his sister's dishes.

About the first second he'd looked at Nessie, and everything he'd thought he was had come loose and drifted off like smoke, and he'd been glad.

"Somebody's going to think of it," Potter said. "Not me. Somebody. A long time from now, maybe, or next week. Somebody's going to look at your pack and see what I saw, and they'll come with a van."

"Nobody's going to touch Emily."

"Then tell Uley to keep her close for a bit."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's what I'd do," said Potter, "if I were him."

---

They stood in the rain.

Down the hill, the river was loud. Somewhere in the trees off to the right a jay was going off about something, over and over. Jacob could still hear the house behind him, faint—Bella not talking, Rosalie talking low and fast to Emmett, Esme running water in the kitchen. Nessie asking Layla something. He couldn't make out what.

"Leah," he said.

"Yes."

"You're going to mess with her head."

"I'm going to try and get into it," Potter said. "And she's going to try and keep me out. That's the whole lesson. That's all it is, for about two years."

"And if she can't?"

"Then I'll know it doesn't work on a wolf, and she'll know she tried." He paused. "She'll have got something out of it. She'll know exactly what it feels like when somebody's in there who shouldn't be. Most people never learn that much."

"She knows what that feels like," said Jacob. "She's had all of us in there for two years."

"Yes," said Potter. "That's why she'll be good at it."

Jacob looked at him.

"You could stop her," Potter said.

He said it quietly, the way you'd put something down on the gravel between you so you could both look at it.

"You've got the voice for it. I heard it in your head. One word, and she'd have to—she wouldn't be able to come to me. Not tonight, not ever. She'd hate you for the rest of her life, but she'd do it."

Jacob's jaw was tight.

"I know," he said.

"Will you?"

"No."

"I know," said Potter. "I was in there."

"Then why'd you ask?"

"I wanted you to hear yourself say it."

Jacob turned away. He looked up the drive at the house, white and glass and lit up in the gray morning like a lantern, and behind it the trees going up the hill into the cloud. He thought about Leah, last night, way out ahead of him on the run north, a gray shape between the trunks. He'd been so close behind her for two hours that he could smell her. He'd heard every single thing in her head. He'd heard her think about a door, over and over, until he'd thought he'd lose his mind. And he'd let her get ahead.

"She's my beta," he said. "She's the best one I've got. She's better than me at half of it."

"I know."

"If she gets a door," said Jacob, "she can shut it on me."

"Yes."

"In the middle of a fight. Whenever she wants."

"Yes."

"And I'll never know what she's thinking again."

"You'll know what she tells you," said Potter. "Same as everybody else in the world knows about everybody else in the world." He looked at Jacob steadily. "That's how it is for the rest of us, Clifford. It's not so bad."

Jacob laughed. It hurt a little. "Easy for you."

"Is it?"

Jacob turned back around.

Potter was looking past him, up at the house. For a second his face did something—nothing much, a shift around the eyes, gone almost before Jacob saw it. Like yesterday at the edge of the lawn, when he'd been talking to Leah about the guy who'd tried to teach him all this, the one who couldn't keep him out for ten seconds. Jacob had seen it from inside Leah's head. He'd thought she'd imagined it.

"What about you?" Jacob said. "Who's on the other end of you?"

Potter didn't answer for a while.

"When I was fifteen," he said, "a man worked out what I'd walk into a fire for. He put a picture of it in my head. Middle of the night. The picture was that he'd got him, and he was hurting him, and he was going to kill him." His voice didn't change at all. "So I flew to London in the dark with five kids from school and walked into the building I work in now. And it wasn't real. He'd made it up. He'd just found the handle and pulled." He looked at Jacob. "And the man I thought I was saving came to save me, because I'd been stupid, and he died in there. In front of me. He was my godfather."

The rain came down.

Jacob didn't say anything. He couldn't think of a single thing.

"So I learned to shut the door," Potter said. "It took me two years, after. I was rubbish to start with. And now nobody gets a picture." He looked back up at the house. "I've got people. I keep them where nobody can see them. You can have people. You just have to have somewhere to put them where nobody else can get at." He paused. "That's what I'm offering her. A room. She can put whoever she likes in it."

Jacob stood in the rain and thought about that.

"Tell Uley I'll find him," Potter said.

He turned and walked off the gravel into the trees.

He didn't look back this time. He went in under the cedars and the salal closed behind him, and Jacob heard him take three steps through it, perfectly silent—not even a twig—and then the crack came, like a branch breaking, sharp and flat and close, and a bird went up out of the trees all at once, and he was gone.

Jacob stood there for a minute.

Then he walked to the edge of the trees, and stepped out of his cutoffs, and tied them to his ankle with the cord, and phased.

---

She was there.

She was a long way north—he could feel it, the way you can feel which direction a voice is coming from in the dark—up in the Sol Duc country somewhere, twenty-odd miles off, curled up in a hollow under a deadfall with her nose under her tail, half asleep. She'd been running most of the night. She came awake the second he was in, all at once, and he felt her feel him, and then he felt her take it.

All of it. There was no way to stop it. The kitchen, the pie, the wand, *Layla, let's count*, Emmett in the air, the letter, Bella's face, *he'll still have a granddaughter*, the venom, the memories, Nessie's hand. *Tying your soul to one person.* And then—he felt her go still, felt her whole body go still under the deadfall in the hollow twenty miles away—

*Except Leah.*

*She's the only one of the lot of you I couldn't do it with.*

And then the drive. The rain. *You could stop her.*

*No.*

*I wanted you to hear yourself say it.*

And the godfather, and the room.

Jacob didn't try to hide any of it. There was no point. He just let it go through him, and through her, and sat there in the wet under the cedars at the edge of the Cullens' drive with his tail around his paws, and waited.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

Then: *Don't.*

*I wasn't going to,* Jacob said.

*I know you weren't. I can hear you.* A pause. *Don't anyway.*

*Okay.*

He felt her put her head back down on her paws. He felt her thinking about Charlie Swan, eating fry bread at her mom's kitchen table on a Sunday with his elbows on the cloth. He felt her think about a letter in a jacket. He felt her shove the thought away so hard it made his own ears go back.

*Tonight,* she said.

*Yeah.* He'd known. *I know.*

*You going to tell Sam?*

*I'm going to tell Sam he wants to meet him.*

*That's not what I asked.*

*I know what you asked.* Jacob put his own head down. *It's yours, Leah. I'm not telling anybody anything. Not Sam. Not your mom.*

She didn't say thank you. She never did. He felt her not say it, the way you'd feel somebody sit down next to you on a step.

*He's a bastard,* she said.

*Yeah.*

*He'd do it. To Charlie. He'd really do it.*

*Yeah,* said Jacob. *I think he would.*

There was another long silence. Twenty miles off, Leah Clearwater lay under a deadfall in the rain and thought about a room with a door in it, and for once she didn't try to stop him hearing it.

*Be careful,* he said.

*Shut up, Jake.*

And she phased out, and was gone, and he was alone in his head for the first time in a long time, and he sat there under the trees in the rain and found that he didn't like it, and that it wasn't his to like.

---

Charlie Swan's cruiser was in the drive when Leah woke up.

She lay on her bed in the half-dark and looked at the ceiling and listened to it tick as it cooled. Five-forty, the clock said. She'd come in at noon, soaked to the skin and so tired she'd walked into the doorframe, and her mom had looked at her across the kitchen and not said anything and put a plate in front of her, and Leah had eaten it standing up and gone to her room and locked the door and slept for five hours without dreaming.

She could hear them in the kitchen now. Her mom, at the stove; the hiss of something going into oil. Charlie, at the table, the scrape of his chair as he leaned it back on two legs the way her mom had asked him not to about four hundred times. Seth, not saying much. Seth had come home from the Cullens' around three and gone straight to his room, and she'd heard him through the wall lie down on his bed with all his clothes on, and not sleep.

She got up and put on jeans and a sweatshirt and went out.

"There she is," said Charlie.

"Here I am."

She sat down across from him. Seth glanced up at her from his plate and down again. Her mom set a plate of fry bread on the table between them and went back to the stove without turning around, and Leah could tell from her back that she was thinking about a great many things and was going to say none of them in front of Charlie.

Charlie took a piece of fry bread and tore it in half. He had his uniform shirt on still, open at the collar, and his hat was on the counter by the door where it always went, upside down, next to the bowl her mom kept her keys in. He'd been doing that so long now that Leah couldn't remember the counter without it.

"Long day?" he said to her.

"Long night."

"Huh." He chewed. "Seth said you were out."

"I was out."

"Yeah, well." He shrugged. "Need to know," he said, and grinned a little, crookedly, the way he did when he said it, which was a lot. He'd made it into a joke a while ago, sometime in the last year. It had stopped being one to Leah the first time she'd heard him say it to Bella on the phone, after, in a voice she wasn't supposed to hear.

She looked at him across the table.

She'd had it in her head all day. Since Jake had phased, that morning, under the cedars, and she'd taken it off him in one long sick gulp under the deadfall up in the Sol Duc—all of it. The envelope. The square upright writing on the front, *the Woolworth Building, New York*. Two people in long coats on the front step of a house she could have walked to in her sleep. *They'll take everything near it. Everything that smells wrong.*

*He'll still have a granddaughter. He just won't know who she is.*

Charlie was telling Seth about a kid Billy knew who'd stolen his uncle's boat out of the marina the week before and run it aground on James Island at four in the morning, and how the uncle had wanted to press charges until he'd found out the kid had done it to impress a girl, and then he'd wanted to buy the kid a beer. He told it slowly, the way he told everything, with pauses in the wrong places. Seth laughed at the right parts. It sounded almost real.

"—so I said to Billy, I said, Harry would've—"

He stopped.

Leah's hand had gone still on the table. She hadn't meant it to. Across the kitchen her mom had stopped moving at the stove, with the spatula in the air.

Charlie looked at Leah. He looked at her for a second longer than people usually did, these days, and then he cleared his throat.

"Your dad would've bought the kid a beer," he said. "That's all. That's what I was going to say. He'd have bought him two."

"Yeah," said Leah. "He would've."

Charlie nodded. He tore another piece off the fry bread and didn't eat it.

He said her dad's name a lot. He always had. He'd been saying it for two years, at this table and at Billy's and at the station and in the truck on the way back from the river, the way you'd keep touching a sore tooth; he said it like he was afraid that if he stopped, it'd go. Leah used to hate it. She'd sat at this table and wanted to scream every time. Somewhere in the last year or so she'd stopped wanting to, and she hadn't noticed when.

She thought about what would be left of Charlie Swan, after. If they took two years out of him in a quarter of an hour on his own front step. He'd still have her dad, probably—her dad went back thirty years with him, further than anything they'd want. He might even get to keep the funeral. But everything after it would be a hole. A two-year hole with his best friend's grave at one end of it and nobody to sit with on the other. He wouldn't know he'd ever sat at this table. He wouldn't know what her mom's kitchen looked like with his hat on the counter.

He'd have a granddaughter he didn't know.

"You okay?" Charlie said. "You look kind of—"

"I'm fine."

"Okay." He held up both hands. "Okay. Need to know."

---

She washed. Her mom dried. Charlie had gone out to the cruiser to take a call, and Seth had gone back to his room and shut the door, and the kitchen was quiet except for the water and the rain.

"You're going somewhere tonight," her mom said.

Leah kept washing.

"Running," she said.

"Leah."

"Mom."

Her mom took the plate from her and dried it, very thoroughly, both sides, and put it in the cupboard. Then she took the next one. She didn't look at Leah. She'd stopped looking at her when she asked things a long time ago—since that week, two years back, probably. Now she asked things to the cupboard.

"Seth won't tell me," her mom said. "Jacob won't tell me. Charlie doesn't know what to tell me. And you've got that look you had when you were fourteen and you'd decided to jump off the high cliff, and your dad told you you couldn't, and you went and did it anyway on a Tuesday when he was at work."

"I was fine."

"You broke your wrist."

"I was fine *after*."

Her mom put the plate away.

"Is it the man," she said. "The one Charlie doesn't know about."

Leah's hands stopped in the water.

"Seth didn't tell me," her mom said. "I'm not stupid. My son came home white as a sheet at three in the afternoon and went into his room and didn't come out till Charlie's car was in the drive. Seth is never in his room. Jacob called here this afternoon to ask how I was, which he never does. And Esme Cullen called *me*, Leah, at two o'clock, to ask if Charlie was coming here for dinner tonight, which is a thing that woman has never once in her life needed to know." She dried the next plate. "I know there's a man."

Leah didn't say anything.

"Is he dangerous?"

"Yes," said Leah.

Her mom nodded slowly. She put the plate away. She folded the towel over the oven door and turned around, finally, and looked at her. Sue Clearwater was a small woman, and she'd gotten smaller in the last two years, and her face had gotten harder, and her eyes hadn't changed at all.

"Is he going to hurt you?"

Leah thought about it.

"No," she said. "I don't think he wants to."

"That's not the same."

"I know." Leah pulled the plug and watched the water go. "He can't, Mom. He said so himself. He's got nothing on me."

Her mom looked at her for a long time.

"Home by morning," she said.

"Yeah."

"I mean it."

"I know."

Her mom reached up—she had to reach up, now; Leah had grown four inches the year she phased—and put her hand flat against Leah's cheek, the way she'd done when Leah was little and had a fever. Leah always had a fever now. Her mom's hand was cold against it.

Then she went out to see Charlie.

---

Jake had taken the patrol himself. In his truck.

Leah found that out at ten past ten, when she stepped into the trees behind the house and stripped and tied her clothes to her ankle and phased, and braced herself for him, and he wasn't there. Nobody was there. She stood in the wet ferns on four feet with her ears up and her heart going and listened to the inside of her own head, and it was empty. Just her.

She knew exactly why. Jake had decided nobody in his pack would be on four legs tonight, and since there wasn't any way to run a patrol on two legs that wasn't stupid, he'd picked the stupidest one and done it himself, in a twenty-year-old Rabbit with no heat, up and down the 110 with the window down so he could smell. He'd be doing it till dawn. He'd be furious about it the whole time.

She stood there in the ferns for a second longer than she meant to.

Then she ran.

It was eighteen miles to Bogachiel, give or take, through the trees, and she did it in a little under forty minutes and didn't think about anything at all. That was the trick. Every wolf in both packs knew it, and every one of them was bad at it, and she was the best of a bad lot. You didn't think. You ran. You filled your head with the run so there wasn't any room left over—roots and rock and moss and the next stride and the next, the smell of the drainage, the cold in your nose, the ground coming up under your paws and going away again. You thought about running so hard that there was no space left in there for anything you didn't want anybody else to see.

It never worked. Not with the pack. They knew what you were doing, and they waited you out. You couldn't run forever.

Tonight there was nobody to wait her out. She ran anyway, out of habit, all the way down the long slope past the river and across the 101 in one long leap between a log truck and its own headlights, and into the second-growth on the other side.

A mile out, she stopped and phased back.

Her body did it on its own, under a big cedar at the top of a cutbank above the Bogachiel, and she stood there in the rain on two bare feet, panting, with her heart going like a hammer, and understood that some part of her had decided that whatever happened next, it was going to happen to Leah, on two feet, with her own face.

She untied the bundle from her ankle and put on her jeans and her sweatshirt, wet through, and walked the last mile barefoot.

---

The lot was easy to find. There was a gravel turnout off the road with a chain across it and a mailbox on a post with PRUETT painted on it by hand, and past the chain a dirt track ran down through alders toward the river. Somewhere off to the right, behind a dark single-wide up on blocks, a dog started to bark and then thought better of it. She didn't blame it.

She smelled him before she saw anything.

Wood smoke. Pine pitch. Rain on stone. Cold river rock, the kind you turned over in a creek and found dark underneath. It was everywhere down here, soaked into everything—the alders, the track, the moss on the big rocks at the edge of the trees. Stronger than at the Cullens'. The way her own smell was all over her mom's porch, and her room, and the inside of her truck: the smell of a place somebody lived.

And under it, the other thing. The thing her wolf didn't have a word for.

It was much stronger here. It was in the duff under the hemlocks, and pressed into the moss in places in shapes she didn't stop to look at, and on the trunk of the biggest hemlock at the edge of the clearing it was rubbed right into the bark, deep, the way a wolf would mark a tree he meant to keep—at a height higher than her own head. Higher than anything that walked on four legs had any business reaching.

Her wolf went very quiet inside her.

Leah stood at the edge of the trees and looked at that tree for a long moment. Then she made herself stop looking at it, and stepped into the clearing.

The tent was under the hemlocks, forty yards back from the water, exactly where he'd said. Green canvas. A squat A-frame, a little sagging at the ridgeline, guy ropes pegged into the needles. It looked like a fisherman's tent that had been there too long and somebody had forgotten to take down.

In front of it there was a small fire, and over the fire a kettle on a little iron stand, and beside the fire, in a folding canvas camp chair, Harry Potter was sitting with a mug in his hands, watching her come.

The rain was coming down hard. It came down on the hemlocks and the clearing and on her, and it came down on the fire too—or it should have. It didn't reach it. It stopped about eight feet up in a wide shallow curve, a dome she could only see by where the rain wasn't, and ran off the edges of nothing in a ring, and dripped into the needles all the way around. Under it, the fire burned straight up without any smoke at all. The ground was dry. The chair was dry. He was dry.

There was a second chair beside his, across the fire. Empty.

He'd set out a second chair.

"You're early," he said.

"You said any night."

"I did."

She came across the clearing to the edge of the ring where the rain was coming down off nothing, and stopped. She could feel the heat of the fire on her face through it. The water was running off her hair into her eyes.

"Come in, then," he said. "You're letting the rain in."

She stepped through.

It was like walking out of a car wash. One second the rain was on her, and the next it was over her, drumming on the air eight feet up with a sound like a tin roof, and her face was wet but nothing new was landing on it, and the ground under her bare feet was dry needles, heated through by the fire. She stood there and dripped.

"Sit down," he said. "Eat something."

There was a plate on the second chair, under a cloth. She lifted the cloth. Sausages—six of them, thick ones—and a stack of bread fried in the fat, and four eggs, and what looked like half a loaf of something with raisins in it. It was still hot. She didn't ask how.

She sat down, and put the plate on her knees, and ate all of it.

She hadn't known how hungry she was until she started. It went quickly. He didn't say anything while she ate. He sat with his mug in his hands and looked at the fire, and once he leaned forward and poked at it with a stick, and once he reached down and moved the kettle an inch farther off the heat. When she'd finished, he took the plate off her knees and put it on the ground and handed her a mug of tea instead. Strong. Too much sugar in it.

She held it in both hands and didn't drink it.

"Charlie was at my mom's tonight," she said.

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"He's there most nights." He drank. "He leaves his hat on the counter by the door."

She stared at him.

"Upside down," he said. "Next to the bowl with her keys in it."

Her stomach turned over.

"Get out of my head," she said.

"I'm not in it. I haven't been since yesterday. I had that out of Balto's." He looked at her over the mug. "He sees the hat every day of his life. He thinks about it more than you'd think."

Leah looked down at her tea.

"He said Dad's name," she said. "At dinner. He says it all the time. He misses him." She made herself look up. "And you'd wipe him. You'd really do it."

"I'd let New York do it."

"That's worse."

"Yes," he said. "It is."

She waited for him to say something else. Some excuse. Some reason. A speech, the kind people gave when they wanted you to understand why the bad thing they were going to do was really for the best. He didn't give one. He sat there with his mug and looked at her, and that was all she was going to get, and she found, a little to her own surprise, that she preferred it.

"Is that why I'm here?" she said. "So you've got something on my mom too? So you've got something on *me*?"

"I've got nothing on you," he said. "That's why you're here."

The rain drummed on the air over their heads.

"I saw what you did this morning," she said. "Through Jake. All of it. Layla. Seth. Bella. The letter."

"I know."

"You're a real piece of work."

"I know that too."

"Did you see Sam?"

"Tomorrow."

"Don't tell me about it."

"All right," he said.

She drank some of the tea, finally, because her hands needed something to do. It was hot and strong and too sweet. She drank more of it.

"So how does it work," she said.

---

He put his mug down on the ground beside his chair.

"The man who tried to teach me," he said, "never told me how. He just came in. Over and over, three nights a week, all winter. He'd stand there and say *clear your mind*, and then he'd come in, and he'd go through everything I had, and I'd end up on the floor of his office with my head ringing, and he'd say *again*." He said it plainly, without anything in his voice at all. "I'm going to tell you how first. Then I'm going to come in."

"Great," said Leah.

"Nobody can clear their mind. Not on the first night. Not for a long time. It's the wrong thing to ask for. Your head's never going to be empty." He leaned forward a little, elbows on his knees. "So put it somewhere."

"Put it where?"

"Pick a room," he said. "A real one. Somewhere you know so well you could walk it in the dark with your eyes shut. Somewhere with a door that closes. And when I come in, you go there, and you shut the door behind you."

"That's it?"

"That's the first year," he said. "It sounds stupid. It works better than anything else I've ever tried. The mind likes having a shape. If you give it a room, it'll stay in the room. If you give it nothing, it'll go everywhere."

Leah sat there in the dry with the rain going all around her and thought about rooms.

She didn't have to think long.

"My room," she said. "At home."

"Tell me about the door."

She almost didn't. Then she did. It came out short.

"There's a bolt on it. Brass. Dad put it on when I was thirteen, because Seth kept barging in without knocking and I said I'd kill him. He did it on a Saturday. Seth held the screws." She looked at the fire. "It sticks. You have to lift it a little to make it go across. I've never fixed it."

"Good," Harry said. "That's very good. That's a real door."

She didn't look at him.

"When I come in," he said, "you go to that room. You go in. You shut the door, and you lift it a little, and you shoot the bolt. And you stand there with your back against it and you don't let me in."

"And if I can't?"

"Then you can't, tonight. And we do it again."

She put the mug down on the needles beside her chair.

"Okay," she said. "Do it."

"Look at me."

She looked at him.

His eyes were very green in the firelight. She held them. She waited to feel something—a push, a cold, a hand—and for a second there was nothing at all, and she thought, *that's it?*—

—and then he was in.

It was like a hand going into a drawer. That was the first thing she thought, somewhere at the back of herself, while it happened. A hand, going through a drawer of her things very fast, not looking for anything in particular, just turning them over, and every one it turned over came up into the light all at once, bright and close, the way it did when she phased, the way it did every night with the whole pack watching—First Beach when she was nine, the gray water, her dad's hands showing her how to open a fish from the vent to the gills with one stroke; the high cliff at fourteen, the wind, the water coming up; her mom's kitchen, a casserole dish going cold on the table, a man in a police uniform in the doorway with his hat in his hands—Sam—Sam in the kitchen, Sam not looking at her, Sam looking at—

*No.*

She tried to find the room. She couldn't find the room. She couldn't find anything. She was in the kitchen and she couldn't get out of the kitchen and he was in there with her and—

He was gone.

She was sitting in the camp chair with both hands gripping the canvas arms and her breath coming hard and the fire in front of her, and the rain drumming on the air overhead, and Harry Potter sitting across from her with his elbows on his knees, watching her.

"Three seconds," he said.

"Shut up."

"That's about what I did, the first time."

"I said *shut up*." Her eyes were stinging. She hated that. "You saw—"

"I saw your dad showing you how to clean a fish," he said. "That's mostly what I saw. That was the loudest thing in there. I didn't go any further than the kitchen."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

"Again," she said.

He looked at her a moment. Then: "Look at me."

She looked at him.

This time she felt him coming. A fraction of a second—a pressure, like the air before a storm—and she didn't go for the room. She didn't think about it; there wasn't time. She did the only thing she'd ever been any good at. She ran.

She ran with everything she had. Trees. Roots. Moss on rock. The smell of the drainage after rain. The cold in her nose. The next stride, and the next, and the ground coming up under her paws and falling away again, and the trail going on ahead of her through the dark up the long slope past the Sol Duc where she'd been last night, and she didn't think about anything at all, there wasn't room, there wasn't room for anything but the run—

And she felt him behind her.

In there. In her head. Chasing her down the trail through the trees, the way the pack did, the way Jake did when she wouldn't let him see something—except he didn't know the trail. He didn't know the country. He was fast, he was so fast, but every time he got close she went round a tree he hadn't seen or up a bank he didn't know was there, and he had to go round, and she kept running, and running—

And then he found a way through the trees, and was beside her, and the trail went out from under her paws, and there was the kitchen again—

He let go.

She was in the chair. She was breathing so hard her ribs hurt.

"What did you do?" he said.

He'd leaned forward. There was something in his face she hadn't seen there before. Not much. A little sharpness around the eyes.

"I ran," she said.

"Ran."

"It's what we do. When you don't want the pack to see something. You think about running. You fill your head up with it so there's no room for anything else." She shook her head. "It doesn't work. Not with them. They know what you're doing. They just wait."

"It worked on me for eight seconds," he said.

Leah looked at him.

"I didn't know the trail," he said. "You did. Every time I got close you went somewhere I'd never been." He sat back slowly. "That's a wall. That's a rubbish wall, but it's a wall. Nobody taught you that. You worked it out yourself, with a whole pack in your head every night, because you had to."

She didn't say anything. She didn't know what to say. She picked up her tea, which had gone cold, and drank some of it anyway, and her hand wasn't steady.

"Do it again," he said. "Run. Same as that. But this time don't just run. Run *home*. Out of the trees, across the yard, in the back door, through the kitchen—don't stop in the kitchen—down the hall to your room. And go in, and shut the door behind you, and lift it, and shoot the bolt."

"While you're chasing me."

"While I'm chasing you."

She put the mug down.

"Look at me," he said.

She looked at him.

She felt him coming, and she ran.

Trees. Roots. The trail. Him behind her, fast, close, closer than last time—he'd learned something, too—and she went round the big cedar and down the cutbank and out of the trees, and there was the yard, her mom's yard, wet grass and the clothesline and her truck, and she was across it in three strides and up the back steps and in the door—

The kitchen. She went through it without looking. She didn't look at the table, or the counter, or the doorway where a man had stood with his hat in his hands. She went through it like it was a room in someone else's house.

The hall. Her dad's coat still on the third hook, where her mom had never moved it.

Her door.

She went in. She shut it behind her. She put her hand on the bolt and lifted it, the way you had to, just a little, so it would go, and she shot it across, and it went home with the sound it had always made, a small dull *clack*, a sound she'd heard ten thousand times and never once listened to.

And she stood there with her back against the door, in the dark, in her room.

And he was on the other side.

She could feel him. Right there, through the wood, waiting, the way you'd know someone was standing outside your door without hearing a thing.

Then he pushed.

The door shook in its frame. She put her shoulders against it and her heels into the floor and held. He pushed again, harder, and the bolt rattled in its keeper, and she held, she held—it was her door, her dad had put the bolt on it on a Saturday with Seth holding the screws, it was *hers*—and he pushed a third time and the whole frame groaned and she felt the bolt start to give, start to slide back out of the keeper the way it always stuck, a quarter-inch, half an inch—

He let go.

She was in the chair by the fire with the rain drumming overhead and her hands locked on the arms so hard the canvas had torn under her right one.

She looked down at it. Then she looked up.

Harry Potter was sitting across the fire with his elbows on his knees and his hands loosely clasped between them. He was looking at her. His face didn't show much; it never did. But he didn't say anything for a while, and when he did, his voice was a little quieter.

"Eleven seconds," he said.

Leah didn't move.

"Is that good?" she said.

"I couldn't do ten seconds in a whole winter," he said. "I told you that." He paused. "You did eleven on your third go. With a bolt that sticks."

She looked away from him, at the fire. She didn't want him to see her face. She didn't know what it was doing. Something in her chest had gone very tight and very hot, and it wasn't the fever, and she didn't have a name for it, and she didn't want one.

Eleven seconds.

For eleven seconds, there'd been a room in her head with a door, and she'd been on the inside of it, and there had been nobody else in there at all.

"Next time you phase," he said, "try it. On your own, first. Run home. Shoot the bolt. See what you can hear on the other side. Then you come back and tell me." He picked up his mug. "That's the only bit of this I can't do for you. I've never been a wolf in your pack. I don't know what it'll do."

She noticed how he'd said it. She didn't know why she'd noticed. Inside her, her wolf had gone quiet again, the way it had at the edge of the clearing, looking at the bark of that tree.

"And if it works?"

"Then you'll have a door," said Harry. "And they'll know you've got one. And that's going to be its own thing, for all of you." He drank. "You'll want to think about that."

She thought about Seth. About Jake, out on the 110 in his stupid truck with the window down, in the rain, all night, so she could have this.

"Yeah," she said. "I know."

The fire popped. The rain went on drumming eight feet up, on nothing. Out past the edge of the ring, the Bogachiel ran high and brown and loud in the dark, and somewhere behind the single-wide up by the road, Dale Pruett's dog gave one short bark at nothing and was quiet again.

Leah wiped her face with her sleeve. She sat up straight in the chair. She put both feet flat on the dry needles.

"Again," she said.

He looked at her across the fire.

"Look at me," he said.

She looked at him, and felt him come, and she ran home, and shut the door.
