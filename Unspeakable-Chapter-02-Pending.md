# Chapter Two — Pending

*The clearing, north of Forks, Washington — January 2007*

## I. Leah

*Leah.*

Jacob's voice came into her head quiet and ragged, from somewhere down in the snow beside Bella, where he had his whole huge body curled around the kid.

*Leah. Seth.*

Leah didn't answer. She was lying on her belly eight yards behind the stranger, with thirty yards of torn-up snow behind her that she didn't remember making, and every part of her that wasn't busy hating itself was busy staring at the back of his leather jacket. A gray smudge of ash on his left shoulder. His black hair moving in the wind. The snow still steaming around his boots.

Something was running toward them out of the south. She could hear it now, too, faint and light, a long way off through the trees. Feet. A heartbeat going like a bird's. And back by the Cullens, Edward had said *Alice* in a voice that sounded like somebody had just handed him back something he'd been sure he'd lost.

None of it mattered. None of it was *him*.

*Leah*, Jacob said again. *I've got to talk to them. All of them. Him. I can't do it like this.*

*So phase*, Leah said. *Nobody's stopping you.*

*I'm going to.* A pause. In it she felt all of him, the way she always felt all of him when he was this tired: the ache in his jaw where he'd hit her, the cold in his paws, the kid's small fingers knotted in the fur of his neck. Renesmee's breath going in and out against his shoulder. Bella's hand on his back. *And I can't think with you in my head.*

*Excuse me?*

*I can't hear her.* It came out of him raw. *I'm trying to listen to her, Leah, I'm trying to feel if she's okay, and all I can hear is you. All I can feel is you. Pointed at him. It's like standing next to a jet engine. It's in everybody. Seth can't think. Quil's about to throw up. Embry's—*

*Oh, I'm so sorry my life falling apart is inconvenient for you.*

*That's not—*

*You want me to just turn it off? Like you turned yours off? Sure. Hang on. Let me find the switch.*

*Leah—*

*Jake, I don't have any—*

She didn't get to finish. She felt him decide. She felt it the way she'd feel a big wave lift under her on the beach at La Push, a long slow rising swell of it, and she knew what it was a half second before it broke.

*Leah. Seth. Phase back.* And then, down in the place where the old voice lived, the one that wasn't really Jacob, the one that had come down through Ephraim and Taha Aki and every wolf back to the first one: *NOW.*

She tried to tell him. She had time for one thought, a single bright flash of it—the backyard at four in the morning, her mom's kitchen light off, the grass silver with frost, and Leah standing there in her sweats and her old Forks High Track hoodie and deciding, very clearly, not to bother with a cord on her ankle and a pair of cutoffs. Because why would she. Because she wasn't planning on needing them again.

Then the command hit, and the wolf went out of her.

It always started in the chest. A hot fist, closing. Then everything went in toward it all at once—her ribs pulling in, her spine shortening, the long bones of her legs drawing up into themselves with a sound like somebody wringing out a wet towel, the fur sliding back into her skin like water running off a windshield. Her muzzle went flat. Her ears went small. Her tail went nowhere. It took less than a second. It always felt longer.

And then she was lying on her stomach in the snow in the middle of a field full of vampires and wolves with nothing on at all.

The cold hit her everywhere at once.

She ran at a hundred and eight. She could have lain there for an hour before it did her any real harm. But the snow under her was a solid sheet of ice from her collarbones to her knees, sharp and wet and shocking, and the wind came down the length of her bare back like a hand, and she sucked in a breath so hard it hurt.

The silence hit her right after that.

She hadn't been out of the pack mind since four o'clock this morning. Seven hours of Seth and Jacob and Quil and Embry in her head, every second of it, every fear and every itch and every stupid joke Embry hadn't been able to stop himself thinking. Now there was nothing. Just her own head. Her own breathing, ragged and loud. Her own heart.

And the pull.

She'd hoped—she'd been stupid enough to hope, for that one half second while the wolf went out of her—that maybe it lived in the wolf. That maybe it was a wolf thing, and when the wolf went, it would go too.

It didn't. If anything, it got worse. With the fur gone and the four legs gone and the pack gone, there was nothing left to stand between her and it. It went straight through her bare skin like the cold did, and it pointed her exactly where it had been pointing her all along. At the leather jacket. At the back of his head.

Behind her, she heard Seth phase. The same wet folding rip. Then his voice, out loud and cracking, "Leah—oh, crap, Leah—"

She got her arms under her. She didn't know what she was planning to do with them. Push up? And then what? Stand there? Run? There was nowhere to run to. There was snow, and trees a hundred yards off, and between her and the trees a whole field of eyes.

She could feel them. Every one. Twenty yards off, the Cullen line, frozen. Esme's face turning away fast, one hand going up. Emmett looking straight up at the sky like he'd suddenly gotten interested in the clouds. Rosalie not looking away at all, her mouth gone hard and flat, like she was memorizing something. Across the field, eleven of Sam's wolves, huge and silent in the snow.

And Sam.

She didn't look. She didn't have to. She knew exactly where he was. She'd always known exactly where he was.

Leah put her forehead down against the ice and pressed her arms in tight against her sides and thought, very clearly and very calmly, *I am going to kill Jacob Black.*

"Whoa—hey. *Hey.*"

His voice.

Leah's whole body went tight.

He'd turned around. She heard it: the crunch of his boots on the wet grass, and then a sudden scuffle, fast, as he turned right back again. She lifted her head an inch off the snow. He was standing with his back to her, exactly the way he'd been standing a second ago, except now his right hand was up beside his face like a blinder on a horse, and his head was cranked so far around toward the trees on the other side of the field that it looked like it hurt.

"Christ on a bike," he said, to the trees. "What the *hell*."

Then, louder, without turning, "Oi! *You!* Big lad! Yeah, you, Clifford. Did you just make her do that?"

Down by the Cullens, there was the same wet folding sound one more time. Leah turned her head and saw Jacob kneeling in the snow, human, six and a half feet of him, bare to the waist and fumbling with the leather cord on his ankle. He got the cutoffs loose and yanked them on, and stood up, and his face was gray and wild and wet. He had one arm out, back, toward the kid, who was clinging to her father's coat.

"She was flooding the pack," Jacob said. His voice came out in a croak. "I couldn't—I couldn't hear—"

"In the *snow*?" The stranger still hadn't turned around. He jabbed his free hand back over his shoulder in Leah's general direction, without looking, like he was pointing at a car crash he didn't want to see. "Starkers? In January? In front of—" the hand swept wide, taking in the Cullens, the wolves, the field "—this lot? Half of 'em are dead and the other half are dogs. What the hell is wrong with you? That ain't no way to treat a woman."

Jacob opened his mouth. Closed it.

Leah felt her face go hot. Hotter. She hadn't known it could get any hotter.

"Hang on," the stranger said, more quietly. "Sorry, love. Hang on."

Nothing happened that she could see. He didn't move. He didn't even turn his head. One second she was lying on a sheet of ice with the wind on her bare back, and the next she wasn't.

There was weight on her. All over. Cloth, soft and close. Jeans. Thick wool socks, and the stiff leather of boots laced up to her ankles. A heavy, close-knit sweater, charcoal-gray, the sleeves coming down exactly to her wrists. Everything else under it too, everything, down to the—she was not going to think about that. She was not going to think about how he'd known her size. She was going to think about it later, alone, in the dark, possibly with a baseball bat.

No coat. She noticed that. A sweater, and nothing over it. A parka would have cooked her alive.

She pushed herself up onto her hands and knees. Then onto her knees. The snow had already started melting under her, the way it did when she sat in it too long; there was a dark wet patch on the knees of the new jeans. The sweater smelled like nothing at all. New. Like a store.

Behind her, she heard Seth say "Whoa," in a small voice. She looked back. He was standing in the snow in jeans and boots and a gray hooded sweatshirt that said nothing on it, with his mouth hanging open, plucking at the front of it with two fingers like he expected it to disappear.

Down by the Cullens, Jacob looked down at himself. He was still in his cutoffs. Nothing else. No shirt, no boots. The stranger hadn't touched him.

"You've got shorts," the stranger said, still to the trees. "You'll live."

Somebody back in the Cullen line made a sound that might have been a laugh. It was Emmett. Rosalie hit him.

"Everybody decent?" the stranger asked.

Leah's mouth was dry. She swallowed. It didn't help.

"Yeah," she said.

Her own voice sounded strange to her. Hoarse. She hadn't used it since four in the morning.

He lowered his hand from beside his face. He turned around.

He looked at her face. Only her face. She saw him do it. His eyes came round and found hers and stopped there, and stayed there.

Then he looked past her at Jacob, and his eyes went flat and hard.

"Next time," he said, "you ask her to go and stand behind a tree."

"There wasn't time to—"

"There's always time for a tree, mate. It's a forest. They're *everywhere*."

Jacob didn't say anything. His jaw was working. He looked at Leah, and she saw it hit him then—what he'd done, exactly what he'd done, the whole of it—and something in his face folded in on itself.

"Lee," he said. "I didn't—I wasn't thinking. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, okay?"

Leah didn't answer him. She wasn't going to answer him for a long time. She got one boot under her, and then the other, and stood up.

It was strange, standing up. She'd forgotten what it felt like to be only five foot ten. The field looked bigger. The trees looked taller. He looked taller, too—six-two, maybe, a solid four inches over her, broad through the shoulders under that beat-up jacket. Eight yards away. The pull stood her up straight and pointed her at him the way a plumb line points at the ground.

She stood there and let it. There wasn't anything else to do.

He looked at her a second longer. She couldn't tell what he was thinking. His face didn't tell her anything at all. Then he looked away, out toward the south, where the feet and the bird-fast heartbeat were getting louder, coming up through the timber.

He let out a long breath. It smoked in the air.

Then he put his hand in his jacket pocket, and when it came out there was a cigarette between his first two fingers.

Leah hadn't seen him take out a pack. She hadn't seen a pack at all. There was just, suddenly, a cigarette, white and new, and he put it in the corner of his mouth, and the end of it went orange all by itself. No lighter. No match. A little curl of gray smoke went up off the tip, and the smell of it reached her a second later across the snow, sharp and dry and ordinary, the smell of the loading dock behind the Thriftway in Forks.

He took a long drag. He let it out through his nose. And he put the heel of his hand against his forehead, just above the right eyebrow, and rubbed there, slowly, round and round, with his eyes shut.

Leah watched him do it, and couldn't look anywhere else, and hated it.

## II. Harry

He'd quit. Twice.

The first time had been for Ginny, years ago, and it hadn't stuck because nothing about him and Ginny had stuck, which was nobody's fault and mostly his. The second time had been for Hermione, who had stood in his kitchen in Grimmauld Place with her arms folded and read him a list of things smoking did to the lining of the lungs, out loud, from a Muggle pamphlet, while Ron ate toast at the table and didn't help. That one had stuck for eleven months.

He took another drag and held it and let it out slow, and watched the smoke go sideways on the wind.

Eleven months. Well. It had been a good run.

The nail behind his eye had settled down to a dull, steady thud, and he rubbed at it with the heel of his hand and kept his eyes shut, because when his eyes were shut he didn't have to look at any of it. The scorch marks. The little gold pendants in the slush. The kid with her face in the big lad's fur. The mum, on her knees. The Romanian's brother, ten feet from his boots.

And the girl.

He didn't need his eyes for her. He could feel exactly where she was. Eight yards back and a little to the left, standing in the snow in the clothes he'd put on her, with her arms at her sides and her fists shut. He could feel it like heat off a stove on the side of his face. He could feel it the way you feel somebody staring at the back of your neck on the Underground, except that this didn't stop when you turned round and looked, and it wasn't going to stop when you got off at your station either.

He knew that now. The mum had known that. It was all in there, in the mum's head, the whole sorry business, picked up in bits and pieces from the big lad and from the doctor and from a lot of late-night conversations in that glass house: *Imprinting*. It didn't wear off. It didn't fade. Distance didn't touch it. The bloke who ran the other pack, the black wolf, had tried to fight his for months and nearly torn himself in half doing it. As far as anybody in the mum's head knew, nobody had ever made one stop.

He opened his eyes and looked at the sky.

"Why couldn't it be demons?" he said.

Nobody answered. He hadn't really expected them to.

"Demons, I can do," he told the sky. "I'm good with demons. Blood sacrifice—fine. Lovely. Give me a cellar in Croydon with a lot of candles and a goat that's having a really bad day, I'll have it sorted by teatime. Dragons." He took the cigarette out of his mouth and pointed at the clouds with it. "I've *done* dragons. I've done dragons twice. One of 'em I *rode*. I'd take a dragon right now. I'd take a dragon with *teeth*."

He put the cigarette back.

"But no," he said around it. "It had to be wolfie soul magic."

Behind him, very quietly, Leah Clearwater said, "Excuse me?"

"Talking to myself," he said, without turning round. "Occupational hazard. Ignore me."

"You said *soul magic*."

"I say a lot of things."

"You said *wolfie*."

"I'm tired," said Harry. "I've been up since four. I've had pie and nothing else. Give me a minute."

She didn't say anything else. He could feel her not saying it, eight yards back, like a held breath.

He let his head drop forward, and rubbed his brow again, and thought.

Right. Options.

He could try a straight *Finite*. That took about half a second to throw out. A *Finite* ended spells, and nobody had cast this one. It had come up out of the ground under the girl's feet, out of a bloodline older than the Statute, older than the Confederation, older than Merlin probably, and you couldn't *Finite* a bloodline any more than you could *Finite* somebody's eye colour.

He could *Obliviate* her. Take out the last twenty minutes, front to back. He turned that one over for about as long as it took to think it, and then put it down, because for one thing he didn't do that to people who hadn't done anything, and for another thing it wouldn't work. The memory wasn't where the bond lived. The mum's head had been very clear on that, if only secondhand: the big lad had looked at the kid one time, one second, and that had been the whole of it. Wipe the girl's memory, and the next time she clapped eyes on him, she'd be right back where she started, except confused and furious about it. And she was already furious.

He could leave. Apparate out of here tonight, go back to London, put the whole Atlantic between them.

It wouldn't help. That was in the mum's head too. The black wolf's girl had been up on the Makah reservation, forty miles away, and it had made no difference at all.

He could write it up. There was bound to be a form. There was always a form. He tried to think which one it'd be and couldn't. *Beings Office, Section Four, Incident Involving Officer and Non-Human Party*, maybe. Or *Inter-Species Entanglement, Unintended*. He tried to picture the face of the clerk on the Confederation duty desk when that came in, and found he could picture it fine, and didn't enjoy it.

And the Quileute file wouldn't help him either. He'd read it on the Portkey along with the rest. It was thin. Twenty-odd pages, most of it from the 1930s, a lot of it in the handwriting of a Confederation field officer called Abernathy who had very clearly not enjoyed the weather. *Hereditary shapeshifting lineage, lupine. Origin unknown. Appears restricted to descendants of a single tribal line. Triggered by proximity of vampire population. Not hostile to humans. Hostile to vampires. Recommend no action.* And then, at the bottom of the last page, underlined twice in brown ink: *Leave them be.*

Nothing about this. Nothing at all about this.

He could take it back to the Department. He thought about that one for a while, because it was the sensible thing, and because sooner or later somebody would make him. There was a door at the end of a corridor on the ninth level under Whitehall that stayed locked, always, and behind it was the thing the Department had been studying for six hundred years and still couldn't name properly. The thing that was older and stranger than anything else in the building. Croaker had the key. Croaker would take one look at a bond that came up out of a bloodline older than Merlin and tied itself round somebody's soul in a second flat, and his pale old eyes would light up like Christmas. And the girl would spend the rest of her life in a room with a very long name on the door, being measured.

He wasn't doing that to her either.

Which left one option. The one he liked least.

He could look.

He dropped the cigarette into the wet grass at his feet. It went out with a little hiss, and then it wasn't there anymore.

Then he turned round.

She was exactly where he'd known she'd be. Eight yards back, a little left, standing straight in the charcoal jumper with her chin up and her arms at her sides. Short black hair, chopped ragged at the jaw, like she'd done it herself with kitchen scissors and dared anybody to say anything. Dark eyes, red-rimmed, very steady. A mouth pulled tight. A face that would have looked at home on somebody about to throw a punch.

She was looking at him. Of course she was.

"I'm going to have a look at it," he said. "This—thing. Whatever it is. I'm not going to touch it. I'm just going to look. You'll probably feel something. It shouldn't hurt."

Her jaw went tighter. "*Probably?*"

"Never looked at one before," he said. "So, yeah. Probably."

She didn't say yes. She didn't say no. Her fists stayed shut. He took that as about as good as he was going to get.

He didn't move his hands. There wasn't any need. He just let his eyes go a little out of focus, the way you do when you're trying to see one of those pictures in the dots that turns out to be a sailing boat, and reached out with the part of him that did the looking, and *looked*.

The field went quiet and gray around the edges. The vampires went to cold blue smudges—the doctor, the mum, the others, each one a little knot of still, dark, frozen light, with no pulse in it. The wolves on the far side went to banked red fires, eleven of them, hot and dense and close to the ground. The kid, behind him, went to something he'd never seen before and didn't have a word for, flickering and fast, half red and half blue, like a candle in a draught.

And the girl went to a fire, too. Hot and red and banked down low.

There was a line coming out of her.

He saw it the moment he looked for it. It came out of the middle of her chest, just under the breastbone, and it was bright. Brighter than anything else in the field. Not much thicker than a guitar string, and so taut it was very nearly singing, a fine hard line of gold-red light that went straight out from her across the eight yards of snow between them, dead level, without a single sag in it.

Straight into him.

He looked down at himself. He couldn't help it. It went into his chest at about the same place it came out of hers, a little lower maybe, and he followed it in. That was the job. You followed the thing to where it went.

It went in, and down. Down past his ribs, past the hot, heavy, steady thing that was his heart, down into the dark underneath. And it kept going. Down to the place he didn't look at very often. The place where the other shape lived. The one with four feet and a tail and a great deal more teeth than he had when he was standing up. The one he'd lived with long enough to stop being surprised by it, and that he'd never once found anybody else who understood.

The line went all the way down there, and it didn't stop at the edge. It went in. It went in and wrapped itself round something deep in the middle of that other shape—something he hadn't known was there to wrap round—and tied itself off. Neat and tight. Like it had known exactly what it was looking for, and found it.

And down in the dark, very faintly, something that was him and was also not him lifted its head.

Harry let go of the looking so fast that the field slammed back into colour round him like a door.

He stood there in the snow and breathed.

She was staring at him. Her hand had come up, without her seeming to notice, and was pressed flat against the middle of her chest, just under the breastbone, over the jumper. Her eyes were very wide.

"I felt that," she said. Her voice wasn't steady anymore. "What did you—what was that? I *felt* that."

"Yeah," said Harry. "Me too."

He put his hands back in his pockets. They weren't shaking. He'd have known if they were shaking.

Right, he thought. Right.

He'd been wrong. That was the first thing. He'd stood here three minutes ago and looked at that grey wolf crawling at him across the snow and thought he knew what he was looking at: a girl from a shapeshifting tribe with a soul-deep instinct she couldn't help, who'd happened to point it at the wrong bloke at the wrong moment, the way you'd step on the wrong bit of pavement. A misfire. Rotten luck. Her problem, mostly. A bit of his.

He'd had it wrong, or only partly right.

The thing had gone looking for something. It had gone straight past everything in this field that had a pulse—the big lad, the brother, the eleven on the far side—and it had gone right down into him, into the one part of him nobody here knew about, and it had found the thing it wanted down there, and grabbed on.

Whatever she'd imprinted on, it wasn't only the bloke with the cigarette.

He could think about what that meant later. He could think about it at three in the morning in a hotel room in Port Angeles with a bottle of something and nobody watching. Not now.

Now he had a dead man's guard to account for, a hybrid to register, a family who were about to start thinking about running, two packs of wolves who didn't know whose side they were on, and whatever was coming up through the trees from the south with a heartbeat like a bird.

He made the box. He'd been making boxes since he was eleven; he was very good at it. He put the girl in it, and the line, and the thing in the dark that had lifted its head. He shut the lid. He put it on a shelf in the back of his mind, a long way back, with a lot of other boxes.

"Look," he said to her. "I can't cut it."

She flinched like he'd slapped her.

"Can't," he said. "As in, I've looked, and there's no way in. Not without cutting something else in you that you'd want to keep. And possibly in me. I don't know enough. Nobody here knows enough." He met her eyes. "So it stays where it is till I find somebody who does. Your elders. Your mum, maybe. Somebody. All right?"

She didn't say anything. Her hand was still pressed against her chest.

"All right," he said again, more quietly, mostly to himself.

The feet in the trees had slowed down.

He turned his head and listened. They'd been running flat out for the last mile, five sets of them and that heartbeat, the light quick patter of vampires who knew where they were going. Now they'd dropped to a walk. Now they'd stopped. Just inside the tree line on the south side of the field, about a hundred and twenty yards off, behind a screen of young firs heavy with snow.

He could smell them now, too, as the wind eddied. Vampire—four of them, that cold, sweet, nothing smell. And something else under it. Something with a pulse. Warm, and close, and odd: like the kid, a bit, but older and deeper and with something wild in it, like woodsmoke and river water.

Behind him, the father had gone completely still.

Harry looked at the firs.

"You might as well come out," he called. "I can hear you. You can hear me. And I've had a really long day."

For a second nothing happened. Then the firs moved, and snow came sliding off their branches in white sheets, and somebody stepped out of the trees.

A girl. Very small, smaller than the kid's mum, with black hair cut short and spiked every which way and a face like something out of a fairy book, all big eyes and sharp chin. She was wearing a coat that had probably cost more than Harry's flat, and the coat was torn at the hem and filthy to the knees with mud and something that looked like jungle.

She stopped at the edge of the snow. She looked out over the field. At the long black scorched line on the far side, and the gold pendants winking in the slush, and the gray smudge ten feet from Harry's boots. At her family. At Harry.

Her face went white. Whiter. And then very, very still.

"I couldn't see you," she said.

She said it straight to him, across a hundred and twenty yards, not loud. He heard every word.

"Yeah," said Harry. "I get that a lot."

## III. Alice

At eleven minutes past ten that morning, somewhere over the Oregon border, the future had gone out like a light.

Alice had been watching it for three days without stopping. That was all she'd done, the whole way north. Through the long green dark of the jungle, and the bus to Manaus, and the cargo plane, and the stolen car, and the last four hundred miles on foot because the car was too slow, she had held one future in her head and turned it over and over like a stone in her pocket. The clearing. The snow. Her family on one side of it and the Volturi on the other, and herself coming out of the trees at the last possible moment with Nahuel, and Aro taking her hand, and Aro *seeing*. Aro deciding. Aro, smiling his paper smile, turning his whole long gray army around and walking it back into the trees.

She'd checked it a thousand times. It held. It held every time. Thin, and fragile, and dependent on a hundred small things going exactly right—but it held.

And then, at eleven minutes past ten, it stopped.

The wolves made things go fuzzy. A mind that hadn't made itself up made things flicker. This was a hard black edge, straight across everything, like somebody had taken a pair of scissors to the film. The clearing, her family, the Volturi, Aro's hand reaching for hers—and then, right at the moment she should have seen him decide, nothing. Black. For the clearing and for everybody in it. Every future she looked for that ran through that field in the next hour ran into that black edge and stopped.

She'd thought she knew what it meant. There was only one thing she'd ever seen do that, cut her family's futures off clean like that. She'd seen it once before, in a mirrored ballet studio in Phoenix, with James standing over Bella on the floor.

She'd run the last forty miles blind.

Jasper had run beside her and not said a word, because he could feel what she was feeling and there wasn't anything to say to it. Nahuel had kept up. Of course he had. Huilen, behind him, had kept up out of sheer stubbornness. Kachiri had run out in front, silent and fast, the way she'd run through the jungle.

And then, three miles out, the wind had changed, and they'd smelled the smoke.

Alice had stopped dead in the trees. She hadn't meant to. Her legs had stopped, and she'd stood there with one hand on a frozen alder trunk, and breathed in, and the smell had gone all the way down into her—that thick, sweet, purple stink, so much of it, so *much*, more than she'd ever smelled in one place in her life—and she had thought, very clearly, *They're dead. They're all dead. I was too slow.*

Jasper had gotten his arms round her before she fell.

Then, very faint, a mile off and down the slope, she'd heard a heartbeat. Fast and light, like a hummingbird. Renesmee's.

And under it, Edward's voice, saying her name.

Now she stood at the edge of the trees with the snow coming down and looked out across the field, and couldn't make it make sense.

Her family were alive. All of them. She counted twice. Carlisle, alone out in the open, with his hands at his sides. Esme, behind him, one hand pressed to her mouth. Rosalie and Emmett, Rose with her arms wrapped around herself. Edward standing with Renesmee on his hip, staring at her across the field with a face she'd never seen him wear. Bella on her knees in the snow beside him, looking up. Jacob, human, half naked in cutoffs, his arm around Renesmee's back. Two more of the Quileutes, human, standing a little way out on their own: Seth, in a gray hooded sweatshirt, and Leah, in a charcoal sweater, with her hand pressed flat to her chest. And across the field, Sam's pack, eleven wolves in a dark silent line.

Everyone was alive.

And on the other side of the field, where the Volturi should have been, there was nothing.

A long black scorch on the snow, from one side of the field nearly to the other, a hundred yards of it, with the snow melted and refrozen into gray glass. The smoke still curling up off it in thin purple threads. And scattered all along it, half sunk in the slush, little winks of gold.

She knew what those were. She'd seen one on a chain round Demetri's neck in Volterra. She'd seen one on Jane.

Her eyes went across the field to the man standing in the middle of it.

She'd known he was there before she came out of the trees. She'd known in the way she knew the shape of a hole in a wall by the light that wasn't coming through it. He was the black edge. He was the scissors. Standing there in the snow in a leather jacket and jeans and work boots, with his hands in his pockets and ash in his hair, in the middle of a circle of brown grass where the snow had melted out from under him.

She tried to look at him. The other way. Forward.

There was nothing there. She'd expected that. It was like looking straight at the sun and seeing only the dark spot it left. But this time she made herself keep looking, and she looked around the edges of it, the way she'd learned to look around the edges of the wolves—and she found that she could see her family again. Pieces. Esme, tonight, in the kitchen. Emmett, an hour from now, standing at the big glass window, looking out. Rosalie, brushing Renesmee's hair. Little things. Little scraps of future, the ones that didn't touch him.

Every single one that did touch him was black.

Which was nearly every one there was.

"I couldn't see you," she said.

She said it to him, across the hundred and twenty yards of snow, not raising her voice. It didn't matter. He heard her.

"Yeah," he said. "I get that a lot."

Then he looked at her for a second longer, and his head tilted, and he said, "You're the one who left the note in the book."

Everything in her went cold.

"*How*," Alice said.

"Your sister-in-law's head." He nodded across the field at Bella, still on her knees. "Long story. Not a nice one. You'll get it from the family, I expect."

Beside her, very quietly, Jasper said, "Alice. Don't."

She looked up at him. He was standing exactly where he'd stopped when they came out of the trees, half a step in front of her, with his weight forward. He hadn't moved. He wasn't going to move. She could see it in his face—the old face, the one from before he'd ever met her, the one he'd worn every night for a hundred years in the south when he was teaching newborns how to kill each other. Major Jasper Whitlock, reading a battlefield.

"What do you feel?" she whispered.

His jaw worked. "The whole field's screaming. Fear. Grief, a lot of grief—Carlisle's drowning in it. Fury. Edward's so angry he can hardly see." His eyes flicked toward the two Quileutes standing out on their own in the snow. "And that girl. Leah. She's like standing next to a bonfire. I've never felt anything like it. She's—" He stopped. Shook his head. "She's pointed at him. Every bit of her."

"And him?"

Jasper was quiet a long moment.

"Almost nothing," he said finally. "He's got it shut down tight. I can get the top of it. He's tired. He's irritated. He's got a headache." His mouth went thin. "Under that, there's a wall, and I can't get under it. But I can tell you this much. He isn't afraid of any of us. Not one bit. He's looking at us the way I used to look at a camp full of newborns, deciding which ones to keep."

Alice looked at him. Then back across the field.

"Thirty-two," Jasper said. "Thirty-three. He did that alone?"

"Yes," Alice said. She didn't know how she knew. She knew.

"Then you walk out there slow," said Jasper. "You keep your hands where he can see them. And you don't run to Edward. You hear me? You don't run."

She walked out there slow.

She kept her hands out from her sides, open, the way she'd seen humans do it on television when they walked out of a building toward the police. The snow crunched under her ruined boots. She could feel Jasper a step behind her and to the left, exactly where he'd put himself, between her and the man in the middle of the field, and she could feel the others coming after him. Kachiri, silent. Huilen, small and fierce and wary. And Nahuel, with his heart going like a drum.

The man watched her come. He didn't take his hands out of his pockets.

When she was halfway, he said, "Hang on. How many of you are there?"

She stopped. "Five."

"Who's the tall one with the spear-thrower's shoulders?"

Kachiri had stopped too. Her dark eyes had gone all the way round the field, once, fast, and come back, and her face had changed. She was looking for her sisters.

"Kachiri," Alice said. "Zafrina and Senna's sister."

"Right." He nodded toward Kachiri. "Your sisters were here. They're gone. I told everybody who wasn't family to clear off, and they cleared off. Tall one put her hand on the kid's head first and gave me a look that took about ten years off my life. They went south." He jerked his chin at the far trees. "You can catch 'em up. I'd go now."

Kachiri looked at him. She looked at the black line on the snow. She looked at Alice.

"Go," Alice said softly. "Tell them we're safe."

She wasn't sure it was true. She said it anyway.

Kachiri went. She was across the field and into the trees on the south side before the snow she'd kicked up had finished falling.

"Ta," said the man. Then his eyes went past Alice and Jasper, to the two behind them, and stopped.

To Nahuel.

Alice watched it happen on his face. She'd watched a lot of faces see Nahuel for the first time over the last three weeks—her own, in a river village above Tabatinga; Jasper's; the old woman at the bus station in Manaus who'd crossed herself. Nobody could look at Nahuel and not see that something was odd about him, even if they couldn't have said what. He was tall, a long-boned young man with skin the deep brown of polished teak and black hair pulled back in a braid, in a cotton shirt and canvas trousers he'd worn through two countries. He looked about twenty. He had a pulse you could hear from across a room. He was very beautiful in a way that made humans step back without knowing why, and he stood in the snow beside his tiny ferocious aunt with his chin up and his hands loose and waited.

The man's head tilted. Alice saw his nostrils flare, very slightly.

"And you're the second one," he said.

Nahuel didn't flinch. "Yes."

"Hybrid."

"Yes."

"Human mum. Vampire dad."

"Yes."

The man shut his eyes for a second. Just a second. Then he opened them. "How old are you?"

"One hundred and fifty years," said Nahuel. His English was careful and soft and edged with Spanish. "Last spring."

The man said a word under his breath that Alice was fairly sure was not English and very sure was not polite.

"Course you are," he said. "Course you are. Why wouldn't you be."

He took one hand out of his pocket and rubbed it down over his face, forehead to chin.

"I came to stand before the Volturi," Nahuel said. "To tell them what I am. So that they would understand the child is no danger to them." He looked across the field at the black scorch, the gold in the slush. His face didn't change much. Alice had learned, these last weeks, that Nahuel's face rarely did. "It seems I am telling you instead."

"Looks that way." The man put his hand back in his pocket. He looked at Nahuel for a long, level moment. Then he looked past him, across the field, at Edward—at Renesmee, on Edward's hip, who had stopped crying and was staring at Nahuel with her mouth open and her eyes enormous.

Alice followed his look, and saw her niece's small face, and saw the way Renesmee's free hand had come up in the air between her and Nahuel, reaching. She'd never seen another heartbeat like her own. She'd never seen anyone who was what she was.

"All right," the man said quietly. "All right. Come on, then. Over here, where her dad can hear you. Let's have it."

He tipped his head toward the Cullen line. Then he started walking that way himself, across the snow, not hurrying.

Alice stood still and watched him pass her, close enough to touch. The snow hissed and went to slush where he stepped. He smelled of cigarette smoke and wet leather and something under that like pine and cold stone and, under that, something she couldn't name at all. He didn't look at her. He didn't look at Jasper, either, though Alice felt Jasper turn on the spot to keep him in front of him every step of the way.

"Easy, Tex," the man said as he went past, without turning his head. "I've seen your scars. I've got no interest in adding to the collection."

Jasper didn't answer. But Alice, who had known him for nearly sixty years, saw him let out a breath he didn't need, and saw his shoulders come down a quarter of an inch, and understood that he'd been looked at, all of him, every crescent-shaped mark on his throat and his hands, in a single glance. And that Jasper didn't know yet whether that made him feel better or worse.

She looked forward, once more, into the place where his future should have been.

Black. Clean black, all the way down.

For the first time since she'd opened her eyes alone in the dark with no memory of her own name, Alice Cullen had absolutely no idea what was going to happen next.

She found she didn't like it at all.

## IV. Edward

Alice's mind reached him before she did.

It came across the snow ahead of her like a dog let off a leash, all of it at once, three weeks of it in a tangle: green dark and rain, a river the color of tea, a village on stilts, a bus with no windows, Jasper's hand in hers on a cargo plane in the dark. Nahuel's face, the first time, in the doorway of a hut, his heart beating. Kachiri, running. A road. A stolen Buick. Snow. And then, very sharp, the moment the future went black—Alice standing in a gas station parking lot outside Medford at eleven minutes past ten, a bag of ice in one hand for the cooler, staring at nothing, while the bag slid out of her fingers and burst on the asphalt.

And under all of it, over and over, a single thought like a heartbeat: *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I left. I had to. I'm sorry.*

"I know," Edward said, before she was close enough for anyone else to hear. "I know why. It's all right."

She reached him. She didn't run. She'd been told not to run; he heard Jasper's voice in her memory, very calm, *You don't run, you hear me*. She walked right up to him and stopped, and looked at Renesmee on his hip, and at Bella, getting up out of the snow beside him, and then she put her arms round all three of them at once and held on, and her small hard body was shaking.

Bella's arms came around her. Over Alice's spiky head, Bella's eyes met his. He still couldn't hear her. Not really. Only that faint, maddening hum, like a radio in another room, there and gone. But he didn't need to hear her for that look.

*She came back*, Alice thought into his head, and he understood she was thinking it about Bella. About all of them. *They're alive. They're alive. How are they alive—*

He told her. Fast and low, the way they spoke when they didn't want humans to follow—the crack, the pie, the mist, Aro, the fire, Vladimir. Renesmee. Bella. He gave it to her in four seconds flat and felt her take it in, piece by piece, and felt her horror rise with each piece like water rising in a lock.

When he got to the part about Bella, Alice's arms tightened so hard round Bella's shoulders that he heard a seam in Bella's sweater give.

"He *what*," Alice whispered.

"Later," Edward said.

Because the stranger had stopped walking.

He'd come across the field at an unhurried pace, hands in his pockets, and stopped about ten yards from them, on the near side of the place where Carlisle had stood all morning. Carlisle had moved back now, to Esme. Rosalie and Emmett had come up on Edward's other side. Jacob stood at Edward's right shoulder, close enough that Edward could feel the heat coming off his bare chest, with one huge hand flat on Renesmee's back. Behind him, out on their own in the snow, Leah and Seth Clearwater.

Edward could hear all of them. Rosalie: *if he touches her again, if he so much as looks at her again—* Emmett: *Rose, Rose, babe, please.* Jacob, in his human head now, so much quieter than in the pack, but with exactly the same note running under it: *her, her, her, is she okay, is she okay.* Leah, out on the right, saying nothing at all in words, a long steady hum of awareness pointed straight at the back of the stranger's head.

And from the stranger, still, nothing. The door in the dark hallway. That low hum from behind it.

Nahuel came and stood in front of him. Huilen stayed at Nahuel's shoulder, small and dark and very still, her red eyes going from face to face.

"Go on, then," the stranger said. "From the top."

Nahuel told it.

He told it simply, in that soft careful English. He told it the way Edward suspected he'd told it to himself a great many times, alone, in the long years when there'd been no one else to tell it to. And as he spoke, Edward heard it twice: once with his ears, in the words, and once in Nahuel's mind, in pictures, behind the words, so much more vivid than the words that it was like watching a film with the sound turned low.

A woman, young and laughing, washing clothes in a river. Pire. His mother. A pale stranger at the edge of the trees, so beautiful the women of the village whispered about him. Pire meeting him in the forest at night. Pire, a month later, sick and frightened, her belly growing so fast it seemed to swell between one sunrise and the next. Her sister—Huilen, human, young, with her hair loose—holding her hand. Pire dying. Huilen, weeping, lifting the bloody newborn up out of the wreck of her sister's body, and the newborn turning his head and biting her on the wrist.

Three days of fire. Huilen waking up with red eyes, and a baby in a basket beside her, crying.

"She meant to kill me," Nahuel said, very simply. "When she woke. For what I had done to her sister. To her." He looked at the small woman beside him. "She did not."

"I was hungry," Huilen said. Her voice was rough, heavily accented. "He was the only thing I did not want to eat. I took this for a sign."

The stranger looked at her a moment. Then he nodded, once, as if she'd said something he'd file away and think about.

"How fast did you grow?" he asked Nahuel.

"Very fast. At the beginning, like the child." Nahuel's eyes went to Renesmee, and stayed there, and something moved under the stillness in his face. "Then slower. By my seventh year, I looked as I look now. I have not changed since."

Edward's whole body went still.

He heard it hit Bella. He didn't need to read her mind. He heard her breath catch—the breath she didn't need—and felt her hand clamp down on his arm hard enough to hurt a vampire. Behind him, Rosalie made a small sound. Esme said, "Oh," very softly.

Seven years.

It had been the thing none of them said. Not in front of Bella. Not in front of Jacob. Carlisle had said it once, alone with Edward, late one night in the study with the growth charts spread across the desk: *If the rate holds, Edward—even if it slows—she could be fully grown in ten years. And if it doesn't stop there, if it doesn't stop at all—* He hadn't finished. He hadn't needed to. Edward had heard the rest of it in his head. Old by twenty. Gone by thirty.

Seven years. And then she would stop.

She would live.

Edward closed his eyes for a moment. When he opened them, the stranger was looking at him. Just for a second. Then the green eyes moved back to Nahuel, and Edward couldn't tell if the man had seen anything at all.

"Seven," the stranger said. "Then you stop. Right. And you've got venom?"

"Yes."

"Sisters?"

Something closed in Nahuel's face.

"Three," he said. "I did not know of them until I was a hundred years old. I went looking for my father. I found him." His jaw tightened. "He was pleased to see me. He told me I was a success."

Edward saw the man in Nahuel's memory, and his hands went cold.

He was handsome. That was the first thing. Dark-haired, fine-boned, with an easy, cultivated voice and the eyes of a man looking at a promising experiment. He'd been standing in the doorway of a house somewhere green and wet, with mountains behind it, with three young women behind him, all of them with Nahuel's dark eyes and a heartbeat. *My son. Look at you. Look at what you are.*

"His name is Joham," Nahuel said. "He believes he is making a new race. A better one. One that can walk in the sun, and eat, and drink, and be strong, and live forever, and pass for human." He paused. "He has made four of us, that I know of. My sisters' mothers died as my mother died. All of them. He does not think of this as a cost. He thinks of it as—" He searched for the word. "As the price of the materials."

The field was very quiet.

Edward watched the stranger. He'd been watching him since the first crack of sound, and he'd learned a little, the way you learn a little about a house by walking all the way round the outside of it. He'd learned that the man's face did almost nothing. That when he was angry, or bored, or surprised, the change was a fraction of an inch at the corners of his mouth and nothing more.

The man's face didn't change now either.

But the snow round his boots, which had been steaming quietly all this time, suddenly went with a soft hiss to bare black soil. All of it. In a circle six feet across. And the grass in that circle curled, and went brown, and smoked.

Huilen took one step back. Nahuel didn't.

"Four women," the stranger said. His voice was the same as it had been. Flat. Ordinary. "That you know of."

"Yes."

"And how many tries that never got as far as a birth?"

Nahuel was silent.

"I do not know," he said finally. "He does not tell me everything. I have not seen him in many years. I did not want to."

"Where?"

"He moves. When I knew him, the south. Chile. The mountains." Nahuel's eyes narrowed. "He takes my sisters with him."

The stranger nodded slowly.

"Right," he said. "I'm going to ask you something. You can say no." He took one hand out of his pocket and held it up, palm out, empty, the way a man shows a dog he isn't holding a stick. "I can take your word for all of that. It's a good word. I believe it. Or I can look. Looking means I'm sure. It means the people I send it to are sure. It means when they go looking for your dad, they've got his face, and the house, and the road to the house." He paused. "I'll be careful. You've got my word on that. But it's your head, and you get to say no."

Edward heard Huilen's answer before she said it. It came up out of her in Spanish, hot and fast, and what she said out loud was "No."

"Aunt," Nahuel said quietly.

"No. He burns them. He burns our kind like dry leaves, and you want to let him put his hands in your—"

"Aunt." Nahuel looked down at her, and she stopped. He looked back at the stranger. Edward heard his decision form, slow and grave and entirely without fear, in that calm deep mind. "Look," Nahuel said.

The stranger nodded once. He didn't move. His eyes went a little out of focus.

And Edward, who had stood with his daughter in his arms and listened to her memories being ripped out of her one after another like pages from a book, stood very still and listened to the same man walk through Nahuel's mind.

It was nothing like the same.

He'd thought of it, before, as something coming in through a window and going through the drawers. Now it came in by the door. Edward heard the moment Nahuel's mind registered it, a faint, cool pressure, very light; heard Nahuel's surprise at how light. Then the memories began to move, the same ones Nahuel had told, and some he hadn't—but slowly now. One at a time. Each one lifted, and looked at, and set back exactly where it had been. Pire at the river. The pale man at the edge of the trees. The house in the green hills. Joham's face, held for a long moment, turned to the light, studied from every angle. The road to the house. A town at the bottom of a valley. A mountain with a notch in it, like a broken tooth.

Then it went out the way it had come. Quietly. Shutting the door behind it.

The whole thing had taken perhaps six seconds. Nahuel blinked, once, slowly, and that was all.

Edward felt something go through him so hot and bright it shocked him. He'd been afraid, all this time. Desperately afraid. He'd been grieving, and in pain, and so angry he could barely see straight. But this was something he hadn't felt since the night in the ballet studio, when he'd come in through the door and smelled Bella's blood on the floor.

"You can do it like that," he said.

The stranger's eyes came back into focus. They found Edward's.

"Yeah," he said.

"You can do it like *that*." Edward's voice was rising. He let it. "Gently. Carefully. You can *ask*. And with my daughter—with my *wife*—"

"Edward," Carlisle said.

"No." Edward didn't look away. "No. I want him to answer it."

The stranger looked at him. For a moment Edward thought he wouldn't answer it at all. Then he took his hand down, and put it back in his pocket.

"Your daughter," he said, "was behind a shield I'd never seen before, on the back of a wolf, smelling like a vampire, with a heartbeat. I'd just burnt thirty-three of your lot to ash and I had no idea what she was. For all I knew she was something wearing a little girl like a coat. So I went through fast." He paused. "I'd do it again. I'm sorry it hurt her. I'd still do it again."

Edward stared at him.

"Your wife," the stranger said, and stopped.

He looked past Edward at Bella. Edward felt Bella go rigid against his side.

"Your wife was a choice," the stranger said. "I was tired, and I'd seen enough, and I didn't have the patience for her. That's on me." He held Bella's eyes. "I'll put it in the report. In my words. It'll be in your file next to everything else, for anyone who reads it. That's the best I've got for you today."

Nobody said anything.

Edward heard what it did to Bella, because he heard the hum from her change. It didn't make words. It went up and sharp and then down again, a long ragged note. He didn't know what it meant. He'd have given a great deal to know.

The stranger had already turned away from them. He took his other hand out of his pocket, and between one breath and the next the silver stag was standing in the snow in front of him again, enormous and bright, its antlers spread against the low gray sky.

Behind Edward, he heard Renesmee draw in a sharp little breath.

"Potter to duty desk," the stranger said. "Priority one, follow-up to my last. Item three. Second hybrid on site. Male. Name, Nahuel. Hundred and fifty years old. Venomous. Full-grown at seven, no ageing since; that's going to be good news for the assessment team, so pass it to them first. He came in voluntarily with a vampire relative, a woman called Huilen. Neither of 'em hostile. Neither of 'em on any register." He took a breath. "Item four. Hybrid's father is a vampire called Joham. He's been producing them on purpose for at least a hundred and fifty years. Human mothers. All dead. At least four, probably a lot more. Three more hybrids in his custody, female, adult. Last known location, southern Chile, Andes. I've got his face, his house, and the road to his house, and I'll put them in a Pensieve vial the second I'm somewhere with a table." His voice didn't change at all. "I want him found. I want him found *first*. Before the hybrid registration, before the Volturi paperwork, before anything. Tell the Director it's mine, and if anybody else on the floor wants it, they can come and take it off me. Potter out."

The stag turned to go.

It stopped.

Renesmee had leaned right out of Edward's arms. He felt it a fraction of a second too late, her small weight shifting, her whole body stretching out toward the silver light with both hands open. Her eyes were huge. Her mouth was open. Her heart had gone from frightened to something else entirely, fast and bright and *wanting*—

Edward caught her before she could fall. He started to pull her back.

The stag looked at her. It turned its great silver head, slowly, and looked at her with its eyes made of light. And then, very deliberately, it took three long steps through the snow toward them—not one hoof leaving a mark—and lowered its head until its muzzle was a foot from Renesmee's outstretched fingers.

Jacob made a sound. Rosalie hissed. Nobody moved.

Renesmee touched it.

Her hand went into the silver light of its nose and partway through. Edward felt a coolness wash up his arm from her, like stepping into the shade. Renesmee gasped. And then she laughed. It came up out of her all at once, high and bright and astonished, the first sound out of her since Irina that wasn't fear or crying. She laughed, and wriggled her fingers in the light, and the light rippled around them like water.

Edward looked at the stranger.

He was watching her. His face didn't do anything. It never did anything. But his hands had come out of his pockets and he'd forgotten to put them back, and he was standing in the snow with his hands loose at his sides, watching a four-month-old child laugh with her fingers in his stag's nose.

Edward couldn't hear what he was thinking. He tried. He'd never wanted to hear anything more.

The door stayed shut.

Then the stag lifted its head, gently, out of Renesmee's hand. It looked at her a second longer. It turned, and went away across the field in long silent bounds, and was gone before it reached the trees.

Renesmee's hand stayed up in the air where it had been. She looked at her fingers. Then she looked at Edward.

She put her hand on his cheek.

He saw it the way she'd seen it: the light, the cool, the enormous patient eyes. *Pretty*, the picture said, and *again*, and under that, very small and confused, a picture of the man in the leather jacket, standing in the snow, with his hands at his sides.

The stranger had turned away. He was looking out over the field, at the long black scorch and the gold in the slush.

"Team'll be here in ten minutes, give or take," he said, without looking round. "They'll want you at the house. Everybody back to the house. You lot walk. I'll walk behind." He paused. "And somebody put the kettle on."

Every small gold pendant in the slush rose up at once, all along the far side of the field, dripping, and came flying across the snow toward him in a long glittering line, and dropped one after another into a canvas bag that had appeared in his left hand, with a sound like coins into a collection plate.

Carlisle made a small noise.

The stranger looked at him.

"I'm sorry, Doc," he said. "Evidence."

Carlisle looked at the bag for a long moment. Then he nodded, and turned, and put his arm around Esme, and began to walk toward the trees, toward home.

One by one, the rest of them followed him.

## V. Bella

They came out of the sky over the river at seventeen minutes past twelve, and one of them fell over.

Bella was standing at the big glass wall in the living room with Renesmee on her hip when it happened. She'd been standing there for eleven minutes. She'd stood there because it was the place in the house where she could see the most of the lawn and the river and the man standing on the lawn, and because she couldn't make herself sit down, and because Renesmee wanted to look out at the snow. Esme had put the kettle on. Nobody had told her not to. It had boiled, and clicked off, and Esme had stood at the stove with her hand on it and not poured anything, because there was nobody in the house who drank tea, and the man it had been for hadn't come inside.

He was out on the lawn by the river, smoking. He'd been doing that for eleven minutes, too. Every so often he looked up at the sky, as if he expected something to come down out of it.

Then something did.

It looked like a twist in the air. A knot of light and color, about twenty feet up, over the dark water, that spun once and came apart, and spat out six people in a tangled ring who dropped the last few feet onto the snow on the riverbank. Five of them landed on their feet, staggering. The sixth, a short round woman in lime-green robes, landed on her backside and sat there in the snow with her legs out in front of her and said something Bella heard very clearly through the glass, which was "*Every bloody time.*"

Between them, falling onto the snow with a dull clank, was an old dented tin kettle.

"Oh my God," said Emmett, from the couch.

"They're wearing *robes*," Rosalie said.

They were. Not all of them, and not the way Bella had pictured, in the half second she'd had to picture anything. Three of them wore long slate-gray coats that might have been robes and might have been very serious raincoats, and all three of them were already moving, fanning out across the lawn in three directions, with thin sticks of wood held out in front of them. A tall, older Black woman in a dark green coat that fell to her ankles stood still and looked around, slowly, at the house and the river and the trees. A man in a gray suit and a gray overcoat and an actual hat, the kind men wore in old movies, had a stick of wood out too, and was pointing it at the house.

And the woman in the lime-green robes got up out of the snow, brushed off her bottom with both hands, picked up the kettle, and tucked it under her arm like a handbag.

The man on the lawn dropped his cigarette in the snow, and walked down to meet them.

---

They let them all out onto the lawn after that. Bella didn't know who decided it. Carlisle, maybe. Or the tall woman in the green coat, who came up the slope from the river with the man in the leather jacket beside her and stopped at the foot of the porch steps and said, in a deep, unhurried voice with an accent Bella couldn't place—West African, maybe, worn smooth by years of somewhere else—"Good afternoon. I'm Senior Beings Officer Adaeze Okonkwo, of the International Confederation of Wizards. I'd very much like to speak to the whole family, if you'd be so kind. Out here is fine. I'm told you don't feel the cold."

So now they were on the lawn. All of them. Carlisle and Esme at the front. Rosalie and Emmett. Alice and Jasper. Edward. Jacob, in the cutoffs, with a sweatshirt of Emmett's pulled over his head that was too short in the arms. Nahuel and Huilen, a little apart. And Bella, with Renesmee on her hip, who had her head turned round to stare at the woman in lime green with absolute fascination.

The woman in lime green stared back. Then she grinned, and wiggled her fingers.

Renesmee wiggled hers.

"Potter," said Officer Okonkwo. She had a clipboard. It had appeared in her hand at some point. Bella was watching the pen clipped to it write on its own, in small neat letters, with no one holding it. "You've had a busy morning."

"Bit," said the man in the leather jacket.

"Thirty-two, the Director tells me."

"Thirty-three."

"Thirty-three." The pen scratched. "The Director has some thoughts."

"I bet she does."

"She'll share them with you in person. At length." Officer Okonkwo didn't look up. "And then she'll buy you a drink. Possibly two. She's been waiting to see the back of that lot since before you were born." The pen stopped. "Right. Introductions." She lifted her head and looked at all of them, one after another, the same way the man in the leather jacket had looked at them on the field. Counting. *May I.*

## VI. Leah

The tent was the size of a pup tent, and it was olive green, and it was patched in four places with what looked like old bits of tablecloth, and it put itself up.

Leah watched it do it. She was standing at the edge of the trees on the river side of the Cullens' lawn, outside the shimmer, where she'd been standing for two hours, and Seth was sitting on a stump beside her with his chin in his hands, and the two of them watched the stranger pull a rolled-up bundle of canvas out of the inside pocket of his leather jacket—a bundle at least three times bigger than the pocket—and drop it on the flat ground under a big cedar fifty yards downriver from the house.

The bundle sat there a second. Then it unrolled. The poles climbed up out of it and snapped together. The canvas went up over them with a sound like a sail filling, and the guy ropes flew out sideways and pulled tight, and eight wooden pegs jumped up off the ground and drove themselves into the frozen dirt one after another, *tock tock tock tock*, like somebody knocking on a door.

He'd watched it the whole time with his hands in his pockets. When it was done, he walked round it once, kicked one of the pegs, nodded, and went inside.

"That's a really small tent," Seth said.

Leah didn't say anything.

"Like, for a guy that size. Is he gonna fit in that? His feet are gonna stick out the end."

"Seth."

"Yeah?"

"Shut up."

"Okay."

He didn't, though. Seth never did. He sat on his stump and kicked his new boots against the side of it and looked from the tent to Leah and back, and she could feel him working up to something the way you could feel a sneeze coming.

"Lee," he said finally. "Are you okay?"

"I'm fine."

"You don't look fine."

"Then don't look."

He didn't say anything to that. After a while he put his hand out and put it on her arm, just above the wrist, and left it there. His hand was hot. Hers was hot. She didn't shake it off.

The light was starting to go. It went early here in January, even on clear days, and this hadn't been a clear day. The sky over the river had gone the color of dishwater, and the snow had started up again, soft and fine. Behind them, the Cullens' house was lit up like a ship, every window gold, and through the big glass wall she could see them all moving around inside it. The strangers in robes had gone in and out all afternoon. The woman in the green coat with the clipboard. The Welsh one, with the kettle. The American in the hat, who'd stood out on the lawn on his own for twenty minutes making a phone call on a phone that was a mirror and had looked, when he was done, like a man who'd lost an argument.

Quil and Embry had gone home an hour ago. Jacob had sent them. He'd come out to the edge of the shimmer—it let him get right up to it, and no further—in Emmett's too-small sweatshirt, and stood on the other side of it from her, and said *Lee* through the shimmer, and she had looked at him until he went away again.

She'd forgive him. Probably. Some day. She wasn't going to do it today.

The pull hadn't let up once. Not in two hours. It had been there the whole time, steady as a heartbeat, pointing her at the tent under the cedar. She'd stood here and felt it, and hated it, and tried, for about the first forty minutes, to walk away from it. Just to see. She'd turned around and gone ten steps into the trees. Fifteen. Twenty.

At twenty-two, she'd stopped. She hadn't decided to. Her legs had stopped, and she'd stood there in the trees breathing hard, with her chest aching like she'd swallowed something too big, and after a minute she'd turned round and walked back.

She hadn't tried again.

Footsteps in the snow behind them. Big ones. Leah knew whose they were before she turned round. She'd have known them anywhere. She'd have known them dead.

Sam Uley came down through the trees in jeans and a flannel shirt and work boots, with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders hunched up around his ears against the snow. He'd gone home and gotten dressed. Of course he had. Emily would have had his clothes laid out for him on the bed. Emily would have made him eat something first.

He stopped about ten feet away. He looked at Seth. He looked at her.

"Hey, Leah," he said.

"Sam."

"Seth."

"Hey, Sam." Seth's hand was still on her arm. She felt it tighten.

Sam looked past them, down the riverbank, to the tent under the cedar.

"I need to talk to him," he said. "About the treaty. About—what happens now. Billy wants to know. The council." He paused. "I didn't want to just walk past you."

"Then don't," Leah said. "Walk around."

He didn't move. He was looking at her. She knew that look too. She'd known it since high school, since the night he'd sat down next to her on the bleachers at a Forks High basketball game and asked her if she wanted half his hot dog. She'd known it the whole time after that. And then she'd known the other look, the one he'd had for two years now, the one that came over his face every time he saw her, guilty and sorry and careful, like he was walking past a dog that had bitten him once.

This wasn't either of them.

She saw it happen. She saw his shoulders come down. Just a little. An inch. Like a man who'd been carrying something heavy up a long hill for two years and had just, finally, set it down at the top. Something in his face let go. His mouth went soft at the corners.

He was relieved.

He looked at her, and he knew, and he was *relieved*.

"Don't," she said.

"Lee—"

"Don't you *dare*." Her voice cracked. She hated that it cracked. "Don't you dare stand there and be glad."

"I'm not—"

"You *are*. I can see it on your face. You think I'm off your hands now. You think now you get to stop feeling bad." She was shaking. She couldn't make it stop. "I was going to die today, Sam. I'd made my peace with it. I'd made my peace with *everything*. And instead I got this. And you're *glad*."

Sam stood there in the snow and took it.

He didn't argue. He didn't say he was sorry, either, which she'd have hit him for. He just looked at her, for a long time, with his big hands in his pockets and the snow coming down on his shoulders, and then he nodded. Once. Like she'd said something true and he wasn't going to insult her by pretending otherwise.

"Okay," he said quietly.

Then he walked round them. The long way, through the trees. Down the riverbank toward the tent.

Leah turned her back on all of it and looked at the river until she could breathe again.

---

She didn't hear what Sam said to him. She tried not to. She could have; the tent was only fifty yards off, and wolf ears didn't go away when the wolf did, not all the way. But she stood there with her back to them and hummed, inside her head, a song her dad used to sing in the truck. She hummed it until it was over and then she started it again.

When she turned round, it was nearly dark, and Sam was walking away up the hill toward the road. He didn't look back. And the stranger was standing outside the flap of his tent, smoking, looking at her.

He'd been looking at her for a while. She could tell.

He took the cigarette out of his mouth and looked at it and seemed to think about something. Then it wasn't there anymore. He walked up the bank toward them, through the snow, not hurrying, and stopped about ten feet away, in the same place Sam had.

"Ex?" he said.

"None of your business."

"Fair." He looked at Seth. "You. Did you eat today?"

Seth blinked. "Uh. Breakfast? Like, early breakfast? Four?"

"Right." He looked at Leah. "You?"

She thought about the glass of orange juice standing at the sink in the dark. She didn't answer.

"Thought not," he said. "Come on, then. I've got bacon."

---

It was bigger inside.

Leah stopped in the doorway with the canvas flap pushed back over her shoulder and stared, and behind her she heard Seth say, "*Dude*," in a voice that cracked right down the middle.

It was a kitchen. A real one, a narrow galley kitchen with a stove and a sink and a little scrubbed wooden table with three mismatched chairs round it. Past the kitchen there was a sitting area with a sagging green armchair and a rag rug and a bookshelf crammed so full the books were stacked sideways on top of each other, and past that, two sets of bunks against a canvas wall, and past *that*, a curtain, and something behind it that might have been a bathroom. There was a lamp lit on the table, an old oil lamp with a smoked glass chimney, and the whole place smelled like canvas and old wood and, faintly, like somebody's grandmother's house. Mothballs and cocoa.

It was about four times as big as the outside of the tent. Maybe five.

"It's a—" Seth turned round in a circle. "It's—how is it—"

"Don't ask me," said the stranger. "I've never understood it either. Sit down."

He'd taken off the leather jacket. He hung it on a hook by the door and rolled up the sleeves of the gray henley to the elbow, and went to the stove, and lit the burner under a big black iron frying pan, the old heavy kind, with his bare hand. Not with a match. He just held his hand over the burner for a second and the gas caught.

Seth sat down at the table so fast the chair scraped.

Leah didn't sit. She stood just inside the doorway with the flap falling closed behind her, and noticed, the moment it did, that something had changed.

The pull had gone quiet.

It was still there. It would always be there. She could feel it, the line in her chest, running across the little kitchen to the man at the stove with his back to her. It had stopped hauling on her. Stopped aching. It had gone slack and easy, like a rope with nothing tied to the end of it, like a dog that's finally gotten to lie down by the fire.

All she'd had to do was stand in the same room.

She hated that more than anything that had happened all day.

He was cooking. She watched him do it. He did it fast and neat and without looking, the way her mom did it, like somebody who'd been cooking for other people since he was tall enough to see over a stove. Bacon first, a whole pound of it, laid out in rows in the iron pan, spitting. Sausages in a second pan. Mushrooms, cut in quarters with a knife he didn't look at. Tomatoes, halved, face down in the bacon fat. A can of beans into a saucepan; the can opened itself while he was reaching for it. Bread into the bacon fat after the bacon came out. Eggs, cracked one-handed, two at a time, eight of them, then eight more.

He didn't use any magic for the cooking itself. She noticed that, too. The can, the gas, sure. But the rest of it he did with his hands.

"Plates," he said, without turning round, and three plates came down off a shelf above the sink and landed on the table in front of Seth and in front of the two empty chairs. Knives and forks after them, clattering.

Seth stared at the plates. Then up at Leah. His face was a whole shouting mess of *Leah, LEAH, are you SEEING this*.

Leah sat down.

He brought the pans over and put food on the plates. A lot of food. More food on Seth's plate than Leah had seen on any one plate outside of Emily's kitchen. Then he put the same again on hers, and put a smaller pile on his own, and sat down across from them, and picked up his fork.

"Eat," he said.

Seth didn't need telling twice. Leah held out for about four seconds, and then the smell of the bacon got all the way down into her stomach, and her stomach, which had been empty since four in the morning and had been through a fight, a phase, and an imprint since then, made a sound like a truck downshifting.

She ate.

It was good. That was the worst part. It was really, stupidly good. The fried bread was crisp all the way through and soaked in bacon fat, and the beans were sweet, and the eggs were runny exactly the right amount, and she ate every single thing on the plate and then sat there looking at the empty plate, and he reached over without a word and put more on it.

Seth was on his third. He'd stopped talking entirely, which Leah had only ever seen happen twice before.

"So that's a full English," the stranger said, mostly to his own plate. "Don't let anyone tell you it isn't a food group."

Nobody said anything for a while. The lamp hissed. The snow ticked on the canvas overhead. Outside, a long way off, the river ran under its ice.

Leah put her fork down.

"What did you see?" she said.

He looked up.

"On the field. When you—looked. I felt it." Her hand had come up again on its own, flat against the middle of her chest. She made herself put it down. "What did you see?"

He looked at her for a while before he answered.

"A line," he said. "From you to me. About this thick." He held up his fork. "Gold. Ish. Tight as a wire."

"That's it?"

"It goes further in than I thought it would." He looked at his plate. "On my end."

"Further in *where*?"

"Somewhere I'm not talking about tonight."

She opened her mouth. He looked up at her, and his face didn't do anything at all, and she closed it again.

Seth swallowed a mouthful of sausage. "You smell like us," he said.

The stranger's fork stopped halfway to his mouth.

It was the only thing he'd done all day that Leah had seen him not mean to do. It stopped for about half a second. Then it kept going, and he chewed, and swallowed, and said, "Do I."

"Yeah, dude. You totally do." Seth pointed at him with his fork. "Under the cigarettes. And the leather and stuff. Like—" He wrinkled up his face, trying to find it. "Like the woods. Like when we're phased. I thought it was me, at first, 'cause I'd just phased back, but it's you."

Leah looked at Seth. Then at the stranger.

She'd smelled it too. On the field. When she was still a wolf, crawling at him on her belly through the snow, before the clothes and the cigarette and everything. Pine and cold stone and wet earth, and under that, something else. Something that made the fur on her back stand up and lie down again. She'd thought it was the imprint. She'd thought it was her own brain, frying.

"Eat your beans," the stranger said to Seth.

"But—"

"*Beans*, Seth."

Seth ate his beans.

Leah didn't take her eyes off him. He didn't look back at her. He finished his eggs and put his knife and fork together on the plate the way they did in movies about English people, and sat back in the creaking chair, and rubbed his eyes.

"Fix it," she said.

He took his hands away from his eyes.

"I told you," he said. "I can't."

"You can do *anything*. You burned thirty-three vampires in a second. You made this—" she waved at the tent "—out of a *pocket*. You put clothes on me without even *looking*—"

"Yeah, and I can't do this." He said it flat. Plain. Not angry. "I looked, Leah. I had a proper look. It's tied off down in something I didn't put there and don't understand, and if I start pulling on it, I don't know what comes out with it. Could be nothing. Could be the bit of you that turns into a wolf. Could be the bit of you that's you." He held her eyes. "I'm not doing that to you to make my life easier. Or yours. Not without knowing."

She sat there.

"Then what happens?" she said finally. It came out smaller than she wanted.

"Don't know." He leaned forward, put his elbows on the table. "Your mum's having a council meeting tomorrow, I'm told. Six o'clock, at Billy Black's. Sam passed it on. I'm going. I'm going to sit there and listen to a lot of old men tell me things, and your mum, and I'm going to find out what this is. And in the meantime I've got a job." He nodded at the canvas wall, toward the Cullen house, the dome, the field, all of it. "That one's not going anywhere."

"And me?"

"You do whatever you want."

She laughed. It came out ugly. "Whatever I want."

"Yeah."

"What I *want* is to go home and sleep in my own bed and never see your face again."

"Then do that."

"I *can't*." Her voice went up and broke. "I tried. I walked into the trees. I made it twenty-two steps, and then I couldn't walk anymore. I just—stopped. And I had to come back. And I *hate* it, and I hate *you*, and I don't even *know* you, I don't know a single thing about you except your last name and that you like *pie*—"

She stopped. Her face was hot. Her eyes were hot. She was not going to cry in a tent in front of an Englishman and her little brother.

Seth had put his fork down.

The stranger was quiet for a long time.

"Harry," he said.

She looked at him.

"First name," he said. "Harry. So that's two things." He picked up his mug, which was full of tea now, which she hadn't seen him make. "I like dogs, too. Real ones. Not the sort that turn into people." He thought about it. "Present company excepted. I hate Brussels sprouts. I'm twenty-six. I was born in July. I've got a godson who's eight and has blue hair, on purpose. I quit smoking eleven months ago and started again this morning because of you lot." He drank some tea. "That's—what, eight? Nine? It's a start."

Leah stared at him.

Seth was grinning. He was trying very hard not to and failing completely. Leah kicked him under the table without looking.

"I hate you," she said.

"Yeah," said Harry. "I got that."

---

He did paperwork after that.

He got it out of a drawer in the kitchen that hadn't been there a minute ago: a thick sheaf of heavy yellowish paper, tied with a black ribbon, with a big red wax seal at the top of the first page stamped with a globe and some letters she couldn't read. A long gray feather with the end cut to a point. No ink bottle that she could see. He sat at the table with the lamp pulled close and untied the ribbon and started writing, and the ink just came out of the end of the feather, black and glossy, as he wrote.

Seth had gone to sleep. He'd lain down on one of the bottom bunks *for one second, just to see*, and he'd been gone inside a minute, flat on his back with one arm over his face and his new boots still on, snoring like a chainsaw.

Leah didn't sleep. She sat in the saggy green armchair with her knees pulled up and her arms round them and watched Harry write. The line in her chest lay quiet and slack and easy. She hated that, too, but she was getting tired of hating things. It took a lot of energy. She'd been doing it since four o'clock in the morning.

He wrote for a long time. He wrote fast, in a cramped, slanting hand, and he talked to himself while he did it, under his breath, so low she had to strain to hear. *Location. Time. Number of beings present, Christ. Number neutralised. Nature of threat. Method.* He paused over that one a while. Then wrote something short. *Witnesses. Ha.* He turned a page. And another. Every so often he stopped and pressed the heel of his hand to the place over his right eye and held it there.

Then he turned a page and stopped altogether.

She watched him look at it. He didn't write anything. He looked at it for a long time, and then he looked up, across the tent, at her. Then back down.

He wrote something. One word. Short.

Leah watched the ink bead up on the paper.

It didn't soak in. It sat on top of the page in a tiny shining black line, like a word written on a waxed table. And then the whole word slid. It ran sideways off the edge of the paper in a single drop, and fell, and vanished before it hit the table. The box was blank.

Harry said something under his breath that she didn't catch.

"What," Leah said.

He didn't look up. "Forms are charmed. Confederation stuff. You can't lie on 'em." He tapped the page with the end of the feather. "Box seventeen. *Relationship of attending officer to any being party to or present at the incident.*"

"What did you put?"

"*None.*"

She didn't say anything.

He dipped his head and wrote again. A longer word, this time. She watched the feather move. The ink beaded. Sat there. Slid. Ran off the edge in a single long drop and was gone.

"What was that one?"

"*Professional*," Harry said, between his teeth.

He put the feather down. He sat back in the chair and rubbed his face with both hands, slowly, forehead to chin, and then he sat there with his hands over his mouth and looked at her across the tent, over the top of his fingers, in the lamplight.

She looked back.

She didn't know what her face was doing. She didn't know what his was doing, either. It wasn't doing much. It never did.

Seth snored.

After a long time Harry took his hands away from his face. He picked the feather back up. He looked down at box seventeen, and he held the point over it for a moment, not touching the paper, like a man standing at the edge of cold water.

Then he wrote.

Leah leaned forward in the armchair. From where she sat she could just see the page, upside down, in the light from the lamp. She watched the ink come out of the feather, black and glossy. She watched it go down onto the paper in that cramped slanting hand. She watched it bead, the way the others had.

It soaked in.

It sat there in box seventeen, dry and black and permanent, and didn't move.

*Pending.*

Harry looked at it for a long moment. Then he turned the page, and kept writing, and didn't look up again.

Leah sat back in the armchair with her knees pulled up. Outside, the snow ticked on the canvas. Somewhere up the hill behind the dome, a wolf howled once, a long way off, and stopped.

She didn't say anything.

She was going to. She had about six things ready. She sat there with all six of them lined up behind her teeth.

"Don't," said Harry, without looking up.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

She was. She didn't.

She put her chin down on her knees, and watched him write, and after a while, without meaning to, she fell asleep.
