# Specimen

## Chapter Two: Need to Know

---

**The Cullen house — Tuesday, October 3 — 12:21 p.m.**

The door opened behind her and closed again, and Leah didn't turn around.

She didn't have to. Nobody else in that house moved without making a sound except all of them, and of all of them, only one would come out after her now. She stood with her hands on the porch rail, looking at the trees, and the rail was wet and cold under her palms and she could feel it starting to warp under her grip, so she let go.

"Don't," she said.

"I didn't say anything," said Edward.

"You were going to."

"I was going to say the rain's stopping." He came and stood at the rail, a good six feet down from her, which she noticed and decided not to give him credit for. "It is, a little."

It was. The sky over the river had gone from slate to the color of dishwater, and the drip off the eaves had slowed to one drop at a time. The air smelled like wet cedar and the river and, behind her, through the walls, the house. Always the house.

She waited for him to say the real thing. He didn't, for a while. That was one of the things she hated about him: he could wait longer than anyone she'd ever met, because he already knew how the conversation was going to go. He just had to stand there until you caught up.

"You heard all that," she said. "In there. What I was thinking when I said I'd go."

"Yes."

"All of it."

"Yes." He said it simply, and then, after a moment, "I'm sorry."

She turned her head and looked at him. He was looking at the trees, the same as she had been. His face in profile was the face of a kid her age, smooth and pale and tired around the eyes in a way that didn't belong on a face that young, and she'd spent three weeks trying not to look at it, because looking at it made it harder to hate him.

"That's new," she said.

"What is?"

"You saying sorry for it."

He was quiet for a second. "I hear it all the time. Things people would rather I didn't. I hear Jacob thinking about Bella, and about the baby, and about me, mostly about me. I hear Seth wondering if anyone at school remembers him. I've heard you." He didn't say what he'd heard of her, and she was grateful for that and angry about being grateful. "I've heard it for eighty-eight years, Leah. I've never once thought to apologize for it. It never seemed like it would help." He moved his hand on the rail. "He apologized to you. In the woods. He heard one thing and he said sorry for it."

"Yeah. I was there."

"I know. I've been thinking about it."

"Don't."

"I'm trying." He almost smiled. "I'm bad at it."

She looked back at the trees. A jay landed on the lawn and walked about with its head cocked, looking for something in the wet grass, and then thought better of it and went away.

"He went in and out," she said. "That's the thing. Did you get that? When he was looking at the leech, it was like—" She didn't have a word for it. "Like he opened a door and walked through it and came back out and shut it behind him. And when he was talking to us, he was in, the whole time. And then at the end, when he said—" She stopped. "When he said sorry. It was like he stepped back out. On purpose."

"I got that," Edward said.

"Can you do that?"

"No."

"Never?"

"Never. Not once." He said it the way you'd say the sky was up. "For me it's weather. You're standing in it whether you want to be or not. I can push some of it back, if there's a lot of people. I can listen harder to one voice than another. That's all. I've tried to turn it off for most of a century, and I can't."

"He can."

"He can," Edward agreed. "And he can go deeper than I can, too. He looked at that nomad for the time it takes to read a page and came out with a woman's shoes and a headlamp and a lake and a day of the week. I only hear what you're thinking now. He went back three days and found something the man wasn't thinking about." He paused. "Aro can do that. With a touch. I've never met anyone else who could."

Leah filed that away. Aro. She knew the name; she'd heard it in Jacob's head, and in Seth's, the stories the Cullens told about the old ones in Italy. Red eyes and black cloaks. She didn't like that she was standing on a porch thinking the man in the diner had something in common with them.

"Why'd he want me to tell Jake the eleven days first?" she said.

"Because he wanted to see what Jacob would do with it," Edward said. "And what we would do. He gave Seth a card for Carlisle and he gave you the one fact that would make everyone in this house angry and frightened at the same time. Then he waited to see who we'd send."

"So he wanted somebody to come."

"I think so. I think he expected Carlisle. Or me."

"Well," Leah said, "he's getting me."

"Yes."

"Is that a problem?"

"I don't know what's a problem with him yet." Edward turned and leaned his back against the rail and looked at her properly for the first time. His eyes were gold, the deep gold they went when they'd fed recently, and it was still the strangest thing about them, that the color should be so bright on something so cold. "I want to drive you in tomorrow."

"I can run."

"You can run to the edge of town. Then you'd have to phase back somewhere, and dress, and walk in from the woods with pine needles in your hair. He'd know. He'd know anyway, but he'd know that." He waited a beat. "And I want to be close."

"Close."

"Within range." He didn't look away. "Alice can't see him, and she can't see you. I can't hear him. But I can hear you. I'll park somewhere nearby and I'll stay in the car, and if anything happens, I'll know before you finish thinking it."

"So I'm wearing a wire."

"Around me, you're always wearing a wire," Edward said. "I'm sorry for that too. But tomorrow it might be useful."

She thought about it. She thought about sitting in that diner with the man with green eyes across from her, and Edward in a car up the street listening to everything that went through her head, every thought about the man, every thought about the pie, every thought about Sam that she wasn't going to have but would have anyway because that was how it worked. Two of them in her head at once. Except one of them would only be there if he felt like it, and the other couldn't help it.

She didn't know which was worse. She suspected she knew which was more dangerous.

"Fine," she said.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. If he does something to Charlie and you're sitting in a car up the street, that's on you."

"Yes," said Edward. "It is."

He said it so plainly that she didn't have anything to throw back at him. She stood there with her arms folded and he stood there with his back against the rail, and inside the house somebody laughed, Emmett, low, at something Rosalie said, and the baby made a small sound and was quiet.

"Where will you pick me up?" Leah said.

"The treaty line. On the 110, where the road crosses into the reservation. Nine-forty."

"Charlie gets there at five past ten."

"Alice says he leaves the station at nine fifty-eight. He'll be there at ten-oh-two. The man will already be there." Edward pushed himself off the rail. "He's always already there."

"Yeah," said Leah. "I noticed."

She went down the porch steps and across the lawn, toward the trees, toward La Push and her mother's house and a shower and a bed she already knew she wasn't going to sleep in. Halfway across the grass she stopped and turned around. Edward was still on the porch.

"Edward."

"Yes?"

"When I'm in there," she said, "and I think something. About him. Or about—anything. You don't say anything about it after. Ever. You don't tell Jake, you don't tell Bella, you don't tell me you heard it. You just—you don't."

He looked at her across the lawn for a long moment.

"All right," he said.

She believed him, which was annoying. She turned and went into the trees, and when she was far enough in that the house was only a smell, she stripped and tied her clothes to her ankle and let the heat come up through her, and ran home on four feet with her mind as empty as she could make it, which wasn't very.

Seth was already home, and her mother had made fry bread, and nobody said the name *Harry* all evening, which was its own kind of noise.

---

**State Route 110, the reservation line — Wednesday, October 4 — 9:40 a.m.**

The silver Volvo was already there when she came out of the trees, parked on the gravel shoulder on the Forks side of the line with the engine running and the passenger door open.

Of course it was.

Leah had slept about two hours. She'd lain on her back on top of the covers listening to Seth snore through the wall and her mother not sleeping either, two doors down, and at four in the morning she'd got up and gone out and run the beach in the dark on two legs until her lungs burned, which took a long time now. Then she'd come home and showered and stood in front of her closet for longer than she'd stood in front of it since high school, which made her so angry she'd grabbed the first things her hand touched. Jeans. Boots. A gray T-shirt. A flannel jacket off the hook by the back door, and it wasn't until she was halfway up the road that she smelled the collar and realized it was her dad's.

She'd almost gone back. She hadn't.

There was a twenty-dollar bill folded in the jacket's front pocket. Her own. She'd checked it three times.

She got into the Volvo and shut the door. It smelled like leather and the house. Edward pulled out onto the road without saying anything, and the car moved the way all his cars moved, smooth and too fast, the speedometer needle sitting at seventy on a road built for forty-five. Something was playing on the stereo, quietly. Piano. A lot of notes, all of them sad.

Leah reached over and turned it off.

Edward didn't say anything about that either. She knew he'd heard her decide to do it before her hand moved, and she knew he'd let her do it anyway, and she couldn't decide if that was manners or a different kind of rudeness.

The trees went by. The road was wet and black, and the sky was low and white, the kind of sky that wasn't going to rain and wasn't going to do anything else either. They passed the turnoff for the Ateara place. They passed the mill.

"It's his jacket," Edward said.

"I know whose jacket it is."

"I'm sorry. I said I wouldn't—"

"You said you wouldn't talk about what I think in *there*." She stared out the windshield. "We're not there yet."

"All right."

"So you've got about ten minutes to get it out of your system."

He drove for a while. When he spoke again, he kept his eyes on the road. "Alice watched Charlie all night. Every few minutes. He's going in. He hasn't changed his mind. He keeps thinking about the man's hands."

"His hands."

"Charlie decided yesterday that he'd watch the man's hands the whole time. He's a policeman. It's what he knows how to do." Edward's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "He's going to pay for the man's coffee. He's decided that too. He's not sure why."

"Because he's Charlie."

"Yes."

Leah thought about Charlie. Charlie in her mother's kitchen these last few months, bringing fish, fixing the drip under the sink he'd noticed and not mentioned until he'd already fixed it. Charlie who'd sat at her father's funeral in a suit that didn't fit and hadn't said anything to anybody, just stood with her mother at the end and held her elbow the whole way back to the car. She didn't know what Charlie and her mother were, exactly. She didn't want to know. She knew she'd put her teeth in anybody who hurt him.

"He's not stupid," she said. "Charlie."

"No," Edward agreed. "He's the opposite. He knows the man's lying to him about nearly everything, and he doesn't know about what. It's driving him a little crazy."

"Join the club."

They came into Forks on 101 from the north. The town looked the way it always looked on a weekday morning, a handful of trucks, a woman walking a dog in a raincoat, the hardware store with its doors propped open. Edward slowed, finally, to the speed limit. He turned onto Forks Avenue and drove past the diner without looking at it, and Leah looked at it, and there in the back corner of the lot beside the dumpster was the black Chevelle. Nose out.

Something went tight in her stomach.

Edward pulled into the Thriftway lot two hundred yards up, the far end, where he could see the diner's front window at an angle through the gap between the pharmacy and the bank. He parked. He turned off the engine. The quiet came down around them.

"He's in there," Edward said.

"How do you know? You said you can't hear him."

"I can't." Edward was looking at the diner. "I can hear the waitress. She's thinking about him. She's put a slice of cherry pie aside under the counter with his name on a sticky note, and she's annoyed with herself for doing it. And there's a logger at the counter who's decided the Englishman is some kind of narc." A pause. "And there's a place in the back of the room where there should be someone thinking, and there isn't."

Leah looked at the diner. From here it was a lit window and some shapes.

"What does it sound like?" she said. "Him. When you can't hear him."

Edward thought about that for longer than she expected.

"Like Bella," he said. "Before. When she was human. Like a room you know someone's in with the lights off."

At ten-oh-two, the Forks police cruiser pulled into the diner lot and parked by the front window. Charlie got out. He stood beside the car for a moment, looking at the Chevelle. Then he hitched his belt and went in.

"He's sitting down with him," Edward said, a few seconds later. His voice had gone careful. "In the booth. The man's given him the seat facing the door. Charlie noticed. Charlie's thinking about it." He was quiet. "Charlie's thinking that's either the friendliest thing anyone's done for him in a while, or the smartest."

"Which is it?"

"I don't know. I can't hear the other half."

Leah watched the window. She counted to two hundred, slowly, which was what she'd decided to do last night at three in the morning, so that she wouldn't walk in on top of Charlie and make it look like she'd been waiting. At a hundred and sixty she heard Edward breathe in sharply.

"What?"

"Charlie laughed," Edward said. "At something the man said. He didn't mean to. He's annoyed about it." A beat. "Five past ten. That's Alice's vision. Exactly."

"Good for Alice."

She opened the door.

"Leah," Edward said.

She stopped with one boot on the asphalt.

"I'll be here," he said. "I'll hear everything. I won't say anything about any of it. And if you need me—"

"I won't."

"—you won't have to say so," he finished. "I'll already know."

She got out and shut the door, a little harder than she needed to, and walked down the sidewalk toward the diner with her hands in the pockets of her father's jacket and the twenty-dollar bill folded under her fingers. The air smelled like rain that hadn't fallen and wet asphalt and diesel and, as she got closer, coffee and fry grease and cherries.

And something else, faint, under it. Pine and wet stone and gun oil.

She pulled open the door, and the bell rang.

---

**The diner, Forks — 10:06 a.m.**

Heat hit her first, then the noise. A radio behind the counter turned low to a country station. Fat spitting on the griddle. Two old men at the counter arguing about the Seahawks with the comfortable tiredness of men who'd been arguing about the Seahawks since before the Seahawks existed. The waitress, a heavy woman in her fifties with reading glasses on a chain, looked up from the register and said, "Morning, hon, sit anywhere," and went back to the register.

Leah looked at the back booth.

Charlie was sitting in it, facing the door. He had his hat off and set on the seat beside him, and a mug of coffee in front of him, and his face was doing the thing it did when he was trying very hard not to enjoy something.

Across from him, with his back to the room, sat the man.

She knew him from behind. She'd have known him from a mile behind. The shoulders were the same, and the black hair going six directions at once, and the set of his head. But there was no suit today and no long black coat. He was wearing a brown canvas work jacket over a gray thermal shirt with the sleeves pushed up, and jeans, and boots that had been somewhere muddy. He looked like half the men who came into her mother's kitchen on a Sunday. He had a plate of cherry pie in front of him with a scoop of vanilla going soft on the side and a fork in his hand, and he was talking.

"—and I've got this trout on, right, first fish of my life, I'm about eleven, and I'm reeling it in, and out of the reeds comes this swan. Massive. Size of a Labrador. And it doesn't want the trout, Chief, I want to be very clear about that. It wants the rod."

"Get out of here," said Charlie.

"Hand on my heart. It took the rod clean out of my hands and swam off with it into the middle of the reservoir, trout and all, and my uncle stood on the bank and laughed until he had to sit down." He ate a bite of pie. "Only time I ever made that man laugh."

Charlie's mouth moved. He put his mug up in front of it.

The man had his back to the door. Leah stood there and looked at that, and something about it bothered her, and then she saw it. The window beside the booth. Outside, the day was flat and white, and inside the diner every light was on, and the big window had turned into a dark mirror that gave back the whole room. The counter. The door. Her, standing just inside it. And in the reflection, very briefly, the man's eyes went to her and away again, without his head moving at all.

He'd seen her come in. He'd seen her before the bell finished ringing.

Charlie looked up past him and his face changed.

"Leah?"

"Hey, Charlie."

"What are you—" He started to get up, the way Charlie always started to get up when a woman came to a table, even a woman he'd known since she was in diapers, and got tangled in his own hat and sat back down. "What are you doing in town?"

"Errands," Leah said. "For Mom."

The man had turned around in the booth. He did it unhurriedly, one arm along the back of the seat, and looked up at her with polite, mild curiosity. Green eyes. Very clear. Nothing in them she could get a grip on at all.

"Friend of yours, Chief?"

"This is Sue Clearwater's girl," Charlie said. "Leah. Leah, this is, uh—"

"Harry Evans." The man slid out of the booth and stood. He was taller than she'd thought from the woods, or she was shorter on two legs; she had to tip her head back a little. He held out his hand. "Pleasure."

She saw Charlie's face do something at *Harry*. A small flinch around the eyes, there and gone. She felt her own face try to do the same thing, and stopped it.

She took his hand. She meant to squeeze it, hard, harder than any human hand could take without something giving. She'd done it before to boys at parties who held on too long.

His hand was hot.

A human hand would have felt cool to her. This one was *hot*. Hot like hers. Hot like Jake's, like Seth's, like every hand in the pack, a hundred and eight degrees of fever that never broke. She felt it go up her arm like a shock, and she forgot to squeeze.

His face didn't do anything at all. He shook her hand once, firmly, the way a man shakes hands at a funeral, and let go.

"Join us," he said. "The Chief's been telling me lies about steelhead and I need a witness."

"I'm not lying," said Charlie. "Leah, sit down, you're—sit down."

Charlie slid over toward the window, collecting his hat. Leah sat down next to him on the vinyl, across from the man, and put her hands flat on the table because she didn't know where else to put them. Her palm was still tingling.

*Hot like mine.* She didn't let herself think the rest of it. Edward was two hundred yards away, listening. She could think about it later. She'd think about it a lot.

The waitress came with the pot. "Coffee, hon?"

"Please."

"Anything to eat?"

Leah looked at the man's plate. "Is there any of the cherry left?"

"One slice," said the waitress, and glanced at the man, as if checking. "You're in luck."

"I'll pay for my own," said Leah.

The waitress looked at her over her reading glasses. "Well, I'd hope so," she said, and went to get it.

The man was looking at his pie. He didn't smile. Something near his mouth did something, briefly, that might have been the beginning of one.

---

For the next twenty minutes, she watched him work.

That was the only word she had for it afterward. She'd seen her mother do it with doctors at the hospital and Old Quil do it with the men from the state who came every couple of years about fishing rights: let the other person think every idea in the room was theirs. She'd never seen anyone do it this well, and she'd never seen anyone do it to Charlie.

He asked about the rivers. Not where Charlie fished; he never once asked where Charlie fished. He asked whether the Calawah was really too high this time of year, the way the woman at the outfitter's had told him, and Charlie said it was, and said the Sol Duc was better, and said a few more things about the Sol Duc, and the man listened like it was the most interesting thing he'd heard all week. He told another story about fishing, a short one, about losing a fly in a tree on his first cast in Scotland and the gillie refusing to speak to him for the rest of the day. Charlie laughed again, a short one, through his nose. The man said he'd always wanted to hook a steelhead. He said he'd been told it was like hooking a freight train. He said he'd probably just lose Charlie's flies in Charlie's trees, and he said it like he didn't expect anything to come of it, and he went back to his pie.

And Charlie said, "You want to come out Saturday?"

Leah watched him hear himself say it. Charlie's ears went a little red. He picked up his mug and looked into it like it had done it.

"I wouldn't want to impose, Chief," the man said.

"It's not imposing. I go out most Saturdays. Me and Billy Black." Charlie cleared his throat. "Billy won't mind. Probably. He'll give you a hard time."

"Billy Black." The man said it slowly, like he was finding the name on a shelf. "That's the gentleman on the tribal council? Wheelchair, I think somebody said?"

"That's Billy."

"I've been meaning to pay my respects, actually." He turned his mug a quarter turn on the table. "Seeing as half the sightings I'm meant to be chasing are on or near tribal land. Seems only polite to introduce myself to somebody before I go poking about in their woods."

*You've been poking about in our woods for eleven days*, Leah thought, very clearly, straight at him.

Nothing. He was looking at Charlie, and his face didn't move.

"Well," Charlie said. "Billy'll be there. If you want to come."

"I'd like that very much, Chief. Thank you."

"Six a.m. At Billy's place. I'll write it down." Charlie patted his pockets for a pen. "Don't be late. Billy doesn't wait."

"I'm never late."

"And don't wear that jacket," Charlie added. "Fish'll see you coming."

The man looked down at his canvas jacket, and then up at Charlie, with an expression of deep injury. "This is my good jacket."

Charlie laughed. That was the third time. Leah had been counting.

She sat there with her coffee going cold in front of her and a slice of cherry pie she hadn't touched, and she felt like she was watching a man pick a lock with his hands in his pockets. She couldn't see how he was doing it. She could only hear the tumblers falling.

Six a.m. Saturday. In a boat. With Charlie, who was Bella's father. With Billy, who was Jake's.

She opened her mouth.

And shut it again, because Charlie was sitting right there next to her with his hat on his knee and his ears still a little red, looking happier than she'd seen him look in a long time, and what was she going to say? *Don't, he's dangerous, he hung a vampire off a tree yesterday and carried it off like a surfboard?* Charlie didn't want to know. Charlie had made that very clear to everyone. Need to know. He'd drawn that line himself, around himself, and he lived inside it, and the man across the table from her knew it. She was suddenly certain he knew it. He'd sat down in the one place in Forks where she couldn't say a single true word.

She looked at him. He was eating pie.

He'd kept his hands on the table the whole time. She noticed that now, too late, the way she'd noticed the window. Both hands, in plain sight, around the mug or on the fork, the whole twenty minutes, never once in his lap, never in a pocket. And Charlie's eyes had kept going to them, the way a cop's eyes do, and every time they went there, there the hands were. Giving Charlie exactly what Charlie wanted to see.

Charlie turned to her.

"How's your mom doing?"

"She's good." Leah heard herself add, "She said to say hi." Her mother hadn't said anything of the kind.

Charlie's face did a thing she wasn't ready for. It went soft around the edges, just for a second, and he looked down at his hat. "Yeah? Well. Tell her hi back."

Across the table the man was cutting a piece of pie with the side of his fork, and he didn't look up, and Leah knew—the way you know a step's missing in the dark—that he'd seen that, too. Charlie's face, going soft at her mother's name. He'd put it somewhere. She'd handed it to him herself.

The radio on Charlie's belt crackled.

*"Chief, you there? We've got a log truck jackknifed on 101 at the Sol Duc bridge, both lanes blocked. Driver's okay. State patrol's twenty minutes out."*

Charlie closed his eyes briefly. "Copy. On my way." He stood up, put his hat on, and fished in his back pocket. He came out with a ten and dropped it on the table next to the man's plate.

"Coffee's on me," he said. "Don't get used to it."

The man looked at the ten, and then up at Charlie. "Wouldn't dream of it, Chief."

"Saturday."

"Six a.m. Billy's place. Not this jacket."

Charlie pointed at him, the way he pointed at teenagers he'd decided not to write a ticket for, and turned to Leah. "Tell your mom I'll call her tonight."

"Okay."

"Okay." He hesitated, as if there was something else, and there wasn't, or there was and he'd decided against it. He touched the brim of his hat to her. Then he went out. The bell rang behind him.

Leah watched him go in the dark mirror of the window. Then she looked at the man.

He'd turned in the booth to watch Charlie through the glass. Charlie crossed the lot to his cruiser, and the man watched him the whole way, his face quiet and attentive and pleasant, and Leah had seen that look before. She'd seen it on her father, a long time ago, sitting in a boat at the mouth of the Quillayute with a line in the water and his eyes on the float. Watching it settle, with all the time in the world.

The cruiser pulled out with its lights on and no siren and turned north.

The man turned back around. He looked at her across the table, and at her untouched pie, and at the ten-dollar bill.

"Right then," he said. "Lassie."

Her ears went strange.

It happened between one breath and the next. The country station behind the counter, the griddle, the two old men and their Seahawks: all of it went soft and far away, as if somebody had wrapped the booth in a wet towel. Under it was a faint hum, high and steady, like bees in a wall. She could still see the old men's mouths moving. She couldn't make out a word.

The man hadn't moved. His hands were still on the table.

"What did you do?" she said.

"Bit of privacy." He picked up his fork. "Muffliato. Old trick. Anybody tries to listen in, they get bees. Saves us both from Dot."

"You just—" She looked at the old men. One of them was frowning and knocking the side of his head with the heel of his hand, like he had water in his ear. "In the middle of a diner."

"Middle of a diner's the best place for it. Nobody looks twice at two people talking in a diner." He ate a bite of pie. "Go on, then. You've been sitting on it for twenty minutes. Your coffee's cold."

She looked at him across the table. In the white light from the window his face was younger than she'd thought in the woods, mid-twenties at most, and older than that around the eyes, and it gave her absolutely nothing. He looked like a man having pie.

"Don't call me that," she said.

"Call you what?"

"You know what."

"Lassie?" He seemed to consider it. "Can't promise. It suits you."

"Call me that again and I'll—"

"Bite me in a diner?" He shook his head slowly. "Dot'd never forgive you. She's very protective of her booths."

Leah put both hands flat on the table and made herself breathe.

"Who are you?"

"Evans. It's on the card."

"Who do you work for?"

"That's classified."

"Yesterday it was Homeland Security."

"Then I expect that's who I work for." He turned his mug. "It says so on the badge. Very official badge. Gold."

"With that accent."

"We've done that one, I think."

"Is it even true?"

"It's true enough for the state of Washington," he said. "Next question."

She had a list. She'd made it at three in the morning, lying on her back in the dark, and she'd run it over and over on the beach until she had it in order. She'd thought it would feel like hitting him. It felt like throwing gravel at a wall.

"Where's the leech?"

"Safe."

"Is he alive?"

He took a sip of his coffee and set it down, and for a second she thought he wasn't going to answer at all.

"He was," the man said, "and he continues to be, most helpful."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you're getting."

"What did you do to him?"

"That's above your clearance."

"I don't have a clearance."

"Then it's above that as well."

She stared at him. He looked back at her, calm and patient and faintly apologetic, like a man at a bank telling her the computer said no.

"The trap," she said. "In the woods. It's still out there."

"Is it?"

"Don't do that. Is it going to grab one of the Cullens?"

"I answered that yesterday."

"Answer it again."

"Guest list." He said it without any weight on it at all. "Nothing's changed. Anything else about it is need to know, and you don't."

"The Cullens are the ones who'll be walking through it."

"Then the Cullens can ask me themselves. The doctor's got my number." He tipped his fork at her. "It's not for civilians, Lassie. No offence."

"I'm not a civilian."

"You're not in my chain of command," he said, "which for my purposes makes you a civilian. A very large one, with a lot of teeth. But a civilian."

She sat back against the vinyl. The hum in her ears went on. Out past the dark window the lot was gray and empty where Charlie's cruiser had been.

Edward was listening. She could feel it, or she thought she could: the knowledge of it, like somebody standing behind her in a line. Edward heard every one of these questions before she asked it and every one of these answers as it landed in her head. And the man across from her—

She tested it.

She didn't plan to. It came up out of her the way the snarl had in the woods. She looked straight at his face and thought, as hard and as clearly as she'd ever thought anything, *You smug, lying, pie-eating son of a bitch, I hope you choke on it.*

He took another bite of pie. He chewed. He swallowed. He reached for the sugar dispenser and tipped some into his coffee, and as his arm went out his sleeve rode up, and she saw a strap of dark leather around his right forearm, close to the wrist, and the end of something slim and brown tucked into it. Then the sleeve came back down.

He hadn't so much as blinked.

"Are you in my head?" she said.

"No."

"Yesterday you were."

"Yesterday was work."

"And this isn't?"

"This is pie." He stirred his coffee. "Different thing entirely."

"How do I know?"

"Think something rude at me."

"I already did."

"There you go, then." He tapped the spoon on the rim of the mug and set it down. "If I'd heard it, I'd have had something to say back. I always do. Ask anyone."

She looked at him for a long time.

It was true. She knew it was true; she didn't know how, and it made her angrier than anything else he'd said. In the woods she'd felt him in there the whole time, a stranger in the room of her head, listening, and then at the end she'd felt him step back out. And now there was nothing. No pressure. No one standing behind her. Just Edward, two hundred yards away, who couldn't step back out if he tried.

"Some people," she said, "can't turn it off."

"Some people never learned how." He said it mildly, looking at his coffee. Then he looked up at her, and there was something in his face for a second that she didn't expect, and it was gone before she could name it. "I go in when I need to. Not before. Not after. It's rude, otherwise, and it's tiring, and most of what people think is the same four things on a loop. Lunch, money, sex, and whether anybody's noticed they've put on weight."

"That's disgusting."

"That's people." He shrugged. "I'd rather have the pie."

Dot came by with the pot and topped up his mug, and looked at Leah's untouched slice, and said, "Something wrong with it, hon?"

"No," Leah said. "It's fine."

Dot looked at the man, and then at Leah, and her mouth went thin in a way that meant she'd decided something about the two of them, and she went away. She didn't seem to notice the bees. She didn't seem to notice anything.

Leah picked up her fork and put it down again.

"What do you want with Charlie?"

"Fishing," the man said.

"Bullshit."

"Language." He glanced at the counter. "Family establishment."

"He's a good man."

"He is."

"He's been through enough this year."

"He has."

"So what do you want with him?"

The man set his fork down on the edge of his plate, very neatly, the tines pointing at her. He looked at her across the table, and his face was perfectly pleasant, and his eyes were green and clear and level, and he said:

"That's need to know, Miss Clearwater."

"And I don't."

"And you don't."

"He's Bella's dad."

"I'm aware."

She leaned forward. She kept her voice down, even with the bees. "If you hurt him—"

"Nobody's going to hurt the Chief." He didn't raise his voice either. He didn't need to. "I need him in one piece."

He picked his fork back up.

Leah sat there.

*I need him.* He'd said it the way you'd say you needed a wrench. And she thought about the twenty minutes before, the swan and the steelhead and the freight train and the good jacket, and Charlie's ears going red, and Charlie's face going soft at her mother's name, and the man watching it all the way her father had watched a float, and she understood something she couldn't have proved to anybody, not Jake, not Edward, not a judge. Whatever he wanted with Charlie, he wanted it for himself. Charlie was the line in the water. Whatever the man was fishing for was somewhere underneath.

And he wasn't going to tell her. She could sit here until the diner closed, and he'd eat pie and drink coffee and say *need to know* in that calm, flat, reasonable voice until she put her fist through the table.

She thought about putting her fist through the table.

She thought about something else instead.

"You know I could have your throat out," she said quietly, "before you got that stick out of your sleeve."

He looked down at his right forearm, where the sleeve covered the strap, and then back up at her. He didn't seem surprised she'd seen it. He didn't seem anything.

"You could try," he said.

She moved.

She was faster on two legs than any human being alive. She knew that the way she knew her own name; she'd tested it, alone, on the beach at night, the first month after she phased, until she'd stopped being afraid of it. Her right hand went across the table and closed around his right wrist before his coffee had stopped rippling from Dot's pour, and she clamped down, meaning to grind the bones together, meaning to watch his face change—

—and her hand wasn't on his wrist anymore.

She didn't see it. She'd think about that for days. One moment she had him, and the next he'd turned his hand inside her grip, a small, sharp twist like opening a jar, and his fingers had closed over the back of her hand and folded it down and in, and her wrist bent the wrong way, and her elbow locked straight out, and her palm came down flat on the Formica with a soft slap. His thumb sat in the hollow on the back of her hand between two bones. He leaned on it, a little.

Pain went up her arm like a lit fuse. From her wrist to her elbow to her shoulder, white, bright, everywhere at once. Her whole arm was a straight bar from the shoulder down, locked, and she understood with perfect clarity that if she moved it an inch in any direction something in it would break, and that it would be her, and not him, who broke it.

She didn't move.

He'd picked up his coffee with his left hand. He took a sip.

At the counter Dot was writing something on her pad. The old men were still arguing. Nobody was looking at the back booth.

"Here's a tip," the man said, in the same mild voice. "Free of charge."

He held her there. He wasn't even leaning hard. She could feel how little he was using.

"The wolf's very impressive," he said. "Lovely coat. Big teeth. Fast. Great for a nap in front of the fire, I'd imagine. But the human one—" he moved his thumb a quarter of an inch, and the white line of pain went up into her jaw, and she bit down on a sound "—when it's honed properly, is a good deal more lethal. Hands. Leverage. Joints. Angles. Your shape's got the same bones in it as everybody else's. You just have to know where they bend."

He let go.

She snatched her hand back and held it against her chest under the table. Her wrist throbbed. Nothing was broken. She knew nothing was broken because he'd made sure nothing would be, and that was worse than if something had been.

"You're human," she said. Her voice came out rough. "You're—you've got a heartbeat. I can hear it. You can't be—"

"That's classified," he said.

She stared at him.

He set his mug down. For the first time since Charlie left he looked something other than pleasant; he looked tired, and faintly irritated, like a man who's been asked the same question at too many parties.

"Do you know what I find absurd?" he said. "Genuinely absurd. Not one of you bothers to learn to fight. Not one. Not the leeches, not the wolves. You've got bodies people would sell their mothers for, faster than anything on earth, stronger than anything on earth, and you use them like a drunk uses a pool cue. Grab, bite, throw, swing. All weight and teeth. It's a pub at closing time. The bloke I met yesterday has had seventy-odd years in that body, and in seventy years it's never once occurred to him to think about where he puts his feet." He shook his head. "It's a waste. That's what gets me. It's such a waste."

"Jasper—" Leah said, and stopped.

She stopped so hard she bit her tongue.

The man looked at her. One eyebrow went up, a fraction of an inch.

"Somebody, then," he said. "Good for him."

He didn't ask. He didn't follow it up. He picked up his fork and went back to his pie, and Leah sat there with her wrist throbbing against her chest and the taste of blood in her mouth and understood that she'd just given him something, a name and a fact to go with it, without his having to reach into her head for it at all. He hadn't needed to. He'd just waited for her to hand it over.

*Damn it.* She thought it so hard it hurt. She didn't care who heard. *Damn it, damn it, damn it.*

He didn't look up.

The bees hummed. Out in the lot, a pickup pulled in and a man in a Mariners cap got out and went into the pharmacy. The light through the window hadn't changed in an hour; it never did, here, in October. It was just white.

Leah looked at the card in her head. Cream-colored, thick, in the rain on the moss in front of Seth's paws.

"Is it your real name?" she said. "Harry."

"It's the one on the card."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I answered."

She looked at her coffee. "My dad's name was Harry."

There was a pause. It wasn't long. She'd remember that, later, that it wasn't long, that it was exactly long enough.

"I know," he said.

She looked up.

He was looking at her. His face was very quiet.

"I'm sorry about your dad," he said.

It went through her like cold water. People had been saying it to her for seven months, people at the store, people at the clinic, teachers she hadn't seen since she was twelve. What went through her was that he *knew*. He'd known when Charlie said *Sue Clearwater's girl* and he'd put out his hand and said *Harry* to her face. He'd known yesterday, in the woods, when he flicked the card down in front of Seth. He'd known all eleven days. He knew whose jacket she was wearing. She was suddenly certain of that, too.

And he meant it. That was the part she'd turn over and over all the way home and into the night, the part she couldn't make fit with everything else. He'd said *I'm sorry about your dad* the way people said it when they'd buried somebody of their own. Not a word extra.

And it hadn't stopped him using it. Not for a second.

"Two more things," he said. He was reaching into his jacket for his wallet. "Then I've got to go and look at some mud. First: tell the doctor I got his message. I'll ring him when I've got something worth saying."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"He'll want more than that."

"I'm sure he will." He took out a twenty and laid it on top of Charlie's ten. "Second. Tell the lad in the silver Volvo outside the Thriftway that the cherry's very good. He should come in some time. Dot'd like him. He looks like he needs feeding."

Leah's whole body went still.

"How—"

"I've got eyes, Lassie." He nodded at the dark window, and the room in it, and the gap between the pharmacy and the bank through which, if you knew where to look, you could just see the back end of a silver car. "Nobody parks outside a grocery shop for the best part of an hour with the engine off and the windows up and doesn't go in for so much as a pint of milk. Either he's casing the place or he's waiting for someone. And he's not the type to rob a Thriftway."

He'd known Edward was there. He'd known the whole time. He'd sat across from her and let her ask her questions and answered them knowing every word was going straight up the street, and he'd chosen every word for that. *Most helpful. Guest list. The doctor's got my number.* He'd been talking to her and to Edward, both, at once.

She reached into her father's jacket and took out the folded twenty and slapped it down on the table next to her untouched pie, harder than she meant to. The coffee cups jumped.

"I'll get my own pie," she said.

He looked at the twenty, and then at her, and he grinned, quick and lopsided and sudden. It took ten years off him. She hated it.

"Fair enough," he said.

He slid out of the booth and stood and took a dark wool cap out of his jacket pocket and pulled it on. The bees stopped. The diner came back all at once, the radio and the griddle and one of the old men saying *—and I told him, I said, that's not a quarterback, that's a coat rack—*

"Ta-ra, Lassie," the man said. "Mind the wrist. Ice it."

He went out. The bell rang.

She watched him in the window. He crossed the lot to the Chevelle without hurrying and without looking anywhere in particular, and got in, and started it, and the engine came up through the glass and through the floor and into her feet like a growl. He pulled out of the lot. He turned north on 101, the same way Charlie had gone.

As the Chevelle passed the Thriftway, its driver's-side window was down, and a hand came out of it and lifted two fingers, lazily, toward the silver Volvo parked at the far end of the lot.

Then it was gone around the bend.

Leah sat in the booth for a while.

Then she picked up her fork and ate the pie, all of it, every bite, because she'd paid for it.

It was very good.

**The Thriftway parking lot — 10:57 a.m.**

Edward had the passenger door open before she reached the car.

She got in and pulled it shut and sat with her right hand in her lap, cradled in her left, and didn't look at him. The Volvo smelled like leather and the house, and after the diner it smelled worse than usual, and she didn't care.

"Let me see," Edward said.

"No."

"Leah. I went to medical school twice."

"Congratulations."

He waited. She knew he'd felt it, all of it, the white line going up her arm into her jaw, the second when she'd understood that if she moved she'd break her own elbow. He'd been sitting right here in this car with his hands on the wheel feeling her arm lock, and he hadn't been able to do anything about it, and he'd known before it happened, a fraction of a second before, because he'd heard her decide to grab.

She held her hand out.

He took it very carefully. His fingers were cold, which on the back of her hand felt good, and she hated that it felt good. He turned her wrist one way and then the other, slowly, watching her face, and pressed his thumb into the same place the man's thumb had been, much more gently, and she hissed.

"Bruised," he said. "Deep. Nothing broken, nothing torn. You'll heal it in an hour." He let go. "He put all the pressure in the one place that would hurt most and damage least. That takes a lot of practice."

"I didn't ask for a review."

"I know." He put both hands back on the wheel. He didn't start the car.

They sat. A woman came out of the Thriftway pushing a cart with a squeaky wheel and loaded bags into the back of a minivan and drove away. The sky stayed white.

"Well?" Leah said finally.

"Well what?"

"You heard all of it. Tell me what you heard. From him."

Edward was quiet for a moment.

"Nothing," he said. "I heard Charlie, until he left. I heard the waitress the whole time; she's decided you're his girlfriend and you've had a fight. I heard the two men at the counter. I heard you." He paused. "And where he was sitting, there was nothing. For an hour. An empty place in the room, the shape of a man, where a mind should have been."

"Like Bella."

"Like Bella was," he said. "Bella was a silence I wanted to get into. This was a silence that knew I was listening."

Leah turned her wrist over in her lap. "He knew you were here."

"I heard you realize it."

"He waved at you."

"I saw." Something moved at the corner of Edward's mouth. "He said the cherry's good."

"It is good."

"I heard that too."

She looked out the windshield at the diner. From here it was a lit window with shapes in it again. Dot was moving behind the counter. Someone was sitting in the back booth already, a man in a Mariners cap with his back to the room, and the window gave him back to the room like a mirror, and the man didn't look at it once.

"Tell me about Charlie," she said.

Edward's hands shifted on the wheel.

"Charlie thinks he's a federal agent," he said. "He's thought so since the second day. He doesn't know what kind. He thinks he might be here about us. About Bella, about what happened to Bella, about Carlisle's hospital records, something. He's frightened of that. And part of him—" Edward stopped. "Part of him hopes it. Part of him would like somebody official to come and tell him what's going on in his own family, so that he wouldn't have to decide every morning not to ask."

Leah didn't say anything.

"And he likes him," Edward said. "He doesn't want to. It makes him angry. He likes him the way he liked your father. He sat there thinking that, this morning, when the man told the story about the swan. *Harry'd have liked that.* Your father, I mean. He thought it and then he hated himself for thinking it about this man."

"Did he mean to invite him fishing?"

"No. He heard himself say it. He was as surprised as you were." Edward looked at the diner. "He's still surprised. He's on the Sol Duc bridge right now directing traffic around a log truck, and half his mind's on the truck and half of it's on Saturday, and he's wondering whether he should call Billy and cancel and he's already decided he won't."

"He's working Charlie," Leah said.

"Yes."

"For what?"

"I don't know." Edward said it slowly, like it cost him something. "I couldn't hear the part of the room that knows."

"He said he needs him in one piece."

"I heard."

"Like a—" She didn't have the word. "Like you'd say about a tool. Like you'd say about your truck."

"Yes."

"Whatever it's for," Leah said, "it's for him. Charlie's the bait."

Edward didn't argue. That was what frightened her, more than anything. In three weeks of living in Edward Cullen's head whether she wanted to or not, she'd learned he'd argue with anything, given half a chance, about Bella, about the baby, about Jake, about the weather. He didn't argue with this.

"Billy's going to be in that boat," she said.

"Jacob will tell him."

"And say what? *Don't go fishing with Charlie?* Billy's been fishing with Charlie for twenty years."

"Billy knows more than Charlie does," Edward said. "Billy will go. I think he'll want to look at the man himself."

"And the man'll look at him."

"Yes."

She thought about that. She thought about Billy in his wheelchair at the end of the dock at La Push with a coffee can of bait in his lap, Billy who had told the stories by the fire every year since before she could remember, Billy who knew every one of the old things. And the man sitting next to him in a boat with a line in the water, looking at him pleasantly, waiting for him to hand something over.

"You said Jasper's name," Edward said.

She'd been waiting for it. "I know."

"He had it already. He'll have all our names. He knew about Carlisle."

"He didn't know Jasper can fight."

Edward was quiet.

"No," he said finally. "He knows now."

"I didn't—it just—"

"I know. I heard. He made you angry and then he gave you something to correct. People can't help correcting a mistake." He turned his head and looked at her. "I'd have done the same, Leah. Anyone would."

"You wouldn't have."

"I'd have thought it," he said. "He'd have known from my face."

She didn't have anything to say to that.

"Jasper will want to hear what he said," Edward added. "About fighting. Jasper's been saying the same thing for a hundred years. That we're all strength and nothing else. That none of us ever learns. Nobody listens to him."

"Well, now somebody's said it in a diner."

"Yes." He almost smiled. Then it went away. "Leah. His hand."

She went still. "You said you wouldn't."

"I said I wouldn't talk about what you thought about him," Edward said. "That's a fact about him, and Carlisle needs it." He waited. When she didn't answer, he said, "How hot?"

She looked down at her own hand, the right one, the one he'd shaken. The skin was flushed and the bruise was coming up already, blue under the brown, and it was hot, the way it was always hot now.

"Like mine," she said.

Edward didn't say anything. He looked out the windshield at nothing.

"And he's got a heartbeat," she said. "I heard it. Sixty-something. Slow. Like he's asleep. And I said *you're human*, and he said—"

"*That's classified,*" Edward said quietly.

"Yeah."

"He's not one of you," Edward said. "Jacob would know. Sam would know. You'd smell it."

"I smelled something." She hadn't told anyone that. She'd told Seth in the woods, and Seth had smelled it too, and they hadn't said it out loud since. *Something big.* "Under the pie. Under the gun oil. Yesterday in the woods and today. Something—" She shook her head. "I don't know what. Something big."

Edward started the car.

He didn't say anything else for a while. He pulled out of the lot and turned north on Forks Avenue, the way Charlie had gone and the way the Chevelle had gone, and drove at the speed limit, which for Edward was a kind of shouting. They passed the diner. Leah didn't look at it.

"He didn't read you," Edward said, when they were out of town and the trees had closed in on both sides.

"No."

"He could have. The whole time."

"Yeah."

"He chose not to." Edward's hands were very still on the wheel. "I've never once been able to choose. Not with you. Not with anyone. Not with Bella, when she was human and I couldn't, and I'd have given anything to—" He stopped. "I'm sorry. That's not your problem."

"No," Leah said. "It isn't."

But she looked at him, sideways, for a second. At his white face in profile and his gold eyes on the road and his hands on the wheel, and for one second, not longer, she felt something for Edward Cullen that was a lot like sympathy, and she knew he'd heard it, and she knew he wouldn't say anything about it.

He didn't.

By the time they turned onto the long unmarked drive to the house, her wrist had stopped hurting. She kept holding it anyway, all the way up through the trees.

*Hands. Leverage. Joints. Angles.*

She'd be thinking about that for a long time.

**The Cullen house — 11:24 a.m.**

Jasper felt the girl before the car came out of the trees.

He'd been feeling her, faintly, since the Volvo turned off the highway: a hot, knotted mass of something coming up the drive at forty miles an hour, getting stronger. By the time the car stopped on the gravel he could pick the knot apart. Humiliation, first, the biggest strand, raw and red. Anger wrapped around it like wire. Under that, something gray and heavy that he'd felt in her every day for three weeks and had learned to recognize as grief. And under all of it, small and hard and very much against her will, something he hadn't felt in Leah Clearwater before at all.

Respect.

She hated it. He could feel her hating it from the porch.

Edward came in first and went straight to Carlisle, who had come home at eleven and was standing by the glass wall in his shirtsleeves. The girl came in behind him with her right hand held against her stomach in her left, and stopped just inside the door, the way she always stopped, as if the house were a cold river she had to make herself wade into.

Edward told it. He told it quickly, quietly, standing beside Carlisle, and the whole family stood or sat around the big room and listened, and Jasper listened too, and watched the girl while Edward talked. He watched her jaw when Edward got to *need to know*. He watched her not look at anyone when Edward got to the wrist. He watched her flinch, very slightly, when Edward got to *Jasper—*

Edward said, "And then she said your name."

"I gathered," said Jasper.

"I'm sorry," said Leah. It came out of her like a rock out of a sling. She wasn't looking at him. "I didn't mean to. He said none of you ever learned to fight. Any of you. And it just—"

"He already had my name," Jasper said.

"He didn't have the rest."

"No," Jasper agreed. "He didn't."

He found, a little to his own surprise, that he wasn't angry. He checked, the way he checked a room. He felt Emmett's amusement and Rosalie's cold, rising fury and Esme's worry and Alice's fierce concentration and Bella's fear, a newborn's fear, huge and simple, all of it pointed at her father. He felt Carlisle, steady as always, and under the steadiness, something that in anyone else Jasper would have called dread. He felt his own feelings last, the way he always did. They were very quiet. Mostly what he felt was interest.

"He'll ring when he's got something worth saying," said Rosalie. "That's what he said? To Carlisle? After Carlisle called him and left a polite message like a gentleman?"

"That's what he said," Edward said.

"Who does he think he is?"

"Someone with a vampire in a box," said Emmett, "and a trap across the whole county, and our phone number." He was sprawled on the couch with his feet on the coffee table, which Esme hated. "Honestly, Rose, if I were him, I wouldn't call us back either."

"He'll call," Carlisle said. He hadn't moved from the window. "He wants something. He wouldn't have left the card otherwise. He's letting us wait so we'll be readier to give it to him when he asks." He turned. "Leah. Edward says his hand—"

"Hot," Leah said. "Like mine."

"As hot as yours?"

"Yeah."

Carlisle was quiet for a moment. "Fever can do that. Some infections. Some poisons." He didn't sound like he believed it. "Or he isn't what he seems."

"He's not one of us," Jacob said. He was on the stairs, where Seth had sat yesterday, with Renesmee asleep on his chest and his big hand spread across her back. His voice was flat. "I'd know. Sam would know. There's no wolf that size anywhere on this coast we don't know about."

"I didn't say he was one of you."

"Leah smelled something," said Edward.

Everyone looked at Leah. She looked at the floor.

"Something big," she said. "That's all. I don't know what."

Nobody said anything. Jasper felt the room go tight, all of them, a single drawn breath. The baby stirred on Jacob's chest and made a small noise and he put his hand on her head without looking down, and she settled.

"Saturday," said Alice.

It was the first thing she'd said since the car arrived. She was sitting on the floor by the piano with her knees drawn up and her eyes half-closed, and she'd been sitting like that most of the night.

"What about Saturday?" said Jacob.

"I can see it now. Charlie decided. He's not going to cancel." Her eyes moved under the lids. "Six in the morning, at your father's house. Billy's on the dock in his chair with a coffee can. Charlie's backing the trailer down. They're putting the boat in. It's cold. There's mist on the water." She opened her eyes. "And there's a hole in the boat."

There was a pause.

"That," said Emmett, "is going to be a very short fishing trip."

"Emmett," said Esme.

"I'm just saying. Boats with holes in them—"

"Billy's going to go," Jacob said, over him. He was looking at the baby. "I already know he's going to go. I'll call him, and tell him all of it, and he'll listen to all of it, and he'll say *thank you, son*, and he'll go. He'll want to see him himself." He looked up. "He'll be fine. He's Billy."

Jasper felt the lie in it, the small, cold lie a son tells about his father, and didn't say anything. Neither did Edward.

---

Afterward, when the room had broken up into smaller rooms the way it did, Carlisle and Edward talking low by the window, Rosalie taking the baby upstairs, Alice back on the floor with her eyes closed, Jasper went to where Leah was standing by the door.

She was still holding her wrist. The pain had gone out of it; he could feel that. She was holding it the way people hold a letter.

"Show me," he said.

She looked at him. A spike of suspicion, then wariness, then a sort of tired defiance. "Show you what?"

"What he did."

"Why?"

"Because I've been trying to teach this family to fight for fifty years," Jasper said, "and in fifty years not one of them has ever listened to me for longer than an afternoon. And this morning a man in a diner said the same thing I've been saying, to a wolf, and the wolf came back with a bruise." He held out his right arm, palm up. "Please."

She looked at the arm for a long time. He felt her decide.

She took his wrist.

She did it slowly, frowning, working it out with her hands, the way you'd try to remember the steps of a dance you'd only seen once. Her grip was hot, almost uncomfortably so, like holding a cup too long. She turned his hand. She folded it down and in. She got it wrong the first time and he felt nothing, and she swore under her breath and started again, and the second time she got the angle, and his wrist bent back on itself and his elbow locked straight, and she pressed her thumb into the back of his hand between the bones.

He felt it.

It took a great deal to make Jasper feel pain, and this was something else. He felt the structure of it. He felt the line of force run from her thumb into the small bones of his wrist and up the long bones of his forearm and lock his elbow and stop in his shoulder, and he understood that the line didn't care how strong he was. It cared only where it was going. If he pulled against it, the line would carry his own strength straight back into his own joint. The harder he pulled, the faster it would break.

He stood there for a while, feeling it.

"Huh," said Emmett, from the couch. He'd sat up. "Do that to me."

"Not you," said Jasper. "Watch."

Leah let him go. He took Emmett's arm, when Emmett came over, grinning, and did it to him: the turn, the fold, the angle, the thumb. He did it right the first time. He'd only needed to feel it once.

Emmett's elbow locked. Emmett stopped grinning.

"Pull," said Jasper.

Emmett pulled, a little. Something in his wrist creaked, a small sharp sound like ice settling on a pond, and he went very still.

"Oh," said Emmett.

"Yes," said Jasper, and let go.

**Field station, Olympic National Forest — 2:14 p.m.**

He let that sit in the log a moment.

"Four. No dismemberment needed. No fire. No bonfire with purple smoke for half the valley to see. One curse, one fragment, done. Clean. Quiet. Fast. Conclusion: the Killing Curse is, by a distance, the most effective countermeasure against this species available to us. More effective than fire. More effective than anything. Recommend it as the primary for the Volterra operation." He paused. "Every officer on that plane will be carrying it anyway. They'll want to know it works. It works."

He stopped at the end of the table.

"Five. Open question." He was quiet for a moment, thinking how to put it. "Range."

The quill waited.

"It went three yards. I want to know if it goes three miles. Thirty. Three thousand. If the thread between the fragment and the body holds over distance, and the Curse runs along it the way it ran along three yards of air in here, then—" He stopped. He looked at the jars on the bench, the ash and the venom and the dull gray fingertip. "Then nobody needs to go to Tuscany at all. We need a piece of each of them. A chip off a cloak clasp, off a shoulder, off the back of a hand during a handshake. And a quiet room in London."

He let that sit in the log too, for longer.

"That wants testing," he said. "Properly. On an intact subject. Separate a fragment. Move it. A mile. Ten. Over water, if I can manage it. Apply the Curse at range and see what happens to the body." He looked at the empty cabinet, its glass door standing open, N.A. 01 engraved on the brass plate above it. "Which I can't do. Because I've used up my specimen. On a fingertip."

He rubbed his face with both hands.

"Requirement," he said. "Specimen North American zero-two. Adult. Human-diet. Intact." He thought. "Volunteer."

The quill wrote it.

It wrote it in its neat, slanted, impersonal hand, and then, on its own, without being asked, it went back and put a small neat quotation mark on either side.

*"Volunteer."*

Harry looked at it for a long moment.

He thought about telling it to take them off. Five years of his habits it had in it. It knew what he meant better than he did sometimes.

He let them stand.

---

He changed the brass plate on the cabinet before he went to bed. He didn't have another one, so he scratched it with the point of the Mason's Edge, small, under the number: *N.A. 01 (dec. 4.10.06)*. He put the body in the cabinet and sealed it, standing upright this time, with a binding to keep it that way, because there was nothing left in it for the snare to hold. Its gray eyes looked out through the glass at the white room. He put the two new jars on the shelf beside the old ones. Ash. Venom. A gray fingertip. A gray sliver of forearm.

Then he went down the corridor to the library and took the map out of the drawer.

It was a Forest Service map of the peninsula, folded soft as cloth from handling, with his forty-one nodes inked in on it in small red crosses, and the roads, and the rivers, and down in the bottom right corner, off the edge of the sheet where he'd taped a second sheet on, the long gray smudge of Puget Sound. Seattle. Three hours, give or take, in the Chevelle. Less if he didn't drive like a man trying not to get pulled over.

A woman who called herself Ro, who smoked Pall Malls, who'd be under the viaduct by the first of November where the runaways were and nobody counted.

He looked at the map for a while. Then he folded it up and put it back in the drawer.

It was Wednesday. Ro wouldn't be in Seattle for another three weeks. He had nodes to walk and readings to log and the dead one to write up properly for Croaker, who'd want it in triplicate. He had the doctor to ring, eventually, when he'd decided what he wanted from him. He had a policeman to go fishing with.

He went into the kitchen and put the kettle on. While it boiled he got a Rainier out of the icebox, and then put it back, and made tea instead, because it had been that kind of day. He drank it standing at the window looking out at nothing, because there was nothing out there, only the inside of a tent in the woods in the dark and the rain on the canvas he couldn't hear.

Then he went to the mirror in the library and unwrapped it and said, "Croaker," and when the gray folded face came up out of the glass, sleep-creased, at three in the morning in London, he said, "It works."

Croaker looked at him for a long moment.

"How well?" he said.

"Read the log, Saul," said Harry. "I'm going to bed. I've got to be up at five on Saturday. Billy Black doesn't wait."
