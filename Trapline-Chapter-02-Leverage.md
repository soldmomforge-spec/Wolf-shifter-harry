# Trapline

## Chapter Two: Leverage

---

### I. What He Is

*The Cullen house — Monday, November 20, 2006, 4:38 p.m.*

"He said he'd bring pie," Seth said, and nobody laughed.

Carlisle turned away from the scorched hemlock. His face had gone back to the calm one he wore at the hospital, which Layla had learned by now meant that underneath it he was not calm at all.

"The house," he said. "All of you. Please."

Edward was already gone, a pale smear between the trees and then nothing. Jacob wheeled and went after him, a red shape crashing downhill through the ferns. Leah stood a second longer, staring at the empty place on the ridge where the man in the suit had been. Then she went too, silver and low and fast, without looking back.

That left Layla, and Seth in his soaking cutoffs, and Carlisle, who was waiting for them with the patience of somebody who could have been home four minutes ago.

"Go ahead, Doctor," Layla said. "I've got him."

"You're sure?"

"I've been to your house four times. I know where your porch is." She held out her hand to Seth. "Grab on, baby. Don't let go. And don't throw up on me."

"Why would I throw up?"

"Everybody throws up the first time."

She'd never taken a passenger before. She'd read about it. She thought about the Cullens' front steps—the wide gray boards, the potted fern by the door that Esme brought inside when it frosted—and the third binding, loose, ten feet off, and she squeezed Seth's burning hand hard enough to hurt and turned on her heel.

The world went through a drinking straw.

They came out on the Cullens' front lawn, six feet short of the porch, and Seth folded over at the waist and put his hands on his knees and breathed like a man who'd been punched in the stomach.

"Okay," he said to the grass. "Okay. That's—okay."

"You didn't throw up."

"I'm thinking about it."

"Think about it out here." Layla straightened her coat. Her hands were still shaking. She stuck them in her pockets.

Esme came out onto the porch with an armful of towels. Alice couldn't see Seth, Layla had learned, but Edward had gotten here first, and in this house that was the same as a phone call.

"Inside," Esme said. "Both of you. Seth, you're soaked through. Layla, you're—" She looked at Layla's face and stopped, and her own face softened. "Come in, sweetheart. I'll make tea."

"I'm fine."

"You're not," Esme said kindly, "and that's all right. Come in."

---

The living room was full.

It had been full the first time she'd come, too, but that had been a different kind of full—everybody arranged and curious and polite, a family meeting a new girlfriend. This was the other kind. Jasper was standing at the glass wall with his back to the river, where he could see the room and the yard both, and his face had nothing on it at all. Emmett had stopped grinning. Rosalie was on the bottom step of the staircase with Renesmee on her hip, and the little girl was watching everybody's faces in turn with that crease between her brows. Alice sat on the arm of the sofa with her eyes half-shut, very still, the way she went when she was looking somewhere nobody else could.

Bella was standing in the middle of the rug. Edward was beside her with one hand on her back. He'd already told them; Layla could see it on all of them. Bella's red eyes went straight to Layla when she came in.

Jacob and Leah came in through the back a minute later, human, barefoot, in cutoffs, Leah in a black tank top with the straps twisted. Leah's hair was wet. She went and stood against the wall by the kitchen door with her arms folded, as far from the vampires as the room allowed, and didn't say anything.

Seth sat down on the floor by the fireplace with a towel over his head. Layla sat down next to him, because her legs wanted her to.

Esme put a mug of tea into her hands. It was hot and too sweet. Layla drank half of it in one go and burned her tongue and didn't care.

"Edward told us," Carlisle said. He stayed standing. "What he saw, and what he heard from Jacob and Leah. I'd like to hear it from you as well. But first—" He paused. "Layla, I'd like you to tell us what we don't know. Because I think it's a great deal."

Everyone looked at her.

She'd stood in this room two weeks ago and lifted Emmett off the sofa, and it had felt like a magic trick. This felt like being called to the front of the class.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Where do you want me to start?"

"He called himself an Unspeakable," Edward said. "You recognized the word. I heard it in your head the moment he said it. You were afraid."

"I'm still afraid."

"Then start there," said Rosalie. "What is it?"

Layla put the mug down on the hearth.

"There's a Ministry of Magic in Britain," she said. "Like MACUSA is ours. It's under London somewhere—nobody I know has ever been. Ten levels, they say. Down at the bottom, on the ninth, there's a department called the Department of Mysteries. The people who work there are called Unspeakables." She heard her own voice and tried to make it steadier. "Because they can't talk about what they do. That's the joke, anyway. The rumor is some of them really are bound so they *can't*. They try to tell you and nothing comes out."

"What do they do?" Jacob asked.

"Nobody knows."

"Somebody has to know."

"Somebody does. They just don't say." Layla rubbed her face. "Okay. Let me try it another way." She looked around the room. "Y'all know what the CIA is."

"Spies," said Emmett.

"Spies. People who go into other countries under fake names and fake jobs and find things out. Recruit people. Run operations nobody ever hears about." She held up one finger. "That's one piece." She held up a second. "DARPA. Anybody?"

"Defense research," Jasper said, without turning from the glass. "The Pentagon's laboratory. They built the beginnings of the Internet. Satellite navigation. They fund the things the military will need in thirty years."

"Right. The people who build the stuff nobody's heard of yet." A third finger. "And the NSA."

"They listen," said Rosalie. "Phones. Radios. Everything."

"They listen to everything," Layla agreed. "So take those three. Put them in one building. Run it with wizards. Put it in a basement under London with no sign on the door." She dropped her hand. "That's the Department of Mysteries. That's what everybody says it is, anyway. They spy, like the CIA. They study and build things the rest of us aren't allowed to touch, like DARPA. And they listen, like the NSA, with Legilimency in place of a phone tap."

"What do they study?" Carlisle asked.

"Whatever's most dangerous." She thought back to the rainy afternoon in Thibodeaux's classroom, the glasses coming off. "Our History of Magic teacher at Ilvermorny went to London once, for something official. He came back and told us what he'd heard. He said there's a room down there full of time—all lit up, sparkling, with clocks on every wall and a bell jar where a little bird hatches out of an egg and flies up and gets old and turns back into an egg, over and over, forever. He said there's a room with an archway in the middle of it, just an arch with a ragged curtain hanging in it, and if you stand close you can hear people whispering on the other side. Dead people." She swallowed. "He said there's a room with brains floating in tanks. A room of prophecies, shelves and shelves of them, glass balls with smoke in them. And one door that's always locked, that nobody opens, and the story is that whatever they're studying in there is the most powerful thing in the whole building."

"What's in it?" Seth asked from under his towel.

"Love," Layla said. "Supposedly."

Emmett snorted. Then he saw nobody else was laughing and stopped.

"Ten years ago," Layla said, "there was a fight down there. In the Department. A bunch of Dark wizards and a bunch of—kids. Fifteen-, sixteen-year-olds, from the school over there, Hogwarts. Nobody's ever really said why. It was in all the papers, even ours. They smashed up half the place. And the story is they broke every single Time-Turner the British had."

"Every what?" said Emmett.

"Time-Turner. It's like a little hourglass on a chain. You turn it, you go back an hour." Layla looked up and found every face in the room staring at her. "They're gone. All of them. Supposedly."

"Supposedly," Jasper said.

"Supposedly is the only word anybody's got for anything to do with that place."

Carlisle had a hand over his mouth. Edward's face hadn't changed, but Layla had spent enough time around him now to know that meant he was listening to every single person in the room think at once.

"And the people who work there," Carlisle said slowly. "The Unspeakables. What are they?"

"The best," Layla said simply. "That's the story. Most witches and wizards are good at one thing. Maybe two. Me, I'm good with plants and potions and I can throw a decent Stunner when I'm scared. My mama's a Healer. My cousin breaks curses on old houses in the Garden District. That's normal. You pick your thing." She shook her head. "The Department doesn't take you unless you're one of the best in the world at something and better than almost everybody at everything else. Charms. Transfiguration. Potions. Runes. Arithmancy—that's like math for spells. Wards. Healing. Dueling. Legilimency and Occlumency. Every single one of them's supposed to be a master of pretty much every field there is." She heard how it sounded and kept going anyway. "That's what Professor Thibodeaux said. He said they're scholars who can kill you. He said the first part's what they want you to remember and the second part's what you should."

Nobody said anything.

Then Emmett said, "Okay, but he swatted a thing. Edward said he swatted your spell. I could swat a spell."

"You'd be stunned, Emmett."

"Would I?"

Layla opened her mouth and closed it. "Actually, I don't know," she admitted. "I don't know what a Stunner does to y'all. Nobody does. Nobody's ever shot one at you." She frowned. "That's probably on his list."

"His list," said Rosalie.

"Of things to find out. On the poor bastard in the red jacket." Layla picked the mug back up just to have something in her hands. "But that's not—let me tell you about the swatting. Because I don't think you understand it, and I didn't either, really, until I stood there and watched it."

She tried to find a way in.

"A spell's a strange thing," she said. "You can't pick it up and put it in your pocket. When I throw a Stunner, what's coming out of the end of my wand is—me. What I mean. My intent, pushed through the wand, moving. It's got direction and it's got force, and it'll do one thing when it hits whatever it hits. That's it."

"Okay," said Jacob, frowning.

"So when somebody throws one at you, you've got three choices. You dodge. You block—there's a Shield Charm, *Protego*, makes a wall, the spell hits the wall and goes splat. Or if you're real good, you deflect. You hit it with your own spell at an angle and send it off somewhere else. That's hard. That's grown-up dueling. You need your wand and you need your timing and you need to have practiced it about ten thousand times."

"He used his fingers," said Seth.

"He used two fingers," Layla said. "No wand. Silent. He went—" she flicked two fingers at the fire "—like he was getting rid of a fly. And my spell turned ninety degrees in the air and went into a tree."

Edward said quietly, "And the second."

"And the second one he stopped." She heard her voice go thin. "Just *stopped*. He held it in the air in front of him like a bug in a jar, and he leaned in and looked at it, and told me what was wrong with it. And he was right. I *do* snap my wrist. Madam Okonkwo used to smack my hand with a ruler for it in seventh year."

"Who's Madam Okonkwo?" asked Bella.

"My dueling teacher. She fought for MACUSA in something they still won't tell students about. She was the scariest woman I ever met." Layla turned the mug in her hands. "She stopped a spell once. In front of the whole class, for a demonstration. She practiced it for a week. She had her wand. The spell was a little jinx, fired at half speed, by a kid who'd been told exactly when to fire it. She did it, and it held for maybe one second, and we all clapped like crazy, and then she sat down and asked for a glass of water and didn't get up for ten minutes."

She looked around the room.

"He did it with his bare hand. On a Stunner I fired at full strength with no warning. Without saying anything. While he was holding a vampire under his other arm. And then he stood there and *studied* it."

There was a long silence.

"Is that rare?" Bella asked quietly.

"I've never heard of anybody doing it," Layla said. "Not in a book. Not in a story. Not ever."

"You said 'without saying anything,'" Carlisle said. "And without a wand. Explain both of those to me, if you would. As if I'm a first-year medical student."

Layla almost smiled. "Okay. When you learn a spell, you learn the words. *Wingardium Leviosa*. *Stupefy*. A No-Maj could shout the words all day and nothing would happen. They're handles. Training wheels. They help you hold the shape of what you mean in your head while the magic goes through. Nonverbal magic is when you don't need the words anymore. You hold the whole shape of the spell in your mind, silent. It's hard. In Britain they don't even start teaching it till you're sixteen. Lot of grown witches never get good at it. I can do maybe five spells nonverbal on a good day, and two of them are for cleaning."

"And the wand?" said Carlisle.

"The wand's a focus. Like—" She looked for it. "Like a magnifying glass with sunlight. The sun's there either way. The glass takes it and puts it on one spot, so it can burn. Without the wand, it scatters." She held up her empty hand. "Kids do magic without wands all the time. That's how you know you're a witch—you're six and your cousin pushes you in the mud and his shoelaces tie themselves together. But it's wild. You don't control it. When you grow up and you get a wand, you mostly lose it. The few grown-ups who can do anything real without a wand—a light, a little push, open a lock—people talk about them like they're a party trick. I've seen one man light a candle without a wand. It took him a minute and he got a nosebleed."

She set the mug down.

"He did both. At the same time. Nonverbal and wandless. Twice. With one hand. And that's not even counting the vampire. Did you see him drop it? He flicked his fingers at a hanging vampire and let it fall, and that was a spell too. Same thing. No words. No wand."

"He caught it with one arm," said Leah from the wall. It was the first thing she'd said. Her voice was flat. "I know what they weigh. I've had one in my jaws. He took the whole weight on one arm and his shoulders moved about an inch."

"Was that magic?" Seth asked. "Like a—making-it-light spell?"

"I'd have felt it," Layla said. "I was right there. I didn't feel anything." She shook her head. "I don't know what that was."

She didn't say the rest of what she was thinking. Nobody needed her to.

"The stone," Edward said.

Layla turned.

"You were thinking about it on the ridge," he said. "After he left. Over and over. You said—you thought—*he read it like a menu*."

Seth pulled the towel off his head. He'd taken the stone off his wrist already; it was in his fist, and he held it out on his palm, gray-green, smooth, the hole through the middle. Layla took it. It was still warm from his hand.

"I built this in a day," she said. "One whole day. I sat at my kitchen table from six in the morning till midnight. There's six things on it. There's a Protean link so it knows me. There's a homing anchor—that's the part that turns it into a door, so when he bites it I get pulled through to wherever it is instead of having to figure out where he is and go myself. There's a strengthening charm so Seth can't bite it in half, because he would. There's a fit charm on the cord so it stays on whether he's a boy or a wolf. There's a trigger, so it only goes when it's Seth biting it and Seth thinking of me. Anybody else picks it up, it's just a rock. And there's a fail-safe, so if the anchor breaks it just goes dead instead of pulling me into a tree."

She turned the stone over in her fingers.

"He looked at this from eight feet away. In the rain. On a wolf's leg. Wet. For about four seconds. And he told me what three of those layers were, out loud, and he told me I'd built the anchor inside out, which I did, on purpose, and he told me the third binding was loose and I'd land ten feet off from where I meant." She looked up. "I landed ten feet to the left of Seth. I thought it was nerves."

"Is that rare?" said Bella again, softer.

"You'd have to know every one of those charms well enough to read 'em backward," Layla said. "Not cast them. *Read* them, sitting on top of each other, from across a clearing, by looking. It's like—" She found it. "It's like a mechanic standing across the street from your car while it's running and telling you which spark plug's bad. And being right."

Jacob let out a breath through his nose.

"And then," Layla said, "he went in my head."

Edward's eyes came up sharp.

"Legilimency," Layla said. "Y'all remember I told you. Reading minds. Some witches and wizards can. It's hard, and it's rude." She stopped. Her throat had gone tight. "It felt like somebody pressing a thumb on the backs of my eyes. From the inside. Like pages turning. I saw my mama's kitchen before the water came. I saw my school. I saw the Thriftway. I saw—" She glanced at Seth. "And I shoved him out. I don't know how. I've never learned. I just shoved, like you'd shove somebody who walked in on you in the bathroom, and he went. And he looked—a little surprised. Like a dog had talked." She shook her head. "And then he told me my name, and my age, and where I was from, and my school, and my *house* at my school. He got all that while I was pointing a wand at him."

"Is that how it works?" Edward asked. "Eye contact?"

"Every book I ever read says you need eye contact. You look somebody in the eye and you push in." Layla looked over at Leah and Seth. "He didn't look you two in the eye. You were wolves. He read you from twenty feet, while he was looking at the vampire. And he heard Jacob."

"From three miles off," Jacob said grimly. "I wasn't anywhere near him. He said hello to me."

"He went through them," Edward said slowly. He was looking at Seth. "Through Seth and Leah. Into the pack. All three of you share one mind when you're phased. He found the door into one of you and walked through into the whole house."

Seth had gone very still.

"He used you like a telephone," Layla said quietly.

"He was in my head," Seth said. He said it to the floor. "He was in there the whole time. He knows everything I know." He looked up, and his face was awful. "Everything. About everybody. About Nessie. About all of you. About Jake's dad, and Mom, and the treaty, and Charlie, and—" He stopped. "I think about you guys all the time. I think about *everything* all the time. That's the whole problem with me."

"Seth," said Esme gently.

"I'm the leak," Seth said. "I'm the hole in the boat."

"You're a kid who got jumped by somebody who does this for a living," Leah said from the wall. Her voice was hard. "Shut up about it."

"But he—"

"Shut *up*, Seth."

He shut up. Layla put her hand on his back. He was shaking, a little, and so hot she could feel it through his skin.

"And he's an Occlumens," Edward said. "That's what you called it. The opposite. Closing the mind."

"A good one," Layla said. "Good enough that you got nothing. You said it yourself. Not a whisper."

"Not a whisper," Edward repeated. "I have heard every mind I've come within two miles of for nearly ninety years, except Bella's. I've never in my life stood ten feet from a man and heard nothing at all." His voice was very even. "Bella is silent. That's different. Bella doesn't know she's doing it. He knew. He was holding it shut, the whole time, while he talked, while he caught your spells, while he read Jacob from three miles away. He never let it slip once."

"What about the trap?" Leah said from the wall. "The thing that hung him up. Was that him too?"

"That was a ward," Layla said. "Has to be. A ward's a spell you put on a place instead of on a person. Keep things out, keep things in, tell you when somebody's crossed a line." She shook her head. "My cousin Remy breaks curses on old houses in the Garden District. He says a good ward on one house takes three people a week to put up, and you've got to be careful about every door and every window and every chimney. And it's dumb. It keeps out whatever you told it to keep out, and that's all it knows."

"This one wasn't dumb," said Leah.

"No. It picked one vampire out of a whole forest. It let the two of you run straight through it at forty miles an hour. It let Edward and Carlisle walk right over it to get to us. It knew the difference." Layla looked around the room. "And he said there's lines like that across this whole peninsula. Does anybody know how big the peninsula is?"

"Thousands of square miles," said Jasper.

"I don't know anybody who could put a ward that clever on one *house*," Layla said. "He put one on a peninsula. In however long he's been here. And not one of us felt a thing."

Jasper turned around from the window for the first time.

"Tell them about the curses," he said.

Layla looked at him.

"In the clearing," Jasper said. His Southern voice was very quiet and very level, the voice of somebody who had commanded men once and had never quite stopped. "You thought it twice. Edward heard you. 'Three of them.' Tell us."

So she did.

"There are three curses," she said. "We call them the Unforgivables. They're the worst things a witch or wizard can do to another living person. Every country's got its own laws about them, but they're all the same law, really. You use one on a human, you go to prison forever. In Britain that's Azkaban—it's a fortress on a rock in the North Sea, and the guards used to be things that ate your happiness until you went mad. MACUSA used to just execute you."

"What are they?" said Rosalie.

"The Imperius Curse," Layla said. "*Imperio*. It takes over your mind. You do whatever the caster tells you. Walk off a roof. Kill your mother. Smile while you do it. Some people can fight it a little, if they're strong. Most people can't fight it at all." She took a breath. "The Cruciatus Curse. *Crucio*. That's pain. Just—pain. Every nerve you have, on fire, all at once, for as long as they want. People have gone insane from it. There's a couple in Britain, Aurors, who got tortured with it during their war till their minds broke. They're still in the hospital. They don't know their own son."

Bella's head turned toward Edward. Edward was looking at Carlisle, and Carlisle had gone the color of paper, which for Carlisle was saying something.

"Jane," Carlisle said.

"Jane," said Edward.

"Who's Jane?" said Seth, and then, "Oh. Oh, the—the one who looks at you."

"Yes," Carlisle said. "The one who looks at you."

"And the third?" said Rosalie.

"The Killing Curse," Layla said. "*Avada Kedavra*. Green light. No mark on you. You just stop. There's no counter-curse. There's no shield that'll stop it. You can dodge, or you can put something solid between you and it, and that's all." She paused. "One person in all of history ever survived it. That's in every history book in the world."

"And Unspeakables," Jasper said, "may use them."

"That's the story," Layla said. "Everybody's heard it. Nobody's ever proven it. The British gave their Aurors permission once—that's like their magic police—during the first war with the Dark wizard. They took it back afterwards. But the story's always been that the Department of Mysteries never gave it back. That Unspeakables have a license for all three." She looked at her hands. "Nobody's ever proven that, either. Nobody's ever proven anything about them. Nobody's ever heard of one being arrested."

"He said he was going to study him," Edward said. "The nomad. He said he wanted to learn what hurts him."

Nobody had to say the rest of it.

Carlisle sat down. Layla had never seen him sit down in this room. He sat on the edge of the piano bench with his hands loose between his knees and looked at the floor.

"He'll do it," Carlisle said. "To that man. Tonight. He'll use the—the pain one. To see if it works on us."

"Yes," said Jasper.

"And if it does," Carlisle said, "he'll have learned something about Jane."

"Yes."

"Which is what he wants." Carlisle closed his eyes. "Nine hundred and sixty-three. He said that number for me. He watched my face when he said it." He opened them. "They're going to war with Volterra. The British. Whatever this Department is. That's why he's here. That's what he meant by *my employers would like to know*. He's here to learn how to kill Aro."

"And he started with a nomad," Jasper said, "because a nomad's the only kind of vampire nobody comes looking for."

"Can he?" asked Emmett. He didn't sound like himself. "Kill Aro. Can a guy like that kill Aro?"

"I don't know," said Carlisle.

"Can he kill *us*?"

Jasper answered before anybody else could. "Today? Yes." He said it without any emphasis at all. "He caught a spell that could stop a horse and examined it while holding a man he'd just paralyzed from a hundred yards off with a trap we never knew was there. He walked through our wolves' minds to reach a third wolf three miles away. He blinded Edward. And he gave us a number to see what Carlisle would do with his face." Jasper looked around the room. "Everything he did on that ridge was a demonstration. He let Seth call her. He saw it coming. Leah saw him see it. He wanted to meet the witch, so he let the witch be called. He never put the nomad down, the entire time, because he wanted every one of us to see he didn't need two hands. He named the girl upstairs to Jacob because he wanted Jacob to know he knew." He paused. "He told us when he was coming and what he'd bring. That's a man who wants us to spend the night thinking about it."

Emmett said, very quietly, "Well. It's working."

"Yes," said Jasper. "It's meant to."

"Could he make us forget?" Esme asked.

Everyone looked at her. She was standing behind Carlisle with one hand on his shoulder, and her face was very calm in the way Carlisle's had been on the ridge.

"There's a charm," Layla said slowly. "The Memory Charm. *Obliviate.* That's what MACUSA does when a No-Maj sees something they shouldn't. There's people whose whole job is that—Obliviators. A good one can take an afternoon out of your head so clean you'll never miss it." She hesitated. "A bad one can take your whole name. There was a man in Britain who wrote books about fighting monsters—his books were in every shop, even ours—and it turned out he'd never fought anything. He'd found the people who did, and wiped them, and written it down as his. A Memory Charm went backwards on him once. He's been in a hospital in London ever since. He doesn't know who he is."

"And on us?" said Esme.

"I don't know. Nobody does. Maybe it'd slide off, like whatever he did to Edward. Maybe it wouldn't."

"Then why hasn't he?" Jasper said. "He had Edward and Carlisle on that ridge. And three wolves. And you. He could have tried it on every one of us and walked away with the nomad, and none of us would have known he was ever here."

Layla thought about it.

"Because he wants you to remember," she said.

"Yes," said Jasper.

Layla looked down at the stone in her hand.

Then she said the thing she'd been not-saying for an hour.

"He told us his name."

Leah's head came around.

Layla saw the flinch go through her again, the same jolt as on the ridge. *Harry.* Seth went still against her side. Layla took his hand.

"I know," she said, to both of them. "I'm sorry. I know. But listen." She looked around the room. "There's a Harry. An English Harry. Every witch and wizard in the world knows that name. Kids at Ilvermorny used to play him on the lawn."

"Play him?" said Bella.

"Like cops and robbers. Like cowboys." Layla took a breath. "Back in the seventies there was a Dark wizard in Britain. Voldemort. Over there they call him You-Know-Who, or the Dark Lord, so they won't have to say it. We say it, mostly, but even here people lower their voices. He killed—a lot of people. Hundreds. He had an army. He had giants. In 1981 he went to a little house in a village in England and killed a man and his wife, and then he turned his wand on their baby boy and used the Killing Curse. The one with no counter." She looked at Carlisle. "And it bounced. Nobody knows why. It hit the baby and came back and destroyed him instead. Gone. The baby lived. That's what they called him in every paper on earth. The Boy Who Lived. He got a scar on his forehead from it, shaped like a lightning bolt."

"The one person in history," Jasper said.

"Yes."

"Go on."

"The Dark wizard came back. When the boy was fourteen. It took a while for anybody to believe it. There was another war. In 1998 it ended, at the boy's school, in a battle. Hundreds of people. The boy—he was seventeen—he faced the Dark wizard in front of everybody, in the Great Hall, and the Dark wizard died." Layla's voice had gone quiet. "There were pictures in the *New York Ghost*. A skinny kid in round glasses with his face all over blood and dirt. Black hair. Green eyes. People said his eyes were his mother's. His name was Harry Potter."

The room was absolutely silent.

"It might not be him," Layla said. "The Harry in the pictures had glasses. And the scar. Everybody knew the scar. This man didn't have a scar. I looked. I couldn't stop looking. There wasn't anything there." She shook her head. "But he'd be twenty-six now. And English. And black hair and green eyes. And after the war he went to work for the Ministry, and the papers said he took some job nobody would name, and then he just—stopped being in the papers. Everybody figured he wanted to be left alone. After all that. Everybody figured he'd earned it."

"The papers said the job had no name," Edward said.

### II. Chits

*The tent, Bogachiel River — Tuesday, November 21, 2006, 3:40 a.m.*

He got back at twenty to four with sand in his turn-ups and an empty bag.

Twenty-six stones, from Shi Shi down to Kalaloch. He'd walked about half of it and Apparated the rest, setting a slab of slate every quarter mile, pressing each one down into the wet sand with his heel until the beach took it, then standing still in the dark while the new line came up out of the ground and knit itself to the old ones at the river mouths. He'd gone round La Push. That stretch of sand was Quileute, and the wolves could look after their own beach. At Rialto the sea stacks had stood up out of the surf like broken teeth, and the noise of the Pacific had been enough to make him want a drink.

He went to the specimen room.

Mattias was where he'd left him, bound from his heels to his chin under the white light, red eyes open. They followed Harry from the door to the bench. They followed him everywhere. They didn't close. Harry was going to have to get used to that.

He'd done the work in two sittings, one before the beaches and one now, and he wrote it up while it was fresh.

The butcher in Port Angeles had asked on Saturday what he wanted with two gallons of pig's blood. Harry had told him black pudding. The butcher had said, "That's a lot of pudding," and Harry had said he had a big family. Three drops of Veritaserum in a pint of it, warmed, and Mattias had drunk it under the Imperius like a man drinking water in a desert. Three minutes later the potion had taken. Slower than in a human. It held for an hour and forty minutes. He'd asked the same questions under Veritaserum that he'd asked under the curse and got the same answers, word for word, which told him the Imperius hadn't been dressing anything up.

*Veritaserum: takes, via blood. Onset approx. 3 min. Duration approx. 100 min.*

*Implication: any potion that can be dissolved in blood can be given to them. They cannot smell it.* He sat back and thought about that, then wrote underneath: *Check last line against a sober subject before relying on it.*

The Pensieve had been the one he wanted most. He'd put the tip of his wand to the vampire's temple and drawn, slowly, and the thread had come out silver like any memory, but it had hung off the end of the wand heavier than it should have, and when he'd let it go into the basin it had gone straight to the bottom like mercury and lain there, and the surface had taken a long time to settle.

He'd gone in.

He had been inside a great many memories. Dumbledore's, at fourteen and sixteen, in a round office full of silver instruments. Snape's, on the dusty floor of a shack outside Hogsmeade, with the man's blood all over his hands. A hundred since, in the Department, from witnesses and prisoners and the dead. Human memory was a watercolour. The edges ran. Things in the corners weren't there at all, because the person hadn't looked at them.

Mattias's memory of a spring morning in Brno in 1871 had no edges. Harry had stood in a cobbled street that smelled of horses and coal smoke and read the headline on a newspaper pinned to a stall forty feet away, in Czech, and the price of bread chalked on a board in a baker's window, and counted the buttons on a passing soldier's coat. All of it there. All of it sharp. He'd come out of the basin and sat on the floor of the specimen room for a minute before he got up.

*Pensieve: extraction possible. Memory heavier than human; sinks. Fidelity total. No degradation at 135 years.*

*A vampire is the best archive I have ever opened.*

He underlined that one too.

The skin had been next. A Severing Charm had skated off the vampire's forearm and left a thin white line like a scratch on a countertop, which faded in a minute. Then he'd used the other one. The Half-Blood Prince had invented it at fifteen, for enemies, and written it in the back of a Potions book, and Harry had used it once on a boy in a bathroom at sixteen and spent a long time afterwards wishing he hadn't. *Sectumsempra* had opened the forearm from wrist to elbow like a stone splitting along a seam. No blood. Clear venom welled up in the cut, thick as honey, and the edges drew together, and in forty seconds there was nothing left but a faint silver line. Mattias had screamed.

*Severing Charm: ineffective. Sectumsempra: effective. Wound closes, approx. 40 sec. Subject reports severe pain.*

*Tremor following pain: recurs. Subject did not know they could shake.*

Fire, he hadn't tried. He only had the one.

The rest had been talk. He'd asked about the Volturi until the vampire's answers started repeating, then asked again in different words.

Demetri, the tracker, Mattias had heard about the way children heard about wolves. *He finds anyone. Anywhere. Once he has the taste of your mind, you can run to the ends of the earth.* Harry had sat with that one for a long time, and then written: *"Taste of the mind." Query: does Occlumency hide it?*

And the law. The secret, first. Then—Mattias had said it under Veritaserum, flatly, the way he'd said everything—children.

*No children. You don't make children. A child can't be taught. A child can't be stopped. A child will kill a village in an afternoon and cry about it after. If you make a child, they burn the child. Then they burn you. There was a woman in the north. Alaska. They burned her and the boy she made. They let her daughters live. Everybody talks about the daughters.*

Harry had asked him what a child was, exactly. A human child, bitten. Frozen at whatever age the venom took them. Never growing. Never learning. That was the law.

Then he'd sat at the bench and thought about a small heartbeat he'd heard yesterday from three miles away, through Jacob Black's head, and a little girl with bronze curls carried across a lawn.

*She grows,* he wrote. *She has a pulse. She is not what the law describes.*

And underneath that: *The law will not care.*

He slept for two hours on the sofa in the sitting room with his shoes on.

### III. Romeo

*The Cullen house — Tuesday, November 21, 2006, 11:51 a.m.*

The house was loud.

It always was, for Edward. Twelve minds under one roof, and every one of them thinking at once, and every one of them thinking, this morning, about the same man.

Carlisle, by the piano: steady, clinical, and under the steadiness a long low note of dread that hadn't stopped since yesterday afternoon. *Nobody attacks him. Nobody. Whatever he says.* Esme, in the kitchen doorway, where she'd been for an hour: *plates, forks, napkins, he said pie, you put out plates for a guest, that's what you do, that's all I can do*. She had set the long dining table that nobody ever ate at with eight plates and eight forks and a stack of cloth napkins, and Edward loved her so much for it that it hurt.

Emmett, on the sofa, cracking his knuckles one at a time: *if he does the spell thing, I go left, Jasper goes right*. Jasper, at the glass wall, running the same three angles of approach over and over with the patience of a man who had done this before battles for a century: *door, stairs, Bella*. Rosalie, upstairs, with Renesmee asleep against her shoulder: *one step toward these stairs. One.* Alice, cross-legged on the piano with her eyes half shut, flinching every few seconds: *heads—no—tails, twelve-oh-two—no—*

The wolves were human, all three of them, which made them quieter. Jacob, on the floor by the loveseat where he could put himself between the stairs and the room: *Sam better stay home. Sam better stay home.* Leah, against the kitchen wall, arms folded: *Thursday. Mom's been looking forward to Thursday for a month.* Seth, on the loveseat, very still: *Think like a wolf. Think like a wolf. Don't think about anything he can use.* It was like watching someone try not to think about a polar bear. Edward had heard Leah use that phrase once and had liked it.

And the witch, sitting beside Seth with her hand in his. Layla's mind was a fast, clever, frightened river, and this morning it was running through the text of the International Statute of Wizarding Secrecy, clause by clause, looking for a door. *Section Thirteen. Beings. Non-human magical—*

Bella's mind was silent. Bella's mind was always silent. She stood beside him in the middle of the room with her hand in his, and Edward could feel the stillness in her that meant she was holding something down with both hands.

"Alice," Carlisle said.

"He's flipping it again." Alice's eyes were squeezed shut. "Tails. Tails. He's listening to a record. He's—"

Her eyes opened.

"Now," she said.

The air cracked.

He was standing in the middle of the living room, between the sofa and the piano, six feet from Bella.

Edward had braced for it. He'd spent a sleepless night bracing for it. It made no difference. Every other person in the room was a voice, and where Harry stood there was nothing at all. Even Charlie Swan's mind came through muffled. This was a man-shaped hole in the sound of the house, as if someone had cut him out of the air with scissors.

He was wearing the same charcoal suit, the same white shirt and narrow black tie, the long black overcoat. Two white cardboard boxes were stacked in his hands, tied with string. His hair was dry. His shoes were clean. He looked around the room once, slowly—the glass wall, the river, the piano, the stairs, the faces—and his green eyes stopped on each of them for about as long as it took to read a name on a door.

"Afternoon," he said. "Brought pie."

Nobody moved.

Then he saw the dining table, and Esme's eight plates, and something in his face shifted very slightly.

"Oh," he said. "You've laid a table." He looked at Esme in the kitchen doorway. "That's kind. Esme, is it?"

Esme's mind went very small and very surprised. "Yes."

"Thank you, Esme." He crossed to the table, set the boxes down, untied the string on the top one, and took off his overcoat and hung it neatly on the back of a chair. Marionberry. The smell of it filled the room—hot sugar and dark fruit and butter—and Edward watched every vampire in the house fail to have any opinion about it at all. Harry cut a slice with one of Esme's forks and put it on one of Esme's plates and stood there and ate a bite.

"That's very good," he said, to nobody. Then he looked up and around the room again, fork in hand.

"Doc," he said to Carlisle.

He looked at Edward.

"Romeo."

Edward heard it land everywhere at once. Emmett, on the sofa, before he could stop himself: *ha*. Jacob: *Romeo. Volterra. He knows about the square.* Rosalie, upstairs, very cold: *of course he knows about the square*. And from Carlisle, a small private wince of something like grief.

"Mystic Meg," Harry said, to Alice.

Alice blinked. "Who?"

"Astrologer. Did the horoscopes in the *Sun*. Big hair. She used to do the lottery on the telly." He took another bite. "Never mind. Major," he said to Jasper. "Grizzly," to Emmett, who grinned before he could help it and then stopped. "Swan," to Bella. "Lassie. Balto. Clifford." He nodded to each of the wolves in turn. "Miss Broussard." His eyes went to the ceiling. "And Blondie's upstairs with the little one. Heartbeat like a sparrow's. That's fine. She can stay up there."

Edward went cold.

The house had two stories of solid floor and plaster between the living room and the room where Rosalie sat with his daughter asleep on her shoulder. A human could not hear a heartbeat through that. A human could not hear a heartbeat across a room. Edward heard Jasper reach the same place a half-second after he did—*he hears like us*—and Leah, against the kitchen wall, think something sharp and wordless and wolfish that came out in his head as the shape of a raised hackle.

At the glass wall, Jasper had begun to push.

Edward felt it the way he always did, the room's temperature going down a degree, the edges of everyone's fear rounding off. Slow. Easy. Calm. Jasper had been doing it to rooms full of frightened people for a century, and no one had ever once noticed.

Harry, without looking up from his plate, said, "Major. Whatever you're doing, stop. It itches."

Jasper stopped.

Edward heard the shock go through Jasper's mind like a dropped plate. *He felt it. Nobody feels it.*

"You flipped a coin," Alice said from the top of the piano.

Harry looked at her. "I did."

"All morning. Eleven times. I counted."

"You counted the ones you saw." He took another bite of pie. "Your gift's very good, Meg. I've read reports on Seers that'd make your hair stand up, and none of them come close to what Balto thinks you can do." He chewed, swallowed. "It's also a gift that only sees choices. Anybody who knows that can stop choosing."

Alice's mind went sharp and furious and, under the fury, very slightly afraid. *He's right. James knew. Victoria knew. Anybody who knows can—*

"That's cheating," Alice said.

"That's tradecraft."

"Right." Harry put the fork down and wiped his fingers on one of Esme's napkins. "Miss Broussard. Sit down properly, would you? We'll start with you."

Layla was already sitting. She sat up straighter. Edward heard her mind seize on the Statute and hold on to it like a railing.

"Nine of you in this house," Harry said, "counting the little one. Three wolves in this room. Sam Uley, as of about ten o'clock last night, when Clifford told him on the beach at La Push." Jacob's head jerked up. "And Sam Uley's whole pack, as of the first time Sam phased afterward, which, knowing Sam, was about ten minutes later. Call it eight. Then Billy Black, Sue Clearwater and Quil Ateara the elder, because Clifford's dad heard it from Clifford over breakfast and rang the other two." He tilted his head. "Twenty-four. Twenty-four beings told about the International Statute of Wizarding Secrecy in sixteen days." He looked at Layla. "That's a record, Miss Broussard. There are people in Azkaban who did less."

He was reading them.

Edward understood it all at once, and the understanding went through him like cold water. Harry hadn't touched Jacob or looked him in the eye. He'd walked in and cut a slice of pie and pulled what he needed out of the surface of Jacob's mind from across the room, the way Edward himself did every hour of every day—and he'd done it while making them watch him eat.

"They're not No-Majs," Layla said. Her voice was steady. Her mind wasn't. "None of them. They're beings. You said so yourself, on the ridge."

"Granted," said Harry. "Not one of them." He picked up the fork again. "So who's Charlie Swan, then?"

The room's minds slammed together like cars.

Carlisle: *oh no*. Jacob: *Charlie—the yard—*. Leah: *Mom*. Seth: *Thursday*. Esme: *Bella*. And Layla, loudest of all, her whole fast river of a mind stopping dead against a wall she hadn't seen coming: *I didn't think—I never even thought about Charlie—*

Bella's hand in his had gone to stone.

"Chief of police," Harry said. "Forks, Washington. Nice man. Takes his coffee black, sits at the end of the counter at the diner on Forks Avenue, asks after the waitress's sister." He ate a bite of pie. "In September, Clifford took his shirt off in the man's front garden and turned into a dog the size of a horse in front of him. To *help*." He glanced at Jacob. "Charlie's head is a brick wall, I'll give him that. Yours isn't, mate. I watched you do it this morning in about four seconds."

Jacob's mind was white noise.

"Since then," Harry went on, "the chief's daughter has come home from her honeymoon with red eyes she covers with brown contacts at Sunday dinner. She doesn't eat. She doesn't sleep. She's very cold and very strong and she's got a little girl who's three months older every time he sees her, who he's been told is a niece of somebody's, and who he's decided not to ask about." He put the fork down. "And on Thursday, he's eating Thanksgiving dinner in La Push at Sue Clearwater's, across the table from a witch who's making gumbo."

Layla had gone gray.

"Under MACUSA's reading of the Statute," said Harry, "Charlie Swan is a No-Maj with direct knowledge of magical beings in his jurisdiction. He's a breach with a badge."

He reached into the inside pocket of his suit jacket and took out a long envelope of heavy parchment, sealed with plain black wax, and laid it on the coffee table between the sofa and the loveseat, facing them, so that the address was right side up.

*To the Director, Department of Magical Law Enforcement, Magical Congress of the United States of America.*

"I wrote that at six this morning," Harry said. "I don't even need to wipe him myself. I don't need to lift a wand. I put that on an owl. It goes to New York. They send a team." He said it without any heat at all, as if he were describing the weather. "American Obliviators are very thorough. They're not gentle. They'll start with Clifford in the front garden and work backward until there's nothing left in his head that shouldn't be there. If they're tidy, he'll still know he has a daughter. He won't know why she stopped visiting. He won't know why he likes Sue Clearwater's cooking." He looked at Leah. "Your mum's seeing him, Lassie. Picture Thursday." He looked back at Bella. "And he won't know he's a grandfather."

Bella moved.

Edward was touching her, his hand in hers, and he still couldn't stop it. He felt her go—every muscle in that newborn body, stronger than his, stronger than Emmett's, uncoiling at once—and his fingers closed on air where her hand had been. She was over the coffee table. She was across the rug. She was a foot from Harry's throat with her lips peeled back and her hands up, and Edward was moving behind her and knew he would be too late—

And she stopped.

In the air. Mid-lunge. A foot off the floor and a foot from Harry's chest, her hair still swinging forward over her shoulders, her hands still reaching. As if she'd driven straight into a wall of glass that had turned to honey around her. Her red eyes were wide. Nothing else on her moved.

Harry hadn't moved either. He was still standing at the table with his hand resting beside his plate. He hadn't raised it. Edward hadn't seen him look away from Bella for an instant.

The room had come apart around them. Emmett was half off the sofa. Jasper had taken one step off the glass. Jacob was on his feet with a growl coming up out of his chest that rattled the windows. Edward was two strides from Bella's back with his hands out.

"Everybody stays where they are," Harry said.

He didn't raise his voice. Everybody stayed.

He was looking at Bella. She was a foot away from him, held in the air, and he looked her straight in her red eyes, close, and Edward saw his face change—saw something in it go sharp and focused and *reach*—

And stop.

Harry blinked.

"Oh," he said softly. "Now, that's interesting."

He lowered her. Slowly. Edward watched his wife's bare feet come down to the rug as gently as if someone had set her there by hand, and then she could move, and she staggered a half-step backward and Edward caught her by the arms and pulled her back against him. She was shaking. She was shaking so hard he could feel it in his own bones.

"Sit down, Swan," Harry said. "I said I could. Whether I do is up to this room."

Bella didn't sit. Edward held on to her. Upstairs, he heard Rosalie get to her feet with Renesmee in her arms, and heard her mind—*if he touches her, if he touches either of them*—and heard her make herself stay where she was.

"You've got a temper," Harry said to Bella. "Good. Hang on to it. You'll need it." He studied her for a moment longer. "Who else has tried to get into your head?"

Bella said nothing.

Harry's eyes moved to Edward's face.

It was the strangest thing Edward had ever felt: to be looked at, and to have no idea at all what the person looking was thinking. To be *read*, by eyes alone. Harry looked at him for perhaps two seconds, the way he'd looked at everyone when he arrived, and Edward realized a half-second too late what he was looking at.

"Ah," Harry said. "The little blonde. In Volterra." He turned back to Bella. "She had a go at you, didn't she, Swan? She smiled at you and she tried, and nothing happened at all."

"How—" Bella started.

"Romeo's face." Harry nodded at Edward without looking at him. "Near a century of never once having to guess what anybody's thinking, and never once having to hide what he's thinking from anybody who could see it. He hasn't got a poker face. He's an open book with the cover torn off." He picked the fork back up. "That's a weakness, mate. You'll want to work on it."

Edward said nothing. He could think of nothing to say. Bella's back was rigid against his chest.

"Last night," Harry said, to Bella, more quietly, "I wrote a question in a notebook. You might be the answer to it." He ate the last bite of his pie. "We'll come back to that. Not today."

"You don't want MACUSA here," Layla said.

Everyone turned.

She was sitting forward on the loveseat with her hands knotted together in her lap. Her face was still gray, but her voice was even, and in her head, under the fear, Edward heard the fast river find a channel and run hard down it. *He doesn't. He can't. If MACUSA comes, they find him too. They find the man in the white room and the line on the beaches. They find a British operation on American soil that they never signed off on.*

"You don't want them here any more than we do," Layla said. "If they come for Charlie, they find your lines. They find you. That letter's a bluff."

Harry looked at her for a moment. Edward couldn't read anything in it. He thought—he wasn't sure—that it was approval.

"No," Harry agreed. "I don't want them here." He set the fork down on the empty plate. "But I can be gone in an afternoon, Miss Broussard. Pack the tent, pull my stones, walk away. Nobody in New York will ever know I came." He let that sit. "Can you?"

Layla didn't answer.

"Can he?" Harry said, and nodded at the letter on the table.

Layla's channel ran into the sand and stopped.

Carlisle spoke for the first time. His voice was very quiet and very controlled, and his mind behind it was anything but. "What do you want?"

"I'm getting there, Doc."

"The man you took yesterday." Carlisle's eyes went to Harry's face and stayed there. "Is he alive?"

Harry considered the question as though it were a reasonable one.

"He's on a steel table under a light," he said. "Whether that counts as alive is more your department than mine."

Carlisle closed his eyes.

"I'll tell you what I've learned, if you like," Harry said. "Since you're the doctor."

Edward heard Carlisle's mind split clean down the middle. Half of him was horrified. The other half—the half that had spent three and a half centuries cutting open the mysteries of his own body in the dark, alone, because there had been no one else to ask—leaned forward like a starving man at a window.

"Yes," Carlisle said. "Tell me."

"The Imperius Curse takes," Harry said. "Slowly. It's like pushing your hand into wet concrete for about four seconds, and then it gives all at once, and he'll do whatever he's told. The Cruciatus takes too." He paused, just long enough. "I asked him afterward if it felt familiar. He said it felt exactly like your Jane."

Carlisle's mind showed Edward a girl's face, small and fair and smiling, in a stone room, three hundred years ago. And a man on the floor in front of her, screaming.

"Veritaserum," Harry went on. "Truth potion. Three drops in a pint of warm pig's blood. He drank it down and three minutes later he'd have told me his mother's maiden name. He couldn't smell it." He glanced at Emmett. "You might want to think about where your supper's been."

"We hunt," said Emmett. His mind was a red flare. "We don't drink out of *cups*."

"Hard to poison an elk on the hoof," Harry agreed. "Not impossible." He turned back to Carlisle. "The Severing Charm skates off your skin. Leaves a white line, like a scratch on a worktop, gone in a minute. There's another curse—a nasty one, invented by a teenager with a grudge, for cutting people open—and that went through his arm like it was paper. No blood. Just venom, clear, thick as honey, and the wound closed in forty seconds and left a silver line." His eyes went, very briefly, to the faint pale crescents on the back of Jasper's hand and neck, and away again. "Like those."

Jasper went utterly still.

"And afterwards," Harry said, "after the pain, he shook. Fine little tremors, all over, for about a minute. Did you know you could shake?"

"No," Carlisle whispered.

"Neither did he."

Carlisle had a hand pressed flat over his mouth. Edward could hear, under the horror, three hundred and forty years of questions lining up in his father's mind to be asked, and Carlisle, with an effort Edward could feel like heat, refusing to ask a single one.

"And the Pensieve," Harry said. "That's a basin. You draw a memory out of somebody's head and put it in, and you can walk around inside it. I've walked through a lot of heads." He was quiet a moment. "Yours are the best I've ever seen. His memory of a spring morning in 1871 is sharper than mine of yesterday. I read a newspaper headline across a street in Brno that he never even looked at properly. Everything you've ever seen is still in there, perfect, from the day you were made." He looked at Carlisle. "Which brings me round to you, Doc. And to Romeo."

Edward knew what was coming before Harry said it. He didn't need to read anyone. He'd been inside Aro's head for twenty minutes last spring, in the long stone room under the clock tower, with Aro's papery hand on his.

"And then," said Harry, "he told me about the children."

The room went silent in a way that had nothing to do with sound.

"No children," Harry said. "That's the rule. Second only to the secret. You don't make a child, because a child can't be taught and can't be stopped. If you make one, they burn it, and they burn you. There was a woman in Alaska, he says. Everybody talks about her daughters."

Edward heard Carlisle's mind go back across a hundred years, to the Denali sisters at his table and the thing none of them ever said aloud. He heard Esme's mind fly upstairs, to the room where Rosalie stood. He heard Jacob's mind go white and silent and then come back up red, every hair on his body standing up, his hands curling on the rug. Upstairs, Rosalie had stepped back from the door so hard her shoulders hit the wall.

Harry looked at the ceiling.

"Relax, Blondie," he said, not loudly. Edward heard her hear it. "I don't study children."

He looked back down at the room.

"She grows," he said. "She's got a heartbeat. She's not what the law means. That's an easy argument to make to a reasonable man." He picked up his overcoat from the back of the chair and folded it over his arm. "It's a harder one to make to a man who decides things by shaking hands, from across a field, with his guard lined up behind him and his mind made up before his plane left Florence."

Nobody said anything.

"When Volterra hears about her," Harry said, "and they will—nothing like her stays quiet for long—you're going to want friends. Friends who aren't afraid of them."

Jacob came up off the floor.

He didn't phase. Edward heard him decide not to, by about the width of a hair. He stood between the stairs and the man in the suit with his whole body shaking and his fists clenched and his voice gone down into his chest.

"You stay away from her," Jacob said.

"Sit down, Clifford."

"I'm not *sitting*—"

"Then stand. But listen." Harry didn't move toward him. He didn't move at all. "Let me tell you what I see when I look at your lot."

Edward heard every wolf in the room stop breathing.

"Yesterday I walked into one of you," Harry said, "and got all three. Balto was thinking about his girlfriend, so I went in through her and came out the other side with Lassie and you, three miles off. Your whole pack's one head with a lot of legs. Find the door into any of you and you're in all of you." He nodded toward Seth. "I learned more about this family from that lad in the time it took him to bite a rock than your Volturi learned from Romeo's hand in twenty minutes. Every name in this room. Where you live. Where the treaty line runs. Your patrol loops, which you run at the same times every night, by the way—I watched you twice from a hill. How you tie your trousers to your legs."

Seth had gone the color of chalk. Edward heard him think *Leah said don't*, and then think it again, harder, and hold on.

"Now say I'd been somebody else," Harry said. "Say I'd been the Volturi's tracker. Demetri. The one who finds anybody, anywhere, once he's got the taste of their mind. And say I'd walked into Balto's head yesterday instead of me." He let that sit. "You'd all be dead by Christmas."

Jacob's jaw worked.

"And then," said Harry, "there's the imprinting."

Leah's head came up off the wall.

"Clifford's tied to a ten-week-old girl the Volturi would burn on sight. Balto's tied to a witch who's been breaking the law since the day she got here. Sam Uley's tied to a woman in La Push. Quil Ateara's tied to a two-year-old." Harry's voice didn't change. "Anybody who wants a wolf can skip the fighting. Skip finding you, catching you, getting past those teeth. All he has to do is pick up whoever's on the other end of the string and give it a tug, and the wolf comes running. Every single time."

He looked from Jacob to Seth.

"Tying your soul to one person," he said, "is brave. I mean that. It's also the most monumentally stupid thing I've ever heard of. Every one of you is walking round with a handle sewn onto your back."

Seth's hand had found Layla's and was holding it so hard her knuckles had gone white. Jacob's mind was a roar, and under the roar, quieter, something worse: *he's right. He's right. Oh God, he's right.*

Harry's gaze moved past them, to the kitchen wall.

"Except you, Lassie," he said.

Leah stared at him.

He held her eyes a moment longer than Edward expected. Then he looked away, back at the room, and Leah's mind—for once in Edward's experience—had no words in it at all.

"And you lot aren't much better," Harry said, to the vampires. "You're all paired off. Find one of you, you've found the other. Romeo walked into the main square of Volterra at noon on a feast day with his shirt off to die in front of a crowd, because somebody told him Swan was dead." He looked at Edward, and Edward felt it like a hand on his face. "That's in a file in London, mate. With a photograph."

Edward said nothing. Bella's hand found his and gripped it.

Carlisle lowered his hand from his mouth. When he spoke, his voice was hoarse.

"What do you *want*?"

"Three things," said Harry.

He held up one finger.

"Doc. You lived in Volterra."

Carlisle's mind flinched. "Yes."

"How long?"

"Some decades. In the eighteenth century. I left in—" He stopped. "A long time ago."

"Long enough to learn the building. The three of them. Who's who. What they're like when nobody's looking. How they choose who to keep." Harry didn't look away from him. "I want all of it. Everything you remember. In the basin."

He turned to Edward.

"And Romeo. You were inside Aro's head last spring. Twenty minutes, near enough. I want that."

Edward felt Bella go rigid.

Harry held up a second finger. "Swan. When you're ready. Not today."

A third. "Clifford. When your lot smells a red-eye on the Olympic Peninsula, you ring me before you kill it. What's on your side of the treaty line is yours. You do as you like. What's on mine is mine."

He lowered his hand.

"In return," he said. "That letter stays in my pocket. MACUSA never hears the word Forks. The Department keeps every one of you on the list of people nobody's allowed to touch, which is a short list and a very good one to be on." He paused. "And when Volterra comes for that little girl—I'll be there."

Edward heard the room's minds turn it over, every one of them at once, and for one dizzying moment he could hear his whole family weigh the same offer on twelve different scales.

Esme: *he means it. He means it. Someone who isn't afraid of them.* Emmett: *I want to hit him so bad.* Jasper, cold and quick, a soldier counting the cost: *It's a good offer. It's a very good offer. That's what frightens me.* Jacob: *Nessie. Nessie. If they come for Nessie—* Rosalie, upstairs, rigid against the wall: *he's using her. He's using her to buy us.* Alice, on the piano, with her eyes shut and a crease between her brows: *it's all fog, it's all fog, I can't see past the wolves—*

And Carlisle. Carlisle's mind was a man standing in a stone hall three hundred years ago, with Aro's hand warm—it had seemed warm, then—on his shoulder, and Aro's voice saying *my friend*.

"You're asking me," Carlisle said slowly, "to help you kill people I once called my friends."

"Nine hundred and sixty-three, Doc," said Harry.

Carlisle closed his eyes.

Esme crossed the room and took his hand.

Edward looked at his father's face. He looked at Bella's. He thought about Aro's thin, dry fingers closing over his own in the turret room last spring, and the long cold rush of Aro's mind opening under them—three thousand years of it, every face Aro had ever coveted, every gift he'd ever collected, every death—and about Aro's eyes when they'd turned toward Bella. He thought about the next time those eyes would turn toward her. And toward his daughter.

"Take mine," Edward said.

Bella's head snapped around. "Edward—"

"Take mine," he said again, to Harry. "What I saw in Aro's mind. All of it. I'll give it to you. Leave Bella out of it."

Harry looked at him for a long moment. Edward had no idea what he was thinking. He would never, he realized, have any idea what this man was thinking.

"That's a start," Harry said. "For now."

"Edward, *no*—"

"For now," Edward said to her, quietly. "Love. For now."

He sat down on the piano bench. Harry crossed the room. He drew his wand out of the sheath along his forearm—dark wood, eleven inches or so, plain—and Emmett's mind went off like a gunshot and Jasper's hand closed hard on Emmett's shoulder.

"This'll feel strange," Harry said. "Think about it. The whole thing. From when he took your hand. Don't try to give it to me. Just remember it."

Edward remembered.

The wand's tip touched his temple, cold and light.

It was like a thread being drawn out through the center of his head. He felt it go—a long, cool, silvery pull that went on and on, unspooling, and as it went he saw it all again, sharp as cut glass: the turret room, the long dark hall, the light falling in bars across the stone, Aro's milky eyes and his papery hand and the rush of three thousand years pouring into Edward through that hand like floodwater through a broken dam. Marcus's boredom. Caius's hate. Jane's smile. Every face. Every gift. Every death. On and on.

Then it was over.

He opened his eyes. Harry was standing over him with his wand held up, and from the tip of it hung a long, shining thread of silver, thick as a pencil lead, swaying, heavy. Harry drew a small crystal vial from a case in his pocket, uncorked it with his thumb, and lowered the thread into it, and it went straight to the bottom and lay there like mercury. He corked it. He looked at it in the light from the window.

"You've still got it," he said. "That's a copy. It doesn't take anything away."

Edward put his fingers to his temple. The memory was still there. Every bar of light. Every face. All of it still his.

It felt like a theft anyway.

Harry tucked the vial into the case and the case into his pocket. Then he went back to the coffee table, picked up the sealed letter, looked at the address for a second, and slid it back into his inside breast pocket beside the badge.

"That stays with me," he said. "Insurance." He looked at Carlisle. "Doc?"

"I need to think," said Carlisle.

"Think fast."

"I'll think as fast as I can," said Carlisle, "and not one second faster."

Something moved at the corner of Harry's mouth. It might have been respect. Edward didn't know. He would never know.

"Fair," said Harry. "Clifford?"

Jacob's hands were still fists. His mind was still a roar. Under the roar, Edward heard him do what Leah had told Seth to do the night before—stop thinking about how he felt, and start thinking like a wolf.

"Your side, yours," Jacob said finally, through his teeth. "Our side, ours. You come over the line, we tear you up."

"Fair," said Harry again. "Swan?"

Bella looked at him for a long moment with her red eyes. Her mind was silent. Her face wasn't.

"Go to hell," she said.

"Noted." He nodded, as though she'd given him an entirely reasonable answer. Then he turned to the loveseat.

"Miss Broussard," he said. "Walk with me."

### IV. Tuesday

*The riverbank below the Cullen house — 12:34 p.m.*

He walked her out through the glass door, across the deck, and down the wet lawn to the river, and he didn't say anything the whole way.

Layla walked beside him because she couldn't think of anything else to do. Her legs felt like they belonged to somebody else. Behind her she could feel the house—the big glass wall, the faces behind it, every one of them able to hear a whisper across a football field. Seth was standing at the glass. She didn't have to look. She could feel him there the way you feel the sun on the back of your neck.

At the edge of the river the grass gave out into round gray stones and the water went by fast and loud and green-brown, full of the week's rain. Harry stopped there. He drew his wand, glanced back up the lawn at the house, and flicked it once toward the glass, the first time she'd seen him use the wand for anything at all.

She heard it: a faint, high, irritable buzzing, like a wasp caught in a jar, somewhere behind her ears.

"*Muffliato*," Harry said. "Fills the ears of anybody close with noise, so they can't hear you." He studied the house a moment longer. "An old teacher of mine made it up. I've never tried it on a vampire. We'll ask Romeo after whether it worked."

"He'll just read it out of my head when I go back in."

"Course he will. Ten seconds after you're through the door." Harry put the wand away. "It's the principle of the thing."

Layla folded her arms against the cold. "What do you want?"

"You keep asking me that. All of you." He looked at the river. "It's very American."

"You keep not answering."

"I answered Doc. Three things." He turned to look at her. "You're the fourth."

She waited.

"Lacewing flies," Harry said. "Twenty-one days stewing in a cauldron in your second bedroom, under a cooling charm that's not quite right, so the room smells like burnt sugar when you open the door. Leeches. Knotgrass, which you can't get up here, so you had it sent from a woman in Metairie. And fluxweed, picked at the full moon—that's what you were cutting in the clearing on the North Fork when Balto stepped on a stick." He tilted his head. "That's Polyjuice Potion, Miss Broussard. MACUSA licenses Polyjuice."

Layla's mouth went dry.

"You went in my head," she said. "In there. Just now."

"You went into it yourself, the second I said the word *Azkaban*. Loudly." He shrugged. "I just listened."

"It's for an apothecary in New Orleans. They sell it to Aurors. For undercover work. It's licensed."

"Is it."

She held his eyes for about three seconds.

"Mostly licensed," she said.

"Mostly," said Harry.

The river went by. Layla thought about the stone in her pocket, and the third binding, and the burnt roux, and Charlie Swan at the end of a counter asking after a waitress's sister. She thought about Professor Thibodeaux taking off his glasses.

"So," she said. "That's the stick. Polyjuice. And the Statute. And the letter. And Charlie." Her voice was steadier than she was. "What's the carrot?"

"Who says there's a carrot?"

"You gave the Cullens one. You gave them that little girl." Layla's jaw set. "*When Volterra comes, I'll be there.* That was a carrot the size of a house, and you held it out right after you threatened to have her granddaddy's head scrubbed. I watched you do it." She took a breath. "So what's mine?"

He looked at her for a while.

"I need somebody here," he said. "Somebody who knows what a ward is and what a werewolf isn't. Who can sit at that table in there and tell me what Doc's thinking before he's finished thinking it, and tell me when Grizzly's about to do something stupid, and tell me when the little one starts doing something new. Who can walk into La Push and get a cup of coffee in Sue Clearwater's kitchen without anybody blinking." He paused. "Who can keep Balto's head a bit quieter, if she's lucky."

"You want a spy."

"I want an asset." He said it as if it were an ordinary word. "Off the books. You'd be working for the Department, and nobody'd know but me and the man I report to, and he doesn't know your name yet."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no."

"And you make your own arrangements."

He didn't answer. He didn't need to.

Layla looked down at the river stones. One of them, near her boot, had a hole worn through it, small, the width of a pencil. She almost laughed.

"Okay," she said. "Then here's what I want."

Harry's eyebrows went up a fraction.

"That letter," Layla said. "I want it burned."

"No."

"I want it—"

"It stays in my pocket," Harry said. "That's what it's for. A letter you've burned isn't worth anything to anybody."

"Then it stays sealed," Layla said, "for as long as I'm working for you. And if I ever stop—if I quit, or you fire me—you give me notice before it goes anywhere. A week."

"Two days."

"Five."

"Three," said Harry.

"Three," Layla agreed. Her heart was pounding. "And Seth. You don't go in his head again."

"Can't promise that."

"*Harry*—"

"He thinks at the top of his voice, love. If I'm standing next to him, I'll hear him. I can't help that any more than Romeo can." He held up a hand before she could start again. "I won't go digging. If he's not shouting it, I won't go looking for it. That's the best you'll get."

She thought about it. "Fine. And I want to get paid."

"The Department pays in Galleons. Badly."

"I've got rent. In *dollars*. And my truck needs a new alternator."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "I'll see what I can do."

"And you teach me."

That one he hadn't expected. She saw it. It was the smallest thing—a stillness, half a second—but she'd been watching his face for twenty-four hours now, the way you watch a dog you're not sure about, and she saw it.

"Teach you what?"

"Whatever you did to my stone." Layla's voice came out rough. "Whatever you did to my spell. Reading a charm off a rock by looking at it. Stopping a Stunner in the air." She swallowed. "I sat at my table last night for twenty minutes looking for that binding. You saw it in four seconds. I want to know how."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Did you fix it?" he said.

"Last night."

"How's it feel?"

"Straight," Layla said. "Like a picture finally hanging straight on the wall. I could feel it settle. I've never been able to feel it before."

Harry nodded slowly, as if she'd told him something.

"If you're any good," he said, "I'll teach you."

"I'm good."

"We'll see."

"The man in the red jacket," Layla said. "The vampire. What happens to him?"

"When I'm finished with him, he goes in the fire."

"When's finished?"

"When he's told me everything he knows," said Harry, "and I've learned everything he is."

"Does it hurt him?" She made herself ask it. "What you're doing to him?"

"Yes."

He said it the way he'd said *I did* to Alice about the coin, and *no* about burning the letter. Layla stared at him.

"There was a name written in the collar of that jacket," Harry said. "Black marker, inside the neck. R. Reyes. Rachel Reyes. She was twenty-eight. She and her husband had been married eleven months, and they drove up from Olympia on Friday with a new tent to see the lake, and he ate them both in their sleeping bags on Saturday night and took her jacket because it was raining." He looked at the river. "I'll lose sleep over a lot of things, Miss Broussard. He's not on the list."

Layla didn't say anything for a while.

The river went by. Up at the house, the buzzing in her ears went on and on. Layla made herself look at him properly, the way she'd been trying not to all morning.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask."

"Are you Harry Potter?"

He held her eyes. He looked, if anything, a little tired.

"Yeah," he said.

She'd known. She'd known since last night. It still went through her like a cold wind.

"You killed—"

"Yes."

"When you were seventeen."

"Yes." He looked at the river. "Don't make it a thing."

"It's a thing," said Layla. "It's the biggest thing that happened to anybody my whole life. Every kid at Ilvermorny played you on the lawn. We used to fight about who got to be you. I got to be you once in third year because I broke Marcus Delacroix's nose."

That got him. She watched it: a short huff of breath through his nose that might have been a laugh if he'd let it.

"Did I win?" he said.

"Every time."

"Lucky kids," said Harry.

Layla didn't know what to say to that. She looked at his forehead, where the scar should have been, in all those pictures, in all those games on the lawn. There was nothing there. Just skin.

"You don't have the scar."

"Not anymore."

He didn't explain. She didn't ask. Something in the way he said it put a wall up across the rest of the question, and she'd felt his walls already, from the inside.

He reached into his jacket pocket and held something out to her. A coin. Gold, heavy, a Galleon, with numbers running around the edge.

"Yours," he said. "Protean Charm. Same idea as your rock. If you need me, you don't bite it. You hold it and say my name, and it'll get hot in my pocket and tell me where you are."

She took it. It was hot from his hand.

"You said the girl who invented this was fifteen."

"She was."

"Who was she?"

"The cleverest witch of her age," said Harry, "and she'd tell you so." He nodded at the coin. "Don't lose it. And don't let Balto chew it."

Layla turned the Galleon over in her fingers. The river went by, loud and cold. Up on the hill, behind the glass, everyone she knew in this whole wet corner of the world was watching her stand on a riverbank with Harry Potter and take his money.

"This is extortion," she said.

"This is Tuesday," said Harry.

He turned and started back up the lawn. Halfway there he stopped, flicked the wand over his shoulder without looking, and the buzzing in her ears cut off like someone had closed a door.

---

They were all waiting when she came in through the glass, and they all looked at her, and Seth crossed the room so fast she didn't see him do it and put his arms around her, and she let him.

"I heard bees," Edward said slowly. He was looking at Harry, in the doorway behind her. "A whole hive of them. I couldn't hear a word."

"Good to know," said Harry.

"Then I heard her come up the lawn." Edward's eyes moved to Layla's face. She felt them land and stay, and she knew what he was doing, and she let him. She let him have it. All of it. She didn't think she could have kept it from him if she'd tried. "Ah," Edward said quietly.

"Yeah," said Layla, into Seth's shoulder.

"What?" said Emmett. "Ah *what*?"

"She works for him now," said Edward.

The room took that in.

Seth's arms tightened around her. She felt his heart going under her cheek, hot and fast.

"Is that true?" he said into her hair.

"Yeah, baby."

"Did he make you?"

Layla thought about it honestly, the way Seth always thought about things, because he'd asked her honestly.

"I made a deal," she said. "He's got three days' notice on that letter now. Instead of none." She lifted her head and looked around the room—at Carlisle by the piano and Esme with her hand in his, at Jasper's cold face and Emmett's angry one, at Bella with her red eyes and her arms folded tight across her chest, at Jacob on the floor by the stairs. "And I'll be in the room. Every time. Whatever he wants from y'all. I'll be the one in the room who knows what he's doing."

Nobody said anything.

Then Jasper, at the glass wall, said, "That's smart."

Layla looked at him.

"It's what I'd have done," Jasper said. "If I'd been you." His eyes went past her, to the man in the doorway. "And he knew it. He knew you'd make it a deal. He wanted you to."

"Course I did," said Harry. He'd picked his overcoat up off the chair and was shrugging it on. "She's no use to me if she's scared. She's a lot of use to me if she thinks she's winning." He buttoned the coat. "And she did. Three days is a lot, Doc. Ask anybody who's ever had none."

He looked around the room one more time—the same slow look as when he'd arrived, the glass, the stairs, the faces.

"Keep the pie," he said. "The apple's not been touched. Clifford looks like he could use it."

Jacob said a word Layla's grandmother would have washed his mouth out for.

"Noted," said Harry, and went out through the glass door onto the deck, and down the steps, and across the lawn toward the trees, walking, the way a man walks when he wants to be watched going.

Leah came off the kitchen wall.

Without a word to anybody, she went out the glass door after him.

### V. A Door

*The tree line behind the Cullen house — 12:52 p.m.*

He heard her coming. Of course he did. He stopped at the edge of the trees, where the lawn ran out into salal and the first big hemlocks started, and stood with his back to her and his hands in the pockets of the overcoat, and waited.

Leah stopped ten feet behind him.

The rain had come back, thin and cold. It ran off the air above his head in a curve, the way it had on the ridge, and none of it touched him. Up close, in the wet, she could smell him properly for the first time without a vampire under his arm to drown it out. Rain on stone. Pine pitch. Cold river rock, the kind you turn over in a creek and find dark underneath. And under all of it something else, faint, that her wolf didn't have a word for and didn't like not having one.

"Lassie," he said, without turning around.

"Don't."

He turned around then. He looked at her for a moment.

"Leah, then," he said.

It threw her worse than the nickname. She hadn't expected it. She stood there in the rain in her cutoffs and her tank top with her bare feet in the wet grass and her arms crossed, and for a second she couldn't remember what she'd come out here to say.

Then she did.

"What did you mean," she said. "In there."

"Which bit?"

"You know which bit."

He nodded slowly.

"Everybody in that house has a string on them," he said. "Doc's got Esme. Romeo's got Swan. Swan's got the little one, and the little one's got Clifford, and Clifford would walk into a fire for her and thank you for the matches. Balto's got his witch. Your mum's got Charlie Swan." He paused. "You haven't got anybody."

Leah's jaw locked.

"Thanks," she said. "Thanks for that. That's great."

"It's a compliment."

"It's—"

"Nobody can pick up the other end of you and pull," Harry said. "There isn't another end. Anybody who wants to move Leah Clearwater has to go through Leah Clearwater, and that's a lot of teeth." He looked at her steadily. "That makes you the most dangerous wolf in La Push. By a mile."

"Because nobody wants me."

"Because nobody's got a handle on you. Different thing entirely."

She didn't have anything to say to that. She didn't like that she didn't.

"I was in your head yesterday," he said. "Through Balto's. I went in after him and you were there, the whole of you, all at once."

Every muscle in Leah's back went tight.

"Get out," she said.

"I'm out. I'm not in there now." He said it plainly. "I'm telling you what I saw."

"I don't want to hear what you saw."

"Sam Uley in your mum's kitchen, not looking at you," Harry said. "Your cousin's name in his mouth. Your dad on the floor." He held her eyes. "And the rest of them watching all of it, every time you phase. Every night. For two years. Every time you so much as think about any of it, the whole lot of them get it, fresh, and you get to feel them get it."

Leah's eyes stung. She hated that. She hated him. She stood there in the rain and hated him so hard her hands shook.

"You'd give anything," Harry said, "for one hour where nobody could hear you think."

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. He'd been inside her head; he knew the answer before he asked.

"Occlumency," he said.

"What?"

"What I do to Romeo. What I did to every one of you in there." He tapped one finger against his temple, once. "Closing the mind. It's a discipline. You don't need a wand for it. You don't need to be a witch. You need a mind and you need patience and you need to practise every single day until you want to scream." Something crossed his face, gone almost before she saw it. "It took me two years, and I was rubbish to start with. A man spent a whole winter trying to teach it to me and I couldn't keep him out of my head for ten seconds."

"So what," said Leah. "So you're good at it now."

"So I don't know if it works on a wolf," Harry said. "Nobody's ever tried. Nobody knew there were wolves to try it on." He looked at her. "If it works—if you can learn it—you'd have a door. In there. You could shut it. On them. On Sam."

The rain came down.

Leah thought about a door.

She thought about a room in her head with a door in it, and a lock, and nobody on the other side. She thought about being able to think about her father without Seth flinching three miles away. About being able to think about Sam—about the way he'd looked at Emily the first time, the way he'd never once looked at her—without Jacob going quiet and kind and careful in that way that made her want to bite through her own leg. About one hour. One single hour of her own head.

It hurt so much to want it that for a second she couldn't breathe.

"So I'd be your lab rat," she said.

"You'd be my student." Harry shrugged. "Some days that's the same thing."

"Why?"

He considered the question as if he'd been expecting it.

"Because it's useful to me," he said. "A wolf who can shut her head is a wolf nobody can walk through to get at the rest. That's worth a lot to the people I work for, if it works." He paused. "And because I want to know if it works. I always want to know if it works. That's most of the job."

"That's it?"

"And because you stood in front of your brother yesterday," Harry said, "when you thought I was going to hurt him. With a vampire under my arm and your friend's spell still fizzing in the air. You put yourself in front of him and you growled at me." He looked at her a moment longer. "Nobody'd told you to. Nobody pulled a string. You just did it."

Leah didn't say anything.

"I used your mum in there," Harry said. "And Charlie. I'll probably do it again." He said it the same way he'd said everything else, plainly, without any apology in it at all. "That's the job too."

"I know," said Leah. "I was there."

"Yeah. You were."

They stood there. The river was loud behind the house. Somewhere up the hill a car door slammed—Layla's truck, maybe, or Rosalie's. The rain kept on.

"Bogachiel," Harry said. "Past the state park, about a mile. There's a lot on the river belongs to a man named Dale Pruett. Green tent, under the hemlocks. You'll smell it." He turned toward the trees. "Any night but Thursday. I hear Thursday's busy."

"Go to hell," said Leah.

"It's on the list."

He stepped into the trees. Then he stopped, the way he had on the ridge, and looked back at her over his shoulder.

"And you don't have to call me Harry," he said. "If it sticks. Potter does fine."

Leah stared at him.

He'd been in her head. Of course he knew. He'd seen her dad's face, and her dad's name hanging over it, and he'd seen what it did to her when he'd said it on the ridge.

She hadn't expected him to care.

"Potter," she said slowly.

"That's the one."

He turned on his heel. There was a crack like a branch breaking, and the trees were empty, and the rain came down through the place he'd been and hit the salal like it had been waiting.

---

Leah stood at the edge of the trees for a long time.

Behind her the house was full of voices. She could hear them from here. Emmett, loud and furious. Carlisle, quiet. Jacob on the phone, saying *Sam, Sam, listen to me*. Seth asking Layla something, and Layla answering, and Seth laughing, shaky, the way he did when he was scared and trying not to be. Esme saying *Who wants pie?* in a voice like she might cry.

She didn't go back in.

She walked into the trees instead, a long way, until the house was gone and there was nothing but the rain and the moss and the big old hemlocks, and then she stripped off her cutoffs and her tank top and tied them to her ankle with the cord, and phased.

Jacob was there. He'd phased the second he got off the phone with Sam, to run the line. Seth was there a minute later.

*Leah,* said Jacob. *Where'd you go? What did he—*

And then they had it. All of it. The tree line, the rain, the smell of pine and stone, *Leah, then*, *nobody's got a handle on you*, *a door*.

*Leah,* Seth said, very quietly. *Are you going to go?*

She didn't answer him. She didn't have to. They could hear her not answering, and they could hear what was underneath it, and there was nothing she could do about that.

That was the whole point.

She ran. North, up the drainage, toward the treaty line, her paws throwing up wet needles, her brother and her alpha in her head going quiet and careful and kind, the way they always did, the way she hated.

She thought about a door.

For the first time in two years, Leah Clearwater wanted something the pack couldn't have, and the pack knew it, and she let them.
