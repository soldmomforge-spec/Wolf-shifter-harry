# Chapter Three: Fortunate Son

She looked up once.

At the edge of the cottonwood flat, where the river swung around the last bend and the big tree stood by itself with its three gray leaders going up into the dawn, she slowed to a walk. She told herself she was checking the wind. She lifted her head.

The branch was empty. The four long cuts in the bark had darkened at the edges overnight, and sap had beaded up along them, gold as honey in the first light. Frost furred every leaf on the ground. The only thing on the limb was a gray jay, which looked down at her with its head cocked and said something that sounded like an opinion.

*Shut up,* she told it, and kept going.

The sun came up behind her somewhere around the second gravel bar, the frost on the stones smoking where the light touched it. Her legs were heavy and good-tired, her belly still full of caribou, and every stride she felt the little leather pouch tap against her ankle where it rode snug against the bundle of her clothes. Every time she noticed it she told herself to stop noticing it.

She checked her head, out of habit. Reached around inside it the way she'd done in the pumpkin field, the way she'd done on Main Street.

Nobody. Her own breathing. The river.

Halfway down the valley she realized she'd been checking for something that wasn't the pack, and stopped checking altogether.

---

The deadfall behind the lake road was white with frost when she came in under the spruce, and the town below it was still dark except for the gas station sign and one light in the back of the Galena where Dot would be firing the flat-top.

She phased behind the fallen trunk.

She knelt in the needles with steam rising off her bare shoulders into the still air and waited for her hands to stop shaking. Then she went to work on the cord.

The three hard knots came loose the way they always did, stiff and wet. The pouch's drawstring was another matter. The second her fingers touched the knot it simply let go, the string sliding out of the cord as if it had been waiting for her and had better things to do. She looked at it with deep suspicion.

She got dressed first. The flannel, the jeans, the hoodie, the boots, the blue toque with the white pompom. Her hair was full of lichen. There was something tacky at the corner of her mouth.

Then she opened the pouch.

The mirror inside was the size of a playing card. It had square corners and a frame of dark old silver, and the silver had been worn smooth along the edges, the way the handle of her dad's filleting knife had gone smooth where his thumb rode it for twenty years. The glass was faintly smoky. Like somebody had breathed on it once and the breath had never quite cleared.

She looked into it, and her own face looked back.

Dark eyes with shadows under them. Black hair in a snarl that was going to take twenty minutes with the brush. A crust of dried caribou blood at the left corner of her mouth, brown and flaking. Lichen. A scratch on her cheekbone that was already closing.

"Great," Leah said to herself, and scrubbed at the blood with the cuff of her hoodie.

She held the mirror a while longer. Her breath fogged it and cleared.

*If you want me, say my name into it.*

Her mouth started to shape the word on its own. It got as far as the H. Then it stopped there, because the H was her father's, and the last time she'd said it out loud and meant someone by it she'd been standing on a mountain with Brynn's weight against her side telling a stranger that her dad was Harry, and that he was a *was.*

She put the mirror back in the pouch and the pouch in the front pocket of her jeans, and pulled the drawstring tight, and went down the hill.

---

Wes was outside the motel office in his slippers and a bathrobe over his jeans, holding a mug of coffee and looking at the lake.

He watched her come down out of the trees at twenty to six in the morning in Sunday's clothes, with lichen in her hair and a scratch on her face. He looked at her for what felt like a long time. Then he lifted his mug an inch, and went back inside, and shut the door.

That was the whole conversation. Leah was starting to like Wes a great deal.

---

At five past six Dot looked up from the flat-top, where she was laying out bacon in rows as straight as a cemetery, and took Leah in from the boots up.

"You look like something the cat dragged in," Dot said. "And the cat's embarrassed about it."

"Thanks."

"Stand still."

Leah stood still. Dot wiped her hands on her apron, reached up, and plucked something out of Leah's hair just above her ear. She held it up between two fingers. It was a curl of pale green-gray lichen the size of a thumbnail, the kind that only grew above the trees.

Dot looked at it. Then she looked at Leah over her glasses.

"Up top?" she said.

"Walking."

"Twenty miles up the valley and back. On a Sunday. Walking."

"I like walking."

"Uh-huh." Dot flicked the lichen into the trash. "Wash my pans."

Leah washed her pans.

---

Brynn came in at seven, pink-cheeked and back to full volume, and talked through the whole breakfast rush about a cousin in Fort St. John who was marrying a snowmobile salesman nobody in the family liked. Brynn talked the way a radio played. You didn't have to stay in the room.

At a quarter past ten the three old men in feed caps settled into their corner table and resumed the argument they'd been having since before Leah got to town, about whether the Canucks could make the playoffs without Bertuzzi, and whether Roberto Luongo was worth the money.

At twenty past ten, the bell over the door jingled, and Leah smelled Belgium.

She knew it before she knew she knew it. Wet beech leaves and old stone and that dark sweet beer. Fry grease. Under all of that, very faint, very dry: earth after rain, and the struck match.

Her hands stopped in the water.

The smell was much too faint for him to be in the room. It was the ghost of him, the way a jacket smells of the person who's been wearing it. And over the top of it, sharp and loud, there was somebody else entirely: kitchen sweat, shallots, white wine, fear, and a very expensive hand cream.

She went to the hatch.

The man standing just inside the door was the skinniest adult Leah had ever seen in person. Mid-thirties, maybe, with a long narrow face and a long narrow neck and black hair cropped close, wearing a white chef's jacket under an unzipped parka that was much too big for him, like he'd bought it from a catalogue in a panic. He had his arms wrapped around a wooden crate the size of a microwave. The crate had black letters stenciled on the side in French. He stood in the doorway of the Galena with the cold blowing in around his ankles, looking at the room the way a man looks at the last obstacle between himself and freedom.

The old men stopped talking about Luongo.

"Help you?" Dot said from the grill.

"Hi. Yes. Hi." The skinny man's voice was high and fast and very tired. "I'm looking for — he said you'd know. He said the tall one who washes dishes and will probably throw it at me." He looked around the café, and then he saw Leah's face in the hatch, and something in his own face sagged with relief. "Oh, thank God. You. It's you, right? He said I'd know."

Leah came out of the kitchen drying her hands on her apron.

The man thrust the crate at her. She took it. It was heavier than it looked. He let go of it with both arms at once and then stood there flexing his fingers, as if he'd been holding it since Brussels.

"I'm Theo," he said. "Theo Pak. I'm the — I cook. At the lodge. I was cooking at the lodge." He took a breath. "I've been here nine days. I have made eleven hundred eggs. I have been woken up at three in the morning to make a full English breakfast for a man who'd just come back from God knows where smelling like a *campfire,* and he ate all of it, and then he asked if there was any more. There is never any more." His voice wobbled. "And at six o'clock this morning a man named Hargreaves called me on the satellite phone from London and said that when I deliver this crate to the tall one at the café, my contract is complete, and the plane leaves at eleven, and I can go home." He looked at the crate in her arms. "So. Here. It's yours. Please don't throw it."

Leah looked at him. She looked at the crate.

"I'm not going to throw it," she said.

Theo Pak closed his eyes briefly, like a man hearing a pardon read out.

"There's a card," he said.

---

There was a card.

It was tucked under the lid of the crate, which was held down with little brass catches. When Leah thumbed the catches open the smell came up out of the box all at once and the whole café went quiet. Chocolate, dark and bitter and deep. Toasted hazelnuts. Butter. Something like cream and something like coffee and something like oranges, and under it the clean smell of new pine from the crate itself.

The crate was packed with boxes. Gold card boxes, each the size of a paperback book, each tied with a narrow brown ribbon. Leah counted the top layer without meaning to — twelve — and then saw there was another layer under that, and possibly another under that.

The card was heavy cream stock. On one side, in gold, was printed *Chocolaterie Wauters — depuis 1931 — Bruxelles,* and a little engraved drawing of a shopfront with a striped awning. On the other side, in black ink, in handwriting that slanted hard to the right and looked like it had been done in a hurry with a pen that didn't want to cooperate, was written:

*Clearwater —*

*Told you. Share it or don't. The hazelnut ones are the ones.*

*In Geneva. Solveig is standing over me while I write this. She says hello. (She did not say hello.)*

*— H.*

*P.S. Tell Theo he's free. He won't believe me.*

Leah read it twice. She was aware that Brynn had come up behind her and was reading it over her shoulder with her chin practically on Leah's collarbone, and that Dot had turned around from the grill with the spatula held up in one hand, and that the old men in the corner had stopped even pretending.

"He says you're free," Leah told Theo.

"He's said that before," Theo said darkly. "Twice. Then he came back from somewhere at three in the morning and wanted eggs."

"*Who's it from?*" Brynn said, right in her ear. "Is it the English guy? The one who bought Ray's bottle? It says *Clearwater.* Is that your *last name?* You never told me your last name! Is that a *love letter?*"

"No."

"There's like a *hundred boxes* in there — "

"Thirty-six," said Theo. "There were sixty. He ate the rest. In two days." He looked at the floor. "I've stopped asking where it goes."

Dot had come out from behind the counter. She stood beside Leah with her arms folded and looked down into the crate for a long time with no expression on her face at all. Then she reached in, untied the brown ribbon on one of the gold boxes with two quick tugs of her short thick fingers, lifted the lid, and took out a chocolate. It was round and dark and had a single toasted hazelnut pressed into the top like a jewel in a ring. She put the whole thing in her mouth.

Everybody watched her chew.

Dot's face did nothing for several seconds. Then, very slightly, her eyebrows went up.

"Huh," she said.

From Dot, Leah had learned, that was a standing ovation.

---

Leah did not throw the chocolate. She didn't keep it, either. She thought about it for about as long as it took her to look at Brynn's face, which was the face of a kid on Christmas morning who'd been told she could only look at the presents, and at the old men in the corner, who had all leaned so far toward the crate in their chairs that one of them was in real danger of tipping over.

"Take some," she said to Brynn. "Take a box."

"*Seriously?*"

"Take two. Give some to your mom." She carried the crate to the end of the counter and set it down by the register. "Everybody take one. Dot. Wes, if he comes in. The highway guys." She looked over at the corner. "Them."

The old men didn't need telling twice. The one in the John Deere cap, whose name was Ernie and who had once, according to Brynn, fought a wolverine for a sandwich and lost, took a box with the reverence of a man in church. The tiny one with the hearing aid ate a single praline and then sat with both hands folded on the lid and his eyes shut.

Brynn ate three in a row standing at the counter. On the third she made a noise that made Theo Pak blush to the roots of his hair.

"Oh my *God,*" she said, with her mouth full. "Oh my God, Leah. Oh my *God.* Is this what Europe *tastes* like?"

"Belgium," Theo said, faintly. "That's Belgium. The shop's on a side street off the Grand Place. He bought it."

Brynn swallowed. "He bought the *chocolates?*"

"He bought the *shop.*"

There was a short, total silence in the Galena Café.

"By accident," Leah said, before she could stop herself.

Every head in the room turned toward her. She felt her ears go hot. She turned around and went back into the kitchen, and put her hands in the dishwater, and stood there with them in the water while the café exploded behind her into everybody talking at once.

---

Theo found her there ten minutes later, with a plate of Dot's hash browns in him and some of the hunted look gone.

"I just wanted to say," he said. "In case you were wondering. He's — " He stopped and seemed to think about how to say it. "I did two years on a yacht in Monaco. I cooked for a guy who made me throw out a whole bouillabaisse because the fish had *looked at him wrong.* This one says please. He eats what you make. He came into the kitchen on the second night and did the washing up, and I tried to stop him, and he looked at me like I'd tried to stop him breathing." Theo frowned. "He's completely insane. I want to be really clear about that. He asked me if I knew how to cook caribou and when I said no he said, 'Never mind, it's better raw anyway,' and then he laughed like it was a joke, and I don't think it was a joke."

Leah kept her face very still. "Probably wasn't."

"Right." Theo looked at her a moment longer. Whatever he saw, it seemed to settle something for him. "Anyway. He talked about you. A little. He only really talks when he's being a smartass. But he said you stole his pie." Something that was almost a smile crossed the long thin face. "He said it like it was the best thing that ever happened to him."

He zipped the parka the last inch.

"Okay," he said. "I'm going home. I'm going to sleep for a week. I'm never cooking an egg again." He turned to go, then turned back. "Does she give out the recipe? For the pie? The purple one? I had it on Thursday. I've been thinking about it for four days."

"No," said Dot, from the grill, without turning around.

Theo looked like he might cry.

Dot sighed through her nose. Then she went to the pie safe in the corner, took out a whole saskatoon pie in a tin, wrapped it in two layers of wax paper and a dish towel, and put it in his arms where the crate had been.

"Bring back the tin," she said. "And the towel."

"I live in *Vancouver.*"

"Then mail them."

Theo Pak stood there in the kitchen holding the pie against his chest with both arms. Then he said, "Thank you," in a voice that cracked, and left. Through the window over the sink Leah watched him go down to the end of the dock in his enormous parka, still holding the pie, and stand there waiting with his back to the town until the yellow floatplane came across the lake for him with its engine droning, its floats cutting two white lines across the gray water.

When the plane had gone up over the mountains and the sound of it had faded out, Leah dried her hands and went out to the counter.

The crate was mostly empty. Brynn had taken two boxes, as instructed, and was eating out of a third with the sincerity of a woman who'd decided it didn't count if nobody saw. Dot had put one box on the shelf above the register, next to the jar of Legion raffle tickets, without comment.

There was one box left at the bottom of the crate.

Leah took it into the kitchen. She waited until Dot was busy with the lunch prep and Brynn was out on the floor. Then she opened it, and took out one of the round dark ones with the hazelnut on top, and ate it standing at the sink with her back to the door.

It was the best thing she'd ever put in her mouth. Better than the Scotch. Better than the pie, which she would never in her life say out loud anywhere Dot could hear it.

She ate one more, slowly, and put the lid back on, and tied the ribbon, and put the box on the shelf over the sink next to the bleach where nobody would think to look.

"Hm," said Dot, behind her.

Leah didn't turn around. "What."

"Nothing." The sound of a knife going through onions, steady and fast. "Not a thing, Washington."


---

She called home on Saturday, because she'd promised.

It had snowed on Friday night for the first time, an inch, wet and heavy, gone from the roads by noon. But when Leah came out of the Galena at four o'clock with her week's pay in her hoodie pocket, the mountains across the lake had gone white halfway down their flanks, and the air smelled of it, clean and metallic, like the inside of a freezer.

She bought a twenty-dollar phone card at the gas station this time. The pay phone was colder than last week. CASSIE + LOSER was still there.

She dialed.

It rang twice.

"Clearwater residence," said a voice that wasn't her mother's, and Leah's heart went into her throat so fast she almost dropped the receiver.

His voice was still doing the thing where it cracked on the high notes and dropped into a basement on the low ones, and he still answered the phone the way Mom had drilled into both of them when they were kids, like a secretary. She hadn't heard it in three weeks. She hadn't heard it outside her own head in longer than that.

"Seth," she said.

There was a silence on the line so complete she thought the call had dropped.

Then he yelled.

"*LEAH?*" Directly into the receiver, so loud she jerked it away from her ear. "*MOM! MOM, IT'S LEAH! MOM — *" A clatter. Footsteps. A door. His voice came back, breathless. "She's in the yard. She's coming. Leah. *Leah.* Oh my God. Hi."

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," he said again. He sounded like he was grinning so hard it hurt. Then the grin went out of it. "You *jerk.*"

"I know."

"You absolute — you know what I thought? You know what I thought, when you went out? You were there, like, really far, and then you just weren't. Like somebody turned off a light. And Jake kept saying it's distance, it's got to be distance, and I kept saying how do you *know,* you've been Alpha for *two days,* how do you know she didn't — " He stopped. She heard him breathe. "I thought you drowned, Lee. I thought you went in the water somewhere and didn't come out."

Leah leaned her forehead against the cold steel of the phone box. Past the gas station a pickup went by with a dead deer in the bed, its legs sticking up stiff over the tailgate.

"I didn't think," she said.

"Yeah. You didn't."

"I'm sorry, Seth."

"Yeah." He was quiet for a second. Then, because he was Seth and there was no way for him to stay mad at anybody for longer than it took to say so, he said, "Mom says you're washing dishes in Canada."

"I am washing dishes in Canada."

"That's so *weird.*" He started to laugh. "You hate dishes. You made me do the dishes for, like, four years."

"These ones pay. Eight bucks an hour."

"*American?*"

"Canadian."

"That's terrible."

"There's pie," she said.

Seth laughed so hard he had to put the phone down. She heard him through the kitchen, faint and helpless, wheezing, and for some reason her own eyes stung, and she stood there in the cold with her hand over her mouth and let them.

When he came back he'd mostly gotten hold of himself. "Mom's coming. She was hanging laundry. She's got a basket. She's taking forever."

"Tell me stuff," Leah said. "Before she gets here. Tell me everything."

He told her everything, out of order and at top speed with all the important parts in the wrong places. The baby was growing so fast it was scary. She was a few weeks old and she looked six months, and she'd been born with teeth. And when she touched you, she showed you things — pictures, right in your head, what she wanted and what she'd seen — and the first time she'd done it to Seth he'd nearly phased on the spot out of pure fright.

"It's like being in the pack," he said. "Kind of. Except it's only one way, and it's just her, and it's mostly pictures of stuff she wants to eat."

"What does she eat?"

A pause. "Okay. So. That part's kind of gross."

"Seth."

"It's blood. Like, from a hospital. The doctor's got bags of it in a fridge. She drinks it out of a sippy cup." Another pause. "Jake's trying to get her to eat eggs."

Leah closed her eyes and thought very hard about nothing for a few seconds.

"And Jake?" she said.

"Jake's good." Seth's voice went careful. "Lee. He's really good. I know you're gonna be weird about it. I know what you think. But I've been in his head, and it's — it's different from Sam's. Way different. He just wants her safe. He wants her to be happy. That's all it is, right now. That's all it is for, like, years."

"I know how it works, Seth."

"I know you know. I'm just saying." He hesitated. "He's not sad anymore. That's all. He was sad for so long, Lee. Since — since forever. Since the spring. And now he's not."

Leah didn't say anything to that. There wasn't a thing in her she could say that she'd want her brother to hear.

"And Charlie," Seth said, brightening, the way he always did when he'd said the hard part. "Jake went over to Charlie's and *phased.* In the backyard. Charlie sat down on the steps for a really long time. Now he comes over and sees Bella and the baby and he just won't ask anything. He calls it need-to-know." Seth giggled. "Mom's over there all the time. At Charlie's. They watch the games. She brings him fish fry."

"*Seth.*"

"I'm just *saying* — "

Then there was a rustle, and a muffled argument, and her mother's voice saying *give me that,* and Seth saying *I'm not done,* and her mother saying *you're done,* and then Sue Clearwater was on the line, slightly out of breath.

"You called," her mother said.

"I said I would."

"You said a lot of things. You said them on the back of the electric bill." But there was a smile all the way through it. "How are you? Are you eating? Are you dressing for it? Your brother says it snowed up there. It said on the news it snowed in Prince George."

"It snowed here last night. A little." Leah looked across the street at the lake, at the white coming down the mountains. "I'm fine, Mom. I've got a coat."

"You've got a *coat?*"

"Kind of. A hoodie. And a hat. It's got a pompom."

There was a pause on the other end of the line.

"A pompom," her mother said.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say *mm-hm.*"

"I was going to say I wish I could see it." Sue's voice went soft. "I'd pay money to see you in a hat with a pompom, baby. I'd pay real money."

They talked for a while about nothing. Dot and the pie and the old men and the Canucks, which made her mother laugh, because her dad had thought hockey was something Canadians did to keep from freezing. Billy, who was fine. Sue's back, which was acting up in the cold. Seth kept yelling things from the background.

Then her mother said, "Seth. Go outside. Go finish the laundry."

"I'm not — "

"Go, Seth."

A door. Quiet.

"Is there someone?" her mother said.

"Mom — "

"Lee." Her mother's voice was very calm. "I've been your mother your whole life. I know what you sound like. Last week you sounded like you'd just been in a fight and won. This week you sound like you've got something in your pocket and you keep checking it's still there."

Leah's hand had gone to the front of her jeans on its own. To the small square shape of the mirror in its pouch.

She took the hand away.

"There's a guy," she said. "Kind of. Maybe. I don't know."

"Mm-hm."

"Mom."

"I'm allowed." A pause. "Is he nice to you?"

Leah thought about it. About a man in a long gray coat tossing a severed finger over his shoulder into the dark. About a wolf the size of a truck with its paws over its muzzle, begging her to stop singing Britney Spears. About *I'll ask.*

"No," she said. "Not really. He's kind of an asshole."

"Ah." Her mother sounded, of all things, satisfied. "Good."

"*Good?*"

"Your father was kind of an asshole," Sue Clearwater said. "Did you know that? When I met him? He was the biggest show-off on the whole coast. He used to take his boat out past the jetty in weather nobody else would go out in just so the girls on the beach would see him do it. I told him he was an idiot and he'd drown. He said he wouldn't, because he'd never be stupid enough to die before I said yes." She paused. "Nice ones bored you to death, Lee. Even when you were little. You liked the ones who talked back."

Leah stood in the cold with her mouth open and nothing to put in it.

"Call me next week," her mother said. "Same time. And Lee? Your brother's been sleeping in your room. Don't tell him I told you."

---

She held out until a little after ten that night.

She'd eaten two cans of soup heated on the hotplate that Wes officially didn't allow. She'd lain on the orange bedspread with the heater clanking and stared at the moose until the moose became unbearable, and then at the wall, which was worse, because there was nothing on it but what was in her head.

Seth, sleeping in her room.

She sat up. She took the pouch off the nightstand and loosened the drawstring and took out the mirror, and held it on her palm.

Her face again. Cleaner this time. Her hair down and wet.

She thought about it long enough to feel stupid, sitting on a motel bed on a Saturday night staring at a mirror the size of a playing card like a teenager working up to calling a boy. She hadn't done this since she was sixteen, on the floor of the Clearwater hallway with the phone cord stretched into the coat closet, dialing six digits of Sam Uley's number and hanging up on the seventh, over and over.

She'd been a lot braver at sixteen. She'd made it to the seventh digit eventually.

"Potter," she said.

Nothing happened.

She frowned at the glass. "Potter," she said again, louder.

Her own face frowned back at her.

*Say my name into it.* His name. Fine. The whole thing.

She took a breath.

"Harry Potter," Leah said, and the glass went cloudy, like a window breathing out, and when it cleared again it wasn't her face in it.

It was a ceiling.

High and white, with plaster molding and a fluorescent light hanging off chains. Then the image lurched sideways in a blur of desk and paper and somebody's elbow, and his face was there, filling the glass, very close, as if he'd snatched the mirror up and jammed it in front of himself in a hurry.

"*Clearwater,*" he said.

He looked tired. His hair was worse than she'd ever seen it, which she would have said was impossible. He was wearing a white shirt with the collar open and the sleeves rolled up past his elbows, and there was a smear of blue ink along his jaw.

"Hey," she said.

"Hi." He blinked at her. Then the corner of his mouth went up on one side, and she saw the long white canine. "Hi. You rang. It works. I wasn't entirely sure it would — you can never tell, with these, I had a bloke in Diagon Alley make them and he's ninety-four — "

"Lord Potter," said a woman's voice somewhere off to his left, flat and Scandinavian and audibly at the end of its rope. "Is that the mirror."

"It's personal, Solveig."

"It is twenty past seven in the morning. We have been in this room since four. There are two hundred and eleven pages left in the Kroll file and you have spent nine minutes of the last hour drawing a moose on the evidence log." A pause. "Is that the unregistered local?"

Harry's eyes rolled up toward the ceiling and then back to Leah. "She says hello."

"I did not say hello."

"She didn't say hello," Harry agreed, to Leah. He tilted the mirror so she could see, for half a second, a long table piled with paper a foot high, and a tall pale woman with her hair scraped back so tight it looked painful, holding a quill and glaring directly into the glass. Then the mirror swung back to his face. "That's Solveig. She's in a mood. She's been in a mood since about nineteen ninety-eight."

"I heard that."

"She hears everything. It's her gift." He looked back at Leah and his face changed a little. The joke went out of it. "You all right?"

"I'm fine."

"You don't call people at ten at night because you're fine."

"It's ten at night *here,*" Leah said. "It's seven in the morning there. You're the one who's up at the wrong time."

"I'm always up at the wrong time." He waited. He didn't push. She saw his eyes move over her face in the glass, the way they'd moved over her on the mountain.

"I talked to my brother," she said.

"Seth."

He remembered the name. She didn't know why that surprised her.

"Yeah. He's good. He's fine." She looked away from the mirror at the moose, and back. "He's been sleeping in my room."

Harry nodded a little, and waited some more.

"Tomorrow," Leah said. "Sunday. The café's closed."

"Is it?"

"You know it is."

"I do know it is," Harry admitted. "Dot told me. She was very firm about it. Something about a club sandwich in nineteen seventy-four."

"Third date," Leah said, before she could lose her nerve. "If you want. If you're not — " she waved a hand at the mirror, at Geneva — "doing that."

His eyebrows went up. Behind him, Solveig said something sharp in Norwegian.

"I'm always doing this," Harry said. "Tomorrow, I'll do it later." His eyes had gone very bright. "Where? Paris is still on the table. Sydney. I know a place in Marrakech that does a — "

"No."

"No."

"I pick," Leah said. "Your lodge. Across the lake. And you cook."

That stopped him for a second. "*I* cook."

"You. Personally. Theo's gone, he's in Vancouver, he's never cooking an egg again. He said so." She narrowed her eyes at the glass. "You can cook, right? You've got two hundred billion pounds, you can probably cook."

"Those two things are completely unrelated," Harry said. "But yes. I can cook. I've been cooking since I was tall enough to see over the hob." Something moved behind his eyes and was put away. "What time?"

"Dark."

"Dark's gone eight, up there, this time of year."

"Then eight."

"How are you getting across? I'll come and fetch you. There's a boat. I think there's a boat. Hargreaves said something about a — "

"I'll run," Leah said. "Around the lake."

He looked at her for a moment in the glass. Then that canine showed again, slowly.

"Course you will," Harry said. "Eight. I'll leave a light on."

"Lord Potter," said Solveig, behind him. "*Page one hundred and four.*"

"See ya later, alligator," Harry said to Leah, and the mirror went cloudy before she could say anything back, and cleared, and it was her own face again, sitting on an orange bedspread in Turnagain, B.C., with her mouth half open.

"In a while, crocodile," she said to it, and felt like an idiot, and lay back on the bed with the mirror on her chest.

She slept better than she had in a week.


---

The lake was eleven miles around. She'd paced it out on Tuesday night, on four feet, because she'd wanted to know.

She left from behind the deadfall at twenty past seven on Sunday evening with the sun going down red behind the western ridges and her clothes tied to her ankle in a clean grocery bag, the mirror knotted into the cord. She went along the north shore, where the road ran out past the last cabins and turned into a trail and then into nothing, across the mouths of two creeks that came down cold and loud out of the mountains. The snow from Friday hung on in the shadows under the trees.

She didn't hurry. She'd thought about hurrying, and then she'd thought about showing up at his door out of breath like a kid who'd run the whole way to a party, and she'd slowed down on purpose.

The light went out of the sky in stages, red and then rust and then a long blue dusk, the lake beside her turning from pewter to ink. A loon somewhere far out on the water, calling that long wavering call that sounded like somebody grieving in a language she didn't speak.

And then, rounding a point of rock at the head of the lake, she saw the light.

He'd meant it literally. A lantern hung from a post at the end of the lodge's dock, an old glass-and-tin thing with a candle in it, the flame steady in the still air. It threw a small gold circle on the black water.

Beyond it, up a slope of rough lawn going white with frost, was the lodge. Logs, real ones, gray with age and as thick through as she was, stacked two stories high under a steep green tin roof with a stone chimney at either end. Every window on the ground floor was lit. Smoke lay along the slope above the roof in a long gray scarf, and the smell of it came down to her across the water: birch and spruce burning, and under that, roast beef. Fat and salt and pepper and onions caramelizing in the drippings. And something she didn't recognize at all — eggy, puffed, browning — that made her stomach turn over twice.

There was a boathouse at the foot of the lawn with a canoe pulled up on the gravel in front of it. She went around the back of it, into the dark between its wall and the trees, and phased.

The cold took her bare skin like a hand. She dressed fast. She'd brought the brush, and she ran it through her hair with her teeth gritted, and then stopped, and stood there in the dark holding a hairbrush behind a boathouse eleven miles from her own bed, and felt like the biggest idiot in British Columbia.

She put the brush back in the bag. She put the mirror in her pocket.

When she came around the corner of the boathouse he was sitting on the steps of the deck.

He was in the cream sweater again, with the sleeves pushed up to his elbows, and dark trousers, and no shoes. His feet were bare on the frosted wood. There was a tea towel over his right shoulder. He was holding a bottle of beer by the neck between two fingers, the way he'd held his wand on the mountain, and looking at the lake. He'd heard her phase. She knew he had. He'd waited until she was dressed before he looked around.

"You walked," he said.

"I jogged."

"You walked the last two miles. You stopped running at the second creek." His mouth went up on one side. "Didn't want to turn up out of breath?"

Leah stopped at the bottom of the steps. "I could leave."

"You could." He held up the beer. "Or you could have one of these. Theo left a whole fridge full."

She took the beer. He got up. When she came up past him onto the deck she felt the heat coming off him, the woodstove heat, the same as her own.

"Your feet," she said. "It's minus six."

He looked down at them. "Is it? Huh. Fancy that." He looked back up at her face. "Come in. The Yorkshires are about to go, and if they go flat I'll have to throw myself in the lake."

---

Inside, the lodge was one huge room. It went all the way up to the roof, two stories of log walls with heavy beams crossing overhead and an iron chandelier made out of an old wagon wheel. A stone fireplace at one end was big enough to walk into without ducking, and over its mantel hung a caribou rack, huge and pale, every point catching the firelight. Leather couches. Hudson's Bay blankets in their red and green and yellow stripes. A long plank table. Two bearskins on the floor, black and brown, that she walked around very carefully without knowing why. The kitchen was open to the room at the far end, a big cream-and-black enamel range with something going on every burner.

And on a sideboard between the kitchen and the fire, there was a record player.

It was old, a wood cabinet with a smoked plastic lid. There was a record turning on it, the volume down so low she hadn't heard it from outside. She heard it now. She stopped in the middle of the room with her beer in her hand and every muscle in her back gone tight.

She knew the song from the first scratchy chord. She knew it from the bottom of her stomach, from the floor of a truck cab, from a radio on a boat with a bad antenna and salt on the speaker grille.

Creedence.

He'd stopped at the kitchen end of the room. He was watching her. His face had gone careful.

"Too much?" he said. "I can take it off. I had Hargreaves find it — it's all of them, in order — I thought, you said, and I'd never actually listened to them properly, and — " He started toward the record player. "I'll take it off."

"Don't."

He stopped.

Leah stood there and listened. It was one of the ones near the start of the record, one her dad used to hum along to and get the words wrong on purpose to make Seth laugh. She could see him doing it. The back of his neck, sunburned, the gray coming in at his temples, his big hand on the wheel of the boat.

"Leave it," she said. "It's fine."

Harry looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded once and went back to the stove.

She took a long pull on the beer, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, which was wet for some reason, and sat on a stool at the end of the kitchen counter and watched him cook.

---

He cooked the way she washed dishes. Fast, without wasting anything, without looking at his own hands. He opened the oven, and a blast of heat came out that she could feel from the stool, and he reached in with his bare hand — no towel, no glove — and the pan slid out to meet his fingers an inch before they touched it, as if it had been asked. A black iron pan full of a dozen golden puffed-up bowls, risen up over the edges of their cups, crackling.

"What are those?"

"Yorkshire puddings."

"Those aren't pudding."

"No," he agreed. "Nobody's ever been able to explain that to me either. You put the gravy in them." He set the pan down. "My aunt made them every Sunday for my cousin. Well. I made them every Sunday for my cousin. She stood behind me and told me I was doing it wrong."

"How old were you?"

He thought about it. "Eight? Nine. I did a whole Sunday roast for twelve once when I was ten. Her husband's boss came round. Big deal. They locked me in my cupboard before the guests arrived and let me out to do the washing up after." He took the beef out of the second oven, a huge dark-crusted joint in a pan of drippings, and set it on a board. "The boss's wife said it was the best Yorkshire she'd ever had. My aunt said thank you." He picked up a long knife. "That's all right. They were very good Yorkshires."

Leah didn't say anything. She drank her beer and watched him carve.

He'd made enough for eight. They ate at the end of the long plank table nearest the fire, and for the first twenty minutes neither of them talked, because there wasn't time. Beef sliced thin and pink in the middle. Roast potatoes so crisp they rattled. Parsnips gone dark and sticky at the edges. Gravy almost black. The Yorkshires, crisp at the rims and soft in the middle, holding the gravy like cups, which were — she did not say this out loud — possibly the second best thing she'd ever eaten, after the praline.

At one point she looked up and found him watching her with a forkful of parsnip halfway to his mouth.

"What?"

"Nothing," Harry said. "I've just never seen anybody else go back for fourths before. I thought it was just me."

"I ran eleven miles."

"You walked the last two."

She pointed her fork at him. He ate the parsnip with great dignity.

There was an apple crumble after, with real custard. By the time they were done the platter was bare and the record had ended and was hissing around and around in the run-out groove, and Leah was sitting back with her hands folded over her stomach feeling like she'd been hit by a truck in the nicest possible way.

Harry got up and turned the record over. He put the needle down without looking. The first song on the new side came on low, all snare drum and guitar, fast and angry. That one she knew too. Everybody knew that one.

He sat back down across from her and waited.

She knew what he was waiting for. She'd been thinking about it all the way around the lake.

"Third date," she said.

"Third date," he agreed.

"You said you'd tell me. What you are. I mean the wolf thing." She turned her bottle on the table. "You're different from me. You smell like a wizard first. Like Kell. The match smell. You smell like *him* more than you smell like us."

"Kroll," Harry said. "His name was Kroll."

"Whatever his name was."

"It matters a bit." He tipped his head. "But you're right. I made mine." He was quiet a second, deciding where to start. "There's a thing some wizards can learn to do. It's called being an Animagus. You go through a lot of very unpleasant business, and at the end of it you can turn into an animal. One animal. Whatever animal's in you. You don't pick. Same one every time, for the rest of your life."

"What kind of unpleasant business?"

"You keep a mandrake leaf in your mouth for a month." He said it the way somebody else might say *you do a lot of push-ups.* "Full moon to full moon. You don't take it out, you don't swallow it. If it falls out, you start again. I started again three times. Then there's a potion, with a few things in it you don't want to hear about over pudding. Then you wait for a lightning storm. Then it hurts a great deal. Then you're a stag, or a cat, or a dog, or whatever you are."

"Or a wolf."

"Or a wolf," he said. "Normally a normal-sized one."

Leah looked at him across the table. "*Normally.*"

"My dad was a stag," Harry said. "Big one. My godfather was a dog. Huge black thing, people used to think he was the Grim — that's a death omen, very bad luck. He thought that was hilarious. There was another friend of theirs who was a rat, and the less said about him the better." His mouth thinned and then let go. "They taught themselves at school. Fifteen, sixteen. For a friend. Remus. He'd been bitten when he was a kid. Werewolf. Every full moon he had to go off on his own and turn into a monster and tear himself apart, because a werewolf will kill a human. But it won't kill an animal. So the other three became animals. So he wouldn't have to do it by himself."

Leah sat very still.

"I did mine after the war," Harry said. "I was twenty when I started. I'd wanted to since I was thirteen, since I found out about them. It took me two years. And at the end of it I stood in a field in Devon in the middle of a thunderstorm, and it hurt like you would not believe, and when it stopped I was looking down at the grass from a very long way up." He turned his beer bottle around with one finger. "I was a bit taller than I'd planned, and a lot heavier, and every sheep in a quarter of a mile had gone over the hedge into the next field."

Leah laughed before she could stop it.

"Don't laugh. It was a big hedge. The farmer found them three fields over the next morning, piled up in a corner against a stone wall like a snowdrift." He winced. "And then I went and registered, because you're meant to, and the lady at the Ministry said I'd have to demonstrate, and I said I didn't think that was a good idea indoors, and she said it was regulations. They had to rebuild the ceiling. She measured me, though. Fair play to her. Got right up on a chair with a tape. Then she went and had a lie-down. I'm in the Register as *Wolf, large.* There was a debate about the *large.*"

"What else could they put?"

"Somebody suggested *please do not approach.*"

She laughed again, the stupid huffing, hiccuping laugh, and put her hand over her mouth. He was grinning at her across the table, the canine showing.

"That," she said, when she could, and pointed at his mouth. "That tooth. And the heat. And the nose. Your dad didn't have antlers when he was a person, did he?"

The grin faded a little.

"No," he said. "Animagi don't usually keep anything. You turn back, you're you. Normal temperature, normal teeth." He touched the long canine with the tip of his tongue, absently, as if checking it was still there. "Mine didn't stay put. It came back with me. The heat, the nose, the ears a bit. The teeth when I'm not thinking. I'm faster than I was. Stronger. I heal faster. I eat — " he gestured at the bare platter — "well. You've seen."

"Why?"

"No idea," Harry said simply. "I've asked a lot of people. There's a woman at the Department of Mysteries who's been trying to work it out for four years, and the last time I saw her she'd started crying. Best guess anyone's got is that whatever came out of me in that field was too big to fit back in properly. So bits of it leak."

Leah thought about her own wolf, lying on its back in the dark under her ribs. About how it had been there before she'd ever phased. About her dad's heart, and the fever, and the two of them growing into each other like trees planted too close.

"You chose it," she said.

"I did."

"Nobody chose mine. It just came. When my dad died. I didn't get a say."

"No," he said. "You didn't." He didn't try to make it better. He looked at her across the table with his green eyes very steady. "But you've got it now. Whatever it is, it's yours. You ran here on it."

On the sideboard the record moved on to another song. She didn't recognize this one. Her dad had never played the back half of that record much.

"Your Remus," she said slowly. "The werewolf."

"He taught me, when I was thirteen. Best teacher I ever had. He died in the war. Him and his wife, both, the same night." Harry's voice didn't change. "They had a boy. Teddy. He was a few weeks old. I'm his godfather."

"How old is he?"

"Eight." And something came into his face, a softening at the edges that Leah hadn't seen there before, not on the mountain, not at the bar, not on the plateau. "Eight and a half. He'll tell you about the half. His hair changes colour when he's cross. He's at his gran's mostly. I get him weekends when I'm in the country. He thinks I'm the best thing that ever happened because I let him ride on my back round the garden, and his gran thinks I'm going to kill him because I let him ride on my back round the garden." He picked up his beer. "Both of them are right."

Leah looked at him for a long time across the plates.

"Show me," she said.

---

"Here?" he said.

"Here."

He looked up at the beams and the wagon-wheel chandelier and made a small calculating face. Then he got up and walked out onto the open plank floor between the couches and the fire, and stood there in his bare feet with the tea towel still over his shoulder.

"Don't scream," he said. "It upsets the neighbours."

"There aren't any neighbours."

"There's a moose about half a mile up the shore. She's very nervous."

And then he was a wolf.

There was nothing to see. That was what got her, afterward, lying awake trying to put it together. When she phased it was a whole production — the fire in the spine, the body tearing open like a seam and coming back together as something else, the clothes exploding into rags. Everybody within a hundred yards knew it was happening. He changed between one second and the next, the way a light comes on.

The room got smaller.

His head was up among the bottoms of the crossbeams. The fire threw his shadow up the log wall all the way to the roof. Black, and black, and black, the coat so dark the firelight sank into it, with something sootier underneath where the heat from the hearth stirred the guard hairs. The green eyes, level with the caribou rack on the mantel, looking down at her. The planks under him were as quiet as if a cat had stepped on them.

"Well," said the wolf, in Harry's voice, with that rumble running under it like an engine, "this is me."

Leah got up off her chair.

She walked toward him slowly and stopped about four feet away and looked up. He lowered his head the way a horse lowers its head for somebody it doesn't want to frighten, until the long black muzzle was level with her face, and she could feel his breath, hot as an oven door, and smell him: earth and pine and stone, and roast beef.

"Can I," she said. And stopped. She hadn't meant to say it.

His ears came forward. "Go on."

She put her hand on his neck.

The fur was deeper than her hand. Her fingers sank in to the second knuckle before they found the hide, and the hide was hot, hot as hers, hotter, and under it she could feel a pulse going slow and heavy as a big drum. She'd touched her own pack in fur maybe a dozen times. A shoulder bump on patrol. Seth falling asleep on her once, in the woods behind the house, the week after the funeral. It had always felt like touching her own skin through a coat.

This felt like touching somebody else.

She took her hand away. "Okay," she said. Her voice came out rough. "Change back."

He changed back.

He stood there on the plank floor in his bare feet and his cream sweater and his dark trousers, exactly as he'd been. The sleeves were still pushed up to the elbows. There was still, she saw, a tiny spot of gravy on the left cuff that had been there all through dinner. The tea towel was still over his right shoulder.

Leah looked at the tea towel for a long time.

"Oh, you son of a *bitch,*" she said.

"There it is," Harry said, sounding pleased.

"Your *clothes.* They go wherever you go, and then they come *back.*"

"Animagus thing. Always has been. My old Transfiguration teacher's been turning into a tabby cat in full tartan robes for about fifty years and never once lost a hat."

"I've lost eleven pairs of shorts," Leah said.

He blinked.

"Eleven pairs of shorts. Six tank tops. Two pairs of jeans, which I *liked.* Every time I don't have time to strip first, every time something comes at us in the woods, every time I'm too mad to think, *boom.* Shredded. I have walked home naked through the woods behind La Push at four in the morning more times than I can count. With *Paul* there. In my *head.*" She jabbed a finger at the tea towel. "And you get to keep your *dish rag?*"

Harry looked down at the tea towel as if he'd never seen it before. "It's a very good dish rag."

She hit him in the arm. Hard. It was like hitting a tree. He laughed, the loud one that had turned three heads at the Prospector, and rubbed the arm, though she was fairly sure it hadn't hurt him at all.

"I could do it for you," he said.

She stopped with her fist still half raised.

"Your clothes. There's a charm. It's fiddly, but I've done harder. It goes on the clothes. You'd still be exactly you — nothing in your head, nothing in your blood. But whatever you had on when you changed would go with you and come back with you. Boots. That hat with the pompom." His eyes went to her face and stayed there. "If you wanted."

She lowered her hand.

She thought about the walk home through the trees behind La Push, about the cord on her ankle and the three hard knots and every time she'd had to decide, before a fight, whether she could afford to lose the clothes she was standing up in. And she thought about *you ask me first,* and how he'd asked.

"Fourth date," Leah said.

He went still. Then his face cracked open into a grin so wide she saw both canines at once.

"Fourth date," he said. "Oh, that's *cruel.*"

"You started it."

"I did start it." He was laughing again. He went back to the table and started stacking plates. "Is there going to be a fourth date, Clearwater, or is this a long con?"

She picked up the gravy jug and followed him into the kitchen.

"Maybe," she said.

---

He washed. She dried. She'd tried to argue and he'd looked at her the way Theo said he'd looked at Theo, like she'd suggested he stop breathing, and she'd given up and taken a towel. Not the one off his shoulder.

He was fast. He didn't break anything. He held each plate up to the light before he handed it over, and she found, to her deep annoyance, that she had nothing to say about his technique.

"What was that one?" he said, when they were almost done. "When you came in. The one that made you stop."

"It was off *Willy and the Poor Boys.* Second on the first side. It doesn't matter. It's one he'd sing wrong to make Seth laugh."

"And the angry one. First on the other side." He handed her the roasting pan. "That's 'Fortunate Son,' isn't it? I listened to it a few times before you got here. I couldn't tell you what it's about. Something about a senator."

Leah dried the roasting pan. She took her time with it.

"It's about the war," she said. "Vietnam. My dad's cousin went. It's about how the rich kids didn't have to go. The senator's son, the millionaire's son. Their daddies pulled strings. And the poor kids went instead and died in the jungle." She set the pan down. "That's the whole song. Some people are born lucky and never have to fight for anything, and everybody else does the fighting for them."

Harry didn't say anything for a while. He washed the last glass. He washed it for a long time.

"Huh," he said at last.

She looked at him sideways. At the sweater with the sleeves pushed up, and the lodge with the two chimneys, and the two hundred billion pounds, and the boy in a cupboard who'd made Yorkshire puddings for his aunt's husband's boss, and the seventeen-year-old who'd gone to a war and come out of it not knowing how to do anything else.

"Not much of a fortunate son," she said. "Are you."

He handed her the glass. His mouth went up a little, on one side.

"I'd have taken the deal," he said. "If anyone had offered."

She dried the glass and put it on the shelf. Everybody she knew had gone to war at sixteen, at seventeen, at nineteen. A whole clearing full of them, last spring, against the newborns. Nobody had offered any of them a deal either.

---

She left a little after midnight.

He walked her down to the dock. He didn't offer to take her across in the canoe, and he didn't offer to pop her home in four seconds, and she noticed both of those things. The candle in the lantern was down to a stub. Out over the lake the stars were thick and hard and close.

They stopped at the top of the dock. She put her hands in the front pocket of her hoodie and found the mirror there in its pouch and held it.

"Thank you," she said. "For dinner."

"Thank you for coming to dinner."

"The Yorkshires were okay."

"High praise."

"They were really good," Leah said, because it was true, and because it was dark, and because he'd stood in a lodge at the end of the world with a tea towel over his shoulder and told her about a boy named Teddy whose hair changed colour when he was cross.

He looked at her in the lantern light with his head tipped to one side.

She thought about it. She thought about it for long enough that it got awkward, and then a little longer than that, standing at the top of his dock in the cold with her heart going.

Then she stepped back.

"Fourth date," she said.

He breathed out through his nose, almost a laugh. "Fourth date."

She went around behind the boathouse and stripped in the cold and tied her clothes to her ankle and let the fire take her. When she came out on four feet he was still standing at the top of the dock with his hands in his pockets.

She went past him at a lope, close enough to bump him if she'd wanted to. She didn't bump him.

Halfway around the lake, in the dark, she realized she'd been grinning like an idiot for three miles, and that there was nobody in her head to see it, and that she'd have been grinning anyway if there had been.

Doreen Pelletier had been feeding strays on Thanksgiving Monday since the fall of 1979, which was the first year after Gil went through the ice on the Dease River with the skidder and didn't come up. She'd closed the café that year to sit at home by herself, and lasted until about ten in the morning, and then she'd gone down and opened the place back up and put a turkey in, and people had come. They'd kept coming. Twenty-seven years.

It was never a big crowd. The ones who came to the Galena on Thanksgiving were the ones without — the bachelors and the widowers and the drifters, whoever was passing through and had nowhere to be. Dot pushed four tables together and put the good cloth on them, the white one with the red stitching her mother had brought from Quebec, and charged nothing, and anybody who tried to pay got told where they could put their money.

This year she'd cooked for twenty-five. She'd had a feeling.

At four o'clock she stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded and counted heads.

Wes, in a clean shirt, who'd brought his own folding chair because he didn't trust hers. Ray, who'd brought a case of beer and the face of a man who'd been wronged by the Bank of Canada. Ernie and Clarence and little Augie with his hearing aid turned all the way up, already arguing about whether Luongo could carry a whole team by himself, which Clarence said no goalie in history had ever done. Lorraine Halvorsen, who'd skipped her sister-in-law's in Dease Lake because the sister-in-law put marshmallows on her yams and life was too short. Brynn, carrying plates, talking. Two highway crew boys from Terrace. A young German couple in a camper van who'd been trying to get to Alaska and had taken a wrong turn three hundred kilometers ago, looking at the turkey like people who could not believe their luck.

And Washington. And Washington's fancy friend.

Dot had known since Wednesday. Washington had stood at her elbow by the flat-top for a full minute without saying anything, the way she did, and Dot had waited her out.

"Monday," the girl had said finally. "The dinner. Brynn says anybody can come."

"Anybody can."

"Can I bring somebody."

Dot had looked at her over her glasses.

"He asked what to bring," Washington had said, with her ears going red, which they did, Dot had noticed, a lot more than the girl would have liked. "He asked me first. I said I'd ask you."

"Tell him nothing," Dot had said. "Tell him to bring his appetite and his manners and keep his wallet in his pants."

And he'd come at half past three with his hair standing up like he'd been out in a gale, in that waxed jacket and boots polished to a shine that hurt to look at, carrying a tin. He'd stopped just inside the door and looked around at the good cloth and the chairs set out, and something had gone across his face that Dot couldn't read and didn't try to.

Then he'd come straight back to the kitchen hatch.

"Mrs. Pelletier," he'd said. "Thank you for having me."

"Dot."

"Dot." He'd held up the tin. "I know she said nothing. I'm sorry. I couldn't. It's a treacle tart. I made it. It's the only pudding I know how to make properly and I've been told it's an acquired taste." He'd set it on the end of the counter where she pointed, next to her four saskatoon pies and her two pumpkin, and taken the lid off without any fuss, and left it there. He hadn't looked at it again.

Dot had looked at it, though. Golden. Shiny on top. A lattice of pastry, a little uneven at one corner. It smelled of syrup and lemon and something toasty.

She'd said nothing. She'd gone back to her gravy.

---

They ate.

He sat between Washington and Augie, who couldn't hear a word he said and didn't care. He ate like a lumber crew. Dot had fed lumber crews, and fire crews off the line after eighteen hours, and she knew what it looked like when a body had been running on empty for longer than a body should. This one went back for turkey three times and stuffing twice, and he looked round the table before he took the last of the mashed potatoes, to see if anybody else wanted them. Nobody did. Most of the table had stopped eating some time ago to watch.

Washington matched him plate for plate and didn't look up.

Ray made it through the first plate before he leaned across Clarence and said, "I still got seven of those things in my till."

"Seven of what?" said the Englishman.

"Your *hundreds.* Seven of 'em. Nobody in this town can break a hundred. Bank in Dease Lake wanted to know where I got 'em. Asked for *ID.*" Ray's beard bristled. "I had to fill out a *form.*"

The Englishman put down his fork and looked at Ray with real sympathy.

"I'm so sorry," he said. "I know exactly how you feel."

Washington made a noise into her napkin. Dot saw her shoulders shake.

Constable Trudel came by at quarter to five, in uniform, on her break, the way she came every year, and Dot fixed her a plate to sit down with. The constable sat across from the Englishman and looked at him the way she looked at everybody new, the way a cat looks at a mouse hole. Where was he from. London. What brought him up here. Consulting, he said, for an international outfit, looking at the old mine.

"Huh," the constable said. "The mine. Funny. That Kell fellow was up there too."

"Was he?"

"He called me, you know. Few weeks back. Said he'd taken a charter over to Dease Lake and wouldn't be back for the car." Trudel frowned a little at her stuffing, as though there was something about the memory she couldn't put her finger on. "Nice enough on the phone. Had his car dropped back to Hertz in Smithers and everything. I don't know. Just struck me funny that nobody saw him go."

"People are strange," the Englishman said.

"That they are." The constable looked at him a moment longer. Then she went back to her plate.

Dot was watching Washington, though, because Washington was the one she'd been watching for a month now. And when the constable said *he called me,* Dot saw the girl's fork stop halfway to her mouth, and her eyes go sideways to the man next to her, very fast, and come back. And she saw him, without looking at the girl at all, move his elbow a half an inch on the table until it touched hers.

The girl's fork went back to moving.

*Huh,* Dot thought.

---

Dale came in at quarter past five.

He came in sober and scrubbed, in a flannel shirt with the creases still in it from the package, holding a gas-station pie in a plastic clamshell like it might bite him. The table went quiet.

Then he walked down the length of the table and stopped behind Washington's chair.

"Hey," he said.

Washington turned around in her chair and looked up at him. The Englishman didn't turn around. He kept eating. But Dot, who'd been watching for exactly this, saw his left hand go flat on the cloth beside his plate.

"I wanted to say sorry," Dale said to the floor. "For the. At the Prospector. I was drunk. That's not an excuse. My mom raised me better." He held out the clamshell. "It's lemon meringue."

Washington looked at the pie, and at Dale, for a long time.

"How's your wrist?" she said.

Dale went red to his ears. "It's okay. It was kinda sore for a week."

"Good," said Washington. Then she took the pie. "Sit down, Dale. There's turkey."

Dale sat down at the far end of the table next to the Germans, who gave him the stuffing. The Englishman's hand came up off the cloth and picked up his fork again.

Dot went and got Dale a plate.

---

At the end, after the saskatoon was gone and the pumpkin was gone and Dale's lemon meringue was gone, there was a quarter of the treacle tart left on the counter. Dot had watched it go around. Augie had two slices. The German girl had one and said something to her boyfriend and had another. The Englishman hadn't touched it.

When the table was breaking up, and people were standing around with their coffee in that loose, full way they did at the end, Dot went to the counter and cut herself a sliver.

It was sweet. Very sweet. Golden syrup and breadcrumbs and lemon zest, with a short buttery crust underneath that broke on her tongue. The kind of thing you'd make if you'd learned to bake from somebody who'd learned from somebody, and nobody had ever let you get creative.

It was a good tart.

She turned around with the plate in her hand and found him standing at the end of the counter watching her, his hands in his pockets, not saying anything. Waiting. The way a kid waits outside the principal's office.

Dot finished the sliver. She put the plate down.

"Too much syrup," she said.

He nodded slowly, like that was about what he'd expected.

"I'd eat it again," Dot said, and went into the kitchen.

---

The kitchen was a disaster. It always was, after. Dot came in and found the sink already full and the water already running and Washington standing at it with her sleeves pushed up to her elbows, and him beside her, drying.

He'd asked. Dot had heard him ask. *Do you want help,* and the girl had said *no,* and he'd said *all right,* and picked up a towel anyway, and she hadn't said anything else.

Dot went to the walk-in with the leftovers and took her time.

When she came back out they were still at the sink with their backs to her. He was drying fast. Glasses, plates, the big roasting pan, without dropping anything. Held each thing up to the window to check it before it went on the rack. Somebody had taught him that. Somebody had stood behind him and made him do it till he got it right.

Washington said something Dot didn't catch. He said something back. And Washington took her hand out of the dishwater and flicked it at him, sharp, so a spray of water went across the side of his face.

He didn't flinch. He stood there dripping. He turned his head very slowly and looked at her.

And Washington laughed.

Dot had heard her laugh exactly once before, across the Prospector, the night she put Dale into the jukebox. Never in this kitchen. A month of grunts and *yeps* and *fines* and cussing at the fryer basket. It came out of her now short and loud and surprised, like a bark, and she clapped her wet hand over her mouth and glared at him over it, and he stood there with water running off his chin and his hair, and his mouth went up on one side.

Dot went back out to the dining room.

Lorraine was helping Brynn stack the chairs. She looked up at Dot's face and stopped.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," said Dot.

"You've got a look."

"I've got indigestion," Dot said, and went to wipe down the tables, and found she was thinking, for no reason, of Gil. Of Gil at the sink in their old trailer in 1966 with his shirt off because he'd spilled coffee on it, doing the dishes because she'd burned her hand, and her flicking water at him, and him turning around just exactly that slow.

She wiped down the tables. She was sixty-four years old. She didn't have time for that kind of nonsense.

*Huh,* she thought anyway.

---

Leah walked him down to the dock at eight o'clock with her hands jammed in her hoodie pocket.

It was properly cold now, the kind of cold that made the hairs in her nose stiffen when she breathed in. The lake had a skin of ice along the shore, thin and gray and crazed with cracks. The stars came all the way down to the mountains.

They stopped at the end of the dock, where she'd stood with a cup of coffee the morning Kroll came down the planks behind her.

"Fourth date," she said.

He looked at her. "Was that a date?"

"You came. You brought a pie. You sat next to me. You ate everything. You did the dishes." She shrugged. "It's a date."

"Dot's going to be furious."

"Dot already knows." She took a breath. "Do it."

He went still.

"The clothes," she said. "The charm. You said fourth date. This is fourth date. Do it."

"You're sure."

"I asked, didn't I?"

He looked at her for a moment longer in the starlight. Then he nodded once.

"Stand still," he said. "It might tickle."

He kept his hands in his pockets. He looked at her — boots, jeans, flannel, hoodie, the blue toque with the white pompom — the way he'd looked at Brynn on the mountain, steady and focused, and something went over her skin like the static off a wool blanket in winter. It started at her feet and went up. It prickled at the back of her neck and the roots of her hair. It settled.

That was it.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it." He let out a breath. "Should hold. I'd test it somewhere other than the dock, though. If I've got it wrong you'll be stood on a dock in the middle of town in nothing but a pompom."

She looked down at herself. Everything looked the same. Same jeans. Same boots. A thread was coming loose on the cuff of her hoodie, the way it had been all week.

She looked back up at him.

His hair was still damp at the front from where she'd flicked dishwater at him. There was a smear of syrup on the corner of his jaw he hadn't noticed. He was looking at her with his head tipped over to one side, waiting to find out if he'd gotten it wrong.

He'd asked. Every single time. Since the tree.

Leah took her hands out of her pocket, grabbed the front of his jacket in both fists, pulled his head down, and kissed him.

He made a sound against her mouth. Surprised. Short. Then his hands came out of his pockets and settled, light, on either side of her waist, as if he was waiting to be told what he was allowed to do with them.

He was as hot as she was. That was what she'd remember. Kissing Sam, at the end, had been like touching a stove. Kissing every boy before Sam had been like kissing a cold glass of water. Kissing this one was like putting her face next to her own skin. Heat meeting heat, the same all the way through.

His mouth tasted like syrup and coffee. She felt the long canine graze her lip, very carefully, and stop.

She let go of his jacket. She stepped back.

He stood there and looked at her. His hands were still up where her waist had been, holding nothing.

"Right," he said, eventually. His voice had gone very rough. "Right. Okay."

"Don't make it weird."

"Not making it weird." He put his hands back in his pockets. "I'm extremely calm. Look at me. Calm as anything."

His ears had gone red. She'd never seen his ears go red. She was going to think about that for a week.

"Come on," Leah said, and turned and walked off the end of the dock onto the gravel, and up the bank into the trees behind the café, and didn't look back to see if he was following. She didn't need to. She couldn't hear him. She knew he was there.

At the edge of the trees, out of sight of the street, she stopped.

She stood there fully dressed, with every instinct she'd built up in a year and a half screaming that she was about to lose eighty-seven dollars of Mercantile clothes in one second, and let the fire come up anyway.

It took her the way it always did. She landed on four feet in the needles and stood there with her heart going, and looked down. Spruce needles. Frost. Nothing else on the ground at all.

*Where are they?*

"With you," Harry said, from the dark beside her. He'd changed too. He was standing among the spruce like a hole in the night, huge and silent, his eyes catching the light from Main Street. "Wherever you go, they go. Pockets and all. Change back."

She changed back.

She was standing in the needles on two feet in her boots, and her jeans, and her flannel, and her gray hoodie with the loose thread on the cuff, and the blue toque with the white pompom sitting on her head exactly where it had been, a little crooked, the way she always wore it.

She put her hand up and touched the pompom.

Then she started to laugh, and couldn't stop, and changed again right there in the middle of it — boots and jeans and pompom and all — and took off up the hill into the dark without telling him where they were going.

He caught her at the second switchback. She beat him to the top anyway.


---

The lake froze in November, all at once, in a single still night at twenty-six below.

There'd been ice along the shores for weeks, gray and rotten, coming and going with the wind. Then a cold snap came down out of the Yukon the day after Halloween and sat on the valley like a lid, and the wind died, and on the morning of the fifth Leah came out of room six and the whole lake had gone black and flat from shore to shore.

Three days later Ernie augered a hole off the end of the town dock and announced to anyone who would hold still that it was eight inches and clear as window glass, and that in sixty years he'd only seen it freeze like that twice.

On Thursday, at four o'clock, in the blue dusk, with the snow squeaking under her boots, Leah called home.

"Two weeks," her mother said, before she'd even finished saying hello. "Two weeks from today."

"I know what day it is, Mom."

"I'm just saying. Thanksgiving. Real Thanksgiving. Not the Canadian one." A pause. "You said yours was nice."

"It was nice."

"With the dishwater fight."

"I should never have told you about that."

"No," her mother agreed comfortably, "you shouldn't have." Somewhere in the kitchen in La Push the radio was on, low. "Your brother has asked me eleven times if you're coming home for it. I counted. He's going to ask me again the second I get off this phone."

Leah leaned her shoulder against the cold steel of the box and watched her breath go up past the plastic hood and freeze.

"I don't know, Mom."

"I know you don't." Sue didn't push. She'd never pushed, not really, not even when Leah was a kid. She waited, which was worse. "Emily asks about you," she said. "Every week. She comes by on Tuesdays with bread. She doesn't say much. She just asks if I've heard, and how you sounded."

Leah closed her eyes.

"And Sam asked," her mother said, more carefully. "Once. At the store in Forks. He came up to me in the cereal aisle and asked if you were all right, and I said you were, and he said good, and then he just stood there. He didn't say anything else. He looked at the Cheerios for about a minute and then he went away."

"Okay," Leah said. Her voice came out flat. "Okay."

Her mother let that sit. The radio in the kitchen moved on to music. "Lee. If there's a whoever. Bring them. I mean it. I'd like to meet somebody who can stand you for more than a month."

"*Mom.*"

"Mm-hm," said Sue Clearwater, and hung up before Leah could say anything else, which was a trick she'd learned, Leah was fairly sure, from Leah.

---

Harry was in Lisbon.

He'd told her on Tuesday night through the mirror, from a narrow room with blue and white tiles on the walls and a window full of orange roofs going down to a river. Something about cursed fado records. He'd been vague on the details and very specific about the sardines. He'd said Friday, probably, and then Solveig had said something in the background about the twenty-eighth, and he'd said a word Leah's mother would have washed her mouth out for, and the glass had gone cloudy.

So he wasn't here. That was fine. She didn't want him here. She'd only ever done one thing in her whole life that was entirely her own idea, and she'd done it on four feet, by herself, in the dark.

She went out onto the ice at eleven o'clock that night.

She changed on the shore behind the café, boots and jeans and hoodie and all. The charm held. It had held every time, eleven times now, and it still surprised her.

There was no moon. The stars were enough. They lay on the black ice from one shore to the other, every one of them, as if the sky went down as well as up, and she couldn't tell where the mountains ended and their reflections began.

She put one paw out onto it.

Under her pads the ice was so clear she could see down through it. Stones on the bottom, round and pale, getting farther away. A little fish hanging still in the cold, that flicked once and was gone. Then the bottom fell away into dark, and it was like standing on nothing at all.

She walked out. Slowly at first. Then faster.

And the lake sang.

She'd heard it from the motel at night these last few days and thought it was a power line. Now she knew where it lived. Every time she put a paw down, something answered from deep in the ice, a high bright *ping* that went racing away across the lake in every direction at once and came back changed. A chirp like a bird. A long falling whoop like somebody sliding a finger down a wet glass. A sound exactly like the laser guns in the Star Wars movies Seth had watched until the tape wore out. The ice was shifting as it cooled, settling, talking to itself, and her weight on it set it off, and the sound went away from her across the whole black lake in rings.

She started to run.

She ran straight out into the middle of the lake, where the black was deepest, and the ice sang under her the whole way, *ping* and *whoop* and *pew-pew-pew,* chasing her, running ahead of her, coming back off the far shore. She ran until the town was a scatter of yellow lights behind her, and then she stopped and listened to the last of the echoes go away toward the mountains.

Then she lay down.

Flat on her belly on the ice. Then on her side. Then all the way over onto her back, the way she had in the pumpkin field, with her legs in the air and her spine on eight inches of frozen lake and a hundred feet of black water under that, and the whole sky over her.

She didn't think about anything for a while.

Then she thought about it.

She'd come up here to be alone in her own head. She'd done it, for ten weeks. She'd thought about Sam all the way through, start to finish, without anybody watching. She'd thought about her dad. She'd washed dishes and paid her own rent, and made Brynn cry twice by accident, and eaten a whole caribou and half a chocolate shop.

Nobody had a plan for her up here. Nobody knew what she was supposed to be. She'd made it all up as she went.

And what she'd found out, lying on her back in the middle of a singing lake at the end of the world, was that she didn't want to hide anymore.

She'd run because she couldn't stand to be looked at. Poor Leah. Sam's Leah. The one who got left. She'd run so she could be somewhere nobody knew any of that, and it had worked, and now she was somebody else here — Washington, the weird American girl who didn't wear a coat, the one who threw Dale across the Prospector, the one with the pompom. She liked that girl. She liked her a lot.

But she'd been that girl the whole time. That was the part she hadn't known. The girl in the pompom had been in there all along — in La Push, in Sam's pack, at the back of every patrol with her teeth clenched. Up here was just the first place anybody had let her out.

And Seth was sleeping in her room.

And Emily came on Tuesdays with bread.

And Sam had stood in the cereal aisle and looked at the Cheerios for a full minute because he didn't know what to say.

She could go back as Leah. Walk in the front door on her own two feet, in her own clothes that didn't explode, with her own money in her pocket that she'd earned in another country, and look every one of them in the face. She could go home because she wanted to. She could leave again if she wanted to. Both. Either. That was the thing she'd come up here to find, and she'd found it on her back on a frozen lake: it was hers to decide. All of it.

*Okay,* she thought, up at the stars.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

She rolled over, and got up, and ran back toward the town with the ice singing under her the whole way.

---

She told Dot at five past six the next morning, before the bacon went down.

Dot was at the flat-top with her back to the door, scraping. She didn't turn around. She listened to the whole thing, which wasn't much — *I'm going home. Tomorrow. For Thanksgiving. The real one. I'm sorry about the notice* — and then she kept scraping for a while.

"Figured," Dot said.

"You figured?"

"You've been looking south for a week, hon. Every time you take the trash out. Like a dog that's heard its name." She set the scraper down and turned around and looked at Leah over her glasses. "Tomorrow."

"Yeah."

"Running?"

Leah didn't answer that. Dot didn't seem to need her to.

"You know why it's called Turnagain?" Dot said.

"No."

"The river, east of here. Fella named Samuel Black came up from the south in eighteen twenty-four for the Hudson's Bay Company. Scotsman. Big. Mean as a snake, they say. Walked up the Finlay and over the mountains looking for new beaver country, got as far as that river, looked at it, said *the hell with this,* and turned around and went back the way he came. So he named it Turnagain." She picked the scraper back up. "Town got the name off the river. Gold rush. Nobody had any better ideas."

Leah stood very still in the kitchen doorway.

"Samuel Black," she said.

"That's right."

Something started up in her chest. She tried to hold it down. It got out anyway, a short wheezing snort through her nose, and then another, and then she had her hand over her mouth and her shoulders were shaking, and Dot was looking at her like she'd lost her mind.

"What's funny?"

"Nothing." Leah wiped her eyes. "Nothing. I know some guys. Back home. A Sam, and a — a guy named Black." She shook her head. "It's stupid. It's just stupid."

"Hm," said Dot. She looked at Leah for a long moment. "Well. Sam Black turned around. Didn't hurt him any. Went on and did plenty."

She laid the bacon down in its rows.

"Job's here," she said, without looking up. "If you come back. Nine bucks. I'm not paying ten, so don't ask."

"Okay."

"And you're working today. Full shift. Don't think you're getting out of the Friday fish fry."

"Okay, Dot."

"Wash my pans," said Dot, and Leah went and washed her pans, and they didn't say anything else to each other all morning, and at the end of the lunch rush Leah found a saskatoon pie in a tin, wrapped in two layers of wax paper and a dish towel, sitting on the shelf over the sink next to the bleach where the chocolate had been.


---

She smelled him at a quarter past nine that night, mopping the floor after the fish fry with the chairs up on the tables.

It came in under the back door with the cold. Salt water, a different sea than hers, softer and greener. Diesel off big ships. Old stone that had been in the sun all day. Grilled sardines, port wine, burnt sugar, orange peel. And under all of it, faint and steady, the earth and the pine and the struck match.

She finished the floor. She dumped the bucket and hung up the mop and folded her apron onto the shelf, all at the same speed she always did. Dot watched her from the register and didn't say anything.

Then Leah put on her hoodie and went out the back.

He was standing out on the ice past the end of the dock with his back to the town. He'd come straight from work: the long charcoal coat with the silver clasps and the red lining, the collar turned up, the black boots patterned like scales. He was eating something out of a little white paper bag and looking up at the sky, where the aurora was starting, a pale green ribbon low over the mountains in the north.

She walked down the dock and stepped off the end of it onto the ice. It pinged under her boot, a small bright note that went away across the lake.

He turned around.

"Clearwater," he said, with his mouth full. He swallowed. "Sorry. I'm late. There was a fado singer and a cursed recording of a song about a dead sailor, and it got out of hand, and there was a sort of ghost." He held out the bag. "Custard tart. From a bakery in Belém. I bought six. I've eaten four. That's restraint, for me."

She took one. It was still hot in the middle, which was impossible, and she didn't ask. Flaky pastry and burnt-sugar custard and a dust of cinnamon. She ate it in three bites standing on the ice.

Then she said, "I'm going home."

He didn't say anything.

"Tomorrow," Leah said. "I'm going home for Thanksgiving. The American one. My mom's. I told Dot this morning." She wiped her fingers on her jeans. "I decided last night. Out there." She tipped her head at the dark lake. "By myself."

He stood very still on the ice. The coat didn't move. He had the bag in one hand with the last tart in it, and he looked at her, and his head tipped over to one side — slowly, the whole head — and stayed there.

Leah waited. For him to say *good,* maybe, the way he'd said it on the dock that first night. For him to say *I'll ring,* and hold up his mirror.

He folded the top of the paper bag over, neatly, twice, and put it in his coat pocket.

Then he reached into the inside of the coat and took out a flat leather wallet, the kind her dad had kept his fishing license in, and opened it.

There were mirrors in it, eight or nine, the size of hers, each in its own leather slot like credit cards in a billfold. Across the top of each slot was a strip of white tape with a name on it in his hard right-slanting scrawl. RON. HERMIONE. TEDDY — BEDTIME ONLY. HARGREAVES. SOLVEIG.

He ran his thumb along the row and stopped at the last one. The tape on it said VOGT — DO NOT ANSWER.

He slid it out.

"Beatrix Vogt," he said to it.

The glass clouded and cleared. A woman's face appeared in it, lit sideways by a bedside lamp. Sixty or so. A sharp gray bob flattened on one side from the pillow. Reading glasses on a chain. She looked like somebody's very strict aunt who'd been woken by the fire alarm.

"*Potter,*" the woman said. Swiss, Leah thought, or German — clipped, precise, each consonant laid down like a brick. "It is six o'clock in the morning. On a *Saturday.*"

"Morning, Director. Good, you're up."

"I am *now* up."

"Brilliant. I'm taking holiday."

There was a pause.

"You are taking what."

"Holiday. Leave. Vacation, they call it over here." He said it the way he'd said *chips* in the cottonwoods. "I've got about four years of it banked. Solveig's got a spreadsheet. Starting now."

The woman in the mirror put her glasses on, as if that would help her understand him. "How long?"

"No clue."

"You have — " She stopped. Leah watched her take a breath, a long one, through the nose. "Potter. You have the Lisbon report outstanding. You have the hearing for the Belgian boy on the twenty-eighth, at which you are the *principal witness.* You have the Kroll file, which Brekke tells me is still forty pages short and has a moose drawn on the evidence log. You have six weeks of expense claims I cannot make head or tail of, including one for a *chocolate shop.* You cannot simply announce, at six o'clock on a Saturday morning, that you are taking leave of indefinite duration. How exactly do you think you can just take off — "

"Beatrix," Harry said, quite pleasantly. "I'm fucking Harry Potter."

He turned the mirror face-down against his palm. The glass went dark.

He slid it back into its slot. Somewhere in the leather wallet, a different mirror had already started to glow, pale blue, and to buzz faintly against the others like a phone on silent. Leah looked down at it. The tape over the slot said SOLVEIG.

Harry looked at it too. Then he closed the wallet on it, and put the wallet back inside his coat, and the buzzing stopped.

He looked up at Leah.

"Right," he said. "Where we headed?"

---

Leah stared at him.

"*We?*"

"We." He put his gloved hands in his pockets. "You said home. I'd like to come. If you'll have me. I eat a lot, but I'm housetrained."

"You just hung up on your *boss.*"

"She'll be fine. She hangs up on me about once a month. It's only fair."

"And — " Leah stopped. Started again. "And what was *that?* *I'm fucking Harry Potter?* Is that supposed to *mean* something?"

"In certain circles," Harry said.

"What circles?"

His mouth went up on one side, and she saw the canine.

"Fifth-date material."

She opened her mouth to tell him exactly what she thought of that, and he held up one hand.

"Clearwater. Hear me out. Thirty seconds." He took a step closer across the ice. It didn't make a sound under him. "Where's home? Properly. You never said."

She thought about Solveig's notebook, and the request he'd filed in Geneva that nobody ask about her, and about telling him on the plateau that she wasn't saying which.

"La Push," she said. "It's on the coast. In Washington. On the reservation. There's a beach. First Beach."

He nodded once, filing it, the way he'd filed *Seth.*

"La Push," he said. "Right. And at La Push there's Sam. And a dozen other blokes you've been stuck inside the heads of for a year and a half. Paul. The one with the toddler. Your Jacob, who's Alpha now, and imprinted on a baby, and, I'm guessing, has opinions about being the biggest one there."

"He's huge," Leah said, slowly.

"I'm huger." He tipped his head. "And for a year and a half every single one of them has watched you be miserable. From the inside. Every time any of them looked at you they thought, poor Leah."

She didn't say anything. Her jaw had gone tight.

"So picture this," Harry said. "Picture it properly. You walk down onto that beach. In your hat. And next to you's me. And somebody — Paul, say — says, who the hell's that? And you say, oh, him? That's Harry. He's a wizard. He's also a wolf. He's bigger than Jacob. And we're dating." He spread his hands. "And then you don't explain. Ever. Not one more word. You just let them stand there with it."

Leah stood on the ice and pictured it.

She couldn't help it. It came up in front of her as clear as a photograph. Paul's face. Paul Lahote, who'd had something disgusting to think about every woman who'd ever walked down First Beach, who'd laughed at her in his head for a year and a half — Paul's *face,* with his mouth open. Embry and Quil standing behind him like a couple of fence posts. Jacob — Jacob, who'd grown into the biggest of them all this year, who walked around like it — Jacob getting his first look at the black wolf standing next to her, and having to look *up.*

And Sam.

Sam, looking at her. Seeing her with somebody. Seeing her not need him.

"And then think about the bit after," Harry said, very quietly, watching her face. "When they phase. Your Jacob and your brother, they'll have me in their heads every time they go out on patrol with you, won't they? Every time you think about me. And the other lot will hear about it. Thirdhand, fourthhand. They'll talk about it for years. You'll be living in a dozen heads for the rest of your life, and every single one of them is going to have to think about you *and me.*" The canine showed. "Think how much you could fuck with them, Clearwater. Honestly. It's the opportunity of a lifetime."

Leah's mouth was twitching. She bit the inside of her cheek. It didn't help.

"There's vampires," she said. "Right next door. Seven of them. Eight now. And one of them reads minds."

His ears didn't move, because he was in his human shape. But something about the whole set of his head did. "Does he."

"Edward. He hears everything you think. Everything. He'll be in your head the second you get within a mile of that house."

"He's welcome to have a go," Harry said mildly.

And she pictured that, too. Edward Cullen, with his face like a dead man who hadn't been told yet, turning to look at somebody new with that little frown of concentration he got — and getting nothing. Hitting the door. Standing on the step outside, where Harry had said he'd stay for her, and finding it locked.

The laugh came up out of her before she could stop it. The stupid one. The huff and the wheeze, standing on the ice with her hand over her face.

"And there's one of them," she managed, "who Seth's teaching to play poker."

"I'm very good at poker," Harry said. "I won a villa."

She laughed harder.

He stood there on the black ice with his breath going out white and let her laugh.

When she could talk again she said, "We're not dating."

"Clearwater." He looked pained. "You kissed me on that dock."

"Once."

"Four dates. A Thanksgiving. You flicked dishwater at me in front of Dot. Dot *saw.* Dot's going to tell people about it at the Legion when we're both dead."

Leah opened her mouth. She shut it.

"Fine," she said. "We're dating."

He didn't answer. His ears went red again, in the cold, the way they had on the dock, and she saw it, and it went straight into the place under her ribs where her wolf was lying on its back with all four legs in the air like a complete idiot.

"There's rules," she said.

"Course there are."

"We run. All the way. No popping."

"No popping."

"You don't buy anything. Not for me. Not for my mom. Not for Seth. Not one thing."

"Not one thing."

"You don't punch Sam."

He sighed. "Recreationally?"

"Not even a little bit."

"Fine." He looked genuinely disappointed. "What else?"

She thought about her mother in the kitchen in La Push, with the radio on, and the chowder, and the salt shaker on the table in the middle, where a note had been.

"You eat what my mom makes," she said. "All of it. You don't say no to seconds. You don't say no to *thirds.*"

His face changed. Something in it went quiet and careful, the way it had at the lodge when he'd talked about Teddy, and she watched him put it away again.

"That," he said, "I can do."

"Okay," said Leah.

She looked at him, standing on the ice under the green sky. At the coat and the hair and the stupid scaled boots. At the man who'd hung up on his boss to follow her home. Who'd asked, every single time. Who'd stayed on the step.

"Okay, Harry," she said.

It was the first time.

She hadn't known that she was going to say it until it was out, and once it was out it sat there in the cold air between them, her dad's name, his name, and it didn't hurt. That was what surprised her. She'd been waiting for it to hurt, every time she thought about saying it, for two months. It just sat there being a name.

He'd heard it. She saw him hear it. He went completely still on the ice, the way he'd gone still on the rock on the plateau when she'd told him about Sam. His head tipped, slowly, to one side.

He nodded, very slightly, as if she'd handed him something and he was going to be careful where he put it.

"Okay," he said. "Leah."

---

Brynn was waiting outside the Galena at six the next morning, in her ski jacket and her fleece headband, with her eyes already red.

"I *know,*" she said, before Leah could open her mouth. "Dot told me, I'm not going to make it weird, I *promised* — " and then she threw both arms around Leah's neck and made it weird for about a minute and a half, crying into the gray hoodie, while Leah stood there with her arms out and then, slowly, put them around her.

"Send me a picture," Brynn said into her shoulder. "Of the ocean. The cold one. *Promise.*"

"I promise."

"And come to Prince George in January. You can sleep on my floor. It's a terrible floor."

"Okay, Brynn."

Brynn let go, and wiped her face with both mittens, and looked at Leah, and then past her, at the man in the waxed jacket waiting on the frosted sidewalk across the street with his hands in his pockets.

"Is he going with you?" she whispered.

"Yeah."

Brynn looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked back at Leah, and something went across her face — a frown, small, puzzled, there and gone, like somebody trying to remember a dream.

"I keep thinking I saw a deer," she said. "A silver one. In the trees. Isn't that dumb?" She shook her head hard and smiled, wet. "Whatever. Go. Go before I start again."

Dot came out onto the step in her apron with her arms folded and the cold steaming off her breath. She didn't hug anybody. She held out an envelope.

"Last week's pay," she said. "And the fish fry. Don't count it in front of me."

Leah took it. It was thicker than it should have been. Through the paper she could feel the bills, and something else, stiff and square. An index card.

She looked up.

"Don't tell Brynn," Dot said, very low, and went back inside, and the bell over the door jingled behind her.

Across the street, outside the motel office, Wes was standing in his slippers and his bathrobe with a mug of coffee. He lifted it an inch. Leah lifted her hand back. He went inside.

That was the whole goodbye. She liked Wes a great deal.

---

They walked out of town together on the shoulder of the highway, in the gray first light, the snow squeaking under their boots.

At the top of the hill the sign stood on its two cedar posts with frost on the plywood. She'd only ever seen the front of it. She stopped and looked at the back, the side that faced you when you were leaving.

It was bare. Raw gray plywood, weathered, with nothing painted on it at all. Only the four bullet holes, with the pale morning light coming through them in four little rays.

"Nobody bothered," Harry said, beside her.

"Nobody ever leaves," said Leah.

She stood there a moment longer. Then she held out the pie tin in its dish towel, which she'd been carrying under one arm since the café.

"Can you," she said. "For the run."

He took it carefully in both hands, and it got smaller — the way Kroll's coat had, on the mountain — down to the size of a matchbox, dish towel and all, and he tucked it into the inside pocket of his jacket and patted it.

"Next to the chocolate," he said.

"There's not still a dead guy in there, is there?"

"Solveig took him." He looked faintly offended. "I do clean out my pockets. Occasionally."

She looked down the highway, south, where it went away between the black spruce toward the gap in the mountains, eighteen hundred miles of it, give or take, to a gray beach and a gray sea and a little house on the edge of the rez with a salt shaker on the kitchen table.

She checked her head. Out of habit. Reached around in there the way she'd done in the pumpkin field.

Her own breathing. The wind in the spruce. Her own thoughts, with nothing pressing in on them from any side. Nobody in there but her.

And beside her, on the step outside, where he'd said he'd be, somebody waiting to see which way she'd go.

She let the fire take her. Boots and jeans and pompom and all.

When she came down on four feet on the frozen shoulder of the road, the black wolf was already standing beside her, huge and silent, a full head taller than her at the shoulder, his breath going out in great slow clouds. The snow under his paws hadn't so much as creaked.

"Which way, Clearwater?" he said.

*South,* Leah thought. *Try and keep up.*

She was gone before he'd finished laughing.
