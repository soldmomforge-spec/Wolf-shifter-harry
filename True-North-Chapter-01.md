# True North

## Chapter One: A Box Nobody Knew Was There

---

### I.

The Cullens' back lawn smelled like somebody had poured bleach over a candy store and left it out in the sun to rot.

Leah Clearwater had been standing at the edge of it for forty minutes, barefoot in the wet grass where the cedars gave up and the lawn began, arms folded so tight across her chest that her forearms ached. The September dark had come on early under the clouds. Behind her the forest breathed out moss and fir sap and the river. In front of her the house breathed out vampire.

The back wall was one long sheet of glass with every light on inside, so it was like looking into an aquarium. Rosalie on the white couch with her legs tucked under her. Esme in the kitchen holding a spatula, which was its own special kind of insane, since none of them ate. Carlisle in an armchair with a medical journal. Edward behind the couch with his hand on the shoulder of the new one.

The new one had been Bella Swan a week ago. Now she was a pale shape with a fall of brown hair and eyes the color of the dregs in a wine glass. She moved too fast, then caught herself and moved again slower, like a kid trying to remember how to walk after a growth spurt. She hadn't stopped looking at the baby since she'd woken up.

Leah had known Bella Swan as the girl who'd spent a whole winter in Jacob's garage falling off motorcycles. Clumsy, pale, always cold, always apologizing. Now she was the most dangerous thing in a house full of dangerous things, and she still apologized. Leah had heard her do it yesterday, to Seth, through the glass, with her red eyes swimming. *I'm so sorry, I'm so sorry.* Like sorry fixed a collarbone.

The baby was in Jacob's lap.

Jacob Black, six-foot-seven of russet skin and hacked-off black hair, sitting cross-legged on a couch that probably cost more than his dad's house, bouncing a thing that had chewed its way out of its mother. Bronze curls, Bella's old brown eyes, the face of a four-month-old on a body that had been alive for seven days. She put her hand on Jacob's cheek, and Jacob went still and stupid in a way Leah had seen exactly once before, on Sam Uley's face, the first time he'd looked at her cousin Emily.

Imprinting. The great mystical gift of the Quileute wolves. You looked at someone, and the world rearranged itself around them, and whatever you'd had before that moment evaporated, and nobody was allowed to be mad about it because it was *destiny*. Sam had looked at Emily and Leah's engagement had evaporated in a heartbeat. Now Jacob had looked at a newborn half-vampire, and the same light had come on behind his eyes, and everybody was supposed to nod along like it was beautiful.

"You're doing the face again."

Seth came up beside her in cut-offs and nothing else, his left arm strapped to his chest in a sling Carlisle had rigged. Four days ago Bella had found out what Jacob was calling the baby and gone for his throat, and Seth, being Seth, had thrown himself in the way. The newborn had cracked his collarbone like a breadstick. It had mostly knit already.

"What face," Leah said.

"The one where you're doing math about how fast you could burn the house down."

"Thirty seconds. Gasoline in the kitchen. They'd never see it coming. They're all too busy staring at the miracle baby."

"Leah."

"I'm kidding."

"You're kind of not."

Through the glass, Jacob lifted the baby over his head and made an airplane noise, and the baby laughed, a bright bell sound that carried right through the window. Rosalie laughed too. Rosalie Hale, who'd spent the last month looking at Jacob like something on her shoe, laughing at his airplane noises, because now they were on the same team. Team Nessie.

The name made Leah's teeth hurt. He'd named her after the Loch Ness Monster. Bella had nearly torn his head off for it, and Leah's opinion of Bella had gone up exactly one notch.

"Esme made enchiladas," Seth said. "She keeps asking if you're coming in."

"I'm not coming in."

"You haven't eaten since yesterday."

"I'll find a deer."

He looked at her with their dad's wide jaw and their mom's eyes, fifteen and grinning and broken, standing in a vampire's backyard in a vampire's sling, wanting a vampire's enchiladas. He liked them. Seth liked everybody. It was the best thing about him and the thing that was going to get him killed.

"It's not so bad," he said quietly. "When you get used to the smell."

"I'm not getting used to the smell."

"You got used to Sam's head."

That landed somewhere under her ribs. She kept her face still. "Go eat."

He went. Esme met him at the glass door and touched his good shoulder, and he laughed at something she said. Jacob looked up from the baby, straight out at the dark lawn where Leah stood. He couldn't see her with all those lights on. He looked anyway. Then the baby patted his face, and he looked back down.

Leah turned around and walked into the trees.

---

She didn't phase. If she phased, Jacob and Seth would hear every thought in her head, including the one she'd been carrying around for seven days like a stone in her shoe.

She jogged the six miles back to La Push on two feet. Her mother's house was dark. Sue Clearwater was at Charlie Swan's again, which was a whole other thing Leah didn't want to think about. Leah let herself in through the kitchen and stood in the dark breathing lavender hand soap and old coffee and, faintly, from the hall closet where his flannel shirts still hung, her father.

Then she got the coffee can down from above the fridge.

Three hundred and forty dollars, babysitting money and gas-station money from before her body had decided she was a wolf. She sealed it in a sandwich bag, rolled it inside a pair of cut-offs and a black T-shirt, and lashed the bundle with a leather cord. She tied the other end around her left ankle. Her fingers did it without her.

On the pad by the phone, under her mom's grocery list—*Milk. Eggs (3 doz). Bread (4). Hamburger (lots)*—she wrote:

*Mom. I'm okay. I need to go away for a while. I'm not hurt and I'm not in trouble. Don't let Seth come after me. I'll call when I can. I love you. L.*

She stuck it to the fridge under the salmon-shaped magnet, walked out the back door into the trees, stripped, and let the heat that always sat under her skin come up and take her.

The phase hit like fire running backward through her bones. Her spine stretched, her jaw pushed forward, and her hands hit the ground as gray paws. The world exploded into smell and sound so sharp it was almost color.

*Leah?*

Seth. Of course. He'd phased to run the perimeter after dinner.

*Leah, what are you doing?*

*Leaving.*

*What? Where? Wait, hold on, I'll—*

*Don't.*

She ran.

---

### II.

East first, because west was the ocean.

Over the Quillayute in one long leap. Up through the hills and the dripping cathedral of the Hoh, the moss so thick under her paws it was like running on carpet. The rain thickened. She didn't care. Rain was the only thing on the whole peninsula that had never asked her for anything.

*Leah, come on.* Seth, stronger now, standing still somewhere and focusing on her. *Mom's going to freak.*

*I left her a note.*

*A note?*

*It was a good note.*

She felt him think about calling Jacob, decide not to, and then change his mind, because Jacob arrived in her head like somebody had kicked a door open.

*Leah.* Jacob's voice in the pack mind always came through big and flat, like a man talking in a church. *Where are you going?*

*Away.*

*Away's a direction, Leah.*

*Then I'm going in it.*

A pair of elk exploded out of an alder stand and crashed downhill. She ignored them. Hunger could wait. Moving was better than anything.

*Is this about Nessie?*

The name went through her like a nail, and she felt him feel it go through.

*It's about everything. I'm sick of the smell. I'm sick of your head and his head and Sam's head and everybody's head. And now your baby puts her hand on people and shows them movies. She did it to me yesterday, Jake. You were in the kitchen. She grabbed my arm and showed me my own face. That's what I am to her. A face. And you're going to stand next to her for the rest of your life, and Seth's going to stand next to you, and I'm supposed to stand there too, and I can't. I'd rather eat a bullet.*

The pack mind went quiet. Seth's horror. Jacob's slow, heavy understanding, and under it, because nobody could hide anything in here, a flicker of guilt.

*I'm not going to make you come back,* Jacob said.

That stopped her for half a stride. He could. The Alpha voice. One word and her legs would turn around whether she wanted them to or not.

*You'd better not.*

*I'm not. Will you at least tell us where?*

*North. That's all you get.*

*Okay.* She felt him swallow an order. *Be careful.*

She pushed him out the only way she knew how. She stopped thinking in words. Paws on wet ground. Breath in, breath out. The burn in her shoulders. She filled her head so full of the run there was no room left, and after a while Jacob let go, the way you let go of somebody's sleeve.

Seth stayed. He didn't say anything. He just ran with her, in her head, all the way down Hood Canal in the dark, past sleeping cabins and boats pulled up on the gravel. She let him. It was the most she could give him.

Past midnight, south of Shelton, he said, *Mom'll be home soon. Somebody has to be there.*

*Thanks.*

*Call her. And Leah? I love you. You're the worst. But I love you.*

*Get out of my head, Seth.*

He went. The silence he left behind was so big and clean that for a second she almost stumbled. For the first time in more than a year there was nobody in her head but her.

She put her head down and ran harder.

---

She crossed Interstate 5 at three in the morning south of Olympia, a gray streak between a logging truck and a minivan. A kid in the minivan's back seat yelled *DOG!* and then she was over the median and gone.

East into the foothills, then north along the spine of the Cascades, where the roads went to gravel and gave up. She slept three hours under a fallen hemlock and woke starving and killed a blacktail doe in a meadow below a glacier and ate most of it standing in the grass with blood on her muzzle. She'd stopped feeling bad about deer around the time she'd stopped feeling bad about a lot of things.

The border was a twenty-foot slash cut through the trees, running east and west over the mountains as far as she could see, with a little concrete obelisk on the ridge. She went over it at a lope.

North of the line the land didn't care that it was a different country. Same fir, same hemlock, same cold rivers banging down out of the snowfields. She swam one of them at dawn, a green glacial torrent so cold it drove the breath out of her even through the fur, and came out on the far bank and shook herself in a spray that caught the first sun and went gold for a second, and there was nobody there to see it, and nobody in her head to remember it but her. She stood there dripping and panting and felt, for one second, like the only living thing in the world. It was the best feeling she'd had in a year.

Then the forests got drier. Lodgepole and spruce now, the cedar gone. She ran the Fraser Canyon in the dark with the river roaring a thousand feet below and a freight train crawling along the cliff on the far side. She ran across high ranch country, burned forest, lakes like dropped coins in the folds of the land. A rancher's dogs came boiling out of a barn at her, saw what she was, and sat down. One of them whined.

She tried to keep Sam out of her head. That lasted about a hundred kilometers.

After that she thought about Sam the way you poke a bruise. Getting left, she could have handled. People got left all the time. Then she'd phased for the first time and found herself inside his head, inside every memory he had of Emily, and there was no way out. A year of it. A year of living in Sam's head while he loved somebody else. Then Jacob's head, full of the baby. Seth's head, full of Jacob. Everybody had a place except her. Only girl wolf in the history of the tribe. No period since the first time she'd phased. She'd figured out what that probably meant one long awful night with a library book, and kept it to herself, and of course the second she phased, the boys knew.

Genetic dead end. The experiment that didn't work.

She ran.

The land went up. Big country, rivers milky with glacier silt, valleys so wide she could run an hour without crossing a road. A sign by a two-lane highway said *Alaska Highway Jct. 340 km*. The nights got cold enough to see her breath. Whole mountainsides of aspen had gone gold. A grizzly stood up on a gravel bar and watched her ford a river, and she watched it back, and neither of them did anything about it.

Seth phased twice more. Once to say, *Mom read the note. She's okay. She's not okay, but she's okay.* Once to say, *I can't find you. You're too far. You're just trees.*

*Good,* Leah said, and stayed human for six hours to be sure.

On the fourth evening she came over a pass and saw the lake.

It went off south between the mountains so far the end of it was a gray line where water met sky. Jade where the sun hit it, slate where it didn't. Dark islands. On the far shore a mountain stood straight up out of the water, white-capped and enormous. On the near shore, three miles below her, a scatter of small yellow lights.

Leah lay down on the ridge with her chin on her paws and looked at them for a long time.

Her pads were raw. Her shoulders felt like somebody had been hitting them with a bat. She'd been alone in her head for four days, and it had been the best four days of her life, and she was tired of her head anyway.

She wanted a bed. A shower. A cup of coffee somebody else had made. A room full of people who didn't know her, didn't need her, and couldn't hear her think.

She got up and went down toward the lights.

---

### III.

She phased back in a stand of spruce behind what turned out to be the town dump.

The cold hit the second her skin came back, a hard bright slap across her shoulders. Anybody else would have been hypothermic in ten minutes. She untied the bundle and shook it out. Damp, creased, smelling of four days of wolf, but intact. The money was dry.

She looked like a crazy person who'd walked out of the woods. Which, fair.

The sign by the road said *ATLIN* in yellow letters with a gold pan nailed under it. The main street was gravel and empty at nine at night. Old buildings with square false fronts like something out of a Western, newer buildings that were just boxes, a lot of grass in between. A pickup with a canoe on top rolled by slow, and the guy at the wheel looked at her bare feet and then looked again, longer.

Down on the lakeshore, sitting high and dry on the gravel like somebody had dragged it out of the water and forgotten it, there was a boat. An old white wooden steamer with two decks and a pilothouse. The name on the bow said *TARAHNE*. It looked like it hadn't floated in seventy years.

Something in her chest went loose for a second. It just looked like how she felt.

She found a bed at the fourth door she tried: six log cabins behind a house at the south end of town, with a hand-painted sign reading *BREWSTER'S CABINS — WEEKLY RATES — NO PETS (WE MEAN IT)*. The woman who answered was in her sixties with a gray braid and reading glasses pushed up on her head. She took in the cut-offs and the bare feet at nine at night.

"You're either running from something or you're a hippie," she said, "and the hippies all left after the festival."

"I'm not a hippie."

The woman waited.

"I need a room. A couple nights. Cash."

"You got a car?"

"No."

"You walk here from Jake's Corner? That's ninety-some kilometers."

Leah said nothing. The woman looked at her feet, which weren't blue, and her arms, which had no goosebumps, and whatever was in Leah's face. Then she sighed.

"Forty-five a night. Cabin four. Shower's hot if you run it a minute. I'm Marg. You need shoes, Leah. Duncan's General opens at nine."

Cabin four had a double bed with a quilt, a two-burner stove, and a window looking out at the lake. Leah took the longest shower of her life and washed her hair three times to get the pitch out. Then she lay under the quilt, listened to the silence in her head, and slept so hard she didn't dream.

---

The café was called the Gold Pan, and at seven in the morning it was the only thing open that sold food. Leah could smell it from her cabin. Bacon, coffee, cinnamon. Her stomach made a noise like a garbage disposal.

Four people looked up when she walked in barefoot: two old men in plaid arguing about moose, a woman with a toddler, a guy in a Carhartt jacket with the *Whitehorse Star*. The moose argument stopped. Leah took the booth at the far end with her back to the wall.

"Morning! Coffee?"

The waitress was maybe twenty-two, short and round-faced, with a blonde ponytail, a gap between her front teeth, and a nametag that said *JOSIE*. She was pouring before Leah answered. Up close she smelled like coffee and maple syrup and drugstore vanilla body spray.

"You're new," Josie said. "I mean, the summer people are gone, so anybody I don't know is new, and I know everybody, so. The Lumberjack's good if you're hungry. Three eggs, three pancakes, bacon, sausage, hash browns, toast. Most people can't finish it."

"I'll have two."

Josie blinked. "Two Lumberjacks. Like, two plates."

"Is that a problem?"

Josie looked at her, five-ten and maybe a hundred and forty pounds. "You know what? I believe in you." She turned and yelled at the kitchen window, "Ray! Two Lumberjacks for six, *not* a typo!"

Ray, fifties, bald, stuck his head out to look at Leah, then went back in and started cracking eggs like a man with something to prove.

She ate both. Every pancake, every egg, six strips of bacon, six sausages, two refills of coffee. By the end both old men had swiveled around on their stools to watch. One said, "Holy jumping." Leah looked up at them with her mouth full and stared until they turned back around.

Josie brought the check and set something next to it: a pair of gray Nikes with pink laces, scuffed at the toes.

"I don't want to be weird," Josie said. "But these have been in the lost and found since July. Women's eight. You look like an eight. Duncan's charges like ninety bucks for sneakers because everything comes up on the truck from Whitehorse, and it's going to frost tonight." She went a little pink. "If you don't want them, put them back in the bin by the door. Nobody'll know."

She went back behind the counter.

Leah looked at the shoes for a long time.

Everybody wanted something from her. Her mom wanted her to be okay, be less angry, be her girl again. Seth wanted her to like the Cullens. Jacob wanted her on patrol. Sam had wanted her to forgive him.

This girl wanted her feet not to get cold.

Leah put the shoes on. They fit. She left a twenty on a thirty-dollar check, which was stupid with three hundred and some dollars to her name, and did it anyway.

---

She spent the day doing nothing, and it was the best day she'd had in two years.

She bought jeans, socks, and a gray hoodie at Duncan's General, which smelled like rubber boots and kerosene and had a whole wall of fishing lures. She walked the length of town in fifteen minutes. She read the sign beside the old steamer, built 1916, carried tourists across the lake to the glaciers until the Depression. She sat on a bench and watched a float plane land on the water, pontoons cutting two white lines across the jade.

In the afternoon she went back to pay Marg for another night. Marg took the forty-five dollars, looked at Leah's feet, and raised her eyebrows at the Nikes.

"Josie," Marg said. It wasn't a question. "That girl'd give away her own kidneys if you stood still long enough. Whole town knows about breakfast, by the way. Ray's telling people you're a competitive eater from Seattle."

"I'm not."

"Didn't figure." Marg folded the bills into her shirt pocket. "Tlingit word, Atlin. Means 'big water,' more or less. Lake's Atlin Lake, mountain's Atlin Mountain, town's Atlin. We're real creative up here."

She stayed human all day. She felt the itch under her skin and ignored it, because the minute she phased Seth would know where she was, and Jacob would know, and it would stop being hers. When her thoughts went places she didn't want them to go, she looked at the mountain across the water until they stopped.

That night at the Gold Pan she ate a hamburger the size of a hubcap, two orders of fries, and a slice of rhubarb pie, and Josie talked to her between tables. Josie was from Whitehorse. Ray paid better than anybody in Whitehorse and gave her a room upstairs, and she was staying through the end of September because she needed eight hundred more dollars for the practical nursing program at Yukon College in January.

"My mom's a nurse," Josie said, and her face went quiet and sure when she said it. "Best person I know. What about you? What do you want to be?"

Leah opened her mouth and nothing came out.

She'd been going to go to college. She'd been going to marry Sam. Two or three kids, and teach them to surf at First Beach the way her dad had taught her.

Now she was a wolf.

"I don't know," she said.

"That's okay. Ray's fifty-six and he doesn't know." Josie raised her voice. "Right, Ray?"

"Right about what?"

"That you don't know what you want to be when you grow up!"

"I want to be retired!"

Josie grinned and went to clear a table, and Leah sat with her coffee and felt something that might have been the beginning of the idea of being okay.

---

### IV.

She smelled him the next morning.

The bell over the door jangled and a gust of cold air came in, and riding on it was a smell that stood every hair on her body straight up.

*Wrong.* Her body knew it before she had a word for it. Her shoulders came up. Her lips pulled back off her teeth before she could stop them, and she had to set her fork down carefully because her hand had started to shake. Under her breastbone, the wolf came up out of sleep like a dog hearing a stranger in the yard.

She knew vampire: sweet and cold and chemical, bleach over sugar. This had a sweetness too, a heavier one. Lilies left in a vase so long the water had gone green and the stems had started to rot. Under that, copper, like a nosebleed. Under that, something that stung her sinuses, like the air right after lightning hits close. Like a struck match.

And over all of it, expensive cologne. A lot of it. Somebody who knew he had something to cover.

He looked like nobody. That bothered her most. A smell like that should come off a monster, and he looked like somebody's uncle. Forty-five, maybe. Medium everything. A soft middle under a good oatmeal-colored wool coat. Dark hair graying at the temples, cut neat. Clean-shaven, long in the jaw, crow's feet like he smiled a lot. Rimless glasses. Leather gloves. Expensive boots that had never seen a trail.

"Good morning, Josephine," he said at the counter. A soft European accent, careful, learned from a very good teacher a long time ago. "The usual, if Ray will allow it."

"Ray *always* allows it, Mr. Vale," Josie said. She was smiling back at him, and the smile came a beat too slow. Her eyes had gone a little glassy, like she was smiling at something just behind him.

On his way to a window table he passed Leah's booth and gave her the polite nod you'd give a stranger—

—and stopped.

Half a step. A hitch, like a pebble in his shoe. He turned his head, and behind the glasses his eyes were a very pale gray, like dirty ice, and something in them sharpened. His nostrils flared, just slightly.

"Good morning," he said. "Forgive me. I know most of the faces in town by now. Martin Vale."

He didn't offer his hand, which was good, because she'd have bitten it.

"Leah."

"Leah." He said it slowly, like he was tasting it. "Leah of the tender eyes. You're visiting?"

"Passing through."

"How lovely." His eyes went over her face, her shoulders, her arms, the way a man in a shop looks at something in a glass case. She knew what it looked like when guys checked her out. He was doing something colder, the way Sam's pack used to watch the Cullens' house from the tree line. Working out what she was made of.

"Enjoy your breakfast," he said, and went and sat by the window and opened a newspaper, and didn't look at her again.

Her pancakes went cold. She watched him. He drank black coffee and ate eggs Benedict and asked Josie how her studying was going and nodded in the right places. Once he reached out and touched Josie's wrist, the way you'd touch someone's arm to make a point, and Josie's eyes went glassy for a full second. Then she blinked and laughed at something he'd said and went back to the kitchen.

He left twenty minutes later. The smell hung by his table like smoke.

"Hey." Josie was back with the coffee pot. "You okay?"

"Who is that guy?"

"Mr. Vale? He's a photographer. He's renting the old Aldridge place down the lake road, past where the pavement ends. Doing a book on the north." Josie frowned a little. Her eyes went somewhere and came back. "He wants to take my picture."

Leah went cold all the way down. Even her.

"For the book," Josie said. "People of the north. I told him I'm not even from here. He said I had the right—" Her forehead creased. "I can't remember what he said. Something about my face. Thursday. After my shift." She shook her head like she was shaking off a fly. "Why? Do you know him?"

"No."

"You look like you want to bite somebody."

"I always look like that."

---

She sat on the gravel by the old steamer and told herself it wasn't her business.

She'd run nineteen hundred kilometers to get away from people needing her. She'd spent a year standing in the rain outside a house full of vampires because Jacob needed her to, and a year before that running Sam's patrols. She was *done*. That was the whole point.

The guy smelled weird. So what. The Germans down at the float-plane dock smelled like garlic sausage and DEET.

She thought about Josie's eyes going glassy.

*He wants to take my picture.*

Leah put her face in her hands and said a word her mother would have washed her mouth out for.

---

The old Aldridge place was four kilometers south, past the end of the pavement, where the road turned to washboard gravel through spruce and aspen. She didn't need to go up to it. She could smell it a kilometer out, lilies and copper and burnt matches soaked into the ground like fog. She left the road, worked down through the trees until she was on the slope just above it, and lay on her stomach behind a deadfall.

An old two-story log house with a green metal roof, a sagging porch, a dock going out into the lake. A black Tahoe with Yukon plates out front.

Her eyes kept sliding off the house. When she looked at the edge of the clearing, the air shimmered, like heat off a July highway, except it was eight degrees out and the shimmer didn't move. It just hung there, all the way around the clearing, in a circle.

She watched for two hours. Once he walked past a window. Once a raven landed on the roof and took off again at once, cawing, like it had touched a hot stove.

No birds in the trees around the clearing. No squirrels. Nothing alive had come within fifty meters of that house in two hours except her and that raven.

When she finally backed off into the trees her legs were shaking, and she couldn't tell whether it was from lying still too long or from wanting to phase so badly her bones were trying to climb out through her skin.

---

### V.

Mattias Vadas had lived for one hundred and sixty-one years, and in all that time he had learned to trust very few things. One of them was his nose.

He sat in the dark front room of the house by the lake with a glass of brandy and thought about the girl in the café.

She had smelled of wild magic. He had met it twice before. Once in a Carpathian village a hundred years ago, where the old women still left milk out for things in the woods. Once in a Siberian shaman who had turned into a bear in front of him and nearly taken his arm off at the shoulder. Blood magic, wandless and raw, older than Statutes and Ministries. It had a heat and a hum, and when he walked past that girl's booth it had come off her like the hum off a power line.

She carried no wand, and she'd looked at him without a flicker of recognition. Whatever she was, she did not know what he was. Her body knew. He'd watched her lips peel back when he walked in.

A shapeshifter. He was fairly sure. The old tribes of this coast had their stories, the kind wizards dismissed as Muggle folklore. Mattias had not lived one hundred and sixty-one years by dismissing things.

The waitress was for Thursday. He had been preparing her for six weeks, a light touch each day, so that when he brought her into the cellar she would come calm. The working required calm. The blood of a frightened girl was bitter and spoiled the ritual. He'd learned that in Vienna in 1903.

Seven girls in forty years, and each one had bought him six. He did not look a day over forty-five.

But that girl. Wild blood. A body that could turn itself inside out and heal. He remembered the shaman's wound closing in front of his eyes. What would blood like that buy him? Twenty years? Fifty?

There was the question of risk. There was always the question of risk. Somewhere behind him, fourteen months behind and closing, the Confederation had a dog on his trail. He had never seen it. He had only felt it: a pressure at his back in Porto, a scent of pursuit in Halifax so close that he'd gone out a hotel window with his shoes in his hand. Since Halifax there had been nothing. Eleven quiet months. He had come to the end of the world, to a lake with no name anybody in Europe would recognize, and drawn his concealment tight around himself like a coat, and waited for the hunting to go cold.

He had grown careful. Careful had kept him alive for a century and a half.

But a man could be too careful. A man could let the best thing he had ever smelled walk out of his life because he was afraid of a dog he had never seen.

She had lain in the trees above the clearing that afternoon for two hours, thinking she was hidden. His wards had felt her the moment she crossed the outer ring. She'd come back. Curious things always did.

He finished the brandy and went down to the cellar to make some adjustments.

It was large enough for two.

---

### VI.

By Wednesday evening Leah wanted to scream.

She'd watched him all day. Watched him touch Josie's wrist at breakfast for three full seconds. Watched him buy salt, two jugs of lamp oil, and a roll of plastic sheeting at Duncan's, and laugh when the guy with the beard joked about *planning a murder, Martin?* Watched him lean on the Gold Pan's counter at four, and heard Josie say *tomorrow, after my shift, yeah,* in a voice that came from the bottom of a well.

She'd stood at the pay phone outside Duncan's with a fistful of quarters for ten minutes and put them back in her pocket. Call Jacob and he'd tell her to come home, or worse, he'd come here, and then the Cullens would hear about it, and the whole circus would come rolling up the Alaska Highway and park on her lake. She'd walked past the two-officer RCMP detachment twice and pictured it: *Hi, I'm a nineteen-year-old with no ID, and I'd like to report that a photographer smells like rotten flowers.*

So she did what she always did when there was too much in her head. She went for a run.

On two legs, in Josie's Nikes. She'd stood in the trees behind the cabins with her hands on her hoodie hem and couldn't make herself phase. The second she did, Seth and Jacob would see all of it, and it would stop being hers.

South along the lake road in the last of the light, faster than any human could go, gravel spraying behind her, the cold tearing in and out of her lungs. The lake on her right had gone flat slate, the last pink light dying on the mountain across the water. She ran past the end of the pavement. She ran past the turnoff to the Aldridge place without looking at it.

A kilometer past it, the wind shifted off the lake and brought her drugstore vanilla and coffee and maple syrup.

Leah stopped so hard she skidded.

Josie's scent came down out of the trees on the uphill side and crossed the road in front of her, twenty minutes old at most, heading down through the timber on the lake side, back toward the house. He'd brought her the long way, over the hill, where nobody on the road would see. It reeked of fear, sour and sharp. But when Leah crouched to look at the footprints in the soft gravel, they were evenly spaced. Steady. Like someone strolling to the store.

Her body was walking calm, and her body was screaming.

And laid around the prints, like a man walking a dog: lilies and copper and burnt matches.

Tomorrow, Josie had said. He'd decided not to wait.

Leah was moving before she finished the thought. Into the dark spruce on the lake side, low and fast, the scent a rope she could have followed with her eyes shut. The shimmer stood ahead of her through the trees, and Josie's trail went straight into it.

Leah went after her.

It was like running through a cobweb made of static. Every hair on her body stood up. Her teeth buzzed. For a second the world went gray and fizzing at the edges and she felt a chord too low to hear, like a pipe organ in her chest. Then she stumbled out into the clearing.

Josie stood in the middle of it in her black work pants and Gold Pan T-shirt and fleece, arms hanging at her sides, facing the house. The wind blew strands of her ponytail across her face, and she didn't brush them away.

"Josie."

Leah came around in front of her. Josie's eyes were open, the pupils huge, her face calm. Tears ran down her cheeks without stopping, like a tap left on. Her lips were moving. Leah leaned close and heard it, the faintest breath:

"Help me. Help me. Help me."

"I've got you. Come on." Leah took her by the shoulders and pulled.

Josie didn't move. Leah had once lifted the back end of a truck on a bet with Embry. She couldn't shift this five-foot-three waitress one inch.

"Remarkable," said Mattias Vadas behind her. "I was told the young men of your tribe were strong. Nobody mentioned the young women."

He was on the porch. She hadn't heard the door. He'd shed the coat and the gloves and the glasses. Without the glasses his eyes were paler, hungrier. In his right hand was a stick a foot long, pale and knobbed and twisted like driftwood. Or bone.

"What did you do to her?"

"Nothing she'll remember." He came down the steps, unhurried, and stopped twenty feet away. "You've been watching me for two days. It makes me feel rushed. What are you, Leah? A skinwalker? No, the smell's wrong. Something local. Something old." He tilted his head. "Will you show me?"

"Let her go or I'll rip your throat out."

He smiled the café smile, crow's feet and all. "I was hoping you'd say that."

He flicked the stick.

A black rope uncoiled out of empty air and hit her across the chest and wrapped her three times before she could move. It cinched. She felt her ribs flex.

And the heat came up from the bottom of her, roaring, and for the first time since she'd walked into Atlin she didn't fight it. She threw herself into it.

The phase hit like a train. The hoodie and the jeans and Josie's Nikes blew apart into rags. The rope snapped in three places as her chest went from a woman's to a wolf's, and she landed on four paws with her snarl shaking the porch windows.

*—EAH?* Seth's voice crashed into her head like ice water. *Leah! Finally! Where have you—WHAT IS THAT? Who is that guy, what's he holding—*

*Not now, Seth!*

*JAKE! JAKE, GET IN HERE—*

She went for Vadas's throat.

She'd killed a newborn vampire in a clearing back in June with her own teeth. She was the fastest wolf the Quileute had ever had. Twenty feet was less than a second—

—and she hit a wall made of nothing.

It was like hitting a cliff face at full speed. Something cracked in her muzzle. She tumbled backward into the grass and lay there with stars going off behind her eyes and blood in her mouth.

*LEAH!* Jacob, slamming into her head like a door in a storm. *Get up! What the hell is that—*

"Oh, *beautiful*," Vadas breathed. He stood behind the fading shimmer of his shield with his face lit up like a child's on Christmas morning. "A wolf. Of course. A werewolf would be a slavering animal by now, and look at you, thinking. Something older. Something *better*."

She got up. Her head rang. The crack in her muzzle was already knitting, a hot itching ache.

*Grab the girl and run!* Jacob said.

*I can't move her!*

*Then just RUN!*

This time she came at an angle, feinting left, going right, low for his legs.

He barely glanced at her. He moved the stick.

It felt like being skinned. Pain in her bones, in her teeth, in the roots of her fur, in the soft wet places behind her eyes. Burning and drowning and breaking all at once, on and on. She heard a high thin awful sound like a dog hit by a car and realized it was coming out of her. Her legs gave. She thrashed in the grass and couldn't stop.

In her head Seth was screaming. It was reaching him through the pack mind, an echo of it, and Jacob was holding onto her through it, and she could feel it hurting him, and he held on anyway.

It stopped.

She lay twitching. Her legs scrabbled at the dirt and wouldn't hold her.

"That's an old curse, and a rude one," Vadas said, crouching just outside the reach of her teeth. He sounded interested. "I'll try not to use it again. It spoils the blood." He watched her muzzle. "You heal. Your jaw was broken a moment ago. Oh, my dear. I'm going to live forever."

He pointed the stick again, and silver wire came out of the air, thin as fishing line, and wrapped her legs and her body and her muzzle. When she fought it, it cut. It bit into her fur and the skin under it, and the harder she pulled the deeper it went.

*I'm coming,* Seth was sobbing. She could feel his paws pounding the ground, running north, two thousand kilometers away. *I'm coming, I'm coming—*

*You won't get there, Seth,* Jacob said, very quietly, and she'd never heard him so scared. *Leah. Stay alive. Whatever he does. Do you hear me?*

She heard him.

Vadas took Josie by the hand like a child crossing a street, and Josie went, docile, still crying, still whispering. At the top of the porch steps he looked back.

"The wire tightens when you struggle," he said. "Try not to bleed out before I come back for you."

He took Josie inside.

A low orange light came on beneath the floorboards. Candlelight, from the cellar. The smell of lilies came pouring out the open door so thick she gagged, and copper, and burning, and under it, faint, vanilla body spray.

She thought about Josie at the counter yelling at Ray. *I want to be retired!* She thought about the gap in Josie's teeth and the pink laces on the Nikes, which were in rags somewhere in this grass now. She thought about a girl with a nursing-school brochure taped to the wall above her bed in the room over the café, because Leah would bet money there was one, and about that girl laid out on a rock somewhere with her blood running in channels.

She'd come two thousand kilometers to stop being anybody's guard dog. That was hilarious. That was the funniest thing that had ever happened to her.

Leah pulled. The wire went into the muscle of her foreleg. She felt it grate on bone. She kept pulling. She'd rather lose the leg. Did they grow back? She didn't know. She'd find out.

*Leah, stop, you're going to—*

*Shut up, Jake.*

She pulled.

### VII.

Eight minutes earlier and thirty kilometers north, on a granite outcrop above the head of the lake, a wolf in a wizard's coat was eating pie out of a cardboard box with a plastic fork.

He was not enjoying it. The pie was strawberry-rhubarb, bought that morning in Whitehorse from a bakery with a sign reading *BEST PIE IN THE YUKON*, which in Harry Potter's professional opinion was a claim that needed investigating by the proper authorities. It had spent nine hours in his inside pocket. The crust had gone to paste. He ate it anyway. He'd been hungry more or less constantly for three years, and the alternative was the last of the beef jerky, which he had begun to suspect was not beef.

He'd been humming "Back in Black" for most of an hour, badly, under his breath, partly because it had been stuck in his head since a truck-stop radio in Whitehorse and partly because it annoyed Hennessey, who'd asked him twice to stop.

He sat with his footpaws dangling over the drop and his tail curled beside him out of the wet. Standing, he was six foot seven, and he did not fit comfortably on most rocks. His coat was long charcoal wool, split up the back for the tail, high-collared, with a silver clasp at the throat stamped with the small globe of the International Confederation of Wizards. Sleeves shoved up past the elbow. The fur on his forearms was charcoal-black like the rest of him, darker in the undercoat. He'd stopped bothering with the human form six days ago, when it became obvious this job was going to involve a lot of sniffing.

A small bronze mirror propped against his knee had been talking for four minutes.

"—remind you," said the face in it, "that the Canadian Ministry granted you a seven-day operational window. It is day nine."

"Aware, Hennessey."

"They've contacted the Confederation twice. The Minister's office used the phrase 'freelance cowboy.'"

Harry's ears went flat. "I'm a *wolf*. They want a cowboy, they can hire one."

"Potter."

"He's here. Within twenty miles of this rock. I can smell him when the wind's right. He's just sat behind a concealment I can't crack from outside, inside a ward ring I can't get a fix on without walking into it, and if I walk into it he'll feel me and run. So I'm waiting for him to do something stupid."

"And if he doesn't?"

"He will. He's done something stupid every six years since 1966. He's due."

Owen Hennessey had been Harry's handler at the Confederation's Enforcement Office for three years and had aged about twenty in that time, which, in Hennessey's frequently stated opinion, was mostly Harry's fault. His face in the mirror now was the face of a man whose ulcer had just woken up.

"I'm also told," Hennessey said, "you've been buying food from Muggle shops in non-human form."

"I put a glamour on."

"A woman at a bakery in Whitehorse described you to her husband as 'the biggest First Nations guy I've ever seen in my life.' Her husband is a Squib. He wrote to his cousin at the Canadian Ministry. Who wrote to us."

"So the glamour worked. Nobody screamed. She sold me the pie." Harry flicked the empty box into the air, where it burst into blue flame and was ash before it hit the moss. "For the record, the pie was shite. Put that in your report."

Hennessey pinched the bridge of his nose. "I will."

"Ta."

Harry looked south. The lake had gone the color of gunmetal. Somewhere down there behind the hills, Mattias Vadas was in a house Harry couldn't see, doing whatever a hundred-and-sixty-one-year-old murderer did with his evenings. Wagner, probably. Harry had read the file eleven times. Vadas struck him as a Wagner man.

Seven women the Confederation could prove. Hungary, Austria, Argentina, Portugal, Ireland, Quebec, and six years ago a backpacker from New Zealand outside Reykjavik. Each found three weeks after she went missing, laid out on bare rock somewhere wild, drained, the blood run into carved channels in a pattern two of the Confederation's best curse-breakers had refused to finish analyzing. Each one described in her last weeks as *distracted. Like she was somewhere else.*

He'd missed Vadas in Porto by two days and in Halifax by six hours. The six hours still woke him up some nights.

His stomach growled. He ignored it.

Three years since the Romanian mountains and a raid that was supposed to be quiet, and a werewolf named Dragomir Pescaru coming out of the dark to sink his teeth into Harry's shoulder before Harry put him down. The healers in Bucharest had done what they could, which was nothing, and then stood around his bed at the next full moon with their wands out.

He'd changed. And in the morning he'd still been shaped like this, with his own mind, and no interest whatsoever in eating the healers. The healers had been confused, then fascinated, then for several months intolerable.

The curse should have taken him the way it took everybody. Something in his blood had fought it instead, something old and asleep that had been sitting in the Potter line for generations and woke up swinging. The head of the Lycanthropy Research Unit at St. Mungo's had spent half a year on his family tree and come back with a chart full of question marks and one name underlined three times: a younger son of the Black family who'd sailed to the Pacific coast of North America with the Hudson's Bay Company in the 1840s and married into a local tribe the Black records hadn't bothered to name. Two generations later a granddaughter had come back to England and married a cousin, and that was how it had gotten into the Blacks, and from the Blacks into the Potters. A wolf gene, or something that behaved like one, sleeping in his blood the whole time. Sirius had probably carried it too. Sirius had turned into a dog for fun, and nobody had ever thought to ask why it came to him so easily.

The healers' notes called the result *a stable, non-lunar, voluntary therianthropic state*. Harry called it the fur coat. He could be a man, six-two and twenty-six and annoyingly normal. He could be a wolf on four legs the size of a small car. Or he could be this. Upright, plantigrade, himself. Teeth that could take a man's arm off. Ears that swiveled whenever he stopped paying attention to them, which annoyed him more than he'd admit.

He'd always meant to go find that tribe. See if anybody out there knew what he was. There was always another file.

"Potter. Are you listening? I need a timeline I can take upstairs. If you can't flush him by—"

The tracer went off.

A silver disk on a chain under his coat, keyed to one magical signature. It went white-hot against his chest so suddenly he nearly went off the rock, and in the same moment, faint on the south wind, he smelled lilies.

And blood.

And something he had never smelled in his life and knew in his bones the second he did, the way you know your mother's voice in a dream. Rain and cedar and salt water and fur. Wolf. A wolf that was no werewolf. A wolf that was hurt.

The disk flared again, hotter. The old bastard was casting something big enough to blow straight through his own wards.

"Son of a bitch," Harry said, and stood. "He's dropped his concealment."

"What? Potter, wait, I'll get you a team—"

"No time." His wand was in his forepaw. He didn't remember drawing it. He hadn't needed it to cast in years; it was a habit, something to point with. "He's got someone. Two someones." His nose flared. "One of them's something I've never smelled before."

"Potter—"

"Fill in the forms, Hennessey. I'll sign 'em later."

He shut the mirror with his footpaw, scooped it into his coat, closed his eyes, fixed the smell of lilies and blood and that wet-cedar wolf in his head, and turned on the spot.

### VIII.

The crack echoed off the lake like a rifle shot.

Leah jerked her head around, and the wire around her muzzle bit deeper, and she looked.

Something stood at the edge of the clearing, just inside the shimmer.

Her first thought was *bear*. It was a bear's height standing upright. Her second thought, for one crazy half-second, was *Jacob*, because the dark shape of it and the smell rolling across the grass made her think of the Blacks' garage on a summer night, motor oil and wolf and the river. Then she saw it properly.

A wolf. Standing up on two legs like a man. Jacob's height, easy, and heavier through the shoulders. Charcoal-black fur so dark it barely separated from the trees behind it. Narrow waist, long legs, a long muzzle, tall pointed ears, and eyes so green they almost glowed in the last of the light. And it was *dressed*. That was the part that shorted out her brain. A long gray coat down to its knees with a silver clasp at the throat, swinging around its legs as it stepped forward, and a long black tail sweeping out under the hem.

It had a stick in its—hand. Paw. A wand. Like Vadas's.

*WHAT IS THAT?* Seth, nearly hysterical. *Is that a werewolf? Is that an actual—*

*Werewolves don't wear coats, Seth,* Jacob said, like he'd stopped breathing. *Leah. Don't move.*

As if she could.

The wolf in the coat looked at her. Its nose flared hard. Its ears shot straight up. For half a second it just stared at her, tied and bleeding in the grass, and something crossed its face she couldn't read, because she didn't know how a face like that worked.

Then it looked at the house, the open door, the orange light under the floor.

Its lip curled back off very long white teeth.

"Vadas," it said.

It *spoke*. A low voice, rough-edged, with a growl running under it like a bass line, in an accent she couldn't place, flat and clipped, the vowels landing in strange spots. It said the name at conversation volume, the way you'd say the name of a dog that had just pissed on your carpet.

"Mattias Vadas. International Confederation of Wizards. You're under arrest. Come out with your wand on the ground. Your choice, but I'd do it."

Silence.

The orange light under the floor went out.

Vadas came up out of the cellar and through the door and onto the porch fast, the bone wand already moving, and a bolt of green light the color of a traffic signal screamed off the end of it straight at the wolf's chest.

Leah had never seen that color before. She knew the second she saw it that it killed whatever it touched.

The wolf stayed where it was. A slab of rock the size of a microwave tore up out of the ground three feet in front of it in a spray of moss and gravel, and the green light hit the rock, and the rock blew apart into dust. The wolf's wand hadn't moved. Nothing about it had moved.

"Strike one," it said.

Fire next, a roaring jet out of the bone wand like a flamethrower, so hot Leah felt it on her fur from forty feet. The wolf flicked its wand, a small, almost bored movement, and the fire stopped. It hung in the air for a second like a photograph of fire, then folded in on itself and vanished with a snap like a sheet on a clothesline.

"Strike two."

Vadas threw out his left hand and turned sharply on his heel.

Nothing happened. He was still on the porch. He looked down at his own feet like they'd betrayed him.

"Ja, about that," the wolf said. "Anti-Apparition jinx over the whole hill the second I landed. You'd have noticed if you weren't so busy trying to kill me."

Vadas's face changed. All the politeness came off it at once like a mask off a hook, and what was underneath was old, the way something left too long in a cellar is old. He pulled a short black knife from his belt and drew it across his own palm and clenched his fist, and blood dripped through his fingers onto the boards, and where it landed, the wood began to smoke. He started speaking, thick wet words in a language Leah had never heard, and the air around him went dark and heavy, and he turned, still speaking, and pointed the bone wand back through the doorway. Down. Toward the cellar.

Toward Josie.

"Don't," the wolf said.

Vadas kept speaking.

The wolf's ears went flat to its skull.

Its wand twitched, the way you'd flick a crumb off a table.

A bar of light came off the end of it, white with a cold violet edge, thin as a pencil. It crossed the clearing faster than Leah could blink. It went through the shield Vadas had thrown up, which burst like a pane of glass hit with a hammer. It went through Vadas's chest, just left of center.

He stopped speaking.

He looked down at the hole in his shirt, the size of a quarter, cauterized black at the edges and smoking faintly in the cold. He looked up at the wolf and opened his mouth. Then his knees folded and he went down the porch steps all at once, the way a puppet drops when you cut the strings, and landed on his face in the dirt, and didn't move.

The bone wand rolled out of his hand into the grass.

Nine seconds, maybe. The whole thing.

Back in June it had taken the whole pack and a houseful of Cullens to clean up one clearing full of newborn vampires, and Jacob had come out of it with half the bones on his right side crushed, pulling one of them off her. This thing had done its job with a twitch, and it wasn't even breathing hard. Leah filed that in the part of her brain that kept track of what to never, ever piss off. The list was getting long.

The smoke off the porch boards thinned and stopped. The heaviness lifted off the air like a weight off Leah's chest. The silver wire around her body went slack, then crumbled like rust and fell off her in flakes.

In her head, Seth said very quietly, *Holy crap.*

Jacob said nothing at all.

The wolf in the coat walked to the body heel-to-toe, upright, long footpaws coming down silent in the grass. It nudged the bone wand away from the dead man's hand with one footpaw, picked it up, snapped it in half, and in half again, and dropped the pieces, which burned down to nothing in blue flame.

"Should've come quietly, boet," it said to the body. "Told you. Told you twice."

Then it turned around and looked at her.

She'd gotten her legs under her, three of them, with the bad foreleg held off the ground. Her lips were back off her teeth and a growl was coming up out of her chest she couldn't have stopped if she tried. Her hackles stood all the way down her spine. She was bleeding in six places. She was the smallest wolf in the pack, hurt, facing something that had just killed a man who'd nearly killed her, and it hadn't even raised its voice doing it.

If it came at her, she'd go for its knees. And hope.

The wolf looked at her for a long moment.

Then it tilted its head.

All the way over, the way a dog does when you say a word it almost knows. One ear up, the other folded halfway down. Its eyes went over her nose to tail, slow, the gray fur and the blood and the bared teeth and the bad leg. Its nose flared again, deep, pulling her scent in like it was trying to memorize it.

The wand in its paw wasn't there anymore.

She was looking right at it. One second there was a wand, and the next there wasn't, just a big black-furred paw with five long fingers—digits—dark pads on the palm and curved dark claws at the tips. Like the wand had never existed.

"Well," the wolf said. "Hello."

She growled louder.

"Ja, fair. Rough night." Its tail flicked once. "Right. Two questions. First one: who might you be? Because you, sweetheart, are the most interesting thing I've smelled in fourteen months, and I've been through Portugal."

Leah stopped growling, mostly out of shock.

"Second question. You can say no. I'm a big boy, I'll live." It tipped its head to the other side. "Might you be interested in going out for a drink?"

The clearing was very quiet.

In Leah's head, Seth said: *Did he just—*

Jacob said: *No.*

*He did. He totally did. Leah, the giant wizard werewolf just asked you out over a dead guy—*

*SETH,* Leah and Jacob said together.

The wolf waited, head tilted, ears up, like he'd asked her the time.

Leah did the only thing she could think of. She sneezed. A huge, offended, full-body wolf sneeze that made her bad leg buckle and sprayed blood off her muzzle into the grass, and said as clearly as any word she knew: *Are you out of your goddamn mind?*

The corner of his mouth pulled back, showing a lot of teeth, which she was pretty sure was a grin.

"Get that a lot," he said. "No rush. I've got a girl in a cellar to check on, and you look like you'd rather answer with a mouth that does words. I'll wait."

He walked up the porch steps, ducked under the doorframe, and was gone into the dark house.

### IX.

Leah stood on three legs in the clearing beside a dead man and listened to her brother and her Alpha argue in her head.

*—has to be a werewolf, did you see the teeth—*

*Werewolves don't talk, Seth.*

*How would you know? You've never met one!*

*Neither have you!*

*He killed that guy in like a second and he didn't even MOVE—*

*Leah.* Jacob cut through it like a knife. *Get out of there. Now, before he comes back. You don't know what he is or what he wants.*

*He wants a drink, apparently.*

*He killed a guy in front of you without blinking, and then he asked you out. That's psycho. Run.*

She looked at her foreleg. The wire had gone deep enough that she could see something white at the bottom of the wound she didn't want to think about. The edges were already pulling together, itching like fire. By morning it'd be gone. Right now, if she tried to run on it, she'd go down on her face.

And Josie was in that cellar.

*I'm not leaving Josie.*

*Who's Josie?* Seth said.

*I'm phasing back, Jake.*

*No. Leah, stay wolf, if you phase back you can't—*

*I'll phase again later and tell you what happened. Or I won't. Either way I'm done having this conversation with an audience.*

*Leah, don't you dare—*

She let go.

The heat went out of her like a tide, and she came down hard on two knees and two hands in the cold wet grass. Naked, shaking, her left forearm laid open from elbow almost to wrist, blood running into the grass. Everything hurt.

And her head was quiet. Nobody in it but her. She knelt there and let the quiet hold her up.

Then footsteps sounded on the porch, and she remembered she was naked in the woods at night with a seven-foot talking wolf who killed people with sticks.

"Ah," said the wolf. "Right. Okay. Hang on."

He'd come out carrying Josie, one arm under her knees and one under her back, her head on his shoulder, her eyes shut, her chest rising and falling. He was looking very intently at a spruce tree twenty degrees to Leah's left.

"Not looking," he told the tree. "For the record. Very interested in this tree. Lovely bark." His ears had swiveled toward her anyway. One of them twitched and flattened, like it had been caught. "Coat's coming your way. Don't bite it."

The long gray coat slid off his shoulders without him touching it, floated across the clearing, and settled itself around her.

Heavy wool, still holding his body heat, which ran even higher than hers. It came almost to her ankles when she stood. It smelled like him: pine, wet earth, cold stone, a creek running over granite. Wolf under that. And something under the wolf that made her think of La Push again for one confusing second, cedar and salt water and the woodstove in Billy Black's kitchen. She held the coat closed with her good hand.

"Okay," she said. Her voice came out raw. "You can look."

He looked.

Under the coat he wore a black sleeveless shirt, dark trousers that stopped above his ankles, and a belt full of small pouches. His arms were long and heavily muscled under the black fur, and his shoulders were as wide as a doorway. She told herself she noticed because it was useful to know the size of something that might try to kill her.

Her wolf, curled up hot and tired under her breastbone, rolled over and *purred* at those shoulders. A low, long rumble she could feel in her teeth.

She had never heard her wolf make that noise. She hadn't known it could.

She told it to shut up. It didn't.

"Is she okay?" Leah said.

"She's fine." He came down the steps, slow, and stopped ten feet off, which she noticed. He was giving her room. "I put her under. He'd been in her head for weeks. A mind gets fragile when somebody's been rummaging around in it that long. She'll wake up tomorrow with a headache and bad dreams. I'll get a mind-healer to see her. A good one. They'll put the worst bits away where she won't trip over them."

Leah's eyes narrowed. "Put them away. Like, erase them?"

"Ja."

"You can do that?"

"Not my department. Something that delicate needs a specialist. I do blunt."

"You'd go into her head and take things out."

"He had her in a cellar, and he'd drained seven girls before her and laid them out on rocks like meat. He spent six weeks making her walk down there on her own two feet while she screamed on the inside." His eyes were steady. "You want her remembering that every night for the rest of her life? Or remembering she felt a bit funny and went for a walk?"

Leah opened her mouth and shut it.

"She keeps everything else," he said. "Her job. Her friends. Whatever she wants to be."

"She wants to be a nurse," Leah said, after a second.

"Ja? She'll be a good one, I'd bet. She was fighting him the whole time. That's why she was still alive. He needed her calm, and she wouldn't go calm." His ears tipped forward. "Takes guts."

Some of the tension went out of Leah's shoulders. Not much.

"What are you?"

His ears flicked sideways. "Rude."

"You just killed a guy with a stick."

"Wand."

"Whatever. Werewolf? Wizard? Werewolf wizard? Is that a thing?"

"Apparently it's a me thing." He shifted Josie higher. "Harry. Harry Potter. Hit wizard with the International Confederation of Wizards. I'm the bloke they send after the ones nobody else can catch. That"—his muzzle jerked toward the body—"was Mattias Vadas. Hungarian. Hundred and sixty-one. Liked young women's blood, all of it. I've been after him since last August. The rest is a long story, and a bit much for a girl bleeding on a lawn in a borrowed coat."

"So you kill people for a living."

"Sometimes. Not if I can help it." Something moved behind his eyes, there and gone. "Couldn't help it tonight. That blood curse would've reached her in a second. Getting between them would've taken me two."

She thought about the knife, and the smoke rising off the boards, and the single word he'd said. *Don't.*

"Your accent," she said. "What is that?"

He blinked; it was clearly not what he'd expected. "South African. Mostly. Grew up in England, eight years in Cape Town after. It sticks." His tail swung. "Why, you like it?"

"No."

"Liar." His nose twitched. "I can smell that, by the way. You've got a tell."

"I'll rip your nose off."

"You'll try." All teeth. "Look. I asked you two questions. Got a sneeze, which I respect, but I'm going to need something with a few more consonants."

"The drink thing? Are you serious?"

"Dead serious." He shrugged, which with his arms full of Josie was a lot of shoulder moving under black fur. "I'm not going to dance around it. I'm crap at dancing. You're gorgeous." His eyes went over her face, her chopped hair with pitch still caught in it, her mouth, and stayed there exactly long enough for her to notice. "Which, fine, on its own, lots of people are gorgeous, I can handle gorgeous. But you're also a *wolf*. And I didn't know that was a box I could tick. Didn't know the box *existed*. I've been the only one of me for three years, and then I walk into a clearing in the middle of nowhere and there you are, all teeth and bad attitude and bleeding on things. So, ja. I'd like to buy you a drink. A couple. Dinner after, if you're still hungry, and something tells me you're always hungry."

"Something tells you."

"Your smell does. Rain on cedar. Salt water. Fur. Blood, at the moment, but I'm told that washes out." His nose flared. "And something I've never smelled before and can't get out of my head. Could be very good or very bad. I'd like to find out which. Over a drink."

Leah stared at him.

Her wolf purred again, louder. It had been watching his teeth while he talked, a whole muzzle of long white teeth, canines bigger than her thumb, and none of them had come anywhere near her. Her wolf liked that a lot more than it should have.

It liked the shoulders even more.

*God,* she thought. *He's seven feet tall and covered in fur and he has a tail, and he's hot as fuck. What is wrong with me?*

The answer came up from underneath, in a voice that was hers and not hers, a low rumble that tasted like blood and pine sap: *Nothing.*

She hated that. She hated that a lot.

"You're kind of an asshole," she said.

"Bit, ja," Harry said agreeably.

"You're asking me out next to a corpse."

He glanced at Vadas. "Fair point. I'll sort him before we go. Paperwork." His tail flicked. "Wasn't going to wait, though. Somebody else might ask you. Bloke can't afford to be shy at my height. People think you're looming."

"You *are* looming."

"See, that's prejudice."

A noise came out of Leah's throat that was very close to a laugh. She was horrified and cut it off. He'd heard it. His ears had gone straight up, and he was grinning at her like an idiot, holding a sleeping waitress next to a dead man, with the moon coming up over the mountain across the lake and turning the tips of his fur silver.

"Okay," Leah said. "Conditions."

"Name 'em."

"One. You're buying."

"Obviously."

"Two. I order whatever I want, as much as I want, and you don't say a word."

"I eat six meals a day and I'm still hungry. Not a word."

"Three." She pulled the coat tighter. "You tell me what you are. What you meant about being the only one. What a hit wizard is. What *that* was." She nodded at the place where his wand wasn't. "Everything."

His ears went sideways, then forward.

"Some of it," he said. "There's laws about what I can tell people, and I've broken about four tonight already, and I'd like to keep my job. But what I can tell you, I'll tell you straight. And you tell me what you are, and how there's a wolf the size of a pony bleeding on a hill in British Columbia that nobody's ever heard of. Deal?"

She thought about the stories her dad used to tell by the fire. Taha Aki and the Third Wife and the cold ones. The secret that stayed inside the tribe. She thought about the two thousand kilometers she'd run to get away from all of it.

"Some of it," she said.

"Deal."

"Four."

"There's four?"

"No more talking about my smell."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. His ears drooped slightly. "Ja. Fair. No more talking about your smell." A pause. "Out loud."

"Oh my *God*."

"Can't promise not to think about it. That'd be lying, and you said straight."

"You're unbelievable."

"Hear that a lot."

The coat started talking.

Leah nearly jumped out of it. A man's voice, tinny and furious, coming from somewhere around her left hip: "*Potter.* Potter, you've been silent for eleven minutes. The Canadian Ministry has detected an unlicensed Killing Curse on their soil and would very much like to know whose it was. Potter, if you are dead I will kill you myself—"

She dug into the pocket with her good hand and came out with a small bronze mirror. A narrow, tired-looking face with thinning hair glared out of it. Its eyes went wide when they found hers.

"Who in God's name are—"

Leah flipped the mirror face-down against her palm. "He's busy."

"*Excuse* me—"

She tossed it at Harry. He couldn't catch it, not with Josie in his arms, so he didn't try. The mirror stopped in midair about a foot from his ear and hung there, turning slowly, like it was on an invisible string.

"Hennessey," Harry said to it. "Vadas is dead. The Killing Curse was his. Tell the Canadians they can check the residue. One civilian recovered alive. Send the cleanup team and the best mind-healer you've got. Four k's south of Atlin town on the lake road. I'll ping the location."

"Who was that woman, Potter? Was that a civilian? Was she *wearing your*—"

"Hiker," Harry said. "Heard a noise. Ran off." The mirror snapped shut on its own with a click and dropped neatly into the pouch on his belt. His ears flicked sideways. "He's going to be unbearable about that."

"He saw me."

"He saw a face for half a second in bad light. I'll handle Hennessey." He looked at Josie, then at the body, then back at her. "Right. Give me twenty minutes. I've got to get her somewhere safe and call this in, and there'll be a cleanup team through here inside an hour with a lot of forms and a lot of questions. I'd rather you weren't here for it." His muzzle twitched. "You're not on any form I've ever seen. They find out there's something like you up here, they'll want to register you. Catalog you. Run studies."

Her lip curled. "Register me. Like a dog."

"Like a creature. Which is worse." His voice went flat. "I'm registered. Took me two years of appeals to get my classification changed from 'beast' to 'being.' The forms still have a box for my teeth."

Leah looked at him for a long second.

"Nobody's putting me on a form," she said.

"Then I never saw you. You were a hiker. You heard a noise. You ran." He tilted his head. "That do?"

"Why?" she said. "You don't know me. Why would you lie on your own forms for me?"

His ears went flat, then came up slowly. When he answered, his voice was low enough that only she could have heard it.

"Because I'd want somebody to do it for me."

The wind came off the lake, cold and smelling of snow, and moved the hem of the coat around her ankles.

"There's a bar in town," she said finally. "At the inn by the water. They do pizza."

"I love pizza."

"Of course you do." She waved her good hand up and down at all of him. "You can't walk in there looking like that. Ray from the café'll have a heart attack."

"I've got a glamour. I look like a big bloke. Works on most people."

"Most?"

"Woman in Whitehorse thought I was the biggest First Nations guy she'd ever seen. I'm told that's a compliment."

"Not if you're First Nations, it isn't." One corner of her mouth went up before she could stop it. "You've got a lot to learn about Canada, Potter."

"Quick study."

"You'd better be."

"Fair warning," he said, hitching Josie higher, tail sweeping the grass. "I'm rubbish at pool."

"I'm great at pool."

"Course you are."

She turned and started up the slope toward the road, barefoot in his coat, bad arm tucked against her chest. She didn't look back. She could feel him watching her go anyway, on the back of her neck, like sun.

Twenty steps into the spruce, she stopped, put her good hand flat against a trunk, and breathed.

Her wolf was purring so loud it was a wonder he couldn't hear it from down there.

Maybe he could.

"Ten minutes," she called back down the hill, without turning around. "Not twenty. Ten. Or I'm eating your pizza."

A pause. Then his voice came up through the trees, low and amused, the growl threaded under it and every consonant clean.

"Ten, then. Order me the one with the most meat on it."

"I'm ordering you the one with pineapple."

"That's a hate crime."

Leah Clearwater put her head down and kept walking, and if she was smiling, there was nobody out there in the dark to see it but the trees.
