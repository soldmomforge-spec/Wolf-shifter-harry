# North of Everything

## Chapter Six: Lock and Key

---

Charlie Swan stayed until four.

Leah hadn't expected that. She'd expected him to eat his elk chili and drink his Rainier and make some excuse about the station and go. Charlie had never been a guy who stayed anywhere. When she was a kid, he used to come by to pick up her dad for fishing and stand on the porch with his hands in his jacket pockets rather than come in, even in the rain, even when her mom told him three times there was coffee.

He came in now. He sat at the kitchen table in her dad's chair—she noticed that, and noticed her mom notice her noticing it, and decided not to say anything—and ate two bowls of chili and talked to Harry about steelhead.

That was the part she couldn't get over.

"Bogachiel," Charlie was saying. "Upper stretch. Above the hatchery. You get a winter run in there after a good rain that'll break your heart."

"Spoon or bait?"

"Drift bobber. Little bit of yarn. Harry used to—" Charlie stopped. He looked at his chili. "Harry Clearwater used to swear by roe. We'd argue about it. Every single Saturday for twenty years."

"Who was right?" said Harry.

Charlie thought about it. "He caught more fish," he admitted.

Harry nodded slowly. "Then he was right."

Charlie looked up at him. Leah watched him do it. Charlie was seeing the glamour, she knew—some big bearded guy in a gray sweater, brown eyes, a face you'd forget—and he was looking at that face across the table for a long moment like he was trying to work out something about it that didn't add up.

"Yeah," Charlie said finally. "He was."

Layla was asleep on Leah's shoulder. Leah had her hand spread over the small furred back, feeling it go up and down. She still hadn't gotten over the smile. She kept wanting to wake Layla up to see if she'd do it again, and kept not doing it, because waking a sleeping baby was a crime against God.

Under the table, Harry's tail was lying across her feet.

---

When Charlie finally left, he kissed her mom on the cheek at the front door.

Just a quick one. Shy, almost. Leah was standing in the hall with Layla and saw it, and Charlie saw her see it, and went red all the way up into his hairline and said "Merry Christmas, Leah," to a spot somewhere over her left shoulder, and practically ran down the steps to his cruiser.

Her mom shut the door and turned around and looked at her.

"Not one word," said Sue.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was gonna say *Merry Christmas*, Mom."

"Hm," said Sue, and went back to the kitchen, and Leah stood in the hall and found, a little to her own surprise, that she was smiling.

Then she turned around and saw Harry in the doorway to her room, with his ears up, and the square bulge still in the side of his coat, and the smile went away.

"Now?" she said.

"Now's good," said Harry.

---

He put the ledger on the star quilt between them.

They were sitting on the bed. The big one, the one that filled the whole room wall to wall now, so that the Huskies pennant hung crooked over the headboard and the closet door could only open about four inches. Layla was asleep in the nest of pillows Harry had built for her at the head of it, on her back, with her paws up in the air and her tail curled around one hind leg.

The ledger was green. Long and narrow, like a shop's account book, the cloth gone soft and gray at the corners. Harry opened it to the page and turned it around so it faced her and then sat back on his heels and didn't say anything.

Leah read it.

It took her a long time. She knew all of them. Sam. Jake. Paul. Jared. Embry and Quil. Collin and Brady. Kids she didn't know at all, the new ones—*Ward, K. 14*, *Hoskins, R. 13*, *Littlesea, D. 13. Changed 14.XII*. Brown ink, small and slanting and neat, like the handwriting of an old schoolteacher.

And then her brother.

*Clearwater, S. 16. Kind. Easy.*

She looked at that for a while. *Kind.* Somebody had watched Seth long enough to write down that he was kind. And then written *easy* after it, like those meant the same thing.

And under that, herself.

*Clearwater, L. 20. Female. Gone north IX.06. Rare.*

*Rare* underlined twice.

Leah sat on the bed in her old room with her hands in her lap and looked at that word.

She'd spent a year thinking she was a freak. The only girl. The genetic dead end. The one the wolf had skipped over when it was handing out the stuff that mattered. She'd thought about it every day. Every single day, in a room full of other people who could hear her thinking it.

And somebody had been out there in the woods writing it down in a little book. Like a price tag.

"She wanted me most," she said.

Harry didn't say anything.

"She's got twenty jars, you said. And I'm the one she underlined."

"Ja."

Leah closed the ledger. She did it gently. She put her hand flat on the green cover and held it there, the way she'd held her hand on the front of his coat that morning before he went out the door.

"Okay," she said.

Harry's ears tipped toward her.

"I mean it. Okay." She looked up at him. "She can underline me as many times as she wants. I've been underlined before. Sam underlined me. The *wolf* underlined me." She picked the ledger up and held it out to him. "Get it out of my room."

He took it. He put it back inside his coat.

Then he took out the other thing. The little bundle of old brown paper, tied with a faded black ribbon, and set it on the quilt where the ledger had been, and untied the ribbon, and spread the leaves out one by one.

"This was under it," he said.

Leah looked at the top of the first leaf. The little inked shield. The chevron. The stars. The ribbon underneath with the two words on it.

"French," she said.

"*Toujours pur.*" Harry said it flat. "'Always pure.' It was carved over the fireplace in my godfather's house. It was on the doorknocker. It was on the spoons." His ears went sideways. "Kreacher stitched it on her blanket. It's the Black crest."

Leah looked at it a moment longer. Then she started to read.

The writing was old and loopy and hard, with long s's that looked like f's, and she had to go slow. But she got through the first line, and then she stopped, and went back, and read it again out loud.

"*—and the people at the river's mouth, who call themselves after the wolf, say that their young men become wolves when the cold dead come among them, and I had thought this a tale, until the night of the fourteenth, when I saw it done.*"

The room was very quiet.

"The river's mouth," Leah said.

"Ja."

"That's here, Harry. That's the Quillayute. That's First *Beach*."

"Ja," said Harry. "I think it is."

She read the rest. All seven leaves. It took twenty minutes, and she didn't understand all of it—some of it was about the ship, and the storm, and somebody called *the master*, and a lot of it was about the weather, the way old journals always seemed to be. But some of it she understood just fine. A village by the river. Longhouses. Canoes. A feast. A girl who laughed at him when he couldn't eat the fish eggs, and showed him how. A girl who walked with him on the beach. A girl whose father was a great man among them, whose brothers watched the stranger with flat yellow eyes.

And the last line, on its own, with the ink blotted where the pen had pressed too hard.

*I have asked her father for her, and he has said no; and so I shall ask her.*

Leah put the leaf down.

"Mom's story," she said.

"Ja."

"The pale man on the ship. And the girl who went with him." She looked at the bottom of the leaf, where it had been torn off. No name. "He was a *Black*. Your Black. The one the Healers found."

"Maybe," said Harry. "Probably. I don't know."

"And she was—"

She stopped.

*Her father said she was dead to them,* her mom had said, at dinner, with her hands flat on the tablecloth. *And her family stopped saying her name.*

Leah thought about a little red house with a wheelchair ramp. A mailbox, hand-painted, flaking. *BLACK.*

She'd been shutting that door for six months. Every time it came up. Every time she heard his title, every time Paul said *like Jake?*

She didn't shut it this time. She sat with it.

"Old Quil wants to see us," she said slowly. "After Christmas. Brady said."

"Ja."

"We should take these."

"I was going to ask," said Harry.

She gathered the leaves back up, carefully, in order, and squared the edges, and tied the black ribbon back around them in a bow. Her hands were steady. She was a little surprised by that.

"Harry," she said. "Who else knows about her? The woman."

"Geneva." He was looking at the ribbon. "Or they will in about ten minutes. I've got to make a call."

---

He made it from the porch.

Leah watched him through the front window, from the couch, with Layla on her chest. He stood at the far end by the old swing with his back to the house and the little square mirror held up in one forepaw. He'd done something to the air around him. She could see it, faintly, a sort of ripple like heat over a road, and she couldn't hear a word through it, even with the window cracked. She could only watch his ears.

They went flat at the start. They stayed flat for a long time. Then one of them came up and swiveled, sharply, like somebody on the other end had said something that annoyed him. Then they both came up. His tail moved, once, a short hard flick.

Then, near the end, she saw him do something she'd only ever seen him do once before, on the ridge above Tamarack Crossing. He lifted his chin, very slightly, and something in the set of his shoulders went still and certain, and she thought, *Oh. Somebody's about to get told.*

He talked for a while longer. Then he shut the mirror.

He stood at the end of the porch looking out at the alders for a moment. Then he came in.

"Well?" said Leah.

"Geneva knows," said Harry. He sat down on the floor by the woodstove with his back against the wall, the way he'd been sitting since they got here. "They're sending somebody."

"Who?"

His ears went sideways. "Tell you when they've said yes."

"*Harry.*"

"They haven't said yes yet." He leaned his head back against the wall and closed his eyes. "They will. They'll want her. They'll want her more than they've wanted anything in about forty years. But they haven't said yes."

Leah looked at him.

"And while they make up their minds," he said, without opening his eyes, "I've got something else I want to do. Can I ask Jacob to come by tomorrow?"

"Jake? Why?"

He opened his eyes. They were very green in the gray afternoon light from the window.

"I want to look at his imprint," he said.

Leah went still.

On her chest, Layla made a small sleepy sound and turned her face into the flannel and went quiet again.

"Look at it," said Leah. "How?"

"Same way I looked at the wards on that rock. Same way I look at you when you come back from a job with something broken." He was watching her face. "You said it's not in the head. You said it's the wolf. I want to see where it lives."

"Why?"

"Because you asked me to find out how it works," said Harry. "In your room. Last night."

She had. She remembered it. *We don't know how it works.* And him, sitting on the edge of her old bed with his arm around her and his paw on Layla's back: *Then I'll find out how it works.*

She'd thought he meant he'd ask somebody. Read something. She'd thought he meant *eventually*.

"Okay," she said. Her voice came out funny. "Ask him."

---

She woke up at ten past three because Layla was hungry, and Harry wasn't in the bed.

Layla was. She was on Leah's side, in the nest of pillows, yelling. Leah fed her in the dark. Then she put her up on her shoulder and climbed out over the end of the bed into the hall and went looking.

There was a light on in the kitchen.

He was at the table. He was sitting on the bench under the window with his knees up near his chin, and the whole table in front of him was covered in paper. Sheets and sheets of it. Some of it was her mom's notepad from beside the phone, and some of it was the backs of old Christmas cards, and some of it was a roll of white butcher paper from the pantry that he'd unrolled across the whole table and weighed down at the corners with coffee mugs. Every inch of it was covered in his handwriting. And drawings. Circles inside circles. Lines crossing them. Little notes with arrows. Something that looked like a family tree, and something that looked like a map of the stars, and something that looked like neither.

The silver rattle from the tower room was sitting in the middle of it all, black with tarnish. Kreacher had packed it in Layla's trunk. Leah had forgotten it was here.

He looked up when she came in. His ears came up.

"She hungry?"

"She was. She ate." Leah looked at the table. "What is all this?"

"Notes."

"On what?"

He looked down at the butcher paper for a second.

"Tell you when it works," he said.

She stood there in the kitchen doorway in his flannel with their daughter on her shoulder and looked at him for a long time. At the paper. At the rattle. At the circles inside circles, and the shadows under his eyes, which she could see even through the fur.

"Okay," she said.

She came over and put Layla in his arms. He took her without looking, the way he always did, and settled her on his chest, and she pushed her face into his fur and went quiet.

Leah went back to bed.

She lay there in the dark for a while listening to the kitchen. The scratch of a pencil. A page turning. And, very low, so low she could barely hear it through the wall, him humming to their daughter. The star song. The sad one.

She fell asleep before he stopped.

---

Jake came on Sunday at ten, on foot, in a pair of cutoffs and a Forks Spartans hoodie that he'd clearly put on at the last second for Seth's mom's sake, because the sleeves were inside out.

Seth let him in the back door. Jake looked past him into the kitchen, at the table, which was still covered in butcher paper, and at the circles and the arrows and the little notes in somebody else's handwriting, and his eyebrows went up.

"Is he doing homework?"

"I don't know what he's doing," said Seth. "He's been doing it since three in the morning. He won't tell anybody."

"Huh," said Jake.

Harry was in the backyard. He'd been out there since about nine, with Layla in the sling under his coat, walking slow circles around the woodshed with his nose down, the way he'd been doing a lot. He came over when he saw them on the back step. He didn't say anything about the hoodie.

"Morning, Jacob," he said. "Thanks for coming."

"Seth said you wanted to look at something." Jake shoved his hands in the pocket of the hoodie. "He didn't say what."

"Your imprint," said Harry.

Jake went still.

Seth watched it happen. Jake had been leaning against the back step railing with his weight on one hip, loose, the way he always stood when he was around people he knew. He straightened up off it. His hands came out of the hoodie pocket. Something went across his face that Seth had seen exactly once before, through the pack mind, in a white room in the Cullens' house in September of last year—the second right after.

"What about it," said Jake.

"I want to see where it lives," said Harry. "I won't touch it. I won't do anything to it. I just want to look." He tipped his head toward the chopping block by the woodshed. "Sit down there, if you want. Takes about an hour, maybe less. You can tell me to stop any time."

Jake looked at him for a long moment.

"Why?" he said.

Harry looked back at him. Then he looked at the house. Seth followed his eyes. Leah was at the kitchen window over the sink, with her arms folded, watching. She'd been there since Jake came up the drive.

Jake saw her too.

He looked at Leah. Then at the bulge in Harry's coat. Seth watched him put it together.

"Oh," said Jake quietly.

"Ja," said Harry.

Jake didn't say anything for a while. Then he walked over to the chopping block and sat down on it, with his elbows on his knees and his big hands hanging between them.

"Fine," he said. "Look."

---

Seth sat on the back step and watched the whole thing, because nobody told him not to.

It was the weirdest hour of his life, and he'd had a lot of weird hours.

All Harry did was walk around Jake. Seth had never even seen the stick—Leah said there was one, and that it disappeared—and if Harry had it now, he didn't use it, and he didn't say any words. He walked. Slowly. In a big circle, about ten feet out, around and around the chopping block in the frost, with his coat buttoned over the baby and his paws folded behind his back and his ears pointed at Jake the whole time. Sometimes he'd stop. Sometimes he'd crouch down, so his eyes were level with Jake's chest, and stay there a long time, with his eyes gone sort of soft and far away, like he was looking at something about an inch behind Jake's ribs. Sometimes his nose would go.

Jake sat on the block and let him. He looked like he was at the dentist.

After about twenty minutes, Harry started asking questions.

"When it happened. What did it feel like?"

Jake was quiet for a second. "Like everything let go," he said. "All at once. Everything that was holding me down. My dad, the pack, the—everything I was supposed to be. Like it all just let go of me and I was going to float off. And then she was the thing holding me down instead. Just her." He looked at his hands. "Like gravity. Or like a bunch of steel cables. I thought about it a lot, after. I never got it right."

"Did you choose it?"

"No."

"Would you have?"

Jake's head came up. He looked at Harry for a long moment.

"I don't know," he said. "I don't know. Maybe. Probably. I love her more than anything. But I didn't get a vote. It just happened." His voice went tight. "And then there was nothing else I could ever want. So. I guess I'll never know."

Harry nodded slowly. He'd stopped walking. He was crouched down about six feet in front of the chopping block, looking at Jake's chest.

"Which way's she?" he said. "Right now. Don't think. Point."

Jake pointed. Instantly, without looking, over his left shoulder. Northeast. Up the hill, toward the La Push road, toward Forks, toward the big glass house in the woods where Seth had spent a lot of last year.

"Ja," said Harry. "That's where it goes."

"Where *what* goes?"

Harry looked at him a moment longer.

"There's a cord coming off you," he said. "Right there." He touched his own chest with one claw, just under the breastbone. "About as thick as my foreleg. Gold. Goes off northeast and up into the trees and I can't see the end of it. It lives in your blood. It comes up out of the wolf in you like a root and it's got hold of something on the other end, and it's not letting go." He paused. "It's very strong. I've never seen anything like it."

Jake was staring at his own chest like he expected to see it.

"Can you see hers? The other end?"

"From here? No. She's six miles off." Harry stood up. "Doesn't matter. I can see enough."

Jake looked up at him. His face was doing something.

"You're trying to stop it," he said. "For Leah's kid."

"Ja."

"Can you?"

"Don't know yet."

Jake was quiet for a long moment. Seth sat on the back step and didn't breathe.

"Don't touch mine," said Jake.

It came out low and rough and fast. Like somebody grabbing something before it could be taken.

"Wasn't going to," said Harry. "It's yours."

Jake let out a breath. His shoulders came down.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Sorry."

"Don't be."

Jake sat on the chopping block a while longer with his elbows on his knees. Then he looked up, past Harry, at the kitchen window, where Leah was still standing with her arms folded.

"Good," he said quietly. Seth wasn't sure who he was saying it to. "I hope you can. I hope you do it." He swallowed. "Nobody should get picked for before they're old enough to pick back. That's—" He stopped. He shook his head. "I didn't think about that. Before. When it was me. I didn't think about it at all."

He got up off the block.

"Thanks, Jacob," said Harry.

Jake nodded. He looked at the bulge in Harry's coat one more time. Then he walked off across the frost toward the trees. At the edge of them he turned around.

"Hey," he said. "If it works. Will you tell me?"

"Ja," said Harry. "I'll tell you."

Jake nodded again, and turned, and was gone. A second later Seth heard him phase, and heard the heavy thud of paws on frozen ground going away northeast, fast.

Toward the other end of the cord.

---

Harry made a call after lunch, and Seth wasn't eavesdropping, exactly. He was just in the hall. Getting a towel. For a really long time.

Harry was in Leah's room with the door half shut. Leah had gone with their mom to the Thriftway in Forks for Christmas Eve groceries—a ham, a turkey, a second turkey, because their mom had watched Harry eat and done the math—and had taken Layla with her, after a long argument in the driveway that Harry lost and four new charms on the car seat that Harry won. So Harry was alone in there with the little mirror, and Seth could hear the voice coming out of it, tinny and small.

It was an old lady. Welsh, Seth thought, or something like that. Sing-songy.

"—Lord Potter, it is a *Sunday*, and I am *seventy-four*, and I was having a *lie-in*—"

"I know, Healer Pye. Sorry. I need you to look at something."

"Is it the baby? Is she poorly?"

"No. She's grand. She smiled yesterday."

"Did she, now." The voice changed completely. It went soft. "Well. Good for her. First one?"

"First one. Saved it for her mum."

"Clever girl." A pause. "What do you need me to look at, then?"

There was a rustling sound. Paper. A lot of paper.

"This," said Harry. "I want to put it on her. In the blood. I want you to tell me if I'm wrong about anything before I do."

There was a very long silence on the other end of the mirror.

Seth stood in the hall with a towel in his hands and waited.

"Lord Potter," said the old Welsh voice, finally, slowly. "Where did you learn to do this?"

"Didn't. Made it up. Last night."

Another long silence.

"Go back to the second page," said the voice. "No. The one with the circles. Hold it closer." Another pause. "And the thing in the middle there. That's the bit that sits in the blood?"

"Ja. It grows with her. Same as the rest of her."

"And it does nothing else? It only shuts that one door?"

"Only that one. Nothing else. I checked it forty times."

"Forty-three, I'd imagine," said the voice drily. "If you're anything like Mrs. Granger-Weasley."

Harry made a sound like a laugh.

"Well," said the voice. Seth heard her take a breath. "It's the neatest piece of blood-work I've seen since 1971, and I've seen some, believe me. It won't hurt her. It won't tangle with anything she's got. She'll never feel it." Another pause. "You'll want to try it on somebody grown first. Somebody with a wolf in them. Somebody who'll tell you if it itches."

"Did," said Harry. "Last night. Me."

Seth's eyebrows went up.

"Of course you did," said the voice. "Well. It's good work, Lord Potter. It's very good. Now stop showing off and go to bed. And give that baby a kiss from me."

The mirror went quiet.

Seth stood in the hall with the towel.

The bedroom door opened the rest of the way, and Harry was standing in it, stooped to fit under the frame, looking down at him with his ears tipped sideways.

"Getting a towel," said Seth.

"Ja, I can see that."

"For a shower."

"Lekker." Harry looked at him a moment longer. "Seth."

"Yeah?"

"Don't tell your sister yet. I want to tell her myself. Tomorrow. At dinner."

Seth looked at him. He thought about the voice on the mirror. *It won't hurt her. She'll never feel it.* He thought about Jake on the chopping block in the frost, pointing northeast without looking. *Nobody should get picked for before they're old enough to pick back.*

"Is it about Layla?" he said.

"Ja."

"Is it—" Seth's voice cracked. He cleared his throat. "Is it good?"

Harry's ears came up. All the way.

"Ja," he said. "It's good."

Seth went and took the longest shower of his life, and didn't tell anybody anything.

---

The knock came at ten past eleven on Sunday night.

Seth was the only one up. Leah had gone to bed at nine with Layla, both of them out cold. His mom had gone at ten. Harry was at the kitchen table again, doing something with the papers. And Seth was on the couch with the TV on low, watching *Elf* on DVD for the third time that week, because he had no self-control.

Three knocks on the front door. Polite. Not loud.

Seth sat up. He hadn't heard a car. He hadn't heard footsteps on the porch. He hadn't smelled anybody come up the drive, and he'd have smelled them. His nose was good. Everybody said so.

He got up and went to the door and opened it.

There was somebody on the porch.

They were standing right in front of the door, under the Christmas lights, in a long gray coat. Gray gloves. A gray scarf. And a face. There was definitely a face. Seth was looking right at it. It was a—it was a person's face, an ordinary one, with eyes and a nose and a mouth, and it was looking back at him, pleasantly, and Seth could not have said if it belonged to a man or a woman, or whether it was young or old, or what color the eyes were, while he was *looking right at it*. His eyes kept sort of sliding off it. Like trying to read a sign in a dream.

"Good evening," said the person. The voice was ordinary too. Middling. No accent Seth could pin. "Lord Potter is expecting me."

Seth opened his mouth.

"He is," said Harry, behind him.

He'd come down the hall without a sound, the way he always did. He had Layla against his shoulder, asleep, and a wooden box under his other arm. A plain one, about the size of a shoebox, with a lid. He came past Seth to the door and held the box out.

The person in gray took it. They didn't open it. They held it in both gloved hands.

"Thank you, Lord Potter," they said. "Everything's in here?"

"The ledger. The broken stone. A sample of the ash from the jars. My statement." Harry's voice was very flat. "And a list of the twenty names. I want a copy of that list back in Samuel Uley's hands by the first of January, with a note saying what you're doing about it."

"That's irregular."

"Ja," said Harry. "It is."

The person in gray looked at him a moment. Seth tried, really hard, to see what their face did. He couldn't.

"I'll see to it," they said.

"And you'll tell me when you've got her."

"We'll tell you when we've got her." A small pause. "We've wanted this one for a very long time, Lord Potter. Since before you were born. Since before your *grandmother* was born." The person tucked the box under one arm. "Thank you for her. And Merry Christmas."

They turned and went down the steps.

Seth watched them go. He watched them walk down the gravel, past the place where his dad's puddle used to be, past the Rolls-Royce and the Subaru, toward the alders at the bottom of the drive. And at the bottom of the drive they were just—gone. No crack. No noise. They were walking, and then there was the dark under the alders, and that was all.

Seth turned around.

"Who was *that*?"

"Tell you tomorrow," said Harry. "At dinner."

"You keep *saying* that!"

"Ja," said Harry. "Because it's tomorrow. At dinner."

Seth stood in the doorway with the cold coming in around him and tried to remember the person's face. Just to see. He'd been looking right at it thirty seconds ago.

He couldn't. Not even a little. He couldn't remember if they'd had glasses.

"That's freaky," he said.

"That's the point," said Harry, and went back to the kitchen.

---

They got the tree on Christmas Eve morning.

Seth's dad had always done it. Every year on the morning of the twenty-fourth, never earlier, because his dad said a tree that went up before Christmas Eve was a tree that thought too much of itself. They'd go out the back with the bow saw into the second-growth on the hill behind the house, Seth and his dad and Leah when she was younger and still did stuff like that. And his dad would walk around for an hour pretending to look for the perfect tree, and then pick the most crooked one in the whole forest, every single year. *This one needs us,* he'd say. And their mom would roll her eyes when they dragged it through the door.

They hadn't done it last year. Last year they'd had a little fake one from the Thriftway that his mom put on the end table. Nobody had said anything about it.

This year Seth got the bow saw down off the nail in the woodshed at eight o'clock in the morning, and stood there holding it, and didn't really know why. And Harry came out the back door with Layla in the sling under his coat and looked at the saw, and at Seth's face, and said, "Tree?"

"Yeah."

"Lekker," said Harry. "Show me where."

They went up the hill behind the house in the frost, Seth in front with the saw, Harry behind with the baby. Harry was humming. Seth knew it after about three notes, and turned around and stared at him.

"That's 'Jingle Bells.'"

"Ja."

"You're humming 'Jingle Bells.'"

"It's Christmas Eve, Seth."

"You hum *everything*," said Seth.

"She likes it," said Harry. "When I hum, she can feel it. Here." He tapped the front of his coat, where her head was against his chest. "She goes straight to sleep. Your sister thinks I'm doing it on purpose."

"Are you?"

"Ja," said Harry. "Mostly."

They walked around the second-growth for a long time. Seth was looking for something. He didn't know what until he found it. Down at the bottom of a little gully, in the shadow of a big cedar, where it hadn't been getting enough light for years, there was a Douglas fir about seven feet tall. It had grown sideways looking for the sun. Its trunk went up and bent and went up again. It had a big bare patch on one side and a lot of branches bunched together on the other, and the top of it leaned off to the left like it was trying to see something over its shoulder.

Seth stood and looked at it.

Harry came and stood next to him. He looked at the tree for a long time with his head tipped to one side.

"That one?" he said.

"It's really crooked," said Seth.

"Ja."

"Dad always picked the crooked ones."

Harry was quiet a moment.

"Then it's that one," he said.

Seth cut it down. Harry didn't offer to do it for him, and Seth was glad. It took about ten minutes with the bow saw and his hands were sweating in the cold by the end of it, and when it went over it landed in the frost with a soft heavy *whump*, and Seth stood there breathing and looking at it, and his eyes were stinging, and he didn't know why.

Harry picked it up. One forepaw, around the trunk. Lifted it up off the ground like a broom and put it over his shoulder, the side away from Layla.

"Your mum's going to roll her eyes," he said.

"Yeah," said Seth. "She is."

She did. When they dragged it in the back door, and stood it up in the corner of the living room in the old red metal stand that came out of the attic every year, leaning off to the left like it was trying to see out the window, his mom came in from the kitchen with flour on her hands and looked at it and rolled her eyes so hard Seth thought she might sprain something.

Then she went over and touched one of the branches. Just touched it. And stood there for a while.

"It needs us," she said.

Leah came in behind her with a box of ornaments from the attic. She looked at the tree. Then she looked at Seth, and then at Harry, and then she put the box down and went back to the kitchen without saying anything, really fast. Seth heard the water running in the sink for a long time.

They decorated it that afternoon. All the old ornaments. The ones from when Seth and Leah were little, the macaroni star and the paper-plate angel and the clothespin reindeer with one leg missing. The glass ones from Grandma Clearwater. And at the very bottom of the box, wrapped in newspaper from 1998, the carved wooden salmon his dad had made, that went on the top every year instead of a star, because his dad said what was the point of a star.

Harry put it on. He was the only one tall enough to reach without the step stool.

At four o'clock, an owl flew in through the kitchen window.

It was a big brown one, ragged and old, with a bad attitude and a parcel tied to its leg almost as big as it was. It came in through the two-inch gap at the top of the window over the sink—Seth didn't know how; it just sort of folded itself up and *came through*—and landed on the kitchen table in a shower of flour and dropped the parcel and stuck out its leg for a treat and glared at everybody.

Seth's mom stood at the stove with a wooden spoon in her hand and looked at it.

"Kreacher," said Harry, and gave it a strip of bacon.

The parcel was wrapped in brown paper and tied with black string. It had a label on it in a handwriting so shaky and spidery Seth could barely read it.

*FOR THE HEIR. FOR HER FIRST CHRISTMAS. FROM KREACHER.*

Leah opened it.

Inside, folded in tissue paper, there was a sweater. A tiny one. It was knitted out of soft dark gray wool, and it had a hole in the back for a tail, and two little holes in the hood for ears, and on the front, in black wool, very small and neat, there was the shield with the stripe across it and the stars.

There was a second, smaller package underneath. It was a tin, and it said *FOR MASTER*, and inside it there was a treacle tart.

And a note, folded small, in the same shaky spidery handwriting.

*Kreacher hopes the Lady and the heir and Master are well. Kreacher has cleaned the house three times. Kreacher has polished the cradle. Kreacher waits.*

*Kreacher wishes the Lady a happy Christmas.*

Leah read it twice. Then she folded it up, very carefully, and put it in the pocket of Harry's flannel, next to the other things she kept in there.

"He called me *the Lady*," she said.

"Ja," said Harry. "He's coming round."

Layla would not settle.

It started around five, while the ham was in the oven and the first turkey was resting on the counter under foil and the second turkey was still going. She'd eaten. She'd been changed. She'd been jiggled and rocked and walked up and down the hall and sung to, by Leah, badly, and by Seth, worse. She'd been licked, by Harry, ears and face and the whole length of her tail, and she'd purred through that and then started yelling again the second he stopped.

"She's overtired," said Leah's mom, from the stove. "You two had her up the hill and down to the beach and out to the lake and back again, and Kreacher's owl, and all those people. She's had a big week."

"She's four weeks old," said Leah. "She's had a big *life*."

Layla agreed with this, loudly.

Harry was on the bench under the window with her lying along his forearm on her stomach, looking down at her with his ears tipped. He'd tried everything. Leah could see him going through the list in his head. He'd tried humming the star song, which usually worked, and Seger, which usually worked, and "Highway to Hell," which Leah had banned and which also usually worked.

He looked at her for a long moment.

Then he lifted her up off his arm, in both forepaws, and put her on top of his head.

Leah stopped with a stack of plates in her hands.

He did it very carefully. He set Layla down on her stomach in the thick black fur between his ears, right on top of his skull, facing forward, the way you'd set a cat down on the back of a couch. Her little forepaws hung down on either side of his forehead, just above his eyes. Her hind paws went down the back of his head toward his neck. Her chin rested on the crown of his head, between the bases of his ears, and her own ears, poking out through the holes in Kreacher's new gray sweater, were pointing forward exactly the same way his were. Her tail hung down the back of his neck and curled.

He took his paws away.

She stayed there.

Leah could see why. There was a faint shimmer around Layla's small body, there and gone, the way the air shimmered around his paw when he was doing something to a ward. She was stuck. She was stuck to the top of his head like a burr on a dog.

Layla stopped crying.

She stopped like somebody had flipped a switch. She lay there on top of his head with her forepaws hanging down over his eyebrows and her little blurry green eyes wide open, and looked out at the kitchen from six and a half feet up, at the lights and the steam and the turkey and her grandmother at the stove. Her nose went. Her ears went.

Then Harry started humming the star song again, low, and Leah saw Layla's whole body go loose. Her eyes went half shut. Her chin sank down into the fur on the top of his skull. And from somewhere deep in her tiny chest came the kettle sound, the rumbling chirrup, and it went on and on.

Harry rolled his eyes up, as far as they'd go, as if he could see her up there.

"She likes it," he said. "She can feel it when I hum. Right through the bone."

"You've stuck our daughter to your *head*," said Leah.

"Ja."

"With *magic*."

"Very gentle magic. She can't fall off. Couldn't if she tried." He reached up, carefully, and touched the tip of one claw to Layla's back, and she purred louder. "And I've got both paws free for the ham."

Leah's mom had turned around from the stove. She stood there with a wooden spoon in one hand and an oven mitt on the other, looking at the six-foot-seven black wolf on her kitchen bench with a sleeping baby balanced on top of his head like a fur hat with a tail, and her face did something Leah had never seen it do.

She put the spoon down. She went into the living room. She came back with the camera—the old silver digital one that Seth had given her two Christmases ago and that she'd used maybe three times—and pointed it at Harry, and took a picture.

The flash went off.

Layla didn't even wake up.

"That's going on the fridge," said Sue.

---

They ate at six.

Her mom had put both leaves in the table, so it went all the way from the kitchen into the doorway of the living room, and covered it with her mother's good white cloth with the embroidered edges. The blue-rimmed plates. The cloth napkins. Candles—two red ones, in the brass holders that only came out at Christmas. The ham in the middle, shiny and studded with cloves. Both turkeys. Mashed potatoes. Sweet potatoes with the marshmallows on top that her dad had always insisted on and that nobody else liked, which her mom had made anyway. Fry bread. Green beans. A huge bowl of stuffing. Cranberry sauce out of a can, still in the shape of the can, the way Seth liked it. Kreacher's treacle tart on the counter, and a huckleberry pie her mom had made out of berries she'd frozen in August.

Harry sat on the bench, because nothing else would hold him. Layla stayed on his head.

He ate a whole turkey.

Leah watched him do it. So did her mom. So did Seth, with his fork stopped halfway to his mouth. He did it slowly and steadily and tidily, working his way through it, a plate at a time, with his napkin on his knee and the baby asleep on top of his skull, purring, the whole time. Every so often he'd hum a few bars of something to keep her going. Every so often he'd reach up without looking and touch one claw to her back.

"Pass the stuffing," he said, at one point, to Seth, and Seth passed it without taking his eyes off Layla.

"Doesn't that—" Seth said. "Doesn't that, like, *hurt*? On your head?"

"She weighs about as much as a bag of sugar."

"But she's on your *head*."

"She's very comfortable."

"How do you *chew*?"

Harry considered this. He chewed. Layla's whole small body went up and down, gently, with his jaw. She didn't wake up.

"Like that," said Harry.

Seth put his face in his hands.

Leah laughed. She'd been laughing on and off all evening. She'd come in from the living room with the plates two hours ago and seen her husband with their daughter on his head, and something had cracked open in her chest, and she hadn't been able to get it shut since. It was Christmas Eve. Her mom was at the head of the table. Her brother was across from her, kicking her ankle under the table on purpose the way he'd done since he was four. The tree was in the corner with her dad's salmon on top, leaning off to the left like it was trying to see out the window.

She hadn't thought she'd ever have this again. She'd written it off. On a ridge in the Cascades fifteen months ago, at a dead run with a dry bag tied to her leg, she'd thought: *that's it, that's done, you don't get that back.*

She looked at Harry, eating his turkey, with Layla on his head.

He'd stopped eating.

He'd put his fork down on the edge of his plate, carefully, and his napkin beside it. His ears had come forward. Layla's ears, on top of his head, had come forward too, though she was still asleep. He was looking at her.

"Leah," he said.

Something in her stomach went still.

"What."

"You can stop worrying about the imprinting," said Harry.

---

The kitchen went quiet.

Leah's mom had been reaching for the cranberry sauce. Her hand stopped. Seth didn't move at all.

Leah sat there and looked at him.

"What," she said again.

"It's done," said Harry. "Layla's got one. It went on this morning. And I've got one for you, if you want it."

"*Got one what*?"

He folded his paws on the white tablecloth.

"Jacob let me look at his," he said. "Yesterday. In the yard. It's in the blood. I could see it. Like a cord, coming up out of the wolf in him and going off into the trees, northeast, to wherever she is. It's got hold of something on the other end, and it's not letting go of it, and it never will." His voice was very plain. "That's why your ring didn't cover it. The ring's for your head. Nothing gets in there. This one comes in a different way. Through the blood. Through the wolf. There's a door, sort of, down there, that it uses. It goes out of you through that door when it's you doing it, and it comes in through that door when it's somebody else."

Leah couldn't say anything.

"So I made a lock," said Harry. "For that door. Only that door. It doesn't touch anything else. It doesn't touch the wolf. It doesn't touch your head, or anything you feel, or anybody you love, or who you pick. It just shuts that one door, and locks it. Nothing comes in that way. Nothing goes out." He paused. "Nobody can ever imprint on her. And she'll never imprint on anybody. If she wants somebody, someday, she'll have to look at them and decide. Same as anybody."

Across the table, Seth made a small sound.

"It's in her blood," Harry went on. "It grows with her. She'll never feel it. Healer Pye looked it over yesterday on the mirror for an hour and said it won't hurt her and it won't tangle with anything she's got." He paused. "Then she told me to stop showing off and go to bed."

"When?" said Leah. Her voice came out in a croak. "When did you put it on her?"

"This morning. On the hill. When we got the tree."

"*What*?" said Seth.

"While you were sawing," said Harry. "You were concentrating. It took about a minute."

"I was *right there*!"

"Ja. You were very good with the saw."

Leah's mom had put her hand over her mouth.

Leah looked up. At the top of his head. At Layla, asleep up there in Kreacher's gray sweater with her chin sunk in his fur, purring, with her little forepaws hanging down over his eyebrows. She looked exactly the same as she had that morning. Exactly the same as she had yesterday. She looked like a baby who'd been out in the cold all day and had a big week and was asleep on her father's head.

Leah thought about Quil on the beach, looking at a toddler in a pink raincoat. She thought about Jacob in a glass house, looking at a baby on the very first day of her life. She thought about every single boy who'd stood in that yard yesterday morning while she said *no*, nine times, and how every single time her heart had been going like a drum, because what if, what if—

She thought about herself at sixteen, at a bonfire on First Beach. And then at eighteen in Sam's mother's kitchen, watching Sam's face as Emily walked in.

"She'll never have to—" Leah stopped. Her throat had closed. She made it open. "Nobody's ever going to just *take* her. Like that. Ever."

"Never," said Harry.

"And she'll never—"

"She'll never wake up one day not able to want what she wanted the day before," said Harry quietly. "Ja."

Leah put her face in her hands.

She thought she was going to cry. She sat at her mom's Christmas table with her face in her hands and her whole body shaking and waited for it, and what came was something she didn't have a name for. Something like setting down a bag of rocks she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten it was there. Her shoulders came down about two inches. She hadn't known they were up.

When she took her hands down, her mom was crying. Seth was crying. Of course Seth was crying.

Harry was watching her face with his ears forward and his paws folded on the tablecloth and his eyes very steady and very green.

"You said you had one for me," said Leah.

"Ja."

"The same?"

"Same lock. Same door. It does nothing else." His ears tipped. "You say yes, and I put it on, and that's it. You say no, and I don't, and I'll never bring it up again. And if you ever want it off, any time, for any reason, you tell me, and it comes off. That's the whole deal."

"Why didn't you just—" She stopped.

She knew why. She'd known the second she started the question. *Nobody gets in,* he'd said, in a round room at the top of a tower, with an old gold ring on her finger. *Not even me, Leah. Ever.*

"You asked me to fix it for her," said Harry. "In your room. You didn't ask me for you."

Leah looked at him for a long moment.

"What about you?" she said.

His ears went sideways.

"You've got a wolf," said Leah slowly. "An old one. Healer Pye said. Older than mine. You've got the Black thing in your blood. If you can see the door in me and in her, then you've got one too, don't you? You could—someday you could walk into some diner, and some girl could—"

"Did mine first," said Harry.

The kitchen went quiet again.

"Saturday night," he said. "About four in the morning. At this table. Before I put anything on anybody else." He shrugged his big shoulders. Up on his head, Layla rose and fell with them and didn't wake. "Needed to know if it worked. Needed to know if it itched. Seemed like I ought to go first."

Leah stared at him.

"It doesn't itch," Harry added.

Leah laughed. It came out of her wet and cracked and too loud. She laughed with her hand over her mouth, and her mom laughed, and Seth laughed, wiping his nose on his sleeve, which their mom didn't even yell at him for.

"Yes," said Leah.

Harry's ears came up.

"Yes," she said again. "Put it on. Right now. I don't want to wait for anything ever again. You know that. I *told* you."

"On the bathroom floor," said Harry. "In Atlin."

"*Yes*, on the bathroom floor in Atlin—"

He reached across the table and held out his forepaw, palm up, the dark pads on it, the claws tucked. She put her hand in it.

He didn't do anything she could see. His eyes went a little soft and far away, the way they did when he was looking at something an inch behind your skin. That was all.

She felt it.

It was very small. It was low down, somewhere under her breastbone, in the place where she'd always felt the wolf sleep when she wasn't wearing it. A feeling like something turning over. Like a key going around in an old lock, slow and smooth, one full turn. And then a click. Soft. Final. Like a door shutting in a house where you can hear everybody you love talking in the next room.

That was all.

Her wolf rolled over, somewhere down there, and stretched, and looked around, and found everything exactly where it had left it. Then it put its head back down on its paws.

It purred.

Leah sat there with her hand in his paw and breathed.

"Done?" she said.

"Done."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

She sat there a while longer. Then she said, very quietly, mostly to herself:

"I'm never going to have to wait for it."

Harry didn't say anything. He didn't let go of her hand.

"Every day," said Leah. "Every single day. Even after Atlin. Even after the oak tree. Even this morning. I'd be standing at the Thriftway, or at the gas station, or in the yard, and some guy would come in, and some little part of me would go *what if*. What if it's now. What if this is the one, and I look up, and that's it, I'm gone, and I can't even want to come back to you." Her voice cracked. "Every *day*, Harry."

"I know," said Harry.

"You *knew*?"

"I could see you do it. You'd go still. Like a deer." One of his digits moved gently over the back of her hand, claw tucked. "Every time a car pulled into the lot at Darlene's. Every time somebody walked into the Bent Antler."

Leah looked at him.

"You never said anything."

"Wasn't anything to say," said Harry. "Till now."

It was Seth who broke it. Of course it was Seth.

He'd been sitting very still on the other side of the table, with his face red and blotchy from crying, looking from one of them to the other, and from them to the baby on Harry's head, and back. Leah could see him holding it in. She could see him holding it in for about ninety more seconds, and then he couldn't anymore.

"Okay but who was the *person*," he said.

Harry looked at him.

"At the *door*. Last night. In the gray. I couldn't remember their *face*, Leah, I was looking right at it and I couldn't remember if they had *glasses*. And you said tell you at dinner. And it's dinner."

Leah turned and looked at her husband.

"What person at the door?"

"Ja," said Harry. "About that."

He picked his fork back up. He cut a piece of ham. He ate it, while everyone watched him, and Layla went up and down on top of his head with his jaw, and purred.

"Geneva said yes," he said. "Last night."

"To what?"

"To her." Harry put the fork down. "You remember I said they were sending somebody. They sent somebody. Seth met them. I gave them the ledger, and everything else I took out of that tunnel except the journal pages, and a list of the twenty names, and my statement." He looked at Leah. "Enforcement signed her over last night. I signed it too. She's the Unspeakables' problem now."

Leah frowned. "The what?"

"Unspeakables." Harry picked up his napkin and wiped his muzzle. "Every Ministry's got a few. The British ones are in a basement in London, in a place called the Department of Mysteries. Nobody knows what they do down there. Nobody's supposed to." His ears went flat for a second, then came back up. "Geneva's got its own. The ICW ones. They're older than the British ones, and there's more of them, and they don't work for any one country. They don't have names. They don't have faces anybody can remember. I've met three, in six years, and I couldn't tell you a single thing about any of them, except one of them gave me a cup of tea once and it was very good."

"Wow," said Seth faintly.

"Thieves. Smugglers. Okes like Vulk. That's Enforcement. That's me." He turned the fork over on the tablecloth. "The Unspeakables get the things that are too old, or too strange, or too big for anybody else to touch. Things that have been around long enough that nobody remembers how they work anymore. And a two-hundred-year-old witch who's been eating hearts since before Napoleon, with a song nobody's ever been able to trace, who can call up the dead out of a lake from a hundred miles away, who's got a ledger with the names of twenty born shapeshifters in it and a plan to eat them all at once and live for a thousand years—"

He paused.

"That's theirs," he said. "They've wanted her for a very long time. That's what the one at the door said. *Since before your grandmother was born.*"

"So they'll arrest her," said Leah's mom slowly.

Harry looked at her.

"They'll study her, Sue," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"There was a man in Bulgaria," said Harry. "In '04. He'd found a way to make dead things walk without a body underneath them. Nobody'd ever seen that before. Enforcement couldn't hold him. He walked out of two cells. Unspeakables took him in the spring." His ears tipped. "He's still in a room in Geneva. They're still asking him questions. Very politely. Every day. He's very sorry about everything." He picked up his fork again. "He's been very sorry for about three years."

Seth had gone a bit pale.

"She's going to have a rough time," said Harry. "A very rough time. For a very long time. She'll be answering questions about that song till the sun goes out."

He ate another piece of ham.

Leah sat at the Christmas table and thought about a ledger in brown ink. *Clearwater, L. 20. Female. Rare.* Underlined twice.

She thought about twenty empty jars.

"Good," she said.

"Good," said her mom, at exactly the same time.

They looked at each other across the table.

Seth looked at both of them and swallowed. "Is it weird," he said, "that I feel a little bit sorry for her?"

"Ja," said Harry. "A bit." He reached up without looking and touched a claw to Layla's back, and she purred. "Don't. She had *kind* written next to your name, Seth. And then *easy*. She meant it as a price."

Seth was quiet a moment.

"Okay," he said. "Yeah. Okay. Good."

"I'm still on the school, though," Harry added. "And this house. Till they've got her. Samuel's lot are doing nights. I'm doing everything else." He looked at Leah. "And the Unspeakables don't come to Christmas dinner, so that's tonight sorted."

"Harry."

"Mm."

"Is there anything else? Anything you've been doing at three in the morning that you haven't told me about? Any more locks? Any more mysterious people at the door?"

He thought about it. Up on top of his head, Layla yawned enormously and settled her chin back into his fur.

"There's a truck," he said.

"A *truck*?"

"Tell you tomorrow," said Harry.

"*HARRY*—"

Sue Clearwater was standing at the kitchen counter at ten past six on Christmas morning, waiting for the coffee, when somebody started her husband's truck.

She knew the sound before she knew what it was. Her body knew it. Twenty-two years of it coming up the gravel road in the dark. A grinding cough, and then another, and then a rattling roar like a tractor that had fallen into a clothes dryer, settling down into a lumpy uneven idle that shook the windows.

She put the mug down on the counter so hard the coffee jumped out of it.

The truck had been sitting in the lean-to beside the woodshed for twenty-one months. Under a blue tarp. She'd had Seth put the tarp over it the week after the funeral, because she couldn't look at it, and she couldn't sell it, and she couldn't stand to see it get rained on. It hadn't started since. The battery had been dead since April of last year. Charlie had offered to look at it four times, and she'd said no four times, and he'd stopped offering.

She went out the back door in her robe and her slippers into the frost.

The tarp was off. It was folded, very neatly, on top of the woodpile, next to an Army blanket.

And the truck was sitting in the lean-to with its engine running and its headlights on, blue, with the paint gone chalky on the hood where the sun had got it, and the dent in the back fender, and the crack in the corner of the windshield. Exactly the same. Exactly the way it had looked the morning he'd driven it down to the river for the last time. Smoke coming out of the tailpipe into the cold. The whole thing shaking gently on its springs.

The radio was on.

She could hear it through the closed windows. The cassette deck. Thin and wobbly, the way that old tape always sounded, because it had been in there since 1994 and he'd never once taken it out.

Bob Seger. "Against the Wind."

Harry was standing beside the open driver's door.

He had Layla on top of his head again. She was wearing Kreacher's gray sweater, asleep, with her forepaws hanging down over his eyebrows and her tail down the back of his neck, and he was standing very still, so as not to wake her. His ears were back, careful, the way they'd gone on her porch the first night, when she'd said *of course your name is Harry*.

"Sorry," he said. "Is it too early?"

Sue couldn't say anything.

"Battery was dead," said Harry. "Plugs were shot. Mice had been at the wiring. The fuel line'd gone." His voice was very low. "I did the engine. I did the brakes. I did the tires, the front ones were bald. I didn't touch the paint." He paused. "I didn't touch the inside."

Sue walked over to the truck.

She stood by the open door and looked in. The seat. The old striped blanket Harry had always kept folded over the driver's side because the vinyl had split. The coffee can of fishing lures on the floor. A Thriftway receipt on the dash from March 2006 that she'd never been able to make herself throw away. His Mariners cap, hanging on the gearshift.

She leaned in.

It still smelled like him. Faint. So faint. Cigarettes and fish and Old Spice and coffee and him.

Her hand had gone to her mouth. She couldn't seem to get it down.

"And the hole," said Harry, behind her. "In the floor. On the passenger side."

Sue looked. There it was. The rust hole, the size of a fist, where you could look down and watch the road go by under your feet. The one she'd been after him to fix for eight years.

"I left it," said Harry. "Seth said you could watch the road go by."

Sue Clearwater stood in the frost in her bathrobe at ten past six on Christmas morning with her husband's tape playing in her husband's truck, and cried.

She didn't make any noise. She just stood there with her hand over her mouth and let it come, and it kept coming, and she didn't try to stop it. After a while, she felt a forepaw settle on her shoulder. Big. Very careful. Claws tucked. It stayed there, heavy and still.

On top of his head, Layla woke up, and looked down at her grandmother with her blurry green-gold eyes, and sneezed.

Sue laughed. She couldn't help it. She laughed with tears running down her face, and reached up, and touched the baby's small forepaw where it hung down over Harry's eyebrow, and Layla's tiny claws closed on her finger and held on.

Behind them, the back door banged open.

"Is that *Dad's truck*?" yelled Seth.

He came across the frost barefoot, in a pair of plaid boxers and the old Spartans T-shirt, and stopped dead at the edge of the lean-to with his mouth open. He looked at the truck. He looked at the smoke coming out of the tailpipe. He listened to the tape.

Then he went around to the passenger side, and opened the door, which screamed on its hinges the way it always had, and climbed in, and sat down.

Sue watched him through the windshield. She watched him put his bare feet on either side of the hole in the floor, the way he'd done when he was nine. She watched him lean forward and look down through it at the frozen gravel underneath.

He started laughing. He laughed with both hands over his face.

"You can still see the ground," he said, muffled. "Mom. You can still see the *ground*."

"I know, baby," said Sue.

On top of Harry's head, Layla let go of Sue's finger and yawned. Harry hadn't moved. He was standing beside the open driver's door with his paw still on Sue's shoulder and his ears tipped toward the tape, listening to Bob Seger sing about running against the wind, flat in exactly the places her husband used to be flat, because her husband had been singing along to that tape for thirteen years and it had worn itself into the shape of his voice.

"He sang it worse than you," said Sue.

"Not possible," said Harry.

"He did. I promise."

The packs came at noon.

Leah had told Jake they could. She'd called him at nine, after the presents, after Seth had sat in the driver's seat of their dad's truck with both hands on the wheel for forty minutes and refused to get out. After her mom had given Harry a cribbage board—her dad's, the old one with the pegs made out of nails—and told him Billy Black cheated and he should cheat back. After Harry had given Seth the keys to the Rolls-Royce for the day and Seth had made a sound only dogs could hear.

"Bring them," she'd said to Jake. "Whoever wants to come."

"You sure?" Jake had said. His voice had gone careful.

"It worked, Jake."

There'd been a long silence on the phone.

"Yeah?" he'd said. Very quietly.

"Yeah."

Another silence.

"Good," Jake had said. "Good. That's—good. I'll bring them."

So at noon she was standing on the top step of the porch, in a clean pair of jeans and Harry's red-and-black flannel, with her arms folded, watching them come up the drive.

There were a lot of them. More than yesterday. Word had gotten around. Jake in front, in an actual shirt, with the sleeves the right way out. Embry and Quil. Seth, who'd finally gotten out of the truck. And the new ones, the kids, in a clump behind—Dylan Littlesea, thirteen, who'd gone through the wall of a portable in social studies; Kyle Ward and Ricky Hoskins, fourteen and thirteen; a couple of others she didn't know at all. All of them big, all of them gangly, all of them in T-shirts in December with their hands jammed in their pockets, looking at the Rolls-Royce and then at the house and then at her, nervous.

And Brady Fuller, out in front of everybody, carrying something folded in both hands like it was a flag.

He came up to the bottom of the steps and held it out. It was the hoodie. The black one, with the kangaroo pocket. And the gray sweatpants and the socks, and the sneakers on top. All of it washed and folded very carefully, with the corners lined up.

"These are his," said Brady. "I washed them. Twice."

"Keep them," said Harry, from the doorway behind her. "They're yours."

Brady looked up at him. His mouth opened.

"Really?"

"Ja. Fit you better than me."

Brady looked down at the hoodie. His pale ear had gone red at the tip. He didn't say anything. He just held it against his chest and stepped back into the clump of boys, who all looked at it like it was a trophy.

Leah looked at Jake.

Jake looked back at her. He didn't say anything either. He just nodded, very slightly, once.

"Okay," said Leah. "Wait here."

She went inside.

---

Layla was on the couch, on her back on a towel, in Kreacher's gray sweater, awake. Kicking at the air with her hind paws. Looking at the ceiling with great interest. Harry had put her down there two minutes ago and was standing in the doorway to the porch, very still, watching Leah.

Leah picked her up.

She held her for a second in the living room by the crooked tree with the wooden salmon on top. She put her nose down against the top of Layla's head, between her ears, the way Harry did, and breathed in. Milk. Clean cold wild. Her.

Her heart was going like a drum.

She knew. She'd felt the lock go around in herself last night like a key, and she knew. Harry had said it. Healer Pye had said it. She'd lain awake until two in the morning feeling for it in the dark, that place under her breastbone where the click had been, and it had been there every time.

Her heart was still going like a drum.

"Okay," she whispered to Layla. "Okay, baby. Let's go meet your uncles."

She went out onto the porch.

Every single one of them went quiet.

She stood on the top step with Layla against her shoulder and looked at them. Ten boys. Eleven, with Seth. Standing in a ragged half circle in her mom's yard on the new gravel where her dad's puddle used to be, in their T-shirts, in the cold, with the steam coming off their shoulders.

She turned Layla around, so she was facing out, and held her up against her chest.

And watched their faces.

She watched every one of them. She'd been waiting for this her whole life, it felt like. She watched Embry, because Embry had the softest face of any of them and if it was going to be anybody it'd be him. She watched Quil, who'd already gone once, for a two-year-old. She watched the new kids, one by one. Kyle. Ricky. The ones she didn't know. Dylan Littlesea, thirteen, with his mouth open.

She watched them look at her daughter.

She watched them see the fur, and the silver at the tips of it, and the pale gray stripe down her face, and the little green-gold eyes blinking at the light. She watched them see the ears poking out through the holes in the sweater. She watched Dylan Littlesea's eyes go wide.

"She's got a *tail*," said Dylan.

That was it.

That was all. Nothing tilted. Every face in the yard stayed exactly the face it had been a second before, curious and cold and a little bit dumb. Ten big boys standing around in the cold looking at a baby, and the baby looking back at them, and that was all it was.

Leah let out a breath she'd been holding for about two years.

"Yeah," she said. "She's got a tail."

"That's so *cool*," said Dylan.

Embry came up the steps.

He came slowly, with his hands held out a little, palms up, like you'd come up to a dog you didn't know. He stopped on the step below her. He bent down so his face was level with Layla's. And he did the face. The big soft brown-eyed face, the one nobody had ever been able to say no to, not his mom, not Leah's mom, not Sam, not anybody.

Layla looked at him for a long moment. Her nose went.

Then she sneezed in it.

Embry went straight backward down the steps, and Quil laughed so hard he had to sit down on the gravel, and then all of them were laughing, all ten of them, and crowding up around the bottom of the steps to see, *let me see, let me see*, and Leah stood there on her mom's porch with her daughter against her chest and her heart going like a drum for a completely different reason now, and let them.

She felt Harry come out behind her. She felt him stop, about two feet back, right where he'd stopped the first night on this porch, and stay there. Not saying anything. Letting her.

She leaned back against him.

His tail came around her legs.

---

Jake waited until the others had wandered off to look at the Rolls-Royce.

He came up the steps slowly, with his hands in the pockets of his jeans, and stopped in front of Harry. Leah had Layla again. Jake looked at her for a second—at the tail, at the gray stripe, at the little sweater with the crest on it—and his face did something soft and sad at the same time.

Then he looked up at Harry.

"You said you'd tell me," he said. "If it worked."

Leah had told him on the phone. She'd said it plain: *it worked, Jake*. And she watched him stand there on her mom's porch anyway, waiting, like none of that counted until it came from the guy who'd walked circles around him in the frost for an hour.

"Ja," said Harry. "I did say."

"So?"

"It works," said Harry. "On all three of us. It holds."

Jake nodded slowly. He looked at Layla again. He didn't say anything for a while.

"Okay," he said finally. His voice was rough. "Good. That's—good." He swallowed. "If she ever asks. When she's older. About me and Nessie. About how it works. Tell her it's not her fault, what happened to me. Tell her I'm happy." He stopped. "Tell her I'm glad she gets to pick."

"I'll tell her," said Harry.

Jake nodded once more. Then he turned around and went back down the steps to the others, and Leah watched him go, and thought about the cord Harry said he'd seen coming off him, as thick as a foreleg, going up into the trees to the northeast where a little girl was having Christmas in a glass house.

He'd never get a lock. He'd never get to pick. And he'd come up the steps anyway to make sure Layla did.

"Huh," Leah said quietly.

"Ja," said Harry.

---

Paul came at one, with Rachel Black.

Leah saw them come up the drive, Rachel with her long hair and her college sweatshirt, back from Washington State for the holidays, and Paul beside her with his hands jammed in his pockets and his jaw set like he was walking into a dentist's office. He looked at the Rolls-Royce. He walked past it. He walked a good four feet out of his way to go past it, all the way around the back of the Subaru.

He didn't lean on it.

"Paul," said Harry, from behind her. In the voice. Round and plummy and drawling, every vowel polished. "How *lovely*. You're not leaning."

Paul looked up at him and, without breaking stride, gave him the finger.

Rachel laughed so hard she had to stop walking.

"*Splendid*," said Harry.

---

Sam came last.

Leah saw him at about half past one, at the bottom of the drive, by the alders. Alone. On two legs, in jeans and a flannel and actual boots, with a round tin held in both hands. He'd stopped where the trees ended. He didn't come any farther. He stood there in the cold under the alders and waited.

The boys had seen him too. Leah watched them go quiet. Watched Paul look at Sam, and at the porch, and at Leah, and then very carefully look at Rachel instead.

Leah handed Layla to Harry. She went down the steps, across the gravel, past the Rolls-Royce and the Subaru and her dad's blue truck, which was parked in the drive now with frost going off the hood in the sun. Down to the bottom of the drive.

She stopped about six feet from Sam.

He looked tired. He looked like he'd been up all night, which he probably had, with two of his own on the school. He held the tin out.

"Emily sent these," he said. "Muffins. Blueberry. She said—" He stopped. "There's a card."

Leah took the tin. It was the old one. The red one with the reindeer on the lid that had been in Emily's mother's kitchen since they were both little. They'd eaten cookies out of it at sleepovers. Leah would know it anywhere.

The card was taped to the top. Her name on the envelope, in Emily's handwriting. Round and loopy, with a little circle over the *i* in nothing, because there wasn't an *i*, because Emily had always drawn little circles anyway.

Leah held it and didn't open it.

"Thanks," she said.

Sam nodded. He looked past her, up the drive, at the porch. At Harry, standing in the doorway with Layla on his shoulder, with the boys clustered around the bottom of the steps.

"Brady told me," he said. "About the lock. And Jake." His throat moved. "Is it true?"

"Yeah."

Sam was quiet for a long time.

"Good," he said finally. Very low. "I'm glad, Leah. I'm—" He stopped again. He looked at the gravel. "I'd have given anything. For somebody to have done that. For you. Before." He swallowed. "I'm glad somebody did it for her."

Leah looked at him.

She thought about a kitchen. About his face as Emily walked through the door. About a whole year inside his head, feeling every second of it. She'd thought, then, that he'd never once thought about what it had done to her. That he couldn't. That the wolf had taken that too.

She'd been wrong about a lot of things, apparently.

"Yeah," she said. "Me too."

Sam nodded. He looked up at the porch one more time.

"Tell him thank you," he said. "For the school. And the list. Brady said he made them send me a copy."

"Tell him yourself," said Leah.

Sam looked at her. Then he looked up at the porch, at the tall black shape in the doorway, and Leah turned around too.

Harry was looking back at them. His ears were up. He had Layla on his shoulder, asleep. He didn't move.

Sam lifted one hand. Just a little. Not quite a wave.

"Merry Christmas," said Harry, from the porch.

In his own voice. The real one, low and rough, with the flat vowels. No polish on it at all. It carried down the drive in the cold air, clear.

Sam stood there for a second. Something went across his face.

"Merry Christmas," he said back.

Then he turned around and walked off into the alders, and a minute later Leah heard him phase, and heard the heavy thud of paws going away east, toward his house and Emily's.

She stood at the bottom of the drive holding a red reindeer tin.

---

She opened the card in her room, by herself, with the door shut.

It was a Christmas card from the Thriftway, the kind that came in a box of twenty. A cartoon moose in a Santa hat. Inside, in Emily's round loopy writing, in green pen:

*Leah—*

*Merry Christmas. Sam says she's beautiful. He says she has your gray.*

*I'm so glad you're happy. I'm so glad you're home.*

*I miss you every single day.*

*—Em*

Leah sat on the edge of the enormous bed and read it three times.

Then she got up and went to the dresser.

The little photo frame was still lying face down where she'd left it, two years ago, the week Sam left her. She'd looked at the back of it every day since she got here. She'd walked past it every morning and every night.

She picked it up. She turned it over.

The photo-booth strip from the mall in Port Angeles. Four pictures. Her and Emily at fifteen. Emily with her tongue out. Leah with her eyes crossed. Both of them laughing so hard in the last picture they were blurry, Emily's face pressed against hers, both of them with their mouths wide open, before anything. Before Sam. Before the wolf. Before any of it.

Leah looked at it for a long time.

Then she set it down on the dresser, face up, standing on its little cardboard leg, next to Emily's card.

She thought: *Em. I miss you too.*

Nobody heard. That was fine. She'd tell her herself. Not today. But soon.

The door opened behind her. She knew it was him without turning around. Pine and cold stone and milk and baby.

"Okay?" said Harry.

She turned around. He was in the doorway, stooped under the frame. He had Layla on his head again. She was asleep up there, with her forepaws hanging down over his eyebrows and her chin sunk in his fur and her ears pointed forward, exactly the same as his. He'd been playing cribbage in the kitchen with Seth. Losing, from the sound of it.

He looked past her at the dresser. At the photo strip standing face up. His ears tipped forward. He didn't say anything about it.

"Yeah," said Leah. "I'm okay."

She went over and put her arms around his middle, as far as they'd go, and her face in his chest. His forepaws came down very carefully on her back, claws tucked.

From somewhere above them, on top of his head, came a small, sleepy, rumbling chirrup.

"She's purring," said Leah, into his fur.

"Ja," said Harry. "She gets that from you."

Behind him, out in the kitchen, Seth yelled "*FIFTEEN-TWO, FIFTEEN-FOUR, AND A PAIR IS SIX,*" and her mom laughed, and somebody turned the radio up. Out in the yard, ten boys were standing around a Rolls-Royce in the cold, not leaning on it. On the dresser, two girls were laughing in a photo booth. On the top of the tree, her dad's wooden salmon leaned off to the left like it was trying to see out the window.

Under her breastbone, in the quiet place where the wolf slept, the lock sat closed. She could feel it there if she reached for it. Small and smooth, like a stone in her pocket.

She was never going to have to wait for anything again.

Harry's tail had started going against the doorframe. *Thump. Thump. Thump.*

"Don't you dare break Mom's door," said Leah.

"Wouldn't dream of it," said Harry.
