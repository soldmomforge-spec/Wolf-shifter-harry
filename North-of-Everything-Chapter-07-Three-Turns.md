# North of Everything

## Chapter Seven: Three Turns

---

"There's more," said Harry.

Leah lifted her face out of his chest. He was still standing in her bedroom doorway, stooped under the frame, with Layla asleep on top of his head, and his ears had tipped sideways in a way she'd learned to be suspicious of.

"More what?"

"Presents." He looked over his shoulder, down the hall, toward the front of the house and the yard, where ten boys were standing around a Rolls-Royce in the cold. "For them. I've been waiting for them all to turn up." His tail moved. "Want to come watch?"

"How many presents, Harry?"

"A few," said Harry.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she let go of his middle and went past him down the hall, because she'd learned that it was always faster to just go and see.

---

The boys were still in the yard. Most of them. Embry and Quil were leaning in at the open driver's window of the Rolls-Royce—Seth had left the keys in it, and Seth had the look of a man who'd been told he could drive it today and intended to make everyone know it. Kyle Ward and Ricky Hoskins were walking slowly around it, staring at the wheels. Dylan Littlesea was crouched down at the front bumper, looking up at the little silver woman on the hood like she might fly away. Brady was standing a bit apart, in his hoodie, with his hands in the pocket. Paul was on the porch swing with Rachel, very carefully not leaning on anything that didn't belong to him.

And Jake was lying flat on his back on the gravel, with his head and shoulders under the back end of the car, looking up at the bottom of it.

"Jake," said Leah, from the top step. "What are you doing?"

"There's no exhaust," said Jake, from under the car. His voice was strange. "Leah. There's no *exhaust*. Where does the exhaust *go*?"

"Ask him."

Jake's head came out from under the car. He looked up at Harry, standing behind her in the doorway with Layla on his head, and his face did something complicated.

"Later," said Harry. "Promise. Everybody come here a sec."

They came. Slowly, in the nervous, sideways way that boys came when somebody's mom called them in from the yard. They stood in a clump at the bottom of the steps in their T-shirts and looked up.

Harry came down two steps, so his head was clear of the porch roof and Layla's ears weren't brushing the light fixture. He reached into the inside pocket of his coat. He felt around for a second, the way he always did, like he was looking for his keys. Then he brought out a bundle.

It was a lot of cords. Thin ones, braided, black leather. They were tied together in a bunch at one end with a bit of string, and they hung down from his paw like the tail of a horse, and on the end of every single one there was a small round silver bead.

He untied the string.

"One each," he said. "It goes round your ankle. Like this." He held one up and showed them, wrapping it twice around his own foreleg, just above the paw, and tying it off. It sat there in his fur, thin and dark, with the silver bead hanging down. "You put it on in your clothes. Anything you like. Jeans, shoes, jacket, the lot. And then you phase."

The boys looked at him.

"And everything you're wearing goes in the bead," said Harry. "And stays there. Dry. And when you phase back, it comes back out. On you." He paused. "Folded properly, the way you had it on. Shoes tied."

The yard was very quiet.

"No more running home naked," said Harry. "No more tying your shorts to your leg with a shoelace. No more getting caught in the woods behind the Thriftway with nothing on but a pine cone."

Somebody in the back—Ricky Hoskins, Leah thought—made a small strangled sound.

Leah was standing on the top step. She'd gone very still.

She was thinking about a leather cord. A long one, the one she'd used all that first summer, tied around her ankle with a dry bag on the end of it. Yellow. Waterproof. Her dad had bought it for her for kayaking the year before he died. She'd run a thousand miles with it banging against her leg. She'd run it into a ring of standing stones on a ridge above Tamarack Crossing, and an iron chain had come up out of the rock through the middle of it, and when she'd gone looking for her clothes afterward she'd found them torn to rags and scattered across the frost. She'd stood behind a pine tree, freezing and bleeding and naked, and a stranger had thrown her a flannel shirt.

She was wearing it now.

"Seth," said Harry. "You first. You're closest."

Seth came up the steps and held out his ankle, and Harry crouched down and tied the cord around it himself, twice round, just above the bone, with a knot so neat it looked like it had grown there. Seth stood up. He looked down at it. He looked at Harry.

"Go on," said Harry.

Seth went down into the middle of the yard. He was wearing his old Spartans T-shirt and a pair of jeans with the knees gone and his good sneakers, the white ones he'd gotten for his birthday that he never let anybody step on. He stood there a second. Then he looked over at Leah, and grinned, and let the wolf come.

He came apart the way they all did, all at once. Every other time Leah had watched him do it, there'd been a sound like tearing canvas and a T-shirt flying off across the gravel in rags. This time there was a flash of silver, very quick, at his ankle. And then there was a big sandy-colored wolf standing in the middle of their mom's yard, the size of a horse, with a thin black cord around one hind leg and a tiny silver bead hanging off it, swinging.

He stood there. He looked down at his own leg.

He looked around the yard. No T-shirt. No jeans. No sneakers. Nothing.

His tail started going.

He phased back.

And he was standing there in the middle of the yard in his Spartans T-shirt and his jeans with the knees gone and his white sneakers, laces done up, exactly the way he'd been standing ten seconds ago. Not a thread out of place. His hair was messed up. That was the only thing.

Seth looked down at himself.

"*Holy crap*," he said.

The yard went off like a firework.

They were all over Harry. All ten of them, jostling up the porch steps, holding out their legs, *me, me next, do me*, Dylan Littlesea nearly crying, Quil already trying to get his sneaker off so he could get the cord on faster before somebody told him he didn't have to. Harry sat down on the top step with Layla still asleep on his head and tied every single one. One at a time. Twice round, above the bone. Neat. He didn't hurry. He tied Embry's and Quil's and Brady's and Kyle's and Ricky's and Dylan's, and each boy jumped down off the steps and phased in the yard and came back with his clothes on and stood there staring at himself like he'd been handed a winning lottery ticket.

Then Harry looked up at the porch swing.

Paul was sitting very still beside Rachel, with his arms folded.

"Paul," said Harry. And there it was, the voice, round and plummy and drawling, every vowel polished up until it shone. "One for you, old chap. Do try not to lean on anything in it."

Rachel elbowed Paul, hard.

Paul got up. He came over. He stood in front of Harry for a long moment, looking down at the cord in Harry's paw with his jaw working. Leah watched him think about saying something. She watched him think about the puddle.

He held out his ankle.

Harry tied it. Twice round, above the bone.

"Thanks," said Paul, to the porch railing, about as loud as a cough.

"*Splendid*," said Harry.

Paul went back to the swing. Rachel was laughing into her hands.

There were more cords left. Leah counted them while Harry was looking at them. Seven. He divided them up and held out a bundle of them to Brady.

"Sam's," he said. "And Jared's, and Collin's, and the others that aren't here. Would you take them? Tell them how it works."

Brady took them like he'd been handed something breakable.

"And Jacob," said Harry.

Jake had been standing at the bottom of the steps through the whole thing with his hands in his jeans and an expression Leah couldn't read. He came up and held out his ankle without saying anything, and Harry tied it, and Jake looked down at it for a long time.

"I've lost about forty pairs of shorts," said Jake quietly. "Since I started. Forty. I counted once. Dad's had to buy them at the Thriftway in packs of ten." He looked up. "This is—" He stopped. "Thanks, man."

"Ja," said Harry.

Then there was one left.

Harry held it in his paw for a second. Black leather. Silver bead. He turned around on the step and looked up at Leah.

She'd known it was coming. She'd counted. She'd done the math somewhere around Embry's. Ten boys here, seven in Brady's bundle, Seth, Jake. Nineteen.

There'd been twenty.

"Twenty jars," said Leah. Her voice came out funny.

"Ja," said Harry. "Seemed like the right number."

She came down the steps and sat on the one below him, the way Seth had, and held out her ankle.

He tied it. Twice round, above the bone, with a knot that looked like it had grown there. His claws were very careful. She looked down at it. Thin and dark against her skin, with the little silver bead lying on top of her foot.

She didn't say anything. She couldn't. She put her hand on top of his forepaw where it still rested on her ankle, and held it there, and he let her.

Behind them in the yard, somebody phased, and phased back, and yelled "*IT DID MY SOCKS*," and everybody laughed.

---

Her mom had been in the doorway the whole time.

Leah didn't see her until she turned around. Sue was standing just inside the front door with a dish towel over her shoulder and her arms folded, watching the yard, watching ten boys she'd known since they were born phasing in and out of their clothes in her front yard and whooping. She had a look on her face Leah didn't think she'd ever seen. Like somebody watching a thing she'd been afraid of for two years turn into a thing she could laugh at.

Harry saw her too. He stood up off the step.

"Sue," he said. "Have you got a second? You too, Jacob."

Her mom looked at him. Then she came out onto the porch, still with the dish towel, and Jake came up the steps, and Harry reached into his coat again.

This time it was a folder. A plain manila one, the kind you'd get at an office supply store, thick with paper. He held it out to her mom.

Sue took it. She didn't open it.

"What's this?"

"A thank-you," said Harry.

"For what?"

"For having me." His ears had gone a little pink inside. "You let a six-foot-seven stranger with a tail and a Rolls-Royce into your house on four hours' notice. You fed me a whole turkey. You let me sleep in your daughter's room. You let me fix your gutter." He paused. "And everybody on this reservation's been looking out for her for a year and a half while she was gone. Your mum. Your brother. Them." He nodded at the yard. "Even Samuel, in his way."

Her mom was still holding the folder closed.

"Harry," she said, carefully. "What's in it?"

"A bank account," said Harry. "In Seattle. I rang them on Sunday. It's set up in the name of the council—you, and Billy Black, and Quil Ateara, as trustees. Everything you need is in there. The cards. The paperwork." He folded his paws in front of him. "Every month, on the first, a hundred thousand dollars goes into it. U.S. That's it. That's the whole thing. It's yours to spend however you see fit."

The porch went completely silent.

Out in the yard, the boys had stopped phasing. Leah didn't know how much they'd heard. Enough, from the way they'd all gone still.

Jake was staring at Harry with his mouth open.

Her mom didn't move at all.

"Every month," said Sue.

"Every month."

"A hundred *thousand*—"

"Ja."

"For how long?"

Harry looked at her like the question surprised him.

"For as long as there's a council," he said.

Leah's mom looked down at the folder in her hands. She opened it. Leah, standing at her elbow, saw the top sheet—a bank letterhead, Seattle, a lot of small print, and a number at the bottom with a dollar sign and more zeroes than she'd ever seen next to each other on anything that wasn't a lottery ad.

Sue closed the folder again.

"Harry," she said. "I can't take this."

"It's for the council. It's not yours to take or not take." His ears tipped. "Take it to them. Show Billy. Show Old Quil. You three decide. If you want it, use it. If you don't, it sits there. If you want me to stop it, I'll stop it. I won't ask what it goes on. I don't want reports. I don't want my name on anything. I don't get a say." He paused. "That's the point."

Sue looked at him for a long time.

"There are strings," she said. "There's always strings. With money. Especially with—" She stopped.

"With rich men who aren't from here," said Harry.

Her mom didn't say anything.

"No strings," said Harry. "I swear it on her." He reached up without looking and touched a claw to Layla's back, on top of his head, and Layla purred in her sleep. "It's yours. It's thanks. That's all it is."

Jake made a sound.

Everybody turned and looked at him. He was standing beside Sue with his hands hanging at his sides, and he had a look on his face Leah had seen on him exactly once before, at his mom's funeral, when he was a little kid in a borrowed tie.

"The school," Jake said.

Her mom looked at him.

"The school's in the tsunami zone," said Jake. His voice was rough. "The whole village is. The school, the elders' center, the store, half the houses. Dad's been going to meetings about it since I was in diapers. If the big one comes, there's like twenty minutes to get everybody up the hill and there's one road." He swallowed. "They've been trying to move the school uphill my whole life. They can never get the money. The Park Service won't give them the land, and then when they get the land, there's no money for the building." He looked at Harry. "A hundred grand a month. That's—that's a school. That's a new school in, like, two years. Up the hill. Where the kids don't die."

Harry didn't say anything. He looked at Sue.

Her mom stood there on her porch with the folder held against her chest and the dish towel still over her shoulder.

"I'll take it to the council," she said finally. Very quietly. "We'll decide."

"That's all I want," said Harry.

"I'm not promising anything."

"I'd be worried if you did."

Sue looked at him a moment longer. Something moved in her face. Then she turned around and went inside with the folder, without saying anything else, and Leah heard her go down the hall into her bedroom and shut the door.

Leah looked at Harry.

"Was that okay?" said Harry. His ears had gone back. "Did I—"

"Give her a minute," said Leah.

On the step below, Jake was standing very still, looking at the closed front door. Then he looked at Harry. Then he looked out at the yard, past the boys, past the Subaru and her dad's blue truck, at the long black shape of the Rolls-Royce gleaming in the low afternoon sun.

"Okay," said Jake. "*Now* tell me where the exhaust goes."

---

Jacob Black had been looking at that car for twenty-four hours, and it was making him crazy.

He'd seen it first through Seth's head, the night before last, coming down the gravel in the rain like a black hole with headlights. Then from the bottom of the drive, standing in Sue's puddle with Sam six feet away and Paul about to get his ass handed to him. Then yesterday morning, when it started up by itself in Sue's yard with nobody in it and drove off down the hill in front of nine wolves, and Quil had screamed, and Jacob had stood there with his mouth hanging open and thought, *I need to get under that thing*.

Now he was under it. And there was no exhaust.

"There's a tailpipe," he said, from under the back bumper. "There's two tailpipes. They're right there. They're not connected to *anything*. They're just—chrome. They're *decorative*."

"Ja," said Harry.

Jacob slid out from under the car on his back and lay on the gravel and looked up at him.

Harry was standing by the rear wheel with his paws folded in front of him and his baby on his head. Layla had woken up. She was lying up there on her stomach between his ears, chin on his skull, forepaws hanging down over his eyebrows, looking down at Jacob on the ground with her little green-gold eyes wide open and her ears pricked. Her tail was moving. She looked interested.

It was the weirdest thing Jacob had ever seen. And he'd seen a lot of weird things in the last year and a half.

"Where," said Jacob slowly, "does the exhaust *go*?"

Harry's ears went sideways.

"Somewhere else," he said.

"Where else?"

"I asked a friend. She did something. I don't really understand it." He looked down at the tailpipes. "It goes away and doesn't come back. I didn't want it near the baby seat."

Jacob lay there on the gravel and thought about that for a while.

"Can I see the engine?" he said.

---

The hood came up by itself. Jacob had stopped being surprised by that kind of thing about thirty seconds after he met the guy.

He stood there and looked at the engine for a long time.

It was a V12. That was the first thing. A big one. Six and a half, maybe seven liters, he guessed, twin turbos, the whole thing so clean you could have eaten off it, sitting in the bay under the cold afternoon light like something in a museum. He didn't recognize it. He knew engines. He'd taught himself on the Rabbit, out of library books and parts from the junkyard in Port Angeles, and he'd spent a whole spring rebuilding two dirt bikes in his garage, and he knew engines. He'd never seen this one. Not in a magazine. Not on the Internet. Not anywhere.

He leaned in and read the plate on the intake.

*ROLLS-ROYCE MOTOR CARS — GOODWOOD — PROJECT CULLINAN — PROTOTYPE 0.*

Prototype *zero*.

"Rolls doesn't make this," said Jacob.

"Not yet," said Harry. He'd come round to stand beside him, with Layla peering over the top of his head down into the engine bay. Seth had drifted over too, and Embry and Quil, and a couple of the new kids, all standing around the front of the car looking in like it was a campfire. "In about ten years they will. Something like it. They've been thinking about it. I asked them for one in August and they built it in four months and made me sign a lot of things saying I'd never tell anybody."

"You're telling us."

"You're not anybody," said Harry. "You're family. Sort of. Through Seth."

Jacob looked at him. Harry looked back with his ears up.

"Cullinan," said Jacob. "Like—what's Cullinan?"

"A diamond. The biggest one anybody ever dug out of the ground. They found it in a mine outside Pretoria in 1905. Three thousand carats." Harry's ear flicked. "I grew up about an hour from there. Sort of. After a while. Long story. When they told me the name, I said, ja, all right, I'll have it."

Jacob looked back down at the engine.

He wanted to take it apart. He wanted to take every single piece of it out and lay it on a tarp on his garage floor and look at it and put it back together again. He wanted to know how it worked. He wanted it so bad his hands itched.

"What's it do?" he said. "Zero to sixty."

"Don't know. Never tried. There's a baby seat in the back."

"*You've never floored it?*"

"There's a *baby seat*, Jacob."

"Oh my God," said Jacob. He put his forehead down on the edge of the open hood. "Oh my God. You have a car that doesn't exist, with a V12 that doesn't exist, and you've never *floored it*."

"Seth's driving it today," said Harry. "Ask Seth."

Jacob lifted his head and looked at Seth.

Seth was standing by the front bumper with the keys in his hand and a look on his face like Christmas had come twice. "I'm not flooring it," he said. "I've got my *permit*, Jake. I've had it for three weeks. Mom'd kill me."

"Take it round the block," said Harry. "Both of you. Jacob, you go with him. If he does anything stupid, take the wheel." He paused. "Don't floor it."

"I won't floor it."

"*Jacob.*"

"I won't floor it," said Jacob. "Much."

---

There was no block, on the rez. There was the road down to the village and the road up to the highway, and the loop around by the marina. Seth drove it twice. Jacob sat in the passenger seat with his hands flat on the cream leather and his head tipped back looking up at the ceiling, which was full of tiny lights like stars, and didn't say anything for the whole first loop.

It didn't make a sound. That was the thing. The engine was running. He could feel it, way down, a low deep hum through the floor and the seat like a big animal purring. But you couldn't *hear* it. You couldn't hear the road. You couldn't hear the tires on the gravel or the wind at the windows or anything. It was like sitting inside a church. It was like being in a boat on a lake on a day with no wind.

The suspension didn't feel like suspension. Seth went over the pothole by the Lonesome Creek store that had been eating axles since Jacob was in grade school, the big one everybody swerved around, and the car went over it like it wasn't there. Like it was floating.

"Air suspension," said Jacob, out loud, to nobody. "Cameras. It's reading the road. It's reading the road *ahead* of it."

"What?" said Seth.

"Nothing. Keep driving."

On the second loop, by the marina, on the long straight bit along the river where you could see a quarter-mile ahead and there was never anybody, Seth looked over at him.

"You want to?" he said.

Jacob looked at him.

They switched seats in the marina lot. Jacob put his hands on the wheel. It was wrapped in leather so soft it felt like skin. He put his foot on the brake and looked at the long straight empty road along the river, gray water on one side and the trees on the other.

He thought about Harry standing in the yard saying *don't floor it*.

He got about three-quarters of the way there.

The car *went*, all at once, the way a big wave goes when it finally decides to break, and Jacob's head went back against the headrest and the trees on the left turned into a green smear and Seth made a sound like a teakettle and grabbed the door handle with both hands. And it was still dead quiet. That was the craziest part. Going like that, faster than Jacob had ever gone in anything that wasn't himself on four legs, and the only sound in the whole car was Seth.

He took his foot off at the end of the straight. The car slowed down, smooth as anything, like a hand closing.

They sat there at the end of the road by the river, with the engine humming under them, breathing hard.

"Don't tell him," said Jacob.

"He knows," said Seth. "He *always* knows."

---

Harry knew.

He was standing at the bottom of Sue's drive when they came back up the hill, with his paws folded and Layla on his head, and as Jacob pulled the car in and parked it beside Leah's dad's old blue truck, Harry looked at him through the windshield with his ears tipped forward and one eyebrow—one brow, one dark furred ridge over one green eye—very slightly raised.

Jacob got out.

"Three-quarters," he said. "I swear. I didn't floor it."

"Ja," said Harry. "I heard."

"You can't have *heard*, it doesn't make any *noise*—"

"I heard Seth," said Harry.

Seth got out of the passenger side looking a bit green.

Jacob stood there by the car. The other guys had mostly gone. Embry and Quil were still on the porch, eating something Sue had brought out on a plate. Paul and Rachel had left, and Brady, with his bundle of cords for Sam's guys. It was getting toward dusk. The sun had gone down behind the trees and the sky over the ocean had gone pink and gold, and the Christmas lights along the rail had come on.

He didn't want to give the keys back.

He held them out anyway.

Harry didn't take them.

"Jacob," he said. "What car do you want?"

Jacob stood there with the keys in his hand.

"What?"

"Any car. Whatever you want. I'll buy it." Harry's ears were up, steady. "No questions asked."

Jacob stared at him.

He thought the guy was joking for a second. Then he looked at his face, and remembered that this was the guy who'd just handed Sue Clearwater a folder with a hundred grand a month in it in front of half the pack, and stood there on the porch saying *I don't get a say*, and he realized the guy had never been joking about anything in his life, except maybe Gerald.

"I don't need a car," said Jacob. It came out too fast. "I've got the Rabbit. I built the Rabbit."

"I didn't say need," said Harry.

Jacob opened his mouth. He shut it.

He thought about the Rabbit. He loved the Rabbit. He'd built it from the frame up, out of junkyard parts and library books, with his own two hands, and it ran, and he'd driven Bella around in it, back when that was a thing he did, and it was *his*.

He thought about his dad.

"My dad's van," he said.

Harry's ears tipped forward.

"It's a '91 Ford. It's got a lift in the side for the chair. The lift's been broken since March. The motor went. I can't find the part, they don't make it anymore, I've been to every junkyard from here to Olympia." Jacob looked at the gravel. "So when he goes anywhere, somebody has to lift him in. Me, or Paul, or Rachel. He hates it. He *hates* it. He'd rather sit in the house for a week than have somebody pick him up like a kid." His throat got tight. "He's missed two council meetings because he wouldn't call anybody."

Harry didn't say anything for a moment.

"Done," he said. "New van. Whatever's best. With a lift that works, and a spare motor for it in a box." He paused. "And I'll have them fix the old one, if he likes it."

Jacob looked up at him.

"That's your dad's," said Harry. "That doesn't count. What do *you* want?"

"Man—"

"*Jacob.*"

Jacob stood there in the dusk in Sue Clearwater's driveway with the keys to a car that didn't exist in his hand.

He knew what he wanted. He'd known since about three seconds after Harry asked. He'd been wanting it since he was eleven, since he found a picture of one in a car magazine in the dentist's office in Forks and tore it out and took it home and stuck it on his bedroom wall, where it still was.

He just didn't want to say it, because saying it out loud made it real.

"'67 Impala," he said.

Harry's head tipped.

"Chevy," said Jacob. He could feel his face going hot. "Sixty-seven. Four-door hardtop. Black. Big-block V8." He swallowed. "And not a nice one. Not a restored one. A wreck. Rust in the floors, seized engine, sitting in some guy's field in Oklahoma for twenty years with a tree growing through the hood. One I can fix." He looked up at Harry. "I want to build it. Myself. Every piece."

"Like the guys on that ghost show!" said Seth, delighted.

"It's just a good *car*, Seth."

"Never seen it," said Harry. "Ghost show?"

"It's—never mind." Jacob shook his head. "It's a good car. That's all. It's a really good car."

Harry looked at him for a long moment, with Layla's small furred face peering down at Jacob from the top of his head, both of them with their ears up the exact same way.

"Done," said Harry. "One wreck. The worst one I can find. Tree through the hood." His tail moved. "And a garage to put it in, if your dad's isn't big enough. And whatever tools you want."

Jacob made a sound. He didn't know what kind.

"Why?" he said.

Harry seemed to think about that for a moment.

"Because you walked up those steps today," he said, "with your own cord going off into the trees, and asked me to make sure she gets to pick." He reached up and touched one claw to Layla's back. "And because you came to look at her, and you didn't have to. And because you've lost forty pairs of shorts."

Jacob laughed. It came out of him cracked and surprised.

He held out the keys again.

This time Harry took them.

---

Jacob left at dark. He ran home in fur, to tell his dad about a folder with a hundred thousand dollars a month in it and a van with a lift that worked, and he ran with the new cord around his ankle and his jeans and his good shirt folded away somewhere in a silver bead the size of a pea, and when he came up the drive of the little red house with the wheelchair ramp and phased back by the mailbox, he was wearing them.

He stood there in his dad's yard in the cold, dressed, and laughed out loud.

Then he looked at the mailbox.

*BLACK*, it said. Hand-painted, flaking. His mom had painted it, the summer before she died.

He stood there for a while, looking at it, thinking about a giant black wolf on Sue Clearwater's porch saying *the London Blacks, dear boy, nothing to do with anyone here*. Thinking about Old Quil on his bench at the marina asking *did any of them ever come here?* Thinking about the cord in his own chest, as thick as a foreleg, going off northeast into the trees.

Then he went inside to tell his dad about the van.

---

Sue Clearwater sat on her bed for forty minutes with the folder in her lap before she called Billy.

She didn't open it again. She didn't need to. She'd seen the number. She sat there in her bedroom with the door shut and the Christmas noise coming through the wall—the boys whooping in the yard, Seth yelling about socks—and held it, and thought about her husband.

Harry would have laughed himself sick. That was the first thing she thought. He'd have stood in the kitchen with the folder in his big hands and read the number out loud, twice, and laughed until he had to sit down. And then he'd have gone very quiet, and put it down on the table, and looked at her, and said *Suzie. The school.*

Then she'd called Billy.

He'd been quiet on the phone for a very long time. Billy Black was quiet a lot, but this was different. She'd heard him breathing.

"Every month," he'd said finally.

"Every month."

"No strings."

"He swore it on the baby, Billy."

Another long silence. Then Billy had said, in a voice she hadn't heard him use since Sarah died: "Jake's on his way home. He says there's a van." And then, quieter: "Sue. The school."

"I know."

"We'll meet. The twenty-seventh. You and me and Quil." A pause. "Tell him—" Billy had stopped. "Tell him thank you. From me. For now. Until we decide."

She'd said she would.

Then she'd sat on the bed a while longer, and wiped her face, and put the folder in the top drawer of the dresser under her good sweaters, and gone out to make dinner for her family.

---

It was nearly eight o'clock on Christmas night before the house went quiet.

Everyone had gone. Embry and Quil had eaten two plates of leftovers each on the porch and gone home in fur and come back to the edge of the yard in fur fifteen minutes later just to phase back in front of the house fully dressed and wave. Jacob had run home to Billy. Charlie had called to say Merry Christmas and that he'd been at Bella's, and that the little girl had asked about the Rolls-Royce, which Sue had decided not to think about.

Now it was just them.

Seth was on the living-room floor in front of the crooked tree, lying on his back on the braided rug with Layla asleep on his chest. He'd asked. Harry had given her to him an hour ago, and Seth had lain down very slowly and very carefully, like a man lying down on thin ice, and hadn't moved since. Every so often he'd look down his own nose at the small furry head under his chin and go pink.

Leah was on the couch with her feet tucked up under her, in Harry's flannel, with a mug of tea going cold. She had the new cord around her ankle. Sue had seen her look down at it about forty times since lunch.

And Harry was sitting on the floor by the woodstove, with his back against the wall, the way he'd sat every night since he walked into this house. His knees were up. His tail was curled across the rug. His ears were half up, half down, the way they went when he was thinking about something and didn't want anyone to notice.

Sue had noticed.

She was in her chair. Her husband's chair, really, the big brown recliner with the duct tape on the left arm. She'd started sitting in it the week after the funeral and never stopped. She had a mug of coffee she didn't want and the remote control for a TV that wasn't on, and she was looking at the tree. At the carved wooden salmon on top of it, leaning off to the left.

She remembered, then, what she'd promised on the phone.

"Billy called," she said. "Before. He says thank you."

Harry's ears came up.

"For now," said Sue. "Until the council decides. He said to say that part, so you'd know we hadn't decided." She looked at the salmon on the tree. "And he said Jacob came in the door talking about a van so fast he couldn't understand a word, and had to make him start over three times." She paused. "He hasn't heard Jacob talk like that since he was fourteen."

"Good," said Harry quietly.

"He'll want to meet you. Billy. After the twenty-seventh."

"Ja," said Harry. "I'd like that. Old Quil, too. We've got something to show them."

Over on the couch, Leah looked up at him. Sue saw her look. She didn't ask.

"Sue," said Harry.

She looked over at him.

"I've got one more," he said. "Present. For you."

"Harry, you've done enough. You've done—" She shook her head. "The truck. The folder. The boys. I don't even know how to—"

"It's only mine to lend," said Harry.

Something in his voice made her put the coffee down.

Leah had gone still on the couch. Seth had lifted his head off the rug.

Harry looked down at his own forepaw, resting on his knee. Then he turned it over. Palm up. The dark pads showing. The long black claws curled.

There was a ring on it.

There hadn't been. Sue had been looking right at that paw. She'd watched him tie twenty cords with it this afternoon and hold a baby with it and drink his coffee with it, and there'd been nothing on it, not a thing, just black fur and dark pads and claws. And now there was a ring, on the third digit, sitting there in his fur like it had always been there.

It was ugly. That was what she thought, first. Heavy and gold and old-fashioned, the band thick and a little lumpy, like somebody had made it by hand a very long time ago and not been very good at it. Set into it there was a stone. Black. Flat. Not shiny. It had a crack running right across the middle of it, a clean hairline, like it had been struck with something once. And cut into the face of the stone, so faint she had to lean forward to see it, there was a mark. A line, inside a circle, inside a triangle.

Harry was looking at it.

He looked at it for maybe a second. Maybe two. That was all. His ears had gone very still. His eyes had gone somewhere very far away. And his face did something that Sue knew. She knew it the way she knew the sound of that truck. She'd seen it every morning for twenty-one months in the bathroom mirror, before she put her face on for the day. It was the look of somebody holding something they could have, if they just let themselves, and knowing they shouldn't.

Then he blinked, and it was gone.

He took the ring off. He did it carefully, with two claws, sliding it down off his digit. He held it in his palm a moment longer.

Then he leaned forward, across the rug, and held it out to her.

---

"What is it?" said Sue.

She didn't take it. She looked at it lying in his big palm.

"It's very old," said Harry. "Older than the Potters. Older than the Blacks. It belonged to a family called Peverell, about seven hundred years ago." His ears tipped. "There's a story. Three brothers, and Death. Death gave each of them a present. The second one asked for something that would bring back the dead."

The room was very quiet. Sue could hear the woodstove ticking.

"It brings back a bit of them," said Harry. "A shadow. Enough. Enough to see. Enough to talk to. They know who you are. They know what's happened." He paused. "For as long as you hold it."

Sue looked at the ring.

"I dropped it in a forest when I was seventeen," said Harry. "I meant to leave it there forever. I meant it." His ears went flat for a second, then came back up. "It came back. Some things don't stay lost. Not from me."

Over on the couch, Leah had sat up very straight. Sue saw her daughter look at Harry's paw. At his other paw. Like she was thinking about something else that came and went.

"It's for tonight," said Harry. "Only tonight. You can't keep it, Sue. The man it was made for kept it, and he couldn't put it down, and it took him." He held her eyes, steady and green. "Tonight. And then you give it back to me."

Sue sat in her husband's chair.

"Why?" she said. Her voice came out very small. "Why are you—"

"Because you told me nobody ever asked you what he was like," said Harry. "And because it's Christmas. And because Seth sings his song." He held the ring out a little farther. "And because he's never met her."

He looked at Seth's chest. At the baby asleep there.

Sue put out her hand.

He tipped the ring into it. It was heavy. Much heavier than it looked. And cold, very cold, like a stone pulled out of the river in January.

"Think of him," said Harry. "As hard as you can. And turn the stone three times."

Sue looked down at the ring in her palm. Then up at him.

"Which Harry?" she said.

His ears went pink inside, all the way to the tips.

"Your one," he said.

---

She held the ring in her left hand, and put the tip of her right thumb on the black stone, and pushed.

It turned.

It turned in its setting, the whole stone, the way a key turns in a lock. She hadn't known it could. It went around once under her thumb, slow and stiff, and as it went there was a sound.

It came from everywhere. From the floor, from the walls, from the woodstove and the windows and the crooked tree in the corner, from the bones of her own face. It was low—lower than anything, lower than the surf on First Beach on the worst winter night, lower than an earthquake. And it was grinding. Stone on stone. Like the whole world, the house and the reservation and the coast and the ocean and everything under it, had been laid down on an enormous grindstone and somebody had leaned on the handle.

Leah's mug hit the floor.

Sue's thumb was still on the stone. She didn't take it off. She thought of him. His big hands. His laugh. The smell of him, cigarettes and fish and Old Spice. His face the morning Seth was born. His face the morning Leah was born, crying, a grown man, standing in the hospital corridor in Forks in his waders because he'd come straight off the river.

She turned it again.

The sound came again. Deeper. The windows shook in their frames. The Christmas lights on the tree flickered and went dim and came back.

She thought of him singing in the truck. Flat. So flat.

She turned it the third time.

The grinding went on and on and on and then, all at once, it stopped.

The room was silent.

And there was somebody standing in front of the tree.

---

He was big. That was what she saw first, because it was what she'd always seen first, for twenty-five years, every time he walked into a room. Big and broad through the shoulders, with a belly on him he'd never been able to do anything about, and big square hands, and a face like a slab of cedar with a nose that had been broken twice playing football at Forks High. His red flannel. His old jeans, worn white at the knees. His Mariners cap, pushed back on his head.

He was a little bit see-through.

Just a little, the way a man looks through a window with rain on it. His edges were soft. The Christmas lights on the tree behind him came through him, very faintly, red and green and blue. He was more solid than smoke and less solid than a man, and he was standing in her living room in front of the crooked tree with his hands in his jeans pockets, the way he'd stood in front of every Christmas tree they'd ever had, looking at it with his head on one side.

He looked up at the carved wooden salmon on top. Leaning off to the left.

"That's a good tree," said Harry Clearwater. "That's a crooked tree. Who picked that?"

Nobody said anything.

Nobody could.

He turned around.

He looked at the room. At Seth, on the floor, with a baby on his chest, staring up at him with his mouth open and his eyes already running. At Leah, on the couch, with her tea all over the rug and both hands pressed flat over her mouth. At the giant black wolf by the woodstove, sitting perfectly still with his ears all the way back and his tail curled tight.

And then at her.

"Suzie," he said.

Sue couldn't breathe.

He grinned. The big one. The one that took over his whole face, until his eyes nearly disappeared.

"Knock knock," said Harry Clearwater.

Sue made a sound she didn't know she could make. It was half a laugh and half something that came up from the very bottom of her, from a place she'd sealed over twenty-one months ago with dirt and a headstone.

"Harry Clearwater," she said. "I swear to *God*—"

"Knock knock, Sue."

She was crying. She was laughing. She had the cold ring clenched in her fist so hard it hurt.

"Who's there," she said.

"Lettuce."

"Lettuce *who*?"

He looked at her. The grin went a little softer around the edges.

"Lettuce in," he said. "It's cold out here."

---

She didn't remember getting out of the chair. She was just up, all at once, across the rug, standing in front of him. Close. As close as she could get. She put out her free hand to touch his chest, his flannel, the place over his heart where she'd put her hand every night for twenty-two years.

Her hand went in a little way, and stopped.

It was like pushing her hand into cold water that didn't wet her. Cold, and a bit thick, and then something only half there. She could feel the shape of him. Just. Like feeling a shape through a curtain.

He looked down at her hand on his chest.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "I know. Sorry, Suzie."

"Don't you *dare* say sorry to me."

"Okay."

"You left me with the *gutter*."

"I know."

"You left me with *that puddle*—"

"Somebody filled it in," said Harry Clearwater. He looked past her, over her head, at the big black wolf sitting by the woodstove. "Somebody filled it in in *one night*. Took me twenty years of not doing that."

By the woodstove, Harry's ears went flat.

Sue laughed. She couldn't stop.

Behind her, Seth had sat up. He'd done it slowly, with one arm curled around the baby, the way you'd sit up holding something that might break. He was sitting on the rug looking up at his father with tears running straight down his face into his collar.

"Dad," he said.

Harry Clearwater looked down at him. His whole face changed.

"Look at you," he said. "Look at *you*. You're taller than me."

"Dad, the truck—"

"I heard." He crouched down. He did it the way he always had, with a grunt, one hand on his knee, like his back hurt, though Sue didn't suppose it did anymore. He got down level with his son. "Heard it start up this morning. That old cough. Heard the tape." He grinned. "You still singing it flat?"

"Yeah," said Seth. He was laughing and sobbing at the same time. "Yeah. So flat. And he does too. He sings it *worse*, Dad."

Harry Clearwater looked over at the woodstove again.

"Not possible," he said.

"That's what *I* said," said Harry, very quietly.

Harry Clearwater looked at him for a long moment.

Then he looked down at what was in Seth's arms.

---

Layla was awake.

Sue didn't know when she'd woken. Maybe the grinding. Maybe before. She was lying in the crook of Seth's arm in Kreacher's gray sweater, with her small round head tipped back and her blurry green-gold eyes wide open, looking up.

At him. Right at his face.

Her ears had come up through the holes in the hood. Her nose was going. Her little tail had started to move.

"Huh," said Harry Clearwater softly.

He leaned in. Close. Closer than he'd let himself get to anybody else in the room.

"Hey," he said. "Hey there. Layla Sue."

Over on the couch, Leah made a sound.

"How do you know her name?" Her voice was cracked. "Dad. How do you—"

"I've been around, kiddo." He didn't look away from the baby. "Here and there. On and off. Can't say how. It's not really something you can say." He watched Layla's tail go. "She's got your gray, Lee. Look at that. Right down the middle of her face. That's your gray."

Layla's small forepaw came up out of Seth's arm. Reaching. The tiny claws spread, like they did when she grabbed for Harry's fur, or Sue's finger, or the ear of a house-elf in a tea towel.

Harry Clearwater held out one big finger.

Her paw closed around it. Or closed around the place where it was. Sue couldn't tell. Nobody could, she thought. But the baby's paw stayed there, curled around something in the air, and held on. And from somewhere deep in her small chest came that sound. The kettle sound. The low rumbling chirrup she'd made in her father's lap every morning and on top of his head all through Christmas Eve dinner.

She was purring at him.

Harry Clearwater crouched there on the rug with a baby holding onto his finger and didn't say anything at all for a very long time.

---

He talked to Leah next.

He went and sat on the couch beside her. He sat down the way he'd always sat down on that couch, all at once, like a tree falling, and the couch didn't make a sound under him, which was how Sue knew for certain he wasn't really there. That couch had made a noise like a dying goose under Harry Clearwater every day for nineteen years.

Leah turned and looked at him.

"Hey, kiddo," he said.

"Dad." She was shaking. "Dad, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I left, I left Mom, I left a note on a *receipt*—"

"You ran," said Harry Clearwater.

"I—"

"You ran. Good." He put his big see-through hand on top of her head, the way he used to when she was little. She'd have felt cold, Sue thought. Cold and a bit thick. Leah didn't move away. "You needed to. I watched you that whole year. All of them in your head. Every day. I'd have run with you if I could." His voice went rough. "I'd have run with you all the way to Canada and bought you french fries."

Leah laughed. It came out wet and broken.

"I did get french fries," she said. "When I got there. In this little town. I ate two bacon cheeseburgers and a poutine and a milkshake and soup."

"That's my girl."

"And then I met—"

She stopped. She looked across the room.

Harry Clearwater looked across the room too.

The big black wolf by the woodstove was still sitting exactly where he'd been sitting when the stone turned. He hadn't moved. His ears were all the way back. His tail was wrapped around his footpaws. He was looking at the man on the couch with an expression Sue couldn't read at all.

Harry Clearwater looked back at him. He looked him up and down, slowly, from the footpaws to the tips of the ears, the way he used to look at a boat somebody was trying to sell him.

"So you're the guy," he said.

"I'm the guy," said Harry.

"Got a good name."

Harry's ears tipped. "Leah said yours was better."

"Damn right it is." Harry Clearwater considered him a moment longer. "You're big."

"Ja. I get that a lot."

"You fixed my truck."

"Ja."

"You left the hole."

"Seth said you could watch the road go by."

Harry Clearwater didn't say anything for a second. Something went across his see-through face, there and gone.

"I'd offer you a beer," he said finally. "But."

"I'd have taken it," said Harry.

Harry Clearwater nodded, slowly. Like that settled something.

"Look after my girls," he said.

"Ja."

"All three of them."

"Ja," said Harry. "All three."

"And my boy."

Harry looked over at Seth, on the rug, with the baby holding onto the air.

"Ja," he said. "Him too."

---

Sue sat with him by the tree for a long time after that.

The others let them. Seth took the baby down the hall. Leah went to the kitchen and ran the water in the sink for a long time and didn't come back. Harry stayed by the woodstove, still, with his eyes on the floor, and Sue understood after a while that he was keeping watch on the ring.

She sat on the floor by the crooked tree with her back against the wall and her husband beside her. Not touching. Close. Like they used to sit on the beach.

She told him about Charlie.

She hadn't meant to. It came out on its own. She told him about the fish Charlie brought round, and the garbage disposal and the butter knife, and the Fourth of July, and the garage. She told him Charlie sat in his chair at the kitchen table. She told him all of it, looking at her hands, and then she stopped, and waited.

"Are you angry?" she said.

He laughed. That big laugh. Silent now, the way everything about him was a little bit silent, but she could see it go through him and shake his shoulders.

"Suzie," he said. "He fixed your gutter."

"He didn't fix the gutter."

Harry Clearwater looked over at the woodstove, at the black wolf sitting very still with his eyes on the floor.

"I know," he said.

Sue laughed too.

"Charlie's a good man," said her husband. "He's the best man I know, after Billy. Twenty years in that boat with him every Saturday. He'll never catch more fish than me." He paused. "Tell him he's still wrong about the roe."

"I'll tell him."

"And Suzie." He turned his head. He looked at her. Right at her. "Don't keep my side of the bed empty on my account. Okay? I'm not there. I'm not gonna be there. Don't leave it cold for me."

She couldn't answer that. She put her face in her hands.

He sat there beside her until she could.

After a while he started humming.

It was low, and flat, a full step under, and it went on, and she knew it after three notes. She'd know it until she died. And from the kitchen she heard the water shut off in the sink, and a second later her daughter's voice came in under it, flat too, the way she'd sung it in the back of the truck when she was seven and thought nobody was listening. And then Seth, from down the hall, louder than either of them, with no idea of the tune at all.

And then, from the woodstove, low and rough, so deep she felt it in the floor, the other Harry. Humming it too. Worse than anybody.

Sue sat on the floor of her living room on Christmas night and listened to all of them sing her husband's song, flat, together.

When it was over, her husband looked at her.

"Give it back to him, Suzie," he said.

"Not yet."

"It's not good for you. Holding it." His voice was gentle. "I can feel it pulling on you. I know you. You'd hold it every night. You'd never put it down."

She knew he was right. She'd known the second the stone turned. She could feel it, in her fist, the way the cold of it had gone up her arm into her chest and settled there, the way it was saying *you could have this every night. You could have this every night forever.*

She'd seen that look on Harry's face. When he'd held it. That second.

"Five more minutes," she said.

He smiled.

"Five," he said.

She held it for five more minutes. Then she held it for one more after that, because she was Sue Clearwater and she'd never in her life left anything exactly on time.

Then she opened her fist, and looked at the ring lying in her palm, and took it in her other hand.

"See you, Suzie," said Harry Clearwater.

"See you," said Sue.

She held out the ring.

The big black wolf by the woodstove got up and came across the rug, very slowly, and knelt down in front of her, and held out his paw. She put the ring in it.

When she looked back at the tree, there was nobody there. Just the Christmas lights. And the carved wooden salmon on top, leaning off to the left, like it was trying to see out the window.

---

Leah came back from the kitchen with her face washed and her hands still wet, and found him kneeling on the rug.

Her mom had gone back to her chair. She was sitting in it with her hands open in her lap, palms up, looking at the tree. She looked about ten years younger and about ten years older at the same time. Seth was in the hall doorway with Layla against his shoulder, his face blotchy, not coming any farther in.

And Harry was kneeling in the middle of the living-room floor, in front of the place where her dad had been standing, with the ring in his open paw.

He was looking at it.

He'd looked at it for a second, before. Leah had seen that second. She'd seen his face do something she'd never seen it do, not on the bathroom floor in Atlin, not in Pye's office, not in a tunnel full of empty jars. She'd seen him look at the ugly old ring like it was the last cold drink in a desert, and then blink, and take it off, and give it away.

He was looking at it like that now. And he wasn't blinking.

Leah crossed the rug and knelt down beside him.

He didn't look up. His ears were back, low and careful.

She looked at the ring in his palm. The cracked black stone. The little mark cut into it. The line, the circle, the triangle.

"When did you last use it?" she said.

"I was seventeen." His voice was very low. "In a forest. At night. I was walking to—" He stopped. "I was walking somewhere I didn't think I was coming back from. I turned it three times and they came, and they walked with me. All the way." He was quiet for a second. "Then I dropped it in the leaves and kept walking. And I meant to leave it there."

"And it came back."

"Ja. About a year later. In my coat pocket. Like a sock in the wash." His ears flicked. "I put it in a box in the vault at Gringotts. It came back again. I put it at the bottom of Atlin Lake. It came back." He turned it a little in his palm. "So I stopped trying. It stays with me. It comes when I want it. I just don't want it."

Leah looked at the side of his face.

"Ten years," she said.

"Ja."

"You've had it in your pocket for ten years."

"Near me. It's hard to explain." He was still looking at it. "Ten years. Ja."

"And you never—"

"No."

"Why not?"

He took a long time to answer.

"Because the second brother couldn't put it down," he said. "And I'm not sure I could either."

Leah didn't say anything.

She looked at her mom, in the chair. At the open hands in her lap. Her mom had put it down. She'd held it six minutes longer than she should have and she'd put it down and given it back, because her husband had asked her to.

She looked at Seth in the doorway, holding Layla, crying.

She looked at Harry.

She put her hand on his back, between his shoulder blades, in the thick black fur under his coat.

"Do it," she said.

His ears came up an inch.

"I'll be right here," said Leah. "I'll hold your paw. When it's time. Do it, Harry. It's Christmas."

He was still for a long moment.

Then he took a breath, a long one, all the way down, and let it out. He put the tip of one claw on the cracked black stone.

Leah watched his eyes. He kept them open, on the stone, green and very bright.

He turned it.

---

The grinding came up out of the floor. Out of everything. The whole world, on a whetstone. Leah felt it in her teeth and in her knees on the rug and in the ring on her own right hand, the heavy old gold one with the green stone, which went hot on her finger all at once like it had heard something it recognized.

He turned it again.

The windows shook. The Christmas lights went down to almost nothing and came back.

He turned it the third time.

And when the sound stopped, there were three people in her mom's living room.

---

They were standing in a row in front of the crooked tree, where her dad had been. A little see-through, like him. Soft at the edges. The colored lights coming through them, faint.

The one in the middle was a woman.

She was young. Leah's age, or about. Small, slim, with long dark red hair falling over her shoulders and a face that was all cheekbones and freckles and a stubborn chin. She was wearing an old cardigan over a dress, the kind of thing a girl would wear around her own house in 1981 on a Sunday. Her hands were clasped in front of her.

Her eyes were green.

Leah had seen those eyes every single day for fifteen months. She'd seen them across a booth at the Bent Antler and on the other pillow every morning and over the top of a toilet bowl on a bathroom floor in Atlin. She'd seen them three days ago, for the first time, looking up at her out of a small furred face on the side of the gravel drive, and smiling.

On the woman's left was a young man. Tall, though nobody was as tall as Harry, and thin, with a mess of black hair sticking up at the back like he'd slept on it wrong and a pair of round glasses sitting a little crooked on his nose. He had his hands in his pockets. He was grinning. It was a crooked grin, lopsided, and Leah had seen that too. She'd seen it on a wolf's muzzle about a thousand times.

On the woman's right was another man. Older than the other two. Late thirties, maybe. Tall, with long dark hair falling to his shoulders, and a lean, handsome, worn-in face, and gray eyes that were looking straight at Harry with an expression Leah didn't have a name for.

Nobody said anything.

Harry knelt on the rug with the ring in his open paw and looked up at them. He didn't move. Leah didn't think he could.

The young man with the glasses was the first one to speak.

"Harry," he said.

And then he looked at Harry properly. All of him. The fur and the ears and the long black muzzle and the tail lying curled across the rug. His eyes went very wide behind the glasses.

"You've got a *tail*," said James Potter.

The man with the long hair made a sound.

It was a bark. That was the only word for it. A short, sharp, delighted bark of a laugh, exactly like a big dog that had just seen the best thing it had ever seen in its life. He put both hands over his face and bent over at the waist and kept doing it.

"*He's a dog*," said Sirius Black, through his fingers. "Prongs. *Prongs*. He's a *dog*. Our boy's a *dog*—"

"Wolf," said Harry. His voice cracked right down the middle.

"*Same family*, Harry, same *family*—"

"Sirius," said the red-haired woman.

She didn't raise her voice. She didn't have to. Sirius Black straightened up immediately, with his hands still over his mouth and his gray eyes streaming.

Lily Potter came forward.

She came across the rug and stopped in front of her son, kneeling there with his ears flat and the ring in his paw, and she looked at him. At his face. Up close. She was so small, standing over him, even with him on his knees. She had to tip her head back only a little.

She reached up and put her see-through hand on the side of his muzzle.

Leah didn't know if he felt it. She saw his eyes close. She saw his whole body lean, very slightly, toward that hand, the way Layla leaned toward his tongue in the mornings.

"Oh, Harry," said Lily. "Oh, look at you. Look at you."

"Mum," said Harry.

That was all he said. It was the only thing he said for a while.

---

They stayed a long time.

Leah knew it was a long time because the woodstove burned down to coals and she got up to put a log on it, twice, and because her mom made a pot of coffee and then another one, though nobody drank it. She knew it was a long time because at some point she realized she was sitting on the couch with her shoulder against Sirius Black's, more or less, a cold, thick, not-quite-there feeling down her whole left arm, while he told her a story about Harry's dad turning a teacher's hair blue in their fourth year, and she was laughing so hard she couldn't breathe.

She'd never in her life thought she'd meet them. Harry's people. She'd thought about them. She'd seen the photo on the table by the bed at the manor, the young witch with the long red hair laughing, holding up a baby. She'd heard him say *Mum* in his sleep, twice. She'd heard him say *Lily*, once, so quietly she wasn't sure.

Now they were in her mom's living room on Christmas night.

James had sat down on the floor next to Harry by the woodstove and was looking at his son's ears like he wanted to touch them, and couldn't, and kept forgetting he couldn't, and reaching. Harry had told him about the bite. About Romania, and the burning village, and the cell in Geneva, and waking up like this with his own head still on. James had listened to the whole thing with his face going pale, and then gone very quiet, and then said, "Moony would've had *kittens*," and Harry had laughed so hard he'd had to put his head down on his knees.

"You know about Moony," Harry had said, when he could talk again. "You know he—"

"We know," James had said. "He's fine. He's with Tonks. He's fine." He'd looked at Harry. "He's very proud of Teddy. He wanted me to say."

Harry hadn't said anything to that for a while.

Sirius had seen the coat and wanted to know where the motorbike was, and Harry had told him it was in the carriage house in Devon, and Sirius had gone very still, and then said, "You *kept* it," in a voice Leah hadn't heard him use yet. And then, in a completely different voice, "Does it still fly?" And when Harry said yes, Sirius had made Leah tell him about the night over Tamarack Crossing, the northern lights and the valley full of yellow lights and *move in with me!* over the roar of the engine, and Sirius had put his face in his hands again.

"He proposed on my *bike*," he'd said, muffled.

"He asked me to move in on your bike," Leah had said. "He proposed under an oak tree."

"Euphemia's oak," Lily had said. She'd been sitting on the arm of Sue's chair. "His grandmother's. James used to climb it."

"I fell out of it," James had said. "Twice. Broke my arm the second time. Mum made Dad cut the bottom branches off."

"The bottom branches go all the way to the ground," Leah had said.

James had grinned. "They grew back."

Then he'd turned and looked at his son with his eyebrows up over the crooked glasses.

"Also," he'd said. "You bought a *plane*?"

Harry's ears had gone pink inside. "So nobody would cough on her."

"You bought an entire aeroplane," James had said slowly, "so that nobody would *cough* on your *daughter*."

"Ja."

James had looked at him for a long moment. Then he'd turned to Lily, on the arm of Sue's chair.

"He gets that from you," he'd said.

"He gets that from *you*," Lily had said. "You had Harry's cot charmed against draughts, dust, damp, spiders, owls, and loud noises. You put a Shield Charm on the *nappies*, James."

"They were very good nappies."

Sirius had laughed so hard he'd slid off the arm of the couch, through the cushion, about halfway into the floor, and had to climb back out, which made Seth laugh so hard he had to leave the room.

---

Lily held the baby last.

She didn't, really. She couldn't. But Seth brought Layla over from the doorway, where he'd been standing for almost an hour without moving, and Leah took her, and sat down on the floor in front of Sue's chair with her, and Lily came down off the arm of the chair and knelt on the rug in front of them.

She looked at Layla for a long time.

Layla looked back. Right at her. Her blurry green eyes with the gold ring around them, wide open, fixed on the young red-haired woman's face. Her ears were up. Her nose was going.

Her tail was going too.

"She can see me," said Lily softly.

"She saw my dad," said Leah. "She held his finger."

Lily looked at the baby's eyes. Green. With a thin ring of gold.

She didn't say anything. She just looked at them. Leah watched her face, and watched it understand.

"Harry told me your name," Leah said. Her voice had gone rough. "Once. In his sleep. And then when he picked hers, I heard it in there. Under it." She looked down at Layla. "Layla. It's got a bit of me in it. And it's got a bit of you."

Lily Potter put both her see-through hands over her mouth.

James came and knelt down behind her. He put his hand on her shoulder. That one, Leah thought, they could do. The two of them. They could touch each other.

"She's got your eyes, Lils," said James quietly.

Lily nodded. She couldn't talk.

"And his tail," said James.

Lily laughed into her hands.

Layla's small forepaw came up out of the blanket. Reaching. The same way it had reached for Leah's dad. The tiny rice-grain claws spread.

Lily held out one finger.

And the baby took it, or took the place where it was, and held on. And purred.

Lily knelt there on the braided rug in front of Sue Clearwater's chair with her granddaughter holding her finger and wept without making a sound, and James held onto her shoulder, and across the room, by the woodstove, Harry sat with the ring in his paw and his ears flat and his eyes on the two of them and didn't move at all.

---

Sirius found Leah's ring.

She was standing in the kitchen doorway, later, with a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking, and he came over and looked at her right hand. At the heavy old gold band with the square green stone in it.

"Euphemia's," he said. "The Lady Potter's ring. Merlin. It opened for you?"

"The night we got married."

"It never opened for anybody. It sat in that tower for thirty years and James tried to pick the lock with his wand when we were fifteen and it gave him a shock that made his hair stand on end for a week." Sirius grinned. "Well. More on end." He looked at her a moment longer. "And you're Lady Potter-Black."

"Apparently."

"My condolences." He was still grinning. "My mother's portrait is screaming in a house in London right now, and it doesn't know why."

"Harry said."

"Harry's very polite about my mother." Sirius leaned against the doorframe. He looked over at Harry, across the room, kneeling by the woodstove now with his father beside him, the two of them talking low with their heads close together. "He hasn't told me what he wants to ask me yet. He's been working up to it for an hour. He's got the face."

Leah looked at him.

"There's a Black," she said. "A long time ago. On a fur ship. He came here. To this coast. Right down there, at the river mouth. He wrote a journal. A mage had pages of it. With the crest on them." She took a breath. "We think he married a girl from here. We think he took her back with him. To England."

Sirius went very still.

His face changed. The grin went out of it. He looked, for a moment, a lot older than he had a second ago. More like a man who'd spent twelve years somewhere very cold.

"There's a burn," he said slowly. "On the tapestry. At Grimmauld Place. Up near the top. Somebody from about—eighteen hundred, I suppose, or near enough. Phineas Nigellus's grandfather's brother, or thereabouts. I used to look at it when I was a kid. When my mother had blasted somebody else off and was in a mood, I'd go up to the drawing room and look at all the burns. Andromeda's. Uncle Alphard's. Mine, eventually." His mouth twisted. "That one was the oldest. Just a black hole in the cloth where a name used to be. I asked my mother once who it was."

"What did she say?"

"She said he'd thrown the blood away on a girl from the edge of the world." Sirius looked at Leah. "And that nobody would ever say his name in that house again. So nobody did."

Leah stood in her mom's kitchen doorway with the mug going cold in her hands.

"I always liked him," said Sirius. "Whoever he was. I used to think about him. Some Black who just—left. Went to the edge of the world and found somebody worth getting burned off the wall for." He looked at her a moment longer. Then he looked over at Harry, and his face went soft. "Seems like it runs in the family."

---

Her mom and Lily talked for a long time, at the kitchen table, with their heads together, with coffee neither of them drank. Leah didn't hear most of it. She didn't try. She caught a bit at the end, when she came in to put the cream back in the fridge.

"—thank you for him," her mom was saying.

"Thank *you*," said Lily. "For her. For raising her. For letting her go, when she needed to go, and for being here when she came back." She looked down at the table. "I didn't get to do any of that."

Her mom reached across the table and put her hand over Lily's. It went in a little way and stopped, cold. Her mom left it there anyway.

"You did the first year," said Sue. "That's the hardest one."

---

It was nearly midnight when Lily said it.

"Harry," she said. "Love."

He was kneeling by the woodstove, still, with the ring in his paw, with James sitting beside him and Sirius on the arm of the couch. He'd been holding it for four hours. He hadn't put it down once. He'd held it open, palm up, on his knee, like something that might fly away.

He looked up at his mother.

She didn't have to say anything else. Leah saw him understand.

His ears went flat. All the way. His paw, with the ring in it, curled. Just a little. Just the claws, coming in, like the paw wanted to close on it and hold on and never let go.

"I know," said Harry. His voice was very low. "I know. Just—a minute."

"Take a minute," said James. "Take two."

Nobody moved for a while.

Leah watched him. She watched him try. She watched him look down at the ring in his palm, and tell his paw to tip it out, and his paw not do it. The claws curled in a little more. His whole arm had started to shake, very slightly, the way her mom's had shaken four hours ago, sitting by the tree.

The second brother, he'd said. *Couldn't put it down.*

She got up off the floor where she'd been sitting with Layla. She gave Layla to Seth, who took her without a word. She crossed the rug and knelt down beside him, in front of the woodstove, where she'd said she'd be.

She put her hand under his paw. Under the ring. She held it there, palm to pad. She didn't do anything else.

"I'm right here," she said.

He looked at her. His eyes were wet, the way they'd been on the bathroom floor in Atlin, and on the dock under the moon, and over a tiny furred body in a bloody bed while he licked it until it breathed.

"I know," he said.

He looked back up at the three of them, standing in front of the tree.

James had his arm around Lily's shoulders. Sirius was beside them with his hands in his pockets. All three of them were looking at him.

"We never go far," said Lily. "You know that. You knew it in the forest."

"Ja," said Harry.

"Look after them," said James. "Look after that tail."

"*Look after the bike*," said Sirius.

Harry laughed. It came out of him cracked, half a sob.

Then he looked down at the ring. And at Leah's hand underneath his paw. And very slowly, a claw at a time, he made his paw open. Flat. All the way. Until the ring was lying on the dark pad of his palm with nothing holding it at all.

Leah turned her hand over, under his. She took the ring.

When she looked up, the tree was just a tree.

---

The ring sat in her palm for about three seconds. It was heavy and cold. She could feel it, very faintly, pulling at her—a thin cold thread going up her wrist toward her heart, saying *you could. You could have him back. Your dad. Every night. Turn it three times.*

The heavy gold ring on her right hand went hot.

The thread snapped.

Leah looked down. The Lady's ring, Euphemia's, the one that kept her head her own, was sitting on her finger glowing faintly green, like it had just had a word with something.

And then the black stone was gone. Her palm was empty. There was nothing in it at all.

"Where—" she said.

"Away," said Harry. He was looking at her empty palm. "Somewhere it can't hurt anybody. It'll come back when I ask." His ears were still flat. "I won't ask for a long time."

Leah closed her empty hand. She looked at him.

He was kneeling on the rug, with his paws open on his knees, looking at the place by the tree where his parents had been. His face was wet. His tail was lying still on the floor behind him, the way it almost never was.

She put her arms around him. As far as they'd go. And held on.

---

Her mom went to bed at half past twelve.

She stopped in the hall on the way, in her robe, and looked at Harry, sitting on the floor by the woodstove with Leah's head on his shoulder. She didn't say anything for a moment.

"Merry Christmas, Harry," she said.

"Merry Christmas, Sue."

She went down the hall. Leah heard her door shut. Then she heard her mom's bed creak, the way it always did, on the left side.

And then, after a while, very faintly, the right side.

Leah lifted her head off Harry's shoulder.

"She's sleeping on his side," she said.

"Ja," said Harry quietly. "I heard."

Seth was asleep on the rug in front of the crooked tree, flat on his back, snoring, with Layla asleep on his chest and both his arms around her. Somebody had put the green and gold star quilt over him. Leah didn't know who. She suspected.

She looked at the tree. At her dad's salmon, leaning off to the left.

"Why did you wait?" she said. "With Mom. Why'd you give it to her first? You could've done yours first. Before anybody. It was yours."

Harry was quiet for a long time.

"Because I knew I could put it down after I'd watched her do it," he said. "I wasn't sure before."

Leah thought about that.

"You couldn't put it down," she said.

"No," said Harry. "You were holding my paw."

She didn't say anything to that. She put her head back on his shoulder.

They sat there in front of the woodstove, in the light from the Christmas tree, for a long time. The house ticked and settled around them. The wind came up off the ocean and moved in the alders. Somewhere down the hall, her mom's bed creaked again, on the right side.

After a while Harry got up, very carefully, and went over to Seth, and lifted Layla off his chest without waking either of them, and came back, and sat down again beside Leah. He lifted Layla up in both forepaws. And he put her on top of his head.

She settled there in the fur between his ears, on her stomach, with her forepaws hanging down over his eyebrows and her chin on the crown of his skull and her tail down the back of his neck. The faint shimmer went over her and was gone. She didn't wake up.

Harry started humming.

Leah knew it after two notes. Low and slow, going up and taking a long step down and turning at the bottom and coming back. The star song. The old Black lullaby. The one Kreacher sang. The one some Black, two hundred years ago, might have sung to a girl at a river mouth, before he asked her father for her, and then asked her.

Up on top of his head, Layla purred.

His tail started going against the floor. Slow at first. *Thump. Thump.*

"It's Christmas," said Leah, into his shoulder. "For like eleven more minutes."

"Ja," said Harry.

"Merry Christmas, Harry."

"Merry Christmas, love."

*Thump*, went his tail, on her mom's living-room floor. *Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Leah closed her eyes and let it.
