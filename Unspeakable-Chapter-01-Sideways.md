# Unspeakable

## Chapter One: Sideways

*The clearing north of Forks, Washington — January 2007*

---

## I. Leah

The ash was still smoking.

Leah Clearwater kept looking at it because it was the only thing in the clearing that wasn't looking back at her. A gray smear on the snow ten yards in front of the Volturi line, steaming a little in the cold, like a cigarette somebody had ground out under a boot and walked away from. Twenty minutes ago it had been Irina. Twenty minutes ago Irina had stood in front of three old men in black cloaks and said she'd been wrong, the kid wasn't what she'd thought, she was sorry. And the white-haired one had nodded at her the way a principal nods at a kid who's owned up to something, and then he'd had her pulled apart.

The sound was what stuck. Leah had heard a lot of vampires come apart since last spring, more than she'd ever wanted to. It always sounded the same. Like somebody wrenching the door off a car.

*Eyes up,* Jacob thought. *Leah. Come on.*

*My eyes are where I want them.*

*Leah.*

She lifted her head.

The clearing hadn't changed since June. Same long bowl of dead grass between the trees, same lumps of granite poking up out of it, same mountains standing around the edges with their heads in the cloud. Back in June it had been green and full of newborns, and she'd gone after one that wasn't hers to go after because she'd wanted to prove something, and Jacob had thrown himself in the way, and she'd listened to his bones break from inside his own head. Half the right side of his body. She still heard it some nights.

Now it was white. Snow had come down in the dark and frozen into a crust, and the sky was so low and gray you could have reached up and dragged your claws through it. No sun. That was the only good news anybody had gotten all morning. No sun meant no sparkle, and no sparkle meant that if a hiker came over the ridge he'd see a lot of pale people in weird coats standing in a field, which was a cult, which was Washington, which was fine.

On the far side of the field, the Volturi stood in their wedge.

Thirty-two of them. She'd counted when they came out of the trees, because counting was something to do that wasn't running. Two more at the very back in solid black, small, kept close by guards, who Edward had said were the wives. The cloaks went from pale gray at the edges of the wedge to near black at the point, and at the point were the three ancients, and right now the three ancients had their heads together like a bunch of old men at a funeral deciding who was going to pay for the flowers.

Edward had said they were voting. He'd said it out loud, so the wolves would know. Leah had gotten the impression from his face that it wasn't much of a vote.

In front of the Volturi, rolling slowly across the snow, came the fog.

It had started maybe five minutes ago. Out of the little dark-haired one standing beside the blonde girl, the twins, Alec and Jane. It came out of him like breath on a cold morning and spread, low and gray and thick, at a walk, across the field toward the Cullen line. It had no smell. That was what bothered Leah most. Everything had a smell. The fog came on like it had been cut out of the world with scissors.

About thirty yards in front of the Cullens it stopped.

Leah watched it pile up against nothing. It climbed. It spread out sideways, feeling along the edge of something she couldn't see, the way water feels along the edge of a dam. It was Bella. Bella's shield, stretched out over all of them, the Cullens and the witnesses and every single wolf on both flanks. Leah couldn't feel it. She'd tried, the first time Bella had pushed it out over the pack in the Cullens' back yard, and there was nothing to feel. You just had to trust that Bella Swan was standing between you and the creepy fog with her face screwed up, holding.

Leah Clearwater's continued existence, as of nine-forty this morning, depended on the concentration of a girl who had once fallen down the stairs at a dance.

*She's doing great,* Seth thought. *Look at it. It can't get in.*

*Yeah,* Leah thought. *Great.*

*You could be nice for, like, one second.*

*I'm being nice. I'm thinking it in a nice voice.*

Seth sent her a picture of himself rolling his eyes. He was beside her on the end of Jacob's line, the sandy one, all legs. He'd grown four inches since summer and he still didn't know where all of himself was. Right now all of himself was shaking, a fine steady tremor running through his shoulders that he probably thought nobody could see.

Leah could see it. She was doing it too.

The pack mind sat in the back of her skull like a room full of people talking too loud, the way it always did. She hated it. She'd hated it from the first night, when the change ripped her open in the yard behind the house and she'd fallen into a head that already had Sam in it. Jacob's pack was better. Five of them instead of eleven, and none of them Sam. But it was still a room. She could still hear Quil thinking about Claire, the way he'd been thinking about Claire all morning, a little girl in a pink rain jacket stamping in a puddle on First Beach, over and over, like a song stuck in his head. She could hear Embry counting the guard. She could hear Jacob.

Jacob was the loudest, even when he wasn't trying. *South,* he kept thinking, to himself, under everything. *South. If it goes, I go. She's on my back and I'm in the trees before they turn around. South. Don't look back.*

Bella had put a backpack on the kid twenty minutes ago. Leah had watched her do it. She'd watched Bella's face while she did it, and something in her chest that she didn't have a name for had gone tight and stayed tight.

Her job, if it went, was to make sure nothing followed Jacob for the first mile. Her and Seth. That was all. One mile. After that Jacob would be faster than anything on earth except maybe the tracker, Demetri, and Demetri would be busy, because Leah would be hanging off his throat.

She'd made her peace with that around the Calawah bridge. Mostly. She'd left the house at four this morning without waking her mother. Her mother hadn't been there to wake. Her mother had been at Charlie Swan's, the way she was most nights now, making Charlie Swan eat something with a vegetable in it. Leah had stood in the empty kitchen in the dark and thought about writing a note and decided there wasn't anything to write that wouldn't sound stupid, and she'd gone out the back door and phased at the tree line and run.

*You should've left the note,* Seth thought.

*Get out of my head.*

*I can't. Nobody can. That's the whole—*

*Seth.*

*Okay.*

Across the field, Caius said something. Short and hard. Aro answered him with a little laugh, soft, the kind you'd use with a kid who'd asked for a second cookie. Marcus didn't say anything at all. Marcus looked like a man who'd been told he had to stay at the party until everybody else left.

The fog piled up against Bella's shield.

Somewhere, someone started whistling.

Leah's ears came up so fast she felt the skin of her skull pull.

It was faint. It was coming from the north, out of the trees at the top end of the clearing, behind her and off to her right, and it was so ordinary that for a second her brain refused to file it as real. A man whistling. A tune, with a beat to it, a little flat on the high notes, the way you whistle walking back to the truck from a long day on a job.

Every head in the clearing turned.

All of them. She saw it happen down the whole length of the Cullen line, a ripple of white faces swinging north. The witnesses. The wolves on her side, the wolves on Sam's side, sixteen sets of ears snapping forward. Across the field, the gray wedge of the Volturi shifted. The ancients broke apart. Aro lifted his head like a deer.

The whistling went on. It got closer.

*Is that—* Seth's thought came out strangled. *Leah. Is that Kansas?*

*Is that what?*

*Kansas. The band. That's "Carry On Wayward Son." Dude's whistling* Kansas.

*Seth, I swear to God—*

*I'm just saying! It's a good song!*

*Who is that?* Jacob, sharp. *Embry, who's out there? Do you smell anything?*

*Nothing.* Embry, rattled. *I don't smell anything. Jake, there's nobody there. There's nothing out there, it's just trees, I'd smell—*

The man walked out of the trees.

He came out of the timber at the north end of the clearing about forty yards from the end of Jacob's line, from between two big spruce with snow on their shoulders, ducking under a branch like a man coming out of a garage. He was still whistling. He had his hands in the pockets of a black leather jacket and his head down a little against the cold, watching where he put his feet, and he came down the slope onto the open snow and kept walking.

He was a man. Just a man. A human, Leah's brain tried to tell her, a human with a heartbeat, she could hear it now, slow and heavy, a big slow drum. Tall. Six-two, maybe. Broad through the shoulders and thick through the chest and arms, built heavy, like a guy who threw hay bales for a living. Black hair, messy, in need of a cut. The jacket was old, the leather gone soft and dull at the elbows. A dark flannel shirt open over a gray Henley. Jeans. Boots, wet to the laces.

He crossed into the open ground between the two lines and turned south down the middle of it, walking right down the gap between the Cullens and the Volturi, the way you'd walk down the aisle in a church, and he didn't look at anybody.

He didn't look at the vampires on his left. He didn't look at the vampires on his right. He walked straight past the end of Jacob's line, twenty yards from Leah's nose, past five wolves the size of horses with their hackles up and their lips peeled back, and he didn't so much as turn his head. He walked like a man who had been told there would be dogs and had decided that was the dogs' problem.

*What the hell,* Quil thought. *What the* hell, *Jake—*

*Hold,* Jacob thought. *Everybody hold. Nobody move.*

The fog had stopped spreading. Leah saw it. The gray stuff that had been piling up against Bella's shield had gone still, like a dog that has heard its name.

The man stopped walking about halfway down the field. Right in the middle. Fifty yards from the Volturi. Fifty yards from the Cullens. He stopped next to the gray smear in the snow that had been Irina, and he looked down at it, and he stopped whistling.

He stood there and looked at the ash for a second. Then he took his right hand out of his pocket, and Leah's whole body went tight, and he pulled the hand down over his face, slowly, from his forehead to his chin, the way you do at the end of a double shift.

The wind changed.

Or something changed. It felt like the wind, a push of cold air coming from where he stood, but it was more like a door opening. One second there was nothing there, no smell at all, a man-shaped hole in the air. The next second it hit them.

Pine. Leah got pine first, sharp and green, like the needles in the deep woods up past the Hoh where nobody ever went. Wet earth under it. Cold stone, river stone, the smell of a creek bed in January when the ice starts at the edges. And under all of that, rank and heavy and so old she felt it in the roots of her teeth—

Wolf.

Every wolf in the clearing jerked. She felt it in the pack mind like five people being shoved at once.

*He's a wolf,* Embry thought. *Jake. Jake, he smells like a—*

*I know what he smells like.*

*He's not one of us. He's not Quileute. That's not—that's not* us, *that's—*

Leah didn't know what it was. She knew what the pack smelled like. Woodsmoke and cedar, wet fur and hot copper. The smell of the res, the smell of her own skin. This was something else. Older. Bigger. A wolf from a long way off, from some cold country with a lot of trees in it, and it came off this guy in his leather jacket like heat off a stove, and her own wolf, which had never once in ten months done what she wanted it to, flattened its ears against her skull and made a sound in her chest she'd never heard it make.

She shut her jaws on it.

Across the field, she heard Caius hiss.

The man lifted his head and looked at the Volturi.

Leah still couldn't see his face. She had his profile from twenty yards back and to the side: a straight nose, a hard jaw with a couple of days of black stubble on it, the edge of one eye. No scars that she could see, no marks anywhere, the skin on his face and hands pale and plain. His breath steamed in the cold. Around his boots, the snow had started to go gray and wet, a dark ring spreading out across the crust from where he stood, as if he were standing on a stove.

He looked at the Volturi like a man looking at a bill.

"Hiya," he said. "Sorry. Don't get up."

His voice was English. Low and a bit rough, flat on the vowels, the ends of the words shaved off. The kind of English, Leah thought, that belonged to the guy who fixes your car in the movie about London and calls you love.

Nobody said anything.

"Right." The man put his hand into his jacket, inside, at the chest, and every vampire on the field moved at once, the whole Volturi wedge sinking a quarter-inch into a crouch, the Cullen line doing the same. He ignored all of it. He came out with a flat black leather wallet, flipped it open with his thumb, and held it up at shoulder height toward the Volturi. Then he turned and held it up toward the Cullens. Then, for about half a second, toward the wolves.

He still didn't look at them. He held the wallet at them and looked at the trees.

Leah's eyes were good. Better than any human's. She could read the card from where she stood. There was a silver seal at the top of it, a circle of little letters around a pair of crossed sticks, and the seal was moving. Turning slowly, all by itself, like a coin spinning on a table. Under the seal, in black type: INTERNATIONAL CONFEDERATION OF WIZARDS. Under that: STATUTE ENFORCEMENT — SPECIAL WARRANT. And a photo. A small square photo of the man in the jacket, and the man in the photo scowled, and then turned his head and looked off to the side, like he was bored of having his picture taken.

The photo *moved.*

*Did that picture just—* Seth thought.

*Yeah,* Leah thought. *Yeah, it did.*

"Potter," the man said. He flipped the wallet shut. "Harry Potter. I'm an Unspeakable, Department of Mysteries, Ministry of Magic, which means I'm not supposed to tell you that, so let's keep it between the hundred and fifty of us, yeah? Currently on loan to the Confederation." He put the wallet away. "Which none of you have heard of. Which is sort of the point of it."

*Harry,* Leah thought.

It went through her before she could stop it. *Harry.* Her dad's name. Her dad in his recliner with the Mariners on, her dad in his flannel at the boat launch, her dad on the kitchen floor with his hand on his chest and her mother screaming into the phone while Leah stood in the doorway shaking, with her skin splitting down her back. Ten months of not hearing anybody say it. Ten months of her mother not saying it. And this guy just said it, standing in a field, like it was any name.

*Lee,* Seth thought, very soft. He'd felt it. He'd felt all of it, the whole picture, the kitchen floor. *Lee, it's just a name.*

*I know it's just a name.*

*Okay.*

*Get out of my head.*

Seth didn't answer. He didn't get out, either. He couldn't.

"So," Harry Potter said.

He spread his hands, palms out, at the field. At the Volturi in their wedge, the ash steaming at his feet, the fog lying still on the snow, the line of the Cullens and their witnesses, the wolves.

"MACUSA's been watching this—" a little circle of one finger, taking in all of it, "—build up for a month. Vampires coming in from four continents. A pack of very large dogs. Forty minutes up the road from a town with a high school, a Thriftway, and a police chief. They've been having kittens about it over there. They rang the Confederation. The Confederation rang London. London rang me." He sniffed. "Which is how I've ended up in a field."

*Police chief,* Leah thought. *Charlie.* She felt Jacob think it at the same second. Felt it go through Jacob like a cold drip down the spine.

"This," the man said, "is a breach of the International Statute of Wizarding Secrecy on a scale nobody's had to write a report about in about sixty years. And I'm the sorry sod who's going to have to write it." He turned and looked at the Volturi, square on. "So. By order of the International Confederation of Wizards, this gathering is unlawful, and it's done. You're to disperse. All of you. Now."

He looked back across at the Cullens. Then, at last, briefly, along the line of the wolves, his head turning but his eyes not quite landing anywhere, skating over the top of them.

"Everybody," he said. "Go home. Back to Italy. Back to your caves. Back to wherever it is the rest of you lot hang upside down."

Silence.

Leah could hear the fog. That was how quiet it was. She could hear it settling against Bella's shield, a sound like a sheet being pulled very slowly off a bed.

Then, across the snow, at the point of the gray wedge, Aro began to laugh.

## II. Edward

Aro's mind was full of light.

Edward had been listening to it for an hour, and he'd thought he understood its shape by now. A great old house of a mind, every room lit, every room crowded with things Aro had collected and could not stop touching. Faces. Gifts. Secrets taken out of other people's heads with the touch of a hand and kept, all of them, in perfect order, for three thousand years. Under it ran the appetite, steady as a river under ice. Aro wanted. He wanted Alice, whom he'd never have. He wanted Edward, whom he'd wanted since the day Edward walked into his tower and asked to die. He wanted Bella's shield now that he'd watched Jane break her teeth on it all morning. He'd been standing across the snow for an hour turning the Cullens' gifts over in his hands like a man in a jewelry shop, deciding which pieces he'd take home.

And now every lamp in that house had been turned up to full.

*A wizard.* Aro's laugh rang off the trees, delighted, and under it his thoughts went racing. *A* wizard. *Oh, Caius. Oh, brothers. After all this time.*

Edward saw what Aro was seeing. A memory, sharp as glass in Aro's perfect recall. A narrow street in Florence, wet cobbles, torchlight, the year fourteen-something. A young man in a black scholar's gown with a stick in his hand, and one of Aro's guard reaching for him, and the guard's sword turning in his grip into a long brown snake that twisted up his arm and bit him on the cheek. Then a sound like a whip crack, and the street was empty from one wall to the other. Aro had stood in that street for an hour afterward, breathing the empty air where the boy had been.

*I looked for them for five hundred years,* Aro thought. *I had begun to think I'd dreamed it.*

"Forgive me," Aro said aloud. He touched one fingertip to the corner of his eye, as if wiping away a tear of laughter. There was no tear. "Forgive me, my friend. I'm rarely surprised anymore. It's the great sorrow of a long life. And you've surprised me so very thoroughly."

"Happens," Harry Potter said.

Edward reached for the man's mind.

He'd done it the instant the whistling started, by reflex, the way a man turns his head at a noise. He was still doing it. He'd been doing it the whole time the stranger walked down the field, and he'd gotten nothing. Bella's silence was soft. It was fog, a gentle blankness he could feel the shape of, like a hand under a sheet. This was different. When Edward reached for Harry Potter, his own mind slid off something cold and smooth and very heavy, like a man laying his ear against the stone wall of a dam and feeling, on the other side, the whole weight of the lake.

Nothing came through. He had the sense that a great deal was on the other side.

"I'm so very curious," Aro went on, in his soft, pleasant voice. "A Confederation. A Statute. A Ministry of—magic, you said? How wonderful. You must forgive an old man his ignorance. You see, my brothers and I have kept order among our kind for three thousand years. We have always been the law."

"Yeah, all right, Judge Dredd," Harry said.

Somewhere on Edward's left, in the middle of the Cullen line, Emmett's mind snorted.

It was so loud and so ordinary that Edward almost flinched. *Judge Dredd. Ha. Stallone. Oh, I like this guy.* Emmett was standing at the front of the line with his shoulders loose and his weight forward, the way he'd been standing all morning, a big body humming with the thing it wanted. He'd wanted a fight since the Volturi came out of the trees. He'd been trying very hard, for Rosalie's sake and Carlisle's, not to want it. *Who the hell is this guy. Rose. Rose, look at him. He just walked in. He* walked *in.*

*I see him,* Rosalie thought back, without words, just a hard flat feeling, the feeling of her hand resting on Jacob's shoulder over Renesmee's small mittened foot. *I see him. Be still.*

Aro hadn't understood the reference. His mind turned it over, found nothing, set it aside. "I don't believe I know your judge."

"You wouldn't. He's a comic." Harry put his hands back in his pockets. "Look, mate. You lot live on somebody's patch. Everybody does. Yours is Italy. The Italian Ministry's had you on file since your tower got its roof. Big file. Lots of pictures. Little note on the front, says *keeps the others tidy, leave alone.*" He tipped his head at the field. At the wedge of cloaks, the fog, the witnesses, the ash at his feet. "This is about as far from tidy as you can get without a marching band."

"We are not party to your Statute."

"Don't have to be. You're on it. You're on it the same way a badger's on it."

On Aro's right, Caius's mind went white.

Edward had heard Caius angry before. Caius was angry the way other people were tall. But this was something else, a single bright filament, a flare of hatred so old and so pure that it had worn a groove in him like water in stone. Edward saw what Caius saw. A forest at night. A full moon. A huge pale shape on its hind legs tearing a vampire in half and throwing the pieces. Caius had hunted the Children of the Moon for a thousand years, across Europe and into Asia, until there were almost none left. He knew that smell. He'd come to Forks hoping to find it.

*It reeks of them,* Caius thought. *Of the moon-dogs. Smell it, Aro. Under the skin. Whatever he says he is, he is* that.

He wasn't wrong, Edward thought, about the smell. It had come across the snow to the Cullen line a minute ago, and every wolf on both flanks had gone stiff with it. It smelled older than the Quileutes. It went down further. It was like smelling a forest you'd never been in, at night, from very far away.

And on Aro's left, under the long gray weight of Marcus's mind, something stirred.

Marcus didn't think much. His mind was a long hallway in an empty house where the dust had settled so thick on everything that the shapes had gone soft. But his gift ran whether he cared or not. He saw ties. He saw the cords between people the way other people saw color, without trying, without wanting to. All morning Edward had been looking at the clearing through Marcus's dull eyes and seeing the threads: the steel cable between himself and Bella, the bright braid between Jacob and Renesmee, the thin brittle strings of fear that tied the Volturi witnesses to their masters.

Marcus had looked at the man in the leather jacket, and he had seen almost nothing. A few long fine threads running off out of the clearing toward the east, so faint and far that they might have been going to the other side of the world. Nothing tying him to anybody in this field.

And one more.

One heavy cord, thick as a ship's rope, dark, that ran from the stranger *into himself.* It went down into his chest and down and down, coiling, the way a rope goes down into a well, and Marcus couldn't see the bottom of it.

*Strange,* Marcus thought. It was the first thing Edward had heard him think with any color in it all morning. *A bond to something within. I have not seen that.* Then, slowly, like a man settling back into a chair: *Well.*

The gray closed over it again.

"I'd like to understand," Aro was saying. He'd taken a step forward from the wedge. Renata came with him, her palm flat between his shoulder blades, her small face tight. "I would so like to understand you. You see, I have a gift. If I might only take your hand. One touch, and I would know everything you know, every thought you've ever had. It would save us so much time." He held out his hand across the snow. Long white fingers, almost translucent, held palm up, like an old man asking a girl to dance. *Let me in,* he thought. *Oh, let me see.*

"You're all right, thanks," Harry said.

Aro left his hand out a moment longer. Then he let it fall.

And he smiled. Edward heard the decision come, smooth and easy, as easy as Aro decided everything. The forty witnesses at the trees. The thing he wanted, the Cullen gifts, the girl's shield. The vote, half-done. And standing in the middle of it all, a single man with a heartbeat and a funny card who wouldn't give his hand, who could be dealt with afterward, at leisure, by Felix, in a quiet room. A wizard. A lovely new thing, to be taken home and opened slowly.

*Later,* Aro thought. *He'll keep.*

"How very droll," Aro said, and turned his back.

He simply turned around. He turned his shoulder on Harry Potter and faced his brothers, and lifted his hands, and put his fingertips together under his chin, and the three ancients drew their heads back in close the way they had been when the whistling began.

"Now," Aro said. "Brothers. Where were we?"

All down the wedge, the Volturi guard relaxed out of their crouch. Edward heard it go through them, a ripple of contempt and boredom. Felix's mind was already somewhere else. *Big one. Heart like a horse. Afterward,* he thought, and thought about Heidi. Demetri was still turning the stranger over, trying for the taste of his mind so he could track him, and finding nothing, and filing that away as a puzzle for a quiet afternoon. Jane had turned back to Bella. Her eyes had been on Bella all morning, and now they went back, and Edward heard her gift wind up again in her like a fist closing around a hot coal. *Burn,* she thought at the shield. *Burn. Burn.*

In front of the wedge, Alec breathed out, and the fog that had been lying still against Bella's shield shivered, and began to climb again.

"When we've finished," Caius said, without turning his head, "Felix. Take that away. I want to know what it is."

"Gently," Aro murmured. "Gently, Felix. I want it whole."

"Master," Felix said, and grinned.

They'd dismissed him. Edward watched them do it. He watched three thousand years of habit close over the man in the field like water closing over a stone. Thirty-four creatures who had never in their long lives been given an order they couldn't ignore had simply put him out of their minds, and gone back to the business of deciding how to kill Edward's family.

In the middle of the field, Harry Potter took his hands out of his pockets.

"Okay," he said.

He said it at an ordinary volume, the way a man says it when the waiter's told him the kitchen's closed.

"Don't say I didn't ask nicely."

He lifted his right hand to shoulder height, and snapped his fingers.

---

It was a small sound. A dry little click, like a twig breaking under a boot.

Edward heard what came after it with everything he had.

Thirty-four minds went out.

They went out together. Edward had once stood at the back of a concert hall in Chicago, in 1927, when the orchestra was playing and the chorus was singing, and the power had failed. Every light in the place and the orchestra's lamps with it, cut off in the same instant in the middle of a chord, so that the singing went on a half-second into the dark by itself before it faltered and stopped. It was like that. Thirty-four voices in the great noisy room of the clearing, and every one of them cut off in the middle of a thought.

He heard Felix think *Heidi*—

He heard Demetri think *where*—

He heard Caius. Caius's last thought was hatred, the same white filament, the same forest and the same moon, and it burned for a hundredth of a second brighter than it had ever burned, and then there was nothing where it had been.

He heard Aro think *mine*—, warmly, the way a man reaches for something on a high shelf, and not get there.

He heard Jane. He would remember Jane longest. Her mind had been pointed at Bella, burning, *burn, burn, burn,* and then the burning came the other way, into her, and for a single instant Jane knew the thing she'd done to thousands of people for eight hundred years. She didn't scream. There wasn't time. There was only understanding, a single bright note of it, huge and astonished, and then there wasn't anything.

He heard Chelsea's threads let go. All of them, all at once. Every cord she'd ever tied between the guard and its masters, every loyalty she had stitched into every one of them, went slack in the same instant and fell away, and for a fraction of a second, a dozen of the guard were free. They had just long enough to feel it.

And Marcus.

Marcus's long gray hallway lit up, once, from end to end. And in the light, Edward heard him think a single word, very calmly, the way a man sets down a heavy bag at the end of a long road.

*Finally.*

Then the quiet.

It came down on the clearing like a hand. A clean hole where thirty-four minds had been, as if somebody had taken a pair of scissors to the noise of the world and cut a piece out.

The fire was white.

It came from inside them. Edward saw it in the same instant he heard the minds go, a pale flame that was almost no color at all in the flat gray light, like a gas jet seen in daylight. It came out of every collar and every cuff and every seam of every cloak at once. For a quarter of a second the whole Volturi wedge stood there wrapped in it, thirty-two figures and two more at the back, motionless, as if nobody had told them yet. Then the white went blinding. The sound came, a single great soft *whump* like a furnace catching, felt in the chest more than heard.

One second.

Edward counted it. He couldn't help counting things; he never had been able to. One second from the snap to the end. The columns of white flared and shrank and fell in on themselves. Where they'd stood, there was ash. Thirty-four slumped gray drifts of it on the snow, some of them holding the shape of a body for a moment, a shoulder, a hood, before they softened and settled. Around each one, a circle of bare black earth where the snow had gone straight to steam. Here and there in the gray a glint of metal: a ring, a clasp, a heavy gold chain with the Volturi crest on it lying across a little heap of ash that had been Aro.

The fog was gone. Alec had been the fog, and the fog had gone up with him.

The heat reached them a heartbeat later. It rolled across fifty yards of snow and hit Edward full in the face, like an oven door opening. Then the smell. Sweet and dry and heavy, like incense in a church, like burnt sugar. The smell of his own kind burning. He'd smelled it at the end of every fight in his long life. He'd never smelled this much of it at once.

The wind took the ash and lifted it.

It came off the snow in long slow sheets and turned in the air and drifted east, toward the Cullen line, toward Edward, and began to come down on all of them, soft, gray, like snow out of a dirty sky.

Nobody on the Cullen side made a sound.

On the far side of the clearing, along the western trees, forty-odd minds that had come to watch a family die began to scream.

The noise of it hit Edward like a wave. At first it was nothing but terror, raw and white, forty voices at once, the sound of a herd that has just watched a lion take the lead animal in a single bite. Then words came up out of it. *Run. RUN. What is he, what IS he. Aro's gone. They're all gone. Gone. All of them. In a* breath. *Don't look at him. Don't let him see you. RUN.*

They went. They broke from the tree line like birds off a wire, in every direction at once, a blur of bodies and a long ragged crack of breaking branches, and then the sound of them going, and going, getting fainter, the forest swallowing them up mile by mile until Edward could only hear the last few, far off, thinking the same thing over and over like a prayer.

*Dispersed. We dispersed. We did what he said. We dispersed. Don't come after us. Please.*

Then they were out of range.

On the Cullen side of the field, somebody started to laugh.

It was Vladimir. Edward heard it in his mind first, a sound like a dam breaking, and then out loud, a dry, cracked, wheezing laugh that seemed to come up out of him from somewhere a very long way down. He'd gone to his knees in the snow. Stefan was standing beside him with both hands pressed flat over his own mouth, staring at the place where the wedge had been, and he was laughing too, silently, his whole thin body shaking with it.

*Fifteen centuries,* Vladimir was thinking. *Fifteen centuries in the dark. Fifteen centuries we sat in that tower and waited and planned and dreamed of it. And a boy in a coat. A boy in a coat snaps his fingers. Like a man calling a waiter. Oh. Oh, Stefan. Oh, look at it. Look at Aro.* The mind was so full of joy it was hideous. *Look at the* ash.

Edward looked away from them.

Bella's hand had found his. He didn't know when. Her grip was a newborn's grip, hard enough to crush stone, and he didn't care. On his other side, Jacob stood rigid with every hair on his back on end, and on Jacob's back, Renesmee sat very straight, her small hands buried in his fur, her face white, and her mind—

Her mind was quiet. Edward had never heard it quiet. Renesmee's mind was always moving, always bright, pictures tumbling over pictures. Now it was holding perfectly still, the way a rabbit holds still under a hawk. One picture. The man in the leather jacket, with his hand up.

In the middle of the field, Harry Potter lowered his hand.

He looked at it for a second. Flexed the fingers. Wiped them, absently, on the thigh of his jeans, as if he'd gotten something on them. He looked at the thirty-four piles of ash and the thirty-four black circles in the snow and the gold chain lying across the heap where Aro had been. Ash was settling in his black hair and on the shoulders of his jacket. He didn't brush it off.

He blew out a long breath. It steamed.

For as long as Edward had been what he was, and for two hundred years before that in Carlisle's memory, the Volturi had been the one fixed point in the world. The thing even Carlisle bowed to. The mountain at the edge of every map. Edward had walked into their tower less than a year ago to ask them to end him, because there was nothing on earth that could stand against them, and they'd been the closest thing to God he had.

A man in a leather jacket had just turned them off like a light.

Harry Potter turned around.

He turned his back on the empty side of the field, on the drifting ash and the black circles, as if none of that needed any more of his attention. He looked across fifty yards of snow at the Cullens. At their witnesses. At the wolves on the flanks. His face didn't do anything at all.

"Right," he said. "You lot."

## III. Leah

"Same goes," the man said. "Same order. Disperse. Everybody home. I don't care whose side you were on, I don't care who started it, I don't care who's right." He jerked a thumb back over his shoulder at the ash, without looking at it. "I've got thirty-four of those to write up, and every one of you that's still stood here in five minutes is a paragraph. So let's not."

Nobody moved.

Leah couldn't have moved if she'd wanted to. Her legs had gone to wood. Thirty-four vampires. She'd been in fights with vampires. She'd been in the newborn fight in June, right here, on this snow, when it had been grass. She knew what it took to kill one. Teeth and weight and luck and a lot of screaming, and then fire, and the fire took its time. She'd stood around a bonfire with the pack afterward, watching the purple smoke go up for an hour.

He'd snapped his fingers.

*Jake,* Embry thought. Just that. *Jake.*

Jacob didn't answer. Jacob was thinking one word over and over, so hard it was almost a sound. *South. South. South.* Under it, the kid's weight on his back, small and warm, and the tremor in her hands through the fur.

The man looked along the Cullen line.

He did it slowly, left to right, from the far end, the way you'd look along a shelf for something. The doctor at the front with his hands half raised. The mother beside him. The Denali women, the dark Amazons, the Irish, the Egyptians. The two Romanians, one on his knees laughing and the other with his hands over his mouth. The man's eyes stayed on the Romanians for half a second, like he was thinking about it, and then moved on.

Then he looked at the wolves.

He'd done everything he could to not look at them, Leah realized. The whole time. Walking past them in the snow, standing in the middle of the field, holding up his card. He'd turned his head in their direction and pointed his face at them and let his eyes go past, over their heads, at the trees. Like a guy who's been told not to look a strange dog in the eye.

Now he looked. He started at the north end of the line. The end nearest to where he'd walked out of the trees.

Her end.

His eyes came to hers.

Green. She'd thought they might be, from the profile. They were very green, a dark plain green like the moss on the north side of a cedar, and they met hers across twenty yards of snow, and—

It was the first phase all over again.

That was the only thing she had to measure it against. That night in the yard behind the house, last spring, when the thing in her blood finally broke out and ripped her open down the spine and turned her inside out on the grass while her mother screamed in the kitchen. Everything she was coming out of her at once. It was like that, except backward. Everything came *in.*

She felt them go. All the things she'd carried around for years like rocks in her pockets. Sam. Sam and Emily on the porch. Emily's scarred face, Emily's sweet sorry smile. Her dad on the kitchen floor. Her mother at Charlie Swan's table. The pack in her head, all of them, always, with their stupid thoughts and their stupid sympathy and their stupid imprints. The way the guys stopped talking when she phased in. Being the only one. Being the freak, the girl wolf, the one the magic got wrong. She felt every one of them come loose at once, like hooks pulled out of her skin.

For one second she weighed nothing. She stood in the snow and didn't touch it.

Then it all came back. Every single hook, every rock, everything she was. It came back and it was all still there, her dad and her mom and Seth and the rez and the beach and even, God help her, even Sam. All of it. But it had been hung back up somewhere new. It all hung from one place now. One hook. And the hook was a man in a leather jacket standing in a puddle of his own melted snow, with ash in his hair.

The ground tilted.

That was what it felt like. The whole clearing tilted toward him, gently, the way the deck of her uncle's boat tilted in a swell, and everything in it slid his way. The snow. The trees. The light. Her.

She knew what it was. Of course she knew.

She'd seen it from the inside five times. She'd been inside Sam's memory of it more times than she could count, because Sam had never been able to stop remembering it: the dirt road, Emily on the porch with a dish towel over her shoulder, his whole world picking itself up and setting itself down facing the other way. Jared's, in a classroom. Paul's, in Billy's yard, over Jacob's sister. Quil's, live, on the beach, over a two-year-old in a pink rain jacket. Jacob's, live, on the floor of the Cullens' house, over a bloody baby in Rosalie's arms. She'd felt it every time. She'd felt the gravity go, every time, standing right there in their heads, feeling it go for somebody else.

She'd hated it more than anything she'd ever hated. More than the leeches. More than her own fur. It was the thing that took Sam. It was the thing that told her, every single time it happened to somebody who wasn't her, that she didn't count.

And now it had happened to her. Here. Today. In the middle of the end of the world.

Over a guy who'd just burned thirty-four people alive by snapping his fingers.

*No.*

She thought it so hard her vision went white at the edges.

*No. No, no, no, NO. Not now. Not this. Not HIM.*

Somewhere small, down at the bottom of her, under the horror, something said: *So it works. It works on you. You were never broken.*

She stamped on it. She stamped on it so hard she felt her own claws drive through the crust into the frozen dirt underneath.

The man's eyes stayed on hers for maybe half a second.

Something happened in his face. Small. A hitch, the way your sleeve catches on a nail going past. His brows came down a fraction. His head tilted maybe an inch, like a man who's heard a noise in another room and can't place it.

Then his eyes went on. Past her. Past Seth. Down the line.

Past Quil, past Embry, to Jacob. And to the kid on Jacob's back.

They stopped there.

Leah felt it in Jacob, the instant it happened. Felt every muscle in Jacob's body lock. Felt the kid's hands knot in his fur.

The man looked at the kid. At the white parka, the bronze curls, the backpack on her small shoulders. Leah could see his nostrils move. The air had gone dead still after the fire, and in the stillness everything carried: the sweet dry vampire smell, the hot copper of the wolves, and the kid. The kid's smell was a strange one. Leah had been around it for months and she still didn't have a word for it. Blood, real blood, alive and moving, braided right in with the leech-sweet, both at once, in one small body. And the kid had a heartbeat. Fast, high, light, like a bird's.

He'd heard it. Leah watched him hear it.

"Hang about," the man said.

He took one step toward the Cullen line.

"What the hell is *that?*"

Emmett Cullen moved.

Leah saw it start. Emmett was standing at the front of the Cullen line, the biggest one, built like a vending machine, and he'd been standing loose all morning with his weight forward and his hands open, the way he always stood when he wanted to hit something. She'd heard him laugh more than once in the last month, out in the Cullens' yard, wrestling Jacob for fun. He wasn't laughing now. His face had gone hard and blank, and he said one word, low.

"Nope."

And he went.

He was fast. He was the strongest of the Cullens and maybe one of the strongest leeches she'd ever seen, and he came off the line like a truck coming off a ramp, low and huge, his arms out to take the man around the middle and carry him away from the kid, away from all of them, into the ground.

Leah's body moved before she did.

She didn't decide it. She'd remember that later, standing on it like a rock in a river: she didn't decide one thing. Her back legs drove and she was off the end of the line and across the snow with her ears flat and her lips peeled back, going for Emmett Cullen. Going for him to get between him and the man. Emmett, who'd thrown a Frisbee for Seth in the Cullens' back yard. Emmett, who she'd been standing next to on the same side of the same fight for an hour. She was going for his throat, two bounds, three, and there was nothing in her head at all except the man in the leather jacket, and the big pale shape coming at him, and *no*—

The man didn't look at Emmett. He was still looking at the kid.

Emmett caught fire in the air.

He was ten feet out from the man, at the top of his leap, both arms reaching. The white flame came out of him the way it had come out of the Volturi. Out of his collar and his cuffs and the seams of his shirt, almost colorless in the gray light, then blinding. Leah was close enough this time to feel it on her face, a slap of heat that singed her whiskers and dried her eyes. One second. He came apart in the air. What hit the snow was ash. It came down in a long soft spray, across the snow, across the man's boots, across Leah, all over her face and her shoulders and her back, as she skidded to a stop five yards from the man with all four legs locked and the crust tearing under her claws. A gust came across the field out of the west and took the rest of him, and carried it back the way he'd come, over the Cullen line.

A little ring bounced on the snow. A plain gold band. It hit the crust and bounced twice and rolled and lay still a foot from the man's left boot.

Behind Leah, Rosalie Cullen screamed.

Leah had heard a lot of screams. It was a new one.

*LEAH.*

Jacob. In her head, loud enough to hurt. And under him, all of them, the whole pack, at once, because they'd all felt it. They'd felt her look into that guy's eyes, and the hooks go out and come back in, and the whole world tilt. In the chaos of the last ten seconds maybe they hadn't put it together. They'd put it together now. They'd just felt her throw herself at a Cullen to protect a stranger.

*Lee?* Seth. Small and lost. *Lee, what are you—oh. Oh. Oh no. Oh, Leah—*

*She imprinted.* Embry. *Jake. Jake, she* imprinted. *On* him.

*On the fire guy?* Quil, high and cracking. *On the snap guy? Leah imprinted on the snap guy?*

*Shut up,* Leah thought. *Shut up shut up shut up SHUT UP—*

*Leah, get back here.* Jacob, one notch short of the Alpha voice. *Get back in the line.*

She couldn't.

She tried. She thought it at her legs, as hard as she could: *turn around, go back, go back to Jake, go back to Seth.* Her legs stood in the snow and didn't do anything. She was standing out in the open, in the middle of the field, five yards from him, covered in Emmett Cullen, alone. Every hair on her body stood straight up. Her whole wolf was turned toward the man like a needle.

The man looked down at her.

It was a short look. Half a second. A gray wolf with ash in her fur, standing between him and the vampires with her teeth out. The crease came back between his eyebrows, and stayed there.

"Ta," he said to her. Dry. Like she'd held a door.

Then he looked back up at the Cullen line.

Leah stood there with her teeth bared and the word *ta* going around and around in her skull and wanted to bite something until it stopped moving.

Along the Cullen line, everybody had gone still.

They'd been halfway to something, all of them, and they'd stopped. Rosalie had come off the line right behind Emmett with her mouth open on that scream, and she'd gotten maybe three strides, and the doctor and Edward had her. They had her by both arms. Edward had let go of Bella to do it. Rosalie was still pulling, hard, harder than Leah had ever seen a leech pull at anything, her heels carving long grooves in the crust, and the two of them were leaning back against her with everything they had, and Carlisle was saying her name, *Rose, Rose, Rosalie,* over and over, in a voice that didn't sound like any voice Leah had heard him use.

Behind them, the blond Denali one, Kate, had blue light crawling over her knuckles, and the buckskin guy, Garrett, had both arms wrapped around her from behind. The tall Amazon was up on her toes with her eyes fixed on the man like she was trying to do something with them. The sandy-haired Egyptian kid had his hands down at his sides with the fingers spread, and the snow around his feet had begun to shiver. Little ripples running out across the crust like water in a pan.

All of them caught there. Halfway. Looking at the ash coming down.

Leah watched it go through them. She watched each one of them do the math she'd done when the Volturi went up. Thirty-four with one snap. Emmett without even a snap. Without the guy so much as turning his head.

Kate's sparks went out. The Egyptian kid's fingers closed. The ripples in the snow died.

Rosalie kept pulling.

"Anyone else?" the man said.

He said it to all of them. Ordinary voice, like he was asking if anybody wanted the last slice.

Nobody answered him.

"No?" He nodded, once. "Good."

## IV. Bella

Emmett was on her.

That was what Bella kept noticing, stupidly, with the part of her mind that hadn't stopped working. He was on her hands. He was on the sleeves of her coat. A gray dust, very fine, settling in the creases of her knuckles, softer than any ash she'd ever touched, and it smelled like incense and burnt sugar, and it was Emmett. Emmett who'd taught her to arm-wrestle. Emmett who'd laughed so hard at her first hunt that he'd fallen out of a tree. Emmett who'd bet against her every single time and paid up every single time and grinned while he did it.

He'd said *nope.* That was the last thing. *Nope.* Like he was turning down a second helping.

Rosalie had stopped screaming. That was worse. She hung between Edward and Carlisle with her arms stretched out in their hands and her heels dug into the snow, and she wasn't pulling anymore, and her face was turned toward the place where Emmett had come down, and her mouth was open, and nothing was coming out of it. Esme had both hands pressed over her own mouth. Esme's eyes were huge and black.

Bella's shield was still out.

She could feel it, every inch of it, stretched over the whole long line of them like a skin. She'd pushed it out an hour ago and held it, against Jane, against Alec's fog, and she'd been so proud of it, so *proud,* and the fire had gone through it like it wasn't there. She hadn't felt anything. Emmett had been under her shield, and the man had burned him, and the shield hadn't even noticed.

Whatever he did, it didn't go through minds. Her shield was a mind thing. It had never been anything else.

So it couldn't stop him. Not that.

*Think,* she told herself. *Think, think.* She was a newborn. She was the strongest thing on this field, except for him. She could be across the snow in under a second. She could have her hands around his throat before his heart beat twice.

Emmett had been faster than that.

Jacob stood rigid at her side with her daughter on his back, his whole body trembling, his lips pulled back from his teeth. Renesmee's hands were buried in his fur. Her eyes were fixed on the man, enormous and dry, her mouth a tiny straight line, and Bella could hear her heart, going too fast.

Across the snow, one of the wolves was standing out in the open by itself.

Leah. The little gray one. She was standing five yards from the man with ash all over her back, facing the Cullens, facing *them*, with her teeth bared. Bella didn't understand it. She'd gone off the line when Emmett went. Bella had seen her go. She'd thought Leah was going for the man, and then she'd seen the angle, and Leah had been going for Emmett.

None of it made any sense.

"Anyone else?" the man said. "No? Good."

He looked at Renesmee again.

Bella felt it.

She felt it on the shield before anything else. Something touched the outside of it, high up, right over Jacob, right over the place where her daughter sat, and it was cold. Thin and cold and very, very sharp. It ran along the outside of the shield for an instant, lightly, like a fingertip running along the seam of a cushion, looking. It found the place over Renesmee where the shield always went thin, the place she could never quite make thick, where her daughter's own strange gift wore it from the inside like a thumb wearing through a sock.

It cut.

It was the cleanest thing she'd ever felt. One long straight stroke, from the top of the shield down, a single line drawn through it like a scalpel through silk. It went through without a shove or a tear, and the edges fell open on either side of it, and the cold thing slid in through the gap so fast she didn't have time to close it.

She felt it in her body. A line of ice drawn down the inside of her left arm, from the shoulder to the wrist. Down her ribs. It didn't hurt, not at first. It felt like a paper cut feels in the half-second before you know you've done it.

Then it started to hurt.

On Jacob's back, Renesmee went stiff.

Her small hands locked in his fur. Her back went straight as a board. Her eyes stayed on the man, open, fixed, and Bella watched the light in them go flat and still, like a pond when the wind stops.

"No," Bella said. "No, no, *no—*"

She moved. She got a step. Edward's voice came at her across Rosalie's shoulder.

"Bella. *Bella.* Don't."

"He's—"

"He's inside her." Edward's face had gone a color Bella had never seen it go. He was still holding Rosalie's arm. He was looking at their daughter and listening, with his whole body, the way he listened. "He's in her head. He's going through it. Bella, if you go at him—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Emmett was on her hands.

"Is he hurting her?" Bella heard her own voice crack straight down the middle. "*Edward.* Is he hurting her?"

Edward didn't answer for a second. A long second.

"He's fast," Edward said. "He's very—" He stopped. "Precise. Clean. He's also not being gentle about it. At all. He's going through everything she's got like a man going through a filing cabinet, and he knows exactly which drawer he wants, and he's not stopping to put anything back. She can feel him. She's frightened. She's trying to push pictures at him to make him go, and they just slide off. She doesn't understand what's happening." His voice broke. "Bella, she's asking for you."

Bella made a sound she didn't know she had in her.

Jacob was shaking under Renesmee. The trembling he'd been doing all morning had turned to a hard, racking shake, all four legs braced and his whole body straining forward toward the man, every muscle in his shoulders bunched to go. And he didn't go. He stood there, straining, as though there were an invisible chain around his neck and he'd hit the end of it. His lips were pulled so far back off his teeth she could see his gums. A sound was coming out of him, low and broken, a sound she'd never heard a wolf make.

Bella didn't understand that either. Jacob would have died for Renesmee a thousand times over without a second thought. He'd have gone for the man the instant he looked at her.

He wasn't going.

Five seconds. Six. Renesmee sat on Jacob's back with her eyes open and nothing in them.

Seven.

The cold thing came back out.

It came out the way it had gone in, straight, fast, through the cut in the shield, and it was gone. Renesmee's whole body jerked. She sucked in a breath like a swimmer coming up. Then her face crumpled, all at once, and she began to cry. She cried without any sound, her mouth open and her eyes squeezed shut and the tears running down her pink cheeks, and her small hands stayed knotted in Jacob's fur so tight the mittens stretched.

Bella reached for the shield, to close it.

The cut was still there. She could feel the edges of it, lying open, a hair apart. She pulled at them, and they came most of the way together and stopped, and wouldn't meet, and the line between them burned like ice. It would heal, maybe. Maybe it wouldn't. It would leave a mark either way. She knew that the way you know a burn will scar while you're still holding your hand under the tap.

The man in the field let out a breath.

He hadn't moved. He stood there in the slush with Emmett's ash on his boots and Leah standing guard in front of him, and he looked at Renesmee crying, and his face didn't change. He frowned a little. The way you'd frown at a crossword clue.

Then his eyes came to Bella.

She knew. She knew before he said a word. He'd gone into her daughter for answers, and her daughter was four months old, and some of the answers weren't in there. Some of them had never been in there. Some of them were in Bella.

"Right," he said. "Mum."

*No.*

She pulled the shield in.

She'd never done it so fast. A month of practice, pushing it out slow and careful, inch by inch, over Edward, over the house, over the whole family, and she'd never once yanked it all back at the same time. She did it now. It came back to her from both ends of the line like a sail coming down in a gale, and every Cullen and every witness and every wolf fell out from under it in the same instant, and it slammed down around her own skin, around her own mind. She folded it. Once, twice, four times, six, layer on layer on layer, as thick and as hard as she could make it, until she was standing inside something like the door of a bank vault.

The cut ran through all of it. Through every layer. She could feel it, a thin cold line, like a crack going down through a stack of plates.

She held it shut with everything she had.

"Bella," Edward said, "what are you—"

The man came in.

He didn't bother with the cut. He didn't bother with anything. He came straight at the front of the vault the way a truck comes through a garage door, and Bella felt it hit, and it was the worst thing she had ever felt in her life, worse than her spine breaking, worse than the three days of fire, because the fire had at least been happening to her body.

The layers broke.

All of them. She heard them go inside her own skull, one after another after another, a sound like plate-glass windows coming down in a row, and there was nothing between her and him at all.

She went to her knees in the snow.

## V. Harry

The kid had come first.

There'd been a shield over her. He'd felt it the second he walked out of the trees: a big soft stretchy thing pitched over the whole of the Cullen side like a circus tent, from one end of the dogs to the other. Somebody's. It had been eating the little blonde Italian's pain thing all morning without a mark on it, and the fog, and it gave when you leaned on it and came back when you stopped. Good shield. Better than most of what he saw at work.

It was thinnest over the kid. Worn through from the inside, almost, like the kid was a thumb pushing through a sock. He'd run the edge of himself along it, found the thin spot, and cut.

It went like cling film under a Stanley knife. One stroke. He'd felt the owner flinch, off to the right somewhere, the girl with the new red eyes. It would leave a line. They always did. You couldn't cut a shield and not leave a line, any more than you could cut skin and not leave one. Snape had never cared about that. Voldemort had cared even less.

Harry had learned this from the inside, from years of having Voldemort living in the back of his skull like a lodger, and then he'd spent the best part of ten years at the Department getting a lot better at it than either of them. He didn't think about Snape much anymore. Sometimes, going in through a shield, he thought about how much Snape would have hated how good he'd got.

Then he'd been in the kid.

---

No words in there.

That was the first thing. He was used to minds with words in them, a layer of chatter laid over everything like carpet. The kid didn't have carpet. She had pictures. Pictures and smells and colors and the feel of things, piled up to the ceiling, bright and hot and moving fast. A lot faster than any toddler's head had any business moving. There was more in here than three years. More than ten. Somebody had been reading to her, a soft male voice, constantly, and she'd kept all of it. Whole books. Whole shelves.

She'd felt him come in. She went for him straight away, all of her at once, every picture in her head turning toward him and shoving, like a flock of birds hitting a window. *Out. Out. Go away. Go.* She could put pictures into people; he felt the shape of the gift as she used it. She was trying to put one in him. Him, walking away across the snow. Him, gone.

He let it slide off. He didn't stop to soothe her. He'd have liked to, maybe, some other day. He had questions, and he went at them one at a time, in order, the way they'd taught him, and pulled what came up and left the rest where it lay.

*Name.*

A face bending over her, very close. A girl's face, young, gray, sweaty, the eyes brown. Lying on her back on something soaked with red. Saying it like a prayer: *Renesmee.* Then the big russet wolf in the shape of a tall brown lad with cropped black hair, grinning, ten thousand times over. *Hey, Nessie. Ness. Nessie.*

Renesmee. Fine.

*Age.*

A glass wall with rain on it. A blonde woman with a calendar, crossing off days with a red pen. Not a lot of days. A Christmas tree in a big white room, a crowd of pale faces, and a man with a mustache and a police jacket holding her on his lap like someone had just handed him a live grenade. *Grandpa Charlie.* The red-eyed girl's voice, from somewhere: *She'll be four months on the tenth.*

Four months. He put that to one side to deal with later.

*Food.*

A sippy cup. Red in it. Cold. From a plastic bag out of a fridge. A blond man's long hands holding the bag. Then a deer, in the woods, the kid sliding off the russet wolf's back onto the deer's neck, hot, and the wolf watching with its ears down, disgusted and proud of her at once. A plate of scrambled eggs. The kid pushing the plate away. The wolf eating the eggs.

Blood, then. Mostly animal. Not all.

*What are you.*

That one came up in a tangle, like a fishing net coming up out of the sea with everything in it at once. Her father: the bronze-haired one, cold, hard, smelling of the dry-sweet smell. Her mother: the red-eyed girl. Her mother *before:* warm, soft, a heartbeat, smelling of something the kid didn't have a word for that Harry recognized as soap and human skin. The kid's own heartbeat. Her own skin, warmer than Daddy's, cooler than Jacob's. Jacob, always, a big hot shape at the edge of every picture, *hers,* in a way that went down deeper than a word.

And an old man with skin like wet paper taking her face in his hands. The kid had shown him everything, every picture she had, and the old man had laughed with delight and said, out loud, to everybody: *Half mortal. Half immortal. Conceived thus, and carried by this newborn while she was still human.* Harry felt the kid hold that memory the way you'd hold a hot pan, because the old man's eyes, while he said it, had been the eyes of a man looking at something he wanted to put on a shelf.

Half and half.

He pushed further back. It got darker. Warm. Close. No light at all. A heartbeat all round him, slow and huge, *Mama's.* Voices through a wall. A girl singing, off-key. A man reading, reading, reading, endless, in a soft voice. Thirst, a dry burn, and it went away when Mama drank, and sometimes Mama drank something cold and strange and the burn went right away and Mama's heartbeat went fast and happy and the man's voice said *good, love.*

Then too big. The dark too small. Kicking, and a crack like a stick snapping, and the heartbeat around her going ragged. She'd done that. She'd remembered doing it. She'd been frightened, in there, and hadn't known why.

Then teeth.

Something tore the dark open from the outside, tore and *bit,* and there was light, white and hard, and cold air and a face, the bronze-haired one, with his mouth red. Then the warm smell, the heartbeat, Mama, and the kid had turned her head toward it and opened her mouth and bitten down.

That was as far back as it went. She didn't know how. She'd been on the inside of it. You don't know how you were made from the inside.

He came out.

He came out the way he'd gone in, straight back up, through the cake and the cup and the deer and out through the cut in the stretchy tent, and the field came back round him. Snow. Ash. The ring by his boot. The little gray dog standing in front of him like a bouncer.

The kid was crying. Silent. Mouth open.

He didn't like that. He noted that he didn't like it and put it to one side with the four months.

He looked at the mum.

---

She'd pulled the whole tent in.

He watched her do it. She'd been holding that thing over twenty-odd vampires and sixteen dogs for an hour, and she yanked the lot back into herself in about a quarter of a second and folded it round her own head, six times over, like a vault door. Fair play. It was a good trick. He'd have given her a job.

He didn't have the patience.

He'd had it for the kid. The kid was four months old and terrified, and he'd gone in with a blade because a blade does the least damage. The mum was a grown woman who'd brought a four-month-old to a war in a field. He'd had a long night, and the Italians had taken the last of his good mood with them, and the big one had made him burn him.

He went straight through the front.

Six layers. He felt each one go, a sound in his head like a row of greenhouse windows going in one after another, and then he was in, and she was all round him, a big loud frightened room, and he didn't wipe his feet.

---

Words in this one. Lots of them. She had a mind like a teenage girl's diary that somebody had spilled tea on and then set on fire. He went through it the way you'd go through a burgled flat looking for one specific thing, pulling out drawers and tipping them on the floor and not putting anything back.

*How.*

A beach. White sand, blue water. A white house. The bronze-haired one carrying her over a threshold, both of them laughing. A big white bed, a moon on the sea, and—

*Nope.*

Feathers everywhere in the morning, all over the floor. A headboard in pieces. Her arms in a mirror, purple, bruises the shape of fingers.

*Nope. Nope. Not looking at that. Christ alive.*

He skipped forward. Two weeks. The same white house. A box of tampons in a suitcase, unopened. The girl in the bathroom, counting on her fingers. Counting again. Her hand going flat on her belly and finding something under it. A little hard bump that hadn't been there a week ago.

A push. From inside.

A phone. The blond doctor's voice. The bronze-haired one's face going white, then whiter.

He went on. A big glass house in the rain. The girl on a sofa in a blanket, her belly huge, too huge, too fast, purple bruises on it from the inside. The blonde one sitting beside her like a guard dog with her arms crossed. The bronze-haired one on the other side of the room looking at the girl like she was already dead. Ribs, snapping. Her face going gray and then yellow and then gray. Bones in her face. Everything she ate coming back up.

Then a cup. A Styrofoam cup with a lid and a bendy straw, like from a petrol station, and red in it. Human. O-negative, off a hospital shelf. The smell of it making her mouth water when it should have made her sick. *He likes it.* She'd called it *he* back then. *He likes it.*

He slowed down there without meaning to.

The blond doctor, later, in a white room with a microscope, explaining it to her in the soft voice doctors use. Pairs of chromosomes. Twenty-five in a vampire, twenty-three in a human. The baby had twenty-four. *Like Jacob,* the doctor had said, and the girl had laughed, and the wolf in the doorway had looked like someone had hit him in the head with a plank.

Then the birth.

She'd had it boxed up, right at the bottom of herself, with the lid taped shut. He opened the box. He didn't want to. He wanted to know.

The floor of a study. Blood coming up in her mouth. Her spine going with a noise like a branch. Her legs just gone, the feeling cut off at the waist. The blonde one with a scalpel, then the blonde one's eyes going black, and the brown lad dragging her out. And the bronze-haired one bending down over his wife's belly and opening his mouth, and his teeth.

Through the skin. Through the rest.

*Venom.* That was the word the doctor kept using, after. The venom did something to the men, the doctor reckoned. Left something in them working that froze up in the women. Venom in all the bits. You get a human girl pregnant with that, and the thing that grows is half of each, and it grows like a weed and eats her from the inside and breaks her bones getting out, and then you pump her full of the stuff before her heart stops, and hey presto.

A syringe. A long needle into her chest. Into her heart. The bronze-haired one biting her, all over, her wrists, her throat, her thighs, to get it moving. The brown lad doing chest compressions and counting out loud and crying. Then fire. Three days of fire, in the dark, without making a sound.

He went through that too. He didn't stay.

Right. So that was *what.* That was the kid.

He kept going, because there was something else. Something that had been snagging at him since the end of the dog line, since he'd looked at the little gray one. He pulled on the brown lad, the russet wolf, Jacob, and the girl's head was full of him. Full. He'd been her best mate. He'd been more than that, nearly. He'd been the one thing in her life with a pulse when she was dying on a sofa. Harry pulled on that, and up came—

The glass house. The girl, new, red-eyed, all of three days old in the vampire way. Jacob in front of her in the kitchen, with his hands up, saying something and backing off. And the girl's mind going white with a rage so big it had shapes in it.

*You imprinted on my daughter?*

Harry stopped.

*Imprinted.*

He pulled the word up and turned it over and looked at the bottom of it. Up came everything that was stuck to it.

The same lad, a year earlier, in the woods, telling her. Explaining it to her, a bit embarrassed, kicking at a log. *It's like gravity moves. You see her, and all of a sudden she's the thing holding you to the ground.* A wolf sees somebody, once, and that's it. Locked. *You become whatever she needs. A brother. A friend. A protector.* Quil and a two-year-old girl. Sam and a woman called Emily with three long scars down one side of her face where Sam had got too close when he lost his temper. The law: no wolf ever hurts another wolf's imprint. Ever. Their oldest law. The pack had come for the kid in the night when she was a day old, and the russet lad had stood in front of the house and told them, and they'd stopped like they'd hit a wall.

Then, from the girl's rage in the kitchen: *Nessie?* she'd screamed. *You nicknamed my daughter after the Loch Ness Monster?*

Harry would have laughed, if he'd been in his own head. He filed it. That was going in the report.

And then Leah.

The girl didn't have much on her. She'd mostly tried not to think about her, because the girl was frightened of her. A small gray wolf. The smallest one. The only girl in the lot. A tall, hard-faced Quileute woman with her hair cut short, staring through the girl in the Cullens' kitchen like the girl was something stuck to the sole of her boot. Sam's girl, before Emily. Emily, her cousin. And a wet gray afternoon less than a year back: the russet lad, soaked through from the sea, with his face gone the color of ash, telling her. *Harry Clearwater. Leah and Seth's dad. Heart attack. He didn't make it.*

The dad had been a Harry.

Of course he had.

He stood there in the girl's head, in the middle of all the pulled-out drawers, and put it together.

The tug at the end of the line. When he'd looked down the dogs and his eyes had gone across the little gray one. That feeling, like a sleeve catching on a nail. He'd felt it and filed it and kept going because there had been a kid with a heartbeat on the back of a horse-sized wolf and that seemed more urgent.

The little gray one, going for the big vampire. Getting between him and it. A wolf who'd been stood on the Cullens' side all morning, going for a Cullen's throat.

The russet one. The one who'd have died for the kid. Who'd stood there shaking like a leaf while Harry went through the kid's head, and hadn't come for him.

Couldn't. *No wolf ever hurts another wolf's imprint.*

Harry felt it land.

"Fuck me sideways," he said, out loud.

He was still in her head when he said it. His mouth did it on its own.

He came out.

He came out the way he'd gone in, without bothering about it, straight through the broken glass. He felt the girl go down on her knees in the slush behind it. He didn't look.

He looked inward instead.

---

He kept his head tidy. He'd had to, after the war. You couldn't grow up with a madman's lodger in your skull and then go to work in the Department of Mysteries without learning to keep the place in order, and he'd had the best teachers on the planet, and he'd built it the way they'd shown him. A house, more or less. Rooms. Front rooms, where his people lived: Ron with his feet up, Hermione with a stack of paper, Teddy, a lot of red-haired Weasleys making a lot of noise. The long fine threads running off from those rooms out to the east, back to England. Corridors, behind. Doors. Stairs going down. Doors on the stairs, which he kept locked. And under the bottom of the stairs, under the floor, the dark.

He didn't go in the dark much.

He found the new line straight away. That was easy. It was the brightest thing in the place.

It came in from outside, from the little gray wolf standing in the snow in front of him, and it went in straight through the wall of his chest, nowhere near a door, as if the wall had never been built. It ran in past the front rooms. He'd have expected it to stop there. That was where the people went. That was where a thing like that should catch and hold: in the front, with Ron and Hermione and the rest, in the part of him he lived in.

It didn't stop.

It ran on past the front rooms, down the corridor, to the stairs. It went down the stairs. It went through the first locked door like a needle through a curtain. He'd built that door himself out of every trick the Department had, and it hadn't even slowed the line down. Through the second. Through the third. Down and down, deeper than anything had any right to go, deeper than any spell or curse or Legilimens had got into him in ten years.

Into the dark.

He followed it down to the edge and looked.

The wolf was there. It was always there. It lay where he kept it, down in the black under the bottom of everything, a shape so big he'd never seen the whole of it at once, black on black. It had been lying with its head on its paws for as long as he'd known it. It didn't move much. It didn't look at things. In all the years since it had turned up in him, it had looked straight at him maybe three times.

The line went into it.

Into its chest, the same place it had gone into his.

The wolf opened its eyes.

Green. The same green as his. Two lamps in the dark. They found him at the top of the stairs and looked at him, and he felt it, the way you'd feel a lorry go past an inch from your shoulder.

Then it lifted its head off its paws.

It turned its head, slow, and looked up the line. Away from him. Out. Up the stairs and through the doors and out through the wall of his chest, all the way along the line to where it ended, in a small gray wolf standing in the snow in a field in America with ash in her fur.

It looked at her for a long time.

Then, very low, so low he felt it more than heard it, a sound came up out of the dark and through the floor and into the soles of his feet. A long, deep, rolling rumble. He'd never heard it make that sound before.

He didn't know exactly what it meant. He knew enough.

He came back up out of himself. The field came back round him. Cold. Ash. Snow. His own breath steaming.

The little gray wolf was standing five yards off with her teeth out, watching him. Her ears were flat to her skull. All four legs were shaking. She'd been staring at him like that since he'd looked at her, he realized, every second, through all of it. Everybody else on the field had looked at the fire. She'd looked at him.

She had no idea.

None. She'd been tied to something, by something old and stupid and wild that lived in her own blood, and it had gone and tied her to the one thing in this field she should have run from. She thought she'd been tied to a bloke.

"Oh, Lassie," Harry said quietly. "You have got no idea what you've tied yourself to."

The gray wolf's lips came back another half-inch off her teeth.

Harry let out his breath. All of it, slowly. He stood there for a second with his lungs empty.

He'd quit. Two years. Hermione had stood over him in his own kitchen with her arms folded until he'd thrown the last packet in the bin. He'd been good. He'd been very good.

He held out his right hand, and there was a cigarette between his first two fingers. A Marlboro Red. He looked at it. He put it in the corner of his mouth, and thought at it, and the end of it lit, a small orange coal in all that gray.

He breathed in. It was the best thing that had happened to him all day.

He blew it out in a long stream that hung in the cold air and drifted off with the ash. He looked down the line at all of them. The blonde on her knees in the ash with the doctor holding her. The mum on her hands and knees in the snow with the bronze-haired one over her. The kid crying on the wolf. The wolf. The gray one in front of him.

"Fuck me," Harry said. "You lot have got a *fucked-up* way of making babies."

## VI. Seth

The pack mind had come apart about forty seconds earlier, and Seth Clearwater was standing in the wreckage of it trying to figure out which feelings were his.

Some of them were Leah's. That was the problem. Most of them were Leah's. She'd looked into that guy's eyes and the whole world had tipped over sideways, and it had tipped over for all five of them, because that was how the pack worked. Seth had felt it happen like it was happening to him. The hooks coming out. The weightless second. Everything coming back and hanging itself on one man in a leather jacket. And now it was still going, a steady pull through the middle of his chest toward a stranger with ash in his hair, and Seth couldn't tell how much of it was coming from his sister and how much from his own stupid heart, and it made him want to throw up.

*Lee,* he kept thinking. *Lee. Lee.*

She wasn't answering. She was out in the middle of the field by herself, five yards in front of the guy, with Emmett all over her back. She hadn't moved since Emmett went up. Every hair on her was standing straight out from her skin.

And Jacob was dying.

That was what it felt like, anyway. Jacob had been standing in the snow for the last twenty seconds with the guy going through Nessie's head, and Seth had been right there inside him for every second of it. Every single thing in Jacob had wanted to go. All of it. He'd have crossed fifty yards in a heartbeat and torn the guy's throat out and not thought about it once, the way he'd have done for Nessie a thousand times.

He couldn't.

Seth could feel what was stopping him. It was the same thing that had stopped Sam's whole pack in the dark outside the Cullens' house when Nessie was one day old. The thing that lived down under everything, under the Alpha voice, under the treaty, under their names. *No wolf harms another wolf's imprint.* Jacob had hit it at a dead run in his own head and it had caught him around the neck like a chain. He was standing at the end of it now with his whole body straining toward the guy and his legs locked under him, and every muscle he had was screaming at every other muscle he had.

It was all of them, too. Seth felt it in himself. Nessie was Jacob's imprint, which meant she was the pack's to keep, all of them, the law said so. Seth's whole body wanted to go to her, to get between her and the man who'd hurt her. And the man who'd hurt her was Leah's. The same law, the same old wild law, stood up in Seth's blood and said *no.* Two hands, both of them his own, both pulling. He was stuck between them like a bone between two dogs.

*I can't,* Quil was thinking. Over and over. Very fast, high, scared, a little kid's thought. *I can't, I can't move, Jake, what do we do, I can't—* And under it, always, Claire in her pink rain jacket. *What if it was Claire. What if somebody did that to Claire and I couldn't—*

*Shut up,* Embry thought. *Shut up shut up, Quil, I can't think—*

*Nobody can think!*

Then the guy had gone into Bella.

Seth had watched it from the outside. Bella pulling her shield in, her whole face changing, and then the guy just looking at her, and Bella's knees going out like someone had cut the strings. She'd hit the snow. Edward had let go of Rosalie and been at her side before Seth could blink, both hands on her, saying her name, and Rosalie hadn't gone anywhere. Rosalie had just folded down where she was standing, onto her knees in the ash, with Carlisle holding her.

*He's in her,* Jacob thought. *He's in Bella now. He's in—Bella, oh God, Bells—*

The chain held.

Across the field, Sam's pack broke.

Seth couldn't hear them. He hadn't been able to hear Sam's pack since the night he'd followed Jacob out of it. But he could see. Eleven wolves on the south end of the Cullen line, who'd been standing in a row all morning like soldiers, and now the row was coming apart. Paul was out of it, his dark silver hackles standing straight up, head low, going back and forth along the front of the line like a dog behind a fence. Jared beside him. Two of the young ones, Collin and Brady, had gone the other way entirely, backing toward the trees with their tails down and their ears flat. One of the new kids was turning in a circle, snapping at nothing. They'd felt it too, Seth realized. Sam's pack couldn't hear Jacob's thoughts, but the law didn't care what pack you were in. The law lived in all of them. Every wolf on this field had just felt the same two hands grab him.

And only some of them knew why.

Sam knew. Sam was standing in front of his pack, huge and black, planted in the snow, and his eyes were on Leah. Out there in the open snow, all by herself, standing guard on a stranger with her teeth out.

Seth had seen Sam's face look like that one other time. Two years ago, in the Clearwaters' yard. The night Sam came over to tell her.

*Don't,* Seth thought at him, uselessly, from across a field, at a man who couldn't hear a word of it. *Don't look at her like that. She'll kill you.*

In the middle of the field, the guy said, out loud, "Fuck me sideways."

Every head on the field turned.

*What,* Embry thought. *What did he—*

Then the guy just stood there. A second. Two. His eyes went funny, unfocused, turned in on themselves, like a man who's suddenly remembered he left the stove on. Behind him, Bella was on her hands and knees with Edward over her, and Leah stood in front of him with her teeth out, and all of it waited.

Then he looked at Leah.

Seth felt her feel it. That look landing on her. Felt the whole world tilt toward the guy one more inch and felt her fight it, and lose, and hate it.

"Oh, Lassie," the guy said, quietly. "You have got no idea what you've tied yourself to."

For one second, the pack mind went completely silent.

Then Embry laughed.

It came out of him the way a sneeze does, a big ugly hysterical bark of a laugh straight up out of the middle of the worst morning of his life, and once he'd started he couldn't stop. *Lassie,* Embry thought, and the word was shaking. *Lassie. He called her—Leah, he called you—*

*I will kill you,* Leah thought.

Then Quil went. *Lassie,* Quil thought, high and breaking, half laughing and half crying. *Timmy's in the well. Oh my God. Oh my God, I'm going to die, we're all going to die, and he called her* Lassie—

*I will kill BOTH of you. I will kill both of you and I will eat you.*

Seth didn't laugh. He was very proud of himself about that afterward. He didn't laugh. He came right up to the edge of it and looked over, and then he heard something under Leah's rage, very small, and it stopped him.

The guy had looked at her like he knew something. Like he'd seen something she couldn't. *You have got no idea.* And Leah had heard it, and under all the fury, way down, something in her had gone very cold and quiet and scared.

*Lee,* Seth thought. *Hey. Lee.*

*Not now, Seth.*

The guy let out a long breath.

Then he held up his hand, and there was a cigarette in it. It hadn't been there. Seth had been looking right at the hand. The guy put it in his mouth, and the end of it lit, by itself, a little orange coal. He breathed in, slow, and closed his eyes for a second like it was the best thing that had happened to him all year. He blew out a long stream of gray that drifted off over the ash.

He looked down the line at the Cullens. At Bella on her hands and knees in the snow. At Rosalie on her knees in what was left of her husband. At Nessie, crying on Jacob's back.

"Fuck me," he said. "You lot have got a *fucked-up* way of making babies."

Jacob went.

He went the way a dam goes. Everything he'd been holding behind the chain for the last minute came down on it at once, the whole weight of him, and Seth felt it in his own skull like a car hitting a wall at sixty. Jacob's legs drove. He got one stride. Half of a second. His forepaws hit the snow and his whole body pitched forward toward the guy's throat—

And stopped. Dead. His legs locked. His claws tore four long furrows in the crust. He stood there, rocking, at the end of the chain, with Nessie clinging to his back and her small wet face pressed into his fur, and he put his head back and howled.

Seth had never heard a sound like it. Nobody on the rez ever had. It came up out of Jacob raw and torn, all the way from the bottom, and went out over the field and off the trees and came back.

And Sam's pack heard it, and Paul came off the line.

He came fast. Low, silver, flat out across the open snow from the south end of the field, straight at the guy. Seth saw Sam's head whip round and his mouth open. He saw Jared skid to a stop. He saw the guy turn his head, slowly, the cigarette in the corner of his mouth, and look at Paul coming, the way he'd looked at Emmett. Without much interest.

*No,* Seth thought. *Oh no. Paul, no, he'll burn you, he'll—*

Leah hit Paul from the side.

Seth had never seen her move like that. Leah had always been the fastest of all of them, faster than Jacob even, small and light. She'd never in her life moved as fast as she moved right then. She came across fifteen yards of snow so fast she left a spray of white hanging in the air behind her, and she hit Paul square in the shoulder at a dead run, all her weight, and he went over.

They went down together in a tangle and rolled. Paul was half again her size. It didn't matter. He came up snarling and she was already on him, low, under his jaw, and she hit him again, and he went down on his back in the snow, and Leah stood over him with her forepaws on his chest and her teeth at his throat, the point of every tooth she had pressed into the fur under his jaw, and held there.

She didn't make a sound. That was the scary part. Leah always made a sound.

Paul lay there with his legs in the air and his eyes rolling.

Then Sam's howl came down the field, short and huge and hard, and Seth didn't have to be in Sam's pack to know what it meant. Every one of Sam's wolves dropped. Paul under Leah went limp as a dropped coat. Jared flattened in the snow. Collin and Brady froze at the tree line.

Sam came across the snow at a walk.

He stopped ten yards from Leah. She didn't take her teeth off Paul's throat. She turned her eyes, only her eyes, and looked at Sam, and her lips came back another half-inch.

Sam didn't come any closer.

He looked at Leah for a long time. Then he looked past her, at the guy, who was standing there smoking with one eyebrow up, watching the whole thing like a man watching a dog show. Then Sam looked back at Leah.

He took one step backward. Then another. And he lowered his head.

Leah let go of Paul's throat.

Paul rolled to his feet and slunk back across the field with his tail clamped and his ears pinned, and Sam turned and went after him without a backward look. Leah stood there in the scuffed-up snow, her sides going in and out, her eyes on Sam's back.

*Lee,* Seth thought. *Lee, that was—*

*Don't.*

*Okay. It was really good, though.*

She didn't answer.

The guy took the cigarette out of his mouth.

"Nice," he said to her. He said it like he meant it. Like a man at a pub watching somebody sink a hard shot at the pool table.

Leah turned her head and looked at him. Her whole body went stiff. Seth felt her think about biting him and felt the law come down on that, too, just like it had on Jacob, and felt her stand there with her teeth showing and the word *nice* sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal.

The guy didn't seem to notice. He'd already turned away. He was fishing in the inside pocket of his jacket. He came out with a phone. A battered silver flip phone with a crack across the front of it, like the kind Seth's mom had.

"Right," the guy said, to nobody, flipping it open with his thumb. "Hang on. Got to ring this in."

## VII. Carlisle

In 1935, in the hills above Gatlinburg, Tennessee, Rosalie had carried a dying man more than a hundred miles in her arms without stopping, because a bear had opened him up and she had looked at his face and decided he was hers.

Carlisle had been in the front room when she came through the door. He remembered the blood. He remembered her face. He remembered her saying *please* to him for the first and only time, and the big ruined boy in her arms opening his eyes and looking at her as if she were an angel come down to collect him. He remembered three days of screaming. He remembered the boy waking, and laughing, a huge startled laugh, in the middle of all that grief, as if somebody had told him a very good joke.

Emmett had laughed at everything for seventy-two years.

Now he was on Carlisle's sleeves.

Carlisle knelt in the snow with his arms around Rosalie from behind. She had stopped pulling. She had stopped doing anything. She knelt in the snow at the front of the line where they'd stopped her, and the wind had brought him back to her. A gray film of him lay over the snow around her knees. She had her hands in it, both of them, palms flat in the gray, very still, the way you'd lay your hands on a sleeping animal. She wasn't making any sound. Carlisle had held a great many grieving people in three and a half centuries. He had never held one so quiet.

Esme was on Rosalie's other side. Her hand was on Rosalie's back. Her eyes were on Carlisle's face over Rosalie's head, and he could see her asking him what to do, and he didn't know.

He did the next thing, because there was always a next thing.

Bella. Edward had her up on her knees now, her face pressed into his shoulder, and she was shaking in long hard waves, the way the newborn body shook when it was trying to cry. Edward was murmuring to her. Carlisle caught Edward's eye and Edward gave him a single short nod: *alive, whole, not all right.* Behind them, Jacob stood with his legs still locked and his head hanging, and Renesmee was curled on his back with her face buried in his fur. Her shoulders were shaking too. Mother and daughter, shaking together, ten feet apart.

The stranger was on the phone.

Carlisle could hear both sides of it. A battered little silver flip phone, held to the man's ear in one big hand while the other held the cigarette. The voice coming out of it was tiny and tinny and a long way off, but Carlisle could have heard a pin drop in the next valley.

"Potter for the duty desk," the stranger said. "Yeah. Yeah, I'll hold." A pause. His face did something. "It's Greensleeves. Course it is."

Twenty seconds of a tinny lute. The stranger smoked through it, looking at nothing. Then a click, and a woman's voice, crisp, accented, West African by the sound of it.

"*Statute Enforcement, Okafor.*"

"Ada. It's me."

"*Potter. Where are you?*"

"Field. Washington State. It's cold." He tapped ash off the cigarette. "Volturi's done."

A pause. "*Done how?*"

"Ash. All of them. Thirty-four. The three old ones, the guard, and the wives at the back. They had a lawful dispersal order off me, in plain English, and they laughed and turned their backs on me and went back to their vote." He drew on the cigarette. "So."

The pause on the line this time was much longer.

"*All thirty-four.*"

"Yeah."

"*At once.*"

"Yeah."

"*God help us. The Italian Ministry is going to—*"

"The Italian Ministry is going to send me a fruit basket, Ada. They've wanted that lot gone since Napoleon." He looked out across the snow at the empty western end of the field. "And forty-odd hangers-on scattered when the Italians went up. Every direction. You'll want MACUSA sat on Seattle and Vancouver and Portland for a week, they'll be scared and hungry." He paused. "And one of the locals. Big lad. Took a run at me afterward."

"*And?*"

"And I've got the same report for him." His voice didn't change at all. "Put it down."

Carlisle closed his eyes.

"*Anything else?*"

"Yeah." The stranger turned, and looked back over his shoulder at Jacob, and at the small shaking shape on Jacob's back. "I've got an unregistered hybrid."

Nothing on the line at all for three full seconds.

"*A what?*"

"Hybrid. Vampire father, human mother. Mother's been turned since. Female. Four months old. Looks three. Thinks about thirty, reads like a librarian. Heartbeat. Body temperature somewhere between her dad's and a dog's. Drinks blood, animal mostly, human when she's offered. Eats food if you make her. Projects pictures into people by touch. Twenty-four pairs of chromosomes, if Healing wants to get technical."

"*Potter, that isn't possible. There's no record of—*"

"Yeah, she's sat on a wolf's back crying at me, Ada. Somebody'll have to update the textbook."

"*Registration?*"

"None. Not on ours. Not MACUSA's. Nobody's. The Italians were about to burn half this county over her when I got here." He took a drag. "So you'll want Beings Registry out. Proper team. A Healer who does children. And a mind-Healer for the mum. I went through her shield. It was a good one. It'll want looking at."

"*Understood.*"

"And Ada." The stranger's voice didn't rise, but something in it went flat and hard. "There's a Muggle. The kid's grandad. Local police chief. He knows a bit. He's been told it's need-to-know, and he's been a good sport about it. Nobody goes near him. Nobody Obliviates him, nobody so much as says hello to him, without going through me first."

"*Noted.*"

"Good."

"*And the shapeshifters?*"

The stranger glanced down at the small gray wolf standing a few yards from his boots. She was standing very still. Her ears were turned toward the phone.

"Local," he said. "Old treaty with the vampires. Leave them out of it for now. I'll do that bit myself."

"*Potter.*" A pause. The crispness went out of the voice for a moment. "*Are you all right?*"

"Me?" The stranger looked at the gray wolf a second longer. "Fine. Got a dog now, apparently."

"*You've got a what?*"

"Tell you later. Twenty minutes, Ada. Mark the map." He flipped the phone shut.

Carlisle stood up.

He did it slowly. He took his arms from around Rosalie, gently, and Esme moved in to take his place without a word, and Rosalie didn't seem to notice either of them. Carlisle walked out from the line across the snow at a human pace with his hands open at his sides, the way he had walked into a great many rooms over the years, toward men with knives and women with guns and frightened animals in cages. He heard Edward say his name, quietly. He didn't stop. The ash crunched under his shoes. It was mostly Volturi this far out. Some of it was his son.

He stopped ten feet from the stranger. The gray wolf's head came round toward him, low, and her lips lifted. He stopped where he was.

"Mr. Potter."

"Doc."

"That was my son."

"Yeah," the stranger said. "I know."

"His name was Emmett."

The stranger took the cigarette out of his mouth. He looked at Carlisle for a moment. His face didn't change, but he took his time before he answered.

"He jumped me, Doc," he said. "Ten seconds after I'd turned thirty-four of your lot to ash with a click, he went for me with both arms out. I don't do warning shots with vampires. Not after that. Not on a day like this." He looked back toward the line, at the blonde woman kneeling in the snow. "I'm sorry for it. I am. He still jumped."

Carlisle found he had nothing to say to that. He had spent three hundred and sixty years learning what to say to people. He had nothing.

"Your call," Carlisle said instead. "*Unregistered hybrid.* What happens to her?"

"She gets registered."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning by tonight she's got a name on a list in a building that's very hard to get into." The stranger put the cigarette back in his mouth. "Meaning she gets looked at by people who know what they're doing. Meaning somebody works out how fast she's growing and when she stops. And meaning the next lot of old men in cloaks who decide she ought to be on fire has to file a form first." He blew out smoke. "And then they've got to get past me."

Carlisle looked at him for a long moment.

"And if we refuse?"

"Doc." The stranger didn't move. "I'm the friendly one."

Behind Carlisle, very softly, Rosalie began to make a sound.

## VIII. Leah

Rosalie Cullen was keening.

It was a thin high sound, very soft, coming out of her in one long breath that didn't need to stop because she didn't need to breathe. Leah had spent most of a year hating Rosalie Cullen. Rosalie was the worst of them, the coldest, the one who called the pack *dogs* to their faces and had served Jacob his dinner in a dog bowl. Leah had a whole speech saved up for Rosalie Cullen.

She stood in the snow and listened to that sound and couldn't remember a word of it.

She knew that sound. She'd made it herself, into a pillow, the night Sam came to the yard with his hands in his pockets.

The doctor walked back across the field to his family. He walked slowly. He looked about a thousand years older than he'd looked at nine o'clock.

*Lee,* Seth thought.

*I know.*

*Are you okay?*

*No.*

*Okay.*

Jacob wasn't saying anything at all. He was a big dark silence in the back of her head, like a storm sitting very still over the water. She could feel the chain still around his neck. She could feel him feeling it. She'd never in her life felt so sorry for anybody, and she'd never been less able to do anything about it.

The guy dropped the end of his cigarette in the snow. It hissed. He looked at it lying there, then made a small face, bent down, picked the butt up, and put it in his jacket pocket.

Then he turned and looked at her.

He didn't come closer. He stood where he was, five yards off, and looked at her, all of her, from her ears to her paws, the way the doctor had looked at Bella. Leah held still under it with her lips off her teeth and her hackles up and every inch of her shaking, and hated it, and couldn't stop the rest of her from leaning.

"Leah Clearwater," he said.

Her ears went flat.

"Yeah," he said. "Sorry. I got it off your mate Bella. I was in a bit of a rush in there. Didn't have time to knock."

*He knows your name,* Seth thought. *Lee, how does he know your—*

*He just told you how.*

"Your dad was a Harry," the guy said.

Everything in her went still.

The whole field went away. The snow and the ash and Rosalie's thin high sound. It was her dad's recliner and the Mariners on the TV and the smell of his coffee. It was the kitchen floor. It was her own skin splitting down her back while her mother screamed into the phone. It was ten months of nobody saying it. Nobody. Not her mom, not Seth, not anybody on the rez, as if the name had died with him.

And this guy just said it. Standing in a field.

She waited for the rest. The *I'm sorry,* the *that must have been hard.* The face people made.

It didn't come. He just looked at her.

"Just noticed, that's all," he said.

He took the pack of cigarettes out of his jacket. Red and white, a little crushed, a pack she'd have bet money hadn't been in that pocket five minutes ago. He shook one loose with his thumb and looked at it and then didn't light it. He put it behind his ear.

"Look, Lassie—"

She growled.

It came up out of her all the way from the bottom, the first sound she'd made since she'd looked into his eyes, low and rough and with every bit of her behind it.

The guy's mouth twitched. One corner. It was the first thing she'd seen his face do all morning that wasn't a frown.

"Yeah, all right," he said. "Leah."

He put his hands in his jacket pockets.

"My lot'll be here in about twenty minutes. Men in robes with clipboards. They'll want to talk to everybody, and they'll want to look at the kid, and they're going to annoy the hell out of you. I'm going to stand here and smoke till they turn up." He tipped his head back toward the line of wolves, toward Jacob and Seth and Quil and Embry, toward Sam's pack strung out along the south end of the field like a bad fence. "You can go back over there. Or you can stay here. Up to you. I'm not going to hold it against you either way."

He sounded like he meant it.

Leah stood in the snow and thought about it. She thought about it pretty hard. She thought about Jake and the chain around his neck, and the kid crying into his fur, and Bella on her knees, and Rosalie's hands in the ash. She thought about her mother at Charlie Swan's kitchen table this morning, not knowing yet. She thought about the word *Lassie.* She thought about how much she hated this guy.

She sat down.

She sat down in the snow right where she was, five yards in front of him, facing out, facing the Cullens and the wolves and the whole wrecked field, with her back to him. She'd decided that much herself. Nobody was going to take that from her. If she had to stay, she'd stay facing out.

*Lee?* Seth thought, very carefully.

*I'm keeping an eye on him.*

*Right. Sure. Totally.*

*Seth.*

*I didn't say anything!*

Behind her, she heard the guy let out a breath. It sounded almost like a laugh. She heard him take the cigarette from behind his ear, and heard the tiny crackle of it lighting itself, and smelled the smoke come over her shoulder, harsh and gray and disgusting, mixed up with pine and cold stone and that other smell under it, the old wolf smell from a long way off.

She turned her head, just a little. To check on him. That was all.

He was standing there with the cigarette, looking up at the sky. It had started to snow again, small dry flakes coming down slow out of the gray, landing on the ash, landing in his hair. His face was turned up into it. His eyes were half shut.

Then he looked down, and his eyes met hers.

For half a second, something else looked out of them.

She'd never be able to say what. Something enormous. Something very old and very patient that had been lying down at the bottom of him in the dark for a long time with its eyes shut, and had just opened them. The green of his eyes went deep, deep as a lake, and from down at the bottom of that green something looked up at her. Took its time. Looked at all of her.

Leah knew, the way she knew her own name, that it belonged to something under him. The guy was up here, in the snow, with his cigarette. The thing looking at her was down there.

Her wolf put its head down.

She didn't tell it to. Her wolf had never once in its life put its head down for anybody. Not for Sam's Alpha voice, which had broken her a hundred times and made her go where she was told, and never once made her lower her head. Not for Jake. Not for anybody. And now it put its head down in the snow without asking her, slow and deep, its chin almost to the ground, the way the little ones in a pack go down in front of the one at the top.

Then the guy blinked, and it was gone, and he was just a guy, smoking, with snow in his hair, frowning down at her like he wasn't sure what had just happened either.

Leah got her head back up. It took everything she had.

She turned around and faced the field again, and sat very straight, with her heart going like a drum, and the line in the middle of her chest pulled tight as a wire.

*You have got no idea what you've tied yourself to.*

She'd thought he meant him.

The line pulled. She felt it go out of her and across five yards of snow and into the guy behind her, and then she felt it keep going. Down. Through him. Down and down, a lot further than any line had any right to go, into somewhere dark and huge and very far below, where the end of it was held.

And down there, at the other end, something very big pulled back.
