# Specimen

## Chapter Five: Measures

---

**The Hoh drainage — Saturday, October 14 — 11:36 p.m.**

*—she'll be coming round the mountain when she comes, she'll be coming round the mountain when she—*

*Jake.*

*—comes. She'll be coming round the—*

*I swear to God, I liked the beer better.*

*I ran out of beer,* Jacob said. *I got to zero on Tuesday. You can't go below zero. Seth said try a different song.*

*Seth said.*

*He said the mountain one goes forever if you keep adding verses. The six white horses. The red rooster. The pajamas.* A pause. *She'll be wearing red pajamas when she comes.*

Leah ran faster. It still didn't help.

They were six miles south of Forks, running the ridge between the Bogachiel and the Hoh in the dark, just the two of them. Seth was at the Cullens' tonight. He'd asked to be, after last Saturday; he'd asked very quietly, looking at his feet, and Jake had said *sure, kid* without making him say why, which was the kind of thing that made it hard for Leah to stay angry at Jake for long. So it was her and Jake, and the rain, and the dark, and Jake's mountain.

She didn't sing. She still hadn't. A week, and she hadn't sung a note. If the man was listening, he could listen to her.

He hadn't been listening. She was almost sure of that now. A week, and nothing. No crack in the woods. No two fingers raised from the trees. He'd been at the diner every morning at ten, Charlie said, in the back booth facing the door, with the seat across from him empty, waiting. Charlie had told her mother, and her mother had told her, on Wednesday night over the dishes, in the careful voice she used now for anything that might be about the things Leah was: *Charlie says that Englishman keeps asking after Dr. Cullen. Every morning. Charlie says he sits there like he's waiting for a bus.*

Carlisle hadn't gone.

Carlisle had said they'd decide together. Everyone. And they had, or they'd tried. The Cullens had argued all week in their glass house, Rosalie and Emmett and Jasper and Alice, round and round, verse after verse, like Jake's song; Edward had told Jake about it, and Jake had told her. And on Thursday night the tribe had had its own say, at the council fire on First Beach, with the rain holding off for once and the driftwood burning green and blue from the salt.

Old Quil had spoken first, the way he always did. Then Billy. Then her mother, in her father's seat, with her hands folded in her lap and her voice very steady. Then Sam, and then Jake, and then Leah had stood up, which she'd never done at a council fire in her life, and said what she thought, and sat down again with her face burning, and nobody had laughed.

The council had decided before the fire burned down.

*The wolves were made for one thing,* Old Quil had said, very old and very slow, looking into the flames. *To destroy the Cold Ones. That is what we are for. That is all we are for. We do not give them to anyone. Not to the doctor. Not to the Englishman. Not to anyone.* He'd looked round the fire at all of them, at Sam's pack and Jake's together, the first time in two months they'd all sat in one circle. *If one comes onto this land, you end it. You do not ask him first.*

Jake had told Carlisle on Friday. Carlisle had listened, and nodded, and said he understood. He hadn't argued. He hadn't said what the Cullens had decided, because the Cullens hadn't.

And the man had sat in the diner every morning, waiting for a bus that didn't come.

*—she'll be coming round the mountain when she comes, toot toot—*

*Jake, if you toot one more time I'm going to bite your tail off.*

*—toot t—*

He stopped.

She'd stopped too. Same instant. It came up the slope at them from the river on the cold wet wind that had been blowing in off the sea since dark: sweet and sharp and wrong, burnt sugar and bleach and struck matches, and under it, so thick and fresh it was nearly a taste, blood. Salt in it. Fish. The cold green smell of the river mouth.

*Hoh,* said Jake. His mind had gone flat and quiet. *Down by the mouth. Fisherman, maybe. Tonight.*

*Tonight,* said Leah.

She was already turning downhill.

---

They found her a mile on, coming up the river.

It was a her, this time. Small. Smaller than Leah on two legs. She was moving fast and low along the gravel bars at the edge of the Hoh in a stolen Gore-Tex jacket three sizes too big, the sleeves rolled to her wrists, a dark braid down her back, and her red eyes, when she turned and saw them on the bank above her, were so bright they looked lit from inside.

She didn't run straight away. She looked at them. Two wolves the size of horses on the bank above the river in the rain. She looked for a long time, three or four seconds, which was forever for one of them, and Leah had the strangest feeling that the woman was *thinking*. Measuring. Doing sums.

Then she ran, and she was the fastest thing Leah had ever chased.

She went upriver along the gravel and then cut into the trees, uphill, through the old Sitka spruce, faster than the leech in the leather jacket, faster than the one in the Carhartt coat, so fast that for the first quarter mile Leah lost ground. She went through ferns that Leah had to go round. She went up the side of a ravine like she was on a staircase. She went over a nurse log twelve feet high without touching it.

Leah went flat out. She heard Jake behind her, hard, heavy, falling back. She didn't care. She was the fastest. She'd always been the fastest. She was gaining. The woman's braid swung ahead of her through the dark trunks, thirty yards. Twenty. She could smell the fisherman on her, his tobacco and his fish and his blood. Fifteen yards—

The woman went between two big cedars at the top of a rise, and the air went *tight*.

The sound. The bowstring. Leah heard it in her sleep now.

The woman went up off the ground by one ankle and swung, inverted, under the low limb of the left-hand cedar, her braid falling down past her head, the too-big jacket sliding down over her shoulders, and hung there, turning slowly. Rigid. Silent.

Her eyes moved. They found Leah. They were furious.

Leah stood under her in the ferns with her chest heaving, and after a few seconds Jake came up through the trees behind her and stopped beside her, and for a while neither of them thought anything at all.

*Same as the others,* Jake said finally. *Big tree. Low limb. He picks them the same every time.*

*Doesn't matter where he picks.*

*No.*

Leah looked up at the woman. The woman looked back. Upside down, turning. A small fierce face with the fisherman's blood still on her chin.

*Council said,* said Leah.

*I know what council said.*

*You said yes at council.*

*I know what I said, Leah.*

*He said he'd re-key them,* Leah said. *For us. Next time. If we burned another one. He said the next thing hanging under a cedar might be furry.*

*I know.*

*Are you scared of him?*

Jake didn't answer straight away. She felt him think about it, honestly, the way Jake did, which was the most infuriating thing about him. He didn't lie in his own head. He didn't know how.

*Yeah,* he said. *Some. Aren't you?*

*Yes,* said Leah.

*So.*

*So council said. And we're what we are. And he can go fuck himself.*

She felt Jake almost laugh. It didn't get out. *You're going to put that on a T-shirt.*

*Watch me.*

*Okay,* said Jake. *Okay. Do it.*

---

It went the way it had gone last time, at first.

The woman didn't fight, because she couldn't. That was still the worst part. Leah would rather it fought. She went up on her hind legs under the cedar and took the left arm in her jaws at the shoulder and pulled, and it came away with that sound, the dishes sound, the plate on stone, and she dropped it in the ferns. Jake took the right. The woman's face did nothing. Her eyes did everything.

Leah went behind the other cedar and let the heat out of her and pulled on her shorts and her shirt with shaking wet hands and got the sandwich bag out of the bundle. New bottle. New lighter. She'd bought them Wednesday, at the same Shell station. The kid behind the counter had said *barbecue season's over, you know*, and she'd looked at him until he stopped smiling.

She came back round the cedar.

Jake had piled the two arms together in the ferns, away from the tree, the way he'd piled the pieces last time. They were moving. Fingers curling. Trying to crawl.

Leah squeezed the bottle over them until it wheezed. The smell of the fluid cut through the leech-stink. She flicked the lighter, and bent, and touched the flame to the soaked sleeve of the Gore-Tex jacket still on the left arm.

The fluid caught.

It went up with a soft *whump*, the way it should, a sheet of flame rolling over the two arms and the wet ferns around them. Leah stepped back with her arm up over her face.

And the flame turned blue.

She saw it happen. It started yellow, the ordinary hot yellow of burning lighter fluid, and then, from the edges in, it went pale. Blue. A cold, clear, pretty blue, the color of the gas ring on her mother's stove, and it stopped roaring and went quiet and soft, like a candle flame in a still room. It ran over the two arms like water. It licked up the sleeve of the jacket and down again. The jacket didn't char. The ferns underneath didn't curl.

The arms didn't catch.

Leah stood there with the lighter in her hand and stared.

The blue flame burned on, quiet, over the pile. The fluid in it was burning off; she could smell it going. But there was no heat on her face. None. She held out her hand toward it without thinking, the way you'd hold your hand out to a campfire, and there was nothing. She held it closer. Nothing. She put her fingers into the edge of the blue, and it was cool, and soft, and it tickled, a little, like a feather run down the back of her hand.

*Leah,* said Jake.

She looked up.

The left arm had crawled out of the fire.

It was dragging itself through the ferns on its fingers, slowly, like some huge pale insect, with the soft blue flame still running over it and doing nothing at all. It reached the foot of the cedar. It started up the trunk. Inch by inch. Fingers into the bark. Up toward the woman hanging under the limb with her one remaining shoulder and her furious eyes.

Leah couldn't move.

The arm reached the limb. It went out along it, fingertip over fingertip, upside down, the way a sloth goes. It reached the woman's dangling shoulder. It pressed itself against the raw white socket where Leah had torn it off.

And it took.

She saw the venom come up out of the socket and run along the seam, glistening. She saw the seam go faint. She saw the woman's fingers on that hand, the left hand, flex once, and go still again, because the snare still had her, and hang there.

Behind Leah, in the ferns, the right arm was crawling too.

*No,* Leah said. Out loud, in her human voice, to nobody. "No. No, that's—"

The world cracked open behind her.

She didn't turn round this time. She didn't have to. She stood there in the ferns with a lighter in her hand and a blue flame that didn't burn curling round her ankles, watching a vampire's arm climb a cedar tree to go home, and she knew exactly who was standing behind her, and she knew exactly what the expression on his face was going to be.

"Evening, Lassie," said Harry Evans.

He was standing at the edge of the ferns with his hands in the pockets of a dark canvas jacket. Jeans. Boots, laced this time. A gray wool cap pulled down over his ears against the rain. He'd been awake; she could tell. His hair wasn't flat on one side. He smelled of coffee and woodsmoke and pine and, faintly, under all of it, the other thing.

He looked at the blue flame curling round her ankles. He looked at the arm on the cedar. He looked at the right arm, still dragging itself through the ferns toward the tree, and at the woman hanging under the limb, turning slowly, watching all of them.

Then he looked at Leah. He didn't smile. Something near his mouth considered it and decided against.

"What did you do?" said Leah.

"I took measures." He came forward, unhurried, into the soft blue light. "Every snare on the peninsula, since Tuesday. All forty-one." He crouched by the right arm and watched it pull itself along for a moment, the way you'd watch a beetle. "Flame-Freezing Charm. Very old. Fourteenth century, give or take. They used to use it, back when people burned people like me in market squares on a Tuesday afternoon. You'd stand on the bonfire with the charm on and scream a bit for the look of the thing, and the fire would tickle." He glanced up at her. "There was one woman who liked it so much she got herself caught forty-seven times in different disguises. Just for the feeling."

Leah stared at him.

"People like you," she said.

"Figure of speech."

"It's not a figure of—"

"Anything hanging in one of my snares won't burn," he went on, as if she hadn't spoken. "Anything that's come off something hanging in one of my snares won't either, for about an hour, while it's still trying to get home. You can pull them apart all you like." He nodded at the arm. "Saves me a job, honestly. They go back together."

He stood up and gave the right arm a little nudge toward the cedar with the toe of his boot, the way you'd help a turtle across a road. It went on up the trunk.

"You said you'd re-key them," said Leah. Her voice came out thick. "For us. You said the next thing hanging off a cedar might be furry."

"I did say that."

"So why didn't you?"

"Thought about it." He put his hands back in his pockets and watched the arm climb. "Sat up a whole night thinking about it, as it happens. Then I thought about having to explain to Billy Black why his son was hanging upside down off a tree in the Hoh valley with his tongue out. And I thought about the Chief, who'd want to know what I was doing in the woods with Sue Clearwater's girl at midnight." He shrugged. "This seemed more neighbourly."

Behind Leah, Jacob made a sound low in his chest.

The man glanced at him. Then his eyes went slightly unfocused, the way they had in the woods the first time, listening, and Leah knew Jacob was thinking at him, hard, the way she'd thought at him in the diner.

"I heard about your council," the man said to Jacob.

Leah went cold. "How?"

"I hear all sorts of things." He kept his eyes on Jacob. "Your Mr. Ateara's right. *That's what you're for.* I'd not argue with a word of it. You were made to destroy them, and you do it very well, and I'd have to be a great deal stupider than I am to try to stop you." He took one hand out of his pocket and pointed, lazily, at the cedar. "So do it. Anywhere you like. There's a few million acres of forest on this peninsula. Forty-one trees in it are mine. That's all. Forty-one. The rest's yours." He put the hand back. "Anything walks into one of my forty-one, it's mine. Anything that doesn't, burn it to your heart's content. I'll bring the marshmallows."

Jacob's growl went down into the ground and stayed there.

"That's not your call," Leah said. "It's our land."

"It's the National Forest," said the man, mildly. "Mostly. Some of it's the National Park. Some of it's state. A little bit's the reservation, and I've not put a single node on the reservation, Miss Clearwater, not one, you can check. I asked the trees very nicely and they said yes." He looked at her. "Forty-one. Think about it. Talk to your Mr. Ateara. He's a sensible man, from what I hear."

Above them, under the cedar limb, the right arm reached the woman's shoulder and pressed itself home. The venom ran. The seam faded. The woman hung there, whole again, turning slowly, her furious eyes going from Leah to Jacob to the man and back.

The blue fire had gone out. The ferns underneath it were green and wet and untouched. Leah realized she was still holding the lighter. She put it in her pocket.

The man stepped up close under the cedar and looked at the woman's face, upside down, a foot from his own. He looked for about as long as it takes to read a page. Leah saw her eyes go wide.

"Hoh River mouth," he said quietly, to nobody. "About nine. Smelt fisherman with a dip net and a thermos. Name of Ray." He was quiet a moment. "She doesn't remember her own name. She remembers his."

"What does that mean?" said Leah.

"Means she reads the names on their fishing licences." He stepped back. "Before."

Leah felt sick.

The phone in his jacket pocket buzzed.

---

He took it out and looked at the little screen, and for a second, in the gray glow of it on his face, Leah saw something she hadn't seen on him before: satisfaction, quick and private, like a man watching a ball go into a pocket he'd called.

He thumbed it open and put it to his ear.

"Doctor," he said.

Leah could hear Carlisle's voice, faint and tinny. She couldn't make out the words. She didn't need to. She could hear the tone of it: courteous, careful, tired.

"Took you long enough," said the man.

A pause. Carlisle's voice again, a little longer.

"No, no. That's all right. Everybody gets a say. I'd heard." He listened. "Tonight? As it happens I'm in the neighbourhood." He looked at Leah over the phone, and at Jacob, and at the woman hanging under the cedar. "I've got one or two things to tidy up first. Give me ten minutes." Another pause. "Yes. I know where it is, Doctor. Everybody knows where it is." He listened. "Lemon? Lovely. Tell her I'll try one."

He closed the phone and put it away.

"Your doctor's invited me round," he said to Jacob. "For a chat. They've decided something, after a week of deciding. I expect you'll want to be there for it." He glanced at Leah. "Both of you."

"What did they decide?" said Leah.

"No idea. That's why I'm going." He reached into the pocket of his jacket again, the other one, and took out something small and round and shiny. A bottle cap. Leah saw the red and gold of the Rainier label on it in the light of his phone as he turned it over in his fingers. He looked at it for a moment as though he were reading it. Then he tapped it once with the tip of his first finger, and said, very quietly, a word she didn't catch.

The bottle cap glowed blue. Just for a second. Then it was a bottle cap again.

He reached up and pushed it into the woman's jacket, into the teeth of the zip just under her collarbone, and pressed it in with his thumb until it stuck.

"Mind your heads," he said, and stepped back.

The woman went.

There was no other way to describe it. One second she was hanging under the cedar, turning slowly, her braid hanging down, her furious red eyes on the three of them. The next the air around her twisted, like heat over a road, and she spun, once, very fast, the whole length of her, as if something had hooked her through the middle and yanked, and there was a sound like a cork coming out of a bottle, and she was gone. The snare under the limb let go of nothing with a faint twang.

The branch swayed. Rain dripped off it. That was all.

"Where did she go?" said Leah.

"Home." The man pulled the wool cap down a little further over his ears. "Kettle's on."

"Home *where*?"

"Mine." He looked at her, and for a moment she thought he was going to say something else. He didn't. He looked at Jacob instead. "Ten minutes, Alpha. Don't be late. The doctor's wife has been baking."

He took a step back into the ferns. He lifted one hand, palm out, and waggled the fingers at them, a little cheerful wave, like a man leaving a pub.

"See you later, alligator," said Harry Evans.

*CRACK.*

The ferns swayed where he'd been. The rain came down into the space.

---

Leah stood under the cedar for a long moment.

Then she stripped in the ferns without bothering to go behind a tree, and tied the bundle to her ankle with shaking fingers, and let the heat come up through her, and was a wolf again, and Jacob was in her head.

*—while, crocodile,* Jacob was thinking.

She stopped dead.

*Did you just—*

*No.*

*You did. You just thought "in a while, crocodile." At him. After he'd gone.*

*I didn't mean to.* Jacob's mind was hot with shame. *It's—it's automatic, okay? Rachel and Becca used to say it. Every single time. When I was a kid. It just comes out.*

*He said see you later alligator and you said in a while crocodile.*

*He didn't hear it. He'd gone.*

*That's not the point, Jake.*

*Can we please*, said Jacob, *go to the house.*

They went to the house. It was five miles. They did it in five minutes, flat out, side by side through the dark and the rain, without singing anything, and the whole way Leah could feel Jacob trying not to think *in a while, crocodile*, which meant he thought it about forty times.

She didn't say anything about it. She'd decided she'd save it.

---

**The Cullen house — Sunday, October 15 — 12:04 a.m.**

Esme had made the cupcakes on Thursday, because she hadn't known what else to do with her hands.

They were lemon, with a glaze on top that she'd got from a magazine at the dentist's office in Port Angeles in 1997 and kept in the drawer ever since. She'd made forty-eight. She made a lot of everything now. Since August, the kitchen had been full for the first time since she'd built it: Jacob, Seth, sometimes Leah, standing at the counter at three in the morning eating whole lasagnas with their fingers. She'd never had anyone to feed before. She'd discovered she liked it more than almost anything in the world.

There were nineteen left on the plate on the counter by the back door. Seth had eaten eleven of them since Thursday. She'd been counting.

She was standing at the sink rinsing a glass that didn't need rinsing when the man arrived.

There was no warning. Edward didn't hear him; Edward never heard him. Alice didn't see him. There was simply a flat hard *crack* in the middle of her kitchen, like a branch snapping, so loud the glass in her hands shook, and when she turned round there was a man standing between the counter and the back door, holding one of her cupcakes.

He was peeling the paper off.

He did it carefully, the way children do, turning it round and round with his thumb so the paper came away in one long spiral and the cake came out whole. He was tall and dark-haired, wet from the rain, in a canvas jacket and a gray wool cap, and he had the back door at his left shoulder and the wall at his back and the doorway into the dining room in front of him, and Esme, who had spent most of a century learning how Jasper stood in rooms, noticed that, before she noticed anything else.

He took a bite.

He chewed. His eyebrows went up.

"Oh, that's *good*," he said. English. "That's very good. Mrs. Cullen, I presume?"

Esme set the glass down on the draining board. Very carefully.

"Yes," she said.

"Harry Evans." He didn't offer his hand. His hands were busy. "Sorry about the kitchen. The front door seemed rude at this hour, and I'd have had to knock, and then everybody'd have had to decide who answered it, and it'd have taken all night." He took another bite. "Lemon. Your husband said lemon. I've had worse cake in five-star hotels."

The kitchen filled up around her.

They came the way they always came when something happened in the house: all at once, from wherever they'd been, without seeming to cross the space between. Carlisle in the dining room doorway. Jasper beside him and half a step in front, his arms loose at his sides, his eyes on the man's hands. Emmett behind Jasper, filling the rest of the doorway. Alice, small and white, at Emmett's elbow. And Edward, coming through from the front room with Bella beside him and his hand flat against her back.

Bella stopped dead in the doorway.

Esme saw it happen. She'd been watching for it all week, for three and a half weeks, every time anyone with a heartbeat came within a mile of the house: the moment the smell reached Bella. Bella had never smelled him. Bella had said so, on Sunday night, in a voice like a wire. *I don't even know what he smells like.*

Now she did.

Esme could smell it too. It was hard not to; the kitchen was small and the man was hot, so hot Esme could feel it across the room the way you'd feel a woodstove, and his blood came off him with the heat. It was human. It was rich and dark and alive, a young man's blood, a strong heart pumping it, and it filled the kitchen, and Esme, who had not drunk from a human being in eighty years, felt her own throat tighten and set it aside, the way she'd learned to.

And under it, faint, there was the other thing. The smell Leah had tried to describe, that Jacob had smelled on Billy's coat. Wolf, and something older under the wolf. Stone. A cave.

Bella's eyes had gone black.

She stood in the doorway with Edward's hand on her back, and every muscle in her body was locked, and her lips had come back off her teeth, just a fraction, and she wasn't breathing. Esme saw Edward lean his head down close to her ear and say something too low to hear. Saw Emmett shift his weight, very slightly, behind them.

The man saw it too.

He looked at Bella. He looked at her for a long moment, chewing, with an expression of mild, attentive interest, as if she were a weather front coming in over the sea.

"You'll be the newest," he said.

Bella didn't answer.

"You're doing very well," he said. "Truly. I'm told the new ones take doors off their hinges for less." He finished the cupcake and licked a bit of glaze off his thumb. "Breathe through your mouth. Or don't breathe. Whichever's easier. I'll not take it personally."

"Stay away," said Bella, "from my daughter."

Her voice came out of her low and rough and very quiet, like something heavy being dragged over stone. Esme had never heard it sound like that.

The man reached for another cupcake.

"She's upstairs," he said, "in the room at the end of the hall on the second floor, asleep, with your sister-in-law in the chair by the window watching her breathe. And I'm down here, in your mother-in-law's kitchen, eating cake." He began to peel the paper. "I'd say I'm keeping my distance pretty well, all things considered."

Nobody moved.

Esme looked at Carlisle. Carlisle was looking at the man. His face was very calm. It was the face he wore in the emergency room, when something had come through the doors that he didn't yet know how to fix.

"Thank you for coming, Mr. Evans," said Carlisle.

"Thank you for asking, Doctor." The man looked up from the cupcake. "Took you long enough."

"We wanted everyone to have a say."

"I know. I'd heard." He took a bite. "Very democratic. I approve, in principle. Never seen it work in practice, but I've not been everywhere."

---

There was a sound from the hall behind the dining room, a quick light step, and Seth came round the corner in a T-shirt and sweatpants with his hair sticking up on one side, the way it did when he'd been lying on the couch. He'd been asleep. He'd been asleep on the couch in the front room for an hour, the way he slept now, all at once and everywhere, and the crack had woken him.

He saw the man in the kitchen and stopped.

The man looked at him. Something happened to his face, very briefly, that Esme couldn't name.

"Balto," he said. "Feeling better?"

Seth's face went scarlet from his collar to his hair.

"I—yeah," Seth said. "Yeah. I'm—thanks."

"Good. No shame in it. First time I saw something taken apart, I was sick in a bathroom for half an hour." The man turned back to his cupcake. "Second time, too, come to think of it. You get used to it. I'm not sure you should."

Seth stood there in the doorway with his mouth open.

The back door opened.

Leah came in first, in shorts and a T-shirt, soaked to the skin, her wet hair plastered to her head and her bare feet leaving prints on Esme's floor. Jacob came in behind her in cutoffs, dripping, steaming faintly in the heat of the kitchen, and the smell of the forest and the rain and the wolf came in with them, and Esme saw the man step aside, half a pace, toward the counter, to let them past without either of them having to touch him.

He'd let them have the door. He'd kept the wall.

"Evening," he said. "You're late. I said ten minutes. That's eleven." He held up the half-eaten cupcake. "I've had two."

Leah looked at him. Then she looked at the plate of cupcakes, and back at him, and Esme watched an expression go across her face that Esme had never seen on Leah Clearwater before, in all the weeks she'd been coming to this house and standing in it as if it were a cold river. It was outrage, so pure it was almost funny.

"Those," said Leah, "are for *Seth*."

"Seth's had eleven," said the man. He nodded at the little lidded bin by the back door. "There's eleven papers in there. I counted."

Esme made a sound.

She didn't mean to. It came out of her before she could stop it, a small helpless breath through her nose, and every head in the kitchen turned toward her. She put her hand over her mouth. It didn't help. She could feel it in her shoulders, in her stomach: the most terrible, inappropriate laughter, rising up out of her in the middle of her kitchen at midnight with a man who'd taken a vampire apart on a table and threatened her granddaughter, because he'd counted the cupcakes. Because she'd counted the cupcakes too. Because he was standing there with glaze on his thumb and rain on his shoulders looking exactly like every hungry boy who'd ever stood in a kitchen at midnight, and she'd wanted so badly, for eighty years, to have a kitchen with hungry boys in it.

"Esme," said Carlisle, very gently.

"I'm sorry," Esme said, through her hand. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. It's—I counted them too."

The man looked at her.

For a moment, just a moment, something went across his face that he hadn't decided to put there. It was quick, and it was gone almost before Esme saw it. But she'd seen it. She'd been a schoolteacher once, before any of this, in a little town in Wisconsin, and she'd seen that look on the faces of the children from the poor farms when she unpacked the extra lunch she'd brought for them.

It was the look of somebody who'd been hungry once, for a long time, a long time ago, and had never quite stopped expecting to be.

Then it was gone, and he was pleasant again, and he said, "They really are very good, Mrs. Cullen," and put the rest of the cupcake in his mouth.

---

"Shall we sit down?" said Carlisle.

He said it the way he said everything, gently, as an invitation, and the kitchen began to unknot itself. Jasper stepped back out of the doorway. Emmett went with him. **The dining room — 12:12 a.m.**

What happened to me in that cellar in London, and to every one of my family since. I've never been able to take one of us apart and look. I never would. You have." He looked down the table. "I'd like to know what you found."

The man was quiet for a long moment.

"From one scientific mind to another," he said.

"If you like."

"I do like, as it happens." He unfolded his hands and put them flat on the table again. "Some of it. Not all. Not how. Not what with. "Your skin's harder than granite. You knew that. What you might not know is how it breaks. It fractures like flint. Shell-shaped breaks, the way glass goes. A tap in the right place and the whole surface webs out. Your venom comes up into the cracks from inside, like water into a dry riverbed, and seals them in about three minutes. Big pieces reattach if you hold them in place. Forty seconds, give or take, for a finger. They leave a line."

Carlisle looked at the faint white crescents on Jasper's throat and jaw. So did everyone else.

"The pieces stay alive," the man went on, "while they're off. They move. You knew that too; your wolves have seen it. They try to go home. What you might not know is that they stay alive exactly as long as the rest of you does. Burn the body, and the pieces die. All of them, at once, wherever they are. The young lady and the Alpha watched it happen a week ago tonight, with a hand I took out of their fire." He glanced at the doorway. "Didn't you, Lassie."

Leah's jaw tightened. She didn't answer.

"Interesting," said Carlisle quietly. He meant it. He couldn't help meaning it. "So whatever animates us is distributed. Through the whole of the tissue, at once, with no center."

"Something like that," said the man. He didn't say any more about it. Carlisle noticed that. He noticed the man noticing him notice. "Fire takes you. You knew that. Low flame hurts more than anything but doesn't mark you. High flame catches, and burns on its own, and the burns heal slowly, if they heal at all. Whatever's burned off, you don't get back. Sunlight doesn't hurt you at all; it just shows you up. Your teeth are ordinary human teeth that happen to come with venom. Your eyes go black as you get thirsty, about at the rate a man gets hungry."

"Yes," said Carlisle. "We knew most of that."

"And you think on four lines at once," said the man. "At least four. Side by side. Full speed. A human being thinks on one, with a few sidings. He glanced at Bella, who still hadn't looked up. "And everything since the change is kept. Every second of it. Everything before it goes to fog."

Carlisle looked at him for a long time.

He'd known that. He'd always known that; it was obvious, from the inside. He'd never once heard it described from the outside, by a man who'd walked through it.

"How do you know that?" he said quietly. "The four lines."

"I'm not going into that."

"You were in his mind. The first one."

"I'm not going into that, Doctor," said the man, pleasantly, and that was all.

The man looked at her. Something in his face shut, very quietly, like a door at the end of a long corridor.

"Yes," he said.

He didn't say anything else. Esme didn't ask.

"Your turn," said the man.

Carlisle blinked.

"Fair's fair, Doctor. One scientific mind to another." The man leaned back again. "The change. Three days, I'm told. Fire in every vein. What does the venom actually *do*? Carlisle hadn't expected that. He found, again, to his surprise, that he wanted to answer.

So he did. He told him what he'd seen and what he thought. How the venom went into the blood and moved through it, slowly at first, then faster, and the heart sped up to drive it, faster and faster, until the venom had gone everywhere the blood could go. How the tissues changed as it reached them, *replaced* cell by cell, the soft wet stuff of a human body turning into something hard and dense and glittering, and how the heart raced and raced to push the last of it through, until, at the end, with everything changed except itself, it stopped. How that was the end of the pain. How he'd sat by Edward's bed in Chicago in 1918 and counted Edward's heartbeats as they went faster and faster and then stopped, and how he'd sat by Esme in a morgue in Wisconsin, and by Rosalie on a street in Rochester, and by Emmett in a forest in Tennessee, and by Bella, in this house, three and a half weeks ago, upstairs, with her heart going like a hummingbird's until it didn't.

"I think so."

"As far as I've been able to tell."

The room went cold.

"You won't," said Carlisle.

**The Cullen house — 12:52 a.m.**

He'd smelled like a person.

That was the first thing Bella thought, when she could think anything at all, sitting at the long table with her face in her hands and the cracked glass at her back and Edward's shoulder against hers. Of everything he'd said and everything he'd done, that was what her mind kept going back to, over and over, the way a tongue goes back to a broken tooth.

He'd smelled like a person.

She'd expected something else. She didn't know what. Something wrong. Something that would tell her body what he was before her head caught up. Instead the kitchen doorway had opened in front of her and the smell had come out of it and hit her, and it had been a man. Just a man. A young strong man with his heart going slow and steady and his blood running hot under his skin, and it had been the best thing she had ever smelled in her life.

Better than the blood in the cups in September. Better than the hiker on her first hunt, the one she hadn't touched. Better than anything.

And under it, faint, so faint she might have imagined it if Leah hadn't described it first, the other thing. A little like Jacob. A little like wet dog. And under that, something she didn't have a word for, something old and dark and cold, like the inside of a stone.

Her throat had caught fire the moment she smelled him. It was still burning now, twenty minutes after he'd gone, a slow red ache at the back of her mouth that hadn't been there since she'd learned to live with it. She'd wanted to drink him. She'd wanted it so badly that for a second, standing in the doorway with Edward's hand on her back, she hadn't been anything except the wanting.

And then he'd said *you'll be the newest*, in that calm interested voice, like a man looking at a weather front, and she'd wanted something else instead.

She'd wanted to kill him.

The two things had been so close together she couldn't tell them apart. They'd sat in her chest side by side like two hands pressed together, and she'd stood in the doorway between them shaking, and Edward had said her name in her ear, and she'd stopped.

She'd stopped twice tonight. Once in the doorway. Once at the table, with the chair broken against the glass and her hands on the wood and the word *promotion* in the air.

She didn't know how. She didn't know if she could do it a third time.

"Bella," said Edward.

She lifted her head.

The dining room had emptied, mostly. Carlisle was in the kitchen with Esme; Bella could hear them talking, low, Esme's voice unsteady. Jasper was standing at the glass wall looking at the crack in it with his hand flat on the pane, as if he could feel the shape of it through his palm. Emmett and Alice had gone out onto the porch with the wolves. "I'm all right," she said.

"I'm not all right," Bella said. "But I'm here."

"Yes."

"I didn't—"

She looked at their hands.

"I want to go up," she said.

Sunlight and honey and the sharper thing underneath, the thing that was all her own, and the smell of her went into Bella's burning throat like cold water, and Bella stood just inside the door and breathed it, deliberately, all the way down, until the fire went back down to where it lived.

"I heard."

"He said he hasn't been in any of ours."

**The Cullen house — 1:21 a.m.**

"Tell me," said Croaker, "what this means."

Harry sat down in the chair across the desk, the hard one, without being asked.

"It means whatever's in them is in all of them at once," he said, "however far apart you put the pieces. Fire on the body kills the pieces. A curse on a piece reaches the body. One second of the Cruciatus went from your desk to Washington State faster than I could see it go." He looked at the fingertip pressing at the glass. "I'd bet my car the Killing Curse goes the same road."

"You haven't tested that."

"I've not got a spare. I will."

"And if it does?"

"Then nobody needs to go to Tuscany," said Harry. "Nobody needs to get on an aeroplane. Nobody needs to walk into a keep under a clock tower full of things that've been killing people since before Rome. We need a piece of each of them. That's all. A chip off a shoulder. A fingertip. A splinter of tooth. And a quiet room. Here. On the ninth level. With a jar for every one of them on a shelf and somebody with a steady hand." He paused. "Fifteen minutes' work. One afternoon. All of them."

Croaker didn't say anything for a while.

"And getting a piece of Aro?" he said finally.

"I'm working on it."

"The doctor."

"Tuesday," said Harry. "Eight o'clock. His dining room. He's going to tell me about the keep. Where they sit. How they move. Who they let close." He looked at the mirror. "And when they're careless."

"Will he?"

"He said he would."

"Will he tell you that?"

Harry thought about Carlisle at the head of the long table, with Bella's broken chair against the glass behind him, talking about counting Edward's heartbeats in Chicago in 1918.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "I've not asked him that yet. I'll have to work up to it."

Croaker nodded slowly.

"There's one more thing," said Harry. "So it's in the record. I told the doctor tonight, and his family, that if the hybrid ever feeds on a human she goes in a snare like the rest of them, and I don't care what age she is." He paused. "I told them you'd probably promote me for her."

Croaker looked at him over his tea.

"I would, you know," he said.

"I know you would, Saul. That's why I said it."

Something happened to Croaker's long gray face, at one corner, that might in a better light have been a smile.

---

Croaker kept the fingertip.

Harry had thought he might. Croaker got up from behind his desk, which Harry had seen him do perhaps a dozen times in five years, and took the jar, and carried it to the window, and set it on the sill, against the glass, where the false morning lay across the false river outside.

The fingertip pressed itself to the side of the jar. West.

"If she ever gets loose," said Croaker, looking at it, "I'll know the moment she's dead."

"Telegraph," said Harry. "That was my idea."

"I know whose idea it was. I read your letter." Croaker stood looking at the jar. "One for each of them. On a shelf. In this room." He was quiet for a moment. "I'd like that very much, Potter."

"I know."

"Go home," said Croaker. "Use this." He held out an old teaspoon, tarnished, with a Ministry crest on the handle. "One-way. Back to your tent. Fill in the forms for the compass before Tuesday, or I'll have you sorting brains with Monday's boy until the Second Coming."

"Yes, Saul."

He took the spoon.

---

He came down in the lab at one fifty-four in the morning, Pacific time, in the white light, between the cabinets, and nearly fell over, and caught himself on the bench.

She was still hanging in N.A. 02, turning slowly. Her eyes found him. They were full of something he'd seen on a great many faces and never got used to and wasn't sure he should.

He didn't say anything to her. There wasn't anything to say.

"Log," he said to the quill. "Range trial complete. Twenty miles, a hundred and thirty miles, approximately five thousand miles. Cruciatus transmitted from fragment to body at all three distances, simultaneously, as far as could be observed by mirror. The line holds at any distance tested. Fragment retained in London on the Director's windowsill as a telegraph." He stopped. "Requirement: confirm Killing Curse transmission at range. Specimen to be preserved for that purpose." He looked at her. "For now."

The quill wrote it. Then it put a small neat full stop at the end, and waited.

"End of entry," said Harry.

---

He went back to the kitchen.

The tin was where he'd left it on the counter, with the Tower of London on the lid and the Beefeater's half-worn face. An hour ago he'd been standing on the flagstones of a hall two hundred feet under Whitehall, a ten-minute walk from Tower Hill, where they sold these things to people from Ohio for five quid.

He opened it.

There were seventeen cupcakes inside, lemon, with glaze on top, packed in two neat rows on a fold of waxed paper. She'd put the waxed paper in. He hadn't seen her do it. She'd done it in the ten seconds before he came through the kitchen door, so they wouldn't stick.

He took one out and peeled the paper off, the way he'd done in her kitchen, round and round with his thumb so it came away in one long spiral, and ate it standing at the counter, looking at nothing.

It was very good.

On a windowsill five thousand miles away, in a room that looked out on a river that wasn't there, a fingertip in a jar was pressed against the glass, pointing west, across the whole of an ocean and a continent and a range of mountains, toward a cabinet in a tent in the rain. Trying to get home. It would go on trying, Harry supposed, for as long as there was anything left on the other end to go home to.

He knew the feeling. He'd had it for most of his life.

He ate another cupcake. Then he put the lid back on the tin and went to bed.

Tuesday. Eight o'clock. She'd said she'd make something else.
