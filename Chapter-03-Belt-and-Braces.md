# Chapter Three: Belt and Braces

*La Push and Forks, Washington — Saturday, October 7, 2006*

---

## I. Leah

The tape was *Physical Graffiti*, side one, and Robert Plant was halfway through "Custard Pie" when the trailhead came up on the left.

Leah had her feet on the dash and her fingers on her neck again. She kept doing that. She'd tell herself to stop, and she'd stop, and a mile later she'd find her hand back up there, two fingers resting on the marks, feeling them hum.

She took her hand down.

"Pull in here," she said.

Potter glanced at her. "Here?"

"Left. The gravel lot. Right there."

He pulled in. It was a small lot cut out of the trees, a dozen spaces, empty except for a beat-up Subaru with Oregon plates and a surfboard rack on the roof. A brown wooden sign at the edge of the trees said SECOND BEACH TRAIL with a little hiker symbol on it, and under that somebody had carved *GO HOME TOURISTS* into the post with a pocketknife a long time ago, and somebody else had tried to sand it off and given up.

Potter turned off the engine. Zeppelin died. The rain ticked on the roof.

"I don't want to be in a car," Leah said. "I've been in a car or a motel or Sam's yard all day. I want to sit on a log and look at the water."

He didn't argue. She'd half expected him to. He got out, and she got out, and they went into the trees.

The trail was a little under a mile, through old spruce and cedar so big that three people holding hands couldn't get their arms around the trunks. It was dark under there even at noon, a deep green dark, with the rain coming down through the canopy in slow, fat drops instead of a drizzle. Moss on everything. Ferns as tall as her waist. Wooden boardwalk over the worst of the mud, slick with rain, and long stretches of plain mud where there wasn't any boardwalk. Leah walked it barefoot the way she'd walked it barefoot since she was six. Behind her, Potter walked it barefoot too, and she listened for him on the boards and on the mud and on the roots across the path and heard nothing.

She was starting to find that creepy. She was starting to find that she didn't mind it.

The trail ended at the top of a bluff. Below it, a pile of driftwood as high as a house—whole trees, silver and smooth and bleached by a hundred winters, jammed together at the top of the beach where the storms had thrown them. Past the logs, the beach. Gray sand going out flat and wide to the water. The sea stacks standing off the south end in a line like broken teeth, black against the white of the fog. Waves coming in slow and heavy and breaking with a sound she'd been hearing her whole life.

Nobody on the beach. Whoever owned the Subaru was out in the water somewhere, or had walked south toward the stacks. In October, in the rain, it was hers.

She climbed down over the driftwood and dropped onto the sand and walked out until she found the log she wanted: a big one, a cedar, lying on its side halfway between the drift pile and the water, its top worn smooth and flat as a bench. She sat down on it. The wood was wet. She didn't care.

Potter came down after her. He climbed over the drift pile the way a cat goes over a fence, and dropped onto the sand without a sound, and walked out, and sat down on the log beside her. Not too close. A foot of wet cedar between them.

They sat there and looked at the water.

The fog was coming in off the ocean in long gray sheets. The stacks came and went in it. A gull stood at the edge of the waves on one leg and looked at them like they owed it money.

Leah thought about the yard. Paul in the mud. Brady crying. Emily in the doorway with flour on her arms.

She thought about the claws resting on Paul's throat, all five, exactly where a wolf would feel them.

"What do you look like?" she said.

Potter turned his head.

"When you're human," Leah said. "You've got one, right? A human form? You said *shifter*. Shifters shift. Into something and *out* of it." She kept her eyes on the water. "I've known you sixteen hours and I've seen you naked, and I don't know what your face looks like."

He didn't answer right away.

She looked over at him. He was looking at the water again. His ears had gone back—not flat, just back—and his tail, lying on the log beside him, had gone very still.

"You don't want to see that," he said.

"I asked."

"Ja. I heard."

"So?"

He was quiet for long enough that she thought he wasn't going to. The waves came in. The gull gave up on them and flew off down the beach.

"Fine," Potter said.

He stood up. He walked a few steps away from the log, out onto the open sand, and stood there with his back half to her, looking at the stacks in the fog.

Then he got smaller.

There wasn't any light. There wasn't any sound. She'd seen Jacob phase and Seth phase and Paul phase, the explosion of it, the shredding cloth, the heat. This was nothing like that. He just—*came down*. The way water comes down when you pull a plug. The ears went first, folding back into the black of his head and then there was no black, there was hair, messy and dark and wet with rain. The muzzle shortened and flattened. The fur went—she didn't see where it went. One second it was there. The next it wasn't. The shoulders narrowed. The tail was gone. The whole huge frame of him seemed to fold in on itself and settle, and drop, a foot and more, until she was looking at a man standing on the sand in a leather jacket and a torn flannel shirt and jeans that fit him perfectly, with no slit in the back.

He was short.

That was the first thing her brain said, very clearly. *He's short.* Five-five, maybe. Maybe not even. Shorter than her. Shorter than Seth. Shorter than *Emily*. A small, compact, narrow man, lean the way some guys were lean because they never had enough of anything, all wiry forearms and ropy neck. Pale, the way people were pale who didn't see a lot of sun.

He reached into his jacket pocket and took something out and put it on. Glasses. Round ones, thin wire frames, a little crooked.

Then he turned around.

Green eyes. That was the same. Bright and very green behind the round lenses, the same exact green as the wolf's, so that for a second the face around them looked like something he was wearing over the real one. A narrow face. Sharp cheekbones. A mouth that looked like it didn't smile much when it wasn't being paid to. He looked older than she'd expected, and younger, both at once. Late twenties, maybe. With the eyes of somebody a lot older than that.

And the scars.

She saw the one on his forehead first, because his wet hair had fallen away from it. A thin, jagged line, old and faded almost to silver, running down through the skin above his right eyebrow. It was shaped like a lightning bolt. Exactly like a lightning bolt. Like somebody had drawn it there on purpose.

Then she saw his hands.

He'd let them hang at his sides. On the back of the right one, there were words. Thin white lines raised against the skin, cut deep enough that they'd healed into ridges, in a neat, slanting handwriting that wasn't his. She could read them from the log.

*I must not tell lies.*

Leah stood up.

Her legs decided before she did. She was standing, and then she was walking across the sand to him, and then she was close enough to see all of it. The faint white pucker on his right forearm where his sleeve had pulled up, like a puncture that had healed wrong. Two more marks on the inside of the same forearm, a little apart, like the bite of something with long thin teeth. A thin line through his left eyebrow. A nick out of the top of one ear. And at the open collar of the torn flannel, low down on his chest, right over his heart, a patch of skin that was a different color than the rest: an oval, dull red, the shiny, puckered red of an old burn.

He stood there and let her look. He didn't move. His face was perfectly calm. His hands had curled a little at his sides, and she watched the words on the back of the right one stretch over the knuckles.

Her wolf had gone quiet.

She noticed it the way you notice a radio switching off in another room. She'd been feeling it all morning, the way she'd been feeling it since the Cullens' front door—the wolf in her chest up on its feet, tail going, nose working, *him*, *him*, *him*. And now it had sat back down.

She knew why. She could smell why. The forest smell was still there, pine and stone and cold, but it was faint now, like a jacket somebody had worn a long time ago. Under it he smelled like a man. Like rain and wet leather and skin. Like any guy.

His eyes flicked to her face. To her nose. She watched him see it—see her catch the difference. Something in his face closed like a door.

"Human nose," he said. "Human smell. There's less of the other thing in it."

"I know."

"Your wolf noticed."

"Yeah," Leah said. "It did." She made herself look at him straight. "That's a smell thing. That's my nose, Potter." She gestured at all of it, the height, the scars, the glasses. "That's all it is."

He didn't say anything. She could tell he didn't believe her.

She looked at the lightning bolt. "What happened?"

"Which one?"

"Start with the forehead."

He laughed. It came out short and wrong. In this voice—a man's voice, ordinary, the accent still there but thinner, more like somewhere else under it—it sounded younger.

"A man tried to kill me when I was one," he said. "He killed my dad first. Then he came upstairs. My mum stood in front of the cot and wouldn't move, so he killed her too. Then he tried me. It didn't take." He touched the scar, briefly, with two fingers. The gesture looked like an old habit. "She'd—what she did, standing there. Choosing that. It made a kind of protection. Old magic. The curse bounced. That's what it left."

Leah stared at him.

"The hand," he went on, in the same flat voice, before she could say anything, "was a teacher. When I was fifteen. I kept saying something true that the government didn't want said out loud, so she gave me detention. Lines. With a special quill. Writes in your own blood and cuts the words into the back of your hand while it does it." He turned the hand over and back, looking at it like it belonged to someone else. "Night after night. Till it scarred."

"A *teacher*—"

"The arm's a snake. Two snakes, actually. Different ones, different years." He pulled the sleeve down over the marks without looking at them. "The chest's a necklace. Long story. The rest is a war."

"A *war*."

"Ja. Back home. Back—" He stopped. "Before South Africa. There was a war, and I was in it, from about eleven on, and it ended when I was seventeen. And then I was nineteen and I couldn't stand being anywhere anybody knew my face, so I got on a plane to the furthest place I could think of that wasn't the moon."

"You said you grew up somewhere hot."

"I did grow up somewhere hot." He looked at her. "I was nineteen when I got there, Clearwater. That's when I grew up. Before that I was just surviving."

The waves came in. Leah didn't know what to do with her hands. She crossed her arms.

"And the height?" she said. "You going to tell me the war made you short?"

She meant it to come out mean. Sharp, the way she'd talk to Seth, something to cut the weight in the air. It came out wrong. It came out sort of quiet.

He looked at her for a long moment.

"My aunt and uncle did that," he said.

She waited.

"Took me in after my parents. Didn't want me. Made sure I knew it." He shrugged, one shoulder, a short, tight movement. "Cupboard under the stairs, till I was eleven. Their son got seconds. I got what was left, if there was anything left, which mostly there wasn't. You don't grow much on that. By the time anybody fed me properly it was a bit late." He spread his hands. "Five-five. Bit of a stoop if I'm tired. Glasses since I was a kid, because nobody took me to get my eyes checked till I was too blind to read the board."

He looked down at himself. At the hands. At the narrow chest under the torn flannel.

"I hate it," he said.

He said it plain. Like a fact. Like telling her the time.

"I hate this body. Every bit of it. I look in the mirror like this and it's all just—" his hand moved, a short, sharp gesture at his forehead, his hand, his chest "—a list. Of every bad thing that ever happened to me, written down where I have to look at it. My mum dying for me. That woman's quill. Their kitchen. The war. Everybody who died in it." He let the hand drop. "The wolf doesn't have any of it. Not a mark. I shift and it's all just gone. Clean. Nobody looks at the wolf and sees a dead woman in a nursery."

Leah didn't say anything. She couldn't think of a single thing to say that wouldn't be stupid.

She'd spent two years being the most bitter person she knew. She'd been proud of it, a little. She'd earned it. Sam and Emily. Her dad's face, his hand going to his chest, looking at her—at *her*, at what she'd just turned into in front of him. The wolf she hadn't asked for, the body that had shut its doors and locked them. She'd carried all of it around like a load of stones and dared anybody to try to take one off her.

She looked at the words on the back of his hand. *I must not tell lies.*

She thought about the motel. About asking him, *would you tell me?* About him lying there in the dark and thinking about it, really thinking, and saying *probably not*.

A guy with *I must not tell lies* cut into his hand by a grown woman with a quill, when he was fifteen, for telling the truth. And he'd looked her in the eye last night and told her the truth anyway, when a lie would've been so much easier.

Her throat had gone tight. She hated that.

Potter looked at her face. He saw something there. She watched him see it. And she watched him decide, right then, that he wasn't having it.

His mouth went up at one corner.

It was a grin. A sly, lopsided, eyebrow-up thing, and it went over the top of everything else on his face like a lid on a pot. She'd seen that grin before, on every guy at every party she'd ever been to who'd said something too true and then needed to make it a joke before anybody could take it seriously.

"Anyway," Potter said. "Plus side."

"Potter—"

He got bigger.

Same as before, backward. Up and out, the shoulders widening, the hair going to fur, black and thick and wet with rain, the face drawing out into a muzzle, the ears coming up tall. The glasses came off in his hand somewhere in the middle and the hand was a paw by the time he tucked them back into the jacket. The tail swept out behind him, and the tear in the flannel was still there but on a much bigger flannel, and he was looking down at her from six-foot-seven again with green eyes that hadn't changed at all.

The forest smell came back like somebody had opened a window. Her wolf was on its feet before she could stop it.

He saw that too. Of course he did.

"Plus," Potter said, and the grin was all the way back now, all teeth, lazy and cocky and absolutely shameless, "a certain kind of girl goes *nuts* for a knot." His ears flicked. "And I'm all for it."

Leah stared at him.

Then she punched him in the arm. Hard. Hard enough to hurt a normal person. It was like punching a tree trunk wrapped in a shag rug.

"*Ow*," Potter said, not meaning it.

"You," Leah said, "are such an asshole."

"Ja."

"That was a dodge. That was the biggest, dumbest, most obvious dodge I have ever seen in my *life*. Seth does better dodges. Seth does better dodges when he's lying about eating the last Pop-Tart."

"Worked, though."

"It did *not* work."

"You're not looking at my hand anymore."

She opened her mouth. She closed it again.

He was right. She was looking at his grin, and his ears, and the whole stupid furry wall of him, and the scars were all gone—gone under the fur, gone into the wolf, like they'd never been there. Exactly like he wanted.

She looked at the place on his right forepaw where the words would be. Black fur. Black pads. Nothing.

"I know they're there," she said quietly.

The grin stayed. Something behind it shifted.

"Ja," Potter said, after a second. "I know you do."

They stood there in the rain on the sand. The fog had come all the way in now, and the sea stacks were gone, and it was just them and the gray water and the waves.

Leah sat back down on the log.

After a moment, he sat down beside her. A foot of wet cedar between them, same as before.

She looked at the water for a long time. Then she said it, before she could think about it too much and change her mind.

"I haven't had a period since I phased."

He turned his head.

"March," she said to the waves. "Seven months. Not one. Not spotting, not cramps, not anything. Just—stopped. Like somebody flipped a switch." She heard her own voice going flat, board-flat, the way it had gone with Jacob that day in the woods. She let it. "There's never been a girl wolf before. Not in all the stories, not ever. So there's nobody to ask. I figure whatever it is that makes me heal, whatever makes me run hot and phase and all the rest of it—it decided I didn't need that part. Shut it down." She swallowed. "Genetic dead end. That's what I called it, to Jake. The freak girl-wolf. Good for patrol and nothing else."

Potter didn't say anything.

"That's why I said *don't bother*," Leah said. "Last night. With the peppermint thing."

"I figured something like that."

"And last night—" She stopped. Started again. "Last night. When I said I looked four months gone. I was joking. And then I wasn't."

She didn't look at him. She looked at the gull, which had come back and was standing at the edge of the water again, on one leg, as if nothing in the world had happened.

"So now you know," she said. "So we're even."

She waited for him to say something stupid. *I'm sorry*. *That's awful*. *Maybe it'll come back*. Anything. Any of the things she'd been bracing for since March.

What he said, after a while, was, "Can I ask somebody?"

She turned her head.

"About that," he said. "About your—about the healing. And the bite. There's somebody I know. She's smarter than me. She's smarter than anybody. She works with—creatures, things, magical biology. If anybody's read something about it, she has." He met her eyes. "I wouldn't use your name."

Leah thought about it.

She thought about her body, and the switch, and seven months of not counting. About the curve under her hand in the dark. About the marks on her neck that had no business still being there, humming like a power line.

About somebody she'd never met, a long way away, reading about her.

"Your friend," she said. "She's going to know it's me."

"She's going to know it's *somebody*."

"Who is she?"

"Somebody who stood in front of me in a war, once. Lots of times." His ears went back, a little. "Somebody I'd trust with my life. Have done. Will do again."

Leah looked at him for a long time.

"Don't use my name," she said.

"I won't."

"And if she says something bad—if she says it's—" She stopped. "You tell me. Whatever it is. You don't decide it's better if I don't know."

He looked at her. He didn't look away.

"I'll tell you," Potter said.

She nodded. She looked back at the water.

Neither of them said anything else for a while. The tide was coming in. The waves came a little further up the sand each time, and a little further, and the gull gave up on them for good and flew off into the fog.

Somewhere around twelve-thirty, Leah's stomach growled so loud it echoed off the drift pile.

Potter looked at her.

"Shut up," said Leah.

"I've got four donuts left in the car."

"Three. I counted. You ate one when I wasn't looking."

"Three donuts," he said, and stood, and held out a paw to help her up off the log.

She looked at it. Big, black, padded, claws tucked away. Somewhere under all that fur, words cut into the skin by a woman with a quill.

She took it.

## II. Harry

He dropped her at her mother's house at five past one.

It was a small single-story place on a side road past the school, white siding gone gray at the corners, a covered porch with two plastic chairs on it and a pair of muddy sneakers by the door that were about Seth's size. An aluminum fishing boat on a rusted trailer sat in the side yard under a blue tarp that had collected a puddle in its middle. Nobody had taken it out in a while. Harry could tell from the moss on the trailer hitch.

Leah sat in the passenger seat with her hand on the door handle and didn't open it.

"Six," she said.

"Six."

"Don't bring carnations."

"I won't."

"Don't bring *roses* either. She'll think you're apologizing."

"What do I bring?"

Leah looked at him for a second. "Figure it out, Potter," she said. "You're a detective."

"I'm an Auror."

"Whatever." She shoved the door open—the hinge screamed—and got out into the rain, barefoot, in his black T-shirt, and walked up the porch steps without looking back. A curtain in the front window moved. A face appeared behind it, young and round and horrified, and vanished again.

Seth.

Harry put the car in reverse and left before anybody could come out on the porch.

---

He stopped at the Thriftway on the way back through Forks. Jenna was gone; the girl at the register now was older, with reading glasses on a chain, and she looked at the shopping basket on her belt and then at the point a foot to the left of his face where she thought his face was.

"Baking?" she said.

"Trying."

Two liters of whole milk. A dozen eggs. A box of cinnamon sticks and a jar of ground cinnamon. Flour, sugar, butter, a carton of cornstarch, a bottle of vanilla. A glass pie dish with a sticker on it that said OVEN SAFE in red. A small steel saucepan from the aisle with the spatulas and the off-brand Tupperware. A wooden spoon.

"Must be some pie," she said.

"It's a tart," said Harry. "Milk tart."

She looked blank. He hadn't expected anything else. He paid and left.

---

The envelope was lying on the bed when he walked into room fourteen.

Somebody had made the bed. Housekeeping, he figured, and he felt a pang of sympathy for whoever it had been, walking into that smell. The window had been cracked open an inch. It hadn't helped much. There was a fresh towel on the rack and a new paper-wrapped bar of cucumber soap on the sink, and lying square in the middle of the brown-and-orange bedspread, where nobody from housekeeping had left it, was a fat manila envelope, so stuffed that the flap was held shut with three rubber bands and a strip of parcel tape.

There was a lilac slip clipped to the front. MACUSA interdepartmental routing, in T's tight little handwriting:

*Rec'd London desk 1:42 p.m. EST, via ICW diplomatic pouch. Fwd. to field. Potter, your friends have very aggressive handwriting. — T.*

And under that, on the envelope itself, in green ink, in handwriting he'd known since he was eleven years old:

*HARRY. READ THE TABBED PAGES FIRST. THEN CALL ME. DON'T SKIP AHEAD. — H.*

He'd sent the memo at seven-twenty that morning. Leah had been in the shower with the fan going and the water running, swearing at the cucumber soap, and he'd sat on the end of the bed with a lilac sheet on his knee and written it fast, before he could think about it too much: *Mione. Need a favour. Bit someone. Shifter, not like me, different tradition. During a tie. Marks won't heal — 7 hrs+, she heals everything else in minutes. Anything you can find. Urgent. — H.* He'd addressed it to the London desk with a note to forward, and it had folded itself into a bird and gone out under the door into the rain, and he'd gone into the bathroom after that and fought the showerhead and lost.

Six hours. It had been six hours, and she'd filled an envelope.

Of course she had.

He put the Thriftway bags on the little round table by the window and took off his jacket and hung it on the back of the chair. He looked at the torn flannel—Paul's claw had opened it from the second button almost to the hem—and decided it could wait. He took off his flannel, too, and laid it over the other chair.

Then he stood in the middle of the room for a minute in his T-shirt and jeans with his tail hanging behind him and looked at the envelope.

He thought about Leah's face on the beach. *You tell me. Whatever it is.*

He opened it.

---

Hermione had tabbed it in four colors. Of course she had.

Red tabs, a photocopied article from what looked like a bound journal, the pages slightly crooked where she'd pressed them flat against the glass of a Muggle copier: *Report of Field Observations among the Hereditary Wolf-Kin of the Southern Carpathians, Department for the Regulation and Control of Magical Creatures, Beast Division, 1954.* Somebody had underlined passages in green ink and written in the margins, in smaller green ink, notes like *(THIS)* and *(see also Lindqvist p. 40)* and once, beside a paragraph about winter mating customs, simply *(!!)*.

Blue tabs, a sheaf of handwritten translations on lined notebook paper, from something called the Hrafnsbók, a fifteenth-century Norwegian manuscript on the *úlfheðnar*. Margin notes: *(translation mine, so don't trust the verbs).*

Yellow tabs, three pages from a German treatise dated 1791. Margin note at the top of the first page: *(Author also wrote three monographs on the Crumple-Horned Snorkack. Salt. Lots of salt. But it agrees with the others on the important part.)*

And one green tab, a single page in Hermione's own handwriting, headed *SUMMARY — FOR HARRY, WHO WILL SKIP TO THIS.*

Harry, who had been about to skip to it, put it at the bottom of the pile.

He got the pie dish and the flour and the butter out of the bag while he read. He'd made melktert maybe forty times in South Africa, in a kitchen in Observatory with a gas oven that ran hot on the left side, from a recipe a woman named Auntie Rozanne had written out for him on the back of a church bulletin and made him promise never to lose. He still had the bulletin. It was in his wallet behind his MACUSA card. He didn't need it; he'd had it memorized since his second year in Cape Town. But he took it out and propped it against the moose mug anyway.

He worked the butter into the flour with his claws while he read the red-tabbed pages.

The Carpathian report was dry, careful, and sixty years old, written by a Ministry field researcher named Agnieszka Wrońska who had spent three winters living in a village in the mountains with a family of hereditary wolf-shifters and had clearly liked them very much. *Not lycanthropes*, she'd written in the second paragraph, and Hermione had underlined it twice. *The Kin change at will and retain their reason in both forms. The condition passes in the blood, father to son and mother to daughter, and is not transmissible by bite.*

Then, a dozen pages in, under a heading that said *Courtship and Pairing*, she got to it.

Harry stopped working the pastry.

*It is the custom among the Kin*, Wrońska had written, *when a man has found the woman he means to keep, that at the height of their joining—during that period when, as among true wolves, the pair are bound together and cannot part—he will take her at the neck with his teeth. They call this* znak, *the mark. It is not given lightly and is not, the elder women assured me, often given by accident, though they laughed when they said this and I was not certain they were being truthful.*

*Where both parties carry the blood, the mark is said to "take." The wound does not close in the usual way. The Kin heal very rapidly, as I have recorded elsewhere, but a taken mark will remain open and red for some days and then fade to a pale scar that does not vanish. I was shown several such scars on the necks of married women of the village, some forty and fifty years old.*

Hermione's green ink, in the margin: *(Harry. Her marks didn't heal. Seven hours. Do you understand what I'm pointing at.)*

He understood what she was pointing at.

He kept reading.

*The Kin say that a taken mark binds the pair. They are vague on what this means, and I suspect they do not fully know themselves. The most common description, given me by several women independently, is that it "comes on like the seasons"—slowly, over weeks and months, a growing sense of the other person. One elderly widow told me that in the first winter after her marking she found she always knew which room of the house her husband was in. By the third winter, she said, she could feel when he was cold. She told me she once walked out into a snowstorm with her eyes shut and found him by the woodpile, a quarter-mile from the house, where he had fallen and hurt his leg.*

*I asked whether the binding ran both ways. She laughed at me and said, "What use would it be otherwise?"*

He read that last line three times.

Then he put the page down and went back to the pastry, because his paws needed something to do.

---

The blue tabs and the yellow tabs said the same thing, more or less, in worse prose. The Norwegian wolf-warriors bit at the neck "at the binding." The German author, between long digressions about the moon, called it *das Paarungszeichen*—the mating sign—and said it took only when "both bodies were alive with the Craft at the same moment" and that its effects "accreted over the turning of the year, and may not be hurried, and may not be undone."

*May not be undone.* Hermione had underlined that too. Then she'd written beside it, smaller: *(He also says the Snorkack can be summoned with honey. I'd want a second source on 'undone.' I'm looking.)*

Harry rolled out the pastry on a sheet of wax paper on the little round table, using the vanilla bottle for a rolling pin, and pressed it into the glass pie dish, and crimped the edges with the tip of one claw the way Auntie Rozanne had shown him, standing at his elbow in that hot kitchen, saying *no, no, my boy, with the claw, like this, you've got the right tools for once, use them.*

She'd been the first person in his life who'd looked at the wolf and seen the tools.

He blind-baked the shell in midair over the bed. A heating charm, a bubble of shimmering dry heat the size of a beach ball, the pie dish turning slowly in the middle of it like a moon. Auntie Rozanne would have had words about it. It worked. The smell of baking butter started to creep into the room and fight with everything else in it. It lost, but it put up a good fight.

Then he read the summary.

*SUMMARY — FOR HARRY, WHO WILL SKIP TO THIS.*

*1. What you've described matches, in three separate traditions with no obvious connection to one another, something called a mating bite. Given by a male shifter at the neck during a tie. Takes when both parties have active magic. Marks don't heal normally.*

*2. Your partner (I'm not asking. I'm NOT asking, Harry) is from a "different tradition." I need to know what that tradition does with pairing. If it already has a bonding mechanism of its own, your bite may have interacted with it. Call me.*

*3. Effects, if any: slow. Weeks to months. All three sources agree on that and almost nothing else.*

*4. You are an idiot. Ron says so too. He also says congratulations, and I've told him that's not appropriate, and he says it's a bit appropriate.*

*5. CALL ME.*

Harry put the summary down, and wiped his paws on a motel towel, and went to his duffel bag on the other bed and dug through it until he found a gray wool sock rolled into a ball at the very bottom. He unrolled it. Inside was a small square mirror in a plain wooden frame, the glass a little foggy around the edges.

He sat down on the end of the bed and looked into it.

"Hermione Granger," he said.

For a second the glass showed him a black-furred face with green eyes and wet ears and a smear of flour on the muzzle. Then it misted over, and cleared, and he was looking at a kitchen in London. Yellow lamplight. A wooden table covered in open books. A mug of tea with a teabag string hanging over the edge. And Hermione Granger, twenty-seven years old, in a gray jumper with the sleeves shoved up to her elbows and her hair pulled back in a knot that was coming loose in about six places, leaning in close to her own mirror on the other side of the Atlantic with a quill behind her ear and a look on her face that he'd last seen right before she slapped Draco Malfoy across the face.

"You bit someone," Hermione said.

"Hi, Mione."

"You *bit* someone, Harry. During a—" She stopped. Her eyes narrowed. "Is that *flour*?"

"I'm baking."

"You're *baking*." She closed her eyes briefly. "You've potentially bonded yourself magically to a woman you've known for—how long have you known her?"

"About eighteen hours."

"*Eighteen*—"

"And a bit."

Somewhere behind her, off-mirror, a man's voice said, "Is that Harry? Tell him he's an idiot."

"Ron says you're an idiot."

"I heard him."

"And tell him he still owes me that Cannons hat!"

"He still owes you the hat," Hermione said, without looking away from the mirror. "Ron, go and check on Rose, please, I need to concentrate." A door closed. She pushed a loose strand of hair out of her face. "Did you read it? Properly? In order?"

"Red, blue, yellow, green."

"And?"

"And I read the bit about the woman in the snowstorm about four times."

Hermione's face changed. Just a little. Some of the sharpness went out of it, and what was underneath was the same thing that had been underneath it since she was eleven and he was eleven and she'd stood in a wrecked girls' bathroom and lied to McGonagall's face about a troll to keep him and Ron out of trouble.

"Yes," she said, more quietly. "I thought you might."

"Is it real?"

"I don't know." She said it plainly, which was one of the things he'd always liked best about her. "Three sources. One reliable, one probably reliable, one written by a man who thought you could summon a Snorkack with honey. They don't know about each other, and they agree. That's worth something. Proof would be worth more." She leaned back. "Tell me about her tradition. Point two. What does her family's magic do with pairing?"

So he told her.

He left out Leah's name, and where. He told her about a tribe on a coast, a line of men—and now one woman—who turned into wolves when something cold and dangerous came near. He told her about the pack mind. About something they called imprinting: one look, and a bond that came down out of the sky and took you whether you wanted it or not. About an Alpha who'd loved a girl for two years and then looked at her cousin once and that was the end of it.

Hermione listened without interrupting, which was how he knew it was bad.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment. In the background he could hear rain on her kitchen window, too. London rain. He'd almost forgotten what it sounded like.

"Oh, *Harry*," she said.

"Ja."

"So her people's magic already knows how to do this. It has a—a groove. A shape it already makes." Her eyes had gone distant, the way they did when she was assembling something in her head. "And then you come along and bite her, at the exact moment the German says both bodies are 'alive with the Craft,' and you're tied, so you're—physically—" she went faintly pink, and powered through it like a train "—*connected*, and you've got whatever magic it is you've got, which God knows nobody's ever properly documented, and she's got hers. And her magic has a shape for bonding already sitting there, waiting." She looked at him. "It's possible the bite did something smaller than the Carpathian mark. That it gave her magic a push, and her magic did the rest. Something like her people's imprint. Partial. Accidental. A sort of—*pseudo*-imprint, for lack of a better word."

Harry sat very still on the end of the bed.

"Possible," he said.

"*Possible*, Harry. I'm extrapolating from three old books and a phone call." She held up a hand. "It's equally possible the marks fade next week and nothing else ever happens. I can't tell you which. Nobody can. You'll have to see what develops."

"And if something develops?"

"Then it'll come on slowly. That's the one part everybody agrees on. Weeks, months. Like the seasons, the Carpathian women said." She hesitated. "And it would go both ways. If it's anything. That's the other thing all three agree on."

*What use would it be otherwise?*

He looked down at the mirror in his paw. At his own face in the corner of it, small, flour on his muzzle.

"She's going to hate this," he said.

"Why would she—" Hermione stopped. He watched it land on her. The Alpha. The cousin. One look out of the sky. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, no."

"Ja."

"Harry—"

"I promised I'd tell her. Whatever it was. She made me promise." He rubbed at the flour on his nose with the back of a paw. "I'm going to tell her."

"Good," said Hermione immediately. "Tell her all of it. Tell her *we don't know*. That part's the most important."

"I know."

"And Harry, if she asks whether this is why she—whether any of what she feels is because of the bite—"

"It started before the bite."

Hermione blinked.

"Long before," he said. "There was a mind-reader in the room. I had a look. I've got the timeline."

"You read somebody's—no. No, don't tell me. I don't want to know." She pressed her fingers to her forehead. "Fine. Good. That's—actually, that's useful. Tell her that too."

"Second question," Harry said.

"There's a *second* question?"

"Same person." He looked at the heating charm hovering over the bed, the pastry shell turning gold in the middle of it. "She hasn't had a cycle since she first changed. Seven months. Not once. Thinks she's sterile. Thinks her body shut it down. She's the first woman in her tribe ever to change—there's nobody to ask."

Hermione went very still.

"Healing?" she said.

"Fast. Faster than me. She'll close a cut while you watch."

"Hold on." She reached off-mirror and dragged a book across the table toward her—big, old, green leather—and flipped through it fast. He watched her lips move. She flipped back. Forward. Stopped. Read. Pulled a second book over, a Muggle one, a thick paperback with a broken spine and a picture of a uterus on the cover in pink and blue, and flipped through that too.

"Harry," she said slowly. "Do you know what a period actually *is*? Biologically?"

"I've got a rough idea, Mione."

"It's an injury." She tapped the Muggle book. "A scheduled one. Every month the lining of the uterus builds up, and if there's no pregnancy, the body breaks it down and sheds it. The tissue comes apart. Small blood vessels rupture. That's the bleeding. It's controlled damage—but it *is* damage." She looked up. "If her healing is as fast as you say, it's entirely possible her body is reading that breakdown as a wound, and repairing it before it can happen. Before anything can bleed. She'd never see a period, because her body would never let it get that far."

Harry didn't say anything.

"Ovulation's a separate process," Hermione went on, faster now, the way she always got faster when the pieces fit. "Nothing you've described suggests she's stopped ovulating. Nothing. She's healthy, she's young, she's—" she waved a hand "—vigorous, I'd imagine. The lining builds up, it doesn't shed, because it's healed as fast as it breaks down. The rest of the machinery's probably working perfectly well." She sat back. "I'd want to examine her. I'd want blood work, I'd want a healer who knows shifter physiology, which is to say nobody, which is to say me. But if you're asking me what I think?"

"I'm asking."

"Ninety-nine percent," Hermione said, "she is not sterile."

The heating charm over the bed wobbled. The pastry shell dipped, and he caught it, and steadied it, and it went on turning.

"Ninety-nine," he said.

"Ninety-nine." Her eyes narrowed again. All the softness went out of her face in about a second and a half. "Which brings me to *my* next question."

"Mione—"

"You cast a contraceptive charm."

"Ja."

"*Properly.*"

"Ja."

"Harry James Potter."

"Belt and braces, Mione. I cast it. First thing. Before anything. She told me not to bother."

Hermione stared at him through the mirror for a long moment. Then she let out a breath and put both hands flat on the table, like someone who'd just watched a car stop an inch from a cliff edge.

"Well," she said. "Thank *God* for that. Thank God you've got one sensible bone in your body, and it's apparently in your—" She stopped herself. "When did you cast it?"

"About twenty past eight last night."

"Then it's good till about twenty past eight tonight, if you did it right, which—"

"I did it right."

"—which means you've been lucky, and it runs out *tonight*, and if there is going to be any more—*any more*, Harry—you will recast it before. Every single time. Do you understand me?"

"I'm having dinner with her mother tonight."

Hermione looked at him.

"Of course you are," she said.

Somewhere behind her, the door opened again. Ron's voice: "Is he still being an idiot?"

"He's having dinner with her *mother*."

A pause. Then Ron, delighted: "*Blimey.* Tell him to wear a decent shirt."

Harry looked at the torn flannel over the back of the chair.

"Tell Ron," he said, "I've got it covered."

Hermione's face went soft again, the way it did when she was about to ask him something he didn't want to answer. He saw it coming and couldn't stop it.

"Harry," she said. "Are you all right?"

"Fine."

"You said *fine* in exactly the voice you used in the tent."

He didn't say anything.

She waited. Hermione was the only person he'd ever known who could wait him out. She'd done it in the tent, for months, while the locket burned a hole in his chest. She'd done it in the hospital wing and the common room and the kitchen at Grimmauld Place and a hundred other places, and she was doing it now, three thousand miles away, through a piece of glass in a wooden frame.

"She asked what I look like," he said finally. "Human."

Hermione's face didn't change. Her eyes did.

"And you showed her."

"Ja."

"And?"

"Her wolf sat down," Harry said. "She says it's a smell thing."

"It probably *is* a smell thing, Harry."

"Ja. Probably."

"Harry—"

"I've got a tart in the air," he said. "I've got to make the custard."

"Harry, *don't* you—"

"Love you, Mione. Tell Ron I'll send the hat."

"*Harry*—"

He put his paw over the glass. It went dark.

He sat there on the end of the bed for a while in the smell of baking butter, holding the mirror, with the pastry shell turning slowly over the bed and the rain coming down outside on the roof of the Impala.

Ninety-nine percent.

He thought about Leah on the beach, saying *genetic dead end* in a voice flat as a board. About her hand on her stomach at midnight in the dark, and the way she'd gone so still. About *don't bother* and *I can't*.

He thought about *belt and braces*. About sitting in a Cape Town kitchen with a woman who'd lost four babies before her fifth one lived, saying *a man who doesn't think about it should not be allowed near anybody, my boy, you write that down.*

He'd written it down. He'd thought about it. Last night, with Leah's back against a motel door and her heat coming off her like an oven and every part of him wanting to just *go*, he'd stopped, and he'd thought about it, and he'd cast the charm, and she'd laughed at him for it.

If he hadn't—

He didn't let himself finish the thought.

He put the mirror back in the sock, and the sock back in the duffel. Then he got up and took the pastry shell down out of the air, golden and blistered at the edges and smelling like Auntie Rozanne's kitchen, and set it on the little round table, and got out the steel saucepan and the milk.

---

He made the custard over a second heating charm, a tight blue-white knot of heat no bigger than a fist, hanging in the air under the saucepan where a burner should've been. Milk, a cinnamon stick, a split vanilla pod he'd bought because the Thriftway didn't have the real kind and the bottle wasn't the same. Eggs and sugar and cornstarch beaten together in the motel ice bucket with the wooden spoon. The hot milk poured in slow, a thread at a time, so the eggs didn't scramble. Back on the heat. Stirring, stirring, stirring, the spoon going round and round, while the clock radio went from 2:41 to 2:58 to 3:10 and the custard thickened under his paw.

He thought about Leah the whole time. He didn't try not to.

He poured the custard into the shell and smoothed it with the back of the spoon and dusted the top with cinnamon from the jar, a fine brown snow, the way Auntie Rozanne did it, in a spiral from the middle out. Then he put the tart in the little motel fridge to set, next to a can of Coke and the leftover donuts, and shut the door.

Then he picked up the flannel.

He held the two edges of the tear together, and the threads found each other and knitted, quick and neat, from the hem to the second button, until there was nothing there but a faint ridge you'd only find by touch. He held it up. He looked at it. It was the same flannel he'd been wearing for two days. It had been through a vampire house, a motel floor, and Paul. It smelled like all three.

He thought about Ron. *Tell him to wear a decent shirt.*

He sighed and put the flannel down and went to the duffel. At the bottom, under the sock with the mirror, folded in a dry-cleaner's bag he'd been carrying around for four years, was a white button-down shirt. Collared. Pressed. Bought for his MACUSA swearing-in, worn exactly once, and then again at a funeral in Boston two years ago, and never since.

He took it out of the bag and held it up.

The tail slit had been cut and hemmed into the back of it by a seamstress in Brooklyn who'd asked no questions at all and charged him triple.

He hung it on the bathroom door and got in the shower and lost to the showerhead again.

At five-twenty he stood in front of the long mirror on the closet door in clean jeans and the white shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbow because buttoning the cuffs over his forearms made him feel like a dog in a costume. He'd brushed his fur. He'd done something to his ears that hadn't worked. The tart was in a Thriftway paper bag on the bed, still cold from the fridge, wrapped in a clean motel towel.

He looked at himself.

He thought about the other one. The small man with the glasses, and the scar on his forehead, and the words on the back of his hand.

He looked at his right forepaw. Black fur. Black pads. Nothing.

*I know they're there*, she'd said.

He picked up the tart and his keys and went out to the car.

## III. Seth

Seth Clearwater was eating his fourth bowl of Lucky Charms at the front window when the black car pulled up, and he knew before the passenger door even opened that he was going to need a fifth.

He'd slept from six-fifteen to twelve-thirty. He'd woken up starving, the way he always woke up after a night shift, and come out to the kitchen in his boxers and an old Mariners T-shirt of his dad's, and found the house empty and a note on the counter in Mom's handwriting: *Council at Sam's. Eat something. Don't phase.* The *don't phase* was underlined twice. He'd stood there looking at it for a long time and thought about why.

Then he'd poured the first bowl.

Now he sat on the arm of the couch with the bowl in his lap and the spoon halfway to his mouth and watched his sister get out of the fed's car in the rain.

She was wearing a man's shirt. A black T-shirt, way too big, knotted at the hip. She was barefoot. Her hair was sticking up at the back like it did when she'd slept on it wrong. She shut the car door with a scream of hinges he could hear through the glass, and she walked up the porch steps without looking back, and the car reversed out of the driveway and was gone before she'd reached the top.

Seth put the spoon down.

The front door opened. Leah came in. The smell came in with her.

"Oh, *no*," said Seth. "Oh, no, no, *no*—"

"Don't," said Leah.

"Leah, I can—*Leah*, I can *smell*—"

"I said don't."

"It's in the *house* now! It's in the—it's going to be in the *couch*—" He'd put a hand over his nose and mouth. It didn't help. It was worse than the vampires. That was the problem. It was just—*a lot*. His sister, and the forest-smell guy, and the two of them, all of it, soaked in so deep it rolled across the living room like fog off the beach. "Oh my God. Oh my God, I'm going to have to burn this couch."

"You're going to have to grow up," Leah said, and walked past him toward the hall.

"I'm *fifteen*!"

"Then act twenty."

She stopped in the hall doorway. She'd seen herself in the mirror by the coat hooks. Seth watched her look—watched her eyes go to the side of her own neck in the glass—and stay there.

That was when he saw them too.

Teeth. On her neck. Left side. Two rows of them, big ones and little ones, red and raw-looking like they'd happened ten minutes ago.

The joke went out of him all at once.

"Leah," he said. "What happened to your neck?"

"Nothing."

"That's a *bite*, Leah." He'd stood up. He didn't remember standing up. The cereal bowl was on the couch arm, tipping. "Did he—did he *hurt* you? Did that guy—"

"*No*." She turned around. Her face was fierce. "No, Seth. He didn't hurt me. Nobody hurt me. It's—" She stopped. Her hand came up and touched the marks, two fingers, light. "It's complicated."

"It should be *gone*. You heal faster than any of us. Leah, it should've been gone in like a *minute*—"

"I *know* that."

"Then why isn't it—"

"I don't *know*, Seth!" It came out loud. It rang off the walls of the little living room. Then, quieter, like it cost her: "I don't know. He doesn't know either. He's finding out."

Seth stood there with his hands hanging at his sides.

His sister didn't say *I don't know*. Not to him. Not ever. Leah always knew, or acted like she did, and if she didn't she'd rather bite your face off than say it out loud. She hadn't said *I don't know* when Sam left. She hadn't said it the day Dad died, when she'd come back out of the woods still shaking and Mom had been on the phone with the ambulance and Seth had been standing in the yard in his socks. She'd just walked into the house and gone into her room and shut the door, and stayed there for two days, and come out with her hair cut off.

"Okay," he said.

She looked at him.

"Okay," Seth said again. "He's finding out. Okay."

Something in her face eased. Not much. A little.

"I'm going to shower," she said. "For like an hour. Don't use all the hot water before I get in there."

"I already showered."

"You smell like Lucky Charms and a dog."

"That's *my natural smell*."

She almost smiled. She went down the hall. The bathroom door shut, and after a second the shower came on, and after another second he heard her swear, because the hot water in this house took forever, and always had, and Dad had always said he was going to fix it, and never had.

Seth picked up his bowl. The milk had gone soggy. He ate it anyway.

---

Mom got home at twenty to three.

She came in with her purse over her shoulder and a crushed bunch of grocery-store carnations in one hand and a sunflower drooping over the top of them like it had been through a war. She stopped two steps inside the door, and her nose wrinkled very slightly, and she didn't say anything about it at all. She just walked into the kitchen and found a vase under the sink—the tall glass one Dad had won at a raffle—and filled it with water and put the flowers in it and set it in the middle of the kitchen table.

Then she got the roast out of the fridge.

Seth watched her from the kitchen doorway. She moved around the kitchen the way she always moved around the kitchen, calm and quick and never wasting a step. Onions. Carrots. Potatoes. The big black roasting pan Grandma Clearwater had given her on her wedding day. She didn't look at him.

"How was council?" Seth said.

"Long."

"What'd they decide?"

"Nothing yet." She was peeling a carrot. The peel came off in one long curl. "Old Quil wants to sleep on it. Billy wants to talk to Jacob. Sam—" Her hands paused. Just for a second. "Sam doesn't want to talk about it at all."

"Did that guy really break Paul's rib?"

"Yes."

"*Seriously*?"

"He came across the yard at Emily," Mom said. The carrot peeler went on moving. "Paul did. Mr. Potter put him in the ground." She set the carrot down and picked up another one. "Paul's fine. He was eating muffins by noon."

Seth thought about that. About Paul, who had once broken Jared's nose over a bag of Doritos, lying in the mud with something's paw on his throat.

"Huh," he said.

"Set the table, please. Four places."

Seth stared at her back.

"Four," he said.

"Four."

"Mom. Four. That's—you, me, Leah, and—"

"Your sister's friend is coming to dinner," said Sue Clearwater. She put the carrot down. She turned around and looked at him, and he knew that look. He'd been getting that look since he was two. "And you are going to be polite to him."

"He's not her *friend*, Mom, he's—they—you can *smell*—"

"I'm aware of what I can smell, Seth."

He closed his mouth.

"Four places," said his mother. "The good plates. The blue ones."

He got the good plates down from the high cupboard. The blue ones, with the white rims, that they used at Thanksgiving and on birthdays. He counted out four. He set them on the table around the vase of crushed carnations. He got out the good forks.

He paused with the fourth fork in his hand, standing over the fourth plate, at the end of the table. The end where Dad used to sit.

Nobody had sat there since March. Not once. Not even Charlie, when he came over; Charlie always took the chair by the window, without anybody asking him to, like he knew.

Seth looked at the plate for a while.

Then he picked it up and moved it around the corner, to the side of the table next to where Leah always sat, and put the fork down next to it. He pulled Dad's chair back in, square to the table, empty.

Behind him, his mother had stopped peeling.

He didn't turn around. After a second, the carrot peeler started again.

---

Leah came out of the bathroom at half past three in a cloud of steam and her own clothes—jeans, a dark green tank top, a gray zip-up hoodie she didn't zip—with her hair wet and slicked back. She'd scrubbed so hard her arms were pink. She still smelled like him. Less. Some. Seth decided not to say so.

The marks on her neck were still there. Red. Clean. Exactly the same.

She didn't cover them up. She didn't pull up the hood. Seth noticed that.

She went into the kitchen and stood next to Mom at the counter without saying anything and picked up a potato and a knife and started cutting. Mom didn't say anything either. They stood there side by side, cutting potatoes, not talking, for a long time, and Seth sat at the table behind them and watched, and thought it was the closest he'd seen them stand to each other since March.

Around four-thirty, while the roast was in and the house was starting to smell like onions and beef and something other than his sister, Seth worked up the nerve.

"Is he nice?" he said.

Leah didn't turn around. "No."

"Is he nice to *you*?"

The knife stopped.

Leah stood there at the counter with her back to him, holding the knife over a half-cut potato. Next to her, very quietly, Mom kept cutting.

"He's honest," Leah said finally.

Seth thought about that. It wasn't the word he'd expected. It wasn't a word Leah used about anybody. Not about Sam. Not about Jacob. Not even about Dad, and Dad had been the most honest person Seth had ever known.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay what?"

"Just okay."

She turned her head and looked at him over her shoulder. There was something on her face he didn't have a name for.

"His name's Harry," Seth said. He hadn't meant to say it. It came out by itself.

"I know what his name is."

"Is that weird? Is it weird for you?"

"I call him Potter," said Leah, and went back to the potato.

Seth didn't push. He was fifteen. He could take a hint.

---

At five-forty, he was at the front window again, watching the rain.

He'd told himself he was watching the rain. He was waiting for the roast. He was thinking about whether he could get away with not phasing ever again for the rest of his life, so he'd never have to be in Leah's head at the same time she was, because the next time she phased, everything in it would be right there, every second of last night, and if he was phased too he'd see it, and then he would have to walk into the ocean and never come back.

He was definitely not watching for the car.

The car came down the road from the school at five-forty-one, long and low and black, wipers going. It turned into the driveway. It stopped. The engine died.

Nobody got out.

Seth watched. Through the rain on the windshield he could see the shape of the fed behind the wheel. He'd changed. White shirt. Collared. Sleeves rolled up. He was sitting there with both paws on the wheel, not moving, looking at the house.

"He's here," Seth said, without turning around.

Behind him, in the kitchen, he heard his mother set something down.

Leah came out of the hall. She stopped beside him at the window and looked out at the car, and Seth heard her breath change—a short, sharp hitch, like when you step in a cold puddle you didn't see.

"He's early," she said.

"He's not getting out."

"I can see that, Seth."

She stood there for a second. Then she went to the front door and opened it and went out, barefoot, into the rain, and down the porch steps, and across the driveway, and pulled open the passenger door of the car—the hinge screamed—and got in, and shut it.

Seth stayed at the window.

He could see them in there. Two shapes behind the wet glass. The big one, the one with the ears, had turned in his seat to face her. His mouth was moving. Leah was sitting very still, facing straight ahead.

Seth could have heard it. If he tried. The rain wasn't that loud, and the car wasn't that far away, and his ears were as good as anybody's in either pack. All he had to do was listen.

He watched his sister's hand go up to her neck. He watched it stay there.

He watched the fed keep talking. Slow. Like somebody laying something down very carefully on a table.

He watched his sister's whole body go rigid in the passenger seat, and her other hand come up and press flat over her mouth.

Seth took half a step toward the door.

"Seth," said his mother, from the kitchen doorway, very quietly. "Leave them."

He stopped.

He stood there with his hand on the back of the couch and looked at the car. Leah's shoulders were shaking. He couldn't tell if she was laughing or crying. He couldn't tell if she was about to phase. He couldn't tell anything at all through the rain on the glass.

He could have listened.

He walked over to the TV instead, and picked up the remote off the coffee table, and turned it on. Some college football game, two teams he didn't care about, slogging through the rain. He turned the volume up. And up. And up some more, until the crowd noise filled the whole little living room and rattled the blue plates on the table, until he couldn't have heard what was being said in that car if he'd been standing right next to it.

Then he sat down on the couch, with his back to the window, and watched the game.

Behind him, he heard his mother let out a long, slow breath.

She came and sat down on the other end of the couch. She didn't say anything. She reached over and put her hand on the back of his head, the way she used to when he was little, and left it there.

They watched somebody fumble on the twenty-yard line, and neither of them turned around.

## IV. Leah

The car smelled like cinnamon.

That was the first thing. She'd pulled the door shut and the rain noise had dropped to a hiss on the roof, and under the leather and the gasoline and him there was cinnamon, and butter, and something sweet and milky, coming from a paper grocery bag on the back seat wrapped in what she was pretty sure was a stolen motel towel.

The second thing was the shirt.

He was wearing a white button-down. Collared. Pressed, once, a long time ago. Sleeves rolled up to the elbows over black fur. Somebody had cut a slit for the tail in the back and hemmed it, very neatly. He'd brushed his fur. He'd tried to do something to his ears. Whatever it was, it hadn't worked; one of them was sticking out sideways.

He looked like a wolf on his way to a job interview. He looked like he'd rather be shot.

The third thing was that he hadn't gotten out of the car.

"You're early," Leah said.

"Ja."

"And you're sitting in the driveway."

"Ja."

She faced straight ahead, through the windshield, at her mother's house. The porch light wasn't on yet. In the front window she could see the back of the couch and the blue flicker of the TV, and the shape of Seth's head, and she watched it turn away from the window and settle, very deliberately, facing the other way.

"You talked to her," Leah said. "Your friend."

"Ja."

He'd turned in his seat to face her. She could feel him looking at her, the way she'd felt it in the Cullens' living room and on the beach. She didn't look back.

"You said you'd tell me," she said. "Whatever it was."

"I'm telling you."

"So tell me."

He took a breath. She heard it. A long one, in and out.

"Her name's Hermione," he said. "She works for the government back home. The magic one. Creatures. Beasts and beings, biology, all of it. She found three old books—one from Norway, one from Germany, and a field report from some woman who lived three winters in the Carpathian mountains with a family of wolf-shifters. Different from you. Different from me. Hereditary, like your lot. They change when they want to."

Leah said nothing.

"All three of them say the same thing." He was talking slow. Laying it down piece by piece, like setting out a hand of cards. "There's a bite. A male gives it. At the neck. During—" he paused, very slightly "—a tie. It's called a mating bite. When both people have magic in them, and it's going at the time, the bite *takes*. That's their word. It doesn't heal the normal way. It stays red for a few days. Then it fades down to a scar, and the scar stays."

Her hand had gone up to her neck. She didn't stop it.

"The Carpathian women had them," he said. "Married women. Scars forty, fifty years old. They showed her."

"So it's a scar," Leah said. Her voice came out very even. "Fine. I've got a scar. You've got about fifty. Big deal."

"That's not—" He stopped himself. Started again. "There's more."

She waited.

"In the three books, the bite *binds* people. They're vague about what that means. Nobody really knows. Hermione doesn't know. But she asked me about your people, what your magic does with—pairing. And I told her." He paused. "I left out your name. I told her about the imprinting."

Leah's whole body went cold. Then hot.

"She thinks," Potter said, very carefully, "that maybe your magic already has a shape for that. A groove. And I bit you at the exact wrong moment, with my magic going and yours going, and maybe it gave your magic a push, and your magic did what it already knows how to do. Something like an imprint. Partial. Accidental. She called it a pseudo-imprint. She said it might be nothing. She said we'll have to see what—"

"*No*."

It came out of her loud. It filled the car.

"No," Leah said again. She'd turned in her seat. She was looking at him now, and she couldn't stop looking at him, and her hands were shaking. "No. No. You did *not*. You don't get to—that's the *one thing*, that's the one thing I—"

The heat was coming up her spine. She felt it start at the base, the prickle, the burn, the wolf getting up off the floor inside her ribs and putting its whole weight against the bars. Her vision went sharp at the edges. Her fingers curled.

If she phased in this car, she'd take the roof off it. She'd take *him* with it, probably. She didn't care. She couldn't make herself care.

"Leah," Potter said. Low. Level. "Breathe."

"Don't you *tell* me to—"

"Breathe anyway."

She breathed. In. Out. It came out ragged. The heat stayed right there under her skin, crackling. She held onto the door handle so hard the plastic creaked.

"Every day," she said through her teeth. "Every single day, I watch it. Sam and Emily. Jared and Kim. Quil and a *two-year-old*. Jacob and the *baby*. Every single one of them, some old magic reached down and *grabbed* them and that was it, done, no choice, no say, no—they don't even get to know if they *want* it. They just *do*. Because it says so." Her voice cracked. "And I was the one who didn't get grabbed. I was the one left over. And I *hated* it, I hated it so much, and then last night I—"

She stopped.

"Last night I *picked*," she said. "I looked at you and I *picked you*. Me. Nobody made me. That was the whole—that was the *only*—"

Her voice gave out.

"And now you're telling me," she said, very quietly, "that maybe I didn't."

The rain hissed on the roof.

Potter sat there in his white shirt with his ears all the way flat against his skull.

"I'm telling you it started at 11:38," he said.

She stared at him.

"The bite. That's when it happened. Eleven thirty-eight." He held her eyes. "You said it. Out loud. You were counting."

"So *what*—"

"So you wanted me before that." He said it plain, flat, the way he'd said *I did* to Bella Cullen. "Long before. You wanted me at about quarter past seven last night in the Cullens' living room, before I'd said more than a dozen words to anybody. You'd built that whole couch thing in your head before I'd finished my lasagna. I know. I've seen it. I went into Edward Cullen's head and it was the loudest thing in there." His ear twitched. "You picked me four and a half hours before I bit you, Clearwater. In front of a mind-reader. On purpose. Just to make him squirm."

Leah opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

"Whatever this thing is," Potter said, "if it's anything—it came after. You'd already decided."

She sat there. The heat under her skin was still there, but it had stopped climbing. It was just sitting, humming, waiting.

She thought about the Cullens' living room. The glass wall at her back, cold through her tank top. The stink. The jeans. *Tall, dark, and furry. Huh. Who knew.*

Edward Cullen's face, like a man passing a kidney stone.

A laugh came out of her. It was a terrible laugh, wet and cracked and half a sob, and she slapped her hand over her mouth to stop it, and it came out anyway between her fingers.

"Oh my God," she said through her hand. "Oh my *God*. You've got a *timeline*."

"I've got a timeline."

"Because you read *Edward's*—"

"It was right there."

She laughed again, harder, and something in her chest that had been pulled tight as a wire let go a little, and the wolf turned around once inside her ribs and lay back down.

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. She hadn't noticed they were wet.

"So what happens?" she said. "If it's something. What does it do?"

"Nobody knows exactly. It comes on slow, all three books say. Weeks. Months. *Like the seasons*, the Carpathian women said." He hesitated. "There was an old woman. Married fifty years. She said the first winter, she always knew which room her husband was in. By the third winter, she could tell when he was cold. One time she walked out into a snowstorm with her eyes shut and found him a quarter mile from the house, where he'd fallen and broken his leg."

Leah didn't say anything.

"The researcher asked her if it went both ways," Potter said. "The old woman laughed at her. Said, *What use would it be otherwise?*"

She looked at him.

"Both ways," she said.

"If it's anything. Hermione says all three books agree on that much."

"So you're stuck too."

"If it's anything."

"Do you—" She stopped. She didn't know how to ask it. "Do you feel anything? Now?"

He thought about it. She watched him do it, honestly, the way he'd thought about *would you tell me* in the dark.

"I don't know," he said. "I thought about you the whole time I was stirring custard. That might just be you."

Leah looked away out the window, fast, before her face could do anything.

The TV was still going in the house. Loud. She could hear it through the glass and the rain—crowd noise, an announcer yelling about a fumble. Seth had turned it up. Seth had turned it all the way up, so he couldn't hear.

Her throat went tight all over again.

"There's something else," Potter said.

She turned back.

He was looking at her differently. She couldn't figure out how, at first. His ears had come partway up off his skull. And there was something in his face—in the set of the muzzle, the eyes—that she'd never seen on it before. Last night or this morning or on the beach. It looked almost like he was scared of what he was about to say. Scared of what it would do to her.

"I asked her about the other thing," he said. "What you told me on the beach. I didn't use your name."

Leah went still.

"She said—" He stopped. He started again, slower. "Hermione said a period's an injury. Biologically. Every month the lining builds up, and if nothing happens, the body breaks it down. Tears it loose. Little blood vessels break. That's the bleeding. It's damage. On purpose, on a schedule, but it's damage."

Leah didn't move.

"She thinks your healing's reading it as a wound," Potter said. "And fixing it. Before it can bleed. Every month. So you never see anything, because your body never lets it get that far." He held her eyes. "But the rest of it—the eggs, all the other parts—that's separate. There's no reason to think any of that stopped. She'd want to examine you, to be sure. She'd want blood work. But she said—"

He stopped.

"Say it," said Leah. Her voice had gone very small.

"She said ninety-nine percent," Potter said, "you're not sterile."

Leah sat in the passenger seat of a 1967 Chevrolet Impala in her mother's driveway in the rain and didn't breathe.

Her body went rigid. All of it, every muscle, from her scalp to her bare feet on the floor mat. Her hand came up off her neck and pressed flat over her mouth, hard, and stayed there.

*Ninety-nine.*

Seven months. Seven months of not counting. Of standing in the shower with her hand on her flat, hard, useless stomach. Of saying *genetic dead end* in a voice like a board so nobody could hear what was under it. Of watching Emily. Of watching every woman on the rez with a stroller and thinking *not me*, *not ever me*, *it shut the door, it locked the door, the wolf took that too*.

The wolf had left it alone.

All this time her body had just been—*fixing* it. Over and over. Every month. Too fast to see.

Her shoulders were shaking. She didn't know if she was laughing or crying. She didn't think it mattered. She pressed her hand harder over her mouth and the sound came out anyway, high and thin and awful, and she bent forward in the seat until her forehead almost touched the dashboard, and she shook.

He didn't touch her. She'd have hit him if he'd touched her. He seemed to know that. He sat there in his stupid white shirt with his paws on his knees and he waited, and he didn't say anything, and he didn't look away.

It took a long time.

When she could breathe again, she sat back up. Her face was wet. She wiped it with both hands, roughly, the way she'd wipe mud off. She didn't look at him.

"Ninety-nine," she said. Her voice was wrecked.

"That's what she said."

"Not a hundred."

"She's careful. She doesn't say a hundred about anything." A pause. "She said ninety-nine about Ron and her getting married, and they did."

Leah made a noise that was almost a laugh.

Then she went still again.

She turned her head, slowly, and looked at him.

"Last night," she said.

He didn't say anything.

"Last night. Against the door. I said *don't bother*. I said *I can't*. I *laughed* at you." Her eyes had gone wide. "And you said—"

"Belt and braces," said Potter.

"*Belt and braces*." She stared at him. "If you hadn't—if you'd just listened to me, if you'd just gone *okay, sure*, like any other guy on earth would've—"

"I didn't."

"Three *hours*, Potter. Four times. And all of it—" She put both hands flat over her stomach, over the place where the curve had been at midnight, and pressed. "Oh my God. *Oh my God.*"

"Ja."

"You son of a *bitch*." It came out cracked. "You were *right*."

"I was being careful," he said. "Being right was an accident."

She laughed. It came out of her in a burst, wet and ragged, and she put her face in her hands and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes again, and when she looked up he was watching her with his ears halfway up and something at the corner of his muzzle that wasn't quite a grin.

"It runs out at twenty past eight, by the way," he said. "The charm. Hermione was very clear."

Leah looked at him.

"We're having dinner," she said, "with my *mother*."

"That's what I told her."

"What did she say?"

"*Of course you are*."

Leah snorted. She leaned her head back against the seat and closed her eyes and breathed. In. Out. The rain. The TV. Cinnamon, from the back seat. Him.

She thought about seven months of not counting. About a body that hadn't locked any doors at all.

About a mark on her neck that might, someday, let her find him in a snowstorm with her eyes shut. Or might be nothing. Or might, slowly, like the seasons, start trying to tell her what to want.

She opened her eyes.

"Okay," she said. "Here's the deal."

His ears came up.

"I picked you," Leah said. "Last night. Before the bite. You've got a timeline, and I've got a timeline, and that's what happened. I know what I wanted before any of this. So if this thing on my neck ever starts—*pushing*, if it ever starts telling me what to want, I'm going to know. Because I'll remember what it felt like when it was just me." She held his eyes. "And I'm going to keep picking. Every day. On purpose. Or not. My call. Every day."

He looked at her for a long time.

"Okay," Potter said.

"And if it starts pushing *you*—"

"Then I'll tell you."

"You'll tell me."

"I'll tell you," he said. "That one, too."

She looked at him. His ears. His nose. His eyes. She still couldn't tell. She thought maybe she was never going to be able to tell, with him, not completely.

She thought about the back of his right hand, under all that fur. *I must not tell lies.*

"Okay," Leah said.

The porch light came on.

They both turned their heads. The front door of the house had opened, and her mother was standing in it, in her good blouse with an apron over it and a dish towel in one hand, looking out at the car through the rain.

"You've been sitting in my driveway," Sue Clearwater called, "for nineteen minutes. The roast is drying out."

Leah looked at the clock on the dash. It didn't work; it hadn't worked since about 1974. She looked at Potter instead.

"She counted," he said.

"She always counts."

"Runs in the family, then."

Leah wiped her face one more time with the heel of her hand. She flipped down the sun visor and looked in the little mirror on the back of it. Her eyes were red. Her face was blotchy. The marks on her neck were exactly the same as they'd been at seven that morning, red and clean and humming, very faintly, under her skin.

She didn't touch them. She flipped the visor back up.

Potter had twisted around and gotten the paper bag off the back seat. He was holding it in both paws, carefully, like it was something that might go off.

"What is that?" Leah said.

"Melktert."

"What?"

"Milk tart. South African." His ear—the one sticking out sideways—twitched. "It's not carnations."

"No," Leah said. "It's not."

She opened the door. The hinge screamed. On the porch, her mother didn't even flinch.

Leah got out into the rain, barefoot, and waited by the front of the car while he unfolded himself from the driver's side, all six-foot-seven of him in a pressed white shirt with a tail coming out the back, holding a milk tart wrapped in a stolen motel towel like a man carrying a bomb.

She looked at him over the roof of the car. Then she came around and walked beside him up the driveway.

Not too close. A foot of wet air between them, same as the log.

At the bottom of the porch steps, her mother looked at the bag.

"That's not carnations," said Sue Clearwater.

"No, ma'am," said Potter. "Milk tart. I made it."

Sue looked at him for a long, long moment. Then she looked at Leah. At her red eyes. At her wet face. At the marks on her neck.

Leah looked back at her mother and didn't look away.

"Come in," Sue said finally, and stepped aside. "Wipe your feet."

He wiped his footpaws. Both of them, carefully, one after the other on the mat, claws rasping on the coir, while Leah stood behind him and watched her mother watch him do it.

Inside, the TV was still blaring. Somebody had just scored. Seth was sitting on the couch with his back very straight, pretending he hadn't been looking at the window, and when the fed ducked under the doorframe into the living room, white shirt, ear sticking out sideways, Seth's mouth fell open.

"Hey," said Potter. "You must be Seth. Sorry about your pie."

Seth looked at him. Then at Leah. Then back.

"You owe me half a pie," Seth said.

"I brought a tart."

"Is it good?"

"Ja," said Potter. "It's good."

Seth thought about that. Then he picked up the remote, and turned the TV off, and the silence came down over the little living room like snow.

In the kitchen, the table was set. Four blue plates around a vase of crushed carnations with a sunflower hanging over the top. Four chairs pulled out. One chair at the end pushed in square to the table, empty, with no plate in front of it.

Leah looked at that chair for a second. Then she looked at the plate that had been moved, around the corner, next to hers.

Her throat went tight one more time. She let it.

She sat down. Potter sat down next to her, in a chair that creaked under him, with his tail curled around the leg of it and his paws folded on the table like a kid in church. Her mother set the melktert on the counter, and lifted the towel, and looked at it, and didn't say anything at all.

Then she brought the roast to the table and sat down across from him, and folded her hands, and looked at him over the top of the carnations.

"So, Mr. Potter," said Sue Clearwater. "Tell me about yourself."

Under the table, Leah's hand found the marks on her neck, and pressed, and felt them hum.

She took her hand away. She picked up her fork.

*Every day*, she thought. *On purpose.*

"Go on, Potter," she said. "Tell her."
