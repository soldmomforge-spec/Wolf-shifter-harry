# Chapter Two: The Tune

*Forks and La Push, Washington — Friday, October 6, into Saturday, October 7, 2006*

---

## I. Leah

Twenty minutes came and went.

Leah knew because there was a clock radio on the nightstand between the two beds, a fake-wood box with red numbers, and it had said 9:14 when the paper bird squeezed out under the door into the rain. It said 9:36 now. Nothing about her situation had changed except that she'd started watching the clock.

"Potter."

"Mm."

"It's been twenty minutes."

"Ja," he said, behind her, into her hair. A pause. "Give or take."

"Which one is it doing right now? Giving, or taking?"

He shifted. Carefully. A couple of inches, testing, the way you'd test a door you were pretty sure was locked. She felt it, all the way through, and she heard her own breath catch, and he felt her feel it, and they both went very still.

"Taking," he said, after a second. "Apparently."

"Great."

"It'd go a lot quicker," he said, in the voice of a man being extremely reasonable under difficult circumstances, "if you'd quit doing that."

"Doing what?"

"You know what."

She did know what. She'd been doing it on and off since about 9:20, partly without meaning to and partly because every time she did it, the big body curled around her back went rigid and he made a noise into the back of her neck like somebody had stepped on his tail.

She did it again. On purpose this time.

He made the noise.

"*Clearwater.*"

"Sorry," Leah said. "Reflex."

"Reflex, my tail."

She laughed. She couldn't help it; it came up out of her belly and shook her whole body, and that made things worse—or better, depending on who you asked—and his arm tightened across her waist and he growled something in Afrikaans that she was pretty sure was a prayer, or a curse, or both.

"Okay," she said, when she could talk. "Okay. I'll stop."

"Thank you."

She didn't stop. She mostly stopped. They lay there in the dark and listened to the rain hit the roof of the Impala outside, and the heater rattle under the window, and somewhere down the row of rooms a TV playing a late movie with a lot of gunfire in it. The lighthouse print hung crooked over the other bed where the headboard had knocked it. The whole room smelled like the two of them. She'd stopped being able to tell where his smell stopped and hers started about an hour ago.

"So," Leah said to the dark. "South Africa."

"South Africa."

"That's all I get?"

"You gave me one word for Uley."

"That's different."

"How?"

"Because it's mine," Leah said.

He thought about that. She felt him do it; his chest moved against her back in a long, slow breath.

"Grew up somewhere hot," he said finally. "Left. Came here. Somebody at MACUSA needed a bloke who could talk to things with teeth without anyone getting eaten, and I was cheap." His ears moved. She felt one brush the top of her head. "That's the long version."

"That's the *long* version?"

"That's all of it you're getting tonight."

She snorted. She thought about pushing and didn't. It wasn't like she'd been handing anything out either.

Then—maybe because it was dark, and she couldn't see his face, and he couldn't see hers, and she'd been holding it in her teeth for two years—she said, "Sam was my boyfriend."

He didn't say anything. He waited.

"From when I was, like, sixteen. Everybody figured we'd get married. *I* figured we'd get married." Her voice had gone flat. She let it. "Then he disappeared for two weeks. No call, nothing. Came back different. Bigger. Wouldn't tell me where he'd been. And then my cousin Emily came down from the Makah rez to visit, and he looked at her one time, and that was it."

"Imprinting," Potter said quietly.

"You know about that?"

"Black was looking at the kid last night like she hung the moon. I'm not blind."

"Yeah, well." Leah stared at the dark wall. "That's what it's like. One look. Boom. Doesn't matter who you were with. Doesn't matter who you loved. Some old magic reaches down out of the sky and grabs you by the throat and says *her*, and that's it. You're done." She swallowed. "He says he's sorry. He means it. That's the worst part. He means it and it doesn't change anything, and he's marrying her, and I get to have both of them in my head every time I phase because the universe thinks it's funny."

"Not anymore," said Potter. "You switched packs."

"Not anymore," she agreed. "Now I just get Jacob. Mooning over a baby."

"Upgrade."

"It's really not."

He laughed, low, the rumble going through her back. She waited for him to say something else—*that's rough*, or *I'm sorry*, or *you deserve better*, any of the things people said when you told them stuff like that and they wanted to look like they cared. He lay there with his arm over her waist and his breath going slow and even in her hair, and said none of them, and after a while she realized he wasn't going to, and that he'd heard all of it anyway.

She didn't know what to do with that. She put it with the list.

At 10:02, the clock radio said, and she watched the 1 turn into a 0 and the 0 turn into a 2.

At 10:09 she did the thing again. She wasn't sure if she meant to.

"Leah," he said, against her neck, and his voice had gone all the way down into the growl.

"Sorry."

"You're not."

"No," she admitted. "Not really."

He moved.

Just a little, at first. Testing, again. Then less like testing. Then not like testing at all. And somewhere around the time the clock said 10:15, lying on their sides in a motel bed in Forks with the rain coming down and a stranger's arm locked across her hips, Leah Clearwater stopped watching the clock.

---

When she found it again, it said 10:51.

She was face-down in the pillow. She didn't remember getting there. Her hair was stuck to her forehead and her throat felt raw and her whole body was humming like a power line in the rain, and somewhere behind and above her Potter was breathing like he'd run here from Seattle.

"That's two," she said into the pillow.

"Two?"

"Two. Since the first one." She turned her head enough to get her mouth free. "I'm keeping count."

"Of course you are."

"Somebody has to."

He laughed. It came out ragged. "You're going to put this on the fridge, aren't you."

"Next to your patrol review."

They were still tied. Of course they were still tied. She'd figured that out somewhere in the middle of things and had decided, with what was left of her brain at the time, not to care.

The guy in the next room had started banging on the wall again around half past ten. He'd stopped around quarter to eleven. Leah figured he'd either given up or moved to the minivan.

---

The third one was at 11:38.

She knew because her eyes were open this time and pointed straight at the clock radio, and she watched the numbers blur and swim and come back. This time his teeth found the side of her neck instead of the back of her shoulder—right below her ear, where the muscle ran down into the collarbone—and closed, and held. Careful and not careful. Wolf-careful. The pressure of it was enormous and very precise, and every one of those teeth was a separate, bright point, and he held on while she came apart, and he made a sound into her skin that she felt in the soles of her feet.

When he let go, she lay there and felt the spots where his teeth had been throb in time with her heart.

They'd heal. Everything healed. The claw marks on her hips had been gone since before ten. The bite on her shoulder from earlier—she reached back and felt for it—was gone, too, smooth skin, like nothing had ever happened.

She didn't reach for her neck. She was too tired to lift her arm that high.

"That's three," she whispered.

"You're going to kill me," said Potter.

"You started it."

"*You* started it. You started it in the Cullens' living room. In your *head*."

"And you finished it in Edward's," Leah said. "So."

He put his forehead down between her shoulder blades and laughed until the bed shook.

---

At 12:20, she put her hand on her stomach and stopped laughing.

She'd been lying there in a sort of warm, wrecked, boneless haze, half-asleep, the heater rattling and the rain slowing to a drizzle outside, and she'd put her hand on her stomach for no reason at all, the way you do, and her hand had found a curve.

A firm, round, unmistakable curve, low down under her navel. Where her stomach had been flat for as long as she could remember. She'd been all hard muscle since she phased, and now under her palm there was a tight little swell that pushed back against her hand.

"Oh my God," Leah said.

"What?" His head came up off her back. "What's wrong?"

"Look at this. *Look* at this." She dragged his paw down from her waist and put it there, on the curve, under her own hand. "I look four months gone."

He was quiet for a second. She felt his paw flex, very gently, against her skin. The pads were rough and hot.

"Ah," he said.

"*Ah?*"

"Wolf," he said. A little sheepish. Not sheepish enough.

"Don't you *dare* sound proud."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"You *are*. I can hear it. Your ears are doing a thing."

"My ears are behind you. You can't see my ears."

"I can *feel* your ears, Potter, they're in my *hair*—"

He laughed, and she started to laugh too, and then she stopped.

She lay there in the dark with her hand on her stomach and his paw under it and looked at the wall.

*Four months gone.* She'd said it like a joke. It had come out like a joke. Except that she'd spent the whole spring and the whole summer counting days, and then counting weeks, and then she'd stopped counting at all, because there wasn't anything to count anymore. Since the day she phased, her body had shut that door and locked it. No cycle. No nothing. A genetic dead end with nice legs. She'd told Jacob once, hunting, a few weeks back, in a voice she'd made as flat as a board so he couldn't hear what was under it. She'd never told anybody else.

And now here she was, round as a—

She swallowed. Her throat was tight.

Behind her, Potter had gone quiet too.

He didn't ask. She waited for him to ask, braced for it—*what's wrong*, *you okay*, *did I hurt you*—and he didn't. He'd felt her go still. She knew he had. He lay there with his paw under her hand on the curve of her stomach and he didn't take it away, and he didn't say anything, and he didn't make it into a thing.

After a while, his thumb—no. The big inner digit of his paw. After a while, it moved. Once. A slow stroke across her skin, side to side.

That was all.

She shut her eyes. "Shut up," she said, thickly.

"Didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"Mm," he said, which wasn't a yes or a no.

The clock said 12:31. Then 12:40. The rain had stopped. Water dripped off the eaves outside the window in a slow, uneven rhythm, *tap*, and then *tap-tap*, and somewhere a long way off to the north, so far that a human would never have heard it, a wolf howled. Once. High and short and sort of aggrieved.

"Seth," said Leah, eyes still closed. "On the ridge."

"Is he okay?"

"He's early. His shift's not till two." She almost smiled. "He's bored and he's mad at me. That's his *I'm bored and I'm mad at Leah* howl."

"You can tell?"

"I've known him since he was born."

Potter's ear twitched against the back of her head. Then he said, slow and practical, "We're not getting untied any time soon."

"No kidding."

"Might as well sleep."

"Like *this?*"

"You got a better idea?"

She thought about it. She didn't. Every muscle in her body had turned to wet sand, and the bed had soaked up his heat, and so had she, and she'd been awake since five that morning running the ridge line. She was so tired she could feel her heartbeat in her eyelids.

"Fine," she said.

Something moved behind her. Something long and heavy that slid over her hip, and across her thighs, and down over her legs, and settled there. Thick. Soft on top, coarser underneath. Hot as the rest of him.

His tail. He'd laid his tail over her like a blanket.

She hadn't been cold since the day she phased. She hadn't needed a blanket in seven months; she slept on top of the sheets with the window open in March and still woke up sweating. She didn't need this. She lay there anyway and felt the weight of it settle over her legs, and something in her chest went quiet and heavy, like a stone sinking to the bottom of a pond.

"Night, Clearwater," Potter said into her hair.

"Night, Potter."

The clock said 12:52. She didn't see it say anything else.

## II. Harry

Harry woke at 6:48 to gray light and a smell like the inside of a den at the end of a long winter.

He lay still and let it come. Old habit. Wake up, don't move, find out where you are before anything finds out you're awake. Motel ceiling, water-stained in one corner. The rattle of the heater. Rain again outside, soft, the kind that would go on all day. The clock radio between the beds, red numbers. And the smell, which was everywhere, which had soaked into the carpet and the bedspread and the curtains and the cheap wood paneling and the fur along his own belly: the two of them. Wolf and wolf and sweat and salt and sex, layered over and over, hours of it, so thick he could practically see it hanging in the air.

Any shifter within a hundred yards of this room would know exactly what had happened in it. Any shifter within a mile, if the wind was right.

He thought about the kid brother up on the ridge at two in the morning, and decided it was a good thing the wind had been out of the north.

Leah was asleep.

She lay face-down beside him with her head turned away, toward the window, her short black hair sticking up in about six directions and one arm hanging off the edge of the bed, the knuckles brushing the carpet. She was snoring. Not loud. A small, steady, whistling snore, like a kettle somebody had forgotten on a low flame. His tail was still lying across the backs of her legs where he'd put it. Sometime in the night they'd come apart; he didn't remember when. He'd been asleep. He'd slept harder than he'd slept in months.

He lay there and looked at her for a while.

There was a regulation about this. He was almost sure. Somewhere in the big blue binder the Department of Magical Security issued every new Auror and nobody ever read, there was a section on conduct during assessments, and he'd bet his car there was a paragraph in it about not sleeping with anybody you might later be required to Obliviate.

He hadn't read the binder in four years. He'd used it to prop up the short leg of a desk in Chicago.

*Local contact acquired.* He thought about T reading that over a first coffee in New York, and what T's face would do. He found he didn't mind the thought nearly as much as he probably should.

He liked her.

That was the inconvenient part. He'd liked the look of her last night across the Cullens' living room, all elbows and attitude and bad haircut. He'd liked the smell of her, which was its own problem and one he'd decided not to examine too closely. But lying here in the gray light with her snoring into the pillow and the whole room reeking of her, the thing he kept coming back to was the dark. Her voice in it, flat as a board, telling him about Uley and her cousin and some old magic reaching down out of the sky. And the way she'd gone quiet after midnight, with her hand on her stomach, and stayed quiet, and he'd felt it and had no idea what it was, and had decided it wasn't his to dig up.

He didn't know what that one was about. He could guess. He wasn't going to.

*Have you been in my head?*

He hadn't. He wouldn't. He'd told her *probably not* when she asked if he'd tell her, because it was true—there were jobs where he'd go into a head and not say so afterward, and he'd done them, and he'd do them again. She'd heard the answer and filed it away somewhere behind those eyes, and he'd watched her do it, and he'd respected her for it more than for anything else she'd done all night.

Which was saying something.

Leah stopped snoring.

He felt her wake up the same way he had: all at once, completely, without moving. Her breathing changed. Her back went tight under his tail. For two or three seconds she lay there and took stock.

Then her nose wrinkled. He watched it happen in profile.

"Oh my God," she said into the pillow, hoarse. "It *reeks* in here."

"Morning."

"It smells like a—like a—" She lifted her head an inch, sniffed, and put it back down. "It smells like every wolf in La Push held an orgy in a gym sock."

"Two wolves."

"Felt like more."

She shifted. Started to roll over. Stopped.

Harry watched her face go through three distinct expressions in about a second and a half.

"Potter," she said.

"Ja."

"The bed's wet."

He considered his options. There weren't many good ones.

"Ja," he said.

"Why," said Leah, very slowly, "is the bed wet."

"You know why."

She stared at him. She had a crease from the pillowcase down one cheek. Her eyes were still puffy with sleep and there was murder in them.

"I'm going to set this motel on fire," she said.

"Hang on." He lifted a forepaw a few inches off the mattress, and the sheet under her went clean. All of it, the sheet and the mattress pad and the brown-and-orange bedspread twisted around their legs: one second damp and rumpled and smelling of exactly what it smelled of, the next crisp and dry and faintly lemony, like it had just come off a hotel laundry line. The lighthouse print over the other bed straightened itself on its nail with a small *click*.

Leah looked at the sheet. She looked at him.

"You could've done that last night," she said.

"Was busy."

"You could've done that at *one in the morning*."

"Was asleep."

She made a noise of pure disgust and rolled out of the bed.

Her legs nearly went out from under her. She caught herself on the nightstand, swore, and stood there with one hand braced on the clock radio, wobbling slightly, naked and furious and covered in his scent from her hair to her heels. Then she glanced down at herself. Just a glance. Quick. At her stomach.

It was flat again. He could see that from the bed. Whatever had been there at midnight had gone down sometime in the night, and her stomach was just a stomach, hard and flat and brown.

Her face did something brief and complicated, and closed.

Harry looked at the water stain on the ceiling and gave it his full attention.

"I'm showering first," Leah said.

"Go for it."

"I'm using all the hot water."

"I run hot. I'll live."

She snatched the nearest thing off the floor—his black T-shirt, he noticed, and not her tank top—and stalked into the bathroom, still wobbling, and shut the door. The fan came on with a groan. The light under the door flicked on, yellow.

Harry lay there for a minute longer.

Then he sat up, swung his footpaws down onto the carpet, and stretched until his spine popped in four places. His shoulders ached. His hips ached. There had been teeth marks in his left shoulder, under the fur, from when she'd bitten him for laughing at her fight with his jeans buttons, and he felt for them with a claw and found them already gone. A long scratch down his ribs, gone. Everything was gone. He healed fast. So did she.

The flannel was on the floor by the door. He picked it up and turned it over. One button missing, third from the top. He glanced at the heater, and the button rolled out from under it, hopped across the carpet, and jumped into his open paw. He held it against the flannel where the thread had pulled through, and the threads knotted themselves back together, quick and neat. He shook out the shirt and hung it on the back of the chair.

The fan in the bathroom groaned on. The shower hadn't started.

He frowned at the door.

"Potter," Leah said, from the other side of it. Her voice had changed. It had gone very flat and very quiet, the same voice she'd used in the dark when she'd told him about her cousin. "Get in here."

## III. Leah

The bathroom was the size of a phone booth. Fake marble counter, a sink with a rust stain under the faucet, a shower stall with a cracked plastic door, and a mirror over the sink that ran most of the width of the wall under a fluorescent tube that buzzed and flickered and made everybody look like they'd been dead for a week.

Leah had flicked it on, thrown his T-shirt over the towel bar, and turned to the mirror to see how bad her hair was.

Her hair was bad. Her hair was very bad.

Then she saw her neck.

Left side. Right below her ear, where the long muscle ran down from behind her jaw into the hollow of her collarbone. Two curved rows of marks, one above the other, facing each other like a pair of brackets. Four deep ones at the corners—the big teeth, the canines, each one a neat dark puncture the size of a pencil eraser—and a line of smaller dents strung between them where the front teeth had pressed. The skin around them was pink and a little raised, like a fresh sunburn. No blood. No scab. No bruising. Just the marks, clean-edged and perfectly clear, as if he'd bitten her ten minutes ago.

He'd bitten her at 11:38.

It was 6:55.

Leah stood in front of the mirror and didn't move.

She'd healed a broken hand in an afternoon once, back in June, when she'd punched a tree on the ridge after a fight with Sam in her head. She'd watched a gash from a vampire's nails close up on her own forearm in under a minute. The claw marks on her hips from last night had been gone before ten o'clock; she twisted now and looked, and the skin there was smooth and brown and completely unmarked. She reached back and felt for the place on her shoulder where he'd bitten her the first time. Nothing. Nothing at all.

She turned back to the mirror. She lifted her hand and touched the marks on her neck, very lightly, with two fingers.

They didn't hurt. They felt like a bruise you'd forgotten about until you pressed on it—a dull, faint ache, way down. And under that, maybe, something else. Something she couldn't name. A sort of hum, like standing too close to a power line.

Or maybe she was imagining it. Maybe she was standing in a motel bathroom at seven in the morning freaking out about a hickey.

She knew what a hickey looked like. This was teeth.

"Potter," she said. She heard her own voice come out flat and quiet. "Get in here."

The door opened. He had to duck, and turn sideways, and even then his shoulders brushed both sides of the frame. The bathroom had not been built for him. The bathroom had barely been built for her. He stood behind her with his head bent under the ceiling and his ears folded flat against the tile and looked at her in the mirror, and she watched his eyes go to her face and then to her neck and then stop.

His ears, which were already back, went further back.

"Huh," he said.

"*Huh?*"

He leaned down. He didn't touch. He brought his muzzle close to the side of her neck, an inch away, and breathed in, slow and deep, the way she'd seen him do at the Cullens' door. She felt the air move across the marks. Goose bumps went up her arms.

He straightened up. In the mirror, his face was doing the thing it had done in the living room last night when Edward said *I can't hear the driver*: going still and careful and closed.

"Huh what," Leah said.

"Huh, I don't know."

She turned around. In the tiny room, that put her face about level with his collarbone. She had to tip her head back to look at him.

"What do you mean, you don't know?"

"I mean I don't know." He said it plainly. "You heal everything else?"

"Everything."

"Me too. That should be gone. It's been—" he glanced at the clock radio through the open door "—seven hours."

"I *know* how long it's been."

"Does it hurt?"

"No. Kind of. Like a bruise. Barely." She hesitated. She didn't mention the hum. "Did you do something?"

"What?"

"A magic thing. Like the peppermint thing. Last night. Did you do something to me?"

"No," Potter said.

She watched his face. She'd been watching faces her whole life—her dad's when he was lying about the doctor, Sam's when he was lying about everything, Jacob's, Seth's, every wolf in two packs. She watched his ears and his nose and his eyes. His ears stayed back. His nose didn't twitch. His eyes stayed on hers.

She couldn't tell. That was the thing that scared her, a little. She looked straight at him and she honestly couldn't tell.

"I didn't do anything on purpose," he said. "I'll give you that much. I don't know if I did anything *not* on purpose. That's the honest answer, and it's a rubbish one, and I'm sorry."

"So find out."

"I will."

"How?"

"Ask somebody who knows more than me."

"Who?"

He just looked at her.

"*Not sharing*," Leah said. "Yeah. That's getting real old, Potter."

"Ja," he said. "I know."

She looked at the marks in the mirror one more time, over her own shoulder. Two neat rows. The upper arch and the lower one, facing each other.

Then she turned on the shower, as hot as it would go, and got in, and pulled the cracked plastic door shut in his face.

---

The water was hot for about four minutes. Then it was lukewarm. Then it was nothing she'd call hot, but she didn't care; she didn't feel cold anymore anyway.

She scrubbed. She used the whole tiny bar of motel soap, the kind that came wrapped in paper and smelled like fake cucumber, and then she used the shampoo in the little bottle on her body because the soap was gone. She scrubbed her arms and her stomach and her legs and the backs of her knees. She scrubbed her neck around the marks, carefully, and the marks stayed exactly where they were.

When she got out and stood dripping on the bath mat and smelled herself, she smelled like fake cucumber.

And under the cucumber, him.

It was in her skin. It was in her hair. Soap could only reach the surface, and this was down in her, the way woodsmoke gets into a jacket after a whole night by a fire. Pine and cold stone and wolf, and her own scent all tangled up in it, and something else on top of both that was just—*them*. The two of them, together. The smell of what they'd done.

Every wolf in La Push was going to know. Sam was going to know the second she got out of the car.

Leah looked at herself in the fogged mirror and thought about that.

Then she thought: *let him*.

---

Potter's turn in the shower went about the way she'd expected.

She sat on the end of the clean bed in a motel towel and listened to him try. There was a thump—his head on the showerhead. Then a second thump—his elbow on the door. Then a lot of muttering in Afrikaans, and the sound of seven feet of wolf trying to fold itself in half under a stream of water designed for an average-sized dentist from Tacoma, and then a long, miserable silence that she figured meant he'd given up and was just sort of crouching in there letting it rain on him.

When he came out, he was soaked to the skin. His fur was plastered flat, which made him look about a third smaller and a lot bonier and extremely sorry for himself. Water poured off his ears. His tail hung behind him like a wet rope.

Leah laughed so hard she had to lie down.

"Glad I'm entertaining," Potter said.

"You look like a drowned rat. You look like a *drowned* six-foot-seven *rat*—"

The water lifted off him, the same way it had at the Cullens'. It came out of his fur in a fine mist and gathered in front of him and floated off into the sink. His fur popped up dry and fluffed out in every direction. He looked like a black dandelion with a bad attitude.

Leah laughed harder.

"Are you done?" he said.

"Almost."

He waited.

"Okay," she said, wiping her eyes. "Done."

She sat up and sniffed the air between them. He'd used the same shampoo she had. He smelled like fake cucumber too. And under the cucumber, exactly the same as her: the two of them. If anything, it was worse on him. Wet fur held a smell like nothing else on earth.

"Soap got the top layer," he said, reading her face.

"Soap got *nothing*."

"Ja. That too."

She got dressed. Her cutoffs were fine, more or less. Her tank top was on the floor by the bed where it had landed last night, and when she picked it up and held it to her face, it smelled like a crime scene. She looked at it for a long moment.

Then she looked at the black T-shirt hanging over the towel bar in the bathroom.

She put on the T-shirt.

It came down to the middle of her thighs. The sleeves came past her elbows. The collar slid off one shoulder. She took a handful of the hem at her hip and tied it in a knot so it didn't hang like a nightgown, and she looked at herself in the long mirror on the closet door: bare legs, bare feet, cutoffs mostly hidden under the shirt, short hair still damp and sticking up, and the neckline of the T-shirt sitting just low enough on the left side to show the top of the marks.

She could smell the shirt from where she was standing. It smelled like him, straight through, no cucumber at all.

Potter was looking at her from the other side of the room. He'd gotten his jeans back on. He was holding his flannel in one paw and hadn't put it on yet. His ears had gone up.

"You sure?" he said.

"About what?"

He tipped his head at the shirt. At the marks. At all of it.

"They're going to smell you a mile off," he said. "Every one of them."

"Yeah," said Leah. "I know."

He looked at her a second longer. Then he nodded, slowly, and put on the flannel. She noticed the button was back.

---

There was a phone on the nightstand. Beige, plastic, with a sticker that said DIAL 9 FOR AN OUTSIDE LINE. Leah sat on the edge of the bed and looked at it for a while.

Then she picked it up and dialed 9 and then the number of the house she'd grown up in.

Her mother answered on the first ring.

"Hello."

"Mom."

A pause on the line. Not long. Just long enough. "Leah."

"Hi."

"Are you all right?"

"I'm fine."

"You're sure."

"I'm *fine*, Mom."

Another pause. Leah could see her, exactly: standing in the kitchen in her robe with her hair in a braid, one hand on the counter, the other holding the phone, the coffee maker going behind her. Sue Clearwater did not pace. Sue Clearwater stood very still and let other people do the pacing.

"Billy called me at eleven last night," her mother said. "Jacob called him. Jacob told him a man came to the Cullens' house. From the government. Some kind of government." A breath. "That he threatened to do something to Charlie. To Charlie's memory."

"He didn't—" Leah stopped. She glanced at Potter. He was sitting in the chair by the window, pulling the cellophane off a pack of cinnamon gum he'd found in his duffel, and he was very obviously not listening, and his ears were very obviously pointed straight at her. "It's complicated. He said it'd only happen if somebody screws up again."

"And Jacob said," her mother went on, in exactly the same voice, "that you left with him."

Leah closed her eyes.

"Seth came home at six this morning," Sue said. "He went straight to his room. He didn't eat. He wouldn't look at me." A pause. "Seth always eats."

"He's fifteen. He's being dramatic."

"Leah."

"*Mom*."

The line was quiet. Leah listened to the coffee maker gurgle on the other end, twenty minutes away, in the kitchen where her dad used to sit with the paper on Saturday mornings and read her the funnies in a dumb voice even after she was way too old for it.

"The council is meeting at Sam's," her mother said. "At ten. Bring him."

"Mom—"

"Ten o'clock, Leah."

Click.

Leah sat there holding the phone. After a second the dial tone came on, loud and flat. She put the receiver back in the cradle very carefully, like it might go off.

"Your mum sounds nice," said Potter.

"Shut up."

"Seriously. Very calm."

"*Shut up*, Potter."

He put a piece of gum in his mouth and chewed it and looked out the window at the rain. "Ten o'clock," he said. "That gives us time for flowers."

---

The Thriftway in Forks opened at seven on Saturdays. At 8:40 there were maybe a dozen cars in the lot, and inside, under the bright, flat lights, a handful of people pushing carts past pyramids of canned soup and a display of Halloween candy that had gone up three weeks too early.

Leah walked in barefoot in a man's T-shirt with her hair still damp, smelling like a wolf den with a cucumber in it. Potter walked in behind her, ducking under the sign over the door that said THANK YOU FOR SHOPPING.

Nobody looked at him twice. She watched it happen again: eyes going over him and sliding off, seeing a big guy in a leather jacket and a flannel shirt, nothing special, nothing worth a second look. A man in a Seahawks cap reached past him for a carton of eggs and said "Excuse me" to his elbow.

Plenty of people looked at her.

Humans couldn't smell for crap, so it was the rest of it. She was barefoot in a grocery store in October in a T-shirt that came down to her knees, and Forks was the kind of town where everybody knew everybody, and half of them knew she was Harry Clearwater's girl from down on the rez. She saw a woman she recognized from the bank look at her feet, and then at the shirt, and then at her face, and then very quickly at a display of pickles.

Leah stared her down until she moved on.

Potter came back from the bakery case with a whole apple pie in a plastic clamshell, a box of a dozen glazed donuts, and two of the biggest coffees the Thriftway sold. He handed her one of the coffees without a word. Then he went over to the bucket of flowers by the registers and stood in front of it for a long time with his head tilted, like he was doing a math problem.

He picked one. He brought it back.

It was a grocery-store bouquet wrapped in crinkly cellophane with a little packet of plant food rubber-banded to the stems. Pink and white carnations, some purple filler stuff, and one big sunflower sticking up out of the middle like it had wandered in from a different bouquet.

Leah looked at it.

"Carnations," she said. "Seriously."

"Only ones they had."

"*Only ones they*—there's a whole bucket. There's *roses* in that bucket, Potter."

"Roses are for when you've done something wrong." He held the bouquet up and inspected it critically. "These are for *pleased to meet you*."

"You're bringing my mother carnations the morning after you—"

She stopped. The girl at the register, a junior from Forks High with braces and a name tag that said JENNA, was looking at them with her mouth slightly open.

Leah closed her own mouth.

Potter put the pie and the donuts and the flowers on the belt, and paid with a twenty and a ten, and said "Ta" to Jenna, who said "You're welcome, sir" to a point about a foot to the left of his actual face. Then he picked up the bag and the bouquet and walked out.

Leah followed him, barefoot, holding her coffee, with her face on fire.

"I hate you," she said in the parking lot.

"You're going to love the pie."

"I *hate* you."

"Ja," Potter agreed, and unlocked the car.

---

La Push Road went west out of Forks and then the town fell away behind them and there was nothing but trees. Big ones, cedar and spruce and hemlock, dark and dripping, crowding right up to the shoulders of the road. Then the trees opened up for a while onto the flat gold grass of the prairie and the little airstrip, and then closed in again, and the road started its long, winding run down toward the coast.

Leah had picked the tape. AC/DC, *Back in Black*. She figured it fit. Potter had opened his mouth when she put it in, and she'd looked at him, and he'd closed it again.

She ate three donuts. He ate five, one-pawed, steering with the other, and somehow didn't get glaze on the wheel.

"So what's the plan?" she said, when the rain had picked up again and the wipers were going.

"Same thing I told the Cullens. Plus one."

"Plus what?"

"Who's in charge."

She looked at him. He was watching the road.

"Sam's in charge," she said.

"Of the pack."

"Of the—" She stopped. She thought about what he'd said in the Cullens' living room. *It's on the letterhead, bud.* "You're going to tell Sam Uley he's not in charge. On his own land. In front of the council."

"I'm going to tell him who he answers to," Potter said. "Same as I told Black. Same as I'd tell anybody."

Leah sat back in the seat and looked out the windshield at the road coming at them through the rain.

She'd wanted this last night. She'd wanted it so bad she could taste it—somebody walking up to Sam's door and telling him he wasn't the biggest thing in the world. She still wanted it. She could feel it in her stomach, sharp and hungry and mean.

But La Push was hers too. Her mom's house. Her dad's grave in the cemetery on the hill. The beach she'd learned to swim at. The council her family had sat on for generations. And this guy, this fed she'd known for fourteen hours, was about to drive onto it with a pie on the back seat and tell everyone she'd ever known that they danced to somebody else's music.

She didn't know how she felt about that. She didn't like not knowing.

"Paul's got a temper," she said, after a while.

"Which one's Paul?"

"You'll know."

The road came out of the trees. Below them, gray under the rain, was the long curve of First Beach, the big black rock of James Island standing off the mouth of the river, the waves rolling in white and slow. The village spread along the shore: little houses, the school, the store, the marina with its boats bobbing at the docks. Smoke from a few chimneys. Leah knew every roof.

"Left up here," she said. "Past the store. Then it's the one at the end, by the trees."

He turned left. Past the store. Down a gravel road with potholes full of rainwater, toward a small house at the edge of the forest that had been gray, once, a long time ago, with a weathered blue door and one narrow window beside it.

The window box under the window was still full of marigolds. Orange and yellow, a little ragged at the edges now, at the tail end of the season. Emily kept them going longer than anyone else on the rez could.

The yard in front of the house was full of people.

Leah saw Billy Black's wheelchair on the little porch, Billy in it with a blanket over his knees. She saw her mother's car parked at an angle at the edge of the gravel. She saw Old Quil on a lawn chair under the eave, out of the rain. She saw Jared and Paul and the two younger boys, Collin and Brady, standing in a loose bunch by the trees, barefoot and shirtless in the drizzle, steam rising off their shoulders. She saw her mother standing by the porch steps with her arms folded.

And she saw Sam, coming down the steps, his head already turned toward the sound of the engine.

Potter pulled the Impala up at the edge of the yard and cut the engine. AC/DC died mid-chord.

Nobody in the yard moved.

Every one of the wolves had lifted his head. She watched it happen, in a ripple, one after another, as the wind came off the car and across the yard to them: Jared, then Brady, then Collin, then Paul. Noses going up. Then going still. Then going very, very wide.

Sam stopped on the bottom step.

"Showtime," said Potter quietly, and got out of the car.

## IV. Sam

Sam Uley had been up since four.

He hadn't meant to be. He'd lain down at midnight after Billy's call and stared at the ceiling and listened to Emily breathe beside him, slow and even, and at some point the ceiling had gone from black to gray, and he'd given up and gone out to stand on the porch in the rain. He'd stood there for a long time. Then Emily had come out in his sweatshirt with two mugs of coffee and stood next to him without saying anything, and at six she'd gone back inside and started baking. She baked when she was worried. By half past nine every flat surface in the kitchen was covered in muffins.

*A man from the government*, Billy had said on the phone. *Some kind of government. Jacob says he's a wolf. Says he walked in on two legs and talked like a person and picked Emmett Cullen up off the floor without touching him. Says he threatened to wipe Charlie Swan's memory clean as a slate if anybody so much as sneezed wrong.*

And then, after a pause that had gone on too long: *And Jacob says Leah left with him.*

Sam had held the phone and said nothing at all.

Now he stood on the bottom step of his own porch in the drizzle, with the council at his back and his pack in the yard, and listened to an engine coming down the gravel road from the store. Big. Old. A V8 that coughed on the downshift and growled on the potholes. It came out of the trees and he saw it: long and low and black, rain beading on the hood, a car from somebody's grandfather's time, with chrome along the sides and a grille like a mouth.

It pulled up at the edge of the yard. The engine died. Music died with it—guitars, cut off in the middle of a note.

Then the wind came off it.

Sam had smelled a lot of things since he first phased. He'd smelled the bloodsuckers' stink, sweet and burning, the first time, and nearly thrown up in the ferns. He'd smelled every one of his brothers come into his head and his pack. He'd smelled Emily's blood on his own claws, once, and that smell had never left him; he'd wake up some nights with it still in his nose.

He'd never smelled anything like this.

Wolf. That was the first thing, and it made his whole spine go stiff—a wolf, a male, big, strange, nothing he knew, pine and stone and cold earth and something under that older than the forest. And tangled all through it, wound into it so tight he couldn't tell where one ended and the other began, *Leah*.

Leah, and the stranger, and what they'd done. All of it. Hours of it, soaked into them both so deep it rolled off the car in a wave, heavy and hot and unmistakable.

Behind him in the yard, he heard Paul say, "Oh, *holy*—"

"Shut up," said Jared.

"Did you—are you *smelling* this? Is everybody smelling this? Leah *Clearwater* got—"

"*Paul.*"

Collin made a strangled sound. Brady, who was thirteen and had only been a wolf since the summer and had no idea how to hold his face yet, had gone bright red to the tips of his ears.

Sam didn't turn around. He stood on the step with his hand on the rail, and he made himself breathe, slow, in and out.

He'd known. Since Billy's call. He'd lain awake and known, the way you know a storm is coming before you can see the clouds. It didn't help. It went through him anyway. A hot, sick, ugly twist low in his gut, something he had no right to feel, not anymore, not for two years—and he felt it, and he hated himself for it, and he turned his head without meaning to and looked for Emily.

She was in the doorway behind him. Leaning on the frame, with a dish towel over her shoulder and flour on her forearms, and the three long scars running down the right side of her face from her hairline to her chin and pulling at the corner of her mouth. She was looking at him. She'd seen his face.

The pull came, the way it always did. The thread that ran from the middle of his chest to wherever she was, tugging gently, steadily, the way it had every second of every day since the first time he'd looked at her. The ugly thing in his gut stayed where it was. It got smaller. It got very small, beside her.

Emily didn't say anything. She held his eyes for a second, steady. Then she looked past him at the car.

The passenger door opened. The hinge made a sound like something dying.

Leah got out.

She was barefoot. She had a coffee in one hand. She was wearing cutoffs and a man's black T-shirt that came halfway to her knees, knotted at the hip, the collar slipping off one shoulder, and her short hair was still damp and sticking up at the back. She shut the car door with her hip. She turned toward the house. She looked straight at him across the wet yard, chin up, and her face was perfectly calm.

She'd worn his shirt.

She'd worn his shirt *here*.

Sam knew Leah Clearwater better than anyone on this earth except maybe her mother. He'd known her since she was a skinny kid running the beach in a too-big sweatshirt. He'd loved her for two years and hurt her worse than anybody had ever hurt her, and he'd felt every second of that hurt in her head for months afterward because the pack mind didn't let you look away. He knew every one of her faces. He knew what this one meant.

*You don't get to say anything*, this face said. *Not one word. Not ever again.*

And then his eyes went to her neck, because the collar had slid down, and he saw the marks.

Teeth. Two rows of them, upper and lower, on the left side below her ear, the big punctures at the corners. A wolf's bite, a clean one, the kind a male gives when he's—

When he's holding on.

They were pink. Fresh-looking. Not scabbed. Not healing.

Sam frowned.

Leah healed as fast as any of them. Faster. He'd watched her heal a split lip in the time it took to say her name. Whatever that bite was, it had happened last night—he could tell that much from the smell—and it should have been gone by midnight.

It wasn't gone.

He filed that away, the way he'd learned to file things away since he took on a pack: carefully, in a place he'd come back to.

Then the driver's door opened, and the stranger got out.

He had to unfold to do it. He came up out of the car in sections, a long black leg, a tail, a pair of shoulders, a head, and kept on coming, until he was standing in the gravel beside the car's long black roof and still had a head and a half on it. Six-seven, maybe more. Black fur. Ears up. A wolf's head on a man's body, built like a linebacker who'd spent his offseason chopping trees. He was wearing a leather jacket over a flannel shirt and jeans, and no shoes, and his feet—paws—were big and black and pressed flat into the wet gravel, and when he walked around the front of the car toward the house, Sam listened for the crunch of the stones under all that weight and didn't hear a thing.

He was carrying a grocery-store bouquet in one paw. Pink and white carnations in crinkly cellophane, with a big sunflower sticking up out of the middle.

In the other paw he had a pie.

Behind Sam, very quietly, Billy Black started to laugh.

The stranger walked across the yard. He didn't hurry. His eyes moved—Sam watched them—over the trees, over the boys by the tree line, over Old Quil in his lawn chair under the eave, over Billy in his wheelchair, over Sue by the steps, over Emily in the doorway. They stopped on Emily for a half-second. On her face. On the scars.

Sam's hackles went up so fast his skin prickled.

The green eyes moved on, unhurried, and found Sam, and stayed there.

He stopped at the bottom of the porch steps. Sam was standing on the first one, and it put them about eye to eye.

"Mr. Uley," the stranger said. The accent was strange—flat, clipped, nothing Sam had ever heard on the rez or in Forks or on TV. "Harry Potter. Magical Congress of the United States of America." A pause. "I promised I'd knock."

He looked around for a door. There wasn't one between them, only the open porch and the rain.

He reached past Sam with the paw that held the pie, and rapped three times on the porch post with the knuckle of one claw. *Knock. Knock. Knock.*

"Morning," he said.

Paul made a noise like a kettle about to go off.

Sam stood very still. He thought about a lot of things he could say. He chose the one that mattered.

"You're on Quileute land," he said.

"I am," the stranger agreed. "Thanks for having me."

He turned before Sam could answer, and looked at Sue.

Sue Clearwater had been standing at the bottom of the steps with her arms folded since half past nine. She hadn't moved when the car pulled up. She hadn't moved when the smell hit the yard. She hadn't moved when her daughter got out of the car in a stranger's shirt, and she didn't move now. She was a small woman, a head shorter than Leah, with a long black braid and Leah's chin, and she looked up at the wolf in front of her the way she'd look at a weather report she didn't care for.

"Mrs. Clearwater," the stranger said. He held out the flowers. "I told your daughter I'd bring you these."

Sue looked at the flowers.

She looked at the stranger.

She looked past him, across the yard, at Leah, standing by the car with her coffee and the marks on her neck and her chin in the air. Then back again.

"Carnations," Sue said.

"Only ones they had."

"The Thriftway," Sue said, without looking away from him, "has roses."

There was a short silence. Over by the car, Leah closed her eyes.

The stranger's ears went back about an inch. "Roses are for when you've done something wrong, ma'am," he said.

Sue Clearwater looked at him for a long, long moment with no expression at all.

"Then you'd better be right about that," she said.

She unfolded her arms and took the flowers.

"Thank you," she said. "We'll talk, Mr. Potter. After."

"Yes, ma'am."

The stranger turned to the doorway. To Emily. He held out the pie.

"Ma'am. Heard you're the one who feeds everybody." The ears came back up. "Figured you could use one you didn't have to make."

Emily looked at him for a second, at the pie in its clear plastic clamshell from the Thriftway, and then at Sam, and then—slowly—she reached out and took it.

"Thank you," Emily said. Her voice was soft, the way it always was, and careful. "Would you like a muffin?"

"Very much."

"*Emily*," said Sam.

"He brought a pie, Sam." She tucked it against her hip. "I'll get the muffins."

She went inside.

Sam turned back to the stranger and found the green eyes waiting for him, patient, a little amused, and completely unafraid. Sam had seen bloodsuckers look at him like that. Never anything else.

"So you're the one," Billy Black said from the porch, "who wants to wipe Charlie Swan's head."

The stranger turned toward the wheelchair. "*Want*'s a strong word, Mr. Black."

"My son says everybody's dad hates you."

"News travels."

"It does on the rez." Billy's mouth was doing the thing it did when he thought something was funny and didn't want you to know. His eyes were not laughing at all. "Sit down, Mr. Potter. You're giving me a crick in my neck."

"House is too small," Sam said. It came out harder than he meant. He didn't take it back. "We'll do this out here."

He came down the last step into the wet grass. The rain was a fine mist now, beading on everything, collecting in the stranger's black fur in little silver drops. Behind Sam, Old Quil got up out of his lawn chair, slowly, leaning on his cane, and came to the edge of the porch. Behind the stranger, Leah had come halfway across the yard and stopped, coffee in hand, a careful distance from everyone.

From the tree line, Paul said, under his breath but not far enough under, "He smells like he rolled in her."

The stranger's ears turned. Both of them. Toward the trees.

"Which one's Paul?" he said.

Nobody answered.

He didn't need them to. Sam watched him look at the boys by the trees, one face after another, and stop on Paul. Paul, with his chin out and his arms folded and the fine, hot tremor already starting in his shoulders.

"Right," the stranger said. "Thought so."

He turned back to Sam. He folded his paws behind his back, like a man waiting for a bus. He looked like he'd done this a hundred times in a hundred yards and had a pie waiting in the car for when it was over.

"Okay," he said. "I'll keep this short."

## V. Harry

Thirteen.

That was the number Harry couldn't get past. He'd clocked the two youngest by the tree line the second he got out of the car, and he'd hoped he was wrong, and he wasn't. The smaller one, the one who'd gone red to the ears, had the round face and skinny wrists of a kid who'd been sitting in a seventh-grade classroom last spring. He was standing barefoot in the rain with no shirt on and steam coming off his shoulders, and somewhere inside him was something the size of a horse with teeth.

Thirteen. In a village on a tourist beach.

He put that in front of everything else and kept it there.

"Here's where we are," he said. "Two packs. However many of you there are, I don't know and I'm not going to make you tell me. A coven of eight up the road, one of them a month old. A kid in that house who's something nobody's ever seen, growing faster than anybody can measure. A police chief who knows more than he should." He let his eyes go to the tree line. "And kids. Turning into wolves the size of pickup trucks, in a town where people come to surf in August."

Sam Uley stood in the wet grass in front of him with his arms at his sides and his face like a closed door. He was big—as big as Black, maybe bigger, and older, with a hard, heavy stillness to him that Harry recognized. It was the stillness of a man who'd been made responsible for something too young and had never once been allowed to set it down.

"We've kept this secret," Uley said, "for generations."

"Ja. You have. Good job. I mean that." Harry kept his paws behind his back. "Then last month, one of yours turned into a horse in front of the police chief."

"That was Jacob's choice. Not my pack."

"Your tribe. Your council." Harry glanced at the porch. Billy Black was watching him from the wheelchair with a face like a poker player's. "His dad's sitting right there."

Billy's face didn't change. Something behind it did.

"Here's what I need," Harry said. "Nobody phases where a No-Maj can see. Not on the beach, not on the road, not in the woods behind the school. Not *once*. Every summer that beach out there fills up with people with cameras, and every one of them's got a phone in their pocket that takes video now. There's a website where anybody can put up a clip of anything, and the whole world's watching it by Tuesday." He looked at the boys. "I'd like to keep your kids off it."

"YouTube," said the older of the two young ones, before he could stop himself. Collin, Harry thought. Brady was the red one.

"That one," Harry said. "Second thing. When a new kid goes furry, I want to know. A memo. A phone call. Smoke signal—" He stopped. He heard what he'd said a half-second after it was out. He watched Billy Black's eyebrow go up a quarter-inch on the porch, and Sue Clearwater's mouth go thin, and he took the hit. "Sorry. That was stupid. A phone call. Pacific Inn, room fourteen. Or ask Clearwater. I don't need names. I need numbers."

"And then what?" said Uley. "You come and do what to them?"

"Nothing. Unless they need it."

"And who decides that?"

"Me," Harry said.

The yard went quiet. The rain whispered on the grass.

"Charlie Swan," said Sue Clearwater from the bottom of the steps. She had the carnations in the crook of one arm, like she'd forgotten she was holding them. "What happens to Charlie?"

Harry turned to her.

He thought about the diner. About thirty seconds in a tired man's head, and what had been sitting at the front of it: coho in the Sol Duc, and whether Sue would come over Saturday for the game. He thought about saying it. Right here, in front of the council and her daughter's ex and half the young men of La Push. It'd land. It'd get him something.

He didn't say it.

"Nothing happens to Charlie," he said. "As long as nobody gives me a reason. He knows what he knows. He doesn't learn any more. Your people don't show him anything, the Cullens don't show him anything, and he goes on living his life and watching his games, and nobody touches his head."

"You touched it," Sue said.

"Ja. Thirty seconds. Surface."

"He's a good man."

"I know," Harry said. "I saw."

She looked at him a long moment. Something in her face didn't trust a word of it, and something else was filing it away to think about later. He'd seen that look before. Last night, on her daughter.

On the porch, the old man stirred.

Old Quil Ateara had been sitting under the eave since Harry arrived, wrapped in a red-and-black wool coat, both hands folded on top of a carved cane. He was very old—older than anything else in the yard that had a heartbeat—and his face was a map of deep brown creases, and his eyes, deep in all those creases, were black and bright and completely clear.

"Our treaty," he said, "is with the United States."

His voice was thin and dry as paper, and everybody in the yard went quiet to hear it.

"Eighteen fifty-five," Harry said. "Treaty of Olympia. Your Beta told me last night."

"Then you know how that went."

"She told me that too."

The old man studied him. "And still you come."

"And still I come," Harry agreed.

He took a breath. He'd been putting it off. He'd been putting it off since the car, if he was honest, since Leah had looked at him across the front seat and said *you're going to tell Sam Uley he's not in charge, on his own land*, with a look on her face he couldn't read.

"Your treaty's with Washington," he said. "The No-Maj one. Paper between your people and theirs. It's real, and it matters, and I'm not here to touch it. But the people who signed it didn't know you turn into wolves. Nobody in that government has the first idea what you are. So nothing they signed covers this." He spread his paws. "The Statute of Secrecy does. It's older than the United States. It doesn't stop at county lines, or state lines, or the edge of a reservation. It covers every witch and wizard and vampire and shifter on this continent. It covers me."

He looked around the yard. At Uley. At the boys. At the porch.

"The Magical Congress reserves the right to supersede any deal any No-Maj government has ever made with anybody, when it comes to keeping magic hidden. Treaties. Sovereignty. All of it. And we've got the means to back that up." He let that sit. "I know how that sounds. Out here. After eighteen fifty-five. I'm sorry for it. I am. But as much as you're going to hate it, the wolves dance to the government's tune. Same as the Cullens. Same as every witch in Salem. Same as me."

Nobody said anything.

Then, from the tree line, somebody laughed.

It was an ugly laugh. Short and hard and mean. Harry didn't have to turn his head to know who it was. He'd known which one was Paul since the car.

"*Dance,*" Paul said.

He came forward out of the trees. He was medium height for one of them, which still made him six feet and change, all lean muscle and black hair cut short, and he was shaking. The fine tremor from before had turned into big, hard shudders, rolling up through his chest and out along his arms, the skin of his shoulders twitching like a horse's flank under flies.

"Paul," Uley said. "Back up."

"No. No, I want to hear this." Paul's lip had come up off his teeth. "This guy shows up. Shows up on Sam's land, with a *pie*, and some flowers, and says we *dance*. Says we're gonna do what he says, like a bunch of—" His voice cracked and dropped half an octave. "And he shows up smelling like that. Like he *rolled* in her. Like she let him—"

"*Paul,*" Leah said.

Harry's ears went flat.

"Careful," he said quietly.

"Or what?" Paul grinned with all his teeth. "Or *what*, fed? You gonna float me? Like the bloodsucker? Go on. Do it."

"Uley," Harry said, without taking his eyes off Paul. "Get your boy."

"Paul," Sam said, and his voice had changed—it had gone deep and heavy, a voice with weight behind it, and Harry felt it go across the yard like a hand pressing down on everything in it. "*Stop.*"

And Paul exploded.

It happened the way Harry'd seen it happen in Black's shoulders last night, only all at once and much faster. One second there was a young man standing in the wet grass, shaking. The next, there was a sound like a sheet being torn in half, and the young man burst apart, and out of the shreds of his cutoffs came something huge and silver-dark and *moving*. Four legs. A chest like a barrel. A head as long as Harry's arm, all teeth.

It came across the yard at him low and fast and dead straight, faster than anything that size had any right to move.

And Emily Young had just stepped out onto the porch.

She had a tray of muffins in both hands. She was looking down at the tray. She was three steps above and behind Harry, square in the line of the charge, and she looked up in time to see a wolf the size of a horse coming at her across the grass with its lips peeled back, and her face did something Harry would remember for a long time.

He moved before he decided to.

He reached back without looking, and the woman and the wheelchair beside her and the old man under the eave all went backward through the open door of the house in one smooth, gentle shove, like a wave rolling up a beach, and the door slammed behind them. The tray stayed where her hands had been. It hung in the empty air for a heartbeat, loaded with muffins, and then it dropped. Sue Clearwater, at the foot of the steps, staggered sideways three yards and sat down hard in the wet grass with the carnations still in her arms.

Then Paul hit him.

Or tried to. Harry turned sideways into the charge, the way you'd step into a door to take the weight of it, and let the big silver body come past his shoulder. He caught the ruff at the back of Paul's neck in his left paw, a fistful of thick fur and the loose skin under it. He caught the left foreleg in his right paw just above the joint. He used every ounce of Paul's own weight and speed, and he turned, and he put the better part of a ton of furious wolf into the ground.

The impact shook the porch. It shook the windows in the house. Mud and grass and gravel went up in a spray around them, and under the noise of it Harry heard the sound he'd been expecting: a short, sharp, wet *crack*, like a green branch going, from somewhere in Paul's ribs.

Paul screamed. It was a wolf's scream, high and thin, and it went out across the village and echoed off the trees.

Harry came down after him. One knee on the big silver shoulder, all his weight on it, pinning the leg. One paw on Paul's throat, spread wide, every claw out. He didn't cut. He let them rest. Five black points pressed into the fur over the big vessels, exactly where a wolf would feel them, exactly where a wolf would know.

Paul went still.

The yard behind Harry had exploded too. He heard it without looking: the rip of fabric, a snarl, the sound of the bigger young one—Jared—starting to go, and the two kids, both of them, shaking so hard their teeth chattered—

"*Nobody phases.*"

Sam Uley's voice came down on the yard like a falling tree. He wasn't in Uley's pack, and he still felt the weight of it go past him like a gust, a hand on the back of the neck meant for somebody else.

Behind him, everything stopped. The snarl cut off. The shaking stopped. Jared stood frozen halfway through the change with his shirt torn open at the shoulders and his face twisted, and then, slowly, eased back.

Harry stayed where he was.

Under his paw, Paul's heart was going like a hammer. His breath came in short, whistling gasps around the broken rib. His eyes, rolled back to look up at Harry, were brown, and wide, and very young.

Harry bent down until his muzzle was a few inches from the big silver ear.

"Bru," he said. Low. Just for Paul. "You just phased ten feet from a woman with three scars on her face."

He didn't look at the door. He didn't need to.

"Think real hard," he said, "about where scars like that come from."

Paul made a sound. A whine, thin and miserable, way down in his throat.

Behind him, Harry heard someone draw breath sharply, and he turned his head, and found Sam Uley standing a few yards away in the wet grass. Uley's face had gone gray. He looked like a man who'd been hit in the stomach with a bat and was trying very hard not to let anybody see it.

*Ah*, Harry thought. *There it is.*

He'd guessed. Three parallel scars, claw-width, a big animal. A woman who lived with the Alpha. A girl-wolf who'd said *ex* and nothing else. He'd guessed, and now he knew, and he wished—just for a second—that he didn't.

The door of the house opened.

Emily Young stood in it. She wasn't holding the tray anymore. Her hands were empty and there was flour on her arms and her chin was up, and the scars stood out pale against the deep flush of her cheek. She looked at Harry, pinning Paul to the mud of her front yard with his claws at the boy's throat.

"Don't," she said.

Harry looked at her.

"Don't you use me," Emily said. Her voice was shaking, a little. It was also very clear. "To make your point. Don't you *ever*."

There was a long silence.

"Fair," Harry said. He took his paw off Paul's throat. "Sorry, ma'am."

He meant it. She looked at him like she could see that he meant it, and like it didn't make any difference, and she was right about that too.

He stood up. He took his knee off Paul's shoulder and stepped back, and Paul rolled onto his side in the mud with a groan and lay there, panting, his ribs heaving. Already Harry could hear the bone grinding back into place under the fur. He'd be fine by lunch. He'd be sore till dinner.

Harry looked down at himself. There was mud up to his knees and down the front of his jeans, and grass in his fur, and a long tear across the front of the flannel where Paul's claw had caught it on the way past. He sighed.

He looked around the yard.

The muffins were everywhere. The tray had come down on the edge of the porch and flipped, and the muffins had gone everywhere, and now there were muffins on the porch and muffins on the steps and muffins in the grass and one, improbably, sitting upright and unharmed in the bottom of the hole Paul had made in the yard. Sue Clearwater was getting up out of the wet grass with a face like thunder and the carnations crushed against her chest. Billy Black's wheelchair had rolled to a stop just inside the door; he was sitting in it, looking out, with his blanket in his lap and an expression Harry couldn't read at all. Old Quil was behind him, leaning on his cane, looking at the hole Paul's body had made in the yard.

Jared stood by the trees with his shirt in rags. The two young ones stood behind him, very close together. Brady was crying. He was trying not to. He wasn't managing it.

And Leah stood in the middle of all of it, where she'd been the whole time, holding her coffee. She hadn't moved an inch. She hadn't spilled a drop. She was looking at Harry with an expression that he couldn't read either, and he wasn't sure he wanted to.

"That," Harry said, to the yard, "is what I mean by *the means*."

Nobody answered.

"I didn't need a wand," he said. "I didn't need a second. I'm one bloke, and they've got more like me in New York, and a lot of them are a lot less polite." He looked at Uley. "I'd rather it was me. That's the honest truth. I'd rather it was me with a pie than one of them with a list."

"You broke his ribs," said Billy Black from the doorway. Very quiet.

"One rib. He'll heal by lunch."

"On Quileute land."

"Ja." Harry met his eyes. "And if he'd done that on First Beach in August, Mr. Black, I'd be breaking a lot more than a rib to clean it up. I'd be wiping forty tourists' heads, and one of them would've had a camera, and I'd be chasing that tape across three states." He shook his head. "Everybody dances. *Me too.* I've got a boss in New York who'd have my badge if I'd let that one take a bite out of anybody."

Old Quil spoke from behind Billy's chair. "We heard you," he said, in his thin, dry voice. "We'll decide what we think of it. Without you."

"Fair."

"Council," said Sam Uley. His voice was hoarse. His face was still gray, and he wasn't looking at Harry, and he wasn't looking at Emily, and Harry thought he probably wouldn't be able to look at either of them for a while. "Inside. Now. Jared, get Paul up. Get him in the back."

"Room fourteen," Harry said. "Pacific Inn. Or ask Clearwater."

Uley's eyes went across the yard to Leah, and stayed there. To the shirt. To the marks on her neck.

Then he turned around and went up the steps into the house, stepping carefully over the muffins, and he didn't look back.

## VI. Leah

Leah stood in the middle of Sam Uley's front yard with a cup of Thriftway coffee going cold in her hand and muffins all around her feet, and tried to figure out what she felt.

She'd wanted this. Last night, in the Cullens' living room, she'd wanted it so badly she could taste it. Somebody walking up to Sam's door and telling him he wasn't the biggest thing in the world. Somebody making him look small, in front of everybody, the way he'd made her feel small for two years.

And she'd gotten it. She'd watched Sam's face go gray. She'd watched him walk up his own steps without looking back, with his shoulders up around his ears like a kid who'd been sent to his room. Some ugly, mean little part of her had sat up and wagged its tail at that, and she was ashamed of it, and it went on wagging anyway.

But she'd also watched Brady cry. She'd watched Emily's face when she looked up from that tray and saw Paul coming. She'd watched Potter put his claws on Paul's throat, and bend down, and say *think real hard about where scars like that come from*, in a voice she'd heard every word of from twenty feet away. And she'd watched Emily stand in that doorway with flour on her arms and tell him not to.

Emily had been right. Leah hated that. Emily was right about a lot of things and Leah hated every one of them.

It tasted like coffee that had gone cold. That was all.

Across the yard, her mother was walking toward the car.

Sue had gotten up out of the grass and brushed herself off, and there was a wet patch on the seat of her slacks and mud on her shoes and the carnations were crushed flat against her chest, the sunflower bent over at a sad angle. She walked straight past Leah without looking at her and stopped in front of Potter.

He was leaning against the side of the Impala, picking grass out of the fur on his forearm. The flannel was torn open across the front. There was mud on him up to the knees. He'd found a muffin somewhere that had survived and he was holding it in his other paw, and when Sue stopped in front of him he straightened up off the car and put the muffin behind his back like a kid caught with a cookie.

Leah could hear every word. She'd have been able to hear every word from the other end of the village.

"You pushed me into the grass," her mother said.

"Ja. Sorry, ma'am. You were in the way."

"You pushed Emily through a door."

"She was more in the way."

Sue looked up at him. She had to tilt her head back a long way to do it.

"My daughter," she said, "has been through enough this year."

"I know."

"You don't." Sue's voice didn't rise. It never rose. It just got quieter and harder, like a creek freezing over. "You've known her for one night."

Potter was quiet for a second.

"Fair," he said.

Sue held his eyes a moment longer. Then she said, "Dinner. Tonight. Six o'clock. My house. Leah knows where it is."

Leah's coffee nearly went down the front of the T-shirt.

Potter blinked. His ears went up and then sideways, like they didn't know what to do. "Ma'am?"

"If you're going to be anywhere near my daughter," said Sue Clearwater, "then I'm going to look at you across my own table." She shifted the ruined carnations in her arms. "Bring something besides carnations."

"Yes, ma'am."

Sue turned around and walked back across the yard toward the house. She stopped when she got to Leah.

For a second she just looked. Her eyes went over the shirt, and Leah's bare feet in the mud, and the coffee, and then they went up to Leah's neck and stayed there. Leah felt them land on the marks like a hand.

Her mother reached up. Leah held still. Sue's fingers touched the side of her neck, very lightly, just beside the bite, the way she used to check Leah's forehead for a fever when she was little.

"Those should have healed," Sue said quietly.

"I know, Mom."

"Does it hurt?"

"No."

Sue's eyes went past her to the car, to the wolf leaning against it with a muffin behind his back. Then back to Leah. Leah couldn't read her face. She'd never been able to read her mother's face. Her dad had been the easy one. Her dad had worn everything on the outside.

"Six o'clock," Sue said.

She went up the steps and into the house, stepping over the muffins, and shut the door behind her.

---

Leah stood there for a minute. Then she bent down, picked up a blueberry muffin off the bottom step, brushed a little gravel off the top, and ate it in three bites, out of what she was pretty sure was spite.

It was a really good muffin. Emily's always were.

She was licking the crumbs off her thumb when the door opened again behind her.

She knew it was Sam before she turned around. She'd know his step on a porch with her eyes shut, a hundred years from now. She turned around anyway.

He stood in the doorway with one hand on the frame. His face had gotten some of its color back. Not much.

"Leah," he said.

"Don't."

He didn't. Not for a second. Then his eyes went to her neck, the way everybody's eyes kept going to her neck, and his forehead creased.

"Your neck," he said.

"I know."

"It should've—"

"I *know*, Sam."

He stood there. Behind him, through the door, she could hear the council talking low in the kitchen: Billy's voice, and Old Quil's, and her mother's, and somewhere in the back of the house Paul groaning and Jared telling him to shut up and hold still. Emily wasn't saying anything. Leah couldn't hear Emily at all.

"Are you okay?" Sam said.

Leah opened her mouth to say something sharp. Something with an edge on it, something that would cut, because that was what she always said to Sam, every time, because it was the only thing she had left that he couldn't take back.

She stopped.

She thought about it. Actually thought. She thought about the dark in a motel room in Forks, and a voice saying *upgrade*, and a tail laid over her legs like a blanket she didn't need. She thought about the fact that for fourteen hours, she hadn't once reached for the stone in her shoe.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm okay."

It came out plain. No edge on it at all. She watched it land on him.

His face did something complicated. Relief, mostly. And under the relief, something that looked like it hurt. She didn't know what to do with that, and she decided it wasn't her job to.

"Go to your council, Sam," she said.

He looked at her a second longer. Then he nodded, once, and went back inside, and pulled the door shut.

Leah turned around and walked to the car.

---

Potter had gotten in. He was sitting behind the wheel with the door open and one footpaw still out on the gravel, eating the muffin he'd hidden from her mother. When she came around the front of the car he leaned over and pushed the passenger door open for her from the inside.

The hinge screamed. Somebody in the house would have heard it. She didn't care.

She got in and pulled the door shut. The car still smelled like them. After the yard, after Paul and the muffins and the whole stinking, ugly, sad morning, it was almost a relief.

They sat there for a minute with the rain ticking on the roof.

"You used Emily," Leah said.

"Ja."

"That was low."

"It was."

"She was right."

"She was."

Leah looked at him sideways. He was looking out the windshield at the house, at the window box full of tired marigolds, at the muffins in the mud. His ears were halfway back.

"It worked, though," she said.

"It did." He finished the muffin. "Still low."

She didn't have anything to say to that, so she didn't say anything.

"Paul's going to hate you forever," she said after a while.

"Paul can take a number."

"You didn't tell my mom about the game."

He turned his head and looked at her.

"Charlie," Leah said. "Saturday. The game. You could've said it. In front of everybody. Right there in the yard. It would've shut her up."

"Could've."

"Why didn't you?"

He shrugged one shoulder. "Wasn't mine to say."

Leah sat with that.

She'd been keeping a list on him since the Cullens' front door. Things to watch. Things not to trust. *Went into Charlie's head without asking. Won't say where he's from. Says probably not.* It was a long list. She'd been adding to it all night.

She found, sitting there in the car with the rain on the roof, that she'd started a second one next to it, and she didn't remember deciding to.

*Didn't ask about the stomach. Put the tail over me. Didn't tell Mom about the game.*

It was a much shorter list. She didn't trust it either.

"She invited you to dinner," Leah said.

"I heard."

"That's a threat, Potter. You get that, right? That's an interrogation with pot roast."

"Ja, I got that."

"She's going to ask you about your intentions."

"I'll tell her I intend to eat."

Leah laughed before she could stop it. It came out short and surprised, and he grinned at her, that big lazy wolf grin, and she looked away out the window before it could do anything else to her.

Her hand went up to her neck. She didn't mean it to. Her fingers found the marks—two neat rows, the four big points at the corners—and pressed, lightly. The same dull ache, way down. The same faint hum under it, like standing next to a power line. She hadn't imagined it.

She looked at Potter.

He was looking at her neck. At her fingers on the marks. His ears had gone all the way back now, flat to his skull, and his face had gone still and closed, the way it had in the motel bathroom. He didn't say anything.

She noticed that he didn't say anything.

"You figure it out yet?" she said.

"No."

"Would you tell me? If you did?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that she started to think he wasn't going to answer.

"This one," he said. "Yeah."

She watched his face. His ears, his nose, his eyes. She couldn't tell. She still couldn't tell, and that still scared her a little, and she decided that was going on both lists.

She took her hand off her neck.

"So where to?" Potter said. "Council's going to be a while. Your mum said six."

"Your call. You're the government."

"Driver picks the—"

"Shotgun," said Leah, reaching for the shoebox, "is entitled to an opinion."

He shut his mouth. His ear flicked. He turned the key, and the engine coughed and caught and settled into its rough, rumbling idle, and the wipers thumped across the glass.

Leah dug through the tapes. Zeppelin again. She found the one she wanted and shoved it in the deck, and the deck ate it with a clunk, and somewhere in the back of the house behind them she heard Paul yelp as somebody set his rib.

Potter put the car in reverse. Gravel crunched. The little gray house with the blue door and the ruined muffins slid away across the windshield, and the trees closed in, and the road started its long climb back up out of La Push.

She had to phase eventually. She knew that. Sometime tonight, or tomorrow, she'd have to go out on the ridge and let the wolf out, and the second she did, Jacob and Seth would see all of it. Every minute. The motel. The clock radio. Her hand on her stomach in the dark. This morning. Her mother's face. Sam in the doorway.

The marks on her neck, still there.

She put her bare feet up on the dash and watched the trees go by in the rain, and touched the marks one more time without meaning to, and felt them hum.

Six o'clock, she thought. Pot roast.

God help him.
