# Chapter Three: Home Range

The sun came up while she was still in the trees above the lake road, and she was late.

She knew it the second the light changed. One minute the spruce were black shapes against a gray sky, and the next the tops of them had gone copper, and down below her through the trunks the lake was turning from pewter to pale blue, and somewhere in Turnagain a truck was warming up in a driveway with its heater roaring. She'd been running for three hours, down off the plateau and through the gold cottonwoods and along the river, with her head full of nothing and a little leather pouch bumping against her ankle, and she hadn't once thought about what time it was.

Monday. The Galena opened at six. Dot had been on the flat-top since a quarter past five.

*Shit.*

She came down the last slope at a dead run and skidded in behind the deadfall where she'd phased the day before. The heat went out of her and the fur went in and she was on her knees in the frosted moss, naked and steaming, picking at the knots on her ankle with fingers that didn't want to work yet.

Three hard knots. The way they'd all learned.

There was a fourth now. Small and neat and tight, the drawstring of the pouch threaded through her cord and tied off snug against the plastic bag. It hadn't slipped. Three hours of hard running over rock and river gravel, and it sat exactly where he'd put it.

She got her own knots loose and left his. She pulled on the jeans and the flannel and the hoodie, jammed her feet into her boots without socks, and only then went back and worked the little knot open with her thumbnail. It came apart the second she touched it. Like it had been waiting for her and not for anybody else.

She looked at the pouch in her palm. Soft dark leather, worn shiny at the seams. It weighed about as much as a deck of cards.

She shoved it into the front pocket of her hoodie and ran for town.

---

Wes was standing in the door of the motel office in his slippers with his coffee mug, watching the road the way he did every morning. He watched her come up it at a jog with her hair full of twigs. He watched her go past him to room six. He watched her come out again ninety seconds later with her hair yanked back in an elastic and her face still wet from the sink.

"Morning," Wes said.

"Morning."

He took a sip of his coffee. "Dot called."

"I bet she did."

"Twice," Wes said, and went back inside to his fishing show.

---

The bell over the door jingled. Heat and noise and bacon. The highway crew guys in their orange vests, the three old men in the corner with their feed caps and their argument, a couple of hunters Leah hadn't seen before. Brynn was coming down the counter with a coffee pot in each hand and her eyebrows went straight up into her bangs.

Leah went through the swinging door into the kitchen.

Dot didn't look up from the grill. She had six eggs going, a pile of hash browns, a row of bacon, and four pancakes, and she was flipping all of them in an order that made sense only to Dot. The sink was full. The racks beside it were full. There was a stack of dirty plates on the floor beside the sink because there was nowhere else to put them.

"It's seven twenty," Dot said.

"I know."

"You know what time your shift starts?"

"Six."

"So you know." Dot flipped two eggs. "Where were you?"

"Running."

That made Dot look up. She looked at Leah's face, at the wet hair, at the hoodie, and then her eyes went down to Leah's hands, and stayed there. Leah looked down too. There was dried blood under every one of her fingernails, packed in dark and brown, and a smear of it in the creases of her knuckles that the sink at the motel hadn't touched.

"Hunting," Leah said.

Dot's eyes came back up to her face. "Hunting what?"

There didn't seem to be much point in lying about it. "Caribou."

"Up on the plateau." Dot said it flat. "On foot. In the dark."

"Yeah."

Dot looked at her for a long time. The eggs started to go brown at the edges. Out front, somebody laughed.

"Huh," said Dot. She turned back to the grill and plated the eggs and rang the bell. "Wash your hands. Twice. Then wash my pans. You're staying till three to make it up."

"Okay."

"And next time you go off killing things in the middle of the night, you set an alarm."

"Okay."

"Don't *okay* me, Washington," Dot said, without heat. "Move."

Leah washed her hands. Twice. She used the nail brush Dot kept by the sink for the potatoes and scrubbed until the water ran clear and the skin around her nails was pink. Then she tied on her apron and started on the stack on the floor, and for the next three hours she didn't think about anything except plates.

Almost.

The pouch was a small hard square against her stomach, through the hoodie, under the apron. Every time she leaned into the sink it pressed against her. She didn't take it out. She didn't look at it.

She kept knowing it was there.

---

"Okay," Brynn said at ten, in the lull, coming through the swinging door with a bus tub on her hip. "Okay. So. *Rumor.*"

"No."

"I haven't even said it."

"I don't want it."

"You haven't even *heard* it." Brynn dumped the bus tub on the counter by the sink and leaned on it. Her cheeks were pink. She'd been back at work three days now and seemed fine, better than fine, back to talking a mile a minute and humming at the coffee machine. If anything was still missing from Thursday night, Leah couldn't see where. "Ray says some English guy tried to buy the Prospector on Thursday. Like, the *whole bar.* So he could keep drinking after one. Ray had to physically push him out the door."

Leah rinsed a plate.

"And *Wes* says there was a gunshot down by the dock that night." Brynn's eyes were very wide. "And *Ray* says the English guy left with *you.* And *Dot* says — "

"Dot says," said Dot, from the grill, without turning around, "you've got four tables."

"They're all eating, Dot."

"Then go watch them eat."

Brynn ignored her. She leaned in closer to Leah and lowered her voice to a whisper that could probably be heard in the Yukon. "Dot says he's your *fancy friend.*"

Leah racked the plate.

"You have a *fancy friend,*" Brynn breathed. "You've been here, like, a *week.* I've lived here my whole *life* and all I've got is Tyler Nakamura, who works at the gas station and calls me *dude.*" She grabbed Leah's wet forearm. "Ray says he's got a *lodge.* The one with the green roof. And a *chef* — "

"Brynn."

"*What?*"

"He's not my friend."

"Okay, so what *is* he?"

Leah opened her mouth, and closed it again.

Brynn's eyes went even wider, which Leah would not have said was possible. She let go of Leah's arm. She put both her hands over her mouth.

"Oh my *God,*" she said through her fingers.

"Four tables, Brynn," said Dot.

Brynn went. She went backwards, through the swinging door, with her hands still over her mouth and her eyes on Leah the whole way, until the door swung shut between them.

Leah stood at the sink with her hands in the water and looked out the window at the lake. Across the bay the lodge sat on its point with its green roof catching the light, and the yellow floatplane was tied at its dock, and there was no smoke coming out of the chimney.

"He's away," Dot said behind her.

Leah turned her head.

Dot was scraping down the flat-top. "Ray saw the chef at the Mercantile. Chef says the boss came back Sunday afternoon for a box of chocolates the size of a suitcase and went off again before supper." She knocked the scraper against the grease trap. "Says he's never seen the man use the plane or get in a car. He goes for a walk in the woods and doesn't come back, and three days later he walks out of the woods in the same clothes, wanting risotto."

Leah didn't say anything.

"Strange fella," Dot said.

"Yeah."

Dot looked at her a while. Then she went back to her grill, and that was the end of it. That was the third thing Leah liked about her.

---

That night in room six she took the pouch out of her hoodie and sat on the orange bedspread and turned it over in her hands.

The heater clanked. The moose looked down at her from its lake.

She worked the drawstring open and tipped the mirror out into her palm.

It was smaller than she'd expected. Square, about the size of a coaster, framed in some dull silvery metal that was cold to the touch and stayed cold no matter how long she held it, which nothing ever did, with her. The back was plain. The glass was old, with the same faint ripple the windows in Grandma's house had, so the light lay strange on its surface.

She looked into it and saw herself. Dark eyes, tired. A scratch on her cheekbone from a spruce branch that would be gone by morning. Her hair, which needed cutting.

*If you want me, say my name into it. Wherever I am, I'll hear. I'll answer.*

She sat there with her thumb on the cold edge of the frame for a long time.

Then she put it back in the pouch and pulled the drawstring tight and put it in the drawer of the nightstand, on top of the Gideon Bible, and shut the drawer.

She lay down and looked at the moose.

After a while she got up again and took the pouch out of the drawer and put it on top of the nightstand, where she could see it. Then she lay down and looked at the ceiling instead.

She didn't say his name.

---

He didn't come on Tuesday.

She didn't expect him to. She noticed she'd gone out to the dock on her break anyway, with her coffee, and stood at the end of it with her nose up, and that the wind was coming off the lake from the wrong direction to tell her anything.

He didn't come on Wednesday.

Wednesday night after close she ran, north over the ridge behind the airstrip into a country of small lakes and black spruce. She caught a snowshoe hare already going white at the feet, and ate it, and lay on a granite knob above a beaver pond with her chin on her paws.

Nobody in her head. Still. Clean and ringing.

She lay there and made herself notice it, on purpose, the way you'd make yourself look at a sunset because you'd driven a long way for it. She'd run eighteen hundred miles for this. She'd laughed in a pumpkin field for this. She'd had it for two whole weeks now, and she wasn't going to let some guy with a two-way mirror and a chocolate shop take the shine off it by not showing up.

She didn't notice the wind. She didn't lift her nose into it. Not once.

Twice.

He didn't come on Thursday.

---

"Okay, so the *Alpha* pool," Brynn said Thursday night, upside down.

She was lying on her back on the counter after close with her head hanging off the end and her ponytail brushing the floor, because she said it was good for circulation, and Dot had gone home, and there was nobody to stop her. Leah was mopping. She'd been mopping around Brynn's ponytail for five minutes.

"And the *Beta* pool," Brynn went on. "Which is further up the boardwalk. But nobody goes to the Beta pool because the bears go there. There was this whole thing when I was in grade six, a bear came down the boardwalk and — well, anyway, it was really bad, there's a plaque. But the Alpha pool's amazing. It's like a hot tub, only it's a river, in the woods." She sighed blissfully at the ceiling. "We used to drive up for grad weekend. We'd sleep in the campground in Kayla's dad's camper and sit in the pool till two in the morning and drink peach schnapps and you could see the northern lights right through the steam."

Leah wrung out the mop. "How far is it?"

"Like — five hours? Up to the Alaska Highway and then east. There's bison on the road."

"Bison."

"Wood bison. Like, *herds.* They just lie on the highway like they own it. You have to wait for them to move. Kayla's dad hit one once in his truck and the truck lost." Brynn rolled her head sideways on the counter and looked at Leah, upside down. "Why? You want to go?"

"No."

"You *totally* want to go."

"I've got no car."

"You walked here from *Washington.*"

Leah didn't answer that. She mopped around the end of the counter.

"Are you gonna take your *fancy friend?*" Brynn said, in a singsong.

"I don't — " Leah stopped. She looked at the mop. "He's in Europe."

"So *call* him."

"I don't have a phone."

"There's a pay phone outside the gas station."

"I don't have his number."

Brynn stared at her upside down for a long moment, her face slowly going pink from the blood running into it.

"Leah," she said, with enormous patience, "you are the *worst* at this."

---

That night she sat on the orange bedspread with the mirror in her hand for twenty minutes.

She told herself a number of things in those twenty minutes. That it was a dumb idea. That it was eleven at night here and she had no idea what time it was in Europe, or wherever he was. That he'd said *third date* and she'd said *maybe,* and four days was long enough for anybody to understand what *maybe* meant. That he'd had four days to come down to the dock with his head tilted and he hadn't.

And then, somewhere in the middle of all that, she understood why he hadn't.

*Maybe,* she'd said. And he'd said *I can work with maybe,* and stepped off a rock into the dark. And then he'd stayed gone. Hadn't turned up at the café, hadn't left another bottle on the end of the dock or a box of chocolates outside room six. He'd handed her a mirror and gone away and left it with her.

He was waiting for her to decide.

She sat there with that for a while. She'd have expected it to feel like being let off the hook. It felt more like somebody had put the hook in her hand.

"Harry," she said to the mirror.

Nothing happened. Her own face. The ripple in the glass. The moose behind her shoulder, backwards.

She felt like an idiot. She was about to put it down when the glass went cloudy, like breath on a window, and cleared, and a different face was looking up at her out of it.

Wet. That was the first thing. His hair was wet and plastered flat to his head for once, which made him look about nineteen, and there was steam drifting across the glass in front of him, and behind him she could see a slice of a high white ceiling with fancy plaster along the edges, and a window with gray light in it.

"Clearwater," he said. He sounded surprised. He sounded pleased about it, which she decided not to notice.

"Are you in the *bath?*"

He glanced down, out of frame. Back up. "Possibly."

"It's — what time is it there?"

"About seven in the morning. I've been up all night in a bog in Lincolnshire waiting for a man to dig up something he shouldn't have buried in the first place." He shifted, and she heard water slop. "I got him. Then I fell in the bog. Then I came home."

"You said you'd answer in the bath."

"I said *possibly even then.* I'm a man of my word." The mirror tilted a little, as if he'd propped it against something, and she got a clearer look at his face. Tired. A smudge of something dark along his jaw that might have been bog. Green eyes, very awake now. "You all right?"

"I'm fine."

"Good." He waited. When she didn't say anything else, he didn't fill it. He just lay there in his bath in England with the steam going past, looking out of a mirror at her, waiting.

"Third date," Leah said.

He went still. "Yes?"

"Saturday. After I close. I pick."

"All right." The corner of his mouth started to go up and he got it back under control. "Where?"

"There's a hot spring. Up on the Alaska Highway. Liard." She'd practiced saying it in her head and it still came out stiff. "Brynn says it's like a river in the woods."

"Liard," he repeated. "I'll find it. What time?"

"Midnight. I'll run up."

"It's a long way."

"I'm fast."

"Yes," he said, with feeling. "I'm aware." He shifted again in the water. "Is there a dress code? Black tie? Clothing optional?"

"*No.*"

"No to which?"

"*Goodnight,* Harry."

She saw the grin get away from him, finally, just as she put her hand over the glass. It went cloudy under her palm. When she took her hand away it was only her own face again, which was red, and the moose behind her.

She put the mirror on the nightstand face up.

---

She closed at half past seven on Saturday, mopped by eight, and was in the trees behind the gravel pit by five past, with a black one-piece off the Mercantile clearance rack rolled up in the grocery bag on her ankle, along with her hoodie and the mirror.

She ran.

East first, along the river that drained the far end of the lake, down through the cottonwood flats where the big tree stood with its four long scars, and she went past it without slowing down. Then down into the valley of the Turnagain itself, a real river, broad and gray and fast, running northeast out of the mountains with its gravel bars pale in the starlight. She followed it for a long time, down through a canyon where the water roared and out into a wide valley where it ran into a bigger river coming down from the south. On the point between the two stood a few old log walls with their roofs fallen in, fireweed growing out of the floor. A trading post, maybe. Nobody had traded anything there in a long time.

North, then, down the big river. Moose in the shallows. An owl that dropped off a snag and sailed across the river in front of her, silent, and she thought about him coming off that cottonwood branch without a sound, and ran harder.

And then the big river ran into a bigger one, a monster, wide as a lake and moving like a freight train in the dark, and that was the Liard. She turned east along its bank. A little after eleven she came up out of the trees onto a road.

Two lanes. Gravel shoulders. Faded yellow line. A green sign, shot full of holes, with a white mile marker and a number in kilometers she didn't bother to read. The Alaska Highway.

There were bison lying on it.

Nine or ten of them, dark humps across both lanes in the starlight, chewing. They were enormous, with heads like boulders and great shaggy capes over their shoulders, and they'd picked the road because the asphalt still held the last heat of the day. One lifted its head, blew out through its nose at her, and went back to chewing.

She went around them. Wide.

---

She smelled the springs a mile before she reached them. Something mineral in the air, faint and eggy. Wet moss. Steam. And then, a quarter mile out, threaded through all of it the way it had been threaded through the wet leaves in the cottonwood flat, so faint she thought for a second she'd imagined it:

Earth after rain. Pine. Cold stone.

There was a parking lot by the highway with two RVs at the far end, dark, curtains drawn. A log building with a sign about bears. And the start of a boardwalk, gray planks on stilts, running off into the trees.

She went down it.

It ran out over a marsh, and the marsh was steaming. Late September, frost on the grass up by the road, and the water under the boardwalk was warm, rising off black pools between hummocks of sedge, with ferns still green in it. A frog went *plip* as she passed. A frog. In the north, in September.

Halfway along the boardwalk a wolf was lying across it.

He took up the whole width of the planks and then some. Black on black, in the dark, so that the only way she could see him at all was where the steam coming off the marsh behind him curled around his outline. His chin was on his forelegs. His ears were up. The planks under him didn't sag.

"Evening, Clearwater," he said. Quietly, because sound carried out here. "You're early."

She stopped ten feet away.

He looked at her for a moment. Then his eyes went down to the bundle on her ankle, and back to her face, and he got up, without hurry, and turned around, so his back and his long black tail were toward her. He sat. He looked off into the steam with great interest.

"I'll be over here," he said. "Admiring the moss."

She stood there with her ears forward, staring at the back of his head. He didn't move.

She went off the boardwalk into the spruce and let the fire go.

---

When she came back out she was in the black swimsuit and the gray hoodie with the hood up, barefoot on the frosted planks, with the bag of clothes and the mirror tucked under her arm. The cold didn't touch her. Her breath smoked a little. That was all.

He was still sitting there, facing the other way. She'd been gone four minutes. He hadn't turned around once; she'd have heard it. Or she wouldn't have, with him, and that was the whole problem. She stood and looked at his back for a while and decided she believed him.

"Okay," she said.

He turned his head. He looked at her standing there in her hoodie and her bare legs, and his ears went straight up, and stayed up.

"Right," he said, after a second. "Hello."

"Hello yourself."

"That's — " He seemed to lose the end of the sentence. He found it again. "That's a good hoodie."

"It cost twelve bucks."

"Money well spent." He stood. On four legs on a boardwalk, he was taller than she was. "Hang on. One moment. I owe you something."

"Owe me *what?*"

"Third date," Harry said. "You asked about the clothes."

And he changed.

She'd seen it happen to other people a thousand times. From the inside, mostly, the way the pack felt it, a bone-deep wrench, a shuddering heat, fur bursting out or sucking in all at once. From the outside, it was fast and ugly and loud. Seams splitting. Bodies jerking. Somebody's shorts in shreds on the ground.

This was quiet.

The black wolf stood on the boardwalk, and then he was getting smaller, and she couldn't have said which part of him went first. The fur drew in. The shape of him came down and in and up, into something narrower that stood on two legs, and there was no tearing and no wrench and no noise at all except, at the very end, the soft sound of a boot setting itself down on wet wood.

Harry Potter stood on the boardwalk in the steam.

He was wearing the olive waxed jacket and the cream cable-knit sweater and dark trousers and the brown boots with their idiot shine. The jacket was buttoned. The sweater was dry. There was not a single crease in anything, and his hair was standing up exactly the way it had stood up in the bar, and he looked at her with his hands in his pockets.

Leah stared at him.

She looked at his boots. At the jacket. At his dry, intact, buttoned sweater.

She looked down at the plastic grocery bag tucked under her arm, with its knots and its cord and the clothes she'd tied onto her own ankle with three hard knots every single time she'd phased for a year and a half.

"Are you *kidding* me?" she said.

"Bit of a perk."

"You *keep your clothes?*"

"And whatever's in the pockets." He took one hand out of a pocket. There was half a bar of chocolate in it, in a purple wrapper. He looked at it fondly and put it back. "Wallet. Keys. Wand. All of it."

"I have been tying my underwear to my *ankle,*" Leah said, "for a *year and a half.*"

He opened his mouth.

"I have walked home *naked,*" she said, louder, "through Forks, Washington, at four in the morning, because the cord snapped on a fence. I have had to steal a *tarp off a woodpile.* Paul Lahote has seen me — Jared has *seen* — we *all* have — do you have any idea — "

"I'm sorry," he said, and his mouth was doing something it shouldn't have been, at the corner. "Truly. That sounds dreadful."

"Don't you *laugh.*"

"I'm not laughing."

"Your face is laughing."

"My face," he said, with great dignity, "is doing its best."

She looked at him a while longer. At the dry sweater. At the face, doing its best.

"Can you teach me?" she said.

The laugh went out of his face. He looked at her properly, head tipped, and thought about it the way he'd thought about it on the dock, before he answered.

"I don't know," he said. "Honestly. I'd have to know a lot more about how yours works. And how mine works, which I'm less sure about than you'd think." He paused. "I can try. If you want."

"I want."

"Then I'll try." He looked around them at the steam, the marsh, the dark spruce, the boardwalk running on ahead into the dark. "In the meantime. I believe I was promised a river in the woods."

---

The boardwalk ended at a wooden deck with a little change house on it, and a set of steps going down, and below the steps a pool.

She'd been picturing a hot tub. What she got was a creek coming out of the forest at the top end, out of a jumble of moss and rock and dark trees, widening into a long pool with a gravel bottom and the forest leaning in over it on both sides, and the whole surface of it was smoking. The steam went up in slow white columns into the cold and hung there, lit faintly by the stars, so it looked like the pool was breathing. At the bottom end the water poured over a lip of rock in a little falls and ran off into the warm marsh. She could hear it, a soft, steady, pouring sound.

There was a sign on the change house. Wooden, routed letters painted white.

ALPHA POOL.

Leah stood on the deck and looked at it.

"Of *course* it is," she said.

Behind her Harry made a small noise, not quite a cough.

"There's another one," he said, in a careful voice. "Up the boardwalk. Ten minutes. The Beta pool. Smaller. Bit deeper, I gather. The bears like it."

She turned around and looked at him.

He held up both hands. "Merely providing the information."

"I'm not," Leah said, "*anybody's* beta. Not anymore."

She dropped the bag on the bench and pulled the hoodie off over her head and went down the steps into the water.

The heat hit her shins like a slap. She hissed. She went in anyway, down into the gravel, up to her knees, her thighs, her waist, and stopped there, breathing through her teeth. It was *hot.* Actually hot. For the first time since she'd phased, something was hotter than she was, and her skin didn't know what to do about it. It prickled and burned and then, slowly, it gave up and let the heat in.

She ducked under, all the way, and came up with her hair streaming, and stood there in the middle of the Alpha pool with the steam pouring off her shoulders and the stars overhead, and laughed out loud.

When she pushed her hair out of her eyes he was standing at the top of the steps. He'd done something; the jacket and the sweater and the trousers and the boots were folded on the bench next to her bag, very neatly, and he was in a pair of dark swim shorts and nothing else.

She'd lived with half-naked men for a year and a half. Wolves didn't wear shirts if they could help it; shirts were one more thing to tie to your ankle. She'd seen the whole pack wandering around in cut-offs in the rain so often she'd stopped seeing them at all.

She looked anyway. It was dark and there was nobody to see her do it.

He was big. Heavier through the shoulders and chest than she'd guessed under the jacket, thick through the arms, with the kind of muscle that came from work. Long legs. Big square hands. And there wasn't a mark on him. That was what she kept looking at. No scars on his knuckles or his shins or his shoulders, not even the little round mark on the upper arm that everybody had from their shots. Her dad had carried a scar across the back of his hand from a filleting knife for thirty years. Everybody she'd ever known had something on their skin from before. He fought for a living. And there wasn't a mark on him anywhere.

She filed it. She'd ask. Some other date.

He came down the steps and into the water and didn't hiss at all.

"Bit warm," he said mildly.

"Shut up."

They went up toward the top end, where the creek came in out of the rocks and the water got hotter with every step, until even she had to stop. They sat down on the gravel with the water up to their collarbones and their backs against the rocks, a couple of feet apart, and for a while neither of them said anything.

It was very quiet. The pour of the little falls at the bottom end. The tick of water dripping off the rocks. Out in the marsh, something big moving through the sedge, slow and splashing.

"Moose," Harry said, before she could.

"I know."

"Course you do."

She tipped her head back against the rock and looked up through the steam at the stars. She could feel the heat of the water going all the way in to her bones, slow and thick, the way the cold went into normal people. Her shoulders came down. Something in her neck that had been tight since about the age of nineteen let go a little.

"How was the bog," she said.

"Wet. Cold. Smelled like a dead sheep." He'd tipped his head back too. "There was a dead sheep."

"Who was the guy?"

"Collector. Of a sort. He'd dug up a barrow, an old grave, Bronze Age, a chieftain in it with everything he was buried with, and he'd taken a sword out of it that should've been left there, and then he couldn't put it down. Literally. It wouldn't let him." He was quiet a moment. "He'd been holding it for four months. Took his fingers off one at a time to get it away from him."

Leah turned her head.

"They'll grow back," he said. "Took them off clean. Hospital in London grows them back in a fortnight." He breathed out. "He thanked me. Cried. Thanked me. Then tried to grab it with the other hand."

She looked at his face in the starlight for a while.

"When did you last take a day off?" she said.

He looked at her, surprised. "I take days off."

"When?"

"Last Sunday. I went to Canada and ate a caribou."

"That was a *date.*"

"It was very restful."

"Before that."

He thought about it. It took him a long time.

"Define day off," he said.

"A day where nobody tries to kill anybody and you don't go anywhere you have to fill out a form about after."

He was quiet so long she thought he wasn't going to answer.

"Ron and Hermione's wedding," he said finally. "May. Two thousand and three." He frowned up at the steam. "No. I left after the speeches. There was a thing in Albania."

"*Three years?*"

"I'm sure there were others."

"Name one."

He opened his mouth. Then he shut it again and slid a little lower in the water, till it was up to his chin, and looked at the stars with the expression of a man who has just lost an argument with himself and would rather not discuss it.

Leah didn't say anything. She let it sit there in the steam.

---

"Third date," she said, later.

She didn't know how much later. Long enough for the moose to wade off through the marsh and for the stars to wheel a little way west. Long enough that her fingers had gone wrinkled and pale and she'd stopped noticing the heat.

He turned his head.

"You promised," she said. "The tree. You said you'd tell me how. How you're a wolf. You said third date."

"I did."

"So."

"So," he agreed.

He sat up a little in the water. He wiped his face with one wet hand and pushed his hair back off his forehead, where it immediately stood back up. He looked out at the steam drifting over the pool, and he opened his mouth to start.

And she watched his face.

She knew that face. She'd never seen it on him, but she knew it. She'd seen it on her mother in the hallway at Forks Community Hospital, in the second before the doctor came out, when her mother already knew. She'd seen it on herself, in the bathroom mirror the morning after the first time she phased, trying to find the first word to tell her mother there was something wrong with her. Somebody standing at the edge of a thing they had to walk back through to tell you about it. Getting ready to go in.

His eyes had gone somewhere a long way from Liard. Green, and very still, and old, the way they'd gone old on the dock right before the canine. He drew a breath.

"Fourth date," Leah said.

He stopped.

He turned and looked at her, and for once he looked like he had no idea what was going to come out of her mouth next.

"You said — " he started.

"I know what I said."

"I promised."

"So you owe me." She looked back at the steam, because it was easier than looking at him. "Fourth date. Or fifth. Whenever. You stayed out of my head when I told you. You can keep yours for a while."

He didn't say anything for a long moment. She could feel him looking at her. She kept looking at the steam.

"Thank you," he said. It came out low and plain.

"Don't make it a thing."

"I'm not making it a thing."

"You're making it a little bit of a thing."

"Well," he said, and his voice had gone back toward normal, rough around the edges, "you've just stolen my best line, Clearwater. Allow a man a moment to grieve."

She snorted.

They sat. The pool poured on over its lip into the dark. Overhead, faint at first, then less faint, the north went pale green, and the light came up through the steam in long slow ribbons, so that the whole pool and the trees around it and the two of them in it went the color of the inside of a wave.

She looked at it. Then she looked at him, looking at it, with his wet hair standing up and the green light on his face and the steam coming off his shoulders.

Her wolf rolled over, all the way, and lay there with its belly to the sky.

*Fine,* she thought at it. *Fine. You win.*

She shifted along the gravel. A foot. Two. Until her shoulder was against his under the water, and it was like leaning against the side of a woodstove, and he went completely still.

"Don't make it weird," she said.

And she kissed him.

It was clumsy. She'd forgotten how. The last person she'd kissed was Sam Uley, in his truck, on the bluff over First Beach, a few days before Emily came to visit, and she'd been eighteen and she'd thought she'd be kissing him for the rest of her life. She caught the corner of Harry's mouth more than the middle of it and had to fix it. His face was wet and hot and he tasted like hot spring and, very faintly, chocolate.

He didn't grab her. She'd have bitten him if he'd grabbed her, and maybe he knew that. He stayed exactly where he was for one long second, letting her do it, and then one of his hands came up out of the water and touched the side of her face, very lightly, the way you'd touch an animal you'd been warned might bolt. And he kissed her back.

Her wolf went out of its mind.

She pulled back first. Not far. Their foreheads were almost touching. The steam went up between them.

Then she did what she'd done in the pumpkin field and on Main Street and on top of a mountain at the end of the world. She reached around inside her own head. Checked every corner of it.

Her head. Her thoughts. Her mother was still her mother and Seth was still Seth and the mountain was still the mountain. Nothing had slid. Nothing had fallen away. There wasn't any gravity pulling her toward him that she hadn't walked into on her own two feet.

There was just her. Wanting something. And having gone and got it.

"You checked," he said quietly. He was watching her face. "Just then. You went somewhere and looked around."

"Yeah."

"Everything where you left it?"

"Yeah."

"Good," he said, and meant it.

She looked at him, close, in the green light. She thought about saying something. She couldn't think what.

Out in the marsh the moose came back, sloshing, and stopped thirty feet from the pool with water streaming off its belly. It stood there and looked at the two of them with an expression of deep disapproval. Then it snorted, loud and wet, and turned its back on them, and waded away.

Leah put her forehead down on Harry's shoulder and laughed until she couldn't breathe.

---

The Sunday after that, she called home the way she'd promised, and her brother answered the phone.

"Clearwater residence," Seth said, in his best grown-up voice, which cracked in the middle of *residence.*

Leah stood at the pay phone outside the gas station with the steel cord in her fist and couldn't say anything at all.

"Hello?" Seth said. A pause. Then, completely differently: "*Lee?*"

"Hey, Seth."

He made a noise down the line she'd never heard him make. Then he was yelling, away from the phone — "*MOM! MOM, IT'S LEAH, IT'S* — no, *I* got it, I *got* it, go *away* — " — and there was a scuffle and a thump, and her mother saying something sharp in the background, and then Seth was back, breathing hard, like he'd run a mile.

"You're okay," he said. "You're really okay. Mom said, but — you're *okay.*"

"I'm okay. Seth — "

"I thought you were dead." It came out of him in a rush. "I'm sorry, I know Mom told you, I just — you were there, all the way up past Port Townsend, and you were getting quieter and quieter, and I kept *talking* to you, and then there was this — " He stopped. "And then you weren't there. And Jake kept saying it was distance, and I *knew* it was probably distance, but I couldn't — I couldn't *feel* you, Lee. Anywhere. I'd never not been able to feel you since we phased."

She leaned her forehead against the steel box. "I'm sorry."

"It's okay."

"It's not. I didn't think. I was so — I didn't *think* about you, Seth. Not once. I was so happy to be by myself that I didn't think about what it'd feel like back there."

"I know," he said. "It's okay. I mean — I get it. Honestly. I've been in Sam's head too." He was quiet a second. "And I've been in yours."

She closed her eyes.

"Mom says you're washing dishes," Seth said. He was trying to sound normal. He was mostly managing it. "In Canada. Like, actual Canada."

"Actual Canada."

"Is it cold?"

"It was minus nine this morning."

"*Cool,*" Seth breathed, with total sincerity, and she laughed and felt her eyes sting at the same time.

He told her things. He told her the way he'd always told her things, since he was little, all of it at once, in no particular order. That Bella was a vampire now and she was weird about it but kind of okay, like she still sounded like Bella, but she smelled like the rest of them. That she'd thrown him into a tree. That she'd done it because she found out Jacob had imprinted on the baby and went for Jake, and Seth had got in the way, and she'd busted his shoulder.

"She *what?*"

"It's *fine,* it healed in a day. Dr. Cullen set it. She said sorry like eight hundred times." A pause. "Honestly it was kind of awesome. She's *really* strong."

"Seth."

"And Jake calls the baby *Nessie,*" Seth said, "and that's actually why Bella went for him."

Leah lifted her head off the phone box. "*Nessie?* Like the *Loch Ness monster?*"

"I *know.*"

"He named his imprint after a *lake monster.*"

"Bella said exactly that. Well — she *screamed* exactly that." He was laughing now. "She's — Lee, she's so weird. She's like three weeks old and she looks like she's one already. She grows like every day. And she doesn't talk much, but she puts her hand on your face and you just — see stuff. What she's thinking. Pictures. Like the pack, kind of, only one way." He was quiet. "She showed me Jake. What she thinks about Jake. It was nice."

Leah didn't say anything.

"Jake's happy," Seth said. "Like — actually happy. I haven't seen him happy since before Bella. He just sits there holding her and smiling like an idiot. It's kind of gross."

"I bet."

"He says to tell you hi. He says to tell you it's way too quiet without you yelling at everybody in his head."

That got a laugh out of her, short and surprised.

"Lee." Seth's voice changed. Got careful. Got young. "Are you coming home?"

The question hung there.

Across the street a dog was barking at a truck. The lake was blue. The first real snow was on the mountains behind it, low now, coming down the slopes a little further every morning.

"I don't know," she said.

"Okay."

"I'm not saying no."

"Okay." He breathed out. "Mom cries sometimes. When she thinks I'm not looking. In the kitchen. She stands at the sink and doesn't do anything."

Leah closed her eyes again.

"Don't tell her I told you," Seth said quickly. "She'd kill me."

"I won't."

"And she's at Charlie's a lot. She's helping him. With — you know. Everything with Bella. Charlie knows about us now. Jake phased in his yard."

"He *what?*"

"It's a long story. Charlie took it really well, actually. For Charlie. He mostly said *okay* a lot and then went and got a beer." Seth hesitated. "Mom goes over there a lot," he said again, in a voice that was trying not to mean anything.

Leah didn't say anything to that.

"Put her on," Leah said. "Before my card runs out. And Seth — "

"Yeah?"

"I'm sorry. About the — about going out like that. In your head."

"Lee," Seth said. "You came back in. On the phone. That counts."

And he gave the phone to their mother, and Leah stood with her forehead against the cold steel and listened to her mother say *mm-hm* at her for six more minutes, and didn't say a single word about hot springs, or bison, or a moose with opinions, or who'd been sitting next to her when it snorted. Her mother said *mm-hm* anyway.

---

"Thanksgiving," Dot said, the Wednesday before. "Monday. Four o'clock. Here."

Leah looked up from the sink. "You're open on Thanksgiving?"

"I'm *closed* on Thanksgiving. Which is why it's here." Dot was making pastry, cutting lard into flour with two knives, fast. "Every year since Lionel died. Anybody in town who's got nowhere better to be. Wes. Ray, because his wife left him. Gus and Arvid and Lyle, because they've got nowhere better to be since about 1985. Brynn and her mom. Whatever strays turn up." She didn't look up. "You're a stray."

"Okay."

"Bring your fancy friend."

Leah's hands stopped in the water.

"He's been on my dock twice since Brynn's hot springs," Dot said, cutting lard. "Ray's seen him. Wes has seen him. Lorraine Halvorsen's seen him, and when Lorraine's seen something, the whole valley's seen it by suppertime. Bring him. I want a look at him in daylight." She sniffed. "And his chef. Poor bastard's been up in that lodge for a month by himself cooking risotto for nobody."

---

She told him that night, on the dock, with the mirror in her hand and his face in it. He was somewhere in a dark stone room with a lot of shelves behind him, and he'd answered on the first word.

"Thanksgiving," he repeated. "Is that the turkey one?"

"Yeah. Canadian. It's a month early."

"Lovely. I'd be delighted." He paused. "Can I bring something?"

She looked at him.

"I'm asking," he said. "As instructed. You said if it's somebody you know, I ask. You know Dot."

Leah sat with that. She hadn't expected him to remember. She'd half expected to show up at the Galena on Monday and find a crate of champagne on the counter and a new walk-in freezer in the kitchen with a bow on it.

"Bring something you made," she said. "With your hands. Not the chef. Not bought. Not — " she waved a hand at the mirror — "*magicked.*"

He looked at her through the glass for a moment, and the corner of his mouth went up.

"Right," Harry said. "Challenge accepted."

---

The Galena at four o'clock on Thanksgiving Monday had its CLOSED sign up and its windows steamed solid, and every table in the place had been shoved together down the middle into one long one and covered with three different tablecloths that didn't match.

It smelled like turkey. Sage and butter and onion and roasting fat. Potatoes. Something with cinnamon. Leah stood in the doorway with snow on her shoulders, because it had started coming down at noon, big slow flakes out of a white sky, and felt the smell go all the way down to her toes.

Everybody was there. Wes with his beard combed. Ray in a sweater somebody had knitted him a long time ago. The three old men from the corner table, who turned out to be Gus and Arvid and Lyle, already arguing about whether the Canucks' new goalie was worth losing Bertuzzi over. Brynn in a dress, waving, and beside her a plump woman with Brynn's face twenty-five years on who could only be Lorraine. Two of the highway crew guys. And at the end of the table, out of uniform in a blue fleece, with her dark braid down her back, Constable Trudel.

Leah's stomach tightened.

"Washington," Dot said, coming out of the kitchen with a gravy boat in each hand. "Sit. Where's your — "

The bell over the door jingled behind Leah.

She smelled him before she turned. Earth after rain and pine and cold stone, and snow. And butter. And burnt sugar.

Harry stood on the threshold in his waxed jacket with snow in his hair, holding a glass pie dish in both hands with a dish towel folded under it. Behind him, half a step back, stood a skinny man of about thirty in a white chef's jacket under a parka, with a long sad face and dark rings under his eyes, holding a bottle of wine against his chest like a hostage.

The whole table looked at them.

"Evening," Harry said. "Sorry we're late. There was an incident with the oven." He nodded at the man behind him. "This is Danny. Danny, everyone."

"Hi," said Danny, faintly.

Dot looked at Harry for a long moment, the way she'd looked at Leah over the counter on her first morning. Then she looked at Danny. Her face changed slightly.

"You're the chef."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You've been up in that lodge a month."

"Five weeks," said Danny. "On Tuesday."

"Sit down by Wes," Dot said. "Ray, get the man a drink." And, as Danny went past her, she put a hand on his shoulder for a second, the way Leah had seen her do to Brynn exactly once, and then took it away like it hadn't happened.

Then Dot looked at the dish in Harry's hands.

"What's that?"

"Treacle tart."

"What's treacle?"

"Golden syrup, mostly. And breadcrumbs. And lemon." He held it out. "It's English. Bit of a — national treasure. I made it."

Dot took the dish. She looked down at it. The pastry was golden on one side and distinctly brown on the other, and along one edge there was a dark crescent where it had caught.

"It caught," she said.

"That's the incident with the oven."

"You made this."

"Danny supervised," Harry said. "From across the kitchen. With his hands over his eyes."

At the table, Danny nodded. "He wouldn't let me touch it," he said, to nobody in particular. "He kept saying *she said with my hands.* I didn't know who *she* was." He accepted a beer from Ray and drank about a third of it. "I still don't."

Several people looked at Leah.

She sat down. She did not go red. It was the heat of the kitchen.

---

Dot said grace. It was short. She thanked God for the food and the roof and the people at the table, and for Lionel, wherever he'd gone, and asked Him to keep the highway open through January, and said amen like she was closing a door. Then she handed Ray the carving knife.

Ray carved with a great deal of confidence and no particular skill, and halfway through the first breast Dot took the knife away from him without a word. Arvid said it was the best turkey he'd had since 1971. Lyle said Arvid had said that every year since 1972. Lorraine Halvorsen asked Harry where in England he was from, and Brynn kicked Leah under the table three times in a row.

Leah ate. Turkey and stuffing and mashed potatoes swimming in gravy, and a thing with sweet potatoes and marshmallows that Wes had brought, which everyone pretended not to like and which disappeared in four minutes. She ate three plates. Beside her, Harry ate four. Down the table, she watched the highway crew guys notice and start counting.

"Kell," said Constable Trudel.

Leah's fork stopped.

Trudel was talking to Wes, across from her. Easy. Off duty, a glass of wine in her hand, her cheeks pink from the heat of the room. "That fella in your number twelve. The one with the rental. Funny thing."

"What's funny?" Wes said.

"He called me. That Friday. Said he'd gone up to Dease Lake on a charter and he'd sort the car out himself." Trudel turned her glass slowly by the stem. "I remember the call. I could tell you what he said word for word. Time of day. Where I was standing." She frowned at the wine. "I just can't for the life of me remember what his voice sounded like."

Wes shrugged. "Bad line, probably. Dease Lake."

"Probably," Trudel said. She was still frowning. Then she shook her head and laughed and drank. "Getting old. Forget my own name next."

Leah looked down at her plate.

Beside her, Harry had gone very still. She didn't look at him. She didn't need to. She could feel it through the six inches of air between their shoulders, the way she'd felt the heat of him in the pool: something in him going quiet and flat and paying close attention. His fork had stopped too.

Then it started again. He asked Lyle a question about the Canucks, and Lyle was off and running about some goalie called Luongo, and the moment closed over like water.

Leah didn't open it again. She filed it with the scars.

---

Dot brought out the desserts at seven. Pumpkin pie. Apple pie. Saskatoon pie, which Leah had already decided was hers. And on a plate by itself at the end of the counter, the treacle tart with its burnt crescent turned to the back.

Dot cut a slice of it. A small one. She put it on a saucer and stood at the end of the counter and ate it with a fork, slowly, while the whole table pretended not to watch.

She ate the whole slice. She put the fork down.

"Who taught you pastry?" she said.

"Nobody," Harry said. "I taught myself."

"How old?"

He thought about it. "Eight. Nine. My aunt liked things a certain way, and I did the cooking. There was a lot of trial and error." He shrugged. "Mostly error, early on."

Dot looked at him over her glasses. A long look. The table had gone quiet, the way the café went quiet when a stranger walked in.

Leah looked at her plate. *A cupboard under the stairs,* she thought. *Spiders. He named them.* Eight years old and cooking for people who kept him in a cupboard. Standing on a stool, probably, to reach the stove.

"Hm," said Dot.

She cut herself a second slice. Bigger.

"Good crust," she said, and took it back into the kitchen with her.

Down the table, Arvid leaned over to Lyle. "Dot's never said *good crust* to anybody in her life," he said, in the carrying whisper of the slightly deaf.

"She said it to Lionel," said Lyle.

"Once," said Gus. "In 1968. And she married him."

The whole table laughed. Brynn kicked Leah under the table again. Harry looked at his plate with the expression of a man who has just been handed something and isn't quite sure where to put it down.

Leah laughed with the rest of them.

And then, without any warning at all, with the saskatoon pie in front of her and the snow coming down outside the steamed windows and Ray and Dot arguing in the kitchen doorway about the gravy, she thought about her dad.

Thanksgiving in La Push, the American one, the last Thursday in November. The kitchen table with the leaf in it and the folding chairs from the community center. Her dad at the head of it with the electric carving knife he'd bought off the TV and was so proud of, singing *I see a bad moon a-rising* over the turkey in his terrible voice while her mother swatted him with a dish towel. Seth stealing the crispy skin off the drumsticks when he thought nobody was watching. Sam at the table the last Thanksgiving before, his knee against hers under it.

Her dad laughing so hard at his own joke he had to put the knife down.

The table at the Galena was loud and warm and full of strays, and she liked every one of them, and none of them were hers.

She put down her fork. She got up.

"Bathroom," she said, to nobody in particular, and went out the front door into the snow instead.

---

He found her ten minutes later on the dock.

She'd heard the bell, and his boots on the porch, and then nothing. She was standing at the end with her hands jammed in her hoodie and the snow coming down all around her, big wet flakes melting the second they touched her face. The lake was black. The lodge across the bay was a dark shape with one light in it.

He came and stood next to her. Not too close.

"Dot sent me out with this," he said, and held out a plate. Saskatoon pie. A big slice. A fork stuck in it. "She said, and I quote, *go get Washington, she's out there sulking.*"

"I'm not sulking."

"I told her that. She told me to mind my own business and take the pie."

Leah took the pie.

She ate it standing up, at the end of the dock, in the snow. He didn't say anything. He stood there beside her with his hands in his pockets and the snow landing in his hair and melting, the way it melted in hers.

"You heard her," Leah said, when the pie was gone. "Trudel. About Kell."

"Yes."

"She can't remember his voice."

"No," Harry said. "She can't. Because he never called her. Solveig built her a memory of a phone call and put it in, and it's a good memory, it's got the time and the place and the words, but nobody can build a voice that well. So there's a gap where the voice ought to be. She'll notice it, now and then. For the rest of her life, probably. Like a draft from a window she can't find."

Leah looked at him.

"That's what we do," he said. "That's the job. Somebody sees something they're not meant to, and we go in and tidy it, and sometimes the tidying shows."

"That's what *you* do."

"Yes," he said.

He didn't argue. He stood there in the snow and let the word sit.

"I'd have been in her notebook," Leah said. "If Solveig hadn't fixed it. Trudel would've called somebody in Washington."

"Very likely."

"So she's got a draft in her head because of me."

"She's got a draft in her head because of Kroll," Harry said. "And me." He was quiet. "I'll ask Solveig to look at it again. It won't ever be right. It might be less wrong."

Leah nodded slowly. She looked at the empty plate in her hands.

"That's not why you came out," Harry said.

"No."

He waited. The snow came down.

"My dad used to sing over the turkey," Leah said. "Every year. Creedence." She stopped. "He couldn't carve either."

"No," Harry agreed. "Ray really can't."

"It's a good table," Leah said. "In there. It's a good — they're good people. Dot's good people."

"They are."

"They're not mine."

He didn't say anything.

She looked out across the black lake at the one light in the lodge, and the snow falling into the water without a sound, and the dark mountains beyond with their white tops she couldn't see. She'd run eighteen hundred miles to get away from a table like the one in her mother's kitchen. And she'd sat at a stranger's table tonight with turkey and gravy and saskatoon pie, and the whole time some stupid part of her had been looking around it for her brother.

"I miss home," she said. It came out of her rough. "I didn't think I would. I ran all this way. And I miss it."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"Yeah," Harry said. "That happens."

He didn't tell her what to do about it. He didn't tell her anything. He just stood there next to her at the end of Dot's dock with snow in his hair, and after a while she leaned sideways, not much, just enough that her shoulder was against his arm, and he let her, and they watched the snow fall into the lake until Dot came out on the café porch and yelled at them both to get inside before they froze to death, which was never going to happen to either of them, and they went.

---

The snow stayed.

It came down all night after Thanksgiving and all the next day, and when it stopped on Wednesday there was a foot of it on Main Street and the plow had been through twice and the lake had frozen out thirty feet from shore, gray and rough, with the open water black beyond it. Brynn came in wearing snow pants under her apron. Gus and Arvid and Lyle stopped arguing about the Canucks and started arguing about whether the highway would close before Halloween.

Thursday morning Leah walked to work in the blue dark at a quarter to six, with the snow squeaking under her boots and her breath going up straight in the still air, and somewhere between the motel and the café she knew.

Nobody needed her back. Seth was fine; she'd heard it in his voice. Jacob was happy. Her mother had Charlie's kitchen table to sit at, apparently. Nobody was going to get hurt if she stayed in Turnagain and washed Dot's dishes until she was forty.

She just wanted to go home.

She wanted to stand on First Beach in the rain. She wanted to eat her mother's chowder at the kitchen table and listen to Seth talk with his mouth full. She wanted to go home as herself, on her own four feet, because she'd decided to. Because she'd come eighteen hundred miles to find out what she wanted, and it turned out that part of what she wanted had been sitting at the end of the road the whole time, behind her.

It was the stupidest thing she'd ever figured out. She stood in the middle of Main Street in the snow and laughed at herself, once, out loud. Wes, in the window of the motel office, lifted his coffee mug at her.

She went in and washed Dot's dishes.

---

After close she went around the lake.

It was eleven miles, most of it snowy spruce and deadfall past the point where the shore road gave up. She did it on two legs, at a run, because she wanted to arrive with hands.

The lodge sat on its point with snow on its green roof and its windows lit yellow, bigger than it looked from across the lake, the logs gone silver with age. The yellow floatplane had been hauled up the beach for the winter under a tarp.

She smelled garlic and wine and butter from fifty yards out. And him.

The door opened before she reached the porch steps.

"Hello," Harry said.

He was in his shirtsleeves, a dark sweater with the sleeves pushed up, a dish towel over one shoulder. Behind him, in a big open room with a stone fireplace you could have parked a car in, Danny the chef stood at the stove with a wooden spoon, looking at her with naked curiosity.

"Hi," Leah said.

"You came round the lake."

"Yeah."

"On foot. In the snow."

"Yeah."

"Come in," he said, and stepped back. "There's risotto. There's always risotto. Danny's mushroom risotto has reached a sort of — spiritual plane. And there's chocolate." He hesitated. "I've been meaning to give you the chocolate for three weeks. You keep not coming over."

"You never asked me over."

"You'd have said no."

"Probably," Leah admitted, and went in.

---

The chocolate was in a wooden box the size of a small suitcase on the long table by the window, with a name and a street in Brussels burned into the lid. Inside, in rows of little paper cups, were shells and leaves and little domes, dark and milk and white. A few cups were empty.

"Danny," Harry said, looking at the empty cups.

"I was *guarding* them," Danny said from the stove, without turning around. "Quality control."

Leah took one. Dark, a little dome. She bit it in half and a soft hazelnut middle came out, and it was so good she stood still with it in her mouth for a while and didn't say anything.

"The praline," Harry said, watching her face. "Told you."

"I'm not crying."

"I didn't say you were."

She ate the other half, and two more. Danny brought out risotto, and then, after a look at the two of them, put the whole pot on the table with a ladle in it and took his own bowl and a paperback upstairs.

They ate. The fire popped. Outside the big windows the frozen lake was blue-white in the dark, and across it, very small, the lights of Turnagain. She could pick out the Galena. The motel. The one streetlight on Main Street.

She put her spoon down.

"I'm going home," she said.

He went still.

He didn't put his own spoon down. He just stopped, with it halfway, and looked at her across the table, and his face didn't change at all. That was how she knew it had landed.

"When?" he said.

"Saturday. Maybe. I have to tell Dot." She looked at the bowl. "I just — I want to. Go. I figured it out this morning in the middle of Main Street. It's stupid."

"It's not stupid."

"I ran eighteen hundred miles to get away from it."

"And now you want to run back." He put the spoon down, finally. "That's allowed."

"I know it's allowed."

"Do you?"

She looked up. He was watching her, his head a little to one side.

"Nobody's making me," she said. "That's the thing. Nobody needs me back. Seth's fine. Jake's fine. My mom's — fine, probably. I could stay here. I could stay here till I'm forty. I just don't want to." She breathed out. "I want to go home. Me. On my own feet. Because I said so."

"Then you should go," he said quietly.

She looked at him.

There was a question sitting in her chest. It had been sitting there since Main Street, since before Main Street, since the end of Dot's dock on Thanksgiving, maybe since a hot spring in the woods, and every time she'd tried to look straight at it, she'd looked at something else instead.

She looked at it now.

It was a big question. Bigger than *third date.* Bringing somebody home meant her mother. And Seth. And Jacob, and the pack, and Sam, and Emily, and the whole rez watching. It meant this — whatever this was — out in the open on First Beach, where everybody had watched her get engaged at eighteen. She didn't know which scared her more, him saying no or him saying yes.

She sat there with it for a long minute. She didn't say anything. He didn't either. The fire popped. Upstairs, Danny turned a page.

"Do you want to come?" she said.

Harry didn't move.

He sat across the table from her with his hands flat on the wood on either side of his bowl and looked at her. One second. Two. She watched something go across his face that she didn't have a name for, and then go away again, and then he breathed in through his nose, slow, and let it out.

"Hang on," he said.

He reached into his back pocket and took out a mirror.

It was a different one. Bigger than hers, rectangular, in a plain black frame, with a crest stamped into the back of it that she couldn't make out. He held it up in front of his face.

"Bassi," he said to it.

The glass clouded and cleared. Leah leaned a little, so she could see past his shoulder.

A man in the mirror. Sixty or so, with a long lined face, a big white mustache, and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead. He was in a dark red dressing gown, sitting up in bed with a lamp on behind him and a window full of black sky, his white hair flat on one side from the pillow.

"Potter," the man said. An accent, Italian. Very tired. "It is six o'clock in the morning."

"Is it? Sorry. Morning, Aurelio."

"Is someone dead?"

"Not yet today. I'm taking some leave."

The man in the mirror looked at him. "Leave."

"Holiday. Vacation. The thing with the beaches."

"*Now?*"

"Now."

The man pulled his reading glasses down onto his nose and looked through them at Harry as though Harry might look different through glass. "For how long?"

Harry glanced across the table at Leah. He looked at her for about a second. Then he looked back at the mirror.

"No idea," he said.

"*No idea.*" Bassi took the glasses off again. He rubbed his eyes with his thumb and finger. "Potter. You have the tribunal in Lyon on the thirtieth. You have the Kroll report, which Brekke informs me does not exist. You have the Bulgarian business, and the Lincolnshire sword, and nineteen open files, *nineteen,* and three governments asking me every week where you are and I do not ever know. You cannot simply — what makes you think you can simply take off whenever you — "

"Aurelio," Harry said.

The man stopped.

"I'm fucking Harry Potter," said Harry.

And he put the mirror face down on the table, and the voice coming out of it cut off in the middle of a word.

There was silence in the big room. The fire crackled. Upstairs, very distinctly, Danny stopped turning pages.

Leah stared at him across the table.

Harry put the mirror back in his pocket. He picked up his spoon. He looked at her with his eyebrows up, calm, as though he'd just finished a phone call about the weather.

"Right," he said. "Where are we headed?"

Leah opened her mouth. She shut it again. Something was coming up in her chest that she couldn't tell yet was a laugh or something else.

*Where.* She'd never told him. *The coast. Washington. There's a reservation. I'm not saying which.* She'd lain on a rock on top of a mountain and kept it from him, the way she'd kept the tribe's name out of Solveig's notebook, because it was the one thing she had that belonged to more people than her.

She looked at him sitting there with his spoon, waiting.

"La Push," Leah said.

He tried it out. "La Push."

"It's French. Sort of. *La bouche.* The mouth. Of the river." She swallowed. "It's on the coast. There's a river and a beach and a lot of rain. And my mom. And my brother." She stopped. "And a whole lot of people who are going to look at you funny."

"People always look at me funny." He considered. "How far?"

"Eighteen hundred miles. About."

"I could have us there in four seconds."

"No."

"Didn't think so." The corner of his mouth went up. "On your own four feet?"

"On my own four feet."

"Then we run," said Harry, and went back to his risotto.

She told Dot on Friday, at the sink, with her back to her.

Dot didn't say anything for a while. The flat-top hissed. Then: "Told you. Gone by Halloween."

"It's the thirteenth."

"Close enough." Dot flipped something. "Trudel wanted to know if you were heading off. I'll tell her."

"Thanks."

"Don't thank me." A long pause. "That job's here. If you come back."

Leah turned around. Dot was looking at the grill.

"Okay," Leah said.

"Don't *okay* me," said Dot. Her voice was rougher than usual. "Wash my pans."

Saturday morning, early, with the sky going pink over the frozen lake, she settled up with Wes.

He took her money for the last week and wrote her a receipt in pencil on a pad from 1994. Then he looked at her over the counter.

"Anything else?"

Leah looked at the key fob in her hand. Plastic. A 6 on it.

"The moose," she said.

Wes's eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch, which for Wes was a shout. "The painting?"

"Can I buy it?"

He looked at her a long time. Then he reached under the counter and came up with a screwdriver.

Brynn was waiting outside the Galena when Leah came across the street with the moose under her arm. She'd heard. Everybody had heard. She had her ski jacket on over her apron and her arms wrapped around herself and her eyes were already red.

"You're *leaving,*" she said.

"Yeah."

"With *him.*"

"Yeah."

"You've been here five *weeks.*" Brynn's voice wobbled. "You never told me *anything.* I don't even know where you're *from.* I don't know your *last name.* You just — walked in out of nowhere in your *shorts* and ate five breakfasts and now you're walking *out* again — "

"Clearwater," Leah said.

Brynn stopped.

"My last name's Clearwater," Leah said. "I'm from La Push. It's a reservation on the coast in Washington. My dad's name was Harry and he died a year and a half ago and that's — part of why I left. I've got a little brother who's fifteen and too tall and he's the best person I know." She shifted the moose under her arm. "That's what you get. That's everything."

Brynn stared at her with her mouth open and her eyes full.

Leah dug in her hoodie pocket and came out with a napkin from the Galena. She'd written on it the night before, in pen, in her neat small hand. Her mom's address. Her mom's phone number.

"There's an ocean," Leah said, and held it out. "On First Beach. Right there. It's cold and gray and it rains all the time and the sand's the color of a sidewalk. But it's the ocean." She cleared her throat. "When you get your nursing thing done. Before Sydney. If you want to see one. You come down. You can stay. My mom'll feed you till you can't move."

Brynn looked at the napkin. She took it. She held it in both hands like it was a ticket.

Then she flung her arms around Leah's neck, moose and all, and cried into her hoodie.

Leah let her. She put the arm that wasn't holding the moose around Brynn's back and stood there in the snow outside the Galena while Brynn soaked her shoulder. People hanging onto her usually made her want to climb out of her own skin. This felt fine.

Through the steamed-up window behind Brynn she could see Dot at the counter, not looking at them. Very carefully not looking at them.

"You're the *worst* at this," Brynn said into her hoodie.

"I know."

"Text me. Or — you don't have a phone. *Write* me. Like a pioneer."

"Okay."

Brynn pulled back. She wiped her face with her sleeve. She looked past Leah's shoulder and her eyes went wide.

Leah didn't have to turn around. Earth after rain. Pine. Cold stone.

"Morning," Harry said behind her.

Brynn looked at him for a long, long moment. Something moved behind her eyes. Some faint shape of something — a man on a hill in a long gray coat, a voice from a long way off. It came and went across her face like a cloud across the sun and didn't stay.

"Have we met?" Brynn said slowly.

"Briefly," Harry said. "You won't remember. You were having a rough night."

Brynn kept looking at him a second longer. Then she looked at Leah, and up at Harry, and back at Leah, and her face did something complicated and ended up somewhere close to delighted.

"Oh my *God,*" she whispered.

"Goodbye, Brynn," Leah said.

The door of the Galena banged open. Dot came out onto the porch in her apron with a pie in her hands. Saskatoon. Wrapped in tinfoil, still warm, so the smell of it came across the snow and hit Leah in the face.

Dot came down the steps and put it in Leah's free hand on top of the moose.

"For your mother," Dot said.

Then she looked at Harry. Up and down, the stray-dog look, the one Leah was never going to forget.

"You," Dot said.

"Ma'am."

"She can take care of herself."

"I know," Harry said. "I've watched her do it."

Dot looked at him a while longer. Then she nodded, once, as if he'd finally said a thing she'd decided to accept, and turned around and went back up the steps.

At the door she stopped, with her hand on the frame and her back to them.

"Wash your hands, Washington," Dot said, "wherever you end up."

The bell jingled. The door shut. Through the window Leah watched her go back behind the counter and pick up a rag and start wiping something that was already clean.

---

Behind the deadfall above the lake road, with the moose and the pie and the duffel bag of Mercantile clothes at her feet in the snow, Leah looked at all of it and then at the cord in her hand.

"May I?" Harry said.

She looked at him.

"I can carry it," he said. "Wherever I keep things when I'm — " he made a vague gesture that took in his whole body — "the other way. It'll come out exactly as it went in. Pie included. Still warm, if you like."

She thought about it. She looked at the moose. Then she nodded at the moose and the pie and the duffel. "Those."

He looked at them, and they weren't there anymore.

She kept the rest. The ziplock with her license and her money and the picture of her and her dad on the boat. The mirror in its leather pouch. She tied them to her ankle herself, three hard knots. Then she told him to turn around, and he did, and she stripped and held her clothes out behind her without looking, and those went wherever the moose had gone.

Then she phased, and he did, and they ran.

---

They went south.

Down the Turnagain and over the passes with the snow deep on top and the larches gone gold along the slopes. Past the place where the grizzly sow had stood under her cottonwood. Down out of the snow into the dry pine country, where it was autumn again, as if they'd run backwards in time.

She let him in, the first night. Out on the plateau with the small round lakes like dropped coins, lying on the frost with their sides heaving. He'd asked again, out loud — *May I?* — and she'd dipped her head, and after that they talked. Not much. They were mostly too busy running.

Down the Fraser canyon before dawn, where the river roared through its slot under the cable car, and the salmon were gone, only a few battered red bodies turning in the eddies.

On the second morning they came to a cut through the forest twenty feet wide, running dead straight over the ridges in both directions, with a little aluminum obelisk in the middle. A gray jay sat on top of it. It might have been the same jay. It cocked its head at them and didn't move.

*Customs,* Leah thought.

"This is very illegal," Harry said. "For the record."

*You're a cop.*

"Hit wizard. Different department." He stepped over the line beside her, both of them at once, and looked down at the obelisk. "We'll call it diplomatic immunity."

The jay said something sarcastic and flew off.

They slept that afternoon in the snow above Ross Lake, the two of them curled on a ledge with the turquoise water far below, and she slept nine hours without dreaming. Then they came down the Skagit in the dark, past the dams and the lights of the little company towns.

---

They came out onto the Skagit flats in the gray before dawn on the third day.

Miles of it. Flat as a table, black and wet, the cornfields cut down to stubble and the river somewhere off to the east. It smelled like turned dirt and manure and rain. There'd been rain all night. Her fur was soaked through and it felt wonderful. It smelled like home.

And there was the pumpkin field.

U-PICK, said the hand-painted sign at the corner of the road. OPEN DAILY. Hay bales stacked by a farm stand with a tarp over it. And the field, picked over now, but still scattered with pumpkins, hundreds of them, orange in the gray light, lying among their dead vines.

Leah slowed. She stopped in the middle of the field, in the same rows, maybe the same spot, where she'd rolled on her back six weeks ago and laughed until she couldn't breathe. Away off to the east, huge and white, Mount Baker was standing up off the edge of the world.

Harry stopped beside her.

"Here?" he said quietly.

*Here. This is where they went out.*

She stood still, and listened, harder than she'd ever listened to anything.

Wind in the stubble. Rain dripping off the farm stand. Harry breathing beside her, slow and even. Her own heart.

And then, very faint. Like a nightlight coming on in another room.

Somebody.

Thin as a thread. Grainy, like a voice from the bottom of a stairwell. Somebody running, a long way south, through wet trees, with the smell of the ocean in his nose. Somebody bored and sleepy and humming something tuneless to himself in his head, the way he always did on patrol.

Leah's legs locked.

*Seth?*

The humming stopped.

Nothing. A long nothing. She could feel him stop running, a hundred and twenty miles away. Feel his ears go up.

*...Lee?*

*Hey, Seth.*

*LEAH?*

It came through like a firework. Not words, at first. Just *him,* all of him, bursting up the thread so hard it nearly knocked her over. Joy. Pure, stupid, fifteen-year-old joy, so loud and so bright it filled her whole skull, and behind it, underneath it, a great wash of relief that went out of him like air out of a balloon.

*You're BACK. You're — where ARE you? You're so faint, you're — are you coming HOME? Are you —*

*Skagit. I'm in Skagit. I'm coming home.*

*YOU'RE COMING HOME.* He was running again. She could feel him tearing through the trees on the other end, not going anywhere, just running because he couldn't hold still. *I'm gonna tell Mom. I'm gonna tell Jake. I'm gonna — Lee, I KNEW it was distance, I knew you were too stubborn to — wait.*

He stopped.

She felt him go quiet. Felt him look. The way you looked through somebody else's eyes in there, without meaning to, because whatever they were seeing was right in front of you too.

She was standing in a pumpkin field. And next to her, filling up the whole left side of her vision, was a wall of soaked black fur and a pair of very green eyes.

*Lee,* Seth said, slowly. *What is that.*

*That's Harry.*

*Why is he so BIG?*

*He just is.*

*He's BIGGER than JAKE. Lee, is he a WOLF? Is he one of us? Is there a TRIBE up there? Why's he LOOKING at you like —*

Then he saw the rest.

She'd been holding it down since the border, the way she'd held things down in Sam's pack. But Seth had come up that thread so fast and so happy that she hadn't been ready, and stuff was coming through the cracks. The mine. Kroll's face going to paper. The bar. A caribou on a plateau. A hot spring in the steam, and two people in it, and the green light coming down —

*OH MY GOD,* Seth said. *EW. EW. LEAH.*

*Stop looking.*

*I can't STOP looking, it's IN HERE —*

"Is that your brother?" said Harry, aloud, beside her.

Seth went dead silent.

He'd heard it. Through her ears. A deep voice with an English accent coming out of the mouth of a wolf the size of a pickup truck, in a pumpkin field, in the rain.

*Did that wolf just TALK?*

*Yeah.*

*Like, out LOUD?*

*Yeah.*

"Tell him hello," said Harry. "From me."

"He heard you," Leah told him, inside her own head, where Harry could hear her and Seth couldn't hear him unless it came through her ears. It was the strangest thing she'd ever done. Two conversations at once. One in the pack and one on the step outside the door.

"Then hello, Seth," Harry said, louder, to the field in general. "I've heard a great deal about you. All of it good."

Down at the bottom of the thread, Seth made a sound in his head she'd never heard before. Like a kettle. Like somebody trying not to scream.

*I gotta go,* he said. *I gotta go tell Mom. I gotta — JAKE. I'm gonna get Jake. Don't go anywhere. Don't go ANYWHERE, Lee —*

He was gone. Phased out. She felt the absence where he'd been, and for a second she braced for the old empty panic of the pumpkin field.

It didn't come. She knew where he was now. He was in a kitchen in La Push, yelling for their mother.

Jacob came in twenty minutes later, out on the flats west of Mount Vernon, while she was crossing a dairy pasture full of cows who were extremely upset about it.

He came in slow. Careful. Like somebody opening a door into a room they weren't sure was empty.

*Leah.*

*Hey, Jake.*

She felt him take a long look at her. At the inside of her, the way the Alpha could, without meaning to, the way he couldn't help any more than she could. She felt him find the mine, and the bar, and the plateau, and Liard, and go still at each one.

She didn't stop him. She didn't hide it. He'd see it anyway, and she was tired of hiding.

*Jesus Christ, Leah,* Jacob said finally.

*Yeah.*

He looked out through her eyes at the black wolf loping beside her, keeping pace, not even trying.

*Is it,* Jacob said. He stopped. Started again. *Did you. Is he —*

She knew what he was asking. He'd been in Sam's head too. He'd felt the world tip under his own feet five weeks ago, in a room in the Cullens' house, for a baby. He knew what it looked like, and he was asking.

*No,* she said. *I checked. Every time. It's just me.*

She felt his relief go through the link like a long breath out. He'd been afraid for her. She hadn't expected that.

*Okay,* Jacob said. *Okay.* A pause. *He's — he's really big, Lee.*

*Everybody keeps saying that.*

*And he's a wizard.*

*Apparently.*

*Like with a wand.*

*He doesn't use it much.*

Jacob was quiet for a long time, running somewhere with her, through her. She could feel the trees around him. Cedar. Rain. The faint, rotten-sweet ghost of bleach and burnt sugar on the wind. He was near the Cullens' house.

*Edward's gonna know all this,* Jacob said, *in about ten minutes. Soon as I go back in that house. Soon as Seth does. He's gonna hear it in our heads.*

Leah's ears went back.

"What is it?" Harry said beside her.

*One of them reads minds,* she told him. *The vampires. The one Bella married. Anything Seth and Jake know, he's going to know. You. Kroll. The magic. All of it.*

Harry ran on beside her for a few strides without saying anything.

"Does he now," he said. He sounded interested more than anything else. Then, after a moment: "Your people have a line, you said. A treaty. They don't cross it."

*Not onto our land.*

"Then I'll stay on your side of it," Harry said. "For now. He'll know I'm there. That's fine. I'd rather he knew and kept his distance than came looking. When I've had a proper look at things, I'll go and say hello." His ears flicked. "Politely."

Leah passed it on.

*Politely,* Jacob repeated. She felt him look at the size of the black wolf again. *Yeah. Okay. Tell him that's — yeah.*

He was quiet a while longer. Then, in a different voice, the one she'd heard on the riverbank six weeks ago, too tired and too honest:

*It's good you're coming home, Lee.*

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. He could feel it.

---

They crossed Admiralty Inlet at noon, under a low gray sky, with the lighthouse at Point Wilson standing up white on its spit ahead of them.

She swam it. Forty minutes of cold chop and the tide dragging her north.

Harry walked across.

On top of the water. Paws on the surface, as if it were a frozen pond, with the waves rolling under him and not one of them so much as splashing his legs. He kept pace beside her the whole way, strolling, looking down at her with his head tipped while she swam.

*I'm going to kill you,* she thought, with her chin out of the water.

"Not today," said Harry. "You've a pie to deliver."

---

Sue Clearwater had been sitting on the porch since four o'clock.

Seth had come through the back door at twenty to seven that morning, soaking wet, with his cut-offs on inside out, yelling. It had taken her five minutes to get anything out of him that made sense, and then she'd had to sit down.

*She's coming home. She's in Skagit. She's coming home, Mom, today, tonight, and she's got — there's a guy. He's a wolf. He's — he's a wizard, Mom. Like a real one. And he's HUGE. He's bigger than Jake. And he talks. Like, out loud. As a wolf. And he's English.*

She'd made him say it all twice. Then she'd driven to the Thriftway in Forks for clams and bacon and potatoes, and come home and made chowder, the big pot, enough for twelve, and cleaned the kitchen twice. Charlie had called at three and she'd told him not tonight, and he'd said *okay* in the way he did, which meant he understood more than he was going to say.

Now the chowder was on the back of the stove on low, and the sky over the trees was going dark blue, and she was sitting on the top step of Harry's porch in her coat with a cup of coffee she'd stopped drinking an hour ago.

Seth was inside, pretending to watch television. He'd been pretending for two hours.

The light was almost gone when something moved behind the woodpile.

Sue put the cup down.

Two people came out of the trees. One of them was her daughter.

Leah was in jeans and boots and a gray hoodie Sue had never seen, with her hair loose, longer than it had been, hanging down past her shoulder blades. She walked up through the wet grass the way she always walked, like she was on her way to hit somebody. But her face was different. Sue couldn't have said how. Something had come loose in it.

The man beside her was tall, a little shorter than the boys got after they phased, and broad through the shoulders, in an olive jacket of the kind people wore for hunting in old English movies, and dark trousers and brown boots. His hair was black and stood up in every direction. He was carrying a foil-wrapped pie in one hand and, under the other arm, what appeared to be a framed painting of a moose.

They stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"Hi, Mom," Leah said.

Sue stood up. She didn't remember deciding to. She came down the steps and put her arms around her daughter and held on, and Leah was hot as a stove under the hoodie and smelled like rain and salt water and wet dog and something piney Sue didn't recognize, and she was here. Sue held on. After a second Leah's arms came up around her, stiff at first, then not.

"You ate the chowder," Sue said into her hair.

"I know."

"Eleven days. A *note.* Under the *salt shaker.*"

"I know, Mom."

Sue let go, finally. She held Leah by the shoulders and looked at her. Then she looked past her at the very tall man with the pie and the moose, who was standing a polite distance back on the grass and looking at the porch, not at them. Giving them the moment. She noticed that.

"Mom," Leah said. Her voice had gone careful. "This is Harry."

Sue's hands tightened on her daughter's shoulders before she could stop them.

*Harry.*

She looked at Leah. Leah was looking right back at her, steady, and in her daughter's dark eyes Sue saw that she knew exactly what that name would do, and had known it for weeks, and had brought him home anyway.

"I know," Leah said quietly.

Sue let out a breath. It came out shaky, and turned into something that was almost a laugh.

"Of course it is," she said.

She let go of Leah and turned to the man. He'd come a step closer. Up close he was younger than she'd thought from the porch. Mid-twenties. Green eyes. A sharp jaw with nothing on it, not a nick, not a scar. He shifted the moose under his arm and held out the pie.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said. English. Very. "Harry Potter. I'm sorry about the name. I gather it's a bit much."

"Sue," she said. "And it's fine."

"This is from Dot," Leah said. "My boss. In Turnagain. She sent it for you."

"Saskatoon," Harry said. "It's extraordinary. I've been carrying it eighteen hundred miles and I've not touched it, which I'd like noted somewhere."

Sue took the pie. It was warm. She looked at it, and then at him, and then at the moose.

"That," Leah said, "is a long story."

The front door banged open.

Seth came out of it like a cannonball. He went past Sue and down the steps in one jump and hit his sister at full speed, and she staggered back two steps and caught him, and he picked her up off the ground. He was a head taller than her now. He hadn't been in September. He held her up in the air and spun her around in the wet grass and she was yelling at him to put her down, *put me DOWN, you giant idiot,* and laughing, and he didn't.

When he finally did, he turned around and looked at Harry. Down, a little. Seth was six-four. The last time he'd seen this man, through his sister's eyes in a pumpkin field, he'd been looking up at a wall of black fur.

"Hi," Seth said.

"Hello, Seth."

Seth stared at him. "You're smaller like this."

"Most people say that," Harry said.

Seth grinned, enormous, all teeth, the way their father used to.

---

They ate in the kitchen.

He took his boots off at the door without being asked and set them side by side on the mat. He ate four bowls of chowder and said it was the best thing he'd eaten in Washington, which Leah pointed out was the only thing he'd eaten in Washington, and he said that didn't make it less true. Seth asked him about forty questions and he answered most of them and dodged the rest in a way that made Seth laugh instead of push. He called her *Sue* after she asked him to and not before.

He looked at Leah a lot. Sue had stood by that counter and watched Sam Uley look at Emily the first day, and she would never forget it: like a man falling off a roof. Harry looked at Leah the way you'd look at a long hard road you'd decided to walk anyway, because you wanted to see where it went.

Leah looked at him less. But when she did, Sue saw her face.

Halfway through the second bowl, her daughter stopped with her spoon in the air and sniffed. She looked at the dish drainer, where two coffee mugs stood upside down. One of them was Sue's. The other was the big brown one with the chipped handle that Charlie Swan always used, because it was the only one in the house big enough for him.

Leah looked at the mug. Then at her mother.

"Mm-hm," said Leah.

Sue felt her face go hot.

Across the table, Seth choked on his chowder. Harry looked at the ceiling with great interest.

"Eat your soup," Sue said.

---

It was after nine when Leah stood up and took the bowls to the sink and washed them, all of them, before Sue could get up.

"Your room's made up," Sue said. "And there's the couch. For — " she looked at Harry — "for whoever."

Leah dried her hands. She stood at the sink with her back to the room for a moment, looking out the dark window at the woodpile.

"I can't stay here, Mom," she said. "I mean — I'll be here. All the time. But I can't sleep in my old room. I just can't." She turned around. "We'll find somewhere."

Sue looked at her daughter. Then at the man at the table, who was very carefully saying nothing, and at Seth, who was saying nothing at all for once in his life.

She got up and went to the drawer by the phone, the junk drawer, and dug through it, past the rubber bands and dead batteries and takeout menus from Port Angeles, until she found what she was looking for. A key ring with no key on it. A little brass fish, worn smooth, on a loop of fishing line gone yellow with age.

She held it out to Leah.

"Your father's place," Sue said.

Leah went very still.

"Up on Qw'aatilla," Sue said. "In the rocks above the arch. He told me about it the year we got married. He went up there when he needed to be quiet. When the boat was bad, or the money was bad. After his own father died. He'd be gone a night, sometimes two, and come home and be fine." She put the brass fish in Leah's hand and closed her daughter's fingers around it. "He'd want somebody using it."

Leah looked down at her fist.

"He showed me," she said. Her voice had gone strange. "When I was eleven. Just once. He made me promise not to tell anybody. Not even Seth."

"Hey," said Seth.

"I know," Sue said. "He told me that, too." She smiled, and her eyes stung, and she let them. "He said you were the only one of us who'd understand what it was for."

Leah didn't say anything. She stood in the middle of the kitchen with the brass fish in her fist, and her chin went up the way it had gone up since she was two years old, every time she was trying not to cry.

Then she looked at the man at the table.

He was already getting up.

---

She found it in the dark on the first try.

South along the top of First Beach, with the surf loud below them and the sea stacks black against a sky that was only a little less black. Into the trees at the south end where the land climbed. Up through salal higher than her head and spruce bent sideways by a hundred years of wind, onto the back of the headland. She hadn't been up here since she was eleven. Her feet knew the way anyway.

A jumble of rock under a dead spruce that had come down long ago and gone soft and green with moss. Behind it, low, a crack in the stone you'd never see unless you were looking for it. She went in on her hands and knees. Six feet of tight dark, smelling of wet rock and old smoke. Then the roof went up and she could stand.

"Hang on," Harry said behind her, and a small light came on over his shoulder and hung there in the air. Amber. The color of a lantern.

The cave was the size of her mother's kitchen. Dry. A sandy floor, a low ceiling with a black patch of old smoke on it, a ring of fire-stones under the patch. On the far side, a ragged opening in the rock, as wide as a door, and through it the sound of the ocean and the cold salt wind, and a long way down, white water breaking around the foot of the headland.

His things were where he'd left them.

A folding camp stool with a canvas seat. A Coleman lantern. A coffee can full of fishing lures, rusty hooks and faded feathers. A sleeping bag rolled up in a black garbage bag and tied with twine. And on a flat shelf of rock by the opening, a battered silver boombox with a cassette door, and a cassette in it.

There was a nail driven into a crack beside the entrance. Nothing on it.

Leah opened her fist and looked at the brass fish. Then she hung it on the nail.

She stood there a while with her hand flat on the cold rock beside it.

Harry didn't say anything. He stood just inside the entrance with his little light over his shoulder and waited, and didn't touch anything.

She went to the boombox. She knew what was in it before she looked, but she looked anyway. *Creedence Clearwater Revival. Chronicle.* Side A. The label on the cassette was in her father's handwriting, block capitals, in ballpoint: HARRY'S. KEEP OUT. THIS MEANS YOU SETH.

She laughed. It came out wrong.

She pushed play. Nothing. The batteries had been dead for years.

"May I?" Harry said quietly.

She didn't trust her voice. She nodded.

He didn't move. The little wheels in the cassette window started to turn. There was a hiss, a click, a drumbeat, and a guitar, and then John Fogerty was singing about a bad moon rising, out of a boombox in a cave over the Pacific, tinny and too loud, the way her dad had always played it.

Leah sat down on the camp stool and put her face in her hands.

He let her. He came and sat on the sandy floor a few feet away, with his back against the rock and his knees up, and looked out the opening at the dark ocean, and let the song play all the way through. He didn't say anything. He didn't touch her. When it ended and the next one started, he reached over without getting up and turned it down, a little, so it was background.

After a while she took her hands away from her face.

"Okay," she said. Her voice was rough. "Okay."

"What stays?" Harry said.

She looked around. At the stool. The lantern. The coffee can. The boombox. The nail with the fish.

"All of it," she said. "Exactly where it is."

"Done." He got up and dusted off his trousers. "Anything else, you tell me. Stop me if you hate it."

"What are you going to do?"

"Make it so you can live in it," said Harry, and stood in the middle of the cave with his hands in his pockets, and did.

---

She'd seen him stop a fire-snake in midair. She'd seen him turn a man to dust. She'd never seen him build.

The walls went back.

The rock stayed whole and silent, and the cave just got bigger, the way a room seems to when you turn the lights on, except it kept going. The ceiling rose. The sand went smooth and turned to wide cedar planks that smelled like a fresh-split log. Rugs unrolled themselves across them. The ring of fire-stones sank back into the far wall and became a deep stone hearth, with the old smoke patch above it kept exactly where it was, and a fire caught in it by itself and the smoke went up into the rock and disappeared.

Lights came on along the walls, a dozen of them, small and amber, hanging in the air the way the blue ones had hung at the mine. These were the color of a porch light.

A kitchen opened up along one side. A black iron range, an icebox, a deep sink, shelves, a long wooden table with benches. The sea opening stayed open, rough-edged, exactly the way it had been, and the sound of the surf came through it as loud as ever, but the wind stopped at the edge like it had hit glass.

Two doorways appeared in the back wall, side by side, with heavy wooden doors.

And off to one side, down three steps cut in the rock, a smaller chamber, and in it a pool. A long pool, with a gravel bottom, and water coming up through the gravel at the top end, and steam rising off it in slow white columns into the amber light.

Leah stood up off the stool.

"Is that — "

"Hot spring," said Harry. "More or less. There's a lot of hot water quite a long way down under this coast. I asked some of it to come up."

"You built me the *Alpha pool.*"

"Well," he said, "it's yours. So I suppose that's what it is."

She went to the top of the three steps and looked down at the steam, and something happened to her face that she couldn't stop.

He went on talking, a little fast, to the room in general. "The doors are bedrooms. Left one's yours. Right one's mine. If you want. Or I find a cave of my own, there's an enormous number of caves on this coast — "

"Harry."

He stopped.

"Leave both doors," Leah said.

He looked at her for a long second. Then he nodded.

"Wards," he said, after a moment, in a more normal voice. "I'll do the wards. Anybody who doesn't already know it's here won't find it. Hikers, kids, whatever. They'll just feel like going the other way." He glanced at her. "That's a nudge on the place. Not the person."

"I know."

"And anything cold and dead that comes over your line within a few miles of here, I'll know about it the second it does. Nothing that hurts them." His ears would have gone flat, if he'd had the right ears. She could see it in his face anyway. "I'm not starting a war in your mum's back garden. Not on the first night."

"Second night, maybe."

"Second night's negotiable."

Then he picked up the moose from where he'd leaned it by the entrance.

He looked around the room, at the big stone hearth with its fire going, and the smoke patch above it. He looked at Leah. She looked back at him.

He hung the moose over the fireplace.

It looked down at them from its lake at sunset, with its front legs bending the wrong way at the knee, like a man in a moose costume trying to remember where he'd parked. The fire lit it from below. It had never looked so happy.

Leah laughed so hard she had to sit down on the floor.

---

Later, much later, with the fire burned down to coals and the tape played through twice and turned over, she went out onto the cedar planks in front of the hearth, and stripped, and let the fire take her.

She'd slept in her fur every night on the road home. She wasn't going to stop now.

The second her paws hit the floor, she heard it. Faint. Sleepy. Close.

*...Lee?*

Seth, on patrol, out along the line somewhere north of the river. She could feel the wet ferns around him, the drizzle on his ears. He'd been half dozing on his feet.

*Hey, Seth.*

*Whoa.* He was looking through her eyes at the room. The hearth. The amber lights. The steam drifting up from the three steps to the pool. *Whoa. Is that — is that DAD'S? Is that the — Lee, that's a CAVE. You're in a CAVE. With a HOT TUB. Why does it have a —* He stopped. *Why is there a moose?*

*Go to sleep, Seth.*

*I'm on PATROL.*

*Then go patrol.*

He laughed. In her head. She'd forgotten what that felt like, his laugh coming through from the inside, and it went through her all the way down to her paws.

*Night, Lee,* Seth said. *I'm really glad you're home.*

And then, from further off, so faint she almost missed it, from somebody lying in the wet grass under a lit window in the trees, keeping watch over a sleeping baby:

*Welcome home, Leah.*

*Night, Jake.*

They went quiet. They didn't go away. She could feel them out there, Seth in the ferns and Jacob in the dark, the way you could feel people asleep in the other rooms of a house.

She waited for the panic. For the old crowded feeling, the bus-at-rush-hour feeling, everybody pressing up against her from all sides.

It didn't come.

She'd gone eighteen hundred miles to get her own head back. She had it. It was still hers. There were people in the other rooms, that was all, and the door was on her side, and she'd come back and opened it herself.

On the rug in front of the hearth, the floor took the weight of something very large and didn't creak. A wall of black fur lay down a few feet from her, chin on its paws, and a pair of green eyes looked at her in the light of the coals.

"All right, Clearwater?" Harry said, low.

She thought about it. Really thought about it, the way she'd learned to on a rock at the end of the world.

*Yeah,* she said. *I'm all right.*

"Good," he said, and put his head down.

She lay there and listened. The surf. The fire ticking. The tape, hissing out the last of side B. Her brother out in the rain, humming to himself. Somebody very big breathing slow and even on the other side of the rug.

Over the hearth, the moose watched over all of it.

Leah Clearwater put her chin down on her paws, at home, in her father's cave, with the door open, and slept.
