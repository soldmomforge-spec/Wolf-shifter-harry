# Specimen

## Chapter One: North American Example

---

**Forks, Washington — Sunday, October 1, 2006 — 7:41 a.m.**

The rain came in off the Pacific at a slant and stayed. It had been doing that for sixteen days, which was exactly as long as Harry had been in Forks, and the town took it the way the town took most things: with a shrug and a refill.

He had the booth by the front window. He'd had it every morning since his second day, when he'd worked out that the waitress put people she planned on talking to by the window and people she planned on ignoring in the back corner. A man who sat in the corner every morning with his back to the wall got remembered. A man who sat in the window, read the *Peninsula Daily News* and complained about the Seahawks' secondary got waved at.

So he sat in the window, and he got waved at.

The diner smelled of fryer oil, burnt coffee and wet wool. Two old men in suspenders held down the far end of the counter, arguing about a gravel permit out on the Bogachiel with the slow, comfortable hostility of men who'd been having the same argument since the Carter administration. A young mother in a fleece jacket was cutting pancakes into squares for a boy who wanted them in triangles. The radio behind the register was tuned to a country station out of Port Angeles, low enough that it was only there when nobody was talking.

Harry turned a page of the paper he wasn't reading and watched the room in the window glass.

The reflection gave him the door, the register, the pass-through to the kitchen and the parking lot beyond, all overlaid on the grey street like a double exposure. Four pickups. A Subaru with a kayak rack. A Forks Police cruiser that hadn't been there yesterday at this hour. Nobody he hadn't already catalogued, and nobody who'd changed their habits since he'd catalogued them, which was the only thing he really cared about in a town this size.

He was six foot two and broad across the shoulders, and in Forks that put him in the same category as half the men who worked the mills. Black hair cropped short at the sides and left a bit long on top because he'd stopped bothering with it. Three days of stubble. Green eyes. A canvas work jacket over a grey Henley, jeans gone pale at the knees, Danner boots with real mud on them. He'd bought every item at the Swain's in Port Angeles on the afternoon he arrived and worn them hard since, because new boots in a logging town were a louder announcement than a brass band.

"More coffee, hon?"

Darlene was somewhere in her fifties, with reading glasses on a beaded chain and a pen tucked behind each ear in case one of them made a break for it. She'd been the first person in Forks to ask him what he did. He'd told her, and she'd forgotten it by the end of the sentence, which was the point of the job he'd chosen.

"You're a saint, Darlene."

"I'm a waitress." She filled his mug to the line. "You want the usual?"

"Two eggs over medium, bacon, hash browns." He folded the paper. "And is there pie?"

She looked at the clock above the kitchen door. "It's quarter to eight in the morning."

"That's not a no."

"Marionberry. I made it last night."

"I don't know what a marionberry is," Harry said, "and I'd die for it."

Darlene made a noise through her nose that in a less disciplined woman would have been a laugh and wrote it down. "You English," she said, and went away, which was a whole sentence in Forks.

He had a legend, and he lived in it. Harry Evans, thirty-one according to the passport, twenty-six according to his mother's gravestone. Contract timber cruiser, out of a consultancy in Reading that existed as a brass plate, a telephone that rang in a basement in Whitehall, and an accountant who'd been Confunded so thoroughly in 2004 that he now sincerely believed in the place. The consultancy was doing a valuation survey on several thousand acres of second-growth hemlock and Douglas fir for a pension fund in the City of London that was thinking about buying timberland in Washington State. Harry's job, as far as Forks knew, was to walk around in the rain with a clinometer and a diameter tape measuring trees for rich people who'd never see them.

It was a boring job. Boring was armour. It explained the odd hours and the mud and the truck parked on logging spurs at dawn. It explained the accent. It made people's eyes slide off him at the exact moment they might have started paying attention, because nobody in a logging town wanted to hear about cubic board feet from a man who wasn't buying them.

On his third day he had driven down Forks Avenue at nine at night with his left tail light deliberately smashed and let the Chief of Police pull him over. It had cost him a fix-it ticket and four minutes of polite conversation, and it had put Harry Evans in the town's system as a minor nuisance with valid paperwork. The instructors at the Department ran that exercise with trainees in Ottery St Catchpole every spring. *Let them meet you on your terms first,* the old witch who taught field legend had said. *A man the police have already met is a man they've already decided about.*

The bell over the door went.

In the window glass, Charlie Swan came in out of the rain with his jacket dark across the shoulders and his hat in his hand. He was middle height, solid, with a moustache that had gone grey at the corners and curly brown hair retreating from his forehead. He moved like a man who'd slept badly for a long time and had stopped noticing.

Harry had been noticing for sixteen days.

"Just coffee, Darlene," Charlie said. "To go."

"On a Sunday?" Darlene was already reaching for the pot. "Thought Sundays were for fish."

"Grays Harbor's got a missing trucker." Charlie put his hat on the counter. "Rig turned up at a pullout on 101 down past Kalaloch. They want eyes on every road between there and Port Angeles."

"Oh, that's awful."

"Yeah."

Harry let it sit for the length of two breaths, then lifted his mug an inch off the table. "Chief."

Charlie looked over. It took him half a second to place him, then his face settled. "Mr. Evans." His eyes went, by reflex, toward the lot. "Get that tail light fixed?"

"Fixed it myself. Cost me eleven dollars and a knuckle."

That got a twitch of the moustache. Charlie Swan was a man who approved of people who fixed their own trucks, and Harry had bought a truck that needed fixing for exactly that reason.

"Missing trucker," Harry said. "Anyone I'd know?"

"Doubt it. Dale Kowalski. Out of Hoquiam." Charlie took the coffee Darlene held out and fished in his jacket for his wallet. She waved him off and he put two dollars on the counter anyway, which was apparently a ritual. "Red Kenworth. Doors open, keys in it, thermos still on the dash. He hauls chips for the mill down there. Wife says he called her from Amanda Park Thursday night, said he'd be home by midnight."

"And he wasn't."

"And he wasn't." Charlie looked at him properly then. A cop's look, quick and tired and thorough. "You're out on those spur roads every day, right? Up the Sol Duc, out toward the Hoh?"

"Most days."

"You see a guy wandering around in a Carhartt jacket looking lost, you call me. Fifty-six, heavyset, beard. You see anything weird, you call me."

"Define weird."

Charlie held his eyes for a second longer than the question deserved.

"You'll know," he said.

Harry nodded as if that were a perfectly ordinary answer from a small-town policeman, and filed it under the heading he'd started on day four, the one in his head marked *Swan, C., what he knows*.

Darlene came back with the plate. Eggs, bacon, a slab of hash browns the size of a paperback, and on a separate saucer a wedge of pie with dark purple filling leaking from the lattice. She set it down with ceremony.

"Breakfast of champions," Charlie said, dry.

"Breakfast of a man who's been up since four counting hemlocks," Harry said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"There's pie."

Charlie snorted, picked up his hat and turned for the door. Then he stopped. He turned back with the look of a man who'd been working on something since he walked in.

"It's Harry, right? Harry Evans."

"That's me."

"Huh." Charlie looked down at the coffee in his hand. "Had a buddy named Harry. Harry Clearwater. We fished the Calawah together every Sunday for — hell, near enough twenty years. Him and me and Billy Black." He made a small motion with the cup, out toward the rain and the river somewhere beyond it. "He passed in March. Heart."

There were a dozen ways to answer that. The legend had several stock replies, all of them adequate. Harry didn't use any of them.

"I'm sorry, mate," he said. "Twenty years is a long time to have a man in the boat."

Charlie's jaw moved. "Yeah," he said. "It is."

And that was when Harry went in.

He did it the way he'd been taught by the worst teacher he'd ever had, in a dungeon office that had smelled of pickled things, and then the way he'd taught himself afterward, over years, which was quieter. Charlie Swan was looking at him. Eye contact made it simpler. It had stopped being necessary around 2002, but he never turned down a gift.

He leaned, gently.

Charlie's mind was hard going. It always had been. The first time Harry had brushed it, on the side of Forks Avenue with a torch in his face and a ticket book between them, it had been like listening to a radio in a car going under a bridge. Words came through in pieces. Feelings came through whole. It was a mind that kept its own counsel without knowing it was doing it, and Harry had met maybe four like it in his life.

He leaned a little harder.

Fragments. A river the colour of slate, and a man in a camp chair laughing with his whole chest. A woman with short black hair and tired eyes pressing a foil-covered dish into Charlie's hands on a porch, and the foil still on the counter in his kitchen a week later because he couldn't bring himself to throw it out. *Sue.* A girl's face, pale and painfully lovely, wrong in a way he couldn't name, eyes brown and too steady. *Contacts,* his mind said, and then, firmly, *Don't.*

Then a backyard. Dusk. A tall Quileute boy with cropped hair pulling his shirt over his head, saying something, and then no boy at all — a wolf the size of a horse, rust-red, standing on Charlie's lawn with its breath smoking and its eyes perfectly, horribly sensible.

Charlie's fear hit like a gust through an open door. Under it, laid down hard and deliberate like a man nailing a board over a window: *Need to know. Don't ask. Don't ask.*

And one more, which came through clearer than all the rest because he thought about it more than all the rest. A baby. Bronze curls. The pale girl's brown eyes, the real ones, the ones she'd had at seven. Bigger every time he saw her, bigger than she ought to be, and Charlie looking at her and choosing, every single time, not to count the weeks. *Edward's niece,* the official version said, in Charlie's voice, and then underneath, in the same voice, flat as a tabletop: *Like hell she is.*

Harry came out the way he'd gone in. Gently. No tracks.

Charlie winced and put two fingers to his temple. "Damn," he muttered.

"Weather," Harry said. "Murder on the sinuses, this place."

"Tell me about it." Charlie settled his hat on his head. "You see that rig, or anything — "

"Weird. I'll call."

"Do that." He nodded to Darlene and went out into the rain, and the cruiser pulled away a minute later with its wipers going.

Harry picked up his fork and ate his eggs.

He didn't feel bad about it. He'd tried feeling bad about it, years ago, when the Department had first sent him into a stranger's head on purpose, and found that the feeling didn't change anything except how well he slept. What Charlie Swan carried around was safer for Charlie Swan if fewer people knew it, and Harry intended to be one of the fewer.

So. The red-eyed one was Isabella Swan. Cullen now, by marriage, which tallied with the passenger manifest out of Florence in March and the wedding announcement in the *Forks Forum* in August. Charlie had seen her since the change and walked away with his throat intact, and he'd been close enough to notice contact lenses. That fitted what Harry had watched from a ridge two miles off: a three-week-old vampire who took down an elk in the Hoh like she'd been born to it and never once turned her head toward the road.

The wolf on Charlie's lawn was rust-red. Jacob Black, then, Billy's boy. The one who'd made himself head of the smaller pack.

And the baby.

Harry looked out at the rain and didn't speculate, because speculation without data was just a story you told yourself so you'd feel clever. He had a child who existed in Charlie Swan's memory, who looked like Charlie's daughter and Charlie's son-in-law, and who grew faster than a child should. That was all he had. He wrote it down in his head under its own heading and left it there.

The pie was very good. He ate all of it.

When Darlene came back with the bill he put down a twenty for a nine-dollar breakfast. A big tipper got remembered for being a big tipper, and nothing else.

"You have a good Sunday, hon," Darlene said. "Don't let the trees push you around."

"They're bigger than me, Darlene. I'll make no promises."

Outside, the rain had settled into the fine, persistent drizzle the locals didn't bother calling rain. His truck was a black 1979 Ford F-150 with a 351 under the bonnet and a cassette deck somebody had bolted under the dash in about 1988. He'd paid cash for it at a lot in Tacoma and spent his first two evenings in Forks swapping the carburettor by torchlight, by hand, with a Haynes manual and no magic at all. Arthur Weasley would have wept to see it.

He started the engine, thumbed a tape into the deck without looking at it, and Robert Plant came on halfway through "Over the Hills and Far Away," which was where he'd left him.

A missing man in a Carhartt jacket. A truck with its doors open on 101, south of the Hoh, a hundred miles of road and forest north of it. A thermos left on the dash.

If something had walked away from that pullout carrying Dale Kowalski's last few pints, it was walking north.

Harry turned up the volume and drove toward the Sol Duc.

**Olympic National Forest, Sol Duc drainage — 11:54 a.m.**

The tent stood in a hollow of sword fern and salal under three Douglas firs that had been big when Captain Cook was a boy, and it looked like something a hunter had pitched in 1975 and walked away from. Olive canvas gone grey-green with algae, a sagging ridgeline, one guy rope tied off to a root with a granny knot. A man who stumbled across it would feel a sudden urgent need to be somewhere else and would not remember, by the time he got there, why. Nobody had stumbled across it yet. The nearest spur road was nine hundred metres away through brush that didn't want to be walked through.

Harry had walked through it at half past eight, and the wards had tasted him on the way in and let him pass. Five layers. Muggle-repelling at the outside, then the scent-damper he'd built for this job specifically, then a Disillusionment over the hollow, then the alarm lattice, then the last one, which wasn't for keeping things out.

Inside, the tent was a corridor of oiled oak floorboards that ran back forty feet farther than the hollow had room for.

He'd built it over two years out of a Ministry-surplus frame and a great deal of stubbornness. Expanding a space was simple. Making the expansion stable enough to survive Apparition shock, a direct hit from a Blasting Curse, and being folded into a rucksack had taken him eleven months and a long correspondence with an elderly witch in Bruges who'd made her living building ships in bottles. The doors along the corridor were mismatched, salvaged from all over, and he'd stopped apologising for that around the time he'd stopped apologising for most things.

The first door on the left was a kitchen with a cast-iron range and a sofa that had seen things. A record player on a crate in the corner. Second on the left, the library: shelves to the ceiling, a rolling ladder, three chalkboards covered in arithmantic notation he'd been chewing on since August and still didn't like. First on the right, his bedroom, which was a bed. Second on the right, the armoury. At the far end, behind a door of riveted steel he'd pulled out of a decommissioned Gringotts vault car, the specimen room.

The third door on the left was the map room, and that was where he was standing with a mug of tea when the mirror on the wall cleared.

It was a plain oval of glass in a pewter frame, Department issue, paired to a twin on the ninth floor of the Ministry of Magic in London. For a moment it showed Harry his own face, unshaven, mug in hand. Then the silver went smoky, and the face that looked back at him belonged to a thin, stooped man in his seventies with a long chin and a cardigan buttoned wrong.

"Report," said Saul Croaker.

"Hello, Saul. Lovely to see you. Yes, the weather's dreadful, thanks for asking."

"It's eight o'clock at night, Potter, and I've been in a budget meeting since four. Report."

Harry drank some tea. Behind Croaker he could see the corner of a filing cabinet and a window that looked out onto a perfect enchanted dusk over a London that wasn't there.

"Array's finished," he said. "Went live Thursday night. Four hundred and twelve nodes. Port Angeles west to Neah Bay, down the coast to the Queets, back up through the Hoh and the Bogachiel and round the top of Crescent. Anything that comes onto the peninsula by road or by forest crosses at least two lines."

He nodded at the table in the middle of the room, though Croaker couldn't see it from that angle. It was a relief map of the Olympic Peninsula he'd charmed out of a single sheet of Ordnance-grade parchment, mountains standing up off it in grey paper ridges, rivers moving in thin silver threads. Four hundred and twelve pins stood in it, each one the size of a sewing needle with a head of dull iron. Out there in the real forest, each pin was a cut nail as long as his hand, inscribed along its length in runes so small he'd had to do them under a loupe, driven into a living tree or a fixed rock over eleven nights of walking. Every pin on the map glowed a faint, steady silver. Armed. Quiet.

"Selectors," Croaker said.

"Three. No heartbeat. Core temperature under fifteen Celsius. And human blood in the gut, anything taken in the last e
ight weeks or so. If it's eaten a person since August, it goes up the nearest tree by one ankle and stays there with a Full Body-Bind cycling on it every ninety seconds until I come and fetch it."

"And the Cullens."

"Deer. Elk. The odd cougar. The big one, Emmett, likes bear. There's not a mouthful of people among the lot of them. They've crossed nodes forty times since Thursday and the array hasn't so much as twitched."

Croaker's long face did something that on another man might have been approval. "The Swan girl. Your last report had her eyes red."

"Red from her own blood. She's three weeks into it and she's never had a human. I've watched her hunt." He thought of Charlie's mind, the foil on the counter, *contacts*. "Had it confirmed this morning, too. She's had her father within arm's reach and he walked away. Ward reads what's in the belly, not what's in the eyes. She reads clean."

"You've watched her hunt."

"From two miles off, through Omnioculars, Occluded to the eyeballs and downwind. I'm not daft, Saul."

"That has never once been established." Croaker took off his spectacles, which he did when he was about to say something he'd decided on in advance. "FISHER brought another group in on Thursday."

Harry put the mug down.

FISHER was the Department's name for a tall, striking woman with dark hair who ran tours of the old town in Volterra. She spoke six languages, she wore the same deep red lipstick every day, and she had a way of standing at the edge of the Piazza dei Priori that made coach parties drift toward her like iron filings to a magnet.

"How many?" Harry said.

"Thirty-eight. A coach from Lucerne."

"And out?"

Croaker put his spectacles back on. He didn't answer, which was the answer.

Harry looked at the map. The rivers moved in their silver threads. Somewhere under the paper hills a pin for every nine square miles stood waiting.

"The Minister wants options by the new year," Croaker said. "He wants to know what holds them, what kills them, and how many of ours we lose finding out. The Wizengamot will want to know nothing at all, which suits everyone. I have told the Minister that I have a man in the field who will tell us. Please don't make a liar of me. I'm too old to start now."

"And the Italians?"

"The Italian Ministry has expressed no interest whatsoever in Volterra." Croaker's voice went dry as paper. "Which tells me somebody in Rome has a great deal of interest in Volterra. So the Italians don't know you exist, the Americans don't know you exist, and if MACUSA asks, you're on a walking holiday."

"In a suit."

"In whatever you like. Your licence stands. Section Nine, all three, countersigned by Shacklebolt in his own hand. Log every use."

"I log everything."

"You logged a Boggart as 'a right little sod'."

"He was a right little sod."

Croaker let that go by. "The dogs."

"Wolves. And you'd want to be careful saying 'dogs' within a mile of them, they're touchy." Harry pulled one of the pins up a fraction to clear the paper and pushed it down again, a habit. "Two packs, both Quileute. Big one stays down at La Push under a lad called Uley. Small one's three of them, Jacob Black running it, and they've been on the Cullen place round the clock for three weeks." He paused. "Since the baby."

The pause on the other end went on long enough for the enchanted dusk in Croaker's window to deepen a shade.

"Baby."

"There's a kid at the Cullen house. Bronze hair, the Swan girl's eyes. Officially Edward Cullen's niece. Unofficially she looks like both of them and she's growing faster than she ought to. I got her out of the grandfather's head at breakfast. That's all I've got, and I'm not guessing past it."

"Vampires," said Croaker slowly, "do not have children."

"Then someone should have a word with the kid."

"Potter." Croaker leaned close enough that his nose fogged the edge of the glass. "You will not approach that house."

"Wasn't planning on it."

"The Cullens are flagged valuable alive. That flag did not come from me. It came from upstairs, and upstairs did not reach that conclusion lightly. Seven of them — eight, apparently — living among Muggles for decades without eating them. A mind-reader. A girl who sees what's coming. Whatever the Swan girl turns out to be. If this goes wrong, I would very much like it to go wrong somewhere other than their front garden."

"Noted."

"Is it?"

"It's noted, Saul. I've got a pen and everything." He drank the last of his tea, which had gone cold. "Something's already coming, anyway. Missing trucker out of Hoquiam, rig left open on 101 south of the Hoh, Thursday night. If it's what I think it is, it'll be walking north into the lines by tonight or tomorrow."

Croaker considered him through the glass for a moment.

"When you have your specimen — "

"You'll be the first to know," Harry said. "Well. Second."

The mirror went smoky, then silver, then gave him back his own face.

The armoury had been a broom cupboard in a Bethnal Green safe house before Harry had gutted it and moved it in here. Now it was thirty feet long, lit by a strip of bare bulbs, with pegboard on both walls and a workbench at the end with a vice, a reloading press and a jar of cleaning patches. It smelled of Hoppe's No. 9 and cold steel. He liked it better than any room he'd ever owned.

The guns hung in order of how often he needed them.

A Remington 870 with a shortened barrel and a sling worn soft. An L96A1 bolt-action in olive drab, the same rifle the British Army issued its snipers, with a suppressor and a scope he'd modified himself. A Browning Hi-Power, because that was what he'd learned on. A pair of Glock 17s for days he had to be someone ordinary. And on its own peg, by itself, a Colt M1911A1 with yellowed ivory grips, an old American service pistol that had come to him in a box of effects from a dead Auror in Philadelphia in 2003, with a note in the dead man's hand: *Give it to the English kid. He'll use it.*

None of them would have done much against what he was hunting, if they'd been loaded with ordinary rounds. The Volterra surveillance had established that much early on, at great expense, when an asset had watched a Muggle policeman put three nine-millimetre rounds into one of the Italian coven's guards in a dark lane and the guard had looked down at his shirt as if a moth had landed on it.

Harry didn't use ordinary rounds.

He sat at the bench and pulled a tray of shotgun shells toward him. They were brass-headed and hand-crimped, and each one had a band of runes cut around the hull just above the head, fine as hair. Inside each one, packed under the wad with the shot, was a spell.

That had been the hard part. Anyone could charm a bullet. Charms held fine on a bullet in a drawer. What they didn't do was survive the moment of firing, the pressure spike and the heat and the violence of the thing, which unravelled them like wet paper. He'd spent four years on the problem. He'd blown the ends off two workbenches and most of an eyebrow and filled three notebooks with dead ends, and then one night in a rented room in Prague he'd stopped trying to make the charm stronger and started trying to make it *sleep*: fold it down small and dormant into the runes on the case, and let the firing itself be the thing that woke it up.

It had worked on the first try, at four in the morning. He'd laughed until the landlady banged on the wall.

These shells carried a concentrated Incendio that woke on impact. If the Volterra intelligence was right, and everything he'd learned about this species said it was, fire was the one thing they couldn't shrug off. He loaded six into the 870's tube, racked one, thumbed a seventh in to replace it, and hung the gun back up.

On the shelf above the bench was a row of notebooks. Black, cloth-bound, numbered on the spines in white paint from 1 to 61. He didn't need anything from them. He took down the first one anyway, the way some men touched a photograph on the way out of the door.

The cover was water-stained. Inside, the first page was dated *14 September 1998*, in handwriting that was a good deal worse than his handwriting now, and headed *THINGS I DON'T UNDERSTAND ABOUT MAGIC (ALL OF IT)*.

Underneath, a list. *Why does a wand choose? What IS a Patronus — memory? Emotion? Something else? Why does Apparition hurt? Why do some spells need words? Why can't anyone explain what a Horcrux actually does to*

He'd stopped there and never finished the line.

In the margin, in small, very neat writing, someone else had added: *Start with Adalbert Waffling's* Magical Theory, *chapters 1–4. You will hate it. Read it anyway. — H.* And under that, in his own scrawl: *Did. Hated it. Now what.*

He'd been eighteen. He'd taken Divination at school because his best mate had taken it, and he'd come out of a war knowing how to kill and how to survive and not much else, and it had bothered him. It had bothered him in a way nothing else had since, a slow itch at the back of his skull every time a spell went off and he couldn't say *why*. So he'd gone back. Night lessons in Ancient Runes from Professor Babbling, who'd been suspicious for a month and delighted for a year. Arithmancy with Professor Vector in the summer, three days a week, until he could see the shape of a charm on paper before he ever cast it. Goblin metallurgy, through Bill Weasley and a lot of Firewhisky, from a smith in a workshop under Gringotts who'd called him "wand-boy" for two years and then one day stopped.

Then the Department, which had wanted him for his name and kept him for everything else.

Half of the Department of Mysteries built things nobody had asked for yet. The other half went places nobody had officially sent them. In eight years he'd done both. He'd stopped needing a wand in 2002 and stopped needing to speak in 2003, and at some point in between he'd stopped counting the number of times he'd been the last one standing in a room.

He put notebook 1 back next to notebook 61, and went to the far end of the corridor.

The steel door opened on a room that was colder than the rest of the tent by ten degrees and lit so brightly that it had no shadows anywhere. White tile on the walls. A drain in the floor. Glass-fronted cabinets along one side, with instruments laid out on felt: scalpels, retractors, calipers, a jeweller's loupe, a diamond-tipped scribe, a rack of crystal vials stoppered with silver. On the other side, shelves of labelled jars from other jobs. Inferius tissue, Moscow, 2001. A Lethifold sample under a permanent Patronus that he renewed every six months. A small lead box with *BOGGART — DO NOT* stencilled on the lid and nothing after *NOT*, because he'd run out of paint and decided it was clear enough.

In the middle of the room stood a stainless steel table, long enough for a tall man, with eight hinged bands of dark goblin-worked iron fixed along its edges, open and waiting. A circle of runes was cut into the tiles around its feet.

On a small desk by the door sat a grey Olympus digital voice recorder, because he trusted his own hand, and next to it a long green quill standing upright on a sheet of parchment, because he trusted a second record. The quill had started life as a Quick-Quotes Quill. He'd stripped out the embellishment charm with tweezers and a strong cup of coffee, because the last thing he needed in a research log was something that wanted to call a specimen *brooding* or *tragically handsome*.

He walked once around the table, checking each band and each rune with his eyes the way a climber checks a rope. Then he stood at the head of it with his hands in his pockets and looked at the empty steel.

"Come on, then," Harry said to it, quietly. "I haven't got all year."

**North of the Cullen property, Calawah drainage — 6:21 p.m.**

*(Nine miles north, at that minute, Harry was in the tent's kitchen with the kettle on, making beans on toast.)*

Leah ran the outer loop because she was the fastest, and because the outer loop was the one farthest from the house.

Rain dripped off the sword ferns and beaded on her coat and ran off again without ever getting through. The light was going, the slow wet grey dusk of October on the peninsula, the kind of evening where the forest seemed to lean in closer every minute. Her paws knew the route by now. Down the long slope through the hemlocks, across the creek where the bank had slumped, up through the vine maple to the ridge, along the ridge to the big burned snag, and back. Nine miles. She'd done it four times since noon and she'd do it twice more before Jacob phased in and took over and she could go lie on the floor of the Cullens' garage like a rug and pretend not to smell them.

*Nine miles,* Seth thought somewhere behind her and to the east, cheerfully. *That's like a marathon a day. More than a marathon. Do you think we'd win a marathon? Like, in human form, if we just showed up and —*

*Seth.*

*I'm just saying, we could win a marathon.*

*You'd get disqualified for being a freak.*

*For being too fast? That's not a rule.* A pause, while he worked through it. *Is that a rule?*

She didn't answer. He was running the inner loop, close in around the big white house with all the glass, and she could feel where he was the way you feel where your own hand is in the dark. She could feel, too, when his attention drifted toward the house, toward the thick sweet stink of the Cullens and something softer and stranger under it that was the baby. It drifted a lot. Seth liked the baby. Seth liked everybody.

*She grabbed my nose today,* he thought, unprompted, with a little burst of pleasure he didn't bother to hide because he couldn't. *Nessie. She just reached out and grabbed it. And then she showed me — you know, the thing she does — she showed me me. What I look like as a wolf. From her side. I'm way bigger than I thought.*

*You're a moose.*

*She thinks I'm cute.*

*She's three weeks old. She thinks Emmett's cute.*

*Emmett IS kind of cute,* Seth thought, and then, *Not like THAT, God, Leah, stop thinking like that, I can hear you thinking that.*

She let herself have a small, mean flicker of amusement, and let him hear that too. It was the most fun she'd had all day.

That was the thing about Jacob's pack, about running with only her brother and Jacob in her head instead of Sam and Emily and Paul and all the rest of them. It was quieter. It was *easier*. No Sam, with his guilt coming off him like smoke every time he caught her thinking of him. No Emily, a shape in every other wolf's thoughts, scarred face and soft hands and the smell of muffins. Only Seth, who was fifteen and ridiculous and had never in his life thought a thought he'd be embarrassed for her to hear, and Jacob, who mostly thought about the kid now, in a way that made Leah want to take a long walk into the ocean.

Three weeks since the birth. Three weeks since Bella Swan had been carried up those stairs looking like something out of a famine poster and come down again as one of *them*, red-eyed and fine-boned and stronger than any of them, and Jacob had looked at the bloody, squalling thing she'd made and gone soft and stupid forever.

Three weeks of the same nine miles in the rain.

She came down off the ridge at the burned snag, turned west along the creek, and the wind changed.

It came up out of the river bottom in a long, cold breath, and it carried a smell.

Leah stopped so fast that her back paws skidded in the mud and threw up a spray of needles. She stood with her head up and her ears forward and her nose working, and the hair along her spine came up in a hard ridge from her skull to her tail.

Sweet. Cloying, chemical, sickly sweet, like a bleach-soaked flower, like the inside of a hospital that sold perfume. It burned at the back of her nose and sank all the way down her throat into the pit of her stomach and lit something there that had been lit in her since the first time she'd phased, kneeling naked and screaming in her parents' backyard on the day her father's heart gave out at the sight of her.

Vampire.

She knew all the Cullens' scents. She'd had them shoved up her nose for three weeks straight, all of them, the blonde one's sharp rose and the big one's musk and the doctor's antiseptic and the little one's lemon, and Bella's, which was new and still settling. This was none of them.

And there was something under it. Copper and salt. Old. Two, three days old, carried along on the breath of the thing like the smell of beer on a drunk.

Blood. Human blood.

*Seth.*

He felt it in her before she'd finished thinking it, and he was moving. She felt him swing off the inner loop and come north through the trees at a dead run, felt the wild, bright spike of his heart, and under it a thread of something else, steadier, that would have made her proud if she'd let it. *Where? I've got you. I'm coming. Where?*

*Creek. West of the snag. It's not one of theirs.*

*Jake —*

*Jake's phased out. It's us.* She was already moving, nose low, quartering the ground. The trail was a minute old. Less. It came down from the north, from the direction of the Sol Duc road, crossed the creek in a single clean leap — she could see where it had landed on the far bank, two deep, narrow prints in the mud, sneakers, a man's size — and then went west. Toward the river. Toward the coast. *It's heading for the Bogachiel.*

*Should I go get Edward?*

*No.* The word came out of her with a snap of teeth that she felt in her actual jaws. *We don't need them for this. It's one. It's a nomad. It's been eating people on its way up here and it walked right past their house and none of them noticed. We'll handle it.*

*Jake said —*

*Jake said we protect the house. I'm protecting the house. Stay on my left flank and don't be a hero.*

She heard him think *Okay* and felt how much he meant it, and then she stopped thinking in words at all and ran.

The thing about chasing one of them was that you had to be faster than physics wanted you to be.

The leeches moved like nothing on earth. They went from standing still to thirty yards away with nothing in between, and the air where they'd been would still be closing up around the hole. Leah had watched Jasper Hale teach the others how to fight newborns in a clearing in June, and she'd learned from it. The trick was to run where they were going to be.

She caught sight of it at the bottom of the long slope above the river, and it caught sight of her at the same instant.

It was a man, or it had been. Average height. Thin. Jeans and a brown canvas jacket that was too big for it in the shoulders, hood down, dirty-blond hair plastered flat by the rain. It had been moving at a fast, easy lope through the hemlocks, the pace of a predator that didn't expect anything out here to be faster than it was. It turned its head as she came over the rise.

Its eyes were red. A deep, wet, bright red, the colour of the inside of a pomegranate, of the stuff on the trail.

It saw her: a pale grey wolf bigger than a horse coming down the slope at it with her ears flat and her lips peeled back. Then it saw Seth, sandy and enormous, breaking out of the vine maple on its other side.

Its face did something complicated.

And then it ran.

It went west so fast it left a wake. Leah went after it. The forest turned into a long smear of green and black, trunks flicking past close enough to brush her fur, ferns exploding into spray around her chest. Her lungs went to fire and then past fire into the clean cold place on the other side, where there was nothing in her head but the pull of her own legs and the smell up ahead. She could hear it. Its feet barely touched the ground, but she could hear them, *tk, tk, tk*, like fingernails on a table.

*It's faster than the newborns,* Seth thought, breathless, beside and behind her. *Leah, it's faster —*

*It's older. Older ones are smarter.* She swung a few degrees south. *It'll try the river. It'll try to lose us in the water. Get ahead of it on the left.*

*How do you KNOW that?*

*Because it's what I'd do.*

The ground fell away. She heard the river before she saw it, the low steady roar of the Calawah running high and brown with three days of rain, and the leech went toward it like a bullet. Leah went too, and the gap between them, which had been forty yards and then thirty, closed to twenty.

Twenty. She could smell it so clearly now that it was like having its hand over her face.

It hit the bank and jumped. It cleared fifty feet of white water and landed on the far side on a mossy boulder, sure-footed as a cat, and Leah didn't think about it, didn't let herself, just gathered her hindquarters under her and threw herself after it.

For one second she was flying over the river with the water snarling under her belly and the rain in her eyes.

She hit the far bank short and to the right, all four feet in the shallows, and came up the rocks in two scrabbling bounds with river water streaming off her and the leech twenty-five yards ahead, running again. Behind her she heard Seth land, a huge clumsy splash, and swear in his head in a way he'd have died before doing in front of their mother.

*I'm good, I'm good, I'm GOOD —*

*Left flank.*

*I'm going, I'm GOING —*

Old growth on this side. Western hemlock and Sitka spruce, trunks wide as cars, the ground a soft, deep carpet of moss and fallen needles that swallowed sound. The leech went into it like a needle into cloth. Leah had it now; she had its line. She could feel the shape of where it was going to go a half-second before it went there, the way she could feel a ball coming before it left someone's hand. It was angling toward a dark stand of hemlocks on a little rise ahead, older and taller than the rest, their lowest branches thirty feet up and thick as a man's waist. Behind it, somewhere a long way west, was the reservation line and the coast. If it got to the coast —

She dug in. The gap went to fifteen. Ten.

The leech looked back over its shoulder at her, red-eyed, its mouth open. It was close enough that she could see its teeth.

It passed between the first two hemlocks.

Something went *crack.*

It was a sound like a sheet of ice breaking on a lake, high and sharp and somehow coming from everywhere at once, and the leech's left foot left the ground at the bottom of a stride and kept going.

Leah's front paws hit the moss and she sat back on her haunches and slid, and Seth came barrelling into her flank from the left and the two of them went down in a heap in the needles, a tangle of legs and fur and teeth, and rolled, and came up.

They stood there.

The leech was hanging upside down from the lowest branch of the biggest hemlock, thirty feet up.

It was hanging by its left ankle. There was nothing holding it. No rope, no vine, no anything. Its leg went straight up, and at the top of the leg the ankle simply ended against the air a few feet below the branch, as if someone had pressed it there and the air had agreed to hold on. The rest of it hung straight down, rigid as a board, its arms fixed against its sides, its hair hanging. Its jacket had fallen down around its armpits. It was turning, very slowly, in a quarter circle and back.

It wasn't moving. It wasn't struggling. Its red eyes were wide open, looking at nothing, and its mouth was still open the way it had been when it looked back at her, and it stayed that way.

The rain fell on it. The rain fell on Leah.

The air smelled of the leech and the river and the trees and something else. Something sharp and clean she didn't have a name for. Like a struck match. Like the air before lightning.

*What,* Seth thought. Then, again, with feeling, *What.*

Leah didn't think anything for a full second, which hadn't happened to her in about a year. Then she shook the river out of her coat in one long shudder, nose to tail, and walked forward.

*Leah —*

*It can't move.*

*How do you know it can't move? What if it's — what if that's a thing they do? What if it's like a bat?*

*There's no such thing as bat stuff, Seth.* She stopped under it. Up close the smell was overpowering. The leech's eyes didn't follow her. She could see a tiny flutter at the corner of one of them, though, like a man straining against something with everything he had and getting nowhere. *It's stuck. Something's got it.*

*Something WHAT?*

She didn't know. She didn't care. Thirty feet was nothing; she'd jumped higher than that. If she went up the trunk and got her jaws around its neck, its own weight would do half the job. She'd done it before. It made a sound like tearing marble and it was the best sound in the world.

She crouched.

Behind her, in the empty air between the hemlocks, something went *CRACK* — a flat, hard, deafening report, like a rifle fired right beside her head — and Leah spun around with every hair on her body standing straight up.

There was a man standing in the clearing where a second ago there'd been nothing at all.

**The tent — 6:38 p.m.**

Harry was on the sofa in the kitchen with his boots on the crate and a plate of beans on toast balanced on his chest when the coin in his pocket went hot.

He'd made the coin in 2004 out of a Gringotts Galleon and an idea he'd stolen, with full credit, from a sixteen-year-old girl who'd once used a Protean Charm to run a secret army out of a school. Hermione's coins had told the members of Dumbledore's Army when and where to meet. His told him where something had stepped into one of his traps, and what.

He set the plate on the crate and pulled it out. The gold was hot enough to sting. The tiny numerals around the rim, which usually read the date, were shifting under his thumb.

*217. CALAWAH. S/1.*

Node two hundred and seventeen. Calawah drainage, the old-growth hemlocks a mile and a half below the Cullens' north boundary. Selector one. Snare closed, specimen held.

"Well," Harry said, to nobody. "Hello, Dale."

He was on his feet before the plate stopped rattling.

He crossed to the map room in four strides. On the table, among four hundred and eleven quiet silver pins, one had gone red. He bent over it. The red was steady, which meant the bind was cycling. Next to it, so faint he'd have missed them if he hadn't spent eleven nights building the thing, two smaller sparks of reddish gold hung in the air just above the paper like fireflies. The array wasn't meant to track anything but its targets, but at a closed node it picked up anything big and hot within a few dozen yards. He'd wanted to know, when he arrived, if a bear was having a sniff at his specimen.

Two big hot things. Close in. He knew what was that big and that hot in the Calawah drainage, and they weren't bears.

"Course you are," he said.

He considered it for about as long as it took to straighten up.

He could wait. Let the wolves have their look, let them try the tree and fail, let them go and fetch the Cullens. He could arrive in an hour to an empty clearing, or a clearing full of people with gold eyes asking polite questions. Either way, he'd lose control of the next hour. Or he could go now, and the wolves would see his face, and his face would be in the head of a mind-reader within the hour. Croaker would have kittens.

Croaker could have kittens. Harry had been told on his first day in the field that a man who was afraid of being seen would never see anything. He'd been seen in a lot of places since. It hadn't killed him yet.

He went into the bedroom and stood in the middle of the floor and changed his clothes without touching them.

The canvas jacket and the Henley and the faded jeans folded themselves on the bed. In their place: a white shirt, starched. A black tie, knotted narrow. A charcoal suit, single-breasted, cut by a tailor on Savile Row who'd made suits for three generations of men who didn't exist. Black Oxfords, which would stay black, which would never meet mud. A long black overcoat with the collar turned up. A shoulder rig under the left arm with the Colt in it, because he didn't need a gun and never would, and because a man in a suit with a gun under his arm was a sentence anyone on earth could read. He took a pair of dark glasses out of the inside pocket and slid them on, though the sun had been down since quarter past.

He checked himself in the wardrobe mirror. A tall man in a dark suit and dark glasses. He looked like every spook in every film he'd ever seen at the Odeon in Leicester Square.

"Bloody marvellous," he said, and took the badge wallet off the dresser and turned on the spot.

The forest came up around him with a hard, flat bang of displaced air.

Cold. Rain. The smell of moss and wet bark and river, and under it two other smells that hit the back of his throat at the same time. One was sickly and sweet, like rotting lilies soaked in petrol, and it was hanging from the tree in front of him. The other was rank and musky and huge, like a kennel the size of a house, and it was all around him.

He took in the clearing in one sweep of the dark glasses.

The specimen, thirty feet up, upside down, turning gently on the end of a snare he'd built in a library in London and carried across an ocean in his head. Male. Slight. Jeans, trainers, a brown Carhartt jacket two sizes too big for it. Its eyes were red. Its mouth hung open. The bind was holding.

And two wolves.

He'd seen them before, through Omnioculars from a long way off. That hadn't done them justice. The nearer one was a pale, silvery grey, lean and long-legged, the smaller of the two only by comparison; she'd have stood eye to eye with a carthorse. She'd spun around at the sound of his arrival, low to the ground, her lips pulled back off a set of teeth like a row of tent pegs. The other one was sandy-coloured, bigger, broader in the chest and gawkier in the leg, the way a teenage boy is gawky, and it was standing behind her with its ears straight up and its mouth hanging open in what he would, in a dog, have called astonishment.

The grey one was soaking wet. River water was still dripping off her ruff.

Harry raised his left hand, slow, with the badge wallet open in it.

"Evening," he said.

He let Legilimency come up behind his eyes as he said it, the way he'd bring up a torch in a dark room. He kept it light. He'd learned the hard way that a mind which knew it was being pushed would push back. He opened himself and listened.

And it was loud.

He'd expected something. He'd read the old Quileute stories in the library at Peninsula College in Port Angeles, the ones the anthropologists had written down in the twenties, about the warriors who went out of their bodies to run as one. He'd expected a link. What he got was like stepping into a pub at closing time. Two voices on top of each other, no edges between them, thought and feeling and smell and noise all running together down a single channel:

*— where the HELL did he —*

*— he just APPEARED, Leah, he just, he was NOT there and then he —*

*— that's a gun, he's got a gun under his —*

*— is that a SUIT —*

*— doesn't smell like a leech, doesn't smell like — what does he smell like —*

There was no barrier on either of them at all. A wolf's mind was built to be open. They'd been sharing their skulls for so long that it would never have occurred to either of them that somebody could be listening from outside.

Harry held the badge a little higher.

"Federal agent," he said. "This situation is contained. I'm going to have to ask you both to vacate the premises. As of right now, this is the site of a federal operation."

Silence in the clearing. Inside the channel, absolutely none.

*Is he — is he talking to US?* That was the sandy one. Seth, the grey one had called him in her head, without thinking about it. *Leah, he's talking to us like we're —*

*He's showing us a BADGE.* The grey one. Leah. She was furious, and under the fury, cold and alert and calculating, she was afraid, and she hated that she was afraid more than she hated him. *He knows what we are. He knows we can read. Who the hell IS he —*

"Yes," Harry said. "I'm talking to you, Lassie."

The grey wolf went absolutely still.

It was a different stillness from before. Before, she'd been a predator getting ready. Now she'd had the floor go out from under her.

*Did he —*

*He — Leah, he — did he just —*

*Shut up. Shut up, Seth, don't think, don't THINK —*

"Bit late for that, love," Harry said.

Seth's thoughts went off in about eight directions at once, every one of them some variation of *he can hear us he can hear us he can HEAR us*, overlapping and tripping over each other. In the middle of it Harry caught one, very clear, very indignant and fifteen years old: *LASSIE?*

"And you're Balto," he told the sandy one. "Don't look at me like that. Balto was a hero. Ran a sled full of medicine through a blizzard to Nome. There's a statue of him in Central Park. You should be so lucky."

*...Balto's actually a pretty good movie,* Seth thought, in a small voice.

*SETH.*

*What! It is!*

Harry closed the badge wallet and put it back in his inside pocket. He didn't take his eyes off the grey one while he did it. She was the dangerous one. The boy was big and strong, and he'd fight if she told him to, and he'd fight well. She was the one doing sums.

He could see them, more or less, the way you can see a sum on the other side of frosted glass. *Eight feet. Left side. The gun's on the left. If I go low he has to reach across himself. If Seth goes high at the same time —*

"Don't," Harry said.

He didn't raise his voice. He didn't move his hands.

"You're thinking about going low on my left while Balto goes high, because you've noticed the gun's under my left arm. It's a good plan. It'd work on most people. I'm not most people, I've had a very long day, and you've both got a mum at home who'd miss you." He tilted his head a fraction at the hemlock. "And I didn't come here for you. I came for him."

Inside the channel, Leah's thoughts had gone hard and quiet and very far away, like a woman holding her breath underwater. All that came through was a hum, tight as a wire. She was trying not to think at all.

It was quite impressive, actually. Not many people could do it on the first go. He didn't tell her that.

*How does he know about Mom?* Seth, with no wire at all. *How does he know about —*

"Educated guess, Balto. Everybody's got a mum."

*Stop DOING that!*

"Stop thinking so loud."

The grey wolf took one slow step back. Her ears were still flat. Her lips were still off her teeth. She took another, angling herself between Harry and her brother without, as far as Harry could tell, being aware of doing it.

Through the hum, one thought escaped her, quick and hot and involuntary as a hiccup:

*What do you WANT with it?*

Harry looked up at the specimen.

It had been watching him. The bind held its body, but it didn't hold its eyes; those had been tracking him since the moment he arrived, very slightly, at the edges, red and wide and wet. He'd have liked to know what was going on behind them. He would know soon.

"He's evidence," Harry said. "He killed a man called Dale Kowalski on Thursday night on Highway 101, south of Kalaloch, and left the man's thermos on the dash of his truck. That makes him mine." He paused. "You were going to take his head off. Fair enough. I'd have done the same in your shoes. Paws. Whatever. But I need him with his head on."

*How do you know that?* The thought came out of Leah in spite of herself. *How do you know his — you've got no — how do you KNOW what he —*

"It's my job to know."

He walked forward.

Both wolves moved. Seth jerked back half a step with a deep, startled *wuff*. Leah stood her ground with a low, rolling snarl that he felt through the soles of his shoes. He walked past the pair of them at a distance of about six feet, close enough that he could feel the heat coming off their hides in the cold air, and stopped under the hemlock with his hands in his coat pockets and the rain running off the brim of nothing. He hadn't brought a hat. He regretted that, slightly.

*He just walked past us. He just — he turned his BACK on us.*

*Because he can hear us coming,* Leah thought, bleak and fast.

"Got it in one, Lassie."

*I'm going to bite his FACE off.*

"You're welcome to try. You'd be the first. Well — you'd be the first in a while."

Behind him, he heard the boy think, quite distinctly and with a sort of horrified glee: *Oh my God, he's like Emmett, if Emmett had a badge.*

Harry filed the comparison with Emmett Cullen away for later, and looked up at the snare.

He'd tied it off. That was how he thought of it, in English; in arithmancy it was a tensioned closed loop with a conditional release keyed to a twelve-sided rune sequence, and in practice it was a knot. He reached up with his mind, found the place where the loop met the air below the branch, and untied it.

The vampire fell thirty feet.

It came down headfirst, rigid as a post, and if he'd let it hit the ground it would have punched a hole in the moss and very possibly in the earth under it, and walked away with nothing but a dent in its dignity. He didn't let it hit the ground. He stepped in and took it on his right forearm and shoulder, one arm across its back and under the far side of its chest, and turned with the weight.

It was heavy. Much heavier than a man its size should be, as dense as a statue, and the impact went through his arm and his shoulder and down his spine into the ground. He'd taken worse. He had been built — had built himself, rebuilt himself, in ways that didn't appear on any Ministry form — to take a great deal more than that.

When he straightened up, the vampire was tucked under his right arm, horizontal, stiff from heel to crown, its nose pointing at the river and its feet pointing back the way it had come, the way a man carries a surfboard down to the water.

He shifted it a bit, to get the balance right.

In the channel behind him, for about two full seconds, there was no thought at all.

Then: *He's carrying it. He's carrying it like a — Leah, those things weigh like five hundred pounds, I've PICKED UP the pieces, he's carrying it like a —*

*I can see that, Seth.*

*— like a SURFBOARD —*

*I CAN SEE THAT.*

Harry turned round, with the specimen under his arm, and faced them.

The light was nearly gone. The grey wolf's eyes caught what was left of it and threw it back at him, pale and steady. She hadn't moved. She was still between him and her brother. The sandy one had come up alongside her, and his tail had stopped its nervous little twitching and gone out stiff behind him, and for the first time since Harry had arrived both of them were thinking the same thing at the same moment, so that it came down the channel in a single voice, clear as a bell:

*The Cullens.*

Harry nodded.

"Yeah," he said. "I know about the Cullens."

He let that land.

"You'll tell them. You'll tell your Alpha, and he'll tell them, and the one who reads minds will pull it straight out of your heads before you've opened your mouths. That's fine. Tell them they're on the nice list. Tell them to stay there." He looked from one to the other. "And the two of you, Lassie, Balto — you ran this thing down from the Sol Duc road and across the Calawah in spate in the dark. That's good work. Go home and get dry."

*He's — did he just say we did a good job?*

*Shut UP, Seth.*

*He did though.*

Harry tipped two fingers to his forehead, a lazy little salute that he'd learned from a man in Ohio who'd hunted things for a living and never once said thank you to anyone.

Then he turned on the spot, with forty-odd stone of vampire under one arm, and the clearing went away with a bang.

**The specimen room — 6:44 p.m.**

He laid it on the steel table on its back, and the table rang under the weight like a struck bell.

The eight iron bands swung up and closed on their own as he walked around the table: ankles, knees, hips, wrists, elbows, chest, throat, forehead. Each one locked with a heavy click. Under the table the circle of runes cut into the tile lit up one after another, a slow ripple of pale blue light, until the whole ring was burning steady. Nothing that lay inside that ring was going to Apparate, Disapparate or walk out of it without his say-so, and nothing that was not him was going to open the steel door.

He took off the dark glasses and the overcoat and hung them on the hook by the door. He took off the suit jacket and the shoulder rig and hung those too. He rolled his shirtsleeves to the elbow, washed his hands at the steel sink in the corner, dried them, and pulled on a pair of dragon-hide gauntlets that came halfway up his forearms.

The specimen's eyes followed him round the room.

He renewed the Body-Bind with a thought, a fresh layer laid on top of the one the snare had been cycling, and felt it settle over the thing like a sheet. Then he went to the desk by the door, pressed the red button on the Olympus recorder, and touched the green quill. It lifted itself and hovered over the parchment, nib wet.

Harry pulled a stool up to the head of the table and sat down on it.

"Unspeakable zero-seven-two-one," he said. "Research log zero-one. North American example."

The quill scratched. The little red light on the recorder glowed.

"Sunday, first of October, two thousand and six. Eighteen forty-six local. Specimen recovered from node two-one-seven, Calawah drainage, Olympic Peninsula, Washington, at eighteen thirty-nine. Held by the snare, no damage on recovery. Witnesses present on recovery, see ancillary note at the end. Programme as follows."

He didn't need to look at anything to recite it. He'd written it on the flight over.

**Initial examination.**

He started where he always started, with the boring part, because the boring part was where people missed things.

"Male. Apparent age mid-twenties. Height one hundred and seventy-six centimetres. Build slight." He ran the tape from heel to crown and across the shoulders and read the numbers out. "Hair dark blond, worn longish, wet. Clothing: denim jeans, grey hooded sweatshirt, brown canvas work jacket, size XXL, too large for the specimen. Running shoes, Nike, heavily worn. No socks."

He went through the pockets. The jacket had a pair of leather work gloves, a packet of Marlboro Reds with four left, a Bic lighter, and a brown leather wallet curved to the shape of a big man's backside. He opened it. A Washington driver's licence. A fishing licence. Forty-three dollars. A photograph of two grinning girls in matching Easter dresses, worn white at the folds.

The licence said *KOWALSKI, DALE M.*, with an address in Hoquiam.

Harry looked at the photograph for a moment. Then he put everything back in the wallet in the same order and laid it on the instrument tray.

"Personal effects recovered from the specimen identify one Dale Kowalski of Hoquiam, Washington, reported missing Thursday last. Specimen is wearing his jacket." **Earlier — the hemlocks below the Cullens' north boundary — 6:39 p.m.**

When she saw Seth, her whole face lit up. She let go of the hair and held out both hands toward him and made a small, imperious noise.

Bella was right beside them. She was sitting very still with one hand on the baby's back, and her eyes were the colour of blood. Three weeks hadn't touched that. They'd started to go a little darker at the edges, Edward said, but in the lamplight they still looked like something from a horror movie. She'd been looking at the door, too, and when she saw Leah's face, she pulled the baby out of Rosalie's lap and into her own arms in one fast, smooth movement that Leah's eyes didn't quite catch.

Rosalie let her. That was how Leah knew it was bad.

Jacob was on his feet in the middle of the room in jeans and nothing else, his chest still printed with the pattern of the sofa cushion, his hair sticking up on one side. He'd been asleep. He looked at Leah. He looked at Seth.

"What happened?" he said. "Edward said — what *happened*?"

"There was a leech," Seth said. "A nomad. Red eyes. It came down from the Sol Duc road and we chased it — Leah chased it, I mostly chased Leah — all the way across the Calawah, Jake, she jumped the Calawah, it's like *up*, it's really high right now — and then it ran into these trees down past the north line and it just — *got grabbed*. It went up in the air. Upside down. By its ankle. There wasn't anything there. And it just hung there. It couldn't move. And then this guy *appeared* —"

"Appeared," said Jasper.

"Out of nowhere. There was this bang, like a gun, and he was just *there*." Seth was talking with his hands now, the way he did. "In a suit. Like a black-suit kind of suit, with a tie. And sunglasses. And he had a badge. He showed us a badge. FBI."

"The FBI," said Emmett, slowly, with enormous delight.

"He was *English*," said Seth.

Emmett opened his mouth.

"He could hear us," Leah said.

The room went still.

A room full of people never went all the way still. People shifted. People breathed. When the Cullens went still, all eight of them at once, it was like somebody had pressed pause on a video. Even Alice's fingers, which were always moving, stopped. The only things in the room that kept moving were Jacob, and Seth, and Leah, and the baby, who'd just discovered Bella's collar and was trying to fit it into her mouth.

"Hear you," Carlisle said. "In wolf form."

"In our heads. He heard everything we thought." Leah folded her arms across her chest. Her hands had nearly stopped shaking. "He answered it. Out loud. Like a conversation. I thought something, and he answered me before I'd finished thinking it."

All seven of them looked at Edward.

Edward was standing by the glass wall with his back to the dark lawn, and his eyes were unfocused again, going back and forth across nothing.

"It's true," he said. "I'm watching it now. In Seth's memory. In Leah's." His voice was very quiet. "There's no lag. He's hearing the pack link directly, the way I do, and he's answering it as fast as they think."

"Like you," Bella said.

"I can't tell." Edward shook his head once, sharply. "I can only see what they saw and hear what they heard. I can't hear *him* in a memory. There's nothing there to hear. There's just his face and his voice."

"Show me," said Alice.

"I can't show you, Alice."

"Then tell me. Everything. Exactly. What does he look like?"

Edward told her. He did it the way he did everything, quick and precise, as if he were reading it off a page. "Six foot two. Two hundred and twenty, two-thirty pounds. Dark hair, short. Some stubble. Mid to late twenties, I think. It's hard to see his face properly; he's wearing dark glasses and it's nearly dark. A dark grey suit, white shirt, black tie. A long black overcoat. A shoulder holster on the left side. There's a pistol in it." He paused. "An old one. A Colt, I think." He paused again. "And the rain is steaming off his shoulders. The way it does off Jacob."

Alice closed her eyes.

She went very far away. Leah had seen her do it before, in June, before the fight in the clearing, and it was creepy every time. The little sharp face went smooth and empty like a doll's, and her eyes moved behind her lids like someone dreaming.

Everybody waited.

After about ten seconds, Alice's eyes opened. She looked at Jasper, and then at Edward, and her small face did something Leah had never seen it do before.

It looked lost.

"I can't," she said.

"Can't what?" said Jacob.

"I can't see him." Alice's hands found each other in her lap and held on. "I tried him tomorrow. I tried him tonight. I tried him in a suit and out of one, in Forks, in Port Angeles, anywhere. I tried him coming here." Her voice went thin on that last part. "It smears."

"Smears," Carlisle said gently.

"When I try to see you" — Alice tipped her head at Jacob without looking at him — "or Leah or Seth or Nessie, it just goes black. Like a hole. I'm used to it now. I know what it is. This is something else. There's something there. I can feel there's someone there. But every time I try to look at him it's like trying to watch through a car windshield in a rainstorm with the wipers off. I get colours. Shapes. I can't hold any of it." She looked at Edward. "I've never had that. Ever."

Jasper's hand had tightened on her shoulder. Leah could see his knuckles.

"Then we don't know if he's coming here," Rosalie said.

"No."

"We don't know if he's coming here *tonight*."

"No, Rose."

Rosalie stood up.

She did it slowly, for her, which meant Leah could actually see it happen. She was furious. It came off her like heat off a road. She looked at Leah.

"You led him here."

"Excuse me?"

"You chased a nomad straight into some kind of — of *trap*, set by God knows what, and then you stood there and let him read your minds, and now he knows everything you know. About this house." Rosalie's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "About *her*."

Every head in the room turned to the baby.

Renesmee had got Bella's collar thoroughly wet and was now trying to fit Bella's thumb in her mouth instead. She seemed perfectly happy about it.

Leah felt something cold go down the middle of her.

"I didn't think about the baby," she said.

"You don't have to *think* about her. Your brother loves her. Your brother thinks about her every ten minutes —"

"I tried not to," said Seth, in a very small voice. Everybody looked at him. He'd gone grey under his tan. "When I figured out he could hear us. I tried really hard not to. And I — I thought about her anyway. Just for a second. Her face. Her hand on my nose." He swallowed. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, Bella. I couldn't stop."

Bella didn't say anything. She'd gone so still she could have been a statue somebody had carved of a girl holding a baby. Her red eyes were on Seth, and Leah honestly didn't know, for one long second, what was going to happen.

Then Edward said, "Seth. Look at me."

Seth looked.

"I've been through it," Edward said. "All of it. Every second, in both your heads. He was looking at Leah when you thought about her. He didn't turn. He didn't stop. His voice didn't change. He didn't ask a single question about her, then or afterward. He was interested in the nomad, and in the two of you, and that's all I can see."

"That doesn't mean he didn't hear it," said Jasper.

"No," Edward agreed. "It doesn't."

Bella looked down at the baby in her arms. Renesmee took the thumb out of her mouth, reached up, and put her small damp hand flat on Bella's cheek, and whatever she showed her made some of the stone go out of Bella's face. Not all of it.

"It's okay, Seth," Bella said. Her voice was low and careful, the voice she used for everything now, as if she were afraid of breaking it. "You couldn't help it. I know."

Seth's eyes had gone wet. He nodded hard and looked at the floor.

Rosalie sat back down, very slowly, next to Bella. She didn't look at Leah again. That was as close to an apology as Leah was going to get from her this century, and frankly she'd take it.

"Tell us exactly what he said," Carlisle said. "Leah. Word for word, if you can."

So she told him.

She was surprised by how much of it was still there. She'd always had a good memory for the things people said when they were being bastards to her. *Evening. Federal agent. This situation is contained. Vacate the premises.* Lassie. The plan she hadn't even finished making, read off the inside of her head and handed back to her. *You've both got a mum at home who'd miss you.* Seth flinched when she said that one. *He's evidence. He killed a man called Dale Kowalski on Thursday night on Highway 101. That makes him mine.*

Carlisle closed his eyes at the name.

"There's a missing man from Hoquiam," Esme said quietly. "It was on the news this afternoon. They found his truck."

"Then he wasn't lying about that," said Jasper.

"He wasn't lying about anything," said Edward. "That I can tell. He didn't need to. He had all the cards."

Leah kept going. *I know about the Cullens.* The thing about the mind-reader, pulling it straight out of their heads — she saw Edward's mouth go tight at that. And then the last part.

"He said to tell you that you're on the nice list," she said. "He said to tell you to stay there."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Stay there," Jasper repeated.

"That's what he said."

"That's a term," said Jasper. "Terms come with conditions." He came out from behind the sofa and walked to the glass wall and stood beside Edward, looking out at the dark. When he talked again it was in a different voice, flat and practical, the one he'd used in the clearing in June when he was teaching them all how to kill newborns. "He told you two things, and he meant you to carry both of them to this room. He knows exactly what we are and where we live, and he's decided not to do anything about us. For now. Conditionally."

"Conditional on what?" said Emmett.

"On us staying out of whatever this is."

"And if we don't?"

Jasper didn't answer that.

"Leah," he said instead. "The trap. Tell me about it."

She told him about the nail. The square head, the fresh-split bark, the hum in it she'd felt in her teeth. Seth chimed in about how they'd run right past it and nothing had happened to them. Jasper listened with his head tilted, the way a mechanic listens to a bad engine.

"We run that ground," he said, when they were done. "All of us. Every day. Alice and I hunted the north drainage on Thursday. Emmett, you were down past the river on Friday."

"Saturday too," said Emmett. "There's a bear down there I've been meaning to have a conversation with."

"Bella," said Jasper. "You've been out that way."

"Twice this week," Bella said. "With Edward."

"And none of us were taken."

Leah saw it land on the room. One by one, she watched them get there.

"So it's selective," said Carlisle.

"It's more than selective." Jasper turned around. "It took a nomad with red eyes and passed over a pack of wolves running at full speed. That's one thing. Fine. He wants vampires, and he doesn't care about wolves. But it's passed over every one of us, every day, for — Leah, how old was the bark?"

"Fresh. A couple of weeks."

"For a couple of weeks. So it can tell us apart from him. And it can tell *Bella* apart from him." Jasper looked at Bella. "Your eyes are as red as his were. If it was looking at eyes, you'd have been hanging from a tree on Tuesday."

Bella's hand tightened very slightly on the baby's back.

"So either it can tell what we've been eating," said Jasper. "Or it can tell *who we are*. Individually. By name."

"And if it's the second one," Edward said quietly, "then at some point in the last few weeks he's been close enough to each one of us to take our measure."

"You'd have heard him," said Alice.

"Yes," said Edward. "I would have."

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to.

The room was quiet. Outside the rain was coming down harder now, rattling on the glass. The baby made a small sleepy noise and pushed her face into Bella's neck.

Emmett broke it.

"Okay," he said. "I just want to make sure I've got this right. Some English guy in a suit and sunglasses, with an FBI badge, teleports into the woods with a bang, reads the dogs' minds, hangs a vampire upside down from a tree with nothing, and then catches it — catches it, falling thirty feet — and carries it out of there under one arm."

"Like a surfboard," said Seth.

"Like a *surfboard*." Emmett leaned back in his chair and laced his hands behind his head. "Honestly? I kind of want to meet this guy."

"No," said Rosalie, "you don't."

"I kind of do, babe."

"Strummer," Edward said suddenly.

They all looked at him.

He had the faintest frown on his face, the kind he got when he was trying to remember where he'd put something. "The name on the badge. In Seth's memory. *Special Agent J. Strummer.*" He looked at Carlisle. "Joe Strummer. He was the singer for a band called the Clash. London. Late seventies. Punk."

"It's a fake name," said Jacob.

"It's a *joke*," Edward said. "The badge is a joke. He used the name of a dead English rock star on a federal ID and flashed it at two werewolves in the woods." He was quiet a second. "He wanted us to see that. He knew I'd be looking."

Emmett made a noise that was half laugh and half something else.

"Okay," he said. "Now I *really* want to meet this guy."

"*Emmett*."

"I'm just saying, Rose. Punk-rock fed. That's a whole thing."

Jacob hadn't sat down. He was standing in the middle of the room with his arms folded and his jaw working, and Leah could see him doing the thing he'd had to learn to do in the last month, the Alpha thing, where he stopped being Jacob for a minute and started being the person who decided.

"Okay," he said. "Nobody runs alone. Not anymore. Not until we know what this is. Seth, you're with me. Leah —"

"I'll run with whoever I want."

"Leah."

"I outran it, Jake. I outran it across the river. He said so himself." She hadn't meant to say that last part. It came out anyway, and her face went hot.

Jacob looked at her for a long second. Something passed over his face that she didn't want to look at too closely.

"Fine," he said. "You run the outer loop with Seth. I'll take the inner with — I'll take the inner. We stay in each other's heads and we stay close to the house. And I'm going down to La Push tonight to talk to Sam."

Leah didn't say anything to that. She'd known he was going to say it. It still landed like a kick.

"He's got to know," Jacob said, quieter, to her alone. "If this guy's got traps all over the place, they're going to be all over Sam's land too. He's got kids running patrols down there. Collin and Brady are thirteen."

"I know."

"I'll go," said Seth. "With you. If you want."

"Yeah. Yeah, okay." Jacob scrubbed a hand over his face. "Carlisle?"

Carlisle hadn't moved from beside the piano. He was looking out through the glass wall at the dark, in the direction of the river and the north boundary and the hemlocks, and his face was the face of a man listening to a bad diagnosis about someone he'd never met.

"He'll be alive," he said. "Wherever he is."

It took Leah a second to understand who he meant.

"The nomad?"

"Whoever he is. Whatever he's done." Carlisle's voice was very soft. "He's alive, and he can't move, and a man who carries a gun he doesn't need and reads minds he has no right to read has taken him somewhere none of us can follow." He turned from the window. "I don't know what he wants with him. I find I can imagine it."

"He ate a trucker, Carlisle," said Leah.

"I know."

"He ate a trucker with a thermos," Leah said, "and he was going to keep walking north and eat somebody else, and the next one might've been somebody's kid on the beach at First, or Charlie, or anybody. I don't care what happens to him." She heard her own voice, hard and flat in the big white room, and didn't take any of it back. "Whatever that guy's doing to him. I hope he takes his time."

Esme made a small sound.

Carlisle looked at her for a long moment, and Leah made herself look back. His face stayed gentle. That was the worst thing about him. It always stayed gentle. He looked at her like she was a patient who'd told him something sad about herself.

"I understand," he said.

She couldn't stand it.

"I'm going back out," she said, to Jacob, and went out the back door into the rain before anybody could say anything else.

---

The porch was dark and wet and smelled of cedar, and the rain was coming down so hard now that it roared on the roof. She stood at the top of the steps with her arms wrapped around herself, staring into the trees.

Behind her, through the glass, she could hear them start talking again, low and fast, the way leeches talked when they forgot anybody else could hear. Jasper's voice. Edward's. Alice saying something sharp. The baby starting to fuss, and Bella shushing her.

The door opened behind her and Jacob came out and stood beside her.

He didn't say anything for a while. That was one of the good things about Jacob. He'd learned, eventually, when to shut up.

"He said we did good work," Leah said, finally.

"I know. Seth told it twice more in the kitchen while you were out here." Jacob had his hands jammed in his pockets. "He's going to be replaying it for a year."

"He called me Lassie."

"Yeah."

"He walked right past me with his back turned. Like I was nothing. Like I was a *dog*." She was shaking again, all the way down to her hands. "And then he told me I did a good job, and the worst part, Jake, the worst part is, for about one second I *liked* it."

Jacob didn't laugh. She'd have bitten him if he'd laughed.

"He's dangerous," he said.

"Yeah."

"He's — whatever he is, he's not human. Not normal human. People don't smell like that. People don't run that hot." Jacob was frowning out at the rain. "Edward said the rain was steaming off him."

"I know."

"So what is he?"

Leah thought about it. The pine and the stone and the cold-rock smell under the gun oil. The heat coming off him as he walked past her, like an open oven door. The way he'd taken the full weight of that thing on one arm and barely bent his knees.

She didn't have an answer. She didn't like not having an answer.

"I don't know," she said. "But he knows what *we* are. And he knows what they are." She tipped her head back at the glass, the bright room, the family of leeches and the half-leech baby and the boy who'd given his whole stupid heart to her. "And he knows where we live, and he decided to leave us alone. Today."

"Today," Jacob said.

"Yeah."

She stepped off the porch into the rain. It hit her like a cold shower and she didn't care. She walked to the edge of the trees and pulled the tank top off over her head and kicked off the shorts, and tied them to the cord around her ankle with fast, practised fingers, and let the wolf come.

It came up through her like fire through dry grass, and then she was standing on four feet in the ferns, grey and soaked and huge, with the whole forest pouring into her nose all at once.

Rain. Cedar. River. The house, behind her, sweet and sickly. Jacob on the porch, wood smoke and motor oil and boy.

And somewhere out there, north, in all that dark, a man in a suit with a vampire under his arm, who'd smelled like the forest had gotten into him.

*I'm on the outer loop,* she thought, to nobody, to the empty link. *Tell Seth to catch up.*

Then she put her nose down and ran.
