# Specimen

## Chapter One — North American Example

---

**Sol Duc Valley, Clallam County, Washington**
**Monday, 2 October 2006 — 4:52 a.m.**

The last anchor stone went into the ground under a nurse log the size of a bus.

Harry knelt in the moss to do it, because the stone had to sit against mineral soil and the moss here was a foot deep and holding about a bathtub's worth of rain. He peeled it back with both hands. The cold went up his sleeves to the elbow. Under the moss was black dirt, under the dirt was the grey glacial grit the whole peninsula sat on, and he pressed the stone into the grit until it seated with a small, final click he felt in the bones of his wrist.

It was a river cobble from the Hoh, flat, a little bigger than his palm. He'd carved it on the bonnet of the car three nights ago by head torch with a goblin awl, eleven runes around the rim and one in the centre, and he'd sworn at it the entire time because the stone had a quartz seam that kept trying to split. Two hundred and eleven others just like it were sitting in holes from the outskirts of Port Angeles down past Lake Quinault and out to the cliffs at Kalaloch.

When this one seated, the rest of them woke up.

He felt it go around the peninsula like a struck bell. A long pulse ran out from under his hands, west to the sea and east into the mountains, and came back to him a few seconds later from the other direction, having gone all the way round. The hair on his forearms lifted. Something in his back teeth hummed and then went quiet.

Harry stayed on his knees in the wet for a moment longer and listened to the lattice settle. It took a while for a thing that size to stop ringing. When it had, he could feel the whole of it if he reached for it, the way you can feel where your feet are in the dark. Two hundred and twelve points. A ring around the outside and three lines across the middle, like the spokes of a cartwheel somebody had dropped on Clallam and Jefferson counties.

He'd counted the stones twice. Croaker would ask. Also, counting things was how you knew you hadn't lost any of them.

"Right," he said to the nurse log. "Let's see who comes to dinner."

The nurse log, being a dead tree, kept its opinions to itself.

He stood, and his knees cracked, and he pressed the moss back down over the stone with the flat of his boot so that nobody walking by would see anything except moss. Nobody was going to walk by. The nearest trail was two miles north, and he'd laid a Muggle-Repelling Charm over this whole slope a week ago that made people remember, with real urgency, that they'd left the oven on.

The rain came down the way it had been coming down for eleven days, which was steadily, from no particular direction, without any apparent plan to stop. Water dripped off the brim of his cap. The forest smelled of cedar and rot and cold river, and under all that, very faint, of the ward itself, which smelled to him like a match just after it's been blown out.

He checked the lattice one more time before he left. He couldn't help it. It was the best piece of work he'd ever done and he wanted to look at it.

The ward asked two questions of anything that crossed it.

The first was simple: *Is there a heartbeat?* Deer had heartbeats. Elk had heartbeats. The black bears that were stuffing themselves stupid on the last of the salmonberries had heartbeats. So did hikers, fishermen, lost dogs, teenagers with a bottle of vodka and a tent, and the very large wolves that ran these woods and weren't wolves. Anything with a pulse went through the line and never knew it was there.

The second question was the clever one. It only got asked if the answer to the first was *no.*

*Is there human blood inside you that isn't yours?*

If the answer to that was also *no*, the ward let you go. A cold-bodied thing that ran on deer and elk and mountain lion went through untouched. Harry had spent two of his eleven days making very sure of that, because the Department had looked at seven yellow-eyed residents of a big glass house outside Forks and written MORE VALUABLE ALIVE across the top of their file in red ink, and Harry had no wish to explain to Saul Croaker why one of the Department's prospective assets was hanging by its ankle from a Douglas fir.

If the answer was *yes*—cold body, no pulse, somebody else's blood sloshing around in there—the ward closed.

He'd built the snare out of an old Gringotts deep-vault binding and a spell he'd invented in 2003 to catch an Erumpent in Zambia without exploding it or himself, and he was prouder of it than he was of almost anything. The Department called it the Laqueus Array, because the Department liked its Latin. Harry called it the Rabbit Trap.

He pulled his cap down, turned on the spot, and went home.

**Bogachiel River, south of Forks**
**5:04 a.m.**

The tent was pitched in a gravel clearing above the Bogachiel, where the river bent around a stand of alders and the logging road gave out into ferns.

From the outside it looked like something the Territorial Army had thrown away in 1974. It was a grey canvas box with a ridge pole, a guy rope that sagged, and a patched rip near the door flap. Anybody who saw it would assume an unemployed man was living in it. Nobody saw it. The clearing had seven layers of ward on it, starting with the Repelling Charm and ending with a thing Harry had learned from a very old woman in the Mountains of the Moon who'd refused to tell him its name.

He ducked through the flap and stood up straight on the other side.

The hall was thirty feet long with a slate floor, a row of brass hooks, a boot rack, and a mirror that said "You look knackered, love," every time he walked past it, because it had come with the tent and he'd never found the charm that shut it up. He hung his waxed jacket on a hook. Water ran off it and onto the slate and vanished before it got anywhere.

"Cheers," he told the mirror.

"Shave," said the mirror.

The kitchen was on the left. He put the kettle on with a glance, and while it ticked and grumbled toward boiling he pulled open the tall cupboard by the window. The inside of the cupboard was under a Stasis Charm, which made everything in it look painted. Half a marionberry pie from the diner on the highway sat on a plate on the middle shelf, exactly as fresh as it had been on Saturday afternoon. He looked at it for a while. Then he shut the cupboard, because it was five in the morning and even he had standards, and then he opened it again and took the pie out, because he'd just finished the best piece of work of his life and his standards could go and get stuffed.

He ate it standing up, with a fork, out of the dish.

There was a shoebox on the counter by the kettle with cassette tapes in it. Somebody in Tacoma had sold him the car with the shoebox still in the boot, and he'd kept the box out of respect. *Led Zeppelin IV. Back in Black. Paranoid. Machine Head. A Night at the Opera. Physical Graffiti,* both tapes, one with a cracked case. He'd brought a tape deck in from the car and spent a long Tuesday night working out how to keep it running inside a tent that was mostly made of magic, which had led to a rune sequence he'd ended up writing a paper on, which had led to Croaker telling him to stop writing papers on things he'd done because he was bored. He fished out *Zeppelin IV* and slotted it in. *Black Dog* came on loud enough to rattle the spoons. He turned it down by about half.

The kettle clicked off. He made tea with three sugars and drank it with his hip against the counter and his eyes on the window, which looked out on a view of the Bogachiel that didn't match the actual direction the tent was facing. He'd stopped noticing that years ago.

Eleven days in. Twelve hours from now or twelve weeks from now, something with red eyes was going to wander onto the peninsula and get snared. He had no way to make it happen faster. Waiting was most of the job. People who thought the Department of Mysteries was all time-turners and brains in tanks had never spent a fortnight in a damp tent in Washington State waiting for the phone to ring.

He finished the tea. He went to check the room, because he'd checked it every morning for eleven days and he wasn't going to stop now.

---

The corridor went on past the kitchen and the little sitting room with its sagging sofa and its wireless, past the bathroom and the bunk room and the library, which was smaller than the one in London but had better chairs. At the end of the corridor it split. Left was the armoury. Right was the specimen room.

He went left first.


The armoury was brick, because brick held runes better than canvas did. Every rack on every wall carried something with a sigil cut into it. The guns hung along the left side in a neat row, each one etched by a Gringotts smith with lines of goblin script that caught the light and seemed to move when you weren't looking straight at them, and none of them cared what kind of world they were fired in. There was a goblin-silver machete on a peg by the door. There were crates of Weasleys' Wizard Wheezes Peruvian Instant Darkness Powder, which George sold to the Department at a discount and to the Aurors at full price, stencilled DO NOT DROP in red, with SERIOUSLY underneath in George's handwriting. There was a sword in a glass case that had come out of a barrow in Wiltshire and hummed when it was hungry.

He walked the racks. He touched nothing. Everything was where he'd left it, and every rune was lit the steady blue that meant it was awake. He'd never once needed half of what was in here, and he'd stopped apologising for bringing it. Hermione had called it his toy box once, and he'd said yes, it was, and that was the point of being a grown-up.

He went back up the corridor and turned right.

---

The specimen room was the reason the Department let him keep the tent.

It was long and cold and very clean. The floor was black slate. The ceiling was high enough that the light-globes floating up there looked like a scatter of low moons. Down the centre of the room sat the table, a slab of basalt he'd Transfigured out of a lump of Snowdonia and worked over with anchor runes until it weighed what a lorry weighed and could hold anything that lay on it as fast as the Earth held a mountain. Beside it stood a trolley of instruments, most of which had no name outside Level Nine of the Ministry, and a lectern with a heavy grey logbook open on it and a long white quill standing upright in its stand, waiting.

The walls were shelves, and the shelves were the last eight years.

A jar with an Acromantula's eye in it, big as a grapefruit, the pupil still contracting now and then when the light changed. A slip of black cloth in a silver case, frost on the glass, which had been part of a Dementor until he'd taken it off one in the Orkneys; you could feel the cold of it through the case if you stood close, and he didn't. A grey hand in brine that occasionally tapped one finger against the side of its jar, very politely, like a man waiting for a bus. A whisker from a Nundu. A scale from a Chimaera, the colour of a bruise. A stoppered phial labelled LETHIFOLD — DO NOT OPEN — NO REALLY in his own handwriting. Notes. Boxes of notes. A Pensieve in the corner on a stone plinth, its surface still and silver, waiting for memories he'd want to keep exactly.

At the far end of the room was the Pantry.

He'd called it that once, as a joke, and the name had stuck. It was a cell eight feet by eight, walls of Transfigured obsidian two feet thick, the inside faces cut with so many wards they looked frosted. One wall was clear as glass from the outside and solid as rock from the inside. It was empty. He'd built it for this job specifically, using every number in the file and a few he'd made up, and it had been standing empty for eleven days, and every morning he came in and looked at it to make sure it was still empty and still ready, and it always was.

He stood in front of it now with his hands in his pockets and thought about Croaker's office.

---

It had been three weeks ago, a wet Thursday in London, the kind of afternoon where the lifts in the Ministry smelled of other people's umbrellas. Croaker's office was a small round room at the end of a corridor that didn't exist on any floor plan. The man himself was seventy-something and narrow and grey, with a face like a disappointed heron and a habit of looking at you over the top of his glasses as if he were reading your file off your forehead. Considering what Croaker could do, he might well have been.

"Forty-one," Croaker had said.

He'd put a folder on the desk. Harry had already read the folder. He'd written half the folder. He'd let Croaker say it anyway, because Croaker liked to say things.

"Forty-one British passport holders since 1971," Croaker said, "whose last confirmed movements end on a guided tour inside the walls of Volterra. Muggle police in four countries closed every one of them as a mugging, a drowning, a runaway, or a man who wanted to start a new life with a woman from Pisa."

"Could be the gelato," Harry said. "Very good gelato."

Croaker looked at him over the top of his glasses.

"Sorry," said Harry.

"You spent three weeks in that town in April."

"Twenty-two days."

"You watched a woman named Heidi lead a tour group of thirty-eight people into the Palazzo on the twenty-ninth."

"I did."

"How many came out?"

"None of them. I waited till four in the morning."

Croaker nodded slowly. "And then you sat in a café on the Piazza dei Priori for an entire week and read the mind of the human woman who answers their telephones."

"Gianna," said Harry. "She takes her coffee with too much sugar and she wants them to make her into one of them more than she's ever wanted anything. She thinks they will. She thinks it's a matter of time."

"Is it?"

"I think it's a matter of lunch."

Croaker didn't laugh. He rarely did. "None of this," he said, "would be on my desk if a boy hadn't tried to walk into the sun in the middle of a public festival on the nineteenth of March. A witch from Basingstoke was on holiday in that square. She reported a glittering youth and a group of men in dark cloaks pulling him into an alley, and the Beasts Division filed it under hallucinations brought on by Chianti. It came to us by accident. Do you know how many of the most important things in this Department come to us by accident, Potter?"

"Most of them?"

"All of them." Croaker opened the folder and turned it around. A photograph, taken with a long lens from a rooftop: three figures walking under the arch of the city gate, a short dark girl, a tall boy with bronze-coloured hair, and a brown-haired human girl between them, looking like she hadn't slept in a week. "Your glittering youth. His name is Edward Cullen. He lives on the Olympic Peninsula in Washington State with a family of six others who drink, as far as we can tell, nothing but animal blood. Their patriarch is a physician at the local hospital. He has, by the estimate you pulled out of Gianna's head, been alive since roughly the reign of Charles the First. He has lived among the Volturi. He has walked out of their house and kept his head, which is more than most. That is an asset. We do not dissect assets."

"What if they dissect themselves? Asking for a friend."

"You will not go near them."

"I'll be very near them. It's a small town."

"You will not *touch* them." Croaker took his glasses off and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "The Minister has signed off on contingency planning. At some point, Potter, someone is going to walk into that hill in Tuscany and take it apart, and I would very much like to know what is waiting inside it before we do. There are thirty-odd of them in that palazzo. Some of them can do things that make the Imperius Curse look like a parlour trick. And we know nothing about their bodies. Nothing. We have a family of seven yellow-eyed creatures in America whom I wish to keep friendly, a series of disappearances in Seattle this summer that stopped rather suddenly in June, and a whole continent of nomads that wander about eating hikers. Find me one of the hiker-eaters. Red eyes. Hostile. Bring it back in a condition that can still answer questions."

"Define condition."

"Talking," said Croaker. He put his glasses back on. "The full licence applies. All three, if you need them."

Harry had nodded. He hadn't asked whether Croaker meant it. Croaker never said anything he didn't mean, and the licence had been Harry's since 2003. It was in a drawer somewhere in his flat in Islington, a sheet of parchment with the Minister's signature and Croaker's and a seal that tasted of iron if you touched it. Eight years ago the thought of carrying it would have turned his stomach. These days it sat in the drawer with the takeaway menus and he thought about it about as often.

"And Potter?"

"Sir."

"Wear a tie."

---

He looked at the empty Pantry a while longer. Then he went back down the corridor, rinsed the pie dish, and went to get dressed for town.

Town meant the jacket. It was old brown leather, cracked at the elbows, bought in a shop in Camden when he was nineteen and too thin for it. He'd filled it out since. Eight years of running up mountains and down into vaults and across deserts, of being thrown off things and climbing back on, had put a lot of weight on him, and all of it was the useful kind. He was six foot two in his socks. The leather sat tight across the shoulders.

He checked the man in the bathroom mirror while he shaved, because the hall mirror had a point. Black hair that did what it liked no matter what he did to it. Green eyes. A face that had got harder and squarer since the war and looked, in this light, about thirty. He was twenty-six. The skin of his forehead was smooth. The scar had faded in the summer of 1999, a few weeks after he'd started the work that turned into all of this, and one morning it had simply been gone. He'd stood in front of a mirror in Grimmauld Place for twenty minutes, touching the spot. Then he'd gone to work.

He'd been a junior in the Department then, barely a year out of the war, with a name everybody knew and a skill set that was almost entirely about killing one specific man. The Aurors had wanted him. He'd done six months of their training and spent most of it bored stiff. Everything they taught was built to fight the last war, and the last war was over. So he'd gone downstairs to the black door instead and asked Croaker what he'd need to know to never be surprised again.

Croaker had laughed. It was the only time Harry had heard him do it.

Then he'd given him a list.

Wandless casting had taken fourteen months in the Mountains of the Moon at Uagadou, with the old woman who'd taught him the ward that had no name, and a lot of very humiliating mornings in which he couldn't light a candle with his hands while ten-year-olds did it with their eyes. Warding had taken three winters with a goblin breaker in the deep vaults under Gringotts, a creature called Gornuk who never once said his name without spitting afterwards and who'd taught him more about how magic held things together than anyone at Hogwarts ever had. Legilimency had been a mind-healer in Vienna who'd broken him open and put him back together twenty times before he got the knack of keeping her out, and twenty more before he could get into her. Runes in Norway. Old law magic in a monastery in Armenia where nobody spoke English and the food was terrible. Duelling everywhere, with anyone who'd agree to it, until people stopped agreeing.

He'd invented things along the way, because once you understood how a spell was built you started seeing where it could be built better. A silent shield that caught physical force and threw it back. The rune sequence that let the tape deck in the kitchen play *Black Dog* without catching fire. The Rabbit Trap. About a dozen others, half of them classified and half of them so boring that nobody had bothered.

None of it had happened fast. Most of it had happened at three in the morning, in borrowed rooms, while he failed at something for the four hundredth time. He'd never minded the failing. The failing was where the good stuff lived.

He rinsed the razor. He pulled on a grey T-shirt, a flannel shirt over that, jeans, boots, and the jacket. He put the holly wand up his left sleeve in its holster, out of habit; he hadn't needed it to cast in years, but a wand was a good thing to have for people to look at while you were doing something else. He slung a camera bag over one shoulder, a big Nikon inside it with a long lens and a card of photographs of moss, because Harry Evans was a freelance photographer from Reading doing a coffee-table book about the temperate rainforests of the Pacific Northwest, and Harry Evans needed to look like he'd taken at least some pictures.

He'd picked the surname for his mum. Nobody had ever noticed.

He went out through the flap into the rain. The car was parked under the alders, beaded with water: a 1967 Chevrolet Impala, four-door hardtop, black, the chrome gone a bit cloudy at the edges. He'd bought it in Tacoma from a widower who'd cried when he handed over the keys. It was the least sensible car in the state of Washington. He loved it with his whole heart.

He got in. It smelled of old leather and road. He turned the key and the engine came up rough and deep and he sat for a second just listening to it.

Then he drove into Forks to get breakfast.

**Forks, Washington — the diner on Highway 101**
**7:18 a.m.**

Charlie Swan saw the car before he saw the man.

It was parked across two spaces at the far end of the lot, nose out, where nobody could box it in. A '67 Impala. Black. Waxed. In Forks, in October, waxing a car was like ironing a shirt to go swimming. Charlie looked at it for a second longer than he meant to while he got out of the cruiser. Then he looked at it a second longer again on his way to the door.

Washington plates. Clean.

Inside, the diner smelled the way it always smelled at quarter past seven: coffee that had been sitting, bacon, the bleach Maxine used on the counter. The radio over the grill was playing something with a steel guitar. Charlie took off his hat, nodded at Maxine, nodded at old Ron Hollis and his brother in their usual booth, and slid into his own spot at the counter, the third stool from the end, where his knees fit and he could see the door in the mirror behind the coffee machine.

The man was in the corner booth.

Charlie would have spotted him anyway. Forks had about three thousand people in it and he'd arrested or given a ride home to a good chunk of them. Strangers stood out like a hand-painted sign. This one stood out more than most. Big guy. Six-two, maybe a hair over. Shoulders on him like a linebacker who'd kept up with it after college. Dark hair that looked like he'd towelled it and walked away. A brown leather jacket so old it had gone soft and pale at the seams, a camera bag on the seat beside him, and in front of him, at seven-eighteen on a Monday morning, a cup of coffee and a slice of pie.

Charlie squinted at it in the mirror. Apple. With ice cream.

"Morning, Chief." Maxine put his coffee down without asking. "The usual?"

"Yeah. Thanks." He tipped his head a quarter-inch toward the corner. "He been in before?"

Maxine didn't look. She'd been waiting tables for twenty-six years; she didn't need to. "Most mornings, last week and a half. Sits right there. Tips like he's got a guilty conscience." She lowered her voice. "Calls me *love*."

"He what?"

"He's English. They do that." She seemed to have made her peace with it. "He's a photographer. Doing a book on the trees."

"On the trees."

"That's what he said, Charlie. Don't make that face at me."

She went off to put his order in. Charlie drank his coffee and watched the guy in the mirror. It had been a long few weeks, and watching people was something he could still do that made sense.

Bella was... Bella was okay. That was the thing he'd decided. Bella was okay, and better than okay, and she was married and she had the kid, Edward's niece, Renesmee, who was the most beautiful baby Charlie had ever laid eyes on and who seemed to have grown about two inches since Saturday. Jacob had taken off his clothes in Charlie's front yard and turned into a wolf the size of a horse, and then turned back into Jacob, and Charlie had sat down on the porch steps and not gotten up for a while. And then they'd driven him out to the Cullens', and Bella had come down the stairs, and she'd been so careful with him, moving so slow, and her eyes...

*Need to know*, Jacob had said. That was the deal. Charlie didn't need to know. Charlie got to keep his daughter and hold his granddaughter, who was not his granddaughter, who was absolutely his granddaughter, and he didn't ask.

So he didn't ask. He watched an Englishman eat pie.

The guy ate it like it had done something to deserve it. Big forkfuls. He didn't look up much. When he did, though, Charlie noticed where his eyes went: the door, the window, the kitchen hatch, the hallway to the restrooms and the back exit, the door again. Charlie had seen plenty of nervous men. This one went to the same four spots in the same order every couple of minutes, like he was counting them.

Charlie's breakfast came. Two eggs over easy, bacon, wheat toast. He ate it.

At seven-thirty-one the Englishman got up, took his cup to the counter to get a refill himself, because Maxine was in the back, and stopped by the corkboard next to the register to read the notices. Lost cat. Firewood for sale. The Quileute Days raffle results from July that nobody had taken down. And the flyer Charlie had pinned up himself on Sunday afternoon, with the two faces on it.

The guy stood in front of the flyer for a long time.

Charlie knew it by heart. MISSING. Mark Ostrander, 44, and his son Danny, 14, of Olympia. Last seen Friday, September 29, Hurricane Ridge trailhead, Olympic National Park. Mark in a red rain shell. Danny in a blue one, with a lift pass from Crystal Mountain still hanging off the zipper because the kid wouldn't take it off, his mom had said on the phone, and her voice had gone at the end of that sentence and not come back. The Park Service had a team on it. Clallam County had a team on it. Charlie had put up flyers, because it was what he could do.

The Englishman read it. Then he read it again; Charlie could see his eyes go back to the top.

"Hurricane Ridge," the man said, to nobody, in an accent like something off public television. "That's up past Port Angeles, yeah?"

Charlie took a second to realize the question was for him.

"About an hour east," he said. "Up in the park."

The man turned. Up close he was younger than Charlie had figured, mid-twenties maybe, though his face had the kind of set to it that made you add a few years. Green eyes. Very green. He looked at Charlie's uniform and then Charlie's face and then, briefly, Charlie's hands, which Charlie noticed because he'd been trained to do the same thing.

"You're the chief," the guy said.

"Charlie Swan."

"Harry Evans." He didn't offer to shake, which Charlie appreciated, since he was holding a piece of toast. He nodded at the flyer instead. "Bears, d'you reckon?"

"Probably weather," said Charlie. "Fog comes in fast up there. People get turned around."

"Probably," said Evans.

He said it the way you'd agree with a man about a football score you both knew was wrong. Charlie felt the back of his neck go tight, and didn't know why, and didn't like it.

Evans looked back at the flyer one more time. At the kid. Danny Ostrander had a gap between his front teeth and a sunburn across his nose in the picture, and he was grinning at whoever was holding the camera like they'd just said something stupid. Evans's jaw moved slightly, like he was grinding something small between his back teeth. Then he turned and went back to his booth and sat down.

Maxine came out. "Oh, you got your own coffee, honey, I'm sorry—"

"No trouble, love," said Evans.

Maxine gave Charlie a look that said *see?*

Charlie finished his eggs. He told himself to finish his eggs and go to work. He had a stolen outboard motor, a dispute between two neighbors on Calawah Way over a fence, and a meeting with the county about the budget that was going to make him want to lie down on the floor of his office. He finished his eggs. He drank his coffee. And then, because his granddaughter had grown two inches since Saturday, and because his son-in-law was cold to the touch, and because a boy he'd known since diapers was a wolf, Charlie Swan got up with his cup and walked over to the corner booth and stood there.

Evans looked up. He didn't seem surprised.

"Help you, Chief?"

"Just being neighborly." Charlie nodded at the camera bag. "Maxine says you're doing a book."

"Trying to." Evans pushed the plate a few inches away, like he was done, though there was still a corner of crust on it. "The rainforests. Hoh, Quinault, Queets. You've got moss here like nowhere I've ever seen. My publisher's got this idea that people in London want to sit on their sofas looking at pictures of moss."

"Do they?"

"God knows. They're paying for it." Evans smiled, briefly, a sideways thing that was there and gone. "You get a lot of tourists?"

"Summers. Not so much now."

"No. Bit quiet." He turned his cup around on the table. "Somebody at the outfitters said you've had big wolves round here. Bigger than they ought to be. A couple of years back, people saw them all the time."

Charlie didn't move. He was proud of that later. He didn't move at all.

"Folks see all kinds of things in the woods," he said.

"That's what the lad at the shop said. Mike, his name was." Evans looked up at him. "Said they're probably just big dogs gone wild. Said not to worry. Said it about three times, actually." He picked up the last of the crust with his fingers and ate it. "I'd love a picture, if they're real. Imagine it. Moss and a wolf. The publisher'd wet himself."

"I'd stay out of the woods after dark," said Charlie. "Wolves or no wolves. Ground's bad. Lot of drops you can't see till you're in them."

"That's good advice," said Evans. He said it like he meant it. "Cheers, Chief."

Charlie nodded. He went back to the counter. He paid. He put on his hat. When he passed the corner booth on his way out, Evans had his eyes on the window and his hand flat on the table beside his cup, very still, and he didn't look up, and Charlie had the strange idea that the man knew exactly where Charlie was in the room anyway, the way you know where the stove is in your own kitchen in the dark.

Outside, it was raining.

Charlie got into the cruiser. He sat there with the engine running and the wipers going, and he looked at the Impala in the rain at the far end of the lot, and after a while he picked up his pen and wrote the plate number on the back of his hand.

He ran it at the station at eight-fifteen. It came back registered to a Harry J. Evans of Tacoma, purchased in a private sale September 19th. No warrants. No wants. Not so much as a parking ticket. A British passport on file with Immigration, visa in order, the kind of clean that took paperwork.

Charlie sat looking at the screen for a while.

Then he thought about Jacob, sitting at his kitchen table with a plate of eggs and his big hands spread out flat on the wood, saying, *Charlie. You don't want to know. I mean it. It's better if you don't know.*

He cleared the screen. He went to deal with the stolen outboard.

At the diner, when Maxine went to bus the corner booth, she found a twenty and a ten folded under the coffee cup for a seven-dollar check, and on the corkboard by the register someone had unpinned the Ostranders' flyer and pinned it back up again, straighter, in the middle, where people would see it first.

**North of the Calawah River**
**5:31 p.m.**

The rain had been coming down since Thursday and Leah had stopped feeling it somewhere around Saturday.

That was the one good thing about the fur. Water ran off the top coat and never got down to her skin, and the cold couldn't get in anyway, not with her running at a hundred and eight degrees. **The same creek**
**5:44 p.m.**

Cole Haskett had been dead for a hundred and nineteen years, and in all that time he had never once been unable to move.

He was thinking about that from six directions at once.

It was how their minds went. He'd had a long time to get used to it. One part of him was counting: four seconds since the jolt, five, six. One part was reaching for his own legs, his hands, his jaw, the way you'd reach in the dark for a light switch, and finding nothing there to reach with. One part was listening to the two dogs below him—their hearts pounding, their claws on the rock, the big sandy one shaking water out of its coat. One part was thinking, very clearly and in his mother's voice, which he hadn't heard in a century, *Lord, Lord, what have you got yourself into now.* One part was looking at the creek. One part was hungry. That part was always hungry.

None of the parts could make his body do anything.

He'd been in the air, over the water, and something had closed on his right ankle like a fist made of ice. Then it had gone through all of him. He'd felt it go: up his leg, through his hips, up his spine, out to the ends of his fingers, setting every part of him behind it, like water freezing in a pipe. The world had swung over. Now he was hanging upside down over a creek in the rain and he couldn't so much as blink.

His eyes moved. That was all. He could look.

He looked at the grey dog. It was the smaller of the two, the fast one, the one that had nearly had him on the ridge. It had its front feet up on a rock and its back legs bunched and it was looking at his arm the way he looked at a throat.

He thought, *Here it comes, then*.

And then the air across the creek split open with a crack like a thunderbolt hitting a barn, and a man was standing in the ferns.

Cole hadn't heard him coming. He'd have heard a man coming from half a mile. He heard everything—he could hear the beetles in the bark of this hemlock if he cared to—and there'd been nothing. Rain. Dogs. The creek. Then the crack, and the man, standing there as if he'd been planted.

He was tall. Broad through the chest and shoulders. He wore a black suit, a white shirt, a narrow black tie, and over it all a long black coat that came to his knees and was somehow not wet, though the rain was coming straight down on it. His shoes were black and shone like piano keys. The mud didn't seem to want to touch them. He had on a pair of dark glasses, at dusk, in the rain.

He looked up at Cole first.

Cole saw him do it. Saw his head tip back, saw the glasses take in the whole of him—ankle, legs, the red jacket, the hands hanging down. The man's face didn't change much. It went a little satisfied around the mouth, like someone who'd set a mousetrap and come down in the morning and found it had worked.

"Well," the man said. "Look at you."

His voice was English. Cole hadn't heard an English voice in thirty years.

The dogs had both swung round to face him. The big sandy one was on the far bank, crouched, ears back, teeth bared. The grey one had come down off the rock and was standing stiff-legged in the shallows, and the growl coming out of her was so low Cole felt it more than heard it.

The man looked at them. Then he reached into his coat.

Both dogs dropped lower. The man took his hand out slowly, holding a flat black leather wallet, and flipped it open with his thumb and held it up toward them. Cole could see it from where he hung: a silver crest on one side, a lion and a unicorn rearing up either side of a shield, and on the other side a card with writing he couldn't read upside down and through the rain.

"Evening," said the man. "This situation is contained. I'm going to have to ask you both to vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

The dogs didn't move.

Nobody said anything. The creek ran. The rain came down. Cole hung there with the blood of the two hikers he had killed on Friday slowing in him like cold syrup, and stared at the Englishman, who was holding up a badge to a pair of wolves as though he expected them to read it.

Then the man said, "Yes, it's a badge."

Nobody had asked him anything.

"Yes," he went on, "I'm showing it to a pair of wolves. I don't make exceptions for fur. It's a professional thing."

He shut the wallet and slid it back inside his coat. Then he took off the dark glasses, folded them one-handed, and tucked them into his breast pocket, and looked at the two dogs with his bare eyes. They were green. Cole could see that even from fifteen feet up in the dusk. Very green, and very steady, and moving between the dogs, the grey and then the sandy and then the grey again.

The sandy one's ears went flat.

"Course I can hear you," said the man. "You're both shouting. It's like standing outside a pub at chucking-out time."

Cole's mind, all six parts of it, went still at once.

He'd known one vampire, years ago, outside Omaha, who said he could hear what you were thinking when you were close. Cole hadn't believed it until the man told him what he'd had for supper in 1888. He'd stayed away from that one afterwards. This was different. This was a man. A living man; Cole could hear his heart from here, slow and big and steady, slower than any man's he'd ever heard. A living man standing in the rain answering two dogs that hadn't made a sound.

The grey one snarled. Her whole lip came up off her teeth.

"Then stop leaving the door open, Lassie," said the man.

The grey dog's head jerked as if she'd been slapped. On the far bank, the sandy one made a noise. A short, choked huff, its shoulders twitching. Cole had heard enough dogs to know what that sounded like. The big sandy one was laughing.

The man glanced over at it. "Don't laugh, Balto. You're next."

The sandy dog stopped laughing.

Then it cocked its head, all at once, and the man looked at it for a moment and said, sounding mildly offended, "Do I look like I sparkle?"

A pause. The sandy dog's head went the other way.

"Cheers," said the man. "I showered."

The grey dog had stopped snarling. She was standing stiff in the shallows with the water breaking around her legs, and she was staring at the man in a way Cole recognized, the way he imagined he'd been staring himself. Then her head swung up, toward Cole, and back toward the man, and her lips peeled back again.

"My snare," the man said to her. "My tree. My operation. Your kill's been requisitioned. I'll send you a form."

Her back legs bunched.

Cole saw it. The man didn't look at her; his eyes stayed on the sandy one across the creek. But he said, in exactly the same voice, "I wouldn't, love. I've had a long week."

And he snapped his fingers.

Whatever was holding Cole's ankle let go.

He fell. Headfirst, rigid, fifteen feet, straight down toward the creek and the rocks, and every part of his mind shouted at once and his body did nothing at all, and the man took one long step forward into the water and caught him.

He caught him around the middle, one arm, the way you'd catch a sack of feed tossed off a wagon. Cole weighed what a stone statue of himself would weigh. He'd broken the backs of horses by landing on them. The man took all of it on one arm and didn't so much as bend his knees. He swung Cole up and round, quick and neat, until Cole was lying flat and stiff along his side with his head forward and his feet out behind, tucked under the man's arm with his face six inches from the man's ribs.

"Surf's up," said the man, to nobody in particular.

The grey dog hadn't sprung. She was standing in the water with her mouth a little open and the rain running off her ears.

Cole could smell the man now. He'd been waiting to, through all of it. A living man's smell was the first thing he knew about anyone, and with a man this big, standing this close, with his heart going under Cole's face, it ought to have been a smell like supper.

The man smelled wrong. He smelled of turned earth and pine and wet stone, and under that of heat, like a dog's coat in front of a fire, and under that of something sharp and clean and electric, the way the air smells for a second after lightning hits a tree. There was blood in him. Cole could hear it going round. But the smell of it made something in the back of his throat close up as if he'd swallowed a mouthful of creek mud. His thirst—his thirst, which had been his whole life for a hundred and nineteen years—turned its face away.

That frightened him more than the falling had.

The man was still looking across the creek. Cole couldn't see the sandy dog anymore; all he could see was the man's coat and the creek going by below his face. He heard the man's breath go in.

"Tell Black and the doctor that the Cullens are on the good list," the man said. "Tell them to keep it that way."

A pause. Something changed in the man's face. Cole saw it from underneath: a small hitch in his jaw, a tilt of his head, like a man hearing a radio in the next room.

"Four," said the man. "It's four, Balto. *x* equals four. Show your working."

Another pause. Then, quieter: "And whatever it is you two are both trying very hard not to think about—I'm not asking. Not tonight."

The rain came down on all of them.

Then the man went still. It was a different stillness than before, and it lasted longer. Cole felt it through the man's side, felt the big slow heart under his face give one heavier beat, and then another.

"Harry," the man said. Very quietly.

He stood there for a second, maybe two. When he spoke again his voice had gone rough at the edges.

"...Sorry, mate."

Nobody answered him. Nobody had answered him the whole time.

He cleared his throat. He shifted Cole a little higher under his arm. "Stay south of the Sol Duc for a few weeks, would you? Both of you. I've got things in these woods that won't care what you smell like."

He lifted his free hand. It was a small movement, two fingers, a sort of flick toward the far bank, low down, the way a man might flick a bit of lint off a coat. Cole didn't see what it did. He saw the man's eyes move with it, and he saw the corner of his mouth go up.

"Lassie," said the man. A nod to his left. "Balto." A nod across the creek.

Then he turned on his heel in the water, and the world went black and closed around Cole like a fist.

It was the worst thing he'd ever felt. Worse than the fire of his own changing, a hundred and nineteen years ago in a barn in the Dakota Territory, because that had at least happened inside him. This happened to all of him from the outside, at once. He was being pressed through something narrow. Something with no air and no light and no sides. He was being pushed down a pipe that was smaller than he was, and he couldn't breathe, though he didn't need to breathe, and he couldn't scream, though every one of the six parts of his mind was screaming—

And then he was through.

Light. Cold, still air. The smell of stone, and of old things in jars, and of the man.

The man said, "Right," and set him down on his back on something hard and flat and very cold, and Cole stared up past him at a high dark ceiling where a dozen little globes of white light hung in the air like moons.

He laid two fingers against the side of the vampire's neck and held them there for a full minute by the clock on the wall, and felt nothing at all. The skin was very hard, and very smooth, and the cold of it went into his fingertips like the cold of a gravestone in January. "Skin temperature..." He drew a thin line in the air above the specimen's forehead with one finger, and a small number glowed there for a second and faded. "Nine degrees Celsius, which is what the creek was. He's the temperature of wherever he's been. No body heat of his own."

He tapped the vampire's forearm with the silver probe.

It rang. A short, high, clean note, like a spoon on a crystal glass.

"Christ," said Harry, and did it again, because it was the strangest sound he'd ever heard a body make. It rang again. "Skin's hard. Harder than stone. It rings when you hit it, like quartz. Consistent with the crystalline theory. Consistent with the glittering."

He put the probe down and looked at the vampire's face.

"Mouth," he said, and with a small movement of two fingers he loosened the snare's bind from the jaw up. Only the jaw. The rest stayed locked.

The specimen's mouth opened. Its lips drew back. Its teeth were very white and very even and looked, at first glance, like anybody's teeth, until you saw the edges on them, fine as a razor along every one. A clear fluid had pooled under the tongue and along the gums. It caught the light like water and moved like oil.

"Venom," said Harry. "Clear, slightly viscous. Pooling, presumably because he's looking at me." He took a glass pipette from the trolley and, without putting a single finger anywhere near the teeth, drew off a few drops with a Summoning Charm so small it barely counted, and sealed the phial and labelled it. "Venom sample one. Goes to the box. Nobody touches it."

The vampire's jaw worked. Then it spoke.

"Where am I?"

Its voice was low and smooth, too smooth, with a slow drawl under it like a man from somewhere flat and dry. It had a strange quality, a kind of resonance, like a voice coming out of a cello.

"Tent," said Harry.

"What tent?"

"Mine." He picked up the silver probe again. "What's your name?"

The vampire looked at him. Its eyes went to the probe and back. "You can't hold me," it said. "Whatever you are. I've been dead a long time, son. Longer than your granddaddy's granddaddy. There's not a rope made—"

"There's no rope," said Harry. "You noticed that, I think. Name."

"Go to hell."

"Been. Didn't care for it." He made a note. "Specimen is verbal. English. American accent, Midwestern or plains, older pattern of speech. Hostile. Making threats. Let's see what else he does."

He went down the list.

He checked the fingernails, which were hard and smooth as the skin and wouldn't take a scratch from a silver blade. He checked the hair, which wouldn't cut with ordinary scissors and only parted, grudgingly, under a Severing Charm. He bagged the lock that came away. He measured the specimen with a tape. He took the weight off the table runes and wrote it down with a note to recheck it in London, because it was a number he didn't quite believe; the specimen was a good deal heavier than any man his size. He looked into the eyes with a little light. The pupils tracked it, hard and fast, and contracted to pinpoints, and never once watered.

"Doesn't blink," said Harry. "Doesn't need to. When he does it's for show. Same with breathing, I expect." He looked at the vampire. "Take a breath."

The vampire didn't.

"Go on. Smell me."

The vampire's nostrils flared, once, and its whole face changed. Its lip curled back off the teeth on one side.

"There we go," said Harry. "Breathes to smell. Breathes to talk. Doesn't need it for anything else." He wrote. "Behaviour. Specimen tracks motion constantly, eyes only. Tracks me, tracks the door, tracks the trolley. Fixates on objects associated with feeding." He tipped his head toward the envelope. "Exhibits total stillness. When it isn't speaking it doesn't move at all, not even in the bits I've left it. Humans fidget. This doesn't. This waits."

The vampire stared at him.

"Now," said Harry. "Apparent vulnerabilities."

He went to the shelf behind him and came back with a wicker basket. It had a garland of garlic in it, a silver crucifix, a stoppered bottle of water, a little silver knife, a hawthorn stake, and a mirror.

The vampire looked at the basket. For the first time it looked something other than angry or afraid. It looked insulted.

"You're joking."

"British vampires don't like garlic," said Harry. "That's the Sanguini type. The registered sort. The ones in my country are scared of it; it's on the Ministry's information leaflet. I've got to check." He held the garland of garlic six inches from the vampire's nose. "Breathe in."

The vampire breathed in, deliberately, and looked him in the eye while it did it.

"No reaction to garlic," said Harry. "Bit of contempt." He put it back. He held up the crucifix. Nothing. He uncorked the bottle and let a drop fall on the vampire's cheek. "Holy water. Westminster Abbey. I nicked it off a verger." The drop sat on the cold skin like a bead on a car bonnet, and stayed there, and nothing happened at all. He drew the little silver knife lightly across the back of the specimen's hand. It skated off with a squeal and left nothing. He touched the hawthorn stake to the centre of its chest and pressed, and the point of the stake broke off.

He held up the mirror.

The vampire's face was in it: pale, long-jawed, red-eyed, the rain still beading in its hair.

"Reflects," said Harry. He sighed. "Bram Stoker's estate owes me a refund."

"What *are* you?"

"Busy." He put the basket away. "Last one."

He stepped back from the table. He held his left hand out over the specimen with the palm turned down, and said, quietly, "*Lumos Solem*."

Sunlight came out of his hand.

It was a small spell. Hermione had used it in their first year to get rid of a lot of Devil's Snare. Harry had spent a summer in Cairo working out how to fix the colour of the light, and what fell on the table now was noon on a clear day, white-gold and hard-edged, so bright the specimen's shadow went black on the basalt.

And the vampire lit up.

Every inch of exposed skin threw the light back. It broke and scattered off the face and the hands and the bare feet in a thousand tiny points, as if somebody had taken a statue of a man and glued diamond dust all over it. The ceiling above the table filled with dancing specks. The jars on the shelves glittered. The Acromantula's eye contracted to a slit.

The vampire lay there glittering. No smoke came off it. It made no sound at all.

Harry stood and looked at it for a long moment.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, you have got to be kidding me."

The vampire's eyes moved to him, sparkling.

"Okay," said Harry. "Okay. For the record." He cleared his throat. "Specimen exposed to simulated sunlight, full spectrum. Skin refracts it. Strongly. It's... it sparkles. The Volterra witness reported a glittering youth and she was right; I owe her an apology and a box of chocolates. No visible damage. No apparent pain. No smoke, no burning, no change in skin temperature. Sunlight does not harm the specimen in any way I can see. It just makes him look like a disco ball." He closed his hand and the light went out. "So that's why they live in Forks. Rains three hundred days a year. Nobody's going to notice you sparkling in the Thriftway if the sun never comes out."

The vampire's face, in ordinary light again, looked very slightly humiliated.

"Apparent vulnerabilities, summary," said Harry. "None of the classical ones. Not garlic, not silver, not holy items, not wood, not sunlight. Intelligence from Volterra says they can be killed by being torn apart and burned, and the Quileute apparently do exactly that. We'll get to it." He looked at the clock. "End phase one. Eighteen nineteen."

> **PHASE TWO — MAGICAL RESPONSE**
> Objective: establish whether standard spells, including Stunning Spell, Full Body-Bind, and Legilimency, function as expected.

"Method," said Harry. He walked to the far end of the table and picked up his wand. Holly and phoenix feather, eleven inches, the handle worn pale where his thumb went. "First set's cast to standard Ministry form. Wand, spoken incantation, regulation output. What an Auror would throw on a normal day. I want to know what the average professional gets. Then I'll do it my way, for comparison."

The vampire watched the wand.

"You'll feel this," said Harry. "Sorry."

He didn't sound very sorry. He knew that. He didn't try to fix it.

He released the snare's bind completely, left the table's anchor where it was, and stepped back.

The vampire came alive all at once.

Everything that wasn't touching the stone moved. Its head snapped up off the slab and slammed back down and came up again. Its fingers raked the basalt either side of its hips, ten hard white hooks scraping for purchase, and found none. It made a noise through its teeth like a sheet of tin tearing. The anchor runes flared blue under it and held, and held, and held, and after a few seconds the specimen worked out that they were going to keep holding and went still again, as suddenly as it had started, staring up at Harry with its lips drawn back.

"That's a lot of energy," said Harry. "Good. *Stupefy.*"

Red light hit the vampire square in the chest.

Its head dropped back against the stone. Its eyes rolled, and its jaw went slack, and for a moment it looked like any Stunned man Harry had ever seen.

He counted. One. Two. Three. Four—

The eyes rolled back down and fixed on him.

"Four seconds," said Harry. He wrote it in the air with his finger and the quill took it down. "Standard Stunning Spell, regulation output, centre mass. Four seconds of incapacity. In a human, that's somewhere between five minutes and an hour, depending on the caster. Here it's four seconds. That's enough time to say 'Oh, bugger,' and not much else."

He put the wand away in his sleeve and looked at the vampire, and Stunned it again without moving his hands or opening his mouth.

This time there was no red light to see. The vampire's head simply went back against the basalt and stayed there.

Harry pulled a stool over from the wall and sat on it and watched the clock.

At forty-one seconds the specimen's eyes opened.

"Forty-one," said Harry. "Silent, my output. That's about ten times the regulation spell. Still nowhere near what it would do to a man. Note for London: a Stunner doesn't knock these out. It puts them on pause. Useful if you're already running away."

The vampire turned its head and looked at him. It didn't say anything. Its eyes had a new kind of care in them.

"Next," said Harry.

He stood and traced a circle on the basalt around the specimen's right arm with one fingertip. The line glowed, faintly, and stayed. Inside the circle, he lifted the table's anchor off the arm and left it on everything else.

The arm moved before he'd finished lifting.

It came up off the stone like a spring. The hand shot at his throat, fingers hooked. Harry had already said "*Petrificus Totalus*" with the wand back out and pointed, and the arm locked solid in the air, hand open, eighteen inches from his neck.

He didn't step back. He looked at the hand, and then at the clock.

"Full Body-Bind, regulation, applied to the right arm only. Holding." He counted under his breath. "Five. Six. Seven."

At nine seconds he heard it. A fine, high creak, like ice on a pond in the first mild week of spring.

At ten, the fingers twitched.

At eleven the bind broke with a sound like a cracked plate, and the hand went for him.

It got as far as his tie. He felt the fingers close on the silk and pull, a short hard jerk that would have taken his head off his shoulders if his head had been where the vampire expected it to be. Then the table's anchor slammed back down on the arm without Harry moving anything but his attention, and the arm hit the basalt so hard the whole slab rang like a bell, and the specimen lay there with its fist on the stone and two feet of black silk hanging out of it.

Harry looked down at what was left of his tie. About eight inches of it, ending in a frayed tuft at his breastbone.

"Eleven seconds," he said. "Write that down twice."

The quill wrote it down twice.

"That was a good tie," said Harry.

The vampire looked up at him and, for the first time, smiled.

"Next time," it said, "it won't be the tie."

"There's no next time, mate. You're on a table." He pulled the stub of the tie off over his head and dropped it in the bin. "Standard Body-Bind holds for about eleven seconds under strain. That's not long enough to finish a sentence with *Incendio* in it. My bind's held him since quarter to six without a flicker, so it can be done. But that's not a spell an Auror's going to learn in a weekend course."

He tried *Incarcerous* next, on the same arm, in the same ring. Ropes sprang out of the air and wrapped the forearm from wrist to elbow, thick hemp, a dozen turns. The vampire flexed once and every turn of rope snapped at the same time and fell in pieces on the stone.

"Conjured restraints, useless," said Harry. "Under half a second." He took the anchor back over the arm and rubbed the glowing ring off the basalt with his thumb.

"Confundus," he said, and cast it. The vampire's eyes went loose and wandering for a few seconds, drifting over the ceiling as if it had forgotten what ceilings were for. Then they snapped back. "About six seconds," said Harry, "and I'd bet he knew it was happening the whole time. Those minds run on more than one track. You fog one, the others notice."

He put the stool back against the wall.

"Imperius," he said.

The vampire's eyes came to him very fast.

"You'll like this one," Harry told it. "Doesn't hurt. Feels quite nice, I'm told." He pointed the holly wand at its forehead. "*Imperio.*"

He felt the spell land, and he felt the mind under it.

It was like closing his hand on a bucket of eels. The specimen's thoughts went everywhere at once, slippery and strong and fast, six or seven of them twisting in different directions, and every one of them was fighting. Harry closed his hand harder. The eels went still.

"What's your name?" he said.

The vampire's mouth opened. It shut again. The muscles of its jaw stood out like cords.

"Name," said Harry.

"Cole," said the vampire. "Cole Haskett."

"When were you turned, Cole?"

"Eighteen eighty-seven. August."

"Where?"

"A barn. Outside Fort Pierre. Dakota Territory." The words came out flat and even, without any of the drawl. "I was driving freight. She came in out of the dark."

"Who?"

"I never knew her name."

"Where have you been for the last week?"

"Coming down out of the Cascades. Over the water to Port Angeles. Into the park. I was headed for the ferry. Victoria. I was going north."

"Did you kill Mark and Danny Ostrander?"

The eels thrashed. All of them at once. Harry felt it run up his arm like a current through a wire and he set his feet and held on, and it was hard, harder than any man he'd ever held, the way holding a rope with a horse on the end of it is harder than holding a rope with a dog.

"I didn't know their names," said Cole Haskett.

"Did you kill them?"

"Yes."

Harry let go.

The vampire's head came off the slab and hit it again. Its eyes were on him, and the hate in them was a new thing, much bigger than anything before. Harry had seen that look on men he'd put under the Imperius before. It was what happened when you made someone tell the truth with their own mouth.

"Imperius Curse functions," Harry said to the log. "My hold was stable, but he was fighting the whole way, on all channels at once. I'd estimate a regulation-strength Imperius from an average caster breaks inside ten seconds. Maybe less, if the specimen's older or angrier." He paused. "He's both."

He put the wand back up his sleeve.

The licence covered three curses. Harry had used one of them. He looked at the vampire on the table and thought about the second, and he thought about Gianna in her café in Volterra, remembering a small blonde girl in a grey cloak who could put a man on the floor screaming just by looking at him. He already knew these things felt pain. He knew it from Gianna and he knew it from the noise the specimen had made when the table first took hold of it. Two more seconds of screaming on a stone slab would give the Department a number, and the number wouldn't change a single thing he wrote in phase five.

"Cruciatus Curse: not tested," he said. "Pain response already established. No operational value in confirming it. Killing Curse: not tested. Single specimen. Requires a second specimen, and I'm not wasting this one on a question I can answer later." He looked at Cole. "You're welcome."

"For what?"

"You'll work it out."

"Legilimency," he went on, to the quill, "to be covered in phase four. End phase two. Eighteen fifty-two."

> **PHASE THREE — BIOLOGICAL RESPONSE**
> Objective: examine regeneration, structural damage, pain response, and reaction to heat and fire.

The chip was lying on the basalt by the vampire's right hand.

Harry hadn't noticed it until the light-globes drifted lower. It was about the size of his little fingernail, curved, white, with one edge sharp as broken porcelain. When the anchor had slammed the arm back onto the stone, one of the vampire's knuckles had hit the slab hard enough to flake. Harry leaned down. There was a small pale crater on the specimen's middle knuckle, rough-edged, like a nick out of a china cup.

"Well," he said, "that saves me a job."

He picked up the chip with silver tongs and laid it in a shallow glass dish on the trolley.

The chip moved.

It was a tiny movement. It rocked in the bottom of the dish, once, toward the table. Then again. Then it slid, a quarter-inch, across the smooth glass, toward the vampire's hand.

Harry looked at it for a long moment.

"Right," he said quietly. "That's horrible."

He moved the dish a foot along the trolley. The chip turned, slowly, in the bottom of it, and faced the table again, and began to inch.

"Structural damage," he said to the quill. "Accidental. Impact against the table at full force removed a fragment of the right middle knuckle. Fragment remains... active. It's moving. It's trying to get back to the body." He bent over the vampire's hand. "Wound site. A clear fluid's coming up from the crater—venom, same as the mouth, I'd guess. It's filling in the gap. It's sealing over."

He watched it happen. It took about a minute. When it was done there was a smooth shallow dip in the knuckle where the chip had been, glazed over like the surface of a pot, slightly silvery in the light.

"Sealed," said Harry. "No regrowth. The hole's still there; it's just covered up. If the fragment were returned..."

He looked at Cole.

"Does it hurt?" he asked.

The vampire looked at him for a long time. "What do you care?"

"Professionally."

"It burns," said Cole. "Like a hot coal in the bone. It burns until you put it back."

Harry picked up the dish and tipped the chip out onto the vampire's knuckle, into the dip it had come out of.

It fitted itself in. He saw it. It slid the last fraction of an inch into place by itself, like a magnet finding steel, and the clear venom welled up around the edges and ran and set. In thirty seconds there was nothing but a fine bright seam, a little silver crescent line on the white skin, the kind of mark you'd only see if you knew to look.

The vampire's face changed. Relief, quick and private, gone almost before it arrived.

"Reattachment confirmed," Harry said. "Separated material wants to go home, and goes home if you let it. Leaves a seam, silver, very faint. Consistent with the crescent scars Gianna remembered on some of the Volterra guard. So you can break them. You can break pieces off. But if you leave the pieces lying about, they'll put themselves back together. Which is why the Quileute burn them." He straightened. "Heat."

He traced a little circle in the air above the vampire's left forearm, and the air inside the circle shimmered and began to heat.

"Tell me when," he said.

"When what?"

"When you notice."

He raised it slowly. The skin under the shimmer didn't flush or redden. It didn't do anything. A thermometer charm sitting beside it read forty degrees, sixty, ninety. At about a hundred and thirty the vampire's eyes went to the circle. At a hundred and eighty it said, through its teeth, "There."

"Discomfort at a hundred and eighty Celsius, surface," said Harry. He let the circle go, and the air above the arm cooled. "That's a hot oven. No visible damage to intact skin. Heat on its own does very little. They're hard. They're cold. Heat can't get in."

He picked up a brass burner from the back of the trolley and put it on a stand at the far end of the table, near the vampire's feet, about four feet from anything.

Then he lit it.

It was a small flame. A candle flame, blue at the bottom, yellow at the top. The moment it caught, the vampire's whole body tried to go away from it. Every free inch of it strained toward the head of the table. The anchor runes flared and held. Its eyes went wide and white-rimmed and fixed on the flame, and a sound came out of it that Harry had heard before, once, from a horse in a stable fire in Cornwall.

Harry put the cap back on the burner. The flame went out.

The vampire lay there, rigid, staring at the place where it had been.

"Fear response to open flame," said Harry, more quietly. "Immediate. Extreme. I've seen a lot of things scared of a lot of things, and that's the biggest reaction I've had out of anything since the Dementor." He wrote something in the air. "Which tells me fire is the answer. It doesn't tell me how."

He took the lock of hair from phase one out of its envelope and set it in a ceramic crucible. He relit the burner and moved it right down the far end of the room, by the Pantry, a long way from the table. He held the crucible over the flame with tongs.

It caught at once.

It went up like a bit of paper soaked in paraffin, a quick bright flare and then a thick curl of purple smoke, almost violet, heavy as syrup, that sank and crept along the top of the crucible. The smell hit him a second later: sweet, cloying, so sweet it made the back of his throat close. Like burnt sugar. Like an incense shop on fire.

Ten seconds and the hair was gone. There was nothing left in the crucible. No ash. No mark.

Harry put the cap on the burner and stood there for a while letting the purple smoke settle and fade.

"Specimen material ignites readily once separated," he said. "Burns hot and fast. Purple smoke, heavy, sweet smell. Burned material is not recovered. Nothing's left to recover. I think that's the answer to how. The skin's too hard for heat to get in. But under the skin there's the venom, and the venom burns like lamp oil. Break them open, then burn the pieces. In that order." He nodded to himself. "Crack, then burn."

He went back to the table.

"Cold," he said, and cast *Glacius* across the vampire's legs. A sheet of ice formed on its jeans and its bare feet. It didn't seem to notice. "No reaction to cold." He banished the ice. "Air," he said, and cast a Bubble-Head Charm over the vampire's head, and then drew the air out of the bubble until there was none.

The vampire lay in the vacuum, eyes open, unconcerned, for one minute. Two. Five.

Then its eyes began to move faster. Back and forth. Back and forth.

Harry let the air back in. The bubble popped. The vampire's nostrils flared, and flared again, and it dragged in a long, deep, completely unnecessary breath, the way a man coming up from deep water would.

"No need for air," Harry said. "Five minutes in a vacuum, no ill effect. But he didn't like losing his sense of smell. Started panicking around four minutes. Smell's how he finds his way round the world. Take it off him and he's lost. Useful." He made a note. "Last one."

He took the silver probe, and pricked the pad of his own left thumb with the point of it.

A bead of blood came up. Dark, round, bright.

He held it out over the table, eight inches from the vampire's face.

Cole Haskett's nostrils flared. His lip curled. And then his whole face screwed up, eyes squeezing half shut, head turning aside on the stone as far as the table would let it, as though Harry were holding a dead rat up to his nose.

"Get that away from me."

"Feeding response," said Harry, "nil."

"You smell like a wet dog," said Cole, "that somebody struck with lightning in a church."

Harry looked at his thumb. The little puncture had already closed.

"Rude," he said, and wiped his thumb on his trousers.

"Feeding response to the investigator: absent. Possibly aversion," he told the quill. "I'll take that up with Department Medical. End phase three. Nineteen thirty-four."

> **PHASE FOUR — COGNITIVE STUDY**
> Objective: investigate memory, awareness, mental resistance, and differences from the human mind.

He went to the kitchen first and made a cup of tea.

He took his time over it. Three sugars. He stood at the counter and drank half of it looking out at the wrong view of the river, and *When the Levee Breaks* came round on the tape and he let it play through to the end. Then he took the rest of the tea back to the specimen room and set it on the trolley, and pulled the stool up beside the head of the table, and sat.

Cole Haskett's eyes slid sideways to him.

"Phase four," Harry said. "Bit of a chat, this one."

"I've got nothing to say to you."

"That's fine. I wasn't going to ask." He leaned forward, his elbows on his knees, until his face was a couple of feet above the vampire's, and looked into its red eyes. "*Legilimens.*"

He went in.

He'd been inside a lot of minds. Voldemort's, once, by accident, and then on purpose. Snape's memories in the Pensieve. A dragon's, in Romania, for about four seconds before he was thrown out so hard his nose bled for an hour. The mind-healer in Vienna, Frau Adler, over and over, until she'd stopped being able to keep him out and started buying him lunch instead. Human minds were a kind of house. You went in through the front door and there were rooms and corridors and locked cupboards, and you learned your way around.

This was a house with six front doors and all of them were open and every room had a radio in it, playing a different station at full volume.

His elbows stayed on his knees. Inside, where it counted, he staggered. Six streams of thought went past him at once. One was counting. One was a song, an old one, a hymn, over and over. One was the burner, the flame, the burner, the flame. One was a long, low, constant red pulse that he understood after a second was thirst, wanting, endless, under everything like the hum of an engine. One was trying to work out how to kill him. One was cursing, inventive, continuous, in an accent from a century ago.

They all ran at the same speed. They didn't take turns. They were all Cole, all at once.

Harry let them run past. He'd learned in Vienna that you couldn't fight a current. You found a rock in it and you stood on the rock. He found the counting thread, which was the calmest, and stood on it, and looked around.

The vampire felt him there. Harry felt it feel him. All six streams turned on him at once and came at him in a flood: blood and screaming and a woman's face and a lot of very loud noise. It was a crude thing, a man throwing everything in his pockets at a burglar. It had no structure to it. There was no wall, no lock, no cupboard. He'd been in the heads of eleven-year-olds who could shield better.

He waited for it to wear itself out. It took about forty seconds, which, for a man, would have been a very long time to scream inside his own head.

Then he went looking.

The memories were laid out in two halves, and the difference between them was so sharp it was like stepping out of a cellar into daylight.

Everything before August 1887 was brown. That was the only word he had for it. Faded, blurred, soft at the edges, like old photographs left in a window. A woman's hands in flour. A dog called Buck. A long flat road and grass the colour of a lion. The faces were smudges. The voices were murmurs. When Harry reached for the mother he found only the hands, and the flour, and a feeling like a door closing.

Everything after August 1887 was perfect.

Perfect. Harry could see every single blade of straw in the barn outside Fort Pierre. He could see the pattern of the rain on a hotel window in Denver in 1902 and count the drops. He could hear every word of a conversation in a saloon in Butte in 1911, every clink of glass, the creak of a particular floorboard under a particular boot. Nothing had faded. Nothing had been lost. A hundred and nineteen years of nights, stacked up and labelled, every one of them sharp as the moment it had happened.

Harry thought, *God. You can't forget anything.*

He went to Friday.

He didn't stay long. He saw what he needed. Fog over the Hurricane Ridge trailhead, white and thick. Two shapes on the trail coming out of it, one tall in a red shell and one shorter in a blue one, the smaller one bouncing, talking, pointing at something in the trees with a lift pass flapping on his zip. He saw where it happened, which was a quarter-mile below the trail where a little gully cut down toward the Elwha and nobody would be walking for a while. He saw the place afterwards, under a bank of ferns at the bottom of the gully, by a split cedar stump, and he took the place and put it somewhere safe in his own head where he could find it again.

He didn't look at the middle part. He'd seen the flyer.

He came out of Friday very carefully, and for a second he sat on the stool with his elbows on his knees and his eyes on the floor and didn't do anything.

Then he went back in, and went to 1974.

He found it because it was the brightest thing in there. Brighter than the blood, brighter than the thirst. Chicago in March. Wet snow on the El tracks. A woman with black hair and red eyes and a laugh like a bottle breaking, called Ruth, who'd been Cole's for forty-four years, which Harry understood, in the way you understand things in another person's head, meant *his*: his other half. The thing he'd been bent around for half his life, like a tree grows round a fence.

Ruth had been careless. Ruth had liked the newspapers. Three bodies in a month, left where they'd be found, and Ruth clipping the stories out and laughing.

And then the cloaks had come.

Harry watched them come down the alley off Wabash Avenue through Cole's perfect eyes. Four of them. Grey cloaks, the hoods up, walking in step. The biggest was enormous, broad as a door, with a face like a boxer's. Beside him walked a man with a narrow, attentive face, his head turning slightly from side to side as if he were listening to a song only he could hear. And in front of them, small and fair, with a child's face and hair the colour of straw, was a girl who couldn't have been more than fourteen when they'd made her.

Cole had run.

Jane. Felix. Demetri.

"Legilimency functions," he said. "Specimen mind is very fast and runs on at least six simultaneous tracks. It's like walking into a room with six radios on. Cole stared at him.

> Objective: estimate how an average trained wizard would fare against the species, and identify practical countermeasures.

He didn't sit for this part. He walked.

He started walking again.

"So. Average wizard sees the specimen at twenty yards and starts an incantation. Specimen's on him before he finishes the second one. He stopped by the table and looked down at Cole.

"Average trained wizard," he said, "is dead inside two seconds. Probably inside one. Without ever really understanding what happened."

The vampire looked back up at him. It didn't argue.

"Countermeasures," said Harry. "In order of how much I'd bet my own life on them."

He held up a finger.

Two fingers.

Three.

Four.

He looked at the wall of jars for a while.

"Last thing. The shifters." He turned around. "I watched two of them run this specimen down through old-growth forest at forty miles an hour, in the rain, and they'd have had him if I hadn't. They're fast enough. They're strong enough. Their teeth go through what our spells bounce off. They hate these things by nature and they've been killing them on this coast since before there was a Ministry. "Recommendation: the Department does not make an enemy of the Quileute. Ever. If we ever want help, we ask nicely, and we bring food. Big lads. Eat a lot, I expect."

The quill paused, as though waiting to see if he was serious, and then wrote it down.

"Summary," said Harry. "An average trained wizard would not survive an encounter with this species at close range. A well-prepared team with silent casters, pre-laid wards, Apparition discipline, and fire might. I could. That's not much use to the Department, because there's only one of me and I'm not going to Italy on my own." He thought about that. "Probably not."

He went to the lectern and put his hand flat on the open page.

"Unspeakable zero-seven-two-one. End research log zero one. Twenty-one forty."

The quill wrote the last line and laid itself down.

**The creek, tributary of the Sol Duc**
**Earlier — 5:44 p.m.**

The crack nearly put Seth back in the water.

He flinched so hard his back legs skidded on the wet rock and he had to throw his weight forward to stay on the bank. Across the creek, Leah dropped off her rock into the shallows and spun to face the sound with every hair on her spine standing straight up.

There was a man in the ferns.

He hadn't been there. Seth had been looking right at that spot a second ago, at a big clump of sword ferns between two hemlocks, and there'd been nothing in it but ferns. Now there was a man standing in the middle of them, tall, wearing a black suit and a long black coat and sunglasses, like he'd stepped out of a movie where somebody says *we were never here*.

*Where did he come from where did he COME from—*

*Shut up. Shut up, Seth, I'm thinking.*

Seth smelled him a second later, when the wind brought it across the water. No sweet. No burn. That was the first thing, and it hit him so hard he almost sat down. People smelled like soap and sweat and whatever they'd had for lunch, and there wasn't much of that on him either. This guy smelled like pine needles and rain on rocks and something hot underneath, like a dog lying in front of a woodstove. And on top of all of it, sharp and clean, the stink of lightning. Seth had smelled lightning once, up on the ridge behind La Push, when it hit a fir a hundred yards off and split it down the middle. It smelled like that.

*What is he?*

*I don't know.*

The man looked up at the vampire hanging in the air. His mouth moved a little at one corner, like he was pleased with himself.

"Well," he said. "Look at you."

English. Like, *actually* English. Like the guy on the nature shows.

Then he looked down at the two of them, and Seth's lips came up off his teeth by themselves, and he heard Leah's growl go so low it was almost too low to hear.

The man reached into his coat.

*Gun,* Leah thought, *he's got a gun,* and Seth dropped low with his weight on his back legs ready to go sideways—

The man took out a wallet. A black leather wallet. He flipped it open with his thumb and held it up at them, at arm's length, like a cop on TV. Seth could see a silver crest on one side, two animals standing up on their back legs on either side of a shield, a lion and a horse-thing with a horn. There was writing under it. He couldn't read it from there.

"Evening," said the man. "This situation is contained. I'm going to have to ask you both to vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation."

For a second nobody thought anything at all.

*Is he—* Leah's thought was slow, unbelieving. *Is he showing us a badge?*

*Dude,* Seth thought. *He's showing us a badge.*

*Is he serious? Is this guy serious? We're* wolves.

"Yes, it's a badge," said the man.

Seth's ears shot up.

"Yes," the man went on, "I'm showing it to a pair of wolves. I don't make exceptions for fur. It's a professional thing."

*Did he just—*

*He heard us.* Leah's thought came out of her like ice water. *Seth. He* heard *us.*

The man put the wallet away. He took off his sunglasses, folded them with one hand, slid them into his top pocket, and looked at Seth. Right at him. His eyes were green, bright green, and it felt like being looked at by Edward, except Edward always looked a little bit sorry about it. This guy didn't look sorry at all.

Seth's ears went flat to his skull.

"Course I can hear you," said the man. "You're both shouting. It's like standing outside a pub at chucking-out time."

*Get the hell out of my head,* Leah snarled at him. Her whole lip came up. *Get out. Get* out.

"Then stop leaving the door open, Lassie."

Seth didn't mean to. He absolutely did not mean to. But the look on Leah's face—the look in her *head*, the pure stunned outrage of it, *LASSIE?*—went straight through him and the laugh came out of his nose before he could stop it, a big wet huff that shook his whole body.

The man's eyes came to him. "Don't laugh, Balto. You're next."

Seth stopped laughing.

*Balto,* thought Leah, with deep satisfaction. *Balto.*

*Balto was a hero, Leah, he carried medicine across Alaska—*

*He was a* sled dog.

Seth tilted his head. He couldn't help it. He was trying to work it out, and his head did what his head did. *Are you a vampire? You're not, right? You don't smell like one. You don't smell like anything I've—*

The man looked offended. "Do I look like I sparkle?"

*How do you know they*—Seth's head went the other way—*okay, but seriously, you don't smell like one. You don't smell like a person either. You smell like—*

"Cheers," said the man. "I showered."

Leah had stopped snarling. Seth could feel her thinking now, under everything, fast and cold and angry. She looked up at the vampire hanging over the water, with its red eyes rolling between the three of them. She looked at the man. Then at the vampire again.

*That's our kill,* she thought, flat and hard, right at him. *We ran it down. It's ours.*

"My snare," the man said to her. "My tree. My operation. Your kill's been requisitioned. I'll send you a form."

Seth felt her decide.

He felt it in his own legs, the way he always felt her when she was about to move: the weight going back, the muscles winding up tight. She was going to go up the bank and over the man and get the vampire before he could do anything about it. She had it all mapped out: the bank, the rock, the jump. Seth was halfway to telling her *don't* when—

"I wouldn't, love," said the man, without even looking at her. He was still looking at Seth. "I've had a long week."

And he snapped his fingers.

The vampire fell.

It dropped straight down out of the air, headfirst, stiff as a board, and the man stepped into the creek and caught it under one arm. One arm. He didn't even bend his knees. Seth had fought vampires; he'd felt how heavy they were when they hit you, like getting hit by a car made of rock. The man caught it the way Seth's mom caught a bag of groceries, and swung it round flat and tucked it against his side with its head forward and its feet out the back.

"Surf's up," he said.

*Leah,* Seth thought, very carefully. *Leah, I don't think we should fight that guy.*

Leah didn't answer him. She was standing in the water with her mouth open. Seth could feel her running the numbers and he could feel the numbers coming out wrong.

The man looked back across the creek at Seth. His face was very calm.

"Tell Black and the doctor that the Cullens are on the good list," he said. "Tell them to keep it that way."

*The good list?* Seth's thoughts tumbled over each other. *What list? There's a list? Who's on the—*

And Leah's voice cut through him like a knife.

*Seth. Whatever you do, do NOT think about the b—*

She bit it off. Seth felt her bite it off. She slammed something down over the end of it like a lid on a pot and started thinking, loud and furious, about the Mariners' starting rotation from 2001, every name, over and over.

And Seth panicked.

He knew what she meant. Of course he knew. There was only one thing in the whole world they'd both be trying not to think about in front of somebody who knew about vampires and had a badge and a list, and the second she'd said not to, it was right *there*, bronze curls and brown eyes, a little hand on Jacob's cheek—

He grabbed the first thing he could find and threw it on top. *Three x plus seven equals nineteen. Three x plus seven equals nineteen. Subtract seven from both sides. Three x equals—three x—*

"Four," said the man. "It's four, Balto. *x* equals four. Show your working."

Seth's brain went completely white.

"And whatever it is you two are both trying very hard not to think about," the man said, "I'm not asking. Not tonight."

*Leah. Leah, he knows there's something—*

*Mariners,* Leah thought, savagely. *Moyer. Garcia. Piñeiro. Sele. Abbott. Moyer. Garcia.*

Seth tried to think about nothing. He'd never in his life been able to think about nothing. He thought about his dad instead, because that was what his head did when it was scared—his dad, who had known all the old stories, every one of them, the cold ones and the spirit warriors and Taha Aki and all of it. Dad would've known what this guy was. Dad would've told them what to do.

The man went still.

It was the first time he'd gone still since he got there. He'd been relaxed the whole time, easy on his feet, like none of this was any harder than ordering food at a drive-through. Now he stood in the creek with the vampire under his arm and didn't move at all, and Seth had the weird, prickly feeling that the man had just seen his dad's face too.

Seth didn't know why he asked it. It came out by itself, the way his thoughts usually did.

*Who* are *you? Do you even have a name?*

The man looked at him for a long moment.

"Harry," he said. Very quietly.

Seth's whole body went cold under his fur.

*Harry.*

He didn't mean to think it. He didn't mean to think any of it. But it was all right there: the hospital corridor with the bad lights, his mom's hands shaking on her purse, Charlie Swan standing in the doorway with his hat in his hands. The funeral in the rain at La Push. The coffin going down. HARRY CLEARWATER on the stone. Six months. Six months, and it still came out of nowhere and knocked him flat, like a wave.

Across the creek, he felt Leah feel it too, and the Mariners stopped.

The man stood there in the water for a second. Two. When he spoke his voice had gone rough.

"...Sorry, mate."

Nobody thought anything.

The man cleared his throat. He shifted the vampire a little higher under his arm. "Stay south of the Sol Duc for a few weeks, would you? Both of you. I've got things in these woods that won't care what you smell like."

He lifted his free hand. Two fingers. A little flick in Seth's direction, low, at the ground, like he was flicking water off his fingertips.

Seth felt something tug at his left back ankle, where his cutoffs were tied on with the leather cord. One small tug, quick, like a fish taking a hook, and then nothing. He looked down. There was nothing there. The cord was where it always was. The shorts were rolled up tight in it, dark with rain.

When he looked up, the man was smiling at him a little. Just the corner of his mouth.

"Lassie," said the man, with a nod to Leah.

"Balto," with a nod to Seth.

Then he turned on his heel in the creek, and the air cracked like a gunshot, and he was gone.

So was the vampire.

The ripples he'd made in the water spread out, hit the banks, and came back. The rain came down. The creek ran. There was nothing in the ferns but ferns, and nothing in the air over the water but air, and the only thing left was the smell—pine and wet rock and heat and lightning, fading fast—and under it, very faint, the sweet sick stink of the vampire, already going.

Seth stood on the bank and shook.

He didn't know when he'd started. He couldn't stop. It started in his shoulders and went all the way down to his paws, and when he looked across the water Leah was doing it too, standing in the shallows with her legs braced and her head low and her whole body trembling.

*Leah?*

She didn't answer for a long time.

*Leah, he said Harry. He said—*

*I heard what he said.*

*Do you think he knew? Do you think he did it on purpose?*

*I don't know.* Her thoughts were tight and small and very far away. *I don't know, Seth. I don't know anything. I don't know what he is. I don't know where he went. I don't know what that thing in the air was. He was in my head. He was in my* head *and I couldn't—*

She stopped. He felt her stop. Felt her take everything she was feeling and squash it down into a hard little ball somewhere he couldn't see, the way she'd learned to do in Sam's pack, the way she did every single day.

*We go home,* she said. *Right now. We tell Jake.*

*Okay.*

*And Seth. Listen to me. Nobody thinks about her. Not one time. Not until we're inside that house and we're sure that guy is a long way away.*

*Okay.*

*Mariners,* she said, and turned, and went up the bank.

Seth went after her. He always did.

They ran south through the dark trees with the rain on their backs, and they didn't slow down once, and the whole way home Seth thought about baseball as hard as he had ever thought about anything.

Esme came out of the kitchen with a dish towel over her shoulder, and the kitchen behind her smelled of garlic bread, which in that house, with that sweet sick stink over everything, was like a raft in the middle of the ocean.

Carlisle came down the stairs. Alice came down after him, with Jasper a step behind her, and Jasper's eyes went straight to Leah and stayed there, and she felt a sudden cool wash of calm go over her like a hand on the back of her neck.

"Don't," she said. "Don't do that. I want to feel it."

Jasper let it go. She felt the calm drain off her.

"Sorry," he said.

"Leah?" Jacob was on his feet with Renesmee tucked against his shoulder. His face had gone hard. "What happened? Are you hurt? Is Seth—"

"We're fine."

"There was a vampire," said Seth.

The room went quiet. Leah felt the change go through all of them. It was like watching a herd of deer lift their heads at the same time.

"A nomad," said Leah. "Red-eye. Coming down out of the park toward the highway, north of the Calawah. We picked it up a little after five-thirty and ran it west. It was fast. I nearly had it."

"And?" said Emmett.

"And then a tree grabbed it."

Emmett looked at her.

"Show them," said Leah to Edward. "You've got it. You've got all of it. Just show them, I don't want to—" She stopped. She crossed her arms. "Say it out loud. Whatever you're seeing."

Edward was standing in the middle of the room with his eyes half closed. He'd been reading it off both of them since the lawn. Leah could see it going past behind his face, the creek and the jump and the snare and the crack, the man in the ferns. She saw him get to the part where the man took off his glasses and looked right at Seth, and saw him stop breathing, which he didn't need to do anyway.

"Edward," said Bella quietly.

"Give me a moment."

"Edward."

"There was a man," Edward said.

He told it. He told it better than Leah would have; he told it like he'd been standing there. The trap with no rope and the vampire hanging upside down fifteen feet over the water. The crack. The suit. The coat that didn't get wet. The badge. Then he said, in a perfectly flat voice, "*Evening. This situation is contained. I'm going to have to ask you both to vacate the premises. This is now the site of a national security operation,*" and the accent he did it in was so exact that Seth flinched.

"He's English," said Edward. "London, I think. Something rougher underneath it."

"He showed you a *badge*?" said Emmett.

"He showed two wolves a badge," said Edward. "And then he answered them."

Leah watched it land on them.

She watched it land on Carlisle, who went very still on the bottom stair. She watched it land on Alice, whose eyes went wide. On Jacob, whose arm tightened around the baby. On Bella, whose head turned, slowly, to look at Edward.

"Answered them," said Bella.

"Every thought," said Edward. "Word for word. As they thought them. Seth thought *is he showing us a badge* and he said *yes, it's a badge*. Leah told him to get out of her head and he told her to stop leaving the door open." His mouth went tight. "He called her Lassie."

Emmett made a noise.

"Don't," said Leah.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

Emmett held up both hands. His shoulders were shaking.

"And Seth," said Edward, "is Balto."

Emmett lost it. He put his face in the couch cushion and howled. Rosalie hit him in the back of the head without looking, hard enough to make a sound like a dropped brick, and he kept howling.

"Emmett," said Esme.

"I'm sorry," Emmett said into the cushion. "I'm sorry. Balto. Oh my God."

"Balto was a hero," said Seth, to nobody.

"Go on," said Carlisle quietly. He hadn't smiled at all.

Edward went on. The vampire falling. The man catching it under one arm and tucking it against his side, and saying *surf's up*. Emmett stopped laughing at that part.

"Under one arm," Emmett said.

"Under one arm."

"Like a surfboard."

"Like a surfboard."

Emmett sat up slowly and looked at Rosalie. Then he looked at his own arm, like he was trying to picture it, and didn't say anything else.

"He said something to tell you," said Edward. He turned to Jacob, and then to Carlisle. "*Tell Black and the doctor that the Cullens are on the good list. Tell them to keep it that way.*"

Nobody said anything for a while.

"The good list," said Rosalie at last. Her voice was very calm. Leah had known her long enough to know that was when she was scariest. "There's a list."

"There's a list," said Edward.

"And we're on it."

"The good one, apparently."

"I don't want to be on any list." Rosalie had uncrossed her arms. She came across the room, past Emmett, and stopped beside Jacob, and Leah saw her eyes go to Renesmee and stay there. "Edward. Did he know? About—"

"No," said Leah.

They all looked at her.

"He didn't get it. I didn't let him." She heard her own voice and didn't like how thin it was. "He said the thing about the good list, and I almost—I started to tell Seth not to think about her, and I stopped myself, and I put the Mariners on top of it. And Seth did his algebra."

"His algebra," said Jacob.

"Three x plus seven," said Seth. "Equals nineteen."

"And he said," said Edward, very quietly, "*It's four, Balto. x equals four. Show your working.*" He looked at Leah, and something in his face was close to gratitude, which she'd never seen on him before and didn't know what to do with. "And then he said, *Whatever it is you two are trying very hard not to think about, I'm not asking. Not tonight.*"

Jacob's face went dark.

"*Not tonight,*" he said.

"Yes."

"So he knows there's something."

"He knows there's something," said Edward. "He doesn't know what. He didn't push. I don't think he wanted to. I think he wanted them to know he could have."

Bella had come up on Jacob's other side. She looked down at her daughter, who was asleep again on Jacob's shoulder with one fist in his T-shirt, and her face did something that Leah would have called scared on anybody else.

"Charlie," said Bella. "Is he—could he have gone near Charlie?"

Jacob turned his head.

"Charlie called me at lunch," he said slowly. "He said some English guy at the diner this morning was asking about big wolves. Wanting a picture. Said he's a photographer. Charlie said he drives a black Impala and sits with his back to the wall." Jacob's jaw worked. "I told Charlie it was probably nothing."

"It was not nothing," said Leah.

"Yeah. I got that."

---

Carlisle came down off the last stair.

"Seth," he said gently. "The badge. Can you picture it for me? As clearly as you can."

Seth screwed up his face. Leah watched Edward watch the picture.

"There was a crest," said Seth. "Silver. There was, like, a shield in the middle, and a lion on one side and a horse on the other—a horse with a horn—"

"A unicorn," said Carlisle.

"Yeah. Standing up. Both of them standing up on their back legs. And a crown on top. And some words underneath, but I couldn't read them."

Carlisle closed his eyes.

"The Royal Arms," he said. "The lion of England and the unicorn of Scotland. I saw it carved over doorways in London before any of you were born." He opened his eyes again. "That's a British government crest."

"He said national security," said Leah.

"Whose nation, I wonder."

"Why would the *British government*—" Esme began, and stopped.

"Volterra," said Edward.

He said it like he'd been holding it in his mouth for a while.

"There were tourists in the square," he said. "That day. Hundreds of them. I was standing in the shadow by the clock tower, and I didn't care who saw. I didn't think I'd be alive afterward to care." He didn't look at Bella. "If one of them saw something. If one of them went home and told someone who listened..."

"Then a government knows about us," said Carlisle. "Some government. Some part of one."

"And the Volturi," said Jasper.

Everybody looked at him. He hadn't said anything until then. He was leaning against the wall by the stairs with his arms crossed and his eyes on the window, the black glass with the rain on it.

"If the Volturi find out that a human government has a man in a suit hunting our kind in Washington State," Jasper said, "they won't care whose fault it is. They'll come and look at whoever lives nearest. And they'll see us." His eyes moved to the couch, to the baby, and back to the window. "And they'll see her."

The room went silent.

"The trap," said Jasper, after a moment. "Tell me about the trap again."

Leah told him. She didn't want to, but she told him. The vampire in midair. The ankle. The smell of a blown-out match laid across the ground in a straight line, running off through the trees as far as she could smell it. How Seth had fallen straight through it into the creek and nothing had happened to him at all.

Jasper listened with his head down.

"It took a red-eye," he said, "mid-leap. It let a wolf through. Emmett, where were you hunting this morning?"

Emmett looked up. "North. Rose and me, before it got light. Up by—" He stopped. "Up by the Sol Duc."

"Did you feel anything?"

"No. Nothing. I got an elk."

"So it let you through, too," said Jasper. "It lets wolves through. It lets us through. It took him." He looked at Carlisle. "It knows the difference between what we eat and what he ate."

Leah felt the cold go down her back.

"So it's picky," said Emmett slowly. "It's like a—"

"A filter," said Jasper. "He's been here a while. You don't lay something like that in a night. He's been in our woods, laying wire, for days. Maybe weeks. And he made very sure that it wouldn't touch any of us."

"Because we're on the good list," said Rosalie.

"Because we're on the good list."

"Alice," said Carlisle.

Alice had been quiet the whole time. That was strange in itself. She was sitting on the stairs, small and upright, with her hands in her lap and her eyes far away. Leah had seen her like that before, when she was looking for something. She'd never seen her look like that for so long.

"I can't see him," said Alice.

Her voice was small.

"I've been looking since Seth and Leah came over the river. I looked before Edward got outside. I've looked at the creek, and at Forks, and at the diner, and at every road out of town. When the wolves are around it goes blurry, the way it always does, like looking through fog." She shook her head. "Him, I can't find at all. There's no fog. There's nothing. There's no shape where he should be. It's like trying to see a hole."

Jasper went to her. He sat on the stair beside her and put his hand on her knee.

"I can't see where he took the nomad," said Alice. "I can't see what he's doing to it. I can't see if he's coming back. I can't see *anything.*"

Nobody said anything.

The rain came down on the glass.

---

Seth was standing by the door with his arms wrapped around himself in nothing but his cutoffs, dripping on Esme's floor, and Leah saw him remember.

She saw it in his face. He looked down at his right hand, the one that had been in a fist the whole time without either of them noticing, and opened it.

The white thing was a card. A business card, plain, thick, the edges a little damp.

"He did this," Seth said. "At the creek. At the end. He flicked his fingers at me and something pulled on my cord. I thought—I didn't think anything. I forgot."

"Give it here," said Jacob.

Seth held it out. Jacob didn't take it; his hands were full of baby. Carlisle came across the room and took it from Seth very carefully, by the edges, like a doctor handling something that might be evidence, and turned it toward the light.

"*H. Evans*," he read. And then a number. "Plus forty-four. Twenty. Seven-nine-four-six, zero-seven-two-one."

"Forty-four's Britain," said Edward. "Twenty's London."

Carlisle turned the card over. There was writing on the back, in pen. Quick, slanted, not very neat.

He read that out too.

"*If another one turns up, ring. Don't ring for anything else.*" He paused. "*Harry.*"

Leah had known it was coming. She'd heard the man say it in the creek. She'd felt it go through Seth like a knife and felt it go through her too.

It didn't matter. It hit her anyway.

Five letters, in a stranger's handwriting, on the back of a business card in a vampire's hand. Her father's name. Her father, who'd taught her to fish off the rocks at First Beach, who'd laughed so loud at dinner that her mom used to throw napkins at him, who'd dropped dead on his kitchen floor six months ago because the first time his daughter turned into a wolf in front of him his heart couldn't take it.

She felt Seth looking at her. She didn't look back.

*Sorry, mate*, the man had said. Standing in the creek with a vampire under his arm, his voice gone rough. Like he'd meant it. Like he'd seen the funeral in Seth's head and it had stopped him dead for two whole seconds.

She didn't know what to do with that. She didn't know what to do with any of it.

"Nobody's calling that number," said Jacob.

"No," said Carlisle. He was still looking at the card. "Not tonight."

He'd said it without thinking. Leah watched him hear himself say it, and watched his face change.

"Alice," said Edward quietly. "If one of us decides to call it. Can you see that?"

Alice shut her eyes.

Leah watched her look. They all did. Even the baby had woken up, and was watching Alice from Jacob's shoulder with her huge brown eyes, very serious, as if she understood every word.

"I see the phone," Alice said, after a long time. "I see Carlisle's hand. I see him dial."

"And then?"

Alice opened her eyes.

"And then nothing," she said.
