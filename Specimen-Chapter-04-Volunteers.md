# Specimen

## Chapter Four: Volunteers

---

**East of Forks — Saturday, October 7 — 11:48 p.m.**

*—had a farm, E-I-E-I-O, and on that farm he had a—*

*Seth.*

*—cow, E-I-E-I-O, with a moo-moo here, and a—*

*Seth, I swear to God.*

*Jake said we had to!*

*Jake said think a song. He didn't say think the worst song ever written by anybody in the history of the world for three straight hours.*

*It's not the worst. It's got animals in it.* A pause, and then, defiantly: *And on that farm he had a PIG—*

Leah ran faster.

It didn't help. You couldn't outrun the inside of your own head. She went up the side of the ridge east of the Calawah in the dark at a flat lope, through salal and sword fern and the black trunks of the hemlocks, with the rain coming down cold and steady on her back, and Seth's farm came with her every step of the way. And underneath Seth's farm, steady and grim and absolutely relentless, like a drum in another room:

*—seventy-three bottles of beer on the wall, seventy-three bottles of beer. Take one down, pass it around—*

That was Jake.

He'd started at ninety-nine when they phased at ten past eleven behind the Cullens' garage, and he hadn't stopped once. He didn't even sound like he was singing. He sounded like a man reciting his own name and rank under torture. Leah had tried to think about other things for a while, the rain, the smells, the ground going by, and every time she got a little way into one of them she'd hear *seventy-one bottles of beer* in the back of her head and lose it.

That had been Edward's idea. Of course it had.

He'd called the Black house at four in the afternoon, when everybody was still sitting around Billy's kitchen table with the jacket on the back of the door making it impossible to think about anything else. Jake had put him on speaker. *If he can hear the pack when you're phased,* Edward had said, *he'll hear whatever's on your minds. Alice does something when she wants to keep something from me. She translates things into other languages in her head. Or she sings. It's loud and it's boring and it fills up the space, and I can't get past it.* A pause. *Think about the baby as little as you can. If you have to think, sing.*

Sam had looked at Jake across the table. *My pack too?*

*Your pack doesn't think about her,* Jake had said.

*They do now,* Sam had said, and looked at the jacket.

So now the whole coast was full of wolves singing. Sam's lot down at La Push, running the south half. Jake's lot up here, running the north, the two of them meeting at the Sol Duc in the middle the way they hadn't met since August. Leah didn't know what Sam's pack was singing. She was glad she couldn't hear it. She suspected Paul had picked something filthy.

She'd picked nothing at all. She'd decided at four o'clock, sitting in Billy's kitchen with the smell of that thing on the back of the door filling up her nose, that she wasn't going to sing for anybody. If the man wanted to listen to her think, he could listen. She'd think as loud as she liked. She'd think about exactly what she thought of him, and he could have it.

She'd also decided something else. She hadn't told anybody about that. She'd been keeping it under the anger, where even Jake would have a hard time getting at it without digging.

It was tied to her ankle now, in the bundle with her shorts and her shirt, rolled up tight in a plastic sandwich bag: a red Bic lighter, and a little squeeze bottle of charcoal lighter fluid she'd bought at the Shell station on Wednesday afternoon, on her way home from the diner, with her wrist still aching.

*Sixty-eight bottles of beer.*

*E-I-E-I-O.*

---

The call had come at half past ten.

Alice, on the Cullens' house phone. Leah had heard it through the wall of the living room, where she'd been standing at the glass looking out at the river because she couldn't stand to sit down in that house. A nomad, Alice said. Off the night ferry from Victoria, into Port Angeles at ten past nine. A man. Tall. A shaved head and a leather jacket and red eyes. He'd fed in Victoria the night before, an old man on a bench in Beacon Hill Park. He'd come across on the ferry because he'd heard things in Canada. About a coven on the Washington coast that ate animals. He wanted a look.

He'd decided, at ten twenty-five, to come down 101 on foot through the night. He'd be at Forks a little after midnight.

*Then I lose him*, Alice had said. *He comes into the trees east of town and he goes blurry, which is you. And then a minute after that he's just gone. A hole. Like the last one.*

*The snares*, Jake had said.

*I think so.*

Carlisle had been standing by the piano with his arms folded. *We drive him off, if he can be driven,* he'd said. *Or we let the snare have him. And we call Mr. Evans.*

Nobody had said anything to that.

Leah had looked at Carlisle across the room, at his calm, fair, tired face, and thought about the nurse at Lake Crescent who looked after sick kids. About the old man on the bench in the park in Victoria. About her father, and how there had been nobody to blame for that but her. And about a man in a black suit picking a leech up off the moss and tucking it under his arm like a surfboard and taking it away somewhere to ask it questions, and what *run its course* meant, and what *volunteers* meant, and how the man had said *I've a use for them* like he was talking about scrap metal.

She hadn't said anything either.

But she'd gone out to the garage, and checked the sandwich bag in her bundle, and tied it on tight.

---

*Fifty-nine bottles of—*

Jake stopped singing.

Leah felt it the same instant he did, because it hit her nose the same instant: a thread on the wind coming down off the high ground to the northeast, where the old logging roads cut across the ridge toward the Sol Duc. Sweet. Sharp. Wrong. Burnt sugar and bleach and something under it like a struck match, and under all of that, thick and fresh and dark, blood.

Fresh blood, this time. A day old. Less.

Seth's farm stopped too. The quiet in the link was so sudden it rang.

*There he is,* said Jake.

*Northeast. Coming down off the ridge,* said Leah. She was already turning. *Toward the river. Toward town. Same line as the last one.*

*Same line as the last one,* Jake repeated. She felt him think about that, quickly. *He's coming through the snare line. Like the last one.*

*So we let him walk into it,* said Seth. He sounded relieved. *Right? That's what Carlisle said. We let the snare get him and we call the—*

*We'll see,* said Leah.

She ran.

She heard Jake come after her, and Seth after him, and for a few seconds none of them thought anything at all, which was its own kind of song. Then Jake said, very steadily, *Fifty-eight bottles of beer on the wall*, and Seth said, a little breathless, *Old MacDonald had a farm*, and they went up the ridge in the dark with the rain in their faces and their minds full of noise.

She didn't sing. She thought about the leech.

They came up over the shoulder of the ridge into an old clear-cut gone back to alder and young fir, and the smell was so strong now she could taste it, and she saw him.

He was a hundred yards off, coming down through the alders at a walk. Tall. Leather jacket, black, shining wet. Head shaved so close it gleamed in the little light there was. He walked the way the last one had walked, like the woods were his living room, and he had his face up and his nostrils working, smelling the valley.

Smelling the town. Smelling the house by the river.

He stopped.

He turned his head and looked straight up the slope at them.

Three wolves on the skyline, black against the clouds. The biggest russet one in the middle, the size of a horse. A gray on his right. A sandy one on his left. Leah watched him see them and understand, the way the last one had understood, and she watched him make the same choice the last one had made.

He ran.

He went downhill, toward the Sol Duc, in that terrible upright sprinting blur, the coat flaring out behind him. Leah went off the ridge after him.

*Leah!*

*I've got him.*

*Leah, the snares, we don't need to—*

*I've got him.*

She had him. She'd always been the fastest, and he was running downhill on wet ground through alder thickets that slapped at his face, and she was built for exactly this. She was forty yards behind him. Thirty. He went through a stand of young firs and she went through it after him and came out the other side into a strip of older timber along a creek, big cedars, ferns to her shoulders, and she was twenty yards behind him and she could hear him breathing, which they didn't need to do, and she could smell the old man on the bench in Victoria on every breath—

He went past a big cedar at the edge of the creek, and the air went *tight*.

The same sound. The deep bowstring thrum she'd been hearing in her sleep for four nights. The leech went up off the ground by one ankle like he'd been yanked by a crane and swung, inverted, eight feet up, under the big low limb of the cedar, and hung there, arms dangling, coat falling inside out over his shoulders, turning slowly.

Rigid. Silent. Only the eyes moving.

Leah stopped six feet short of him with her chest heaving and her lips back.

Jake came out of the firs behind her and stopped. Seth came out behind Jake and stopped.

For a moment nobody sang anything.

The leech hung there. Turning. His eyes found Leah's, upside down, and they were red, very red, bright with what he'd had in Victoria the night before, and they were terrified.

*Okay,* Seth said. His mind was shaking. *Okay. Okay, so that's—that's the snare, he's caught, now we—now we call him. Right? Jake? We call the Cullens and they call him and—*

*No,* said Leah.

Jake looked at her.

She didn't look back. She was looking at the leech.

*Harry,* she said, *whatever his name is, can go fuck himself.*

The link went very still.

*Leah,* said Jake.

*He wants them alive. He wants them alive so he can take them home and put them on a table and cut bits off them. He said so. He said, I've a use for them.* She took a step forward. The leech's eyes followed her, wider. *I don't care what he does to them. I don't. They kill people. That one killed an old man in a park last night, I can smell him, Jake, I can smell what kind of soap he used. I don't care what happens to them. I care that he wants them.* Another step. *He wants them. So he doesn't get this one.*

*Carlisle said—*

*Carlisle isn't my Alpha.*

She felt that land on Jake. She felt him take it, and turn it over, and not like it, and not argue with it either.

*Seth's right,* Jake said quietly. *We could let the snare have him. We could call.*

*We could.*

*He's going to be angry.*

*Good.*

Jake didn't say anything for a long moment. She could feel him thinking. Under the thinking, where he couldn't quite keep it down, she could feel the jacket on the back of Billy's kitchen door, and the patch between the shoulder blades, and the two fingers raised in the trees at the marina. *I was here, and I touched this, and you didn't stop me.*

*He put his hand on my dad,* Jake said, very low.

*Yeah.*

*He sat in a boat with my dad and went through his head like a drawer.*

*Yeah, Jake.*

Jake looked at the leech turning slowly under the cedar.

*Do it,* he said.

---

It was very fast, after that.

It was strange, and she'd think about how strange it was afterward, for a long time. The clearing in June had been different. In the clearing the newborns had fought, and twisted, and screamed, and you had to get your teeth into them while they were trying to get theirs into you. This one hung there. He didn't fight. He couldn't. He just hung under the cedar with his arms dangling and his red eyes rolling, turning slowly on nothing, while three wolves pulled him apart.

It made a sound like breaking dishes.

She took the head. She didn't let herself think about it. She went up on her hind legs and closed her jaws and pulled, and it came away with a sound like a plate snapping on a stone floor, and she dropped it in the ferns, and Jake had an arm, and Seth, white-minded and silent, not singing anymore, took the other. The pieces twitched on the ground. Some of them tried to crawl. The leg that was still held in the snare hung there from the empty air under the cedar, alone, still turning slowly.

*Pile,* said Jake. His mind was flat and quiet. *Pile them up. Here. Away from the tree.*

They piled them up. Seth was shaking. Leah could feel him shaking, in the link, a fine high tremor, like a dog in a thunderstorm.

*You okay?* she said.

*Yeah. No. I don't know.* A pause. *It didn't fight, Leah.*

*I know.*

*It just—it just hung there.*

*I know, Seth.*

Then she went behind the cedar, out of sight of the pile, and let the heat go out of her.

The cold hit her bare skin like a slap. Rain, and the night, and October, all at once, after hours of fur. She stood there for a second on two shaking legs in the ferns, naked and steaming in the dark, and then she tore the bundle off her ankle and pulled on the shorts and the shirt with her wet hands, and got the sandwich bag open, and took out the lighter and the little bottle.

She came back round the cedar.

Jake was standing over the pile, huge and russet and dark. Seth was a little way off with his head down. The pile was moving, very slightly, the pieces shifting against each other, trying to find each other, the way the man had said the pieces of the first one did.

She uncapped the bottle and squeezed it over the pile. All of it, back and forth, until the bottle wheezed empty. The smell of the fluid cut through the sweet stink like a knife.

Then she flicked the lighter, and held it down to the edge of the pile, and the flame touched the fluid.

It went up all at once. *WHOOMPH.* A wall of yellow fire, roaring, so bright after the dark that she threw her arm over her eyes and staggered back into the ferns, and the heat hit her face like an open oven door. The pieces in the pile caught. She heard them catch. A crackling, hissing, sizzling sound, like fat in a pan, and then the smoke came up out of it, thick and dark and heavy, and in the light of the fire she could see its color.

Purple. Like a bruise. Rolling up into the rain.

The smell hit her. Sweet, so sweet, cloying, overwhelming, like a church full of lilies, like a funeral.

She stood in the ferns with the empty bottle in one hand and the lighter in the other and the fire on her face, and watched it burn, and felt nothing at all. Then she felt something. It started very small, low down, under her breastbone. It was ugly and hot and it felt, God help her, like satisfaction.

*There*, she thought, as loud as she could, at the dark, at the trees, at the whole wet peninsula and anybody who might be listening. *There's your volunteer.*

The world cracked open behind her.

She didn't jump. She'd been waiting for it since she flicked the lighter. She turned round slowly, with the fire at her back and its light throwing her shadow out long and thin across the ferns.

He was standing at the edge of the light, twenty feet away, where there had been nothing a second before.

He was in jeans and a gray T-shirt and unlaced boots, and a leather shoulder holster strapped on over the T-shirt with the black gun in it, as if he'd pulled on whatever was nearest in the dark and the holster had been nearest of all. His hair was flat on one side, the way hair gets when you've been asleep on it. The rain was already darkening his shoulders.

He looked at the fire.

He looked at it for a long time, with the light moving on his face, and the purple smoke rolling up past the cedar, and the one leg still hanging in the snare under the branch above the flames, turning slowly in the heat.

Then he looked at her.

---

**The Sol Duc drainage — 12:21 a.m.**

So that was him.

Jacob had seen him twice before. Once walking down the boat ramp at La Push at ten to six in the morning in a coat the color of a traffic cone, and once lifting his father into a wheelchair and looking straight into the trees. Both times from thirty yards and more. Both times with his heart in his mouth.

Now he was twenty feet away, in the firelight, in a T-shirt and unlaced boots, and Jacob could smell him.

Pine. Wet stone. Coffee, old, from the morning. Gun oil. Sleep: the stale, slept-in smell of a bed and a pillow. And under all of it, faint, so faint Jacob wouldn't have caught it if he hadn't spent the whole afternoon in his father's kitchen with his nose full of it: the other thing. The big thing. The cave and the old forest and the stone that never saw the sun.

Every hair on Jacob's back went up at once.

The man didn't look at him. He was looking at Leah.

She stood in the ferns in her wet shorts and T-shirt with the fire roaring behind her and the empty bottle of lighter fluid in one hand and the lighter in the other, and she looked back at him with her chin up and her eyes flat. Rain ran down her face. She didn't wipe it.

"What was that for?" the man said.

He didn't sound angry. That was the thing Jacob noticed first, and would remember. He sounded like a man who'd come home and found the kitchen floor wet and wanted to know who'd left the tap running.

"For the old man in Victoria," said Leah.

"Mm." The man looked back at the fire. The pile was burning fiercely now, the yellow flames gone white at the heart, the purple smoke rolling up in a thick slow column into the rain. The smell was everywhere. "You know you're burning a perfectly good volunteer."

"He wasn't a volunteer."

"They never are." He sighed through his nose. "Who started it?"

"I did."

"I'd assumed." He scratched the back of his neck. "And I'd assumed you'd tell me, so that's two for two. What did you say to them, before? To the other two. Before you went for him."

Leah's mouth thinned. Then she said it, clear, to his face, in a voice that carried over the noise of the fire.

"I said Harry whatever-your-name-is can go fuck himself."

Seth made a sound in his throat.

The man didn't seem to mind. He nodded slowly, as if she'd told him the forecast.

"Evans," he said. "It's on the card."

Then he did something with his hand.

He didn't point. He didn't say anything. He just turned his palm toward the fire, low, the way you'd hold your hand out to feel the heat, and closed it, and something came flying out of the flames toward him. It was a hand. A leech's hand, the right one, severed at the wrist, charred black at the cut and white everywhere else, and still moving, the fingers curling and uncurling as it came. It landed in the wet ferns a yard from the man's boots with a hiss of steam and lay there, flexing.

Jacob's lips came back off his teeth.

The man crouched down and looked at it. He didn't touch it. The fingers clenched, unclenched. It tried to drag itself back toward the fire.

"Hm," said the man.

Then, behind him, something in the pile collapsed with a soft crunch and the fire leapt up white, and the hand in the ferns stopped. All at once. In the middle of a clench. It lay in the ferns, white, dead as a glove.

The man looked at it for a moment longer.

"Huh," he said, quietly. "Both ways, then."

He turned his palm again and the hand went back into the fire. Then he looked up at the cedar, at the one leg still hanging in the air under the branch with the heat shimmering around it, and closed his fingers, and it dropped straight down into the heart of the flames. The snare let go with a faint sound like a string being cut.

He stood up.

"Right," he said, and turned round, and looked at Jacob for the first time.

---

He looked at Jacob the way you'd look at a large dog you'd been told about: carefully, with interest, from the top of his head to his feet and back again. He took his time. Jacob stood there and let him. He'd been looked at by vampires that way, and by Sam, and by his father on the nights he came home in the morning. This was different. This was a man pricing a car.

*Thirty-nine bottles of beer on the wall,* Jacob thought, as hard as he could. *Thirty-nine bottles of beer. Take one down—*

*—and on that farm he had a DUCK, E-I-E-I-O—*

The man winced.

It was a real wince. His whole face went into it, eyes screwed up, mouth pulled sideways, like a man who's just bitten into tinfoil.

"Oh, for God's sake," he said. "You can stop singing that. Both of you. Two at once is worse than one, did nobody tell you? It's like being stuck between two buskers."

Jacob kept singing. *Thirty-eight bottles of beer—*

"I know about the Loch Ness Monster," said the man.

The song died in Jacob's head.

It died in Seth's too, mid-quack. And in the sudden silence of the link, before he could stop it, before he could get anything in the way, the thing he'd spent all night not thinking about came up into the front of Jacob's mind like a cork popping up out of water: bronze curls, brown eyes, a small hot hand flat against his cheek.

He shoved it down. Too late. Way too late. He knew it was too late before he'd finished doing it.

The man watched him do it. Something went across his face that might, on anybody else, have been sympathy.

"Your dad's got a very orderly head, son," he said. "Lovely filing. Everything in its place. I had a look round on Saturday, in the boat, while he was telling me about Taha Aki." He tilted his head. "And there she was. In a room all of her own, right at the front. The day she was born, the day you looked at her, and the law came down. He's very proud of you, you know. He's frightened half to death, but he's proud."

Jacob's whole body went hot. The wolf came up in him like a wave and he felt his weight go forward onto his front paws and his lips peel all the way back and a sound come up out of his chest that made Seth step sideways.

"Don't," said the man.

He didn't raise his voice. He didn't move. He just said it, the way you'd say it to a dog that had its head in the bin.

Jacob didn't. He didn't know why he didn't. Afterward he'd think that maybe some old part of him, older than the wolf, had looked at the man standing in the firelight with his hands empty at his sides and his heart going sixty-four beats a minute, and understood something that the rest of him hadn't caught up to yet. He stood there with his teeth out and his whole body shaking, and he didn't.

"Good lad," said the man.

*I'm going to kill you,* Jacob thought at him, very clearly. *Someday. I'm going to kill you.*

"Get in line," said the man. "There's a queue. It's mostly vampires." He put his hands in his pockets. "Now listen, because I'm only saying this once, and I'm saying it to you because you're the Alpha and she won't listen to me and the young one's going to be sick in a minute."

Seth, who had been feeling sick for ten minutes, looked at the ground.

"I don't much care what a vampire eats," the man said, "so long as it isn't people. Deer, elk, mountain lions, the odd unlucky bear. Blood bags out of a hospital fridge. I'm not fussed. The doctor and his family can go on eating Bambi till the sun burns out, for all I care, and they're on the guest list till they stop." He looked at Jacob. "That goes for your Nessie too."

Jacob's growl went down into the ground.

"She leaves people alone, she's fine," said the man. "She's a baby. She drinks out of a cup. Nobody's interested in her." He paused, and in the pause, the fire cracked behind Leah and threw up a gout of sparks. "Only so long as she does."

He let that sit.

"The day she doesn't," he said, quite gently, "is the day we have a different conversation, you and me. I'd rather we didn't. I've an idea you'd rather we didn't too. So let's both of us hope she takes after her mother. From what I hear, her mother's doing very well."

Jacob stood in the ferns and shook.

He hated him. He'd never hated anything like it, not the leeches, not even Victoria. He hated him so much it was a taste in his mouth, coppery and hot. And under the hate, small and cold and absolutely certain, was the thing that made it so much worse: the man had said exactly what Jacob would have said, if it had been the other way round. If there'd been a baby leech in a white house, and it had been Jacob's land, and Jacob's people. *She's fine, so long as she leaves people alone. Only so long.*

Sam had said almost the same thing about the Cullens a hundred times. Jacob had heard him think it.

"Right." The man turned to Leah. "And you. Lassie."

"Don't call me that."

"I'll make you a deal. Every time you call me Harry whatever-my-name-is, I'll call you Lassie. Seems fair." He nodded at the fire. "You want me gone, yes? Out of your woods, out of your fur, out of your heads, back across the water where I came from. Yes?"

Leah said nothing.

"I'll take that as a yes. Here's how that works. I'm here till I've got what I came for. What I came for is volunteers. Every one of them you set fire to is one I've got to go and find somewhere else." He looked at the pile, burning down now, the purple smoke thinning. "That one had red eyes and a full stomach and walked straight into my snare from the Port Angeles ferry. He was *perfect*. Do you know how hard he was to get? He wasn't. He came to me. He came to *you*. All you had to do was ring the doctor and the doctor rings me and I come and pick him up and take him away and you never see him again. Done. Instead—" he waved a hand at the fire "—bonfire. Lovely. Very festive. And now I've got to go to Seattle and dig one out from under a viaduct, which'll take me a fortnight, and that's a fortnight longer I'm here." He spread his hands. "The longer it takes me to get more volunteers, the longer I'm in your fur. You want me gone? Stop setting fire to my homework."

Leah stared at him.

"That's it?" she said. "That's your argument?"

"That's it. It's a very good argument. It's got the advantage of being true." He tilted his head at her. "And before you ask, I'm not in your head. Not tonight, not at the diner, not ever without a reason. Theirs are open." He nodded at Jacob and Seth. "You lot shout when you're furry. I can't not hear it from twenty feet unless I go to the trouble of putting my fingers in my ears, and I wasn't asleep long enough tonight to want to make the effort."

"So you heard what I said before," said Leah. "When I was a wolf."

"Not a word of it. I was in bed. I got here when the fire did." He shrugged. "But I know you. You said something rude. And I asked, and you told me. Two for two."

Leah's mouth opened and shut.

*Ask him,* Jacob thought at her. He couldn't help it. He knew the man would hear it, and he thought it anyway, because it was the only thing in the world he wanted to know. *Ask him what he is. Ask him who he works for. Ask him who the hell sent him.*

The man glanced at Jacob. He didn't answer. He waited.

Leah's eyes went from the man to Jacob, and back. She couldn't hear Jacob. But she'd been in his head for seven months, and she didn't need to.

"Who are you?" she said. "Really. Not Evans. Not Homeland Security. Who sent you? Who do you work for?"

"Classified," said the man.

"Then why should we do anything you say?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the fire, and at Jacob, and at Seth, who was standing with his head down and his flanks heaving, trying very hard not to be sick on his own feet. Then back at Leah.

Jacob saw him decide something. He couldn't have said how. It was just a stillness that came over the man's face for half a second, the look of somebody putting a coin into a machine.

"All right," said the man. "One thing. Because I'd like you to stop burning my volunteers, and I'd like you to tell the doctor what I am in a way that'll stop him worrying about the wrong things, and a little goes a long way." He held up one finger. "You want to know who I work for? Think of the CIA."

Nobody moved.

"Crossed with DARPA," he said.

*What's DARPA?* said Seth faintly, in the link.

"Look it up, Balto," said the man.

"It's the Pentagon," said Leah slowly. She was looking at him as if she'd never seen him before. "The research part. Military science. Weapons. Stealth planes. They made the internet. There was a thing about it on the Discovery Channel." She stopped. "You're not American."

"No," the man agreed.

"So it's—some other country's—"

"Leave it at that," said the man.

"But—"

"That's what you're getting, Lassie. It's more than the doctor's had. It's more than anybody on this coast has had, and it's a sight more than I was supposed to give you." He put his hands in his pockets again. "Spies and science. People who go and find things out, and people who work out what to do with what's been found. Put them in the same building, give them a long leash and a very small budget, and there you are. Leave it at that."

Jacob looked at him in the firelight, at his flat sleep-mussed hair and his unlaced boots and the gun under his arm, and he thought, very clearly, so that there was no way the man could miss it: *We're the things being found out.*

The man looked back at him.

"Some of you," he said. "Not all of you. Not if you stop setting fire to things."

He turned to go. He stopped.

"Oh. Alpha." He looked back over his shoulder. "Sorry about your dad's jacket. Tell him cold wash and a cup of white vinegar. It'll come out in a week."

Jacob's growl came up out of the bottom of the earth.

"And next time," said the man, mildly, "ring the doctor. You've got till Seattle to decide you're going to. After that I start re-keying the snares, and the next thing that hangs upside down under a cedar might be furry." He paused. "I don't want to do that. I'll do it."

He nodded to them, one by one. To Seth, who didn't look up. To Jacob. To Leah, last, and longest.

"Ta-ra," he said.

*CRACK.*

**Field station, Olympic National Forest — Sunday, October 8 — 12:44 a.m.**

The kettle took four minutes. Harry stood at the counter in his wet T-shirt with the holster still on and watched it the whole time, which never made it go faster and which he did anyway.

The coin had gone hot against his thigh at nine minutes past midnight. Node thirty-one, the Sol Duc drainage, a mile and a half east of the highway bridge. He'd been asleep for forty minutes. He'd been out of bed in five seconds and dressed in thirty and he'd stood in the corridor with his boots unlaced counting, because you gave a snare two minutes to settle before you went to it, always, so the specimen was locked tight and stopped fighting the hold. That was the rule. He'd written the rule. He'd stood there in the dark corridor and counted to a hundred and twenty.

At a hundred and twelve he'd felt the node let go.

The snare had still been holding the leg when he arrived; what the node had felt was the rest of the specimen leaving it. Fast. In pieces.

He'd gone anyway. He'd known what he'd find.

The kettle boiled. He made tea, strong, with two sugars, which he only did after something had gone wrong, and sat down at the kitchen table, and the quill came down the corridor on its own and hung at his shoulder, and he looked at it for a while before he said anything.

The quill wrote it.

"Adult male. Human-diet. Fed within the last twenty-four hours, by the smell; the shifters said Victoria. Off the Port Angeles ferry and down the 101 on foot, walking straight into node thirty-one at about nine past midnight. Textbook. Lovely." He turned the mug on the table. "Taken out of the snare by the smaller pack before I arrived. Dismembered. Burned. Accelerant: charcoal lighter fluid, out of a squeeze bottle. The grey one. She'd been carrying it tied to her leg since Wednesday afternoon, I'd guess, waiting." He thought about the empty bottle in her hand and the firelight on her face, and the look on it. "Planned. Premeditated. Executed with the Alpha's consent and the adolescent's participation, though the adolescent was sick afterward." A pause. "Credit to her. I'd have done exactly the same, in her shoes. I'd have done it sooner."

He sat with that for a minute.

"Implication," he said slowly. "A fragment kept somewhere safe, in a jar, on a shelf, is a telegraph. It's alive as long as its owner's alive. It dies when its owner dies, wherever its owner is." He looked at the quill. "You'd know. In London. The moment one of them went, anywhere in the world. You'd just have to look at the jar."

He thought about the shelves in the Hall of Prophecy, row on row, humming. He thought about another room, smaller, with other jars on its shelves. One for each of them. Aro. Caius. Marcus. Jane.

The quill struck it.

**The Cullen house — 12:51 a.m.**

Two of the old guys at the counter turned round on their stools to look at him. Dot stopped with the coffee pot halfway to a cup.

He saw Charlie, and smiled, and came down the aisle.

Charlie stood up. He didn't know why; it was just what you did, when it was Carlisle. Your daughter's father-in-law. The man who'd saved your daughter's life, twice now, if you counted Atlanta, which Charlie didn't let himself think about too closely. The man whose hand was always cold when you shook it.

It was cold now.

"Charlie," said Carlisle. "Congratulations. I hear you caught a whale."

"A tuna," said Charlie. He felt his ears go red. "Just a tuna."

"It's all over the hospital. Half the nurses have a steak of it in their freezer, and the other half are angry with the first half." Carlisle's smile was very kind. It always was. "Bella's thrilled. She called me Saturday night to tell me." A small pause. "She asked me to tell you she's proud of you."

Something went through Charlie's chest, quick and sharp. He didn't let it get to his face. "Yeah? Well. Tell her—tell her thanks. Tell her I'll bring her a steak."

"She'd like that."

And then Carlisle turned, and looked at the Englishman, who had stood up too, on the other side of the booth, and was standing with his hands at his sides.

"Mr. Evans," said Carlisle.

"Doctor," said the Englishman.

Charlie looked from one of them to the other.

It was nothing. They were two men saying hello. Carlisle's face was as kind and calm as ever, and the Englishman's was pleasant, and they shook hands across the corner of the table, briefly, the way men do. Carlisle's cold hand. The Englishman's hot one. Charlie felt something pass between them in the second their hands met that he couldn't have described if you'd put him on the stand, a kind of stillness, like two dogs meeting on a sidewalk and neither of them moving a muscle.

"You two know each other?" Charlie said.

"The doctor's been very kind," said the Englishman. "He's been helping me with the report. Hospital records on the hikers last year. The injuries. Bite marks, that sort of thing." He nodded at Carlisle. "He did the post-mortems, I gather. On some of them. Very useful."

"Yes," said Carlisle, after a moment. "I'm glad to help."

Charlie looked at Carlisle. Carlisle hadn't said anything about any report. He'd been at the house on Sunday, at Charlie's, dropping off a casserole from Esme, and he hadn't said a word about it.

But then, he wouldn't. Patient records. Confidentiality. He was that sort of man.

"I wondered," said Carlisle to Charlie, "if I might borrow Mr. Evans for a few minutes. There are one or two things in the records he asked about. I don't want to keep you."

"Keep me? I've got to get to work. Bunch of kids knocked over the Hendersons' mailbox again." Charlie picked up his hat and the newspaper, and then, after a second, put the newspaper back down on the table. "Keep that. I've got five more." He dug in his pocket for a ten, put it under his mug. "Coffee's on me again, Evans. Don't let him pay for anything, Carlisle, he tips like a drunk sailor."

"I'll keep an eye on him," said Carlisle.

"You do that." Charlie put his hat on. He looked at the Englishman. "Same time tomorrow?"

"Wouldn't miss it, Chief."

Charlie nodded. He went up the aisle and out the door, and the bell rang behind him, and he crossed the lot to the cruiser.

He didn't look back until he was behind the wheel. Then he did, through the windshield and the diner's front window, the way he always did, because he was a cop and you always looked.

The Englishman had come back round the table and taken the seat Charlie had just left, facing the door, the way he'd sat every morning before Charlie joined him. Carlisle had sat down across from him, with his back to the room and the door and the whole of the town of Forks.

Charlie sat there with the engine running and watched them through two panes of glass.

Two men in a diner booth. One of them his daughter's father-in-law. One of them his good-luck charm. Neither of them touching their coffee.

*Be careful, Charlie.*

He thought about Bella. About a white couch and brown eyes a shade off. About *she's proud of you*. About a casserole on Sunday, and a cold hand, and a doctor who'd been helping a stranger with a report and hadn't mentioned it.

Then he put the cruiser in reverse, and backed out, and drove away to deal with the Hendersons' mailbox, and he didn't let himself think about it again until he was almost at the station, and by then he'd decided not to.

**The diner — 10:31 a.m.**

The man had taken the seat facing the door.

Carlisle noticed it, sitting down, the way he noticed most things. A minute ago Charlie had been sitting there; Carlisle had watched him through the window from the car, and had watched the man give him the seat, and had understood what it was for. Now Charlie was gone, and the man had come round the table and taken it back without a word, and left Carlisle the seat with its back to the room.

Carlisle had sat across a great many tables from a great many people in three and a half centuries, and he knew rudeness, and this was something else. This was a man who had given the good seat to someone he wanted to like him, and taken it back from someone he didn't need to.

Carlisle found he didn't mind. He sat with his back to the door, which he'd done for most of three hundred years, because there had never been very much in any room that could hurt him.

His ears went strange.

A high thin hum, like a mosquito trapped behind glass. The noise of the diner, the radio, the griddle, the old men at the counter, all of it went soft and far away and muffled, as if somebody had laid a blanket over the booth. Carlisle had heard it described. Leah had said *bees*.

"Doctor," said the man.

"Mr. Evans." Carlisle folded his hands on the table. "Or is it?"

"It'll do for now."

Up close he was younger than Carlisle had expected from the voice on the telephone. Twenty-five, twenty-six. The face of a young man who'd been an unhappy boy, Carlisle thought, from long habit, the way he'd read faces across hospital beds for a century: the jaw set a little too hard, the eyes too watchful, old scar tissue under the skin of the expression even where the skin itself was smooth. Black hair. Green eyes, very clear, watching him with the same calm attention Carlisle remembered from the anatomy theater in Padua, two hundred years ago, on the faces of the students who would go on to be good surgeons.

And his heart. Sixty-two beats a minute. Steady. Unhurried.

And his blood.

Carlisle had been not drinking human blood for so long that he could sit in an operating theater with a man's chest open in front of him and think about nothing but the work. Most of the time. He held himself the way he always held himself, and he breathed, because it would have looked strange not to, and he let the man's scent come to him across the table.

It was human. That was the first thing. Rich and hot and very much alive, the smell of a strong young man with a strong heart, and Carlisle registered its pull the way he'd register a draft from an open window and set it aside.

And under the human, there was something else.

Faint. Very faint. The same dry musky note Carlisle had smelled on Jacob Black every day for a month: the smell the family wrinkled their noses at and were too polite to mention. Wolf, but older than Jacob's, and deeper. Like a cellar under a cellar.

And the heat. Carlisle could feel it from across the table: the man's body temperature, the heat coming off his skin. A hundred and seven, a hundred and eight. A fever no human being would be walking around with, eating pie.

The man watched him notice all of it. He didn't say anything about it.

Dot came, with a mug and the pot. "Coffee, Doctor?"

"Please," said Carlisle. "Thank you."

She poured it and went. Carlisle put his hands round the mug. He wouldn't drink it. He never did; he held cups of coffee in diners and break rooms and hospital cafeterias for the heat he didn't feel, because people trusted a man holding a cup of coffee.

"You can leave it," said the man. "Dot won't take it personally. She'll just think you're English."

---

"Jacob told me," said Carlisle, "what you said to him on Saturday night."

"I imagine he did."

"He said you told him you work for something like the CIA crossed with DARPA."

"I did."

"That's more than you've told me."

"You've not set fire to anything," said the man. "Yet."

Carlisle looked at him for a moment. "I'm sorry about Saturday night. For what it's worth. I'd asked them not to."

"I know you had." The man picked up his fork. "They're not yours to ask. You know that and I know that and, more to the point, Miss Clearwater knows it. I'd have done the same, in her shoes. I've told her so. Well. I told the log." He ate a piece of pie. "Doesn't mean I'm not annoyed."

"No."

"Never mind. Seattle's nice in November."

Carlisle didn't ask what that meant. He thought he could guess. He thought about the young man with the shaved head who'd come off the ferry from Victoria on Saturday night, and the old man on the bench in the park, and found that he couldn't make himself grieve for one of them very much, and that he was sorry about that.

"I have some questions," he said.

"I've some answers. Not as many as you've got questions." The man set his fork down. "Go on, then. Get the ones you're not going to like out of the way first. Saves time."

"The man in the snare on Tuesday. The first one."

"Run its course, Doctor. I said."

"Is he dead?"

"I'm not going to go into that."

"Then I'll assume he is."

"Assume what you like." The man didn't seem to mind. "He killed a pediatric nurse at Lake Crescent on the first of October. He killed a hunter from Raymond in September and took his coat. He'd been doing that sort of thing since nineteen thirty-one, and he'd never once in seventy-five years asked anybody's name. If you want me to say I'm sorry about him, I'm not going to do that either."

Carlisle didn't ask him to.

"Renesmee," he said.

The man's face didn't change. "Go on."

"You told Jacob she was safe *only so long* as she leaves people alone."

"I did."

"She's a child. She's four weeks old."

"She's a child who drinks human blood," said the man, "out of a plastic cup. You bring it home from the hospital in a blue cooler with a white lid, twice a week, Tuesdays and Fridays, at the end of your shift. O-negative mostly. Some A-positive. Bags that are a day or two off their expiry date and would have gone in the incinerator otherwise." He picked up his mug. "That's between you and the hospital and your conscience, Doctor, and I don't care. Nobody's dying for it. The day it's out of somebody's neck instead of a bag, I'll care."

Carlisle sat very still.

He'd been careful. He was always careful. He'd been careful for three hundred and fifty years. He took the bags out through the loading dock, and only the ones that were already marked for disposal, and he signed nothing, and he'd have sworn on anything he held holy that nobody living had ever seen him do it.

"You've been watching the hospital," he said.

"I've been watching everything. It's sort of the job." The man drank. "You were careful. You were very careful. You'd have been fine if anybody but me had been looking."

"And if she does?" Carlisle heard his own voice. It was quiet. "If she grows up and one day she—"

"Then I'll come and have a conversation with her," said the man. "And with you. And you know what? You'd have it with her before I would. You'd have had it already. You'd have had it the first night." He set the mug down. "You and I are on the same side of that one, Doctor. We just disagree about who gets to be the last word."

Carlisle looked at him across the table for a long moment.

"Bella asked me to ask you," he said, "what you want with her father."

"I thought she might."

"Well?"

"That's need to know."

"She's his daughter."

"I'm aware." The man was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, something in his voice had gone down a little, the way it had on the telephone when he'd said *the newest one included*. "Tell her nothing's going to happen to her dad while I'm here. Nothing. I told Billy Black the same thing on Saturday, in the boat. I keep my promises."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the one she's getting," said the man. "Tell her I'm sorry it's not a better one."

---

He let the silence go on after that. Carlisle let him.

Outside, in the gray light beyond the window, a logging truck went by on 101 with its air brakes hissing, and the window rattled, and behind the muffling hum Carlisle could hear, very faintly, Dot singing along to something on the radio.

"You told me on Wednesday," Carlisle said, "that we'd talk properly when you'd decided what you wanted from me."

"I did."

"Have you decided?"

The man looked at him. He looked at him for a long time, and Carlisle had the strangest sensation, one he hadn't had in three and a half centuries: that he was being weighed. He'd been read, by Aro, more times than he could count, Aro's cool dry palm against his own and every thought he'd ever had pouring out of him like water from a jug. This was different. This was a man looking at him from the outside, carefully, the way you'd look at a bridge before you drove a truck over it.

"Yes," said the man.

"Then tell me."

"I'd like to talk about Italy."

Carlisle went still.

He went so still that the coffee in the mug between his hands stopped moving.

"Italy," he said.

"Volterra," said the man. Quietly. Pleasantly. As if he were naming a holiday resort. "The years you spent there. Under the clock tower. The keep. The corridors. The stairs. The people." He picked up his fork again, and looked at it, and put it down. "I'd like to hear about all of it. Everything you remember. Which, with your sort, I gather is everything."

"How do you know about that?"

"I hear all sorts of things."

"From whom?"

"Classified."

"*Why?*"

He hadn't meant to say it like that. He heard it come out of him with an edge on it he hadn't heard in his own voice in a very long time, not since he was a young man in a dark coat in London, standing in the smoke behind his father. The man heard it too. He didn't flinch.

"Need to know," he said.

"Then I need to know."

"No," said the man, almost gently. "You don't. Not yet. And I'm not going to tell you, Doctor, because the moment I tell you, you'll have something in your head that a man you used to be very fond of could take out of it by shaking your hand. And he would. And then a great many people would die, and some of them would be mine, and some of them would be yours." He let that sit. "So I'm not going to tell you why. I'm going to tell you something you already know, and let you think about it."

Carlisle waited.

"You know what they do in there," said the man. "You sat at the table. You watched. You know what they eat, and how they get it, and how many. You know about the tours. The pretty one who takes the coach parties round the square and brings them in through the front door. You know that nobody on any of those coaches has ever come back out." He tilted his head. "So you know why somebody might like to know where all the doors are."

Carlisle didn't say anything.

He couldn't. He sat at the table in the diner in Forks with a mug of coffee going cold between his hands and the old men arguing about football at the counter behind him, and he saw the shape of it. He'd lived long enough to know the shape of a thing like this when he saw it coming over the horizon, the way a sailor knows the color of a squall before it hits.

A government. Somebody's. Spies and science. Of *his kind*, whatever his kind was. That had found out about Volterra, somehow. That had been counting coach parties. That had sent a man to the other side of the world to take one of Carlisle's kind apart on a table and find out how it worked.

*How to kill them.*

"You're going to war with them," Carlisle said. It came out of him very quietly.

"That's above your clearance," said the man.

"If you go up against Aro—if anyone does, if anyone even *tries*—" Carlisle could hear his own voice and couldn't stop it. "He'll come here. Do you understand? He won't stop at whoever did it. He'll come for everyone who helped. Everyone who knew. He'll come for my family. He'll come for the baby. He'll come for *Charlie*—"

"I know," said the man.

"Do you?"

"Better than you'd think, Doctor." He was looking at Carlisle with something in his face that Carlisle couldn't read at all. "I've done this before. Not with them. With somebody else. There was a war, where I come from, when I was younger than your Edward looks. And the man on the other side came for everyone who'd helped, and everyone who knew. That's what they do, his sort and Aro's sort. It's the only thing they've got." He paused. "I know exactly what happens. I was there when it did."

Carlisle looked at him. At the young face and the old eyes and the jaw set too hard.

"I'm not asking for an answer today," the man said. "I'm asking you to think about it. Talk to your family. Talk to your son who reads minds and your daughter who sees what's coming, though I'd be grateful if she'd stop looking at me now, I've not got any more coins." Something that wasn't quite a smile went across his face and left. "And take your time. Just not too much."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no." The man shrugged. "You're still on the guest list. I'm not that sort. I'll find out what I need to know some other way. It'll be slower. And some people I'd rather didn't will get hurt finding it out."

"You could just take it," said Carlisle. "Couldn't you." He heard himself say it and couldn't believe he'd said it out loud. "Out of my head. Here. Now. The way you took the third wife out of Billy Black's head, in the boat, while he was telling you about whaling."

The man was quiet for a moment.

"I could," he said.

"Then why don't you?"

The man looked at him. Then he looked down at the table, at the newspaper Charlie had left behind, at the photograph of Charlie on one knee beside his fish with his hand on its side and his face split open with joy, and the slice of orange elbow at the edge of the frame.

"Because I'm asking," he said. "Some things you take. Some things you ask for." He looked up. "If you can't tell the difference anymore, you're in the wrong job. Somebody told me that, when I was starting. I thought it was the sort of thing old men said."

He reached into his jacket and took out his wallet, and put a twenty on top of Charlie's ten under the mug, and slid out of the booth and stood.

"Same time tomorrow, Doctor," he said. "And the day after. And the day after that. I'll be here. When you've decided, come and sit down."

He pulled on a dark wool cap. The hum in Carlisle's ears stopped. The diner came back all at once: the radio, the griddle, Dot singing, one of the old men saying *—I'm telling you, he was offside—*

"Ta-ra, Doctor," said the man, and went out. The bell rang behind him.

---

Carlisle sat in the booth.

He sat there for a long time, with his back to the room and his hands around a mug of coffee he hadn't touched, looking at the newspaper on the table in front of him. At Charlie's face. At the fish.

He thought about Aro.

He didn't want to. He tried, for a long time, not to. But he thought about him anyway. Aro in the high room at the top of the tower, two hundred years ago, with the afternoon light coming in through the narrow windows and lying across the floor in long gold bars, holding out his hand. *Carlisle, my friend. Stay. Stay with us. You could have everything.* Aro's laugh. Aro's dry cool palm, every time, against his own, and the feeling of being emptied, gently, like a pocket being turned out. Aro, who had loved him, in his way, and still did, as far as Carlisle knew, in his way.

He thought about the coach parties. He'd never seen one. They'd come after his time. But he'd seen what came before them, the other ways, the older ways, and he'd sat at the table, and he'd watched, and he'd said nothing, for a long time, until he couldn't anymore and left.

He thought about a young man with green eyes saying *I was there when it did.*

He thought about his father, who had never once asked anybody for anything. Who had taken. Who had kept them in the cellar under the church and asked his questions and written the answers in a clerk's hand and locked the book in his desk, and never once wondered whether there were some things you asked for instead.

Dot came by with the pot. She looked at his full mug and didn't pour.

"Your friend's a sweetheart," she said. "Isn't he?"

Carlisle looked up at her. She was smiling. She meant it.

"He tips very well," Carlisle said.

"Doesn't he just." She went off, humming.

Carlisle sat a moment longer. Then he folded the newspaper carefully, the way Charlie would want it folded, and put it under his arm to take home to Bella, and stood, and went out into the gray morning.

Alice would be waiting. He'd have to tell her she wouldn't be able to see any of this. He'd have to tell all of them.

He thought, crossing the wet lot to his car, that there was a shape coming over the horizon toward them, the size of a war, and right in the middle of it, where Alice would want to look, there would be nothing at all. Just a hole. The shape of a man.

He got into the car, and sat with his hands on the wheel, and for the first time in a very long time, Carlisle Cullen did not know what he was going to do.

**The Cullen house — 11:14 a.m.**

Edward heard Carlisle a mile before the car turned off the highway, and he knew at once that something in Carlisle had broken.

Carlisle's mind had always been the quietest in the house. Three and a half centuries of practice. It was orderly and calm and kind, like a well-kept library, and even when it was troubled it was troubled in neat rows, the way a library is still a library when the books are on the floor. Edward had listened to it for eighty-eight years. He'd learned more about how to be a person from listening to Carlisle think than from anything Carlisle had ever said out loud.

What was coming down the road toward the house now was Volterra, and it was a mess.

It came at him in pieces, all at once, the way memories come up in a mind that's stopped trying to hold them down. The high room at the top of the tower, with the afternoon light lying across the floor in long gold bars. Aro's hand, dry and cool, held out. *Carlisle, my friend.* The long table. The things that happened at the long table, that Carlisle had sat and watched and said nothing about, for years, until he couldn't. The stairs. The corridors. The doors. A courtyard with a fountain. A small barred window, very high up, through which you could see a slice of Tuscan sky. Faces. Caius, sour and cold. Marcus, bored to the bone. Aro, smiling.

And over all of it, in the front of Carlisle's mind, sitting in a diner booth in Forks with his hands around a mug of coffee: a young man with green eyes, saying, pleasantly, *I'd like to talk about Italy.*

Edward stood up from the piano.

Bella looked at him. She'd been sitting beside him on the bench all morning, not playing, not doing anything, waiting. She saw his face.

"What," she said.

"Wait," said Edward. "He's nearly here."

---

Carlisle came in and took off his coat and hung it on the hook by the door and stood there for a moment with his hand still on the coat, as if he'd forgotten what came next.

Then he turned round and told them.

He told it all, the way he always told things, carefully and in order, standing in the middle of the room with Charlie's newspaper folded under his arm. Edward didn't need to hear it; he'd had all of it already, in Carlisle's head, in the car. He listened anyway. He watched the rest of them hear it. The seat by the door. The bees. The heat coming off the man across the table, and the smell under the human smell, old, like a cellar under a cellar. The blue cooler with the white lid, Tuesdays and Fridays, and how the man had known. *The day it's out of somebody's neck instead of a bag, I'll care.*

*Tell her I'm sorry it's not a better one.*

And Italy.

When Carlisle said *Volterra*, Alice made a small sound and put her hand over her mouth. Jasper went very still. Rosalie, on the stairs with the baby, turned her head away. Emmett, for once, didn't say anything at all.

Bella's hand found Edward's.

He felt it go through her the way he felt everything go through her, which was to say not at all. He couldn't hear her. He'd never been able to. He could only feel her hand, cold in his, tightening. And he could see her face, and he knew what she was seeing behind it, because it was the same thing he was. A square in the sun. A clock tower. The bell striking noon. Her body hitting his in the shadow. A small fair girl in a dark cloak, smiling at him. The throne room. Aro taking Bella's hand and holding it and holding it and getting nothing.

She'd been there. They'd both been there. Seven months ago.

"He wants us to tell him where the doors are," said Jasper quietly.

"He wants to know everything I remember," said Carlisle. "Which is everything."

"He's going to war with them," said Rosalie, from the stairs. Her voice was very flat. "That's what this is. Some government nobody's ever heard of has found out about Aro and they're going to go in there and—" She stopped. "And they want us to draw them a map."

"He wouldn't say," said Carlisle. "I asked him. He said it was above my clearance."

"It's above everybody's clearance," said Emmett. "That's sort of the point of a war."

Nobody laughed.

"I can't see it," said Alice.

She'd taken her hand away from her mouth. She was standing by the glass wall, very small, with her arms wrapped round herself, looking at the river.

"I've been trying since Carlisle sat down with him. I tried to look at Volterra. I tried to look at Aro. I tried to look at us, a month from now, six months, a year." She shook her head slowly. "There's nothing. It's a hole, and it's different from the wolves' blur. Right in the middle of everything. Like somebody's taken a pair of scissors to the next year and cut it out." She turned round. "I can see Charlie fishing. I can see Esme planting bulbs. I can see Rosalie on the back lawn holding her arms out to somebody I can't see, which is the baby. And then right where everything important should be, there's a hole the shape of a man, and I can't see past it."

"Because he hasn't decided," said Jasper. "Or because we haven't."

"Or because he's standing in front of it," said Alice. "With his coins."

---

"What did he say about my dad?" said Bella.

Carlisle looked at her. Then he told her. All of it. *Need to know. Nothing's going to happen to her dad while I'm here. I told Billy Black the same thing. I keep my promises.* And the last part, which Carlisle said very gently, looking straight at her: *Tell her I'm sorry it's not a better one.*

Bella was quiet for a long moment.

"He said sorry," she said.

"Yes."

"He threatens my daughter, and he won't tell me what he wants with my father, and he says sorry it's not a better answer." She laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "That's—"

"That's him," said Edward.

She turned and looked at him.

"Leah said it," Edward said. "In the diner. He said he was sorry about her father, and he meant it, and it didn't stop him using it. Not for a second." He paused. "He's sorry. He's always sorry. He just doesn't let it change anything."

Bella looked at him for a long time.

"I hate him," she said.

"I know."

"I don't even know what he smells like."

Edward didn't answer that. He'd heard Carlisle think about the smell, in the car, all the way home. The human, rich and hot and alive, and the pull of it, set aside. And the other thing underneath. He'd heard Carlisle think *a hundred and seven, a hundred and eight* about the heat, the way a doctor would. He'd heard Carlisle think *a fever no human being would be walking around with*.

He'd also heard Carlisle think the thing he hadn't said out loud yet, the thing that had broken the library open on the road home. Edward heard it again now, quietly, under everything Carlisle was saying.

*He could have taken it.*

*He could have sat across from me and looked, and gone in, and had all of it, every door and every face, and I'd have felt a headache and nothing more. He could have done that to Billy and he did. He could have done it to me and he didn't.*

*He asked.*

Edward stood very still in the middle of the room, with Bella's hand in his.

He'd spent eighty-eight years in other people's heads. He'd never once been asked. He'd never once asked. He couldn't. For him it was weather; he'd told Leah that on the porch, and it was true. Everyone in this room had lived with him walking through their minds for decades, and they'd made their peace with it, the way you make your peace with a leak in the roof. Nobody had ever been given the choice.

And a man in a diner in Forks, who could have walked into Carlisle's mind and taken the most valuable thing in it like lifting a wallet off a table, had looked at Carlisle across a cold cup of coffee and asked instead.

He didn't know what to do with that. He didn't know why it frightened him more than anything else the man had done.

*He'll ask me next*, Edward thought. *I was there too. In March. In the throne room. With Bella. He'll know that. He knows everything. He'll sit across from me in that booth and ask me.*

*And I'll have to decide whether to tell him.*

"We decide together," said Carlisle.

Everyone looked at him.

He'd put the newspaper down on the piano. Charlie on one knee beside his fish, grinning like a boy, with a slice of orange elbow at the edge of the frame. Carlisle stood with his hand flat on it.

"All of us," Carlisle said. "Everyone in this house. And Jacob. And Billy, if he'll come. Sam too, if he's willing. Leah." He looked round the room at them. "He said take your time. Just not too much. So we'll take a little. We'll talk about it. Everyone will have a say. And then—"

He didn't finish.

He didn't need to. Edward could hear the end of the sentence in Carlisle's head, and he knew that everyone in the room could see it in Carlisle's face. *And then I'll decide.* The way he always did. And nobody would argue.

Upstairs, Renesmee woke from her nap and called out, a small high wordless sound, and Rosalie went to her.

Bella let go of Edward's hand and went up the stairs after them, fast, the way she always did when the baby called.

Edward stood by the piano and listened to the house: to Alice, looking at a hole in the future and trying not to be afraid of it; to Jasper, already drawing maps in his head of a place he'd never been; to Esme, thinking about bulbs; to Emmett, thinking *war* with something in it that was very nearly excitement and that he'd never say out loud; to Carlisle, standing with his hand on the newspaper, thinking about Aro.

And under all of it, very far away, twenty miles east in the woods, a place where there should have been someone thinking, and wasn't.

He listened to the silence for a long time.

**Field station, Olympic National Forest — 2:20 p.m.**

"You told them about the CIA," said Croaker.

His face in the mirror was long and gray and folded and lit green by the lamp, and it was ten past ten at night in London, and he had Harry's notebook open on the desk in front of him, and he did not look pleased.

"I told them to think of the CIA," said Harry. "Crossed with DARPA. Then I told them to leave it at that."

"And did they leave it at that?"

"The grey one's seen a documentary." Harry leaned back in the desk chair. "She knew what DARPA was. She worked out I'm not American. That's the lot. No names. No country. Nothing about us. Nothing about Italy."

Croaker looked at him over the top of the notebook for a while.

"Why?" he said.

"Because they'd burned one, Saul. And they'd have burned the next one and the one after, because they didn't know what I was and so they'd decided I was the worst thing they could think of. Now they've got something to hang it on. Spies and science. Big and cold and official." He shrugged. "Frightened people do as they're asked."

"And are they frightened?"

Harry thought about the Alpha, standing in the ferns in the firelight with his whole body shaking and his teeth out, not moving. He thought about the grey one, saying *it's the Pentagon* as if she'd never seen him before.

"Yes," he said.

Croaker turned a page. Harry heard it crackle, five thousand miles away.

"Spies and science," Croaker said, after a while. He said it slowly, the way he tasted a wine he hadn't decided about. "Hm." Another page. "That's not bad, actually."

"I thought so."

"Don't do it again."

"No, Saul."

"And the doctor?"

"Came this morning. Like I said he would." Harry rubbed his eyes. "I asked him about Italy."

The lamp in London hummed.

"And?"

"And I didn't tell him why. And he worked it out anyway, in about four seconds, because he's not stupid and he's been alive since Cromwell. He asked me if we were going to war. I told him it was above his clearance." Harry paused. "He told me that if anybody goes up against Aro, Aro will come here. For everyone who helped. Everyone who knew. His family. The baby. The Chief."

"He's right."

"I know he's right."

"Did you tell him that?"

"I told him I'd been there when it happened before. Somewhere else." Harry looked at the bookshelves. "I told him to think about it. I told him I'd be at the diner every morning until he'd decided, and when he had, he could come and sit down."

Croaker was quiet for a long moment.

"You could have just gone in," he said finally. "Sat across from him and looked. You'd have had the whole keep in ten minutes. Every stair. Every door. Every face. We'd have had it in the planning cell by Wednesday."

"I could have."

"And you asked him instead."

"I asked him."

"Why?"

Harry was quiet for a while.

"You told me, the first week," he said. "Some things you take and some things you ask for, and if you can't tell the difference anymore you're in the wrong job. I thought it was the sort of thing old men said."

Croaker looked at him out of the glass. His long gray face did something Harry had seen it do perhaps three times in five years. It was very nearly a smile.

"It is the sort of thing old men say," said Croaker. "That's how you know it's true. We only say the true ones. We haven't got time for the others." He closed the notebook. "How long will he take?"

"A week. Two. He'll talk to them all. He'll talk to the reader and the Seer and the soldier and the one with the baby, and the baby's wolf, and the wolf's father. He'll want everyone to have a say." Harry smiled slightly. "And then he'll decide by himself, and nobody'll argue."

"And if he says no?"

"Then I'll go to Seattle on the first of November and get a volunteer, and run my range test, and if the jar works at a distance you won't need the doctor at all." Harry paused. "But I'd rather he said yes."

"Why?"

"Because he's the only one of them who ever walked out of that place on his own feet," Harry said, "and they let him. I'd like to know how."

When the glass had gone back to being a mirror, he wrapped it in its velvet and put it away and sat for a while at the desk with his hands flat on the wood.

Then he got up and went down the corridor to the lab, and stood in the white doorway and looked at cabinet N.A. 01: Merritt Lowe, gray and upright and dull as an old bar of soap, with his filmed eyes looking out through the glass at the jars on the shelf in front of him. Ash. Venom. A gray fingertip. A gray sliver of forearm.

There was a space on the shelf beside them. There was another cabinet beside his, standing open and empty, with a brass plate above it that said N.A. 02.

He'd engraved it a fortnight ago. He'd thought it'd be filled by now.

He looked at it a long time.

The girl had burned his second volunteer to ash with a bottle of lighter fluid from the Shell station and a two-dollar Bic, and stood in the firelight afterward with her chin up and told him to his face exactly where he could go. She'd been right to. He'd have done the same.

He found, to his own mild surprise, that he didn't mind as much as he should. He'd have to go to Seattle. He'd have to dig one out from under the viaduct. He'd have to wait for the doctor. He'd be here a good while longer than he'd planned, in the rain, in the tent, in their fur.

There were worse places.

The pie was very good.

He turned the lab lights off and went to the kitchen and put the kettle on, and while it boiled, he stood at the counter and looked at nothing, and thought about a girl in a burning wood saying *Harry whatever-your-name-is*, and found, after a while, that he was grinning.

"Lassie," he said to the kettle.

The kettle didn't say anything. It boiled. He made tea. Tomorrow was Tuesday. Ten o'clock. Cherry, if Dot had kept him a slice.

He'd see who came and sat down.
