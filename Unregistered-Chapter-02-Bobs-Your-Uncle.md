# Bob's Your Uncle

---

The trees closed over her like water.

Leah went in at a lope and was running flat out inside ten strides, low and long, her belly a hand's width off the ground. Under the firs the snow lay thin and crusted, and it broke under her paws with a sound like somebody stepping on cornflakes. She didn't care about the noise. She wanted distance. She wanted every yard of forest she could get between herself and that field, and the ash, and the black star, and the tartan blanket, and the stupid treacle tart, and the man who had sat on the far side of it with his tea going cold, watching her eat.

*Leah! LEAH! Did you SEE the—*

*I saw it, Seth.*

*He was so SMALL. He came up to my KNEE. He had a TEA TOWEL on. And the Bulgarian, Leah, he hung a guy UPSIDE DOWN in a LIBRARY, in SWITZERLAND, for a BOOK—*

*I was sitting right there.*

*I know! I know you were, I saw it through you, it was like the best thing I've ever seen in my whole life, and you were looking at the pie the whole time—*

She had been looking at the pie. She'd been looking at the pie because the other choice was looking at the man, and Seth knew that, because Seth had come crashing into her head the second she phased and gone straight through everything she'd been thinking for the last hour like a dog through a screen door. He knew all of it. He was being nice about it. That was worse.

He ran at her shoulder, sandy and gangly and loose-limbed, bouncing even at a dead run the way he had bounced since he was old enough to walk. Every few strides he bumped her with his shoulder, lightly, the way he did when he was nine and wanted her to look at something. She snapped at his ear. He dodged and kept bouncing.

Further off, faint, like somebody talking in the next room, she could feel Jacob.

He was still back near the field. He'd gone with the Cullens; she could feel his paws on the snow and the bloodsucker stink all round him and, under everything, that steady fixed hum that was the kid, the way there was always that hum with Jacob now, like a fridge running in a kitchen. Bella had the kid in her arms. Jacob was walking so close behind them his nose kept nearly touching Bella's elbow.

*Seth,* Jacob said, without much heat. *Quit yelling.*

*Sorry, Jake.*

*Leah.*

She ran.

*Leah. You okay?*

She didn't answer him. She let him feel her not answering, the whole flat wall of it, and after a second she felt him take the hint and pull back, his attention swinging away from her toward the kid in Bella's arms like a compass needle going home.

*Okay,* Jacob said. *Okay.*

And then, quieter, so quiet she nearly didn't catch it: *I'm sorry, Leah.*

She ran harder.

The ground dropped away under her in a long slope of fern and deadfall and she took it in three bounds, landed in a creek bed, went through the water without slowing, and came up the far bank in a spray of mud and snowmelt. The cold went up her legs and into her chest and she welcomed it. It was a real thing. It hurt in the normal way. It had nothing to do with anybody.

And all the time, the whole time, behind her—

She could feel him.

She'd been trying not to. She'd been trying since she got up off the blanket. It was like trying not to feel the floor under your feet. The line ran out of the middle of her chest and back the way she'd come, back through the trees, across the field, to a point a mile behind her that was moving. Walking. She could feel it walking. Steady and unhurried, a man with his hands in his pockets, going the same way she was going, as if he had all the time in the world and knew exactly where she'd end up.

*He's following us,* Seth said, delighted.

*I know.*

*Is he walking? He's walking. Leah, we're doing like forty miles an hour and he's WALKING, he'll never—*

The crack came from up ahead.

It went off in the trees in front of her like a gunshot, sharp and flat, and Leah threw all four legs out and skidded, her claws ripping long brown furrows through the snow and the needles underneath. Seth went straight past her, yelped, tried to stop, and went sideways into a sword fern.

He was standing at the top of the next rise.

He was leaning on a cedar with one shoulder, his hands in his jacket pockets, the big book from the field nowhere to be seen. His boots were dry. There was no snow on his jeans. He looked down the slope at her the way somebody looks down a platform at a bus they've been waiting on.

*How,* Leah said, inside her own skull, to nobody. *HOW.*

"Don't know where you live," he said.

She snarled at him.

He took one hand out of his pocket and tapped the middle of his chest, twice, with two fingers, right over the place where the line went into him.

"Following the string," he said. "Saves asking directions."

The crack again. The cedar was empty. A little twist of snow lifted off the ground where his boots had been, spun round once, and settled.

Seth came out of the sword fern with a frond hanging off one ear.

*OH my God,* Seth said. *Oh my GOD, Leah, he TELEPORTS. Did you see that? He just—* and Seth replayed it for her, the crack and the empty tree and the little twist of snow, three times in a row, faster each time, like a kid rewinding a tape. *Jake! JAKE! He teleports!*

*I saw,* said Jacob, from very far off. He sounded tired. *Bella says it's called Apparating. The Registry guy told Carlisle.*

*He APPARATES!*

*Seth,* said Leah. *If you say one more word I'm going to bite your tail off and make you eat it.*

Seth said nothing. He thought it instead, very loudly, a long bright happy wordless shout that went through the pack-mind like a firework. Leah put her head down and ran.

She went left. She didn't decide to; she just went, veered off the line she'd been running and cut away north and west through a stand of alder, up over a ridge she didn't need to cross and down the far side through a tangle of salmonberry that took the skin off her nose. It was stupid. She knew it was stupid while she was doing it. She did it anyway, the same way she'd dragged her paws at the treeline an hour ago, because there was still some part of her that believed if she ran fast enough and crooked enough she could leave the whole thing behind her in the trees like a burr scraped off on a branch.

The line didn't care which way she ran.

She felt it swing round behind her, smooth as a door on a hinge. She felt him stop, wherever he was. Turn. Start walking again, the same unhurried pace, only now in the new direction.

The crack came from the bottom of the slope. He was standing beside a mossed-over stump in the middle of a logging cut, looking up at her coming down the hill toward him with salmonberry thorns stuck in her coat.

He didn't say anything this time. He raised his eyebrows.

The crack. Gone.

She stood at the bottom of the slope with her sides heaving and her nose stinging and thorns in her ruff, and she understood, in the slow, total way she'd understood things her whole life just after it was too late to do anything about them, that this was how it was going to be. Wherever she went. However she went there. He'd feel it, and he'd follow, and he'd turn up, and he'd lean on something.

*Leah,* Seth said carefully, coming down the slope behind her. *Um.*

*Don't.*

*I was just going to say, I think he can feel where you're going. Like you can feel where he is. I think it goes both ways.*

*I KNOW it goes both ways, Seth, I read the BOOK.*

*Okay.* A pause. *What did the book say?*

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. She felt him go and look for it in her head, quick and nosy, and find the torn page and the green ink and *the parties are on no account to be separated*, and she felt him go very quiet.

*Oh,* Seth said.

She turned back toward home.

She didn't run straight. She wasn't going to give him that. But she didn't go out of her way again, either, and she found, about two miles on, that she'd stopped thinking about the line at all, the way you stop thinking about a shoe that's rubbing once you've walked far enough on it. It was there. He was there. He turned up four more times, at the bend in the creek below Gooseberry Point and the old logging road and the gravel turnout on the 110 where the high-school kids parked on Friday nights, and twice more in the trees above the river, and every time she came into sight of him he was standing still and she was running, and every time, before she'd got near enough to bite, the crack came and he was gone.

The fourth time, she realised he'd been timing it.

He was standing on the far bank of the Quillayute where it ran wide and brown and shallow over the gravel, a quarter mile above the bridge, and she came out of the trees on the near bank at full stretch and saw him and didn't stop. She went straight into the water. She went through it in a long churning plunge with her chin up and the river trying to take her legs out from under her, and came out the other side twenty feet from him, streaming, and he was still there.

He let her get to ten feet. She saw him decide it.

Then the crack, and the little twist of air, and the empty gravel.

Leah stood on the riverbank and shook herself, hard, from her nose to the end of her tail, so that the water came off her in a sheet and pattered down on the stones like rain.

*He let you get close that time,* Seth observed, wading out behind her.

*Shut up, Seth.*

*I'm just saying. That's progress.*

She nipped him on the shoulder. He yelped anyway, out of politeness.

The snow ran out before the trees did.

It thinned and went patchy and grey and then it was gone, and the forest floor under her paws turned soft and black and sodden, and the air changed. It came up the slope at her off the sea, wet and cold and heavy with salt and kelp and woodsmoke, and she felt her whole body unclench a notch at the smell of it without asking her permission. Home. Rain started as they came down off the last ridge, the thin, steady, slanting rain that never really stopped from October to May, and it ran down through her coat and took the last of the snow with it.

Through the trees below her she could see the roofs. Grey-green tin and dark shingle, chimneys smoking. The school. The water tower. The long curve of First Beach beyond, grey on grey, the sea stacks standing out of the water like black teeth with fog wrapped round their tops. Somebody's dog barking down on the road. A truck going by with its wipers on.

Seth peeled off without being asked, at the bottom of the slope, the way he always did. He went into a thick stand of salal behind the Littlesea place, and she felt him phase, the heat going up through him like a match struck in a dry barn, and then his voice was gone out of her head, and the quiet came down.

She stood alone in the trees and let it.

Then she went on, the last two hundred yards, down through the alders that grew along the back of her mother's lot. She went slowly. She'd stopped feeling the line move a while ago. It had stopped, somewhere ahead of her. It was sitting still. Waiting.

She knew where before she got there. She hated that she knew.

She came to the edge of the alders and stopped.

He was standing in her mother's back yard.

It wasn't much of a yard. A square of rough wet grass gone yellow for the winter, a woodpile under a blue tarp held down with tyres, a shed with a crooked door, her mother's clothesline strung out empty between two posts. A plastic chair on its side that nobody had bothered to pick up since September. And, parked up against the shed where it had been parked for nearly two years, her father's truck.

It was a Ford. Old and square and blue gone chalky with sun, with a dent in the tailgate where Seth had backed it into the mailbox the one time Dad let him drive it, and a rack across the back of the cab for fishing rods. The tyres had gone soft. There was moss in the rubber of the windshield wipers. Mom had never sold it. Nobody had ever asked her why.

He was standing beside it in the rain, with his hands in his pockets, looking at the rod rack.

He kept his hands in his pockets and stood there looking at it, the way you'd look at something in a museum, and the rain was coming down on his shoulders and his black hair and running off the leather of his jacket, and where it hit him it steamed. A thin grey haze rose off the back of his neck and his shoulders and went up into the cold air. She'd seen the same thing on Seth a thousand times, coming in out of the rain. On Jacob. On her father's—

On nobody else. Nobody else she'd ever known.

He turned his head and looked at her, standing in the alders.

She waited for him to say something. Something about the truck. Something about the rod rack, or the soft tyres, or the moss. Something about her dad.

*Turn round,* Leah thought at him, as hard as she could, uselessly, with a wolf's mouth that couldn't shape the words. Then she put her ears back and jerked her muzzle at the corner of the house, sharp, twice.

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he turned his back on her, and stood facing the shed with his hands in his pockets, and waited.

She went back a few yards into the alders where the brush was thickest and let it go.

The heat came up her spine and the wolf went out of her like a held breath, and then she was on her hands and knees in the wet leaves and the cold was on her bare skin, and the rain, and the silence in her head was total. She stayed there a second with her palms flat in the dirt. Then she untied the bundle from her ankle. It was soaked. Of course it was soaked, she'd swum a river. She pulled the cut-offs and the tank top on anyway, and they clung to her and were cold, and she didn't care, and she stood up and pushed her wet hair off her face and walked out of the trees.

From inside the house, through the kitchen window, she could hear Seth.

"—and he just SNAPPED, Mom, like THIS, and they all went up, every single one, whoomp, the guy with the white hair and the creepy little girl and the big guy who was going to grab him and everybody, and the old ones' necklaces all went runny in the—"

"Seth Clearwater. You put a shirt on in this house."

"—and then the Romanian guy JUMPED him and he did LIGHTNING, Mom, out of the SKY, out of a BLUE hole in the—"

"Shirt."

"—and he's got this little guy, this little elf guy, and he's coming HERE, Mom, Leah said he could have the couch, and his name's—"

Seth's voice stopped.

Leah stood in the wet grass and listened to her brother not finish the sentence, and to her mother not say anything, and to the rain coming down on the tin roof of the shed.

The man by the truck had turned round. He'd heard it too. Of course he had.

"Front door?" he said.

It was the first thing he'd said to her since the field, apart from the thing about the string. He said it quietly. He was looking at her, and not at the house.

"Back door's for family," Leah said.

She didn't know why she said it. It came out of her mouth flat and hard, before she'd decided anything, the way things came out of her mouth all the time.

He nodded, as if that was a reasonable answer to a reasonable question.

"Front door, then," he said.

He walked off round the side of the house, past the woodpile, past the kitchen window, and she heard his boots on the gravel of the drive and then on the wooden steps of the porch, one, two, three. Steam coming off him in the rain. She stood there with her arms wrapped round herself in her wet clothes and understood that he was going to knock. That he was going to stand on her mother's porch in the rain, after everything, and knock on the door like a guy selling encyclopaedias.

She went in the back door.

The kitchen was hot and smelled of coffee and the woodstove and the cold chicken Seth was eating out of the fridge with his bare hands. Seth was standing by the open fridge in his wet cut-offs with a drumstick in each fist and his mouth hanging open. Her mother was standing at the counter with a dish towel in her hands.

Her mother looked at her.

She'd been up all night. Leah could see it. Her mom had her hair scraped back and her reading glasses pushed up on top of her head and the grey cardigan on, the old one with the hole in the elbow that she'd worn every day for a month after the funeral, and her eyes were red round the edges, and they went over Leah from her wet hair to her bare muddy feet, and stayed on her face, and asked her about a thousand questions without saying a word.

"Don't," Leah said.

She went straight past her. Through the kitchen and into the hall and up the stairs, two at a time, the fourth step creaking under her the way it always did. Into her room. She shut the door. It slammed. She hadn't meant it to slam. She didn't open it again to shut it quietly.

She stood in the middle of her bedroom, dripping on the rug.

Downstairs, three knocks on the front door, quiet and evenly spaced.

Leah sat down on the edge of her bed in her wet clothes, and put her face in her hands, and listened to her mother walk down the hall to answer it.
---

Sue Clearwater dried her hands on the dish towel all the way down the hall, and kept drying them after they were dry.

It was eleven steps from the kitchen to the front door. She'd counted them once, years ago, carrying a feverish Seth up and down the hall at two in the morning, and she had never been able to stop counting them since. She counted them now. Behind her, in the kitchen, her son had gone quiet for the first time since he came through the back door, and the fridge was hanging open and humming, and upstairs her daughter's door had just slammed hard enough to shake the pictures on the wall.

Eleven steps. She used them.

She had been up since a quarter to four. The phone had gone at a quarter to four, and it had been Billy, and he'd said only *They're moving, Sue*, and she had said *All right*, and hung up, and then sat at the kitchen table in the dark in her nightgown with both hands round a mug of coffee she never drank while the sky outside the window went from black to grey. At a quarter past eleven the phone had gone again. Billy again. *Sam says it's over.* And then, after a silence so long she'd thought the line had dropped: *Sue, Sam says they're all alive. Ours. All of them.* She didn't remember putting the phone down. She remembered sitting on the kitchen floor with her back against the cupboard under the sink, and the linoleum cold through her nightgown, and the sound she'd made, which had come up out of her from somewhere very low and which she hoped to God nobody on the street had heard.

Then Seth.

Seth, eleven minutes ago, crashing in through the back door in nothing but a pair of wet cut-offs with the rain running off him in streams, talking before he was through the door and still talking now, or he had been until a moment ago. She'd caught about one word in five. The Volturi. Dead. All of them. A man. English. Fire, and lightning out of a clear sky, and a little grey creature with ears, and a book, and a pie. And then, in the middle of it all, with a drumstick in his fist and his face lit up like the Fourth of July, as though it were simply one more marvellous thing in a morning full of marvellous things: *And Mom, Leah IMPRINTED.*

She had put her hand down on the counter.

*On HIM, Mom. On the wizard guy. And he's coming here, she said he could have the couch, and his name's—*

And then Seth had stopped, and looked at her, and his face had changed, and she had watched her fifteen-year-old son work out in the middle of a sentence what the next word was going to do to his mother.

She hadn't needed him to finish. She'd heard it in the way he stopped.

Then Leah had come in through the back door, soaking wet and barefoot and white around the mouth, and looked at her, and said *Don't*, and gone upstairs.

Sue put the dish towel over her shoulder, and opened the front door.

He was standing at the far edge of the porch, as far back from the door as the porch allowed, so that the rain coming off the eaves fell between them in a thin silver curtain. Tall. A head taller than her, more. Broad through the chest and shoulders, in a brown leather jacket gone dark at the shoulders with rain, and a grey shirt under it, and jeans, and heavy boots with the laces wrapped twice round the ankle. Black hair, wet and flattened. A couple of days of stubble. Green eyes.

And steam.

It was coming up off him in the cold. Off his shoulders and the top of his head and the backs of his hands, a thin grey haze rising into the porch light, the way it rose off Seth when he came in from a run, off Leah, off every one of those boys who sat round her kitchen table eating her out of house and home. She had seen it every day for two years. She had never once seen it come off anybody who wasn't one of theirs.

She looked at the steam for longer than she looked at his face. She couldn't help it.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said.

"Yes."

"Potter. From the ICW." He kept his hands where she could see them, loose at his sides. "Your daughter offered me your couch. If that's a problem, say so. I'll sort something else."

English. Seth had said so, three or four times, as though it were the most extraordinary part of the whole business. London, or somewhere like it, rough and flat, with the corners worn off.

"Seth says your name is Harry," Sue said.

He didn't answer straight away. The rain came down between them off the eaves. Somewhere down the road a dog barked twice and stopped.

"It is," he said.

"Leah told you about her father."

"Bit of it."

"Then you know."

"Could go by Potter," he said. "Most people do. Told her the same."

She looked at him.

She thought about it. She gave it the time it needed, standing in her own doorway with a dish towel on her shoulder and the cold coming in round her ankles, and he let her take it; he stood on the porch in the rain and steamed and didn't fill the silence with anything at all.

She thought about the kitchen, two years ago, and the phone, and the way people had stopped saying his name to her. Everybody. All at once, as if they'd agreed it between them. Billy, who had known Harry since they were both in diapers, saying *him* and *your husband* and once, God help him, *the deceased*. Old Quil, at the council, saying *your late husband's seat*. Charlie Swan, who had fished with Harry every Sunday for twenty years, who had stood at the graveside with his hat in his hands and not been able to say anything at all. They'd done it to be kind. She knew that. She had let them, because she couldn't stand it either, and for two years the name had lived in her mouth and nowhere else, and sometimes she said it in the kitchen when she forgot and had to stop halfway.

"I was married to a Harry Clearwater for twenty-two years," Sue said. "I said that name every day of them. I'll say it every day for the rest of mine, and I'll say it to you, too, if you're sleeping on my couch. I can manage two."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"All right," he said.

"Wipe your feet."

He wiped his feet. He did it properly, the way her husband never once had, both boots on the mat, twice each. Then he came in out of the rain and stood in her front hall, and the hall got smaller.

She shut the door behind him.

He stood where he'd stopped, just inside, and looked at the hall the way she'd seen the boys look at a new stretch of woods: quickly, all of it, without moving his head much. The coat hooks with Seth's jacket and Leah's old school hoodie and Sue's raincoat. The shoes kicked into a heap under the bench. The pictures on the wall going up the stairs. She saw his eyes go up them, one after another, and stop on the third one.

It was Harry. Her Harry. Standing on the end of the dock at the marina in his orange waders, holding up a king salmon nearly as long as Seth had been at the time, grinning so wide his eyes had disappeared. Leah had taken it. She'd been eleven. The frame had a crack in one corner where Seth had knocked it off the wall with a football.

He looked at it for a second, two. Then his eyes moved on to the next one, Leah in her cap and gown, and the next, Seth with his front teeth missing. He didn't say anything about any of them.

"Kitchen's through here," Sue said.

Seth was gone from the kitchen when they came through. She heard him upstairs, banging drawers in his room, and then his voice through Leah's door, low and coaxing, and Leah's voice, short and savage, and then his door again. The fridge had been shut. Most of the chicken was gone.

"Sit down," Sue said. "Coffee or tea?"

He sat at the kitchen table. He chose the chair at the end, the one with its back to the wall and a clear view of both doors and the window, and she noticed that he'd chosen it, and that he'd done it without seeming to think. Her husband's chair. She didn't say so.

"Tea, if it's going."

Sue opened the cupboard over the stove and looked at what was in it. Then she took down the box and held it out to him. It was the tea Seth had bought her for her birthday from the Thriftway in Forks, because he'd thought she wasn't sleeping, which had been true. There was a bear on the front in a nightcap and a striped dressing gown, sitting in an armchair in front of a fire.

He looked at the bear.

"That's tea?" he said.

"It's chamomile. And spearmint, I think. Something."

"With a bear on it."

"Seth bought it."

He looked at the bear a moment longer, with the expression of a man being shown a photograph of somebody else's baby and searching his heart for something to say.

"Coffee's fine," he said.

She poured him one from the pot she'd made at a quarter to four and reheated twice since. It was black and bitter and had gone thick at the bottom. She put it down in front of him and sat across the table with her own cup, which she'd been holding on and off for nine hours and not drinking, and they looked at each other.

Up close, he looked tired. Seth had told her what he'd done in the field. Thirty people, burned. A man struck out of the air. Seth had told her with his eyes shining like he was talking about a ball game. And Sue had sat with that in her kitchen and found that what she felt, mostly, about the thirty people, was nothing at all. They had come to her town to kill a baby. They'd have killed her children for standing in the way of it. She had spent four months lying awake listening for the phone, and now they were ash, and she would not lose a minute's sleep over them as long as she lived.

The man who'd done it looked like a man who'd been up since three and hadn't eaten properly.

"Seth says you burned them," she said.

"Yeah."

"All of them."

"All the ones in cloaks."

"Good," said Sue.

His eyebrows went up a fraction. Then something happened at the corner of his mouth.

"You're the second person today who's said that," he said. "First one was a hundred and forty and lives in my kitchen."

"Seth said there was a—something. With ears."

"Kreacher. He'll be along. Fair warning."

She nodded as though that meant anything to her. She turned her cup round on the table.

"What are you?" she said.

"Tired."

"Seth said you'd say that."

"Seth was listening, then." He drank some of the coffee. It must have been dreadful; he didn't make a face. "Government," he said. "Theirs, not yours. There's a—" he looked for the word "—a lot of people like me, Mrs. Clearwater. People who can do things. We've got our own laws and our own courts and our own very boring paperwork, and we keep out of everybody's way, mostly, and everybody keeps out of ours. We keep a register. Things like the Cullens. Things like your boys. It's so we know who's who, if anything goes wrong."

"And your register's got our boys in it."

"Since nineteen-thirty-something. In the wrong place." He put the cup down. "Somebody saw one of your lot change in about nineteen-thirty-two and wrote it down as werewolves. It's been sat in a drawer ever since under *Werewolves, Probably*. Nobody's been out to check. That's what I'm meant to be sorting. That's what I'm here for, officially. I'm to talk to your council about putting it right."

"Billy," Sue said.

"Sam said he's been sat by the phone since sunup."

"Since before that." She looked at the clock over the stove. Half past one. "I called him while you were coming round the side of the house. Council's tomorrow morning, at ten. At his place."

"I told Sam I'd go round today."

"And I told Billy you wouldn't," Sue said. "He's been up since four. So have I. So have you, by the look of you. Ten o'clock tomorrow is soon enough for anybody."

He looked at her across the table. Then he nodded, once.

"Ten," he said.

"He'll want to know what it means. Your register."

"It means a number in a book. Somebody comes round once a year to see you're all right. It means if one of your boys ever gets into trouble somewhere he shouldn't, somebody on our side knows who to call. That's all. Nobody's taking anybody anywhere. Nobody's moving into your heads." He paused. "I'll tell him that tomorrow. I'll tell him as many times as he likes."

She believed him. It annoyed her a little, how easily. She had spent most of her life being careful who she believed.

"There's a man in Forks," she said. "Charlie Swan. Bella's father."

He didn't say anything. He was watching her.

"He knows something," Sue said. "Not much. He saw Jacob change, back in the fall. In his own yard. And he decided, right there, that he didn't want to know any more than that. He's been very—" she hunted for the word "—very brave about not knowing. He's got a granddaughter he isn't allowed to ask about. He holds her on his knee at Christmas and doesn't ask." She turned her cup again. "Seth said something about your people having a way of dealing with people who know things."

"Memory charms."

"Seth said they take it out of people's heads."

"They can."

"Are they going to do it to Charlie?"

"No," he said.

He said it at once, flat and plain, without anything round it. She waited for more. There wasn't any.

"No?"

"He's on a form," he said. "Investigator from the Americans asked Bella about him this morning. I told her to write *see Potter*." He shrugged, very slightly. "It means he's mine. I've looked at him, and I've decided, and nobody goes near him without coming through me first. He keeps what he's got. All of it." He picked his coffee up again. "Including the kid on his knee at Christmas."

Sue sat very still.

She hadn't known how frightened she'd been about that until it stopped. It went out of her like air out of a tyre. She'd been carrying it since Seth said *wizards*. She'd been carrying it, if she was honest, for a lot longer than that, since the day Charlie stood in his own front yard and watched a boy he'd known since diapers explode into a wolf, and she'd driven over that night with a casserole and found him sitting on his porch steps in the dark with his gun across his knees, staring at nothing. She had been more or less holding his hand through it ever since. He was a good man. He was a better man than he knew. He'd lost his daughter to those people one way and then lost her another way and had decided, somehow, to love her anyway without asking a single question, and Sue had thought, if anybody comes near that man's head with anything, I don't know what I'll do.

"Thank you," she said.

"Nothing to thank me for. It's a form."

"Thank you anyway."

He looked at her, and then down at his coffee, and didn't argue.

She let a moment go by. The rain ticked on the window over the sink. Upstairs, through the ceiling, she could hear nothing at all from Leah's room, which meant Leah was sitting very still and listening to every word.

"Leah," Sue said.

He put his cup down.

"Seth says she imprinted on you."

"Yeah."

"You're not one of ours."

"No."

"Then how?"

"Don't know," he said. "Honestly. I don't. I was standing in a field minding my own business, and she looked at me, and that was that. It got through some things in me it had no business getting through. And then something in me—" he stopped. His jaw moved. "Something in me took hold of the other end."

"The other end," Sue said.

"Your boys' imprinting runs one way, from what I can find out. Shifter to whoever. The other end doesn't feel it. Not with me. It runs both ways now." He touched his chest, the middle of it, with two fingers. "I can feel her. Where she is. Roughly how she is. She's sat on her bed upstairs right now with her knees up, and she's angry, and she's listening."

Upstairs, something hit the floor. It sounded like a shoe.

He didn't look up.

"There's a book," he said. "Two books. Old. The first one was useless. The second one said this sort of thing has happened before, a handful of times, and nobody's ever undone it, and nobody knows much about it except one thing."

"What thing?"

"It gets stronger at the full moon. And at the full moon the two of us aren't to be separated, on any account."

"On any account," Sue said slowly.

"Those were the words."

"And if they are?"

"Don't know. Page after that's been torn out." He paused. "Somebody wrote *Don't* in the margin."

Sue sat with that for a while.

"When's the full moon?" she said.

"Thursday."

"This Thursday."

"This Thursday."

She looked at him across her husband's end of the table. He looked back. He didn't seem to be hiding anything, or else he was so good at it there was no point in trying to see. His face just looked tired, and a bit fed up, the way her husband's face had looked when the boat engine wouldn't start and there was nothing for it but to sit there in the swell and wait for somebody to come and tow him in.

"Are you going to hurt her?" Sue said.

"Not if I've got any say in it."

"That's not a promise."

"No," he agreed. "It's what I've got."

She thought about that. She thought about every boy who'd ever sat at this table and promised her things. Sam Uley, nineteen and earnest, holding her daughter's hand under the table and promising Sue he'd look after her forever. He'd meant it. Sue had believed him. Every word.

"I'll take it," she said.

Something cracked in the middle of her kitchen.

It went off like a firecracker between the table and the stove, and Sue's hand jerked so hard her coffee went over the rim of her cup and across the table, and she found she was on her feet with her back against the counter and the bread knife in her hand, and she didn't remember picking it up.

There was a creature standing on her linoleum.

It came up to about her hip. It was grey and wrinkled all over like a walnut, with ears like a bat's that stood out from either side of its head, white hair growing in tufts out of them and drifting in the air, and huge pale eyes, and a long thin nose. It was wearing a tea towel. A clean white one, knotted at the shoulder, with a crest on the front in silver thread. It had a heavy gold locket on a chain round its neck. And it was holding, in its arms and on its head, a battered leather suitcase, a pair of black boots polished to a high shine, and a flat little leather case of the kind men used to keep razors in.

It saw her.

It looked at the knife in her hand. It looked up at her face. Its ears, which had been flat, came slowly up off its head.

Then it put down the suitcase and the boots and the razor case, very carefully, on her kitchen floor, and bowed.

It bowed the whole way down, until the tip of its nose touched her linoleum, and stayed there.

"The lady's mother," it said, into the floor, in a voice like gravel in a bucket.

Sue looked at the man at the table.

He was sitting exactly where he'd been sitting, with his coffee in his hand. He hadn't spilled a drop.

"That's Kreacher," he said.

"Kreacher," said Sue.

"He works for me. Sort of. Kreacher, up. You're frightening Mrs. Clearwater."

The creature came up. Slowly, with dignity. It looked at Sue's bread knife again, and then up at Sue's face, and something in its enormous eyes shifted. Sue had no idea what. It looked, if anything, faintly approving.

"Kreacher begs the lady's mother's pardon," it said. "Kreacher did not mean to startle the lady's mother in her own kitchen."

"That's—" said Sue. "That's all right."

She put the bread knife down on the counter behind her. Her hand was shaking, a little. She made it stop.

"Kreacher has brought Master's shirts," the creature said, turning to the man. "And Master's good boots, and Master's razor. And Master will use the razor *today*, because Master is sitting in the lady's mother's kitchen looking like a man who sleeps under a bridge, and Kreacher will not have it said—"

"Fine. All right. I'll shave."

"Today."

"Today, Kreacher."

The creature nodded, satisfied. Then it turned round on the spot and looked at Sue's kitchen.

It took its time. It looked at the stove, and the woodstove in the corner, and the pots hanging over the counter, and the jars of flour and sugar and cornmeal lined up along the back. It looked at the dish rack, and the floor, and the window over the sink, and the curtains Sue had made out of an old tablecloth the year Leah was born. It went over to the stove and stood on its toes to look into the pot on the back burner, and sniffed, and came back. Sue watched it the whole way. She felt, absurdly, the way she'd felt the first time Harry's mother came to dinner.

"Kreacher will cook," it announced.

"No," said Sue, "you won't."

The creature turned round. Its ears went flat.

"Kreacher," it said, "will cook for Master, and for the lady, and for the lady's mother, and for the lady's brother, and the lady's mother will sit at her table and rest, because the lady's mother has been up all the night, Kreacher can see it—"

"This is my kitchen," Sue said.

The creature stopped.

"I've cooked in this kitchen every day for twenty-two years," Sue said. "I cooked in it the week my husband died. I cooked in it when Leah was so sick with the flu I thought we'd lose her, and I cooked in it the day my son came home with his arm in a sling because a vampire had broken it." She folded her arms. "Nobody cooks in my kitchen but me."

There was a silence.

Sue had no idea what she expected to happen. She had a sense, from the way the man at the table had gone very still and was looking from her to the creature and back with his coffee halfway to his mouth, that he didn't know either.

The creature looked at her for a long, long moment.

Then she went to the cupboard under the sink and took out the sack of potatoes, and a bowl, and the peeler, and put them all down on the end of the counter, and pulled the step stool over with her foot.

"You can peel," she said.

The creature looked at the potatoes. It looked at the peeler. It picked the peeler up between two long fingers, examined it, and put it down again with an expression of profound contempt. Then it reached into the knot of its tea towel and drew out a small, black-handled knife no longer than Sue's little finger, climbed up onto the step stool, took a potato out of the sack, and peeled it.

It peeled it in a single unbroken spiral, thin as paper. It didn't look at what it was doing. The skin came off the potato in one long curl and dropped into the bowl, and the potato went into the pot, and the creature reached for another.

Sue watched it do three.

"Fine," she said. "You can peel."

"The lady's mother," said Kreacher, without looking up from the fourth potato, "keeps a clean kitchen."

At the table, the man made a noise into his coffee. Sue looked at him.

"Sorry," he said. "That's the nicest thing he's said about anybody since about nineteen-ninety-eight."

"Is it."

"He doesn't like anybody."

"Kreacher likes the lady's mother," said Kreacher, to the potato.

Sue looked at the little grey creature on her step stool, peeling her potatoes with a knife the size of a pen, and then at the man sitting at her husband's end of the table in a wet leather jacket with steam coming off his shoulders, and she put her hand over her mouth, and for the first time since a quarter to four that morning, since the phone, since the floor, she very nearly laughed.

She took her hand away.

"Bathroom's at the top of the stairs," she said. "Second door. Towels are in the closet on the landing. Leah's room is the first door. Knock on it, and I'll put you out in the rain myself."

"Wasn't going to."

"Go and shave," said Sue, "before your elf has a stroke."

He got up. He took the suitcase and the boots and the razor case off her floor, and went out into the hall, and she heard him on the stairs. Heavy, a big man's tread. The fourth step creaked under him the way it creaked under everybody.

Then nothing for a moment. He'd stopped on the landing. Outside Leah's door, or near it.

Then the bathroom door, and the lock, and after a minute the pipes knocked in the wall the way they always did, and the shower came on.

Sue stood in her kitchen and listened to it run.

She thought about the bathroom cabinet. She'd been meaning to clear it out for two years. There was a shaving brush in it, on the top shelf, with a worn wooden handle, and a mug with dried soap in the bottom, and a razor with a chipped ivory handle that had been Harry's father's. She'd moved them to the top shelf, out of the way, the week after the funeral. She hadn't been able to do more than that.

He'd open the cabinet. Men always did. He'd see them up there.

She found she didn't mind.

She went to the counter, and moved Kreacher's bowl of peelings a few inches to the left, and got down the big iron skillet from the hook over the stove, and the flour, and the cornmeal, and the paprika, and the jar of bacon fat from the back of the fridge. There were four good lingcod fillets wrapped in paper in the fridge that Charlie had brought over on Sunday. She'd been saving them, without knowing what for. She took them out and laid them on the board, and reached for the salt, and stood there a moment with the box in her hand.

It was Harry's fish fry. It had always been Harry's. Twenty-two years, and he'd never once let her make it, and then he was gone and she'd had to learn it out of her own memory of watching him, standing at his elbow with a beer in her hand while he talked and talked and dropped flour on the floor.

Upstairs, the shower ran.

*Harry*, Sue thought, trying it. Then, in her head, in her own voice, quite clearly: *Harry's upstairs in the shower.*

It hurt. It hurt about as much as she'd thought it would.

She could manage two.

She salted the fish.

Seth woke up because the house smelled like his dad.

He didn't know that was why at first. He just woke, all at once, lying face-down across his bed in his cut-offs with one arm hanging off the edge and the sheet stuck to his cheek, and the room was dark grey with rain light, and there was a smell coming up through the floorboards that went straight past every thought he had and down into some part of him that was still about seven years old.

Cornmeal. Hot bacon fat. Paprika, and fish, and lemon.

He lay there with his eyes open and breathed it in.

Mom had made the fish fry maybe four times since the funeral. Every time, the whole house went quiet for the night. She'd make it, and they'd eat it, and nobody would say anything about it, and afterward she'd wash the big skillet by hand and dry it and hang it back on the hook over the stove and stand there for a while with the dish towel in her hands. Leah wouldn't come down at all, some of those nights. She'd eat hers on the stairs.

The clock on his desk said 5:12. He'd slept for three hours without meaning to. He'd lain down to think for a second, just one second, about the lightning.

He got up and found a shirt, a real one, with buttons, and put it on inside out and took it off and put it on again the right way, and went down.

The fourth step creaked. Behind Leah's door, nothing moved. He could hear her in there, though, the way he could always hear her: her heart going too fast for somebody lying still, and the rain ticking on her window, and her breathing, slow and deliberate, the way she breathed when she was trying not to think about something.

He went on down.

Mom was at the stove with the big skillet going and her sleeves pushed up to the elbow and flour all down the front of her apron. She didn't turn round. "Set the table, honey."

"Yeah, Mom."

The little elf was standing on the step stool at the end of the counter with a potato masher in both hands that was nearly as long as he was, mashing potatoes in the big blue bowl with a kind of fixed, savage concentration, like the potatoes had said something about his mother. Next to the bowl there was a whole pound of butter with about half of it gone.

Kreacher, Seth thought. His name was Kreacher. He'd heard the man say it in the field.

Kreacher looked up at him with those huge wet eyes, all the way up and all the way down, and then went back to the potatoes without saying a word.

"Hi," said Seth.

The masher went in and out.

"I'm Seth. I'm Leah's brother. I saw you in the field. When you did the—when you came with the book, and the hamper. That was so—"

"Kreacher knows who the lady's brother is," said Kreacher, to the potatoes.

"Oh. Okay. Cool."

The masher went in and out.

Seth got the plates down. Five of them, then he stopped and looked at the elf and got down a sixth. He laid them out round the table. When he got to the end chair, the one with its back to the wall, the one nobody had sat in for two years except Charlie, sometimes, when he came for dinner and didn't know better, he saw that there was already a mug at that place. A coffee mug, empty, with a ring at the bottom. The chair was pulled out a little way from the table, the way you leave a chair when you've just got up out of it.

He looked at it. He looked at his mom's back at the stove.

He put a plate down there anyway, and a fork, and a knife, and didn't say anything about it.

"Where's—" he said.

"Back porch," said Mom. "He's smoking. I told him not in my house."

Seth went out the back door.

The rain had got heavier. It came down off the edge of the porch roof in a long sheet, so that the yard and the woodpile and the shed and Dad's truck all looked like they were on the other side of a waterfall. It was nearly dark already. The kitchen light came out through the window and lay in a long yellow square on the boards of the porch, and the man was standing just outside the edge of it, at the porch rail, with his elbows on it and a cigarette in one hand.

He'd shaved. That was the first thing Seth saw. The stubble was gone, and his hair was wet and pushed back off his face, and he was wearing a clean dark blue shirt with the sleeves rolled up and a pair of black boots so shiny the porch light showed up in the toes. He looked younger without the stubble. Older than Jacob, younger than Sam, maybe. He looked like a guy you'd see at the gas station in Forks buying jerky, if you didn't know anything.

He'd been looking at the truck. Seth could tell. His eyes came round to Seth when the door opened, but his head was still pointed at the shed.

"All right," the man said.

"Hey."

Seth went and leaned on the porch rail next to him, close enough to talk. About as close as he'd stand to Paul, which was about as close as you could stand to Paul without Paul doing something about it.

The cigarette smelled sharp and sour in the wet air. Seth breathed it in and made a face, and the man saw him do it and moved the cigarette to his other hand, the far side, without saying anything.

"Is that a real one?" Seth said.

"Yeah."

"Mom'll kill you."

"Your mum told me to smoke it out here."

"She told Dad that for like fifteen years," Seth said. "He used to stand right there. Right where you are." He pointed at the boards under the man's boots. "He used to say if she didn't want him smoking she shouldn't have married a fisherman, and she used to say she didn't marry a fisherman, she married a guy who owned a boat and went fishing twice a year, and he used to—"

He stopped. He hadn't meant to start.

The man didn't say anything. He drew on the cigarette, and let the smoke out away from Seth, off the far side of the porch into the rain, and waited.

"That's his truck," Seth said.

"I saw."

"I backed it into the mailbox. That's what the dent is. In the tailgate. I was thirteen, he let me drive it up and down the driveway, and I put it in reverse instead of drive and went straight into the mailbox, and the mailbox went right over, and he laughed so hard he had to sit down on the step." Seth looked at the truck through the rain. "Mom never sold it. She never talks about selling it. Leah says she's going to drive it into the sea one day and swim back."

"Would she?"

"Leah or Mom?"

"Either."

Seth thought about it.

"Mom wouldn't," he said. "Leah might."

The man nodded, as though that were useful information, and filed it somewhere.

"His rods are still in the shed," Seth said. "All his fishing stuff. Rods and reels and his tackle box and his waders. Do you fish? You could—"

"Got a rod for Christmas," the man said. "Never used it."

"You could use one of Dad's. He had like six. He wouldn't mind. He'd have *loved* you, he'd have—"

"Your mum's rods, now," the man said.

He said it quite quietly. He didn't look at Seth when he said it. He looked at the truck, and the dark shed beside it with its crooked door.

Seth stopped.

"Yeah," he said, after a second. "I guess. Yeah."

They stood there. The rain came down. Inside, through the window, Seth could hear the skillet hissing and the masher going in and out of the potatoes and his mom humming something under her breath, very quietly, a song he didn't know, which she hadn't done in—he couldn't remember how long.

"Can I ask you something?" Seth said.

"You can ask."

"The lightning."

"Mm."

"Could you teach me?"

The man looked at him then. He looked at him for quite a long time, with the cigarette halfway to his mouth, the way Seth's chemistry teacher had looked at him the time he asked if they could make thermite.

"No," he said.

"Because I'm a wolf?"

"Because you're fifteen and you'd blow your eyebrows off."

"I'm almost sixteen."

"Then you'd blow your eyebrows off and your mum would have me arrested." He took a last drag on the cigarette and looked at the end of it, and it was gone, the way it had gone in the field, out of his fingers between one second and the next, smoke and all. "Also, I can't teach anybody that. Nobody taught me. It's a thing I do when someone jumps at me."

Seth thought about the Romanian. The hole in the sky. The black star.

"Okay," he said. "Yeah. Okay."

"*Seth,*" said his mom, through the window. "Go get your sister."

Leah came down.

Seth had thought she wouldn't. He'd knocked on her door and said "Mom says dinner," and she'd said "I'm not hungry," and he'd said "It's Dad's fish," through the door, quietly, and she hadn't said anything at all, and he'd gone back downstairs. And then about a minute later, the fourth step creaked, and she was standing in the kitchen doorway in jeans and a grey hoodie with her hair still damp, and her face set like she'd come down to fight somebody.

She looked at the table.

She looked at the end chair, where the man was sitting. Mom had put him there. Mom had pointed at it, when he came in off the porch, and he'd sat down in it, and that was that. Seth watched Leah look at him sitting in Dad's chair. He watched her mouth go tight. He waited for her to say something about it.

She didn't. She came in and pulled out the chair at his left, the corner place, the one nearest him at the table, because the other places were Mom's and Seth's and the one Seth had put down for the elf. She stopped with her hand on the chair back. Seth saw her realise it. Then she sat down in it anyway, hard, with her jaw clenched, and put her elbows on the table and didn't look at anybody.

"Elbows," said Mom.

Leah took her elbows off the table.

Kreacher wouldn't sit.

He stood at the man's right elbow with his hands folded over his tea towel and his ears flat, and when Mom pointed at the sixth chair he looked at it as though it were a toilet. "Kreacher does not sit at Master's table."

"It's my table," said Mom.

"Kreacher does not sit at the lady's mother's table."

"You peeled the potatoes," Mom said. "You mashed them. You put a pound of butter in them, which I saw, and I didn't say anything, because it's not my heart. You sit at the table in my house and you eat what you made." She put a plate down in front of the sixth chair with a bang. "Sit."

Kreacher looked at the man.

"Don't look at me," the man said. "Her kitchen."

Kreacher sat.

He climbed up onto the chair and knelt on the seat, because sitting in it his chin would have been about level with the tabletop, and he knelt there bolt upright with his plate in front of him and his face working, like somebody being made to sit at the head table at a wedding. Seth had to look at his own plate very hard for a second.

Then Mom brought the fish.

There was a lot of it. There were the lingcod fillets Charlie had brought, cut into big pieces and fried in Dad's cornmeal crust till they were brown and crackling, and there was a mountain of fry bread, and the potatoes, which were more butter than potato, and a dish of green beans nobody was going to eat, and a jar of the hot pepper relish Quil's mom made every summer. Mom put it all down in the middle of the table and sat.

Nobody said anything for a while. They ate.

The man ate like the pack ate. Seth had noticed it in the field, the pie and the chicken and the ham, and now he watched it happen again: steady and fast and without stopping, fish and fry bread and potatoes and fish again, with the kind of total focus Seth recognised from his own body after a long run, when you were so hungry the food barely touched the sides. Mom watched it too. Seth saw her watch it, and saw her get up without a word and put the rest of the fish on the man's plate, and sit back down.

"This is good," the man said, after a while. He said it to Mom. He'd stopped eating to say it.

"It was Harry's recipe," said Mom.

Leah's fork stopped.

Seth stopped too. He felt it go round the table, the name. Mom said it so easily. She'd said it to the man like she was telling him where the bathroom was. Seth hadn't heard her say it out loud at dinner since before the funeral.

The man looked at Mom. Then down at the fish.

"Then he knew what he was doing," he said, and went back to eating.

Mom didn't say anything. She picked up her own fork. But Seth saw her face, for a second, before she looked down, and it was the face she used to have at the end of the dock when the boat came in.

Leah started eating again. Faster.

"So," said Seth.

"Seth," said Leah.

"I'm just asking a question."

"You're going to ask him if he's a werewolf."

"I was not going to ask him if he's a werewolf." He had been going to ask if he was a werewolf. "I was going to ask what the ICW is."

"International Confederation of Wizards," the man said, with his mouth full. "Big building in Geneva. Lots of meetings. Lots of people from lots of countries arguing about the same three things for two hundred years. I work for them some days, and for my own lot in London the rest."

"Doing what?"

"Turning up in fields."

"Do you do that a lot?"

"More than I'd like."

"Have you ever fought a dragon?"

The man thought about it. "Yeah."

"*No way.*"

"Once. I was fourteen. Mostly I ran round it a lot."

"You were *fourteen*?"

"Seth," said Mom.

"I'm just—he was *fourteen*, Mom, and they let him—"

"It was a whole thing. Long story." The man reached for the fry bread. "Ask me something else."

"Are you a werewolf?"

Leah put her fork down with a clack.

"No," the man said.

"He's lying," said Leah.

"I'm not lying."

"How would anybody know? You said yourself, Maggie's not here."

"Werewolves turn into a wolf at the full moon," the man said, "and they don't know who they are while they're doing it, and they'll bite anything that moves, including the people they love, and in the morning they don't remember any of it and they feel like they've been hit by a lorry. I've known a few. I was very fond of one of them." He picked up a piece of fish. "I'm not one."

There was something in the way he said it, the *very fond*, that made Seth decide not to ask about that one.

"What are you, then?" said Leah.

"Full," the man said, and put the piece of fish in his mouth.

Leah made a noise of pure fury and stabbed her potatoes.

Seth looked at his sister. He tried to do it without her noticing, which was hard, because she always noticed. She was hunched over her plate with her hood half up and her shoulders tight, eating like it was something she had to get done, and she was so close to the man she could have touched his elbow with hers. She was angry. She was always angry. Tonight the angry had a different shape, and it had taken Seth most of the run home to work out what had changed.

He'd been in Leah's head for a year and a half. Since the day she phased into Jacob's pack. Before that, in Sam's. In all that time, every single minute he'd ever spent in his sister's head, there'd been Sam. It ran under everything she thought like a radio left on in another room. Sam and Emily. Sam and Emily on the beach. Sam's hands. Sam's voice. Emily's face, and Leah hating herself for hating it. Every thought she had, it was there underneath, sad and hot and sore. Seth had got so used to it he'd stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing the fridge.

Today, on the run home, he'd heard it stop.

He'd gone looking. He hadn't been able to help it. In the trees above the Quillayute, when she wasn't paying attention, he'd gone looking for it in her head, the way you look for a light switch in the dark, and there was nothing there. The radio was off. Sam was just Sam. A guy. A big black wolf.

Seth had kept running and kept his thoughts very loud and very stupid about the elf and the Bulgarian so she wouldn't notice he'd noticed. But he'd wanted to stop in the middle of the forest and howl.

The man reached for the salt.

He was only starting to move his hand toward it, at the other end of the table by Mom's elbow, and before he'd got his arm halfway out Leah had picked up the salt shaker without looking and put it down next to his plate.

Then she froze.

Everybody at the table froze. Seth froze. Mom froze with her fork halfway to her mouth. Kreacher, kneeling on his chair, went still with a piece of fry bread in both hands.

Leah stared at the salt shaker sitting next to the man's plate as though she'd never seen it before. Her face went a slow dark red from the neck up.

The man looked at the salt. Then at her.

"Cheers," he said, and salted his fish.

Seth grinned. He couldn't help it.

Leah kicked him under the table. She did it hard enough that the whole table jumped an inch off the floor and the green beans slopped over the side of the dish and Mom said "*Leah Clearwater*," and Seth yelped and grabbed his shin, and Kreacher said something under his breath to his fry bread that sounded a lot like satisfaction.

Nobody said anything else for the rest of dinner.

Jacob knocked at a quarter to seven.

Seth got the door. Jacob was standing on the porch in nothing but cut-offs with the rain running down him in rivers, and he had a pile of blankets in his arms so tall he had to look round the side of it. They were nice blankets. Thick, soft, cream and pale blue and grey, with satin edges, the kind of blankets you saw in catalogues. They smelled like a bloodsucker. Sweet and cold and sharp, like a pile of sugar left out in the snow, and something flowery on top, lavender, like somebody had tried to cover it up.

"Esme sent these," Jacob said. "For your mom. For the couch."

"Oh," said Seth. "Cool. Thanks. Come in?"

"Can't stay." Jacob's eyes went past him, down the hall, into the kitchen. Seth didn't need to turn round to know what he was looking at. "Nessie's waiting up."

The man came out of the kitchen. He came down the hall and stopped a little way behind Seth, not too close, with his hands in his pockets. He and Jacob looked at each other.

Jacob had been looking at him across that field all morning like he wanted to take his throat out. Seth had felt it. Felt Jacob want it with his whole body and not be able to, because of Leah. It was still there in Jacob's face now. Seth could see it in the set of his jaw, the way he was holding the blankets a little too tight. But there was something else in his face, too, something that hadn't been there in the field.

"Bella says thanks," Jacob said. "For Charlie."

"Nothing to thank me for."

"She says to tell you she still hates you."

"Fair," the man said.

Jacob looked at him a while longer. He shifted the blankets in his arms.

"Leah says you won't hurt Nessie," he said.

"I won't."

"She said you said she's a toddler with a backpack."

"She is a toddler with a backpack."

Jacob's mouth did something. It was nearly a smile, if you were feeling generous. It went away again fast.

The man wrote his statement at the kitchen table after Mom had cleared the plates.

It sat there under his breastbone the way his own pulse sat there if he thought about it, and he was thinking about it, so it was louder than it should have been. That was all.

He closed his eyes and went down.

It was easy enough. He'd done it every night for five years before he went to sleep, the way some people checked the back door was locked. Down through the stone. Past the furnace in the cellar, his magic, banked low and quiet for the night. Down to the bottom of the dark, where the walls stopped.

The wolf was up, on its feet.

In five years he'd come down here last thing at night on every night that wasn't a full moon and found it lying down. Chin on its forepaws, eyes half shut, as settled as a dog by a fire. Tonight it was on its feet. It was walking. Up and down, up and down, in the dark at the bottom of him, the long black shape of it going one way and then the other, its claws clicking on the stone. Every time it turned, it lifted its head and looked up the line.

The line went up from the middle of its chest and straight through the ceiling of the dark, through everything, through the plaster and the joists and the floorboards of Sue Clearwater's front room, to the girl lying awake upstairs.

The wolf turned at the end of its walk and looked at him.

*Thursday,* Harry told it.

The wolf looked at him a moment longer. Then it sat down, very upright, with its tail curled round its forepaws and its ears pricked forward, and stared up the line toward the ceiling.

*Lie down.*

It went on sitting.

*I mean it. It's Tuesday. Go to sleep.*

He slept.

He woke at ten past four because his hands itched.

He knew it was ten past four because there was a clock on the wall over the television, one of those cheap ones shaped like a ship's wheel, and the hands glowed faint green in the dark. He knew his hands itched because he'd been dreaming about running, and in the dream he'd been running on four feet over wet sand at the edge of the sea with something grey running beside him, and then the itch had come up through the dream and woken him, and he lay there with his eyes open and his hands lying on the blanket in front of him and the itch running up the backs of them like ants.

He looked at them.

There was fur on the backs of his hands.

A dark shadow of it, coming up out of the skin the way stubble comes up in the morning, charcoal-black, thickening as he watched. He felt the bones under it start to move.

He went down. Fast.

The wolf was gone from the bottom of the dark.

He'd never seen that before. He'd never once come down and found the floor empty. There was only the bare stone, and the line running up out of the place where its chest had been, and the long scratches its claws had left where it had walked up and down, up and down, half the night.

It was above him.

He could feel it. It had come up the line. It had come up through the walls he'd built at seventeen, the walls nothing got through unless he let it through, and it had come through them the same way the line had come through them in the field that morning, as though they were made of wet paper. It had come up past the furnace without stopping. It was all round him now. It was in his chest and in his shoulders and in the long bones of his legs, and in his jaw, which was starting to ache, and in his spine, which was starting to lengthen, and it was coming up the way it came up once a month at moonrise, unstoppable and unhurried and enormous, like a tide.

It was a Wednesday.

The moon was somewhere up behind the cloud, fat and lopsided and one night short, and he could have pointed to it through the ceiling, and it had nothing to do with this. This was coming up from underneath.

He put his weight against it.

He did it the way he'd put his weight against Bella Cullen's shield in the field, all of it at once, the way you'd put your shoulder to a door with somebody on the other side. Five years of practice went into it. Five years of moonrises in the garden at the Burrow, at Grimmauld Place, in a safe house in Bucharest, in the back of a Ministry van on the M25 with Ron driving and swearing; five years of knowing exactly where the wolf ended and he began and holding the line between them right up to the moment the moon came over the horizon and took the decision out of his hands.

The wolf went straight through it, the same way it had gone through everything else, without any hurry, and kept coming.

His spine popped. Then again.

He had maybe two seconds.

He spent the first of them on arithmetic. The big one, the shape the moon brought up out of him, was thirteen feet long nose to tail and stood six foot one at the shoulder and weighed something in the region of a ton and a quarter, and Sue Clearwater's front room was about fourteen feet by twelve with a sagging floor over a crawlspace, and under that was mud. He thought about a ton and a quarter of wolf coming up off that couch in the dark. He thought about the joists. He thought about Sue at the end of the landing and Seth snoring and the girl asleep directly over his head, and the floor going, and the walls.

He spent the second one getting hold of the shift with both hands.

He knew he couldn't stop it. He took hold of it the way you take hold of a car in a skid, and steered.

The middle shape. He'd only taken it a dozen times. On jobs, mostly. Twice in the garden at the Burrow, for the kids, because Victoire had wanted to see what it would be like if he could still do the voices when he read them a story. Upright. Two legs. The size of a tall man and a bit, and no heavier than a big one. A shape that would fit through a door.

The wolf felt him steer. He felt it think about whether to let him.

It let him.

The shift came up the rest of the way like a wave breaking.

It always hurt. He'd stopped minding years ago. His jaw went first, pushing forward and out, his teeth lengthening in his gums, his nose and mouth stretching into a muzzle with a long slow pull he could feel in the roots of his eyes. His ears drew up and back and to points. His shoulders broadened and his ribs deepened and his chest filled out, and his spine went on lengthening at the bottom until it was a tail, thick and heavy, and the fur came up all over him in one long shudder, charcoal-black, darker underneath. His feet lengthened and widened and the pads came up under them, but they stayed under him heel and sole, flat, the way he'd steered them. His hands drew in on themselves and became forepaws, the palms thickening into pads, the digits shortening and broadening, and the claws slid out of the ends of them and then back in again as he tried them.

His clothes went where they always went. The shirt and the jeans just weren't there anymore. They'd be back when he was.

He came up off the couch onto his footpaws in one movement, because the couch was suddenly too short by about two feet and the spring had gone into his back like a spike.

His ears hit the light fitting.

It was a hanging lamp, one of those glass bowls on a chain, and it swung away from him on the chain and came back and hit him on the side of the head, and he flinched sideways from it, and his tail, which he hadn't had for five seconds and had forgotten about, swept the lamp off the side table by the couch. It went over and hit the floor with a crash, and the bulb went with a pop, and the shade rolled off under the television.

The house went silent. The snoring along the landing stopped mid-snore. Somewhere upstairs a bedspring creaked.

Harry stood in the middle of Sue Clearwater's front room in the dark, six foot seven on his footpaws with his ears flattened against the ceiling and the glass lampshade still swinging past his muzzle on its chain, and looked down at his forepaws in the green glow of the ship's-wheel clock.

Down at the bottom of the dark, the floor was empty.

"Oh," Harry said, to the room, in a voice that had dropped an octave and come out round a mouthful of teeth, "you are *fucking joking.*"

Leah heard it at ten past four, and lay there, and told herself it was a dream.

The crash came up through the floor under her bed and woke her the way a gunshot wakes you, all at once with her heart already going. Glass, and a pop, and something rolling. Down the landing Seth's snoring stopped dead in the middle of a snore. Then a voice. His voice, from the front room, directly underneath her, except it had dropped about a foot and come out rough and thick, like somebody talking round a mouthful of gravel.

*Oh, you are fucking joking.*

And then the line.

It had been lying slack in her chest when she fell asleep, slack and humming, like a phone line with nobody on it. Now it went taut so fast it pulled her up off the pillow. It was louder. Everything about it was louder, as if somebody had reached into her and turned a dial all the way up, and the thing on the other end of it, the thing she'd felt sitting under his breastbone like a stone in the field, wasn't sitting anymore. It was standing up. It was right underneath her. It was *big*.

Then the smell came up through the floorboards.

She'd caught it in the field, faint, under the earth and pine and wet stone, that thick dark animal musk that had made her hackles lift. Now it filled the house. It came up through the boards and through the rug and through the mattress and through her, and every hair on her body stood up at once, and she felt the heat go up her spine in a long hot shudder, and her fingers curled into the sheet, and she knew with total certainty that if she let go of the sheet she was going to phase right there in her own bed and put her paws through the wall.

She held on.

Down the landing, her mother's door opened.

"Harry?" Her mother's voice, careful and sleepy, from the top of the stairs.

A pause. Then, from the front room, in that deep, gravelly, wrong voice: "Fine. Broke your lamp. Sorry. Go back to bed, Sue."

Another pause. A long one.

"All right," her mother said, and her door closed again.

Leah lay rigid in the dark with the sheet in her fists and the smell in her nose and the line pulled tight as wire, and waited for something else to happen.

Nothing did.

She heard him moving around downstairs for a while. Very quietly. She'd never heard anything that big move that quietly; she could hear the glass being swept up, the little chink of it going into the dustpan, and the scrape of the lampshade coming out from under the television, and almost nothing of him at all, as if he were barely touching the floor. Then the couch creaked under a weight that made it groan. Then nothing.

Then his heart.

She'd been listening to it all night, before. Forty-eight beats a minute, slow and heavy and steady. She'd lain there for hours hating it, because it was the slowest heart she'd ever heard on anything alive and it went on and on under her floor like a clock in a quiet house, and she'd felt her own heart slowing to match it, and she'd hated that most of all.

It had changed. It was slower still, and deeper, and it came up through the floor so heavy she could feel it in the bed frame, like a big drum played softly a long way off.

*Not my problem,* Leah told herself. *Not my problem, not my problem.*

She'd been awake for twenty-six hours before she'd fallen asleep the first time. She'd fought, and run, and swum a river, and eaten half a pie, and found out the one thing she'd been sure of in the whole world was a lie. The drum went on under the floor.

She didn't remember falling asleep again.

When she woke up, the light in the room was grey and the house smelled of coffee.

Bad coffee. Burnt, and too strong, the way her mother's coffee smelled when she'd left the pot on the hot plate all morning. And under the coffee, the other smell, thick in the air like woodsmoke, so that she breathed it in before she was awake enough to stop herself and felt it go right down into her and her whole skin prickle.

7:40, said the clock.

The line was still pulled tight. It went down through the floor and through the hall and into the kitchen, and it hummed.

Leah got up. She put on sweatpants and an old Tribal School T-shirt with a hole in the hem, and stood in the middle of her room barefoot for a long minute with her hands in fists at her sides, and then she went downstairs, because she was not going to stand in her room all day hiding from her own kitchen.

The fourth step creaked.

She stopped on it. Below her, in the kitchen, the coffee machine was gurgling. Somebody was moving around. A cupboard opened and closed so gently it barely clicked. A spoon chimed once against a mug.

She went down the rest of the way and along the hall and stopped in the kitchen doorway.

There was a wolf at her mother's kitchen counter, making coffee.

It was standing up. It was standing on two legs, with its back to her, at the counter by the sink where Mom's old Mr. Coffee machine lived, the one that was older than Seth and made a noise like a drain. It was tall. Its ears, pricked straight up from the top of its head, came to within an inch or so of the ceiling, and it was standing a little to one side so they'd miss the hanging lamp over the sink. It was covered all over in thick fur, charcoal-black, so dark it seemed to swallow the grey light from the window, and where the fur parted over the shoulders it was blacker underneath. A tail hung down behind it, long and thick and black, quite still.

It had one of the bloodsucker's blankets knotted round its waist. The pale blue one, with the satin edge. It was wrapped round like a towel after a shower and tucked in at the side, and the tail came out over the top of it at the back.

It was standing on Mom's linoleum on its heels. She saw that and didn't know why she saw it. Big wide flat footpaws, the whole length of them down on the floor, heel and sole and toe, with thick dark pads underneath and black claws at the ends. Its forepaws were up at the counter. It was holding the yellow plastic coffee scoop in one of them, five thick clawed digits closed round the handle with the claws drawn in, and it was levelling the scoop off on the edge of the Folgers can with the side of one claw, very precisely, the way her father used to level off flour.

It tipped the scoop into the filter.

Leah's body went up like a match.

The heat came up her spine so fast it hurt. She felt it go into her shoulders and down her arms, felt her skin ripple and crawl and get ready to split, felt the wolf come up in her the way it came when Paul said something about her father, sudden and total. And under the heat, worse than the heat, something that wanted to go *toward* it. Across the kitchen. Right now. Her feet had already moved; she'd taken a step forward off the hall carpet onto the linoleum before she knew she'd done it. She grabbed the doorframe with both hands so hard the wood creaked.

"Don't phase in your mum's kitchen," the wolf said, without turning round. "She's only just got the floor how she likes it."

His voice.

It was deeper. It had come down into his chest, and there was something running under every word now, a low rough sound like a big engine idling. But it was his. The flat, unhurried London voice from the field, every word in it clear. The same voice that had said *Morning* to a hundred vampires and *Easy, Lassie* to her and *Sorry* about her dad.

He put the scoop down. He pressed the button on the coffee machine with the side of one claw, and it began to gurgle. Then he turned round.

The muzzle was long and black and narrow, with a black nose at the end of it and the lip drawn back a little at the corner where a white tooth showed. The ears turned toward her, both at once. The fur of his throat and chest was thick and rough, ruffed up where the blanket wasn't. He was broad through the shoulders and narrow at the hip, and he stood upright, square on his heels, with his forepaws hanging at his sides.

The eyes were his. That was the thing she couldn't get past. Green, a clear bright green, the green from the field. His eyes, in a wolf's face, looking at her across her mother's kitchen with exactly the same expression they'd had over the rim of a teacup on a picnic blanket in the snow.

"Morning," he said.

Leah held on to the doorframe.

"I hope you know," he said, "that I'm blaming you for this."

"*Me?*"

"You. Specifically." He lifted one forepaw and pointed a claw at her, the way a man points a finger. "You and your wolf."

"What did *I*—"

"It's Wednesday." The tail moved behind him, once, a short hard flick. "It's not the full moon. The full moon's tomorrow night. I should be stood here in jeans with a bad back off your mum's couch, drinking her terrible coffee and feeling sorry for myself like a normal person. Instead—" he spread both forepaws, indicating all of himself from the ears to the blanket "—this."

"I don't—"

"Five years," he said. "Five years. Up at moonrise, down at moonset, regular as the seventy-three bus. Never once off the timetable. Never. Not when I've been hexed, not when I've been stabbed, not when I fell off a roof in Prague, not at Ron's stag do, which you'd think if anything was going to do it—" He stopped. His ears went back. "Never. And then I'm stood in a field minding my own business, and your wolf has a go at mine. Snarling at him. Showing him all her teeth. And he likes that. He sits up and takes notice. You get him all worked up. He spends the whole night pacing up and down in there, and then at ten past four he decides he can't be arsed waiting for Thursday, and—" the ears flicked sideways "—Bob's your uncle."

Leah stared at him.

"Who," she said, "the hell is Bob?"

He looked at her.

"It's an expression."

"I don't have an uncle named Bob."

"Everybody's got an uncle named—it's an *expression*. It means *and there you go*. It means *and that's that*."

"Then say *that's that*."

"I'm English," he said. "We say Bob's your uncle."

She opened her mouth to say something about the English, and found she didn't have anything. The heat in her spine had gone down a little while he talked. Not all the way. Enough that she could take one hand off the doorframe. She did it carefully, the way you'd take your foot off the brake on a hill.

"Can't you just—" she gestured at him, the whole of him "—change back?"

"Tried."

"And?"

"Tried at half four. Tried at five. Tried in your mum's shower at quarter to six, which was a mistake, by the way, there's going to be a plumber." The ears went flat again. "Tried everything I've got. Some of it twice. He's not having it. He's up, and he's staying up, and he's not taking calls."

"So you're stuck."

"I'm stuck."

"Till when?"

"Your guess," he said, "is exactly as good as mine."

The coffee machine gave a long, dreadful gurgle, like a sink draining.

He turned round and took two mugs down from the cupboard, Mom's blue one with the chip and the Thriftway one with the salmon on it, and poured the coffee. He did it neatly. He held the pot in one forepaw, the thick dark pads of it closed round the handle, the claws drawn back in out of the way, and didn't spill a drop. He put the salmon mug down on the end of the table nearest to her and stepped back from it. All the way back, to the counter by the sink, as far across the kitchen as he could get without going out the back door. Then he leaned against the counter with his own mug in his forepaw and his tail hanging down behind him, and waited.

He'd left her the whole kitchen. She saw that. She hated that she saw it.

She let go of the doorframe and came in and sat down at the table, in the chair at the corner, and wrapped both hands round the mug. Her hands were shaking. She held the mug tighter until they stopped.

She drank some of the coffee.

"This is terrible," she said.

"I know."

"How do you even make coffee this bad?"

"Never made coffee in my life. I'm English. We've got kettles." He drank some of his. He drank it out of the side of his muzzle, tipping his head, and some of it went down the fur of his chin, and he wiped it off with the back of his forepaw. "Your mum's got no tea. Well. She's got the bear."

"Sleepytime."

"I'm not drinking anything with a bear on the box."

Leah drank more of the terrible coffee. It was something to do with her mouth.

"Where's Mom?" she said.

"Gone to Billy's."

"She *saw* you?"

"She came down at half six. I was sat there." He pointed his muzzle at the chair at the end of the table, Dad's chair, the one he'd sat in at dinner. "In this. With the blanket. She stood in the door for a bit. Then she said *Morning*, and made her own coffee, and stood there and drank it, looking at me, and then she wrote you a note and went."

He tipped his head at the fridge.

There was a note on the fridge. Leah hadn't seen it. It was stuck up under the salmon-shaped magnet Seth had made in fourth grade, on the back of an envelope, in her mother's square, neat handwriting:

*Council at Billy's, 10 o'clock.*
*Feed him. Bring him.*
*Be nice.*
*—Mom*

And under that, in smaller letters, squeezed in at the bottom:

*P.S. Leah. Be NICE.*

Leah read it twice.

"She saw you like this," she said, "and *left*."

"Said she'd better get over there early and warn them." He drank his coffee. "Then she stood in the door with her coat on for a bit, and said actually, she wouldn't warn them, she wanted to see Billy's face."

Leah put the mug down on the table, very hard.

She didn't know what to do with it. It came up in her chest like a bubble, and she didn't know what it was, and she pushed it down. Her mother. Her mother, who hadn't laughed at anything in two years, standing in this doorway in her coat at half past six in the morning looking at a wolf taller than the kitchen door in a vampire's blanket, and deciding not to warn anybody.

"What *are* you," Leah said. "Seriously."

"Late." He looked at the clock over the stove. "In about an hour and three quarters."

"That's not—"

"Big one," he said, "is about thirteen feet long. Four legs. Ton and a quarter, give or take. That's the one the moon brings up. Wouldn't fit in your front room; your mum's floor would've ended up in the crawlspace with me on top of it, and you three on top of that." The ears tilted. "So I got hold of it halfway up and pointed it at this one instead. This one fits." He looked up at the ceiling an inch above his ear tips. "Mostly."

"There's a *bigger* one."

"There's a bigger one."

She looked at him. At the tail. At the claws, drawn in, on the forepaw wrapped round Mom's blue mug.

"So you are a werewolf."

"No."

"You *literally* turn into a wolf at the full moon."

"So do you," he said. "Bit more often, from what I hear."

She opened her mouth. Shut it.

"I keep my head," he said. "Every time. Whatever shape. I know who I am and what day it is and who's stood in the room with me. I've never bitten anybody who didn't have it coming." He drank his coffee. "That's what I am. That's what you're getting, before ten o'clock."

She sat there and looked at him across the kitchen, and the heat came up her spine again, slower this time, and settled in her chest round the place the line went in, and sat there. Her leg was bouncing under the table. She made it stop. It started again. She realised, after a while, that she'd been leaning. Forward, over the table, toward the counter. She'd been doing it the whole time she was talking, without knowing it, an inch at a time.

She sat back hard.

Across the kitchen, behind him, the tail moved.

It swung. Once. A slow, heavy sway from one side to the other and back, the blanket shifting with it, and then it went still again so fast she might have imagined it.

She hadn't imagined it.

"Your tail," Leah said.

"Leave it."

"It wagged."

"It did not."

"It *wagged*. I saw it. Just now. When I—" she stopped.

He looked at her. The ears had gone back, just a little. The tail hung behind him like something nailed there.

"He's pleased you came down," he said finally. "I'm not responsible for him. I'd like that on record."

"*He's* pleased."

"He's very pleased. He's been sat up waiting since you went to bed." The tip of the tail twitched. He looked round at it with an expression of deep personal betrayal. "As you can see."

The fourth step creaked.

It creaked under a lot more weight than Leah's, and fast, and then Seth came down the rest of the stairs three at a time in a pair of boxers and one sock, yelling, "Mom, did you hear the—something broke, last night, I think the couch—" and came round the kitchen doorway, and stopped.

He stopped so suddenly he had to grab the doorframe exactly where Leah had grabbed it.

He looked at the wolf by the sink.

The wolf looked at him.

"Morning, Seth," said the wolf.

Seth's mouth opened. It stayed open. Leah watched his face go through about nine things in a second, and arrive at the last one, and stay there, and it was pure, total, incandescent joy.

"*No way,*" Seth said.

"Way, apparently."

"No *WAY*. Dude. *Dude.* Is that—are you—you're a—" He came into the kitchen. He came into it the way he'd come into Christmas morning when he was six, sideways, staring, like the tree might disappear if he looked away. "You're a *wolf*. You're a wolf on *two legs*. You've got *ears*, you've got a—is that one of Esme's blankets?"

"Yes."

"Why are you wearing one of Esme's—"

"Because the alternative was nothing, Seth."

"Oh," said Seth. "Right. Okay." He was right up close now, a couple of feet away, staring up at the muzzle, which was nearly a foot above his head. "Can I touch your ears?"

"No."

"Can I just—"

"No."

Seth's hand came up anyway. It went toward the tail, which was nearer, and hanging right there, black and thick and—

Leah growled.

It came out of her before she knew it was in her. It came up out of her chest and through her teeth, a low, rough, ugly sound, the sound she made when she was a wolf and somebody went near her food. She'd never in her life made that sound with a human throat. She hadn't known she could.

Seth's hand stopped in mid-air.

The whole kitchen stopped.

Seth turned his head very slowly and looked at his sister. By the sink, the wolf's ears had come straight up and swung round toward her, both of them, like radar dishes, and he was looking at her over Seth's head, and his eyes had gone very still.

Leah put her hand over her mouth.

She sat there at the kitchen table with her hand over her mouth and her face going hot, and nobody said anything for a long time.

"Okay," said Seth, at last, very carefully. He lowered his hand, very slowly, and put it behind his back. "Okay. Not touching. Nobody's touching anything."

"Leah," said the wolf.

"*Don't.*"

He didn't. He looked at her a moment longer. Then he looked away, out of the window at the rain, and drank his coffee, and the tail behind him swung once more, slow and heavy, from one side to the other.

"*Stop that,*" said Leah, through her fingers.

"Not me," he said. "Told you."

Something cracked in the middle of the kitchen, and all three of them jumped.

Kreacher was standing on the linoleum between the table and the stove with a bundle of clothes in his arms that was bigger than he was.

He looked up at his master. He looked at the ears, and the muzzle, and the fur, and the blue satin-edged blanket, and the tail. He didn't seem very surprised by any of it. He looked like a man who'd come home and found the dog on the sofa again.

"Master is early," Kreacher said.

"Master knows."

"Master is wearing the bloodsucker's blanket."

"It was that or nothing, Kreacher."

Kreacher sniffed. Then he turned his head and looked at Leah, sitting at the table with her hand still pressed over her mouth and her face red to the ears, and his own ears came slowly up, and something in his face changed that Leah didn't like at all.

"Ah," said Kreacher.

"Don't," said the wolf.

"Kreacher said nothing."

"You said *ah*."

"Kreacher," said Kreacher, "is permitted to say *ah*." He bowed to Leah, a short one, from the waist. "Good morning, Mistress."

"Don't call me that," said Leah, through her fingers.

Kreacher ignored her. He put the bundle of clothes down on the table. Then he went to the counter, climbed up onto the step stool still standing where Mom had left it, and looked into the coffee pot. He sniffed it. He looked up at his master with an expression of real pain.

"Master made coffee," he said.

"I did."

Kreacher took the pot. He poured it down the sink, all of it, and stood there watching it go down the drain. Then he snapped his long fingers, and the coffee can came down off the shelf and opened itself, and the old filter flew out of the machine and into the trash, and a new one flew in, and Leah had to put her mug down because the whole kitchen was moving round her.

Two minutes later, Kreacher put a fresh mug down in front of her.

She drank it.

It was the best cup of coffee she'd ever had in her life. It was dark and smooth and rich and not bitter at all, and it tasted like it had come out of a different can from a different country, and she looked at the mug in her hands, and then at Kreacher on his step stool, and then at the wolf.

"Why didn't you just let *him* make it?" she said.

The wolf looked at his forepaw, the claws, the black pads.

"Wanted something to do with my paws," he said.

---

Kreacher had brought clothes.

He'd brought them for the middle shape, which, he informed Leah, while the wolf was upstairs in the bathroom ducking under the door frame, Master took perhaps twice a year and always without any warning to Kreacher. A pair of trousers made of heavy brown canvas, cut long in the leg and wide, with a slot let in at the back for the tail and the edges of it sewn neatly round with a double row of stitches. And a jumper.

The wolf came downstairs in them, ducking under the kitchen doorway with his ears laid flat.

The jumper was hand-knitted. It was emerald green, a deep bright green almost exactly the colour of his eyes, in a thick, slightly lumpy wool, and it was enormous, and it fitted him, and there was a big gold letter *H* knitted into the middle of the chest.

Seth made a small noise and sat down on the floor.

"Molly," said the wolf, before anybody could ask. "My—a friend's mum. Knits me one every Christmas. For every shape. Don't."

"I didn't say anything," said Leah.

"You were going to."

She had been going to. She drank her coffee instead.

He went out to the hall. Leah heard him take something off a hook. He came back with his leather jacket in his forepaw, which was the size of a toddler's coat against him now, and took a little square silver mirror out of the inside pocket, and hung the jacket back over a chair. He sat down in Dad's chair at the end of the table. It creaked under him. He opened the mirror, and tapped the glass twice with the tip of one claw.

"Sabine."

The glass fogged. It cleared. Leah, from the corner of the table, could see a face in it, small and dark-haired, with a lamp behind her and a window, and through the window, a long way off, lights on water.

"Potter," said the woman in the mirror. "It is six o'clock in the evening. I have been at this desk since four o'clock yesterday morning. If you are calling to tell me about the forms—"

She stopped.

There was a long silence from the mirror.

"It itches," said the wolf.

The silence went on.

"You said if it itches you wanted an owl," he said. "I haven't got an owl. You'll have to make do."

"Potter," said the woman in the mirror, very quietly. "It is Wednesday."

"I'm aware it's Wednesday."

"It is *Wednesday*. The moon is—"

"Tomorrow. I know. Tell him."

Another silence. Leah heard something set down hard on a desk, a long way away.

"When?" said the woman.

"Ten past four. Local."

"Can you—"

"No."

"Have you tried—"

"Yes."

"All of them?"

"Twice."

"*Mon Dieu,*" said the woman in the mirror, and then a great deal more, very fast, in French, none of which Leah understood and all of which she got the general idea of. The wolf held the mirror and waited for it to finish, with his ears tilted, looking patient.

"And the girl?" the woman said finally. "Mademoiselle Clearwater?"

The wolf turned the mirror a little, so that it was pointed down the table toward Leah.

Leah looked into it. A small, tired, dark-eyed face looked back at her. Leah had seen it in the field, beside the ash, with a clipboard. It looked her over, briefly and thoroughly, the way a nurse looks at somebody in a waiting room.

"Bonjour, mademoiselle," said the woman. "Are you all right?"

"I'm drinking his coffee," said Leah. "It's terrible."

The woman in the mirror looked at her a moment longer.

"Bon," she said. "Then she is all right." The mirror turned back to the wolf. "The council?"

"Ten o'clock."

"You are going like *that*."

"Unless you'd like to come and explain why I'm not."

Another silence. Then a long sigh, crackling and tinny through the glass.

"Go," said the woman. "Be nice. Take the book. Do not eat anybody. And Potter—"

"What?"

"If it does anything else," said the woman, "*anything*—"

"You'll get a mirror."

"I will get nothing, and on Friday I will read about it in the *Prophet*," said the woman, and the glass fogged and went blank.

The wolf closed the mirror and sat there looking at it for a while. Then he put it back in the jacket on the chair, and looked up at the clock over the stove.

Twenty to ten.

---

He had to duck to get out the front door.

He did it without thinking, the ears going flat and the head going down and the whole tall black length of him bending under the lintel, the tail coming through after him with an inch to spare, and he stepped out onto the porch into the rain and straightened up, and stood there.

Leah came out behind him and pulled the door shut.

It was raining the way it always rained. Grey, steady, slanting in off the sea. The road in front of the house was black and shining, with puddles in the potholes, and the houses on the far side were grey and green and brown, with smoke going up from the chimneys and lights on in the kitchens. Somebody's radio was playing, two doors down. Down the hill, past the end of the road, the sea.

Across the street, old Mrs. Tallie was on her porch in her housecoat with a mug in her hand, the way she was every morning of the year. She'd stopped with the mug halfway to her mouth.

At the bottom of the Clearwaters' drive, the Littleseas' big yellow dog had been nosing in the ditch. It had lifted its head when the door opened. Now it lay down. It lay all the way down on its belly in the wet gravel, with its ears flat against its skull and its chin on its paws, and didn't move.

The wolf stood on the porch in the rain in his canvas trousers and his green jumper, and looked at the street.

The rain came down on him. It came down on his ears and his muzzle and the shoulders of the jumper and the thick black fur of his forepaws, and where it hit him, it steamed. A thin grey haze went up off him into the morning, off the top of his head and his shoulders and the tip of his tail, the way it went up off Seth. Off Jacob. Off her.

Leah stood beside him and looked at it.

She thought about the field. About standing at the treeline yesterday morning with her claws in the frozen dirt, working out which of the bloodsuckers she'd go for first. About the crack in the middle of the snow, and one look, and the whole world letting go of her. About the treeline she couldn't walk past, and the blanket, and the book with the page torn out, and *the parties are on no account to be separated*, and *I'll wish it*, every day, for the rest of my life.

She'd meant it. She still meant it.

She pulled her hood up.

"Which way?" the wolf said.

He didn't know. Of course he didn't. He'd never been to Billy's house. He didn't know La Push from a hole in the ground; he'd come here yesterday by following a string through the woods to her mother's back yard, and that was the only road in the whole place he knew.

Leah went down the porch steps into the rain.

"Follow the string," she said.

She started walking. She didn't look back to see if he was coming. She didn't need to; she could feel him, the whole tall heavy length of him, come down the steps behind her on his broad flat footpaws without a sound and fall in at her shoulder, a pace behind, and the line between them went slack and easy in her chest, and sat there, and didn't pull at all.

Across the street, old Mrs. Tallie put her mug down very carefully on the porch rail.

At the bottom of the drive, the Littleseas' yellow dog watched them go past with its chin on the gravel.

And behind her, where she wasn't looking, Leah heard it: the soft, heavy swish of a tail, going once from one side to the other, in the rain.
