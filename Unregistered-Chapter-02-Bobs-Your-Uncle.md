# Bob's Your Uncle

---

He followed her.

Leah didn't need to look back to know it. She could feel exactly where he was the way she could feel where her own tail was without turning her head: behind her and a little to her left, forty yards back, coming through the firs at the same pace she was. The line ran out of the middle of her chest and back through the trees to him, and it didn't pull. It just sat there, the way a leash sits when the dog and the person holding it happen to be going the same direction.

She hated that it felt like that. She hated that she'd thought of the word *leash.*

*He's coming,* Seth said. He was bounding along on her right, his sandy bulk crashing through the low salal, snow showering off the branches he clipped. *Leah. He's coming. He's actually coming. Is he going to walk? It's like twenty miles. Is he going to walk twenty miles? Should we slow down? Should I go back and show him the—*

*No.*

*But he doesn't know the way—*

*Then he can get lost.*

From a long way east, faint and getting fainter, Jacob's thoughts came through the pack-mind like a radio station fading in and out between hills. He was loping along beside a blur of cold bodies on the far side of the Cullens' river, and Leah got flashes of it whether she wanted them or not: Bella's back, the kid asleep against Bella's shoulder with one fist still knotted in the collar of her mother's sweater, Rosalie carrying the backpack, the white house coming up through the trees like something out of a magazine.

*He won't get lost,* Jacob said. *He'll just follow the—*

He stopped.

*Say it,* Leah said. *Go on. Say it.*

*The line,* Jacob said, unhappily.

She ran faster.

She didn't decide to. Her body did it, the way it did most things these days, and she let it, because running was the one thing in the whole stupid world she was still better at than anybody. Her stride opened out. The snow came up under her paws in hard white bursts and the trees started to go by in long grey streaks, and she put her ears back and leaned into it, and Seth gave a startled yelp and fell in behind her, scrambling.

*Leah—*

She went faster.

She could feel it through the line. She didn't want to, but she could. He'd noticed. He'd taken his hands out of his pockets. And because Seth couldn't stand not knowing anything for more than four seconds at a time, Seth looked back over his shoulder, and Leah saw it through his eyes.

The English guy was running.

He ran like somebody running for a bus he knew he'd catch. Long strides, elbows in, head up, the brown leather jacket flapping open behind him. A cedar lay across the trail ahead of him, a big one, blown down in some storm years back and furred over with moss and snow, and he went over it without breaking stride, one palm flat on the trunk and his boots swinging over and landing on the far side. Seth could hear a vole shift under two feet of snow at thirty yards. Seth hadn't heard the boots land.

*He's not even breathing hard,* Seth said. *Leah. He's on* two legs.

*I can see, Seth. You're showing me.*

*Sorry.*

He wasn't sorry. She could feel how not sorry he was. It was coming off him in bright excited bursts, like a puppy that had just been shown a tennis ball.

She went faster again.

This was as fast as she went. She knew it to the inch. She'd raced every one of them, back when she was still in Sam's pack and wanted to rub it in their faces: Paul and Jared and Embry and Quil, even Sam. Even Jacob, when he wasn't Alpha and couldn't make it an order. She'd beaten all of them. The trees stopped being trees and turned into a smear, grey and white and black, and the cold air tore at her ears and the world narrowed down to the ground coming at her and the next place to put her feet, and Seth's thoughts went thin and breathless and fell away behind her.

*Leah! Leah, wait up, I can't—*

The line didn't stretch.

That was the thing. That was what she'd wanted to know, and now she knew it. It didn't stretch. She ran as hard as she'd ever run in her life and the line stayed exactly the same length behind her: forty yards. No more. No less. She didn't have to look back. She could feel the far end of it coming on through the trees at precisely the speed she was going, as steady as if she'd been towing him.

She stopped.

She did it at the bank of a creek, all four feet planted, and slid three yards on the snow and spun round with her hackles up and her lips already coming back off her teeth.

He stopped too.

He stopped ten yards off on the trail behind her, the way somebody stops when the person in front of them on the sidewalk suddenly stands still. Snow was melting off the toes of his boots. His chest wasn't heaving. His breath wasn't fogging, either, the way hers was, a big white cloud of it rolling out between her teeth; nothing came out of him at all.

He looked at her.

"Problem?" he said.

She snarled.

"Right," he said. "Can't talk like that, can you. Forgot." He glanced back down the trail. Seth was coming, crashing through the salal with his tongue hanging out the side of his mouth and his ears flopping, and he skidded to a halt behind the English guy and stood there panting. "One woof for yes."

Leah didn't woof.

Seth woofed.

"Not you, mate," the English guy said, without turning round.

Seth's tail went round in a circle anyway.

The English guy put his hands back in his jacket pockets. He looked at Leah across the snow, and then past her, at the creek. It was running high and fast and the colour of strong tea, with shelves of grey ice along both banks and big round stones sticking up out of the middle of it with snow on their tops like little hats.

"I'm not trying to keep up with you," he said, "if that's what this is. I'm following. I don't know where your mum lives. I could Apparate, but I've never been, and I'd rather not land in somebody's septic tank." He tipped his head toward the water. "So. Lead on. Or don't, and we'll stand here. I've got all week."

She stared at him.

"Literally," he said. "I've been posted."

*He has,* Seth said. *He's been posted. Leah, that lady said, I heard her—*

*I know what she said. I was sitting right there.*

She turned her back on him. It was the only thing she could think of to do. She turned her back and went down the bank and into the creek, and the cold came up her legs like knives and she didn't feel it, not really, not the way a human would have. The current shoved at her. She leaned into it and crossed in six big splashing bounds and came up the far bank shaking herself, water flying off her in a spray.

Behind her, he waded in.

She'd half expected him to jump it, or do something with his hands, or walk across the top of the water, after this morning. He walked into it, up to his knees and then his thighs, his jeans going black with the wet, and walked straight across with his hands still in his pockets, the current piling up against him in a brown bulge and parting round him like he was another one of the stones. He came up out of it onto the bank beside her.

His jeans were steaming.

Seth came out of the water behind him and saw it and nearly fell over. *He's* steaming.

*We all steam, Seth.*

*Not like that.*

He was right. Not like that. Leah watched the steam come off the soaked denim in thick white curls, the way it came off a horse in winter, and then the wet patches started to shrink. She could see them doing it. The black went grey at the edges and crept down toward his knees and then it was just a damp patch and then it was nothing, and he was standing on the bank in dry jeans looking at her.

"Well?" he said.

She went.

She didn't run flat out again. There wasn't any point. She went at a long easy lope, the pace she could hold all day, and Seth fell in beside her, and behind them she felt the line come along at forty yards like it had never been anywhere else.

*Where are you guys?* Jacob said, coming back into range, clearer now. He'd stopped moving. Leah got the edge of a porch, the white boards, a big glass wall with the grey river beyond it. *You sound like you're in a washing machine.*

*Crossed the creek,* Seth said. *The one past the old logging road. Jake, he walked* through *it. And then his pants dried. By* themselves. *And he ran the whole way behind us on two legs, he jumped a whole tree, he didn't even—*

*Seth.*

*Sorry.*

*Leah.* Jacob's voice changed. Careful. The way you'd talk to somebody standing on a roof. *You okay?*

*Fine.*

*Leah—*

*I said I'm fine. Don't do that. Don't do the voice.*

*I'm not doing the voice.*

*You're doing the voice. You did it to Bella for a year. You've got a whole special voice for people you think are going to jump off something.* She could feel him wince. Good. *What do you want?*

He let it go. That was the only good thing about Jacob as an Alpha: he knew when to let things go, mostly, which was more than Sam had ever known. *Patrols,* he said instead. *Tonight. All those red-eyes that ran off this morning, the witnesses. Thirty, forty of them. They went every direction.* She saw it in his head the way he'd seen it: a burst of blurred bodies scattering into the trees like quail out of a hedge. *Some of them are gonna be thirsty. Some of them are gonna be thirsty and scared and stupid. Sam's already got his guys out on the north side, Embry says—*

*You talked to Embry?*

*Saw him at the edge of the field before they went. He was human. He said Sam wants the north and east, round Forks. We take the south and the coast, midnight to six. Me and Seth.*

*And me.*

*You're off.*

*I'm not off.*

*You're off, Leah. You've been on since Sunday. You haven't slept. I can feel how much you haven't slept.* A pause. *And you've got a guest.*

She didn't answer that.

*He's gonna be at your house,* Jacob said. *Tonight. On your couch.*

*I know where the couch is, Jacob.*

*That's not what—* He stopped. Started again. *Sam'll have gone straight to my dad's. You know that, right? Soon as they got back. He'll have phased out and gone straight up the road and told him everything.*

Leah's stride hitched.

She hadn't thought about it. She'd thought about everything else. She'd thought about the line, and the stupid book, and the torn page, and the word in the margin, and her tail going down in front of two packs and two dozen bloodsuckers and Emmett Cullen. She'd thought about the treacle tart, God help her. But she hadn't thought about this, and now it was right there in front of her, so obvious she couldn't believe she'd missed it.

Sam went to Billy. Billy called Mom.

Billy had been sitting by the phone since sunup. Sam had told them that in the field. And her mom would have been sitting by her own phone in her own kitchen, since before that, with the coffee going cold in her cup and the radio on low so she wouldn't have to listen to the house being quiet. Waiting to find out if her kids were alive.

And the phone would have rung, and it would have been Billy, and Billy would have said, *They're alive, Sue. Both of them. Everybody.* And her mom would have sat down, probably, right there on the kitchen floor, because that was what her mom did when her legs stopped working, she didn't make a fuss, she just sat down wherever she was.

And then Billy would have said, *Sue. There's something else. It's Leah.*

And Billy would have known what to say next because Sam had told him.

Sam.

Sam, who had stood in the middle of his pack this morning and watched it happen to her, and watched her stare across the snow at a stranger the exact way he'd once stared across a kitchen at her cousin. Sam, who knew exactly what it was because he'd done it to her. Sam had walked up the road to Billy Black's little red house and sat down at his kitchen table and told him.

And Billy had picked up the phone and told her mom.

*Leah,* Jacob said quietly.

*Shut up.*

*I'm sorry. I should've—I don't know. I should've thought.*

*Shut up, Jacob. Please.*

He did. For a while there was nothing in the pack-mind but the three of them breathing. Seth's paws on the snow. Her own. Jacob standing on the Cullens' porch with the big glass wall behind him and the kid asleep inside on the sofa where Leah could see her through his eyes, curled up small with somebody's coat over her, one bronze curl stuck to her cheek.

*Maybe Sam didn't tell him everything,* Seth tried. *Maybe he just said the Volturi were dead and the wizard guy and—*

*Sam saw my tail go down, Seth.*

Seth didn't say anything to that.

*He saw it go down in front of everybody.* She heard her own thoughts go thin and hard. *Everybody saw. Paul saw. Paul was* laughing. *You think Sam left that out? You think Paul's not going to tell the whole rez by dinner?*

*Paul's an asshole,* Seth said loyally.

*Paul's an asshole who tells everybody everything.*

Jacob shifted his weight on the porch. Somebody had come out behind him. Leah got the smell of it through his nose, cold and sweet: Bella. *I gotta go human,* Jacob said. *Nessie's gonna wake up and want me, and Carlisle's got about a hundred questions about this Registry thing, and Bella—* He stopped. *Bella's kind of a mess.* A pause. *Leah. Midnight. Me and Seth. You stay in. Okay? Get some sleep.*

*Is that an order?*

He didn't answer for a second.

*No,* he said. *It's me asking.*

And then he was gone, folding up out of the pack-mind all at once the way they always went, like somebody stepping out of a room and pulling the door shut behind them. It got quieter. Just her and Seth now. Just two wolves running through the snow with the line paying out behind them, forty yards back, steady as a heartbeat.

The trees started to change.

She felt it before she saw it. The snow got wetter under her feet. It started to clump and drag between her toes, heavy and grey, and then it started to thin out altogether, and there were patches where the ground showed through, black earth and dead brown ferns and moss so green it hurt. The air changed. It got softer, and wetter, and the smell of it changed. Under the pine and the cold there was salt now, and kelp, and the low grey mineral smell of a beach at low tide, and Leah breathed it in and something in her chest that had been clenched since before dawn let go a quarter of an inch.

The sea. They were getting close.

It started to rain. Not much. A fine cold drizzle that came down through the canopy and beaded on her coat and ran off, the way it rained at La Push about three hundred days a year. The snow gave up completely. By the time they came down the last long slope toward the highway the ground was all mud and needles, and the drizzle had settled in for the afternoon.

*Leah,* Seth said.

*What.*

*Can I say something?*

*No.*

*It's not about him.*

She didn't answer, which Seth had always, his whole life, taken to mean yes.

*Dad would've liked him,* Seth said.

The world went white at the edges for a second.

*Dad liked everybody,* she said. It came out flat. She put everything she had into making it come out flat.

*Not everybody. He didn't like Charlie's cousin from Spokane.*

*Nobody liked Charlie's cousin from Spokane.*

*He'd have liked the jacket,* Seth said. *And the Lassie thing. He'd have laughed so hard at the Lassie thing. You know how he did, with the—* And Seth's head was suddenly full of it, the picture and the sound together, their dad at the kitchen table with his head thrown back and his big hands flat on the table, laughing so hard at something on the TV that he couldn't breathe, wheezing, slapping the table, tears running down his face, Mom saying *Harry, Harry, you'll give yourself a hernia*—

*Seth.* It came out of her hard enough to make him flinch. *Stop it.*

The picture went away.

*Sorry,* Seth said, very small. *Sorry. I didn't mean—I just. His name. I keep thinking about it.*

*So do I.*

She hadn't meant to say that. It was out before she could stop it, and then it was in Seth's head, and there was nothing she could do about it.

He didn't say anything else. He came closer, though, as they went down the slope through the drizzle, until his shoulder was almost brushing hers, and stayed there.

They came out of the trees above the highway.

It was just a road. Two lanes of wet black asphalt with a yellow line down the middle and a guardrail on the far side, running from Forks down to the coast. A logging truck went past while they waited in the trees, hissing on the wet road, throwing up a long dirty spray, its headlights on even though it was barely two in the afternoon. Leah watched it go. Behind her, she felt him stop at the edge of the trees.

Then she felt him keep going.

She turned her head. He'd walked straight out of the trees and down the bank onto the shoulder of the road, with his hands in his pockets and the drizzle starting to darken the shoulders of his jacket, and he was standing there on the gravel looking left and right like a kid at a crosswalk. A pickup came round the bend from the Forks end. An old Ford, dark green, with a canoe strapped on top. The driver was an old guy in a Mariners cap, and Leah knew him, sort of, one of the Hoh River guys who came up to La Push to sell smoked salmon in the summer. He slowed down a little as he passed and looked out of his window at the man standing on the shoulder in a leather jacket in the rain.

The English guy lifted one hand. Just two fingers off his pocket.

The old guy lifted two fingers off the steering wheel, the way everybody out here did, and drove on.

Then the English guy looked up the bank at the trees where Leah and Seth were standing hidden in the shadows, and raised his eyebrows, like, *Well? Are we going or not?*

*Oh my God,* Seth said, delighted. *He waved at Mr. Hoskins.*

Leah came down out of the trees and crossed the road in two bounds. She heard Seth come after her. She went up into the woods on the other side without looking at the English guy, and she felt the line come with her, and then she felt him jog across the road behind them and come up the bank, and it settled back into place at forty yards.

It wasn't far after that. A mile, a little more, through second-growth spruce with the rain coming down through it and the sound of the surf getting louder all the time, the long slow boom and drag of it on the rocks at First Beach. Then the trees started to thin out, and there were backyards. Woodpiles under tarps. A swing set with one swing. Somebody's old Bronco up on blocks with moss growing on the hood. Leah knew every one of them. She'd been walking through these backyards since she was three.

She stopped behind the big cedar at the bottom of her mom's yard.

From there she could see the house. It was the same as it always was. Grey shingles gone dark in the rain. The back porch with the sagging step that her dad had been going to fix for six years. The woodpile under its blue tarp. The kitchen window with the light on, a yellow square in the grey afternoon, and the faded curtains with the little blue fish on them that her mom had sewn the year Leah was born.

Her mom was standing on the back step.

She had the cordless phone in one hand, held down at her side, the way you'd hold it if you'd been holding it for hours and had forgotten it was there. She had her old grey cardigan on, the one with the elbows going, and her hair was pulled back the way she wore it when she was cooking. She wasn't cooking. She was standing on the step in the drizzle looking out at the trees at the bottom of the yard.

She was looking right at the cedar.

Leah's mom wasn't a wolf. She'd never phased in her life and never would. She couldn't hear a heartbeat across a room, or smell what somebody'd had for breakfast, or see a squirrel move in the dark at a hundred yards. But she'd raised two of them, and she'd been married to a man who'd sat on the council his whole life and known every one of the old stories, and she'd lived on the rez her whole life. She knew where her kids came out of the woods.

Leah stood behind the cedar with the rain running off her coat.

*Leah,* Seth said.

*I know.*

She went behind the cedar to phase.

The heat came up her spine and her bones folded in, and then she was on two legs on the wet needles with the rain on her bare shoulders, and the pack-mind slammed shut. The quiet came down. Seth was gone. Her own heart, her own breath, the rain on the cedar boughs, the surf. And behind her, forty yards back in the trees and not coming any closer, him.

She untied the bundle from her ankle and dressed with her back to the yard. The cut-offs. The tank top with the hole under the arm. They were damp from the snow and the creek and they stuck to her, and she yanked them into place.

"Lovely tree, this," said a voice behind her, a good way off.

She turned her head.

He was standing in the trees at the far end of the line with his back to her, looking with every sign of deep interest at the trunk of a Sitka spruce.

"Very straight," he said to the spruce. "Good bark. Lot of moss. I'll just be over here, then."

Leah looked at the back of his head for a long moment.

Then Seth came out from behind a stump on two legs, hopping on one foot and dragging his cut-offs up with both hands, and said "Oh, hey, *sorry,*" to the back of the English guy's head, and the English guy said "No bother" to the tree, and Leah turned round and walked up the yard toward her mother before she could think about any of it.

The grass was long and wet and cold under her bare feet. Her mom watched her come the whole way. She didn't move off the step. She just stood there with the phone hanging from her hand, and Leah watched her face, and her mom's face didn't do anything at all. That was the worst part. Her mom had a face like that, when she needed one. She'd had it at the funeral. She'd had it every day for a month afterward.

Leah stopped at the bottom of the porch step.

"Mom," she said.

"Leah."

Leah opened her mouth. Nothing came out. She'd had twenty miles to think of something to say, and she'd spent all twenty of them thinking about Sam, and now there was nothing.

Her mom looked at her. Then she put the phone down on the porch rail, very carefully, the way you'd put down something that might go off. She came down the one step. She put her hand up, the way she'd done when Leah was little and had a fever, and laid it flat against the side of Leah's face.

Her mom's hand was cold. Everybody's was, to Leah, now. It was cold and it smelled like coffee and dish soap and the lotion she put on her hands at night, and it was small, much smaller than Leah's had got, and Leah stood there in the rain and let it stay there. One second. Two. Three.

"I need a shower," she said.

Her mom took her hand away. "Go on."

Leah went past her. She went up the step, and across the porch, and in through the back door into the kitchen, which smelled like old coffee and the woodstove and the vinegar her mom cleaned the counters with. The radio was on low on top of the fridge. Some guy on the Port Angeles station talking about the weather. The coffee maker was still on, the pot burnt down to a black ring at the bottom.

She was halfway up the stairs when she heard it.

She was meant to be out of earshot by then. She would have been, two years ago. But she wasn't anything she'd been two years ago, and the walls of her mom's house were thin, and she heard it from the yard as clearly as if she'd been standing on the step: her mother's voice, level and quiet and a little rough at the edges, the way it went when she'd been up since four.

"You'd be Mr. Potter."

Leah went up the rest of the stairs two at a time so she wouldn't hear the answer.

She heard it anyway.

"Potter's fine," he said.

Sue Clearwater had sat down on the kitchen floor at ten past one.

She hadn't meant to. She'd been standing at the counter with the phone in her hand when it rang, the way she'd been standing at the counter with the phone in her hand on and off since half past four that morning, and she'd answered it on the first ring, and Billy had said, *They're alive, Sue. Both of them. All of them,* and the next thing she knew she was on the linoleum with her back against the cupboard under the sink and her knees up, and the phone still pressed to her ear, and Billy was saying her name.

She'd stayed down there for the rest of it. It had seemed easier.

*It's over,* Billy had said. His voice was strange, high and fast, the way it had gone the night Jacob first phased. *They're dead. The Italians. All of them, every one, Sam says it took a second. Less. A man came out of nowhere, Sue. A man. Some kind of—Sam says he said he was a wizard. Like in a storybook. He snapped his fingers and they burned.*

She'd sat on the floor and listened, and she'd understood maybe one word in three, and none of it had mattered, because both of them were alive.

Then Billy had stopped talking, and the line had gone quiet for long enough that she'd thought it had cut out, and then he'd said, *Sue. There's something else.*

She'd known before he said it that it was Leah. She'd known the way she'd known, two years ago, standing in the hospital corridor in Forks with a vending-machine coffee going cold in her hand, a full minute before the doctor came round the corner with his face arranged.

*Sam's here,* Billy had said. *He came straight up the road. He's standing in my doorway dripping on the mat and he won't sit down.* A pause. *Sue, she's imprinted.*

Sue had sat on her kitchen floor and closed her eyes.

*On the wizard,* Billy had said. *The one who did it. The Englishman.* And then, slower, the way he'd have walked her across ice: *Sam says it's not like the others. Sam says the old one, the Volturi one, the one who sees—Sam says he said it went deeper than he'd ever seen in all his years. And then the wizard made a noise, some kind of growl, and every wolf on that field put its ears down, Sue. Both packs. Sam too.* Another pause. *And Leah's tail went down.*

Sue hadn't said anything. There hadn't been anything to say.

*There's one more thing,* Billy had said. *His name.*

And then Billy had told her, and there had been a long silence on the line, and Billy had said, very quietly, *I'm sorry, Sue.*

*It's a common name, Billy,* she'd said, and hung up, and sat on the floor for another ten minutes looking at the fridge.

Now she stood on her back step in the rain and watched him come out of the trees at the bottom of the yard.

He came out behind Seth. Seth was dragging his cut-offs up with one hand and running with the other, barefoot through the wet grass, and he hit the bottom of the porch step at full speed and came up it in one jump and threw both arms round her and lifted her clean off her feet. He was soaking. He smelled like snow and creek water and wet dog and, under that, something burnt and sweet that turned her stomach.

"Mom," he said into her hair. "Mom. Mom, you should've *seen*—"

"Put me down, Seth."

"—there was this *crack* and he was just *there*, in the middle, and they all—and then he snapped his fingers, Mom, he *snapped his fingers*—"

"Seth. Down."

He put her down. He kept his hands on her shoulders, grinning at her like a lunatic, and his eyes were wet, and he was fifteen years old and almost a foot taller than her now and still had the same round face he'd had when he was six. She put her hand on his cheek. He leaned into it, the way he always had, like a dog.

"I know, baby," she said. "Billy called."

Over Seth's shoulder, the man had stopped at the edge of the grass.

He'd come out of the trees and stopped where the lawn began, right at the edge of it, with the drizzle coming down on him. He hadn't come any further. He was standing with his hands in the pockets of a brown leather jacket and his weight even on both feet, and he was looking at the house. At the back of it. Up at the second floor, at the bathroom window, where Sue could hear the water had just started running in the pipes.

He was tall. Six two, six three. Black hair, cut short and badly. A couple of days of stubble. No coat, no hat, no gloves, a grey shirt under the jacket with the sleeves shoved up, jeans and heavy boots. The rain was soaking into his shoulders and he didn't seem to have noticed. He was younger than she'd expected. She didn't know what she'd expected. Somebody older. Somebody with a long beard, maybe, like a storybook.

He looked like any of the young men who came through Forks in the summer on their way to the Hoh to go hiking. Like somebody who'd ask you for directions at the gas station.

He looked away from the window, then, and down the yard at her, and she saw his eyes. Green. Very green. Even from there.

"You'd be Mr. Potter," Sue said.

"Potter's fine," he said.

The voice was English. Billy had said that, but hearing it was something else. A low voice, a little rough, not loud. He didn't raise it to carry up the yard, and it carried anyway.

Sue looked at him.

"Billy said your first name is Harry," she said.

He didn't answer right away. He looked at her for a second, across the wet grass, and something in his face moved, very slightly, at the corners of his mouth and eyes.

"Billy's right," he said.

"Leah tell you? About her father?"

"Bit of it."

"Then you know."

"Potter's fine," he said again. "Most people use it anyway."

Seth had gone quiet. He was standing beside her on the step with his hands hanging at his sides, looking from her to the man and back.

Sue had been married to Harry Clearwater for twenty-three years. She had said his name in this kitchen, on this porch, out of that window, up those stairs, she couldn't have counted how many times. *Harry, dinner. Harry, your boots. Harry, the step. Harry, for God's sake.* She'd said it in the hospital corridor, once, out loud, to nobody, when the doctor came round the corner. She'd stopped saying it, after. She'd made herself stop. And then she'd go to say something to him in the kitchen without thinking, and it would come out—*Harry, can you*—and stop there, and she'd stand at the counter with her hand on the edge of it until the feeling went away.

She thought about Leah walking up the yard just now with her face like a door slammed shut, and going past her without a word, and up the stairs.

"Potter," Sue said. "For now."

He nodded once.

"You'd better come up out of the rain," she said. "Seth, go and put a shirt on."

"I don't have a—"

"Then go and find one."

Seth went, looking back over his shoulder all the way through the door.

The man came up the yard. He walked the way he'd stood, unhurried, and when he got to the porch he stopped at the bottom and looked at the step. The sagging one. The one Harry had been going to fix for six years, which gave under your weight and creaked like a gunshot if you trod on the middle of it, and which Sue had stopped seeing a long time ago.

He looked at it, and then he stepped over it, and came up onto the porch without a sound.

He wiped his boots on the mat. He did it without being asked, both of them, twice each, and then he stood there in front of her on the porch with the rain running off the eaves behind him.

Up close he was very tall. She had to tip her head back. Up close he gave off heat, the way her kids did now, the way all of them did; she could feel it on her face from a yard away, like standing in front of an open oven. Up close he smelled like the woods. Pine, and earth, and wet stone. And under that, very faint, the burnt sweet smell that had been on Seth.

He saw her catch it. She watched him see it.

"Sorry," he said. "It gets in the jacket."

"Is that them?"

"That's them."

Sue looked at him for a moment longer. Then she turned round and opened the back door.

"Come in," she said. "I'll make coffee."

---

The kitchen looked smaller with him in it.

That was the first thing she noticed. It was a small kitchen anyway, it always had been, the table pushed up under the window and the fridge humming in the corner and the woodstove in the alcove where the pantry used to be, and four people in it at once had always been a squeeze. With him standing in the middle of the linoleum with his hands in his pockets it felt like a kitchen built for children. His head wasn't that far off the light fixture.

He looked around. She watched him do it. He looked at the radio on top of the fridge, which was still going on low, the weatherman from Port Angeles promising more of the same through Thursday. He looked at the curtains with the fish on them. He looked at the calendar from the tire shop in Forks, still on December because nobody had remembered to turn it over. He looked at the fridge, and the photographs stuck all over the front of it with magnets shaped like fruit, and his eyes stopped on one of them and stayed there.

She knew which one without looking. It was the one in the middle. Harry and Charlie Swan on the end of the dock at the marina, four summers ago, each of them holding up one end of a king salmon nearly as long as Seth was tall, and both of them grinning like idiots. Harry had his cap on backward. Charlie had a sunburn that had taken a week to fade.

The man looked at it for a while. He didn't say anything.

Sue went to the coffee maker. The pot had burnt down to a black tarry ring at the bottom, hours ago. She'd forgotten it was on. She took it out and ran it under the tap, and the hot glass hissed and spat, and she stood there scrubbing at the ring with a sponge with more force than it needed.

"Coffee's in the cupboard over the machine," she said, without turning round. "Filters are in the drawer under it. If you're staying, you can make your own after today."

"Right."

"The machine's older than Seth. You have to hold the button down for a count of three or it doesn't take."

"Count of three."

"And it leaks if you fill it over the six."

"Over the six," he said. "Got it."

She got the can of Folgers down and put a filter in and spooned the grounds in without measuring, the way she'd done it every morning for twenty years, and filled it to the six, and held the button for a count of three. The machine thought about it. Then it started to gurgle.

When she turned round he was reaching for the mugs.

They hung on hooks under the cupboard, a row of six, none of them matching. He'd taken one down without asking, the way somebody would in their own kitchen, and he was holding it in one big hand, and it was the chipped one. The blue one, with the steelhead jumping on the side of it and the words *QUILLAYUTE RIVER* in white capitals going round the rim, half worn off by the dishwasher.

"Not that one," Sue said.

He stopped.

He looked at her. Then he looked down at the mug in his hand for a second, at the leaping fish and the chip out of the rim on the side you'd drink from if you were right-handed. Then he hung it back on its hook. He did it carefully. He turned it so the fish was facing out, the way it had been.

He took the one next to it instead, a plain white one from the bank in Port Angeles, and held it out to her, and didn't say anything at all.

Sue took it from him. Her hand wasn't quite steady. She turned her back and poured the coffee.

"Milk?" she said.

"Please. No sugar."

She put milk in it and gave it to him and poured her own, black, in her own mug, and sat down at the table under the window. After a second he pulled out the chair across from her and sat down too. The chair creaked. He was too big for it. He put the mug on the table in front of him and both hands round it and looked at her, and waited.

She looked back at him across her kitchen table.

Upstairs, the water was running in the bathroom.

"Billy'll have told you most of it," he said.

"Billy told me what Sam told him."

"Then you've got most of it."

"Sam said you set them on fire."

"Yeah."

"All of them."

"All the ones in cloaks."

"With your fingers."

He didn't snap them. She'd half thought he might, to show her, and she found that she'd tensed in her chair in case he did. He just nodded, and drank his coffee.

"And a man jumped at you," Sue said, "and you brought down lightning. Out of a clear sky."

"Bit of cloud about," he said. "There was a hole in it."

She looked at him.

"That's what Sam said," she said. "He said there was a hole in the clouds. He said it was blue."

"It was blue."

"He said the man was just gone. Nothing left of him."

He didn't say anything to that. He looked down into his coffee for a moment, and then back up at her, and his face stayed exactly as it was. It waited, the way it had waited for her on the lawn, to see what she'd do with it.

Sue drank her coffee. It was too hot. She drank it anyway.

"And my daughter," she said.

"Yeah."

That was all. *Yeah.* He didn't look away when he said it.

Sue put her mug down.

"Sam says it isn't like the others," she said. "He says the old Volturi one said it went deeper than he'd ever seen. And then you made a noise, and every wolf on that field put its ears down. Sam too." She heard her own voice, level and quiet. Two years on the council, and twenty before that sitting at the back of the room at the tribal center while Harry and the old men argued, had taught her how to keep it level and quiet. "Sam's never put his ears down for anybody in his life."

He didn't say anything.

"So I'm going to ask you," Sue said. "In my kitchen. Since you're going to be sleeping on my couch. What are you?"

He looked at her for a long moment.

Then he picked up his coffee and drank some, and put it down again, and turned it round on the table a quarter turn, so the handle pointed at her.

"There's a wolf in me," he said.

Sue sat very still.

"Has been for a while. Years. It's big. Bigger than yours. It comes out at the full moon, and it goes back in when the moon goes down, and the rest of the month it stays where it's put." He said it flatly. Like a man reading a list off a form. "This morning your daughter looked at me and something went from her to me. And the wolf took hold of the other end of it. It's still got hold. It won't let go." He turned the mug another quarter turn. "That's the lot. That's everything I know about it. I'm not holding anything back. There isn't anything to hold back. I found out the same minute she did."

"Thursday," Sue said.

He looked at her.

"The moon's full Thursday," she said. "Harry used to—" She stopped. Started again. "My husband used to fish by the moon. The tides. I know when it's full."

"Thursday," he said.

"Is it like ours? Your wolf?"

"Don't know. I've never been inside one of yours." He paused. "I've never been inside one of mine, much, either, if it comes to that."

"Will you hurt her?"

"No."

He said it straight away, one word with nothing after it, and then he stopped and let it sit there on the table between them.

"You'd say that," Sue said.

"I would," he agreed. "Doesn't make it less true."

She looked at him. She looked at him the way she'd looked at the doctor in the corridor in Forks, and at Sam Uley the morning he came to the door to tell her that her daughter was a wolf. She didn't have any gift for it. She couldn't hear a lie in somebody's heartbeat the way her kids said they could, now. She just had forty-odd years of looking at people's faces, and two kids, and a husband who'd been the worst liar on the reservation, God rest him, and she knew what it looked like when somebody wasn't trying to sell you anything.

This one was sitting at her table drinking her coffee and waiting to see whether she'd buy it, and that was all.

Upstairs, the water was still running.

"She can hear every word of this," Sue said. "You know that."

"I know."

"She'll have the bathroom door open a crack and she'll be standing there with the towel round her."

He tipped his head back a little, and looked at the ceiling, and listened. She watched him do it. It was a strange thing to watch: his eyes went unfocused, as if he were looking at something a long way off, and his head turned an inch, the way Seth's did now when he heard a car turn off the highway a mile away.

"Shower's running," he said. "Nobody's in it."

Sue looked up at the ceiling.

Directly overhead, there was a sudden sharp rattle of shower-curtain rings, and the sound of the water changed, the hollow drumming on the bottom of the tub going muffled and broken all at once, as if somebody had just stepped very fast into the middle of it.

He drank his coffee.

Sue put her hand over her mouth. She didn't laugh. She was very careful not to laugh. Her eyes stung anyway.

---

The phone rang at twenty to three.

She knew it was Billy before she picked it up. It was always Billy. She'd put the cordless down on the porch rail and Seth had brought it in and left it on the counter, and it went off next to the bread bin like a fire alarm, and the man across the table didn't flinch. Seth did. Seth was in the doorway in an old Seahawks sweatshirt that was too small for him now, a bowl of cereal in one hand and a half-eaten sandwich in the other, and he jumped about a foot and slopped milk on the floor.

Sue picked up the phone. "Billy."

"Is he there?" Billy didn't say hello. He never said hello when he was excited. She could hear his TV on in the background, and the creak of his chair. "Sam said he was heading your way. Is he there?"

"He's here."

"What's he like?"

Sue looked across the table.

The Englishman was looking back at her. He'd heard the question. She could tell he'd heard it, every word, from four feet away, out of a telephone pressed against her ear. He raised his eyebrows very slightly.

"He's drinking my coffee," Sue said.

"Sue—"

"Tomorrow, Billy."

"I want to see him tonight. I want—Sue, I've been sitting by this phone since five o'clock this morning, I want to look at him. Old Quil wants to look at him. Old Quil's been on the phone to me eleven times, he says he wants to know is it true about the lightning, he says it's in the stories, there's a story about—"

"Tomorrow," Sue said. "He's been up since God knows when and he's been on a plane and he's been in a field all morning setting people on fire. You can look at him tomorrow." She looked across the table. "Eleven o'clock. Your place."

The Englishman nodded.

"He says fine," Sue said.

"Is Jake coming home? Do you know if Jake's coming home?"

"Jake's at the Cullens'. Seth says he's going on patrol at midnight."

"Patrol? What patrol?"

Sue looked at Seth.

"The red-eyes," Seth said, with his mouth full. "The witnesses. They ran off into the woods, there's like forty of them, Jake says Sam's taking the north and we've got the south."

The man at the table had gone still.

Sue saw it. She didn't know what she was seeing. He'd put his mug down and his eyes had gone to the window, to the grey rain and the dark wet trees at the bottom of the yard, and his face didn't do anything, exactly. It closed. That was the only word she had for it. Like somebody pulling down a blind.

"Billy," Sue said. "Seth says they're patrolling for the ones who ran off. I'll call you back."

"Sue—"

She hung up on him. She'd been doing that to Billy Black for thirty years and he'd never once held it against her.

"They ran," the Englishman said. He was still looking at the window. "This morning. When I burned the cloaks. I let them go."

"Forty of them," Seth said. "Jake thinks. Maybe more."

"Red eyes." He said it quietly, more to himself than to them. "All of them."

"Most of them." Seth had stopped eating. He was standing in the doorway with the cereal bowl in his hand, looking at the man the way he used to look at his father when Harry got a phone call late at night and went quiet like that. "Sam says some of them were probably just scared. Like, they just wanted to get away. But some of them—"

"Yeah," the man said. "Some of them."

He sat there for a moment longer. Then he took his hands off the table and put them flat on his thighs and closed his eyes.

Sue didn't know what he was doing. She'd find out later, from Seth, who'd asked him that evening, that he'd gone looking. That he'd put something out from himself, a long way, the way you'd let out a fishing line, out over the trees and the hills and the rivers, as far as it would go. But at the time all she knew was that a man was sitting at her kitchen table with his eyes closed, perfectly still, not breathing, for a full minute by the clock on the stove, and that every hair on her arms stood up, and that Seth, in the doorway, very slowly put his cereal bowl down on the counter and backed up a step.

Then he opened his eyes.

"There's two," he said. "Still on the peninsula. One up near the lake, the big one, east of here."

"Crescent," Seth said faintly.

"Crescent. One out on the coast, south, a long way down. Moving south fast." He looked at Seth. "The rest have gone. Canada, mostly. Couple went east, over the mountains. They're still going. They were still going an hour ago. They'll be going for a week."

"How do you know that?" Seth said.

He didn't answer. He reached into the inside pocket of his jacket and took out the little silver mirror Sue had heard about from Billy, the one Sam had said he talked into, and flipped it open, and tapped the glass twice.

"Ruth," he said.

The glass fogged. A woman's voice came out of it, small and American and tired. "Potter. I said tonight."

"Two of your witnesses are still on the peninsula. One at Lake Crescent, east end. One going south down the coast past the Hoh, fast. The packs are going to be out tonight looking for them. If your lot get there first, take them off the peninsula. If the wolves get there first, don't give the wolves any grief about it." A pause. "And tell your people the wolves are on the right side, so nobody hexes a Quileute kid in the dark by mistake."

The mirror was quiet for a moment.

"Lake Crescent and south of the Hoh," the woman's voice said. "Got it." Another pause. "You could just go get them yourself."

"I've been told to stay put."

"Since when do you do what you're told?"

"Since Sabine started reading my forms," he said, and snapped the mirror shut, and put it away.

He looked at Seth.

"Tell Jacob," he said. "And Sam. Lake Crescent, east end, and south past the Hoh. The rest have gone."

Seth stared at him. Then he nodded, fast, three times, and turned and went out of the back door at a run, and Sue heard him hit the bottom of the porch step and keep going, down the yard, and then a soft ripping sound that she was going to have words with him about later, and something big landing on four feet in the wet grass, and the rattle of the gate, and a long rush of paws going away into the trees.

The back door swung on its hinges behind him.

Sue looked at it. Then she looked back across the table.

The Englishman picked up his coffee again. It must have gone cold by now. He drank it anyway.

"The American woman," Sue said. "At the field. Sam said she asked him questions."

"She asks everybody questions. It's her job."

"She asked him who knows. About us. People who aren't—" Sue stopped. "Who aren't wolves. Or on the council. Sam wouldn't tell her. He said the council would talk to her, not the packs."

"Good for Sam."

"What happens to them?"

He looked at her over the top of his mug.

"People who know," Sue said. "What happens to them?"

He put the mug down.

"Who are you thinking about?" he said.

Sue didn't say anything.

She didn't mean to look at the fridge. She didn't. Her eyes went there by themselves, the way they'd been going there all afternoon. To the photograph in the middle. To the two of them on the end of the dock with the salmon between them, grinning, Harry's cap on backward, Charlie's sunburn.

"Chief Swan," the Englishman said.

Sue's hand closed on her mug.

"How do you know about Charlie?"

"Bella," he said. "I had a look in her head this morning. Wasn't polite about it. Charlie was in there. A lot of Charlie." He paused. "Christmas. A baby on his knee. Him not asking."

Sue didn't move.

"They'd have taken it out," he said. He said it plainly, without any weight on it, the way he'd said everything. "If he'd gone in a file. What he's seen. Jacob changing in his living room. The kid. Anything else he's worked out and decided not to look at. There's a charm for it. They're very good at it. He'd never know. He'd just wake up one morning and think his granddaughter was a bit tall for her age."

"Would have," Sue said.

"Would have." He met her eyes across the table. "I told the American to write *see Potter*. That's me. Nobody's going near Charlie Swan's head unless they go through me first, and nobody's going to go through me for that. Not over Charlie. He's on my page and he stays on my page."

Sue sat there.

She thought about Charlie in his chair in front of the game on Sunday afternoons, the way he'd been the last three months, since she'd started going over there with casseroles and staying to watch. The way he'd look at her sometimes, sideways, when something came on the news about wolves, or bears, or animal attacks out on the peninsula, and then look back at the TV and not say anything. The way he'd sat with the baby on his knee at Christmas, that strange beautiful baby who was much too big for her age, with Bella's eyes, and had looked down into her face for a long time, and then up at Sue, across the room, and Sue had looked back at him and shaken her head, very slightly, and he'd nodded and looked away. Need to know.

She thought about him waking up one morning and not knowing why he'd ever looked at her like that.

Sue got up from the table.

She went to the sink. She turned the tap on and stood there with her hands on the edge of the counter and let the water run into the empty basin, over nothing, the way you would if you'd forgotten what you'd come over to wash. She looked out of the window at the rain. The gate at the bottom of the yard was still swinging where Seth had gone through it.

After a while she turned the tap off.

"More coffee?" she said, without turning round.

"Please," said the man at her table.

She took his mug from him. She filled it from the pot. She put the milk in, the right amount this time without asking, and gave it back to him, and he took it and said, "Ta," which she'd only ever heard in old British movies on the late show, and she sat down again across from him and put her elbows on the table.

"Where will you be Thursday night?" she said.

"Wherever she is."

Sue looked at him.

"There's a book," he said. "Old one. Kreacher fetched it from Geneva this morning. Kreacher's my—he's a house-elf. You'll meet him. Sorry in advance." He turned his mug round. "There's a page in it about this. Whatever this is. It says that at the full moon the two of us aren't to be separated, on any account. Then it says there were two times somebody tried it, and what happened. And then the rest of the page is torn out."

"Torn out."

"Ripped clean off. Long time ago. Somebody wrote *Don't* in the margin next to it." He looked at her. "That's all I've got. I don't know what happens if we're apart on Thursday night. I don't know what happens if we're not. Nobody does, as far as I can find out. Best I can tell you is I'll be wherever she is, and I'll be keeping an eye on it."

"That's not very reassuring," Sue said.

"Wasn't meant to be."

Sue looked at him across the table for a long moment.

Then she laughed.

She didn't mean to. It came out of her short and rough and surprised, the first time she'd laughed since she didn't know when, since before the Italians, since before the baby, maybe, and she put her hand over her mouth again, and this time she couldn't stop it. She sat at her own kitchen table and laughed into her hand until her eyes ran.

He watched her do it. He didn't laugh with her. Something happened at one corner of his mouth, though, and stayed there.

Upstairs, the shower had gone off.

Sue wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. She looked at the ceiling, and then at the fridge. At the photograph. At Harry with his cap on backward, holding up his end of the salmon, his mouth wide open on whatever stupid thing Charlie had just said.

He'd have laughed himself sick at all this. At a wizard from England sitting at his kitchen table, too big for the chair, drinking coffee out of the bank mug. At *Potter's fine*. At his daughter, who'd spent two years telling everyone who'd listen that she'd never let it happen to her, standing in a bathroom upstairs dripping wet with the door open a crack, listening to every word.

He'd have liked this one, Sue thought. She didn't know why she was sure of it. She was.

The Englishman pushed back his chair.

"I'm going to have a smoke," he said. "If that's all right. Outside."

"Not in my house," said Sue.

"Wouldn't dream of it," he said, and went out through the back door into the rain.

Sue sat at the table and listened to the door close behind him. She listened to his boots cross the porch. She listened for the creak of the sagging step, and it didn't come; he'd stepped over it again.

"Potter," she said, quietly, to the empty kitchen. Trying it.

It was all right. It was just a word.

She sat there a while longer with her hands round her mug. Then she looked at the photograph on the fridge one more time, and said the other one, very quietly, under her breath, the way you'd put your weight on a step you weren't sure of.

It held.

She put her hand flat on the table, and kept it there until it stopped shaking.

---

The back step had a roof over it, more or less. Harry sat on the dry end of it with his boots on the bottom step, the one he'd stepped over, and lit a cigarette off nothing and watched the rain come down on the yard.

The line sat under his breastbone. Upstairs and a bit to the left, through the ceiling and a wall, it ran up to a room at the front of the house where somebody was sitting on the end of a bed with wet hair, not moving, so angry he could feel it from here like a hand held close over a hob. She'd heard all of it. Every word. *There's a wolf in me.* He'd known she was listening when he said it, and he'd said it anyway, to her mum, across the kitchen table, and he'd watched the shower curtain go.

He drew on the cigarette and let the smoke go up into the wet.

The mug had been her dad's. Obviously. He hadn't needed telling. He'd picked it up because it was the nearest, and the look on Sue's face had told him the rest before she opened her mouth. He'd hung it back where it came from. It had felt like the least he could do and also like nothing at all.

Down at the bottom of the dark, the wolf lay with its chin on its paws and its eyes open, looking up the line toward the front bedroom.

*Pack it in,* he told it.

It went on looking.

The crack came from the middle of the lawn and made a pair of crows go up out of the spruce at the bottom of the yard, complaining. Kreacher stood in the wet grass with a battered brown suitcase in one hand and a pair of black boots in the other, both volumes of *Bonds of Soul and Shape* balanced on his head like a pile of washing, and a wicker basket hooked over one elbow. A razor in a leather case stuck out of the knot of his tea towel.

He looked at the house. He looked at the yard, and the woodpile under its blue tarp, and the Bronco on blocks two gardens over with moss on the bonnet. He looked at the sagging step. His nose wrinkled.

Then he looked at Harry.

"Master," said Kreacher, "is sitting on a step. In the rain."

"I'm under the roof."

"Half of Master is under the roof."

Harry looked down. His boots and the knees of his jeans were out in the drizzle. They were steaming slightly. He moved them.

Kreacher came up the yard. He didn't step over the bad step; he looked at it, and the board gave a short, frightened creak without anybody standing on it and lay still, and he walked up it as if it had never been anything else. He set the suitcase down by the door and the boots beside it, toes aligned. He took the books off his head and held them out.

Harry took them. They weighed about as much as a small child.

"Kreacher will speak to the lady's mother," Kreacher said, "about the kitchen."

"Kreacher—"

But the elf had already rapped twice on the back door with his knuckles, very formally, and when Sue opened it and looked down at him, he folded himself in half at the waist and stayed there with his ears hanging and his nose an inch off her doormat.

Sue looked at him. Then she looked at Harry, over the top of Kreacher's bent back.

"Sorry in advance," Harry said. "Like I said."

"Kreacher," said Kreacher to the doormat, "begs the lady's mother's permission to cook for the household."

"I'm cooking," said Sue.

There was a short silence.

Kreacher's ears, which had been hanging, drooped a little further.

Sue looked at him for a long moment. Harry watched her do it. She looked at the tea towel and the crest on it, and the locket on its chain, and the long fingers folded on top of one another, and the ears. Something went over her face that he didn't try to name.

"You can peel the potatoes," she said.

Kreacher came upright so fast his ears flapped.

---

By half past five it was dark, and the kitchen smelled of roast chicken and onions and the wet wool of Esme Cullen's blankets, which Seth had gone up to the treaty line on four legs to fetch at a quarter to five and brought back in a duffel bag in his teeth. There'd been a foil-covered dish in the bag as well, packed in a towel and still hot, and a card in a cream envelope with *Sue* on the front in a slanting, old-fashioned hand. Sue had read the card standing at the counter. She'd put it in the drawer with the filters and not said anything about it, and then she'd taken the foil off the dish, and looked at the lasagna under it for a while, and put it in the oven on low next to her chicken.

Kreacher had taken the blankets out of the duffel bag one at a time, at arm's length, and sniffed each one. Then he'd carried them out onto the back porch and shaken them, hard, one after another, and something had come out of the wool in a faint pale shimmer like dust in a sunbeam, and when he brought them back in they smelled of nothing but wool and cedar.

"Bloodsuckers," he'd said to Harry, darkly, folding them over the back of the couch. "Good blankets, though."

Harry was at the table with the forms.

He had a stack of them in front of him. Kreacher had brought them in the suitcase, under the shirts; a block of Confederation incident reports about an inch thick, pale yellow, with the silver seal in the top corner and a lot of very small boxes. He was on the sixth. He'd done *Incident: refusal to disperse (Volturi, Italian, Cold Ones, 32)*. He'd done *Action taken*, which had a box about the size of a postage stamp, in which he'd written *Fire*. He'd done *Collateral*, and had to think for a while, and then written *Field. Bit.* He was now on a form headed *Discharge of Lethal Force Against a Being Not Party to the Original Incident*, and he'd got as far as the name.

*Vladimir,* he'd written. *Romanian.* And then he'd sat and looked at the next box for about five minutes.

"How'd you know?"

Harry looked up. Seth was sitting on the counter by the sink, which Sue had told him to get off three times, in a T-shirt that said *FORKS SPARTANS WRESTLING* and nothing on his feet. He'd come back from the treaty line and then gone straight out again to Sam's and then come back again, and he'd eaten two sandwiches and most of a box of cereal standing up, and he'd been watching Harry fill in forms for twenty minutes with the face of somebody watching a magic trick.

"Know what?"

"About the red-eyes. Where they were. You just sat there and closed your eyes and then you knew. Jake wants to know. Sam wants to know too, he didn't say so, but I could tell."

Harry put the biro down.

"Had a look," he said.

"How?"

"Same way you'd listen for a car. Just further."

Seth's mouth opened.

"Seth," said Sue, from the stove, without turning round. "Off the counter. Go and get your sister. Dinner."

Seth went, but he went slowly, looking back.

Leah came down at twenty to six.

Harry heard her coming. He'd felt her coming before that, through the line; felt her get up off the end of her bed and stand in the middle of her room for a full minute, and then go to the door, and stop, and go back, and then go to the door again. Seth had knocked three times. Then Kreacher had gone up the stairs, which nobody had asked him to do, and knocked once, and said through the door in a carrying, reproachful voice, "The lady's dinner is getting cold," and gone back down again. The door had opened about four seconds later.

She came into the kitchen in a grey sweatshirt and black sweatpants with her hair still damp at the ends, and she didn't look at him. She went round the far side of the table, as far from him as the kitchen would allow, and sat down in the chair against the wall with her back to the window, and put her elbows on the table.

Kreacher put a plate in front of her. It had about half a chicken on it.

She looked at it. She looked at Kreacher.

"Lady," said Kreacher, "is too thin."

"You said that already."

"Kreacher," said Kreacher, "will keep saying it."

She ate.

They all did. Sue's roast chicken and potatoes, and Esme's lasagna, which Seth ate half of on his own and pronounced *actually really good*, in a voice of deep surprise, and Kreacher's gravy, which had appeared in a jug nobody had seen him make, and bread, and green beans that Sue put on everybody's plate and nobody ate except her. Seth talked. Seth talked the whole way through it, with his mouth full and without, about the field and the crack and Jane's face and the lightning and Kreacher and the Bulgarian upside down in the reading room and whether Harry could do the lightning again, not *at* anybody, just to see, and whether the book had any pictures, and whether there were more house-elves, and whether house-elves could phase, and Sue said *Seth* about every ninety seconds, and it made absolutely no difference.

Leah said nothing. She ate three plates and said nothing at all.

Harry said very little. He ate. He answered when Seth stopped to breathe. *No. Some. Yeah, loads. No.* Across the table, he could feel her not looking at him, which was a thing he hadn't known you could feel until today; a steady pressure off to one side, like standing next to a woodstove.

The line sat between them over the table, humming.

---

At half past seven he went out on the back step for another smoke, and she came out after him.

He'd known she would. He'd felt her get up from the couch, where she'd been sitting with the TV on and not watching it, and stand at the back door with her hand on the knob for a while. Then the door opened and she came out and stood behind him on the porch with her arms folded, in the dark.

He didn't turn round.

"You told my mom," she said.

"Yeah."

"You told her there's a wolf in you."

"There is."

"You didn't tell *me.*"

He drew on the cigarette.

"You didn't ask nicely," he said.

"I asked."

"You asked like you were going to bite my face off."

"I *was* going to bite your face off."

"Couldn't, though," Harry said. "Could you."

She didn't answer that. He heard her breathe in through her nose, hard, and let it out again. The rain had eased off to almost nothing. Out past the bottom of the yard the surf was going on the rocks at First Beach, slow and heavy, a long way off.

"She asked me in her kitchen," he said. "Over coffee. Looking at me. You were upstairs with the tap running."

"I *heard.*"

"I know you heard. I'm telling you why I said it to her and not you. She asked me straight. Sat down across a table and asked straight. That's all."

There was a long silence behind him.

"Is it like us?" she said. "Your wolf."

"Don't know. Never been inside one of you."

"You were inside Bella."

"Bella's not a wolf. Bella's—" He stopped. He thought about the island and the feathers and the bendy straw and three days of fire. "Bella's had a year."

Something that was very nearly a laugh came out of her, short and surprised, and she stopped it.

He turned his head and looked up at her. She was standing a yard behind him against the wall of the house, with her arms folded tight and her shoulders up round her ears, in the light from the kitchen window. The little blue fish on the curtains made shadows on her face.

"How big?" she said.

"Big."

"Bigger than Sam?"

"Haven't measured Sam."

"Bigger than *Jacob?*"

He looked at her.

"Yeah," he said.

She looked back at him. He could see her trying to picture it, and failing, and deciding she didn't want to, and wanting to anyway. Then her jaw set.

"Thursday," she said.

"Thursday."

"I'm not going to be anywhere near you on Thursday."

"Book says otherwise."

"I don't care what the book says."

"Yeah," Harry said. "You've said."

She stood there a moment longer. He felt it go through the line, everything she wasn't going to say: a big tangled hot knot of it, too fast and too many things at once to pick any one of them out. Then she turned round and went back inside and shut the door behind her, firmly.

He finished his cigarette.

---

Sue went up at a quarter to ten.

She stopped in the kitchen doorway on her way. Harry was still at the table. He'd finished the forms, all of them, eleven in the end, including one he'd invented a heading for because the Confederation didn't seem to have a form for *Toddler, Hybrid, Unregistered, Riding a Wolf*, and he'd written Ruth Calloway her statement: four pages, plain, with nouns. Kreacher had taken it ten minutes ago with a crack and an air of grievance, and taken the forms with him for the Geneva pouch.

Now Volume Two was open on the table in front of him at the torn page.

Sue looked at it, from the doorway. She looked at the biro in his hand.

"Goodnight, Potter," she said.

"Night, Sue."

She went up. He heard her on the stairs, and on the landing, and the door of the back bedroom across the landing from Leah's, and the creak of a mattress, and the click of a lamp.

Seth went out at twenty past eleven. He went out the back door in his cut-offs, whistling, and said "Night, Harry!" over his shoulder before he remembered, and stopped dead on the porch with his hand still on the door. Harry heard his heart jump. Then Seth went on down the yard without another word, very fast, and Harry heard the soft thump of a big body landing in the wet grass by the gate, and four feet going away into the trees toward the south.

Upstairs, Leah's light was still on.

Harry looked down at the page in front of him. At the long ragged tear running down it, soft and brown with age. At the word in faded green ink in the margin, in small neat letters.

*Don't.*

He uncapped the biro.

He thought about it for a second. Then he wrote, underneath it, in black, in his own handwriting, which had never been neat and wasn't now:

*Noted.*

He looked at it. It looked stupid. He left it.

He shut the book and went through to the front room and lay down on the couch.

It was exactly as advertised. It was lumpy and it smelled faintly of grape soda and old popcorn underneath the clean wool of Esme Cullen's blankets, and it was about six inches too short for him, so that his boots hung off the end over the armrest. He'd taken them off. His socks hung off the end over the armrest. Halfway down, under the middle cushion, something hard and coiled was waiting to find his hip, and when he turned over onto his side it found it.

"Fair enough," Harry said, to the spring.

He turned onto his back. The spring went back to waiting.

He lay there and looked at the ceiling. There was a water stain on it the shape of Belgium. Upstairs, through the plaster and the joists, the line ran up to the front bedroom, where the light was still on and somebody was lying on top of the covers with her arms crossed over her stomach, staring at her own ceiling, and not sleeping either.

He could feel her not sleeping. That was a new one, too.

Down at the bottom of the dark the wolf was awake. He didn't need to go down there to know it. He could feel it lying with its head up, the way it lay on the last few nights before the full, ears pricked, listening up the line toward the front bedroom like a dog listening for a car in the drive.

*Two days,* Harry told it. *Go to sleep.*

The light upstairs went off at ten past twelve.

He went to sleep some time after that.

---

The back door woke him.

He was awake before it had finished opening, lying still on the couch in the dark with his eyes open, listening. The stove clock said 3:04 in green figures through the kitchen doorway. The house was quiet. The fridge hummed. The rain had stopped altogether and the surf was the only sound outside, long and slow and far off.

Somebody had come down the stairs without his hearing them, which took doing. Somebody had crossed the kitchen in bare feet and eased the back door open and gone out onto the porch, and was standing there now, with the door pulled to behind her and her hand still on the knob, breathing.

He didn't move.

She went down the yard. He felt her go. He heard her bare feet in the wet grass, quick and light, and the gate, and then at the bottom of the yard by the big cedar the line went hot.

It went up the line like a struck match. It always would, he thought; he'd felt it twice now. The heat of her coming apart and putting herself back together in a different shape, and then four feet in the wet needles and a long shake that rattled her coat, and then nothing for a moment except a big grey animal standing under a cedar tree in the dark with her ears up.

She was going on patrol. He could feel that, more or less. A hard bright purpose sitting on top of everything else in her like a lid on a pan: *South. The coast. Jacob said I was off and Jacob can go to hell. Something to DO.* She turned toward the south. She took four strides into the trees.

She stopped.

She stood there. He felt her stand there, at the edge of the trees, with the line running back up the yard and through the wall and into the middle of his chest. The line lay slack. It hadn't pulled since the field. It had let her go into the trees at the edge of the clearing that afternoon and it would have let her go now. He could feel that it would. Nothing was holding her there but her.

She turned round.

She came back up out of the trees and across the bottom of the yard, and along the side of the house, under the kitchen window, past the woodpile. He heard her the whole way. She didn't make much noise, for something her size, but the gravel at the side of the house shifted under her and the wet ferns brushed against her coat, and he could hear her breathing, big slow lungfuls of cold air going in and out. Round the front of the house, under the window of the room where he lay. Round the far side. Past the Bronco two gardens over, and back, and along the back of the house again, past the porch.

Round again.

He lay on the couch and counted.

On the third time round, the wolf at the bottom of the dark stood up.

He felt it do it. The whole long weight of it heaving itself up off the stone, the way a big dog gets up off a hearthrug when it hears its name. It stood there in the dark with its head up and its ears forward, listening up the line, and its tail had started to move. Slowly. Side to side.

*No,* Harry told it. *Lie down.*

It didn't lie down.

On the fourth time round, she stopped under his window.

He heard her stop. He heard her stand there, a few feet away through the wall, on the wet gravel under the front window, and he could smell her through the glass and the wood and the plaster: wet fur and cedar and snow and the sea, and under that, that particular dark note that the wolf at the bottom of him had picked out of the air across a field that morning and hadn't let go of since.

He lay very still and put the walls up. All of them. The ones he'd built at seventeen, brick by brick, in the dark. He put his back against them and held.

On the fifth time round, she howled.

It was quiet, as howls went. He'd heard wolves howl before, proper wolves, in the forests round the dragon reserve in Romania. This was lower than that, and shorter, and it didn't go up into the sky the way theirs did. It stayed close to the ground. It came from somewhere round the back of the house by the porch, one long low note that dropped at the end, and it went straight through the wall and through the walls he'd built and down the line to the very bottom of him, where the wolf was standing, and touched it on the nose.

The wolf answered.

He had his teeth shut. He had his jaw locked and the walls up and every bit of him that he'd learnt how to hold, held. It came anyway. It came up out of the bottom of his chest under the blankets, low and slow and deep, a long rolling rumble that he felt in his back teeth and in the springs of the couch. The window over his head buzzed in its frame. Out in the kitchen, the mugs on their hooks under the cupboard chimed against each other, very faintly, one after another down the row.

Then it stopped.

Outside the house, everything stopped. He heard her go dead still, somewhere out by the back porch. Not a breath.

Upstairs, in the back bedroom across the landing from Leah's empty one, a mattress creaked. A pair of feet found the floor. He heard Sue Clearwater's heart going, quick and steady, and he heard her stand there in the dark beside her bed for a long time, listening.

She didn't come down.

After a while the mattress creaked again.

Outside, he heard four big feet come slowly round the side of the house. Slowly, this time. Not pacing. They came along under the kitchen window, and past the woodpile, and round to the front, and stopped under his window. He heard her turn round once, twice, on the wet gravel, the way a dog turns before it lies down, and then the long slow slump of a heavy body settling, and a sigh, and a soft bump against the outside wall of the house, a few feet from his head, as she put her back against it.

Then nothing. Just breathing. Hers, through the wall. Slow, now. Getting slower.

The wolf at the bottom of the dark stood there a moment longer with its head up. Then it lay down, with its head raised and its chin well clear of its paws, looking up the line, and its tail went once against the stone, soft, and was still.

Harry lay on his back on the couch with the spring waiting under the middle cushion and his own heart going a good deal faster than forty-eight, and looked at the water stain on the ceiling.

*Two days,* he told it.

It didn't answer. It didn't need to. It was looking up the line, and the line went through the wall, and on the other side of the wall a grey wolf was lying on the wet gravel with her back against the house, asleep.

He lay there and listened to her breathe until the window over his head started to go grey.

He didn't feel the wolf get up again.

---

Leah woke up at eight minutes to ten with the sun on her face, and lay there with her eyes shut, and remembered.

It came back in pieces, the way stuff did when you'd been in the other shape. Clearer than a dream, and worse, because she couldn't tell herself she'd made any of it up. Lying on her bed in the dark at three in the morning with her arms crossed over her stomach and the line humming up through the floor like somebody had left a fridge running in her chest. Getting up. *Patrol. South. Something to do.* The back door. The yard. The cedar.

Four steps into the trees.

Turning round.

She opened her eyes and stared at the ceiling and wanted to die.

She'd gone round the house. She'd gone round and round and round it, like a dog on a chain, like one of those stupid tetherballs at the school playground in Forks, with her nose full of him through the walls and her whole body humming. She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to go south. She'd stood at the edge of the trees and told her legs *south* and her legs had turned her round and taken her back up the yard as calmly as if they'd been doing it all their lives.

And then she'd—

Leah put both hands over her face.

And then the thing had come up through the wall. That sound. The same sound as the field, low and slow and so deep she'd felt it in the gravel under her feet, and every hair on her body had stood up and then lain down flat, and she'd stood there by the back porch shaking. And then she'd gone round to the front of the house and lain down under his window with her back against the wall, like a—

Like a—

She didn't have a word for what she'd been like. She didn't want one.

And Seth had been phased. Seth had been out on the south patrol with Jacob, three miles down the coast, and the second she changed under the cedar he'd been right there in her head. She remembered his voice going *Leah? Leah, I thought you were off—* and then, two minutes later, very small and very awed, *Leah, are you going round the house?*

She remembered telling him to shut up. She remembered telling him a lot of things.

She remembered that Jacob hadn't said a single word the entire time, which was worse.

She'd woken up at first light on the wet gravel under the front window with her nose tucked under her tail and the sun coming up grey behind the trees, and she'd got up and slunk back to the cedar and phased and come in and gone up the stairs past the front room without looking at the couch, and got into bed in her damp clothes, and gone out like a light.

And now it was nearly ten, and the sun was out for the first time in a week, and the house smelled of coffee.

And of him.

She sat up.

It was everywhere. It came up through the floorboards and under the door and filled up her room like smoke. Earth and pine and cold wet stone, the same as the field, the same as the blanket and the porch step and the walls last night. But there was more of it. A lot more. It was the dark musk underneath all that, the one that had made her hackles lift across a clearing; except yesterday it had been a thread, faint, something only a wolf would catch, and this morning it was so thick she could taste it at the back of her throat. It was like the difference between one guy in a room and a whole pack.

Her skin crawled up the back of her neck. The fine hairs on her arms stood up. She felt her lips start to come back off her teeth before she knew she was doing it.

Somebody had phased in the house.

That was her first thought, and it was stupid, because none of them would, not in her mom's house, not ever, her mom would kill them. And none of them smelled like that.

She got out of bed.

---

The stairs creaked under her the way they always did, the third one and the seventh. She went down in her bare feet in the sweatpants and the grey sweatshirt she'd slept in, and at the bottom she stopped.

The front room looked like something had happened in it.

The couch had been shoved about a foot out from the wall, crooked, so that one end of it was jammed up against the end table and the lamp on the end table was leaning drunkenly against the wall. Esme Cullen's blankets were on the floor in a heap. The middle cushion was split down its length, and the spring her mom had been promising to get fixed since Seth was in fourth grade had come straight up through the split, all the way, a big rusty coil of it sticking up out of the stuffing like a jack-in-the-box. There was a tuft of black hair caught on the end of it. Coarse black hair, three or four inches long, with a charcoal-grey underfur at the root.

Leah looked at it for a long time.

On the coffee table, beside the couch, somebody had left a book.

It was one of the big ones. The second one. It was lying open, face up, and she knew which page it was open at before she got close enough to read it, because she could see the tear from across the room. The long ragged diagonal, soft and brown at the edge. And the green ink in the margin, small and neat.

*Don't.*

And under it, in black biro, in a messy slanting hand that sloped downhill to the right like whoever'd written it had been leaning on their elbow:

*Noted.*

Something happened in her stomach. She didn't look at what.

She turned round toward the kitchen.

There was a tail in the hall.

It came out of the kitchen doorway and lay across the floor of the little hallway at the bottom of the stairs, all the way across, from the kitchen door to the foot of the bottom step, where the tip of it was curled against the newel post. It was black. It was as thick as her thigh at the base and it tapered down to the tip, and the fur on it was long and thick and so dark it looked blue where the light from the front window fell across it.

She'd nearly trodden on it coming down. She'd have trodden on it if she hadn't stopped to look at the couch.

As she stood there staring at it, the tip uncurled from the newel post. It lifted, slowly, and drew back across the hallway floor a couple of feet, out of the way of the bottom step. Clearing a path. The way you'd move your feet for somebody coming past you in a movie theatre.

Then it lay down again.

Leah stood at the bottom of the stairs.

From the kitchen, low, under the noise of the coffee machine gurgling, a voice said, "One. Two. Three."

---

She didn't go in through the hall door. She couldn't have. The doorway was full.

She went round through the front room instead, past the wrecked couch, to the little archway at the far end that led through to where the kitchen table was, under the window, and she stopped in the archway with one hand on the frame, and looked.

The kitchen was full of wolf.

All of it. Wall to wall. He was lying, or half lying, along the whole length of the room, from the hall doorway to the counter under the cupboards on the far side, and he still didn't fit. His hindquarters filled the hall doorway behind him like a cork in a bottle. His body ran the length of the kitchen past the fridge and the woodstove alcove, a long black slope of shoulder and ribs and flank rising higher than the kitchen counters, higher than the top of the fridge, higher than her head. He was lying with his forelegs folded under him like a cat, and even like that the ridge of his shoulders was up past the bottom of the cupboards. His head was raised on a neck as thick as a tree trunk, and it was carried low, bent down, because if he'd lifted it any higher his ears would have gone through the ceiling. As it was, the tips of them were brushing the bottom of the light fixture. The glass shade was swinging very slightly on its chain.

Black. Black all over. Black so deep it looked like a hole cut in the kitchen. And where the sun came through the window over the sink and fell across his shoulders, she could see the charcoal grey of the underfur through the black, like smoke under water.

He was making coffee.

The coffee maker was on the counter in front of him, under his nose, which was about the size of a dinner plate. One great black forepaw was resting on the counter beside the machine. The pad of it was as wide as a frying pan, and it was resting very lightly, so lightly the counter wasn't even creaking, and out of the end of one toe a single claw had slid out, black and curved and about as long as her finger. The very tip of it was pressing the button on the front of the machine.

He took it off.

The machine thought about it. Then it started to gurgle.

The cupboard door over the machine opened by itself. The can of Folgers was already out, lid off, sitting on the counter. Two mugs came floating out of the cupboard where the mugs lived when they weren't on hooks, one after the other, and drifted down through the air like leaves and settled on the counter side by side. The fridge door opened. The milk came out, sailed across the kitchen, and hung in the air above the first mug, tipped, poured a slug of milk into it, and righted itself. The drawer under the machine slid open. A spoon came out and went into the sugar bowl and came out heaped and went into the mug. And again. And again.

Three.

Under the cupboard, on its hook, the blue mug with the jumping steelhead on it hung exactly where it always hung, with the fish facing out. Nothing had touched it.

Leah looked at it for a second longer than she meant to.

The floor wasn't making a sound.

That was the thing her brain caught on, in the end, out of all of it. The floor. Her mom's kitchen floor was old linoleum over old boards, and it creaked if Seth walked across it. It creaked if *she* walked across it. There was a soft spot in front of the stove that groaned like a ship if you stood on it too long. And there was something lying on that floor that had to weigh more than her mom's car, and the floor wasn't making any sound at all. Not a groan. Not a tick. It was as quiet as if nobody was in the room.

Her knees wanted to go.

She felt them try. She felt the whole of her body try, all at once, from the bottom of her spine up. The same thing as the field. The thing that had made her tail go down. Something very old down in the bottom of her had taken one look at what was lying in her mom's kitchen and wanted to get down low and put its ears back and stay there.

She held on to the archway. She locked her knees. She stood there with her hand white on the frame and her heart slamming, and she did not go down.

The wolf turned his head.

He did it slowly, the great black head swinging round on the thick neck under the light fixture, and the shade bumped against the top of his skull and swung, and he ignored it. He looked at her over his own shoulder, down the length of the kitchen. His eyes were green. Bright, clear, ridiculous green. The same as the field, the same as across the blanket and the porch step.

They were right at her eye level, from where he was lying. He was lying down and they were right at her eye level.

His ears went back. Just slightly. Flat-ish. Then forward again.

"Morning," he said.

She heard it come out of him. She heard it come out of the wolf, out of that enormous black muzzle, out of a mouth with teeth in it as long as her fingers, and it was his voice. His exact voice. The low English voice from the field, a bit rough, a bit tired. The jaws barely moved. The words came out clean and clear, every one of them, with something underneath them like a truck idling a long way off, a rumble she could feel in the floor through the soles of her feet.

Leah's mouth opened.

"You can *talk.*"

"Apparently."

"We can't talk. Like that. None of us can. Not one, not ever—"

"I'd noticed. Your brother spent half an hour yesterday trying to tell me something with his eyebrows." He looked back at the coffee machine. The second mug was getting milk now, and one sugar. "Still don't know what it was."

She stood in the archway and stared at him.

"It's *Wednesday,*" she said.

"I know what day it is."

"The moon's not till tomorrow. You said. You said Thursday, you told my mom Thursday—"

"I did say Thursday." He watched the coffee drip. His tail, out in the hall, moved once against the floor. "I'm stood here in your mum's kitchen at ten o'clock on a Wednesday morning, looking like this, so I'd say we can both agree Thursday's gone a bit pear-shaped."

"Then *change back.*"

"Oh, brilliant. Why didn't I think of that." He swung his head round again to look at her, and this time the light shade bumped him on the ear and he flicked it, irritably, the way a horse flicks off a fly. "I tried at six. When I woke up. I tried at half six. I tried at seven, and at quarter past, and every quarter of an hour since, standing in the middle of your front room trying not to tread on the coffee table. Nothing. It's like pushing on a door somebody's bricked up from the other side." His ears went flat. "It's like a full moon night. Once it's out, it's out, I'm out, and I don't get to come back till the moon goes down and lets me. Except there's no moon. It's broad bloody daylight. It's sunny. It's the first sunny morning since I got here."

Leah didn't say anything. She couldn't think of anything to say.

The coffee machine gave a last gurgle and a hiss and went quiet. The pot lifted out of it by itself and poured, first one mug and then the other, neat and careful, and slid back onto the hot plate.

The wolf looked at the two mugs. Then he looked at her.

"I hope you know," he said, "I'm blaming you for this."

"*Me?*"

"You."

"I didn't do anything!"

"You went round the house."

The blood came up into her face so fast it hurt.

"I was *patrolling.*"

"You were patrolling this house."

"There are vampires in the woods—"

"There are vampires in Canada. There's one in Lake Crescent and one halfway to Oregon. There is not a vampire in your mum's woodpile." His muzzle wrinkled, very slightly, at the side, showing about three teeth. "There was a wolf going round and round under my window at three o'clock this morning. Six times."

"*Four.*"

"Six. I counted."

"I went round four times—"

"You went round six times, and on the fifth one you stopped by the back step and sang."

"I did *not sing.*"

"Howled, then. Long one. Low." He looked at her, and his ears went sideways. "Went straight through the wall. Straight through the walls I've got that aren't made of wood, either. Straight down to the bottom of me, where it's been lying for five years doing exactly what it's told, and it stood up and said *oh, hello,* and that was that. I sat on it all night. And at about half five this morning I woke up with my back legs already gone and got off the couch before I finished. Mostly." He glanced through the archway behind her, at the front room. "I've had a word with the spring."

Leah turned and looked at the couch. At the spring, standing up out of the middle of it, with the black tuft on the end.

She turned back.

"All I can think," the wolf said, "is your wolf got mine a bit excited. And Bob's your uncle."

She stared at him.

"Who the hell," Leah said, "is *Bob?*"

The wolf closed his eyes.

"It's a saying."

"Who's Bob?"

"Nobody's Bob. It means—*there you go.* It means *and then that happened.* It's what you say when you've—" He opened his eyes again. "Never mind Bob. Bob's not the point. Five years, Leah. Five years. Not one slip. Not one early night, not one late morning. Moon comes up, I change. Moon goes down, I change back. Regular as the tide. I've done Christmas like this. I've done six kids hanging off my ears in a back garden in Devon and never once put a paw wrong. Five *years*." He blew out a breath through his nose, and it went across the kitchen like a gust of wind and made the curtains with the fish on them flap. "And then you go and do laps of the house at three in the morning and sing at it, and here we are."

"I didn't *mean* to."

It came out of her before she could stop it. Too loud, and too high, and it sounded exactly like what it was.

The wolf looked at her.

"I didn't mean to," Leah said again, lower. Her hand was still white on the archway. "I meant to go south. I told my legs south. I stood at the edge of the trees and they just—turned round. I didn't decide anything. I was just—going round. And then I couldn't stop going round. And then I was singing. *Howling.* I didn't decide any of it, it just—"

She stopped.

Without the leave of either party, she thought.

She'd read it sitting on a tartan blanket in the snow. She hadn't thought about it since, because she'd been too busy thinking about everything else. *The beast in the one will answer to the beast in the other, in voice or in posture, without the leave of either party—*

"—and very frequently," the wolf said, "against the express wishes of both."

She looked at him.

He was looking back at her down the length of the kitchen with his head low under the light and his ears halfway between flat and forward. He'd been thinking the same thing. She could see it. She couldn't get into his head, she never would, but she could see that.

"It's in the book," she said.

"It's in the book."

"My wolf did it," she said slowly. "To yours."

"And mine answered. Rattled every mug in the kitchen." His tail moved in the hall again, a slow sweep across the boards. "I didn't decide that either. So." He tipped his head toward the counter. "I'm blaming you. You can blame me back. Saves time."

Leah stood there.

Then she let go of the archway. She did it one finger at a time; her hand had cramped. She stood up straight in the archway of her mom's kitchen in her bare feet, with the biggest thing she'd ever seen in her life lying three yards away from her filling up the entire room, and she didn't go down, and she didn't back up.

"Fine," she said. "Blame me. I'm blaming you for the couch."

"The couch was broken."

"It was broken in *one place.*"

"It's still only broken in one place," the wolf said. "It's just more obvious about it now."

She didn't laugh. She was very careful.

---

One of the mugs lifted off the counter. It came floating down the kitchen toward her at about chest height, steady as if it were on a tray, with the steam coming off the top of it, and stopped in the air in front of her.

She looked at it.

"Milk and three sugars," the wolf said.

"That was *tea.*"

"Kreacher gave you three. You drank it."

She looked at the mug a moment longer. Then she took it out of the air. It was hot through the china. It was the bank mug, the plain white one from Port Angeles. She held it in both hands and looked over the top of it at the steelhead mug hanging on its hook under the cupboard, with its fish facing out.

She drank some. It was terrible. Her mom's coffee had always been terrible. Three sugars made it worse.

She drank some more.

"I need to tell Sabine," the wolf said.

"Who?"

"My boss. The French one. Coffee, plait, patted me on the face." He was looking at the window now, at the sun coming in. "She said if it itched she wanted an owl. And the mirror's in my jacket pocket, and my jacket's—" He looked down at himself, at the long black slope of his own shoulder and flank, with an expression Leah would have called disgust on anything with a human face. "Wherever it goes. So."

Something came out of the air in front of him.

It was silver. That was the first thing. Silver and white and bright, brighter than the sun on the floor, so bright it left a mark on her eyes, and it came out of nothing at all and stepped down onto the linoleum between his forepaws on four slim legs, and it was a deer.

A stag. A big one, with a big spread of antlers, every point on them shining. It was see-through. She could see the stove through its flank. It turned its head and looked at her with eyes that were just light, and the tips of its antlers passed straight up through the bottom of the cupboards like they weren't there.

Leah backed up into the archway so fast she slopped coffee on her hand.

"Sabine," said the wolf, to the stag, low. "It itched. Don't say *weird.*"

The stag turned. It bounded once across the kitchen, light as anything, and went straight through the back door without opening it, and was gone. A faint silver glow hung in the air where it had been, and then that went too.

Leah looked at the back door. She looked at the wolf.

"That was a *deer.*"

"Stag."

"You're a wolf. You're the biggest wolf I've ever seen. And your—your ghost thing, whatever it is, is a *deer?*"

The wolf's ears went sideways.

"Long story," he said.

"Does it involve a straw?"

He turned his head and looked at her, and for a second something happened at the side of the great black muzzle, a pull at the corner of the lip, and she realised with a weird lurch that a wolf could do that. That his could, anyway.

"No," he said. "That one's a different long story."

---

The back door opened.

It opened about eight inches and hit something and stopped, and the something was the wolf's ribs. A face appeared in the gap at about the height of a doorknob, peering in sideways under the long black overhang of fur: wet hair, round cheeks, eyes the size of saucers. Seth. Seth in cut-offs, bare-chested and soaked to the knees, smelling of salt and pine and wet dog.

He looked at the black flank filling the doorway in front of his nose. He looked up. And up.

"*Whoa,*" Seth said.

"Morning, Seth," said the wolf.

"*WHOA.* Is that—is that *you?* You're *huge!* You're bigger than Sam! You're bigger than Sam and Jake put *together!* You *talk!* How do you *talk?* Leah, he *talks*—" Seth's head swivelled and found her in the archway, and his face lit up even further, which she wouldn't have thought possible. "Leah! Leah, you were *singing* to him last night! Jake said it was a song! Jake said he'd never heard you do anything like that in his whole—"

"*Seth,*" Leah said.

"—you went round the house *seven times*, I was counting, Jake was counting too, he said six but I said—"

"Seth, shut the door," said the wolf. "You're letting the heat out."

Seth shut the door. Then he opened it again about two inches and put one eye to the gap.

"Can I come in?"

"Not unless you go round," said the wolf. "I'm in the way."

Seth vanished. Leah heard him hit the porch and go thundering round the side of the house on the gravel, and the front door bang open, and his feet in the front room, and a long, reverent silence that she knew without looking meant he'd seen the couch.

"Oh, *man,*" Seth breathed, from the front room. "Mom's gonna kill you."

---

Her mom came down at ten past ten.

Leah heard her on the stairs. The third one, the seventh one. She heard her stop at the bottom, where the tail was. There was a pause. Then Leah heard the tail draw back across the hall floor out of her way, slowly, and her mom step over the tip of it anyway, out of habit, the way she stepped over the bad porch step, and come along the hall to the kitchen doorway.

She couldn't get in. The doorway was full of wolf.

There was a longer pause.

Then her mom's voice came from somewhere behind the black haunches, perfectly level.

"I heard the two of you last night."

Leah closed her eyes. "*Mom.*"

"The howling, Leah."

From the front room, Seth made a noise like a kettle.

"Morning, Sue," said the wolf, without turning his head. "Coffee's on. Sorry about the couch."

"Is it fixable?"

"Probably. Not by me. Not this morning."

"Hm." There was a shuffle, and the creak of the bad floorboard in the hall, and then her mom came round through the front room, past Seth, who was standing in the middle of the floor with his mouth open looking at the spring, and into the archway beside Leah. She was in her bathrobe, the old blue one, with her hair down and her reading glasses pushed up on top of her head, and she stood there next to Leah and looked down the length of her kitchen at the thing lying in it, from its haunches in the hall doorway to its head bent under the light fixture.

She looked at it for a long time. Her face didn't do anything. Leah watched it not do anything, the same way she'd watched it in the yard.

Then her mom's eyes went to the hook under the cupboard, where the blue mug was hanging with its fish facing out, and stayed there for a second, and came back.

"Is this Thursday?" she said.

"Wednesday, Sue," said the wolf. "That's the problem."

"I see." Her mom stepped past Leah into the kitchen. She went along the wall, between the table and his foreleg, which he drew in for her, and got her own mug down off its hook, and poured herself coffee from the pot, black, and stood there at the counter beside his enormous head and drank it, looking up at him. The top of her head came about level with his jaw.

"Billy's expecting you at eleven," she said.

"Yeah."

"You can't go up to Billy's like that."

"Doubt I'd fit in the truck."

Her mom drank some more coffee. Leah watched her think. She knew the look; it was the look she got at the council table, Seth said, when the old men had all finished shouting.

"Then Billy can come here," her mom said, and put down her mug and picked up the phone off the counter beside the bread bin, and dialled without looking.

It rang once.

"Billy," said Sue Clearwater. "Change of plan. You're coming here. Bring Quil." A pause. Leah could hear Billy's voice out of the phone from across the kitchen, high and fast. Her mom listened to it for a while with her eyes on the wolf. "No, Billy, he's here. He's—"

She looked up at the great black head bent under her kitchen light, and the green eyes looking back down at her.

"—he's not going anywhere," she said, and hung up on him.

Seth, in the front room, sat down on the floor.

---

Leah sat down at the table.

She didn't mean to. Her legs did it. She pulled out the chair against the wall, the one she'd sat in last night for dinner, and sat in it with the bank mug in both hands, three feet from his foreleg. It was the size of a fence post. The fur on it was so black the sun didn't seem to land on it so much as go into it. The paw at the end of it was resting on her mom's linoleum, flat and huge and quiet, with the claws all drawn back in.

She drank her terrible coffee.

Her mom was at the counter, getting eggs out of the fridge. All of them. Three cartons. She looked at the eggs, and then at the wolf, and then went back into the fridge for the bacon. Seth had come to the archway and was leaning in it with his arms folded and his whole face shining, looking from the wolf to Leah and back again, and for once in his life he wasn't saying anything at all, which was somehow worse.

Out in the hall, the wolf's tail moved.

It came up off the floor, slow, and came down again on the boards. *Thump.* Not hard. The house didn't shake. But Leah felt it in the legs of her chair, and in the table, and in the coffee in her mug, a tiny ring going out across the top of it. And she felt it somewhere else, too. Somewhere under her breastbone, where the line came out.

She looked at the tail. Then up at him.

He was looking at the tail too. He'd turned his head right round on his neck, the light shade bumping off his ear, and he was looking back along the whole length of his own body to the hall doorway, at the long black tail lying there on the floor, as if it were something that had followed him in off the street and he wasn't sure who it belonged to.

"Was that you," Leah said, "or it?"

The wolf looked at his tail for a moment longer.

Then he turned his head back round, and looked at her with his bright green eyes, and the rumble under his voice went very low.

"Ask me Friday," he said.
