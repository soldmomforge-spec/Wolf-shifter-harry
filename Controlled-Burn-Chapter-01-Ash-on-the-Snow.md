# Controlled Burn

## Chapter One: Ash on the Snow

---

The Volturi had stopped speaking aloud.

Bella could still hear them. Every vampire in the clearing could. Aro, Caius, and Marcus had drawn their heads together at the center of the formation, and the guard had closed around them in a ring of grey wool, and the words passing between the three ancients had dropped to a register that was mostly breath. *Danger. Unknown. The child grows. We cannot know what she will become.* Caius did most of the talking. Aro did most of the listening, which was worse.

Thirty-two cloaks on the snow. She'd counted them four times since they came out of the trees, and the number hadn't changed, and she kept counting anyway because it gave the newborn part of her brain something to do besides scream.

The snow had come the night before. It lay over the clearing in a clean, unbroken sheet except where feet had broken it, and the sky above was a hard, cloudless winter blue, the last day of the year, but the sun hadn't cleared the ridge to the east yet. The whole field sat in shadow the color of skim milk. Every breath of air tasted of frozen pine and the sweet, heavy ghost of Irina's smoke. Kate and Tanya had stopped making noise about Irina. Somehow that was worse than the noise had been.

Renesmee was on Bella's hip, her small weight nothing, her heartbeat going like a bird's against Bella's ribs. The backpack Bella had packed for her—passports, cash, the documents from J. Jenks—hung off her shoulders, straps cinched tight. If it came to it, Jacob would take her and run. That had been the plan for two weeks. Bella hated the plan with everything she had, and she held on to it the way she held on to her daughter.

Jacob stood at her side, close enough that the heat coming off his fur moved the hair on her arm. The russet bulk of him blocked half her view of the left flank. She could hear his breathing—slow, deliberate, the breathing of something big deciding not to move yet.

She held the shield.

It was stretched as far as she'd ever stretched it, a thin elastic skin over her family and their witnesses and the wolves, out past the last of Sam's pack where they stood in a loose, bristling line along the trees. It hummed at its edges the way a muscle hums after too long under load. Twice already she'd felt Jane push against it—a pressure like a thumb on a bruise—and twice it had held. Edward stood at her right shoulder. She didn't look at him. She didn't need to look at him to know what his face was doing.

Across the field, one of the guard spoke up, a little louder. Bella didn't know his name. "We vote, then."

"Yes," Aro murmured. "Let us be orderly."

Edward's hand found the small of her back. *They're going to fight,* that touch said. *Caius has the numbers.*

Then something cracked like a rifle shot in the middle of the clearing, and there was a woman standing in the snow.

There'd been nothing there. Bella's eyes didn't miss things anymore—she'd been looking straight across that ground a quarter of a second earlier, at the trampled no-man's-land between the two lines—and there'd been nothing. Now there was a woman sixty yards from Bella and sixty yards from Aro, and the air around her was still shivering with the echo of the crack.

Every head in the field turned. On the left, a wolf snarled. On the right, two of the Volturi's witnesses flinched back so hard they collided.

The woman was tall. Six foot, give or take, broad through the shoulders, with black hair tied back in a messy knot at the base of her skull and coming loose around her face. A battered brown leather jacket over a dark henley, jeans, scuffed boots. Green eyes; Bella could see the color at sixty yards, and the faint lines at the corners of them. She looked somewhere between twenty-five and thirty. She had a paper coffee cup in one hand. Bella could read the logo on it. *Thriftway.*

Her heart was beating. Slow and even, sixty-something a minute. Bella heard it the way she heard Renesmee's, a living sound in a field full of dead ones. Then the breeze shifted and the woman's scent came across the snow—pine, turned earth, cold stone, and under it something with a great deal of heat in it—and Bella braced for the burn in her throat that came with every human she'd been near since September.

It didn't come. The scent went down like air.

Around the woman's boots, the snow was going soft and grey, sagging into slush.

"I can't hear her," Edward said, very quietly.

Bella's head turned an inch toward him before she could stop it.

"Nothing," he said. "Like you. Except there's something there, and I can't get through it."

The woman finished her coffee. She tipped her head back to get the last of it, made a small, unimpressed face at the cup, and the cup vanished between one frame of Bella's vision and the next. She looked left, at the Cullen line and the wolves. She looked right, at the Volturi. She took her time about it.

"Morning," she said.

English. Southern English, a little rough on the vowels. Her voice carried across the snow without her raising it much.

"Which one of you lot's in charge? No—hang on, don't tell me." She squinted at the knot of black cloaks in the middle of the Volturi formation. "Bloke in the middle. Long hair. Looks like he's about to tell me he's been expecting me."

Aro had already stepped forward through his guard. His face was alight. Bella had seen that expression on him once already this morning, when he'd held Renesmee's hand and seen her memories, and it had frightened her more than anything Caius had done.

"A witch," Aro said. He breathed it. "Caius—Marcus—a witch. Here. How extraordinary."

The woman reached into her jacket. Every one of the guard tensed. She came out with a leather wallet, flipped it open one-handed, and held it up at shoulder height the way a cop on television held a badge. Bella could see the inside of it from where she stood: a seal pressed in silver, a ring of script she couldn't read around a device she didn't recognize, a line of black lettering beneath.

"Unspeakable," the woman said. "Department of Mysteries, seconded to the International Confederation of Wizards. And yeah, Merlin's pants, I'm a witch, well spotted, gold star." She snapped the wallet shut and put it away. "Right. Listen up, because I'm only saying this once and I've had about four hours' sleep. There's a town four miles that way—" she pointed east without looking "—with a school and a hospital and a police chief and a Thriftway, and you've brought two armies and the best part of a hundred spectators to a field just outside it. That's a breach of the Statute of Secrecy whichever way you slice it. So, by order of the International Confederation of Wizards, this gathering is dispersed."

Silence.

"That means everyone goes home," she said. "Pale ones, that way. Furry ones, that way." She hooked a thumb over her shoulder at the black cloaks without turning around. "And the Hammer Horror lot back to Italy."

Somewhere on the left, in Sam's pack, a wolf made a noise that might have been a cough. Behind Bella, Emmett made a noise that definitely was.

Caius came forward to stand at Aro's shoulder. His white hair lay flat and fine as cobweb against the black of his cloak. "The Confederation," he said, every syllable clipped off with a knife, "has no authority over our kind."

"It has this morning, Legolas."

Caius's face didn't change. Bella, who had spent the last hour watching it, saw the muscles along his jaw lock.

"There has been an understanding," Aro said, gently, as if gently were the only way he knew how to speak, "between the Confederation and my family for a very long time. We do not trouble your world. Your world does not trouble ours. I confess I had thought it would outlast us all." He spread his hands. "We are here on a matter of grave importance to our own law. There is a child—"

"I can see the kid." The woman's eyes didn't move to Renesmee, but Bella felt the attention land on her daughter like a hand. "She's got a heartbeat. I can hear it from here."

"As can I," Aro said. "That is the very question we are deliberating."

"Deliberate in Italy."

"Ah." Aro smiled. He took another step toward her, and another, out ahead of the guard, until Renata glided after him with her hand hovering at his back. He extended one hand, palm up, the paper-fine fingers open. "Forgive me. I find that so much talking can be saved, between friends, if I might simply—"

"Keep your hands in your sleeves, Elvira."

Aro stopped.

"I've read your file," the woman said. "You touch me, you'll be drawing back a stump."

Bella felt Edward go still against her. In her arms, Renesmee had twisted round to look at the stranger with her whole face, her brown eyes wide, one small fist bunched in the collar of Bella's sweater.

"Jane," Caius said.

It came out flat and almost bored, the way someone would ask for the salt. Jane was already looking at the woman. She had been looking at the woman since the second she'd appeared, her pale, pretty child's face very calm, and now she smiled.

Bella's shield didn't reach that far. She'd felt Jane's power slide up against the edge of it all morning; she knew its shape the way she knew the shape of the fear in her own stomach. She thought of the woman standing out there alone—*she's human, she's got a heart, she'll go down screaming*—and before she'd decided anything she was pushing a long thin tongue of the shield out across the snow toward the stranger.

It reached her and slid off. Like trying to wrap a sheet around something that was already wearing one.

Jane smiled. The woman stood there.

"Something on my face, Wednesday?" she said.

Jane's smile thinned. Her eyes narrowed to slits. Bella could hear—everyone could hear—the tiny involuntary sound Jane made in her throat as she leaned into it.

The woman tilted her head. "Was that meant to do something?"

Beside Jane, Alec raised his hands, and the mist came out of him—that thin, curling, heavy mist Bella had heard about and never seen—pouring onto the snow and rolling outward in a low wave toward the stranger. It moved slowly. It looked like nothing at all. Bella knew that once it touched you, you would stop feeling anything, stop seeing, stop hearing, while they took you apart.

The woman looked down at it.

The mist stopped. Its leading edge folded back on itself, curled, and lay there quivering eight feet from her boots like a wave that had hit a wall nobody could see.

"No," the woman said.

Aro had gone completely still. His eyes were wide and shining. "Extraordinary," he whispered. Bella heard Edward's breath catch. *He wants her,* Edward breathed, almost no sound at all. *He wants her more than he ever wanted Alice.*

"Last chance," the woman said. "Turn round. Walk."

Caius didn't look at Aro. "Felix," he said. "Demetri."

They went.

Felix was the size of a door and Demetri was faster, and they came off the front of the Volturi line at a speed that would have been a blur to Bella six months ago, two dark shapes splitting to come at her from both sides. Snow exploded behind them in twin rooster tails. Somebody on the Cullen side shouted. Jacob's whole body coiled.

The woman lifted her right hand to shoulder height and snapped her fingers.

Afterward, Bella would understand that it had taken exactly one second. At the time, with her new eyes and her new brain and that terrible endless room inside her head where a single moment could stretch and unfold like a flower, she watched every part of it.

It started inside them. All of them at once—Felix in the air, Demetri a stride behind him, Aro with his hand still half lifted, Caius with his mouth open on the next name, Jane and Alec, Renata at Aro's back, Chelsea, Afton, Heidi, Santiago, Corin, the whole silent ring of the guard, the cloaked shapes at the rear whose names she had never been told—a light came up in the center of each body, behind the breastbone, white-gold and too bright to look at. It spread outward. It went through them the way a lamp goes through paper held up in front of it. Their skin, that hard white stone that didn't break, turned translucent; she saw, for a sliver of an instant, the shadow-shapes of the bones inside Aro's lifted hand.

She saw Aro's face. It was still curious.

She saw Marcus. Marcus, who had stood at the back of the three all morning looking at nothing, who had looked at nothing for longer than Bella's country had existed—Marcus's eyes closed. They closed slowly, as if he were settling into a chair. Bella would never be able to say for certain what she saw on his face in the next hundredth of a second. She would think about it for a long time.

Then the fire came out through their skin.

No smoke yet. No sound. Thirty-two columns of white flame, each one the exact shape of the vampire it had been, standing in the snow: Felix a burning silhouette in mid-leap; Demetri's burning foot coming down. Then the shapes lost their edges. Then they fell in on themselves.

The second ended.

Felix's ash hit the snow at a run. It kept going, carried by his momentum, and sprayed across the ground in a fan of grey powder that stopped a yard short of the woman's boots. Demetri's lay in a long scorched stripe where he'd been. Out across the Volturi line, thirty grey heaps sat steaming on black, bare ground, the snow around each one flashed off into vapor right down to the dirt. Small things glinted in the ash—a clasp, a chain, a ring—and hissed as they cooled.

The smoke came last. Thick and purple, rolling up off the ash in heavy, slow coils, and the smell reached the clearing a breath behind it: sweet, cloying, like incense left burning in a closed room.

The woman in the leather jacket put her hand back in her pocket.

For one more heartbeat—Renesmee's; Bella counted it—nobody in the clearing moved.

Then the Volturi's witnesses broke.

There had been forty-odd of them, strangers the Volturi had collected on their way to watch justice done, and they went in every direction at once. One woman screamed. A man in a long coat turned so fast he slid in the snow and went down on one hand and was up and gone into the trees before Bella's eye could follow. A pair of them ran straight toward the Cullen line before they saw where they were going and veered off at a sharp angle into the timber. The sound of them crashing through the forest went on a long time, branches cracking, getting fainter.

"That's it!" the woman shouted after them. She cupped her hands round her mouth. "Off you pop! Tell your mates!"

The crashing got fainter.

She dropped her hands. Bella heard her let out a long breath through her nose, like someone who'd just finished putting up a shelf.

Then she turned around to face the rest of them.

---

Jacob's head was full of noise.

With five of them in the pack there was always noise. This was the kind that came off a crowd when a car went through a storefront: everyone at once, no words, just the shape of shock bouncing around the inside of his skull.

*—what the—*

*—she just—all of them, all of them, Jake, did you see Jane, did you SEE—*

*—is that smoke? Is that them? That's them—*

*Shut up,* Jacob thought, and put some of the alpha into it, and the noise flattened. *Everybody shut up and hold.*

They held. Leah on his far left, grey and narrow and quivering. Quil and Embry a little behind, two big dark shapes shoulder to shoulder. Seth on the end of the line, next to Leah.

Seth's head had been quiet since the crack.

Jacob noticed it now the way you'd notice a radio that had been playing in another room and then stopped: an absence he'd been too busy to clock. The kid's mind had been a nervous babble all morning—*okay okay okay we can do this, Jake's got it, Edward's got it, oh man that's a lot of them*—and then the crack had come, and in place of the babble there'd been one long note. Like a wire somebody had plucked and left humming. Jacob had shoved it aside. There'd been thirty-two Volturi.

There were no Volturi now.

He looked at the ash. He couldn't help it. His wolf eyes picked out the heaps of it on the scorched dirt in more detail than he wanted: some grey, some almost white, the lumps already settling as the heat went out of them. The purple smoke was drifting their way on the breeze, thick enough to taste. It tasted the way all of them smelled, that sweet burnt-sugar stink that made his lip curl, times thirty-two.

He'd come here to die. He'd been pretty sure about it. He'd figured on maybe taking Felix with him if he got lucky, and on Nessie getting far enough away on his back before somebody caught them. He'd thought about it a lot last night, lying in front of the Cullens' fireplace with her asleep against his side, her hand on his ribs showing him dreams about snow.

The woman in the leather jacket was looking at them.

She'd turned around in one motion and planted her boots and was standing in the slush, looking along the line: the Cullens, the witnesses in their clumps, the wolves. At sixty yards his eyes could see her as clear as if she were across a kitchen table. She had a long face and a sharp nose and a mouth that looked like it laughed a lot when there was something to laugh at. There was nothing to laugh at right now and she didn't. Her heart hadn't sped up. Jacob could hear it under everything else in the clearing. Sixty-two, sixty-three a minute. The heartbeat of somebody standing in line at the post office.

She didn't smell like a leech. She didn't smell like a person, quite, either. The wolf in him had a word for it, and the word was *other*, and the fur along his spine had been standing up since she'd arrived and wasn't going down.

"Right," she said. "That's them sorted. Now you lot."

Carlisle stepped out of the Cullen line. He moved slowly, hands open at his sides where she could see them, the way he'd walked out to the Volturi an hour ago. Jacob could have told him how much good that had done.

"My name is Carlisle Cullen," he said. "We—"

"Same order," the woman said, over him. "Disperse. Go home."

"We live here," Carlisle said.

"Then go home. Shouldn't take long." She tipped her chin at the witnesses. "Your mates can go home too. Bit of a trek for some of them, I'd imagine, but they've got legs."

Carlisle's mouth opened and closed. Jacob had never seen him do that.

On the right end of the Cullen side, past the Amazons, the Romanians were staring at the ash.

Jacob had never liked the Romanians. Nobody had. They'd turned up two weeks ago looking like a couple of dried-out scarecrows and spent every minute since talking about the Volturi the way a drunk talked about an ex-wife. They'd come to watch Aro fall. They'd said so, over and over, to anybody who'd stand still: fifteen hundred years they'd waited to see it.

They'd seen it.

Stefan's face was doing something Jacob didn't have a word for. His mouth was moving.

"Fifteen hundred years," Stefan said out loud. His voice cracked on it like dry wood. "Fifteen *hundred* years."

Vladimir put a hand on his arm. "Stefan."

"She took it." Stefan's red eyes came up off the ash and found the woman in the jacket. "Some—*child*—with a little badge—"

*Uh oh,* Embry thought.

Stefan went.

He went the way a vampire went when it meant it: from standing still to sixty miles an hour with nothing in between, a low, flat, ugly launch across the snow with his lips skinned back off his teeth. Jacob saw the snow kick up behind him. Saw the woman's eyes slide left to track him, unhurried.

The sky cracked open.

There wasn't a cloud anywhere. Jacob had been looking at that sky all morning: pale clean winter blue, empty from one edge to the other. Out of the middle of it, straight down, came a white line so bright it burned itself on the backs of his eyes and stayed there in a purple afterimage, and it hit Stefan in the middle of his leap thirty yards from the woman's boots.

The noise came a hair behind: one flat, enormous slam, like the sky was a door and someone had kicked it in. He felt it in his chest and his teeth and the pads of his paws. Every wolf on the line flinched. Half of Sam's pack went down into a crouch. Somebody yelped.

When Jacob could see again, there was a circle of bare, black, smoking earth in the snow, eight feet across. A shallow crater, the dirt in the middle of it fused into something glassy that ticked as it cooled. The air stank of ozone, sharp enough to sting the inside of his nose.

Stefan wasn't anywhere.

A fine grey dust was coming down over the crater, slow, out of the clear sky. Like snow. That was all.

Vladimir screamed.

It went on a long time. It was the worst sound Jacob had ever heard come out of anything, and he'd heard Bella in that bed, back in September. When it stopped, Vladimir was on his knees at the edge of the crater with his hands spread over the glassy dirt, not touching it, and he didn't make another sound.

The woman hadn't moved. Her hands were in her jacket pockets.

"Anyone else?" she said.

Nobody moved.

Jacob looked down the line of the Cullens' witnesses. He'd gotten to know them some these last weeks, whether he'd wanted to or not. Garrett, standing with his hands loose and his eyes narrowed to slits—Garrett would have gone at the Volturi alone if Carlisle had let him, and he wasn't moving now. The Irish woman, Siobhan, had her arm across the girl Maggie like a seatbelt. The Amazons had gone still the way snakes go still. And Benjamin, the young Egyptian, who could pull water up out of the ground and raise a wind in a closed room, was staring up at the sky. Then at the woman. Then at the sky. His uncle Amun had a hand clamped on his shoulder hard enough to dent a car.

*Benjamin couldn't do that,* Jacob thought. *Could he? Nobody could do that.*

*Nobody did,* Leah thought back, sharp and shaky. *She did. She didn't even take her hands out of her pockets.*

*Jake,* Quil thought. *Jake, what do we do?*

*Hold.*

*Hold for what? She just—*

*I said hold.*

Across the clearing, Sam's black wolf had come up out of his crouch and was standing stiff-legged at the front of his line, head low. Jacob couldn't hear Sam's pack anymore, not since the split, but he didn't need to. Paul's silver hackles were up so high he looked twice his size. Jared and Collin and Brady and the rest of the young ones were bunched tight behind him, all of their eyes on the stranger. Every one of them was waiting for somebody to tell them what to do. Sam kept glancing across the field, at Jacob.

Jacob didn't have anything to tell him.

Seth's head was still humming. One long, bright note. Jacob reached for it, tried to get a word out of it, and got something too big to fit through the pack-sense, like trying to see the whole ocean through a keyhole. The kid's body was rigid. His eyes hadn't moved off the woman since she'd shown up.

*Seth?* Leah thought. *Seth. Look at me. SETH.*

Seth didn't look at her.

Something cold went down Jacob's back, under the fur, under everything. He knew that note. He'd been the one playing it, back in September, in a room full of blood.

He didn't get to finish the thought, because the woman's eyes had moved again.

They went along the Cullen line, past Carlisle, past Esme and Emmett and Rosalie. Past Edward. They stopped on Bella, and then they dropped about eight inches and stopped on Nessie.

Jacob felt it like a draft. The woman's head tilted a little to one side.

Nessie was looking back at her. She'd been looking at the stranger the whole time, fascinated, the way she looked at everything new: mouth a little open, brown eyes huge under the bronze curls. She didn't look scared. She had one fist wrapped in Bella's collar and she was leaning out to see better.

Bella's arms tightened around her.

Jacob was moving before he'd decided to. One step, then two, his shoulder coming around in front of Bella's legs, his head going low and his lips peeling up off his teeth. The growl started somewhere down around his ribs.

The woman's eyes didn't come off Nessie's face.

The kid had brown eyes. The colour of strong tea.

Her mum's were red, gone dark and rusty round the rims, and Layla had looked from one pair to the other from across the field and done a bit of maths, and the maths hadn't come out to anything she liked.

The kid's heart was going at about a hundred and forty. Fast even for a toddler. There was colour in her cheeks. She smelled of blood—her own, the thick salt-iron smell of something alive—and over it, on her hair and her clothes and on the inside of every breath, the cold sugared smell of the rest of them. Two smells that had no business coming off one body. The file in Layla's head on this kid—the one Croaker had handed her at four o'clock that morning, London time—had consisted of a single line from an intercepted rumour, *immortal child, Forks, Washington, the Cullens*, and underneath it the word *verify* in Croaker's handwriting.

Layla looked at the kid. The kid looked at her.

Between them there was a wall.

She'd felt it go up when the little blonde girl tried her party trick—a skin of something stretched out over the whole left side of the field, pale and taut and tingling at the edges, the bit of it nearest her flexing in and out like a lung. At one point it had reached out to her, a long thin tongue of it sliding across the snow, and touched her own shields, and backed off. She'd let it. Now she followed it back to where it came from and found the mum standing at the root of it with everything she had poured into keeping it up. A shield. Mental. Strong, too. Untrained—it had the sloppy edges of something run on raw power and fear—but strong.

The big red wolf was coming round in front of the mum's legs, growling. Layla let him.

She went in.

She didn't push at the shield. Pushing was for people who'd learned this out of a book. She'd learned it from a man who'd lived inside her head for seventeen years and taught her, by example, what a mind felt like from the inside—how it was put together, where the joins were—and she'd learned the rest by doing it, nearly every week for eight years since, to people who didn't want her to. She found the place in the shield where it ran thinnest. Over the kid, as it happened, where the mum had wrapped it tight round her daughter first and then flung it out wide to cover everyone else, so it stretched thin at the seams. Layla put an edge on her attention and went through.

It parted. It made no sound. It had been built by someone who'd only ever needed to stop people shoving, and she wasn't shoving. It left a line behind her, the way a scalpel does.

The mum flinched. Layla felt it, a jolt down the whole length of the shield, like someone treading on a garden hose.

Then she was in the kid's head.

It was bright in there.

That was the first thing. Kids' heads were always bright—everything new, nothing filed—but this was the brightness of a room with every light on and the curtains open and somebody shining a torch in your face. Everything the kid had ever seen sat right there on the surface, perfectly sharp, with no fuzz on any of it. Layla had been in a lot of heads and most of them kept their memories like a pile of photographs left out in the rain. This one kept them like film in a vault.

She moved carefully. She'd been inside children's heads three times before, for work, and she'd hated it every time. They had no walls. Anything you knocked over stayed knocked over.

She didn't knock anything over.

*Who are you?*

The question came at her straight off the top of the kid's mind, clear and curious and not frightened, in a voice that was all thought and no words. The kid was looking out of her own eyes at a stranger in a leather jacket and wondering, that was all, and the wondering was loud.

Layla left it alone and went sideways, down, into the stacks.

*Dark.* The first thing in there was dark, and close, and a sound like a drum, and through the dark a voice—*Momma*, the kid's mind supplied, soft as a hand—and another voice, lower, that came through the drum-dark and made the kid feel safe. *Daddy.* Then the dark getting tighter, and tighter, and Momma's voice changing, going thin and wet and hurt, and the kid frightened in the wordless way of something that didn't know what frightened meant yet, and wanting *out*—

Red. Light. A lot of red and a lot of light and noise, and a face over her.

Layla didn't stay on that. She didn't need to. She'd seen enough births and enough of the other thing to know what she'd glanced at, and a cold, careful part of her made a note: *came out hard. Very hard. Mum shouldn't have lived through it.* And next to the red light, at the edge of everything: the mum's face. Human. Brown-eyed. Grey and slack and going.

Next. Faster now, skimming.

A big red wolf, enormous to the kid, with a smell she loved—*Jacob*—who let her ride him through the trees. A big hand on her hair. The word *Nessie* in a deep voice that was grinning. A grey-haired man with a moustache who smelled of coffee and gun oil and made her heart do a happy jump—*Grandpa.* A golden-haired doctor holding a tape measure against her and frowning at it. A tall blonde woman who carried her everywhere. A small dark-haired woman who danced. A deer in the snow, and the hot rush of its throat, and the kid being proud of herself.

The kid putting her hand on people's faces and showing them things. Layla slowed on that. Watched it happen a few times from the inside—the kid's palm against a cheek, and a memory flowing out down her arm and *into* somebody. A gift. Like the mum's shield. Like the blonde girl's.

The black cloaks. That was recent. Weeks of everybody being frightened and talking quietly over the kid's head. This morning: the field, the snow. A thin old man with skin like onion paper, holding her hand and looking at her memories with his mouth open, *delighted*, and the kid not liking him and not knowing why. A blonde woman, a stranger, held between two of the cloaks. Fire. Smoke the colour of plums.

Then the kid saw Layla appear in the snow. Saw the fire. Saw the old man burn.

Layla stopped skimming.

The kid's mind had snagged on that, on the old man's lifted hand going see-through with the light inside it. It had hit her hard and she didn't know what to do with it yet. It sat there in all that brightness, a small cold knot.

Layla looked at it for a moment. Then she backed out, slow, touching nothing.

*Who:* Renesmee Carlie Cullen, the stacks said, in a dozen different voices. Three and a half months old by the kid's own count of days, going by the calendar on a kitchen wall she'd seen in one of the memories. Looked about three. Mum and Dad at the front of everything, the red wolf right up beside them.

*What:* No idea. That was the trouble. The kid knew she was different. She knew the word *half*, because she'd heard the grown-ups use it. She knew she drank blood and liked it and ate eggs and hated them. She knew Momma had been *different* before, with a heartbeat, and then she wasn't. She didn't know how she'd happened. She was three and a half months old.

Layla came back up through the shield the way she'd gone in. The line she'd cut tried to close behind her, slow, and didn't quite manage it.

Back in her own eyes, she blinked once. Out in the world, about two seconds had gone by.

The kid was still looking at her. Puzzled, now, as if she'd felt a draft. Her small hand came up and touched her own forehead, uncertain.

The mum's head snapped round.

Layla felt the shield slam in. Every bit of it that had been spread across the field came yanking back at once, like a fishing net hauled up out of the water, and wrapped itself round the kid as thick as it would go, layer on layer, until it hummed. The mum's lips peeled back. Her eyes came up and found Layla's across the snow, and they were about as angry as Layla had ever seen a pair of eyes get.

"*Get out of her,*" the mum said. Her voice cracked like ice in the middle of it.

*I'm out, love,* Layla thought. *Now you.*

She'd had a long night. She'd had four hours' kip in a chair in the Department, then a Portkey to Seattle that had spun her like a washing machine, then the drive, then an hour sat in a hire car in the Thriftway car park drinking bad coffee and waiting for the Volturi to show. She'd just burned down a royal family. And these people had been hiding a kid born out of a dying woman for three and a half months, and getting their friends to come and stand in a field and die for it, and Layla wanted to know how. She had no patience left for doing it nicely.

She didn't bother finding a seam this time.

She went straight at the mum, through the middle of the shield, where it was thickest. It held for a fraction of a second. Then it split. She felt it go—a sense of tearing, of something with a lot of give being forced past where it could give—and she was through, and out in the world the mum was making a noise, and Layla didn't care.

The mum's head was a mess.

Layla had been told vampires' heads were roomy and she'd believed it, but this was something else. Two minds, stacked: the old one and the new one. The new one on top—cold, huge, sharp as the kid's, three and a half months of it in brilliant detail—and under it, sunk like a village under a reservoir, eighteen years of human memory gone soft and greenish and blurred, as though she was seeing it through water. And all of it, top to bottom, screaming at her to get out.

She didn't sort it. She grabbed.

Underwater. Human. A boy with bronze hair in a biology classroom: *Edward.* Hands. A meadow. A wedding dress. White sand, an island, a dark house on a beach. Feathers everywhere, a broken bed. Then—two weeks later, the memory said, *two weeks*—being sick over a bathroom sink and looking at her own stomach in the mirror and watching it *move*.

Layla shoved through. Faster.

The belly getting huge. Weeks, not months. A spine like a string of beads under skin gone grey. The bronze-haired husband with his face turned to stone. A tall blonde—*Rosalie*—planted beside a sofa like a sentry. Ribs cracking, one, then another, the mum feeling each one go. Then a polystyrene cup with a lid and a bendy straw, held to her lips by the golden-haired doctor, and a smell coming off it that was good, *so good*, and the mum drinking it down, human blood, while the boy—*Jacob*, the boy who was the red wolf—sat in the corner of the room watching her with his face pulled tight like he was going to be sick.

Then the birth. Layla didn't slow down for it. She caught it the way you'd catch a car crash out of the corner of your eye: the mum on a table, a snap she heard from inside her own body and everything below her waist going away; blood coming up in her mouth; the husband's face, and his teeth; then a syringe going into her heart, and fire. Three days of fire. The mum lying still in it because she'd decided to. Layla registered that too, and moved past it.

The new mind. Sharp as glass.

Waking up. Thirst. The husband. The kid, in the blonde's arms, and the mum not trusting herself to hold her. Then holding her.

And the red wolf, the boy, Jacob, standing too close to the kid. Looking at her like she was the sun coming up. The mum not understanding—*why is he looking at her like that*—and then understanding, and then—

—*You IMPRINTED on my DAUGHTER?*

Layla slowed down.

—*You nicknamed my daughter after the Loch Ness Monster?*

The mum, fresh-turned and strong as a lorry, going for the boy's throat out on the front lawn of a big white house in the trees. A lanky sandy-haired kid—Layla knew the face, it had been in the toddler's memories too, grinning, chucking a ball—throwing himself between them, and the mum's hands hitting him. The crack of his shoulder going. His collarbone. A noise out of him like a kicked dog.

Something in Layla's chest moved at that, low down. She noticed it. She kept going.

The boy, Jacob, explaining. On the lawn, on the porch, in the kitchen, with his hands spread, saying the same things over and over until the mum stopped trying to kill him. *Imprinting.* That was the word. A wolf saw somebody and that was it: the ground moved under him, everything he'd been tied to before was cut loose, and this one person was what held him down. *Like gravity,* the boy said, in the mum's memory. *She's what holds me to the planet now.* No choice in it. No switching it off. And after that: whatever she needed. *Whatever she needs me to be.* A brother. A friend. A protector.

And later, when she was old enough—

The mum's memory had the boy's face in it, right there, going red, going stubborn.

Layla rummaged harder, dragging more of it up. There was more. There was a list. The tribe's alpha, a man called Sam, had seen a woman called Emily and dropped his girlfriend on the spot, and the girlfriend was the grey wolf at the end of the line with a face like a slapped arse—Leah. Another one, Quil, had imprinted on a two-year-old. *Two.* Claire. The mum had held the kid, Claire, at a bonfire on a beach. Quil gave her piggybacks. *Whatever she needs.*

Layla stood in the mum's head for a second, perfectly still.

Then she went back to the start of the boy's explanation—the bit she'd skimmed over, the first time, because she'd been looking for something else.

*You see her,* the boy said, in the mum's memory, *and everything just stops. You can't look anywhere else.*

The crack of her own Apparition, ten minutes ago, echoing off the trees.

The line of wolves on the left of the field, every one of them swinging its head round to her. One of them—a lanky, sandy-coloured one at the end, next to the grey—going stiff as a post and staring. Staring, and staring, and not looking anywhere else.

The hook.

It had caught her in the chest about a second after she'd landed. She'd felt it go in, small and hard and hot, like a fishhook through a jumper, and she'd filed it under *ward—hostile—deal with later*, because she'd had the Volturi to deal with first and whatever it was hadn't been hurting. She'd forgotten about it. It was still there.

She came out of the mum's head like she'd been fired out of it.

The world came back. The snow, the smoke, the ash. The mum was down on one knee in the slush with her free hand over her face and her other arm still locked round her kid. Her husband stood half a stride from her with one arm reaching out, frozen in place, every muscle in his body cranked tight and not one of them moving. Layla had pinned him a second and a half ago when he'd come for her, and she'd done it without looking.

She kept him there. She'd get to him.

"Fuck me sideways," she said.

It came out quite quietly. Nobody answered.

She didn't look at the sandy wolf. She shut her eyes and went the other way instead—in, down, into her own head, which was a place she knew very well and went to as little as she could.

It looked like it always looked. She'd built it like a house, on Snape's instructions, back when she was fifteen and Snape was still giving lessons, and she'd built it badly and knocked it down and built it again until it held: long corridors, doors that locked, thick walls. Nothing came in here that she didn't let in. That was the point. That had been the whole point of all of it.

There was a line coming in through the wall.

It came in through the front, through the middle of the outermost wall of her, and the wall hadn't even noticed. No breach, no damage, no crack in the plaster. The line went through the way light goes through a window. It was thin and taut and silver-bright and it hummed when she looked at it, one long note, like a wire somebody had plucked. She followed it back out with her mind's eye just far enough to see where it went: across the snow, across sixty yards, into the chest of a sandy-coloured wolf at the end of the line, where it was sunk right in, right through the middle of him, so deep it looked like it had always been there.

Then she turned round and followed it the other way. In.

It went past the front rooms. That would've been bad enough. It went past the rooms behind those, where she kept the things she'd been told and the things she knew, through doors that should have been locked and were locked and didn't stop it. Past the rooms where she kept Sirius, and Remus, and Tonks, and Fred. Past the cupboard. Down the stairs. Down further than that, past where the house stopped being a house at all and turned into something older and darker—earth and stone and the smell of pine needles—a part of herself she had never built and had never been able to build anything over.

The line went down into that, deeper than it had any right to go, and stopped.

Something had hold of the end of it.

It lay in the dark the way it always did, a long low shape blacker than the dark around it. Midnight fur, charcoal underneath. A head the size of a car door resting on its forepaws. The line came down out of the dark and ran between those forepaws, and the wolf had one paw laid across it, heavy and deliberate, the way you'd put your hand on something that was yours.

It lifted its head. Its eyes were green. Her eyes.

It looked at her. She looked at it.

She'd half expected it to be angry. She'd seen it angry. It had the look it got when it had caught something interesting on the wind and already made up its mind about it and was only waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

Then it put its head back down on its forepaws, with the line under them, and shut its eyes.

Layla opened hers.

The sandy wolf was still staring at her from the far end of the line. Long legs, big paws, a young face, the fur on his neck all on end. He was trembling. She could see it from here.

"Christ," she said. "Poor bastard. Balto's got no idea what he's tied himself to."

She let out a long breath.

Then she held up two fingers of her right hand, and there was a cigarette between them that hadn't been there a second before. She looked at the end of it. It caught. A small orange coal, a curl of grey smoke, the smell of it cutting clean through the purple sweetness hanging over the field. She put it in her mouth and dragged on it, deep, and held it, and let it out slow through her nose.

She let the bronze-haired one go. He dropped to his knees beside his wife and had his arms round her and the kid both before he'd finished landing.

Layla looked at the three of them. Then at the red wolf in front of them, still growling, low and steady, like an engine left idling. Then down the line to the sandy one at the end.

"Fuck me," she said, to the whole field. "You lot have got a *fucked*-up way of making babies."

For three seconds, he had heard her.

Edward knelt in the slush with his wife and his daughter inside his arms, and his body still remembered being held—a total, impossible stillness, every muscle he owned locked from outside, as if the air around him had set like concrete—and he couldn't think about any of that. He could think about the three seconds.

The shield had torn. He'd heard it tear, if that was the word for it: a vast silence he'd lived beside for two years suddenly splitting down the middle, and through the split, Bella. Her voice. Her own voice, the one that lived behind her eyes. He'd wanted it for two years. He'd lain awake beside her through all the human nights, aching for one stray thought.

When it came, it was screaming.

*Edward—EDWARD—get her out get her OUT she's in my—no—NO—don't look at that, don't look at that, that's mine—*

And under it, threaded through it, cool and flat and English, a second voice that was nowhere near Bella's and was inside her anyway: *Move.*

Then the stranger had come out of Bella's head and the silence had closed back over, and Edward had found he couldn't move his body.

Bella's forehead was pressed into the side of his neck. She was shaking—she was a vampire, and she was shaking, a fine fast tremor all the way through her like a plucked string. Renesmee was crushed between them, both small fists knotted in his shirt.

"I'm here," he said into Bella's hair. "I'm here. Love. Look at me."

"She *cut* it." Bella's voice was very small. "Edward, she cut it, both times, and it won't—there's a line in it. It won't close."

He didn't know what to say to that. He had no way of seeing what she meant.

Renesmee put her hand flat against his cheek.

The picture came into him the way her pictures always did, clear and whole: the stranger in the snow, looking at her across the field. Green eyes. And then a *draft*—Renesmee had no better word for it—a touch, very light, somewhere inside her, like a moth landing. Then gone. Renesmee showed it to him twice, puzzled, and then showed him her own small hand coming up to touch her forehead.

*Did it hurt?* he asked her, without words, holding her gaze.

She shook her head. Then she showed him Bella's face, just now, twisting.

"She went into Renesmee first," Edward said aloud. His voice sounded strange to him. "Carefully. Then—"

"Then me." Bella pulled back to look at him. Her eyes were wild. "She just *shoved*. She didn't care. She went through everything. Edward, she saw the—she saw the *birth*—"

"I know."

"She saw *you*—"

"I know."

And then, because he couldn't help it, because the clearing had suddenly become the loudest place he had ever been, Edward stopped listening to his wife.

The wolves.

It had been building for the whole of those three seconds, underneath everything, and now it hit him all at once. Jacob's pack was in chaos. Five minds he knew nearly as well as his own family's, all going off at the same time like a kicked hive:

*—no. No no no no no. NO. Not you too. Not her. She just killed—Seth, she just killed THIRTY PEOPLE, Seth, look at me, look at ME—*

That was Leah. Leah, whose own life had been broken in half by this exact thing; Leah, who had watched it happen to Sam and then to Jared and to Paul and then, God help her, to Quil and to Jacob, and who had held herself together with the conviction that it could never happen to her little brother, because the universe could not possibly be that cruel twice to one family.

*Dude,* Quil was thinking, over and over. *Dude. Dude. Dude.*

*Oh, man,* Embry thought. *Oh, man, she's like—she's got to be thirty—*

*Everybody SHUT UP.* Jacob, putting the alpha into it, and the minds flattening for a moment and then boiling straight back up, because Jacob himself couldn't stop thinking it: *He imprinted. The kid imprinted. On her. Oh God. Seth. On HER.*

And at the center of it, Seth.

Edward had been inside the minds of every wolf in both packs at one time or another. He'd been inside Jacob's mind when Jacob imprinted on his daughter—he'd been standing over her with Rosalie and he had heard it happen, the whole world folding over in Jacob's head and reorganizing itself around a single point. He knew what it sounded like.

Seth's mind sounded like that. Every thought he had ran downhill in one direction. The world was very big and very bright and it had a middle, and the middle was standing in the slush with a cigarette in her mouth. Edward caught flashes of her through Seth's eyes, through the wolf's eyes: the green of hers; a strand of black hair blowing loose across her mouth; the line of her jaw; her voice saying *Balto*, and Seth not knowing what a Balto was and not caring, the word going into him like a hook. And under all of it, Seth's own fear, very young and very far away: *what is she, what is she, she burned them, she burned them all, I don't care, I don't—what's wrong with me—*

Across the field, Sam's pack had no access to any of it, and they didn't need any. They'd seen Seth's face. Edward could hear Sam's wolves thinking at one another, a separate hive, almost as loud:

*Did the Clearwater kid just—*

*He's not taking his eyes off her—*

*Oh, you have GOT to be—*

*Hold,* Sam thought, heavily. *Nobody move. Hold.* But Sam was thinking it too, Edward could hear it underneath: *On a—what is she? What IS she?*

Edward stood. He kept one hand on Bella's shoulder.

"Seth imprinted on her," he said.

He said it to his family, quietly. Every head in the clearing turned anyway; there were two dozen vampires in that field and any one of them could have heard him whisper from the far side of it. Rosalie's mouth fell open. Emmett said "*What?*" out loud. Somewhere among the Irish, Liam muttered something in Gaelic that Edward didn't bother to translate.

The stranger took the cigarette out of her mouth.

"Yeah," she said. "Caught that."

She didn't look at Seth. She looked at Edward. He looked back and listened as hard as he had ever listened to anyone, and got nothing. With Bella, there had always been a smooth, soundless absence, and he'd learned to live inside it. With this woman there was a wall. He could tell it was there because he could feel the shape of the place where there ought to have been thoughts, and where there was something solid and closed, built on purpose, the way people built walls round things they didn't want touched.

"You had no right," he said.

"Had every right." She tapped the front of her jacket, where the wallet was. "Got a badge and everything."

"What you did to her—"

"She'll mend." The stranger's eyes moved to Bella, still kneeling, and stayed there. "It'll feel like that for about a week. The line'll fade. I'd stop poking at it if I were you, love. You'll only make it itch."

Bella looked up at her. Edward watched her gather herself, the way she'd learned to in these last months—everything vast and wild and new in her pulled in and pinned down by sheer stubbornness.

"You could have asked," Bella said.

"You'd have lied to me."

Bella opened her mouth. Shut it.

"Yes," she said.

"There you go." The stranger put the cigarette back between her lips. "Don't feel bad about it. I'd have done the same. But I needed to know what she was, and I needed to know it before anybody in this field did anything else stupid, and you'd have lied, and I'd have had to go in anyway." She blew out smoke. "So I went in anyway."

Edward listened. Even without her mind he could hear her heart. It hadn't changed—not when she said that, not when she'd burned the Volturi, not once. Either she was telling the truth, or she was something he'd never met.

He thought about going for her throat again. He thought about it for a long and very detailed time, the way his mind let him think: how fast he'd need to be, from what angle. He thought about Felix's ash spraying across the snow a yard from her boots. He thought about Stefan, and the glassy crater, and the clear sky.

He stayed where he was.

"Who taught you to do that?" he said instead. "What you did. In her mind."

The stranger looked at him a moment longer than she needed to.

"Bloke with no nose," she said. "He's dead."

Behind and to Edward's left, the russet wolf rumbled. Jacob had planted himself across the front of Bella and Renesmee and hadn't budged, and his mind was a mess of half a dozen things at once—*Nessie's safe; is Nessie safe; Seth; the kid's gone, he's gone; what the hell is she*—and rising over all of it, the thing he'd been carrying since the stranger said what she said: *She knows. She saw it in Bella's head. She knows about me and Nessie.*

The stranger's eyes went to him.

"And you," she said. She pointed the cigarette at him like a dart. "Clifford."

Jacob's lips came up off his teeth.

"She's three and a half months old, mate."

The growl climbed a notch. In Jacob's head it came out in words, furious and defensive and achingly familiar to Edward, because he had heard Jacob say them to Bella and to Rosalie and to Charlie and to himself, over and over, at every hour of the night: *It's whatever she needs. That's all it is. Whatever she needs.*

"He says it's whatever she needs," Edward said.

"Yeah, I got the brochure." The stranger flicked ash into the snow. "Saw it in your missus's head. Brother, best mate, babysitter, bodyguard. And then one day she's eighteen and there he is, same as he ever was, stood in the kitchen with flowers." She looked at Jacob for a long moment. "Bloody hell."

Jacob's mind went white and hot.

"We've had this argument," Edward said. He heard how tired he sounded. "Many times."

"Not with me, you haven't."

The stranger looked down the line at the sandy wolf, then, for the first time. Only for a second. Seth's whole body jerked as if she'd touched him.

Edward heard Seth's mind lift toward her like a plant toward a window.

Something crossed her face. Edward couldn't read it. She wiped it off, if that was what it was, before he'd finished looking.

Bella got to her feet. She did it slowly, one hand still on Renesmee's back, and when she was standing she put the other hand flat against her own sternum, as if she could press the torn place in her shield closed from the outside. Edward watched her stare at the stranger with an expression he had seen on her face exactly once before, on the day she'd first held their daughter and looked up at Jacob: the face of someone measuring exactly how far she would go.

"If you ever," Bella said, "go into my daughter's head again—"

"I'll ask first," the stranger said. "Next time. Probably. Depends what she's done." She considered Bella through the smoke. "She's a good kid. Head like a bloody lighthouse. You did all right there."

Bella's mouth stayed open on whatever she'd been going to say. Nothing came out of it.

"Right," the stranger said, to everyone. "Hang on, all of you. Don't move. I've got to phone this in."

She reached into her jacket again. This time what came out was a small round mirror in a battered bronze case, the size of a woman's compact. She thumbed it open, looked into it, and said, "Potter. Field. Put me through to Croaker, would you, and tell him it's not good news."

Carlisle had known Aro for three hundred years.

It was not a thing he said often, even to Esme. He had lived in Volterra for a few decades in the eighteenth century—a young vampire then, younger than Bella was now in every way that mattered, desperately grateful to be among people who could talk about something besides blood. Aro had shown him the library. Aro had sat up with him through long Italian nights arguing philosophy and medicine with a delighted, all-consuming curiosity that Carlisle had never met in any human scholar and never would again. There was a painting of the four of them on a balcony, Solimena's work, hanging on the wall of Carlisle's study three miles from here.

There was a heap of grey ash on blackened ground a hundred yards away, and the morning air was still full of its smoke. Ten minutes ago it had been Aro.

Carlisle stood at the front of his family's line and found he did not know what he felt about that. He thought he would probably be finding out for some time.

The stranger had the mirror open in her palm. Carlisle could see the face in it from where he stood—his eyes could resolve a face in a mirror at forty yards as easily as the print on a medicine label—an old man, very old, with a long lined face and pale watery eyes and white hair that stuck up at the back as though he'd been lying on it. He was in a dim room. Something blue and glittering moved on the wall behind him, out of focus.

"Potter," the old man said. "It's half past five in the evening and I'm eating my dinner."

"Sorry, Croaker."

"No, you're not. Go on."

"Volturi turned up on schedule. Thirty-two of them. Plus about forty hangers-on they'd picked up on the way, plus twenty-odd on the other side, plus the Cullens, plus two packs of shapeshifters. Everybody in a big field four miles from a Muggle town with their teeth out. I gave the order."

"And?"

"And they didn't disperse."

A pause.

"Define 'didn't disperse,'" Croaker said.

The stranger—Potter—shifted her weight. She held the mirror up a little and angled it, and Carlisle realized she was showing the old man the field. Slowly, the whole sweep of it: the Volturi line where there was no more Volturi line, the thirty-two grey heaps, the curls of purple smoke still rising off them, the scorched fan where Felix had died running. The crater, still ticking.

Croaker was quiet for a long time.

"All of them?" he said.

"All the ones in the field."

"Aro?"

"Yep."

"Caius? Marcus?"

"Yep. Yep."

"Potter. The instruction was *disperse*."

"I said disperse. I said it nicely. I said it twice. The one with the white hair told two of his heavies to rip my head off, and they had a go, and I wasn't about to stand there and find out whether they'd manage it." She took the cigarette out of her mouth. "So I lit 'em."

The old man in the mirror rubbed his eyes with a thumb and forefinger.

"And the crater?"

"One of the Cullens' lot. Romanian. Didn't like me nicking his revenge. Took a run at me."

"Was there a—"

"There was a choice. It was a short one." She put the cigarette back. "Look, I've got a list. You want the list?"

"I want the list," Croaker said heavily, "and then I want to go back to my dinner and pretend for ten minutes that I haven't just lost the only people in the world who could be relied on to keep several thousand vampires from eating Muggles in the street. Go on."

Carlisle's stomach turned over, slowly, like something settling to the bottom of a lake.

He had not thought of it like that. He was not sure why he had not. The Volturi had been cruel and old and greedy, and they had come here to kill his granddaughter—and they had also been, for three thousand years, the only thing standing between the vampires of the world and the kind of exposure that would have ended all of them. Every nomad in every city knew the rules. Every coven knew what happened to those who broke them. The rules held because there were people in Volterra who would come and enforce them.

And there were no more people in Volterra. None who mattered.

"One," the stranger said. "Unregistered hybrid. Half human, half vampire. Female. Born the eleventh of September—" she glanced at Bella, who was on her feet with Edward's arm around her, and who gave one short, hard nod, as though the date had been dragged out of her "—so that's three and a half months. Looks about three years old. Grows fast. Heartbeat, blood, the lot. Eats food, drinks blood. Got a gift: shows you things when she touches you. Mum was human at conception, dad's a vampire. Mum got turned during the birth to stop her dying. I've been in both their heads. Mum's and the kid's."

"*Both?*"

"Kid gently," the stranger said. "Mum less gently."

Croaker said a word Carlisle had not heard since the seventeenth century.

"How in Merlin's name does a vampire father a—"

"I'll send you the memories. You'll want to sit down first. And you'll want to not be eating." She drew on the cigarette. "Two. The shapeshifters. They've got a thing called imprinting. Some kind of soul-binding, far as I can tell. Kicks in on sight, no consent on either end, can't be switched off. Goes through shields like they aren't there."

"How do you know it goes through—"

"Because one of them's just done it to me."

Silence in the mirror.

Carlisle, without meaning to, looked down the field at Seth. The boy—the wolf—had not moved an inch. Leah was pressed against his flank with her lips peeled back, snapping and snarling into his face in a frenzy that made no sound in the human register but that Carlisle could see in every line of her. Seth's eyes were on the woman with the mirror, and only on her.

"Potter," Croaker said, with great care, "are you telling me you are currently subject to an unknown binding?"

"I'm telling you there's a line, and it's on my end, and it went through everything I've got without touching the sides. Hasn't done anything yet. It's sitting there." A pause. "I'm handling it."

"You'll come in and have it looked at."

"When I'm done here."

"Potter—"

"*When I'm done here.* Three. Muggle exposure." Her eyes moved to Bella again. "One. Mum's dad. Local police chief. He's watched one of the wolves change in his back garden and he's been told enough to keep his mouth shut and nothing else, and he's chosen not to ask. He's been sat on that for three months and hasn't breathed a word to anybody. I'm leaving him."

Bella made a sound. Carlisle didn't look at her. He didn't need to.

"You're leaving him," Croaker repeated.

"He's a decent bloke, he loves his kid and his granddaughter, and he's got the sense to know when to stop asking. You want him Obliviated, send someone else to do it, and I'll be stood on his front step when they get there."

Croaker looked at her out of the mirror for a while with his watery old eyes.

"Noted," he said. "Anything else?"

"Yeah. Request." The stranger dropped the end of her cigarette into the slush. It hissed and went out, and then it was gone too. "Requesting authorization for a controlled burn."

"On what?"

"On this lot." She turned the mirror again, slowly, panning it along the line of the Cullens and their witnesses, and for a fraction of a second as it passed Carlisle found himself looking straight into the old wizard's eyes. "The leeches. The whole group. Cullens and all. Twenty-five of them. Kid comes home with me and we find her somewhere decent. I can do the rest in one go, same as before. Clean slate, Croaker. I'll be home by lunch."

Every vampire in the field heard it.

Carlisle felt the change go through them, behind him and around him: a stiffening, a shudder of small sounds. Esme's sharp intake of breath. Emmett's low growl. The faint grinding of Rosalie's teeth. Somewhere to his right Tanya said "*No*," very softly. Kate's hands had begun to spark; he could hear the electric crackle of it. Bella had gone so still beside Edward that she might have been carved there, her arms locked round Renesmee and her eyes on the stranger's face.

Carlisle did not move. He kept his own face still. He was aware, as he had never been in nearly four centuries, of exactly how much of him there was that could burn.

In the mirror, Croaker sighed.

"Denied," he said.

"Oh, come *on*—"

"Denied, Potter. Kate's hands wouldn't stop sparking.

Alice had watched the futures go out from the back seat of a rented Jeep on a logging road forty miles south of Forks.

She knew what fading looked like. A future went thin and thinner as a decision was made against it, until it was a ghost of itself and then nothing. This had gone out all at once. One moment she was holding all of them in her head, the way she'd held them every minute for two weeks—Aro's future, Caius's, Jane's, the whole black glittering tangle of the Volturi's next hour, every branch of it ending in blood on the snow—and the next moment there was nothing there. All thirty-two of them. Snuffed together, like candles in a room when somebody throws open a door onto a gale.

Jasper had put his hand on her knee as she went rigid in her seat. Nahuel had turned around in the front passenger seat to look at her with his dark, worried eyes, and Huilen beside him had said something sharp in a language Alice didn't speak, and Kachiri, driving, had nearly put the Jeep in a ditch.

"Alice."

"They're gone," she'd said. "Jazz. They're all gone. The Volturi. I can't see any of them."

"Gone where?"

"*Gone.*"

He'd tried to calm her. She'd felt him do it, the soft heavy blanket of his gift settling over her and her own panic pushing up against it from below. It hadn't worked very well. She'd gone looking for her family next. Carlisle—there. Esme—there. Edward, Emmett, Rosalie. Bella. All of them still there, their futures still running on into the afternoon, the evening, the new year. Alive.

With holes in them.

That frightened her more than the Volturi going out. Every one of her family's futures had holes in it now, gaps, places where something stood beside them that she couldn't see, the way she'd never been able to see the wolves or Renesmee. Bigger, though. Something standing in the middle of all their days to come, like a black shape cut out of a photograph with scissors.

They'd left the Jeep where the logging road ran out, and run the rest of the way.

Now Alice came out of the trees at the north end of the clearing at a flat sprint, with Jasper at her shoulder and Nahuel and Huilen and Kachiri behind, and she saw the field.

The sun was on it. Everyone she loved was standing in the snow, glittering, alive. That was the first thing, and for a second it was the only thing, and her knees nearly went.

Then she saw the ash.

It lay in a long ragged line across the far side of the field, where she'd seen the Volturi standing in every vision for two weeks. Thirty-two grey heaps on black ground. A little smoke still came off them. And in front of the Cullens, hands in the pockets of a leather jacket, stood a tall woman with black hair whom Alice had never seen in a vision in her life.

Alice stopped so fast she skidded.

The woman turned her head and looked at her.

"Oh, *great*," she said. "More of you."

"Alice!" Esme's voice cracked straight down the middle.

Alice didn't run to Esme. She didn't run to anyone. She was staring at the woman, and the woman was staring back, and Alice was reaching for her future—any of it, the next ten seconds, anything—and finding nothing. Blank. She had never in her life looked at someone standing right in front of her and seen nothing at all.

She heard Jasper's breath go in hard beside her. Felt his gift go out, sweeping across the field and the people in it the way it always did when he came into a room: tasting. Fear, from everybody. Grief, hot and confused, from the Denalis. Hilarity, bizarrely, from Emmett. Shock from everyone else. Then he came to the woman, and Alice felt him check, and stop, and push.

"Stop that," the woman said, without looking at him.

Jasper went still.

"I said stop it, Major. Whatever you're doing with the feelings. It tickles."

"What is she?" he said to Alice, low.

"I don't know," Alice said. "I can't see her."

"Witch," the woman said. "Unspeakable. Interim enforcement for the whole lot of you, worldwide, God help me, as of about forty minutes ago. You'll be the one who went to South America." Her eyes moved past Alice and Jasper and settled on the young man behind them. "And who's this, then?"

"You have a field."

"Croaker."

The woman shut it and stood holding it.

Alice had already turned inward. She was looking for Joham.

She'd never met him. She'd never seen him. She had nothing but a name and the shape Huilen had drawn with her voice—a vampire, a man, somewhere in the green under the rainforest canopy—and that was very little to hold on to. Alice knew that stop now. She'd seen it twice this morning. It had no fade to it at all. One moment his future ran on into the green and the rain, and the next there was a black space where it had been, sharp-edged, as if somebody had taken the scissors to it.

"You're going to go after him," Alice said.

The woman looked at her.

"His future stops," Alice said. "About three weeks from now. The same way theirs did." She pointed, without looking, at the ash.

The woman considered her for a moment. Then she took out a cigarette—out of nowhere, between two fingers—and looked at the end until it lit.

"Huh," she said. She drew on it. "Good to know."

Huilen was staring at the stranger. Nahuel was staring at the stranger. The flint in Huilen's face had cracked, very slightly, at one corner.

"Is she telling the truth?" Huilen said, to nobody. "This one? The small one with the visions?"

"She usually is," Jasper said quietly.

Huilen looked at the stranger for a long time.

"I will not thank you for something you have not done yet," she said finally.

"Wouldn't expect you to."

"But if you do it—"

"Yeah," the woman said. "I'll come and find you after. You can buy me a drink."

She turned away from them then, and looked across the field to the line of wolves along the trees—to Seth, who was still standing a step out in front of the others, trembling, with Leah's teeth an inch from his ear.

She went back instead to her family's futures, to the blank patches the wolves had always made in them, and found Seth's. It had always been a small, boy-shaped gap at the edge of things. It had changed. A line ran out of the middle of it now, across the snow and into the much larger dark where the stranger stood, taut and humming, like a wire somebody had plucked and left to sing.

Seth couldn't feel his paws.

He could feel everything else. Leah's teeth an inch from his ear and her voice in his head, going and going. The cold coming up through the snow. *Jake,* Seth thought.

Jacob was still planted in front of Nessie, way back by the Cullens. His mind came across to Seth tired and heavy and complicated—a lot of things in it Jacob wasn't saying. Then it went simple.

*Go on,* Jacob thought. *I'm not going to stop you, kid. Couldn't if I wanted to. Just—*

*Yeah?*

*Be careful. We don't know what she is.*

*She's English,* Seth thought, which was stupid, and Jacob's mind did something that was almost a laugh.

*Go on.*

Seth went back into the trees, far enough that nobody would see, and let go of the wolf. It took a second longer than usual. When he was standing on two legs in the snow, shaking with the cold that he couldn't feel and the line he could, he unknotted the leather cord from round his ankle and pulled on the cutoffs that had been tied to it. That was all he had. He went back out barefoot.

Leah came with him, staying a wolf, glued to his side, her shoulder against his hip. Her growl was going the whole time, low and continuous, like a fridge.

The woman watched them come. Up close her eyes were very green and very tired. There was ash on her sleeve. She was a couple of inches shorter than he was, which was weird, because from across the field she'd seemed about nine feet tall.

"Seth," he said. His voice came out cracked. He cleared his throat. "My name. It's Seth. Seth Clearwater."

"Layla," she said.

It went into him like the other thing had, like a hook. He stood there and took it.

"Potter," she added, after a second. "Layla Potter."

"You're English," Seth said.

"Well spotted."

"Who's Balto?"

"Famous sled dog. Ran medicine to a town in Alaska in a blizzard. They made a cartoon." She blew smoke off to one side, away from him. "He was a good dog."

Seth didn't know what to do with that, so he didn't do anything with it. Beside him Leah's growl climbed a notch.

The woman—Layla—looked at Leah. Then she looked a while longer.

"You're the sister," she said. "The one who got dumped for the cousin."

The growl cut off.

Seth felt the shock go through Leah, white and cold. Nobody said that. Not out loud. Not even Paul said it out loud anymore.

"That's shit," Layla said. "What happened to you. Sorry."

Leah stood very still with her head low, staring at her. Seth could feel her trying to find the angle, the joke, the knife in it. There wasn't one. The woman had just said it, flat, like you'd say it was snowing.

Leah's lips came down over her teeth. She didn't stop staring. She didn't growl again, either.

"How old are you, Seth?" Layla said.

"Fifteen."

She closed her eyes.

"Course you are," she said. She opened them again. "Right. Ground rules. You listening?"

"Yeah."

"I'm twenty-six. You're fifteen. Whatever's on your end of this thing—" she tapped the middle of her own chest, right on the spot where the line went in, and Seth felt it, felt the tap go all the way down the line and land in him "—on my end, you're a kid I met this morning. That's what you are, and that's what you're staying. You'll be fed, you'll be looked after, and if anybody gives you grief about any of it, they can come and see me. That's the lot. That's the whole deal. Clear?"

"Yeah," Seth said. And then, because it was true and it was what you said, because he'd heard Jake say it and Quil say it a hundred times: "It's okay. It's whatever you need—"

"*Don't.*"

He stopped.

She'd taken the cigarette out of her mouth. Her face had gone hard in a way it hadn't been, even when she'd snapped her fingers.

"Don't say that," she said. "Don't ever say that to me. Not to me, not to anybody. You're fifteen years old. You've got your own needs, and you'll have your own life, and you'll be whoever you turn out to be. Got it?"

Seth stared at her.

"Got it?"

"Yeah," he said. "Got it."

"Good." She put the cigarette back. She looked away from him, at the trees, and he heard her let out a breath. "Christ. Fifteen."

They were quiet a while. The line hummed. Seth could feel it, right in the center of him, steady as her heart.

"Something looked at me," he said.

She looked back.

"Before. Down the—this." He touched his own chest. "Right after you said—that thing, about Balto. Something at the other end looked at me. Something really big." He swallowed. "It didn't feel like you. Or it did, kind of, but—"

"Yeah," Layla said. "That was me."

"It didn't—"

"It's the other bit of me."

Before he could ask what that meant, there were footsteps in the snow to his left, quick and heavy, and three guys came out of the trees in cutoffs: Sam in front, Jared and Paul behind. They'd phased. All three were barefoot and breathing hard and steaming a little in the cold. Sam's face was set like concrete. Paul looked like he'd swallowed a wasp.

Sam stopped ten feet from the log and looked at the woman on it. Then at Seth. Then back at her.

"I'm Sam Uley," he said. "I lead the La Push pack."

"I know who you are," Layla said. "Saw you in Bella's head."

Something went across Sam's face at that. Seth knew what. Everybody knew what Bella had seen, back when she was human, when she'd met Emily for the first time: Emily's face. The scars. The day Sam had lost his temper standing too close to her.

"Then you know about the treaty," Sam said.

"I know there is one. I didn't sign it, I'm not a vampire, and I'm not on your land."

"You're bound to one of ours now." Sam's jaw worked. "One of our kids. In front of my whole pack. A Clearwater. His dad was on the council. His mom's on it now." He looked at the ash out on the field, then at her. "You just burned thirty-three of the most dangerous things on the planet without breaking a sweat, and now you're tied to a fifteen-year-old boy from my reservation, and I don't know what you are. The tribe has the right to know what you are."

Paul made a noise of agreement. Jared's eyes kept flicking to the crater.

Layla looked at Sam for a while.

"Fair," she said.

She took the cigarette out of her mouth and held it out to one side, and stood up off the log.

Seth didn't see it happen. He'd seen wolves phase a thousand times—he'd done it himself that morning—and it always came with a shudder, a burst, a split second of fur and heat and torn air. This had none of that. One moment she was standing in front of the cedar with the cigarette held out to the side. The next there was a wolf there.

Black. Black like the inside of a closed eye, with a charcoal undercoat that showed where the wind lifted the fur. Bigger than Jake. Bigger than Jake by a lot. Her shoulder was level with Seth's head. She was long, too, long and heavy through the chest and the haunches, built more like a big cat than any wolf he knew, and she had come down onto the snow without making a sound. The snow hadn't even creaked. Her paws were as broad as dinner plates, and when she shifted her weight there was nothing, not a crunch, not a hiss. Her eyes were green. The same green.

Sam took a step back. He did it before he'd decided to; Seth saw his face catch up with his feet a second later. Behind him, Paul said, "*Holy crap,*" in a high, cracked voice. Out on the field, Seth heard every vampire still standing there go dead quiet at once.

Leah had flattened herself against Seth's legs.

The black wolf turned her head and looked at Sam.

"This is what's on the other end of Balto's string," she said.

Her voice was the same. It was her voice, English and dry and a little rough, coming clearly out of a wolf's mouth with no strain in it at all. Paul made another noise.

"Any questions?" she said.

Sam stared up at her. Seth watched him try to find the alpha in himself and stand on it.

"Are you one of us?" Sam said.

"Different place. Different magic. Same idea." Her ears flicked, one then the other. "I don't do the hive-mind thing. I don't take orders. And I don't imprint." The ears went back, briefly. "Apparently that's a one-way street."

"Are you a danger to my people?"

"I'm a danger to anyone who comes for this kid," the black wolf said. "Or for Leah. Or for the toddler. Or for the police chief, or the town. Your people are fine."

She turned her head, then, and looked down at Seth.

He stood very still. Her head came down, slowly, till it was level with his, and her nose touched his shoulder. Just touched it. He heard her breathe in, a long deep draw of air, the way a wolf did when it wanted to know something. He smelled her, too, close up for the first time: pine needles and turned earth and cold stone, and heat. A lot of heat.

Down the line, in the middle of his chest, something huge and heavy turned around once and lay down.

The black wolf lifted her head.

Then she was a woman again, standing in the snow in front of the cedar log in her leather jacket, with not a hair out of place that hadn't been out of place before. The cigarette was still between her fingers. It was still lit.

She put it back in her mouth.

"Right," she said. "Anything else?"

Sam opened his mouth. Closed it. Seth had never seen Sam Uley stuck for words in his life.

"No," Sam said at last. He looked at Seth. Then he looked at her again, and something in his shoulders changed. "No. That's—you'll come to the council. Billy Black. Old Quil. Sue Clearwater. They'll want to hear it from you."

"I'll come. Give me a day." She glanced at Seth. "His mum'll want to have a go at me, I expect."

"Probably," Sam said.

"Fair enough. She's entitled."

Sam nodded, once. Then he turned and went back into the trees with Jared, and Paul followed after a second, looking back over his shoulder the whole way.

Leah was still pressed against Seth's legs. She'd stopped growling a while ago. Seth didn't know when.

*Leah?*

His sister's mind was very quiet. There was a lot in it, and none of it was ready to be words yet. But under all of it, one thing she kept turning over and over and looking at, the way you'd turn over a stone you'd found on the beach: *She said it was shit. What happened to me. Out loud. She said sorry.*

Leah stepped away from his legs. She looked up at the woman for a long moment. Then she turned and trotted back toward the rest of the pack, head up, tail down, not looking back.

Layla watched her go.

"She'll do," she said.

Seth's stomach growled.

It was loud. It was really loud, a long, rolling, embarrassing noise, in the sudden quiet of the clearing, and he felt his face go hot all the way down to his collarbone.

Layla looked at his stomach. Then at him.

"When'd you last eat?"

"Breakfast," Seth said. "Like—four hours ago. I had, like, eleven eggs. It's a wolf thing—"

"Yeah, I know it's a wolf thing." She tipped her head back and looked up at the sky, the clean blue, the whole morning gone. "Is there a diner in this town?"

"There's the diner," Seth said. "Like, *the* diner. In Forks."

"Do they do pie?"

"Yeah. Cherry. Apple. Marionberry—"

"What the hell's a marionberry?"

"It's like a blackberry thing? It's really good."

"Huh." She thought about it. "Right. Diner. Get your shirt."

"I don't have a shirt."

She looked at him, standing barefoot in the snow in a pair of cutoff jeans. Then she held out her hand, and there was a shirt in it. A flannel shirt, red and black check, folded.

He took it. It was soft and heavy and it smelled of nothing at all, like it had never been anywhere.

"It's flannel," Seth said.

"It's a classic. Put it on."

He put it on. It fit. He didn't ask.

She turned, then, and looked back out across the field: the Cullens in their tight knot around Bella and Nessie, Jake still in front of them, still a wolf; the Denalis, Garrett with his arm round Kate; the Amazons standing apart, watching; Alice and Jasper and the half-vampire guy and his aunt; and the long line of ash where the Volturi had stood, already starting to blow a little in the breeze, grey streaks running out across the white.

"Doc!" she shouted.

Out in the field, Carlisle's head came up.

"Three o'clock! Your place! Have the kettle on!" She paused. "And somebody find me a bloody table!"

Carlisle lifted one hand. Seth saw Esme, beside him, put her hand over her mouth. It might've been a laugh.

Layla turned back to Seth.

"Where'd you park?" he asked. "Like—how'd you even get here?"

"Hire car's at the Thriftway."

"That's four miles."

"Yeah." She held out her arm, elbow crooked. "Hang on to that. Don't let go, whatever happens. You're going to hate this."

Seth looked at her arm. Then at her.

He took it.

The line in the middle of his chest hummed, one long steady note, and down at the far end of it, in the dark, something very big and very heavy lay with its head on its paws and its eyes half open, watching.

There was a crack like a rifle shot, and the clearing was empty of them both.
