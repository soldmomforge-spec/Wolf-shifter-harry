# Registry

---

Sabine Moreau had worked the Registry duty desk for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that when Harry Potter said *long story*, he meant three forms, a headache, and a Floo call from somebody in a ministry she had never heard of.

She looked at him. She looked at the wolf at the edge of the trees. The wolf did not look at her; it looked at Potter, and only at Potter, with its ears flat and its tail clamped down between its legs, and the way it stood there made Sabine think of a dog tied to a post outside a shop.

She drank some of her coffee. It had gone lukewarm somewhere over Vancouver Island.

"Later," she said.

"Lovely."

"I did not say *never*, Potter. I said *later*." She turned away from him before he could answer and raised her voice, and it carried well in the cold, flat air of the clearing. "Ingrid. Penhallow. The hybrid. Gently. You are going to be very gentle, and very slow, and you are going to explain every single thing you do before you do it. Her parents are the two by the red wolf. The grandfather is the blond one who will want to talk to you. Let him."

Ingrid Solberg nodded once and started across the snow in her anorak and her walking boots, the clipboard tucked under her arm. The quill clipped to the top of the clipboard was already scratching away to itself. Penhallow followed her, his tray of empty vials chiming faintly, and he walked the way a man walks across a frozen pond.

"Auror Tillman." Sabine turned to the tallest of the Americans, a woman of fifty or so with iron-grey hair under the brim of her fedora and a face like a closed door. "Your soil. The wolves are yours if you want them."

"We want them," Tillman said. She was looking past Sabine at the long, disordered line of wolves, and her eyes were narrowed, counting. "Christ. There's more than the file said."

"There are always more than the file said."

"Okafor. Hollis. With me. Nobody pulls a wand on a wolf unless a wolf pulls one on you first." Tillman paused. "That was a joke."

"Yes, ma'am," said the younger of the two men, who did not look like he thought it was funny.

"Nobody touches the ash." Sabine raised her voice again, to the two in the ICW's long dark travelling cloaks. "Nobody. Not until every heap of it has been photographed and catalogued and somebody has counted the pendants. Halvorsen, Muggle-repelling perimeter, five miles out. And somebody find out what the town thinks that smoke is."

"Forest service controlled burn," Tillman said, without turning. "We've already got a guy on the phone to the local paper."

"In January?"

"It's Washington. Nobody's going to check."

Sabine let that go. She had spent a long time learning which things to let go. The cloaked wardens split off toward the ash with their wands out and their cameras clicking, and the Americans went down the line toward the wolves, and for a moment Sabine stood in the middle of the field with her coffee and let herself look at it.

Ash. Thirty-one heaps of it at a first count, though she would want a second count, and a third. Each one in its own black wet ring of melt, steaming gently. Gold glittering in them, the heavy old pendants the Italians had worn, some of them melted half to nothing. Beyond them, a long fan of grey sprayed across the snow where one of them had been walking at Potter when he went. In front of Potter's boots, a black scar the shape of a star, burned into the ground, glassy at its heart. And above it all, high up, spread flat and thin under the white sky, the last of the smoke. It had been a column, in the photograph the Canadian desk had sent her. It was a stain now, a long purple-grey smear across the cloud from one side of the valley to the other.

The International Confederation of Wizards had kept a file on the Volturi since sixteen-forty. It had been the single most dangerous file in the Registry for three hundred and sixty-seven years. It had a red border. It had a warning on the inside cover in eleven languages.

Sabine had read the whole thing on the way in. It had taken Potter less than a second.

She heard him come up beside her. He did it without any hurry, his hands in his jacket pockets, the big book tucked under one arm.

"Sabine."

"Potter."

"Can I say something?"

"Can I stop you?"

"No." He looked out across the field at the wardens bent over the ash with their cameras. "You sent an Unspeakable."

"I am aware."

"A fucking *Unspeakable*, Sabine. For this." He took one hand out of his pocket and made a short, flat gesture that took in all of it: the ash and the wolves and the vampires and the scorch and the purple smear in the sky. "For a dispersal order. In a *field.* You know what I'm supposed to be doing this week? I've got a thing in the Department. I can't tell you what the thing is, but it's in a room with no doors, and it's been there since thirteen-hundred, and it's been getting *louder*, and I'm the only bloke on the floor it's never tried to eat. I left it on a Tuesday with a note on it saying *back Friday*. A note. On *it*. And instead I'm standing in a car park in Washington State in the snow, telling Count bloody Chocula to go home."

"It is not a car park."

"It's a field, it's the same thing. Why couldn't a Hit Wizard do it? That's what Hit Wizards are *for*. You've got a whole squad of them sat on their arses in Geneva playing Exploding Snap, I've seen them, they've got a league table. You send four of them out with a warrant and a nice firm voice, they tell everybody to go home, everybody goes home. Job done. Back in time for tea."

"Everybody would not have gone home."

"You don't know that."

"I do know that. It is my job to know that." Sabine took another mouthful of cold coffee and swallowed it. "The ICW has sent Hit Wizards near the Italians twice. Once in eighteen-ninety-one, and once in nineteen-thirty-eight. Seven Hit Wizards, in total. Do you know how many of them came home?"

He didn't answer.

"None," Sabine said. "The file has their names in it. It has the letters we sent to their families. The second time, the little blonde one did something to the leader, and he sat down in the middle of the Piazza dei Priori and screamed until his heart stopped. In front of four hundred Muggle tourists. We were Obliviating Tuscany for a month." She looked at him sideways. "Her gift goes through every shield charm we have. All of them. We tested. The only thing in the whole world that keeps her out of a wizard's head is a trained Occlumens. A very good one. And the boy's mist, the same. And the big ones are faster than any Hit Wizard alive, and harder to kill than a dragon. So. I needed somebody who could stand in front of the girl and not scream. Who could stand in the boy's fog and still see. Who, if the Italians refused the order and decided to kill him, could kill all of them first." She paused. "There was one name on the duty roster that could do all three."

"Mine."

"Yours."

"I hate you, Sabine."

"Yes, I know. It is in your file too." She tipped her cup toward the ash. "And it came to it."

"It came to it." He was quiet for a moment, looking at the heaps. His face didn't change much. "They didn't leave me a lot of room. The big one was coming to fetch me. The old one in black wanted me in Italy. *Honoured guest*, he said. Read his file on the way in. I know what happens to the honoured guests."

"I know. It was authorised, Potter. I said so. I will say so again in writing. You will have it by tonight." She hesitated. "I will need you to write it, too. All of it. The Romanian as well."

"Yeah." He said it flatly. "Tonight."

They stood there for a while without speaking. Behind them, across the field, Ingrid's voice was carrying, low and careful and very slow, the way Sabine had told her to speak: *I'm going to take out a tape measure now. It's just a tape measure. It won't do anything. Would you like to hold it first?*

"It is very visible," Sabine said eventually. "The smoke."

"I noticed."

"Geneva noticed. MACUSA noticed. I believe it is visible from the *Moon*, Potter."

"Speaking of," he said, and then stopped, because he'd heard what he'd said.

Sabine turned and looked at him properly.

"Yes," she said. "Speaking of."

"Don't."

"The moon is full on Thursday."

"I know when the full moon is, Sabine. I've got a calendar. I've got a calendar with *pictures* on, Hermione gave me it, it's got little drawings of the phases and everything—"

"That is two days."

"I can count to two."

"Then count this." She lowered her voice, though there was no lowering it enough; every vampire on the field would hear her, and probably the wolves as well, and she did not care. "In two days, whatever you are doing and wherever you are, the moon is going to come up, and you are going to change. You cannot stop it. You have told me yourself you cannot stop it. You can put the other shape on any time you like, I have seen you do it in the canteen to frighten the interns, but on the full moon it puts itself on, and it stays on until the moon goes down."

"And I'm the same bloke the whole time. I'm still me, Sabine, I'm just—" he waved a hand vaguely in the air around his own head—"fluffier."

"I know that. *You* know that. They do not know that." She jerked her chin toward the wolves. "Look at them. Two packs of shapeshifters who have never met a wizard, who have been filed under *werewolves, probably* since before the war, who have been told the stories about what a real werewolf is since they were children. And you, a man who is bitten, standing in their forest on the full moon, at the other end of—" She stopped, and looked again toward the grey wolf at the trees. "Of whatever that is. If this is not resolved by Thursday, things may become—" She searched for the word in English and settled on it with distaste. "Weird."

"Weird."

"Weird."

"Weirder than *this?*" He gestured again at the ash, the scorch, the sky.

"I have no idea," Sabine said. "That is my point. Nobody has any idea. I would like very much to know what a bond like that does when one end of it grows a tail."

He looked at her for a long moment.

Then he laughed. It was a short laugh, more air than sound, and there was not very much amusement in it, but it was real.

"Sabine," he said, "I'm going to be honest with you. Right now? I could not give a toss. If I spend the next couple of days as every furry on the internet's wet dream, then I'm every furry on the internet's wet dream. I'll live." He shrugged. "I'm a blast when I'm furry, anyway. Kids love it. I'll have a field full of teenagers who turn into dogs and a toddler who does slideshows with her hands. They'll think it's Christmas. I'll deal with it when I get there."

"That is a terrible plan."

"It's a mood, Sabine."

"I am going to write in my report that you said that."

"Spell 'furry' right." He looked down at the book under his arm. Then he looked back across the field at the grey wolf, and his jaw worked a little, and he looked away again. "Right. Two days. Fine. Then I need Volume Two."

"Volume Two is in Geneva."

"I know where it is."

"In the restricted stacks. Third sub-basement. Behind two Fidelius layers and a goblin-built door."

"I *know* where it is, Sabine, I'm the one who put it back." He raised his voice, not very much, and said a name into the cold air as though he were calling a dog in from the garden. "Kreacher."

There was a crack, small and sharp, like a twig snapping.

Something was standing at Potter's feet in the snow that had not been standing there before.

Sabine had met house-elves. She had never met this one, though she had read about him, because he was in Potter's file too. He came up to a little above Potter's knee. He was very old. His skin hung off his bones in pale grey folds, and his ears were huge and bat-like and sprouted tufts of white hair, and his eyes were large and bloodshot and wet. He wore a clean white tea towel, ironed, with a small black crest embroidered on the chest of it. Around his neck hung a heavy gold locket on a chain, much too big for him, that he kept one knobbly hand pressed over as though someone might try to take it.

He bowed so low that the end of his long snout touched the snow.

"Master called Kreacher."

"Yeah. Hiya, Kreacher."

The elf straightened. He looked around. He looked at the vampires standing in their clumps, at the Cullens and the Irish and the Egyptians, at the line of wolves, and his face, which had not had far to go, twisted into an expression of the deepest and most profound disgust.

"Master is in a field," he said, "of *bloodsuckers.*"

"I am, yeah."

"And *dogs.*"

"Got it in one."

Kreacher's wet eyes travelled across the snow to the heaps of ash, and stopped there, and stayed there. Something changed in his face. Sabine could have sworn it was approval.

"Kreacher sees," he said, slowly, "that Master has been tidying."

"Bit, yeah. Listen. I need a book. *Bonds of Soul and Shape*, Volume Two. It's got a brown cover like this one." He tapped the book under his arm. "It's in Geneva. ICW archive, restricted stacks, third sub-basement, the shelf on the left as you go in, about head height. Bring it here."

"Potter," said Sabine. "Absolutely not."

Kreacher didn't look at her. Kreacher did not seem to understand that she was there.

"He'll put it back after," Potter said, to her.

"That is a restricted archive. It is restricted. That is what the word means. There are wards on that room that it took forty wizards nine years to lay."

"Yeah, there are wards on that room for *wizards*." He looked down at the elf. "Kreacher."

"Master."

"Don't break anything."

"Kreacher never breaks anything, Master."

"You broke a vase on Sunday."

The elf's ears drooped very slightly. "That vase," he said with dignity, "was a gift to Mistress from the Lestranges. And it was ugly." He bowed again, so low his ears brushed the snow on either side of his head. "Kreacher will fetch the book."

The crack came again, and he was gone. A small round print in the snow, two smaller prints where his feet had been, and nothing else.

Sabine looked at the place where he had been. Then she looked at Potter.

"I am writing that down," she said.

"Put it on the pile."

---

Bella heard all of it.

She didn't want to. She was trying very hard to listen to nothing but the woman with the clipboard who was walking toward them across the snow, and the thin, nervous man behind her with the tray of glass vials, and Carlisle's quiet voice saying *Good morning* to them as they came, the same voice he used on frightened patients. But her ears were a vampire's ears, and the field was quiet, and the Englishman and the Frenchwoman were standing forty yards away talking in perfectly ordinary voices about the full moon.

*A man who is bitten.*

*On the full moon it puts itself on.*

*Two packs of shapeshifters who have been told the stories about what a real werewolf is since they were children.*

Edward's hand was flat against the small of her back. She felt his fingers tighten when the Frenchwoman said *werewolf*. She felt them tighten again, harder, at *a toddler who does slideshows with her hands*.

"Children of the Moon," Edward murmured.

"Like Caius said?"

"Like Caius said." He didn't take his eyes off the Englishman. "Caius nearly died fighting one, a long time ago. It's why he hunted them."

"He said he was the same. On the moon. *I'm still me*, he said."

"I heard."

"Was he lying?"

"I don't know." Edward's voice was very even and very tight. "I can't hear him, Bella. I can't hear a thing. I'm standing in a field with a man who burned the Volturi with a snap of his fingers, and every time I reach for him there's nothing there. It's like reaching for you." He stopped. His hand moved on her back, very slightly, a small restless movement. "It *was* like reaching for you."

She turned and looked up at him.

His face was still. His eyes weren't. They were moving over her face the way they moved when he was trying to memorise something, and they had been doing it, on and off, ever since she got up out of the snow.

"Edward."

"I heard you," he said.

She had known he had. She had seen it on his face when she lifted her head. But hearing him say it was different.

"When he went through," Edward said, very low, "the shield came down. For a second. Less. And I heard you." He swallowed, though he didn't need to. "I've wanted to hear you since the day I met you. Since the biology lab. I used to lie awake at night wondering what it would sound like."

"What did it sound like?"

He thought about it. He actually took the time to think about it, standing there in the middle of a battlefield with the ash still smoking behind them, and she loved him so much for it that it hurt.

"Loud," he said finally. "Very loud. A lot of things at once. Renesmee, mostly. Renesmee, and me, and Charlie, and Alice. And the lightning." His mouth moved, almost a smile. "And you were swearing at him. In your head. You were calling him things I didn't know you knew."

"I didn't know I knew them either."

"It sounded like you," Edward said. "That's what it sounded like. It sounded exactly like you."

She leaned her head against his chest for one second. One. Then she straightened up, because the woman with the clipboard had reached Carlisle, and the shield was still stretched across all of them, sore and ragged and humming at the edges like a burned hand, and she had a daughter.

The woman with the clipboard was tall and fair and somewhere in her thirties, with a long plain face, her hair scraped back in a ponytail, and the kind of rosy, windburned cheeks that came from a lot of time outdoors. The anorak was red and very ordinary. The boots were muddy. She looked like someone Bella might have seen at a trailhead off the 101 with a backpack and a water bottle. The clipboard under her arm had a quill clipped to the top of it, a long grey feather, and the quill was writing all by itself, scratching across the paper in small fast loops, and nobody was holding it.

"Dr. Cullen?" the woman said. "Ingrid Solberg. ICW Registry of Beings. This is Healer Penhallow." She nodded at the thin man with the vials. "He's a doctor, too. Of a sort."

"A Healer," Penhallow said faintly. "Of a sort. Hello."

"We'd like to meet your granddaughter," Ingrid said. "If that's all right."

Carlisle looked back over his shoulder at Bella. It was a question. He was asking her. Of course he was asking her.

Bella looked at Renesmee.

She was sitting up straight on Jacob's back with her small hands resting on his ruff and the backpack still on her shoulders. Her cheeks were pink with the cold. She was looking at Ingrid with a great deal of interest, and at the quill even more, and she didn't look frightened at all. Bella had not seen her frightened all morning. She'd seen her quiet, and watchful, and solemn. But not frightened. It was Bella who'd been frightened, for both of them.

"She stays on Jacob," Bella said. "You don't take her anywhere. You don't touch her without asking her first."

"Of course," Ingrid said at once. "Of course not."

"Edward."

"She means it," Edward said quietly, beside her. "She's thinking that she's never seen anything like her. That she's beautiful. That she hopes she doesn't cry, because then Penhallow will cry, and then she'll have two of them to deal with." A faint pause. "Penhallow is terrified of us. He keeps thinking about the ash. He's thinking that if any of us move quickly he's going to drop the tray. He means her no harm."

Penhallow had gone a shade paler. "You can hear—"

"Yes," Edward said.

"Oh," said Penhallow. "Oh, good. That's—good." He swallowed. "I'll try to think nicer things."

Bella walked to Jacob's side. Edward walked with her. The two Registry people followed at a careful distance, and Carlisle went beside them, and Rosalie—Rosalie had never really stopped standing between Renesmee and everyone else on the field, and she didn't stop now; she simply moved with them, keeping herself at Jacob's shoulder, her arms folded and her face set like marble.

Jacob turned his huge head and looked at the Registry woman. Bella felt his low growl through his flank where her hip touched it.

"Hello," Ingrid said to Renesmee. Not to Jacob, not to Bella. To Renesmee. She crouched in the snow so their eyes were on a level. "I'm Ingrid. What's your name?"

"Renesmee," said Renesmee.

"That's a very pretty name. Is it all right if I ask you some questions, Renesmee?"

Renesmee looked at Bella. Bella nodded, though everything in her wanted to say no.

"Okay," Renesmee said.

It took longer than Bella had expected and less time than she'd feared. Ingrid's questions were simple and she asked them slowly. How old are you. What do you like to eat. Do you sleep at night. Does anything hurt. Can you show me how tall you are. The quill scratched and scratched. Penhallow took a tape measure out of his pocket and held it up for Renesmee to look at, and let her hold the end of it, and then measured her very carefully from the top of her head to her boots while she sat on Jacob's back, and from fingertip to fingertip with her arms held out. He took his wand out—a slim dark stick of wood, nothing at all, the kind of thing you'd pick up off a forest floor—and he asked her if he could make a little light around her, and when she said yes he waved it once over her head and a soft blue glow ran down her from her curls to her toes, and faded, and Renesmee laughed out loud with delight.

Bella's whole body had gone rigid. Edward's hand stayed on her back.

"It doesn't hurt," Penhallow was saying. "It's just a look. A look inside. Oh, that's—Merlin, that's *remarkable.* Two heartbeats a second, near enough. Her temperature's high. A hundred and four. Is that normal for her?"

"Yes," said Carlisle. He was leaning forward slightly, watching the wand, and Bella saw his eyes and thought, in a strange cold detached corner of her mind, that she had not seen him look at anything like that in a very long time. "She's always run warm. What else can you see?"

"Bones are dense. Very dense. Much more than a human child. Growth plates are—hm. Very active. Very." Penhallow looked up at Bella. "Mrs. Cullen. Does she grow quickly?"

"Yes."

"How quickly?"

Bella didn't answer.

"About a year's growth every few weeks, at the start," Carlisle said quietly. "It has been slowing down. We don't know when it'll stop."

Penhallow nodded slowly and said something under his breath to Ingrid, and the quill scratched faster.

Then Penhallow picked up one of the empty vials from his tray and a small silver thing that Bella, after a moment, recognised as a lancet.

"Now, I'll just need a little blood," he said. "Just a drop or two. For the Registry. It'll hardly sting at—"

"*No.*"

Bella didn't decide to say it. It came out of her by itself, hard and loud. Rosalie said it at the same moment, and so did Jacob, in his own way: his lips peeled back off his teeth in a snarl so sudden and so close to Penhallow's face that the Healer jerked backward and nearly sat down in the snow. The vials on the tray rang like bells.

"Jacob," Carlisle said sharply.

Jacob didn't stop snarling. Renesmee put her small hand flat against the side of his neck and he stopped.

"Nobody," Rosalie said, in a voice like broken glass, "is putting a needle in her."

"It's—it's just a lancet," Penhallow said weakly. "It's very small, it's really very—"

"Use a hair."

The voice came from across the field. Ordinary, flat, a little bored.

Bella turned her head. Everyone did. The Englishman was still standing out in the open snow beside the Frenchwoman, forty yards off, with his book under one arm and his other hand in his pocket. He wasn't even looking at them. He was looking at the ash heaps, where the wardens in their long dark cloaks were bent over with their cameras.

"Use a hair, Penhallow," he said again. "She doesn't want a needle, she doesn't get a needle."

"Potter, it's not the same—a hair's only good for half the tests—"

"Then do half the tests."

"The Registry protocol says—"

"Sabine," said the Englishman, still without turning his head, "does the Registry protocol say Penhallow's got to stick a toddler with a needle in front of her mum and dad and her auntie and a horse-sized dog who'll have his arm off?"

The Frenchwoman sighed, audibly, from forty yards away.

"Use a hair, Penhallow," she said.

Penhallow looked at the lancet in his hand. He looked at Jacob's teeth. He put the lancet back on the tray.

"Hair," he said. "Right. Yes. Hair's fine. Hair's lovely."

Before anyone could say anything else, Renesmee reached up with both hands, took hold of one of her own bronze curls, and pulled. It came out with a tiny sound. She held it out to him.

"Here," she said.

Penhallow took it from her as though it were made of spun glass. "Thank you," he said. "Thank you very much. That's very brave."

"It didn't hurt," said Renesmee, who had pulled out her own hair before for less reason. She was looking at Ingrid's clipboard again, at the quill, which had stopped writing and seemed to be waiting. "What does that say?"

"It says what you've told me." Ingrid turned the board so Renesmee could see, though Bella doubted she could read the tiny looping hand. "Name. Age. Height. It writes it all down so I don't have to."

"Can I show you something?"

Ingrid hesitated. She glanced at Bella. Bella, after a moment, nodded.

"All right," Ingrid said.

Renesmee reached out and laid her hand on the back of Ingrid's wrist.

Bella saw it happen. She'd seen it a hundred times now, every time Renesmee touched someone new, and she still watched for it, the moment of it: Ingrid's face went slack with surprise, and her eyes went wide and unfocused and very far away, and her lips parted. She stayed that way for perhaps three seconds. Then Renesmee took her hand back, and Ingrid blinked, hard, twice, and put her own hand to her mouth.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's *lovely*."

The quill had gone mad. It was racing across the page so fast it was smoking slightly at the nib. *Projective,* Bella read upside down. *Tactile. Visual and emotional content transmitted. Penetrates*— and then it was writing too fast and too small to follow.

"What did she show you?" Carlisle asked.

"The snow," Ingrid said, a little dazed. "This morning. From up here. Everyone in a line. And—" she glanced across the field, at the Englishman, and back—"him. Coming. The way she saw it. With the—with the noise, when he came, and then him just standing there with his hands in his pockets." She laughed a little, unsteadily. "She thought he was funny."

"He *is* funny," said Renesmee firmly. She twisted around on Jacob's back and looked across the field at the Englishman and the Frenchwoman. Then she looked at Bella. "Mama. Can I go and see the funny man?"

"No," said Bella.

"Please?"

"No, baby."

"He said I'm a toddler with a backpack," said Renesmee, with dignity. "He said I wasn't dangerous. He told the lady in the mirror. And he said use a hair." She paused. "And he smells like Jake."

Jacob's ears went flat.

"He smells like Jake," Renesmee said again, insistently, "but different. Like Jake if Jake was older. And bigger. And under a rock." She leaned forward over Jacob's neck. "Jake, can we go and see?"

Jacob made a sound in his chest that Bella had never heard him make before. It was half a whine and half a groan and it said, quite clearly, *please don't make me.* He swung his head around and looked at Bella with his huge dark eyes, and in them she saw exactly what she was feeling herself.

Then she saw something else.

She saw him look across the field, past the Englishman, to the trees, where the grey wolf was still standing at the very edge of the forest with her tail tucked and her ears flat. And she saw his eyes come back, to the Englishman, and stay there, and something in them changed. Something settled. It took her a moment to understand what it was, and when she did she almost didn't believe it.

Jacob had stopped being afraid of him.

Not completely. Not all at once. But something in the set of his ears and the line of his shoulders had gone from *threat* to something else, something more complicated, something she couldn't name.

"Jacob?" she said quietly.

He huffed at her. Then he looked at Edward, a long look, and Edward said, after a moment, very low, "He says Leah would know if he meant her harm. He says Leah *does* know. He says it's the one thing about all this he's sure of." A pause. "He says he'll carry her, and he'll stand right there, and if the man so much as looks at her wrong he doesn't care what the pack law says."

"What pack law?"

Edward's mouth tightened. "Later," he said.

Bella looked at her daughter. Renesmee looked back at her with her chocolate-brown eyes, patient and certain and four months old.

"Two minutes," Bella said. "And I'm coming with you."

They crossed the field together: Jacob, with Renesmee on his back, and Bella on one side of him and Edward on the other, and Rosalie a step behind, because nothing short of the end of the world was going to keep Rosalie a step further away than that. The snow crunched. Out in the open the stink of the ash was thick and sweet and Bella stopped breathing. The Englishman watched them come. He didn't move toward them, or away. He just stood there and watched, and when they were ten feet away he looked at Jacob, and then at Renesmee, and one eyebrow went up.

"Alright, sprog," he said.

"Hello," said Renesmee. "I'm Renesmee."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

He glanced at Bella. It was a short, flat glance with nothing in it at all, and it still made Bella want to go for his throat. "Your mum told me," he said.

"I did *not*—" Bella began.

"Can I show you something?" Renesmee said.

He looked at her for a moment. Then, without a word, he crouched down in the snow in front of Jacob, so that his face was on a level with hers, and waited.

Jacob had gone so still he might have been carved. A low sound was running through his chest, too low to be called a growl. The Englishman didn't look at him. He kept his eyes on Renesmee.

She reached out and put her small hand flat on his cheek.

Bella watched his face. She couldn't help it. She'd watched every face Renesmee had ever touched, and she watched this one. His eyes went unfocused, the way everyone's did. Then they came back, faster than anyone's she'd ever seen. And for a fraction of a second something moved across his face that she had not seen there all morning, through the burning and the lightning and the cigarette and the whole long ugly business of tearing through her mind. She couldn't have named it. It was gone almost before it arrived.

"Hello to you too," he said.

"Are you a doggy?" said Renesmee. "Like Jake?"

"Bit of one. Different sort."

"Can I see?"

He looked up, past her, at Bella. At Edward. At Jacob's flat ears.

"Not today," he said. "Ask me again in a couple of days. You might get a look whether I like it or not."

"Why?"

"Because the moon's a bossy cow." He stood up. His knees cracked. "Go on. Your two minutes are up. Your mum's going to bite me."

"Mama doesn't bite people," said Renesmee.

"Your mum," he said, looking straight at Bella, "very much wants to bite me. And I've earned it." He held her eyes for a second. "I went in rough. You didn't deserve rough. I'm sorry about that."

Bella had been ready for almost anything but that. She stood there with her mouth half open and the shield aching in her head and couldn't think of a single thing to say.

"Your shield's good," he went on, before she could find anything. "It's better than good. Best I've seen, and I've seen a few. Most people, I go through their walls and they're in pieces after. Yours is already knitting. Give it a day." He turned away. "Go on. Mind the ash."

Edward's hand closed around hers. He drew her back a step, and another, and Jacob turned with Renesmee on his back, and they walked back across the snow toward the others.

Halfway there, Bella looked back.

The Englishman had sat back down in the snow. Somewhere along the way the green camping chair had reappeared, and he was sitting in it with the big book open on his knees again, and he wasn't reading it. He was looking at the trees. At the grey wolf.

The grey wolf was gone.

Leah phased behind a cedar.

It took her two tries. The first time, the heat went up her spine the way it always did and then stuck halfway, like a sneeze that wouldn't come, because her whole body was still pulled tight toward the field and the man in it and some stupid animal part of her didn't want to be anything less than a wolf with him standing out there. The second time she shut her eyes and thought about nothing but her own two feet, her bare human feet in the snow, and it came.

The cold hit her skin and slid straight off. It always did. She was a hundred and eight degrees inside and a January morning was nothing. She untied the leather cord from around her ankle with fingers that were shaking, and shook out the bundle tied to it: cutoffs, a black tank top, both creased flat and damp at the folds. She pulled them on. She stood there for a second with her back against the cedar and her palms flat against the bark, breathing.

*Leah.* Jacob, in her head, and then gone, because she wasn't a wolf anymore and the pack-mind went quiet the instant the fur did. The silence afterward was enormous. It was the only good thing that had happened all day.

It didn't last.

She could still feel it.

That was the part she hadn't been ready for. She'd thought—she didn't know what she'd thought. That it was a wolf thing. That it lived in the fur, in the part of her that had come out of nowhere less than a year ago, when her dad's heart gave out and her whole body caught fire. That if she took the wolf off, it would come off with it, like a coat.

It hadn't come off. It sat right where it had sat since the crack in the clearing: in the middle of her chest, under her breastbone, thick and heavy and humming, and it ran out of her and through the trees and across the snow in a straight line, and at the other end of it was him.

She could have pointed to him blindfolded. She could have walked to him in the dark.

She pushed off the cedar and walked out of the trees.

The field had changed while she was gone. There were strangers everywhere now, wizards, she supposed, though most of them looked like hikers or cops. People in long dark cloaks were bending over the ash heaps with cameras that flashed. Three in coats and hats were working down the line of Sam's wolves, and one of them was holding up a tape measure with an expression of grim determination while Paul, enormous and silver and very obviously enjoying himself, kept moving his head just out of reach. The Cullens were clustered around the little girl again. The other bloodsuckers had drifted back into their clumps.

Every single one of them turned to look at her when she came out of the trees.

She felt it, the eyes, all of them. Vampire eyes, red and gold. Wizard eyes. And down the line, wolf eyes. She didn't look at Sam. She didn't need to look to know where he was, any more than she needed to look to know where the Englishman was. She'd been able to find Sam with her eyes shut for years. It had been the worst thing about phasing, the way she could feel Sam in her head every time, every thought about Emily, every single—

It occurred to her, walking across the snow, that she couldn't feel Sam like that anymore.

She could see him. Down the line, big and black and still, watching her. She knew he was there. But the old ache, the one that had lived under her ribs for two years and pulled toward him every time he was near, the one she'd hated and fed and carried around like a stone—

It was gone.

She stopped walking.

She stood in the middle of the snow, barefoot, with her arms wrapped around herself, and looked at Sam Uley across thirty yards of trampled snow, and for the first time since she was eighteen years old she felt nothing at all. Not love. Not hate. Nothing. He was a big black wolf. That was all he was.

Something rose up in her throat that was so close to a laugh it scared her.

It hadn't gone, of course. She wasn't stupid. It had just been crushed under something so much bigger that there was no room left for it. She'd had a hole in her chest for two years, and somebody had come along and filled it with a fucking *anvil*.

She started walking again.

He was sitting in a camping chair.

That was the first thing she saw clearly, and of all the things she'd seen today it was somehow the one that made her angriest. He was sitting in a cheap green folding camping chair with silver tape on the seat, in the middle of a field full of dead vampires, with a book the size of a gravestone on his knees. He wasn't reading it. He'd been watching the trees. He was watching her now, coming toward him, and his face didn't do anything at all.

The Frenchwoman was standing a little way off, with her paper cup. She looked at Leah, and at him, and walked away without a word toward the ash.

Leah stopped six feet from the chair.

Up close he was bigger than he'd looked. Up close he smelled stronger: the pine and the earth and the cold wet stone, and under it the dark heavy animal musk that she'd caught across the field, much thicker here. And something else. Ash, and tobacco, and the sharp clean bite of the ozone from the lightning, still clinging to his clothes. She could hear his heart. Slow. Heavy. Calm, which made her want to hit him.

The line in her chest pulled toward him so hard her ribs ached with it.

"Get it out," she said.

He looked at her.

"Hello to you too," he said.

"I don't care. Get it out. You did this. I don't know how, I don't know what you are, but you did something, and I want it *out.*"

"I didn't do anything, love. You looked at me."

"Don't *love* me."

"Alright."

"And don't call me Lassie."

"Didn't know your name then." He closed the book with a thump. "Do now."

"Yeah, I *know* you do. You took it out of Bella's head. I heard Edward." She was shaking. She hated that she was shaking. "You went into her head like you owned it and you took everything, didn't you? Everything she knew. About all of us. About me." She heard her own voice go up and couldn't stop it. "About Sam."

He didn't deny it. He didn't say anything at all for a second. Then he said, "Yeah."

"So you know."

"I know what Bella knows. Which is some of it." He looked at her steadily. "Bella thinks you're the angriest person she's ever met and she doesn't blame you a bit. If that helps."

It did not help. It did something to her chest that was the opposite of helping.

"Get it out," she said again, but it came out lower.

He was quiet for a moment. He put the book down on the snow beside the chair, carefully, and leaned forward with his elbows on his knees and his hands loose between them, and looked up at her.

"I had a look," he said. "Before. When I was standing there with my eyes shut and you lot thought I'd gone barmy. I went and had a look at it. At my end."

"And?"

"And your end's yours. Comes out of you, goes to me, that's yours. I can't do anything about your end without going into your head, and I'm guessing you'd rather I didn't."

"You'd guess right."

"Thought so. My end." He rubbed his thumb across his knuckles. "My end's the problem. From what I saw in Bella's head, when one of your lot does this, it's supposed to go here." He tapped two fingers against the left side of his chest. "Heart. Goes to the heart, stops there. Big deal, I'm sure. Gravity, and all that. But it stops."

"So?"

"So mine didn't stop."

The line in her chest hummed, low.

"It went past the heart like it wasn't there," he said. "It went down through all of me. Through—" He stopped and seemed to change his mind about what he'd been going to say. "Through a lot of stuff. All the way to the bottom. And there's something at the bottom of me, and it took hold of the end of your line, and it's not letting go."

"Something."

"Yeah."

"What *something?*"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "Close your eyes."

"Screw you."

"I'm not going to do anything. I'm not going to touch you, I'm not going in your head. I'm going to sit right here in my chair. Just close your eyes and feel down the line. Go on. Feel all the way to my end. You'll see."

She didn't want to. She did it anyway, because the alternative was standing there with her eyes open looking at him, and that was worse.

She shut her eyes.

The field went away. The cold, the smell of the ash, the wizards' voices, the click of the cameras. What was left was the line. It was much clearer with her eyes shut, almost a physical thing, a cable of heat running out of the middle of her and away. She followed it. It was easy. It was the easiest thing she'd ever done. She went along it the way she'd run along a trail in the dark, without thinking, out of her own chest and across the snow and into him—

—and down.

It went down. Further than she would have believed. Past the slow, heavy thump of his heart. Past something huge and bright and humming that she didn't understand, that felt like standing next to a power station at night, that made her teeth buzz. Down, and down, into a dark so deep and so wide it had no walls at all.

There was something in the dark.

She couldn't see it. There was nothing to see. But she could feel it there, the way you feel a big animal breathing in a pitch-black barn. Something enormous. Something that took up the whole of the dark, the way a mountain takes up a sky. The end of her line ran straight into it.

And it turned its head.

She felt it turn. She felt it notice her, down there at the bottom of him. She felt its attention come up the line toward her like heat from an opened oven door, and settle on her, huge and patient and old and absolutely, completely unafraid, and the feeling that came with it—

She'd been afraid it would feel like hunger. What came instead felt like *there you are*. It felt like something that had been waiting a long time and wasn't in any hurry, now. It felt pleased.

It felt *pleased*.

Leah's eyes snapped open. She took a step back so fast she nearly fell.

"*What is that?*"

"My wolf," he said.

She stared at him.

"You're a—" Her mouth wouldn't make the word. "You're one of us."

"No."

"You *are*, I can smell it on you, I could smell it from the—"

"I'm a wolf." He said it quite gently, for him. "Different sort. Your lot are Quileute, you've got it in your blood, it comes down through your families. Mine came from somewhere else. It's big, it's old, it's mine. Don't ask me where I got it, because that's a long story too, and it doesn't even involve a straw." He shrugged. "And it likes you. Which I'm sorry about."

"It *likes* me?"

"You saw." He shrugged again. "You heard it, earlier. That noise. That was it saying hello."

"That was it saying—" She thought about the noise. About her own hackles going down, about her tail tucking between her legs, about every wolf in both packs putting their ears down as if somebody had flicked a switch. "That was *hello?*"

"I said I was sorry."

"You said you ate something wrong."

"That was a lie." He sounded faintly embarrassed. "I'm not proud of it."

Leah stood there in the snow and looked at him. Her hands had closed into fists at her sides. She was so angry she couldn't see straight, and underneath the anger, underneath everything, the line hummed and hummed and pulled at her, and the thing at the bottom of him was still looking up it at her, she could feel it looking, and it was so calm.

"And the other thing?" she said.

"What other thing?"

"Don't." She jerked her head toward the ash, toward the Frenchwoman. "I heard her. We all heard her. The moon. *A man who is bitten.*" Her lip curled. "Caius said it this morning. Children of the Moon. The real thing. The thing all the old stories were about before they were about us. The bloodsucker said they hunted them almost to nothing."

"Is that what you call them?" He considered it. "Sounds nicer than what we call them."

"*Are you one?*"

"I got bitten," he said. "So, a bit. Not the usual way. The usual way, the moon comes up and you go mad and you'd eat your own mum. I don't do that. On the full moon I go a bit—" He made the same vague gesture around his own head that she'd seen him make to the Frenchwoman. "Furrier. Taller. Ears. Tail. Same bloke, though. Same head. I talk. I don't eat anybody. I'm very well-behaved. Ask Sabine, she's seen me do it in the canteen."

"So you're two kinds of wolf."

"Two kinds of wolf, one kind of wizard. Three on a bad day."

"And Thursday."

"Thursday I don't get a choice about it. That's all." He leaned back in the camping chair. "Thursday I'm furry for the night whether I like it or not."

"And the line?"

"No idea."

She stared at him.

"I've got no idea, Leah," he said. "That's the truth. Nobody's got any idea. That's why I'm sitting in a field reading a book the size of a fridge. I've never had one of these before. Nobody's had one of these before, as far as I can tell. A wolf of your sort doing that thing you do, landing on a wolf of my sort, with a werewolf bite on top. It's not in Volume One. I've got Volume Two coming. If it's in Volume Two, great. If it's not—" He shrugged. "Then it's me and you working it out. Sorry."

It was the second time he'd said her name. Something happened in her chest when he said it. The line hummed louder, and down at the bottom of him the thing in the dark shifted, and she felt it, and she knew he felt it, because his jaw tightened.

"Don't do that," she said.

"I'm not doing anything."

"*It* is."

"Yeah, well. It doesn't listen to me on the big stuff. Never has." He looked away from her, toward the trees, and his jaw worked. "I'll sort it. Whatever it is. I'm not going to leave you stuck with it."

"You don't know that."

"No," he agreed. "But I'm not going anywhere till I do."

She didn't believe him. She told herself she didn't believe him. And underneath her, quiet and steady as bedrock, the line said that he meant every word, and she knew it the same way she'd known, an hour ago, that he wouldn't hurt the kid. She knew it like she knew the ground was there.

She *hated* that.

And the thing she hated most, the thing she'd never in a million years say out loud, the thing that had been crawling around the back of her mind since the crack in the clearing, came up now and sat there and wouldn't go away.

Imprinting was for the gene. That was the theory, anyway, the one half the pack believed and nobody could prove. It was how the spirit warriors found the ones they were supposed to have their kids with, the ones who'd make more of them. That was the point of it. That was what it was *for*.

And she'd been so sure. She'd been so sure, all these months, that there was something wrong with her. That she was the end of the line. That her body had picked her out of the whole tribe and turned her into this and then shut down every other part of her, because what was the point of a wolf like her having kids? She was a dead end. A mistake. A glitch.

And now her body had looked across a field full of monsters and picked out an English werewolf with a cigarette habit and a camping chair.

*You lot have got a fucked-up way of making babies,* he'd said.

She almost laughed again. It came up in her throat like a sob.

"Leah," he said.

"Don't."

"Alright." He was quiet for a moment. "Go home. Get warm, or whatever you lot do instead of getting warm. Eat something. I'm not going to vanish while you're not looking."

She turned on her heel and walked away from him without a word.

She got about fifty yards. She got all the way to the place in the field where she'd stopped before, where the line went from humming to pulling. She felt it go tight. She made herself keep walking. Five more steps. Six. Every one of them harder than the last, the line dragging at her chest like a hook in a fish's mouth, and on the seventh step she stopped, because her legs simply would not take her any further.

She stood there in the snow, with her back to him and her fists clenched and her eyes burning, and didn't turn around.

Behind her, very quietly, so quietly that only she could have heard it, he said, "Yeah. Me too, love."

She didn't know what he meant by it. She didn't ask.

The woman in the fedora had been trying to measure Paul for ten minutes, and Jacob had finally had enough.

He handed Nessie down to Bella first. That took a while, because Nessie didn't want to get down, and Bella didn't want to let go of her once she had her, and Rosalie had to be convinced that it was safe to take her eyes off the kid for the thirty seconds it took Jacob to get behind a tree. Then he phased. He came out of the trees in his cutoffs with his bare feet crunching the snow and his whole body steaming slightly in the cold, and he walked straight down the line to where the Americans were.

Sam had done the same. He came out of the trees on the other side of the gap between the packs, in his own cutoffs, his face set and closed, and the two of them reached the woman in the fedora at almost exactly the same moment.

She looked at them both. She had iron-grey hair and a face that looked like it had been left out in the rain for fifty years.

"Well," she said. "Look at that. They come in people."

"Who are you?" said Sam.

"Auror Ruth Tillman. Magical Congress of the United States of America. Department of Magical Law Enforcement, Seattle field office." She held up a small gold badge with an eagle on it. "Register us," said Jacob.

"Put your names in a book. Count you. Find out what you are. Make sure you're in the right file." She looked at Paul, who had sat down on his haunches behind Sam with his tongue hanging out of the side of his mouth, grinning a wolf grin. "That one bites?"

"Only if you're lucky," said Jacob.

Sam gave him a look. Jacob ignored it.

"Which of you is in charge?" said Tillman.

"Both of us," said Sam.

"Him," said Jacob, at the same time, pointing at Sam.

Tillman looked from one to the other. "Two packs," she said. "Two bosses. Sure. Why not." She flipped open a notebook, an ordinary little spiral-bound one, and took a pen out of her coat pocket that was as ordinary as the notebook. "Names."

Sam gave them. All of them, his pack and then Jacob's, in a level, steady voice, and Tillman wrote each one down, and Okafor, the younger of the two men, walked up and down the line with a wand held loosely at his side, pointing it at each wolf in turn. A faint silver light flickered over each one and faded.

Okafor stopped at the far end of Jacob's side of the line. He looked at the gap there, where the trampled snow showed a set of big paw prints going out toward the field and, a little further along, a set of small bare human footprints going the same way.

"Ma'am," he said. "I've got one short."

"Short?"

"I've got one more name than I've got wolves." He pointed his wand down the line, counting again, and the silver light flickered across each wolf in turn, and stopped at the end. "One's missing."

"She's not missing," said Jacob. He jerked his head toward the middle of the field, where a girl in cutoffs and a black tank top was standing on her own in the snow, barefoot, with her arms wrapped around herself and her back to everyone. "She's right there."

"In people," said Tillman.

"In people."

"Why's she standing out there on her own?"

Jacob opened his mouth and shut it again.

"Long story," he said finally, and heard, with a horrible lurch, exactly who he sounded like.

"Ages," said Tillman.

Sam gave them. When he got to Brady and Collin, Tillman stopped writing.

"Thirteen?" she said.

"Fourteen, Collin," said Sam. "Last month."

And it says he's got a lycanthropy bite on him, and a registered moon-form, and a note in red ink that says *does not need Wolfsbane, do not offer*. "I don't know," said Jacob.

He didn't. That was the truth. All he knew was what Leah had felt, and what Leah had told him, in the pack-mind, in that howling storm right before she phased. *There's something else in him. Something big.* And he knew what he'd felt himself, earlier, when the guy made that noise in his chest. He'd felt it go through him like an Alpha order. Worse than an Alpha order. Her arms were wrapped around herself and her chin was down and her shoulders were stiff, and she hadn't moved, and Jacob knew—because he'd done it himself, four months ago, tried to walk away from Nessie's crib to go and get some air and found he couldn't make it to the front door—exactly why she hadn't moved.

He looked at Sam.

Sam was looking at Leah, too. His face was doing something Jacob had never seen it do before. For a long time, since before Jacob had phased even, Sam's face had done one thing whenever Leah was anywhere near him: it went very still and very careful and very guilty, like a man walking past a house he'd burned down. Now it had changed into something Jacob had no word for. Something complicated. Relief, and worry, and something like grief, and something that might have been the beginning of being sorry for her in a whole new way.

"Is she okay?" Sam said, very low. He wasn't asking Tillman.

"She's Leah," said Jacob.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." Jacob let out a breath. "She's okay. She's not okay. She's going to be pissed at all of us for a year. You know."

"Yeah," said Sam. "I know." He was quiet for a second. "Pack law."

"Yeah."

"Covers him."

"I know, Sam."

"All of us. Both packs. Doesn't matter whose she is now. Nobody touches him." Sam's voice was steady, but his jaw was tight. "Even if he—"

"I *know*, Sam."

They stood there, the two of them, side by side in the snow in their cutoffs, not looking at each other. Paul's tail thumped. Tillman looked at the two of them, and then at Leah, and then at the guy in the camping chair, and then she shut her notebook with a snap.

"I'm gonna need a bigger file," she said.

There was a crack from the middle of the field.

Every single wolf on the line came up on its feet. Jacob's head whipped around so fast his neck clicked. Down the line, Embry yelped.

There was something standing next to the camping chair.

It was small. It came up to about the guy's knee. It was the wrinkled, grey-pink color of an old potato, and it had ears like a bat's—huge, flapping, sprouting white hair—and a long droopy nose, and big wet bloodshot eyes, and it was wearing what looked like a white tea towel. It was carrying a book. Or the book was carrying it; it was hard to say. The book was nearly as big as the thing was, another slab of cracked brown leather with brass on the corners, and the little thing was holding it up over its head with both skinny arms like a waiter with a tray.

And behind it, floating gently in the air at the height of the guy's elbow, there was an actual tray. Silver. With a teapot on it, and a cup and saucer, and a little jug of milk, and a plate piled high with sandwiches cut into triangles.

"Volume Two, Master," said the little thing, in a croaky, wheezy voice like a door that needed oiling.

"Cheers, Kreacher."

The guy took the book. It was obviously very heavy. He took it like it weighed about as much as a magazine and set it on his knees on top of the first one, and the camping chair creaked.

"Any trouble?"

"No trouble, Master. Kreacher is very quiet. The goblin door did not hear Kreacher. The wizards in the stacks did not see Kreacher." The thing looked smug. "One of them was asleep. Kreacher left him a blanket."

"That's very decent of you."

"Kreacher is a decent elf." The creature turned, and looked at the floating tray, and the tray drifted forward and hung in the air in front of the guy, just below his chin. "And Master will eat."

"I'm alright."

"Master will *eat.*" The thing folded its arms. It came up to his knee and it folded its arms and looked up at him with its huge watery eyes, and Jacob, who had grown up with Billy Black and knew exactly that look, felt something in his chest unclench for the first time in what felt like weeks. Master went to the Department at six o'clock and did not come home, and then Master went to Geneva, and then Master went on the big aeroplane, and then Master came *here*, and Master has not eaten one single bite. Kreacher made these. Kreacher cut the crusts off. Master will eat."

The guy looked at him for a long, flat second. Then he reached out, took a sandwich off the plate, and bit it in half.

"Ham," he said, with his mouth full. "Lovely. Thanks, Kreacher."

"Jake!" said a high voice.

Nessie. Of course. She'd wriggled out of Bella's arms—or Bella had let her down, which seemed more likely—and she was standing in the snow at Bella's knee with her eyes like saucers and one finger pointed straight at the little creature.

"Jake! Jake, it's a *goblin!*"

The creature turned around.

It looked at Nessie. It looked at her for a long time, with its head on one side and its bat ears twitching, and Jacob had the very distinct feeling that it did not like what it saw.

"Kreacher," it said, with enormous and offended dignity, "is a *house-elf*, half-breed girl, and not a filthy *goblin*, and Kreacher would thank the half-breed—"

"Oi," said the guy.

The creature froze.

"Manners," the guy said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't have to.

There was a long pause. The creature's ears drooped, slowly, all the way down until the tips touched its shoulders. It turned back to Nessie and bowed. It was a very stiff bow, and a very short one, and it looked like it hurt.

"Kreacher," it said, through what Jacob suspected were gritted teeth, "is a house-elf. Young miss."

"Oh," said Nessie, delighted. "Hello, Kreacher."

The creature looked at her a moment longer. Something passed over its wrinkled face that might, in a better light, have been the very faint beginning of reluctant approval.

"Hello," it said shortly, "young miss," and turned its back on her and went to stand by the guy's chair with its hands folded in front of its tea towel.

Jacob realised his hands were shaking.

He looked down at them. He didn't understand it, at first. He was standing in the snow in his cutoffs, and he wasn't cold, he was never cold, and the guy had just eaten a ham sandwich, and the little elf-thing had bowed to Nessie, and everything was—everything was—

He looked past the camping chair at the ash.

Thirty-one heaps of it. The wizards in cloaks were still going around them with their cameras, flashing, bending over every one. He could see the gold glinting from here. There was a big wet black ring around each heap where the snow had melted, and the ring at the front, the one with the long spray of grey fanned out from it, that one had been Felix. And somewhere in the middle, one of them had been Aro. And Caius, who'd wanted to burn Nessie, who'd been the one pushing for it, the one who'd come all this way to watch her die.

They were gone.

He'd watched it happen. He'd seen them burn with his own eyes. It only landed now, standing in the snow watching a wrinkled little elf fold its hands beside a camping chair. For four months, every single day, every single minute, there had been a clock running in the back of his head. Every time he looked at Nessie. Every time she laughed, or slept, or grew. *They're coming. They're coming for her. Alice saw them. They're coming.* It had been there so long he'd stopped hearing it.

He heard it stop.

There was no clock. They weren't coming. They weren't coming anymore, not this winter or next winter or ever. Nobody was coming. She was going to grow up. She was going to grow up, and he was going to be there, and nobody was going to take her away.

His eyes were burning.

"Jacob," said Sam quietly, beside him.

"I'm fine."

"Jake."

"I'm *fine.*" He scrubbed the heel of his hand across his eyes, hard. "I'm fine. I just—they're *gone*, Sam."

"I know."

"They're really gone. Like, *gone* gone."

"I know." Sam put a hand on his shoulder. Just for a second. It was heavy and warm and it was the first time Sam had touched him since he'd broken away from the pack. Then Sam took it back. "I know, Jake."

Down the field, Nessie had come running across the snow to Jacob on her short legs, with Bella a step behind her, and she wrapped both arms around his knee and looked up at him.

"Jake, why are you crying?"

He crouched down and picked her up and held her against his chest, and she put her hand on his face, and the picture she showed him was of him, his own face, from her height, with wet eyes, and a big question mark of feeling attached to it.

"I'm not crying," he said. "It's the smoke. It's in my eyes."

She looked at him very seriously. She didn't believe him. She didn't say so.

Across the field, the guy in the camping chair had opened the second book.

The second book smelled the same as the first. Dust, old glue, dried sage. And very faintly, under that, of Kreacher, who smelled of furniture polish and the cupboard under the stairs at Grimmauld Place, because that was where he still insisted on sleeping, whatever Harry said.

Harry ate a second sandwich and opened it to the contents page.

*Kitsune (cont.)* through *Zouwu*. Six hundred and twelve pages. The ink was a different colour from Volume One, browner, and the handwriting was smaller and more cramped, as though whoever had written it had been running out of paper and patience at the same time.

He ran his finger down the list.

He was aware, while he did it, of a great many things. That was how it went in the field; you read with one part of you and kept the rest out on the snow. He was aware of Kreacher standing at his elbow with his hands folded, giving off the silent satisfaction of an elf who had made his master eat a sandwich in front of witnesses. He was aware of the wardens at the ash heaps, counting. Of the Americans down the line with the wolves, and of the big lad, Jacob, crouched in the snow in his cutoffs with the kid in his arms and his face wet. Harry didn't look at that for long. He'd seen that face before, on other people, at the end of other things. It was the face of somebody hearing a clock stop.

He was aware, most of all, of Leah.

She was fifty-odd yards behind him and to his left, standing on her own in the snow with her back to him. She hadn't moved since she'd tried to walk away. He didn't need to turn round to know it. He could feel her the way you can feel a fire at your back. The line ran out of the middle of his chest and away behind him, humming, and every so often it tugged, very slightly, when she shifted her weight or clenched her hands. Right now it was humming low and steady. She was standing very still and being very, very angry.

He'd lied to her a bit.

Only a bit. He'd said he had no idea, and that was true. He'd said nobody had ever had one of these before, and as far as he knew that was true too. What he hadn't said was that when she'd stood in front of him with her eyes shut and gone down the line, he'd felt her go. He'd felt her come down through him, light and quick and furious, past his heart and past his magic and all the way to the bottom, and he'd felt the wolf lift its head to meet her. And he'd felt something he wasn't going to name yet, standing in a field, with his mouth full of ham.

So he hadn't said that. He'd said *me too, love*, which was stupid, and which she'd heard, and which he was going to pay for later.

He turned the page.

*Lamia (Hellenic): blood-bonds with thralls.* No. *Leanan sídhe: muse-bonds.* No. *Life-debts (wizarding).* He knew more about those than the book did. *Lycanthropes: bonds involving.* He slowed down, read it twice. *No bond of any recognised category has been verified as forming with or upon a lycanthrope in either of his shapes. Lycanthropic infection is understood to sever or suppress pre-existing familiar-bonds (cf. the case of Mr. Bartholomew Hepzibah Crumb, 1803, whose Kneazle left him).* Poor Bartholomew. Poor Kneazle. No.

*Merpeople: song-bonds.* No. *Patronus resonance.* No. *Phoenix-bonds.* He stopped on that one for a second, and thought about a red and gold bird and a tower office and an old man with a long white beard, and then he turned the page a bit harder than he needed to.

"Mr. Potter."

He looked up.

The Romanian was standing a few yards off, in the snow, the one who'd been left. Stefan, the file had called him. He was a thin, stooped man with skin like chalk and a fine, powdery look to it, like the old ones in Italy had had, and his eyes were a deep, dull crimson under a faint milky film. He'd come up without making a sound. Harry had heard him anyway.

Kreacher bristled. Harry put a hand out, flat, and Kreacher stopped bristling.

"Stefan," said Harry.

The vampire didn't seem surprised that he knew his name. He looked down at the black star in the snow, the one still faintly steaming. He looked at it for a long time.

"Did he feel it?" he said.

"No," said Harry. "Too quick."

"You are certain."

"I'm certain. Faster than you can feel things. Faster than you can think. He was there and then he wasn't."

Stefan's eyes moved, slightly. Not to Harry. Past him, to the left, where the Irish lot were standing in a little knot. The girl, Maggie, was watching them. She met Stefan's eyes, and after a moment she nodded, once.

"True," she said quietly.

Stefan looked back at the star.

"I am going," he said. "As you ordered. I am dispersing."

"Give the lady in the grey coat your name on the way out. She'll want it for the book."

"My name." Something happened at the corner of the chalk-white mouth, too thin to call a smile. "Very well. I will give her my name." He looked up at Harry. "You understand that I will hate you. For the rest of my existence. Which is likely to be very long."

"Get in the queue, mate."

"There is a queue?"

"There's a whole waiting room. There's magazines."

Stefan looked at him for another long moment. Then he inclined his head, very slightly, the way men had done it in courts that had burned down before England had a king, and he turned and walked away across the snow toward Sabine. He walked slowly. He didn't look back. When he'd given Sabine his name—Harry heard it, a long, old, many-syllabled thing in a language nobody had spoken in a thousand years—he walked on into the trees, and the trees closed behind him, and he was gone.

Harry watched the place where he'd gone for a while. Then he looked down at the book again.

"Master," Kreacher said quietly.

"I'm fine, Kreacher."

"Master should have another sandwich."

He had another sandwich.

He'd got about two pages further when the next one came.

This one made noise on purpose. Harry heard the boots crunching on the snow from twenty yards off, long careless strides, and a second, lighter set behind them hurrying to keep up, and a woman's voice saying *Garrett* in the tone of somebody who'd been saying *Garrett* for a very long time. He looked up.

The bloke was tall and rangy, with long sandy hair tied back at his neck and red eyes and a big crooked grin on his face. He came to a stop just short of the scorch and planted his hands on his hips. The woman who'd been hurrying after him stopped at his elbow, very blonde and very pretty and very obviously ready to drag him away by the ear. Kate, Harry thought. The file had a note on Kate. Something about her hands.

"Sir," said the bloke. "I won't keep you. I'm Garrett."

"Alright, Garrett."

"I fought in the Revolution," Garrett said. "Back in seventy-five. Stood in a line with a borrowed musket and watched the redcoats come up the road." He grinned wider. "I've been waiting two hundred and thirty years to see somebody tell a king no again. And mean it."

"They weren't kings, mate. They were three blokes in bathrobes with a tourist trap."

"That's what I said about George the Third," said Garrett.

Despite himself, Harry snorted.

Garrett held out his hand.

Kreacher made a small offended noise at knee height. Harry ignored it. He looked at the hand for a second, and then he took it, because the bloke clearly meant it and there was no reason not to. It was like shaking hands with a marble statue left out overnight. Garrett's grip was very strong and very cold, and Harry felt him feel the heat of Harry's palm and saw the red eyes widen a fraction.

"Hot as a stove," Garrett said.

"Runs in the family."

"Does it, now." Garrett let go. He looked at Harry a moment longer, measuring, the way a man who'd stood in a lot of battle lines looks at another man he'd quite like on his side of one. Then he nodded, once, satisfied, as if he'd settled something with himself. "Thank you," he said. "That's all. I wanted to say it to your face."

"You've said it."

"I have." He turned to the blonde. "Kate, I've said it."

"I heard," said Kate. She hadn't taken her eyes off Harry. She'd been looking at his hands, Harry realised, the whole time. "The lightning," she said. "That was you."

"Yeah."

"From a clear sky. No storm. Nothing to pull it from." Her fingers flexed at her side. "How?"

"Long story."

"I've got time."

"I haven't, love. Sorry." He tipped his head at the book. "Homework."

She looked at him a second longer, and then, unexpectedly, she smiled, a quick sharp private smile, and took Garrett's arm and steered him away across the snow. Harry heard her say, quite low, *I want to know how he did that*, and Garrett say, *Course you do, sweetheart*, and then they were back among the Denalis and he went back to his book.

*Shifter-ties.* That was a whole chapter, forty pages, and he went through it carefully, every heading. *Animagi (consult Vol. I, Animagus pair-bonds)*. *Selkies: skin-bonds*. *Skin-changers (Norse)*. *Kitsune-human (consult Vol. I, Kitsune, which runs to eleven pages, God help you)*. And near the end, a single line: *Quileute (N. America): see Imprinting, Vol. I.*

"Brilliant," said Harry. "Love a cross-reference."

*Soul-bonds (classical).* Ritual-made, both parties wizards, both parties consenting, both parties standing in a chalk circle at midsummer with a lot of candles. No. *Soul-bonds (spontaneous).* Recorded only between twins. No.

He went to the index.

It was even smaller and fainter than the one in Volume One, the ink nearly gone to nothing, and he had to hold the page up close to his face to read it. *Bonds, involuntary.* That ran to three pages of cross-references and every one of them was something that happened to somebody else. *Bonds, involving more than one form of the same party.* One entry, a Russian Animagus in the seventeen-hundreds whose familiar had bonded to him in his human shape and refused to have anything to do with him as a bear. No.

*Bonds, reaching the beast.* He followed it. It was a short paragraph about werewolves again, and about how nothing reached the beast, and the beast reached nothing, and that was the whole tragedy of lycanthropy, and wasn't that sad. No.

*Bonds, between shifters of differing kinds.*

There was an entry.

He sat up.

It was short. It was in a different hand, he thought, from the rest of the index, older and spikier, as if it had been copied out of somewhere else.

*No verified case. One unverified account only, Gwynedd, c. 1340: of a man "under the moon's curse," and a woman "of the old shape," who was "of a people that took the wolf's coat by right of blood and not by any curse." The two are said to have been bound at first sight, the woman to the man, "as the women of her people are bound"; but the binding "went into the man past his heart and took hold of the man and the beast in him both, so that the beast knew her and would not let her go." The account records that the pair lived some years together in the hills above the Conwy, and that at the full moon—*

And there it stopped.

There was plenty of page left. It stopped in the middle of a line, and under it, in the same spiky hand, it said: *Remainder of account lost. Source: the chronicle of the Cistercian house at Maenan, in the valley of the Conwy. House dissolved 1537; library dispersed; manuscript not recovered.*

And under that, in faded green ink, in the margin, the same hand as the *Ha!* in Volume One:

*Typical.*

Harry read it three times.

Then he closed the book, very gently, and sat back in the camping chair and looked up at the sky.

"Henry the bloody Eighth," he said.

"Master?"

"Nothing, Kreacher." He rubbed his eyes. "Some Welsh monks wrote down exactly what I needed nearly seven hundred years ago, and then a fat bloke in a big hat burned their library down because he wanted a divorce."

Kreacher considered this.

"Kreacher could look," he said. "For the book."

"It's been gone for four hundred and seventy years, Kreacher."

"Kreacher has found things that were gone longer." The elf's hand had gone to the locket at his neck. "Kreacher is very good at finding things that were gone."

Harry looked at him.

"Yeah," he said, after a moment. "Yeah, you are. Alright. When we're done here. Have a look."

Kreacher's ears lifted slightly.

*The beast knew her and would not let her go.* Harry thought about that. He thought about the wolf at the bottom of the dark with its head up and the line going into its chest. *At the full moon—*

At the full moon what?

"Potter."

Sabine was walking back toward him from the ash, her coffee cup finally empty and crushed in one hand. She had a sheet of parchment in the other. She had the expression of a woman who had been on a Floo call with Geneva and had not enjoyed it.

"Thirty-one pendants," she said. "Thirty-one heaps. Thirty-one. I counted three times."

"I'm thrilled for you."

"And the Romanian has gone. He gave me a name I cannot spell."

"I heard."

"And Geneva has decided."

He looked at her.

"You are staying," Sabine said.

"Sabine."

"You are staying through the moon. The Confederation appoints you liaison to the newly registered Quileute packs, and to the hybrid, and to—" she glanced over his shoulder at Leah's back—"to whatever that is. You will supervise the remainder of the registration. You will report to me daily. You will be present on the full moon in a supervisory capacity—"

"*Supervisory capacity?*"

"—and you will be in your other form, which the Confederation considers a *learning opportunity.*"

"The Confederation can stick its learning opportunity up its—"

"I have already told them you would say that. They have noted it." She held out the parchment. It was sealed with silver wax, the ICW's seal, the globe and the wand. "Your Head of Department has been informed. He says, and I quote, *the thing in the room has waited since thirteen hundred and it can wait until Saturday*. He also says to tell you to *please not burn Washington State*."

Harry took the parchment and looked at it. He didn't open it. He knew what it said.

"Where am I supposed to sleep?" he said.

"I don't care. In a tree. In your chair. Somewhere you can be found."

"Mr. Potter."

It was Carlisle. Of course it was. He'd been standing in the same spot for the last twenty minutes, near enough, with his hands folded behind his back, pretending not to read over Harry's shoulder. He stepped forward now. "Our home is a few miles from here. You'd be more than welcome. We have room."

"Cheers, Doc. No offence, but no." Harry tucked the parchment into his jacket. "Your house'll smell like a sweet shop on fire. I'd be up all night sneezing."

Carlisle's eyebrows went up, very slightly. He didn't argue.

"My dad's got a couch."

Harry turned his head.

It was Jacob. He was still crouched in the snow with the kid on his hip, and his eyes were red, and he was looking at Harry with an expression that was trying very hard to be neutral and not quite getting there.

"In La Push," Jacob said. "It's lumpy. And the heating's crap. And my dad's going to have about a million questions, and he's in a wheelchair, so you can't outrun him, he'll just roll after you." He hesitated. "But it's on the rez. It's—" His eyes flicked past Harry, to the left, toward the place where Leah was standing. "It's close."

Harry felt the line in his chest go tight.

Behind him, fifty yards away, Leah had gone rigid. He didn't need to turn round. He felt her hear it. He felt the jolt go up the line, sharp and hot, and under that, much fainter, buried, something else, which she'd have killed him for noticing.

He didn't turn round.

"Lumpy's fine," he said.

The kid on Jacob's hip clapped her hands.

Sabine let out a long breath through her nose. "Good. Then that is decided. Then you are staying in La Push on a lumpy couch, and I am going back to Geneva to write forty forms, and if you burn anything—"

She stopped.

Harry had already turned his head.

So had every vampire in the field. All of them at once, the way they'd all turned at the crack when he arrived, every head swinging south toward the far side of the clearing. A beat later the wolves did the same. Down the line, ears went up.

Something was coming through the forest.

Several somethings. Fast. Very fast. He heard them before anyone human could have: the long rushing hiss of bodies moving through trees at a speed no animal had any business moving at, the soft crack of snow shaken off branches. Four. Five. Coming from the south, straight at the clearing, flat out.

Kreacher stepped in front of the camping chair.

"Easy," Harry said, and stood up.

The two books went under one arm. The chair folded itself up and vanished. He put his other hand back in his pocket, and waited.

Beside him, Carlisle had gone very still. And then, quite suddenly, his whole face changed.

"Oh," Carlisle said. "Oh, thank God."

The first of them came out of the trees.

Alice had been running blind for forty minutes.

Before that, she'd been running with her eyes wide open. She had been running for days, from the Amazon to the coast and the coast to the north, with Jasper at her side and Kachiri ranging ahead and Huilen and Nahuel behind, and every step of the way she'd had the clearing in her head. She'd been watching it the way you'd watch a pot about to boil over. Aro's choices, flickering. Caius's choices, hard and bright and ugly. The guard lined up in the snow. The decision forming and unforming and forming again: *we fight*, *we wait*, *we listen*, *we fight*. She had known exactly how long she had. She had known, to the minute, when she needed to come out of the trees with Nahuel to have any chance at all. She'd been on time. She'd been going to make it.

And then, at nine minutes past ten that morning, somewhere in the forests south of the Columbia, every single one of them went out.

All at once. Aro and Caius and Marcus and Jane and Alec and Felix and Demetri and all the rest of them, every future she'd been holding in her head, every flicker and branch and choice, gone. Like a row of candles in a gust. One second they had been there, burning; the next, nothing. Not a wisp of smoke.

She had stopped running so suddenly that Jasper had gone ten yards past her before he could turn.

She had stood there in the snow, staring at nothing, and searched. She'd searched for them the way you search a dark room for a light switch, frantic, patting the walls. There was nothing to find. There was no Volturi anymore. There was nowhere in the world that they were going to be, ever, at all.

And then she'd looked for her family.

She'd looked for Edward, and found a white blank where Edward should have been. Bella, blank. Carlisle, Esme, Rosalie, Emmett. Blank, blank, blank, blank. The Denalis, the Irish, the Egyptians, all of them, everyone she'd left standing in that clearing: one solid blinding sheet of white, like snow-glare, like staring into the sun.

She'd been blind to the wolves since the beginning. She'd been blind to Renesmee since before she was born. She had got used to the little blank spaces in her visions where they stood, had learned to see around them, the way you learn to see around a scratch on your glasses. This was different. This was all of it. This was the whole clearing gone white, everyone she loved in it, all at the same moment that every Volturi future had gone out.

She had thought they were dead.

She'd thought it with absolute, cold certainty for about the length of time it took Jasper to reach her and take her by the shoulders. Then she'd thought: *no*. Because she'd found one thing. A single flicker, at the very edge of the white. One of the Romanians, Stefan, walking. Walking away through the trees on his own, slow and stooped, with snow on his shoulders. Alone. Without Vladimir.

She didn't know what it meant. She didn't know what any of it meant. She'd grabbed Jasper's hand and said *run*, and they'd run.

They had been running ever since. She hadn't seen a single thing the whole way. She'd felt Jasper's hand in hers like a lifeline, and she'd kept her eyes on the trees in front of her, and she'd run.

They came out of the forest on the south side of the clearing.

She saw the ash first.

Heaps of it, grey and soft and steaming slightly, scattered across the snow where the Volturi had been going to stand. Each one sat in a black ring of melt. There were people bent over them, people in long dark cloaks, holding up boxy things that flashed. There was gold glinting in the grey. There was a smell over everything, thick and sweet and sickening, that she knew. Everybody of their kind knew it.

She saw the wolves. All of them. Both packs, scattered across the field in a long ragged line with strangers walking among them.

And she saw them. Her family. Edward and Bella, Carlisle and Esme, Rosalie and Emmett, standing in the snow in the middle of the field. On their feet. Whole.

Alice made a sound. She didn't know what it was. She was across the clearing before it had finished coming out of her, and Edward had turned, and she hit him so hard he rocked back a step, and she had her arms around his neck and then she had Bella too, both of them, and she was holding on as tight as she'd ever held anything.

"You're here," she said into Bella's hair. "You're here, you're here, I couldn't see you, I couldn't see any of you, I thought—"

"I know," said Edward. He was holding her just as tightly. "I know, Alice. I heard you coming. I heard you from three miles away. You were screaming in your head."

"I was not."

"You were."

"Well, I *thought* you were *dead*—"

Bella was laughing. It was a cracked, wet, wobbly kind of laugh, and she was squeezing Alice so hard that Alice would have had bruises, if she'd been able to bruise.

"Where's Renesmee?" said Alice. "I can't see her, I never can, where—"

"Here," said Jacob's voice, from very close by, and she looked down and saw him crouched in the snow in a pair of cutoffs with his eyes red and Renesmee on his hip, beaming at her.

"Auntie Alice!"

Alice let go of Bella long enough to scoop her niece up off Jacob's hip and hug her too, and Renesmee put both hands on her face and showed her, all at once, in a great tumbling rush: the line of black cloaks in the snow; the mist; the crack; a man, standing in the middle of the field; the fire, blue-white and blinding; the ash. Lightning out of a clear sky. A cigarette. A mirror. A chair. A little wrinkled creature in a tea towel bowing to her. A man crouching down in the snow so his face was level with hers.

Alice stood there with Renesmee's hands on her face and her eyes full of all of it.

"Oh," she said faintly, when it was done. "Oh. Okay."

Jasper had come up beside her. He'd come across the field more slowly, the way he always did, watching everything, and his hand settled on the small of her back. She felt his attention go out across the clearing like a net being cast. He'd be feeling all of it. All of them. Every emotion in the field, the relief and the shock and the grief and the fear and the leftover terror, pouring into him from every direction.

"Jazz?"

"They're alright," he said quietly. "Mostly. They're in shock. All of them. Bella's furious about something. Rosalie's still afraid. Carlisle's—" He stopped. His head had turned. "Who is *that?*"

She followed his gaze.

There was a man standing out in the open snow, a little way past the ash, with two big leather-bound books under one arm and his other hand in the pocket of a battered brown jacket. He was tall and broad, with black hair that wanted cutting and a couple of days of stubble, and he was watching them. Beside him stood a short woman in a long grey coat with a dark plait over her shoulder, and at his feet, in front of him, stood a tiny wrinkled creature in a tea towel, with its feet planted and its bony arms folded across its chest, glaring at the newcomers as if it had personally been put in charge of the clearing and didn't approve of the guest list.

The man in Renesmee's pictures.

Alice looked at him. She looked properly, the way she looked at anyone new—not just with her eyes, but with the other part, the part that went forward into the tangle of what someone was going to do and where they were going to be.

There was nothing there.

It was a different nothing from the soft blank of the wolves, or the shimmer she'd got used to around Renesmee. It was as if she were looking for him in a part of the world that wasn't there. She reached harder. She reached as hard as she could, and it was like reaching into a hole in the ice, cold and black and very, very deep, with no bottom to it at all, and she pulled back so fast that she swayed against Jasper's side.

"I can't see him," she whispered.

"I know," said Edward. "I can't hear him."

"Jasper—"

"He's tired," said Jasper slowly. He was frowning. He was standing very still, the way he stood when he was concentrating. "Irritated. Hungry, still, a bit. He's—worried, I think? About something behind him. Something to the left." His eyes flicked, briefly, to a young Quileute woman standing alone in the snow in cutoffs and a tank top with her back to the field. Then they came back. "And underneath all of that, there's—something else. Something big."

"Big how?"

Jasper didn't answer for a moment.

"Do you remember," he said finally, very quietly, "the bear? The grizzly. In the Yukon. The one we came on asleep, in the den in the snow. Before it woke up."

Alice remembered.

"It's like that," said Jasper. "It's like standing next to that."

The man in the jacket had heard every word. Alice could tell. He didn't react. He only tilted his head slightly and looked at Jasper, a long, flat, considering look, and then he looked at her.

"You'll be the one who sees things," he said. He was English. "The one who ran off."

"Alice," said Alice.

"Alice. Right. You're in the file." He considered her. "Can't see me, can you?"

"No."

"Good." He seemed to think about it. "Don't take it personally. Nobody can."

Alice found she had nothing to say to that at all.

"Mr. Potter," said Carlisle, and Alice heard something new in Carlisle's voice, something she had not heard there for weeks: a kind of shaky lightness. "This is my daughter, Alice. And Jasper. They've been away."

"Yeah, I got that." The man's eyes moved past Alice, past Jasper. To the others who had come out of the trees behind them and stopped there, at the edge of the field, uncertain.

Kachiri had already slipped away to her sisters. Zafrina and Senna had her between them, their hands on her shoulders, their heads bent close, talking fast and low in their own tongue. That left two standing alone at the edge of the clearing. A woman, slight and dark and ageless, her eyes a deep red, standing with her body angled protectively in front of the second figure.

And the second figure.

Alice had brought him thousands of miles. She'd found him in the forests of the Amazon after weeks of searching, following stories, following rumours, following the faint old trail of a legend. She'd persuaded him to come. She'd run with him every step of the way north, and all that way she'd been thinking of the moment she would bring him out of the trees in front of the Volturi, of Aro's face when he saw. That had been the whole point. That had been the plan.

There was no Volturi to show him to.

He stood there anyway, very still, at the edge of the snow. He was tall. He looked to be about twenty, with long black hair tied back at his neck and skin the warm dark brown of polished teak, and he had a face that could have been carved for a coin. His eyes were dark, and very watchful. And the thing about him that every vampire in the clearing would have noticed in the first second, the thing Alice had noticed in the first second when she found him, was his heart.

It was beating. Fast and light and steady, like a bird's.

Like Renesmee's.

Alice watched the man in the jacket hear it.

She saw it land. He didn't move. His face didn't change much at all. But his eyes went to the newcomer, and stayed there, and then went very slowly to Renesmee in Alice's arms, and then back again. And the woman in the grey coat beside him, who had been holding a crushed paper cup, opened her hand and let the cup fall into the snow.

"Alice," said Carlisle, very softly. "Is he—"

"This is Nahuel," Alice said. "And his aunt, Huilen. Nahuel is—" She took a breath she didn't need. "Nahuel is like Renesmee. His mother was human. His father was one of us." She looked at Bella, and then at Carlisle. "He's a hundred and fifty years old. He stopped growing when he was seven. He's been exactly like this ever since."

There was a long, perfect silence.

Then Bella made a sound beside her, a small, broken sound, and turned and put her face into Edward's shoulder. Edward's arms went around her and held on. Over her head, Alice saw his face, and he was looking at Renesmee, and his eyes were shut.

*He stopped growing when he was seven.*

*She's going to live.*

Alice didn't need to see the future to know that was what they were thinking. All of them. Esme had both hands pressed over her mouth. Rosalie had turned away and was standing with her back to them all, very straight, very still. Carlisle had taken a step toward Nahuel, and then another, his face naked in a way Alice had almost never seen it, and he held out his hand.

"Nahuel," he said. "Thank you for coming. Thank you so very much for coming."

Nahuel looked at the hand for a moment. Then, slowly, he took it.

In Alice's arms, Renesmee had gone very quiet. She was staring at Nahuel with her lips parted and her whole small body leaning toward him, and Alice could hear her heart, fast and light, beating almost in time with his.

"Auntie Alice," she whispered. "He sounds like me."

"He does, sweetheart."

Renesmee wriggled. Alice, after a glance at Bella, carried her the few steps across the snow, and Renesmee reached out with one small hand and laid it flat against Nahuel's cheek.

Nahuel went absolutely still. Alice watched his dark eyes lose their focus and go far away, the way everyone's did, and stay there. When Renesmee took her hand back he blinked, slowly, twice. He looked down at her for a long moment.

"Hello, little one," he said softly, in his careful English.

Renesmee beamed at him. Behind Nahuel, Huilen let out a long breath, and for the first time since they had come out of the trees some of the stiffness went out of her narrow shoulders.

Out in the snow, the woman in the grey coat said something in French. It was short and heartfelt and Alice was fairly sure it was not polite.

"Potter," she said, in English.

"Don't," said the man in the jacket.

"Potter, that is another one."

"I can see that, Sabine."

"That is another *unregistered hybrid.*"

"I *know*, Sabine." He shut his eyes. He stood there in the snow with his eyes shut and the two big books under his arm and took a long, slow breath in through his nose, and let it out again. When he opened his eyes he was looking at Nahuel. "Alright, mate. Nahuel. Hundred and fifty, yeah?"

Nahuel let go of Carlisle's hand. He turned to look at the man in the jacket. His eyes went over him, slowly, as if he were trying to work out what he was looking at and not getting very far.

"Yes," he said. His English was careful and accented. "A hundred and fifty years."

"Your dad. Vampire."

"Yes."

"Mum. Human."

"Yes. She died when I was born." He said it plainly. "My aunt raised me."

"Right." The man in the jacket nodded slowly. "And you've never been in a book. Anywhere. Nobody's ever written you down."

"No."

"Course not." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "And is that it? Is it just you? Just you and her—" a tip of his head toward Renesmee in Alice's arms—"in the whole wide world? Please say yes."

Nahuel was quiet for a moment.

Alice, who had heard the whole story already, on a riverbank in the Amazon a week ago, closed her eyes.

"My father's name is Joham," Nahuel said. "He believes he is creating a new race. He has been trying for a long time." He paused. "I have three sisters. That I know of."

The silence this time was longer.

The woman in the grey coat looked at the man in the jacket. The man in the jacket looked at the sky. The tiny creature at his feet looked from one to the other and back again, and its huge ears drooped slowly down until the tips of them were touching its shoulders.

"Three," said the man in the jacket.

"That I know of," said Nahuel.

"That you know of." He nodded. He nodded for quite a long time. "Course there are. Course there bloody are."

"Potter," said Sabine. Her voice had gone very quiet. "Is that everything?"

The man in the jacket laughed. It was a short laugh, and tired, and there was no humour in it at all, and somewhere behind him, out in the snow, the young Quileute woman who had been standing with her back to everyone turned her head, just slightly, at the sound.

"No, Sabine," he said. "It's never everything." He looked down at the creature at his feet. "Kreacher."

"Master?"

"Put the kettle on." He shifted the two books higher under his arm and looked out over the clearing, over the ash and the wizards and the wolves, over the Cullens and their witnesses, over Nahuel and Huilen at the edge of the trees, and the grey sky, and the long purple stain of smoke still smeared across it. "We're going to be here a while."
