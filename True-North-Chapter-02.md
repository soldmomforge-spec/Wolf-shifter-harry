# True North

## Chapter Two: Fake Wolves

---

### I.

The smile lasted until she hit the road.

Then Leah stood on the washboard gravel in the moonlight, barefoot, in a stranger's coat, with blood drying on her arm and a dead man four hundred meters behind her, and it hit her what she'd just done.

She'd said yes. To a drink. With a seven-foot wolf in a wizard's coat who'd killed a man in front of her with a stick that turned out to be a wand, and then asked her out over the body like he was asking whether she wanted fries with that.

"Okay," she said out loud, to nobody. "Okay. Cool. Great."

Her wolf rumbled, low and pleased, somewhere under her breastbone.

"Nobody asked you."

She started running.

Four kilometers to town. She'd given him ten minutes, which meant she'd given herself ten minutes too, and she'd forgotten that part when she said it. The coat flapped around her shins. She fumbled the silver clasp at the throat shut one-handed, and then it held, heavy and cold against her collarbone, and the wool stopped trying to come open every time she took a stride. The gravel bit her feet. She didn't care. Her left forearm itched like a nest of fire ants under the skin, the wound pulling itself shut, and every time the coat sleeve brushed it, she hissed.

A kilometer from town she stood in the willows in the ditch while an old Dodge rattled past without seeing her. Nobody saw anything at night on these roads. That was probably how a guy like Vadas had gotten away with it.

How many girls, Harry had said. Seven. Laid out on rocks.

She ran faster.

---

Marg was on the porch of the big house at Brewster's Cabins with a cigarette and a mug of something steaming, a blanket around her shoulders. She watched Leah come up the drive in the moonlight: barefoot, wearing a man's charcoal-gray coat that came almost to her ankles, with dried blood all the way down her left forearm to the fingertips.

Marg took a long drag and let it out slowly.

"I'm not going to ask," she said.

"Good," Leah said.

"That's not your coat."

"No."

"Uh-huh." Marg's eyes went to the arm. In the yellow porch light the wound was a long pink seam from elbow almost to wrist, already closed, the skin around it shiny and new. A half hour ago it had been open to the bone. Marg looked at it for a long time. "You want a Band-Aid?"

"I'm fine."

"Uh-huh," Marg said again. "Huh." She tapped ash off the end of her cigarette into a coffee can. "That Vale fella's place is down the road you just came up."

Leah kept walking. "Is it."

"Saw you heading down there earlier. Running." Marg's voice was flat and careful, like someone walking out onto lake ice in October. "Anything I ought to know about?"

Leah stopped at the door of cabin four with her hand on the knob. She thought about Josie, asleep with her head on a wolf's shoulder. She thought about Vadas lying on his face in the dirt, and about the mind-healer Harry had mentioned, and the forms, and the cleanup team.

"He's gone," she said. "He's not coming back."

Marg was quiet for a while.

"Never did like him," she said finally. "Tipped too much. Man tips that much, he's paying for something." She pulled the blanket tighter around her. "Go get cleaned up. You're dripping on my steps."

---

The shower took ninety seconds. Leah stood under the hot water and watched the blood run off her arm and swirl pink down the drain, and she scrubbed the last of it out from under her nails, and she did not look at herself in the steamed-up mirror on the way out.

Her clothes situation was bad. The jeans and the hoodie she'd bought at Duncan's were rags in the grass back at the clearing, along with Josie's Nikes. What she had left was what she'd carried out of La Push tied to her ankle: one pair of cut-offs, one black T-shirt. She put them on. She counted her money on the bed. One hundred and seventy-one dollars.

She looked at the coat.

It was lying across the quilt where she'd dropped it, long and dark and heavy, the silver clasp catching the light from the bedside lamp. She picked it up. She meant to fold it. Instead she stood there holding it, and the smell came up off the wool, pine and wet earth and creek-water over cold granite, and wolf, and under the wolf that other thing she couldn't name, the thing that kept making her think about home.

Her wolf rolled over onto its back.

"Oh my God," Leah said. "You're embarrassing."

She put the coat on anyway. It was September in northern British Columbia and she was walking into a bar in cut-offs and bare feet. People were going to look at her no matter what. They might as well look at her in a coat.

On the way out she stopped at the door, and stood there with her hand on the frame, and thought about Seth.

Seth, running north. Seth, two thousand kilometers away, crying in her head. *I'm coming, I'm coming.* The last thing he'd seen through her eyes was a giant talking wolf asking her out over a corpse, and then she'd phased back and cut him off, and he had no idea whether she was alive or dead or worse.

She could phase. Two minutes in the trees behind the cabins and they'd know she was fine.

They'd also know everything else. Every thought she'd had in the last half hour. The coat. The shoulders. The purring.

"No," she said. "Absolutely not."

---

There was a pay phone on the wall outside Duncan's General, under a little shingled overhang, with a light over it that had moths batting around it even this late in the year. Leah dialed collect. The operator asked for her name.

"Tell them it's Leah."

It rang once.

"*Leah?*"

Her mother's voice. Thin and cracked and so close to the phone that it buzzed. Leah's throat closed up so fast she couldn't answer for a second.

"Hi, Mom."

"Oh, thank God. Oh, thank God, thank God." Sue Clearwater was crying. Leah had seen her mother cry exactly twice in her life, once at her dad's funeral and once the night Leah had phased for the first time. "Seth came through the door screaming. He said—he said somebody had you, he said you were tied up, he said there was a man with a—"

"I'm okay. Mom. I'm okay."

"He said you were *bleeding*—"

"I was. I'm not now. You know how it is. It's healed." Leah leaned her forehead against the cold metal of the phone box. "The guy's dead. It's over. I'm fine."

A noise on the other end, something being grabbed, a short scuffle, and then her brother.

"*Leah.*"

"Hey, Seth."

"Are you okay? Are you hurt? What happened? Who was that? Was that a werewolf? Jake says werewolves can't talk but I think he's just saying that because—"

"Seth."

"—he had a COAT, Leah, he had a whole coat, and a stick, and he did a thing with a rock—"

"*Seth.*"

Her brother stopped. She could hear him breathing hard, like he'd been running.

"I'm okay," she said. "I'm really okay. I promise."

"Okay." His voice wobbled. "Okay. I didn't even make Port Angeles. Jake made me turn around. He said I'd never get there in time. He used the voice on me." A pause. "He was right, but I'm still mad at him."

"He was right, Seth."

"I know." Another pause. Then, in a completely different tone, a fifteen-year-old's tone, she could hear the grin through the phone: "So. Leah. Who was that?"

"Nobody."

"He asked you *out*."

"Seth."

"Over a *dead guy*."

"I'm hanging up."

"Are you going? Are you actually going? Leah. Leah, are you—"

"I'm going to get pizza."

There was a silence on the line so total she thought she'd been cut off.

"With *him*?" Seth said.

Behind him, she heard another voice. Deeper. Jacob, saying something she couldn't make out, and then the phone changing hands again.

"Leah." Jacob sounded tired. He sounded like he'd been holding something heavy for a long time and hadn't put it down yet. "You okay?"

"I'm fine, Jake."

"Good." A breath. "Come home."

"No."

"You don't know anything about this guy. You don't know what he is. You saw what he did to that—"

"I saw what he did. He did it to the guy who was going to drain a twenty-two-year-old waitress in his basement. And then me." She heard her own voice go hard. "You weren't here, Jake. You couldn't be here. He was."

That landed. She heard it land. The silence on the line went stiff.

She closed her eyes.

"Sorry," she said, quieter. "That was mean. I'm okay, Jake. I'm going to eat some pizza and find out what he is. Tell Mom I'm okay. Tell Seth to go to bed."

"Leah—"

"I'll call."

She hung up before he could say anything else. Then she stood there under the moths with her hand on the receiver, breathing, until her heart slowed down.

The clock in Duncan's window said she'd blown her own ten minutes about five minutes ago.

She walked to the inn.

### II.

Fifteen minutes earlier, standing in the clearing with Josie Marchand asleep in his arms, Harry watched Leah Clearwater walk up through the spruce until the dark took her, and he kept his ears on her until the sound of her bare feet on the gravel faded out too.

Then he looked down at the dead man in the grass and said, "Right."

His tail was still going. He made it stop.

There was blood on the grass where she'd been tied, a lot of it, dark and drying. There were rags of gray fleece and denim scattered across the clearing, and the shredded remains of a pair of gray running shoes with pink laces. There were paw prints in the soft dirt by the porch steps, deep and wide, the prints of something that weighed a lot more than any wolf had a right to. He crouched beside one and looked at it. He put his forepaw down next to it. Her print was smaller than his, but not by as much as he'd have guessed.

He breathed in. Rain on cedar. Salt water. Fur.

He told his nose to cut it out. It didn't.

The first crack of Apparition came at the eleven-minute mark, which was good for the Confederation and terrible for Harry, because it meant he'd be late. Two more cracks followed it, then a fourth, and the clearing filled up with people.

The cleanup team was two of the Confederation's regulars, Kowalski and Mbeki, in grubby field robes, carrying satchels of evidence jars and a folding stretcher. Kowalski took one look at Harry and said, "Christ, Potter, you could've at least closed his eyes," and went to work on the body. Mbeki started walking a slow circle around the clearing with her wand out, mapping the wards.

The healer was a tall, gray-haired Norwegian woman named Solveig Brandt who looked as if she'd been dragged out of bed, which she had. She took one look at Josie in Harry's arms, put two fingers against the girl's temple, and closed her eyes. She stood like that for almost a minute.

"Six weeks," she said, opening them. "Perhaps seven. He was very patient. Very careful." Her mouth went thin. "She resisted the whole time. I can feel the places where she pushed back. It's a mess in there."

"Can you fix it?"

"I can tidy it." Healer Brandt took Josie from him, gently, and laid her on the stretcher one of the techs had unfolded. "She will keep almost everything. She will lose him, and tonight. She will remember a man who came into the café and was polite, and that she didn't much like him, and nothing else. It will take me three hours, and I'll do it in her own bed. A mind settles better somewhere it knows. Where does she live?"

"No idea." Harry breathed in. "She smells like coffee and bacon and maple syrup. There's one café in town."

"Then I'll find it. She'll be tucked up within the hour with a story about a migraine. She gets them; I can feel the old ones." Brandt looked up at him. "You're bleeding."

Harry looked down. The fur of one footpaw was dark and sticky where he'd crouched in the grass by the place she'd been tied. "Not mine."

"Whose?"

"Hiker."

Brandt's eyebrows went up a fraction. She didn't say anything. She just looked at him for a second longer than was comfortable, and then she put her hand on the stretcher, and she and Josie were gone with a soft crack.

The fourth arrival was the problem.

She was about forty, short and compact, in a dark blue Canadian Ministry robe with the maple-leaf pin of their Department of Magical Law Enforcement on the collar. She had a severe dark bob and a clipboard and the expression of a woman who had driven a long way in the snow to find out that someone had parked in her spot. She walked straight up to Harry and stopped about a foot short of his chest, which put her eye level with the bottom of his ribs. She looked up. A long way up.

"Senior Auror Danielle Thibodeaux," she said. "Ottawa. You'd be Potter."

"Ja."

"Your operational window closed two days ago."

"I'm aware."

"We registered an Unforgivable on Canadian soil at eight forty-one tonight."

"His. Check the residue on his wand." He nodded at the little pile of blue ash in the grass. "Well. What's left of it."

Thibodeaux looked at the ash. Then she looked back up at him, and her expression did not change at all.

"You destroyed the perpetrator's wand."

"Bone wand. Blood-bound. You don't want that thing sitting in an evidence locker, trust me."

"I would have liked the opportunity to decide that for myself." She wrote something on her clipboard. Harry couldn't see what. It was short. "And the perpetrator?"

"Dead."

"I can see that."

"He was casting a blood curse at a hostage. I told him not to. He did it anyway."

"Uh-huh." More writing. "Witnesses?"

"One. The hostage. She was asleep. She'll remember nothing."

"Uh-huh." Thibodeaux turned and looked around the clearing, slowly. At the blood on the grass. At the rags of fleece and denim. At the shredded running shoes with the pink laces. At the paw prints by the porch. She walked over to the prints and crouched down, the same way Harry had, and looked at them for a long moment.

Then she looked back at Harry.

"Those aren't yours," she said.

"Nope."

"They're too small."

"Ja."

"Whose are they?"

"There was a hiker," Harry said. "Woman. Came up the hill when she heard the noise. Vadas got a binding on her before I arrived. I cut her loose and she ran off."

Thibodeaux pointed her pen at the rags in the grass. "In those?"

"She got tangled. Clothes didn't survive."

"So she ran off naked."

"I lent her my coat."

"Uh-huh." Thibodeaux pointed the pen at the prints. "And these?"

Harry looked at the prints. He looked at the sky. He looked back at Thibodeaux.

"Her dog," he said.

Kowalski, kneeling by the body, made a noise that might have been a cough.

"Her dog," Thibodeaux said.

"Big dog."

"These are bigger than my hand, Potter."

"It's a very big dog. Canada. Everything's big."

Thibodeaux looked at him for a long time. Somewhere in the trees an owl started up, a long low hoot, and stopped.

"And where," she said, "is the hiker's very big dog now?"

"Ran off with the hiker."

"And where is the hiker?"

"No idea," Harry said. "She was fast."

Thibodeaux wrote on her clipboard for quite a while this time. When she was done, she turned it around so he could see. In neat, small capitals, under *WITNESSES*, it said: *ONE (1) HIKER, FEMALE, UNIDENTIFIED. DEPARTED SCENE WITHOUT CLOTHING IN ICW OFFICER'S COAT. ACCOMPANIED BY LARGE DOG. SEE ATTACHED: ANOMALY.*

"I'm attaching a form to this," she said. "It's called an Anomaly Report. It goes to my director, and then it goes to your director, and then it goes into a file that somebody in Geneva reads when they're deciding whether to let you back into my country."

"Fair enough," Harry said.

"I'll need your statement by oh-eight-hundred."

"You'll have it."

"Uh-huh." She tucked the clipboard under her arm. "Was the hiker hurt?"

He hadn't been expecting that. His ears went up before he could stop them.

"Bit," he said. "She'll be fine."

Thibodeaux studied the ears for a second. Something shifted in her face, very slightly, and Harry couldn't tell what it was.

"Go on, then," she said. "Whatever you're late for. We've got this."

"I'm not late for—"

"Potter. You've checked your watch four times since I got here, and you don't wear a watch." She jerked her head at the road. "Go."

He went.

He was most of the way to the trees when the mirror buzzed in the pouch on his belt.

"I'm told," said Hennessey, without preamble, "that there was a woman."

"There was a hiker."

"Who left in your coat."

"Ja."

"That's your good coat."

"I'm aware it's my good coat, Hennessey."

"I need you in Geneva at nine for the debrief. Nine this morning, Central European time."

Harry stopped walking. He did the maths. It was past nine at night here. Geneva was nine hours ahead. "That's in under three hours."

"Then I suggest you don't sleep."

"Make it Friday."

"Nine, Potter. The Director wants to hear it from you. You've been fourteen months on this. You closed it. There will be handshakes. Possibly a small reception. Fizzing Whizbees."

"I'll be there at nine," Harry said, "and I'll shake every hand you put in front of me, and then I'm coming back here."

A pause. "Why on earth would you come back there? The job's done."

"Left my coat."

"*Buy a new coat.*"

"It's my good coat," Harry said, and shut the mirror.

He stood at the edge of the clearing, in the dark under the spruce, and closed his eyes, and pulled the glamour up over himself the way you'd pull on a jumper. He'd built it over three years of trial and error and several unfortunate incidents in Muggle supermarkets. Anybody who looked at him would see a big man, black-haired and green-eyed, a bit shaggy, a bit rough around the edges, in a red flannel shirt and jeans and work boots. Anyone who brushed against him would feel flannel. Anyone who heard him would hear a man.

He hesitated, then added a twist to the end of the spell. A small one. A key. One person who'd see straight through it.

Then he turned on the spot.

He was twenty-three minutes past his own deadline and the better part of half an hour past hers, which meant she'd almost certainly eaten his pizza.

### III.

The inn was a big two-story log building down on the waterfront, with a deep porch and a green roof and yellow light pouring out of every window. The bar was through a side door. It had a woodstove going in one corner and a pool table in the other, a moose head over the bar wearing a Santa hat that nobody had bothered to take down since last December, and a neon sign in the window that said *KOKANEE* in blue, buzzing faintly. It smelled like woodsmoke and beer and fryer oil and the Germans in the red jackets, who were at a corner table with a plate of nachos and a map of the Llewellyn Glacier spread out between them.

Gord from Duncan's General was at the bar with a beer. Two hunters in camo, big Alberta-looking guys with sunburned necks, were at a table by the pool table. An old couple in matching fleece vests were doing a crossword. That was it. That was the whole Wednesday night crowd in Atlin.

Everybody looked at her when she walked in. Barefoot. Cut-offs. A man's coat to her ankles.

Leah walked up to the bar and sat on a stool.

The bartender was in his thirties, with a graying ponytail and a Grateful Dead T-shirt and the long-suffering patience of a man who had been a bartender in a town of three hundred people for a long time. He looked at her.

"Help you?"

"Beer," Leah said. "Whatever's on tap. And a large pizza with every kind of meat you have on it. And a large Hawaiian. And two dozen wings."

The bartender looked at her for a while.

"ID," he said.

"I left it at home."

"Where's home?"

"South."

"Then it's a Coke," the bartender said.

"I'm nineteen."

"Maybe. Without ID, you're a Coke."

"Aw, come on, Wes," said Gord from Duncan's, three stools down. "That's the Lumberjack girl. Two plates. Ray told everybody."

"I don't care if she's the Pope," Wes said. "No ID, no beer. I'm not losing my license over a competitive eater." He looked at Leah. "Coke?"

"Fine."

"Pizza for how many?"

"Two."

Wes looked at the empty stool beside her. He looked at her bare feet. He looked at the coat. Whatever conclusion he came to, he kept it to himself, which she appreciated.

"Twenty minutes," he said, and went to put the order in.

Twenty minutes came and went. The pizza came. The wings came. Harry didn't.

Leah ate the meat pizza.

She ate it slowly at first, a slice at a time, telling herself she was just having one while she waited. Then she was on the fourth slice and the door hadn't opened and her stomach, which hadn't been fed since breakfast and had spent the evening healing a broken jaw and a forearm cut to the bone, decided that it did not care about anyone's feelings. She ate the whole thing. She ate eighteen of the wings. She left the Hawaiian sitting there in its box, the pineapple glistening under the bar lights, untouched.

Gord watched her finish the last slice of meat pizza and said, reverently, "Holy jumping," to nobody.

The door opened.

It was Ray, from the Gold Pan. He was in a down vest over his apron, bald head shining, and he looked worried. He went to the bar.

"Wes. Anybody seen Josie?"

Leah went still on her stool.

"No," Wes said. "Why?"

"She took off at six. Said she was going for a walk. Her door's shut and her light's off and she's not answering when I knock, and her car's still out back." Ray rubbed his head. "Probably nothing. Probably she's asleep. But that girl doesn't go to bed before midnight, ever. She's a night owl. And she's always—"

"She said she felt sick," Leah said.

Ray turned around. He looked at her, in her coat, with an empty pizza box in front of her.

"You saw her?"

"Earlier." Leah made her voice as flat and bored as she could get it. "Walking. She said she had a migraine. She was going to go to bed early."

Ray looked at her for a second longer. Then his shoulders came down about an inch.

"She does get those," he said. "Bad ones." He blew out a breath. "Okay. Okay. I'll check on her again in a bit." He went back out the door into the cold.

Leah sat there with her heart going and looked at the clock over the bar. Nine thirty. Harry had said a healer would see to Josie. She hoped to God the healer was fast.

The door opened again.

---

The man who came in had to duck under the frame.

He was huge. Six seven, maybe, and broad as a door through the shoulders, in a red flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow and jeans and scuffed work boots. Black hair that stuck up at the back like he'd slept on it wrong. Green eyes. A rough, square-jawed face with a couple of days of stubble on it, the kind of face you'd see on a guy who worked on a fishing boat.

That was what Leah saw for about half a second.

Then the image wavered, like a reflection on a pond when you drop a stone into it, and she was looking at the wolf.

He was standing in the doorway, ducking under the frame, with his tall black ears flattened sideways to clear it. Charcoal fur. Green eyes. The black sleeveless shirt and the belt with the pouches. Long black tail behind him, swinging. Around the edges of him the air shimmered faintly, like heat haze, and if she looked through the shimmer she could see the ghost of the flannel guy laid over him, like a picture printed on glass.

The whole bar was looking at him, and she could tell by where their eyes were that every one of them was looking at the flannel guy's face. Nobody was looking at the ears.

Harry's nose flared. His head turned toward her. His eyes went to her, and then to the empty pizza box in front of her, and then to the box beside it, the one with the Hawaiian, and his ears went all the way flat.

"No," he said.

"I said ten minutes."

"You *ate* it."

"I said I would."

He crossed the bar in four long strides, silent on the old wooden floor in a way that made the two hunters glance down at his work boots with small frowns, like they'd expected to hear them. He sat down on the stool next to her. The stool creaked. He looked at the Hawaiian. He looked at her.

"All of it," he said.

"And eighteen wings."

"There's six wings left."

"Those are yours."

"That's very generous." His ears were still flat. His nose twitched. "You put pineapple on it."

"I told you I would."

"I thought you were joking."

"I don't joke," Leah said.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached out with one big black-furred paw and pulled the Hawaiian toward him and lifted out a slice, and the cheese stretched in long strings, and a piece of pineapple fell off onto the bar.

"This is a hate crime," he said, and took a bite. He chewed. His ears stayed flat the whole time. He swallowed. "I want that on the record."

"Noted."

He ate the slice in three more bites. Then he took another one.

"I thought you said it was a hate crime."

"I'm *hungry*, Leah. I'll eat the box." He took a third slice and looked at her sideways, chewing. "You can see me."

"You did that on purpose."

"Ja. Keyed the glamour to you." He swallowed. "Everybody else sees a big bloke in flannel. Seemed rude to make you look at him all night when you already know what's underneath."

"How do you know what I see?"

"You're looking at my ears."

She was. She made herself stop.

"What do they see?" she said. "Them."

Harry glanced over his shoulder at the room. The hunters were still watching him. One of them had the specific look of a guy who'd spent his whole life being the biggest man in the room and had just found out he wasn't. "Bloke from a fishing boat, mostly. I've had Viking. Had a lumberjack. Had 'the biggest First Nations guy I've ever seen.'" His ears flicked sideways. "I'm told I need to work on that one."

"You do."

"I'm working on it."

Wes came down the bar with his towel over his shoulder. He looked at Harry the way you'd look at a large dog that had walked into your living room uninvited, wondering whether it was going to knock something over.

"Get you something?" he said, to a point in the air roughly where the flannel guy's face would be.

"Pitcher of whatever she's not allowed to have," Harry said.

Wes looked at him. Wes looked at Leah. Wes looked at the Coke.

"She's not allowed to have it because she doesn't have ID," Wes said.

"Ja. I'm getting it for me."

"Uh-huh. And the second glass?"

"I'm very thirsty."

Wes stared at the flannel guy for a long, long moment. Then he looked at Leah again, at her bare feet, at the coat, at the empty pizza box.

"If the Mounties walk in," Wes said, "that's your beer. You hear me? Yours."

"Wouldn't have it any other way."

The pitcher came. Kokanee, pale and cold, with a foam head that went over the lip. Harry poured two glasses and slid one across in front of Leah, without looking at it, and drank half of his in one go. Leah watched Wes watching them from the other end of the bar and picked up the glass and drank, and it was cold and thin and tasted like every summer at First Beach, and she put it down.

"So," Harry said. "Hello."

"Hi."

"You never told me your name."

She hadn't. She'd thought about it on the way up the hill, and in the shower, and at the pay phone, and she'd decided not to. Names were how people found you.

"Leah," she said.

"Leah." He said it slow, like he was trying it out, the vowel landing somewhere odd in that flat South African accent. It came out *Lee-ah*, with the second syllable clipped short. "Leah what?"

"Just Leah for now."

"Fair." His tail swept the floor behind his stool. "Harry Potter. Already told you that."

"Is that supposed to mean something?"

He looked at her. His ears went up, and stayed up, and something went across his muzzle that she was starting to recognize as a grin.

"Not a thing," he said. "Not a single thing. That's lekker."

"What's lekker?"

"Nice. Good. Great." He finished his beer and poured another. "Means you've never heard of me."

"Should I have?"

"In some places." He shrugged, a lot of shoulder under black fur. "Not here. Here I'm a bloke in flannel who just walked in and got the worst pizza in Canada."

"Second-worst. I didn't let them put anchovies on it."

"You're a merciful woman."

"I'm really not."

"No," he agreed, "you're not," and drank.

---

Ray came back at ten fifteen. He came in the side door with his down vest unzipped and stood just inside, looking for her, and when he found her, he crossed the room fast.

"You were right," he said. "Josie. She's in bed. Out cold. I knocked and knocked and she didn't answer, so I got the spare key, and she's—" He laughed, short, relieved. "She's snoring. Shoes off and everything. Had a cold cloth on her head."

Leah let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

"Good," she said.

"Migraine, you said?"

"That's what she said."

"Huh." Ray scratched his head. "She looked okay. Peaceful." He glanced over at Harry, really looking at him for the first time, at the flannel guy taking up two-thirds of the bar, and his eyes went wide just slightly. Leah watched him do the math: big guy, stranger, sitting next to the girl from out of town with no shoes. Ray's mouth thinned. He looked back at Leah.

"You good?" he said, quieter.

Leah looked at him. Bald, worried, fifty-six, in an apron that smelled like bacon grease. A guy she'd known for three days who'd made her two Lumberjacks and kept a room over his café for a twenty-two-year-old girl from Whitehorse so she could save for nursing school. He was looking at Harry like he was trying to figure out if he could take him.

He couldn't. Not even close. He was going to try anyway, if she said the word. She could see it.

"I'm good," Leah said.

Ray studied her a second longer. Then he nodded, once, and looked at Harry one more time, a long, flat look, the kind dads gave guys who came to pick up their daughters. Harry's ears went sideways.

"Night, then," Ray said, and left.

The door swung shut behind him.

"He likes you," Harry said.

"He's known me three days."

"He'd have had a go at me. If you'd said." Harry's tail swept once. "Bloke's about five-eight. He'd have lost."

"He knows."

"That's what I mean." Harry looked at the door Ray had gone out of. "That's a good one, that."

Leah didn't say anything. She drank her beer.

"So," Harry said. "Healer did her job."

"How'd you know?"

"Smelled her on him. Lavender and something minty. That's Solveig Brandt's hand cream. She's the best mind-healer in the Confederation, and right now she's sitting in a chair in the corner of Josie's room under a Disillusionment Charm, tidying." His ears flicked. "Your mate Ray walked right past her. The cold cloth's hers." He refilled Leah's glass. "Josie'll wake up tomorrow with a headache and no idea anything happened. Thinks a bloke called Vale was a bit odd, and that's all."

"And the body?"

"Gone by now. Cleanup team. House'll look like nobody's lived in it for a year by morning." His ears flicked. "And there's a very angry woman from Ottawa filing a report about a naked hiker with an enormous dog."

Leah choked on her beer.

"A *what*?"

"Your paw prints," Harry said. "In the dirt. Had to tell her something."

"You told her I was a *dog*?"

"I told her you *had* a dog."

"That's worse."

"It's really not. You don't want to be on her form. She's got very neat handwriting." He drank. "You're welcome, by the way."

Leah stared at him.

"You," she said, "are a *lot*."

"Ja," he said, cheerfully, and ate another slice of Hawaiian. "Hear that a lot."

---

### IV.

"Okay," Leah said, when the pizza was gone, and the wings were gone, and they were most of the way down a second pitcher. "Deal's a deal. You first."

"Ladies first."

"I'm not a lady."

"You're a wolf with a temper and a dead man's blood on your coat sleeve." His muzzle twitched. "I'd still say ladies first."

"That's your coat."

"And it's very fetching on you." He reached into one of the pouches on his belt and came out with a coin. A big one, thick and gold and heavy-looking, bigger than a Canadian toonie, stamped with something she couldn't make out. "Call it. You win, I go first."

"Is that real gold?"

"Ja."

"You carry around gold coins?"

"Wizard money. It's called a Galleon. Long story." He held it up between two claws.

"Heads."

He flicked it. It went up spinning, flashing in the bar lights, came down on the bar between them, and rolled in a little circle and fell flat.

Tails. Some kind of little castle.

"Unlucky," said Harry.

"You're cheating," Leah said.

"How am I cheating? It's a coin."

"You did something. Your ear twitched."

"My ear twitches on its own. It's an ear." He put the coin away. "Go on. Some of it, you said. I'll take what I can get."

Leah turned her glass around on the bar, slowly, leaving a wet ring.

She'd never told anybody outside the tribe. The secret was the oldest thing she knew, older than her dad, older than anybody. It came down through the council fires and the old men's voices in the dark, and you didn't tell, and you especially didn't tell a stranger in a bar in Canada who'd lied to the Canadian government about you having a dog.

On the other hand, he was a seven-foot wolf. Who exactly was he going to tell?

"There's a few of us," she said. "Back home. My people. It runs in families. It's in the blood, but it doesn't always come out. It's like a switch. Most people go their whole lives and it never gets flipped. Then something comes along that flips it, and the kids start changing."

"What flips it?"

"Some of it, Potter."

His ears went sideways. "Fair."

"You get hot first. Like a fever that doesn't break. A hundred and eight. Your temper gets short. You grow, like, a foot in a month. And then one day you get angry, and—" She made a small gesture with one hand, a burst. "Wolf."

"Does it hurt?"

Nobody had ever asked her that. She looked at him.

"Every time," she said.

He nodded slowly. "Mine too."

She didn't know what to do with that, so she kept going. "When you're a wolf, you're in a pack. All of you. And you can hear each other. In your head. Everything. Every thought anybody has, the second they have it. You can't turn it off. You can't hide anything."

His ears pinned all the way back. "Everything?"

"Everything."

"For how long?"

"For as long as you're a wolf."

He was quiet for a second. "That's why you phased back. In the clearing. That's why you didn't want to talk to me with the mouth that does—" He stopped. "Your mates were in there."

"My brother," Leah said. "And my Alpha."

"Watching."

"The whole time."

"Watching me ask you out." His tail went still. "Over a dead bloke."

"Yep."

"Eish," Harry said, and drained his glass. "What did they think?"

"My brother thinks you're the coolest thing he's ever seen. My Alpha thinks you're a psycho."

"Fair on both counts." He refilled. "How many of you?"

Leah thought about it. Sam's pack. Jacob's pack. The new kids, the little ones, the ones who were changing faster now than they'd ever changed before, because of the Cullens, because of what had been sitting in that big glass house for years, flipping switches all over the reservation. "Ten," she said. "Counting me. There are more coming, I think. Kids. Thirteen, fourteen. It's happening younger."

"Ten wolves." He was nodding, putting it together. "Ten of you. And how many girls?"

Leah didn't answer right away.

She'd known this question was coming. She'd known it since he asked *how many of you*, and she'd braced for it the way you brace for a punch, and it hit anyway. It always hit. It hit every time she thought about it.

"One," she said.

Harry stopped with the glass halfway to his mouth.

"One," he said.

"Me."

He set the glass down on the bar. Slowly. Like it was full of something that might go off.

"Just you," he said.

"Just me."

"Nine blokes. And you."

"Yep."

"How long's this been going on? Your lot. The wolves."

"Forever. Hundreds of years. Longer. Since the stories started." Leah turned her glass. "There's never been a girl. Not in any of the stories. Not once. Nobody knows why it happened to me. The elders don't know. They had a meeting about it." She heard her own voice go flat and hard. "They had a meeting about me. Like I was a weather event."

Harry was staring at her.

"You're the only one," he said, very quietly. "The *only* one. In hundreds of years. Of all of them."

"Yep."

"So." He was choosing the words carefully, she could tell; his ears had gone all the way up and stiff, swiveling toward her. "So which one of them's yours?"

"What?"

"Which one of them is yours? Nine blokes, one of you. Somebody's had a go. Which one?"

"None of them."

Harry blinked.

"None of them," he repeated.

"None of them."

"None of them is yours."

"That's what I said."

"No. Hang on. Hang on." He held up one paw, claws out, like he was trying to stop traffic. "Different question. Who's *tried*? Who's had a go? Which one of them asked you out?"

Leah looked at him.

"Nobody," she said.

Harry stared at her.

Leah stared back.

In the corner, one of the Germans laughed at something on the map. The woodstove ticked. The neon *KOKANEE* sign buzzed.

"Nobody," Harry said.

"Nobody."

"Not *one*."

"Not one."

"In how long? How long have you been a wolf?"

"Long enough."

"And not one of them has *ever*—"

"Nope."

"Not even a—not one bloody—" Harry put both forepaws flat on the bar and looked down at them, like he needed something solid to hold onto. His ears had gone in two different directions. "Leah. Sorry. I'm having a moment. I need you to bear with me. Is there something wrong with them?"

"Lots of things."

"Can they see?"

"They can see."

"Can they *smell*?"

"Rule four."

"That was about their noses. Loophole." He swung around on his stool to face her full on, and the stool groaned. His tail was lashing behind him now, short angry strokes across the floor, and the old woman doing the crossword glanced over at the sound and frowned and looked back at her crossword with a puzzled expression, like she'd heard a broom somewhere. "Nine of them. Nine wolves. And the only woman who's ever been one of them in the history of your entire people is standing in the middle of them, looking like *that*, with a temper like *that*, and she can run—how fast can you run?"

"Fastest in the pack."

"*Fastest in the pack*," Harry said, to the moose head over the bar, "and not one of them has had the brains to ask her for so much as a cup of coffee."

"There's a reason," Leah said.

"There'd better be. It'd better be a bloody good one."

She looked down at her glass. At the wet rings on the bar. At anything except him.

"One of them did," she said. "Before."

His ears came forward.

"Before I changed. Before any of it. His name's Sam. We were going to get married." She heard her own voice go strange, and she pushed through it. "He got sick. Disappeared for two weeks. Came back different. He couldn't tell me what was going on—he wasn't allowed. And then my cousin Emily came to visit, and he looked at her."

"Looked at her."

"There's a thing. With us. It's called imprinting." She said the word like it tasted bad, because it did. "You look at someone, and that's it. You're done. Gravity shifts. They're the center of the world. Whatever they need, you are. Nobody gets a vote. It just happens. It's supposed to be the most sacred thing we have."

"And he looked at your cousin."

"And he looked at my cousin," Leah said. "And then I changed. And I was in his head. And I had to hear every single thing he thought about her. Every day. For months. All of it. Every time he looked at her, every time he touched her, I was right there. Because that's how it works."

Harry didn't say anything for a long time.

When she finally looked up, his ears were pinned flat to his skull, and his lip was curled back, just a little, off one long white canine. A sound was coming out of him. Low. So low she felt it in her stool more than heard it. The two hunters at the table by the pool table had both turned around and were looking at the flannel guy with matching expressions of concern, like they'd heard a truck idling somewhere close and couldn't find it.

"Harry," Leah said.

The growl stopped.

"Sorry," he said. His voice came out rough. "Sorry. That's—sorry." He picked up his glass and drank all of it and put it down very carefully. "Your cousin."

"Yep."

"And you had to *listen*."

"Yep."

"Right." He breathed out through his nose, long and slow. His ears came up, partway. "Right. Okay. So that's one. One absolute muppet. What about the other eight?"

"They're in my head," Leah said. "All the time. They know every bitchy thing I think. They know I'm bitter. They know I hate Emily sometimes, and that I love her, and that I hate that I love her. They know I'm a bitch about everything. They'd rather go on patrol with a porcupine."

"You *are* bitter," Harry said.

"Yeah."

"You're allowed. Bloke leaves you for your cousin and then makes you live in his head while he does it? You're allowed to be bitter till you're ninety." His tail lashed. "That's still eight more wolves. And not one of them looked at the only girl in the pack and thought, *ja, I'll take my chances*?"

"No."

"Some of them must have imprinted on somebody else."

"Four of them," Leah said. "So far. Sam on Emily. Jared on a girl from school. Paul on my Alpha's sister." She hesitated. "Quil imprinted on a two-year-old."

Harry went very still.

"I'm sorry," he said. "On a *what*?"

"A two-year-old. Emily's niece."

"Leah."

"I know how it sounds."

"I'm going to need you to explain it very slowly, so I don't have to go down there and—"

"Let me finish." She stopped, because she'd been about to defend it, and she'd promised herself she'd stop defending it. She tried again. "When it's a kid, it's like he's her big brother. Babysitter. Best friend. Whatever she needs. He'd die for her. That's all it is. That's all it'll be until she's grown up."

"And then?"

"And then," Leah said.

Harry stared at her.

"That's worse," he said.

"Yeah."

"That's so much worse."

"Yeah. And my Alpha imprinted on a baby," Leah said, "two weeks ago. That's part of why I'm here."

Harry put his muzzle down on his folded forepaws on the bar.

He stayed like that for a while. Wes looked over from the other end of the bar, at the big flannel guy with his head down on his arms, and then at Leah, and raised his eyebrows. Leah shrugged.

"Okay," Harry said, into his paws. His voice was muffled. "Okay. So. Let me get this right. Nine of them. One of you. One of them dumped you for your cousin and made you watch. Three more have their noses so far up their own destiny they couldn't find their way out with a map, and one of those is in love with a toddler, and your *Alpha* is in love with a *baby*."

"Don't call it that—"

"That's five." He lifted his head and counted them off on his claws. "What about the other four?"

"One's my little brother."

"He's excused."

"Two of them are thirteen."

"Also excused. Obviously. And the last one?"

"Embry."

"Embry," Harry repeated. "So Embry's had you running patrols next to him, in the rain, for however long it's been. Fastest wolf in the pack. Only woman who's ever been one of them. Fierce as all hell. Gorgeous. Can eat a whole pizza in the time it takes a man to file one report." His tail had stopped lashing. It was just lying there on the floor behind him now, like he'd forgotten he had it. "And Embry has never once so much as tried."

"No."

"I've never met Embry," Harry said, "and I'm ashamed of him."

"Nobody's tried, Harry. Not one of them. Not ever."

"Fake wolves," Harry said.

Leah blinked. "What?"

"Fake wolves. Knock-offs. Bootlegs. Off-brand. Made in some factory out the back of a garage." His voice was dead level, and his ears were pointed straight at her. "A real wolf would know a catch like you from a hundred miles out, Leah. I did. I knew from thirty kilometres. Through a blood curse and a whole lake and a pie I wouldn't wish on my worst enemy. I was sitting on a rock, and the wind came round, and I knew. I didn't even know *what* I knew. I just knew I'd better get over there."

The bar had gone very quiet, or maybe that was just in her head.

"Rule four," Leah said, finally. It came out rough.

"That one was about my nose," Harry said. "Loophole."

"You can't keep doing that."

"I'll stop when I run out of loopholes."

Her wolf was doing something she didn't have a name for. It had sat up, under her ribs. Its ears were up. Its tail, if it had one in there, was going. It was preening, there was no other word for it. It had heard a big wolf with very large teeth call her a catch and say the rest of her pack were knock-offs, and it was preening like a cat in a sunbeam, and she wanted to strangle it.

"You've known me two hours," she said.

"Ja."

"That's not enough time to know anything."

"It's enough time to know they're idiots," Harry said. "The rest I'll get to."

Leah looked away from him, at the Germans and their map, at the moose head in its Santa hat. Her face was hot. Her face was never hot. Her face ran at a hundred and eight degrees all the time, and it was *hot*, and she was furious about it.

"I'm telling my Alpha you called him a knock-off," she said.

"Tell him I'll say it to his face."

"He'll hit you."

"He can try. I'd let him have one." His ears flicked. "After that it'd be a conversation."

"You'd lose."

"I've been told that before," Harry said. "Usually right before I don't."

She turned back to look at him, ready to say something cutting, and found him grinning at her with his whole muzzle full of long white teeth, ears up, completely unrepentant, and the something cutting dried up in her mouth.

She drank her beer instead.

---

### V.

"Pool," Harry said, when the second pitcher was empty. "You promised you'd humiliate me."

"I said I was great at pool."

"Same thing."

The two Alberta hunters had finished their game and gone back to their table. Leah racked the balls. She did it fast, tight, the way her dad had taught her on the beat-up table in the back of the community center at La Push on rainy Saturdays, the triangle snapped down over the felt and pushed forward and lifted off clean. She chalked her cue. She looked at Harry.

He was holding his cue like a man who'd been handed a snake and told it was a stick.

"You've played before," she said.

"Twice."

"How'd it go?"

"Badly. I broke a window in a pub in Stellenbosch. Bloke I was playing was very understanding about it." He peered down the length of the cue. "Well, eventually."

"Break," Leah said.

"You don't want to break?"

"I want to watch you break."

He set up. He bent over the table, which took a while, because there was a lot of him to bend, and his tail stuck straight out behind him for balance, and Leah had to step out of the way so it didn't take out her knees. He lined up the cue. His tongue poked out of the side of his muzzle. He drew back.

He hit the cue ball very hard and very slightly off-center. It shot down the table, clipped the rack on the far left edge, sent exactly one ball rolling a lazy three inches, and then ricocheted off the cushion and came back down the table and stopped directly in front of him, like it had never left.

"Hm," Harry said.

The Alberta hunters were watching. One of them snorted into his beer.

Leah broke. Three balls went down, two solids and a stripe. She took solids. She ran four more before she missed, on purpose, on a bank shot she could have made with her eyes shut, because she wanted to see what he'd do.

What he did was line up an easy shot on the eleven, straight into the corner pocket, eighteen inches, and miss it by a foot.

"How," Leah said.

"I've got two left footpaws when it comes to this."

"You use your hands to play pool."

"Paws," Harry said.

"Whatever."

"See, that's the problem. They're not built for it. The pads catch on the cue." He held up his forepaw and waggled it, and the light caught on the thick dark pads and the curved claws, and the old woman doing the crossword glanced up at him and then back down, frowning, like she'd seen a man waving his hand around and hadn't understood why it made her uneasy. "Also I'm rubbish. Mostly I'm rubbish."

Leah ran the rest of the solids. She missed on the eight on purpose again, and stepped back, and leaned on her cue.

"Your shot."

Harry bent over the table. He lined up on the thirteen, which was sitting near the side pocket at a horrible angle. He drew back the cue. He hit the ball.

The thirteen rolled toward the side pocket, very slowly, at the wrong angle, clearly heading for the cushion two inches to the left of the pocket. Then, about six inches out, it curved.

It curved. Like a car turning onto a side road. It made a polite little turn to the right and rolled neatly into the pocket and dropped.

Leah looked at the pocket. Then she looked at Harry.

His ears were very straight. His muzzle was very still. He was looking at the pocket with an expression of honest scientific interest, like a man who'd just witnessed a rare natural phenomenon.

"Did that ball," Leah said, "just turn a corner?"

"Physics," Harry said.

"You're cheating."

"I'd never."

"Your muzzle twitched."

"It does that. It's a muzzle."

"Harry."

He held out for about two more seconds. Then his ears drooped.

"I gave it a nudge," he said. "A little one. Tiny. Hardly anything. I've lost every game of pool I've ever played, and a bit of me wanted to see what the other thing felt like."

"If you cheat again, I'm leaving."

"No more cheating."

"I mean it."

"I can see you mean it." His tail swept the floor. "No more cheating. Hand on heart." He paused. "Paw on heart."

"You're unbelievable."

"Hear that a lot."

He played straight after that, and he was exactly as bad as he'd said. Worse. He scratched twice. He sank one of her balls for her. He missed a shot so badly that the cue ball rolled across the table without touching anything at all, and the Alberta hunter who'd snorted put his beer down and said, to his buddy, not quite quietly enough, "Big fella can't hit water if he fell out of a boat."

Harry's ears went sideways.

He lined up one more time. It was a long shot. The fourteen, down the whole length of the table into the corner. He took a deep breath. He drew back, and back, and back.

"Don't," Leah said.

He hit it.

He hit it so hard that there was a sound like a gunshot. The cue ball came off the tip of the cue and did not go down the table. It went up. It hopped the cushion at the near end, cleared the rail by a full foot, hit the wooden floor with a crack that made everybody in the bar jump, bounced twice, and went rolling away across the room, rattling over the floorboards, under the Albertans' table, between the boots of the one who'd made the crack about the boat, where it fetched up against the baseboard with a sad little *tock*.

There was a long silence in the bar.

"Nice shot, Paul Bunyan," said the hunter.

Leah turned her head to look at the cue ball.

Harry's tail came up.

She saw it coming at the edge of her eye and it was too slow to dodge, deliberately slow, a long, smooth, sweeping arc, the whole length of it coming around from behind him and up and across in front of her face, unhurried, like a curtain being drawn across a stage. It stopped there. Right in front of her eyes. Hanging in the air between her and the cue ball and the hunters and the whole humiliating mess, the black fur thick and soft and close enough that she could see the charcoal undercoat at the roots, the tip curled up and over at the end in a lazy little hook.

All she could see was tail.

"Nope," Harry said, from somewhere behind it, very pleasantly. "Nothing to see over there. Don't look at that. Look here."

Leah stood there, staring at the tail.

She moved her head to the left, to look around it.

The tail moved to the left with her. Smooth as anything. Following.

She moved her head to the right.

The tail moved right.

She leaned back, to look under it. The tail dipped, very slightly, to stay level with her eyes.

From behind it, she could hear him making a sound under his breath, low and contented and very, very pleased with itself, the kind of sound a big dog makes when it's lying in front of a fire on a rainy day and somebody's rubbing its belly. She ducked under the tail and around it, and there he was, leaning on his pool cue like it was a walking stick, with his eyes half-closed and his ears all the way up and forward, and the corner of his muzzle drawn back to show a lot of teeth, and the end of his tail, still hanging in midair where her face had just been, giving a single satisfied little flick.

He looked like a man who had been waiting his entire life to do that to somebody.

"Really?" Leah said.

"Really."

"You think that hides anything?"

"I think it hid a fair bit."

"The ball is under that guy's *chair*."

"What ball?" Harry said. The tail dropped down and curled lazily around his legs, like it had never moved. "I don't see a ball."

Leah looked at him. She looked at the tail. She looked at him.

Something happened in her chest. It came up from under her ribs, up through her throat, and she tried to swallow it, and she couldn't, and it came out.

She laughed.

A real one. Loud. The kind that bent her over the pool table with one hand flat on the felt. The kind she hadn't done since before Sam, since before any of it, since the summer she was sixteen and her dad had fallen off the end of the dock at First Beach trying to show Seth how to cast a line. The Germans looked over. The old couple looked over. Wes looked over from behind the bar, with a towel in one hand, and his eyebrows went up.

Leah couldn't stop. She laughed until her eyes stung.

When she finally straightened up, wiping her face with the back of her wrist, Harry was watching her, and the grin was gone off his muzzle. He was just looking at her. His ears were all the way forward. His tail had stopped moving.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," Harry said. "Nothing at all." He picked up the chalk and chalked his cue, for no reason, since the game was obviously over. "I'll go get my ball before that bloke decides to keep it."

He went to get his ball. The hunter who'd made the crack watched him come, all six foot seven of flannel guy, and pulled his boots in under his chair real fast. Harry crouched down, retrieved the cue ball from against the baseboard, straightened up, and said, very politely, "Cheers, boet. Sorry about that. Lovely boots," and walked back.

Leah watched him come back across the bar and thought: *I am in so much trouble.*

Her wolf, under her ribs, rolled over onto its back with all four paws in the air.

She sank the eight ball without looking.

---

They took the last of the beer back to the booth by the window, where the woodstove was close enough to feel. Outside the glass, the lake was black and still, and the moon was all the way up over the mountain on the far side, laying a long silver road across the water. The *Tarahne* sat on the shore below the inn in the moonlight, white and enormous and stranded.

"Okay," Leah said. "Your turn."

"I won the coin toss."

"You lost at pool. You owe me."

He considered that. "Fair." He turned his glass around on the table. "Where do you want me to start?"

"What are you?"

"Wizard."

"That's it? Wizard?"

"Born one. Most of us are. It runs in families, mostly. Like yours." He shrugged. "There's a whole world of us. Hidden. Schools, shops, banks. Government. There's a law about keeping it secret from everybody who isn't. I've broken it about six ways tonight."

"Why?"

"You saw me kill a man with a wand. I was a bit past the point of pretending it was a magic trick." His tail flicked. "And you're a wolf. You've got your own secret. Seemed fair to trade."

"Where'd you grow up?"

"England. Little town called Little Whinging. Surrey. Lots of hedges."

"With your parents?"

"No." He said it plainly. "They died when I was one. Dark wizard. Bit like Vadas, only a lot worse. He killed them both and tried to kill me. Didn't work."

"Why not?"

"Long story. My mum, mostly." He drank. "After that, I went to my aunt and uncle. They weren't magic. They didn't like magic. They didn't like me."

"How much didn't they like you?"

"I slept in a cupboard under the stairs till I was eleven."

Leah set her glass down.

"You're kidding."

"Nope."

"A *cupboard*."

"It had spiders. I had names for them." He shrugged again. "Then a letter came for me from the wizarding school when I was eleven, and a half-giant broke down the door of the shack we were hiding in and gave me a birthday cake, and I went off to school, and that was that."

"A half-giant."

"Hagrid. He's lovely. Raises dragons."

"Dragons are real."

"Ja. Don't ever let one sit on you." He refilled her glass with the last of the pitcher. "School was good. Mostly. There was a war on, most of the time, and I was in it. It was the same bloke who killed my parents. He came back."

"How old were you?"

"Fourteen, when he came back. Seventeen, when it finished."

"Who won?"

"We did. Mostly." He looked out the window at the lake. "Lot of people didn't come home. Friends. Teachers. A bloke I'd known since I was eleven. My old teacher, who'd been my dad's best mate." His ears went flat, just slightly, and came back up. "We won, though."

Leah didn't say anything. She thought about the clearing last June. The newborns screaming. The weight of the one that had gotten on top of her before Jacob pulled it off. She thought about her father's heart stopping on the kitchen floor the day she'd phased for the first time in front of him, and how the elders had said it wasn't her fault, and how nobody had believed them, least of all her.

"Seventeen," she said.

"How old were you?" Harry said. "First time you fought?"

She looked at him.

"Nineteen," she said.

He nodded slowly, like that answered something.

"After the war," he said, "everybody in England knew my face. Couldn't buy milk without somebody crying on me. Got sick of it. Got very sick of it. So I went to Cape Town to be nobody. Stayed eight years. Learned to surf. Badly. Got the accent." His tail swept. "Joined the Confederation about four years ago. They needed somebody to go after the ones who'd run. Turned out I was good at that."

"Good at killing people."

"Good at finding them. Killing's the part I try to skip." He looked down at his glass. "Sometimes I don't get to."

"And the wolf thing?"

His ears went sideways. His tail stopped.

For a second she thought he wasn't going to answer.

"Three years ago," he said. "Romania. Job went sideways. There was a werewolf in the dark. A real one. Our kind. The kind that turns every full moon and loses its mind and eats people. He bit me before I could stop him." He touched his left shoulder, just under the collar of the sleeveless shirt, where she could see now, if she looked, that the fur grew a little differently, in a long crooked line, like a part. "The healers stood around my bed at the next full moon with their wands out. Waiting for me to go for their throats."

"Did you?"

"No. I changed." He turned his forepaw over on the table and looked at it. "Into this. And I stayed me. I've stayed me ever since. I can be a man, or I can be a big wolf on four legs, or I can be this. Whenever I like. No moon. No madness."

"Why?"

"Nobody knows. Healers reckon there's something in my blood. Something old in the family. Some ancestor went somewhere he shouldn't have, a long time ago, and brought something home with him that's been asleep in us since. The bite woke it up." His ears flicked. "That's all I've got. That's all anybody's got."

Leah looked at him.

"So you're the only one," she said slowly. "Of you."

"Only one I've ever heard of. Three years." He looked up at her. "Till tonight."

The woodstove ticked.

She didn't know what to say to that. She drank her beer and looked at the lake and didn't say anything.

"Your family," she said, after a while. "The one with the something in the blood. What's their name?"

"Potter, on my dad's side. But the healers think it came in through the Blacks. Old family. Mostly horrible." He snorted. "My godfather was a Black. Sirius. Best man I ever knew. Only good one in four hundred years, by his own count."

Leah went very still.

"Black?" she said.

"Ja." He glanced at her. "Why?"

She thought about Billy Black in his wheelchair on the porch of the little red house in La Push, with a blanket over his knees and a fishing hat on, grinning at something on the radio. She thought about Jacob. She thought about Ephraim Black, Jacob's great-grandfather, the last Alpha before Sam, who'd made the treaty with the Cullens seventy years ago. She thought about the woodstove in Billy's kitchen, and the smell of it, cedar and salt water, which was the smell under the wolf in Harry's coat, the one that had been making her think about home all night.

She thought: *That's crazy. That's a crazy thing to think.*

"No reason," she said. "I know some Blacks."

"Everybody knows some Blacks," Harry said. "Common name. Mine were mostly the kind you'd cross the street to avoid. Sirius used to say the family motto was 'Toujours Pur,' and it should've been 'We're Better Than You, Pass The Salt.'" His tail flicked. "He died when I was fifteen. Fell through a veil. Long story."

"You've got a lot of long stories."

"I'm twenty-six. Most of them happened before I was eighteen." His ears went sideways. "I'm hoping the next bit's shorter."

Leah looked at him across the table. At the big black-furred paw wrapped around the beer glass. At the long muzzle and the green eyes and the crooked line in the fur on his shoulder. At the ears, which were pointed at her, and the tail, which had started moving again, slowly, across the floor.

A cupboard under the stairs. A war at seventeen. A werewolf's teeth in his shoulder. A godfather who fell through something. And then three years of being the only one of whatever he was, eating bad pie on rocks in other people's countries, and getting yelled at by a guy in a mirror.

She thought about her own two years. Sam's head. Emily's scars. Her dad on the kitchen floor. The council meeting about her like she was a weather event. The boys, all of them, in her head, every day, hearing every thought, and not one of them ever once thinking she might be worth asking out for coffee.

She'd thought nobody else in the world knew what that was like. Being the only one.

She'd been wrong about that, apparently.

She hated being wrong.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay what?"

"Okay. That was a good trade."

His ears went straight up.

Wes rang the bell over the bar.

"Last call," he said. "And for the record, if anybody else breaks anything, they're paying for it."

He was looking at the flannel guy when he said it.

---

### VI.

The night was very cold and very clear, and the stars over the lake were so thick it looked like someone had spilled sugar across a black tablecloth.

Leah walked barefoot down the gravel path along the waterfront with her hands in the pockets of his coat. Harry walked beside her. The glamour had gone the moment they stepped off the inn's porch into the dark, and he hadn't bothered to put it back. There was nobody out here to see. His footpaws came down silent on the gravel, heel to toe, and she'd noticed that he'd shortened his stride to match hers without being asked.

They walked past the *Tarahne*, up on its cradle on the shore, enormous and white in the moonlight.

"That's a sad boat," Harry said.

"It's retired."

"It's a boat that can't go in the water."

"It's resting." She looked up at the pilothouse. "It did its job for twenty years. It carried people out to the glaciers and back. Then they pulled it out of the water and set it down here, and now it gets to sit and look at the lake. Nobody needs anything from it anymore."

Harry looked at her for a long moment.

"Ja," he said finally. "Fair."

They walked on. Behind them, the inn's lights went out one by one. The town was dark except for a porch light here and there, and the light over the pay phone at Duncan's, and one window above the Gold Pan, upstairs, where a lamp was on, low.

Josie's window.

Leah stopped walking and looked at it.

"She's okay," Harry said. "Brandt's thorough. She'll sleep it off."

"I know."

"You saved her."

"I didn't do anything. I got tied up."

"You got there first," Harry said. "Through his wards. On your own. You found her by smell from a kilometre down the road and you went in after her when you knew he was waiting." His ears were straight forward. "If you hadn't, he'd have had her down in that cellar a good half hour before he made enough noise for me to hear. I'd have walked in on a corpse."

Leah didn't say anything.

"Just saying," Harry said. "For the record. In case nobody else does."

They walked on.

The gravel path turned up from the waterfront toward the south end of town, toward Marg's, and the little row of log cabins with their dark windows.

"So," Harry said.

"So."

"I've got to be in Geneva in about twenty minutes."

"Where's Geneva?"

"Switzerland."

Leah stopped.

"*Switzerland*? *Tonight*?"

"It's tomorrow morning there. Nine o'clock. My boss wants to shake my paw and give me a certificate and possibly a small cake. Then there's going to be a lot of paperwork, and then the woman from Ottawa's going to file her report about your dog, and somebody's going to want to talk to me about that." His tail swept the gravel. "I'll be back Saturday."

"Why?"

"You've got my coat."

Leah pulled the coat tighter around her.

"You could buy a new coat."

"That's my good coat," Harry said. "Also, I was going to ask you something."

"Ask."

"Second date."

"I didn't say this was a first date."

"You let me buy you two pitchers and a pizza and you beat me at pool and laughed at me, which in my book is a first date. Possibly a very good one." His ears flicked. "Second one. Saturday. I want to take you somewhere."

"Where?"

"There's a bar," Harry said, "in Cape Town."

Leah stared at him.

"Cape Town," she said. "In *South Africa*."

"That's the one."

"That's on the other side of the planet."

"Bit more than halfway, ja."

"You want to take me to the other side of the planet for a *drink*?"

"A very specific drink. Hear me out." He held up one paw, claws out. "It's a wizard bar. Down a little alley off Long Street that only opens if you knock on the right brick. It's called Die Ou Draak. The Old Dragon. Run by a goblin called Grimbald and his wife, who's a witch from Durban and frankly terrifying. And they brew a stout there. Drakensberg Red. Black as tar, about nine percent, and there's something in it they won't tell anybody about. Some kind of dragon pepper. Probably illegal in three countries."

"Okay."

"And when you drink it," Harry said, "you burp fire."

Leah stopped walking again.

"You what?"

"Burp. Fire." He mimed it, tipping his muzzle up and opening his jaws. "Proper fire. Not a lot. First pint, you get a bit of smoke. Second pint, little flame, about yay long." He held his claws apart, maybe six inches. "Third pint, they cut you off, on account of a bloke from Pretoria who set his own eyebrows alight in 2002 and sued. The whole ceiling's warded. It's got scorch marks on it going back eighty years. People sign their names on the ceiling in soot."

"You're making this up."

"I've got my name on that ceiling twice."

"You are *making this up*."

"Swear on my good coat." He put his paw on his chest. "Saturday. You'll burp fire, I'll lose at darts, you'll laugh at me again, which, I'll be honest, is mostly why I'm asking."

Leah looked at him in the moonlight. Seven feet of wolf in a sleeveless shirt with that crooked part in the fur on his shoulder and his ears pointed at her and his tail moving slowly side to side across the gravel. He was so stupid. He was so completely stupid.

Her wolf was sitting up under her ribs like a dog that had heard the word *walk*.

"I don't have a passport," she said.

"Don't need one. We don't do passports. We do Portkeys."

"What's a Portkey?"

"Usually an old boot. Or a tin can. Something nobody'd pick up by accident. You grab it at a set time, and it yanks you across the world by your belly button." He considered. "It's horrible. You'll hate it."

"Great."

"First time I took one I landed on my face on a moor. Second time it dropped me in a graveyard." His ears went flat for a second, then came back up. "Long story. Not a good one."

"You've got a lot of those."

"Working on it." His tail swept. "Point is, they're horrible."

"You're really selling this."

"I'm being honest. You said straight. I'll be right next to you. I'll hold onto you. You won't land on your face."

"You don't know that."

"I'll land first," Harry said. "You can land on me."

She almost laughed again. She caught it this time.

"What time?" she said.

"Pick you up nine in the morning, Saturday. Your time. Be six in the evening there. Just when Die Ou Draak's getting going."

"And I'm back when?"

"Whenever you want to be. You say the word, I bring you home." He stopped walking. They were at the edge of Marg's yard, by the row of cabins. The porch light on the big house was off; Marg had gone to bed. "Same as tonight. You say leave, we leave. You say never again, I'll—" His ears went flat, then came back up. "Well. I'll be sad about it. But I'll go."

Leah stood at the bottom of the steps of cabin four and looked at him.

She thought about Jacob saying *come home*. She thought about Seth asking *are you going?* She thought about the pack, all of them, in her head, the second she phased again, hearing every word of this, every second of the pool game and the tail and the laughing and the *fake wolves* and the burping fire.

She thought about how she'd come two thousand kilometers to get away from people who needed things from her.

He'd asked. That was all. He'd asked her, the way people asked each other things, and then he'd waited for the answer.

"Conditions," she said.

His ears went straight up. "Name 'em."

"One. If I hate the boot thing, you carry me home. Your way. Whatever that is."

"Deal."

"Two. If I burp fire on you, that's your fault."

"Fair enough."

"Three. I'm keeping the coat."

His tail stopped.

"That's my good coat," he said.

"It's collateral."

"Collateral for what?"

"Saturday," Leah said. "So you come back."

Harry looked at her for a long time.

Then his muzzle did something she hadn't seen it do yet. The grin came up, slow, but it was different from the other ones. Less teeth. More something else. His ears went soft, tipped slightly forward, and his tail swung once, wide, and then kept going, side to side, steady and slow, sweeping a clean arc in the gravel.

"Ja," he said quietly. "Okay. Keep the coat."

She went up the steps. She opened the door. She stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"Leah," he said, behind her.

She turned around.

"What's the rest of it?" he said. "Your name."

She thought about it. She thought about how names were how people found you. She thought about how he'd found her anyway, from thirty kilometers, through a blood curse and a lake, without one.

"Clearwater," she said.

"Leah Clearwater." He said it slowly, the vowels landing in the wrong places, his accent mangling every bit of it. "Suits you."

"Goodnight, Potter."

"Night, Clearwater. Saturday. Nine."

"Don't be late."

"I'll be early," he said. "I'll bring you breakfast. Two plates."

She shut the door.

She stood on the other side of it, in the dark, with her back against the wood and her hands in his coat pockets, and she listened. There was no sound out there at all. No footsteps on the gravel, no crack of whatever it was he did when he disappeared. Nothing. She stood there for a long time.

Then, very faint, from somewhere out toward the lake, she heard someone humming. Badly. Something with a heavy beat that she was pretty sure was AC/DC. It went off down the shore and faded out into the dark.

Then a crack, like a branch breaking in a hard frost.

Then nothing.

---

### VII.

She lasted about an hour.

She lay on the bed in cabin four with her hands behind her head and the coat over her like a blanket and the moonlight coming in through the window, and she tried not to think about anything, and she thought about everything. The tail. The cupboard under the stairs. *Fake wolves.* The cue ball bouncing across the floor. The way his ears had gone soft when she'd said *so you come back.*

Black.

She didn't know what to do with that one. So she put it away, in a box in the back of her head, and closed the lid on it, and sat on the lid.

At a little after one in the morning she got up and went out the back of the cabin into the trees, and stood in the cold with her bare feet in the spruce needles, and took off the coat and folded it very carefully and laid it on a stump. She took off the cut-offs and the T-shirt and laid them on top. Then she stood there for a second, naked in the starlight, and let the heat come up.

She'd decided. She'd go in for thirty seconds, tell them she was alive, and get out.

The phase hit like it always did. Fire through the bones. Paws. The world blooming open into smell and sound.

*LEAH.*

They were both there. Of course they were both there. She had the distinct impression that Seth had been sitting in his wolf body on a ridge above La Push for hours, waiting.

*I'm fine,* she said. *I'm alive. Everything's fine. Go to bed. Goodnight.*

*Leah, wait—*

But it was already too late. That was the thing about the pack mind. You couldn't choose what went in. The second she phased, all of it was there, laid out for them like photographs on a table: the inn and the pizza and the beer and the pool table and the hunters and the cue ball and the tail sweeping smooth across her face, and her own laugh, bent over the felt, and the moon on the water and the old boat and the green eyes and *Cape Town* and *burp fire* and *keep the coat* and *Clearwater, suits you*.

And in the middle of it, clear as day, a big black wolf at a bar with his ears pointed at her, saying, *Fake wolves. Knock-offs. Bootlegs.*

The pack mind went completely silent.

Then Seth started laughing.

It was the kind of laugh that came out of a fifteen-year-old who'd watched his sister get tortured four hours ago, and had just found out she'd spent the rest of the evening drinking beer and playing pool. It went on and on. She could feel him rolling around on the ground.

*He called us KNOCK-OFFS,* Seth managed. *Oh my God. Oh my God. Off-brand. Jake. Jake, he called you OFF-BRAND—*

*I heard,* Jacob said.

His voice in the pack mind was flat, and big, and very tight.

*We're not fake wolves,* Jacob said.

*Technically,* Leah said, *he said knock-offs.*

*That's the same thing.*

*It's a little different.*

*It's the SAME THING, Leah.*

*He's kind of right, though,* Seth said, still wheezing. *I mean. To be fair. Nobody ever—*

*Seth,* Jacob said.

*I'm just saying! I'm your brother, Leah, I'm not allowed to have an opinion, but like, objectively, if I was one of the other guys—*

*SETH.*

*—okay, okay, shutting up.*

Leah stood in the trees behind cabin four with her ears up and her tail moving, slowly, side to side, and she didn't stop it.

She felt Jacob notice.

She felt him look at it, in her head, the tail moving. She felt him look at the memory of a black wolf's tail curling up in front of her face in a bar, and the sound of her own laughter, and she felt him not know what to do with any of it.

*Leah,* he said. *You don't know this guy.*

*I know some of him.*

*He's a killer.*

*He's a cop.*

*He's a cop who kills people.*

*So are we, Jake,* Leah said. *We just do it to things that are already dead.*

The silence in the pack mind went long.

*You're going,* Jacob said finally. *Saturday. To Africa.*

*I'm going to burp fire,* Leah said. *Yeah.*

*Leah—*

*He asked,* she said. *That's all. He asked, the way people ask each other, and then he waited to hear what I'd say. Nobody's ever done that, Jake. Not once. Not one of you.*

She felt that one land on both of them.

She didn't take it back.

*Goodnight,* she said.

*Leah, wait. One thing.* Jacob's voice changed. Something under it she couldn't read, which almost never happened in the pack mind. *The thing about his family. The Blacks. You thought about it at the bar. I saw it.*

She went very still.

Of course he'd seen it. She'd put it in a box and sat on the lid, and none of that mattered in here. In here there were no boxes.

*It's a common name,* she said. *That's what he said.*

*Yeah,* Jacob said slowly. *Yeah, it is.*

But she could feel him turning it over. She could feel him thinking about his great-grandfather, about the old stories, about the woodstove in his dad's kitchen. She could feel him thinking that it was crazy, the same way she'd thought it was crazy.

She could feel him not quite letting go of it.

*Goodnight, Jake,* she said. *Goodnight, Seth. Tell Mom I love her.*

*Leah—*

She let go.

The heat went out of her. She came down onto her knees in the spruce needles, a girl again, naked in the cold, with her head quiet and her breath smoking in the starlight.

She sat back on her heels for a while.

Then she got up, and pulled on the cut-offs and the T-shirt, and picked up the coat from the stump, and put it on, and did up the silver clasp at the throat, and went back inside.

She lay down on the bed with the coat around her and the smell of pine and cold stone and creek-water all through it, and under that, faint, the smell of home.

Saturday.

She closed her eyes, and her wolf curled up under her ribs with its tail over its nose, and for the first time in two years, Leah Clearwater fell asleep without anybody else in her head, and without wanting anybody there, and smiling about it.
