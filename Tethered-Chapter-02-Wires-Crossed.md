# Tethered

## Chapter Two: Wires Crossed

### I. Harry

She was still showing him her teeth.

All of them. The lips had come back so far that the skin of her muzzle had bunched into ridges between her eyes, and he could see the gums, pink and black, and the long yellow-white curve of every canine, top and bottom. No sound came with it. She stood ten feet off with her claws sunk through the slush into the frozen ground underneath and the wind lifting the pale grey fur along her spine in little ripples, and she snarled at him in total silence, the way a bloke stares at you across a pub after you've said something about his mum.

Harry waited to see what she'd do with it.

She didn't do anything with it. She kept snarling.

Right.

He let his eyes go past her for a second. The red wolf had his ears pinned and his weight back on his haunches, the kid still on his back, still hiccupping, the blonde vampire crouched at his shoulder with one white hand flat on the kid's leg. The rest of the red wolf's lot stood bunched behind him, a sandy one, a chocolate-brown one, a grey one with dark spots down his back, all three with their heads low and their eyes going from the grey wolf to Harry and back again. A dozen yards off, the other pack held its ground. The black one at the front still had his head down. The silver one beside him had stopped growling, which Harry decided to take as progress.

Behind him, the vampires had gone back to doing what vampires did best, which was standing about like expensive furniture. He couldn't hear any of them breathing. He could hear the kid's heart going like a sparrow's, and the long slow knock of the wolves' hearts, and his own, and the wind in the pines, and very far off to the west, a sound underneath all of it that might have been the sea or might have been his ears.

Geneva would be a while.

He knew Geneva. Geneva was quick in the sense that somebody there would already be filling in a form requesting authority to fill in a form, and somebody else would be shouting into a Floo about which division had jurisdiction over very large dogs. He gave it an hour. He'd seen it take four.

He could stand in the slush for an hour while a wolf snarled at him.

Or.

He held his right hand out to the side, palm up, and called for it.

It came the way the cigarette had. One second there was nothing in his hand but cold air, and the next there was a book in it, and the weight nearly took his arm down to his knee before he got his other hand under it and let the magic take the strain. Two feet tall. Eight inches thick, near enough. Green leather gone black and shiny at the edges where two hundred years of hands had held it, brass caps on all four corners, a brass clasp the size of a door latch. A length of iron chain hung off the spine, six links of it, the last one sheared clean through and still glowing a dull orange at the cut.

Somebody in the Department reading room was going to have a great deal to say to him on Monday.

He let go. The book stayed where it was, hanging in the air in front of his chest at reading height, and the clasp came undone with a click that sounded very loud in the quiet field.

Behind him, a voice said, low, "Is he reading?"

"Shut up, Emmett," said a woman's voice, lower.

"I'm just asking. Rose. He's reading. He's got a book. He just burned the entire—"

"*Emmett.*"

Harry turned back the cover. The title had been tooled in gilt that had mostly worn away, and the lettering underneath had been done by somebody who'd been paid by the curl.

*OF BINDINGS. Being a Comparative Account of Vows, Debts, Tethers and Bonds Between Persons, Beings, and Part-Beings, Together With Notes Upon Their Formation and Severance. Revised and Enlarged, 1911. REFERENCE ONLY. NOT TO BE REMOVED FROM LEVEL NINE.*

Underneath, in blue biro and much more recent capitals, someone had added: *AND WE MEAN IT.*

"Index," Harry said.

The pages moved. They went over on their own in one long whispering rush, the way a deck of cards goes when you run your thumb down the edge, and settled near the back with a soft slap. The index was charmed: you said what you wanted and it took you as close as it could get. It had been charmed by a man who'd died in 1888 with very fixed ideas about what people ought to want, which made asking it anything a bit like asking directions from a vicar.

"Bonds," Harry said. "Between a person and a shapeshifter."

The pages flicked. A column of small dense type slid up under his eyes, and a line in the middle of it brightened, very slightly, as if someone had breathed on it.

*SHAPESHIFTERS, Bonds Pertaining To. See: ANIMAGI (none pertaining); LYCANTHROPES (see PACK-SENSE, so-called); SELKIES (see SKIN-BONDS); CHANGERS, HEREDITARY (see KIN-BONDS; also Appendix C, Lines Not Otherwise Studied).*

"Pack-sense," Harry said.

The book went to it.

*PACK-SENSE, so-called. Among lycanthropes who run together through many moons, a sympathy is reported whereby one may know the distress of another at a distance of some miles. The Department has been unable to measure it, owing to the difficulty of persuading lycanthropes to be measured. It is in any case confined to the night of the full moon, ceases at dawn, and is shared equally by every member of the group. It is therefore a condition of the group and no bond in the sense of this work.*

He read it twice.

Shared equally. Every member of the group. That was no good to him. He wasn't in her group; she'd made that clear enough with her face. And it was a quarter past one on a Saturday afternoon with no moon worth mentioning anywhere above the clouds.

He thought about Remus for about one second, which was as long as he usually let himself. Remus had run with a stag, a dog and a rat, and he'd never said a word about any pack-sense. He'd only ever said they were the best nights of his life.

"Skin-bonds."

*SKIN-BONDS. A selkie whose sealskin is taken from her is bound to the taker for so long as he holds it. She will remain in his house and do as she is bidden, and cannot leave him, nor harm him, nor speak against him. The bond resides wholly in the skin. If the skin is recovered and put on, the bond ends at once, and the selkie will commonly return to the sea without a backward look. Cases: Orkney, 1622; Sanday, 1689; Shetland (two), 1790; Fetlar, 1803.*

There were case notes under that. Some of them ran longer than they needed to, written by men who had very clearly considered the whole arrangement a stroke of luck. Harry read about a third of the first one and stopped.

He looked over the top of the book at the grey wolf.

She had all her skin, as far as he could tell, and most of it was bristling. Nobody had anything of hers hidden up a chimney. And the thing under his breastbone had come in through bone and kept on going.

She snarled at him over the top of the page, still silent. He went back to it.

"Kin-bonds."

*KIN-BONDS. Among families in which a beast-form passes by blood, a bond is commonly reported between members of the same line, manifesting at the first change and running between blood relations only. It is strongest between parent and child, and between siblings of the same litter (the authors use the word advisedly).*

Blood relations only. He wasn't her blood. Considering everything, he was glad of it.

"Appendix C."

It went to the back. *LINES NOT OTHERWISE STUDIED.* A page and a half, two columns, small type. Most of it was places. *The wolf-men of the Courland shore, reported 1702, unconfirmed. The bear-line of the upper Pechora, reported 1781, unconfirmed. The jaguar-line of the upper Orinoco, reported by a single informant, 1856, informant subsequently eaten.* His eyes went down it fast. Near the bottom of the second column:

*The Quileute of the Olympic Peninsula, State of Washington, U.S.A. A line of wolf-changers, reported by a MACUSA observer (1937), who states only that they "come when the cold ones come." No further study. Contact not advised.*

That was the lot. Four lines. He'd had longer notes on a cupboard.

"Cheers," he said to it. "Very useful."

Behind him, somebody shifted their weight in the snow. The first sound any of the vampires had made in a while that wasn't Emmett.

He tried it wider.

"Bonds," he said. "Formed on sight. Unchosen."

The pages rushed and stopped. *LOVE POTIONS, Effects Of. A love potion produces an obsession that lasts exactly as long as the potion and not one hour more, and readers are referred to the Department of Magical Law Enforcement.* No good. Nobody'd given him anything to drink and he'd have tasted it if they had. He'd learned that one the hard way at sixteen, off a box of chocolates meant for somebody else.

Below it: *VEELA, so-called "Mating Bond" Of. The Department examined forty-one claims of such a bond between 1790 and 1909 and found every one of them to be either an ordinary marriage or an ordinary infatuation. The allure of a Veela acts upon the observer, fades with distance, and binds nobody at all.*

Fleur would have liked that one. Bill would have liked it more.

He looked at the grey wolf again. Ten feet. The line in his chest hadn't faded. If anything it had got shorter and tighter as she'd walked across the field to him, the way a guitar string goes up in pitch when you wind it.

She bared her teeth further, which he wouldn't have said was possible.

"Soul bonds," Harry said.

The book hesitated. He'd have sworn to it in front of the Wizengamot. The pages lifted, sagged, lifted again, and went over slowly, with something about the way they moved that suggested a man in 1888 sighing through his nose.

*SOUL BONDS, so-called. See note.*

*Note. The phrase "soul bond" appears in no serious literature before 1850 and in a great deal of unserious literature after it. It has been applied by the public to Unbreakable Vows, to life-debts, to twin-sympathy, to the effects of love potions, and to any marriage of which the writer happened to approve. The Department recognises no phenomenon by this name. The reader who has come to this page in hope is referred to the locked room on the ninth level, and wished luck.*

Harry snorted. It came out of his nose in a short cloud of steam.

He'd been in the locked room twice. He had no plans for a third time.

He went through the proper ones anyway, because he was thorough and because the alternative was standing in a field with nothing to do with his hands while a wolf took his measure for a coffin.

*THE UNBREAKABLE VOW.* Chosen. Spoken aloud, each clause of it. A Bonder with a wand, two parties with their right hands clasped, tongues of fire going round the wrists, and if you broke it, you died. Two-way, technically, but only because two people had decided it ought to be. Nobody here had held anybody's hand. He'd been very particular about that with Aro.

*LIFE-DEBTS.* He knew this one without reading it. Dumbledore's voice in an office full of silver instruments: *when one wizard saves another wizard's life, it creates a certain bond between them.* The magic of it sat in the debtor, quietly, and waited, and ended when the debt was paid. He looked across the snow at the grey heaps on the far side of the field, at the wide grey fan of ash where Vladimir had come down. If you squinted, he'd saved everybody on this side of the field, wolves included. If that made a life-debt, then the red wolf owed him one, and the sandy one, and the doctor and his wife and their whole stroppy family, and there'd be lines coming into him from every direction like the strings on a kite.

There was one line.

*PROTECTIONS, SACRIFICIAL.* He turned that page without reading it.

He stood there a moment with his hand flat on the next page, not turning it. The paper was cold and slightly furred, like the skin of a peach. Down in the dark at the bottom of himself, the thing on the other end of the line had put its chin on its paws and was watching her with its ears forward. It didn't think much of the book. It hadn't from the start.

He kept his voice down for the last one.

"Bonds," he said. "Unchosen. Two-way." He thought about it. "Goes past the core."

The pages didn't move.

He waited. Behind him he heard, very faintly, the kid hiccup and then go quiet, and the blonde vampire say *shh, shh* in a voice he'd have bet good money she didn't use on anybody else.

Then the book went. Slowly. All the way to the back. Past Appendix C. Past D, which was tables, and E, which was a list of every Bonder licensed in Britain between 1700 and 1900, to the very last page before the endpapers. There was a heading at the top of it in the old man's curly hand, *APPENDIX F. PHENOMENA OBSERVED BUT NOT CLASSIFIED*, and under the heading the page was empty except for one slip of newer paper that somebody had pasted in, slightly crooked, with a typed paragraph on it.

*Godric's Hollow, West Country, 31st October 1981. Rebound of the Killing Curse upon its caster. Survival of the intended subject, aged fifteen months. Mechanism unknown. See also: file P/80/07/31 (restricted).*

Harry looked at it for a little while.

Then he shut the book with both hands. The clasp snapped home.

"Yeah," he said. "Course."

The book went back to wherever books went when he was done with them, and his hands were empty, and the field was the field again, grey and white and smelling of burnt church.

He looked at the grey wolf.

He caught himself doing it a second after he'd started. He was looking from her to the place in the air where the book had been and back to her, the way you'd look from a diagram to the engine on the bench in front of you, trying to find the part that didn't match the drawing. Her ears. Her chest, broad under the grey ruff. The place in the middle of it, right in the centre, where his end of the line went in.

She saw him do it.

Her lips had been back off her teeth for about ten minutes. Now the sound came up behind them, and this time she let him hear it, low and ugly and pointed straight at him, a snarl that started in the bottom of her chest and rolled up through her throat and came out over her tongue like gravel going down a chute.

Something in his chest answered before he'd decided anything at all.

### II. Leah

She felt it before she heard it.

It came up through the ground. That was the first thing. A vibration in the frozen dirt under the slush, so low and so deep she felt it in the pads of her feet before she understood it was a sound, a long shuddering hum like a truck idling on the far side of a wall. Then it was in her legs. Then her teeth. Then it found the line in the middle of her chest, the one that ran from her to him, and the line *rang*, the way a wire rings when you pluck it, and the whole of her rang with it.

Then she heard it.

It was a growl.

She knew growls. She'd been making them for two years and listening to them for longer, Sam's, Paul's, Jacob's, the high snapping snarls of Collin and Brady when they were showing off, Seth's, which never sounded like he meant it. She knew exactly what a wolf sounded like when it was warning you off, and she knew from the pitch of it how big the chest was that made it. Sam's growl came from a big chest. Jacob's came from a bigger one.

This one came from something that would have made Jacob look like a beagle.

It was coming out of a man in a leather jacket. His mouth was barely open. He was standing in the slush exactly where he'd been standing, his hands at his sides, and the sound was coming up out of the middle of him, out of the place she'd been staring at, the place where the line went in, and it was rolling out across the snow at her like a wave coming up a beach.

Her snarl stopped dead.

It stopped on its own. It cut off in her throat like somebody'd shut a door on it, and before she could even be angry about that, her body did the next thing on its own.

Her ears went flat. Both of them, back against her skull. Her head dropped. Three inches, maybe. Enough. Her muzzle tipping down toward the snow and her eyes sliding off his face to somewhere around his boots. Her tail, which had been up and stiff and bristling like a bottlebrush for ten minutes, went down between her hocks. Her haunches sank. She felt her own weight settle backward onto her hind legs, the beginning of a crouch, the kind of crouch she'd seen Brady do in front of Sam the first week after he phased when he still didn't know where he fit.

Leah Clearwater had never once lowered her head to Sam Uley without the Alpha voice forcing it down. Not once. Not when he'd left her. Not when he'd ordered her round in her own head for months. Not when he'd stood over her in the pack mind and made her listen to him love Emily. She'd made him use the voice every single time, because she'd be damned if she gave him anything he hadn't taken.

She'd just given it to a stranger for growling.

And that wasn't even the worst of it.

The worst of it was underneath. In the place under her ribs where the wolf lived, the part of her that didn't think in words, the part that had gone quiet and calm the first second she saw him and had stayed that way through all the screaming she'd done since. Her whole body was down on its haunches with its ears flat and its tail tucked, and the wolf in the middle of it had pricked up its ears.

*Interested.*

It had heard that sound come up the line and it had pricked up its ears and gone, very simply and without shame, *oh.*

A whine started up the back of her throat. She clamped her jaws on it so hard her teeth creaked.

The pack mind exploded.

*Whoa*—

*Did she just*—

*That's a wolf. That's a WOLF, that's a wolf in him, I TOLD you, Jake, I said he smelled*—

*Holy crap, Leah, your ears*—

*Everybody HOLD.* Jacob. His voice came down over the top of the rest like a lid on a pot. *Hold. Nobody move.* But under the Alpha in it Leah could feel what Jacob's body was doing, because in here she could feel everybody's, and Jacob's ears had gone back too, halfway. Jacob, who'd taken on the whole of Sam's pack to protect Bella. Jacob, the rightful heir, Ephraim Black's great-grandson, with his own hackles half up and his weight shifting back off his front paws and the kid on his back. Something in the oldest part of him had heard that sound and done the sums, same as hers had, and come up with *bigger.*

*Jake*, Seth said. Small. *Jake, what is he?*

*I don't know.*

Over on the left, Sam's pack had come apart. Leah saw it out of the corner of her eye, past the edge of her own lowered head. Collin and Brady had scrambled backward so fast Brady had sat down in the snow. Jared had dropped into a crouch. Paul's growl had stopped mid-rumble, his lip still curled, his eyes wide and white-rimmed. Sam hadn't moved. Sam stood at the front of all of them, enormous and black, and he hadn't moved one inch, and Leah could see from here that it was costing him.

The vampires had gone so still they looked carved.

The blonde one, Rosalie, had moved, though. When that sound had started, Rosalie had come up off her crouch and lifted the kid clean off Jacob's back in one movement, and now she was standing three feet behind him with Nessie clamped against her chest and one white hand cupped over the back of her curly head, and her lips were peeled back off her teeth at the man in the leather jacket. Edward had turned his whole body toward the wolves. His face was doing something complicated. He was hearing all of this, Leah realised. Every word. Every flat ear. The *oh.*

She was going to kill him. She was going to kill all of them.

The growl stopped.

It ran down. That was how she'd describe it later, if anybody ever asked, which they'd better not: it ran down like an engine switched off, a long dropping rumble into silence. The line in her chest went on humming for a second afterward, like a bell does.

Then it was quiet.

The man had closed his eyes.

He stood there with them shut. His jaw moved once, a short hard working movement, like he was swallowing something back down that was trying to come up. He took a breath in through his nose. It was a long one. She watched his chest go up under the green flannel and hold, and hold, and go down again, slow, and the steam of it came out of his nose in a thin double plume and blew away on the wind.

He opened his eyes.

He looked at her. Then down at her ears, which were still flat. Then back up.

He cleared his throat.

"Sorry," he said. "'Scuse me." He put two fingers to his chest, to the middle of it, and tapped twice. "Bit of a cold."

Nobody said anything for a long second.

Then, from somewhere behind the blond doctor, Emmett Cullen said, "A *cold?*"

"Going round," said the man. He sniffed. "Wouldn't come too close."

"Dude. That wasn't a—"

"I've had colds," said Garrett, from the Denali side. He still had his hand on the blonde one's arm, Kate, and he was looking at the man with his head tipped to one side like a crow. "Couple of hundred years back, mind. I don't recall one sounding like that."

"You've not had mine, then."

Garrett's mouth twitched.

Leah's ears came up off her skull. It took effort. It took real effort, more than it should have, like lifting something heavy with her neck, but she got them up, and she got her head up, and she got her tail out from between her legs, and she stood there on four straight legs and stared at him with every ounce of hate she had left, and she had a lot.

*He's laughing at me.*

*I don't think he's laughing*, Seth said carefully. *Lee, look at him. I think he's sort of... embarrassed?*

She looked at him.

He looked the way he'd looked all afternoon, as far as she could tell. Tired. Dry. A man in a leather jacket in the snow with a crease between his eyebrows. But he'd stopped looking at her. He'd turned his head and was looking off to the west, toward the trees, with his hand still resting on his chest where he'd tapped it, and his thumb was moving very slightly, back and forth, against the flannel. The way you'd rub a bruise.

*Embarrassed*, Embry said. *He just made Sam Uley stand still by clearing his throat and you think he's embarrassed.*

*I'm just saying what it looks like.*

*It looks like he's got a bear in his chest*, said Quil.

*Bears don't smell like that*, Jacob said. *You all smelled it.*

Nobody answered that.

Something moved at the far edge of the clearing.

Leah's head snapped round, every wolf's head snapped round, and half the vampires', and for one second the whole field went tight as a fist all over again. Then she saw what it was and didn't understand it.

A bird. A big one. Long-legged and long-necked, coming in low over the snow from the north with slow heavy wingbeats, and it was made of light. Silver light, the same as the deer had been. It came across the ash on the far side of the field without touching it and folded its huge wings and dropped onto the snow in front of the man in a long-legged landing, and stood there, a heron, she thought, a heron as tall as a kid, shining. Its feet didn't mark the snow either.

It opened its long beak and a woman's voice came out.

The voice was tired. It had an accent, French or German or something in between, and the kind of flatness people's voices get when they've been awake for a long time.

"Harry. It's Annelise." A pause, as if she was choosing words. "Registration's approved in principle. Your Healer's in Kathmandu closing a dragon-pox ward and won't leave it until it's closed, and Beings' shapeshifter man is in Ottawa and refuses to cross an ocean without a Portkey, so you'll have the team at sundown your time, not before. Meet them at the family's house. Don't burn anybody else." Another pause, longer. "Thirty-odd, Harry. And a Romanian. The Council's already asking. Write it up properly, and I mean properly, not like Lisbon."

The heron dipped its beak at him once, the way the deer had. Then it came apart into silver mist and blew away.

The man stood looking at the place where it had been.

"Not like Lisbon," he said to nobody. "Lisbon was fine."

He turned round.

"Change of plan, Doc," he said, to the blond vampire at the front. "You heard her. Team's coming to the house at sundown. Go home."

The doctor, Carlisle, didn't move. "You'll be there?"

"I'll be there."

"And until then?"

"Until then you go home and sit in it. Feed the kid. Put a cold flannel on her head, she'll like that." He jerked his chin at Bella, still on her knees with Edward's arms round her. "Her too, probably. If you lot get headaches. I don't know if you lot get headaches."

Bella lifted her face out of Edward's shoulder. Her eyes were very dark.

"And if we don't go home," she said. Her voice was hoarse. "If we're not there when they knock."

"Then I come and find you." He looked at her steadily. "I know where you live, love. I've been in your head."

Edward made a sound with no words in it. Bella's hand closed on his arm before he could do anything with it.

"The rest of you," the man went on, raising his voice a little, toward the Irish, toward the tall dark women from the Amazon, toward the Denalis and the one on her knees in the slush with the other two round her, "go with them or go home. Don't mind which. Just don't go to town." He turned. "And you."

He was looking at Jacob.

"Cujo. I said trousers. There's a beach. Driftwood, a big rock out in the water with trees on top." He paused. "You told her stories there. Wolves and cold ones. She was seventeen and you were trying to pull."

In the pack mind, Jacob went white-hot and silent.

"Give it an hour," said the man. "Then come and find me."

He put his hands in his jacket pockets, turned his back on all of them, every vampire and every wolf on the field, and started walking west.

He walked through the snow the way he'd stood in it. The slush went soft round his boots with every step, and the prints he left behind him steamed. He passed the Cullens without looking at them. He passed within fifteen feet of Jacob's pack without looking at them either. Collin and Brady shuffled back out of his way and he didn't look at them. He walked across the open ground toward the trees on the western edge, toward the big dark wall of spruce and hemlock with the snow sitting on its branches, and his shoulders didn't move much when he walked, and he didn't hurry.

The line went with him.

Leah felt it pull. It had been short and tight and humming for ten minutes, ten feet long, and now it was getting longer, and every yard it gained it pulled at the middle of her chest, gently, the way somebody tugs your sleeve. Twenty yards. Thirty.

Her front paw came up out of the snow.

She hadn't told it to. She looked down at it, hanging there in the air, and put it back down. Hard.

Forty yards.

The other one came up.

*Leah.* Jacob.

She looked back at him. He was standing there with his ears still half back and his empty back, Rosalie behind him with the kid, and he was looking at her, and she braced, every muscle in her braced for it, for the weight of the Alpha voice coming down over her like a hand on the back of her neck. *Stay.* One word. He'd done it before. Not to her. But he could.

He didn't.

She felt him think about it. Felt the word come up in him and sit there and go back down.

*Go*, he said instead. *Seth, go with her.*

*On it.*

*I don't need a babysitter*, Leah snapped.

*He's not for you*, Jacob said. *He's for him. I want eyes on that guy.* A beat. *And on you. Whatever. Go before I change my mind.*

She went.

She went the way she always went, fast, low, flat out across the snow toward the trees, and she didn't look back at Sam, and she didn't look at the ash, and she didn't think about what her ears had done. Behind her, Seth's paws thudded, trying to keep up. Ahead of her, at the edge of the trees, the man in the leather jacket stepped in among the spruce trunks without looking back, and the shadows closed over his shoulders, and the line ran straight in after him like a fishing line going into dark water.

She followed it in.

### III. Rosalie

The child's blood was on her thumb.

One smear of it. Rosalie had wiped it off Renesmee's lip the second the man turned his back, with the pad of her thumb and then with the cuff of her sweater, cream cashmere, ruined, and now the smell of it was on her hand and her sleeve and in the air an inch from her nose, and she was not breathing. She had stopped breathing when the first drop came out of Renesmee's nostril. She would start again when she was a long way from here and the sleeve was in a bin.

Renesmee had her face in Rosalie's neck. Her small body was hot through the red wool coat, hot as a hot-water bottle, and every few seconds a shudder went through her, the tail end of the crying, the hiccups she couldn't stop.

"I know," Rosalie murmured into her hair. "I know, baby. I know. He's gone. He's gone now."

A small hand came up and found her cheek.

The picture came the way Renesmee's pictures always came, all at once and from the inside, bright as a slide thrown on a wall. Cold. A thin cold line drawn through the soft bright bubble of Bella's shield, and then through Renesmee, through everything Renesmee was, like the edge of a sheet of paper drawn across a fingertip. Then pages. Her whole life going over, page after page, faster than she could hold on to any of them, and something in there with her that had no face and no voice and no smell, turning them.

Rosalie's arms tightened. She couldn't help it. Renesmee squeaked, and she made herself loosen them.

"He hurt you," she said.

The picture changed. A headache, pressed flat into Rosalie's mind the way a child presses a sticker onto a page: a tight throbbing ache behind the eyes, here, and here. Then the man's face, across the snow, looking at Renesmee as she cried. His mouth was flat and he looked tired. It moved, and Renesmee had heard it, the way she heard everything, from forty yards away through the wind: *Yeah. Sorry, kid.*

Rosalie had not heard that. She'd been too busy watching the blood.

The picture changed again, and this time it was the deer.

The silver stag, enormous, standing in the snow with light coming off every hair of it, its antlers branching up into the white sky like a tree in winter. Its great head turning. Its eyes. And the feeling Renesmee had about it, which came through the picture with no words attached, because Renesmee was four months old and didn't need words: a huge round wondering *oh*, the way she'd felt the first time she saw snow.

Then the heron. Smaller in the picture, further off, but shining.

Then the deer again.

"Don't you dare," Rosalie said softly, into the curls. "Don't you dare like him."

Renesmee showed her the deer a fourth time, very firmly, and put her head back down on Rosalie's shoulder.

Fine. *Fine.* She was a baby. She liked the shiny animal. Rosalie would deal with it.

Around them, the field was coming apart. The wolves on the far side, Sam's lot, had broken their line completely and were milling, big shaggy shapes in the snow going in circles round each other, the black one standing in the middle of them like a rock in a stream. Jacob's two remaining, the brown one and the spotted one, had come up close behind him and were pressed against his flanks. The Irish had drawn together into a tight knot by the trees with Siobhan at the front, talking low and fast to Liam. The Amazons hadn't moved at all. Zafrina was staring at the place on the western edge of the field where the man had gone into the trees with an expression Rosalie couldn't read and didn't particularly want to.

And Tanya was still on her knees.

Carmen was holding her. Kate was standing over both of them with Garrett beside her, and Kate's face was turned toward the far side of the field, toward the little grey heap that had been Caius. She hadn't looked away from it since the light went out. Rosalie could hear her saying something under her breath, over and over, in a voice that didn't sound like Kate's.

"He didn't look at them. He didn't even look at them."

Garrett's hand moved on her arm. He said nothing. For Garrett, that was a great deal.

Twenty minutes ago, Irina had been alive.

Rosalie looked away.

Bella was getting up. Edward had his hands under her elbows, lifting her, but she was doing most of it herself, getting her feet under her in the slush, and when she was standing she shook him off and turned and held out her arms.

Rosalie handed Renesmee over. She didn't want to. She did it anyway. That was what you did.

Bella took her daughter against her chest and closed her eyes and stood there, swaying slightly, with her cheek pressed to the top of the curly head. Renesmee's hand went to Bella's face. Rosalie watched Bella flinch, very slightly, as the cold sharp line went through her for the second time, from the outside this time, in her daughter's memory instead of her own.

"I know, baby," Bella said. "I know. Me too."

"Your shield," Edward said quietly.

Bella opened her eyes. "It's there." She was quiet for a moment, feeling for it. Rosalie had watched her do it a hundred times this month, in the snow behind the house, with Kate's hands sparking against her skin: the faraway look, the slight tilt of the head, as if she were listening to something in another room. "It's there. It's all there. There's just—" She stopped. "There's a line in it. Like a scar. Where he cut. And where he came through the second time, it's—" Her mouth twisted. "Lumpy. It feels lumpy, Edward. Like a bone that's set wrong."

"Can you put it out?"

Bella did something. Rosalie felt nothing, which was the point. Edward's face eased a fraction.

"It still works," Bella said. "It just feels different." Her eyes went to the western trees. "He said it would knit."

"He said it would scar."

"He said both."

Carlisle came over. He'd been standing in the open snow where the man had left him, very still, with his hands at his sides, and now he crossed to them and put two fingers under Renesmee's chin and tipped her face up to the light, gently, and looked at her pupils, one and then the other. Renesmee let him. She liked Carlisle's hands. They didn't smell of anything.

"Headache," Carlisle said after a moment. "As he said. The bleeding's stopped. A small vessel in the nose, I'd think, from the pressure. She'll be all right." He let go of her chin and touched her curls, once. "He told the truth about that."

"He told the truth about everything," said Rosalie. "That's what I don't like."

Carlisle looked at her.

"Rosalie."

"He said he'd burn them and he burned them. He said she'd have a headache and she's got a headache. He said he could find us and he went through Bella's head like a—" She cut herself off. "A man who means everything he says. That's worse than a liar, Carlisle. You can plan for a liar."

Carlisle didn't argue. Rosalie took that as agreement. Carlisle argued with everything.

Eleazar had come across from the Denalis. He stopped at a polite distance with his hands clasped behind his back, the way he'd stood all through the morning, a slim dark man with the posture of a courtier and eyes that had spent two hundred years picking gifts out of crowds for Aro. He looked older than he had at dawn. They all did, probably.

"I tried to see him," he said, without being asked. "From the moment he arrived. I could not stop myself. It is what I do."

"And?" said Edward.

"With Bella, I can see the shape of the shield even when I cannot see through it. It has edges. It has a size." Eleazar's mouth moved. "With him, I see the outline of something, and nothing inside the outline. A door. Shut. A very large door, and something behind it moving about." He was quiet a moment. "I never saw anything like it in Volterra. Aro would have—" He stopped. "Well. Aro would have wanted it very badly. And look."

He didn't need to say look at what. They had all seen what.

"There's a wolf in him," Edward said.

They turned to him. He was looking at the trees, too.

"The pack felt it," he said. "When he growled. All of them, both packs. I heard all of it at once, every mind on that side of the field." He shook his head slightly, as if there was water in his ear. "Their bodies knew before their heads did. Every one of them. Sam's still shaking. Paul nearly bolted. Jacob—" A glance toward the russet wolf, who was standing with his head low and his ears turning back and forth between Renesmee and the trees. "Jacob's ears went back, and Jacob would rather die than admit that. They all smelled it on him from the start. Wolf. Under the pine." He paused. "And Leah—"

He stopped.

"Leah what?" Bella said.

Edward was quiet.

"Edward."

"That one's hers," he said. "She'd hate that I heard it. She'd hate it more that I said it." His jaw tightened. "Let her keep it."

Rosalie stared at him. Edward, who had stood in her head and every other head in this family for eighty years, airing whatever he found there whenever it suited him. Edward, keeping something back. For Leah Clearwater.

She thought about it and found she wasn't surprised. Leah Clearwater was the only one of the dogs Rosalie had ever been able to stand, mostly because Leah hated everybody with perfect evenhandedness and didn't pretend otherwise. Rosalie had always respected that. She'd never told her so. She'd sooner have eaten a deer raw in the middle of a shopping mall.

The grey wolf had gone into the trees after him. Rosalie had watched it go. Ears flat, tail low, running like something was pulling her on a string.

Something was.

"Poor bitch," Rosalie said, under her breath. She meant it in every sense.

Edward heard. Edward always heard. He didn't say anything.

The man's voice was still going round in Rosalie's head, the things he'd said. She didn't want it in there and it kept coming back anyway, the way a song does.

*Fuck me. You lot have got a fucked-up way of making babies.*

That one had gone in under her ribs like a knife.

She'd wanted it. Of course she'd wanted it. A baby, a real one, a living one, in this house. She'd wanted it more than she'd wanted anything since 1933, and when Bella had come home from that island with something moving under her hand, Rosalie had stood over her like a guard dog and held the line against everybody. Against Edward. Against Carlisle. She'd held the cup. She'd bent the straw. She'd listened to Bella's ribs break, one by one, and stayed in the room. She'd have done it again tomorrow. Renesmee was alive and laughing and showing her pictures of deer, and Rosalie would do every single thing again in the same order.

And it had been fucked up. He was right about that. She'd watched it. She'd watched a girl get eaten from the inside out and called it a pregnancy.

Both things were true. She'd known that for four months. It was just that nobody had ever said the second one out loud.

And then there was the other thing he'd said.

*You'll explain to me in very small words how a lad your age ends up tied for life to a newborn, and why everyone round here seems to think that's a lovely bit of folklore.*

Rosalie had been saying that for four months, more or less, to anyone who would listen, in whatever words she could find. Nobody listened. Bella had gone for the dog's throat and Seth's collarbone had snapped, and then everyone had calmed down and decided it was sweet. Quil and his toddler. Jacob and his baby. *He'll be whatever she needs.* Everybody nodding. Esme smiling. Rosalie at the back of the room going *are you all out of your minds*, and being told she was just being Rosalie.

A stranger had walked in off the air and said it in front of everyone.

She hated him for Renesmee's nose. She'd have gone for his throat over it, if she'd thought for a second she'd have lived long enough to reach him. And she'd have shaken his hand for the dog.

She didn't know what to do with that, so she didn't do anything with it.

The russet wolf had gone. Rosalie hadn't seen him go, which meant he'd gone into the trees behind them while she was thinking, and now he came back out of them on two legs, pulling a cord loose from his ankle, in a pair of cutoff denim shorts and nothing else. Six foot seven of steaming brown skin in a snowfield in January. His feet were bare. The slush hissed under them.

"He said trousers," said Rosalie.

Jacob didn't look at her. "These are trousers."

"Those are a cry for help."

"What do you call a smart blonde?"

"Don't."

"A golden retriever."

"Says the actual dog."

It was automatic. It was the most normal thing either of them had said all day, and they both heard it, and for a second neither of them said anything at all. Jacob's mouth moved like it wanted to do something and couldn't decide what.

Then he went past her to Bella and crouched down in front of her so his face was level with Renesmee's.

"Hey, Nessie," he said. His voice went soft the way it only ever went soft for one person. "Hey, kiddo. You okay?"

Renesmee lifted her head off Bella's shoulder and put both hands on Jacob's face.

Rosalie couldn't see the picture. She could see Jacob's face while he looked at it. She saw the moment the cold line went through him, and she saw what it did to him, and for half a second she very nearly felt sorry for the dog.

"Yeah," Jacob said hoarsely. "I know. I felt it. I'm sorry, Ness. I'm sorry I couldn't—" He stopped. Swallowed. Renesmee's hands stayed on his face, and her picture changed, and Rosalie watched Jacob's eyebrows go up. "The deer? Yeah. Yeah, I saw the deer. It was pretty cool, huh."

Renesmee nodded solemnly.

Then she showed him something else, and his face went still.

"I've got to go talk to him," Jacob said. "Just for a little while. He wants to talk to me about you. About us." He glanced up at Bella, then back at the child. "I'll be back before dark. Promise. Before it's dark, I'm back. Okay?"

Renesmee looked at him for a long moment with her serious brown eyes. Then she took her hands away and put her face back into her mother's neck.

Jacob stood up.

"He's going to ask about the imprint," Edward said.

"Yeah, I got that, thanks."

"He's going to ask about her specifically. He went through her memories. He saw you come through the door that morning." Edward's eyes were very dark. "He saw your face."

"I know what he saw."

"Then tell him the truth."

Jacob turned on him. "I always tell the truth. When have I ever not—"

"I know." Edward held up a hand. "I know you do. That's why I'm saying it. Don't get defensive. Don't get angry. He's not—" He paused, searching. "I couldn't hear him, Jacob. Not a word. I can't tell you what he's thinking or what he wants. All I know is what he did." His eyes went past Jacob, across the field, to the grey heaps on the far side. "Be careful."

"Find out what he is," Bella said. Her voice was steadier now. "Jake. And find out what he wants with Leah."

Jacob looked at the western trees.

"I don't know that he wants anything with Leah," he said slowly. "I don't think he knows."

He went. He walked across the snow toward the trees with his bare feet hissing in the slush, a tall dark shape against all that white, and at the edge of the trees he stopped long enough to shove the shorts down and wind the cord back round his ankle, and Rosalie looked away with feeling. When she looked back there was a shudder in the air between the spruce trunks, a sound like a wet sheet cracking on a line, and a flash of russet fur going away fast through the trees, and he was gone.

Somewhere in South America, Rosalie thought, Alice would have watched the Volturi's futures go out like a row of candles. She wondered if Alice had seen him coming.

She suspected not.

"Home," Esme said gently, beside her. Esme's hand was on her back. "Come on, Rose. Let's go home."

Rosalie went. They all went, in the end: Bella with Renesmee held tight against her, Edward a half-step behind with his hand on the small of her back, Carlisle and Esme, Emmett with his arm around Rosalie's shoulders, the Denalis with Tanya on her feet now and Carmen holding her up, the Irish, the Amazons, Garrett walking beside Kate and saying nothing. They ran, because they were vampires and that was how they went anywhere, and the field fell away behind them, and the smoke, and the ash.

Rosalie looked back once, at the western trees.

For the first time in four months, she hoped somebody was about to make the dog squirm.

She hoped the grey wolf would be all right.

She didn't know which of those two things surprised her more.

### IV. Harry

The trees took the smell away first.

Fifty yards in, the burnt-church stink of the field had thinned to something he could only find if he went looking for it, and a hundred yards in, it was gone, and the forest had closed over him like water. Spruce and hemlock, mostly, the trunks going straight up into the grey with no branches for the first thirty feet, and snow sitting heavy on the boughs above. Moss on everything. Moss on the trunks, moss on the fallen logs, moss hanging off the low dead branches in long pale-green beards that swung when the wind got in under the canopy. Sword ferns poking up through the snow in clumps, dark green and stiff. The ground underneath was soft even through the frost, years of needles and rotting wood, and it gave under his boots like a mattress.

Everything dripped. Where his heat reached the snow on the low branches as he passed, it let go and fell in wet clumps behind him.

He walked west. He didn't need a compass for it. He'd seen the coast in Bella's head, the road out to the reservation, the long beach, the big rock with the trees on it, and he'd felt the sea the moment he landed in the field, a big cold weight off to the west the way you feel a wall in a dark room. He pointed himself at it and walked.

The two wolves came after him.

He knew exactly where they were without turning round. The sandy one was easy; he was making a racket, crashing through the undergrowth a long way back, stopping every so often to pant. The grey one was harder to hear. She ran low and quiet and she'd stopped about eighty yards behind him, and she stayed at eighty yards, and when he slowed down she slowed down, and when he stopped once to step over a fallen trunk the size of a bus, she stopped too.

He didn't need to hear her. He could feel her. The line ran back from his chest through the trees to her, and he knew where she was the way he knew where his own left hand was with his eyes shut.

That was new. He didn't love it.

He kept walking.

Thirty-odd, Annelise had said. And a Romanian.

He did the count properly, because he'd have to, because somebody in Geneva was going to sit across a table from him on Monday morning with a quill and ask him for names. Aro. Caius. Marcus. The two wives, Sulpicia and the other one, Athenodora, the file said. Jane. Alec. Felix. Demetri. Renata. Chelsea. Afton. Santiago. Heidi. Corin. That was fifteen. The rest of the guard had been names in a file he'd skimmed in a corridor, faces he'd matched to the names as he looked down the line in the snow. He could get most of them. He'd write the ones he couldn't as *unidentified, male* and *unidentified, female* and let the Council make of it what they liked.

He'd given them the order three times. He'd told them in English, nice and slow, in front of witnesses. He'd seen what they did to the blonde woman, Irina, before he ever landed; the smoke from her had still been going up when he arrived. He'd read their file. He'd read the bits of their file that nobody in Geneva liked to talk about, the villages in the fourteen hundreds, the covens in Romania, the children on stakes. When Aro had said *bring him to me*, Harry had already known what came next, because he'd known since Geneva, and he'd been ready for it.

It had gone the way he'd thought it would.

He'd still be writing the report till midnight.

He stepped over a little stream running brown and fast between two banks of snow, and kept going.

The kid.

That one sat worse. He let it. He'd gone through that shield like a burglar and through the kid like a knife, because a field full of frightened vampires had needed to know what she was a lot more urgently than she'd needed to be asked, and he'd do it again tomorrow for the same reason. He'd still made a four-month-old bleed out of her nose. Somebody would have to say sorry for that properly, with more than two words, and that somebody was him. Her mum, too. He'd gone through her mum like a man looking for car keys in a burning house, and he'd meant to, and she'd felt every bit of it.

He'd do better than *sorry, kid.* He didn't know how yet.

The grey wolf was still at eighty yards.

He let himself look at it, then. At the thing in the field. The growl.

It had come up out of him before he'd decided anything. That was the part that he kept turning over, the way you turn over a stone you've tripped on to see what's underneath. He'd been looking at her and she'd snarled at him, properly snarled, with sound, and before the thought *she's snarling at me* had finished forming, his chest had answered for him. He'd heard it come out of his own mouth the same as everybody else in the field had.

It had been a long time since the thing at the bottom of him had done anything without asking first. Years. He'd spent a lot of those years making sure of it.

He went and had a look at it now, walking. Only a little way down, the way you'd lean over a banister to look into a stairwell.

It was up on its feet.

It had been lying down in the field, chin on its paws, watching her. Now it was up and walking along with him in the dark, step for step, with its head turned to look back over its shoulder. Back the way the line went. Back eighty yards to the grey wolf in the trees. Its ears were up. Its tail was moving, slow, side to side.

It wanted to turn round.

"Leave it," Harry said, out loud, to the trees.

It didn't argue. It never argued. It kept walking with him, because it was him and he was walking. But it didn't stop looking back either.

He walked for a while longer with that.

The trees opened, after a bit, onto the bank of a proper river, wide and grey-green and loud, running high and fast with snowmelt off the mountains and rattling the stones on its bed. He stopped on the bank. The water smelled of iron and cold and the inside of the mountains. On the other side, the forest went on, the same, for miles. He'd been walking for most of an hour and the sea was still a long way off, twenty miles at least, he reckoned, and the light was already starting to think about going.

The wolves had stopped behind him. Eighty yards. The sandy one had flopped down in the snow, panting like a bellows.

"Lassie," Harry said, not loudly. He didn't turn round.

Nothing. Then, somewhere back in the trees, a single dry crack, a stick going under a paw.

"I'm hopping the rest. It's twenty-odd miles and I've had a long day." He looked at the river. "Beach. The one with the big rock. You'll find it." He paused. "You too, sunshine."

Somewhere back there, the sandy one made a surprised noise, half whine and half yip.

Harry turned on the spot.

He came out of the squeeze into salt air and the sound of surf.

He'd landed in the trees, which was where he'd aimed for: a stand of wind-bent spruce at the top of a bank, their trunks leaning inland, every one of them, the way trees lean on a coast where the wind only ever comes from one direction. Salal underneath, thick and waxy and waist-high, its leaves shining dark green. He stood in it a moment and listened. Nobody had seen. A car door shut somewhere off to his right, and someone laughed, and the sea went on doing what it was doing, which was enormous.

He pushed out through the salal and down onto gravel.

A car park. A wide gravel lot with puddles in it and a handful of cars, two of them vans with surfboards strapped on top. A row of small timber cabins along the top of the bluff, smoke coming out of one chimney. A sign for a resort. And beyond the edge of the lot, past a low wooden rail, the beach.

He'd seen it in Bella's head. It was bigger in person.

It went on for a mile, maybe more, a long curve of grey-brown sand running away to the south toward a headland under the cloud. At the top of it, between the lot and the sand, lay the driftwood. Whole trees, piled up the length of the beach in a long tangled barricade, bleached silver-grey by salt and sun, their roots sticking up out of the pile like antlers. Some of the trunks were bigger round than he was tall. Beyond the logs, the sand, flat and dark and wet where the last tide had left it. Beyond the sand, the sea.

It was grey-green and furious. The waves came in in long lines out of the haze, one behind another, rearing up white and coming down with a sound he could feel in his feet, and the spray off them hung in the air and made everything a bit soft at the edges. Out to the right, at the north end of the beach, the big rock stood up out of the water like the end of a broken bridge, sheer-sided, two or three hundred feet high, with dark trees standing on its flat top. The river came out to the sea somewhere round the far side of it. Further south, out in the surf, smaller stacks stood up out of the white like teeth.

Two surfers were sitting on their boards out past the break in black wetsuits, rising and falling on the swell, waiting. A woman in a yellow raincoat was walking a black Labrador along the waterline a long way down the beach, a tiny figure, the dog going in and out of the foam.

Nobody looked at him. He was a bloke in a leather jacket in a car park.

There was a bench.

It sat at the edge of the lot where the gravel met the top of the driftwood, between two big bleached logs, facing the water. Plain wood, cedar maybe, silvered grey by the weather like everything else here, with a slatted back and a bit of lichen on one armrest. It was the only bench he could see. It had the best view on the beach.

He went and sat on it.

The wood was cold through his jeans and damp. That lasted about thirty seconds. Steam came up off the slats round him in a faint haze and blew away inland.

There was a small brass plaque screwed to the middle of the backrest. He read it because he read everything. He'd been trained to; you never knew which piece of paper was going to be the one that mattered.

*HARRY CLEARWATER*
*HUSBAND · FATHER · FISHERMAN*
*SIT A WHILE*
*— SUE, LEAH & SETH*

He looked at it for a long moment.

He knew the name. He'd had it go past him in Bella's head, through the fog of her human memories, without stopping on it: her dad, grey in the face at a kitchen table, saying it like it hurt his mouth. *Harry Clearwater. Heart.* Then a bonfire by the sea, at night, old men telling stories, and a woman sitting apart from all of them with short black hair and her arms wrapped round her knees, staring across the fire at a couple on the other side of it with a face like a shut door. Her mum beside her, sitting in a dead man's place on the council. A skinny boy on her other side, too young for any of it, listening with his mouth open.

Leah Clearwater. With a dead dad.

Course it was.

He'd started to get up. He was halfway to standing, one hand on the armrest, before he stopped. He looked at the plaque again.

*Sit a while.*

Somebody'd put that on there on purpose. Somebody had stood in some shop in Port Angeles or wherever and picked those three words out, out of all the words they could have picked, so that people would see it and do it. So that people would sit here, on this bench, and look at this sea, and this man would have been for something.

He sat back down.

He held out his hand and called for the second book.

This one came easier. It was thin and cloth-bound, dark blue gone grey along the spine, the corners soft and the pages foxed brown with damp. Somebody's field notes, typed on a machine with a sticky *e* and bound up afterward by an archivist who'd cared more about keeping them than about how they looked. The title page said: *NOTES ON THE CHANGERS OF THE PACIFIC COAST. W. H. Pennington, Observer, MACUSA Northwestern Field Office. Submitted 1938. Copy to the International Confederation, Geneva, for information.*

The source of Appendix C. Four lines out of however many pages this was. He flicked to the back. Sixty-three pages.

He went back to the start and read.

Pennington had been a careful man with a weakness for adjectives. He'd come out to the coast in the autumn of 1936 to look into reports from the local No-Maj papers of wolves seen on the beaches: three of them, black and grey and brown, *of a size exceeding any wolf recorded on this continent by a factor I hesitate to commit to paper.* He'd committed it to paper anyway, on page four, with a little diagram of a wolf next to a horse. The wolf was bigger.

He'd seen them himself, twice, once at night running the length of this beach, by the sound of it, and once at dawn at the edge of the forest inland, and both times they'd scented him at a quarter of a mile and gone.

They'd appeared, Pennington wrote, that same autumn. In the same weeks *a coven of the yellow-eyed sort*, five of them, had taken up residence in the hills inland. Pennington had been very interested in the yellow eyes. He'd gone to some trouble to establish that these five didn't hunt people, and he'd been as surprised by that as anybody would be, and he'd written three pages on it that Harry skimmed. Doctor, the eldest one. Four younger. *A woman of remarkable beauty with fair hair* who'd looked at Pennington across a street in Port Angeles in a way that had made him leave town for a week.

Harry's mouth moved. Rosalie hadn't changed much, then.

The changers and the cold ones, Pennington had been given to understand, had come to an agreement. *A treaty, whose terms I was not told, save that there is a line, and that neither crosses it.*

Then the legends. Pennington had got them from a fisherman *who had been to school in Tacoma and found me very funny*, and he'd written them down carefully, and to his credit he hadn't tried to improve on them. The people of this coast had come from wolves, the fisherman had told him. The first of them had been made out of wolves by a great changer, long ago. There'd been men among them, chiefs and warriors, who could leave their bodies, go walking about as spirits and come back. And there'd been one chief whose body had been stolen while he was out of it by a man who wanted it, and the chief, with nowhere to go back to, had gone to a wolf in the forest and asked it to share its body with him. And the wolf had let him.

*And when he came again to his people,* Pennington had written, *he came as the wolf, and when he wished it, he was a man again, and he was both from that day: wolf and man, the one in the other. And he did not age while he kept the wolf.*

Harry read that paragraph twice.

Then he turned the page.

There wasn't much more. The fisherman had stopped finding Pennington funny round about then. There was a page of notes on what Pennington could and couldn't detect, which came down to *no magic of the conventional sort; no wand, no core that I can find; the change appears to be in the blood alone, as with the European lines, and is in some fashion called up by the presence of the cold ones*. There was a page of local plants. There was a long digression about a sea lion.

And there was a footnote, on page fifty-eight, in smaller type, at the bottom of a page about something else entirely.

*On my last visit I asked after the wives of the changers, having understood that two of them had married that winter, and quickly. My informant laughed and said that was the wolves' business and nobody else's, and would not be drawn further, and shortly afterward asked me to leave.*

That was it. That was all. Pennington went on to recommend no further contact, on the grounds that *they do not like us any better than they like the cold ones and I do not think we could bear the comparison*, and signed off.

"Cheers, Pennington," Harry said.

The wolves' business.

He sat on Harry Clearwater's bench with the book open on his knee and the surf booming and looked at that line for a while.

Then the line in his chest moved.

It had been pointing back inland since he'd landed, east, twenty miles or so, like a compass needle stuck on a magnet a long way off, and it had been getting shorter. He'd felt it shortening the whole time he'd been reading, a slow steady pull, the way you feel a train getting closer through the rails. Now it swung. It came round from east to north-east to right behind him, up the bank, and it stopped there, and settled, and hummed.

He didn't turn round.

He could hear her now, over the surf. A big body moving very quietly through salal, low down, the waxy leaves whispering against fur. Then stopping. A long breath out through a nose. The creak of joints settling as something heavy lay down on the ground. And a second body after her, much less quiet, panting hard, flopping down next to her with a grunt.

At the top of the bank, in the salal, under the leaning spruce. Sixty yards behind him. Lying down. Watching.

He turned a page.

Down at the waterline, the woman in the yellow raincoat had turned round and was coming back up the beach with her dog. One of the surfers had caught a wave and was coming in on it, crouched, a black shape skating down the face of all that grey water.

The line hummed.

At the bottom of him, the thing in the dark had stopped walking. It had lain down again, the way she had, at the top of the stairs. Its head was up. Its ears were pointed back over its shoulder toward the bank, toward the salal, toward her, and its tail was moving, slow.

He let it.

About forty minutes later, he heard bare feet on gravel.

### V. Jacob

Leah had been quiet for twenty miles.

That was what Jacob noticed first, more than the cold or the distance or the long dark line of the trees going past. Leah was never quiet. In the pack mind, Leah was a radio somebody had left on in the next room, a constant low running commentary of everything she hated and everybody who'd ever done it to her, and Jacob had long since learned to tune it out like traffic. He'd phased again at the edge of the clearing and found her already a long way west, already lying down, Seth beside her, the whole of her pointed down a sandy bank at a man on a bench, like a dog pointing at a bird.

And she hadn't said a word. Not one. Not to him, not to Seth, not to herself.

Then, as he came down off the last ridge above the river with the smell of the sea coming up to meet him, she said one thing.

*He's sitting on Dad's bench.*

Jacob nearly ran into a tree.

He knew the bench. Everybody on the rez knew the bench. Sue had had it put up the summer after, at the top of First Beach where Harry used to stand with his coffee in the mornings and look at the water and decide whether it was worth taking the boat out. Leah had never sat on it. Not once. Jacob knew that because he'd been in her head every night for months when her patrol took her past the end of the lot, and every night she'd run straight past it without turning her head.

*Lee*—

*Don't.*

*He read the plaque*, Seth said. Seth's thoughts were small and careful, the way they got when he was trying not to step on something. *I watched him. He read it and he started to get up, Jake. He was halfway up. Then he read it again and sat back down.*

*Shut UP, Seth.*

*I'm just saying what he—*

*I know what he did. I was there. I'm lying right here.* A pause, ragged. *He's reading. He's sitting on my dad's bench reading a book.*

Jacob didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. He ran.

He phased at the top of the road, in the trees behind the resort, where the spruce came down close to the gravel and nobody could see. The pack mind went out of his head like somebody had pulled a plug, all of them at once, Leah and Seth and the distant hum of Quil and Embry trailing the Cullens home, and then it was just him. His own head. Quiet.

He never knew whether he loved that or hated it. Today he didn't have time to decide.

He pulled the cutoffs on, wound the cord round his wrist, and walked out onto the gravel.

A guy he half-knew from the rez was coming up from the beach with a board under his arm and his wetsuit peeled down to his waist, steaming, and he gave Jacob the look everybody on the rez gave Jacob these days, barefoot and shirtless in January, the look that went *I'm not going to ask*. Jacob lifted his chin at him. The guy lifted his back and kept walking. Two cars down, a woman in a yellow raincoat was trying to get a wet black Lab into the back of a Subaru, and the Lab didn't want to go.

Nobody else. Just the wind and the surf, and the gulls, and the bench.

The man was sitting with one ankle crossed over his knee and a thin blue book open on his thigh, and there was steam coming up off the wooden slats all round him in a faint haze, the way it came up off the hood of a car that had just been driven. He'd taken his jacket off and laid it along the back of the bench beside him. Green flannel shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbow. His forearms were bare to the wind and he looked about as cold as Jacob felt, which was not at all.

Jacob stopped about ten feet away. Behind him, up the bank, he knew without looking exactly where Leah was. He didn't look.

The wind came round off the sea and brought the man to him. Pine. Wet earth. Stone. And under it, so much clearer here than in the field with the vampire stink everywhere, the thing they'd all smelled. Musk and fur and frost. It went into Jacob's nose and straight down the back of his neck, and the skin there prickled all the way to his shoulders like he still had a ruff to raise.

The man didn't look up from the book.

"Trousers," he said.

"Shorts."

"They'll do." He turned a page. "Sit down, you're blocking my light."

Jacob looked up at the sky. It was the colour of a dirty sink. "There's no light."

"Then you're blocking what there is of it."

Jacob didn't sit. He looked at the bench. At the plaque, the little brass rectangle in the middle of the backrest, just above the man's shoulder. He didn't need to read it. He'd been at the thing when Sue unveiled it, standing at the back with his dad's wheelchair, watching Leah stand at the edge of the lot with her arms folded and not come any closer.

"That's her dad's bench," he said.

The man closed the book on his thumb. He looked at Jacob for the first time.

"I know," he said. "I read it."

"And you're still sitting on it."

"It says sit a while." He said it flatly, without any weight on it at all. Then he was quiet for a second. "If she wants me off it, she can come down here and say so. She's up there." He didn't turn his head toward the bank. "Been there forty minutes. Her brother's with her. The sandy one."

"Seth."

"Seth. Looks about twelve."

"He's fifteen."

The man looked at him. "Bloody hell."

"Yeah, well." Jacob sat down on the end of the nearest drift log, a huge bleached trunk at right angles to the bench, close enough to talk and far enough that he didn't have to be close. The wood was damp and gritty with sand. "You said an hour. I've got until it gets dark. I told Nessie I'd be back before dark."

"You promised her that."

"Yeah."

The man nodded slowly, as if he were writing it down somewhere. "Then we'll talk fast."

He put the book down on the bench beside him, on top of his jacket. The cover said something about changers and the Pacific coast. Jacob didn't let himself read any more of it than that.

"Imprinting," the man said. "Tell me how it works."

Jacob's jaw tightened. "Why?"

"Because I asked." He sat back. "I've seen it twice today from the outside, in two heads, and in both of them it looks like a lad coming round a door with murder on his face and going soft as butter. That's what it looks like. I want to know what it is. And I want it from somebody it's happened to."

Jacob looked at the sea. A wave came down on the sand like a building falling over, and the foam ran up white almost to the logs and slid back.

"You see her," he said. "That's it. That's how it works. You see her the first time after you've phased, and it happens, right then. You don't pick it. You don't even know it's coming. It doesn't happen to everybody. Most guys never do. Sam did. Jared. Paul." He hesitated. "Quil."

"Your mate Quil. With the two-year-old."

"Claire. Yeah." Jacob's hands closed on the edge of the log. "It's not like that."

"You keep saying that. You lot. Every head I've been in today, somebody's saying *it's not like that.*" The man tilted his head. "So tell me what it's like."

Jacob opened his mouth and found he didn't have it.

He'd explained it before. He'd explained it to Bella, twice, the first time on this beach, a hundred yards from here, when it was Sam and Emily he was explaining, when he'd still thought it was the creepiest thing he'd ever heard of. He'd explained it to Charlie, kind of, in a version with all the parts that mattered taken out. He'd explained it to Rosalie, who had called it grooming and then thrown a fork at him. He'd explained it to himself, a hundred times, at three in the morning on the floor beside Nessie's cot, listening to her breathe.

He had never once explained it to somebody who just sat there and waited.

"Everybody says gravity," he said finally. "It's like gravity moves. Like the earth lets go of you and she's the thing holding you down." He shook his head. "That's what Sam said and that's what I said and it's true, but it's the version you say at bonfires. That's the nice version."

"Give me the other one."

"The other one is—" Jacob stopped. Started again. "You know how there's stuff that holds you to your life? Your dad. Your friends. Your house, your pack, the stuff you want to do. Your car. The girl you've been in love with for a year and a half who married somebody else." His voice didn't do anything when he said that. He was proud of it. "It's like all of that's tied to you with cables. And when it happens, they all let go. All at once. Snap, snap, snap, all of them, and you feel yourself start to fall. And then there's one left. Just one. And it's her. And she's the only thing in the whole world keeping you from falling off of it."

The man was quiet. He was looking at Jacob very steadily.

"That's how it feels?"

"That's how it feels at first. The first second." Jacob looked away. "After that it feels like— I'd walked into that room to kill her. You saw. I thought she'd killed Bella. I thought Bella was dead on that table and the thing that did it was lying there in Rosalie's arms, and I was going to tear it apart and let Edward kill me for it, and I didn't care. I'd been hanging off something for a year and a half and I was done. I was going to let go." He swallowed. "And I looked at her. And it was like somebody unhooked me. Like I'd been hanging off a hook for eighteen months by my ribs and somebody reached up and lifted me off it and set me down on the ground." He let out a breath. "That's how it feels. That's the true one."

The man didn't say anything for a while.

A gull came down on the end of the next log, looked at them both with one yellow eye, and decided against it.

"And then," the man said.

"And then you're whatever she needs." Jacob shrugged, a short jerky movement. "Right now she needs a bodyguard. A big brother. A pony." He nearly smiled. "She needs somebody to give her piggyback rides and let her bite him when she's teething. When she's older, she'll need a friend. Whatever she needs, that's what you are. You don't get to want anything for yourself. You don't *want* to want anything. You want her to be happy and safe and that's it, that's all of it."

"And when she's grown."

There it was. Jacob had been waiting for it since the field. He felt it land, and he felt his shoulders come up, and he made them go back down.

"Then whatever she wants," he said.

"Whatever she wants." The man said it back slowly. "Or whatever the pull wants."

"It's the same thing. That's the whole point." Jacob leaned forward. "I *can't* want anything she doesn't. I couldn't hurt her if I tried. I couldn't make her do anything she didn't want to do. I couldn't even ask. If she wants me to be her brother for the rest of my life, I'll be her brother for the rest of my life, and I'll be happy about it. I'll be glad. That's what it is. That's what it does."

"Say that again. The last bit."

Jacob stared at him. "If she wants me to be her brother forever, I'll be her brother forever, and I'll be happy."

The man looked at him for a long moment.

"Right," he said. "Then this'll be easy." He uncrossed his legs and leaned forward, forearms on his knees, so they were more or less eye to eye across the gap. "When she's grown, and I mean grown, a proper adult with a life she picked for herself, not seven years old and looking twenty because her body's in a hurry, she chooses. You don't. The pull doesn't. And if she chooses somebody else, you stand at the back of the church and you clap."

Heat went up the back of Jacob's neck so fast he felt his hands start to shake. He clamped them on his knees.

"You think I'd—"

"I think you're a teenager," said the man, "and you got tied to a baby by something nobody round here understands, on the worst morning of your life, and every grown-up you know has decided it's sweet." His voice didn't go up. It didn't go anywhere. "I've been in her head, Jacob. You're the floor she stands on. You're in every room. You sleep on the floor next to her cot. You've never done one thing in four months that I'd want to have words with you about, and I looked." A pause. "I've got no complaints about how you've been with her. None."

Jacob's hands were still shaking. He looked down at them.

"I want the promise anyway," the man said.

The surf came in. The surf went out.

Jacob thought about Rosalie throwing the fork. About Bella going for his throat on the lawn and Seth getting between them, and the sound Seth's collarbone made. About Charlie's face. About his dad, who had never said one word about it, not one, and who'd looked at him across the kitchen table for a long time the morning after and then gone back to his newspaper. About Nessie asleep with her fist in his hair.

"I promise," he said.

"Say the whole thing."

"When she's grown. Properly. She chooses." He made himself look up. "And if she picks somebody else, I stand at the back and I clap."

"Good lad."

Jacob's lip came up off his teeth before he could stop it. The man watched it happen and didn't react, except that one corner of his mouth moved slightly, and Jacob had the very strong feeling that he'd been expecting exactly that and had said *good lad* on purpose to see it.

"How old are you?" the man asked.

"Seventeen."

The man didn't say anything to that. He looked at the sea for a bit.

"The kid," he said. "Does she feel it? From her end."

Jacob blinked. "What?"

"The imprint. The line, the cable, the gravity, whatever. Does she feel it on her side? When you're in the room. When you go."

"No." He didn't even have to think about it. "No. She loves me. She's a kid, she loves the guy who gives her piggyback rides and lets her bite him. She'd love me anyway. That's just her. The imprint's ours. It's on our side."

"What about the others. Emily. The one the silver one's got."

"Rachel. My sister." Jacob scowled; that still got him, sometimes. "No. None of them. They don't feel anything like it. Emily told Sam to get lost for weeks. She chose him in the end, but she *chose*. Kim, Rachel, the same. Claire's two, the only thing she feels about Quil is he's the best at the swings." He spread his hands. "That's the whole point. They get to choose. We don't."

"So it goes one way."

"Yeah. One way. Always."

The man nodded slowly.

He looked out at the water. The surfer who'd been out past the break had caught one last wave and ridden it all the way in, and now he was walking up the sand with his board under his arm, and the light was getting lower and yellower behind the haze out to sea. The big rock at the north end of the beach had gone dark against it.

"Huh," the man said.

He lifted his right hand and put two fingers against the middle of his chest. The same place he'd tapped in the field. He tapped twice.

"Then somebody's got their wires crossed, mate," he said. "Because I've got something in here."

Jacob looked at the hand.

He looked at the man's face.

"What?" he said.

"Line." The man kept his fingers where they were. "Comes in here. Goes down a long way. Further than you'd think." He lowered his hand. "Been there since I looked at her. In the field. I felt it go in. Put it down to jet lag."

"That's—" Jacob heard his own voice go up and couldn't stop it. "That doesn't happen. That *can't*—"

"You lot keep saying that." The man looked at him, mild as milk. "You're sitting on a log in your pants in January, son, talking to a wizard about a vampire's baby. I'd go easy on *can't.*"

Jacob opened his mouth, and shut it.

The growl.

He'd heard it in the field, felt it, every wolf on that field had felt it. He'd felt his own ears go back. He'd smelled it on the wind in the first ten seconds and every second since and he was smelling it right now, the musk under the pine, *wolf*, so close to his own smell that if he'd come across it on a trail in the forest he'd have stopped and checked his own tracks to see if he'd been there before.

"You growled at her," Jacob said slowly. "Back there."

"I had a cold."

"You growled at her and every wolf in the clearing heard it. Sam heard it. Sam *stood still* for it, and Sam doesn't stand still for anybody. My ears went back." He'd never in his life have admitted that to anyone who wasn't in his head. It came out of him anyway. "You smell like us. You smell like a—"

He stopped.

"What are you?" Jacob said.

The man looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the beach. The woman with the Lab had finally got it into the Subaru and was backing out of her space. The surfer with the board was loading it onto a van, his back to them. Out on the sand, the last of the light was going gold and flat, the tide pulling back off the beach and leaving it shining.

"Later," he said.

Jacob didn't push. He wasn't stupid. And he'd suddenly realised the thing he should have realised the second the man tapped his chest, the thing that made the back of his neck go cold in a way the January wind never could.

He turned his head and looked up the bank.

Sixty yards up, under the leaning spruce, the salal was dark and thick and waist-high and perfectly still. He couldn't see her. He didn't need to.

"She heard that," he said.

"I know."

"She heard all of it. Wolf ears. She heard every word."

"I know, Jacob," the man said quietly.

It was the first time he'd used Jacob's name.

Up on the bank, at the top of the salal, something big and grey stood up. Jacob saw the leaves shiver all along the top of the bank, a long ripple going sideways as she turned. Then she was going away from them, back into the trees under the spruce, and a second shape went after her, smaller and sandy, and Jacob heard, faintly, over the surf, Seth's high anxious whine.

Then nothing.

Then, from somewhere back in the trees, a shudder in the air like a sheet cracking on a line.

Jacob let out a long breath.

"She's coming down," he said.

The man reached over to the back of the bench, picked up his leather jacket, and stood up off Harry Clearwater's bench, and put it on.

### VI. Leah

She'd heard every word.

Forty minutes in the salal with her chin on her paws and the waxy leaves pressing on her back and the cold coming up out of the ground into her belly, and she'd heard every single word. The pages turning. The gulls. Jacob's bare feet on the gravel. Jacob telling a stranger, in words, out loud, the thing she'd only ever seen in his head: *it was like somebody unhooked me.* She'd been inside Jacob's head the morning it happened, she'd felt every second of it with him, and she'd still never heard him say it like that. Like it was a good thing. Like he'd been grateful.

She'd heard the man make him promise. She'd felt something about that she didn't have a name for and didn't want one.

And then she'd heard the other thing.

*Then somebody's got their wires crossed, mate. Because I've got something in here.*

The world had done something. She didn't know what. She'd been lying flat on the ground and she was still lying flat on the ground, and the spruce was still leaning over her and the surf was still booming at the bottom of the bank, and everything had tipped about two inches to one side and stayed there.

*Lee*, Seth said. Barely a whisper, in here. *Lee. He feels it.*

She didn't say anything.

*He feels it. It goes both ways. Lee, that's never—nobody's ever—*

*I heard him.*

*Are you okay?*

She didn't know how to answer that, so she got up.

She went back under the trees with Seth scrambling after her, back where the spruce trunks stood close and the ground was soft with needles and nobody could see from the road, and she stopped there and let go of the wolf.

It always hurt a bit, coming back. Nobody talked about that. The shrinking. The fur going in, the bones pulling shorter, the cold suddenly getting at bare skin all at once like a bucket of water. She stood up on two feet in the half-dark under the trees and shook, once, all over, and the cold slid off her the way it always did because she ran at a hundred and eight degrees and the cold could go to hell.

The cord was round her ankle where it always was. She got it off and shook out the bundle, black tank top, cutoffs, and dragged them on, fast, without looking at anything. Her hair had grown out a bit. It was still too short to get in her eyes. Good.

Seth was standing in front of her, sandy and huge and anxious, his ears going back and forth.

"Stay here," she said. Her voice came out rough. She hadn't used it in hours. "Stay phased."

He whined.

"Jacob said eyes on him. So keep your eyes on him." She pointed at the bank. "Go lie down where we were. If he does anything—"

Seth tipped his big head at her. *Like what?*

He couldn't hear her answer in his head anymore. She answered anyway.

"I don't know, Seth. Anything." She turned away. "Howl."

She went down the bank.

The salal came up to her waist and she shoved through it, the leaves slapping her bare legs, and slid the last few feet down onto the gravel on her heels. The car park was nearly empty. One van, with its engine running and a board on the roof. The resort cabins all along the bluff with their windows going yellow one by one as the light went. And down at the end of the lot, at the edge of the driftwood, the bench.

The bench was empty.

She'd had a sentence ready the whole way down the bank. *Get off my dad's bench.* Four words. She'd been going to say them before she said anything else, before she let him say anything, and watch his face when she did.

He was standing beside it. His jacket was on. His hands were in the pockets. He'd stood up off the bench before she came down, and he'd moved a couple of steps to one side of it so it was empty, the whole length of it, the brass plaque on the backrest catching the last of the light, and he was standing there next to it looking at her coming across the gravel like he'd been waiting for a bus that was late.

Jacob was sitting on a drift log a few yards off with his elbows on his knees. He looked at her and looked away. Good.

She stopped in front of the man. Six feet off. Close enough to hit.

Up close, he was big. She'd known that from across the field, but from across the field everybody'd been big. He had about four inches on her and probably eighty pounds, and he was just standing there, with the wind off the sea pushing his hair around and a thin haze of steam coming off the shoulders of his jacket. Green eyes. Still green. Same as in the field. Looking at her face like he was reading it.

The line hummed so loud she was surprised Jacob couldn't hear it.

"You got off it," she said.

He glanced at the bench. "Seemed best."

"I was going to tell you to."

"I know. I'd have got off it then." He shrugged slightly. "Saved you the trouble."

She hated that. She hated that he'd thought about it. She stood there and hated it for a second, and then she let it go, because it wasn't what she'd come down here for.

"You feel it," she said.

"Yeah."

"Since when."

"Since I looked at you." He didn't look away while he said it. "In the field. Felt it go in, right here." Two fingers to his sternum, a short tap. "Thought it was jet lag. Then I heard you."

"Heard me."

"That noise you made."

Her face went hot. She'd been hoping, a small stupid part of her, that nobody outside the pack had heard that. That it'd got lost under the wind.

"What's it feel like?" she said, before she could stop herself. "Your end."

He thought about it. She could see him doing it, actually thinking, his eyes going somewhere off to the left.

"Like a wire," he said. "Pulled tight. Hums when you're close. Pulls a bit when you're not. Knew where you were the whole way through the woods without looking." He paused. "Went a bit loud when you snarled at me."

"That wasn't a cold."

"No."

"Break it."

He didn't say anything.

"You're a wizard." The words were coming out of her faster now, hard and flat, and she let them. "You snapped your fingers and they *burned*, all of them, the little blonde one and the big one and the creepy old ones, they just went up. You walked through Bella's shield like it was a screen door. You went into that baby's head and out again. So break it. Cut it. Snap your fingers. Whatever you do. Get it out of me."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"I had a look at it," he said. "In the field, when I found it. My end. It doesn't stop at the heart, Lassie. It goes in here and it goes down. A long way. Past where my magic sits. Past where I keep anything." His voice didn't change; it was the same flat, tired, ordinary voice. "I don't know what's at your end. I'd be cutting blind."

"So cut blind."

"No."

"*Why not?*"

"Because I've had a look at what's at my end," he said, "and if I cut something that goes that deep and I get it wrong, one of us doesn't get up off this beach. Maybe both of us. And I'm not doing that to you on a guess."

"I don't *care*."

"I do."

It stopped her. Two words, and it stopped her dead, the way the growl had stopped her snarl in the field, and she stood there with her mouth open and nothing coming out of it.

Behind her, the van's engine changed note. Tyres on gravel. It was pulling out.

"I said," Leah heard herself say, and her voice had gone strange, low and bitter and much too steady, "if it ever happened to me, I'd walk into the sea."

She hadn't meant to say it. She'd said it in the pack mind a dozen times at three in the morning, but she'd never said it with her mouth, to a person, out loud, and now it was out there in the wind between them and she couldn't get it back.

He turned his head and looked at the sea.

It was going dark. The waves were coming in grey and heavy out of the haze, one after another, rearing and falling, and the light underneath the cloud on the horizon had gone a thin dirty orange, and the big rock at the end of the beach was black against it. He looked at it for a long time. Long enough that she started to think he wasn't going to answer.

Then he looked back at her.

"Don't," he said.

"Don't tell me what to—"

"I'm asking." He hadn't raised his voice. "Don't. Give me time. If there's a way out of this that isn't the sea, I'll find it. Properly. I'll take it apart in a room with the door shut and I'll find out how it comes out without killing either of us. And if you still want it gone when I've found it, I'll cut it out of you myself, and I'll do it right." He paused. "Not today. Today I've burned thirty-odd people and been through two heads I'd no business in and I'm knackered. I'm not cutting anything else today that's got somebody on the other end of it."

"And if there's no way out?"

"Then you can hate me in person for a bit," he said. "Beats the sea."

She stared at him.

She wanted to hit him. She wanted to hit him so badly her hands were shaking with it. She wanted to scream at him that he didn't get to *ask* her anything, that nobody had asked her, that the last man who'd stood in front of her and told her he was sorry and that he'd find a way to make it right had gone home that same night and asked her cousin to marry him.

She didn't. She stood there with her hands shaking.

And the line hummed, steady, the whole time, like it didn't care what either of them had said.

"Why'd you call me Lassie," she said finally.

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "You're a lass. You're a dog. Saves time."

"I'm a *wolf.*"

"I know you are."

"You smell like one," Leah said.

The thing at the corner of his mouth went away.

"You smelled like one in the field. Under the trees. We all smelled it, the whole pack, Sam's lot too. You smell like one right now." She took a step toward him without meaning to. "You *growled* at me. You growled and my ears went flat and my tail went down and I have never, *ever*—" She bit it off. "Jacob asked you. I heard him. You said later." She lifted her chin. "It's later. What are you?"

He looked at her for a long moment.

Then he looked past her at the car park.

The van had gone. The Subaru had gone. The lot was empty except for a scatter of puddles going pink and grey with the sky in them, and the cabins with their yellow windows. He looked up at the cabins. He looked along the beach, both ways, the long curve of dark sand going away to the south under the cloud, empty to the headland.

Something went across the car park.

She felt it go over her skin like a breath. A shiver in the air, like heat haze off a road in August, rolling outward from him in every direction, over the gravel and the logs and the sand and up the bank to the cabins. Then it was gone. Up at the top of the road, a pair of headlights that had just been turning in toward the lot stopped. Sat there for a second. Then backed out again and went away the way they'd come.

"Muggles," he said, by way of explanation, which explained nothing.

He walked past her, down between two of the big drift logs, onto the sand.

Jacob was on his feet. She hadn't heard him get up.

The man walked out onto the beach about ten yards, onto the flat dark sand where the tide had gone back, and turned round to face them, with the sea behind him and the orange light low down under the cloud at his back. He took his hands out of his pockets. He looked at her.

"Right," he said. "Don't scream."

"I don't *scream*—"

He went.

There was no shaking. That was the first thing her brain caught on, afterward, when she was trying to put it back together. When the pack phased there was always a shake first, a shudder that started in the spine and went out to the hands, the heat going up and up until the skin couldn't hold it, and then the burst. He stood there on the sand with his hands at his sides, perfectly still, and the outline of him poured outward and downward all at once, dark, the way ink goes when you drop it into a glass of water. One second there was a man in a leather jacket. The next second the space where he'd been was full of something else, and it kept on filling, outward and upward, and it didn't stop when she thought it was going to stop.

It stood on the sand in front of her and she had to tip her head back to see its eyes.

Wolf.

Black. Black like the inside of a closed fist. The wind came in off the sea and pushed into the fur along its shoulders and parted it, and underneath the black there was a dark charcoal grey, and then the wind dropped and it closed over again. Its shoulder was level with where the man's head had been. Higher. Its head was higher than that, held up on a neck as thick as her whole body, and it was looking down at her, and its eyes were green. The same green. Exactly the same.

It was long. It went on and on. From the end of its nose to the tip of its tail it was as long as Jacob's Rabbit, nose to bumper, and she knew exactly how long that was because she'd helped him push it out of a ditch.

It was standing on wet sand, and its front paws, each of them about the size of a manhole cover, had barely marked it. A thing that size should have sunk to the ankle. The prints round its feet were no deeper than her own would have been.

It hadn't made a sound. Not one. Not a crunch, not a creak, not a breath.

Behind her, Jacob made a noise like he'd been punched.

Her body went wild.

The wolf in her came up all at once, right up under her skin, so fast and so hard that her arms started to shake and her vision flickered at the edges, heat pouring up her spine, the burst right there, right *there*. She locked her knees. She locked her jaw. She stood on the gravel with her fists clenched at her sides and her whole body vibrating like a struck bell and she held it, she *held* it, she'd been holding things in for two years and she was good at it, she was the best of all of them at it, and she held. Out of the corner of her eye she could see Jacob doing the same thing, bent half over, both hands braced on his knees, sweat standing out on his back in the cold.

Up the bank in the salal, Seth yelped. Just once. Then nothing.

The black wolf watched her hold it. It didn't move.

Then it spoke.

"That's my end of it," it said.

It was his voice. That was the part she couldn't get past. It was his voice, the same accent, the same dry flat tiredness, the words coming out clean and clear past those teeth, every consonant where it should be. But underneath the words there was a rumble, very low, threaded through every one of them, like a big engine idling under a floor. She could feel it in the sand. She could feel it in the line.

The line was so loud now she couldn't hear the surf.

"You're a dog," Leah said. It was the only thing that came out.

The green eyes looked at her steadily.

"Wolf, love," it said.

Jacob made another noise. It might have been a laugh. It might have been something else.

Leah walked forward.

She didn't decide to. She was standing on the gravel and then she was walking down between the two logs onto the sand, and her bare feet were cold and wet and she didn't care, and she walked across the ten yards of flat dark beach to where the black wolf stood with the sea behind it, and she stopped in front of it, close. So close she had to tip her head right back.

It lowered its head.

Slowly. All the way down, that huge head on that huge neck, until its eyes were level with hers, a foot away, and she could feel its breath on her face, and it smelled of pine and wet earth and the cold inside of stone. And under it, so close now that there was nothing else, the musk and the fur and the frost. The same family of smell as hers. As Seth's. As every wolf she'd ever run with. The smell she'd woken up to in her own fur every morning for two years and never once thought of as belonging to anybody but them.

She looked into the green eyes and the line went *taut*.

And for one second, less than a second, she felt something come back down it.

From his end. No words came with it; it was nothing like the pack mind. A feeling, coming up the line from somewhere very deep and very dark and very big, the way you feel a truck go past through the floor of a house. And the feeling was calm. It was calm, and interested, and it had its ears up, and it had been lying in the dark at the bottom of him all afternoon looking up the line at her and going *oh*.

The same *oh*. Her wolf's *oh*. From the other end.

She stood there on the sand and shook.

"Does it," she said, and her voice cracked, and she hated that, and went on anyway. "Your end. Does it feel like mine?"

"Don't know what yours feels like," said the wolf.

"Like being gutted." It came out of her raw. "Like everything that ever held me up got cut at once and I started to fall. And then caught. On one thing." She swallowed. "On you. Like a fish on a hook."

The wolf was quiet. Its eyes didn't leave hers.

"Mine felt like something sitting up," it said. "Down at the bottom. Something that'd been lying down a long time. Sitting up and looking."

The surf came in. The surf went out.

"At me," said Leah.

"At you."

She didn't know what her face was doing. She didn't want to know. She stepped back, one step, two, and the wolf lifted its head again, and she stood there on the wet sand with her arms wrapped round herself and stared up at it.

Then the outline of it went in.

It went the way it had come out, all at once, the dark pulling back into itself like ink running backward, folding down and in until there was a man standing on the sand again in a leather jacket and a green flannel shirt and jeans and scuffed boots, with his hands in his pockets, exactly as he'd been.

Exactly.

The jacket. The shirt. The boots. Not a tear in any of it. Not a button gone. The collar of his flannel was folded down just the way it had been folded down a minute ago.

Leah stared at the jacket.

"Your clothes," she said.

He glanced down at himself. "What about them?"

"They came back."

"Yeah. They do that."

"I've been tying shorts to my *leg*," Leah said, "for *two years*."

He looked at her for a second.

"Sounds rubbish," he said.

Behind her, Jacob sat down hard on the drift log and put his face in his hands, and his shoulders started to shake, and this time it was definitely laughing. It was the high, cracked, helpless kind, the kind that comes after a day like this one when there's nothing else left to do, and once he'd started he couldn't stop.

Leah didn't laugh. She stood there with her arms round herself on the cold sand and her heart going like she'd run the whole twenty miles again. She didn't laugh. But something at the corner of her own mouth moved, and she felt it move, and she was so shocked by it that she turned her face away toward the sea so he wouldn't see.

He saw. She knew he saw. He didn't say anything.

Out to the west, under the edge of the cloud, the sun was going into the water. A thin line of red right on the horizon, flat and burning, and then less of it, and less.

He looked at it.

"Sundown," he said. "I'm meant to be at the doctor's house. Team'll be landing about now and somebody has to stop the Healer fainting." He looked at Jacob. "And you promised her before dark."

Jacob's head came up out of his hands. He looked at the sky. "Shit."

"Go on."

Jacob got up. He looked at Leah, a long look, with the laugh still on his face and something else under it. Then he looked at the man.

"This isn't over," he said. "The— whatever you are. Later."

"Later," the man agreed.

Jacob went up the bank at a run. A few seconds after he went into the trees, Leah heard the shudder and the crack and then the long thud of big paws going away east, fast.

It was just them.

"Beings bloke's going to want to talk to your lot," the man said. "Both packs. Tomorrow, probably. Somebody'll have to tell the big black one."

"Sam."

"Sam."

"So tell him."

"Was going to ask you to."

She looked at him. "Why me?"

He considered it.

"Because you'd enjoy it," he said.

She opened her mouth. Shut it.

He wasn't wrong. That was the worst part. She thought about walking up to Sam Uley's door in the morning and telling him that the Confederation of Whatever wanted to see him and his whole pack at a time of *her* choosing, and something deep in her chest that had nothing to do with the line went very still and very pleased.

"Fine," she said.

"Ta."

He turned. Then he stopped, half-turned, and looked back past her, up at the bench at the top of the beach, sitting empty between its two big logs with the brass plaque dull now that the light was gone.

He didn't say anything about it. He looked at it for a second, and then he looked at her, and then he turned round on the spot, and there was a crack like a gunshot that went out across the empty beach and came back off the big rock, and he was gone.

The line went with him.

She felt it stretch. Out, east, inland, fast, ten miles, fifteen, in a single heartbeat, faster than anything she'd ever felt move, and she braced for it to snap. It didn't snap. It went out to the end of wherever he'd gone, a long way off now, somewhere up in the hills past Forks where the Cullens' big glass house sat in the trees, and it stopped there. And held. And hummed, very faintly now, like a phone line with nobody talking on it.

Leah stood on the sand.

After a while, she heard Seth come down the bank. He'd phased back. She heard his bare feet on the gravel and the scuff of him pulling his shorts up as he came, and then he was standing beside her, skinny and shivering, not from cold, his arms wrapped round himself the same way hers were.

"Lee," he said.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

He was quiet for a second.

"He got off Dad's bench," Seth said. "Before you came down. As soon as you got up out of the salal. He got up and he moved out of the way."

"I know."

"I'm just saying."

"I *know*, Seth."

The last of the red went into the sea. The beach went grey, and then blue, and the waves came in white out of the dark, and the cabins up on the bluff were the only lights anywhere.

Leah turned round and walked back up the sand, between the two drift logs, onto the gravel.

She stood in front of the bench.

She hadn't been this close to it since the day her mum unveiled it, the summer after, when she'd stood at the far edge of the lot with her arms folded and watched everybody else go up and touch the plaque and hadn't gone. She'd run past it every night since. Every single night. She'd never looked at it.

*Sit a while.*

The wood was dry, and it still held a little heat.

She sat down on it.

Seth sat down next to her after a minute, carefully, like the bench might break. Neither of them said anything. The sea went on doing what it did. Somewhere a long way inland, up in the hills, at the end of a line she could feel without trying, a man who turned into a wolf the size of a car was walking into a house full of vampires to do paperwork.

She'd hate him tomorrow. She'd already started the list.

But she sat on her father's bench in the dark, for the first time, and the line in her chest held, and for a little while, she didn't spend all of it being angry.
