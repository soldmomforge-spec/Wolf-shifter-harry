# Tethered

## Chapter Two: Wires Crossed

### I. Carlisle

The snarl held.

It made no sound at all. The grey wolf stood ten feet from the young man with her lips pulled back to the gumline and every tooth in her head on show, and she didn't let it drop. Her breath came out in short hard gusts that smoked and tore away on the wind. Her claws were sunk to the root in the frozen turf under the snow. Her whole long body was bent like a drawn bow, weight back on her haunches, shoulders low, head thrust forward, and Carlisle, who had spent three and a half centuries learning to read bodies whose faces could not be trusted, could not tell whether she meant to lunge or to run.

He suspected she didn't know either.

The young man looked at her a little longer. Then he looked away from her, up at the low grey lid of cloud, the way a man glances at the sky outside a shop door to decide whether it's worth going back for an umbrella. He took his right hand out of his jacket pocket and held it out at waist height, palm up, fingers loose, like somebody waiting to be passed something.

Something was.

There was no crack this time, and no light. His hand was empty, and then a book lay across it.

It was enormous. Carlisle had owned books since before there was a lending library in England. He had handled monastery Bibles still chained to their lecterns and atlases so heavy they needed two men and a table, and he had never seen anything quite like this. It stood the better part of two feet tall and was as thick as a man's hand was long, bound in leather gone the grey-brown of old bark, with tarnished brass at the corners and a heavy brass clasp across the fore-edge. A thing that size should have dragged the young man's arm toward the ground. His arm didn't so much as dip. He shifted the book up onto his forearm the way a waiter carries a tray, thumbed the clasp, and it sprang open with a small dry click that Carlisle heard from thirty feet away.

Behind him, very quietly, Emmett said, "Is he reading?"

"Shut up, Emmett," said Rosalie.

"I'm only asking. Is he? Because that looks like reading."

The gilt on the spine had mostly worn away, but Carlisle's eyes did not need much. ON THE BINDINGS OF SOULS, it said, in tall capitals rubbed down to the colour of weak tea by a great many hands. Under that, smaller: *With Remarks upon the Vows, Debts, Pacts and Bonds of the Changeable Peoples.* At the foot of the spine there was a device stamped blind into the leather, and Carlisle found that he could not look at it. His eyes kept slipping off it, the way a tired man's eyes slip off a word he has read four times without taking in. Carlisle had not been tired since 1663.

The young man licked his thumb and turned a page.

Then another.

The grey wolf's lips stayed back off her teeth. The young man never once looked at her. He stood in his circle of slush with that monstrous book open along his arm and read, turning pages at the pace of a man skimming the morning paper for the one story he cares about. Now and then his eyes stopped and went back up a few lines, and one corner of his mouth flattened, and he turned the page.

Carlisle realised he had taken a step toward him, and stopped.

He knew the feeling. He had had it in Leiden and in Padua and in the back rooms of booksellers along the canals of Amsterdam, three hundred years of it: the stupid old ache of the scholar in front of a book he has not read. And here it was again, in a snowfield thick with the stink of burnt vampire, for a book a man had pulled out of the empty air ten minutes after he had erased the Volturi with a snap of his fingers. Carlisle stood there and wanted to read it over his shoulder.

He made himself turn around.

His family was where he had left them. Esme first. She had come up two paces behind him and stopped there, close enough to touch, and she kept her hands at her sides and looked at him, and he let her see that he was all right, which was very nearly true. Beyond her, Edward knelt in the slush with Bella held against his chest. Bella had stopped shaking. That was almost worse. She sat utterly still in the circle of his arms with her eyes on the young man and her face set into something white and hard as a knuckle. Emmett stood over both of them with his arms folded and his weight back on his heels, which for Emmett was a posture of heroic restraint.

And at the edge of the wolves, Rosalie, crouched in the snow against Jacob's russet flank with one hand flat on Renesmee's knee.

The bleeding had stopped. Rosalie had wiped it away with the cuff of her own sweater, and there was a rust-dark smear on the pale grey wool. Renesmee had stopped crying out loud. She was hiccupping instead, small hitching breaths that went right through her, and her eyes were fixed on the young man with the frozen, total attention of a field mouse watching a hawk turn overhead.

Carlisle wanted, very badly, to go to her. To tip her chin up and look at her pupils, to lay two fingers under her jaw and count. To ask her where it hurt. He had been her physician for every one of her days. He had measured her every morning and charted the frightening climb of her growth on graph paper pinned to the wall of his study, and the part of him that had chosen medicine over hunger, a long time ago, was telling him very clearly that a child had been hurt and he was standing in a field looking at a book.

"Carlisle." Edward didn't lift his head from Bella's hair. He pitched it so low only their own kind would hear. "It's Leah."

"I know." He had known from the moment the grey wolf walked out of the line. He had seen that stare twice before.

"You don't. Not all of it." Edward's eyes came up. They were still black. "She's trying to kill him. She's been trying for over a minute. She keeps telling herself to go for his throat, and her body won't do it. She can't make it lift a paw." A pause. "And they can smell wolf on him. Under the pine. All of them. Jacob's pack has been arguing about it since he arrived. Sam's will be the same."

Carlisle breathed in again. It was there. He had caught it himself, at ten feet, and told himself he was imagining things. "Is he one of them?"

"I can't hear him." Edward's voice had a crack running down the middle of it. "I keep listening. There's nothing. There's a shape where he ought to be. It's like walking a cliff path at night. You know the edge is there because the wind changes."

A soft footfall in the snow at Carlisle's other side.

Garrett. Kate had gone back to her sisters, Carlisle saw. She was kneeling in the slush now with Tanya held against her and Carmen's hand resting on both their heads, and Eleazar standing over all three of them like a man in a church. Garrett had come forward alone. He stopped at Carlisle's shoulder with his hands loose and his red eyes on the young man and the book.

"Doctor," he said. "I once watched a man put a ball through a British major at sixty paces and then sit down on the major's own drum to eat his breakfast." He tilted his head. "Never seen one do it with a library book."

"No," said Carlisle. "Neither have I."

"Some kind of manual, you think? How to dispose of an Italian?"

"I think," Carlisle said carefully, "that it's about the wolf."

Garrett looked from the young man to the grey wolf and back, and for the first time since the black cloaks had come out of the trees that morning, something in his lean, restless face went still.

"Huh," he said.

Carlisle turned back and read the page headings as they went past. His eyes were very good. *Of the Unbreakable Vow.* *Of Life-Debts, and Their Discharge.* *Of Sacrificial Protections*, and at that one the young man's face did something brief and closed, and he turned past it without reading a line.

The wolf snarled at him without a sound. He turned another page.

### II. Harry

*Of the Unbreakable Vow*, said the book, in a cramped brown hand that belonged to some long-dead copyist with a grudge against the letter *s*.

He knew the Vow. Two people with their right hands clasped, a third with a wand laid on the knot, and thin tongues of fire wrapping the wrists like red-hot wire. Break it and you died. Snape had made one once, for Draco Malfoy's mother, and kept it to the letter, and in the end it had told Harry more about the man than seven years of detentions ever had.

A Vow went in at the wrist and stopped at the words. If you knew how to look, you could feel one sitting in a person like a band of cold iron. It wanted a Bonder. It wanted a wand and a form of words and three people standing very close together. He'd had none of that. He'd had a wolf looking at him across a field.

He turned the page.

*Of Life-Debts, and Their Discharge.* He knew that one first-hand as well. Pettigrew's silver hand closing round its owner's throat in a cellar in Wiltshire, because its owner had hesitated for one second over a debt he owed Harry Potter. A life-debt sat in the gut like a swallowed stone and waited to be called in. One man owed, one man was owed. Nobody in this field had saved anybody's life today, unless you were feeling generous about the last ten minutes, and even then the debt would run from the Cullens to him. There wasn't a line from him to the Cullens. There was one line. He knew exactly where it went.

*Of Sacrificial Protections.* He went past that without reading it. He could have written it. His mum had, in a nursery in Godric's Hollow, with nothing but herself.

*Of Wand and Hand. Of Hand-fasting and the Lesser Marriage Rites.* Ribbons. Oaths said over a shared cup. Old families binding their children to each other at seven years old for the sake of the silver and the land. Pages and pages of ceremony with very little underneath; most of what the book called a binding, in that section, was a promise with some glitter on, and it went in nowhere at all.

Then a long passage in a different hand, the ink gone the colour of old blood, under the heading *Of the Soul Divided*. He felt his jaw set and went through it fast. Half of it was wrong. The other half he'd lived, with a piece of somebody else's soul lodged in his head for seventeen years like a tenant who never paid rent. Nothing in there about lines. Plenty about holes.

*Book the Second*, said the next page, in red capitals. *Of the Changeable Peoples.*

He slowed down.

Werewolves came first. There was always a chapter on werewolves, and it was always written by somebody who'd never had one round for tea. The bitten. The moon. The packs that sometimes gathered round whoever had done the biting. Fenrir Greyback got a footnote, and under the footnote a list of children's names, which Harry didn't read. Lycanthropic packs, the book said with great confidence, were held together by fear and appetite and the full moon, and by nothing else. No bond. No line.

He thought of Remus. Remus in his patched robes. Remus locked in a cold room once a month for thirty years, who'd have given his right arm for a line to anybody, and who'd fought like a cornered thing when one finally turned up. Tonks's Patronus had changed when she fell for him. Took a new shape off him, a big silver four-legged thing. Love did that, the old books said. So did a bad enough shock.

He'd cast his own not a quarter of an hour ago. Stag. Same stag as ever.

Small mercies.

*Of the Animagus.* A page and a half, mostly about the Registry and the fines for not being on it. He'd known four unregistered Animagi in his life. Three were dead. The fourth wrote for the *Prophet*. An Animagus form came out of you and said things about you, sometimes things you'd rather it kept quiet, but it was yours alone. It didn't tie you to anybody.

*Of Familiars.* Chosen. Cast. Witch and cat, wizard and toad. Paperwork, more or less.

*Of the Veela.* The allure. A long, one-way pull that came off a Veela like heat off a stove and caught whoever it liked; the author had plainly been caught by it at some point and never got over the embarrassment. The pull went out. Nothing came back.

That was the pattern, once he stood back far enough to see it. Two hundred years of somebody's careful handwriting, and every bond in it came one of two ways: with a ceremony and witnesses, or running in one direction only, one end tied and the other free to walk off. None of them went in on sight. None of them went in past the heart. None of them went down past the place where his magic lived and kept going.

He flicked to the back. The index ran to forty pages, in a hand so small he had to bring the book up near his nose.

*Imprinting: see Birds, Charms for the Taming of.*

He snorted.

*Quileute*: nothing. *Olympic Peninsula*: nothing. *Shapeshifters, American*: a single line, *see Beings Division, Restricted Files, Pacific Coast.* *Bond, mutual, involuntary*: nothing. *Bond, cross-species*: *see Familiars; see also Hippogriffs, Courtesy Owed To*, which made him snort again, because Buckbeak would have had something to say about that. Under W, between *Wolfsbane* and *Wormwood*, there was nothing at all about a big black bastard lying at the bottom of somebody's head with a line running into its chest.

Course there wasn't.

He'd been the odd footnote in every book he'd opened since he was fifteen months old. *Survived the Killing Curse: see Potter, H.* *See also: Anomalies.* People had written whole chapters round the edges of him and never once in the middle. He didn't know why he'd expected today to be any different.

The line hummed.

It had been humming the whole time, under his breastbone, a low taut note like a wire in a crosswind. Every time she pulled her lips further back, it thrummed. Every time she took a breath, it shifted. Down at the bottom of the dark, the thing it was tied to lay with its chin on its forepaws and its ears pricked and its eyes on her, through his, and didn't seem to mind at all.

He'd kept that thing on a short lead for a long time. Years. It had never once pulled.

It was pulling now. Gently. Toward her.

He closed the book. The clasp snapped shut. He let go of it and it went back where it had come from, and his arm was empty.

The grey wolf snarled.

Out loud, this time. It ripped up out of her chest, ragged and furious and close, a sound with two years of something packed into it that he didn't have a name for, and she took one stiff step toward him across the slush with her head low and her hackles standing in a ridge from skull to tail.

And the thing in his chest answered her.

He didn't decide to. That was the part he'd come back to later and turn over and over. There was no gap. Between her snarl and the answer there was no room for Harry to stand in at all. The sound came up from under his sternum on its own, from the bottom of the line: a growl, long and low and enormous, with a floor under it that went down into the ground. It came out through his human throat and past his human teeth, and it was nothing a human throat had any business making. It rolled out across the clearing. It went up the slope and broke against the Cullen line, and he felt every one of them go still at once, the way a room goes still when a glass breaks.

The grey wolf's snarl stopped dead.

He shut his mouth. The growl went on a second longer inside him, rumbling away under his ribs like a lorry going over a bridge, and then that stopped too.

Silence. Wind in the pines. Somewhere up the slope behind him, the kid hiccupped.

Harry stood there a second. Took a breath. Put the flat of his hand on his breastbone, briefly, the way you'd put a hand on a dog's head to tell it *that's enough*. Then he took it away and coughed into his fist. Twice. He didn't put much into it.

"Sorry," he said. "Got a bit of a cold."

Nobody laughed. Nobody said a word. The grey wolf stared at him with her ears straight up and her mouth half open, and her tail, which had been standing out stiff behind her like a flag, had gone down somewhere round her hocks.

Well, he thought. That's new.

He turned his back on her.

He'd clocked the lake when he arrived, the way he clocked everything. It lay at the bottom of the field where the ground fell away, a long dark tongue of water two or three hundred yards down, skinned with grey ice round the margins and black as slate in the middle, with a fringe of dead reeds and a thin strip of stony beach. Nobody had looked at it all morning. Nobody had had any reason to.

He walked toward it. He didn't hurry. The snow went to slush under his boots and closed up again behind him, and he left a line of dark wet prints down the slope, and with every step he felt the line go taut behind him like a lead on a dog that's decided to sit.

He didn't look back.

### III. Leah

Her tail had gone down.

She didn't know when. Somewhere between the growl starting and the growl stopping, her tail had dropped from straight out behind her to tucked low against her hocks, and her ears had come up off her skull, and her weight had come off her haunches onto her forepaws, and her head had started to sink. Down. Lower than her shoulders. Lower. Her whole body had begun, all on its own, to do the thing she'd watched Collin and Brady do for Sam a thousand times, the thing Seth did for Jacob without even thinking. The thing she had never, not once in two years, done for anybody.

She locked her legs so hard her shoulders shook.

*What*, said Quil, in the pack mind, very high, *was THAT.*

*That came out of him*, Seth said. *Lee, that came out of him. Out of his MOUTH.*

*People can't make that noise. Jake. People can't—*

*He said he's got a cold*, Embry said.

*That's NOT a cold.*

*I know it's not a cold, Quil.*

*Then why'd you—*

*Because he SAID it.* Embry's thoughts were tight and flat and very fast, the way they got when he was scared. *He growled at her like an Alpha and then he stood there and looked us in the face and told us he had a cold. He's messing with us. He knows exactly what that was.*

Jacob hadn't said anything. Leah could feel why. When the growl hit, Jacob's body had done two things at the same time. The Alpha in him, the Black blood, Ephraim's blood, had come up hackles-first to answer it. And something under the blood, something older, had tried to put his head down. Both at once. She'd felt him feel it. She'd felt him fail to understand it. She could feel him failing to understand it now, and trying to hide that from the rest of them, which was useless in here. It was always useless in here.

*Leah*, he said instead. *Leah. Talk to me.*

She couldn't.

The growl was still in her. That was the problem. It hadn't stopped when it stopped. It had come across ten feet of snow and gone straight into her chest, down the line, the way her own heartbeat went down it, and it was sitting in there now, in the middle of her, still rumbling. She'd snarled at him. After a full minute of her body refusing every order she gave it, she had finally got a sound out of her own throat. And he'd answered.

At her.

To her.

She knew the difference. Every wolf did. You growled at a threat. You growled back at a wolf who'd growled first, to tell her you'd heard. This one had been an answer.

And her body had heard it, and her tail had gone down, and her head had started to drop.

*I hate him*, she thought. *I hate him. I hate this. I'm going to kill him.*

*No you're not*, Seth said, miserable.

*SHUT UP, Seth.*

Down the slope, he was walking away.

He'd just turned his back and gone, like she was a dog that had barked at him through a fence, ambling down toward the bottom of the field with his hands back in his jacket pockets and his boots leaving black wet prints in the snow. She hadn't even noticed the lake. She'd been in this field since before dawn and she'd never once looked at it. Long and dark and flat as a slate roof, with grey ice crusted along the edges.

Every step he took, the line in her chest pulled.

The pull had no pain in it. That was the worst part. Pain she could have fought. Pain she knew how to live in; she'd been living in it for two years. The hook was set gently in the meat behind her breastbone, and the somebody on the other end of it wasn't even tugging. He was walking away and letting the line do the work.

She realised she'd taken three steps after him.

Four.

Five.

She stopped. She was out in the open now, halfway between where she'd been standing and where he was going, alone, with nobody on either side of her. Up the slope she could feel the whole pack's eyes on her back like a hand.

*Leah*, Jacob said.

*I KNOW.*

She'd watched Jacob do this. That was the joke. That was the big cosmic joke. She'd spent four months watching Jacob Black fail to stand in a different room from a baby. Watched him pace outside the Cullens' door at three in the morning. Watched him eat standing up so he could keep an eye on the stairs. She'd laughed at him. In here, where he couldn't help hearing it, she'd laughed at him every single day. *Pathetic*, she'd called it. *A leash with a bow on.*

Down at the water's edge, the stranger stopped.

He stood a second on the strip of grey stones with his back to all of them, looking out over the lake. Then there was a chair behind him.

She didn't see it arrive. It was just there, as if it had been there for years, settled into the shingle at a slight tilt with its stubby feet sunk an inch into the gravel: a big fat sagging armchair upholstered in red velvet that had faded to the colour of old brick, the arms rubbed shiny and pale, a split seam down one side with the stuffing poking out. It was the kind of chair that lived in front of a fireplace in somebody's grandmother's front room and smelled of pipe smoke. It sat on the edge of a half-frozen lake in the middle of nowhere under a white sky and looked completely at home.

He sat in it. He sank into it the way you only sink into a chair you know, with a long breath out, and propped one boot on the opposite knee, and held out his hand.

Another book.

This one was thin and black and plain, about the size of a school notebook. He opened it on his thigh and started to read.

*Is he—* Quil started.

*Yes*, said Embry.

*In a CHAIR.*

*Yes, Quil.*

*Where'd the chair COME from?*

*Where'd the book come from? Where'd HE come from?* Embry's thoughts had a raw, slightly hysterical edge. *Pick one, man. Pick one and stick to it.*

Leah stared at the back of the armchair. At the untidy dark hair just visible over the top of it. At the thin thread of steam going up off him into the cold air, and the slow movement of his elbow as he turned a page.

She'd sworn. That was what kept coming back to her. Three in the morning on a patrol run along the cliffs above First Beach, with nobody in her head but Seth half asleep and the wind coming in off the water. *If it ever happens to me.* She'd looked at the sea when she thought it, and she'd meant it.

There was a lake right there. It was a lot closer than the sea.

She stood in the snow and looked at the water, and her paws stayed exactly where they were. She was too busy holding them back from him to send them anywhere else.

Off to her left, Sam's pack had gone dead quiet.

She didn't look at them. She didn't have to. She could feel the weight of Sam's attention on the side of her face like sun through a window, and she knew without turning her head exactly how he was standing. Head low. Ears back. The big black body gone careful, the way it went careful around Emily when Emily was upset. Paul, at his shoulder, had stopped growling. Even Paul.

She thought about turning round and going for Paul's throat, just to feel normal for a second. Then the line hummed and she forgot what she'd been thinking about.

*Okay*, Jacob said.

The tone of it made all of them look at him. He'd gone still on the slope with Nessie still on his back, and something in him had settled. Leah felt it settle. He'd made up his mind.

*I'm going down there.*

The pack mind went off like a firework.

*Jake, no—*

*Are you CRAZY—*

*You saw what he did to—*

*He's in a chair, Quil. He's not going to set me on fire from a chair.*

*He set Vladimir on fire without TURNING ROUND—*

*He asked for me.* Jacob's thoughts were grim and steady underneath. *You heard him. With my mouth, in English, with trousers on. He wants to talk to me about Nessie anyway. Fine. Let him. He can say what he wants. And then I'm going to ask him what the hell he is.*

Leah snarled at him. Out loud, with her body, a snap of teeth over her shoulder that made Seth flinch. *You don't get to—he's not yours to—*

*He's not yours either, Leah*, Jacob said, very quietly. *Not yet. You don't even want him to be.*

That landed. It landed hard, in the soft middle of her, and she couldn't hide that it had, because nobody could hide anything in here.

*Somebody's got to find out what he is*, Jacob said. *Somebody who isn't you. You can't think straight. I know. Believe me, I know. I couldn't think straight for a month. Let me do this, Lee.*

She hated him.

She hated that he was right.

*Fine*, she said.

She felt him hear her say it. She felt the relief go through him, and the pity under the relief, and she bared her teeth at the pity, and he let her.

Up the slope, Jacob turned his great russet head and looked at the blonde vampire crouched against his side.

Rosalie was already standing up.

### IV. Jacob

Nessie didn't want to let go.

Rosalie had her under the arms, gentle as anything, and Nessie's fists were still wound into the fur of his ruff, and when Rosalie lifted, Nessie came up off his back with both hands still clamped. Jacob felt every hair she was holding pull at the root. He stood still and let it.

"Baby," Rosalie murmured. "Let go for Aunt Rose. Jake's coming right back."

Nessie let go with one hand. She kept hold with the other. She reached the free one out over Rosalie's arm and laid her palm flat against the side of his muzzle.

The cold came first. The cold thin edge sliding in through the shimmer of Bella's shield, through her, the knife going through the drawer of photographs. Then the growl, the big one from just now, the way it had sounded from up on his back: huge and low and close, as if it had come up out of the ground under his paws. Then the man in his armchair by the water, small and far away, with his back to them all.

Then Jacob. Jacob walking away from her, down the slope, toward the man by the water. Getting smaller.

And a question, the way Nessie's questions always came, without words, just the feeling of a hand held out: *Coming back?*

He pressed his nose into her palm. Pushed, gently. *Yes. Yes. Every time.* He didn't have words in this shape and she didn't need them. She felt it, and her fingers went slack in his fur, and Rosalie gathered her up and turned away and tucked her face into the curve of her own neck.

"Go," Rosalie said, without looking at him. "And if he so much as lifts a finger at you, I want to know about it."

Jacob huffed at her. It was the closest he could get to *thanks, Blondie* in this body.

Bella. He had to look at Bella. She was still on her knees in the snow with Edward's arms round her, and she'd unbuckled the backpack from Jacob's shoulders herself half a minute ago with hands that didn't shake. It sat in the slush beside her now, the passports and the cash and the letter she'd written for Nessie, which nobody was going to need to open. She was looking at him.

"Jake," she said.

He waited.

She didn't say *be careful*. She didn't say *don't*. She looked at him a long second with her eyes gone amber-dark and two fingers resting on her temple, on the spot she kept touching, as if there were a bruise there only she could see. Then she said, "Find out what he did to my shield."

He dipped his head once.

Edward said, low and fast, "I won't hear him. Whatever he says. I'll hear you and nothing else. If he—"

Jacob was already going.

He went into the trees behind the wolves' line, twenty yards, far enough that the pines closed up behind him and nobody on the slope had a clear look. Then he let go.

Letting go was always the easy part. You just stopped holding the shape. The heat folded in on itself, and the bones ground and shortened, and the fur dragged back into skin with a feeling like a wet sweater being peeled off, and he came out of it on his hands and knees in the snow, human, naked, steaming like a horse after a race. The snow under his palms went straight to water. His own body felt small and thin-skinned and stupid, the way it always did for the first few seconds.

The cord was where it always was, round his left ankle: a length of leather lace, knotted tight, with his cutoffs rolled and lashed against the shin. He picked the knot loose with fingers that felt clumsy after paws, shook the shorts out and pulled them on. No shirt. He never brought a shirt. It was January in the mountains, and he was barefoot and bare-chested, and his skin was sitting at a hundred and eight degrees, and the snow fizzed where his feet went into it.

Fine.

He walked out of the trees and down the field.

It was a long walk. He felt every yard of it. The upper field was mostly bare grass, trampled to mud and slush where the crowd had stood all morning; below the line it opened out into clean snow nobody had touched. He walked past the place where the pack had been. Past the long grey fan of Vladimir's ash, which he went round wide. Past the patch where the stranger had stood through all of it, a circle of wet black grass a yard across, bare of snow, with the dead stems lying flat as if somebody had ironed them.

Leah was standing halfway down.

She'd stopped exactly where she'd stopped before, out in the open with her paws spread. She turned her head and watched him come with her ears pinned flat, and when he drew level she showed him her teeth.

"I know," he said out loud. His voice came out rough. It always did, the first time after. "I'll ask. You listen."

She didn't snap at him. She just watched him go by with her lip curled and the line of her back trembling, and he felt her eyes on him all the way to the water.

The beach was stones. Grey and black, fist-sized, slick where the lake lapped at them, with a rim of ice along the margin as thin as window glass. The reeds rattled in the wind. The lake smelled of cold and iron and mud. And sitting in a ratty red armchair at the very edge of it, one boot cocked up on his knee and a thin black book open on his thigh, was the man who'd just burned the Volturi off the face of the earth.

He didn't look up.

"Trousers," he said. "Good lad."

Jacob stopped about eight feet from the chair.

With a human nose he got less, and he still got it. Pine. Wet earth. Cold stone. And under all of it, so plain at eight feet that he couldn't believe anybody had ever missed it: wolf. Big. Male. Strange. No Quileute in it anywhere, nothing of the sea or the cedar smoke or the reservation; something colder and older and foreign, like a smell from a country he'd never been to.

"Don't," Jacob said.

The man turned a page. "Don't what?"

"Don't *good lad* me. You went into her head."

"I did."

"She's four months old."

"I know exactly how old she is, mate." He closed the book on one finger and looked up at last, and Jacob found himself looking into those green eyes from eight feet away and felt the hair stand up all along his arms. "I've seen every day of it. I've seen the day she was born from both ends." He let that sit. "She bled. She'll have a headache for an hour or two. She'll probably dream about it. That's on me. I'll own it. If I had to do it again I'd do it the same way, because I needed to know what she was before I put her on a list, and there wasn't a nicer way to find out in the time I had. I don't have to do it again." He paused. "That's the best I've got for you."

Jacob's hands had made fists. He looked down at them. He made them open.

"Her mom," he said. "Bella. She wants to know what you did to her shield."

"Cut it, the first time. Second time I put my shoulder through it." The man said it plainly, with no pleasure in it. "It'll knit. There'll be a seam. If she keeps working it the way she's been working it, it'll come back thicker round the seam than it was before. Scars do." He tilted his head a fraction. "Tell her I said that. Tell her I said it was good. It was."

Jacob stared at him.

"Sit down," the man said. "You're giving me a crick in my neck."

There was a chair behind Jacob's knees.

He hadn't heard it arrive. He turned and looked at it: a plain wooden kitchen chair, the paint worn off the seat in two pale ovals where years of somebody's backside had rubbed it bare, one leg propped level on a flat stone. He turned back.

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." The man opened the black book again. "Listen to this. Confederation's entire file on your lot. Took the Americans sixty years to put it together and I've read longer shopping lists." He read aloud in a flat, bored voice. "'Pacific Coast. Olympic Peninsula. Observer reports, autumn 1936, three wolves of exceptional size in the vicinity of the Quileute settlement at La Push. The animals appear to be young men of the tribe. Transformation hereditary, apparently voluntary, and possibly connected to a coven of vampires settled in the area, with whom the wolves appear to have concluded a treaty.'" He turned the page. "'Not lycanthropy. Not Animagic. Subjects run unusually hot, eat prodigiously, and own very few shirts.'" He glanced at Jacob's bare chest, then back down. "Well. They got that bit right."

Jacob said nothing.

1936. Three wolves. *Ephraim.* Ephraim Black, his great-grandfather, and the two who'd run with him, the year the Cullens came and the treaty was made. Somebody had been out there in the trees with a notebook, watching them. Seventy years ago. And nobody had ever known.

"One more line." The man ran a finger down the page. "'Observer attempted to establish the meaning of a term used by members of the tribe in connection with the wolves, possibly *imprinting*. Meaning could not be established. Observer was asked to leave.'" He looked up. "*Asked.* That's nice. I'd have expected a bit more biting."

"We don't bite people."

"No. You just tie yourselves to them." He shut the book, and it was gone, and his hand lay empty on his knee. "So. Here's me asking. Nicely. How's it work?"

Jacob didn't answer.

"Imprinting," the man said. "How's it work. What's it feel like. And does it go both ways."

"Why do you want to know?"

The man didn't say anything. He just turned his head, slowly, and looked past Jacob's shoulder and up the slope.

Jacob didn't need to turn round. He knew exactly where Leah was standing. He could feel her there like a stove at his back.

"Right," he said.

He sat down on the kitchen chair after all. It creaked under him. He put his elbows on his knees and looked at the stones between his bare feet, where the frost was turning to water.

"You look at her," he said. "That's it. That's how it works. You look at her, and that's it."

The man waited.

"It's—" Jacob stopped. He'd tried to tell this before. He'd tried to tell Bella, on the porch, and his dad, and Charlie sort of, and none of it had ever come out right. "You know how everything's holding you down? Gravity. Your family. Your friends. Your place. The stuff you want, the stuff you hate. There's all these, like, cables. Holding you to the ground. And you look at her and they all just go. Snap. All at once. And you think you're about to fall off the planet. And then there's one. Just one. And it goes to her. And that's what's holding you up now."

Behind him, up the slope, Leah made a sound. A small sound. He didn't turn round.

"And whatever's on the other end of it wants," the man said slowly, "you give."

"Whatever she needs. Yeah." Jacob scrubbed his face with both hands. "Whatever she needs me to be. Brother. Friend. Babysitter. Bodyguard. Right now she needs somebody to carry her around and let her pull his ears and tell her stories at bedtime, so that's what I am. When Sam saw Emily, Emily was grown, so he was—" He stopped. He was not going to talk about Sam and Emily with Leah standing up there listening. "It's different for everybody."

"Grown," the man said. "And Nessie's not."

"No."

"Nessie's four months old and looks four years. I've seen the chart on the doctor's wall. At that rate she'll look eighteen in a few years. And she'll be what? Six?"

Jacob's jaw went tight. "Nobody knows what happens then." It came out harder than he meant. "Carlisle doesn't know. Nobody does. She might stop. She might not."

The man was quiet for a second. Something passed over his face and went.

"Yeah," he said. "I saw that on the chart too." He let a breath go. "So say she stops. In a few years she looks like a grown woman, and she's a little kid in a grown woman's body, and you're, what, twenty-three? Still tied. Still whatever she needs." His voice hadn't changed. It was still flat and tired. His eyes hadn't left Jacob's face. "You see why I've got thoughts."

"I'd never—"

"I know you'd never. I've been in her head. I've seen you with her. I saw your face the first time." Something moved in the man's face again, briefly. "I'm saying I know how it looks from outside. To anybody who isn't you. To her grandad with the moustache—"

"Charlie doesn't know."

"Course he doesn't. To anybody. And I'm saying how it looks to me." He leaned forward a little, forearms on his knees, the same way Jacob was sitting, without seeming to know he was doing it. "Whatever she needs. Fine. Brother, friend, the bloke who carries her about and gets his ears pulled. Lovely. You keep it exactly that until she's grown. Properly grown. Years and years grown, and deciding for herself what she needs, with nobody's thumb on the scale. That's all I want from you." He held Jacob's eyes. "And if it ever turns into something it shouldn't while she's a kid, I'll know. Don't ask me how yet. I'll know. And then you and me won't need a long conversation."

Jacob looked at him.

He wanted to be angry. He was angry. Some stranger walking in out of the sky and saying it out loud, the thing people had been thinking at him in looks and silences for four months. Charlie's face, the first time he'd watched Jacob swing Nessie up onto his shoulders. Rosalie's. His dad's, once, when he thought Jacob wasn't looking. Bella's, at the very start, before she understood. Leah's, in here, in the worst of her moods. And this guy just said it, straight to his face, by a lake, in a kitchen chair, in front of everybody.

But it was also the thing Jacob thought himself sometimes, at three in the morning, on the floor beside her cot. He'd never do anything. Never. He just knew what it looked like, and he wondered whether that mattered.

"Okay," he said.

The man held his eyes a second longer. Then he sat back.

"Okay," he agreed.

The wind came across the lake and set the reeds rattling. Up the slope, faint and high, Jacob heard Nessie say something to Rosalie, and Rosalie answer. He breathed out.

"How's it feel?" the man asked. "Day to day. Being tied."

Jacob thought about it.

"Like a compass," he said at last. "Kind of. You always know where she is. You couldn't point to it on a map. But if she's in the next room, you know. If she's upset, you know. If you're away from her too long it starts to—" He pressed a fist against his own sternum without thinking. "Pull. Right here. Like a hook. It doesn't hurt much. It just never stops." He let his hand drop. "And she's the most important thing there is. All the time. And you're okay with that. That's the weird part. You'd think you'd fight it. You'd think it'd feel like a leash. And maybe it does for a while, if you didn't want it." He didn't look up the slope. He didn't have to. "But mostly you just quit being scared. About anything except her."

The man had gone still.

Jacob didn't notice at first. He was looking at the stones. When he did look up, the man wasn't looking at him. He was looking past him, up the hill, and his right hand had come up off his knee and stopped with the fingertips resting against the middle of his own chest, on the breastbone, exactly where Jacob's fist had been.

"And her end," the man said. His voice had gone quieter. "The one you're tied to. What does she get?"

"Nothing."

The man's eyes came back to him.

"I mean, she gets me," Jacob said. "She gets us. Whatever she needs. But she isn't tied. It's one way. It's just us. The wolf." He shrugged, awkward, his bare shoulders steaming. "She doesn't have to feel anything. That's sort of the point. That's what the old stories say, and it's how it's been every single time. Emily told Sam to get lost. For months. She could've kept on telling him. Claire's two, and she thinks Quil's a pony. Kim, Jared's Kim, she'd been in love with him since grade school and he'd never looked at her twice till the day he did, so with them it worked out, but that was her. That was all her. She'd have felt the same without it." He shook his head. "Nessie loves me. I know she does. She'd love me anyway." He'd said that to himself enough times that it came out sounding rehearsed, and he winced. "Whoever we imprint on, they get to choose. They always get to choose. We're the ones who don't."

"One way," the man said.

"One way."

The man looked at him for a long moment.

Then he sat back in the armchair, slowly, all the way, until his head rested against the faded velvet, and he looked up at the white sky over the lake and let out a long breath that smoked and drifted away over the ice.

"Then somebody's got their wires crossed, mate," he said.

He lifted two fingers and tapped them, twice, against the middle of his chest.

"Because I've got something in here."

Jacob stared at him.

Up the slope, behind him, every sound stopped. He couldn't hear the pack in this shape. He didn't need to. He heard five wolves stop breathing at once.

"What?" he said.

"Something." The man didn't lift his head off the chair. He was still looking at the sky. "A line. Comes in about here." The two fingers on the breastbone again. "Goes down. Long way down."

"That doesn't happen." Jacob's mouth had gone dry. "It doesn't go back. It *can't* go back, the other person's not a—they're not—"

"A wolf," the man said.

Jacob shut his mouth.

The man sat up. He did it slowly, the chair creaking under him, and put both boots flat on the stones and looked at Jacob with an expression Jacob couldn't read at all. Tired, maybe. Patient, the way you were patient with a kid who was about to work something out on his own.

Jacob breathed in through his nose.

Pine. Wet earth. Stone. Wolf.

He'd smelled it from across the field with a wolf's nose. He'd smelled it from eight feet with his own. He'd heard the growl come up out of this man's chest and felt his own body try to answer it two ways at once. He'd been sitting here ten minutes knowing it and not saying it, because saying it would make it real, and he had no idea what it would mean if it was real.

"What are you?" he said.

The man considered him.

"Go back to your kid, Jacob," he said.

It was the first time he'd used Jacob's name. Jacob noticed. He didn't know what to do about noticing.

"You didn't answer."

"No." The man's eyes went past him again, up the slope. "I'll answer it. Not to you first." He looked back. "Go on. She'll be wanting you. Her head'll be pounding. She'll want you more than she wants her mum right now, and her mum knows it, and her mum's going to need you up there being normal while she pretends she doesn't mind."

Jacob stood up. The kitchen chair rocked on its stone.

He should have gone. He wanted to go. Nessie was up there with her face in Rosalie's neck and a headache and the memory of a cold knife going through her, and every cell in his body wanted to be back up that slope with her fists in his fur.

He stood there anyway.

"There's one more thing," he said. "About imprinting. You should know it."

The man waited.

"It's the law. The oldest law we've got. Older than the treaty. No wolf can hurt another wolf's imprint. Ever. You can't even think about it. Sam's pack couldn't touch Nessie, after I—even when they wanted to. Even Sam." He swallowed. "So. Nobody on our side's going to touch you. Either pack. Not now."

The man looked at him for a second.

Then one corner of his mouth went up.

"Lucky them," he said.

Jacob opened his mouth, and shut it, and turned round and walked back up the beach.

Leah was standing exactly where he'd left her.

She hadn't moved a paw. Her ears were straight up, and her eyes had gone huge and wet and wild, and she was staring past him at the man in the armchair as if the sky had cracked open behind his head. As Jacob came level with her, her whole body shuddered, nose to tail, one long hard shiver like a dog shaking off water, except nothing came off her.

"You heard," Jacob said.

Stupid thing to say. Course she'd heard. Everybody on that hill with ears had heard.

She looked at him. Just for a second. Then her eyes went back to the lake.

He put his hand on her shoulder. He didn't think about it first. His palm was just there on the coarse grey fur, the heat of her coming up through it to meet the heat of him. She didn't snap. She didn't pull away. She stood under his hand, shaking.

"Lee," he said. "I'm going to go get Nessie. And then you're going to do whatever you're going to do, and I'm going to let you. Okay?"

She couldn't answer him in that shape. But she leaned, very slightly, into his hand.

Then she leaned the other way. Down the slope. Toward the water.

He took his hand back and let her go.

### V. Edward

Edward heard Jacob decide to stay on two legs.

He'd been listening to him all the way down the slope and all the way back. Jacob in human shape was louder than Jacob as a wolf, his thoughts ragged and full of words instead of the pack's wordless flood, and as he came up out of the slush toward the Cullen line his mind was already running ahead of his feet, reaching for Renesmee. *Hold her. Just hold her. Arms. Not fur. She needs arms right now.*

Rosalie gave her up without a word.

Edward saw it cost her. He heard it cost her. Rosalie's thoughts were a cold clean blaze, the way they always were when she was frightened, and most of the blaze was pointed at the man by the lake. But a corner of it turned toward Jacob as he took Renesmee into his bare arms—*smells like wet dog, always smells like wet dog*—and then turned away again, because Renesmee had made a small sound and pressed her whole face into Jacob's hot neck, and Rosalie loved her more than she disliked anyone alive.

"Hey, Nessie," Jacob said into her hair. "Hey. I'm here. I'm here."

Renesmee laid her hand on his cheek.

Edward saw what she showed him, because he saw everything she showed. The lake, small and far off. The red chair. Jacob getting smaller. Then the question, the hand held out, and Jacob, with his eyes shut, answering it with his whole mind: *Yeah. Came back. Told you I would.*

Bella was watching them. Edward felt her body loosen a fraction in his arms. Only a fraction.

"What did he say?" she asked.

Edward could tell her exactly. He had Jacob's memory of the whole conversation sitting in his head like a recording, every word and every pause, the creak of the kitchen chair and the dry voice and the moment the stranger's fingers had come up and rested against his own breastbone. He'd heard it twice already: once as it happened, through Jacob's ears, and again as Jacob climbed the slope replaying it, over and over, unable to stop.

"About your shield," he said. "That it will knit. That there'll be a seam. That if you keep working it, it will come back thicker around the seam than it was before. That scars do." He hesitated. "And he said to tell you it was good. He said it was. Again."

Bella's jaw tightened. She didn't say anything.

"About Renesmee—" Edward stopped.

"Tell me."

So he told her. All of it. The headache, the dreams, *that's on me*, *I'd do it the same way*. The list. The chart on Carlisle's wall, which the man had seen in Bella's own memory. *Eighteen in a few years. You see why I've got thoughts.* And Jacob saying *okay*, by a lake, to a stranger who had just said out loud the thing that had sat unspoken in a dozen minds around Jacob Black for four months. Rosalie's. Charlie's, without any of the words for it. Billy's, once, very briefly, at the kitchen table. Nobody had ever said it to Jacob's face, because Edward had heard enough of the inside of Jacob's head to know there was nothing in there to find, and had told the ones who would listen, and had made them believe him.

Bella listened with her eyes on Jacob and their daughter.

"Good," she said, when he'd finished.

He looked at her.

"I don't like him," Bella said. Her voice was very even. "He went through my head like a burglar and he made her bleed and I am going to remember what that felt like for the rest of forever. But he said it out loud. To Jake. Calmly. Somebody needed to." Her mouth twisted. "I tried, the first day. I broke Seth's collarbone."

Edward didn't answer. Behind him, Carlisle had come back up the slope with Esme beside him, and Carlisle's mind was a careful orderly hum, the way it went when he was laying instruments out on a tray. Emmett's was simpler. *Bigger than Jake. If he's—I bet you he's bigger than Jake. Ten bucks.* And under that, Emmett being Emmett: *That growl, though. Man.*

And the wolves.

Edward had been hearing the wolves all day the way he always heard them: two choruses, Sam's and Jacob's, never quite blending, like two radios tuned to neighbouring stations. When Jacob had sat down in that kitchen chair and said *one way*, both choruses had gone quiet to listen. Then the stranger had said *wires crossed*, and both had gone up together.

*—he's got something, he's got something—*

*—that doesn't happen, that's never happened—*

*—he's lying. He's LYING. He has to be lying—* Paul.

*—it went back? It went BACK?—* Collin, very young.

And in the middle of Sam's chorus, Sam.

Edward had heard Sam Uley's mind on and off for a year and a half. He knew its shape: deliberate, heavy, responsible, a mind that sorted the world into *mine to protect* and everything else. He had heard what it did when Leah made that dreadful yelping sound on the field this morning and Sam understood what it meant.

He heard what it did now.

*He feels it.* Very quiet, underneath everything. *He feels it too. So she won't be—it won't be like—* And then, faster, crushing the thought flat, ashamed of it: *Don't. Don't you dare be glad. You don't get to be glad about anything that happens to her.*

He was glad anyway. Edward heard that as well. Glad, and hating himself for it, and standing in the snow at the front of his pack with his head low, unable to make it stop.

Edward turned his attention away. He had been inside too many heads today.

"Edward." Carlisle's voice, at his shoulder. "Catch."

He looked up in time to see something coming up the slope toward them.

It came through the air from the direction of the lake, unhurried, turning gently end over end. Small. A glint of glass. It slowed as it came near, the way no thrown thing should ever slow, and Edward put up his hand and it settled into his palm as lightly as a moth.

A vial. Thin glass, stoppered with cork and sealed with a blob of dark wax. A finger's length of something pale gold inside, cloudy, that moved when he tilted it with a slowness like honey. Tied round the neck with a bit of string, a folded square of paper.

Every vampire within twenty yards had turned to look at it.

Edward unfolded the paper with one thumb. Pencil. Untidy slanting handwriting, very small.

*For the kid's head. Works on humans. No idea about her. Healer will know when they get here, shouldn't be long. If you're not sure, don't.* And under that, a scrawled initial. *H.*

He read it out loud. Nobody said anything.

Then Rosalie said, "Throw it in the lake."

"Rose," said Esme.

"He made her *bleed*—"

"Let me see it." Carlisle had his hand out. Edward gave it to him. Carlisle held it up against the white sky and turned it slowly, and then, after a moment's hesitation, worked the cork out a fraction of an inch with his thumbnail and brought the vial to his nose.

Edward heard him sort the scent the way he'd sort a differential. *Willow bark. Something in the mint family. Something like feverfew, but it isn't. Honey; real honey. And something else. Something that isn't a plant at all. I don't know what that is.* Carlisle pressed the cork home. "I don't recognise half of what's in it," he said. "It isn't anything I could make. I have no idea what it would do to her."

"Neither does he," Bella said. She was looking at the paper in Edward's hand. "He says so."

"Bella—"

"He says so, Edward. Right there. *No idea about her.*" She took the note from him and read it again, a long moment. "He just did that to the Volturi, and he wrote down that he doesn't know something. In pencil."

Edward listened to her mind and, as always, heard nothing. But he knew her face.

"We wait," Bella said. "For the Healer. Whatever a Healer is." She handed the note back and turned toward Jacob and their daughter. Renesmee had lifted her head from Jacob's neck and was looking at the vial in Carlisle's hand with interest, the tears drying in stiff tracks on her cheeks. "Does it hurt, baby? Your head?"

Renesmee nodded solemnly. Then she reached out from Jacob's arms and put her palm on her mother's face.

Edward saw what she showed her. The lake, and the red chair, and the dark head of the man sitting in it, small and far away. Then the vial, glinting, coming up the hill through the air all on its own. And under both, wordless and very clear, a feeling Edward had heard in his daughter many times before and never once about anything she was afraid of.

Curiosity.

Bella's face did something complicated.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "Me too."

Off to the side, where the Denalis knelt in the slush, Edward heard Kate lift her head.

She had been listening too. He'd been aware of her mind all through the last quarter hour without attending to it, a long low note of grief with Tanya's silence wrapped inside it like a stone in a sock. Now the note shifted. Kate was looking down the slope at the man in the red armchair, and what she was thinking had no words in it at all, only a picture: Irina, on her knees in the snow, with Felix's hands on her shoulders, and the man's back turned to the far side of the field where Felix now lay in a long grey smear. And a question Kate didn't know how to ask, and didn't know who to ask, and wasn't sure she wanted answered.

Garrett had his hand on the back of her neck. He wasn't saying anything. He was very good at that, when it mattered. Edward had not expected that of him.

Down the slope, Leah Clearwater turned and walked into the trees.

Edward heard her go. He heard her decide, a single hard white thought, *FINE*, slammed down like a door. He heard her mind contract as she pushed into the pines out of sight of the lake, out of sight of all of them, and then he heard her let go of the wolf. Jacob's chorus lost a voice. Something else took its place: a human mind, Leah's, the way he'd heard it a handful of times before at the edges of Cullen land, sharp and fast and bitter as black coffee, all words now, a torrent of them.

*—cord, where's the—stupid knot, stupid, stupid hands, I hate hands—*

He stopped listening. He made himself stop.

There were some things a person ought to get to do without anybody in her head.

### VI. Leah

Hands.

She hated hands. She hated how small they were, and how cold the air felt on them, and how her fingers fumbled at the knot in the leather cord round her ankle like a toddler's. She hated how the snow bit into her bare knees—it shouldn't, she ran at a hundred and eight, it shouldn't bite—and how her own breath sounded, ragged and too loud and too human, in the hush under the pines.

She got the knot loose. Shook out the shorts. Black cutoffs, frayed at the hem. The T-shirt rolled up inside them was grey, men's, too big: one of Seth's she'd stolen two summers ago and never given back. She dragged it over her head. Her hair was short, the way she'd hacked it off the week she first phased, standing in the bathroom at two in the morning with her mother's kitchen scissors because she'd be damned if she was going to be a wolf with a ponytail, and it was wet with snowmelt and stuck to her forehead. She shoved it back.

Then she stood there, barefoot in the trees, and she couldn't hear anyone.

That was the thing she always forgot. Every time. Two years, and every time she came back to two legs, the silence hit her like walking out of a bar onto an empty street at closing. No Seth. No Jacob. No Quil, no Embry. Just her in her own skull, alone, with nobody to hide from and nobody to hear her.

And the line.

The line was still there.

She'd half thought, she'd half hoped, that it lived in the wolf. That if she put the wolf away, she'd put it away too, folded up somewhere with the fur and the teeth, and she'd stand here on two legs and feel nothing, and be able to think.

It ran out of the middle of her chest, under the grey cotton of Seth's shirt, in a straight hard line through the pines and down the slope to the lake. She could feel exactly where he was. She couldn't see him from here. She didn't need to.

*Because I've got something in here.*

She'd heard him say it. She'd heard Jacob say *what*, and heard the answer, *a line*, *comes in about here*, *goes down, long way down*. She'd heard every word from halfway down the hill with a wolf's ears, and she'd felt it hit the pack mind, all of them at once, like a stone through a window.

It went back.

Every story she'd ever heard. Every imprint she'd ever been forced to live through from inside somebody else's head. Sam. Jared. Paul with Rachel Black, which Jacob still couldn't think about without wanting to throw up. Quil. Jacob. Every one of them had been the one on the line. Every one of them had been tied, and the other end had been free, and that was the whole horror of it. That was the part she'd sworn she'd walk into the sea before she lived through. Being tied to somebody who didn't have to care. Who could look at you and shrug. Who could walk away from you down a beach, and you'd follow, and follow, and they'd never feel a thing.

She'd lived the other half of that already. She'd lived it with Sam. She'd been the one who got walked away from, and she'd felt it every day, and he—

He'd felt it. Hadn't he. She'd been inside Sam's head for two years. She knew exactly what it had cost him. That had almost been worse.

She didn't know what this was.

She didn't know if it was better.

She went out of the trees and down the field.

She went the long way, round the edge, so she wouldn't have to pass the Cullens and wouldn't have to pass Sam's pack. It didn't work. The edge of the field brought her within thirty yards of them, and she felt all their eyes come round, and she didn't look. She didn't look at Paul, whose lip was still curled. She didn't look at Jared, or the kids. She didn't look at Sam.

She felt him take a step toward her.

"Don't," she said, out loud, without turning her head.

He stopped.

She went on down.

He was still in the chair.

He'd turned it a little. Or it had turned itself; she didn't know how any of this worked and she wasn't going to start pretending she did. It sat at an angle now so he could see both the lake and the slope, and he was slouched in it with his boots stretched out on the stones and his hands folded on his stomach, and he watched her come the whole way down. He didn't get up. He didn't say anything.

The kitchen chair Jacob had sat on was gone.

She stopped where Jacob had stopped. Eight feet. She stood barefoot on the freezing stones with the lake lapping at the ice a yard to her left and the wind going through Seth's shirt as if it weren't there, and she looked at him, and he looked at her, and for a while neither of them said anything.

He was younger than she'd thought, up close. Older than her, but younger than he'd looked across the field. A lot of jaw and cheekbone and dark stubble. A thin crease between his brows that looked like it lived there. His eyes were very green.

The line hummed under her breastbone so hard she thought he must be able to hear it.

"Lassie?" she said.

One corner of his mouth went up. "Suits you."

"I'm going to rip your throat out."

"Yeah? Go on, then."

She didn't move.

She couldn't. She stood there and told her body to go, to cross eight feet of stones and put her hands round his neck, and her body stood there and listened to her with polite interest and did nothing at all.

"Thought not," he said. He watched her a moment longer. "For what it's worth, if I could cut it, I would. I've had a look. There's nowhere to get a knife in."

"You had a look."

"Inside. My end." He nodded at her. "I can't see yours. Different system."

"You looked in my head?"

"No." Flatly. "I looked in Bella's head. And the kid's. I'm not going in yours."

"Why not? You went in theirs. You went in a four-month-old's—"

"Because I don't need to," he said. "I needed to know what the kid was, and I needed to know fast, before I put her on a list that'll follow her the rest of her life. With you I've got time. And you've got a mouth." He tilted his head. "Which you've been using quite a lot, I'd guess, up the hill. In your head. Your Alpha kept looking at you like you'd said something rude."

Leah opened her mouth and shut it again.

"So use it," he said. "Ask."

She looked at him. At the folded hands, the stretched-out legs. At the steam going up off his shoulders into the cold. At his chest, under the green flannel, where the line went in.

"My dad's name was Harry," she said.

She didn't know why that was the first thing out. She hadn't known it was coming until it was in the air between them, and then it was there, and she couldn't take it back.

Something moved in his face. He didn't look away from her.

"Yeah," he said. "I caught the name. In Bella's head. Charlie's mate. The fishing." A pause. "Didn't put it together till just now. Sorry."

"Sorry for what? Having the same name?"

"Sorry he's dead."

She hadn't expected that. She didn't know what she had expected. She stood there with it, and her eyes went hot, and her throat went thick, and she was furious about it, so angry she could have screamed. She didn't scream. She stood there.

"You said wires crossed," she said. Her voice came out rough.

"I did."

"What does that mean."

He sat up. Slowly. He unfolded his hands and leaned forward with his forearms on his knees, and the chair creaked under him, and he looked up at her from nearly her own eye level. He was sitting and she was standing and he was still nearly at her eye level. That was how big he was.

"When you looked at me," he said, "up there, when I turned round, I got a twinge. Here." He touched his breastbone. "Thought it was the trip. Long hop. Then I saw it in Bella's head, what your lot do, what it looks like when you do it. And I thought, right. She's tied herself to me, poor cow, and I'm the free end, and that's that." His mouth twisted. "So I went and had a look at my end. To see where it stopped."

"And?"

"It doesn't stop."

Leah said nothing.

"Comes in here. Goes past my heart. Keeps going. Goes past—" He stopped, and she watched him pick the next words, and she knew, the way you always know when somebody's leaving something out, that there was a piece he'd decided not to say yet. "Goes past the place where my magic lives. Goes down underneath that. And there's something down there, and the line goes into it, and it's got hold of it." He met her eyes. "Got hold of it with both hands."

"What's down there?"

He didn't answer.

"You said *it*," Leah said. "Down there. Something. You keep saying *it*. What's *it*?"

"Me," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"Only one I've got that's true." He was quiet a moment. "You smelled it. Up there. All of you did. I'd put money on your lot arguing about it in your heads since the minute I landed. Under the pine. What's it smell like?"

Her nostrils flared before she could stop them. Even like this, even with a human nose, it was there. She'd smelled it on the field, she'd smelled it down the slope, and she was eight feet away now and it was so strong she could taste it on the back of her tongue. Pine and earth and stone, and under all of it fur. Musk. Frost. Something old and big and foreign, with teeth.

"Wolf," she said.

"Yeah."

"You're not Quileute."

"I'm from Surrey," he said. "Don't think it counts."

"You don't phase. You'd have phased by now. Up there. With all of them." She flung an arm out at the far side of the field, at the grey heaps on the wet grass where the Volturi had stood. "We can't help it, with that many of them, that close. Your body just goes."

"Your body," he said.

"So you *don't*—"

"Didn't say that." He looked at her. "I said I don't go when I don't want to."

The line hummed so hard she put a hand to her chest. She didn't mean to. It was just there, her palm flat on Seth's shirt, over the place.

He watched her do it. His own hand twitched on his knee, as if it wanted to do the same thing. It stayed where it was.

"Show me," Leah said.

He was quiet a long moment.

She saw him look past her, up the slope, and she knew what he was looking at without turning round. All of them. The Cullens on the hill, the Denalis, the Irish, Garrett, the Amazons. Both packs. A field full of eyes, every one of them on this little strip of beach. She saw him weigh it. Saw something in his face go narrow and careful, the way Sam's face used to go when he was deciding something for the pack.

"Show me," she said again. "You said I've got no idea what I've tied myself to. You *said* it. Out loud, to everybody, like it was funny. So show me. Show me what I'm tied to."

He looked back at her.

"Fair," he said.

He stood up.

The armchair was gone the moment he was out of it. Not so much as a dent left in the gravel. He walked three steps along the beach away from her, out to where the shingle widened, and turned to face her, and stood there with his hands loose at his sides, in his leather jacket and his jeans and his scuffed boots.

"Don't scream," he said. "Your brother'll come running, and I'll have to explain myself to him as well."

"I don't scream."

"Yeah. I heard you earlier."

Then he changed.

She'd seen a hundred phasings. She'd done it a thousand times herself. She knew how it went: the shudder that started in the spine, the body bursting outward, fur and bone and heat exploding out of a person like something tearing its way out of a paper bag, clothes going to rags, a roar of air. It was violent every time. It was a body blowing itself apart and putting itself back together bigger.

He leaned forward, the way a man leans into a strong wind, and the leaning kept going. Down and out. His shoulders broadened. His back lengthened. The leather jacket and the flannel and the jeans darkened and ran together into one colour, black, and the black was fur. There was no sound. No tearing, no crack of bone, no scraps of cloth left on the stones. Just a long silent pour of shape, like ink let into water, like a shadow stretching across a field when the sun goes down, and the ground under her bare feet never so much as shivered.

Then it was finished, and there was a wolf on the beach, and she had to tip her head back to see it.

Black. Midnight black from ear to tail, with a darker grey, charcoal, showing through underneath where the wind lifted the guard hairs along his back. He was long. God, he was long, longer than any of them, longer than Sam, longer than Jacob, a great deep-chested length of shoulder and back and haunch that went on and on and ended in a heavy black brush of tail, held low and still. His shoulder stood above the top of her head. She'd stood next to Jacob's wolf often enough to know exactly where his shoulder came to on her, and this was more. A good bit more. His paws were the size of dinner plates, and he was standing on loose shingle, and the shingle wasn't moving under him.

He turned his head and looked down at her.

Green eyes. The same green. Exactly the same, in a black wolf's face.

Her whole body tried to phase.

It came up from her gut like a wave of sickness, the heat, the shudder, the wolf in her flinging itself against the inside of her skin to get out, to get out *now*, to be the same shape as him. Her spine bowed. Her hands clawed. She heard herself make a noise. She held it. She held it by her fingernails, with her teeth locked and her eyes squeezed shut and every muscle she had clenched round it like a fist round a hot coal, and it went on and on, and then, slowly, it went back down.

She opened her eyes.

He hadn't moved.

"All right?" he said.

Out loud.

With his mouth. With his black wolf's mouth, in the same voice, the same flat dry voice, only lower, much lower, with a growl threaded under the words like a cello under a song, and every consonant clean.

Leah sat down.

She didn't decide to. Her legs just quit holding her up, and she sat down hard on the freezing stones in her cutoffs with the ice-water a yard away, and stared up at him.

"You can talk," she said.

"Yeah."

"We can't talk. Like that. Nobody can talk like that. We have to—"

"I know. Different system." The great head tilted. The ears, tall and black with the insides darker still, swung forward. "You went a funny colour."

"I nearly phased."

"Yeah, I saw. You held it, though." A beat. "That was good."

She laughed. It came out of her with no warning at all, a short cracked bark of a laugh, and she clapped a hand over her mouth, appalled.

He watched her do it. His tail moved. Once. A slow swing, side to side, and then still.

Up the slope behind her, she could hear things now. She hadn't noticed them start. Snarling, a lot of it, from the left, where Sam's pack was. Paul. Jared. Somebody yelping, high and young. And from nearer, on the right, a sound she knew better than her own voice: Seth, howling, a long high wavering howl that went up and cracked, and Quil and Embry going up with him.

She didn't turn round. She couldn't take her eyes off the black wolf.

"You're bigger than Jacob," she said.

"Don't tell him," said the black wolf. "He'll sulk."

"He'll *know*. He's *looking* at you."

"Then don't tell him I said it." He looked past her, up the hill, at all the noise. His ears went back a fraction and came forward again. "That's your lot, is it?"

"Both lots."

"Loud, aren't they."

"You just turned into a wolf the size of a *truck* in front of two packs," Leah said. "What did you think they'd do? Clap?"

"Bit of applause'd be nice."

He lowered his head then, slowly, until his muzzle was level with her face. It was a long way down for him. She could feel the heat pouring off him from a foot away. She could smell him, all of it now, nothing hidden under anything, just him: pine and stone and wet earth and fur and wolf. Big, old, foreign wolf.

"Well," he said, very low. "That's what you're tied to, Lassie. That's the end of the line."

Leah looked at him.

At the green eyes a foot from hers. At the black fur and the long jaw and the teeth she could see the white tips of, at the edges of his lips.

The line in her chest had stopped humming.

It had gone quiet. Steady. Taut and still, the way a wire goes still when both ends are tied off and nothing is pulling on either. She sat on the frozen stones and felt it run out of her into him and felt it, for the first time, land. Hit something, and hold, and stay held. And her whole body, every cell of it, did a thing it hadn't done in two years.

It stopped bracing.

She didn't know what to do with that. She had absolutely no idea.

"Can you hear me?" she said. "If I phase. Would you be—" She waved at her own head. "In here. Like the pack."

He was quiet a moment.

"No idea," he said. "Never been in one."

"You don't *know*?"

"There's a lot I don't know, love. I know a lot about a very few things." He lifted his head away from her. "This isn't one of them."

Up the slope, somebody was shouting her name. A human voice. Jacob.

She ignored it.

"Lassie," she said.

The black wolf looked down at her.

"If you call me Lassie in front of my brother," she said, "I'll find a way. I don't care what the law says."

His tail moved again. Twice, this time.

"Noted," he said.

### VII. Bella

Everybody on the hill could hear them.

Bella didn't think the two of them on the beach had remembered that. Or maybe he had, and didn't care, and Leah was too far gone to think about it. Three hundred yards was nothing. Bella could hear the lake lapping at the ice. She could hear the reeds. She could hear the chair creak when he leaned forward, and Leah's breathing, quick and shallow and furious, and the line of silence after Leah said *my dad's name was Harry* that went on for exactly two seconds before he answered.

*Sorry he's dead.*

Bella closed her eyes.

She remembered the week Harry Clearwater died. Not well. It was on the other side of the fog, with the rest of her human life, smeared and faded like a photograph left in a window. But she remembered Charlie's face. Charlie standing in the kitchen doorway in his uniform with his hat in his hands, looking at nothing, and the casserole dishes that had started turning up on the porch afterward, and Sue Clearwater's voice on the phone in the evenings, and Charlie taking the phone into the other room. She'd never thought much about Leah in any of it. She'd been busy, that spring, with other things. With dying, mostly, one way and another.

She thought about Leah now. Leah sitting on the stones with her arms round her knees. Leah, who'd spent the worst weeks of Bella's pregnancy patrolling the edge of the Cullen lawn and making sure Bella knew precisely what she thought of her, in a voice that carried through glass. Leah, who had called her a leech-lover and a martyr and worse, and who had been, Bella could admit now, from this side of everything, right about some of it.

"Edward," she said.

"I know." He'd been holding very still. "I can hear her. She's on two legs. She's—" He stopped.

"Don't," Bella said. "Don't tell me what she's thinking."

He was quiet a moment. Then he said, "I wasn't going to."

She opened her eyes.

Jacob was standing a few yards off with Renesmee on his hip, her head on his shoulder, her eyes open and fixed on the lake. Rosalie had moved to stand next to them. Esme had moved to stand next to Rosalie, and Emmett behind all of them, and Carlisle a little apart with the vial still held in his closed hand, and the whole family had turned, Bella realised, in a loose half-circle round Jacob and her daughter, facing downhill. Facing the beach. Nobody had decided to do it. They'd all just done it.

Bella reached for her shield.

She did it carefully. It hurt to think about, still, the way a cut finger hurts when you go to make a fist: a thin bright line of cold across the inside of her skull, from front to back, where he'd gone through. She'd been afraid to touch it. She touched it now. She took hold of the whole crumpled, folded thing that was left of the wall she'd built this morning, and she pushed it out, slow, an inch at a time, the way she'd learned with Kate's hands sparking on her arms in the snow behind the house.

It came.

It came out from her the way it always had, and it covered Edward, and it covered Jacob and Renesmee, and it kept going, past Rosalie, past Esme, round Emmett and Carlisle. She ran her attention along it the way she'd run her tongue along a chipped tooth, and she found the seam. A ridge. A fine straight scar, all the way across.

The shield was thicker there.

She stopped. She pushed at it, harder, right on the line where he'd cut, and the scar held, and held, and didn't give at all. It was the strongest place in the whole thing.

*If she keeps working it,* he'd told Jacob, *it'll come back thicker round the seam than it was before. Scars do.*

She hated that he'd been right. She hated that he'd known. She held the shield out over her family and looked down the hill at the man on the beach and hated him, steadily, for a good five seconds, and it didn't help as much as she'd hoped.

"Bells?" Jacob said, low.

"I'm fine."

"You're doing the thing. The shield thing. I can feel it."

"I'm fine, Jake."

Down on the beach, Leah said *show me*.

Bella heard her say it. She heard her say it twice. She heard the second one crack in the middle. And then the long pause, and the scrape of a boot on stones, and *fair*, and the faint creak of a chair that then wasn't there anymore, because Bella's eyes were on it and it simply stopped being anywhere.

He walked a few steps along the beach and turned round.

*Don't scream,* Bella heard him say. *Your brother'll come running.*

"Edward," she said again, and this time she didn't know what she was asking.

Edward's hand found hers and closed round it.

Then the man on the beach leaned forward into nothing, and kept leaning, and the shape of him ran out long and black across the shingle like spilled ink, without a sound, and where he had been there was a wolf.

Bella's shield jumped. She felt it jump, all the way round her family, like a startled animal. She held it.

Beside her, very softly, Emmett said, "Called it."

Nobody told him to shut up.

It was enormous. It was black from end to end, black as the inside of a closed fist, and it stood on that strip of beach with its head above the head of the woman sitting at its feet, and it was longer than Jacob, longer than Sam, longer than anything Bella had ever seen on four legs. It turned its head and looked down at Leah, and even from three hundred yards, even through the grey air, Bella could see that its eyes were green.

The wolves went up all at once.

Seth first, a long high howl that broke in the middle, and then Quil and Embry with him. On the far left, Sam's pack, a ragged snarling roar. Jacob made a sound beside her that was nothing like any sound she'd heard him make on two legs, and then he was shouting, "*Leah!* Leah! *LEAH!*" with Renesmee clutched against his chest and his whole body leaning down the hill.

Leah didn't turn round.

And in Jacob's arms, Renesmee lifted her head off his shoulder. She looked at the black wolf on the beach with her whole face, her mouth open, the dried tear-tracks stiff on her cheeks. Then she reached out, without looking, and found Bella's hand where it hung at her side, and held on.

She held on, and showed Bella nothing at all.

Bella looked down at the wolf, and at Leah Clearwater sitting at its feet with her face tipped up, and at the line of nothing that ran between them that Bella couldn't see and Marcus could have, if Marcus weren't a grey heap on the far side of the field.

*You've got no idea what you've tied yourself to, Lassie.*

"Neither does he," Bella said, under her breath.

Edward looked at her.

"He doesn't," she said. "Listen to him. He's making it up as he goes, the same as the rest of us." She tightened her hand on her daughter's. "He just doesn't look like it."

### VIII. Sam

The black wolf was bigger than him.

Sam had known it would be half a second before the shape finished pouring itself out across the beach. He'd known it from the growl. You could tell a lot from a growl: how deep it went, how much chest it came out of, where the floor was. He'd been Alpha for two and a half years now, since before Jacob, before any of them, since the weeks he'd spent alone in the woods with nobody to tell him what was happening to his body, and he'd learned to hear size in a sound the way you learn to hear a truck's weight in the rumble of its engine before it comes round the bend.

He'd still been wrong. It was bigger than he'd thought.

His whole pack had gone crazy.

Paul first. Paul was always first. The silver wolf had come up off his haunches the instant the stranger started to lean, and by the time the black wolf was standing on the shingle, Paul was up on his toes with every hair along his back on end and his head low and a snarl coming out of him that was half rage and half something Sam recognised from inside his own chest and would never, ever say out loud to Paul, which was fear.

*It's a trick, it's some kind of trick, Sam, it's a TRICK—*

*It talked.* Jared. *Sam, did you hear it? It TALKED—*

*How's it bigger than Jake. How is it BIGGER than—*

Collin was yelping, high and thin, a pup's yelp, over and over. Brady had backed up so far that his hindquarters were in the trees.

*Hold*, Sam said.

Nobody heard him. Or they heard him and the fear was louder. He felt it go through the five of them like wind through tall grass, all of them bending the same way at once—back, away, *away*—and under the fear something else, the thing he'd felt himself when the growl first came across the field. His body wanting to lower its head.

His body. Sam Uley's body, wanting to put its head down to a stranger on a beach.

*HOLD.*

He put the Alpha into it. He didn't like to. He hated to; he always had. It was the one part of all this he'd never stopped hating, the way it reached into another person and closed a hand round them. But he put the whole weight of it behind that one word, and he felt it land on his pack like a hand on five sets of shoulders, and they held. Paul stopped mid-snarl with his lips still back. Collin stopped yelping. Brady stopped backing up.

They held.

Over on the right, Jacob's pack was howling. Seth had started it and the other two had gone up with him, three wolves with their heads thrown back and their throats wide open, and in the middle of them, on two legs, bare-chested in the snow with Renesmee clutched against his shoulder, Jacob was shouting Leah's name.

She wasn't answering. Sam could see her from here. She was sitting on the stones at the edge of the lake in black cutoffs and a grey T-shirt he was fairly sure was Seth's, with her bare legs stuck out in front of her, looking up at the black wolf with her face tipped back. Sam had known Leah all her life. He'd watched her face do every single thing a face could do. He knew precisely what it looked like when she was afraid.

Whatever was on it now, he had never seen there before.

*Sam.* Jared, low and careful. *Sam. The law.*

*I know.*

*She imprinted on it. On him. Whatever he is. We can't—*

*I know, Jared.*

*So if it comes for us—if it decides to—we can't even—*

*It won't.* Sam didn't know how he knew. He knew anyway. *It won't.*

*You don't know that*, Paul snarled.

*No*, Sam said. *I don't.*

Down on the beach, the black wolf lifted its head.

Sam watched it look up the slope. Watched the green eyes, green, God, the same green as in the man's face, move along the hill. Across the Cullens. Across Jacob's pack, still howling, and Jacob himself, still shouting. Then, without any hurry at all, along to the left. To Sam's pack. To Sam.

It looked at him.

It was three hundred yards off, and it was a wolf, and it was looking at him, and Sam understood very clearly that it knew exactly who he was.

It had been in Bella's head. Bella knew who Sam was. Bella knew about Emily, about Leah, about all of it; Jacob had made sure of that, a long time ago, on a beach. The thing down there had sat in a red armchair by the lake and looked at Leah's whole life from the outside, the way Bella had seen it. Sam's part in it too.

Sam's body wanted to put its head down.

He kept it up.

He stood at the front of his pack with his legs braced in the snow and his head high, and he looked back. Three hundred yards, across the snow and the ash and the slush, into those green eyes. He didn't growl. He didn't show his teeth. He stood there and held it, because he was the Alpha of the Quileute wolves, the first of them in seventy years, and because whatever happened next he would not lower his head to the thing that Leah had—

To the thing Leah had.

He held it.

The black wolf held it too. A long moment. Then it dipped its head, barely, an inch if that, and looked away.

Back down at Leah.

Sam let his breath out through his teeth.

*What was that*, Paul said. *What did it just—did it just—*

*I don't know*, Sam said, and he didn't. A challenge. An acknowledgement. Something in between that he didn't have a word for. Nothing had ever looked at him like that.

He didn't get time to think about it.

The sound came from behind them.

From the top of the field, from the north edge where the trees came down thick and dark: a crack. Sharp and flat and loud, like a rifle going off in a small room. Then another, half a second after. Then three more, almost together, *crack crack crack*, slapping off the trunks and rolling down the hill, and every head on that slope, wolf and vampire, snapped round at once.

Five people stood in the snow at the edge of the trees where a moment before there had been nobody.

Sam stared at them.

Long coats. Dark, most of them. One in a green so bright it hurt to look at against all that white, the green of a lime cut open. One of them held what looked like a clipboard. Two of them had sticks in their hands, thin dark sticks held low and pointed out, and they'd stepped apart from each other the way cops step apart when they get out of a cruiser. The last of the five was small and young and had a hand pressed over her mouth, staring past all of them at the far side of the field where the grey heaps lay.

*More of them*, Jared breathed. *There's MORE of them.*

One of the two with sticks turned. His eyes went across the field, across the vampires, and stopped on Sam's pack. On five huge wolves on the edge of the trees, forty yards from him, with their hackles up.

His stick came up.

Paul's snarl came back up with it, full-throated, and Sam felt his whole pack lean forward against the hold he had on them, and felt the hold start to slip.

And down on the beach, the black wolf turned its great head toward the noise, and Sam heard it, clear across three hundred yards of snow, in that low dry growl of a voice.

"Oh, *now* they turn up."

### IX. Harry

Geneva had sent five.

He picked them out from the beach without trying. The wolf's eyes were flatter on colour than his own and a good deal sharper on movement, and they found the five against the treeline at the top of the field like pins stuck in a corkboard.

The lime-green was a Healer. You couldn't miss a Healer; that was the point of the robes. Tall, thin, a man, already turning his head toward the Cullens' end of the field like a pointer that's caught the scent of a shot bird. Good. That was the one he'd wanted.

The one with the clipboard was Registry. Registry always had a clipboard. Short, square, a woman in a charcoal coat, already writing.

The two with their wands out were Enforcement, and somebody in Geneva had sent them because somebody in Geneva had heard *Volturi* in his Patronus message and sensibly decided to bring some muscle. They'd find out in a minute that the muscle wasn't needed. They'd find out a minute after that how little use it would have been if it had been. One of them already had his wand up and pointed at the biggest, blackest wolf on the hill, and the wolf's pack was leaning toward him like a dam about to go.

The fifth one had her hand over her mouth. Young. Duffel coat. Satchel. Staring at the ash heaps on the far side of the field with her eyes gone round.

Beings Division, he'd bet. Somebody's bright junior. Probably the only person in the office who'd ever read the file.

The file had been one page long.

Four seconds, he thought. Maybe three. He thought about shouting from where he was. He thought about how far a voice had to carry, and what that bloke on the hill would do with his wand if a wolf the size of a lorry started bellowing at him across a lake.

He came back up out of the wolf instead.

He did it the way he'd gone in, without fuss, the black running back up into a man's shape like water finding its level, and then he was standing on the shingle on two feet in his boots and his jeans and the green flannel and the leather jacket, every stitch where it had been, the collar still turned up on the left side the way it had been turned up all day. He rolled his shoulders once. He felt the cold properly on his face and his hands for the first time since he'd landed, and it didn't bother him.

Leah was staring at his jacket.

"Your clothes," she said.

"Yeah."

"They didn't—you didn't—"

"Different system," he said, and watched her face go flat with outrage, and very nearly smiled.

Then he turned toward the hill and let his voice go up and out, the way he'd let it go across the field when he first landed. He didn't shout. He didn't need to. It carried up the slope to the treeline as clearly as if he'd been standing next to them.

"Oi. Wands *down*, the pair of you."

The one with his wand up jerked as though somebody had flicked his ear.

"Big black one's the local law, and he's had a long day. They're with me. Mostly." He pointed up the hill at the Cullen end of the field without looking round. "Healer. The kid in the red coat, by the big lad with no shirt on. Head trauma. Legilimency, mine, went through a shield to get there, there was a nosebleed. Her family's got a vial off me. They haven't used it. Check it before anybody does." He paused. "And mind the blonde."

The Healer was already moving.

"And you with the clipboard," Harry said. "Don't you dare start writing till I'm up there."

The woman with the clipboard looked down the slope at him for a long second. Then, deliberately, she clicked her pen and put it in her pocket.

The wand on the hill came down. Slowly. Up the slope, the big black wolf at the front of his pack didn't move, but Harry watched the pack behind him stop leaning, one wolf at a time, until they were back on their heels.

Right.

He looked down at the woman sitting on the stones at his feet.

She'd twisted round to watch all of it. Now she was looking back at him, with her bare knees drawn up and her arms wrapped round them, and the line between them sat quiet and taut and steady as a bridge cable. She'd stopped shaking. He didn't know when.

"Who are they?" she said.

"Paperwork."

"That's not an answer either."

"It's the most honest one you'll get off anybody today." He watched the Healer go down the hill toward the Cullens in a flap of lime-green, and watched the blonde step out to meet him, and watched the doctor step out faster and put himself in the middle, one hand raised, and the Healer stop and look at him, and look at his hand, and hold out his own. Two doctors. They'd be fine. They'd bore everyone senseless. He looked back at Leah. "Coming?"

She looked up at him a long second. At his face, then at his chest, at the place where the line went in, then back at his face.

Then she got up. She did it in one movement, the way the wolves did everything, and stood on the stones barefoot in her brother's shirt and brushed the grit off the backs of her legs with both hands, and didn't look at him while she did it.

"This," she said, and jabbed two fingers into the middle of her own chest, hard, "doesn't get a vote."

He thought about that.

"Fair enough," he said.

"I'm walking up there because I want to see Seth's face when you tell that woman with the clipboard what you are."

"I'm not telling her what I am."

"Then I want to see her face when she asks."

That, he understood completely.

They went up the slope together.

She kept a careful yard of air between them the whole way. She walked on his left. When the wind turned and came off the lake behind them, cold enough to make the reeds hiss, she didn't move in closer, and he didn't either. The line didn't care. It ran across that yard of air as steady as if they'd been standing nose to nose, and down at the bottom of him, in the dark, the thing it was tied to lay with its chin on its forepaws and watched her walk.

Off to the right, across the top of the field, the grey heaps lay on their circles of wet grass where the snow had flashed off them. From down here they didn't look like much. Little drifts of something somebody had swept up and forgotten to put in the bin. He counted them going up the hill, because he always counted, and got the number he'd expected: thirty-odd, and the Romanian's long smear, and two at the back lying side by side where the wives had stood.

He'd write every one of them up tonight. Every name he had, from the Confederation file and from the kid's head and her mum's. The ones he didn't have a name for he'd put down as unknown, with a description, so that somebody somewhere could match them up if anybody ever came asking. Somebody would. Somebody always did. He'd told them to disperse twice, and they'd decided against it, and he'd decided the rest. That went in the report too, in his own words, with his name at the bottom.

He went looking for guilt about it, the way he'd prod a tooth to see if it hurt. He knew the shape of guilt. He'd had plenty. He found the weight of it, thirty-odd and one, and nothing else, and the weight was his, and he'd carry it. That came with the job.

On the hill, one of the Denali women had lifted her head and was watching him come. The fair-haired one, the one whose hands sparked. He'd seen her in Bella's memory, this morning, screaming at the black cloaks while they held her sister down in the snow and lit her. She'd want to talk to him. Not yet. She didn't know yet what she'd say. He could see that from here.

Later, then.

Seth met them halfway.

He came down the hill at a dead run, a sandy wolf the size of a horse, gangly with it, all legs, and he came straight for his sister and then saw Harry and tried to stop and couldn't. He skidded. He went the last ten yards on his haunches in a spray of wet snow and came to a halt in a heap about four feet from Harry's boots, and lay there, panting, with his ears going in two different directions.

"Seth," Leah said, in a voice like a closing door. "Don't."

Seth looked at her. Then he looked at Harry. Then, very slowly, he stretched his neck out along the snow and sniffed, a long, careful, thorough sniff that went from Harry's boots up as far as he could reach without getting up.

Harry stood still and let him.

The sandy wolf's eyes went wide. He looked at his sister. He looked back at Harry. His tail, which had been tucked under him, came out from under and went thump, once, against the snow, and then he seemed to remember himself and tucked it back.

"You'll be the brother," Harry said.

Seth whuffed.

"She's all right. Your sister." He glanced at Leah, who had folded her arms and was staring very hard at the treeline. "Bit cold. Bit cross. All right."

"I'm not *cross*," said Leah.

"Bit cross," Harry said to Seth.

Seth looked between the two of them. Then he scrambled up, all elbows, and fell in on Leah's other side, so close his shoulder brushed hers, and the three of them went on up the hill. Seth kept turning his head to look at Harry. Every time he did, Harry looked back, and every time, Seth looked away fast and pretended he hadn't been looking.

Halfway up, without turning her head, Leah said, "I'm not calling you Harry."

"Didn't think you would."

"Ever."

"Right."

They went a few more steps. The slush squelched under her bare feet. The snow went to water under his.

"Potter," she said, as if she were tasting it, and made a face.

"Could be worse," he said. "Could be Lassie."

She hit him in the arm.

Hard. Properly hard, a closed fist with her whole shoulder behind it, hard enough that a normal man would have been carrying the bruise for a week. It rocked him half a step sideways into Seth, who yelped.

It was the first time she'd touched him. He was fairly sure she hadn't noticed.

He had. So had the thing at the bottom of him, which lifted its head in the dark, and looked, and settled, and put its chin back down on its forepaws.

It went on watching her.
