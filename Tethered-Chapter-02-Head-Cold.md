# Tethered

## Chapter Two: Head Cold

### I. Leah

Up close, his heart was the loudest thing in the field.

She'd stopped at ten feet. She still didn't know whose decision that had been. Her legs had carried her across forty yards of snow and ash without asking her, past the long grey fan of what was left of the Romanian, and then at ten feet they'd locked, and here she was, close enough to see the stitching on the shoulder of his jacket, with her lips peeled back off every tooth she owned and a snarl sitting in her chest like a stone she couldn't cough up. From the pack line his heartbeat had been a drum under the wind. From here it came up through the frozen ground and into the pads of her paws. Slow. Heavy. Unbothered. A heart that had just watched thirty-odd vampires go up like flash paper and hadn't sped up once.

*Thought you might say that*, he'd said.

Then he'd gone on standing there with his hands in his pockets, looking at her, and Leah had gone on snarling at him, because she'd started it, and she was damned if she was going to be the one who stopped.

Leah. Jacob, from the line behind her, from the inside of her skull. Come back.

I can't.

Can't or won't?

Does it matter? She didn't take her eyes off the stranger. Does it actually matter, Jake?

He didn't answer. Which meant it didn't. Which meant he knew.

Is she okay? Seth, high and anxious, the way he'd sounded at the funeral. Somebody tell me if she's okay. I can't see her face from here.

She's standing next to the guy who just set the Volturi on fire, Embry said. Define okay.

He called her Lassie again, Quil said. Like, to her face. On purpose.

I was THERE, Ateara.

The wind came round off the mountains and laid his scent over her, and her nose did what it had been doing since the second he'd arrived, which was refuse to leave it alone.

Pine first. Wet earth, the black kind you turned up under the ferns. Cold stone, creek-bottom stone. At sixty yards that had been nearly all of it, and the other smell had been a thread she'd had to go digging for. At ten feet she didn't have to dig. It came off him in a thick dark wave under the forest smell: fur, musk, frost on old leaves, the hot iron edge of something that ate meat and had been doing it for a long time. Wolf. Big wolf. Her nose kept telling her how big and she kept not listening, because the number her nose came up with was bigger than Sam, bigger than Jacob, bigger than anything that had ever come down out of the hills above La Push, and that was stupid. He was six foot something of English guy in a leather jacket. There wasn't room in him.

Her nose didn't care. Her nose said big.

And the line.

At sixty yards it had dragged at her like a fish hook set behind her breastbone. At ten feet it had gone quiet. It sat in the middle of her chest and hummed, low and steady, the way the power lines hummed out along the 101 on a still night, and the quiet was so much worse than the pulling that she could barely stand still inside it. The pulling she could fight. She'd been fighting things for two years. The quiet felt like getting what she wanted.

She hadn't wanted anything in two years. She'd forgotten what it felt like. Apparently it felt like this: standing ten feet from a stranger in a snowfield with her hackles up, while every part of her that wasn't Leah lay down and sighed.

She snarled harder, out of spite.

He watched her do it. His face didn't change much. There was a small crease between his brows, the same one she'd seen from across the field, and his eyes, which were the bright hard green of a beer bottle held up to the sun, went over her once from ears to tail the way you'd look over a car somebody was trying to sell you. They stopped on her chest for a second. Right on the spot where the line went in. Then they came back up to her face.

Steam was still lifting off his shoulders. Round his boots the slush had spread into a puddle. Leah's own paws had left damp prints where she'd walked, because she ran hot, they all ran hot, a hundred and eight degrees on a January morning. His boots had left water. The snow was going away from him in a circle, slowly, steadily, as if it had been asked to.

He looked up at the sky. A grey lid of cloud, no sun, the purple smoke from the Volturi heaps dragging across it in long greasy rags. He looked over at the Cullens. He looked at the ash. He looked, briefly, at his own left wrist, where there wasn't a watch.

"Could be a while," he said, to nobody.

He looked round the way a man looks round a waiting room. There was nowhere to sit. There was snow, and patches of wet grass, and little grey heaps of what had been people. His eyes went past all of it to the north edge of the clearing, where a dead spruce lay along the treeline with its root-plate up in the air like a dinner plate stood on end.

The spruce cracked.

The noise went across the field like a shot. Every vampire on the Cullen side jerked, a whole row of pale heads whipping round at once. Leah jumped. She actually jumped, all four feet off the ground and a yard sideways, and landed with her hackles standing straight up, and in her head Quil swore and Seth yelped and Embry said *holy*— and Jacob said nothing at all.

A length of the trunk had come away clean at both ends, six feet of it, grey and barkless and split pale at the breaks. It came across the clearing toward them on its own. It slid flat along the ground, ploughing a furrow through the snow as it came and throwing up a low white bow wave on either side like a boat, steady as a sled on a towrope. Forty yards. It slowed as it got near him. It stopped behind his knees, exactly behind his knees, and settled into the snow with a creak.

He sat down on it.

He sat the way her dad used to sit on the tailgate of the truck after a day out on the boat, heavily, elbows on his knees, a man who'd been on his feet too long. The log took his weight and creaked again.

Leah stood in the snow a yard from where she'd been standing and felt her face burn under her fur. She'd jumped. In front of him. Like a cat that had seen a cucumber.

Smooth, Embry said.

Shut up.

He moves logs with his brain, Seth said, in a voice of total wonder. Did everyone see that? He just moved a log with his brain.

We saw, Seth.

The stranger held out his right hand, palm up, the same way he'd held it out for the silver deer. Over on the Cullen side somebody hissed. Leah's weight went back onto her haunches, ready.

Something landed in his palm.

It was a book. It was the biggest book Leah had ever seen outside the library in Port Angeles, where they kept the giant atlas on its own wooden stand by the window. It was the size of a paving stone and as thick as her forearm, her human forearm, and when it arrived his whole arm dipped about an inch under the weight of it and a little spray of slush jumped up off the ground. It was bound in grey leather gone dark and shiny at the corners where hands had held it for a long time. Iron caps on the corners. A heavy iron clasp across the front. It looked like something that ought to be chained to a desk in a room with no windows.

He set it on his knees. The log creaked a third time. He undid the clasp with his thumb and opened the cover, and the pages went past his thumb in a long dry rush like wind through dead grass.

He licked his thumb and turned a page.

For a second the pack mind went absolutely silent.

Then:

He's reading, Quil said.

He's READING?

In the middle of— he's got ash on his JACKET—

Is it a spellbook? Seth. Is it, like, an actual spellbook? Lee, can you see? What's in it?

Leah could see. She could see the spine, because it was turned toward her, and she had a wolf's eyes, and the letters were stamped into the grey leather in gold leaf that had mostly flaked off. She read it without wanting to.

BONDS AND BINDINGS.

Under that, smaller: *Of the Soul, the Oath, and the Shifting Peoples.*

Under that, smaller still, stamped in red over the gold like somebody had been very cross about it: RESTRICTED. NOT TO BE REMOVED FROM LEVEL NINE.

The Shifting Peoples.

She read it again.

Oh my God, she said. He's looking me up.

What?

He's looking me up. In a book. She could hear her own voice going high and thin in her head and she couldn't make it stop. Like I'm a rash. Like I'm some weird bug he found in his kitchen and he wants to know if it bites.

Okay, but, Seth said, and she could feel him trying, could feel him reaching for the bright side the way Seth always reached for the bright side, a puppy going after a ball. Okay, but that's kind of good? Lee? He's trying to figure out what it is. He's trying to work it out.

He's looking me up in an encyclopedia, Seth.

She took a step forward. She didn't decide to. She was going to put her teeth through that book, through the grey leather and the iron and the dry old pages, and see how much reading he got done then.

The line hummed.

She stopped.

She stood there with one forepaw lifted off the snow and her lip still up and the hum going through her chest, low and patient, and her paw wouldn't come down any further forward than it was. It came down where it was. Right where it was, in its own print.

Leah, Jacob said quietly. Lee. Come back to the line.

No.

She sat down instead.

She sat in the snow ten feet from him with her tail wrapped round her front feet and her ears pinned and her eyes on his face, and she told herself she was doing it to keep an eye on him. Somebody had to. He'd burned thirty-odd vampires with a snap of his fingers and an old Romanian without bothering to turn around, and now he was sitting on a log in the middle of a field fourteen miles out of Forks reading about the Shifting Peoples, and somebody had to watch what he did next.

That was what she told herself. Everybody in her head heard her tell it. Nobody was stupid enough to say a word.

From where she sat she could see the pages, upside down, every time he turned one. They were crammed. Tiny black print in two columns, and the margins round it stuffed with handwriting, dozens of different hands in green and red and purple ink, little arrows, crossings-out, one word underlined so hard the pen had gone through the paper. In one corner somebody had drawn a small, careful picture of a hare. He was going through the book backwards, a dozen pages at a time, toward the very end, the way her mom went through the phone book.

Maybe it's a dictionary, Embry said. Maybe he's looking up what Lassie means.

He knows what Lassie means, Quil said. That's the whole problem.

Leah ignored them both.

He got to the back. He stopped turning. He didn't look at her.

Fine. If he was going to sit there and read about her, she was going to sit here and watch him do it.

### II. Harry

The book had been out of the building for four years, and Records still sent him a memo about it on the first of every month.

It came as a violet paper aeroplane, same as every other Ministry memo, and it found him wherever he was. It had found him on a rooftop in Marrakesh, and in a hospital bed in Oslo, and once, memorably, in the bath. The wording never changed. *Mr Potter. The Records Office notes that* Bonds and Bindings *(Restricted, Level Nine, one volume) remains outstanding. Kindly return it at your earliest convenience.* He'd taken it out for a job in Cork, a farmer's wife who'd woken up one Tuesday convinced she was married to a hare, and he'd been meaning to take it back ever since. There was always one more job.

He kept it where he kept the cigarettes. He'd stopped trying to explain where that was.

He went straight to the back.

The index was forty blank pages with INDEX across the top of the first one in a copperplate hand. That was the clever bit, and the reason nobody in Records had ever wanted it out of the building. You put a finger on the paper and thought about what you were after, and the book went and found it. It was fussy about it. Ask it something vague and it gave you three hundred entries and a headache. Ask it something precise and it gave you the page. It had been built by people who thought precision was a moral virtue, which was the Department all over.

So he got precise.

He laid it out in his head the way he'd lay out a report. What he had. Only what he had.

One. It had started on sight. He'd turned his head and looked at the wolves, and the twinge had gone in under his breastbone at the exact second his eyes landed on hers. He'd put it down to the Apparition. Long hop, Geneva to here. He'd been wrong about that.

Two. It came from her. Her end of it matched the thing he'd seen in Bella Cullen's head: a tall brown kid on a beach at night with a fire going, telling a girl who wasn't a vampire yet that it was like gravity moving. The wolves did this. It was theirs.

Three. It went into him. Through the breastbone, past the heart, past the place in the middle of him where his magic sat, past every wall he'd built down there, into the dark at the bottom. A thing like that ought to stop at the heart. This one had kept going.

Four. Something at the bottom had taken hold of it without consulting him. He didn't love that bit.

Five. He could feel her. He could feel her right now, with his eyes on the page. Ten feet off, a bit to his left, low to the ground. Sitting, by the angle of it. And something that might have been her mood, if he squinted, in the way the line drew tight and went slack again, the way an old break in your arm tells you it's going to rain. No words in it. Nothing he'd have called a thought.

That was the lot.

He put his finger on the first blank page and thought: *bond, formed on sight, originating with a shapeshifter.*

Ink came up out of the paper like damp coming through a wall. A column of entries, page numbers down the right-hand side, in a small, neat, faintly smug hand.

He started at the top.

*SOUL-BONDS, so called. p. 212.*

The pages were a mix. Paper mostly, some vellum, a run of sheets near the middle that were thinner than either and slightly see-through and that he'd learned the hard way not to touch with bare skin. The margins were full. Fifty years of Unspeakables had written in this book, in every colour of ink going, and they'd argued with each other in the white space round the print. He read the margins first. He'd learned that in his sixth year at school, from a potions textbook with somebody else's name in it, and the lesson had very nearly got a boy killed in a bathroom. He still read the margins first.

The entry itself was short and tired.

*The Department has examined forty-one claimed instances since 1803. Of these, twenty-nine proved to be the effects of a love potion; eight, the ordinary effects of love; three, Unbreakable Vows taken in drink and subsequently forgotten. One remains under seal. Claimants commonly report shared dreams; the sensation of the bonded party's pain as their own; communication without speech; and a golden light about the pair, visible to sympathetic observers. The Department has never observed the golden light.*

Underneath, in green ink, somebody had written: *Nor has anyone sober.*

Harry breathed out through his nose.

He went through it anyway, because you did. Shared dreams: he hadn't slept since Tuesday. Couldn't say. Pain: she'd yelped twice out there across the snow, an awful thin sound like a kicked pup, and both times the line had jerked in his chest like a float going under, and both times that was all. A jerk. He hadn't felt her pain. He'd felt that she was in some. Communication without speech: he'd called her Lassie out loud and she'd heard it with her ears and stopped whining because of it, which counted as speech in any book he'd ever read. Golden light. He looked down at himself. Leather. Flannel. Steam. A puddle.

No golden light.

Two lines further down the index, the book had volunteered something he hadn't asked for. *MATES, FATED (popular usage). See NOVELS, SENTIMENTAL; see also NONSENSE.* He didn't bother turning to it. Somebody in the Department had had a sense of humour once, about eighty years ago, and the index had kept it like a fly in amber.

*OATH-BONDS. See UNBREAKABLE VOW, p. 9; LIFE-DEBT, p. 31.*

He checked them anyway, quickly, the way you go back and check a door you know you locked. An Unbreakable Vow took two wands and a Bonder and a pair of clasped hands and the words said out loud, three times, and when it took you knew about it, because it came down round your wrists like a line of fire. He hadn't held anybody's hand. The only word he'd said to her was *Lassie*. He didn't think that counted.

A life-debt, then. One life saved, and the magic made a note of it and sat on it till it was paid. He'd saved a fair few lives in the last half hour, if you counted the ones the Volturi had come here to take. Hers wasn't one of them. She'd been sixty yards off with her claws in the ground when it started. And a life-debt didn't hum. He'd been owed one, once, by a man with a silver hand. He'd never felt it at all. The man who owed it had felt it, right at the end, when the hand turned round on him.

He turned the page on that one quite fast.

He went back to the index.

*VEELA, THE CHOOSING OF. p. 1,140.*

He'd had this one explained to him already, at length, by somebody who'd know. Fleur had told him all about it one Christmas at the Burrow, over the second pudding, with her hand on Bill's knee and a great deal of detail that Bill had found very funny and Ron had found a lot to sit through. Her grandmother had been Veela. Veela chose, Fleur said. Once. And it was the Veela who was bound by it, and the man got nothing but the allure, the same as every other idiot in range.

The book agreed with her, more stiffly. *The binding rests wholly with the Veela. The chosen party is affected by the allure as any person in proximity is affected, and by nothing further. He remains free. She does not.*

He read the last two lines twice.

That was her end, near enough. Bound on her side; nothing asked of the other. If it had stopped at his breastbone he'd have said fine, it's a wolf version of that, and shut the book and gone and had a word with the red wolf about his lot's folklore.

It hadn't stopped at his breastbone. And she wasn't Veela. Fleur would have had something to say about the comparison. In French, probably, with her hands.

*PATRONUS, ALTERATION OF, IN CASES OF PROFOUND ATTACHMENT. p. 388.*

He didn't need that entry. He'd watched it happen. Tonks's had changed the year she'd fallen for Remus. It had gone big and four-legged and shaggy, and she'd hated it at first because she'd thought everyone would know what it meant. Everyone had known.

His stag had gone off to Geneva twenty minutes ago with all its antlers on, the same shape it had been since he was thirteen.

He thought about Tonks's wolf for a second longer than he'd meant to. Then he turned the page.

*LYCANTHROPY, AFFINITIES AMONG THE AFFLICTED. p. 1,502.* He skimmed it. Loose packs under the moon. Nothing that survived sunrise. *No bond formed on sight has been recorded.* He'd known a werewolf for most of his life, near enough. He didn't need a book to tell him what the moon did to friendships.

*ANIMAGI, WHETHER CAPABLE OF BONDING. p. 74.*

One line. *They are not. The animal form is the witch or wizard, and nothing besides; there is no second party within to be bound.*

He looked at that one for a while.

He'd have agreed with it, an hour ago. The thing at the bottom of him was him. He'd been clear on that for years. No second party. Just him, with fewer words and worse manners. By the book's logic the line should have gone into him and found nowhere to go.

It had found somewhere. Something down there had come up off the floor and taken hold of it the way a dog takes a stick out of the air. He hadn't sent it. He'd have liked to think he'd have been asked.

*SELKIES, THE SKIN-BOND. p. 1,877.*

He nearly skipped that one. He knew what it said. Whoever holds the skin holds the selkie. And the selkie stays, and keeps your house, and loves you after a fashion, and goes down to the shore every night of its life to look at the water. Tied without a say.

He read it anyway. It was every bit as bad as he remembered.

He looked up from it. First time since he'd opened the book.

She was sitting ten feet off in the snow with her tail wrapped round her front feet like a cat, staring at him. Grey. Pale grey, darker along the spine. Smaller than the others by a fair margin and longer in the leg, built for running. Her ears were flat. Her lip was still up on one side, as if she'd forgotten to put it down. The fur along her back stood up in a ridge from her neck to the base of her tail.

She hadn't been asked either.

He looked back down and turned the pages faster.

*QUILEUTE, WOLF-LINEAGE OF (Olympic Peninsula, Washington). p. 2,206.*

Two thousand pages in, near the back, in the thin part where the entries got short because nobody from London had ever been anywhere. Half a page. Half a page for a whole people and whatever it was they did. The hand was older than the others, in brown ink gone the colour of weak tea.

*Recorded 1911, from the account of a trapper of the Hoh River district, communicated by letter. The writer has not visited.*

Harry snorted.

The trapper's account described the men of a coastal tribe who took the form of very large wolves, said to come upon them in the presence of the cold ones — *see VAMPIRES, Part Two* — and whose numbers rose and fell with the number of cold ones about. They were said to be bound to their country and their chief by a tie the trapper couldn't describe. And:

*The wolves are further said to fix upon a person at first sight, and thereafter to attend upon that person for life, in whatever capacity is required. The person so fixed upon is not affected. No reciprocal effect has been observed or reported. Once fixed, the wolf is as constant and biddable as a good sheepdog to its shepherd, and asks no more reward.*

In the margin, in pencil: *Source: one trapper. ONE.*

Under that, in a different pencil, years later by the look of it: *Did anybody ever go?*

Nobody had gone. You could tell from the half a page.

Harry read the sheepdog line again. He couldn't help himself. It was such a stupid sentence. It had been written in a room in London by a man who'd had a letter from a trapper who'd once seen a wolf, and that was as close to the Olympic Peninsula as the Department of Mysteries had ever got, and it had sat in a restricted book for the best part of a century being the sum total of what anyone knew.

"'As constant and biddable,'" he read out, "'as a good sheepdog to its shepherd.'" He turned his head and looked at her. "Lassie. Told you."

She snarled.

A real one this time, with sound behind it: a ripping, wet, rising snarl that showed every tooth she had and the black gums behind them. The ridge on her back went up another inch. She came half up out of the snow with her forelegs braced and her head low.

The thing in his chest answered her.

He didn't decide to. He'd have liked to be able to say he decided to. There wasn't time to decide anything. The sound came up out of the bottom of him, up the line, the same way the line had come in, and out of his mouth before the part of him that made decisions had so much as looked up from the page. It was low. It was a long way lower than his voice went. It came out of a chest a good deal bigger than the one he was wearing: a deep, rolling, grinding noise like a slab of rock being dragged across another slab, and it went on, and on, and he felt it in the log under him and in the book on his knees and in the soles of his boots.

Her snarl stopped dead.

Everything stopped. He heard the whole field do it at once. Every vampire on the Cullen side went still, the particular stillness their kind went to when something bigger came into the room. The big dark-haired one, Emmett, had come halfway up out of his crouch and stayed there, frozen at the knees. The blonde had swung an arm across the kid on the red wolf's back without looking down. Over on the left flank, both packs had gone flat-eared, the whole lot of them, the red wolf and the three with him and the big black one and the four round him, a whole row of ears going back at the same instant like a field of long grass under a gust. Somebody yipped. The sandy one, he thought.

She'd sat back down. Hard, all at once, like somebody had kicked her legs out. Her ears were flat to her skull. Her mouth was shut.

The growl ran out.

Harry shut his mouth.

He sat there a second. He breathed in through his nose, slowly, and let it go again. Down at the bottom of him, in the dark, the wolf lay with its chin on its forepaws and its ears pricked, looking up the line at her, and its tail moved once against the floor.

It wasn't sorry. He checked.

He cleared his throat.

"Sorry," he said. He didn't sound it, much. He could hear that he didn't. "Got a bit of a cold."

He turned the page.

*No reciprocal effect has been observed or reported.*

He sat looking at that for a while. He could feel the line. Humming. Tighter than it had been, with a sort of thrum still going along it, like a wire just after something heavy has swung on it and let go. She'd be sat there thinking about that growl. She'd be thinking about it for a while.

"Observe that, then," he muttered.

He went back to the index. He'd done it the polite way. Now he'd do it the Department's way, which was to be as precise as he knew how and see what fell out.

He put his finger on the paper and thought: *bond, formed on sight, originating with a shapeshifter, reciprocal, terminating in a wizard who is also—*

He stopped there, because he didn't have a word for the next bit, and the book would want one. He tried anyway. He thought about the dark at the bottom of him, and the long black shape lying in it with green eyes and a slow tail, and he thought: *that.*

The ink came up. One word, at the top of the page, in the neat smug hand.

*See:*

Then nothing. The page sat there with *See:* written across the top of it and white space underneath, and as he watched, the word went pale, and paler, and soaked back down into the paper like water into sand, until the page was as blank as it had been when he started.

He tried it shorter. *Reciprocal shapeshifter bond.*

Nothing.

*Shapeshifter, bonded to wizard.*

*ANIMAGI, WHETHER CAPABLE OF BONDING. p. 74. They are not.*

*Wizard, shapeshifting, neither Animagus nor afflicted.*

Blank. It didn't even give him a *See:* this time. The page just lay there, white, like a face that had decided not to answer.

He took his finger off it.

Course it wasn't in there.

He'd been the only known survivor of the Killing Curse at fifteen months old. He'd been the fourth champion in a three-champion tournament. He'd had a sliver of somebody else's soul in his head for seventeen years without anybody noticing, and then he'd walked into a forest to die and it hadn't taken. Hermione had spent the best part of seven years in the library on his behalf, and the books had never once had the answer, and she'd gone on looking anyway because she was Hermione, and nine times out of ten the answer had turned out to be: Harry's done something daft and nobody's ever done it before.

Nothing about him had ever been in a book. Apart from the books about him, and those were mostly wrong.

He shut it. The iron clasp snapped across. He sat there with both hands flat on the cover and the steam coming up off his shoulders and the log creaking under him, and the grey wolf ten feet off with her ears still pinned and her eyes on his face.

He didn't look at her. He looked at the cover. BONDS AND BINDINGS. *Of the Soul, the Oath, and the Shifting Peoples.*

"Useless," he told it.

The book didn't argue.

### III. Edward

The stranger shut the book, and the only mind in the field that could tell Edward where he was belonged to Leah Clearwater.

He'd been listening for the man for twenty-two minutes now, since the crack. He'd given up on hearing his thoughts inside the first one. There was nothing there to hear. With Bella, before, there had always been something at the edge of the silence, a soft blank hum like the hiss on an empty radio frequency, a place where a mind obviously was even if he couldn't get into it. With this man there was nothing at all. A gap in the world, man-shaped, sitting on a log. Edward had kept listening anyway, the way you go on pressing a light switch after the bulb's gone.

So he'd done what he could. He'd built the man out of everyone else. Two dozen minds on this side of the field were pointed at the stranger, and every one of them was seeing him, and Edward had been piecing the picture together out of their eyes like a portrait made from shards of mirror. Carlisle's view of the man's hands. Emmett's view of his shoulders. Garrett's careful, professional, three-hundred-year-old soldier's view of his feet, his weight, the way he sat. Zafrina's view of his face.

Then the stranger had growled, and every shard had cracked at once.

The echo of it was still going round in them. It had gone through the vampire minds on Edward's side of the field like a fire alarm through a sleeping house. None of them had found words for it at first. Under the words, under everything that centuries of civilisation had piled on top of them, every vampire carried the old animal brain that had been there before the venom, and the old animal brain had heard that sound and said one thing, in every head at once: *bigger.*

Emmett, who wrestled grizzlies for sport, had thought very clearly: *That's the bear. That's the one I never found.* Zafrina had thought of a jaguar she'd heard once in the black above a river, a hundred and fifty years ago, and the way the whole forest had stopped breathing for it. Garrett had thought nothing for a full second, and then a long, low, deeply felt obscenity, and then, with the dry interest of a man who'd seen most things: *Well. That answers the smell.*

The wolves had been worse, because the wolves had words for it.

*That's a wolf*, Seth had thought, and then all of them were thinking it, Jacob's pack and Sam's on top of each other, nine minds at once in two separate choruses that couldn't hear each other and were saying the same thing anyway. *That's a WOLF. That came out of HIM. Did you hear how big—* And under it, from Sam's side, a darker thought from the silver one, Paul, low and furious and getting louder: *That's not one of ours. There's a wolf on our land that isn't one of ours.*

And Leah.

Edward had lived beside Leah Clearwater's mind for five months. He hadn't enjoyed it. She hated them. She hated him in particular, with a cold, precise contempt she'd never bothered to hide, because she'd known he could hear it and she'd wanted him to. Her mind was sharp and fast and bitter and lonelier than any mind he'd known since his own, in the years he'd spent away from Carlisle, and he'd learned to stand at the edge of it the way you stand at the edge of a drop.

When the growl came, Leah Clearwater's body had gone down before she did.

He'd felt it. The snarl cut off, the legs folded, the ears went flat, and all of it came from somewhere underneath her that she didn't run. The wolf under the woman. Then the woman had caught up with what the wolf had done, and what came after was humiliation, raw and total, like a burn across her whole skin. And under the humiliation, quick and quiet and pushed down under all the rest, there was the part that made Edward look away from her across the snow, though looking away did nothing. The wolf in her had heard that sound and gone still. Settled. The way a child goes quiet in bed at the sound of a familiar car pulling into the drive.

*I'm sorry*, he thought, uselessly, at a mind that couldn't hear him and wouldn't have wanted to.

Then the stranger had said he had a cold.

Emmett had laughed out loud, one short helpless bark. Rosalie had hit him in the chest without looking.

Now the book was shut, and the man was sitting on his log with both hands flat on the cover like somebody at a bus stop, and the only place Edward could find him at all was inside Leah.

It was the strangest thing he'd heard in more than a century. In Leah's mind there was a line, and at the far end of the line there was the man. Edward couldn't get any thoughts off it. What it gave him was position: a pressure, a direction, a weight in the world, ten feet off and a little to her right, a sense of heat and size and something enormous lying very still a long way down. Leah felt him the way you feel the sun on the side of your face with your eyes shut. And Edward, who could hear every mind in the clearing except one, found himself leaning on that line to find the one, the way you'd lean on a wall to find your way in the dark.

He made himself stop. It was hers. She'd have hated him for it more than for anything else he'd ever done.

"Edward." Bella's hand on his arm. She was on her feet. When had she got up? He'd been holding her, and then he'd been listening, and now she was standing beside him in the slush with her face very pale and her eyes very dark and her hand clamped round his forearm. "Edward. What did that mean? The sound. What are they thinking?"

"He's a wolf," Edward said.

She stared at him.

"Or something like one. A shapeshifter. They can smell it on him. They've been smelling it since he got here." He could hear his own voice going faster, the way it did when his mind was three steps ahead of his mouth and dragging it. "Bella. Alice can't see the wolves."

He watched it land on her face.

"She couldn't see him," Bella said slowly.

"She couldn't see him coming." Every vision Alice had sent them before she left, every scrap she'd given them about this morning: the snow, the field, the Volturi in their lines. Never once a man in a leather jacket. She couldn't have seen him. He'd have been nothing to her, a blank hole in the future with nothing round its edges, and if he'd stood in the middle of the field she'd have seen only the field. And then the Volturi—

"Then she'll have seen them disappear," Carlisle said.

He'd turned. He'd been listening. Behind him Esme had her hand at her mouth.

"All at once," Edward said. "Every future they had, gone in the same second. With nothing to show her why."

"She'll think it was us," Esme said. Her voice cracked on it. "She'll think we— Carlisle, she'll think it went wrong, she'll think we're all—"

"She'll come," Edward said. "She'll be coming now. Jasper with her." Wherever they were. Somewhere south; he'd made himself not know exactly, so that Aro couldn't take it out of his hand. He'd wanted Aro's hand on his skin and nothing in his head worth finding. And Aro was ash, and Alice was somewhere out there running toward a field she thought was full of bodies, and there was no way in the world to tell her otherwise. "She'll come back as fast as she can."

Esme closed her eyes.

Behind her, at the edge of the Denali group, Edward heard Kate.

She was still standing where she'd been when the stranger arrived, with Garrett's hand round her forearm and Tanya at her feet. Tanya hadn't moved. Carmen was kneeling in the slush with both arms round her, rocking very slightly, and Eleazar stood over them with his hands hanging open at his sides and his gift reaching, over and over, toward the man on the log and finding nothing, the way a tongue keeps going to a missing tooth. Eleazar had spent centuries sorting the gifted from the ordinary for Aro with a glance. He'd glanced at the stranger forty times now. Every time, he got the same answer, which was no answer at all.

And Kate was thinking about Irina.

She was thinking about the ridge, and Irina's face, and the hands on Irina's arms this morning, and the fire. She was thinking that the ones who'd lit it were grey heaps in the snow, every one of them, and that she hadn't lifted a finger to make it happen. Somebody else had. A stranger had, for his own reasons, which had nothing to do with her sister. Under that, very quietly, in a part of her that Kate would never in her life have admitted to having, she was thinking that she ought to go over there and thank him, and that she couldn't make her feet move, and that she didn't know whether that was fear or pride or grief.

Garrett's thumb moved on the inside of her wrist, once. She let out a breath she didn't need.

Edward turned away from her too. He was doing a lot of that today.

Bella's hand left his arm. She'd heard what she needed to hear, and she'd turned, and Edward turned with her, because there was only one place she'd be going.

Renesmee was still on Jacob's back.

She'd stopped crying. That was worse, in some ways. She sat very upright with her fists still knotted in the russet fur and her small face gone white and closed, and there was a smear of dried blood on her upper lip where Carlisle had wiped it away with his handkerchief and missed a little at the corner. Rosalie was crouched at Jacob's shoulder with one hand on Renesmee's knee and her face turned up to her, murmuring, and her mind was a single sustained scream of rage with Renesmee's name running through the middle of it like a thread of gold through black cloth.

Jacob lowered himself as Bella came. He went all the way down onto his belly in the snow, slowly, carefully, so the backpack didn't shift, and Rosalie got her hands under Renesmee's arms and passed her up.

Bella took her. Renesmee wrapped round her like a vine, arms round her neck, legs round her waist, face pressed into her hair, and her hand came up and found Bella's cheek.

Edward saw it as Bella did. A cold thin edge sliding into the soft round world of her head. A door slamming that didn't stop anything. Pictures she'd thrown going past the cold thing like it couldn't see them. Her whole life going over and over, turned like pages, by something with no face.

Bella flinched. She didn't let go.

"I know," she whispered. "I know, baby. I know. I felt it too."

"It hurts," Renesmee said into her hair. Her voice was very small. "Momma. My head hurts."

"I know."

The picture came again. The same one. The cold edge, the pages.

"Doc."

It came from the middle of the field. The stranger hadn't looked up. He was sitting on his log with his elbows on the closed book, looking at the snow in front of his boots, and his voice came across the forty yards at an ordinary speaking volume and arrived perfectly clear.

Carlisle turned.

"She'll have a headache till tonight, give or take," the stranger said. "Somewhere dark, if you've got it. Something cold on the back of her neck. Snow'll do." A pause. "She's going to want to keep showing people what happened. That's normal. Let her do it once or twice. After that, distract her. Get her talking about something else. Anything. If she keeps going back to it, it digs in."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Edward listened to Carlisle hear it. He heard the doctor in Carlisle take the instructions, turn them over, set them against everything he knew of minds and pain and children, and find nothing wrong with them. He heard Carlisle not want to find nothing wrong with them.

"Thank you," Carlisle said.

"Don't you talk about her."

Rosalie had come up off her crouch. She was standing beside Jacob's shoulder with her hands in fists and her lips pulled back and her eyes on the man in the middle of the field, and her voice came out of her low and shaking. "Don't you dare give us advice about her. Don't you dare. You did that to her."

The stranger lifted his head and looked at her across the snow.

"Yeah," he said. "I did."

That was all. He didn't say anything else. He held her eyes for a second or two, and then he went back to looking at the snow in front of his boots.

Edward heard what that did to Rosalie. She'd been braced for an argument. She'd wanted one; she'd had the whole thing ready, every word. And he'd agreed with her, flatly, and left her standing there with all of it and nowhere to put it. Her rage went round and round in her head like a wasp in a jar. Emmett's hand came up and closed on her arm, gently, and she let it.

Bella didn't need the snow. She laid her own palm against the back of Renesmee's neck, under the curls, and her hand was colder than any snow had ever been, and Renesmee made a small sound and went a little looser against her.

"Better?"

A nod against her neck.

"Do you want to tell me about Grandpa Charlie's Christmas tree?" Bella said. "Do you remember the angel on the top? The one with the crooked wing?"

A pause. Then a picture, small and tentative: a tinsel angel, listing to one side. Charlie on a stepladder, swearing under his breath.

"That's the one," Bella said. Her voice didn't shake at all. "Tell me what Grandpa said when he dropped it."

Edward looked at his wife and didn't trust himself to say anything.

That was when he heard Jacob decide.

He'd been hearing it build for a minute, under everything else, under the stream of Renesmee and Rosalie and Carlisle and the rest. Jacob was lying in the snow where he'd gone down to let Bella take her, and his head was full of Renesmee's weight leaving his back, and the cold place where the backpack had been, and the stranger. And one word the stranger had used. *Registration.* The stag, the message, *requesting a registration team.* Jacob didn't know what it meant. He knew what it sounded like. It sounded like a social worker. It sounded like a car in front of the house and a woman with a folder, and Nessie in the back seat, and Billy on the porch in his chair with his face set, and nobody able to do a thing about it.

And under that, the other thing. *We are going to have words, you and me. Long ones.*

*He wants to talk*, Jacob was thinking. *He said so. He said trousers. If he wanted us dead we'd be ash like the rest of them. I have to know what he's going to do with her. I have to know.*

Embry, in Jacob's pack mind: *Jake. Seriously?*

*Seriously.*

*He just growled like a landslide.*

*I know.*

*He'll—*

*If he was going to, he would've.*

Edward said, without meaning to, "Jacob wants to go and talk to him."

Bella's head came up from Renesmee's hair. "Jake. No."

The russet wolf turned his great head and looked at her. Edward heard everything that went into the look: *I have to. You know I have to. If it's about her, I have to.* And under that, very small, a thing Jacob would have died before he said out loud: *He can't hurt me worse than he's already hurt her.*

"Be careful," Bella said. It came out rough. "Please. Jake. Be careful."

Jacob huffed through his nose. He got up out of the snow. He stretched his head out toward Renesmee, slowly, and she lifted her face off Bella's shoulder and put her hand flat on the end of his muzzle, and Edward saw what she showed him. It was Jacob. Jacob as a boy, laughing, holding her up over his head on the beach so she could see the waves. That was all.

Jacob made a sound in his chest that was nearly a whine and turned and loped off toward the trees.

Edward heard him go into them, and heard him find a place behind a big cedar where the snow lay thin, and heard him phase. It always sounded the same from inside: the pack mind sliding away from Jacob all at once like a sheet pulled off a bed, and Jacob alone in his own head for the first time all morning, and the cold arriving on bare skin like a slap. Then the shaking fingers on the cord round his ankle. The roll of cutoffs, damp from the snow. A curse, because the knot had frozen.

He came out of the trees a minute later in nothing but the cutoffs, barefoot, steam lifting off his shoulders the same as the stranger's, and walked out across the field.

Edward watched him go. Past the Cullen line. Past the place where Vladimir had gone up. Toward the man on the log and the small grey wolf sitting ten feet from him with her ears flat.

Everyone kept walking toward him. First Leah. Now Jacob.

Edward stood in the snow with his wife and his daughter, and listened to every mind in the clearing except the one that mattered, and counted the beats of Jacob Black's heart as it climbed.

### IV. Jacob

It was so quiet in his head that his ears rang with it.

That was always the worst part of phasing back. Nobody ever believed him when he said it. The bones were bad, the folding and the shortening and the skin coming back over everything, but you got used to the bones. What you never got used to was the moment the others went. One second there were five of them in there, Seth fretting and Embry muttering and Quil making bad jokes and Leah, Leah being Leah, and the next second there was just Jacob, alone in his own skull, with the wind in the cedars and his own heartbeat and nobody to tell him whether this was the stupidest thing he'd ever done.

He was pretty sure it was in the top three.

The snow was coming up to his ankles. He didn't feel it much. He ran at a hundred and eight; the cold arrived on his skin and sat there like a fact. His feet left dark wet prints behind him in the white, steaming a little at the edges.

Halfway out he walked round the place where Vladimir had come down. He couldn't help looking. It was a long grey smear across the snow, fanned out the way the old man had been flying, with the snow underneath gone to bare wet grass in a stripe. There was nothing in it you'd know for a person. No bone, no tooth, no scrap of that black coat. Jacob had torn vampires apart. He knew what was left when you did it the slow way, the pieces still twitching on the ground till somebody got a fire going. This was nothing. This was what was left in a woodstove in the morning.

He stepped wide round it and kept walking.

Behind him, he could feel the Cullens watching. Bella. Nessie, with her face back in Bella's hair. He didn't turn round. If he turned round he wasn't going to keep walking.

Seth fell in beside him halfway across.

The sandy wolf came trotting out from the pack line with his tail low and his ears halfway between back and forward, the way Seth's ears always went when he'd made up his mind to do something and wasn't sure he was allowed. He bumped his shoulder against Jacob's arm. He was big enough that it nearly knocked Jacob over.

"Go back," Jacob said.

Seth whined.

"I'm serious, man. Go back to Quil and Embry."

Seth looked past him. At the grey wolf sitting in the snow. Then back at Jacob, and he didn't need to be in Seth's head to read that look. *She's my sister.*

Jacob let his breath out. "Fine. Stay with her. Not too close to him. Okay?"

Seth's tail moved, once. He peeled off and went and sat down in the snow beside Leah, a body's length away from her, which was about as close as Leah let anyone get these days. She didn't look at him. Her ears were still flat. She was lying down now, her chin on her forepaws, and her eyes were on the man on the log and nowhere else. Up close, with human eyes, she looked enormous. They always did. It was easy to forget, in the pack, how big they all were. You only saw it from down here.

Jacob stopped about eight feet from the log.

The stranger looked up at him. He had to look up quite a long way; Jacob had a good five inches on him standing, and the guy was sitting down. He took his time doing it. Feet, cutoffs, chest, face. The same tired, unimpressed look Jacob had seen across the field.

"Trousers," the stranger said. "Good lad."

"Shorts."

"I'll allow it."

Jacob stood there with his hands opening and closing at his sides and found that, now he was here, he didn't know what to say. He'd had it all ready in the trees. He'd had a whole speech. It had gone somewhere on the walk across.

"You said we'd talk," he said.

"I did." The stranger rested his forearms on the book on his knees. Up close, the steam coming off his shoulders was thicker than it had looked from the line, and the snow under the log had gone to grey slush. He smelled exactly the way Jacob had known he'd smell. Pine and dirt and stone, and under it, now Jacob was standing in it with a human nose that still worked better than any human nose had a right to, the wolf. Thick. Dark. Old. Jacob's skin prickled all the way up his back. "How old are you?"

"Seventeen."

"And you were sixteen when she was born."

"Yeah."

"And she was, what. An hour old?"

Jacob's jaw went tight. "Less."

The stranger nodded slowly, like that was the answer he'd expected and he didn't like it any better for being right.

"Walk me through it," he said.

"Through what?"

"Your thing. The—" He waved a hand, a short flat gesture at the space between Jacob's chest and the Cullens' side of the field. "Start to finish. I've seen it from the outside twice now, in other people's heads, and once in a book by a bloke from London who'd had a letter off a trapper." He tapped the grey cover under his arm with one finger. "I want it from somebody it's happened to."

"Why?"

"Because I've been in your girl's head, and I've been in her mum's, and I've seen your face come round that door. And because I asked."

Jacob looked at him. Then at Leah, who hadn't moved. Then back.

He'd explained it before. To Bella, on the beach. To Charlie, sort of, without explaining anything. To himself, about a thousand times, at three in the morning on the floor of the cottage next to Nessie's cot, listening to her breathe. He'd never had to explain it to someone who looked at him like this, like a cop who'd already read the report and wanted to hear whether you'd tell it the same way.

"You see her," he said. "That's it. That's all it takes. You look at her and everything else just—goes. Everything you were holding on to. Your family, your pack, whatever you were angry about. It all cuts loose at once. And she's the only thing left holding you up." He stopped. Started again. "And then you'd do anything. Be anything. Whatever she needs. If she needs a brother, you're her brother. If she needs a friend, you're her friend. If she needs somebody to stand between her and a hundred vampires in a field, you're that."

"And when she's eighteen?"

Jacob felt his whole face go hot.

"Then whatever she wants," he said. It came out through his teeth. "If she wants anything. If she wants me to be her brother forever, I'm her brother forever. If she wants me to go away, I go away. It's her call. It's always her call. That's—" He had to stop and breathe. "That's the whole point. I'm what she needs. She doesn't owe me anything. She never owes me anything. I'd never—" He couldn't finish that sentence either. He'd never been able to finish it, not even in his own head, because the shape of the thing at the end of it made him want to throw up. "She's a kid. She's my kid. She's my family."

The stranger looked at him for a long time.

Jacob made himself stand there and take it. He'd been looked at like this before, he realised. By Charlie, the night Charlie found out Bella was married. By Sam, the first week, when Sam was trying to work out whether Jacob was going to be a problem. It was the look of somebody who'd seen a lot of people say the right words and was waiting to see if your face matched them.

He didn't look away. Whatever the guy was looking for, Jacob didn't have anything to hide from it.

"Right," the stranger said finally. "Here's mine, then. When the registration's done, there'll be a file with her name on it. Your name's going in it. The bond's going in it. Somebody in Geneva's going to read that file, and then I'm going to read it, and I'm going to keep reading it, every year, till she's grown. And if I ever see a thing in it I don't like—"

"You won't."

"Then we won't have a problem."

That was it. No threat after it. He didn't need to make one. There were thirty-odd little grey heaps on the far side of the field making it for him.

"Why do you care?" Jacob said. It came out rougher than he meant it. "You don't know her. You were in her head for, what, a minute. You don't know anything about her."

"I know she thinks you hung the moon," the stranger said.

Jacob's mouth shut.

"Every room in there's got you in it," the stranger went on. His voice didn't change at all. "Every one. Sat on the floor by the cot. Holding her up to see the snow. Under her when she's frightened. She's four months old and you're the floor she stands on." He looked at Jacob, steady. "That's why I care. Because if you ever turned out to be the wrong sort, there'd be nothing under her at all."

Jacob stood in the snow and couldn't say anything.

He'd known it. Of course he'd known it. He felt it every day, every time she reached for him. But he'd never heard anybody say it out loud, least of all somebody who'd seen it from the inside, and it went into him somewhere under his ribs and stayed there.

"I'm not the wrong sort," he said finally. His voice came out thick.

"Good," said the stranger. "Keep it that way."

Jacob swallowed. Then he asked the thing he'd walked across the field to ask.

"Are they going to take her?"

The stranger's eyebrows went up a fraction.

"The register," Jacob said. "Geneva. Whatever it is. You said registration. Are they going to come and take her away? Put her somewhere? Because if they are, you should know right now, I'll—"

"Take her where?"

"I don't know. Wherever you people put things."

"From her mum and dad?" The stranger shook his head. "No. Nobody's taking anybody. That's what the register's for. It stops people taking her." He paused. "From you? Nobody's taking her from you either, mate. You'll get a visit now and then. Somebody with a clipboard and a lot of questions. You'll hate them." Another pause. "Be polite."

Jacob stood there and felt something let go in his chest that had been clenched since the silver deer said *registration*. He hadn't realised how hard he'd been holding it. His knees actually went a little loose. He locked them.

"Okay," he said. "Okay."

"Now me," the stranger said. "Question."

"What?"

"Her end." He nodded across the field toward the Cullens without looking, toward Bella and the small shape wrapped round her. "The kid's. When you look at her, when the thing's going. Does anything come back? From her end. Down the line."

"Down the—" Jacob stared at him. "No. There's no line on her end. Nothing pulls on her. She loves me because she knows me, because I've been there since the first day. She'd love me the same if I was just—just some guy who was always around. It's on us. It's only ever on us." He was talking faster now, because this he knew, this was the one thing about all of it he was sure of. "Emily could've said no to Sam. She nearly did. She told him to get lost for weeks, she hated him for what he did to—" He stopped, very fast, and didn't look at Leah. "Claire's two. She thinks Quil's a climbing frame. Nobody feels anything coming back. That's how it works. It's just on the wolf."

"Just on the wolf," the stranger said.

"Yeah."

The stranger turned his head and looked at Leah.

He looked at her for a long time. She was still lying in the snow with her chin on her paws, and her ears had come up off her skull a little while Jacob talked, and now, with his eyes on her, they went flat again. Seth, a body's length away, had gone very still.

"Then somebody's got their wires crossed," the stranger said. "Because mine's pulling."

Jacob felt it hit.

He felt it the way you feel a wave hit you out past the break when you've turned your back on it, all at once and everywhere. He actually took a half-step back in the snow. Beside him, Leah's head had come up off her paws. Not far. An inch. Her ears had gone straight up, both of them, all the way forward, and her eyes had gone wide and fixed on the stranger's face, and Jacob, who'd been in Leah's head for months, who knew every way her body moved, had never seen her look like that. She looked like somebody had hit her.

"That doesn't happen," Jacob said.

"Yeah." The stranger let his breath out through his nose. He looked, for half a second, very tired. "I get that a lot."

"Pulling how?"

The stranger thought about it. Then he lifted one hand and touched two fingers to his own chest, right in the middle, on the breastbone, through the flannel shirt.

"Goes in there," he said. "Keeps going."

"Keeps going where?"

"Down."

He didn't say anything else. He put his hand back on the book.

Jacob opened his mouth. Shut it. He didn't know what to ask. He didn't know what the question even was. Every wolf he'd ever known who'd imprinted, Sam, Jared, Paul, Quil, himself, had described the same thing, and in every one of them it went one way. From the wolf to her. A wolf's thing. A Quileute thing, a thing from the old stories, the gift Taha Aki's line had been carrying round in its blood for a thousand years without anybody really knowing why. Nobody had ever said anything about it coming back.

And this guy had a wolf in him, and a line in his chest, and he was English, and he'd killed the Volturi with a snap.

"Who knows the most?" the stranger said. "About the old stuff. Your lot's stories."

"The council," Jacob said automatically. "The elders. My dad. Old Quil, Quil's grandpa." He hesitated. "Sue Clearwater."

"Clearwater."

"Leah's mom."

The stranger shut his eyes. He kept them shut for about a second.

"Course she is," he said.

When he opened them again he was looking at Seth.

Seth had been sitting in the snow with his tail swept round him through all of it, trying very hard to look like a large sandy rock, and when the stranger's eyes landed on him he froze completely. His ears went every which way at once.

"And you're the brother," the stranger said.

Seth's tail moved in the snow. Once.

"You're the one got between Bella and Cujo here, that morning on the lawn. When she went for him." He tipped his head at Jacob without looking at him. "Took the hit. Collarbone, yeah? Heard it go."

Seth's ears came all the way up. He looked about as surprised as a wolf could look.

"That was decent of you," the stranger said. "Scrappy."

Seth's mouth fell open and his tongue lolled out of the side of it, and his tail went, properly this time, swish, swish, swish, ploughing a little half-moon in the snow behind him. Beside him, low, Leah growled. It was a small growl. It was very clearly aimed at her brother.

Jacob laughed.

He didn't mean to. It came out of him like a cough, short and helpless, the first time he'd laughed since Irina had come over the ridge. He stood in the snow in his cutoffs with his arms wrapped round himself and laughed, and Seth's tail kept going, and Leah kept growling at Seth, and the stranger watched all three of them with no expression at all.

When Jacob had stopped, he wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist. Then he looked at the man on the log. At the steam coming off him. At the circle of slush.

"What are you?"

"Late," the stranger said.

"For what?"

"My tea."

"You growled."

"Cold."

"You smell like us."

The stranger looked at him, flat. "So do you, mate. I'd have a shower."

Jacob stood there. He thought about pushing it. He thought about how far he'd get. He looked at the little grey heaps on the far side of the field, and then at Leah, whose ears were still pricked all the way forward and whose eyes still hadn't left the man's face since he'd said *mine's pulling*, and he thought: that one's hers to ask. Whatever he is, she gets to ask first.

He let it go.

"Okay," he said.

He turned to go. He got about four steps.

"Oi. Cujo."

Jacob turned round.

The stranger was looking past him. Across the field. At the Cullens, at Bella, at the small shape with bronze curls wrapped round her neck. His face didn't change. His voice didn't change.

"When she's had a sleep," he said, "tell her I'm sorry about her head."

Jacob looked at him for a second.

"Tell her yourself," he said.

"She doesn't want me anywhere near her."

Jacob thought about the pictures. The cold edge. The pages turning and turning and the door that wouldn't shut. Nessie's hands on his neck, shaking.

"No," he said. "She doesn't."

"So you tell her."

Jacob stood there in the snow for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and turned and walked back across the field toward his family.

He was halfway there when he saw Sam.

The big black wolf had come out from the middle of Sam's pack and was standing at the edge of them with his head up, looking past Jacob, past the log, past the man on it. Looking at Leah. Paul was crowded up against his shoulder, silver and bristling, and Jared on the other side, and Collin and Brady hanging back with their tails tucked. Jacob couldn't hear any of them. He didn't need to. He knew that stance. He'd been in Sam's pack long enough. That was Sam making up his mind.

Then Sam turned, and loped toward the trees.

"Oh, hell," Jacob said, to nobody.

### V. Sam

He'd heard every word.

They all had. A dozen yards and forty more on top weren't anything to a wolf's ears, not on a still day, not in a field where nobody on either side was moving or breathing if they could help it. They'd heard Jacob's voice and the stranger's, flat and English and unhurried. *How old are you. Walk me through it. And when she's eighteen.* They'd heard Jacob answer, and Sam had felt every answer go through him, because they were his answers too. Every one. He'd given them to Leah's mother, on her porch, two years ago, with his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the boards.

And then the stranger had said *mine's pulling*, and the pack mind had gone off like a flare.

*It goes BACK?* Jared. *How does it go back? It doesn't go back. It never goes back.*

*He's lying.*

*He's not lying, Paul, why would he lie about—*

*Because he's not one of us. Because he's a wolf, and he's not one of us, and he's sitting on Leah like she's his, and we're just STANDING here—*

*Sam?* Brady, small and young, younger than any of them had been. *Sam, is Leah okay? Is she going to be okay?*

Sam didn't answer him. He didn't trust what would come out.

Because there was a thing underneath all of it, under the shock and the fear and Paul's rage, a thing he'd felt first and had been trying to sit on ever since, and he couldn't sit on it any longer. In here you couldn't sit on anything. They'd all felt it. They'd felt it the moment Leah made that sound, that awful high yelp across the clearing, and Sam had turned his head and seen her leaning toward the stranger like a tree in a gale.

Relief.

It had come up in him so fast and so clean he hadn't been able to stop it. A weight he'd been carrying for two years had come off his chest so suddenly he'd nearly staggered. *She's not alone anymore. She won't be alone anymore. It's not on me anymore.* And straight on the heels of it, so close behind it there wasn't any space between them, the shame. Because it had never been on him to take off. Because he'd done this to her. He'd looked at Emily one morning on her mother's porch and walked out of Leah's life without a word, and he'd spent every day since then inside a pack mind with the girl he'd left, hearing her hate him and knowing she was right to, and now the same thing had happened to her and his first feeling, his very first, had been *thank God*.

Every one of them had felt him feel it. Paul had thought, very quietly, *Oh, man.* And none of them had said anything else, which was worse.

Then the stranger had growled, and Sam had felt it in the ground under his paws, and his own ears had gone back without asking him. Every wolf in his pack had flattened at once. He'd been an Alpha since before any of them had phased. He'd never in his life heard a sound that made him want to lie down.

That was the second thing.

Leah was Quileute. Whatever else she was, whatever she thought of him, she was one of theirs. And there was a stranger in the middle of that field who smelled like a wolf the way the sea smells like salt, who'd burned the Volturi with a snap and an old Romanian without turning his head, who wasn't from here, who didn't know the stories, who'd just been told the elders' names and who'd just said, in front of everybody, that whatever had happened between him and Leah went into him and kept going down.

Sue would ask him. That was the thing Sam kept coming back to. When this was over, when they all went home, Sue Clearwater would sit him down at her kitchen table, where Harry's chair still stood empty, and she'd ask him what he'd done. And he'd have to tell her he'd stood a dozen yards off with his pack and watched.

*I'm going over there*, he said.

The pack went still.

*Sam.* Jared. *Jake's already been. He's fine. It's fine. The guy didn't—*

*I'm coming with you*, Paul said.

*No.*

*Sam, he's a—*

*No.* He let a little of it into his voice. Not much. Just enough to put weight behind the word. He felt it settle over them like a hand on the back of a neck. *Stay here. All of you. Whatever happens.*

Paul's thoughts went red and furious and then, slowly, flat. *Fine.*

Sam turned and loped for the trees.

He found the same thin place under the cedars that Jacob had found, he could smell Jacob's tracks there, and he phased. The cold came. The pack went. He undid the cord from his ankle with fingers that felt too big and too clumsy for the knot, and pulled the cutoffs on, and stood there for a second in the trees with his bare feet in the snow and his own heartbeat loud in his ears and nobody in his head but himself.

He didn't like being alone in there. He never had. When he was alone in there, he could hear himself.

He walked out.

Emily would be at home now. She'd be at the kitchen window with the radio off and her hands flat on the counter, the way she'd stood every time he went out to fight, watching the trees at the edge of the yard for a black shape to come out of them. He'd told her this morning that it would probably be over by noon, one way or another. He hadn't told her which way. She'd kissed him on the side of the face and said *come home*, the way she always did, and he'd promised.

He'd have to tell her about Leah. He thought about her face when he did. Emily, who'd told him to get lost for weeks because of Leah. Who still couldn't say her cousin's name at the dinner table without her voice going careful. She'd cry, he thought. And he didn't know which way she'd be crying.

Jacob met him halfway across. He'd stopped walking back toward the Cullens and stood there, barefoot and steaming, waiting, with his arms folded across his chest. He stepped sideways into Sam's path, which he'd never have done a year ago, and Sam stopped.

"Sam," Jacob said. "Don't."

"I need to talk to him."

"About what?"

Sam didn't answer.

"She's not yours, man," Jacob said. Quietly. He said it without any heat at all, which made it land harder. "She hasn't been for a long time."

"I know that."

"Then what are you doing?"

Sam looked past him. At the log. At the stranger on it, and the small grey wolf lying in the snow ten feet off, and Seth a body's length from her with his tail gone still.

"She's one of ours," he said. "Whatever this is. She's Quileute. The council needs to know what he is."

"So ask the council to ask him."

Sam looked at Jacob. Jacob, who was seventeen, and who'd walked out on Sam's pack five months ago because Sam had given an order he couldn't stomach, and who'd been right to. Jacob looked back.

"Fine," Jacob said, when Sam didn't say anything. "Your funeral." He stepped aside. "He's going to call you something. Just so you know."

Sam walked on.

The snow got thinner the closer he got to the log. The last few yards were slush, and then wet grass, steaming. The stranger watched him come. He didn't get up. He sat there with his forearms on the closed book and looked at Sam the way he'd looked at Jacob, feet to face, with that tired, level, unimpressed look.

He smelled exactly the way Sam had known he would. Up close the wolf in it was so thick Sam's lip wanted to come up on its own. He kept it down.

He stopped about where Jacob had stopped.

Leah's head came up off her paws.

He saw her see him. He saw her ears go flat to her skull and her lip go up off her teeth, slowly, all the way, and the ridge of fur on her back rise. She didn't make a sound. She didn't need to. He'd been on the other end of that look for two years. He knew it better than he knew his own face.

The stranger looked at Sam. Then at Leah. Then back at Sam.

"And you're the ex," he said.

Sam felt the blood come up into his face.

"Sam Uley," he said.

"I know."

Of course he knew. He'd been in Bella's head. Bella knew everything. Bella had stood in Emily's kitchen with a muffin in her hand and looked at the scars down the side of Emily's face, the long pulled lines from her eye to her jaw to her shoulder, and Bella had understood what they were, and so the stranger had seen them too. Sam watched the man's eyes go down to his hands. Rest there a second. Come back up.

The stranger didn't say anything about it. He didn't have to.

Sam made himself keep going. "This is—these are our people. She's Quileute. Whatever this is, whatever's happened, the council has a right to know what you are. What you intend. The elders—"

Leah snarled.

It ripped out of her, louder than anything since the stranger's growl, and she came up onto her feet in one movement with her head low and every tooth showing, and every bit of it was aimed at Sam. It had two years in it. It had Emily in it, and the porch, and every single night in the pack mind she'd spent hearing Sam love someone else.

"She's sat right there, mate," the stranger said. He hadn't moved. He tipped his head toward her. "Ask her."

"Leah—" Sam said.

She turned and ran.

She went past Seth like a grey streak, so close he flinched, and across the snow toward the trees on the north side, toward the same place Jacob and Sam had used. She ran the way she always ran, low and flat and impossibly fast, faster than any of them, and she hit the treeline without slowing and was gone into the dark under the branches.

Sam stood there and watched the place where she'd gone.

"She's going to phase," he said. He didn't know who he was saying it to.

"Looked that way," the stranger said. He was watching the trees too. Then he looked back at Sam. "I'd have a think about where you want to be standing when she gets back."

Sam didn't move.

He didn't know how to. He'd come across the field with something to say, and it had been the wrong thing, and he'd known it was the wrong thing before he'd got halfway through saying it. He'd known it in the trees. He'd known it two years ago. He kept saying it anyway. He kept walking across fields toward Leah Clearwater to tell people what she needed, and every time he did it she turned round and ran, and every time she was right.

"What are you going to do?" he said. "With her."

"Right now?" The stranger shifted on the log. "Sit here till my lift turns up."

"You know what I mean."

"Yeah, I do." The stranger looked at him. "And it's up to her."

Sam flinched.

He felt it go through him, all the way, before he could stop it. *It's up to her.* Nothing had ever been up to Leah. Not him leaving. Not the phasing, not the pack, not her father going down in the kitchen with his hand on the counter. He'd stood on her mother's porch and said *I didn't choose this* as if that made it better, as if the choosing was the part that mattered, and he'd never once, in two years, said the other half out loud: *and neither did you*. He'd never said it because he hadn't been able to bear it.

This man had said it in five words, sitting on a log, without looking like it cost him anything.

The stranger watched him flinch. He didn't say anything else. He just sat there, steaming gently, with his hands on his book.

Behind Sam, somewhere off to the left, Seth whined.

Sam looked at him. The kid was standing now, sandy and anxious, looking from Sam to the trees and back again with his ears going every direction at once. He looked about twelve. He looked like he had at the funeral.

"It's okay, Seth," Sam said. He didn't know whether it was true.

Seth whined again.

There was a sound from the treeline. Bare feet on snow.

Sam turned his head.

Leah came out from under the cedars on two feet. She was in a pair of cutoff jeans and a black tank top that she was still tugging down over her stomach as she walked, and her feet were bare, and her short black hair was standing up all over her head where the phasing had left it. Steam rose off her bare arms and shoulders in the cold air, thick as smoke. She didn't slow down. She came across the snow toward them with her arms swinging and her chin up and her eyes fixed on Sam, and the snow hissed under her feet.

Sam had seen her walk at him like that before. Once. The morning after he'd told her. She'd come across her mother's yard at him exactly like that, in exactly that silence.

He stood his ground. He owed her that much, at least.

### VI. Leah

Her head was empty.

She'd forgotten what that was like. Two years of other people's voices going round in there day and night, Seth's worrying and Embry's grumbling and Quil's dumb jokes and Jacob being all Nessie all the time, and before that, worse, so much worse, Sam. Sam in her head every time she phased. Sam's thoughts about Emily's hair, Emily's hands, Emily's laugh in the kitchen. She'd have cut off a leg for five minutes of quiet in there, most days.

Now she had it, and the only thing in it was Sam Uley's face.

He was standing in the slush by the log in his cutoffs with his big arms hanging at his sides, and he was looking at her the way he'd looked at her across her mother's yard, the morning after. Like she was a car crash he'd caused and couldn't stop slowing down for. She hated that look. She'd hated it for two years. She walked straight at it.

The snow hissed under her bare feet. She could feel the cold coming up into her soles and stopping there, going no further, the way the cold always stopped with them. Her skin was steaming. She didn't look at the stranger. She didn't have to look at him; she knew exactly where he was, ten feet to her left, on his log, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark. The line sat in her breastbone and hummed.

In this body it was worse. She'd hoped it wouldn't be. She'd thought, in the trees, with her bones shortening and her skin coming back and the pack sliding away from her like a tide going out, that maybe the human part of her would be harder for it to get hold of. That maybe she'd stand up on two feet and find it had stayed with the wolf.

It hadn't. If anything it had got clearer, without fur to muffle it. Like taking earplugs out.

She stopped three feet from Sam.

"Go back to your pack, Sam."

"Leah." His voice was low. "I just—"

"You just what?" She heard her own voice come out flat and hard and she let it. "Came to check on me? Came over here to tell him what I am? What the council needs? What the elders want to know?" She jerked her chin at the stranger without looking at him. "I'm not yours. I haven't been yours since you walked into my mom's yard and couldn't look me in the face. You don't get to come over here and talk about me to some guy like I'm part of the reservation. Like I'm a fishing right."

His face moved. It was a small movement. She'd been in his head for two years and she knew every one of his faces, and this one was the one he made when something went in under his ribs.

"I'm sorry, Lee," he said.

"I know you are." She was so tired. She was so tired of him being sorry. "Go home to Emily."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and turned round and walked away across the snow, back toward the trees, big and dark and bare-backed with the steam coming off him, and he didn't look back.

She watched him go.

And the ache came, the way it always came. She stood there braced for it, because it had been coming for two years every time she looked at him, a deep sick wrench in her stomach like missing a step in the dark.

It came. It was there. But it was small.

She stood in the snow and felt it, and turned it over, and couldn't believe it. It was the same ache. She knew its shape better than she knew her own face. But it was small, and far off, like a bruise from last month that only hurt when you pressed it. And it didn't reach. That was the thing. It used to go everywhere, through everything, into her chest and her throat and the backs of her eyes. Now it stopped about halfway, and the line was sitting there in the middle of her, humming, and the ache went round it like water round a rock.

One of the cables. One of the hundred cables that had snapped loose in her when she'd looked across the field and seen him. That had been one of them. The Sam one.

She'd waited two years for that to stop hurting. She'd tried everything. Running. Hating him. Hating Emily, which had been worse, because she loved Emily, because Emily was her cousin and they'd been close as sisters, and Emily had cried at her father's funeral. Switching packs. Nothing had worked.

And this had done it in about a second, without asking.

She hated it. She hated it so much she could taste it. She'd have given anything to stop hurting about Sam, and she'd got it, and it had come from the thing she'd said she'd rather die than have.

Seth whined behind her.

She turned. He was standing in the snow a few yards back, sandy and anxious and enormous, with his ears going every direction at once.

"Go back to Jake, Seth."

He whined again.

"I'm fine," she said. "Go on. I'm fine."

He didn't believe her. She could see him not believing her. He came forward a step anyway and pushed the top of his huge head against her hand, once, the way he used to when he was a little kid and she was sad and he didn't know what to do about it, and then he turned and trotted off across the field toward Jacob, looking back twice.

Then it was just her and the stranger.

She turned round and looked at him properly for the first time with human eyes.

He was sitting where he'd been sitting the whole time, on his log, with that huge grey book on his knees and his forearms resting on it. The snow around the log had gone to slush and then to wet grass, a circle of it six feet wide, steaming. Steam came up off his shoulders and the top of his head. It came off her too. She could see it, out of the corner of her eye, curling off her bare arms. The two of them, sitting in a snowfield, steaming like a pair of kettles.

He looked at her face. Only her face.

"Lassie," he said.

"Don't."

He considered that. "Leah, then."

She hadn't told him her name. Nobody had. But he'd been in Bella's head, and Bella knew everybody's name, and he'd said it like he'd always known it. He said it once and didn't make anything of it.

"And I'm not calling you Harry," she said.

He didn't ask why. He looked at her for a second, and something went across his face that she couldn't read.

"Your dad," he said.

Every muscle in her body locked.

"How do you—"

"Bella's head. He went fishing with her dad." His voice didn't change. "Her dad came home the night it happened looking about a hundred years old."

"Stay out of my dad."

"Fair enough." He didn't push it. "Potter's fine."

"Potter."

"That's the one."

She stood there with her arms wrapped round herself. She was never cold. She just didn't know what to do with her hands, and if she didn't hold on to herself she didn't know what she'd do with them.

Across the field, she could feel them watching. All of them. The Cullens in their line, Jacob with Nessie now, the Irish, the Amazons. Seth and Quil and Embry. Sam's lot over on the left. Sam himself, somewhere in the trees, phasing, going back into a pack mind where four other wolves would be waiting to see what his face had done. She could feel every one of them staring at the back of her head.

She didn't care. She was done caring who watched.

"You don't have a cold," she said.

"No."

"Wolves don't get colds."

"Don't they?"

"No."

"Huh." He seemed to file that somewhere. "Learn something every day."

"So you're a wolf."

"Yeah."

Just like that. He said it the way you'd say yes, it's raining.

"What kind?"

"Big black one."

"How big?"

"Bigger than your lot."

"My nose said that."

"Your nose is good," he said.

She didn't know what to do with that, so she ignored it. "How?"

"Long story."

"I've got time."

"You've got about as long as it takes Geneva to find its boots." He shook his head slightly. "Another day."

"Are you even from here?" She heard how stupid it sounded as she said it, and said it anyway. "Are you, like—are you Quileute? Is there some cousin nobody told us about?"

He looked at her. Then down at himself: the leather jacket, the green flannel, the pale hands, the boots. Then back up.

"Do I look Quileute?"

"You look like a tourist."

"Then no."

"So what, there's English wolves now? There's a whole—there's a pack of you over there?"

"There's one," he said.

She stared at him.

"Just you."

"Just me."

She thought about that. One wolf. On his own. In a country where nobody else turned into anything. No pack mind. No Alpha. No one in his head, ever, at three in the morning, when it got bad. She'd spent two years wishing for exactly that, and she thought about it now, really thought about it, and it made something in her chest go cold.

"You growled at me," she said.

"I did. Sorry about that."

"You said you had a cold."

"I lied."

"Why?"

He took a moment over it. "Because I didn't do it on purpose. And 'I've got a cold' is shorter than explaining."

"It made me sit down." It came out bitter. She couldn't help it. "My body just—sat down. I didn't tell it to. It just did it. In front of everybody."

"Yeah," he said. "I saw."

"I hate that."

"I'd hate it too."

He said it plainly. No pity in it. That threw her more than anything else he'd said. She'd been pitied by every single person she knew for two years and she could smell it a mile off, and there wasn't any. He just said it like it was true. He'd hate it too.

She stood there a moment longer. Then, because her legs were starting to feel strange and she was damned if she was going to sit down in the snow in front of him twice in one day, she walked over to the far end of the log and sat on it. As far from him as the log went. Five feet, maybe. The wood was wet and steaming and hot from him. She put her elbows on her knees and stared out at the field.

He didn't move. He didn't look at her. He let her sit.

"You said yours pulls," she said, after a while.

"Yeah."

"Like mine?"

"Dunno what yours feels like."

She thought about telling him. She thought about how it would sound out loud. She'd never said it out loud. In the pack you didn't have to say anything; they just saw it.

"Like a hook," she said. "Right here." She pressed her knuckles into her breastbone, hard. "Like everything else I had got cut at once and that's the only thing still holding me up. Like if I let go of it I'd fall." She took her hand away. "Like gravity. That's what they all say. Like gravity moved. I always thought that was the stupidest thing I'd ever heard."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's the stupidest thing I've ever heard and it's true."

He nodded slowly.

"Yours?" she said.

He was quiet a second. He looked down at his own chest, as if he could see through the shirt.

"Goes in where you said. Same spot." He touched it, two fingers, the way he'd done for Jacob. "Then it keeps going."

"Down. You said. Down where?"

"Where the wolf is."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"It's in there?" she said. "Like—separate? Like a whole other—"

"It's me." He said it immediately. "Just the bit of me that doesn't bother talking."

"And it—"

"Grabbed it. Yeah." His mouth went flat at one corner. "Didn't ask."

Leah laughed. It came out of her short and harsh, more like a bark than a laugh, and it surprised her as much as it seemed to surprise him.

"Welcome to the club," she said.

He looked at her for a second. Then the corner of his mouth went up, very slightly.

"Cheers," he said.

They sat there.

The field was very quiet. She could hear the smoke still going up off the Volturi heaps, a faint crackle, and the wind in the tops of the spruces, and somewhere across the snow Nessie's small hiccupping breaths against Bella's shoulder. She could hear his heart. Slow. Heavy. She'd been hearing it for half an hour and she'd stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing the ocean when you live next to it, and now she noticed it again.

"Jake's got a theory," she said. "About imprinting. Did he tell you?"

"No."

"He thinks it's about babies." She said it to the field. "Stronger wolves. You imprint on whoever gives you the best shot at more of you. Kids with the gene. That's why it happens now and it didn't for ages. There's more vampires around, so there's more of us, so it's trying to make even more." She laughed again, and this time it hurt her throat. "Joke's on it, then."

He let her talk.

"I'm a dead end," she said.

She hadn't meant to say it. She'd said it to Jacob once, months ago, in the woods, in a fight, and she'd hated herself for it afterwards. She'd never said it to anyone else. And now she was saying it to a stranger on a log with half the Pacific Northwest watching, and she couldn't stop.

"My body stopped. When I phased. It just—stopped. Two years. Nothing." She pressed her lips together. "So there won't be any. Ever. So whatever this thinks it's doing, whatever it's for—" she knocked her knuckles against her breastbone again— "it picked the wrong girl."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Okay," he said.

She turned her head. "Okay?"

"Wasn't planning on asking."

She looked at him. He looked back. There wasn't anything in his face at all except attention, the same flat, steady attention he'd had all day, and she couldn't find a single thing in it that was pity or awkwardness or that horrible soft careful look people got.

"And I'd not lean too hard on Jake's theory," he said. "If I were you. It's a theory from a lot of blokes whose lines go one way. Mine doesn't. So whatever it's for, they don't know either."

She sat with that.

"So nobody knows," she said.

"Nobody knows."

"Not even your stupid book."

"Specially not the book." He patted the grey cover. "Written by people who know all about shapeshifters. That's who's on the way. Somebody who knows shapeshifters. I asked for one specially."

She looked at the book. She looked at him.

"Supposedly," she said.

"Supposedly."

And she laughed. Properly this time. It came up out of her before she could stop it, a real laugh, the kind she hadn't heard come out of her own mouth since before her father died, and she clapped her hand over her mouth, appalled, and it kept coming out round her fingers anyway. Across the field she heard somebody, Quil maybe, make a strangled noise.

He watched her laugh. He didn't laugh with her. But his eyes did something at the corners.

When she'd got hold of herself she sat with her hand still over her mouth, breathing hard through her nose, and stared at the snow, and felt her face burning. That was twice. Twice in an hour, in front of him. Jumping, and now this.

"I used to say," she said, when she could, "that if it ever happened to me, I'd walk into the sea."

She felt him go still beside her, a man's kind of still, the kind with breathing in it.

"I said it a lot. In the pack. At night. Everybody heard me." She wasn't looking at him. "I meant it. Every time. I'd rather be dead than be one of them, following some guy around with my tongue hanging out."

He didn't say anything for a while. When he did, his voice was exactly the same as it had been all day. Level. Unhurried.

"Are you going to?"

She thought about it. Actually thought about it. She owed herself that much. She thought about the beach at La Push in January, the grey water, the cold. She thought about walking into it. She thought about the line, and the quiet in her chest where the Sam ache used to go, and Seth pushing his head into her hand.

"No," she said.

"Good."

He let it sit a second.

"If that changes," he said, "you tell somebody. Your brother. Your mum. Me. Whoever's closest."

"You?"

"I'll be about."

She looked at him sideways. "Will you?"

He looked back at her. Then down, at his own chest, at the spot where she'd pressed her knuckles into hers.

"Looks that way," he said.

She looked away first. She told herself it was because the light was in her eyes, which was stupid, because there wasn't any light, just the grey lid of cloud and the smoke.

They sat.

"Can you undo it?" she said.

"I don't know."

"You're a wizard."

"I'm a wizard who's never seen this before." He tapped the book. "I looked. That's what all that was. It's not in there. Nothing like it's in there. I tried it every way I know and it gave me a blank page and a bit of attitude." He shrugged. "Can't undo a thing when you don't know what it is. You'd just be cutting wires."

"So you've got nothing."

"I've got about what you've got."

She huffed through her nose.

Then he said, "Would you want me to?"

She turned her head.

"If I could," he said. "If I worked out what it was, and I could cut it clean. Would you want me to?"

She opened her mouth to say yes.

It was right there. It had been right there for half an hour, since the second the ground fell out from under her. Yes. Yes, obviously, cut it, get it out of me, give me back my own head. She opened her mouth and the word was in it.

The line hummed.

And she thought about the Sam ache, small and far away, going round the line like water round a rock. She thought about the quiet in the wolf, the first quiet in two years. She thought about the growl, and her body sitting down, and how much she'd hated it, and how under the hate there'd been something that had felt for half a second like coming home. She thought about him sitting alone on the other side of an ocean with nobody in his head at three in the morning, ever.

She shut her mouth.

"I don't know," she said.

It came out very quietly. It was the most honest thing she'd said in two years.

He nodded, as if that was the answer he'd expected. He didn't look pleased about it or sorry about it. He just nodded.

"Then we find out what it is first," he said. "And then you decide."

"Me."

"You."

"What about you? Your end?"

"My end didn't ask me either," he said. "I'll have a word with it."

She stared at him. Then she looked down at the snow between her bare feet, at the wet grass coming up through it, steaming, and felt something in her throat that she didn't have any name for.

Nobody had asked her anything in two years. Not one person. Sam had told her. The phasing had told her. The pack had told her, the elders had told her, her own body had told her every month that didn't come. Everything that had happened to her had happened without anybody once stopping to ask what she wanted, and she'd stopped expecting it, and she'd stopped wanting anything anyway, so it had stopped mattering.

And this guy, this total stranger, who'd come out of the sky with a bang and burned the Volturi and gone through Nessie's head like a knife, had just turned round and handed it back to her. Like it was obvious. Like it was hers and always had been.

She didn't know what to do with that. She sat there and held it, and didn't know.

"Fine," she said finally. Gruff. "Okay. Fine."

"Fine," he agreed.

They sat on the log. The smoke went up. Across the field, she heard Nessie say something small and sleepy to Bella about an angel with a crooked wing.

"What's Geneva?" Leah said.

"Switzerland."

"I know where Geneva is, Potter."

"Then you know what Geneva is."

She shot him a look. He was looking at the sky.

"Who's coming?"

"Registration team. Couple of clerks. A Healer. The shapeshifter bloke." He paused. "And whoever else got curious."

"Will they want to—" She stopped. She didn't know how to finish it. Look at her. Poke at her. Write her down in a book that said she was as biddable as a good sheepdog.

"They'll want to," he said. "They can ask."

She opened her mouth.

The air cracked.

It came from the far side of the field, near the grey heaps where the Volturi had stood, and it came as five cracks, one after another, sharp and flat and close together, like a string of firecrackers going off on the Fourth of July. The sound slapped back off the trees. Every vampire on the Cullen side jumped. Leah jumped too, half up off the log, every muscle in her body going tight at once, the wolf in her surging up toward her skin so hard her vision went sharp at the edges.

Five people stood in the snow at the far end of the clearing.

Leah saw them all at once with eyes that still worked better than any human's. A tall dark-skinned man in a long grey coat with a heavy leather case in one hand. A short woman in a parka zipped over long robes the colour of a lime, already pulling on a pair of gloves, already looking straight across the snow toward the Cullens, toward Nessie. A young guy with red ears holding a clipboard that had a feather pen on it, and the feather pen was writing on its own, scratching across the paper so fast Leah could hear it from here. An old woman in a hat. And a fifth, a thin pale man in a tweed jacket with a scarf wrapped up to his nose, who'd stopped dead on the spot where he'd landed and was staring down at the nearest grey heap at his feet with both hands pressed over his mouth.

Beside her, the stranger stood up.

The book was gone. She hadn't seen it go. One second it had been on his knees and the next his hands were empty and he was on his feet, brushing off the seat of his jeans, looking across the field at the five of them with that same tired, flat expression he'd had all day.

"That'll be Geneva," he said.

Leah got up too.

She hadn't decided to. She was just standing, next to him, on her bare feet in the slush, with the line humming in her chest, low and steady. She didn't step back. She noticed that she hadn't. She thought about stepping back, and didn't.

He glanced at her sideways.

"Coming, Lassie?"

"Call me that in front of them," Leah said, "and I'll bite you."

"Noted," he said.

He started walking. Across the snow toward the five strangers, his boots sinking into it and the snow giving way round them into water, steam coming off his shoulders in the cold air.

Leah went with him.

She told herself it was because of the guy with the clipboard. Because of the shapeshifter specialist, and the pen that wrote on its own, and every single thing they were about to start writing down about her. Somebody was going to talk about her today, write her down, decide what she was, and she'd had two years of other people doing that.

Nobody else was going to do it without her standing there. Not today.

She walked beside him across the snow, a foot and a half away, close enough to feel the heat coming off him, and the line in her chest hummed the whole way, and she let it.
