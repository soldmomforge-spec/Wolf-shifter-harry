# Chapter Four: First Beach

She woke up because something was frying.

Bacon. A lot of bacon. And eggs, and bread in butter, and coffee strong enough to strip paint, and under all of it the cedar of the floor and the salt coming in through the sea opening and the ash of last night's fire. Gray light lay across the planks in a long bar from the opening. The surf was loud. It had been loud all night, and she'd slept through every wave of it like a kid in the back seat of a car.

Leah lifted her head off her paws.

The rug beside her was empty. Over by the black iron range, with his back to her, a man in a flannel shirt was pushing bacon around a skillet the size of a manhole cover and singing under his breath. Badly. Something about a misty mountain.

Music was coming from somewhere. Not her dad's boombox; that sat on its shelf by the opening, quiet, the tape still turned to side B. This was coming from a little wooden radio on the end of the counter that hadn't been there last night, and it was playing a man who sounded like he was screaming at a cliff from very far away, and a guitar that was trying to start a fight.

She watched him for a while without moving.

Jeans. That was the first thing. Actual jeans, faded at the knees, the kind of jeans people on the rez wore to fix trucks in. A red-and-black flannel shirt with the sleeves shoved up past his elbows, over a gray T-shirt. Boots she'd never seen, brown leather, with scuffs on the toes. His hair was standing straight up on one side where he'd slept on it. Over the end of the long table, thrown down like he'd come in from somewhere and dropped it, was a leather jacket. Dark brown. Old. The leather gone soft and cracked at the elbows and pale at the seams.

He looked like every guy who'd ever leaned on a pickup outside the gas station in Forks. Except he stood different. And he was singing to bacon.

She checked her head first, out of habit.

Quiet. Nobody phased. Seth would be asleep, face-down in his bed with one foot hanging off it, the way he'd slept since he was four. Jacob would be in a house she wasn't going to think about. The place where they lived in her head was empty and swept and waiting, like a room with the door left open.

She got up and went into the left-hand bedroom without making a sound, which was something she could still do even if he could do it better.

The room was small and square and smelled of new wood. A bed with a heavy wool blanket on it, gray and red. A window cut into the rock that looked out at the gray ocean and the sea stacks with the tide going out around them, which made no sense at all, because the room was on the wrong side of the cave for that. Her duffel bag sat on a chest at the foot of the bed. She hadn't put it there.

She phased back. It still hurt a little, being poured into the smaller shape. She stood shivering in the cold that didn't touch her for a second, then dug in the duffel for jeans and a flannel of her own, and pulled them on, and went out barefoot with her hair down her back.

"Morning," Harry said, without turning around.

"What are you wearing?"

He looked down at himself. Then over his shoulder at her. "Clothes."

"Those aren't your clothes."

"They're entirely my clothes. I bought them." He flipped a piece of bacon with his fingers and didn't seem to notice it was hot. "There's a shop in Port Angeles that's open at five in the morning for fishermen. Lovely woman called Darlene. She sold me four shirts and told me I looked like her second husband."

"Was that good?"

"I didn't ask. She seemed fond of him." He went back to the bacon. "When in Rome."

Leah walked over to the table and looked at the jacket.

Up close it was older than she'd thought. Somebody had worn it for years, hard. There was a long scar down one sleeve where the leather had been torn and sewn back up with thick black thread by somebody who wasn't very good at sewing. The collar was rubbed shiny. It smelled like old leather and motor oil, the ghost of it, soaked in so deep it would never come out, and like Harry, and under that, very faint, like somebody else. Somebody who'd been gone a long time.

"That was my godfather's," Harry said.

She looked up. He hadn't turned around.

"He had a motorbike," Harry said, to the bacon. "When he was young. Before everything. Big black thing, ridiculously loud. He used to ride it around with that on and frighten the neighbors." He shrugged one shoulder. "I got the jacket. A friend of mine's got the bike. He's far too big for it."

"You never wear it."

"Hadn't been anywhere it seemed right." He lifted the skillet off the heat. "Seemed right here."

She didn't say anything. She touched the stitched-up tear on the sleeve with one finger, once, and then left the jacket alone.

---

He'd made enough for nine people. Bacon in a heap on a platter. Eggs, a dozen of them, fried hard at the edges. Toast. Tomatoes, cut in half and cooked in the bacon fat, which seemed like a strange thing to do to a tomato. Mushrooms. Some kind of beans in orange sauce that he spooned out of a can onto both plates as if it was the most natural thing in the world.

"Beans," Leah said. "For breakfast."

"Full English."

"It's disgusting."

"It's *heritage,*" he said, and pointed his fork at her. "Eat your beans."

She ate her beans. They were sweet and weird and she ate all of them. She ate four eggs and half the bacon and six pieces of toast, and he ate the rest, and somewhere in the middle of it the song on the little radio ended and another one started, a slow one this time, the singer going on about a woman and a road, and Harry hummed along to it with his mouth full.

"What is this?"

He looked genuinely offended. "Led Zeppelin."

"I know who Led Zeppelin is."

"Then you know what this is."

"My dad was a Creedence guy. He said Zeppelin was English people screaming."

"Your dad," said Harry, "was a great man with one tragic blind spot." He reached over and turned the radio up a notch. "This is 'Ramble On.' This is a song about a man who can't stay put. I'd have thought you'd relate."

She threw a piece of toast at him. He caught it without looking up and ate it.

"Where'd the radio come from?"

"London. I nipped back at four this morning." He reached into his back pocket and dropped something on the table between them. A shoebox, old and soft at the corners, with the lid held on by a rubber band. "And these."

She pulled off the rubber band. Cassette tapes. Dozens of them, in cracked plastic cases with handwritten labels. *ZEP IV. ZEP II. SABBATH — PARANOID. PURPLE — MACHINE HEAD. QUEEN — NEWS OF THE WORLD. WHO'S NEXT. AC/DC BACK IN BLACK. STONES — STICKY FINGERS.* Some of the handwriting was faded almost to nothing.

"Also his," Harry said. "Sirius. The godfather. He had a theory that everything worth listening to was recorded between 1968 and 1980 and everything after that was a crime against God."

"That's insane."

"He was. A bit." He was smiling, but only at one corner. "He missed most of the eighties. Long story. He didn't feel he'd lost much."

She turned one of the tapes over in her hands. *THE WHO — LIVE AT LEEDS. LOUD.* Underlined twice.

"What are you going to play them on?" she said. "That radio doesn't have a tape deck."

"Ah," said Harry, and his face did something that she'd come to recognize over the last month. The look of a man about to say something he knew was going to get something thrown at him.

"What did you do."

"I bought a car."

She put the tape down.

"Last night," he said. "Well — this morning. Early. After you were asleep. I rang Hargreaves."

"You can *apparate.*"

"I can. But I've decided I'm in America now." He leaned back on the bench and laced his fingers behind his head. "And in America, you have a car. A loud one. With a tape deck. Everyone knows that. It's in all the films."

"You've never been to America."

"I've been to America loads of times. I've never *driven* in America." He considered. "I had a mate whose dad had a flying car once, but it ended up living wild in a forest, so I'm owed."

"A *flying car* — "

"Long story. Second year. There was a tree involved." He waved it away. "Anyway. I told Hargreaves I wanted something black and American from the sixties that sounded like it wanted to fight somebody, and he rang me back at half four and said he'd found a man in Kansas."

"Kansas."

"Lawrence, Kansas. Collector. Restored it himself, apparently, nut by nut. Didn't want to sell. Hargreaves made him want to." He looked pleased. "It's on an aeroplane to Seattle as we speak. Then a lorry. It should be at your mum's by about three."

Leah looked at him across the wreckage of the full English.

"How much," she said.

"I didn't ask."

"Of *course* you didn't ask."

"It seemed rude. The man loved it. You don't ask a man how much he loved something." He sat up, and the grin went across his face, wide and crooked and completely unrepentant. "It's a 1967 Chevrolet Impala. Four doors. Black. Hargreaves says you can hear it coming from the next county."

"You don't know what any of that means."

"Not a word," Harry agreed happily. "Isn't it marvelous?"

She meant to stay annoyed. She'd been annoyed at him for most of the last month, in one way or another, and she was good at it, and it was important to keep in practice.

She laughed instead. Loud, out loud, in her dad's cave, with her head back, the way she hadn't laughed in a kitchen in a year and a half.

He watched her do it with his head tipped to one side. Then he picked up the last piece of bacon off the platter and held it out to her across the table, without a word.

She took it.

---

Later, washing the skillet at the deep stone sink because he'd cooked and that was the rule in her mother's house and therefore the rule in this one, she said, without turning around: "You know Seth's going to lose his mind."

"About the car?"

"About everything." She scrubbed. "He's fifteen. He's going to follow you around like a puppy. He's going to want to see you do magic. He's going to ask you about forty thousand questions."

"I like questions."

"You don't answer half of them."

"I like *asking* questions. Answering's a separate skill." He was drying plates behind her with a dish towel over his shoulder, the way he'd been at the lodge. "He's all right, your brother."

"He's the best of us."

"You said." A pause. "And Jacob?"

She stopped scrubbing.

Jacob was going to be the problem. She knew it the way she'd known about the hunt on the plateau before it happened, the way the herd would run. Jacob had been in her head yesterday on the Skagit flats. He'd seen the mine and the bar and Liard. He'd asked her the imprint question and he'd been relieved. But Jacob had been in her head on the flats from fifty miles away, and today he'd be standing in her mother's yard, and he was the Alpha, and he'd been raised on the same stories she had. And there was a stranger living in the Clearwater cave on tribal land, a stranger who smelled like something none of them had a name for, who walked on water and talked out of a wolf's mouth.

"Jacob," she said slowly, "is going to look at you very hard."

"Fair enough."

"He's going to want to know what you are. And what you want. And whether you're going to be a problem."

"Am I going to be a problem?"

She turned around with the skillet dripping in her hands and looked at him. He was standing in the middle of her father's cave in a dead man's jacket, with a dish towel over his shoulder, eating a cold piece of toast he'd found somewhere.

"Probably," Leah said.

"Brilliant," said Harry, and took the skillet off her, and dried it.

---

Seth had been sitting on the porch steps since a quarter past two, and Jacob had been sitting next to him since half past, and neither of them was pretending anymore that they were there for any other reason.

"He said three," Seth said.

"You said."

"Leah said he said three."

"Seth," said Jacob, "I heard you the first nine times."

Jacob smelled like the Cullens. Seth didn't mind it the way Leah did; he'd stopped minding it somewhere around the third week of running around their house. But it was all over Jacob, in his hair and his T-shirt, bleach and burnt sugar, and every so often the wind shifted and Seth's nose wrinkled up on its own. Jacob had come straight from there. He'd been there all night, under the window, and he looked it. He'd said he was going home to sleep. He'd been saying it for forty minutes.

"You're not going home," Seth said.

"I'm going home in a minute."

"You want to see the car."

"I want to see the *guy,*" Jacob said. He was looking at the trees behind the woodpile. "The car's — whatever."

"He said it's a sixty-seven Impala."

Jacob didn't say anything.

"Black," Seth said.

"Shut up, Seth."

They came out of the trees at five to three.

Seth saw his sister first, because he always did, because she was his sister. She was in jeans and boots and a flannel shirt of Dad's she must have taken out of the hall closet that morning, a blue one, too big for her, with the sleeves rolled. Her hair was loose. She was walking fast, the way she always walked, and she was talking with her hands, and she was laughing.

Seth had been in her head, before. He'd been in there on the worst nights of both their lives. He knew her better than anybody did, better than Mom, better than Sam ever had. And he'd been in there yesterday on the Skagit flats and seen her happy, he'd *felt* it, like sun coming in a window.

But he hadn't seen her face do it. Not since he was about twelve.

The man next to her was wearing a leather jacket.

Seth had seen him through Leah's eyes and through his own, last night, across the kitchen table, and he still wasn't ready for it. Tall, but not as tall as Jake. Big through the shoulders, black hair standing up. Green eyes. Hands in the pockets of the jacket. And there was a smell to him — dirt after rain and pine and cold rock and a sharp hot thing like a struck match, all of it threaded through with Leah, now, after a night in the same cave — that made every hair on Seth's arms stand up, not because it was scary but because it was *big.* Like standing too close to a waterfall.

Beside him, Jacob had gone still. Not the dangerous kind. The Alpha kind. The kind where he got very quiet and his shoulders came down and he looked at something the way Seth had seen him look at Sam across the treaty line.

Jacob stood up off the steps.

Leah stopped laughing. She looked at Jacob, and then at Harry, and she didn't say anything. She just stopped walking and let them come together the last ten feet.

Harry stopped in front of Jacob and looked up. Not by much. Two or three inches, maybe five. But up.

"You're Jacob," he said.

"Yeah."

"You smell like a vampire."

Seth's mouth fell open. Jacob blinked.

"Sorry," Harry said. "That came out wrong. I've never smelled one. Leah said bleach and burnt sugar and something dead in the sun." His nose wrinkled. "She undersold it."

Jacob looked at him for a long moment.

Then something at the corner of Jacob's mouth went, very slightly, sideways. "Yeah," Jacob said. "You get used to it."

"Do you?"

"No," Jacob admitted. "Not really."

Harry held out his hand. Jacob took it. Seth watched both their hands, the same size, the same brown-and-pale against each other, and felt the heat coming off the two of them from five feet away, and thought that if anybody walked by right now they'd probably think there was a fire somewhere.

They shook once. Neither of them squeezed. Neither of them let go first, either, for about a second too long, and then they both did at the same time.

"You're living on our land," Jacob said.

"I am."

"In Harry Clearwater's place."

"Sue offered," said Harry. "I said yes." He didn't look away. "If you'd rather I didn't, say so. I'll go back to Canada and sit on a dock and pine."

Jacob glanced at Leah.

Leah was standing with her arms folded and one eyebrow up, looking at Jacob in a way Seth knew very well, the way that said *I dare you.*

"She'd kill me," Jacob said.

"She would," Harry agreed. "I've seen her with a caribou."

And Jacob laughed. A short one, surprised out of him, the first time Seth had heard him laugh at anything that wasn't the baby in weeks. He rubbed the back of his neck and shook his head and looked at Leah again.

"He's — " Jacob started.

"I know," said Leah.

"Okay," said Jacob.

Then a truck came down the road.

---

It was a car hauler. A big one, a red Peterbilt with a long empty trailer behind it and one single car strapped down at the very end under a gray cover, like somebody had driven a hundred and fifty miles from Seattle with a trailer for nine cars to carry one. It came down the road too slow, grinding through its gears, and stopped in front of the Clearwater house with a hiss of air brakes that sent every crow in the trees up into the sky.

The driver climbed down. A heavy guy in a Seahawks cap with a clipboard. He looked at Jacob, and at Seth, and at Harry, and he swallowed.

"Uh," he said. "Potter?"

"That's me."

"I got a — I'm supposed to — " He held out the clipboard. "Man in Kansas said if there's a scratch on it, he'd find me. He said it twice. He gave me his home number."

"Fair enough," said Harry, and signed.

It took the driver ten minutes to get the straps off and the car down the ramps, and he did it like he was handling a bomb. Then he looked at Harry, and Harry nodded, and the driver pulled off the cover.

Seth heard Jacob stop breathing.

It was long. That was the first thing. Long and low and black, black like the inside of a closed eye, so polished the clouds over La Push slid across the hood in it. Chrome everywhere, the bumpers and the grille and the trim along the sides, and the sun came out for about three seconds right then, which it never did, and every piece of chrome lit up at once like somebody had flipped a switch. Four doors. A long flat trunk. Tail lights in sets of three.

"Huh," said Harry. "That's nice, isn't it."

Jacob walked toward it like a man in a church.

He went all the way around it, slowly, without touching it. He crouched by the back wheel and looked at the tire. He looked underneath. He stood up and looked at Harry, and his face was doing something Seth hadn't seen it do since Jacob was fourteen and building the Rabbit in his dad's garage out of junk.

"Can I," Jacob said. "Can I pop the — "

"The what?"

"The *hood.*"

"Oh. The bonnet. Yes. Go mad."

Jacob found the latch. The hood went up. Jacob bent over the engine and went completely silent.

"What?" Harry said. "Is it broken?"

"It's a 396," Jacob said, in a strange voice.

"Is that good?"

Jacob turned his head and looked at Harry the way you'd look at a man who'd just asked if water was wet.

"It's a *big block,*" Jacob said. "Turbo-Jet. Somebody rebuilt the whole — look at this. *Look* at it. There isn't a speck of — this is all original, the carb, the — " He reached in and touched something with one finger, very lightly, like he was afraid it might break. "Do you have any idea what you've got here?"

"Not the faintest," said Harry cheerfully. "Hargreaves said it sounds like it wants to fight somebody."

Jacob stared at him.

Leah, who had been leaning on the porch rail through all of this with her arms folded, made a sound Seth had never heard come out of her. A snort. A real one. Then she put her hand over her mouth.

"Keys," Harry said to the driver.

The driver handed them over like he was glad to be rid of them, climbed into the Peterbilt, and drove away a lot faster than he'd come.

Harry tossed the keys up and caught them.

"Right," he said. "Who wants pie?"

---

"I can't," Jacob said. "I have to — I said I'd be back by — "

"Jake," said Seth.

"One hour," said Jacob. "*One.*"

They went to Forks.

Jacob sat in the back behind Leah, and Seth sat in the back behind Harry, and the inside of the car smelled like old leather and new wax and a little bit like Kansas, whatever Kansas smelled like, dust and grass. The seats were wide and flat and slippery. Harry put the key in and turned it, and the car woke up underneath them with a sound like a bear coming out of a cave, a deep rough rumbling that Seth felt in his back teeth and his stomach and his feet.

"Oh," said Harry softly. "Oh, I see."

"Yeah," said Jacob, in the back seat, like he was praying.

Harry reached under the seat and came out with an old shoebox full of tapes. He picked one without looking and pushed it into the dash. There was a hiss. Then a guitar came out of the speakers so loud Seth jumped.

"Rules of the road," Harry announced, over the guitar. "Driver picks the music. Passenger shuts her cakehole."

Leah turned her head and looked at him.

She didn't say anything. She just looked. Seth, in the back seat, had seen people get that look from his sister before. Paul had gotten it, once, at a bonfire, about two seconds before Leah threw a full can of Coke at his head from thirty feet away and hit him square in the middle of the forehead.

"Say that again," Leah said pleasantly, "and you're going to be picking that tape out of your teeth."

Harry considered this.

"Passenger," he said, "picks the music on alternate Tuesdays."

"It's Tuesday."

"Is it?" He looked delighted. "What are the odds."

He ejected the tape. Leah dug through the shoebox in her lap, reading the faded labels, and Seth watched her stop at one. She turned it over in her hands. The label was different from the others: fresh white, the writing on it in black pen, neat and slanted. Different handwriting.

*CREEDENCE — CHRONICLE.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked at Harry. He was looking at the road, very carefully, with both hands on the wheel.

She didn't say anything about it. She put it in.

And as they pulled out onto the La Push road, the song that came on was one Seth knew all the way down in his bones. Dad's truck. Dad's boat. Dad's shower, on Saturday mornings, too loud. It was the one about the senator's son, the rich kid who doesn't have to go to the war.

Leah listened to the first verse with her head tipped back against the seat. Then she turned and looked at Harry.

"This one's about you," she said.

Harry listened. Seth watched his ears go red. Actually red, the tops of them.

"It's about rich boys who don't get sent to fight," Harry said.

"Yep."

He drove a little way in silence, the car rumbling, the trees going by.

"I went to the war," he said. "For the record. I was seventeen."

"I know you did."

"Just so long as that's noted."

"It's noted," Leah said. "You're still a rich boy."

"I am *still a rich boy,*" Harry agreed, and turned it up.

And Leah sang.

Seth's sister. In the front seat of a black car, with her bare feet up on the dashboard and her hair whipping around in the wind from the window, singing at the top of her lungs, off-key, *terribly,* exactly like Dad. Harry singing along, worse. The car doing seventy on the curves of the La Push road with the trees going by in a green blur, and Harry steering with one hand, his other arm along the back of the bench seat behind Leah's head.

Seth looked over at Jacob.

Jacob was looking at Leah, too. His face had gone soft and kind of stunned, like somebody who'd walked into a room he'd been in a hundred times and found all the furniture moved.

"Did you know she could do that?" Jacob said, under the music.

"Sing?"

"*Smile.*"

Seth didn't say anything. He didn't trust himself to. He looked out the window at the trees and let the wind hit his face until his eyes stopped stinging.

---

Coming into Forks, where the highway ran along through the trees a couple of miles north of town, Harry's nose wrinkled.

"Hm," he said.

"Their road's up there," Jacob said from the back. He didn't need to say whose. "Couple of miles. On the right."

Harry glanced in the mirror at him. "And they know I'm here."

"Edward read it all out of my head last night. Everything Leah had in hers." Jacob hesitated. "He wants to meet you. Carlisle too. They're — Carlisle's *really* curious. He's got about nine hundred questions."

"I bet he does." Harry drove past the turnoff without slowing down, without even looking at it. "Tell them I'll come by. Not yet. I'm on holiday."

"That's it?"

"That's it. I'll knock on the door when I've got my bearings. Politely." He met Jacob's eyes in the mirror. "I'm not here for them, Jacob. I'm here for her. And pie."

Jacob looked at him for a while in the mirror. Then he nodded, once, and sat back.

Seth let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

---

The diner hadn't changed. It never did. The same booths with the same tape on the same rips in the vinyl. The same smell of coffee and fryer grease. The same bell on the door. Marcy was behind the counter, the way Marcy had been behind the counter for as long as Seth had been alive, with her reading glasses on a chain and her hair the same red it had been since 1983.

She looked up when the bell rang and her mouth fell open.

"*Leah Clearwater,*" Marcy said. "Your mom said you went to *Canada.*"

"I did."

"For how long?"

"A while." Leah slid into the corner booth, the big one, and Harry slid in after her, and Seth and Jacob took the other side, and the table was suddenly very full of very large people. "This is Harry."

Marcy looked at Harry. Harry smiled at her. Marcy's eyebrows went up over her reading glasses and stayed there.

"Hi, Harry," Marcy said slowly.

"Hello, Marcy. I'm told there's pie."

"There's apple, cherry, marionberry, and banana cream."

"Yes," said Harry.

Marcy waited. "Which one, hon?"

"Yes," Harry said again, as if she might not have heard the first time.

Marcy looked at Leah.

"He means all of them," Leah said.

"All four?"

"And a cheeseburger," Harry said. "With bacon. And chips. Fries. Sorry. Fries. And a coffee." He thought. "And then the pie."

Marcy wrote it down. She looked at Jacob. Jacob ordered two cheeseburgers. She looked at Seth. Seth ordered three. She looked at Leah, who ordered a cheeseburger and a club sandwich and a side of onion rings and a chocolate shake, and Marcy wrote it all down without blinking, because she'd been feeding Quileute boys for twenty years and she'd seen Leah eat before.

"Some things don't change," Marcy said, and went to the window.

Somebody at the counter turned around on his stool.

It was Chief Swan. Seth hadn't seen him when they came in; he'd been at the far end, in his uniform, with his back to the door and a plate of fried fish in front of him and a newspaper folded next to it. He looked at the booth. At Jacob, and at Seth. Then at Leah, and his face did something. Then at Harry, and it did something else.

Charlie Swan had known about them for almost three weeks now. Jacob had stripped off his shirt and phased in Charlie's yard, and Charlie had sat down on his own front steps and said *okay* about eleven times. Since then he'd been dealing with it the way he dealt with everything, which was by deciding exactly how much he needed to know and not one single thing more.

He got up off his stool and came over, slow, with his coffee cup in his hand.

"Leah," he said.

"Hi, Charlie."

"Your mom's real glad you're back."

"I know."

"So am I." He said it gruff, looking at his coffee, and Seth saw Leah's face go strange and then go still. Charlie had been at their house a lot since Dad died. More than a lot. Seth had thought, for a while, that Leah hadn't noticed. Last night, in the kitchen, with the mug in the dish drainer, he'd figured out she'd noticed everything.

Then Charlie looked at Harry. Harry had stood up out of the booth. Not all the way — there wasn't room — but he'd stood, and he held out his hand.

"Harry Potter," he said. "Pleasure."

Charlie's hand stopped halfway.

"Harry," he said.

"I'm afraid so."

Charlie looked at him for a long moment. Seth knew what he was thinking. Everybody at the table did. Charlie Swan and Harry Clearwater had fished together for twenty-five years. They'd sat in the same boat every Saturday morning from April to October and not said ten words between them and been perfectly happy. Charlie had been the one who drove Mom home from the hospital that night. Charlie had carried the casket.

"Huh," Charlie said, finally. He shook Harry's hand. "You one of them?" He tipped his head at Jacob and Seth.

"Sort of," Harry said. "Different sort."

Charlie held up his free hand, palm out.

"Don't," he said. "I don't need to know."

"Fair enough."

"That your car out front?"

"It is."

Charlie looked out the window at the Impala, long and black and gleaming in the diner's little lot between Marcy's Buick and a logging truck. He looked at it for a while.

"That's a nice car," he said.

"Thank you."

"I could hear it from the station." Charlie took a sip of his coffee. "If I catch you doing seventy on the La Push road again, I'm gonna tell Sue."

Harry looked at Leah. Leah was biting her lip.

"Noted, Chief," Harry said.

Charlie nodded. He looked at Leah one more time, and something went across his face that Seth didn't have a word for. Then he dropped a twenty on the counter for his fish and went out, and the bell jingled behind him, and they watched him through the window get into his cruiser and sit there for a minute without starting it before he finally drove off.

Nobody said anything for a bit.

"He was my dad's best friend," Leah said quietly.

"I gathered," Harry said.

The food came.

---

It took Harry fourteen minutes to eat a bacon cheeseburger, a basket of fries, and four slices of pie, and he did it while answering Seth's questions.

Not all of them. Seth had about a hundred. Harry answered the ones about the wand (holly, eleven inches, had it since he was eleven, "it doesn't come out much these days, it gets jealous"), and the ones about the school (castle, Scotland, moving staircases, a ghost with his head half off), and the one about whether magic could fix a busted transmission ("probably, but Jacob would never speak to me again"). He didn't answer the one about how he'd turned into a wolf the size of a truck. He just pointed his fork at Seth and said "fourth date," and Leah snorted into her shake.

"That doesn't even *mean* anything," Seth said.

"It means a great deal to me."

Seth reached across and stole three of Harry's fries.

Old Quil's knees had been right. Saturday came in clear.

It never did that in October. By four o'clock the clouds had pulled back off the ocean like somebody drawing a sheet off a bed, and the whole sky over First Beach went wide and pale and cold. The sea stacks stood up black out of the water. James Island sat at the mouth of the river with the light behind it. The surf came in long and slow and loud over the gray sand, and the wind off it smelled like salt and kelp and the end of the year.

Sam Uley stood by the fire with his hands in his pockets and watched the sun go down toward the water and did not look at the road.

They'd built it big. Paul and Jared had been hauling driftwood since noon, the old silver logs that came down the river every winter and piled up along the top of the beach, and the fire was taller than Sam by now, roaring, with the salt in the wood turning the flames blue and green at their edges the way it always did. Somebody had brought the old plywood boards so Billy's chair could get across the sand. Somebody had strung a volleyball net between two driftwood posts down near the water. There were coolers. There were hot dogs. There was a card table with a stack of paper plates on it and a bag of buns and two loaves of white bread for toast, and a big Tupperware box of Emily's muffins with the lid off, and the kids had already eaten half of them.

Everybody was there.

Billy, in his chair on the plywood, with a blanket over his legs. Old Quil beside him on a camp chair. Sue on a log with her coat buttoned to the neck. Rachel sitting on the sand between Paul's knees, laughing at something he'd said. Jared and Kim, sharing a blanket. Embry and Quil throwing a football back and forth over the fire, and Claire on Quil's shoulders, shrieking every time he caught it. Collin and Brady, thirteen, eating hot dogs one after another with the grim concentration of boys who'd only recently found out how much they could eat. Jacob, who'd come at five and said he'd stay an hour, sitting on a cooler with his elbows on his knees. Seth, who had been standing at the top of the beach looking at the road for twenty minutes.

"You're doing it," she said quietly.

"Doing what."

"Not looking at the road. Very hard."

"She's coming home," Emily said. "That's good, Sam. That's a good thing."

"I know."

"Then let it be a good thing."

He'd been trying to. All week. He'd stood in Billy's front room on Wednesday night and looked down at Sue Clearwater telling him whose fire it was, and he'd heard her say *my daughter laughed at that table,* and something in his chest had gone tight and hot and he still didn't know what to call it. Relief, maybe. Guilt. The thing that was always there, under everything, whenever he thought about Leah. For a year and a half he'd had her in his head every time they both phased. Every thought she'd tried not to have. Every night she'd tried not to think about him, and thought about him anyway.

He'd felt all of it. He'd been the reason for all of it. And there'd never been one single thing he could do.

He'd told the boys to be civil. Paul had said a word Sam didn't let him say in front of Claire.

He didn't look at the road.

And then he didn't have to, because everybody on the beach heard it at once.

It started as a sound under the surf. Low. Deep. Something big coming down the La Push road, a rumble you felt in the sand under your feet before you heard it in your ears. Then over the top of the rumble, getting louder fast — a guitar. A hard, slamming, crunching guitar, and drums like somebody kicking a door down, and a man screaming over the top of it about being back. *Back in black.* So loud it carried over a quarter mile of open beach and the whole Pacific Ocean, like the car was driving straight out of the sky.

Every head on First Beach turned.

It came into the lot at the top of the beach and stopped. Long and black and low, the last of the sun lying along it like gold paint, chrome blazing. The music cut off in the middle of a word. The engine ran on another second, rough and deep, and then that cut off too, and in the sudden quiet the surf seemed very loud.

The driver's door opened.

Leah got out.

Sam heard Paul say, behind him, "What the *hell* — "

She came around the front of the car with the keys spinning on one finger. She was wearing jeans and boots and a blue flannel shirt that Sam knew. He'd seen it on Harry Clearwater a hundred times, out on the boat, at the co-op, in the Clearwaters' kitchen. And over it, too big for her through the shoulders, the sleeves pushed up to her elbows, a beat-up brown leather jacket that hung almost to her thighs.

The passenger door opened.

He was carrying pies.

Four of them, in white bakery boxes, stacked up so high he had to look around them. He got out of the car with the boxes balanced on one hand and his other hand out for balance and said something to Leah over the roof of the car that Sam couldn't hear. And Leah —

Leah laughed.

It came down the beach on the wind, and it went into Sam like a fishhook.

He knew her laugh. He'd known it since she was fifteen. He'd heard it behind the community center during the fireworks, and in his truck on the bluff, and in her parents' kitchen. He'd thought he'd memorized it.

He'd never heard this one.

It was louder. It came all the way up from the bottom of her and didn't care who heard it. She threw her head back and laughed at whatever the man had said, and reached across the hood of the car and took the top pie box off his stack, and he said something else, outraged, and she said something back and opened the box and stuck her finger straight into the middle of the pie and licked it off.

Tall. Not as tall as Sam, not as tall as any of the older boys, but big, and built like he used it. Black hair standing up every direction in the wind. A flannel shirt and a gray T-shirt and jeans and boots, all of it so ordinary that it looked wrong on him somehow, like a costume. He walked across the sand without his boots sinking in. Sam noticed that. He noticed it the way you'd notice a dog that didn't bark.

And the smell.

It hit Sam about forty feet out, coming down the wind ahead of them. He'd smelled it on the trail on Wednesday night, on the trees up on the headland, but that had been old and cold. This was fresh, and it was the man himself, and the wolf in Sam's chest came straight up off the floor. Wet earth. Pine. Cold rock, like the back of a sea cave. And over it all, through it, something sharp and bright and electric, like the second after lightning hits close. Underneath it was heat. A lot of it. More than Sam's own, more than any of them. It came off the man like the fire coming off the driftwood behind Sam's back.

And something else. Something Sam couldn't name, and didn't want to, because it was very big, and very old, and it didn't smell like it was afraid of anything on this beach.

Sam's hackles were up. He didn't have hackles. They were up anyway.

Beside him, Emily took her hand out of his pocket and laced her fingers through his and held on.

The sun went into the sea.

It did. A little. Down in the old place, the place that would always be a bit bruised.

But mostly, to his own surprise, what he felt was something he hadn't felt in eighteen months when he looked at Leah Clearwater.

Lighter.

"Occasionally."

"So do a trick."

The talk around the fire didn't stop, exactly. It just went thinner. Embry, across the fire, stopped with a marshmallow halfway to his mouth. Jared and Kim, under their blanket, turned their heads. Collin and Brady looked at each other.

"Can't," Harry said. "I'm on probation."

"Probation," said Paul.

"He's on probation," Leah agreed. She reached down over his shoulder and took one of the hot dogs off his plate. "Nobody gets turned into anything."

"Who said anything about turning anybody into anything?"

"I've met you," said Leah, with her mouth full. "I know what you're like."

"I've been very good."

"You put AC/DC on so loud it set off a car alarm at the Oceanside."

"That was a *character witness* for the car."

Paul looked between them, his eyebrows climbing.

"Come on," Paul said. "One. A little one. Make something float or whatever."

Harry tipped his head back against Leah's knee and looked up at her, upside down. A question.

She thought about it. She looked across the fire at Paul Lahote, who'd spent a year and a half in her head and made sure she knew exactly what he thought about having a girl in the pack, every single night, in detail.

"Not a person," she said. "Not a single person."

"Not a person," Harry agreed. He looked at the beer can in Paul's hand.

Nothing happened. He didn't move or say anything. He just looked at the can for about a second, and then he looked back at his plate and picked up his pie.

Paul waited. He looked at the can. He shook it. He took a long, suspicious swallow.

His face changed.

"What — " He took another swallow. He looked at the can. It was the same can. Silver and blue. *Rainier.* "Is this — this is *root beer.*"

"Is it?" said Harry.

"*Change it back.*"

"No," said Harry, and ate his pie.

Rachel laughed so hard she fell over sideways in the sand. Embry dropped his marshmallow in the fire. Over on the plywood, Old Quil made a wheezing, creaking sound that it took Leah a second to realize was laughing. Paul looked at the can, and at Harry, and at the can, and then — Leah would remember this for the rest of her life — he took another swallow, because it was apparently very good root beer.

"I hate this guy," Paul said, to Rachel.

"No, you don't," said Rachel, still on her back.

"I *really* hate this guy."

"You're drinking it," Leah pointed out.

"It's *good,*" Paul said, betrayed.

Leah laughed. She put her head back and laughed at Paul Lahote across a bonfire on First Beach, loud and long, and she didn't care who heard it. When she came back down, Embry was staring at her across the fire with his mouth open.

"What," she said.

"Who *are* you?" Embry said.

She picked a marshmallow out of the bag at her feet and threw it at his head. He didn't even try to dodge. It bounced off his forehead and fell in his lap.

"Shut up, Embry," she said, and she was smiling, and she could feel herself smiling, and she let him see it.

Embry looked down at the marshmallow in his lap. He looked back up at her. Something slow and amazed came across his face.

"Hi, Leah," he said quietly.

"Hi, Embry."

---

She got cold, later. Not the way normal people got cold — she couldn't, anymore — but the kind of restless she got when she'd been sitting too long, and she shifted on the log and pulled the jacket tighter. Harry didn't look around. He just reached back over his shoulder with one hand, palm up, and she put her ankle in it without thinking, and he held on to it the way he had in the cave, and they both went on watching the fire.

Across the fire, Kim nudged Jared under the blanket and nodded at them.

Leah ignored it. She'd had a year and a half of people watching her across fires. This was different. This kind she could stand.

She was halfway through Harry's last hot dog when she felt him go still.

It was a small thing. She'd only have caught it because she was touching him. His back went tight against her shins. His hand stopped moving on her ankle.

She looked where he was looking.

Quil. Down by the water, at the edge of the firelight, swinging Claire around by her hands in a big circle while she shrieked. Claire's little feet flying out over the sand, her hair streaming, her face lit up. Quil laughing with her. He set her down and she staggered dizzily and fell on her bottom and laughed harder, and he scooped her up and put her on his shoulders, and she grabbed two big fistfuls of his hair and kicked her heels against his chest like he was a pony.

"She's sweet," Harry said quietly. "Whose is she?"

Leah swallowed the hot dog.

"She's Emily's niece," she said. "Her sister's kid."

"And Quil's the — babysitter? Uncle?"

"Quil imprinted on her."

He turned his head and looked up at her.

"She's two," Leah said. "And a half. He was there when Emily's sister brought her over last spring, and he looked at her, and that was it." She kept her voice flat. "He's not — it's not like that. It's not ever going to be like that, not while she's little. He's whatever she needs. Big brother. Babysitter. Pony." She watched Claire bounce on Quil's shoulders. "When she's older, maybe it changes. Maybe it doesn't. It's up to her. That's what they say."

Harry looked at Claire for a long time.

He didn't say anything. But Leah watched the color go out of his face. It went slowly, from the top down, the way it had gone out of Kroll's at the end, until he looked like he'd seen something in the water nobody else had. He set the paper plate down on the sand very carefully, with the last of the pie still on it.

He'd never left pie on a plate before. Not once. Not in a month.

---

"It's the best thing that ever happened to me."

Jared said it. Leah never found out who'd asked; somebody had, while she was watching Harry's face, and Jared was answering, in that soft proud voice he always used when he talked about Kim. He had his arm around her under the blanket. Kim was looking up at him like he'd hung the moon.

"You just — see her," Jared said. "And everything else goes away. Everything. It's like the whole world's sitting on top of you and then all of a sudden it's not, and there's only one thing holding you down, and it's her." He kissed the top of Kim's head. "I'd do anything for her. I'd be anything she wanted. Doesn't matter what."

Around the fire, people were nodding. Sam, with Emily against his side. Paul, with Rachel's head on his knee. Quil, down by the water, holding Claire's ankles on his shoulders. Even Jacob, on his cooler, with his eyes gone soft and far away, thinking about a white house in the trees and a baby asleep inside it.

Leah had heard it a hundred times. She'd *felt* it, from the inside, through Sam, through Jared, through Quil, till she thought she'd drown in it. She sat on her log and looked at the fire and didn't say anything, because there was nothing she could say that she hadn't already thought a thousand times at the inside of her own skull while the whole pack listened.

"What about you guys?" Jared said, across the fire, to Harry. Friendly. Curious. "Wizards. You got anything like it?"

Harry didn't answer right away.

He sat with his back against Leah's knees and his hands loose between his own knees, and looked into the fire, and Leah could feel him thinking about it. Not about what to say. About whether to say it.

"We've got something that looks a lot like it," he said finally.

"Yeah?" Jared grinned. "What do you call it?"

"Bonds." Harry's voice was very even. "Old magic. Older than most of what we've got. It ties one person to another, so the second person wants whatever the first one needs. Can't leave. Can't want to leave. Would do anything. Be anything." He looked at the fire. "They were used on servants, mostly. Prisoners of war. People who'd been bought."

The fire cracked. Nobody said anything.

"Most of them have been illegal for about four hundred years," Harry said. "There's a few old families that still keep them on the quiet. I've broken nine." He was quiet for a second. "It's a bit of a speciality of mine, as it happens."

Jared's grin had gone away.

"So no," said Harry. "We've nothing like *yours.* I'm sure. But from where I'm sitting — " he looked across the fire at Claire on Quil's shoulders, and his jaw went tight, and then let go again — "I'll be honest with you, mate. It looks eerily like a slave bond."

The silence around the fire went all the way out to the edge of the light. On his cooler, Jacob had gone very still, looking down at his own hands.

Leah didn't breathe.

Then Harry shrugged one shoulder, and something came back into his face, a dry tired crookedness, and he picked his plate back up off the sand.

"But whatever floats your boat, man," he said, and ate the last of the pie.

---

Sam stood up.

Leah felt Harry not move. He didn't look up. He didn't put the plate down. He just went on chewing, and waited, the way he'd waited on the dock for her to decide.

"You don't know," Sam said. His voice was low and very controlled, and it was shaking underneath. "You don't know *anything* about it."

"No," Harry agreed. "I don't."

"Then you don't get to sit at our fire and — "

"I can tell you what it looks like. From out here. That's all I've got." Harry looked up at him then. His face was calm. "You asked."

"*Jared* asked."

"Then I'm telling Jared. And you're listening. And you're angry, which I understand." He paused. "I'd be angry. If it were me. I'd also want to know."

Sam's hands had closed into fists at his sides. Leah could see them shaking. She could see the tremor going up his arms. She knew exactly what that tremor was. She'd seen it a thousand times, on all of them. She'd felt it in herself.

Beside Sam, Emily stood up too, and put her hand on his arm.

"Sam," she said.

He didn't look at her. He was looking at Harry.

"Sam," Emily said again, and this time he did.

Emily turned to Harry. The firelight was on the scarred side of her face. Leah had spent a year and a half not looking at that side of her cousin's face. She looked at it now.

"I chose him," Emily said. Her voice was quiet and very clear. "I want you to know that. Afterward. After it happened, after — " she touched her cheek, briefly, with two fingers — "after this. I didn't have to. I could have gone home. I chose him anyway."

Harry looked at her for a long moment.

Then he set the plate down, and got up off the sand, and stood in front of her, and didn't come any closer.

"Then I'm glad," he said. Plainly. "Truly. I hope it's a good life."

Emily searched his face. Whatever she found there, she nodded.

"It is," she said.

"Good."

"I'd never," came another voice, from the dark by the water. Quil had come up from the surf with Claire asleep on his shoulder now, a heavy little bundle in a pink jacket with her thumb in her mouth. His face was very red. "I'd *never.* Not — she's a *baby.* I'm whatever she needs. That's all. That's all it is."

"I believe you," said Harry.

Quil stopped.

"I do," Harry said. "I've watched you with her for an hour. I believe you." He looked at the sleeping girl on Quil's shoulder, and something moved in his face that Leah didn't have a name for. "I just hope somebody asks her someday. What she wants. When she's old enough to say."

Quil opened his mouth. Then he closed it again, and looked down at Claire, and didn't say anything at all.

Over on the plywood, Old Quil cleared his throat.

"The spirits don't ask permission," the old man said. His voice was thin and dry as paper. "Never have. Not of the wolf, not of the girl."

Harry turned to him. And he did something Leah would think about for a long time afterward. He went down on one knee in the sand in front of the old man's chair, so he was looking up at him, and he spoke to him the way you'd speak to somebody at a funeral.

"No, sir," he said. "They never do. I've had magic decide things for me, too. Before I was born. It killed my parents and it nearly killed me and I spent seventeen years doing what it said." He held the old man's eyes. "I meant no disrespect to your people. None. Only to the magic. I'm afraid I've not much love for anything that does people's choosing for them."

Old Quil looked at him for a long, long time with his black eyes glittering in the firelight.

"Huh," said Old Quil, finally.

Billy, in his chair, was rubbing his jaw slowly with one hand. He looked at Sue. Sue, on her log with her coat buttoned to the neck, had her eyes on her daughter.

"Mm," said Sue Clearwater, very quietly, to nobody.

And Leah sat on her log in the dark, with her hands in the pockets of a dead man's jacket, and felt something come loose in her chest that had been knotted there so long she'd stopped knowing it was a knot.

Somebody had said it.

Out loud. At this fire. In front of all of them. The thing she'd been thinking for a year and a half at the inside of her own skull while the whole pack listened and pretended they couldn't hear. She hadn't had to say it. She hadn't had to be the bitter one, the broken one, the one on the log who couldn't let it go. Somebody else had looked at it from the outside, with clear eyes, and said what it looked like, and then said *whatever floats your boat, man,* and gone back to his pie.

She wanted to laugh. She wanted to cry. She did neither. She sat very still and let it go through her.

Across the fire, Sam had sat back down. Emily's hand was still on his arm. He was staring at the flames, and his face looked the way it had looked in the weeks after Emily, when he'd been trying to work out how to carry something too heavy for him. But the fists at his sides had come open.

Harry stood up from in front of Old Quil's chair and brushed the sand off his knee and looked around at the fire. At all the faces, turned toward him in the blue-green light, not one of them sure what to do.

"Right," he said. "Sorry. Bit of a mood killer, that."

Nobody said anything.

Then Seth jumped up off the sand so fast he knocked over a cooler.

"*VOLLEYBALL,*" Seth said.

---

It was too dark to play. Seth knew that. He just didn't care, and after a second, from the way everybody started getting up off the sand, neither did anybody else.

The net was down by the water, a dark line between two driftwood posts with the surf hissing white behind it. The firelight barely reached it. Seth could see fine, all of them could, but the ball would be a gray blur against gray sand, and Kim and Rachel and Emily wouldn't see a thing.

Harry looked at the net. Then he looked over at the plywood boards.

"Mr. Black," he said. "Mr. Ateara. Would you mind if I turned some lights on?"

Billy looked at Old Quil. Old Quil waved one thin hand.

"Go ahead," said Billy.

Harry didn't move. Seth was watching him, and he didn't move at all, not his hands, not his face. But down on the sand by the net, little points of light came up out of the beach like sparks rising off a fire. Amber. The color of a porch light on a rainy night. A dozen, then twenty, drifting up slow and steady until they hung in a long loose line about ten feet over the net and stopped there, bobbing gently in the wind like lanterns on a string that wasn't there.

The whole end of the beach went gold.

Brady said a word his mom would have killed him for. Collin grabbed his arm. Kim made a little *oh* sound under the blanket, and Claire, asleep in Emily's lap now, stirred and opened her eyes and looked up at the lights and said *pretty* and went back to sleep.

"Okay," said Seth. "Okay, that's — okay. Teams."

---

It was Jacob's pack against Sam's. Nobody decided that out loud. It just happened, the way water runs downhill. Jacob and Seth and Leah and Harry on the near side. Paul and Jared and Embry and Quil on the far side, because Sam said he'd ref, and stood off to the side by the post with his arms folded and Emily sitting on a log behind him with Claire.

Jacob looked at his watch and said one game. He'd been saying one more hour for three hours.

Seth had played a lot of beach volleyball with wolves. Everybody on the rez had, since the summer before last, when it turned out that once you phased you could jump four feet straight up and hit a ball hard enough to bury it six inches in the sand. The rules had changed some. Mostly that nobody normal was allowed to play anymore, for safety.

He'd never played with anybody like Harry.

He wasn't the best at it. He didn't know the rules. He kept calling the net "the net thing" and asking whether you were allowed to catch the ball, and the first time Paul spiked at him he just stood there and let it hit him square in the chest and said, "Ah. Right. No catching. Got it." But he was *fast.* Faster than Seth, faster than Jacob. He moved across the sand like somebody had cut the strings holding him to it, and he got to balls nobody could have gotten to.

"Used to play a game on broomsticks," he said, when Jacob stared at him. "Bit like this. More falling."

But the thing Seth kept watching, the whole game, was him and Leah.

They didn't talk. That was the thing. Everybody else on the beach was yelling — *mine, yours, got it, Jake, JAKE* — and the two of them didn't say a single word to each other. Leah would go up for a ball and not even look, and Harry would already be there underneath her. Harry would set it, high and soft, without calling it, and Leah would already be in the air. Once she dove for a ball that Seth would've sworn was gone, a long low skidding dive across the sand, and got a fist under it, and it went straight up, and before she'd even hit the ground Harry was there and put it down on Paul's side of the net so hard it bounced back up to Paul's chin.

Leah came up out of the sand spitting, with sand in her hair and all down the front of the jacket, laughing.

"*Show-off,*" she said.

"*You* were showing off."

"I was *diving.*"

"Very showily."

"*You* hit it at his *face.*"

"I hit it at the *ground,*" Harry said. "His face was in the way. That's a matter for his face."

Paul, on the other side of the net, said something that made Sam say "*Paul*" without even turning his head.

They were like that the whole game. Seth would remember it for years. His sister on a beach under a line of floating lights, sand in her hair, trash-talking Paul Lahote and laughing at him while she did it, high-fiving Jacob, shoving Harry when he got in her way and then catching his arm when he stumbled, without either of them looking. Like they'd been playing together for ten years. Like they'd been doing a lot of things together for ten years.

They were up by two when Paul went up for the big one.

Seth saw it coming. Everybody did. Paul had been getting madder all game, and Jared had set him a perfect one right up against the net, and Paul went up for it like he was trying to put it through the earth. Jacob went up to block. Seth heard the crack of Paul's palm on the ball like a gunshot, and the thump of it off Jacob's forearms, and then it was going *up.*

Up, and back, and over all their heads, spinning, past the end of the beach, past the line of the floating lights, out over the dark water. They all turned and watched it go. It came down somewhere way out past the breakers with a small white splash, and then it was a pale dot bobbing on the black water, fifty yards out at least, going up and down on the swell.

"Nice," said Jacob to Paul.

"*You* hit it."

"*You* hit it at me like a — "

"I got it," Seth said.

He was already kicking off his shoes. He pulled his shirt off over his head and threw it on the sand. The water was going to be freezing, and dark, and the rip off First Beach could take you halfway to James Island if you weren't careful, but he was a wolf, and it was fifty yards, and he'd done stupider things for less. He ran down toward the surf.

Somebody walked past him.

Harry didn't run. He didn't even hurry. He walked past Seth at the edge of the water with his hands in the pockets of his jeans, like a man walking down to the end of his driveway for the newspaper, and he didn't stop when the sand ran out.

He just kept walking.

Seth stopped dead in the shallows with the cold water rushing in over his feet.

Harry walked out onto the ocean.

His boots came down on the water and the water held them. It moved under him, it went up and down, the foam slid around his ankles. It held anyway. He walked out across the shallows where the waves were breaking white, and a wave came in, a big one, curling over, and he stepped up onto it as it rose and stepped down off the back of it as it went past under him, the way you'd step over a log on a trail. He walked out through the next one and the next one. Past the breakers, onto the long black swell beyond, under the green-gold edge of the light from the floating lamps. Fifty yards out. He bent down, without any hurry at all, and picked the ball up off the water.

He turned around and walked back.

He tossed the ball up and caught it once on the way. A wave came in behind him, and he stepped over it. He came in through the shallows, past Seth, who was standing there up to his ankles in freezing water with his shirt off and his mouth open, and stepped up onto the dry sand.

His boots weren't wet. Seth looked. They weren't even damp.

He walked back up the beach to the net, and held out the ball to Jared, because it was Jared's serve.

Jared didn't take it.

Nobody on the whole beach was moving. Seth turned around, slowly, in the shallows. Everybody was staring. Paul and Jared and Embry and Quil on the far side of the net. Jacob. Leah. Up by the fire, Kim and Rachel on their feet. Sue with both hands over her mouth. Billy in his chair, leaning so far forward Seth thought he might tip out. Old Quil, with his eyes very wide, glittering. Collin and Brady clutching each other. Emily, with Claire in her lap. Sam, by the post, with his arms dropped to his sides.

Harry looked around at all of them. He looked down at the ball in his hand.

"What?" he said. "Why are you all looking at me like that?"

Nobody answered.

He looked at the ball again. He looked at the ocean. Seth watched it land on his face, slowly, like a man realizing halfway through a dinner party that he's had spinach in his teeth all night.

"Oh," said Harry. "Right. That." He scratched the back of his head. "Yeah. Sorry. Should've mentioned."

He looked around at all of them one more time. Then he put on a very serious face.

"By the way," Harry said. "Jesus was a wizard."

And for one second, he wasn't Harry.

Seth saw it. Everybody saw it. Standing there on the sand under the floating lights where Harry had been, there was a man in a long white robe that went down to his sandals, with brown hair falling past his shoulders and a short brown beard, and his arms held out a little from his sides, palms open, and a soft gold light coming off him all around, especially around his head, so bright it made a ring.

He looked exactly like the picture in Grandma's hallway. The one over the phone table that Seth had stared at every Sunday until he was ten.

It lasted about a second. Then it was gone, and it was just Harry again, in his flannel shirt, with sand on his knees.

Nobody breathed.

Harry turned to Leah. He looked into her eyes, very solemnly. He held out his hand, palm up, and there was something in it. Seth hadn't seen where it came from. It was a slice of white bread, square and soft, out of the bag on the card table by the fire forty yards up the beach.

"And for you," Harry said gravely, "only the best of my body."

Leah looked at the bread.

She looked at him.

She took it, and bit it, and chewed, with her face dead straight.

"It's stale," she said.

And then her face broke in half.

She doubled over. She went down on her knees in the sand, holding the bread, laughing so hard no sound came out at all, just a wheeze, her whole body shaking with it. Harry stood over her looking deeply offended, which made it worse.

Then the whole beach went.

Jacob went first. He sat down hard in the sand like somebody had cut his legs out from under him and fell over backwards and lay there howling at the floating lights. Embry grabbed the net to stay upright and pulled half of it down. Quil was bent double with his hands on his knees. Paul had both his hands over his face and was shouting something through them that sounded like *I hate him, I hate him so much, I hate him so MUCH —* and Rachel was hanging off his arm, crying. Up by the fire, Old Quil was making the wheezing creaking sound again, so hard that Billy had to reach over and pound him on the back, and Billy himself was laughing so hard his chair was rocking.

"That's going in the stories," Billy said, when he could. "That's going in the *stories,* Quil."

"It's *blasphemy,*" Old Quil wheezed, delighted.

Sue had her face in her hands. Her shoulders were shaking. Seth couldn't tell if she was laughing or crying, and then she took her hands away and she was doing both.

Kim was laughing. Collin and Brady were rolling in the sand. Emily had her hand over her mouth and her eyes were streaming and Claire had woken up again and was laughing too, because everybody else was, clapping her little hands.

Seth stood up to his ankles in the Pacific Ocean in the dark with his shirt off and laughed until his stomach hurt.

And when he could see again, he looked over at the post by the net. At Sam.

Sam Uley was standing with his arms at his sides, looking at Harry. His face was doing something Seth had never seen it do. It was trying very hard to stay still.

It lost.

The corner of Sam's mouth went. Then the rest of it. He put his head down and one big hand over his eyes, and his shoulders shook once, twice. And Seth heard it, low, under all the rest of the noise on that beach, surprised out of him like a cough. Sam laughing.

Seth hadn't heard Sam laugh since before Dad died.

"Can you do the loaves and fishes?" Embry gasped, from the sand under the net.

"Fishes are extra," said Harry.

That set them all off again.

---

Jacob left at ten, four hours after he'd said he would, still wiping his eyes.

"Give my regards to the mind-reader," Harry told him.

"He's gonna see all of that," Jacob said. "In my head. The — " he waved at the ocean, at the bread, at all of it — "the whole thing."

"Good," said Harry. "Tell him fishes are extra."

Jacob went up the beach laughing, and changed in the trees at the top, and was gone.

Old Quil got his ride at half past. Harry carried the old man up the path to the lot in his arms like a child, because the sand was too soft for the camp chair and Old Quil refused point-blank to be pushed in Billy's, and put him in the front seat of the Impala with a blanket over his knees, and Old Quil said, "Loud," and Harry said, "Yes, sir," and put on something with a lot of drums. Leah heard the engine come awake up at the top of the beach and roar off down the La Push road with the old man's high cracked cackle trailing out the open window behind it.

The fire had burned down to a low red heap with the blue and green flickering through it. People were folding blankets. Kim was asleep against Jared's shoulder. Emily had taken Claire home, and Collin and Brady had been sent home, protesting. The floating lights still hung over the half-collapsed volleyball net, bobbing in the wind, because nobody knew how to turn them off and nobody wanted to.

Leah was down by the water, shaking the sand out of the jacket, when she smelled Sam come up behind her.

She didn't turn around.

"Leah," he said.

She shook out the jacket one more time and put it back on and turned.

He was standing a few feet back on the wet sand, at the edge of where the floating lights reached, with his hands in the pockets of his jeans. He looked tired, the way her mother had looked in the hospital hallway.

"Walk with me?" he said. "For a minute."

She looked up the beach at the fire. Seth was there, helping Paul stamp out the edges. Her mother was on her log, with her coat buttoned, watching. Their eyes met across forty yards of dark sand, and Sue didn't nod or shake her head. She just looked.

Leah turned back to Sam.

"One minute," she said.

They walked south along the water. Not far. The surf came up and slid back. Neither of them said anything for a while, and that was strange, because she'd spent a year and a half in his head and never once had to wonder what he was thinking. Now she had no idea at all. It was like walking next to a stranger who happened to have his face.

"I'm sorry," Sam said.

She stopped walking.

He stopped too. He didn't look at her. He was looking out at the water.

"I've never said it," he said. "I've *thought* it. Every day. Every time I phased, you had to hear me thinking it. I know you did. And I let that — I let that count. Like you'd heard it, so I didn't have to say it." His jaw worked. "I never said it out loud. Not once. Not in your kitchen. Not to your face."

"No," Leah said. "You didn't."

"So I'm saying it. I'm sorry, Lee." His voice cracked on her name. Nobody but family had called her that in a year and a half. "For all of it. For what it did to you. For what I did. For not — for not being able to stop it."

The surf came up over their boots and went back out.

Leah stood there and waited for it to feel the way she'd always thought it would. She'd imagined this. A thousand times, at the beginning, lying awake in her bed at the edge of the rez with the window open. Sam on her porch, Sam in the rain, Sam saying *I'm sorry* and meaning it, and something in her finally healing over, or finally breaking for good. One or the other.

Neither happened.

She just heard it. A man she used to love, standing on a beach in the dark, saying a true thing a long time too late.

"Okay," she said.

He turned his head and looked at her.

"Okay?"

"I heard you. Okay." She pushed her hair out of her face. "Sam. I'm not in your head anymore. You get that, right? I'm in Jake's. You don't have to keep — you don't have to phase every night and feel sorry for me in there, where I can hear it. There's nobody listening." She breathed out. "You can stop."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"I don't know if I know how," he said finally.

"Then figure it out." She almost smiled. "You've got Emily. She'll help. She's good at that."

He looked at her like she'd hit him. Then something in his face gave way, a little, the way a log gives in a fire when it's burned through.

"He said slave bond," Sam said quietly.

"Yeah."

"Do you think he's right?"

Leah thought about it. Really thought, the way she'd learned to up there on a rock at the end of the world, with nobody else's voice in her skull to tell her what to think.

"I think it doesn't matter what I think," she said. "It's yours. Yours and Emily's. She stood up in front of everybody tonight and said she chose you." She looked at him. "Believe her."

Sam looked out at the black water for a long time.

"Is he good to you?" he said.

"He's an arrogant prick."

"Leah."

"He's an arrogant prick," Leah said, "who walked on the ocean to get a volleyball and then pretended to be Jesus." She looked down at the sand. "And yeah. He's good to me."

Sam nodded slowly.

"You looked happy," he said. "Coming down the beach. I never — "

"Don't."

He stopped.

"Don't," she said again, more gently. "You don't get that one. That one's mine."

Sam looked at her. Then he nodded again, once, and didn't finish.

Up at the top of the beach, headlights swung into the lot. The engine rumbled once and died. A door opened, and there was a high cracked voice demanding to go around *again,* and an English voice saying *tomorrow, sir, I promise, I'll come and fetch you,* and Billy's voice from the fire saying *Quil, it's eleven o'clock,* and Old Quil saying something back in Quileute that made Billy laugh.

Sam heard it too. He looked up at the lot and back at her.

"Go on," he said.

"Yeah."

She turned to go. Then she stopped and looked back at him. He was standing there at the edge of the water with his hands in his pockets, big and dark and alone against the white of the surf, like a sea stack.

"Sam," she said.

"Yeah?"

"Tell Emily the muffins were good."

She walked back up the beach before he could answer.

---

They left the car in the lot and walked home.

South along the top of First Beach in the dark, with the surf on their right and the black wall of the headland ahead. The floating lights had followed them a little way off the end of the beach and then stopped, like dogs at the edge of a yard, and hung there bobbing over the sand behind them. Leah looked back at them once. They were the last thing she could see of the fire.

Harry walked beside her with his hands in his pockets, not saying anything. His boots didn't sink in the sand. Hers did.

"Old Quil said a word in the car," he said, after a while. "Quite a long one. In your language. When I took the bend by the river at eighty. What does — " He tried to pronounce it.

Leah laughed.

"You don't want to know."

"I'm almost certain I do."

"You really don't."

"Was it about my driving?"

"It was about your mother."

"Ah," said Harry, and seemed pleased.

They walked a while longer.

"I didn't punch him," Harry said.

"I noticed."

"I'd like it noted."

"It's noted."

"He went down to the water with you. I watched. I sat in the car with a ninety-year-old man telling me to go faster and I watched the whole thing in the rearview mirror, and I didn't punch him. I'd like that noted *separately.*"

"It's noted separately," said Leah.

"Thank you."

"He said sorry."

Harry glanced at her.

"Out loud," Leah said. "First time." She looked down at her boots. "It didn't feel like anything. I thought it would. I used to think it'd fix everything or break everything." She shrugged. "It was just words. True ones. Way too late."

"That's usually what sorry is," Harry said.

"Yeah."

"You all right?"

She checked. She didn't even have to think about it anymore; it was a reflex now, the reach around inside her own head, the quick look into all the corners.

Her head. Her beach. Her mother up by the fire. Her brother. The sound of the surf she'd been born hearing.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm all right."

They came to the end of the sand, where the beach ran out against the rocks of the headland and the trail went up into the trees. Leah stopped. She turned around and looked back the way they'd come. The fire was a red spark at the far end of the beach. The floating lights hung over the sand like a string of lanterns somebody had forgotten to take down after a party.

"You said it," she said. "At the fire. Slave bond."

"I did. I'm sorry. I didn't mean to wreck the evening — "

"Don't be sorry."

He stopped.

"I've thought it," Leah said. "For a year and a half. Every day. And I could never say it, because if I said it, I was just the bitter one. The one on the log who couldn't get over Sam." She looked at the fire. "You said it, and you ate your pie."

He was quiet.

"Thank you," Leah said.

"Don't make it a thing," said Harry, after a second.

She turned around and grabbed the front of his flannel shirt with both hands and pulled him down and kissed him.

There at the end of the beach, in the dark, with the surf coming in and going out behind them and sand in her hair. He made a surprised sound against her mouth, and then he didn't, and his hands came up and held her face, and she held on to his shirt like she was afraid the tide would take him.

When she let go, she didn't let go very far.

"That," she said, "was a thing."

"I noticed," said Harry, a bit unsteadily.

"You want your jacket back?"

He looked down at her in it. The sleeves shoved up. The sand still in the seams. The old stitched-up tear on the arm.

"No," he said. "Keep it. It looks better on you." He paused. "He'd have liked you. Sirius. He'd have liked you a great deal. You'd have frightened him to death."

Leah didn't know what to say to that. So she didn't say anything. She put her hand in the jacket pocket instead, and found something in there, soft and square, and pulled it out.

The slice of bread. With one bite out of it.

She looked at it. She looked at him.

He looked at it too, very seriously.

"Communion," said Harry.

Leah put her forehead against his chest and laughed until she couldn't stand up, there at the end of First Beach in the dark, and he held her up, and the lights bobbed over the sand behind them, and the surf came in.

Then they went up the trail through the salal to her father's cave. She went first, because she knew the way. She'd always known the way.

She kept the bread. She never could have told anybody why.
