# North of Everything

## Chapter Three: Homecoming

---

He woke up the way he always did, all at once.

One second he was asleep on his back on the library sofa with *Beast-Blooded Babies* rising and falling on his chest and one ear flopped over, and the next his eyes were open and both ears were straight up and his nose was working. He didn't move anything else. Leah had watched him do it a hundred times now. He woke up like somebody had flipped a switch, and the first thing he did, every time, before he even turned his head, was smell the room.

His nostrils flared. His eyes went to her hand on his chest. Then to the paper in her other hand.

"You've been crying," he said.

"I haven't."

"You smell like salt." He sat up, carefully, so she wouldn't slide off the edge of the sofa, and the book fell off his chest onto the rug. He didn't look at it. "What's wrong? Is it her? Is it—"

"She's fine. She's kicking my liver." Leah held out the advisory. "It came this morning. Read it."

He took it. He read it. She watched his eyes go down the page, and watched his ears go back, slowly, one notch at a time, until by the time he got to *Cause unknown* they were lying flat against his skull. Then he flipped it over and looked at the newspaper clipping on the back, the grainy photo of the logging road in the rain, *HORSE-SIZED WOLVES NEAR LAKE CRESCENT*. He looked at it for a long time.

"La Push," he said.

"Yeah."

"That's home."

She didn't answer. She hadn't ever said the name out loud to him. Not once in thirteen months. But he'd seen her face when she read it, and he'd heard her say *Quillayute* that first night at the Bent Antler, and he wasn't stupid.

"Ja," he said quietly. "I figured it was somewhere round there. You smell like that coast. Cedar and cold salt water." He looked down at the advisory again. "High teens. How many were there when you left?"

"Ten. With me."

"So eight or nine more."

"Kids," Leah said. Her voice came out rough. "They'd be kids, Harry. Thirteen, fourteen. Somebody's little brother, running a fever of a hundred and eight in the kitchen and then blowing out the back wall. It only happens when they're around. The leeches. The more of them come through, the more of us phase. That's how it works. That's the whole point of us." She pressed her lips together. "Eight more kids phased in a year. Something's been there. A lot of something."

"And three dead in Port Angeles."

"Yeah."

Harry was quiet. His tail had gone still against the sofa cushions.

"My mom's there," Leah said. "And Seth. He's sixteen now. He was running around with the Cullens when I left because he thought they were his *friends*." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And she doesn't know, Harry. Mom doesn't know anything. She doesn't know I'm married, she doesn't know about you, she doesn't know there's a—" She put her hand on her stomach. Under her palm, the pup kicked, hard, like she was trying to make a point. "In a month she's gonna have a granddaughter and she doesn't even know I'm alive, except for one letter with no return address. And I keep thinking, what if something happens? What if one of those things comes through her kitchen and she never—"

She stopped. She was crying again. She hadn't meant to be.

Harry put the advisory down on the rug and pulled her into his lap, the way he always did, and she let him, the way she always did. She put her face in the fur at his throat. He smelled like the library fire and old paper and pine.

"I want to go," she said into his neck. "I want to go look. Just look. I want to see if she's okay."

He didn't say anything for a while. One of his forepaws moved slowly up and down her back.

"Okay," he said.

She lifted her head.

"Okay," he said again. "We'll go."

"Really?"

"Ja. Really. We'll go. We'll go see your mum, and your brother, and we'll find out what's been eating people in Port Angeles, and if it's still around, I'll have a word with it." His ears came up a little. "After."

"After what?"

"After she's born."

Leah opened her mouth.

"Not before," Harry said. He said it very calmly, the same way he'd said *okay*. "After. She comes first. You have her here, in this house, with Pye, behind four sets of wards—"

"Three and a small one."

"—behind four sets of wards, and she's born, and she's healthy, and you're healthy. And then we go. I'll take you myself. I'll take you anywhere you want." He looked down at her stomach, at the curve of it under his flannel. "But I'm not taking you within five thousand miles of a place with leeches in it and dead people in the papers while you're carrying her. I'm not doing it."

"Harry—"

"The only way we go before," Harry said, "is if I burn the whole peninsula down first."

Leah stared at him.

"The whole thing," he said. "Every tree. Every leech. Every rock on every beach. If we go before she's born, I'm going first, by myself, and I'm burning the entire Olympic Peninsula to the ground. Because as far as I'm concerned, right now, all of it's a threat."

"You'd burn down the Olympic Peninsula."

"Ja."

"There's a *national park* on it."

"I'd feel bad about the park," Harry admitted. "I'd send them a card."

"There are *people* on it. My *mom* is on it."

"I'd go round your mum. I'm very precise." His ears were all the way up now, but his eyes were steady and green and he wasn't smiling. "Leah. I'm only half joking. You know that, right? Somebody's been killing people on that coast and something's been bad enough to make eight kids turn into wolves in a year, and you're a month off having a pup who's half of everything I've got. I'm not walking you into that. After she's born, I'll walk in there with you and Layla and we'll knock on your mum's door in broad daylight, and anybody who looks at either of you wrong is going to have a very short, very bad day. But not before. Before, it's me alone with a lot of fire, or nobody."

Leah sat in his lap and looked at him.

Then she went back over what he'd said.

"Who's Layla?" she said.

His ears went sideways.

"Harry."

"Nobody."

"*Harry.* Who's Layla?"

"It's just a name," Harry said. "I was trying it out. In my head. I've been trying a lot of them out. I didn't mean to say it out loud." He rubbed the back of his neck with one forepaw. "You don't have to like it. We haven't even talked about names. I've got a list—"

"Of course you've got a list."

"—it's a long list, it's on the back of the other list—"

"Say it again," Leah said.

He stopped. "Layla?"

She listened to it. She listened to the shape of it. *Lay-la.* It had a little of her own name in it, the *L* and the long *a*, like an echo. And it had something else in it too, softer, that she'd heard him say exactly twice in thirteen months, both times late at night, both times when he thought she was asleep. *Lily.* His mom's name.

She thought about Darlene's note in the pocket of the flannel she'd been wearing ever since. *NAME HER SOMETHING WITH A BACKBONE.*

Under her hand, the pup kicked. Once. Hard.

"Huh," said Leah.

Harry's ears went straight up. "Did she just—"

"She kicked."

"When you said it?"

"When *you* said it." Leah looked down at her stomach. "Layla," she said to it, experimentally.

Nothing.

"Layla," said Harry, low.

*Kick.*

Leah looked up at him. His eyes had gone very wide and very wet. His tail had started moving again, slowly, against the cushions.

"Well," she said. "I guess that's decided."

"You like it?"

"I like it. She likes it. She likes *you* saying it, apparently, which, great, she's already a daddy's girl, I'm doomed." Leah wiped her face on her sleeve. "Okay. Layla. And after she's born, we go."

"After she's born, we go," Harry agreed.

"And you don't burn down the Olympic Peninsula."

"I don't burn down the Olympic Peninsula," said Harry. He paused. "Unless."

"Unless *what*?"

"Unless it's a threat," said Harry, and pulled her back in against his chest, and his tail went *thump, thump, thump* against the sofa, and the pup kicked in time with it, and Leah closed her eyes and laughed into his fur until the crying was all gone.


---

Layla Sue Potter-Black came into the world on the twenty-fourth of November, under a full moon, in the middle of a sentence.

The sentence was Leah's, and it was mostly swearing.

It had started at breakfast. Leah had been standing at the kitchen counter eating cold roast beef off the bone with her fingers, because sitting down had stopped being comfortable sometime in early November, and Harry had been at the stove doing something to a pan of eggs, and Kreacher had just finished waxing the flagstones, which he did every Saturday whether they needed it or not. And Leah had felt something go *pop*, low down, like a knuckle cracking.

She'd looked down at the floor. Kreacher had looked down at the floor.

"Oh," Kreacher had said, in a voice of the deepest, most profound joy and horror Leah had ever heard come out of anything. "Oh. Kreacher's *floor*."

Harry had dropped the pan.

---

By four in the afternoon she'd broken two of his claws.

She hadn't meant to. She hadn't meant to do a lot of things that day, including bite through a pillow and call Healer Margery Pye a word that Leah's mother would have washed her mouth out for. But she was a wolf, even when she wasn't, and she was strong, and every forty seconds or so something took hold of her from the inside and squeezed like a fist the size of the house, and there was nothing to hold onto but Harry. So she held onto Harry. She held onto his forepaw with both hands, and at a quarter past two she'd heard something crack in it, and looked up at him, horrified, and he'd said, "It's fine. Keep going," and hadn't let go.

He'd been beside the bed all day. He'd sent his Patronus for Healer Pye at breakfast—a silver stag that came out of the end of his wand and went bounding out the kitchen window and across the moor, and Leah had stared after it with her mouth open, and Harry had said, "My dad's," and not explained, and gone back to helping her up the stairs. Pye had come through the kitchen fire ten minutes later with a carpetbag in one hand and her knitting in the other, looked at Leah, looked at the puddle on the flagstones and Kreacher on his knees beside it with a rag, sighing, and said, "Right. Upstairs, then. Kettle on, Kreacher. Lord Potter, you'll be wanting to stop shaking."

He was still shaking. He'd just stopped showing it. He'd been sitting on the floor beside the big carved bed for eight hours now, in his tall shape, with his ears back and his tail curled around his footpaws and his forepaw in Leah's grip, and every time a contraction came he breathed with her. In for four. Out for four. He'd read about it in *What to Expect*. He'd read about it in three other books, too. He'd practiced it with her every night for a month while she rolled her eyes.

She'd stopped rolling her eyes around noon.

The window was open. She'd made him open it. It was November on Dartmoor and the air coming in off the moor was cold and wet and smelled of peat and rain, and she was burning up anyway, a hundred and eleven at least, the gray-green shawl around her shoulders working so hard she could feel it hum. The necklace on her chest was pulsing fast. *Big-quick, small-quick, big-quick, small-quick.* Every so often Harry glanced at the watch lying open on the bedside table, where two tiny lights pulsed in time, and his ears went back another notch.

"How long," Leah panted, as the latest one let go of her. "How long is this—how long—"

"Not long now, love," said Healer Pye, from the foot of the bed, where she'd been knitting something small and gray for most of the afternoon and doing things with her wand at regular intervals that Leah didn't want to think about. "Wolf-blooded babies come quick once they decide to come. I'd say she'll be here by moonrise."

"When's—moonrise—"

"Half past four," said Harry instantly. "Four thirty-eight. I looked it up."

"Of course you did," said Leah, and then the next one hit, and she screamed something at him that started with *your stupid freaky magic* and ended somewhere around *and your stupid giant*—

"Lord Potter," said Healer Pye mildly, over the top of it, "remind me to bring the Niffler next time."

"*There is no Niffler!*" Leah howled.

"There's always a Niffler somewhere, love. Push."

---

She pushed.

The sky outside the window had gone dark blue, and then darker, and then, as she pushed again, and again, and again, the light changed. It came in through the open window low and orange, like the light off a fire. Somewhere at the edge of her vision she saw it lay itself across the end of the bed, across Pye's knitting, across Harry's shoulder and the fur on his arm.

"There's the moon," said Healer Pye softly. "There she is. Come on, then. One more, Leah. One big one."

Leah looked at Harry.

He was looking at her. His ears were all the way back. His eyes were very green and very wide, and there was something in his face that she'd never seen before, something raw and scared and wide open all at once. His broken forepaw was in her hands. He didn't say anything.

She pushed.

And then there was a sound—a small, wet, slithering sound—and something let go inside her all at once, like a rope snapping, and the whole room went quiet.

Too quiet.

Leah lifted her head off the pillow.

Healer Pye had something in her hands. Something small and dark and wet, curled up tight. It wasn't moving. Pye had her wand out and was doing something with it, fast, murmuring, a little whisper of air going over the small dark thing, and her face had gone sharp and serious in a way Leah hadn't seen it go once in five months.

"Come on, little one," Pye murmured. "Come on now. Breathe for me—"

It didn't breathe.

Leah couldn't breathe either. She couldn't move. She lay there on the soaked sheets with her heart stopped in her chest, staring at the tiny curled-up thing in Pye's hands, and somewhere very far away she heard herself make a sound.

Harry moved.

She didn't see him get up. He was just there, all at once, on his knees at the foot of the bed beside Pye, and he reached out with both forepaws and Healer Pye looked at him for half a second—looked at his face—and let him take the baby.

He brought her up close against his chest. He bent his head down over her.

And he licked her.

Long, firm strokes of his tongue, from the tip of her tiny muzzle up over her closed eyes and between her folded ears. Again. And again, harder, rough, against the grain of the wet fur on her face, and then down over her throat, her chest, her ribs, rubbing, rubbing. His ears were flat. His eyes were shut. He wasn't thinking about it. Leah could see that he wasn't thinking about it at all. Something older than thinking had taken over his whole body, and it was doing what it knew how to do.

The tiny thing in his paws sneezed.

It was the smallest sound Leah had ever heard. A wet little *tchff*.

Then it coughed. Then its whole body jerked, and its tiny mouth opened, and it *screamed*.

It was a thin, high, furious sound, half a baby's wail and half a pup's yelp, and it went up and up like a kettle coming to the boil, and it was the most beautiful thing Leah had ever heard in her life.

She burst into tears.

Harry didn't stop. He kept going. Long slow strokes now instead of fast ones, gentle, patient, over and over, from the top of the tiny round head down the length of the little curved back, along each folded ear, down each limb to each tiny paw. Down the tail, which was about as long as Leah's finger and was lashing back and forth with fury. Everywhere his tongue went, the wet slicked-down fur dried and lifted, and the color came up out of it. Dark charcoal, nearly black. And at the tip of every single hair, silver-gray. So when the moonlight from the window touched her, the whole tiny body shimmered.

And down the middle of her furious little screaming face, from between her ears to the end of her nose, a blaze of pale gray.

Leah's gray.

Healer Pye sat back on her heels at the foot of the bed and let out a long breath, and put her wand away in her bun.

"Well," she said, a little unsteadily. "Wolves were doing that a long time before there were Healers, I suppose."

Harry lifted his head. He looked at Pye. Then down at the tiny screaming furious thing in his paws, now dry and fluffed up and shimmering silver and charcoal in the moonlight. He looked at her for a long, long time, as if he'd never seen anything before in his life.

"Did you just lick our daughter?" said Leah, through her tears.

Harry looked up at her. His tongue was still sticking out a little bit at the front of his muzzle. He pulled it back in.

"Ja," he said. His voice was barely a voice at all. "Ja. I think I did."

"Is that a *thing*?"

"Apparently," said Harry, and his voice broke right in half.

---

He brought her up the bed and laid her on Leah's chest.

She was tiny. She was so tiny. Five pounds, eleven ounces, Pye said later, though Leah didn't hear it at the time. She was soft and damp and she smelled like the inside of Leah and like Harry's breath and like something else, something new, something that was only her. Her eyes were shut tight, sealed shut, the way a puppy's are. Her ears were folded down soft against her round head. She had a short blunt muzzle with a nose like a black jellybean, and a mouth that was wide open and still screaming, and two tiny forepaws with five tiny curled digits each, with claws on the ends of them no bigger than grains of rice.

Leah put her hand on her back. Under her palm, through the fur, Layla's heart was going like a hummingbird.

The screaming stopped.

Layla turned her face toward Leah's skin, blind, and rooted, snuffling, the tiny black nose working, and found what she was looking for, and latched on, and went quiet. Just like that. Her tiny tail stopped lashing and lay still against Leah's arm. One small paw opened and closed against Leah's chest.

The necklace pulsed. *Big-slow, small-quick.* On the bedside table, the watch pulsed with it.

"Hi," Leah whispered. "Hi, baby. Hi. I'm your mom."

Harry was kneeling beside the bed with his chin on the edge of the mattress, about six inches from Layla's head. He wasn't saying anything. His eyes were huge and wet and fixed on her. His broken forepaw lay on the sheet beside Leah's arm, the two cracked claws already starting to knit.

"Harry," Leah said.

He didn't seem to hear her.

"*Harry.*"

He looked up.

"Say hi."

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He closed it again. He leaned in, very slowly, until his nose was just barely touching the top of Layla's head, between her folded ears, and he breathed in. A long, slow breath. Smelling her.

Layla's ear twitched.

"Hi," said Harry, so quietly Leah almost didn't hear it. "Hi, Layla. I'm Dad."

Layla let go of Leah long enough to make a small, cross sound, and then latched back on.

Harry made a noise that Leah would remember for the rest of her life. Half a laugh and half a sob, and it came out of him like something breaking open.

"Layla," Leah said. "Layla Sue."

Harry looked up at her.

"Sue," Leah said. Her voice shook. "After my mom. Layla Sue."

"Layla Sue Potter-Black," said Harry.

"That's so much name."

"It's a lot of name," Harry agreed. "She'll grow into it."

From the doorway, where he'd been standing for the last ten minutes with a pile of clean towels in his arms and tears streaming down his face into the towels, Kreacher made a sound like a kettle being stepped on.

"The heir," he whispered. "The *heir*. Layla Sue Potter-Black. The House goes on." He looked at the towels in his arms. Then he flung them in the air, and Disapparated with a crack, and from somewhere far below them in the house came a long thin wail of joy and the sound of something very heavy and very old being dropped down a flight of stairs.

Healer Pye got up off her knees with a small groan. She looked down at the three of them for a long moment over the tops of her glasses. Then she picked her knitting back up off the end of the bed. It was finished. It was a tiny gray hat, with two holes in the top.

"For the ears," said Healer Pye, and set it on the bedside table beside the watch. "I'll go and see what that elf has broken. I'll be back in a quarter of an hour to check on the both of you. Don't do anything I wouldn't do." She paused at the door. "Congratulations, love. Lord Potter." Her voice had gone gruff. "That was well done. The both of you."

The door closed behind her.

It was very quiet. The moon was all the way up now, big and orange and low over Hound Tor, and its light came in through the open window and lay across the bed, across Leah and the small shimmering bundle on her chest, across Harry's head resting on the mattress beside them.

Harry's tail had started moving.

Leah heard it before she saw it. Slow at first, against the side of the bed frame. *Thump. Thump.* Then faster. Then faster still. *Thump-thump-thump-thump.*

"Harry," she said.

"Mm."

"Your tail."

"Can't help it."

"You're gonna—"

There was a loud wooden *crack* from the foot of the bed. One of the four carved oak bedposts, four hundred years old and as thick as Leah's leg, leaned slowly sideways and fell over onto the rug with a *thud*.

They both looked at it.

On Leah's chest, Layla let go, and opened her tiny mouth, and yawned. A huge yawn, much too big for her face, showing a tiny pink tongue and no teeth at all. Then she sneezed. Then she went to sleep.

"Huh," said Harry, very softly.

"You broke the bed," whispered Leah.

"I'll fix it."

"You broke a four-hundred-year-old bed."

"I'll fix it *tomorrow*," said Harry, and put his head back down on the mattress beside his daughter, and closed his eyes, and his tail kept going against the floor, slow and steady, all night long.


---

Kreacher had made a nursery.

He had made it with his own hands, over five months, in the second-best bedroom in the east wing, two doors down from Master's. He had scrubbed the floorboards with sand and lemon until they shone like honey. He had beaten the dust out of the old Potter tapestries in the yard and hung them back up, and then, after a great deal of thought, he had stitched a small black Black family crest into the bottom corner of each one, very neat, where Master might not notice. He had polished the Potter cradle for three days with beeswax until the carved oak glowed like a chestnut, and he had found the old enchantment in it that made it rock by itself and hum, and he had coaxed it back to life, and now when you touched it, it rocked, very gently, and hummed a tune older than Kreacher. He had hung a mobile over it made of tiny silver wolves that ran in circles when you breathed on them. He had laid out forty-two nappies on a shelf, each with a hole cut in the back for the tail, each folded into a perfect square.

It was the finest nursery in England. Kreacher was sure of it. He had been in a great many nurseries, in two hundred years of service to the Most Ancient and Noble House of Black, and none of them had been as fine as this one.

The heir had slept in it for four seconds.

---

On the first night, Master had carried her in. Kreacher had been there, hovering in the doorway, with a fresh nappy over his arm in case. Master had come in very slowly, with the heir held against his chest in both forepaws, and had looked around the nursery at the honey-colored floor and the tapestries and the little silver wolves, and his ears had gone up, and he had said, "Kreacher, this is beautiful."

Kreacher had nearly died of it.

Then Master had leaned over the cradle and laid the heir down in it, on the tiny mattress Kreacher had stuffed with lambswool, under the tiny blanket Kreacher had knitted, and the cradle had rocked, very gently, and hummed.

Master had stood there and looked down at her.

One second. Two. Three. Four.

Then he'd picked her back up.

"Too far," Master had said.

"Master?"

"It's too far, Kreacher." Master's ears had been flat. He'd been holding the heir up under his chin, against the fur of his throat, and she had made a small sleepy grumbling noise and pushed her face deeper into it. "It's two rooms away. I can't hear her breathing from two rooms away."

"Master has the ears of a *wolf*," Kreacher had said, before he could stop himself. "Master could hear her breathing from the *moor*."

"Ja," Master had said, and carried her out.

The next night, Kreacher had moved the cradle into Master's bedroom. All by himself, without being asked, because a good elf anticipated. He'd set it at the foot of the big carved bed, where the broken bedpost had been mended so well you couldn't see the join. The heir had slept in it for eleven minutes. Kreacher knew it was eleven minutes because he had been standing outside the door, counting. For all of those eleven minutes Master had lain in the bed, rigid, with his eyes wide open and his ears pointed at the cradle like a pair of arrows, and then he had got up and lifted her out and lain back down with her on his chest.

He hadn't put her down since.

Not at night, anyway. At night the heir slept on Master. Right in the middle of his chest, curled up small in the thick black fur over his breastbone, rising and falling with his breathing. Master slept on his back now, every night, flat and still as a dead thing, his arms spread out on either side so he couldn't roll over on her, and Mistress slept curled up against his side with one hand on the heir's back, and every time the heir so much as twitched in her sleep, Master's ears came up off the pillow. Kreacher had seen it. Kreacher had seen all of it, from the doorway, every night, because somebody had to make sure Master didn't roll over, and Master clearly thought it was going to be Master.

"Kreacher made a *nursery*," Kreacher had said, on the fourth day, in the kitchen, when he could bear it no longer.

Master had been sitting at the long table with the heir asleep on his shoulder and a mug of tea going cold in front of him. He'd looked at Kreacher with his ears tipped sideways.

"It's a lovely nursery, Kreacher," he'd said. "She'll use it when she's older."

"*When*, Master?"

Master had thought about it.

"When she's eighteen," Master had said.

Mistress, at the stove, had laughed so hard she'd had to hold onto the counter.

---

And then there was the licking.

Kreacher had bathed four generations of the House of Black. He had bathed Master Regulus in a silver basin in the nursery at Grimmauld Place, when Master Regulus was smaller than a house-elf, and he had dried him with heated towels and powdered him with violet powder and dressed him in a nightgown with the crest on the collar. He knew how an heir was bathed.

He had brought the silver basin all the way from Grimmauld Place. He had filled it every morning with water heated exactly right, and laid out towels on the radiator, and the violet powder.

Master licked her.

Every morning. Every morning, at first light, before Master had even had his tea, he would sit up in the big bed with the heir in his forepaws and wash her with his tongue. Slowly. All over. Her face first, the tiny blunt muzzle and the closed eyes and the pale gray stripe down the middle. Then her ears, one at a time, inside and out, very carefully. Then down her back, and her belly, and each of her tiny paws, and every one of her tiny claws, and her tail. And the heir *liked* it. She lay in his paws with her eyes shut and her little mouth open and made a noise Kreacher had never heard a baby make—a soft rumbling chirrup, deep in her chest, over and over, like a kettle thinking about boiling.

"She's purring," Mistress had said, the first morning, sitting up in bed in Master's flannel with her hair everywhere. "Harry. She's *purring*."

"Ja," Master had said, between licks. "She gets that from you."

"I don't purr."

"You purr all the time. Your wolf purrs every time I take my shirt off. I can hear it."

Mistress had hit him with a pillow, very carefully, so as not to hit the heir.

Kreacher had gone downstairs and poured the bathwater out of the silver basin into the garden, with a feeling in his chest that he did not have a name for.

---

On the twelfth day, the heir opened her eyes.

Kreacher was there. It was the morning, in the kitchen, and Master was sitting at the long table with the heir lying along his forearm on her back, the way he held her when he wanted to look at her, which was always. Mistress was across the table eating her fourth plate of eggs. Kreacher was at the stove, frying a fifth.

The heir yawned. Her whole small face scrunched up around it. And then, when the yawn was over, she blinked.

Her eyes were open. Just a crack. Just two small wet slits of color, blurry and blinking. But open.

Master went completely still.

Kreacher put down the spatula and crept to the end of the table, and looked.

They were green.

A clear, bright, shining green, like new leaves with the sun behind them. Greener than the moor. Greener than bottle glass. There was a thin ring of dark gold around the very edge of each one, like Mistress's own eyes when the light caught them. But inside the ring, they were green.

Kreacher had seen that green before. He had seen it twice. Once on a young man in a battered old Muggle shirt, sitting at the kitchen table in Grimmauld Place eating Kreacher's French onion soup. And once in the old photograph Master kept on the table by the bed: a young witch with long red hair, laughing, holding a baby up to the camera.

*The Mudblood's eyes*, Kreacher thought, out of two hundred years of habit.

And then, hard, the way you'd slap a fly: *No. Master's mother's eyes.*

Master didn't say anything at all. He just looked down at his daughter, and his daughter looked blurrily up at him, and her tiny paw reached up and closed on a clump of fur on his chest and held on. Then Master put his head down on the kitchen table, very slowly, with his forehead against the wood, and his whole body shook, and he didn't make a sound.

Mistress came around the table and stood behind his chair and put her hand on the back of his neck, and left it there, and didn't say anything either.

Kreacher went back to the stove and fried the fifth plate of eggs very, very carefully, so that nobody would see his face.

---

Kreacher was allowed to hold the heir once.

It was on the sixteenth day. Master needed a shower; Mistress said so, loudly, and said that he smelled like baby sick and sour milk and that if he didn't wash she would make him sleep in the stable. Master looked at the heir. Then at Mistress, who was eating. Then, slowly, as if it hurt him, at Kreacher.

"Four minutes," Master said. "Don't move. Don't go near the stairs. Don't go near the fire. Don't sneeze."

Kreacher held out his arms.

She was very light. Lighter than Master Regulus had been. She smelled of milk and of Master and of something else, something wild and clean, like the moor after rain. She opened her blurry green eyes and looked up at Kreacher's face, and her ears twitched, and her tiny nose worked, smelling him.

Kreacher sang to her. He had not meant to. It came out of him all by itself, cracked and low. It was the song he had sung to Master Regulus, the old Black lullaby, about the stars that the family was named for and the dark sky that held them.

The heir reached up and took hold of Kreacher's ear in one small paw, with her rice-grain claws, and held on tight.

Master came out of the bathroom three minutes and forty seconds later with his fur still dripping and shampoo in one ear. He found Kreacher sitting very still on the edge of the bed with the heir asleep in his arms and his ear in her fist, and tears running down his face, singing.

Master didn't say anything. He just sat down on the floor next to the bed, dripping, and listened until the song was over.

---

On the twenty-first day, in the evening, Kreacher was bringing a tray of tea into the library when he heard Mistress say the word *after*.

He stopped in the doorway.

Master was on the sofa in front of the fire with the heir asleep on his chest. Mistress was beside him, cross-legged, with a piece of parchment in her lap that Kreacher recognized: the one that had come by owl in October, the one with the green ink, that she kept in the drawer of the writing desk in the Lady's room and took out sometimes and read and put away again.

"It's after," Mistress said.

Master looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked down at the heir on his chest.

"Ja," he said. "It's after."

"Pye says no Portkeys for her. No Floo. No Apparating. Not for three months. It could tangle her magic up." Mistress's voice was steady, but her hands were tight on the parchment. "I asked. I've been asking for two weeks. I'm not waiting three months, Harry. It's almost Christmas. I want to see my mom before—" She stopped. "I just want to see her."

"I know," said Master.

"So I don't know how we—"

"I've sorted it," said Master.

Mistress turned her head and looked at him.

"Sorted what?"

"How we get there." Master's ears tipped sideways. "I bought a plane."

Kreacher dropped the tea tray.


---

"You bought a *plane*," Leah said.

"Ja."

"A whole plane."

"Well, you can't really buy half of one." Harry scooped Kreacher's dropped teacups off the rug with a glance and set them back on the tray, unbroken, and the spilled tea ran backward up into the pot. "It's a jet. A Gulfstream. G550. I rang a man in Farnborough last week. It's got the most legroom of anything he had." He stretched out one long leg, footpaw flexing, by way of explanation. "And it can go nonstop to Seattle. I checked."

"When did you *do* this?"

"Tuesday. You were asleep. She wasn't." He looked down at Layla on his chest, who was asleep now with one tiny paw curled in his fur. "We had a long talk about it. She was very supportive."

Leah stared at him.

"Pye said no magic," Harry said. "No Portkeys, no Floo, no Apparating, not till February. So, no magic. Muggles have been flying across the Atlantic for sixty years. Hermione's mum and dad fly to Florida every Easter." His ears went back a little. "I wasn't gonna put her on a commercial flight with three hundred strangers, Leah. I just wasn't. Somebody coughs, somebody looks at her funny, somebody's a leech in a hoodie in row thirty-one—"

"Harry."

"—so I bought a plane. It's got two pilots. They're both ex-RAF and they think I'm a very eccentric property developer from Cape Town with a beard. I've got it booked into Exeter for Friday morning." He paused. "If you want to go Friday. We can go later. Whenever you want."

Leah sat on the sofa and looked at him.

She'd grown up in a two-bedroom house with blue trim on the reservation. Her dad had driven the same Ford pickup for fifteen years, until the floor rusted through on the passenger side and you could watch the road go by under your feet. When she was twelve, she'd gone to Seattle with her mom and Seth for the weekend for a cousin's wedding, and they'd stayed at a Motel 6 by the airport, and it had been the fanciest thing she'd ever done in her life.

"You bought a plane," she said again, "so our daughter wouldn't have to sit near anybody who coughs."

"Ja," said Harry.

She looked at Layla, asleep on his chest. At his forepaw, lying very lightly over her back, claws tucked all the way in. At his ears, which were back, and his eyes, which were watching her face to see if she was angry.

"Friday's good," she said.

His tail thumped once against the sofa.

"There's one more thing," he said. "You should probably come see it before you hear about it from Kreacher."

---

It was in the old carriage house behind the stables, under a gray dust sheet.

Harry pulled the sheet off with a flick of one forepaw, and it slid away like water and folded itself on the floor, and Leah looked at what was under it for a long time.

It was a car. She thought it was a car. It was huge, and tall, and square-shouldered, and so black it looked wet, black like the bottom of a well, black like Harry's fur. It had a tall upright silver grille on the front like the front of a Greek temple, and on top of the grille a little silver statue of a woman leaning into the wind with her arms flung back like wings. The wheels were bigger than any wheels Leah had ever seen on anything that wasn't a logging truck. The doors were the wrong way round—the back ones hinged at the back instead of the front. Through the tinted windows she could see seats the color of cream, and wood, and a ceiling that looked like it was full of stars.

"What," Leah said, "is that."

"It's a Rolls-Royce," said Harry.

"Rolls-Royce doesn't make—*that*." She walked around it. It was taller than she was. It was nearly taller than Harry. "They make the long ones. The posh ones. For the Queen. They don't make—big ones. Trucks. Whatever this is."

"They don't," Harry agreed. "Well. They made one. For me." He rubbed the back of his neck. "They've got a department down at Goodwood that'll make anything you want if you pay them enough and promise not to tell anyone. I asked them in August. I said I wanted something big and high and heavy that could go up a logging road in the rain with a baby seat in the back and not feel a thing." He shrugged. "They'd been thinking about building something like it anyway. Ten years from now, they reckon. Their engineers called it Project Cullinan."

"Cullinan."

"After the diamond. Biggest one anybody ever dug out of the ground. They found it in a mine outside Pretoria in 1905." His ears tipped sideways. "I heard that, and I thought, ja, okay. Had to have it."

"You bought a car that doesn't exist yet."

"It exists," said Harry. "It's right there. It just doesn't exist for anyone else."

Leah put her hand flat on the black hood. It was cold and smooth and so polished she could see her own face in it, and his behind her, and the little dark bundle of Layla in the sling on his chest.

"How are we gonna get *that* to Washington?" she said.

Harry held up his free forepaw. The car shrank.

It didn't make a sound doing it. It just got smaller, smoothly, all at once, like something going away down a long road, until it was sitting on the carriage-house floor about the size of a loaf of bread, black and perfect, the little silver woman on the front no bigger than a grain of rice. Harry bent down and picked it up and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.

"Like that," he said.

---

They left on Friday at dawn.

Kreacher packed for Layla. It took him three days. He packed forty-two nappies with tail holes and the little gray knitted hat with the ear holes that Healer Pye had made and nine tiny sleepsuits, also with tail holes, and the silver rattle, and a blanket he'd knitted himself out of wool so soft it felt like smoke, with a small black crest stitched into one corner. He packed a hot-water bottle. He packed a jar of violet powder, which Leah had never once seen anyone use and which he packed anyway, with an air of quiet defiance. He packed it all into a trunk with *L. S. P-B.* painted on the lid in gold, and he made Harry shrink it in front of him and put it in his coat next to the car, and then he made Harry show him that it was there, twice.

Then he stood in the doorway of Potter Manor in his best tea towel and watched them walk up the hill to the top of the combe, where the taxi to Exeter was waiting on the road, and when Leah looked back from the top of the tor he was still standing there, very small, in the doorway of the big gray house, waving a handkerchief.

"He's going to clean the whole house while we're gone," said Harry. "Top to bottom. Twice."

"He cleaned it top to bottom yesterday."

"He'll do it again. It's how he worries."

Leah looked at Harry sideways. He had Layla in the sling on his chest, under his coat, with only the top of her small round head poking out between the lapels, the little gray hat on it and her folded ears sticking out through the holes. He had one forepaw spread over her back. He'd had one forepaw spread over her back since six o'clock that morning, and he would keep it there, Leah knew, for the next eleven hours.

"Yeah," she said. "I know a few people like that."

---

The plane was white, and long, and sharp-nosed, and it sat on the wet tarmac at Exeter Airport in the gray morning drizzle with its stairs down and two men in navy-blue uniforms standing at the bottom of them. They both said "Good morning, Mr. Potter," and "Good morning, Mrs. Potter," and the older one, who had a gray mustache, smiled at the baby carrier and said, "And who's this little one, then?"

"Layla," said Harry.

"Lovely," said the pilot, and Leah watched his eyes go over Layla's furry face and her folded ears and her black jellybean nose, and see—whatever he saw. A pink bald baby, probably. A normal one. He didn't blink. Harry's glamour was very good.

Inside, the plane was all cream leather and pale wood and soft lights, and there were big wide seats like armchairs, and a sofa along one side, and a little kitchen at the back where somebody had left a fruit bowl and a plate of sandwiches with the crusts cut off. Leah stood in the aisle with her duffel bag over her shoulder and looked at it all.

"This is stupid," she said.

"Ja," said Harry happily, and folded himself down onto the sofa, which was just long enough for him, and settled Layla on his chest, and put his forepaw back over her.

The engines started. The plane rolled. Leah sat in one of the armchair seats with her hands gripping the armrests, because she'd been on exactly two planes in her life before this and both of them had been small and loud and full of people eating peanuts. And then there was a long rising roar, and the ground fell away under the window, and Exeter went small and gray, and then the clouds came up and swallowed it, and they were gone.

Layla didn't wake up.

---

They were over Greenland when Leah woke up.

She'd fallen asleep somewhere over Ireland, curled up in the armchair under Kreacher's blanket. Now the cabin lights were down low and amber, and the engines were a steady hum, and out the window the world had gone white. Nothing but white, mile after mile, glaring and empty under a pale cold sky, with long blue cracks running through it like veins.

Harry was still on the sofa. He hadn't moved. Layla was still on his chest, still asleep, one tiny paw clutching a fistful of his fur. His eyes were open. They were on the window.

"Did you sleep?" Leah said.

"No."

"Harry—"

"I'll sleep at your mum's." His ears flicked. "Probably. Maybe. Come here."

She got up and went over and lay down on the sofa beside him, squeezed in between him and the back of it, and put her head on his shoulder and her hand on Layla's back next to his. Layla's heart went fast and light under her palm. Against her own chest, the necklace pulsed in time.

They lay there and watched Greenland go by.

"What if she's mad," Leah said, after a while.

Harry didn't say anything. He waited.

"I left," Leah said. "I left a note on the fridge on a Thriftway receipt and I left. I didn't call. I sent her one letter in a year and a half. Four lines. No return address. I didn't tell her I was married. I didn't tell her I was *pregnant*. I just—" She stopped. "What if she opens the door and looks at me and shuts it."

"Then we go back to the car," said Harry, "and I drive you to the nicest hotel in Seattle, and we order every single thing on the room-service menu, and you cry, and I hold her, and in the morning we fly home." He was quiet for a second. "And then we come back next year, and try again. And the year after that. As many times as you want."

Leah pressed her face into his shoulder.

"She won't shut the door," Harry said.

"You don't know that."

"I know you," Harry said. "And she made you. So."

Layla stirred on his chest. She made a small sleepy grumbling noise and pushed her face deeper into his fur, and her tail curled up around her own small body like a question mark, and she sighed, and went still again.

Out the window, the white went on and on.

---

They landed at Boeing Field in Seattle at two in the afternoon, local time, in the rain, and a bored-looking man from U.S. Customs came on board with a clipboard and looked at their passports, which said *Harry James Potter* and *Leah Potter-Black* and *Layla Sue Potter-Black*, and looked at Harry, and looked at Layla in her carrier, and said, "Cute kid," and stamped everything and left.

Twenty minutes later they were in the air again. It was a short hop. Over the gray water of Puget Sound, over the dark ridges of the Kitsap, toward a wall of mountains with snow on their tops and clouds dragging across them like smoke.

Leah sat with her forehead against the window and watched the Olympic Mountains come up out of the rain.

She'd run along the bottom of those mountains fifteen months ago, on four legs, with a dry bag tied to her ankle, heading the other way. She'd crossed I-5 between a semi and a minivan at three in the morning and somebody had honked.

The plane banked. Below them, the Strait of Juan de Fuca spread out flat and gray, and on its edge, a small gray town strung along the water. Port Angeles. Leah had been there a hundred times. School trips. Shopping with Mom. The Swain's General Store, the ferry terminal, the Safeway where Paul had once shoplifted a whole rotisserie chicken on a dare and eaten it in the parking lot.

Three unexplained deaths, the advisory had said. Attributed to animal attack.

The wheels came down with a thump.


---

Harry put the car back behind the hangar, where nobody was looking.

He took it out of his coat pocket, the little black loaf of a thing, and set it down on the wet asphalt between a fuel truck and a stack of pallets, and stepped back, and it grew. Smoothly, silently, all at once, the way it had shrunk, until it was sitting there in the rain at full size, black and tall and gleaming, with the little silver woman on the front leaning into the weather.

The fuel truck driver, who had been smoking a cigarette by the hangar door, looked at it. Then at Harry. Then down at his cigarette. Then he put the cigarette out on the wall and went inside.

"He'll think he missed it coming in," said Harry. "People always do."

The baby seat was already in the back. Harry had installed it himself at the manor on Wednesday, with a tape measure and the instruction manual and a level, and then put about nine charms on it that Leah couldn't see but could feel, humming, when she put her hand near it. He strapped Layla in now, very slowly, checking every buckle twice, and tucked Kreacher's blanket around her, and the little gray hat. Layla woke up long enough to look at him with her blurry green eyes and make a cross grumbling noise about the cold, and then went back to sleep. Harry climbed in next to her. He had to fold himself nearly in half to do it, and his tail went across the floor and up over the other seat, and his ears brushed the starry ceiling.

"You're riding in the back," Leah said.

"Ja."

"Of your own car. That you bought. That doesn't exist."

"I'm next to *her*," said Harry. "You know the way. You drive."

So she drove.

---

She went west on 101, out of Port Angeles, past the Safeway and the Les Schwab and the Chinese buffet where Seth had thrown up on his tenth birthday. The rain came down gray and steady. The wipers went back and forth. The car was so quiet inside that she could hear Layla breathing in the back seat, and Harry breathing next to her, and her own heart.

She knew every curve of this road. She'd been driven down it a thousand times as a kid, asleep in the back of Dad's pickup. She'd driven it herself the summer she got her license, Sam in the passenger seat with his hand on her knee and the radio up loud.

She didn't think about that. She drove.

The road went up into the trees and came down along the edge of Lake Crescent, long and deep and dark under the clouds, black water with the mountains going straight up out of it on the other side and the rain making rings on the surface. It was beautiful. It always was. She'd forgotten how beautiful.

In the back seat, Harry made a sound.

She looked in the mirror. He'd cracked his window an inch, and his nose was right up at the gap, and his nostrils were working hard. His ears had gone flat. His tail, which had been lying across the floor, had pulled in tight around his footpaws and gone completely still.

"What," Leah said.

"Leech," said Harry.

She took her foot off the gas without meaning to.

"Old?"

"Fresh." His voice had gone low and flat. "Two days. Three, maybe. In the trees on the north side of the lake. More than one." He breathed in again, slowly, deeply. "And wolves. Lots of wolves. All over. Big ones. They've been through here all week."

Leah put her foot back on the gas. Her hands were tight on the steering wheel.

"Keep driving," Harry said. "Don't stop. I've got her."

She looked in the mirror again. He'd put his forepaw over Layla's car seat, over the blanket, and his eyes were on the trees going by outside the window, and they didn't leave them.

She kept driving.

---

Forks hadn't changed.

That was the first thing she thought, coming in past the sign that said *THE CITY OF FORKS WELCOMES YOU*, and it made her want to laugh and cry at the same time. Somebody had wrapped Christmas lights around the sign. Somebody had wrapped Christmas lights around everything, actually—the lampposts on Forks Avenue, the window of the Forks Outfitters, the big plastic Santa on the roof of the hardware store, gone green with moss on one side. The rain made them all blur. There was the high school, gray and low, where she'd spent four years being Leah Clearwater, Sam Uley's girlfriend, and then one year being Leah Clearwater, Sam Uley's ex. There was the Thriftway where she'd worked weekends for two years, bagging groceries and saving every dollar in a coffee can. There was the diner. There was the police station.

A police cruiser was pulling out of the station as she drove past. She saw the face behind the wheel—mustache, tired eyes, Seattle Mariners cap. Charlie Swan. He glanced over at the huge black Rolls-Royce gliding past him through the rain, and did a double take so big she saw his whole head move, and then he was gone behind her in the mirror.

"Friend of yours?" said Harry, from the back.

"Chief of police," said Leah. "Bella's dad."

"The one who married the vampire?"

"That's the one."

"Huh," said Harry, and craned around to look out the back window.

She turned left at the light, off 101, onto La Push Road.

The trees closed in on both sides, tall and dark and dripping, Sitka spruce and western red cedar and hemlock, so thick the light went green. The road wound through them. Fourteen miles. She knew every one.

And somewhere around mile nine, she smelled it.

Wolf.

*Her* wolves. The smell came in through the vents and hit her right in the chest, so familiar it hurt. Musk and wet fur and earth and that hot, sharp, electric something under it all that was the smell of a body running a hundred and eight degrees. Jared. That was Jared's. And Embry's. And Paul's, she'd know Paul's anywhere, Paul smelled like he'd been rolling in something on purpose. And a dozen others she didn't know at all. Young ones. Strangers. Kids.

They were everywhere. They'd been everywhere for weeks. The whole forest was soaked in them.

She heard Harry breathe in, behind her, long and slow.

"Ja," he said quietly. "That's what you smell like. Under the cedar."

"We're about to cross the line," Leah said. Her voice came out tight. "The treaty line. Onto the rez. As soon as we're over it, every wolf on patrol is gonna smell you. They'll smell me, too. They'll know something's here that shouldn't be."

"Good," said Harry.

She looked at him in the mirror.

"Let them smell me," said Harry. His forepaw was still spread over Layla's car seat. His eyes were steady and green. "I want them to know exactly what's in this car."

The sign came up on the right, in the rain. *QUILEUTE INDIAN RESERVATION. LA PUSH.*

Leah drove past it.

---

The house was where it had always been, at the end of the gravel road behind the stand of alder.

It was smaller than she remembered. That was stupid—it was the same size it had always been, a little two-bedroom with blue trim, the trim her dad had painted the summer she was twelve. But she'd spent five months in a house with a tower now, and it looked small to her, small and low and a little bit tired, with moss on the roof and the gutter on the left side sagging where it always had. There was smoke coming up out of the chimney into the rain. There were Christmas lights along the porch rail, the old colored kind, the big bulbs, red and green and blue and orange. Mom's car was in the drive. Somebody had put a wreath on the door.

Leah pulled the Rolls-Royce into the drive behind her mom's Subaru and turned the engine off.

She sat there.

The rain drummed on the roof. The wipers had stopped halfway across the windshield. She could see the kitchen window from here, yellow light behind the curtain, a shape moving back and forth behind it.

"Leah," said Harry, behind her.

"I know."

"We can sit here as long as you want."

"I know."

She sat there. The shape behind the curtain moved back and forth, back and forth. She knew how it moved. She'd know it anywhere.

Behind her, in the car seat, Layla woke up, and opened her blurry green eyes, and made a small cross noise, and sneezed.

Leah opened the door.


---

Sue Clearwater was rolling out fry bread dough on the kitchen counter when she heard the car.

She heard it because she didn't hear it. That was the strange thing. Every car that had ever come down that gravel road, in the twenty-two years she'd lived at the end of it, had announced itself—the crunch of the gravel, the engine coughing, the squeal of brakes that needed doing. Harry's old Ford had sounded like a tractor in a dryer. Charlie's cruiser rattled. Billy's van wheezed. But this one came down the road through the rain like it was floating, a low smooth hush that she only noticed because the gravel crunched under it, and then even that stopped, and there was just the rain on the roof.

She wiped her hands on her apron and went to the window and pulled back the curtain.

There was a car in her driveway. It was the biggest car she had ever seen. It was black, and tall, and so shiny the rain ran off it in sheets, and it had a silver grille on the front like a church organ and a little silver lady on the hood. It was parked behind her Subaru, and it made her Subaru look like a toy.

Sue stood at the window with the curtain in her floury hand and frowned at it.

Somebody lost, probably. Tourists. Rich ones, heading for the resort at First Beach in the wrong season, who'd taken a wrong turn off the La Push Road. She'd have to go out in the rain and give them directions, and they'd look at her house and her moss-covered roof and her sagging gutter with that look rich people got.

The driver's door opened.

Somebody got out. Tall—a woman, very tall, in a long dark coat with the hood up against the rain. She stood beside the car for a moment with her back to the house. Then she turned around and pushed the hood back.

Sue's hand closed on the curtain so hard the rod came out of the wall.

---

She didn't remember crossing the kitchen. She didn't remember the hallway. She was just at the front door, all at once, with her hand on the knob and the curtain rod still in her other hand and her heart going so hard she could hear it in her ears. She stood there.

She'd stood at this door a thousand times in fifteen months. Every time the gravel crunched. Every time the phone rang late at night. Every time Seth came home from patrol with that look on his face, the one he got when he'd been thinking about his sister, which he wasn't supposed to do in front of the others, because the others could hear it. She'd stood at this door and listened and hoped and been wrong, and gone back to the kitchen, and kept going. Because that was what you did.

She'd kept the note. The one on the back of the Thriftway receipt. It was still on the fridge, under the salmon magnet, gone soft and gray at the edges from her touching it. And next to it, the letter. The one from November, with the Whitehorse postmark and no return address. Four lines. *Mom. I'm okay. I'm safe. I'm happy, I think. I'm really happy.*

She had read it so many times the paper had torn along the fold.

There were footsteps on the porch. Two sets. One light and quick, one so quiet she almost didn't hear it, like something very big walking very carefully.

Sue put down the curtain rod and opened the door.

---

Her daughter was standing on the porch.

Leah. Her Leah. Taller than she remembered, though that couldn't be right; she'd stopped growing at nineteen, when the wolf came. Her hair was long again, black and heavy, past her shoulders, the way it had been before she'd hacked it all off with the kitchen scissors in the bathroom the week after her father's funeral. Her face had changed. It had filled out, softened, the sharp hungry edges of it gone. She looked older. She looked like a woman. She was wearing a long dark wool coat that had never come from any store in Port Angeles, and under it, a red-and-black flannel shirt three sizes too big for her, and on her left hand there was a ring with a gray stone in it that flashed green when the porch light caught it.

Her eyes were wet. Her mouth was trembling.

"Hi, Mom," Leah said.

Sue couldn't say anything. She couldn't make a single sound.

Then she looked at the man standing beside her daughter, and for a second she couldn't breathe either.

He was enormous. That was the first thing. He filled the porch. He was a big, broad, bearded man in a long black coat, with rain on his shoulders, taller than anyone she knew, taller than Sam Uley, as tall as Jacob Black, so tall he had to stoop a little under the porch roof. He had a gray knitted cap on, pulled down low. Brown eyes, in a weathered face.

And then she blinked, and for one second the bearded man wasn't there.

There was something else there instead. Something with black fur, and a long dark muzzle, and two tall pointed ears where the knitted cap had been. Something with eyes as green as new spring leaves, looking at her steadily over the top of a small bundle he held against his chest.

She blinked again. The bearded man was back. Brown eyes. Gray cap.

Sue's heart was pounding. She had seen her own son turn into a horse-sized wolf in the backyard. She had stood in this kitchen while Sam Uley explained to her, very gently, what had happened to her husband's heart. She knew what she had just seen.

The man was holding a baby.

It was wrapped up in a soft gray blanket against his chest, inside his coat, with one of his big hands spread over its back. All Sue could see of it was the top of its head. It had a tiny gray knitted hat on. There were two little holes in the top of the hat. Two little ears stuck out through them, soft and dark and folded down, covered in fur, the tips of the fur silver-gray in the porch light.

One of the little ears twitched.

Sue looked at the ear. Then at the man's face. Then at her daughter.

"Mom," said Leah. Her voice cracked right down the middle. "Mom, I—"

Sue stepped out onto the porch in her apron and her slippers, into the cold and the rain blowing in under the roof, and took her daughter's face in both her floury hands, and looked at her. Just looked at her. Then she pulled her down and wrapped both arms around her, as tight as she could, so tight she could feel Leah's ribs and Leah's heart going like a drum and the burning heat that came off her now, that had come off her ever since the wolf. And Leah grabbed onto the back of her apron with both fists the way she used to when she was four and had a nightmare, and put her face in Sue's neck, and they stood there on the porch in the rain and held on to each other, and neither of them said anything at all.

Behind them, the big man stood very still with the baby against his chest and his eyes on the trees, and waited.

---

"I'm sorry," Leah said, a long time later, into her neck. "I'm sorry, Mom. I'm so sorry. I should have called. I should have—"

"Hush," said Sue.

"I left a *Thriftway receipt*—"

"I know. I kept it." Sue pulled back and held her daughter's face again and looked at her. She was crying too, she realized. She hadn't noticed when she'd started. "You're here. You came home. Hush."

Leah laughed, wetly. "You've got flour on my face."

"You've got flour on your face," Sue agreed, and wiped at it with her thumb and made it worse. Then she looked over Leah's shoulder, at the very tall man in the black coat, still standing at the edge of the porch in the rain. He hadn't moved. He hadn't said a word. The baby against his chest made a small sleepy noise, and he looked down at it, quick, and then back up at Sue.

"Leah," Sue said. "Who is this?"

Leah let go of her. She wiped her face on her sleeve. She stepped back, and reached out, and took hold of the man's arm, and brought him forward into the porch light.

"Mom," she said, "this is Harry. He's my husband."

Sue stopped.

The rain went on. A gust came in under the porch roof and blew cold drops across her face, and she didn't feel it.

*Harry.*

She looked at the big man. He was looking back at her, and his face had changed. It had gone very careful, very still, and she understood, all at once, that he knew. That Leah had told him. That he had known since before he walked up those steps exactly what that name was going to do to her when she heard it, standing on this porch, at this door, under the Christmas lights that Harry Clearwater had hung on that rail every December for twenty years.

"Mrs. Clearwater," the big man said. His voice was low and deep, and his accent wasn't from anywhere around here. "I'm sorry. I know. Leah told me about him. It's just my name. I'd change it if I could."

Sue opened her mouth. Nothing came out. She closed it.

Then, without quite meaning to, she laughed. A small, cracked, astonished sound.

"Of course it is," she said. "Of course your name is Harry."

She turned around and went back into the house and down the hall and into the kitchen, and sat down hard on one of the kitchen chairs, and put her face in her floury hands.

---

They came in after her. She heard them. Leah's quick footsteps, and the other set, so quiet she'd never have known he was there if the floorboards in the hall hadn't creaked under his weight. Leah knelt down in front of her chair. Sue felt her hands on her knees.

"Mom."

"I'm all right." Sue took her hands down off her face. "I'm all right. Give me a minute." She looked up.

He was standing in her kitchen doorway, stooped to fit. The bearded man, with his gray cap and his brown eyes and the baby in his coat. He looked around her kitchen—the yellow curtains, the old stove, the fridge with its magnets and its school photos and Leah's note on the Thriftway receipt under the salmon—and his eyes stopped on the receipt for a long moment.

"Mom," Leah said. "There's something else."

"I know," said Sue.

Leah blinked.

"I saw him," Sue said. She looked at the big man in the doorway. "On the porch. Just for a second. I blinked, and he wasn't—that." She waved a floury hand at the beard, the cap, the brown eyes. "He was something else."

Leah's mouth fell open. She turned around on her knees and looked at Harry.

The big man's eyebrows had gone up. Under the cap, Sue had the oddest feeling that something else had gone up too.

"Huh," he said.

"She can see you," Leah said.

"A bit of me." He was looking at Sue very hard now, with his head tipped a little to one side. "Pye said it. Kin-magic. Old blood recognizes old blood." He was quiet a moment. "Huh."

"Show her," said Leah.

The big man looked at Leah. Then at Sue.

"You're sure?"

"She's my mom," said Leah. "She's seen worse. Show her."

He didn't move. He didn't do anything Sue could see.

The bearded man in the gray cap went away, the way a reflection goes away when you open a window.

Standing in her kitchen doorway, stooped so his ears wouldn't hit the frame, there was a wolf.

He stood on two legs, like a man. He was six and a half feet tall at least, maybe more, covered from the top of his head to his feet in thick black fur that went charcoal at the edges under her kitchen light. He had a long black muzzle and a black nose and a mouth that, when it opened a little, was full of long white teeth. His ears were tall and pointed and swiveling, one toward Sue, one toward the window. A long, heavy black tail hung down behind him under the hem of his coat, and it moved, very slightly, side to side, as she looked at it. His eyes were bright green. The forepaw spread over the baby's back had five long padded digits, each one ending in a black claw.

He looked at her and waited.

Sue Clearwater, who had buried a husband and watched both of her children turn into wolves in the space of three months, and had sat through more council meetings about cold ones and treaties and the old stories than she could count, sat at her own kitchen table in her floury apron and looked at her daughter's husband for a long, long time.

"Well," she said finally. "You're a big one."

His ears went pink inside. "Ja. I get that a lot."

"Where's that accent from?"

"South Africa. Mostly. Some of it's England." He shifted his weight, very carefully. "It's a long story, ma'am."

"Sue."

"Sue," he said.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then at Leah, kneeling on the kitchen floor beside her chair with her eyes huge and wet and her hands clenched on Sue's knees. Then, slowly, at the small bundle in the gray blanket against his chest.

"And is that—" Sue's voice failed. She tried again. "Leah. Is that—"

Leah got up off the floor. She went over to Harry and held out her arms, and he leaned down and put the bundle into them, very carefully, both forepaws under it until he was sure she had it. Then Leah came back across the kitchen and knelt down again in front of Sue's chair and folded back the corner of the gray blanket.

"Mom," she said. "This is Layla."

---

The baby was awake.

She was very small. Smaller than Seth had been, smaller than Leah had been, smaller than any newborn Sue had ever held. And she was covered in fur. Soft, thick, fine fur, dark charcoal, nearly black, every hair of it tipped with silver-gray so that she shimmered in the kitchen light like frost on a dark window. Under the little knitted hat her ears were folded down soft against her head. Her muzzle was short and blunt, with a tiny black nose that was working, smelling the kitchen, smelling the flour and the fry bread dough and the woodsmoke and Sue. One small paw had come out of the blanket, five tiny curled digits with five tiny claws no bigger than grains of rice, and it was opening and closing on the air.

And down the middle of her face, from between her ears to the tip of her nose, there was a stripe of pale, soft gray.

Sue knew that gray. She'd seen it a hundred times through the kitchen window, moving at the edge of the trees at dusk. Her daughter's wolf had a face that exact color.

The baby opened her eyes.

They were green. Bright, clear, astonishing green, the color of the big wolf's eyes in the doorway. But around the edge of each one, there was a thin ring of dark gold. Sue knew that gold too. She'd been looking at it for twenty years. Leah's eyes went that color when the sun hit them.

The baby looked up at her, blurry and blinking, and her tiny nose worked, and her little forepaw reached up out of the blanket and closed on a fold of Sue's floury apron, and held on.

"Layla," Leah whispered. "Layla Sue."

Sue looked up at her daughter.

"Sue," said Leah. Tears were running down her face. "After you, Mom. Layla Sue Potter-Black. She's your granddaughter."

Sue Clearwater put both hands over her mouth.

She didn't make a sound. She couldn't. She sat in her kitchen chair and looked at the tiny furred face in her daughter's arms with its silver-tipped fur and its pale gray blaze and its green-and-gold eyes, and she shook, all over, and the tears ran down between her fingers.

Leah held the baby out.

Sue took her.

She was light as a loaf of bread. She was hot, the way Leah was hot now, the way Seth was hot, the way the wolves were all hot. She smelled like milk and like something wild and clean and cold, like the beach at First Beach on a winter morning. The baby squirmed in the crook of Sue's arm and turned her face toward Sue's chest and snuffled, and a tiny pink tongue came out and touched Sue's wrist, and went back in. Under the blanket, something moved. Something small and furry, lashing back and forth against Sue's arm.

A tail.

Sue laughed. She was crying so hard she could barely see, and she laughed out loud in her kitchen, holding her granddaughter, who had a tail.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, look at you. Oh, look at *you*." She bent her head down over the baby. "Oh, your grandpa. Your grandpa would have—oh, Leah, your father would have—"

She couldn't finish it.

Leah put her arms around both of them, around Sue and the baby together, and laid her head on Sue's shoulder. Sue felt her shaking.

In the kitchen doorway, the big black wolf named Harry stood with his head bowed under the frame, very still, and watched them. His ears had gone soft. His tail had started moving behind him, slow and steady, back and forth against the doorframe.

*Thump*, it went. *Thump. Thump.*

"Harry," said Leah, without lifting her head.

"Mm."

"Don't you dare break Mom's door."

"I won't," said Harry.

*Thump*, went his tail, against the frame. *Thump-thump.*

Sue looked up at him over her daughter's head and her granddaughter's small furry ears, with her face all wet and floury, and found that she was smiling.

"Her father would have gone for his shotgun the minute he saw you," she told him. "And then he'd have offered you a beer."

Harry's ears came up.

"I'd have taken the beer," he said.

---

Sue made coffee, because her hands needed something to do. She made the fry bread too, because the dough was sitting there on the counter and it would go to waste otherwise, and because she wasn't sure she could stand to let go of the kitchen just yet. The kitchen was the one thing in the house that still made sense.

Leah sat at the table with Layla in her arms. Harry sat beside her, on the bench under the window, because none of the kitchen chairs would hold him. He'd folded himself down onto it with his knees nearly up at his chin and his tail curled around the bench leg, and he took the mug of coffee Sue handed him in both forepaws, very carefully, the way you'd hold something you'd been warned was breakable.

"Thank you," he said.

"Do you take anything in it?"

"Four sugars," said Harry, and then, when Leah looked at him: "Five."

Layla started fussing halfway through the first batch of fry bread. A thin, cross, rising whimper, and her small face screwed up, and her tiny tail lashed under the blanket. Leah jiggled her. It didn't help. Harry put down his coffee and held out his forepaws without a word, and Leah handed her over, and he lifted her up under his chin and bent his head down over her, and began, very slowly, to lick her ears.

Sue stopped with the spatula in the air.

He did it the way she'd seen a dog do it, once, with a litter of puppies under the Atearas' porch. Long, slow, patient strokes of his tongue, from the base of one small folded ear to the silver tip of it, and then the other one, and then over the top of her head between them, smoothing the fur flat. Layla's whimper stopped. Her face unscrewed. Her eyes slid half shut. And from somewhere deep in her tiny chest came a sound Sue had never heard a baby make in her life, a soft rumbling chirrup, over and over, like a kettle just starting to think about boiling.

"He does that," Leah said. She'd seen Sue's face. "Constantly. Every morning. Every time she cries. He did it the second she was born."

"She was—" Harry stopped licking long enough to say it, and his ears went back. "She wasn't breathing. Right at first. I didn't think about it. I just did it."

"And she breathed," said Leah quietly.

Sue looked at the big black wolf on her kitchen bench with her granddaughter purring under his chin, and put the spatula down.

"Harry," she said.

His ears came up.

"I'm going to ask you something," Sue said, "and I want you to answer it straight. I'm her mother. I get to ask."

"Ja," said Harry. "Ask."

"Are you good to her?"

Harry was quiet for a moment. He looked down at Layla. Then at Leah, beside him.

"I try to be," he said. "Every day. I get it wrong sometimes. I moved all the knives in the house when she told me she was pregnant, and she yelled at me on a riverbank for ten minutes." His ears went pink. "But I try. And she'd tell you if I wasn't. She'd tell you before I'd even finished getting it wrong."

"He bought a *plane*, Mom," said Leah. "So nobody would cough on her."

Sue looked at her daughter.

"A whole plane," said Leah. "A jet. That's how we got here. And that car outside? He had it built. It doesn't exist. Rolls-Royce isn't going to make that car for ten years."

Sue looked at Harry for a long time.

Then she looked at Leah's left hand, on the table, and the ring on it, the gray stone flashing green and gold under the kitchen light. And at the other ring, on her right hand, the heavy old gold one with the square green stone, that looked like it had come out of a museum.

"You said Potter-Black," Sue said slowly. "Layla Sue Potter-Black."

"Yeah."

"*Black*?"

Leah's face did something. Sue saw it. A quick hard flinch, there and gone.

"Different Blacks, Mom," Leah said. "English ones. It was his godfather's name. He left it to Harry. It's a whole—it's a title thing. It's stupid."

"Hm," said Sue.

She looked at Harry again. At the black fur and the green eyes and the long dark muzzle bent over her granddaughter's small silver-tipped ears. She thought about Billy Black's great-grandfather, Ephraim, the last chief, who'd turned into a wolf. She thought about the old stories, the ones the elders told at the bonfires, about the spirit warriors and the cold ones and the first wolves. She thought about a man from England with an English name, a long time ago, in one of the stories Old Quil used to tell when she was a girl, that she hadn't thought about in thirty years. A pale man who came on a ship, and stayed a while, and left.

*Old blood recognizes old blood*, Harry had said, in her kitchen doorway, about the way she'd seen through him.

"Hm," said Sue again.

The fry bread was burning. She turned around and took it off the heat, and didn't say anything else about it. But she kept looking at him, every so often, over her coffee, with a small line between her eyebrows that Leah would have recognized from every report card she'd ever brought home.


---

Seth came through the back door twenty minutes later without opening it first.

Leah heard him coming. They all did. Harry's ears snapped around toward the back of the house a full ten seconds before anything happened, and his tail went still, and he was out of his chair and between the back door and the kitchen table before Leah had even registered the sound—bare feet pounding on wet earth, fast, coming through the trees behind the house like a freight train.

Then the screen door banged open so hard it came off its top hinge, and her little brother came through it.

He was huge. That was the first thing. He'd been fifteen and gangly when she left, all elbows and knees, a puppy that hadn't grown into its feet. Now he was sixteen and six-foot-four and built like the rest of them, broad and hard and shirtless in December, in a pair of cut-off sweatpants and nothing else, soaked to the skin, steaming in the kitchen air. His hair was plastered to his head. His chest was heaving. There were pine needles stuck to his shoulders.

He stopped dead just inside the door, face to face with Harry.

Harry looked down at him. Seth looked up. It was a long way up, even for Seth now.

Neither of them moved. Leah watched Harry's nostrils flare once, slowly, smelling.

"You smell like her," Harry said.

Seth's mouth opened. Nothing came out of it. His eyes went from Harry's ears to Harry's teeth to Harry's tail and back up to Harry's ears.

Then they went past him, to the kitchen table, to Leah.

"*LEAH!*"

He went around Harry like Harry was a piece of furniture. Harry let him. And then Seth had her, had both arms around her and had lifted her clean off the kitchen chair and right off the floor, the way he used to try to do when he was eight and couldn't, and he was spinning her around in the middle of the kitchen and shouting, and she was shouting too, *put me down, put me down, you idiot*, and he wasn't putting her down. His skin was burning hot and soaking wet and he smelled like rain and pine and wet dog and *Seth*, and she grabbed onto his neck and held on and didn't care that he was getting her soaked.

"You came back," Seth was saying, into her hair. "You came back, you came back, I knew you'd come back, I *told* them—"

"Seth—"

"I smelled you! I was on patrol up by the Hoh road and the wind changed and I *smelled* you, and I thought I was going crazy, and then I smelled—" he put her down, finally, but didn't let go, and looked over her shoulder at Harry "—*that*, and I just ran, I didn't even phase back till I hit the yard, I—" He stopped. He looked at Harry. He looked at Leah. "Leah. Who is that. *What* is that."

"Seth," said Leah, "this is Harry. My husband."

Seth's mouth fell open.

"Your *what*?"

"Husband."

"Your *HUSBAND*?" Seth's voice cracked right up into the rafters like he was thirteen again. He looked at Harry. Harry looked back at him, ears tipped sideways. "He's—Leah, he's like—he's a—*dude*, are you a werewolf?"

"Long story," said Harry.

"He's like seven feet tall!"

"Six-seven," said Harry.

"He's got a *tail*!"

"I've noticed," said Harry.

"Seth," said Sue, from her chair. Her voice was very calm. "Sit down before you break something else. And come and meet your niece."

Seth went very still.

Then, slowly, he turned his head and looked at his mother. At the gray blanket in her arms. At the tiny furred face looking up out of it, and the two small folded ears poking out through the holes in the little gray hat.

"My—" said Seth. "My *what*?"

---

It took a while after that.

Seth sat down on the kitchen floor, because his legs wouldn't hold him, and Sue put Layla in his arms, and he held her like she was made of soap bubbles, his huge hands shaking, and stared at her. Layla stared back at him with her blurry green eyes. Her tiny nose worked. Then she sneezed in his face.

Seth burst into tears.

"I'm an *uncle*," he kept saying. "Mom. Mom, I'm an uncle. Leah, I'm her *uncle*. Look at her. Look at her *ears*. Look at her little—oh my God, she has a *tail*, she has a tiny little *tail*—"

"We know, Seth," said Leah.

"She's *gray*! She's got your gray! She's got your stripe!"

"*We know*, Seth."

Harry had come back from the door. He was standing beside Leah's chair, with one forepaw resting on the back of it, very lightly. He hadn't sat down. His ears were up, but one of them kept turning toward the back door, the one hanging off its hinge, and the dark trees beyond it. Leah felt his eyes go to the window every few seconds.

"Seth," she said. "What's going on? Here. The advisory said—" She stopped. "There's a lot of wolves now. Eighteen, it said."

"Nineteen," said Seth, without looking up from Layla. "Since last week. Dylan Littlesea. He's thirteen. He phased in the middle of social studies and went through the wall of the portable." He sniffed. "Ms. Hernandez had to go home."

"Nineteen," said Leah.

"Jake's got eight. Sam's got eleven. Well, ten and the Littlesea kid, he hasn't picked yet." Seth wiped his nose on his bare shoulder. "It's because of the leeches. They keep coming through. Ever since the thing in January."

"What thing in January?"

Seth looked up at her. He looked at Sue. Sue looked back at him and didn't say anything.

"A lot happened," Seth said slowly. "After you left. Like, a *lot*. There were these vampires from Italy—like the vampire *government*—and they came here to kill Bella's kid, and all the Cullens' friends came, from all over, like fifty vampires, and both packs stood with them in this field in the snow and—" He stopped. "It's really long. It's a really long story. Nobody died. Well. One of the vampires. Not one of ours."

Leah stared at him.

"And since then," said Seth, "they just keep *coming*. Nomads. Ones who heard about it. They want to see her. Nessie. They come through, and they look, and mostly the Cullens make them leave. But some of them don't feel like being polite about it. There were some in August who killed a couple of hikers up by Port Angeles before Sam's lot ran them off."

"Who," said Leah, "is Nessie?"

Seth's face went through several things.

"Renesmee," he said. "Bella's kid. The—the thing she was carrying. When you left. She's half. She's like half vampire, half human. She grows really, really fast, she's fifteen months old but she looks about five, and she can show you stuff by touching your face, it's freaky. And she's—" He winced. "Jake imprinted on her."

The kitchen went very quiet.

"On *who*?" said Leah.

"Nessie."

"On Bella's *baby*?"

"She's not a baby anymore, she looks like she's—"

"*Jacob Black imprinted on Bella Swan's vampire BABY?*"

Seth winced harder. Sue closed her eyes.

Harry's ears had swiveled all the way around. "Sorry," he said. "Imprinted?"

"I'll explain it later," said Leah, through her teeth. "It's disgusting. I need to sit down. I *am* sitting down. I need to sit down *more*."

"It's fine," Seth said quickly. "It's totally fine, Leah, he's like her big brother, he's—"

"I don't want to *know*, Seth."

"But it's fine! It's mostly fine! Except—" Seth stopped.

Leah looked at him. "Except what?"

Seth looked down at Layla, asleep now in his huge shaking hands. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quieter.

"There's something else," he said. "Since October. Out in the woods north of the lake. We can't catch it. Jake's lot can't catch it, Sam's lot can't catch it, the Cullens can't catch it. It leaves a trail and then the trail just stops. Like it went up into the air." He swallowed. "And it smells wrong. Kind of like a leech, but off. Edward says it smells like something burnt. Like a fuse box. Like—"

"Rot," said Harry.

Everybody looked at him.

He was standing very still beside Leah's chair. His ears had gone flat against his skull. His tail had stopped moving. His eyes had gone past all of them, to the window, and the dark wet trees beyond it.

"Rot and copper," Harry said quietly. "Under something sweet. Like a dead thing wearing cologne."

Seth's mouth fell open. "How did you—"

Leah's skin had gone cold all over.

She knew that smell. She'd smelled it in a diner in Tamarack Crossing. She'd tasted it, burning, in the back of her throat.

"Harry," she said.

"Later," said Harry. He hadn't taken his eyes off the window.

And then, from somewhere out in the trees behind the house, a wolf howled.

---

It was a long, deep, rising howl, and it went up and up into the rain and hung there. And before it had even finished, another one answered it. And another. And another, from the other side of the house, from the road. And another. Until it sounded like the whole forest around the little two-bedroom house with the blue trim was full of wolves, all of them howling at once.

Seth's head came up like somebody had yanked it. "That's Sam," he said. "That's Sam's lot. And—" Another howl, from further off, different, a long rising note that Leah would have known anywhere. "That's Jake."

"They smelled him," said Leah.

"Of course they smelled him. *I* smelled him from the Hoh road." Seth looked at Harry. "I was phased when I smelled you guys. Jake heard everything I was thinking. *Everybody* heard. And Jake would've told Sam, because there's a—" he waved a hand at Harry "—a giant unknown *thing* on the rez—"

"Nine," said Harry.

They all looked at him.

"Nine in the trees behind the house," Harry said. He hadn't moved. His nose was working, slowly, and his ears were turning, one and then the other, like a pair of radar dishes. "Six on the road out front. Three more coming in from the beach. And two on foot, on two legs, coming up the drive." His nostrils flared again. "One of them smells like you, Seth. Like this whole coast. And like motor oil."

"Jake," said Seth.

"The other one—" Harry's lip curled, just slightly, off one long white canine. "The other one smells like somebody Leah used to know."

Leah got up out of her chair and went to the front window and pulled back the curtain.

The rain was coming down hard now. The Christmas lights along the porch rail made red and green and blue and orange blurs in it. The huge black Rolls-Royce sat in the drive behind her mom's Subaru, gleaming. And walking up the gravel drive toward the house, side by side but with a good six feet of space between them, came two men. Both of them shirtless. Both of them barefoot. Both of them enormous.

On the left, with his hair cut short now and his face older, harder, a little thinner than she remembered: Jacob Black.

On the right, walking with his shoulders squared and his jaw set, the way he'd always walked when he'd decided something and wasn't going to discuss it: Sam Uley.

Sam stopped when he saw the car. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked up, at the house, at the window. At her.

Leah let the curtain fall.

Behind her, in the kitchen, Harry turned around. He went to Sue's chair. He bent down, very carefully, his ears brushing the top of the kitchen doorframe, and he held out his forepaws to Seth, and Seth gave him Layla, and Harry held her against his chest for a moment, his nose against the top of her small furry head between her ears, breathing her in.

Then he turned and held her out to Sue.

"Sue," he said. "Would you hold her for me? Just for a minute."

Sue looked up at him. Then she held out her arms, and he laid the baby in them, and Sue folded the gray blanket around her and held her close against her floury apron.

"Stay inside," Harry said to Seth. "Stay with them. Don't open the door unless it's me or Leah."

Seth opened his mouth to argue. Then he looked at Harry's face, and shut it, and nodded.

Harry straightened up. He walked through the kitchen and down the hall to the front door, quiet as a cat, and Leah went with him. At the door he stopped and looked down at her.

"You don't have to come out," he said.

"They were my pack," said Leah. "Both of them. Kind of. I'm coming out."

"Then I'm right next to you."

"I know."

He put his forepaw on the doorknob. Outside, the howling had stopped. There was just the rain, and the crunch of bare feet on wet gravel, coming closer.

"Harry," said Leah.

"Mm."

"Remember what you said. In the library. About the peninsula."

His ears tipped toward her. His eyes were very green in the dim hallway.

"Don't burn it," said Leah.

Harry looked at her for a moment. Then the corner of his muzzle went up, just a little, off a lot of teeth.

"No promises," he said, and opened the door.
