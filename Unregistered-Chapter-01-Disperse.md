# Unregistered

## Chapter One: Disperse

*The clearing, north of Forks, Washington — January 2007*

---

## I. Bella

Garrett's voice was still hanging in the cold air when the first of the Volturi witnesses walked away.

Bella heard him go. A crunch at the back of the far line, out past the last of the gray cloaks, where forty-odd vampires who had come to watch a family die stood in a loose, uneasy crowd at the edge of the trees. One foot lifted off the frozen crust. Then the other. A dark-haired male in a red ski jacket turned around and walked into the forest at a human pace, carefully, the way a man walks out of a bar fight he wants no part of, and nobody in a cloak turned to stop him.

Two more went after him. A woman with a shaved head and a nose ring. A man in a charcoal business suit with his tie still knotted at his throat, as if he'd come straight from a meeting.

"Cowards," Caius said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. It carried across a hundred yards of snow like he was standing at Bella's elbow.

Garrett stood ten feet out in front of the Cullen line with his hands loose at his sides and his chin up. He'd said what he came to say. The Volturi had crossed an ocean to wipe out a coven and keep the gifts they liked, he'd told the witnesses, and the crime was the excuse. The witnesses could stay and watch, or they could start thinking about whose house would be next. Now he was breathing hard, which he didn't need to do, his chest going up and down under the old buckskin coat. Kate stood a step behind him. Her hand had found the back of the coat and closed on a fistful of it.

Nobody else said anything.

The clearing was a long, wide oval of dead grass and stone, a quarter mile from end to end, and the snow lay over it in a thin crust that had frozen hard in the night. The sky sat low and white and close, the kind of sky Forks did best. No sun. No glitter. Under the crust Bella could smell last summer's grass, dry and brown. Over it she could smell everything else in the world.

The witnesses on the far side, forty minds' worth of sweet, dry, dusty vampire. Her own family at her back. The wolves on both flanks, wet fur and cedar smoke and the hot, bright copper of their blood, sixteen huge bodies spread through the snow with their heads low and their hackles stiff. And in front of her, a hundred yards off across the white, the Volturi.

They hadn't moved in twenty minutes.

Thirty-two cloaks. She'd counted them three times when they came out of the trees, because counting was something to do with her mind that wasn't screaming. Gray at the edges, darker toward the middle, darkest of all at the center, like ink bleeding into water. They stood in a shallow wedge, shoulders almost touching. At the point of it, the three ancients. Caius on the right, his white hair lying on his black cloak like frost on slate, his face like a knife somebody had left out in the rain. Marcus on the left, eyes half-lidded, his hand resting on Aro's forearm, looking at nothing in particular, looking bored to the bone. And Aro in the center, smiling his thin papery smile, his skin so translucent it seemed lit from inside, like a lampshade.

A small woman stood pressed to Aro's back with one palm flat between his shoulder blades. Renata. His shield. Bella had felt her the moment the Volturi stepped out of the trees, a dense little pocket of *keep away* wrapped around Aro like a second cloak.

Two steps out on Aro's left, the smallest figures in the guard. Jane and Alec. Jane's cloak was so dark it was nearly black. Her hood was down. Her pale hair was pinned up tight, her face was a doll's face, and her red eyes had been fixed on Bella for the better part of an hour.

Behind the wedge, Felix, enormous, the hood thrown back off a head the size of a cinder block. Demetri. Heidi with her mahogany hair. Chelsea, whose gift cut the ties between people and stitched them to something else. A dozen Bella didn't have names for. And at the very back, half hidden, two more black cloaks, slighter than the rest, flanked closely by guards. The wives. Edward had told her the wives never left the tower in Volterra.

They'd brought the wives. They'd planned on staying awhile.

Ten yards in front of the Volturi, a smudge of gray-white ash sat steaming gently in the snow. It had been Irina twenty minutes ago. Caius had asked her if she still accused the Cullens and she had said no, she'd been wrong, the child was not immortal. And Caius had nodded, and Felix and Demetri had taken her arms, and there had been a sound like a car crash in a tin shed, and then the smoke. Tanya and Kate had gone for them. It had taken Emmett and Rose and Garrett and Eleazar all at once to hold them back. Tanya was still on her knees in the snow behind Carlisle, both hands over her face. She hadn't made a sound since.

The ash steamed. A little curl of it lifted in a breath of wind and settled again.

"Brothers," Aro said, in his soft, pleasant voice. "I believe we should confer."

The three ancients turned toward each other. It was an odd motion, slow and precise, like three old men turning their heads at a funeral. Marcus didn't seem to care. Caius's lips were already moving. Aro bent his head to listen, his hands steepled under his chin.

Beside Bella, Edward went still in a way she hadn't felt in him since the meadow, a year and a lifetime ago.

"Edward."

"They're voting," he said, very low. His eyes were on Aro. She could see his pupils moving in tiny jerks, tracking something she couldn't see, the way they did when he was reading a crowd. "Caius has wanted this since the plane. Marcus—" A tiny pause. "Marcus doesn't care what happens. He hasn't cared in a long time. And Aro—" His jaw shifted. "Aro made up his mind before they left Italy. He wants it to look like a vote. He wants the witnesses to see a decision."

"How long?"

"Minutes."

Bella felt the word land. She'd known. She'd been knowing it since Alice left, since the note in the book and the name of a lawyer in Seattle and two passports with false names in an envelope in the bottom of a backpack. She'd known it the way you know the floor is coming when you fall. It didn't help.

She looked down at her daughter.

Renesmee was in her arms, wrapped in a white parka Alice had bought her before she left, the hood trimmed in fake fur. She looked about three years old. She'd been alive for a little under four months. Her cheeks were pink with the cold, which she felt, a little, the human part of her, and her bronze curls were spilling out of the hood, and her brown eyes, Bella's old eyes, were wide and serious and fixed on the old men in black across the snow. She hadn't cried once all morning. She'd touched Aro's face with her own hand and shown him everything, and she hadn't cried then, either.

She knew. She'd always known more than any of them wanted her to.

"Jake," Bella said.

The wolf on her right turned his head. He was the biggest thing in the clearing, bigger than Sam, a russet-brown animal the size of a horse with his ears pinned flat and his lips lifted off teeth as long as Bella's fingers. He'd stood at her elbow since the Volturi came out of the trees. He hadn't taken his eyes off Renesmee for more than a second at a time.

He'd seen the backpack. He knew what it meant.

Bella slipped it off her own shoulder. It was a small black daypack, the kind high school kids carried. Inside it were two passports for Jacob and Vanessa Wolfe, a birth certificate, a driver's license with Jacob's photo and a birthdate that made him twenty-one, and more money than Bella had ever seen in one place before she died. She'd packed it herself, three nights ago, sitting on the floor of the cottage at four in the morning while her daughter slept. She'd folded a change of clothes in the bottom for each of them. She'd put in a hairbrush.

She slid the straps over Renesmee's small shoulders and pulled them tight. Renesmee let her do it. She didn't ask what it was for.

"South," Bella said to Jacob. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, distantly, the way you'd be proud of a stranger. "Like we said. Don't stop. Don't come back for us. Whatever you hear."

Jacob made a sound low in his chest, a sound a wolf's throat was never built to make. His head dipped, once, heavy.

Bella lifted Renesmee and set her on Jacob's back, in the deep rough fur between his shoulder blades. Renesmee's little mittened hands went into the fur by reflex and knotted there. Jacob braced. Bella could see the muscles of his back change shape under the girl, gathering her in, getting ready.

Renesmee pulled one mitten off with her teeth.

She reached for Bella's face. Her palm was very warm, the way it always was, warmer than Bella's skin by forty degrees, and the picture came the second she touched. Bella's own face, seen from below, from the crib, from the floor of the cottage, from the back of a wolf. Her own face in a hundred different lights. Every one of them full to bursting with the thing Renesmee had no word for yet.

"I know," Bella whispered. "I know, baby. Me too."

*I love you more than my life,* she thought, and couldn't make her mouth say it, and then made it.

"I love you more than my life."

"I love you too, Momma," Renesmee said. Her voice was very clear in the cold. Across the snow, Jane's head tilted a quarter-inch, the way a cat's does at a sound in the walls.

Edward's hand came down over Bella's, over Renesmee's, the three of them. He didn't say anything. He looked at his daughter like he was trying to memorize her, which was stupid, Bella thought, wildly, because they'd never forget anything again, any of them, they'd remember this for all of the forever they had left, which might be about four minutes.

Then he took his hand away and looked at Bella, and she looked at him.

"Bella," he said.

"I know."

"No. Look."

She looked.

Something was moving across the snow.

It came from the Volturi line, from the place where Alec stood with his small hands clasped in front of his cloak and his eyes half closed as though he were praying. It rolled out across the white like ground fog off a lake at dawn, low and slow and thick, the color of wet ashes. There was a little wind out of the north, and the fog came south against it, straight at them, at the steady unhurried pace of a man walking.

Alec's mist. Edward had told her about it. It didn't hurt. That was the terrible thing. It just took everything away, sight and sound and touch and smell, one sense at a time, and left you standing alone in the dark while Felix walked up and took your head off.

"Bella," Edward said again.

"I've got it."

She reached for the shield.

It was easier now than it had been a month ago. Back then it had been a hard rubbery thing that lived somewhere under her breastbone and fought her every time she tried to push it out. Now she could feel the whole of it at once, a long, thin, elastic dome that started at her own skin and bellied out over the snow and over every member of her family and every witness who had come to stand with them, all the way to the farthest wolf on the farthest flank. She could feel where each of them stood inside it, the way you feel the furniture in a dark room you know. Carlisle. Esme. Rose and Emmett. Garrett and Kate. The Irish, the Egyptians, the Amazons, the Romanians. Peter and Charlotte. Every one of the wolves, sixteen hot coals in the snow.

Jacob, right beside her. And Renesmee on his back, small and bright and warm in the middle of it, the one place in the whole shield where the thing seemed to get thinner, the way it always did around her, like a window.

She pulled it taut.

The mist reached the edge of it about thirty yards out. Bella felt it hit. It pressed, slow and patient, the way water presses on a dam, and when it couldn't get through, it began to spread along the surface, climbing the dome, feeling for a gap, a weak place, a seam.

She didn't give it one.

"Holding," she said, through her teeth.

"Good," Edward said. "Good. Keep—"

Jane looked at her.

It was the same look Jane had been giving her for an hour, but now it had force behind it, and the force hit the shield like a fist into a mattress. Bella grunted. The dome bowed in toward her and then sprang back out. Jane's lips pressed together, white. She did it again. And again. Little hard punches against the outside of the shield, three, four, five of them, a rhythm, like somebody knocking at a door they knew was locked.

"She's furious," Edward said. Bella could hear something under it. It was almost a laugh. "She's never had this happen in her life."

"Good," Bella said.

Across the snow, the ancients had stopped conferring. Aro straightened. He let his hands fall to his sides. He turned his head and looked, smiling, at his line, and then at the witnesses on their side, and then across the snow at Carlisle, and his smile got a little wider and a little sadder, like a priest at a deathbed.

"My dear friends," he began.

Something cracked in the middle of the clearing.

It was loud. It was much louder than it had any right to be, a single flat bang like a rifle shot or a tree trunk splitting in a freeze, and in the silence of that morning it went off like a bomb. It bounced off the trees on both sides and came back twice. Sixteen wolves flinched as one animal. Somebody behind Bella hissed. Across the snow, half the Volturi guard dropped into a crouch in the same instant, cloaks flaring, the wedge tightening on itself like a fist.

Bella's eyes went to the sound, and stopped there.

There was a man standing in the snow.

He was exactly halfway between the two lines. Fifty yards from the Volturi, fifty yards from her. He hadn't walked there; she'd have seen him. He hadn't come out of the trees; she'd have smelled him. One second there'd been nothing but white crust and Alec's gray mist spreading over it, and the next second there was a man in the middle of the mist with the stuff curling around his knees.

He was tall. Six-two, maybe a little more. Broad through the shoulders and heavy through the chest and arms, in the way of a man whose body had a job to do. Black hair, messy, too long on top, with a few flakes of old snow caught in it. He had on a brown leather jacket, worn pale and soft at the elbows and collar, over a dark plaid flannel shirt and a black T-shirt. Jeans. Heavy boots, laced to the ankle, scuffed at the toes.

His breath steamed.

That was the first thing Bella's mind caught on and held. It came out of his mouth in a long white plume, and drifted, and broke up. Then another. He was breathing like somebody who'd just climbed a hill.

Under him, the snow was melting. She could see it. A dark ring spreading out from his boots, the frozen crust going wet and gray and then going to slush.

And she could hear his heart. It was slow. Much slower than a human heart, slower even than a wolf's, a heavy, deliberate thud, like somebody tapping a bass drum with a padded mallet. Fifty yards away, and she could hear it clearly over the wind.

"Edward," she said.

"I can't hear him." Edward's voice had gone flat and strange. "Bella. I can't hear anything. There's nothing there."

The wind shifted. It came around from the north and brought the man's scent across the snow to her, and Bella's newborn throat, which caught fire for anything with a pulse, didn't catch fire. Pine. Wet earth. Cold stone, like a riverbed in December. And under all of that, something else, rank and wild and very old, a smell that made the hair on her arms stand up and every instinct she'd been given in the last four months say *dog*. *Wolf*. *Wrong*.

On her right, Jacob's whole body went rigid under Renesmee.

On her left, out on the edge of Jacob's pack, a slim gray wolf with a pale chest jerked as though somebody had put a bullet in her.

It was Leah. Bella knew her by the color and the size, the smallest of the wolves. Leah's head had come up and around, toward the man. Her ears were flat to her skull. Her eyes were wide, wide, the whites showing all the way around, and her front legs had gone stiff and splayed in the snow, like a foal's, like an animal that had just felt the ground drop out from under it.

She made a noise. Bella would remember the noise later. A high, thin, broken whine that seemed to come out of her without her permission, the sound a dog makes when it's been kicked.

The man in the snow didn't look at Leah. He looked to his left, at the Volturi, for one long second. Then he looked to his right, at the Cullens and the wolves and the witnesses and Bella, for another.

Then he rolled his neck, slowly, side to side, like a man getting the kinks out after a long drive. Bella heard it crack.

"Morning," he said.

## II. Leah

For about two seconds after the bang, Leah Clearwater was thinking about her mother.

She'd been thinking about her mother all morning. It was a stupid thing to be thinking about with thirty-two vampires in matching cloaks staring at her across a field, but there it was. Sue at the kitchen table at five a.m. in her bathrobe, not crying, both hands around a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking. *You come home*, Sue had said. *Both of you. I don't care what that Cullen girl needs.* And Leah had said *Sure, Mom*, the way she said it about the dishes and the laundry and calling if she was going to be late. Then she'd gone out the back door and phased at the tree line and run north with Seth on her shoulder, and the whole way here she'd been doing the math. Thirty-two of them. Four of the bad ones, Jane and Alec and the tracker and the big one. Sixteen of the pack. Twenty-ish Cullens and Cullen hangers-on. Bella's shield, which might work and might not.

The math didn't come out. She'd known it wouldn't. She'd done it anyway. It was something to do.

*South*, Jacob had been thinking, steady, under everything, like a pulse. *South. If it goes, I'm gone, and Seth comes with me, and Leah—*

*I know my job, Jake.*

*Leah—*

*I know my job.*

Her job was Jacob's left side. If it went, she and Seth broke with him and ran, and whatever came after the kid came through the two of them first. It was a good job. It was the kind of job that meant you didn't come home to your mother, but it was a good one. She'd made her peace with it somewhere around the Calawah bridge.

Then the bang, and every muscle in her body locked, and her head came around, and she saw him.

And the world let go of her.

That was the only way she'd ever be able to describe it, later, and she'd never describe it to anyone. Every line that tied her to anything snapped at once. She felt them go. Her mother at the kitchen table. Seth's dumb laugh. The rez, the beach, the smell of the woodstove in her dad's old truck. Sam. Even Sam, the oldest and ugliest line of all, the one she'd been dragging around behind her for three years like a dog with a can tied to its tail. Snip. Snip. Snip. Like somebody walking down a row of ropes with a pair of shears.

For one hanging second she was nothing. Weightless. Untied. A gray wolf in the snow, floating.

Then everything came back at once, every single line, and every single one of them was tied to *him*.

Her legs went out. Her front legs, both of them, stiff and splayed in the snow, and she felt the whine come up out of her throat and couldn't stop it. Gravity moved. It came up off the frozen ground under her paws and went and stood in the middle of the clearing, and it was him. He was the center of the clearing and the center of the morning and the center of the whole cold turning world, a tall man in a leather jacket standing in a puddle of his own slush with fog around his knees, and every inch of her body was leaning toward him like a compass needle swinging to north.

*No.*

She thought it as hard as she'd ever thought anything.

*No. No, no, no. Not me. Not this.*

She knew what this was. Of course she knew. She'd watched it happen to Sam in the dirt road outside Emily's house, when Emily came out on the porch with the dish towel over her shoulder, and he'd looked up, and Leah had watched his whole face change. She'd watched it happen to Jared in a classroom and to Paul in Billy Black's front yard and to Quil over a two-year-old in a pink jacket. She'd watched it happen to Jacob on Esme Cullen's living room floor over a baby with a bloodstain on her chin. She'd been inside all their heads afterward, every one of them, and she'd felt this. Felt the gravity go. Felt them get picked up and set down again somewhere else, facing a different direction for the rest of their lives.

She'd hated it. She'd hated it more than she'd ever hated anything, more than she hated the vampires, more than she'd hated her own skin the first night it split open on her. It was the thing that took Sam. It was the thing that told her, every single day, that she was the one who didn't count. The leftover. The genetic dead end, the girl wolf who'd stopped getting her period and hadn't told anybody, the one the magic had skipped because there was no point.

And now here it was, doing it to her. In the middle of the end of the world. Over a stranger who'd just come out of thin air.

Somewhere under the horror, very small, something whispered *so it works. You work. You were never broken.*

She stamped on that so hard she nearly bit her own tongue.

*LEAH.*

Jacob. In her head, loud as a car horn. And under Jacob, everybody, all of them, all at once, because the pack mind didn't let you keep anything, not ever, and the four of them had just felt the bottom drop out of her world exactly the way she had.

*Leah? Leah, what— oh.* Seth. *Oh. Oh no. Oh, wow. Oh,* wow, *Leah—*

*Is she— did she just—* Quil.

*No way.* Embry. *No* way. *Not Leah. Leah doesn't— she can't—*

*Shut up.* She tried to throw them out. She'd never managed it once in three years and she didn't manage it now. *Shut up, all of you, get OUT, get out of my head—*

*Leah.* Jacob again, and this time she could feel what was behind it, and what was behind it was fear. He knew. He'd known before the others, because it had happened to him. He'd felt her lines go the way his had gone. *Not now. God, Leah, not now. Not* him. *We don't know what he is.*

*You think I don't KNOW that?*

*Then stop—*

*I can't STOP it, Jacob, you of all people—*

She made herself look at him. At the man. She made herself do it the way she'd look at anything that walked into a fight, the way she'd been taught, because if she was going to be stuck with this she was going to be stuck with it with her eyes open.

He was tall. Six-two or so. Big through the shoulders and arms, thick through the chest, a heavy man who stood on the balls of his feet. Black hair, wind-messed. A day or two of black stubble along his jaw. Green eyes; she could see the color from fifty yards out. He was pale for somebody that size, an indoor kind of pale, with no marks on him anywhere she could see, no scars on the knuckles, no old breaks in the nose. The jacket was brown leather, old, gone soft and cracked at the seams. A hole was wearing through the left knee of his jeans. His boots were wet to the laces.

And the smell. The wind had brought it to every wolf in the clearing at the same time, and she felt the whole pack mind jerk on it like a hooked fish.

*He smells like a wolf.* Embry.

*He doesn't* look *like a wolf.*

*He smells like a* wolf, *man. Like— I don't know. Like a wolf wolf. Like something out of a zoo.*

*He's not Quileute.* Quil. *He's not anything. What the hell is he?*

Pine. Wet dirt. Cold stone, like the creek behind the house in winter when the ice started at the edges. And under it, rank and old and wild, wolf. A different wolf from the cedar-and-woodsmoke smell of the reservation. Older. Something from much farther off, from a forest she'd never been in, on the far side of the world.

Her wolf, which had never once in its life listened to her, wanted to roll over in the snow and show him its belly.

She dug her claws into the frozen crust until she felt it crack.

On the far flank, across the Cullens and the kid and Jacob's whole huge body, a big black wolf had turned his head and was looking straight at her.

Sam.

She didn't need his pack's thoughts to know what was in his eyes. She'd seen it in a mirror. She'd seen it on him, three years ago, the night he came over to tell her, standing in her mother's yard with his hands in his pockets. Sam Uley had imprinted, and now Sam Uley was looking across a battlefield at her, and he knew. He'd recognized it from the outside. The legs. The whine. The whole body tilted toward one point in the snow.

And his face, his big black wolf face, was doing something that looked a lot like pity.

*Don't you dare*, Leah thought at him, at a man who couldn't hear her, with everything she had. *Don't you* dare *look at me like that, Sam Uley. Don't you ever.*

Sam's head turned away.

In the middle of the clearing, the man had finished cracking his neck. He put a hand into the inside pocket of the leather jacket. Across the snow, the Volturi guard shifted, all of them, a ripple of gray cloth. Leah's hackles went up so fast it hurt. Every muscle in her back wanted to move, wanted to go, wanted to put her body between him and whatever was about to come at him from that side of the field, and she had taken two steps forward before she knew she'd done it.

*Leah.* Jacob, and this time it had the other thing in it, the thing under the words, the weight that came down on her like a hand on the back of her neck. The Alpha voice. He almost never used it on her. *Stay.*

She stayed. She stood there with all four legs shaking and the snow cracking under her claws.

The man took his hand out of the jacket. He was holding a wallet, black leather, the kind that folded. He flipped it open with his thumb, one quick motion, and held it up at shoulder height toward the Volturi, the way cops did it on TV. Then he turned and showed it to the Cullen side, too, for about a second, and flipped it closed.

"Harry Potter," he said.

His voice was English. Flat and rough, the vowels squashed and the ends of words dropped off, the kind of English you'd hear out of the guy fixing your car in a movie about London. He didn't shout. He didn't need to. The clearing was so quiet she could hear the fog moving.

"Department of Mysteries, Ministry of Magic, currently on loan to the International Confederation of Wizards. Which none of you have heard of." He put the wallet back in his jacket. "Which is sort of the whole point of it."

*Ministry of* what? Embry.

*Did he say magic?* Quil.

*Shut UP*, Leah thought, and for once in their lives, all of them did.

"Right," Harry Potter said. He looked at the Volturi. "So. I've had a very long night. I've been on a plane from Heathrow to Seattle with a bloke behind me kicking my seat for eleven hours, because some genius in Geneva thought it'd be more discreet than Portkey. I've had one cup of coffee, and it was rubbish." He looked at the Cullen side. "And I've just found out that a hundred-odd vampires and" —his eyes moved along the line of wolves, slow, and stopped for a half-second on Jacob, and moved on— "a pack of very large dogs have all decided to have a war in a field forty minutes up the road from a town with a Thriftway, a high school, and a police chief."

Leah's ears went back. *Charlie.*

*He knows about Charlie,* Seth thought. *How does he know about—*

"That's a breach," Harry Potter said. "That's a breach of the International Statute of Secrecy on a scale nobody's had to write a report about since about 1945, and I'm the sorry sod who's going to have to write it. So." He spread his hands a little, palms out, then let them drop. "By order of the International Confederation of Wizards, under Clause Seventy-three of the Statute, this gathering is unlawful, and it is over. You're all to disperse. Now. Everybody goes home. Or wherever it is you lot go. Your castles. Your caves. Whatever."

He looked at the Volturi.

"Disperse," he said.

For a second nobody moved at all.

Then, across the snow, in the middle of thirty-two cloaks, Aro began to laugh.

It was a lovely laugh. Light and delighted and full of affection, like an old man watching a grandchild take its first steps. It went on and on. It rang off the trees. And at the sound of it, every hair Leah had stood straight up along her spine, and something in her that had never once in her whole life belonged to anybody but her bared its teeth at the thing in the black cloak and thought, very clearly, *Touch him and I'll tear your arms off.*

*Leah*, Jacob said, softly, in a voice she'd never heard him use before.

She didn't answer him.

She was watching Harry Potter. He was standing in the slush with his hands at his sides, listening to Aro laugh. His face didn't do anything. It looked like the face of a man waiting at a bus stop.

He sniffed, once. Then he sighed.

"Yeah," he said. "Thought you might."

## III. Edward

Aro's mind was singing.

Edward had been inside it for most of the morning, and he would have said he knew all its keys by now. The bright, avid curiosity that ran under everything like a river under ice. The patience, centuries deep. The affection, which was real, and which made the rest of it worse. The appetite. Aro wanted things the way a fire wants air, simply and completely and without any sense that the wanting might be wrong. He had wanted Edward from the first touch in Volterra. He wanted Alice. He wanted Bella's shield, now that he'd felt Jane break herself against it. He had stood across the snow all morning running his hands over the Cullen gifts in his mind like a collector turning porcelain in a shop window.

And now there was a man in the middle of the field whose mind Edward could not hear, and Aro was delirious with joy.

*A wizard. A* wizard. *Oh, Caius, did you hear? After all this time. I'd believed them a story. Or extinct. Or so far gone into hiding that they might as well be. Hundreds of years. And he steps out of the air. Out of the air! What else can he do? What else are there?* A flicker of an image: dusty parchment, a candle, a monk's hand on a page, Latin in brown ink. A rumor Aro had heard in Florence in fifteen-something. *And Edward can't hear him. Look at the boy's face. He can't hear him at all.*

Then, delicately, like a man setting down a glass: *Can I?*

"Forgive me," Aro said aloud, still smiling, wiping at the corner of one eye with a fingertip as though he'd laughed tears. He hadn't. "Forgive me, my friend. I'm so rarely surprised anymore. It's a terrible thing, at my age. And you have surprised me so *completely*."

"Happy to help," Harry Potter said.

"A Ministry. A Confederation." Aro said the words as if tasting them. "A Statute of Secrecy. How marvelous. Do you know, young man, my brothers and I have kept the secret of our existence from humanity for three thousand years? We are the law among our kind. We have always been the law. And in all that time, no one has ever told us to *disperse*."

"Yeah, well." Harry put his hands in the pockets of his jeans. "First time for everything."

On Aro's right, Caius's mind was a single white-hot wire. *Impudence. Insolence. Out of the air, into our judgment, a* human, *a* thing, *with a heartbeat. With a smell like that. Smell it, Aro. It reeks of them. Of the dogs.* Under the words, a picture so old it had gone brown and cracked at the edges: a forest at night, a full moon, a huge pale shape tearing a vampire in half. Caius had been hunting werewolves for a thousand years. He'd smelled something on the wind just now that he hadn't smelled since the fifteen-hundreds, and every part of him wanted to kill it.

Edward glanced at the wolves. Then he looked at the stranger, harder.

On Aro's left, under the bored, dead weight of Marcus's mind, something moved.

Edward had almost never heard Marcus think. Marcus didn't, much. His mind was a long gray hallway in an empty house, and it had been that way for so long that the stillness had worn grooves in it. But Marcus's gift was always running, the way a heart runs, whether he cared or not. He saw the ties between people. He saw them the way other people saw color, without trying, cords and threads and ropes of light running from one person to another all over the clearing. Edward had been seeing them through him all morning: the steel cable between himself and Bella, the thick bright braid between Jacob and Renesmee, the tangle of strands holding the Cullens together, the brittle thread of fear tying the Volturi witnesses to their masters.

There was a new one.

It hadn't been there five minutes ago. It ran from the stranger in the snow to the smallest of the wolves on Jacob's flank, the gray one, and it was the brightest thing in the clearing. Bright and hard and new, like a wire pulled out of a furnace. It was as thick as the cord that tied Jacob to Edward's daughter.

Marcus regarded it without interest. *Strange*, he thought, dully. *Well.* And then the gray hallway closed over it again.

Edward looked at Leah Clearwater. She was standing at the end of Jacob's line with all four legs shaking. He didn't need to hear her. He'd seen Jacob's face over a newborn in a bloody blanket on Rosalie's arm, three and a half months ago. Leah's eyes were doing the same thing.

*Oh*, Edward thought. *Oh, no.*

"Your Confederation," Aro was saying, soft and gentle. "Your Statute. I'm sure they're very fine things. But you must understand, we know nothing of them. We are not party to them. We have never signed anything."

"You didn't have to sign." Harry tipped his head toward the Volturi line. "Clause Seventy-three. Every wizarding government's responsible for all the beasts, beings, and spirits on its patch. You lot count as beings. Your patch is Italy. The Italian Ministry's been signing off on you since sixteen ninety-two, Mister Aro, the same way you'd sign off on a big dog in a locked garden." He glanced along the gray line. "You've got a lovely little tourist trade going on in Volterra, haven't you? Busloads. Straight in through the front door. Your receptionist takes them on a tour and they never come out the other side." His eyes found Heidi and stopped there. "That's you, isn't it. The redhead. The one who does the fishing."

Heidi didn't move. In her mind, a white shock: *How does he know my face.*

"We've had files on you since the Medici," Harry said. "Thousands of pages. Rome's got a whole room. You were allowed to play sheriff because you kept your lot tidy and off our side of the fence. Fine. Everybody's happy." He took one hand out of his pocket and gestured, a short flat sweep, taking in the whole clearing. The fog. The wolves. The ash where Irina had been. "This isn't tidy."

"Enough," Caius said.

Aro lifted one hand without looking at him. Caius stopped. In his mind, the white wire went whiter.

"Please," Aro said to Harry. He took a step forward. Then another. Renata came with him, her palm flat on his back, her small face tight with concentration. "Please. This is all so terribly complicated, and I am an old man, and I would so much rather understand than argue. I have a gift of my own, you see. If I could simply—" He extended his hand. Long white fingers, translucent as wax, held out across the snow, palm up, as if he were asking for a dance. "One touch. That's all. And I would know everything you know. Every thought you have ever had. It would save us both so much time."

*Let me in*, Aro thought. *Oh, let me in. Let me see what you are.*

"No," Harry said.

Aro's hand stayed out. "I must insist."

"Insist away, mate."

Aro smiled at him a moment longer. Then his hand came down slowly, and he folded it into his other hand in front of his cloak, and he stood there looking at the stranger with his head tilted, the way a man looks at a lock he's going to have to pick.

*He won't come to me*, Aro thought. *Very well. Then I'll have him brought.*

"Jane, dear," Aro said softly.

Edward felt Bella go tight beside him. She didn't need to hear Jane's mind; she'd been taking Jane's punches on her shield for an hour. But the shield ended thirty yards in front of them, and the man in the snow stood fifty yards out, and he was standing in the open with nothing over him at all.

Jane turned her head.

Edward heard her gift go. It was the ugliest thing he'd ever heard in another person's mind. A child's mind, eight centuries old, curled around a single furious bright point of pain like a fist around a coal. She pushed it out of herself in a line, straight across the snow at the stranger, and Edward heard her thinking, as she did it, the way other people hummed: *Burn. Burn. Burn. Burn.*

Harry Potter blinked.

Jane's face sharpened. She pushed harder. Edward heard the pain in her mind go up and up, a sound like a kettle, *burn burn burn BURN*—

"Are you," Harry said, "doing something?"

Jane's lips peeled back off her teeth.

"Only you've got a face on you like you're trying to pass a kidney stone." He looked at her, then at Aro. "Is she doing something? Is that a thing? Is that your thing, sweetheart?" He looked back at Jane. "Is that the pain one? I heard about you. In the file. Little blonde girl, does a pain thing." He sniffed. "Love, I've had the Cruciatus Curse off the darkest wizard of the century in a graveyard when I was fourteen. Bloke didn't have a nose. You're about a two."

Jane screamed.

It was a high, cracked, raging shriek with no words in it, the sound of a child in a tantrum, and she threw everything she had at him, and Edward heard it go out of her in a wave so hard it made him flinch inside Bella's shield even though none of it touched him.

Harry Potter rolled his shoulders.

"Two and a half," he said. "For effort."

*Impossible*, Aro thought. And then, a second later, with a pleasure so deep it was almost lust: *Wonderful.* "Alec."

The mist moved.

All of it. The gray fog that had been creeping along the outside of Bella's shield, climbing it, feeling for its seams, suddenly let go and swung away across the snow like a flock of birds turning. It went toward the man in the middle of the field. It came at him from three sides at once, low and thick, and rolled up over his boots and his knees and his chest and closed over his head. For a second there was nothing in the middle of the clearing but a gray column of fog, ten feet high, churning slowly.

Edward heard Alec's mind. Alec was calmer than his sister, always; his gift didn't need rage. He thought of it as a kind of blanket. He laid it over people and tucked them in, and they went quiet. *There*, Alec thought, with something like tenderness. *Sleep. There.*

Then: *What?*

The fog column thinned. It thinned the way breath fades off a window. Edward could see the shape of the man through it, then the color of his jacket, then his face. He was standing exactly where he'd been. He had his eyes open. He was looking through the last of the fog at Alec with the expression of a man looking at a waiter who had brought him the wrong order.

*He feels it*, Alec thought, baffled, almost hurt. *He should feel it. It's touching him. It's right there on him. It doesn't* go *in.*

"That's damp," Harry said. He brushed at his jacket. "That's just damp, that is."

In the back of the Volturi line, Demetri's mind had been working the whole time, quietly, the way it always did. Demetri didn't care about the conversation. Demetri was finding the stranger's mind, so that if he ran, Demetri could follow him to the ends of the earth. He did it with everyone. He'd done it with every Cullen and every witness in the first ten minutes. He tasted the *tenor* of a mind, he called it, the flavor of it, and once he had a taste he could find it anywhere on the planet.

*Nothing*, Demetri thought. And then, with growing unease: *Nothing. Nothing. He's standing right there. I can see him. There's nothing to find. It's like trying to track a stone.*

"Aro," Caius said, very low. "He makes fools of us. In front of them all."

Aro didn't answer for a long moment. Edward listened to him decide. It didn't take long. It never did, with Aro. He weighed the man in the snow against the forty witnesses watching from the trees, against the three thousand years, against the thing he wanted, and the scales tipped the way they always tipped.

*Bring him to me*, Aro thought. *Alive. Felix will be careful. Mostly. A wizard can lose an arm and still be very useful.*

"Felix," Aro said. "Would you?"

Felix grinned.

He came out of the wedge like a boulder coming down a slope, and Demetri came with him on the left, and Santiago on the right, three of the best fighters in the Volturi guard moving together across the snow at a speed that would have made them invisible to human eyes. Behind them, the wedge moved too. All of it. Thirty-two cloaks surging forward in a single motion, because that was what the guard did when the guard moved, it moved together, and in Caius's mind there was a scream of joy, *finally*, *finally*, and he was already turning his eyes to the Cullen line, to the wolves, to the end of the morning.

Edward felt Bella's shield snap tight around them.

He felt himself drop into a crouch. Emmett on his right. Rosalie behind him with her hand on Jacob's shoulder. Carlisle at the front, his hands up, his mouth open to call out something. The wolves on both flanks dropping their heads. Fifty yards. Forty. Felix was halfway to the stranger, his mouth open, his arms reaching—

"Yeah," Harry Potter said, to nobody in particular. "Didn't think so."

He lifted his right hand to shoulder height, and snapped his fingers.

Edward heard it.

The snap itself was nothing, a small dry click, a sound like a twig breaking. What came after it, Edward would hear for the rest of his existence.

Thirty-four minds caught fire.

All of them at once, every mind in a black or gray cloak, from Aro at the center to the wives at the back. There wasn't any warning. There wasn't any build. One instant they were all there, thirty-four voices in the great noisy room of the clearing, and the next instant each one of them was a single searing flash of white, the way a filament flares in a bulb right before it blows.

He heard Felix, running, think *what*—

He heard Demetri think nothing at all.

He heard Caius, in a scream of fury that went on for a fraction of a second and seemed to go on forever.

He heard Jane, and he would remember Jane longest. Because for the very last instant of her life, for the first time in eight hundred years, Jane knew what it felt like. The thing she had done to thousands of people. The fire. Edward heard her understand it, all at once, in a single blinding note of shock, and then he heard her stop.

He heard Aro think *mine*—, as if reaching for something on a high shelf.

And he heard Marcus.

Marcus's long gray hallway lit up from end to end, once, like a house with all its lamps switched on together. And in the light, for a fraction of a second, Edward saw what was at the end of it. A woman with dark hair laughing in a sunlit doorway. *Didyme*, Marcus thought, with something Edward had never heard in him before, something that sounded very much like relief.

Then nothing.

Then silence where thirty-four minds had been. A great clean hole in the noise of the world, as if somebody had cut it out with scissors.

The fire was white.

Edward saw it in the same instant he heard it. It came out of the cloaks, all thirty-four at once, from the inside, a white so bright it hurt even his eyes. White-gold, the color of the sun on the island when Bella was human. It burst out of every seam of every cloak and up out of every collar and made thirty-four standing pillars of flame across the snow, and the sound of it was a single great *whump*, like a gas burner catching, but enormous, a sound he felt in his chest.

Felix was still moving when it took him. His momentum carried him three more yards, a man-shaped torch with his arms still reaching, and then he came apart in midair.

One second.

That was all it took. Edward counted it, because he couldn't help counting things. One second, from the snap to the end. The white columns went up and blazed and shrank in on themselves, and fell, and where they'd stood there was ash. Thirty-four soft gray drifts of it lying on the snow, some still in the shape of a man for a fraction of a moment, then slumping. Thirty-four circles of bare black ground where the snow had flashed straight to steam. Under the ash, here and there, a glint: a silver clasp, a ring, the Volturi crest on a chain. Felix's ash lay in a long smear fifteen yards from the stranger's boots, as if he'd been thrown.

The heat crossed the field a second later. It hit Edward in the face like the door of an oven opening. The fog was gone. Alec's mist had gone up with Alec. Every scrap of it burned off the snow in a single breath.

Then the smell came. Sweet and dry and heavy, like incense in a church, like burning sap. The smell of his own kind going up. He'd smelled it at the end of every fight he'd ever been in, and he had never smelled this much of it.

The wind lifted the ash. It rose off the snow in slow gray sheets and turned over in the air and began to drift south, toward the Cullens, toward Edward, falling around them like the snow had fallen in the night.

Nobody on the Cullen side made a sound.

On the far side of the clearing, at the edge of the trees, forty minds that had come to watch a family die began to scream.

It hit Edward like a wall of noise. At first it was only terror, raw and white, forty voices at once, the sound of a herd that has seen a lion take the lead animal. Then the words came up through it. *Run. RUN. What is he what is he what IS he. Aro. Aro's gone. They're all gone. All of them. Run. Don't look at him. Don't let him look at you. RUN.*

They went. This time nobody walked. They broke and scattered like a flock of crows off a field, in every direction at once, into the trees, a blur of bodies and a sound of snapping branches and then the sound of them going, going, going, getting fainter, miles of forest swallowing them up, until there was nobody on the far side of the clearing at all.

Edward heard the last of them thinking *dispersed, dispersed, we dispersed, we did as he said, don't come after us*, and then they were out of range.

The ash kept falling.

In the middle of the field, Harry Potter lowered his hand. He looked at it for a second, as if checking it was still there. He wiped the fingers absently on the leg of his jeans. Then he looked at the thirty-four piles of ash, and the black circles, and the long smear that had been Felix, and he blew out a breath that steamed in the air like a man coming out of a cold shop.

"Right," he said.

He turned around.

He turned his back on the empty side of the field, the place where three thousand years of law had been standing a moment ago, as if it didn't matter anymore. Which it didn't, Edward supposed. And he looked across fifty yards of snow and falling ash at the Cullens, and the witnesses, and the wolves, and Edward's wife, and Edward's daughter on the back of a wolf, and his face did nothing at all.

"I'm fairly sure," he said, "I said *everybody*."

## IV. Harry

Thirty-four was a lot.

He'd known it would be. He'd counted them from the treeline at half past eight, crouched in the bracken downwind of the dogs under a Disillusionment Charm, with the snow soaking through the knees of his jeans, while the old one with the white hair had a girl in a gray jacket torn in half and set on fire. Thirty-four in cloaks. Forty-three behind them, the hangers-on. He'd counted the other side, too. He'd sat there in the cold for twenty minutes listening to the old one in the middle talk about law in a voice like a vicar, and the whole time he'd been doing the sums on what it would take.

It took about what he'd thought. There was a hollow feeling behind his breastbone, like he'd skipped breakfast and lunch both. A faint, high ringing in his left ear. Nothing a fry-up wouldn't fix.

The ash was still coming down. It fell on the shoulders of his jacket and in his hair and on the backs of his hands, and it was weirdly soft, softer than wood ash, and it smelled like a Catholic church at Christmas. He didn't brush it off. Brushing it off would just smear it.

He looked at what was left.

Twenty-odd vampires across the snow, give or take. Red eyes on some of them and that odd gold color on more of them, the animal-drinkers. He'd read about that. A man with blond hair and a doctor's hands in the front. A woman with soft brown hair beside him, and a big dark-haired one, and a blonde built like a pin-up who was standing behind the biggest wolf with one hand on its shoulder. A bronze-haired young one, crouched. A brown-haired girl beside him with red eyes, newborn red, very bright. Clumps of others behind and to the sides. Two tall dark women in skins with long black hair. Four in a knot who looked Egyptian. Three with Irish skin. A pair of white-haired, dust-colored men standing a bit apart from everyone, like they'd turned up to the wrong party and stayed for the drink.

And dogs. He'd known about the dogs from the treeline. He hadn't known the size of them. Sixteen of them, on both sides of the line, every one of them as big as a carthorse, all their hackles up, and a great deal of growling going on.

The biggest one, the russet one in the middle, had a little girl on its back.

The little gray one on the end had been staring at him since he landed.

He'd noticed that. Hard not to. She hadn't taken her eyes off him once, not when he flashed the badge, not when the blonde kid did her pain thing, not even when he lit up the whole other side of the field. All the others had looked at the fire. The gray one had looked at him. She was standing with her legs braced out like a newborn deer's and her whole body shaking, and every time he moved, her ears moved with him.

He filed it. He had other things on.

"I said everybody," he said again, because nobody seemed to have heard it the first time.

The blond man at the front of the Cullen line straightened up out of his crouch. He did it slowly, palms out, the way you'd stand up in front of a dog you didn't know. "Mr. Potter." American, with something older and English under it, worn smooth. "My name is Carlisle Cullen. This is my family. We have no quarrel with you, or with your—Confederation. We had no wish for any of this. We came here only to—"

"I know who you are, Doc."

The man stopped.

"You're on page four of a very boring file," Harry said. "Carlisle Cullen. Doctor. Animal blood only. Three hundred and sixty-odd years old, never so much as nicked a pint off a blood bank. Nice house. Good on you." He jerked his chin at the trees. "Go home."

"This is our home," Carlisle Cullen said quietly. "Forks is our home."

"Then go inside it and shut the door."

"Mr. Potter—"

Somebody laughed.

It came from the two dust-colored men at the side. One of them, the shorter one, with his hair the color of an old bone, had his hand over his mouth, and he was laughing into it in a dry, coughing, breathless way, like it hurt him. He was looking past Harry at the black circles in the snow. His shoulders were shaking.

The other one, taller, with a long narrow face and eyes like a pair of claret stains, walked out from the Cullen line into the open snow, slowly, and stopped about twenty yards off.

Romanians. Harry knew them from the file, more or less. Stefan and Vladimir. Last of a lot who'd run half of Eastern Europe before the Italians burned them out, fifteen hundred years back.

"Fifteen centuries," Vladimir said.

His voice was thin and very precise, as if he'd learned English from a dictionary a long time ago and never forgiven it.

"Fifteen centuries we waited. We sat in our tower in the dark and we waited for him. For the day. We came here to see it. To see Aro *fall*. To stand in front of him and watch his face when he understood." His mouth moved. "And you took it from us. In a breath. With your fingers. As if he were nothing. As if *we* were nothing."

"Mate," Harry said, "I've had a really long night."

"You stand there and say *disperse* to us. *Go home*. As if you were Aro." Vladimir took another step. Behind him, Stefan had stopped laughing. "We were kings, wizard. We were kings when your Ministry was a hut in the mud. We bowed to Aro because he left us no choice. We will not bow to *you*."

"Bowing's optional, mate. Pissing off isn't."

"We have heard that before," Vladimir said.

He went.

He was fast. He was very fast, faster than the big one had been, low to the ground and straight, a long gray streak across twenty yards of snow, his hands up, his mouth open. Somebody on the Cullen side shouted. One of the dogs barked.

Harry didn't bother with his hand this time.

Vladimir went up four yards out. Mid-leap, white, the same as the others. One second. The heat of it hit Harry in the face. The ash came down in a long soft spray across the snow and across the toes of Harry's boots, and a little silver ring bounced twice on the crust and rolled and lay still beside his foot.

The scream came from behind it. The other Romanian. Stefan. He was on his knees in the snow where he'd been standing, with his hands in front of him, palms up, as if somebody had just taken something out of them. He screamed once, very high and very long, the kind of noise you don't forget. Then he stopped and just knelt there with his mouth open.

The whole Cullen line went rigid.

Harry watched them do it. He watched each of them start to move and then not move. The two tall women in skins, the Amazons, had both come up on their toes, and the taller one had her eyes fixed on him in a way that meant something. Some gift, probably. He didn't feel anything. She didn't do whatever it was. A sandy-haired lad in the Egyptian lot had both hands down by his sides with his fingers spread, and the snow around his feet had started to shiver, little ripples running out across the crust like water in a pan. He stopped. The ripples stopped. A tall blonde with long straight hair had blue sparks crawling over her knuckles, and a lanky man in buckskin had both arms wrapped around her from behind, and he was saying into her ear, very fast, *Kate. Kate. Kate. No. No. Don't. Kate.*

She didn't. The sparks went out.

Nobody else moved.

Harry let it sit for a moment. Then he looked down the line at all of them, one at a time.

"Anyone else?" he said. "Anyone want a go?"

Nobody did.

"No? Good." He sniffed. "Look. I don't care what you all came here for. I don't care who was right. I've got thirty-five piles of ash and one very annoyed Confederation and a report to write, and every one of you who's still stood here in five minutes is a line in it. So let's not, eh? Let's all just go h—"

He stopped.

The wind had come around again. It came across the snow from the line of dogs, and it brought him the dogs, wet fur and woodsmoke and that odd bright copper, a smell he knew from the inside. It brought him the vampires, sweet and dry. And it brought him something else, riding on top of both, sharp, that hadn't been there a second ago because the wind hadn't been coming from that angle. A heartbeat. Too fast. Much too fast, a little high flutter like a bird's, like a hummingbird's. Blood. Real blood, live blood, moving in somebody. And under the blood, the sweet dry vampire smell, braided into it. Both at once. In the same body.

He looked at the little girl on the russet wolf's back.

She was looking at him. Brown eyes. Bronze curls coming out of a white fur hood. A backpack on her shoulders, a small black one, straps pulled tight. Pink cheeks with the cold. A heartbeat. Breathing. Breath steaming, just a little, much less than his. Three years old, give or take, by the look of her.

The old one in the middle had been ready to burn this whole valley over her.

He didn't know what she was. He didn't know what she could do. Nobody in the file had mentioned her. Nobody in Geneva had mentioned her. The last time he'd been standing in a field and not known what something was, he'd been fourteen, and a boy he knew had died about four seconds later.

He didn't like not knowing things.

"Hang on," he said. "What the hell are you?"

The bronze-haired vampire moved. So did the blonde. So did the russet wolf, a sudden great bunching of muscle under the girl, and the newborn with the red eyes, and the doctor, and the little gray dog on the end, all of them at once.

"Nobody move," Harry said, and leaned on the field.

They stopped. All of them. Holding them took about as much effort as putting his hand flat on the lids of a lot of jars. The bronze-haired one hung frozen in the first inch of a lunge with his mouth open. The blonde's hand stayed clenched in the russet wolf's fur. Sixteen dogs stood in the snow like they'd been stuffed. He felt every one of them pushing back. The dogs pushed hardest.

He looked at the kid. She looked back.

He went in.

---

There was something in the way.

He'd felt it since he landed. A big, soft, stretchy dome sitting over the whole Cullen lot, from one end of the line of dogs to the other, like a circus tent. Somebody's. A shield, a mental one. It had soaked up the blonde kid's pain thing and the creepy little boy's fog all morning without a scratch, which put it among the better shields he'd come across outside the Department. It gave when you pushed it. It bowed in and came back. You could lean on it for a week and it would just keep giving.

He ran along it, looking, the way you run a thumb along the seam of a cushion. It was thinnest right over the kid, which was odd. As if the kid were wearing a hole in it from the inside. He found the thinnest place and put the edge of himself against it, and cut.

It parted. It parted clean, one long straight line, like a sheet of cling film under a scalpel. He felt it flinch. He felt something to his right flinch with it, a long way off and very close at once, the girl with the red eyes, all of her jerking like he'd cut her skin.

He went through the cut. It'd heal, probably. It'd leave a line.

Then he was in the kid.

No words in here. That was the first thing. He was used to minds with words in them, words like carpet, a layer of chatter over everything. The kid didn't have that. The kid had pictures. Pictures and smells and colors and the feel of things, all of it at once, piled up to the ceiling, bright and hot and moving fast, a lot faster than any three-year-old's head had any right to move. More than three years in here. More than ten. She'd read a lot. Somebody had read to her, all the time, the bronze-haired one, in a soft voice, and she'd kept all of it.

She was frightened. He felt it hit him. A big panicked shove, all her pictures turning toward him at once and pushing, *out out out out*, like a flock of birds flying at a window. She had a gift, he could feel the shape of it: she could put pictures into people. She was trying to put one into him. Him, going away. Him, gone. She was trying it with everything she had.

He didn't let it land. He didn't stop to soothe her, either. He had questions, and he went through them in order, one at a time, the way he'd been taught, and he pulled what came up.

*Name.*

The newborn with the red eyes, bending over her. Younger, softer. Brown eyes like the kid's. Lying on her back on a table that was soaked through. Saying *Renesmee*. Saying it like a prayer. Then the russet wolf, in the shape of a tall brown-skinned lad with cropped black hair, grinning at her: *Hey, Nessie.* A hundred times. A thousand. *Nessie, Nessie, Ness.*

Renesmee. All right.

*Age.*

A kitchen, a glass wall, rain. A blonde woman with a calendar. Days crossed off in red. Not many. A cake on a table with sparklers in it, and a crowd of faces, all of them pale, singing, and the kid in a high chair she was already too big for. A man with a mustache and a police jacket, holding her and looking like he'd been hit with a shovel. *Four months, almost*, the red-eyed girl's voice said somewhere. *She'll be four months old next week.*

Four months. He held that one up and looked at it. Then he put it down and kept going.

*Food.*

A cup with a lid and a straw. Red in the cup. Blood, thick, cold, from a plastic bag out of a fridge in a garage. The blond doctor's hands holding the bag. Deer. The kid on the russet wolf's back in the woods, sliding off, the deer's neck, the hot rush in her mouth, the wolf watching with his ears flat and his face disgusted and proud of her at once. Eggs on a plate. The kid pushing the plate away. The wolf eating the eggs.

Blood, then. Both kinds. And she'd eat human food if you made her.

*What are you.*

That one came up in a great tangle, all at once, the way a fishing net comes up out of the sea with everything in it. Her father: the bronze-haired one, pale and cold and hard. Her mother: the red-eyed girl. Her mother before, warm, soft, with a heartbeat. Her own heartbeat, fast. Her own skin, warm, warmer than Daddy's, cooler than Jacob's. The old man in black touching her face with his papery hand, and the old man's thoughts sliding in behind her eyes, *how extraordinary, how utterly extraordinary, half and half*.

Half and half.

He pushed further back.

It got darker the further he went. The pictures went soft and red and close. He was in a small hot place with no light in it. Very warm. A heartbeat all around him, huge, slow, *hers*, Mama's, and voices through a wall. A girl's voice, tired, laughing. Singing, sometimes, off-key. A man's voice reading. ## V. Jacob

For eleven seconds, Jacob Black was a statue with a little girl on its back.

He'd counted them. There was nothing else to do. Jacob saw it. He saw Sam look at her, down the length of the whole field. And then he saw Sam look at the guy. And then he saw Sam drop his head, slowly, all the way down, nose almost to the snow, and hold it there.

## VI. Bella

There was a cut in her.

Bella could feel it. She'd been feeling it since the second it happened, since the instant the stranger's eyes went to Renesmee and something thin and cold and unbelievably sharp slid along the inside of her shield and opened it. "Right," he said around the cigarette. "Mum."

Something in Bella's chest went cold and very clear.

She knew what was coming. She'd known it as soon as she felt where he'd cut. He'd gone into Renesmee for answers, and Renesmee was four months old, and she hadn't had all of them. So now he was going to come for the rest.

She pulled the shield in.

She'd never done it this fast. "Bella," Edward said. "Bella, what are you—"

The stranger walked into her head.

This time he came straight at her, straight through the front of it, through all five layers and the crack and everything she had holding it shut, the way a truck comes through a garage door. She felt it hit. It was the hardest thing she'd ever felt. It was harder than the birth. It was harder than the three days of fire. The vault walls she'd folded around herself *broke*. Her memories came up in a torrent.

*Stop*, she thought. *Get out. Get out of my head. GET OUT—*

He didn't stop.

The island, two weeks later. A box of tampons in her suitcase, unopened. Counting days on her fingers in the bathroom. Counting them again. Her hand on her stomach, and something under her hand. A little hard bump that shouldn't be there. A push. From *inside*.

Rosalie. Rosalie on the couch at home with her arms around her, and the whole family on the other side of the room, and Edward looking at her like she was already dead. Her own stomach, swelling, stretching, so fast. Purple bruises on it, from the inside. The pain. The pain everywhere, all the time. Her ribs. And then the cup.

A Styrofoam cup with a lid and a bendy straw, the kind you get at a gas station. Red in the cup. The smell of it, when it came close, which should have made her sick and instead made her mouth water. Her lips on the straw. Rosalie's hand on her back. Jacob's face across the room, horrified. The taste. The baby going quiet. *She likes it*, she'd said. *He,* she'd said, back then, *he likes it.*

He went through that one more slowly, the way you'd slow down to read something written small.

Then the birth.

She didn't want it. She'd never wanted it, not even for herself. She'd kept it in a box at the very bottom of herself since the day she woke up, and she'd taped the box shut, and she'd never opened it, not once. He opened it.

The floor of Carlisle's study. Her own blood coming up into her mouth, a mouthful, then more. Her spine. The noise her spine made. Her legs going away, all at once, the feeling just *gone* below her waist like someone had cut a string. Rosalie with a scalpel. Rosalie's eyes going black. Jacob dragging Rosalie out of the room. And Edward. Edward leaning over her, and his mouth going down, and his teeth. She'd felt his teeth. She'd felt them go through her. Through her skin and through the—

*Please*, Bella thought. *Please. Please stop.*

—and then the baby in her arms. Warm and wet and red and perfect. Her own voice, croaking. *Renesmee.* And the baby turning her head, and opening her mouth, and biting down. A tiny sharp pain over Bella's heart.

Then darkness. Edward's voice in the dark, *Bella, Bella, stay*. A needle, a long one, going into her chest. Into her heart. Then the fire. Three days of fire. She was back in it, for a second, for an eternity, burning from the inside out, unable to move, unable to scream, the way she'd lain there and burned and never made a sound because she knew if she screamed Edward would never forgive himself.

He went out the way he'd come in, straight through, without stopping to close anything behind him. She felt him leave. She felt her own head empty out around the place where he'd been, and it rang, like a church after the bell stops.

She was on her hands and knees in the snow.

There was ash under her fingers. Somebody's. She didn't know whose. She was making a noise, a high, dry, broken sound, over and over, and she couldn't stop it. Her shield was gone. She reached for it, out of a month's habit, and found nothing. Pieces. Shreds of it lying around inside her like broken glass after a car wreck, and the long thin cold line of the cut running through all of them.

"Bella." Edward was on his knees beside her. His hands were on her shoulders. His voice was coming apart. "Bella. Love. Look at me. Look at me."

She looked at him.

"I can hear you," Edward said.

He said it in a whisper. His eyes were huge. And she understood he meant the rest of it. Her thoughts. Her shield was in pieces all over the inside of her head, and for the first time since he'd met her in a biology classroom two years ago, Edward could hear every single thing in her mind, without her doing anything to let him.

She saw his face, and she knew he'd heard what she'd just been through. All of it. In order. Fast.

*Don't*, she thought at him, and watched him hear it. *Don't look at me like that. Don't you dare.*

Edward closed his eyes.

Across the field, the stranger let out his breath. Bella turned her head and looked at him through her hair.

He was standing exactly where he'd stood the whole time, in his puddle of slush with the ash still in his hair. The cigarette had burned down a quarter-inch while he'd been in her head. He took it out of his mouth and looked at the end of it. Then he looked at her. Then he looked at Edward, kneeling beside her, and at Renesmee crying in Rosalie's arms, and at Carlisle, who had come up beside them all with his face gray and still.

"Huh," he said.

That was all. *Huh.*

He put the cigarette back in his mouth and took a long pull on it, and the tip glowed, and he blew the smoke out the side of his mouth, away from them, as if that were a courtesy.

"Venom," he said. "That's the trick, is it. Bloke's got venom in all the bits, and it does to the bits what it does to the rest of you, and you get a human girl knocked up with a—" He waved the cigarette at Renesmee. "Half and half. Sire's a vampire, dam's human. Grows like a weed. Eats her mum from the inside out, near enough. Drinks blood, out the womb and in it. Gets born with teeth. Comes out biting." He sniffed. "Then you finish off the mum with the venom before she bleeds out on the carpet, and hey presto. Happy families."

He looked at Bella for a second longer. His face stayed empty. He looked at her like a man looking at a page he'd just finished reading.

"Sorry about the door," he said. "Hadn't got all day."

Then he turned his back on them, and put his hand inside his jacket, and took out something small and round and bronze that caught the white light off the snow.

"Right," he said to it. "Potter for Geneva. Duty desk. Yes, I'll hold."

## VII. Carlisle

Carlisle Cullen had been a doctor for a very long time, and he had learned, over most of three centuries, to do the next thing.

"Then ring them at home," the stranger said. "Twenty. Mark it on the map. I'll be stood in the middle of a field covered in ash. You'll find it."

He snapped the mirror closed and put it back in his jacket.

Carlisle had already started walking.

He did it slowly, at a human pace, his hands open and visible at his sides. He'd done the same thing in a great many rooms, over the years, walking toward a man with a knife or a woman with a gun or a frightened animal in a cage. He heard Esme say his name, softly, behind him, and he heard her not try to stop him. The ash crunched under his shoes. Twenty yards. Fifteen. The stranger watched him come with the cigarette in the corner of his mouth and didn't move.

Carlisle stopped ten feet away. Close enough to talk. Far enough that nobody could call it anything else.

"Mr. Potter."

"Doc."

"What does *containment* mean?"

The stranger looked at him for a moment. Then he took the cigarette out of his mouth.

"Means she gets registered," he said. "Means she gets looked at by people who know what they're doing. Proper Healers. Somebody works out what she is, what she can do, how fast she's growing, what happens when she stops. Means she ends up on a list with a name and a number, and anybody who wants to set her on fire has to come through Geneva first." He put the cigarette back. "Which, by the by, is a better deal than the one you were getting about ten minutes ago."

"And then?"

"Dunno, Doc. Never had one before."

"She's a child," Carlisle said. "She's four months old. She has a mother and a father. She has a family. Whatever your Confederation's laws say, you cannot simply—"

"Doc." The stranger's voice didn't rise. "I'll tell you what I can do. I can stand here. I can wait for twenty minutes. And when my people get here, I can make sure the first thing they do is talk to you, and not the other way round. That's what I can do." He looked past Carlisle at the family behind him. "That's me being nice, by the way. You'll want to tell Blondie. She looks like she's planning something."

Carlisle didn't need to turn around. He could hear Rosalie breathing.

"You're not taking her," Rosalie said, very low. "You're not taking her anywhere. Over my ashes."

"I can do that, love," the stranger said. "I'd rather not."

"Rose," Carlisle said. "*Please.*"

Something moved on the edge of his vision.

He turned his head. So did everyone else.

The small gray wolf at the end of Jacob's line had begun to walk.

She came out of the line slowly, one paw at a time, with her head low and her ears pinned back flat, like an animal crossing ice it doesn't trust. Every wolf on the field watched her. Jacob, still standing rigid in the slush where the law had stopped him, swung his great head around and made a sound in his chest that was half growl and half something much worse. Seth, the sandy one, took two quick steps after her and then stopped as if he'd hit a wall. On the far flank, Sam Uley lifted his black head and watched her go, and his whole pack watched with him.

She walked out into the open snow. She walked through the fallen ash. She walked past Carlisle, close enough that he felt the heat coming off her fur, and she didn't look at him.

She stopped beside the stranger.

She turned around, so that she was facing the Cullens. Facing all of them, Rosalie and Edward and Bella on her knees and Jacob and Carlisle and the rest. And then, slowly, as if every inch of it cost her something, she sat down in the snow at the stranger's left side, close against his leg, with her front paws together and her head up and her lips drawn back off her teeth.

She was shaking from nose to tail.

The stranger looked down at her.

He looked at her for a long time. Long enough for the cigarette to burn down another quarter-inch. Long enough for Carlisle to see something happen in his face for the first time since he'd stepped out of the air. It was very small. A crease between his eyebrows. A tightening at the corner of his mouth. The look of a man who has just realized he doesn't know something, and doesn't like it.

"Right," he said to the wolf. "Okay. And what's all this, then?"

## VIII. Leah

She'd done it. She was sitting there.

She hadn't decided to. Leah wanted that on the record somewhere, carved into a rock, for whoever came after. She hadn't decided one single thing since the bang. Her legs had walked her out of the line and across the snow and through the ash, and turned her around, and sat her down next to him, against him, so close she could feel the heat coming through the leg of his jeans, and every inch of the way she'd been screaming at them to stop.

Now she was sitting with her teeth bared at her own people. At Jake. At Seth. At the Cullens, at Rosalie with the kid, at Bella on her knees in the slush. Guarding the man who'd just done that to all of them.

*Leah,* Seth thought, very softly, from the line. *Leah, are you—*

*Don't.*

*I'm not. I just—*

*Don't, Seth.*

Jacob wasn't saying anything at all. That was worse than if he'd been screaming. She could feel him in the back of her head, a huge dark silence, like a storm cloud sitting very still.

"Right," the guy said above her. "Okay. And what's all this, then?"

She looked up at him.

Up close, his eyes were very green. Darker around the edges. There were three flakes of gray ash caught in his eyelashes on the left side, and a smudge of it on his cheekbone, and his stubble had a couple of red hairs in it along the jaw. He smelled like pine and cold stone and wolf and cigarette smoke. He was frowning down at her the way you'd frown at a stray that had followed you three blocks and sat down on your feet.

She couldn't talk to him like this.

She got up. Her legs let her, which surprised her. She turned and went for the trees at a trot, and the second she was five yards away from him something in her chest pulled tight like a fishhook, and at twenty yards it hurt, and at the treeline she was gritting her teeth so hard her jaw ached. She went in behind a big cedar. She let go of the wolf.

It went out of her the way it always did, the heat and the stretch and the sudden smallness, and then she was on her knees on the frozen needles, naked and steaming, with her hair in her face. The pack mind went with it. Every voice in her head cut off at once, Jake and Seth and Quil and Embry, and for one second there was nobody in there but her.

She knelt there and breathed in the quiet.

Then she untied the leather cord from around her ankle, shook out the black T-shirt dress she'd rolled into it, and pulled it on over her head. It was the only thing she'd brought. She didn't bother with shoes. She didn't own any she'd risk losing.

She walked back out barefoot through the snow.

Every eye in the clearing tracked her. She didn't look at any of them. She walked straight across to him and stopped about four feet off, and stood there with her arms crossed, and the fishhook in her chest let go so suddenly she almost swayed.

"You're the grey one," he said.

"Leah."

"Harry."

"I heard." She jerked her chin at the empty field behind him. "Everybody heard. You did the badge thing."

"It's a good badge."

"You burned Aro."

"Yeah."

"And all of them."

"Yeah."

"And you went into Nessie's head." She heard her own voice go hard and was glad of it. "She's four months old."

"I know." He took the cigarette out of his mouth. "I saw the cake."

She stared at him.

He looked back. His face didn't do anything. After a second he looked away, out at the Cullens, at the kid in Rosalie's arms.

"She'll be all right," he said. "Kids bounce. She'll have a headache and some bad dreams for a week. She'll want her dad." He tipped his head toward Bella, who'd gotten up off her knees with Edward's arm around her and was standing with her face turned away from all of them. "The mum'll take longer. That was a good shield. It'll grow back, mostly. There'll be a line."

"You broke her."

"Bent her a bit."

"You *broke* her, Potter."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Yeah," he said. "All right. I broke her a bit."

Leah let her breath out through her nose. It steamed.

"Your big mate," he said. "The red one with the kid. He had a go at me, before. Made it about a stride. Then he stopped dead, like he'd hit the end of a lead." He looked at her sidelong. "And the black one over there sat on his whole lot to stop them having a go. And then you came and parked yourself on my foot." He put the cigarette back. "I'm not daft, love. Something's up. What is it?"

She'd thought about how she'd say it. On the way back from the trees, barefoot in the snow, she'd turned it over twenty ways. Every one of them had been some kind of speech.

She skipped the speech.

"I imprinted on you," she said.

He looked at her.

"You what?"

"It's a wolf thing. Ours. It happens when we see somebody. One look, and that's it. You're stuck." She kept her voice flat and her arms crossed. "And no wolf can hurt another wolf's imprint. That's the law. The oldest one. Jake couldn't touch you. Sam's pack can't. None of us can. Ever." A beat. "Including me. Which I'd like you to know I'm really pissed off about."

He was quiet for a long time.

"One look," he said.

"One look."

"When?"

"When you landed."

He looked at her for another second. Then he looked up at the sky, which was white and close and had started, very lightly, to snow again, small dry flakes coming down through the last of the drifting ash. He breathed out a long stream of smoke at it.

"Fuck me," he said.

"You said that already."

"It's been that sort of morning." He looked back down at her. "So what's it make you, then? Is it a mate thing? Is that what this is?"

"It makes me whatever you need." It came out bitter. She let it. "That's how it works. Friend. Sister. Bodyguard. Whatever. Whatever you need, that's what I turn into, and I don't get a say." She looked him dead in the eye. "So don't get any ideas."

He thought about it.

"I need a coffee," he said. "And pie."

"I'm not getting you coffee."

"Okay."

"Or pie. I'm *really* not."

"Okay."

They stood there in the snow. Across the field, Seth had phased back too; she could see him at the treeline in his cutoffs with his arms wrapped around himself, staring at her with his mouth open. Jake was still a wolf, still standing where the law had stopped him, with his head low. Sam's whole pack was watching her. Sam was watching her. She didn't look back at any of them.

"There's a diner in Forks," Leah heard herself say. "On the main drag. Opens at six. The coffee's terrible. The pie's okay."

He didn't smile, which was lucky for him. He took a pack of cigarettes out of his jacket pocket, a pack that she was fairly sure hadn't been there a minute ago, and shook one loose, and held it out to her.

She looked at it.

She'd never smoked in her life. Her dad had smoked for twenty years and quit the year Seth was born, and he'd still died with his heart giving out in his chest in the kitchen.

She took it.

It lit itself the second it touched her lips, a small orange coal at the end. She breathed in. It was like inhaling a campfire. She bent double and coughed until her eyes ran.

"That's disgusting," she said, when she could.

"Yeah," he said.

She straightened up and took another drag, a small one. It was still disgusting.

Somewhere off to the west, at the far end of the field, the air cracked.

Then again. And again. A string of flat sharp bangs like a line of firecrackers, half a dozen, a dozen, and where each one went off there was suddenly a person standing in the snow. Men and women in long dark coats, carrying cases. Somebody with a stretcher folded under one arm. Somebody in white robes, who had to be the doctor. A tall woman at the front with iron-gray hair and a clipboard, already looking around the clearing at the ash and the wolves and the vampires with an expression that said she was going to need a much bigger clipboard.

Every wolf on the field came up off its haunches. Across the snow she heard Rosalie hiss and saw Edward step in front of the kid.

"Twelve minutes," Harry said. He checked his watch. "Son of a bitch. Early. That's a first."

He dropped the end of his cigarette in the slush. It hissed. He put his hands in his jacket pockets, and he started walking toward the woman with the clipboard, through the falling snow and the falling ash.

Leah stood there.

The fishhook pulled.

She thought about Jake behind her, and the kid, and Bella's face, and her mother at the kitchen table in her bathrobe at five a.m., waiting. She thought about how much she hated him. She thought about it pretty hard.

Then she dropped her own cigarette in the snow, and went after him.
